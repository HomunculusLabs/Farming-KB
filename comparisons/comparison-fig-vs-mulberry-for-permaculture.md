## Growth Rate and Size

Figs grow at a moderate to fast pace, typically reaching 3–6 m in cultivation but capable of 10 m in ideal conditions. Their spreading, multi-stemmed habit makes them excellent shade trees for small spaces. Mulberries are faster growers and significantly larger at maturity, with some species (especially white mulberry, *M. alba*) reaching 18 m or more. Mulberries can become dominant canopy trees in a food forest if unmanaged. For tight spaces, dwarf fig varieties and weeping mulberry cultivars exist.

## Wildlife Value

Both are outstanding wildlife trees. Figs produce fruit that attracts birds, squirrels, raccoons, and opossums. In Mediterranean climates, figs are keystone resources for many species. Mulberries are even more universally attractive — their early-summer fruit timing fills a critical gap when few other fruits are ripe. Both provide nesting sites and shelter; mulberries additionally serve as the sole food source for silkworms (*Bombyx mori*) and support numerous Lepidoptera species.

## Timber and Fodder Uses

Mulberry wood is dense, fine-grained, and moderately durable — used in furniture, cabinetry, fence posts, and wood turning. Fig wood is soft, brittle, and not useful for timber. Both species provide excellent fodder: fig leaves and fruit supplement poultry, goat, and pig diets, while mulberry leaves are among the highest-quality ruminant fodders available, with protein content rivaling alfalfa (15–28% crude protein).

## Pruning Needs

Figs benefit from annual pruning to maintain shape, remove dead wood, and encourage the main crop. They fruit on both old and new wood, giving flexibility. Mulberries are often left unpruned with no ill effect, but can be pollarded or coppiced to control size and maintain accessible harvest height. Pollarded mulberries produce long, straight shoots useful for fencing or garden stakes.

## Time to First Fruit and Lifespan

Figs often fruit within 1–2 years from cuttings, making them one of the fastest fruiting trees available. Mulberries from cuttings may fruit in 1–2 years; seed-grown trees take 3–5 years. Figs typically live 30–50 years in cultivation (centuries in their native range), while mulberries routinely live 50–100+ years, with some specimens exceeding 300 years.

## Suitability for Containers

Figs are superb container plants — their forgiving root systems, small mature size, and tolerance for root constriction make them ideal for patios and small spaces. Dwarf varieties like 'Petite Negra' thrive in 40–60 L containers. Mulberries are less suited to containers due to their larger size and more vigorous root systems, though weeping mulberry can work temporarily.

## Espalier Potential

Figs adapt well to informal espalier and fan training against warm walls, which also improves fruit ripening in cooler climates. Their flexible branches are easy to train. Mulberries can be espaliered but require more aggressive management due to vigorous, stiff growth — the effort-to-reward ratio is lower.

## Food Forest Placement

Both trees fit the canopy or sub-canopy layer. Figs are ideal near south-facing walls or buildings (heat reflection aids ripening). Mulberries work well as nitrogen-fixing companion understory trees (their leaf litter is nutrient-rich) or as pioneer trees on disturbed sites. In a multi-layered food forest, mulberries can serve as the upper canopy with figs, currants, and berries beneath.
