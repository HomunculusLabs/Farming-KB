# Straw Bale vs Cordwood Construction

## Overview

Straw bale and cordwood construction are two of the most accessible natural
building methods for owner-builders. Both use inexpensive or free materials to
create walls with above-average insulation, but they differ fundamentally in
structure, aesthetics, and the skills required. Straw bale walls are thick,
plastered monoliths offering superb thermal performance, while cordwood walls
reveal the exposed end-grain of short logs set in mortar, combining insulation
with significant thermal mass.

## Straw Bale Construction

[[cordwood-construction]]

Cordwood construction (also called stackwall or cordwood masonry) lays short
logs -- typically 16 to 24 inches long -- in beds of mortar like giant bricks.
The log ends remain exposed on both interior and exterior faces, producing a
distinctive polychromatic pattern that many builders consider the primary
aesthetic appeal. The technique has European roots over a thousand years old
and was widely used by North American settlers in the 1800s.

Softwoods are preferred: cedar, pine, spruce, and larch dry faster, shrink
less, and resist rot better than most hardwoods. Logs must be fully debarked,
split if over 6 inches in diameter, and dried to below 20% moisture content
before laying. The mortar bed typically includes soaked sawdust to improve
insulation and reduce weight. Lime-based mortars offer self-healing cracks and
better breathability than Portland cement mixes.

Cordwood walls are extremely heavy (100 to 150 lbs per square foot) and
require a robust foundation. A 24-inch wall achieves R-24 to R-30 depending
on insulation fill in the cavity between log ends. The mortar provides
significant thermal mass, making cordwood walls well-suited to passive solar
design where heat storage and slow release are desirable.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Straw Bale | Cordwood |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| Primary Material | Agricultural straw bales | Short log sections (cordwood) |
| Wall Thickness | 18 inches (standard bale) | 16 to 24 inches |
| Insulation (R-value) | R-30 to R-40 | R-16 to R-30 |
| Thermal Mass | Low (straw is lightweight) | High (mortar and wood) |
| Structural System | Load-bearing or post-and-beam infill | Masonry wall (self-supporting) |
| Wall Weight | Light (8 to 15 lbs/sq ft) | Heavy (100 to 150 lbs/sq ft) |
| Finish | Plaster (earthen, lime, or cement) | Exposed log ends, no finish needed |
| Fire Resistance | 2-hour rating when plastered | Moderate (wood is combustible) |
| Critical Vulnerability | Moisture (rot, mold) | Mortar separation from wood shrinkage |
| Foundation Requirement | Moderate (light walls) | Heavy-duty (massive walls) |
| Aesthetic | Smooth, plastered monolith | Exposed log-end mosaic pattern |
| Climate Suitability | Excellent in cold and temperate | Best where thermal mass is valued |
| Code Acceptance | IRC Appendix S in some areas | Generally no prescriptive code |
| Skill Level Required | Low to moderate (stacking, plastering) | Moderate (mortaring, leveling) |
| Typical Cost | Low to moderate | Low to moderate |

## When to Choose Straw Bale

Straw bale construction excels in cold climates where maximizing insulation
is the priority. The high R-value per dollar makes it one of the most
affordable paths to a super-insulated envelope. Owner-builders with minimal
carpentry experience can learn the basics in a weekend workshop. The method
shines for single-story homes, studios, and outbuildings where the thick
plastered walls create a quiet, soft, and naturally beautiful interior.

Choose straw bale when your site has access to affordable bales, your climate
demands high insulation, and you prefer (or need) lighter walls that do not
require an engineered foundation. It is also the better choice if you value
a smooth, seamless interior and exterior finish over an exposed-material
aesthetic.

## When to Choose Cordwood

Cordwood construction is ideal for builders who want thermal mass combined
with insulation -- a combination that works well with passive solar design in
climates with significant diurnal temperature swings. The exposed log ends
create a visually striking wall that requires no additional finish, which
appeals to builders who want the structure itself to be the finish surface.

Choose cordwood when you have access to abundant softwood from your own land,
are building in a rural area with relaxed code enforcement, and are willing
to invest in a heavier foundation. The method suits smaller structures,
outbuildings, and accent walls particularly well. It is also a strong choice
if you enjoy masonry work and want a building process that reveals beautiful
results course by course as you build.

## Hybrid Approaches

Some natural builders combine both methods in a single structure. A common
pattern uses cordwood for south-facing walls (gaining passive solar thermal
mass) and straw bale for north, east, and west walls (maximizing insulation
on sides with less solar gain). Cordwood also works well for interior
partition walls, fireplaces, and accent features within a straw bale
building, adding visual interest and thermal mass where needed.

Another hybrid approach uses a post-and-beam timber frame with a mix of straw
bale and cordwood infill in different bays, letting the builder tailor each
wall section to its solar orientation and functional requirements.
