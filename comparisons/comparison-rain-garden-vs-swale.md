# Rain Garden vs Swale

A comparison of two water-harvesting earthworks for managing stormwater and rehydrating landscapes.

## Overview

Rain gardens are shallow vegetated depressions designed to capture stormwater runoff from impervious surfaces, filtering pollutants and recharging groundwater through amended soil media. Swales are level channels built on contour that intercept surface water flow across slopes, spreading and infiltrating it to passively irrigate large landscapes.

Both are essential tools in regenerative land management, but they operate at different scales and serve distinct primary functions.

## Rain Garden Deep Dive

Rain gardens are designed as bioretention systems. Stormwater flows into a shallow depression, typically 4 to 8 inches deep, where it ponds temporarily and infiltrates through an engineered soil mix. This media filters sediments, absorbs nutrients, and breaks down pollutants.

The design centers on three moisture zones. The bottom zone supports wetland species like blue flag iris and cardinal flower. The sloped sides transition to moisture-tolerant species like bee balm and switchgrass. The upper edges host drought-tolerant natives.

Rain gardens are sized based on drainage area, soil infiltration rate, and desired ponding depth. A residential rain garden serving a 1,000 square foot roof might be 100 to 200 square feet in surface area, depending on native soil percolation rates.

## Rain Garden Construction

Construction begins with a percolation test to determine native soil infiltration rate. If the rate is slower than 0.5 inches per hour, install an underdrain to prevent prolonged ponding.

Key construction steps:

1. Excavate the bowl shape to designed depth
2. Install optional gravel reservoir and underdrain
3. Fill with engineered soil media
4. Plant according to the three-zone plan
5. Apply 2 to 3 inches of hardwood mulch
6. Install river rock at inlet to prevent erosion

A layer of double-shredded hardwood mulch suppresses weeds and retains moisture during establishment. Avoid dyed mulches that may leach chemicals. River rock at the inlet disperses water flow and prevents erosion.

Cost ranges from $3 to $15 per square foot depending on whether you install it yourself or hire a contractor. The primary materials expense is the engineered soil media, typically 60 percent sand, 20 percent topsoil, and 20 percent compost.

## Rain Garden Planting

Select plants native to your region for reliability and pollinator support. Strong choices for [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America include:

- Blue flag iris for the wet center zone
- Swamp milkweed for pollinators and monarchs
- New England aster for late-season color
- Cardinal flower for hummingbird attraction
- Blue vervain for vertical accent
- Native sedges and grasses for structure

Plant in drifts of 5 to 7 plants per species for visual impact. Space plants 12 to 18 inches apart and mulch thoroughly. Water regularly during the first [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. By year two, the rain garden should thrive on rainfall alone.

## Swale Deep Dive

Swales are level ditch-and-berm structures excavated on contour across slopes. Water flowing downslope enters the swale channel, spreads along its level length, and infiltrates into the soil profile. The downslope berm, built from excavated material, provides a planting zone.

Swales work at scale. A single swale on a gentle slope can intercept water across hundreds of feet of hillside, rehydrating the entire slope profile below it. Multiple swales in a staggered pattern create a series of infiltration zones that transform dry slopes.

The berm is where swale systems deliver their greatest productivity. Fruit trees planted on the berm have consistent access to deep soil moisture, reducing or eliminating irrigation needs. Nitrogen-fixing trees and shrubs on the berm build soil fertility.

## Swale Design Considerations

Spacing between swales depends on slope, soil type, and rainfall. A common guideline is one swale for every 10 to 20 feet of vertical elevation change. Steeper slopes require closer spacing while gentler slopes can space them farther apart.

A spillway at the lower end of each swale provides a controlled overflow path. Without a spillway, water will find the lowest point and carve an erosion channel. The spillway should be lined with rock and vegetation.

Swale depth typically ranges from 1 to 3 feet, with width proportional to expected water volume. A 3-foot-deep swale on a 5 percent slope with well-drained soil might be spaced 50 feet apart, while heavy clay soil might need swales every 20 feet.

## When to Choose Rain Garden

Choose a rain garden when your situation includes:

1. Urban or suburban lots with roof and driveway runoff
2. Impervious surfaces generating polluted stormwater
3. Local stormwater management requirements
4. Parking lot medians or roadside drainage
5. Desire to filter runoff before it reaches waterways
6. Limited space requiring compact water management
7. Community demonstration projects

Rain gardens are particularly effective at removing nitrogen, phosphorus, petroleum hydrocarbons, and sediments from urban runoff. They also provide pollinator habitat and aesthetic value.

## When to Choose Swale

Choose a swale when your priorities include:

1. Sloping land needing rehydration and [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]]
2. Tree crop establishment on hillsides
3. Arid or semi-arid broadacre water harvesting
4. [[mollison-designers-cool-humid-climate-design-overview]] slope stabilization
5. [[fukuoka-textdoc-agricultural-land-law-obstruction-return-farming]] with sheet erosion problems
6. Large properties with landscape-scale needs
7. Integration with [[query-how-does-keyline-design-work-in-permaculture]]

Swales transform how water moves through a landscape. Instead of concentrating and accelerating flow, they spread, slow, and sink water into the soil, creating underground reservoirs.

## Hybrid Approach

Many permaculture sites integrate both. Use swales on slopes to capture and distribute water, then place rain gardens at discharge points to filter remaining runoff before it reaches waterways.

This combination addresses water at multiple scales. Swales handle landscape-level distribution while rain gardens provide point-source filtration where concentrated runoff enters from built surfaces.

## Drawbacks and Limitations

Rain garden drawbacks include standing water attracting mosquitoes if drainage is too slow, limited capacity during extreme storms, need for engineered soil media, and potential failure if native soil has very low percolation rates.

Swale drawbacks include significant earthwork volume, risk of berm failure during extreme rainfall, unsuitability for flat terrain, and potential for landslides on steep unstable slopes if improperly designed.
