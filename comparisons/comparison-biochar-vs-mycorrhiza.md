     9|# Biochar vs Mycorrhiza
    10|
    11|## Overview
    12|Biochar and [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are two of the most powerful tools for building
    13|    13|    13|long-term soil health, but they work through entirely different mechanisms and
    14|    14|    14|operate on different timescales. Biochar is an inert carbon structure created
    15|    15|    15|through pyrolysis that improves soil physically and chemically by increasing
    16|    16|    16|water retention, cation exchange capacity, and providing microbial habitat.
    17|    17|    17|Mycorrhizal fungi are living organisms that form symbiotic partnerships with
    18|    18|    18|plant roots, actively mining nutrients and water from soil in exchange for plant
    19|    19|    19|carbohydrates. Understanding how they interact is critical because their effects
    20|    20|    20|are not just complementary but genuinely synergistic: biochar provides the
    21|    21|    21|habitat that supports mycorrhizal colonization.
    22|
    23|## Biochar
    24|[[biochar]]
    25|
    26|Biochar is produced by heating biomass in a low-oxygen environment through
    27|    27|    27|pyrolysis at temperatures ranging from 300 to 800 degrees Celsius. The resulting
    28|    28|    28|carbon-rich material has an extraordinarily high surface area of 100 to 500
    29|    29|    29|square meters per gram, with a negative charge that attracts and holds
    30|    30|    30|positively charged nutrients like calcium, magnesium, potassium, and ammonium.
    31|    31|    31|This cation exchange capacity (CEC) reduces nutrient leaching and keeps
    32|    32|    32|fertility in the root zone where plants can access it. Over time, biochar's CEC
    33|    33|    33|actually increases as weathering creates more oxidation sites on its surface.
    34|
    35|Beyond nutrient retention, biochar improves soil structure by creating stable
    36|    36|    36|aggregates, increases water-holding capacity by up to 20 percent in sandy soils,
    37|    37|    37|and provides a persistent habitat for beneficial microorganisms. Its porous
    38|    38|    38|structure creates microscopic refugia where bacteria, fungi, and protozoa are
    39|    39|    39|protected from predation by larger soil organisms. Biochar is essentially
    40|    40|    40|permanent in soil, persisting for hundreds to thousands of years, making it a
    41|    41|    41|one-time amendment with cumulative benefits that increase over decades. It also
    42|    42|    42|sequesters carbon permanently, making it a significant climate-change mitigation
    43|    43|    43|tool.
    44|
    45|However, raw biochar applied directly to soil can cause problems. Its high
    46|    46|    46|surface area and charge initially adsorb nutrients and water from surrounding
    47|    47|    47|soil, potentially creating a nutrient deficit zone that stunts young plants. For
    48|    48|    48|this reason, biochar should always be pre-charged or inoculated before
    49|    49|    49|application, either by soaking in compost tea, mixing with compost for several
    50|    50|    50|weeks, or combining with manure. The production process also matters:
    51|    51|    51|low-temperature biochar retains more volatile compounds that can be phytotoxic,
    52|    52|    52|while high-temperature biochar is more stable but has fewer functional groups
    53|    53|    53|for microbial colonization. See [[biochar-production-guide]] for production
    54|    54|    54|details.
    55|
    56|## Mycorrhiza
    57|[[mycorrhiza]]
    58|
    59|Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the roots of 90 percent or
    60|    60|    60|more of plant species. The fungal hyphae extend the effective root zone by 700
    61|    61|    61|to 1,000 times, mining phosphorus and other nutrients from soil that plant roots
    62|    62|    62|cannot reach. In exchange, the plant provides 10 to 20 percent of its
    63|    63|    63|photosynthetic carbon to the fungus. This partnership is one of the oldest and
    64|    64|    64|most widespread symbiotic relationships on Earth, dating back over 400 million
    65|    65|    65|years, and is arguably the most important mutualism in terrestrial ecosystems.
    66|
    67|Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), the type associated with most garden and
    68|    68|    68|agricultural plants, penetrate root cells and form arbuscule structures for
    69|    69|    69|nutrient exchange. They produce glomalin, a glycoprotein that contributes 27 to
    70|    70|    70|30 percent of soil carbon and is the primary biological glue creating stable
    71|    71|    71|soil aggregates that resist erosion and improve water infiltration.
    72|    72|    72|Ectomycorrhizae (ECM) form sheaths around tree root tips and are critical in
    73|    73|    73|forest ecosystems, often producing edible fruiting bodies. Both types provide
    74|    74|    74|pathogen protection through physical barriers and antibiotic production, and
    75|    75|    75|both extend plant access to water during drought.
    76|
    77|Mycorrhizal populations are sensitive to soil disturbance. Tillage breaks
    78|    78|    78|hyphal networks, high-phosphorus fertilizers suppress colonization since plants
    79|    79|    79|stop trading carbon when phosphorus is abundant, and certain fungicides are
    80|    80|    80|directly toxic. Building and maintaining mycorrhizal communities requires
    81|    81|    81|minimal soil disturbance, appropriate nutrient management, and living root
    82|    82|    82|networks maintained through cover crops or perennial plantings. Once
    83|    83|    83|established, mycorrhizal networks persist and improve soil structure
    84|    84|    84|continuously through glomalin production and hyphal growth and turnover. See
    85|    85|    85|[[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]] for establishing mycorrhizae in disturbed
    86|    86|    86|soils.

    88|## Key Differences
    89|| Aspect | Biochar | Mycorrhiza |
    90||--------|---------|------------|
    91|| Nature | Inert carbon structure | Living symbiotic organism |
    92|| Mechanism | Physical and chemical soil improvement | Biological nutrient
    93|    93|    93|exchange |
    94|| Persistence | Hundreds to thousands of years | Seasonal; requires living
    95|    95|    95|roots to persist |
    96|| Primary benefit | Nutrient retention and water holding | Nutrient mining
    97|    97|    97|and pathogen protection |
    98|| Input method | One-time soil amendment | Ongoing biological partnership |
    99|| Cost | Moderate (production or purchase) | Low (inoculant or native
   100|   100|   100|populations) |
   101|| Sensitivity to disturbance | Very low (permanent structure) | Very high
   102|   102|   102|(tillage destroys networks) |
   103|| Carbon contribution | Direct carbon sequestration | Glomalin and hyphal
   104|   104|   104|carbon cycling |
   105|| Soil structure role | Pore space, aggregation sites | Glomalin glues
   106|   106|   106|aggregates together |
   107|| Phosphorus role | Retains P in root zone | Mines P from locked soil forms |
   108|| Water role | Increases water holding capacity | Extends root access to deep
   109|   109|   109|moisture |

   111|## When to Choose Biochar
   112|Biochar is most valuable in sandy, low-organic-matter soils where nutrient
   113|   113|   113|leaching and poor water retention are primary constraints. It provides permanent
   114|   114|   114|infrastructure that improves soil physical properties regardless of biological
   115|   115|   115|activity. Biochar is also the preferred approach in contaminated soils where its
   116|   116|   116|adsorption capacity can bind heavy metals and organic pollutants, reducing their
   117|   117|   117|bioavailability to plants. In tropical soils where organic matter decomposes
   118|   118|   118|rapidly, biochar's stability provides long-lasting soil improvement. For
   119|   119|   119|carbon-conscious growers, biochar offers verifiable permanent carbon
   120|   120|   120|sequestration. See [[biochar-soil-remediation]] for contamination applications
   121|   121|   121|and [[biochar-soil-amendment]] for general soil improvement rates.
   122|
   123|## When to Choose Mycorrhizal Focus
   124|Mycorrhizal management is most critical in systems with living perennial root
   125|   125|   125|networks: orchards, food forests, pastures, no-till gardens, and perennial
   126|   126|   126|vegetable beds. Any system that minimizes soil disturbance and avoids excessive
   127|   127|   127|phosphorus fertilization will benefit from protecting and encouraging native
   128|   128|   128|mycorrhizal populations. Inoculation is worthwhile for transplanted crops,
   129|   129|   129|disturbed sites, and cannabis cultivation where phosphorus uptake directly
   130|   130|   130|affects yield and quality. [[mycorrhiza]] are also essential in low-fertility soils
   131|   131|   131|where their nutrient-mining capacity compensates for limited available
   132|   132|   132|nutrients. See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for the complete biology.
   133|
   134|   137|   137|ideal habitat for mycorrhizal hyphae and spores, protecting them from predation
   135|   138|   138|and desiccation. Pre-charging biochar with mycorrhizal inoculant and compost
   136|   139|   139|creates a long-term biological reservoir that benefits plants for years.
   137|   140|   140|Research shows that biochar-amended soils often support greater mycorrhizal
   138|   141|   141|colonization and root length, though the effect depends on biochar production
