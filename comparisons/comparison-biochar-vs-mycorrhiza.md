# Biochar vs Mycorrhiza

## Overview
Biochar and [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are two of the most powerful tools for building
long-term soil health, but they work through entirely different mechanisms and
operate on different timescales. Biochar is an inert carbon structure created
through pyrolysis that improves soil physically and chemically by increasing
water retention, cation exchange capacity, and providing microbial habitat.
Mycorrhizal fungi are living organisms that form symbiotic partnerships with
plant roots, actively mining nutrients and water from soil in exchange for plant
carbohydrates. Understanding how they interact is critical because their effects
are not just complementary but genuinely synergistic: biochar provides the
habitat that supports mycorrhizal colonization.

## Biochar
[[biochar]]

Biochar is produced by heating biomass in a low-oxygen environment through
pyrolysis at temperatures ranging from 300 to 800 degrees Celsius. The resulting
carbon-rich material has an extraordinarily high surface area of 100 to 500
square meters per gram, with a negative charge that attracts and holds
positively charged nutrients like calcium, magnesium, potassium, and ammonium.
This cation exchange capacity (CEC) reduces nutrient leaching and keeps
fertility in the root zone where plants can access it. Over time, biochar's CEC
actually increases as weathering creates more oxidation sites on its surface.

Beyond nutrient retention, biochar improves soil structure by creating stable
aggregates, increases water-holding capacity by up to 20 percent in sandy soils,
and provides a persistent habitat for beneficial microorganisms. Its porous
structure creates microscopic refugia where bacteria, fungi, and protozoa are
protected from predation by larger soil organisms. Biochar is essentially
permanent in soil, persisting for hundreds to thousands of years, making it a
one-time amendment with cumulative benefits that increase over decades. It also
sequesters carbon permanently, making it a significant climate-change mitigation
tool.

However, raw biochar applied directly to soil can cause problems. Its high
surface area and charge initially adsorb nutrients and water from surrounding
soil, potentially creating a nutrient deficit zone that stunts young plants. For
this reason, biochar should always be pre-charged or inoculated before
application, either by soaking in compost tea, mixing with compost for several
weeks, or combining with manure. The production process also matters:
low-temperature biochar retains more volatile compounds that can be phytotoxic,
while high-temperature biochar is more stable but has fewer functional groups
for microbial colonization. See [[biochar-production-guide]] for production
details.

## Mycorrhiza
[[mycorrhiza]]

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the roots of 90 percent or
more of plant species. The fungal hyphae extend the effective root zone by 700
to 1,000 times, mining phosphorus and other nutrients from soil that plant roots
cannot reach. In exchange, the plant provides 10 to 20 percent of its
photosynthetic carbon to the fungus. This partnership is one of the oldest and
most widespread symbiotic relationships on Earth, dating back over 400 million
years, and is arguably the most important mutualism in terrestrial ecosystems.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), the type associated with most garden and
agricultural plants, penetrate root cells and form arbuscule structures for
nutrient exchange. They produce glomalin, a glycoprotein that contributes 27 to
30 percent of soil carbon and is the primary biological glue creating stable
soil aggregates that resist erosion and improve water infiltration.
Ectomycorrhizae (ECM) form sheaths around tree root tips and are critical in
forest ecosystems, often producing edible fruiting bodies. Both types provide
pathogen protection through physical barriers and antibiotic production, and
both extend plant access to water during drought.

Mycorrhizal populations are sensitive to soil disturbance. Tillage breaks
hyphal networks, high-phosphorus fertilizers suppress colonization since plants
stop trading carbon when phosphorus is abundant, and certain fungicides are
directly toxic. Building and maintaining mycorrhizal communities requires
minimal soil disturbance, appropriate nutrient management, and living root
networks maintained through cover crops or perennial plantings. Once
established, mycorrhizal networks persist and improve soil structure
continuously through glomalin production and hyphal growth and turnover. See
[[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]] for establishing mycorrhizae in disturbed
soils.

## Key Differences
| Aspect | Biochar | Mycorrhiza |
|--------|---------|------------|
| Nature | Inert carbon structure | Living symbiotic organism |
| Mechanism | Physical and chemical soil improvement | Biological nutrient
exchange |
| Persistence | Hundreds to thousands of years | Seasonal; requires living
roots to persist |
| Primary benefit | Nutrient retention and water holding | Nutrient mining
and pathogen protection |
| Input method | One-time soil amendment | Ongoing biological partnership |
| Cost | Moderate (production or purchase) | Low (inoculant or native

| Sensitivity to disturbance | Very low (permanent structure) | Very high

| Carbon contribution | Direct carbon sequestration | Glomalin and hyphal
carbon cycling |
| Soil structure role | Pore space, aggregation sites | Glomalin glues
aggregates together |
| Phosphorus role | Retains P in root zone | Mines P from locked soil forms |
| Water role | Increases water holding capacity | Extends root access to deep
moisture |

## When to Choose Biochar
Biochar is most valuable in sandy, low-organic-matter soils where nutrient
leaching and poor water retention are primary constraints. It provides permanent
infrastructure that improves soil physical properties regardless of biological
activity. Biochar is also the preferred approach in contaminated soils where its
adsorption capacity can bind heavy metals and organic pollutants, reducing their
bioavailability to plants. In tropical soils where organic matter decomposes
rapidly, biochar's stability provides long-lasting soil improvement. For
carbon-conscious growers, biochar offers verifiable permanent carbon
sequestration. See [[biochar-soil-remediation]] for contamination applications
and [[biochar-soil-amendment]] for general soil improvement rates.

## When to Choose Mycorrhizal Focus
Mycorrhizal management is most critical in systems with living perennial root
networks: orchards, food forests, pastures, no-till gardens, and perennial
vegetable beds. Any system that minimizes soil disturbance and avoids excessive
phosphorus fertilization will benefit from protecting and encouraging native
mycorrhizal populations. Inoculation is worthwhile for transplanted crops,
disturbed sites, and cannabis cultivation where phosphorus uptake directly
