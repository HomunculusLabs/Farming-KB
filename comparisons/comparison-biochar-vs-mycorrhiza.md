# Biochar vs Mycorrhiza

## Overview
Biochar and [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are two of the most powerful tools for building
    13|long-term soil health, but they work through entirely different mechanisms and
    14|operate on different timescales. Biochar is an inert carbon structure created
    15|through pyrolysis that improves soil physically and chemically by increasing
    16|water retention, cation exchange capacity, and providing microbial habitat.
    17|Mycorrhizal fungi are living organisms that form symbiotic partnerships with
    18|plant roots, actively mining nutrients and water from soil in exchange for plant
    19|carbohydrates. Understanding how they interact is critical because their effects
    20|are not just complementary but genuinely synergistic: biochar provides the
    21|habitat that supports mycorrhizal colonization.

## Biochar
[[biochar]]

Biochar is produced by heating biomass in a low-oxygen environment through
    27|pyrolysis at temperatures ranging from 300 to 800 degrees Celsius. The resulting
    28|carbon-rich material has an extraordinarily high surface area of 100 to 500
    29|square meters per gram, with a negative charge that attracts and holds
    30|positively charged nutrients like calcium, magnesium, potassium, and ammonium.
    31|This cation exchange capacity (CEC) reduces nutrient leaching and keeps
    32|fertility in the root zone where plants can access it. Over time, biochar's CEC
    33|actually increases as weathering creates more oxidation sites on its surface.

Beyond nutrient retention, biochar improves soil structure by creating stable
    36|aggregates, increases water-holding capacity by up to 20 percent in sandy soils,
    37|and provides a persistent habitat for beneficial microorganisms. Its porous
    38|structure creates microscopic refugia where bacteria, fungi, and protozoa are
    39|protected from predation by larger soil organisms. Biochar is essentially
    40|permanent in soil, persisting for hundreds to thousands of years, making it a
    41|one-time amendment with cumulative benefits that increase over decades. It also
    42|sequesters carbon permanently, making it a significant climate-change mitigation
    43|tool.

However, raw biochar applied directly to soil can cause problems. Its high
    46|surface area and charge initially adsorb nutrients and water from surrounding
    47|soil, potentially creating a nutrient deficit zone that stunts young plants. For
    48|this reason, biochar should always be pre-charged or inoculated before
    49|application, either by soaking in compost tea, mixing with compost for several
    50|weeks, or combining with manure. The production process also matters:
    51|low-temperature biochar retains more volatile compounds that can be phytotoxic,
    52|while high-temperature biochar is more stable but has fewer functional groups
    53|for microbial colonization. See [[biochar-production-guide]] for production
    54|details.

## Mycorrhiza
[[mycorrhiza]]

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the roots of 90 percent or
    60|more of plant species. The fungal hyphae extend the effective root zone by 700
    61|to 1,000 times, mining phosphorus and other nutrients from soil that plant roots
    62|cannot reach. In exchange, the plant provides 10 to 20 percent of its
    63|photosynthetic carbon to the fungus. This partnership is one of the oldest and
    64|most widespread symbiotic relationships on Earth, dating back over 400 million
    65|years, and is arguably the most important mutualism in terrestrial ecosystems.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), the type associated with most garden and
    68|agricultural plants, penetrate root cells and form arbuscule structures for
    69|nutrient exchange. They produce glomalin, a glycoprotein that contributes 27 to
    70|30 percent of soil carbon and is the primary biological glue creating stable
    71|soil aggregates that resist erosion and improve water infiltration.
    72|Ectomycorrhizae (ECM) form sheaths around tree root tips and are critical in
    73|forest ecosystems, often producing edible fruiting bodies. Both types provide
    74|pathogen protection through physical barriers and antibiotic production, and
    75|both extend plant access to water during drought.

Mycorrhizal populations are sensitive to soil disturbance. Tillage breaks
    78|hyphal networks, high-phosphorus fertilizers suppress colonization since plants
    79|stop trading carbon when phosphorus is abundant, and certain fungicides are
    80|directly toxic. Building and maintaining mycorrhizal communities requires
    81|minimal soil disturbance, appropriate nutrient management, and living root
    82|networks maintained through cover crops or perennial plantings. Once
    83|established, mycorrhizal networks persist and improve soil structure
    84|continuously through glomalin production and hyphal growth and turnover. See
    85|[[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]] for establishing mycorrhizae in disturbed
    86|soils.

## When to Choose Biochar
Biochar is most valuable in sandy, low-organic-matter soils where nutrient
   113|leaching and poor water retention are primary constraints. It provides permanent
   114|infrastructure that improves soil physical properties regardless of biological
   115|activity. Biochar is also the preferred approach in contaminated soils where its
   116|adsorption capacity can bind heavy metals and organic pollutants, reducing their
   117|bioavailability to plants. In tropical soils where organic matter decomposes
   118|rapidly, biochar's stability provides long-lasting soil improvement. For
   119|carbon-conscious growers, biochar offers verifiable permanent carbon
   120|sequestration. See [[biochar-soil-remediation]] for contamination applications
   121|and [[biochar-soil-amendment]] for general soil improvement rates.

## When to Choose Mycorrhizal Focus
Mycorrhizal management is most critical in systems with living perennial root
   125|networks: orchards, food forests, pastures, no-till gardens, and perennial
   126|vegetable beds. Any system that minimizes soil disturbance and avoids excessive
   127|phosphorus fertilization will benefit from protecting and encouraging native
   128|mycorrhizal populations. Inoculation is worthwhile for transplanted crops,
   129|disturbed sites, and cannabis cultivation where phosphorus uptake directly
   130|affects yield and quality. [[mycorrhiza]] are also essential in low-fertility soils
   131|where their nutrient-mining capacity compensates for limited available
