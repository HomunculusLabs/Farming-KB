# Pigeon Pea vs Cowpea

Pigeon pea (pigeon pea) and cowpea (cowpea) are two of the most important tropical legumes in smallholder farming systems worldwide. Both fix atmospheric nitrogen, thrive in poor soils, and provide high-protein food and fodder. However, they differ markedly in growth cycle, water requirements, and agronomic role. This page provides a detailed side-by-side comparison to help farmers, permaculture designers, and agronomists choose the right crop for their context.

## When to Choose Pigeon Pea

Pigeon pea is the better choice when:

- **Longer growing season is available** (≥4 months). Its extended growth cycle produces deep taproots (up to 2 m) that break hardpans and improve soil structure over multiple seasons.
- **Perennial biomass production** is desired. In [[permaculture]] systems, pigeon pea serves as a living hedge, windbreak, and multi-year nitrogen source.
- **Dual-purpose food/fodder** is needed over a long window. Fresh green pods can be harvested for 2–3 months before dry grain matures.
- **Integration with cereal intercropping** at wider row spacings (e.g., 150 cm alleys) is planned. Pigeon pea's slow early growth avoids competition with cereals.
- **Marginal, moderately saline soils** need rehabilitation. Pigeon pea tolerates EC up to ~6 dS/m.

Pigeon pea also excels in systems where its woody stems are valued for firewood or mulch (2–4 t/ha of woody residue).

> **Key metric:** A single pigeon pea plant can fix 30–45 kg of nitrogen over its lifetime in perennial systems, making it one of the most productive legume nitrogen contributors per plant.

## When to Choose Cowpea

Cowpea is the better choice when:

- **Rapid turnaround** is essential. With maturity in as few as 55–60 days, cowpea fits into tight rotational windows and double-cropping schedules.
- **Severe drought** is expected. Cowpea requires less total water and exhibits superior osmotic adjustment under water stress compared to pigeon pea.
- **Strongly acidic soils** (pH 4.5–5.5) dominate the landscape. Cowpea's aluminum tolerance outperforms most legumes in this range.
- **Striga suppression** is needed. Cowpea root exudates have been shown to reduce *Striga hermonthica* germination by 40–60% in maize-cowpea intercropping trials.
- **Erosion control on sloping land** is a priority. Cowpea's trailing habit provides faster ground cover (full canopy in 30–45 days).
- **High seed protein** (up to 27%) is a nutritional priority for human consumption or livestock feed.

Cowpea's shorter cycle also makes it an ideal catch crop following a failed cereal harvest or as a green manure crop turned under within 45–60 days.

> **Key metric:** Cowpea can produce 2–3 t/ha of biomass in just 45 days when used as a green manure, contributing an estimated 50–80 kg N/ha in that short window alone.

## Can They Be Intercropped Together?

Yes — pigeon pea and cowpea can be successfully intercropped, and the combination is practiced in parts of India, East Africa, and Brazil. The key agronomic rationale is **temporal niche separation**:

- Cowpea matures and is harvested in 60–90 days, freeing resources for pigeon pea.
- Pigeon pea's slow early growth avoids competition with the faster cowpea.
- Combined nitrogen fixation can reach 200–350 kg N/ha in favorable conditions.
- Total Land Equivalent Ratio (LER) values of 1.3–1.6 have been recorded, indicating 30–60% more efficient land use than sole cropping.

**Recommended intercrop configurations:**

| System | Pigeon pea rows | Cowpea rows | Row spacing | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1:1 strip | 1 | 1 | 75 cm | Simple; cowpea harvested first |
| 2:1 alley | 2 | 1 | 100 cm pigeon / 50 cm cowpea | Higher pigeon pea density; suited to dhal production |
| 1:2 relay | 1 (perennial) | 2 (each season) | 150 cm pigeon / 50 cm cowpea | Perennial pigeon pea hedge with seasonal cowpea understory |

**Challenges:** Both crops share susceptibility to Fusarium wilt, so avoid intercropping in fields with known wilt history. Rhizobial competition between *Bradyrhizobium* strains may reduce effective nodulation on one or both species — inoculate both with specific strains. Pod borers (*Helicoverpa armigera*) that attack pigeon pea can also move to cowpea during overlap periods, so monitor pest populations closely and consider integrated pest management (IPM) strategies including pheromone traps and targeted biopesticides (e.g., *Nucleopolyhedrovirus* sprays).

## Variety Recommendations by Region

### South Asia (India, Bangladesh, Nepal)
- **Pigeon pea:** ICPL 87119 (medium-duration, Fusarium wilt-resistant), UPAS 120 (early, 120 days), LRG 41 (late, high-yielding dhal type).
- **Cowpea:** C-152 (dual-purpose grain + fodder), Pusa Komal (early, 55 days), VBN 1 (high protein, 26%).

### Sub-Saharan Africa
- **Pigeon pea:** ICEAP 00040 (short-duration, drought-tolerant), ICEAP 00557 (medium, adapted to 600–800 mm rainfall), Afyavi (Malawi-released, 160 days).
- **Cowpea:** IT89KD-288 (drought-tolerant, 65 days), IT97K-499-35 (Striga-resistant), B301 (iron-tolerant for West African soils), KVu 277 (Kenya, dual-purpose).

### Latin America & Caribbean
- **Pigeon pea:** ICP 7035 (Brazil, short-season), Habanero (Cuba, traditional), Aparicio (Dominican Republic).
- **Cowpea:** BR 17 Gurguéia (Brazil, semi-arid adapted), IT84S-2246 (widely adapted), CLI-72 (Caribbean, high biomass).

### Australia (Northern Territory & Queensland)
- **Pigeon pea:** Quest (grain type, 140 days), Amanzi (forage type, high biomass).
- **Cowpea:** CPI 28215 (grain), Red Caloona (widely adapted green manure), Murray (fodder).

### Southern USA (Texas, Florida, Georgia)
- **Pigeon pea:** Not widely commercialized; research lines from USDA-GRIN (PI accession series) show promise for cover crop use.
- **Cowpea:** Iron and Clay (cover crop standard, ≥90% hard seed), Red Ripper (grain + cover, vigorous), Texas Cream (edible green pod).

## Final Verdict

Neither pigeon pea nor cowpea is universally superior — the right choice depends on **time horizon, soil conditions, climate, and system objectives**.

- **Choose pigeon pea** for multi-year perennials, deeper soil improvement, hedge/windbreak functions, and systems where a longer commitment (4–8 months to grain harvest) is acceptable. Its woody biomass and perennial nature make it uniquely valuable in [[agroforestry]] and [[permaculture]] designs.

- **Choose cowpea** for speed, flexibility, extreme drought resilience, acidic soil tolerance, and situations where rapid ground cover or a short-season catch crop is needed. Its broader climatic adaptation and higher seed protein give it the edge in food-insecure, moisture-limited environments.

- **Use both together** in intercrop or relay systems to maximize temporal resource use, total nitrogen fixation, and yield stability across variable seasons.

For most smallholder tropical systems, a **cowpea–pigeon pea relay** (perennial pigeon pea hedge with seasonal cowpea understory) offers the best of both worlds: rapid food and soil protection from cowpea, sustained fertility and woody products from pigeon pea.
