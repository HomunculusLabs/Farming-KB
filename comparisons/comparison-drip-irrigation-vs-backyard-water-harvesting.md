# Drip Irrigation vs Backyard Water Harvesting

## Overview

Drip irrigation and backyard water harvesting represent two fundamentally
different philosophies of garden water management. Drip irrigation is a
precision delivery system that puts water exactly where plants need it,
minimizing waste through targeted application. Backyard water harvesting
takes a broader approach, capturing and storing rainwater in the landscape
itself through earthworks, soil building, and ecological design so that
irrigation becomes largely unnecessary. Understanding both approaches helps
gardeners choose the right strategy for their climate, budget, and goals.

## Drip Irrigation

[[drip-irrigation]]

Drip irrigation delivers water directly to plant root zones through a
network of emitters, tubes, and valves. It reduces evaporation by 50-90%
compared to sprinkler systems and is considered by Bill Mollison the most
effective dryland garden watering method. Systems range from high-tech
automated setups with soil moisture sensors to ultra-low-tech solutions
like unglazed earthen jars and inverted bottles.

Water use drops to 10-50% of sprinkler consumption in orchard and tree
establishment. Only low head pressure is needed, and normal household
wastewater can supply a good-sized home garden through underground seepage
pipes. The cost amortizes within months even for modest households given
the food produced.

Cautions include salt accumulation above 800 ppm on bare ground and the
need for filters to prevent emitter blockage. Stand pipes at root level
help prevent salt buildup in saline conditions.

## Backyard Water Harvesting

[[backyard-water-harvesting]]

Backyard water harvesting is a design philosophy that integrates water
capture and storage into the garden ecosystem itself. Rather than piping
water to plants, it shapes the land to slow, spread, and sink rainwater
where it falls. The five core techniques are: building organic matter and
soil (which holds several times its weight in water), contour swales,
basins and dishes, fishscale swales on slopes, and deep mulching.

A well-designed water-harvesting garden cycles toward lush growth without
constant irrigation. One foot of rich, moist soil holds as much water as
a 3-inch-deep lake of the same area. Compost can reduce irrigation needs
by 75% compared to soils with less than 1% organic matter. Backyard
wetlands at the lowest property point serve both water storage and habitat
functions.

The approach works at any scale. Even a modest 6-inch swale along a
property line significantly reduces runoff and builds soil moisture.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Drip Irrigation | Backyard Water Harvesting |
|--------|----------------|--------------------------|
| Approach | Active delivery to plants | Passive capture in landscape |
| Water source | Municipal, well, tank | Rainfall and runoff |
| Setup cost | Moderate (tubes, emitters) | Low to moderate (earthworks) |
| Maintenance | Filters, emitters, timers | Swales, mulch, soil building |
| Best for | Precise crop watering | Whole-garden resilience |
| Dryland use | Excellent with any supply | Depends on rainfall volume |
| Automation | Full automation possible | Largely self-sustaining |
| Learning curve | Moderate (plumbing skills) | Moderate (design skills) |

## When to Choose Drip Irrigation

Choose drip irrigation when growing high-value crops that need precise
moisture control, such as vegetables in raised beds, cannabis, or fruit
trees in arid regions. It excels where water supply is limited, expensive,
or saline. Drip is the clear winner for greenhouse and enclosed growing
where passive water capture is impossible. Gardeners with existing plumbing
infrastructure can add drip systems incrementally with low cost and
immediate results. It pairs well with fertigation, delivering nutrients
directly to root zones alongside water.

## When to Choose Backyard Water Harvesting

Choose backyard water harvesting when designing a new garden or
rehabilitating a landscape for long-term self-sufficiency. It suits
permaculture food forests, polycultures, and any situation where the
gardener wants to reduce or eliminate dependence on external water
supplies. It is ideal for sloped properties where runoff is a problem
and for regions with seasonal but substantial rainfall. The approach
builds soil health as a co-benefit, creating a virtuous cycle of
improved water retention over time.

## Combined Approaches

The most resilient gardens combine both strategies. Use earthworks and
soil building to capture and store rainwater passively, then add drip
irrigation as a supplement during extended dry periods. Mollison himself
noted that drip systems can use household greywater directed to
underground seepage pipes, effectively harvesting water that would
otherwise be wasted. A swale system with drip emitters along the berm
provides both broad moisture retention and targeted delivery. During wet
seasons the passive system handles everything; during drought the drip
system activates to bridge the gap.

## Decision Guide

| Your Situation | Best Choice |
|----------------|-------------|
| Arid climate, limited rainfall | Drip irrigation |
| Temperate with seasonal rain | Water harvesting |
| Greenhouse or indoor grow | Drip irrigation |
| New permaculture design | Water harvesting first |
| Existing garden, adding efficiency | Drip add-on |
| Sloped property with erosion | Water harvesting |
| Saline water supply | Drip with stand pipes |
| Budget under $50 | Low-tech drip or swales |
