# Nettle vs Comfrey

## Overview

Stinging nettle (*Urtica dioica*) and comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*) are the
two most celebrated dynamic accumulator plants in permaculture. Both mine
minerals from deep subsoil layers, produce nutrient-rich biomass for mulch and
fertilizer, support beneficial insect populations, and offer significant
medicinal value. Yet they differ markedly in their growth habits, nutritional
profiles, practical uses, and management requirements. Understanding these
differences allows growers to choose the right plant for each function, or to
combine both for maximum soil-building benefit.

## Stinging Nettle

[[query-how-to-grow-and-use-stinging-nettle]]

Nettle is a herbaceous perennial that spreads aggressively through rhizomes,
forming dense stands in moist, nitrogen-rich soils. It typically grows 1 to 2
meters tall and produces multiple harvests per season when cut back. The plant
is both a food crop and a medicinal herb: young leaves are rich in protein,
iron, calcium, and vitamins A, C, and K, making them one of the most nutritious
wild greens available. Nettle leaf tea is a traditional remedy for allergies,
anemia, and inflammation, while nettle root extract is clinically validated for
benign prostatic hyperplasia.

As a dynamic accumulator, nettle excels at pulling nitrogen, iron, calcium,
magnesium, and potassium from the soil. Fermented nettle tea (made by steeping
fresh nettle in water for 2 to 4 weeks) produces a nitrogen-rich liquid
fertilizer that promotes vigorous leafy growth. Nettle's spreading root system
stabilizes soil and its foliage supports over 40 insect species, including
several butterflies whose larvae feed exclusively on it. The primary
disadvantages are its aggressive spread via rhizomes and its painful stinging
hairs, which require gloves for handling.

## Comfrey

[[comfrey-as-dynamic-accumulator]]

Comfrey is a deep-rooted perennial that produces enormous quantities of
biomass from a dense, clumping root system. The roots can extend 2 meters or
more into the subsoil, mining potassium, phosphorus, calcium, and trace
minerals from depths inaccessible to most garden plants. Bocking 14, a sterile
cultivar developed in the 1950s, is the most widely recommended variety because
it does not set seed and cannot spread invasively. Comfrey leaves can be cut
4 to 6 times per season, with each plant producing several kilograms of
biomass annually.

Comfrey's primary permaculture function is as a biomass engine for chop-and-drop
mulch and compost activation. The leaves have a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of
approximately 12:1, meaning they decompose quickly and release nutrients into
the soil without drawing nitrogen from surrounding soil organisms (a risk with
carbon-rich mulches). Comfrey tea is potassium-rich, making it particularly
valuable for fruiting and flowering crops. Medicinally, comfrey contains
allantoin, a compound that promotes cell proliferation, and has been used
topically for centuries to heal wounds, fractures, and skin conditions.
However, internal use of comfrey is controversial due to hepatotoxic
pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) present in the roots and to a lesser extent in
the leaves.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Nettle | Comfrey |
|--------|--------|---------|
| **Growth Habit** | Rhizomatous, spreading | Clumping, non-spreading (Bocking 14) |
| **Root Depth** | 0.5 to 1.5 meters | 1.5 to 3+ meters |
| **Primary Nutrients** | Nitrogen, iron, calcium | Potassium, phosphorus, calcium |
| **Edible Leaves** | Yes, highly nutritious | No, contains PAs |
| **Medicinal Safety** | Generally safe internally | Topical only; PAs are hepatotoxic |
| **Biomass Production** | Moderate, multiple cuts | Very high, 4 to 6 cuts per season |
| **Insect Support** | Butterfly host plant | Bee forage (flowers) |
| **Fiber Crop** | Yes, bast fiber for textiles | No |
| **Spread Control** | Requires root barriers | Self-contained (sterile cultivars) |
| **Liquid Fertilizer** | High nitrogen (leaf growth) | High potassium (fruit/flower) |
| **Shade Tolerance** | High, grows in full shade | Moderate, prefers full sun |
| **Drought Tolerance** | Moderate | High once established |

## When to Choose Nettle

Nettle is the better choice when you need an edible nutrient-dense green, a
medicinal herb for internal use, a butterfly host plant, or a nitrogen-rich
fertilizer. Its shade tolerance makes it ideal for forest garden understories
and shaded garden corners where other plants struggle. Nettle excels in
riparian zones and damp areas, where its spreading habit provides soil
stabilization along waterways. Choose nettle when you want a multi-functional
plant that feeds both humans and soil simultaneously.

## When to Choose Comfrey

Comfrey is the better choice when maximum biomass production is the priority.
Its deeper root system accesses minerals from greater depth, and its high
potassium content makes it the preferred fertilizer for fruit trees, tomatoes,
peppers, and other fruiting crops. The sterile Bocking cultivars are
self-contained and will not spread, making comfrey safer for placement near
garden beds and between rows. Choose comfrey when you need a reliable,
low-maintenance mulch crop that produces large quantities of organic matter
without any risk of invasive spread.

## Hybrid Approaches

Many experienced permaculture designers plant both species for complementary
benefits. A common pattern is to establish comfrey around fruit tree drip lines
for potassium-rich mulch and to allow nettle patches in designated areas for
food, medicine, and nitrogen-rich fertilizer. The two plants can be combined
in compost piles: comfrey provides the bulk carbonaceous material and
potassium, while nettle supplies nitrogen and accelerates decomposition. In a
fermented liquid fertilizer, blending both plants produces a balanced tea with
both nitrogen (from nettle) and potassium (from comfrey), suitable for all
growth stages.
