# Reciprocal Roof vs Timber Frame

## Overview
Reciprocal roofs and timber frames represent two distinct structural
    philosophies for roof construction. The reciprocal roof uses a
    self-supporting spiral of rafters where each member rests on and supports
    the next, eliminating the need for a central column or ridge beam and
    creating a dramatic open interior. Timber framing uses large timbers
    joined with precise mortise and tenon connections in a rectilinear grid of
    bents and plates, offering centuries of engineering precedent and extreme
    durability. The choice shapes the building geometry, aesthetic character,
    material requirements, and construction process.

## Reciprocal Roof Frame
[[reciprocal-roof-frame]]

A reciprocal roof is a self-supporting structure where rafters are
    arranged in a spiraling circle, each resting on the one before it in a
    closed loop of mutual support. The design eliminates the need for a
    central column or ridge beam, creating a striking open interior with a
    central oculus that can serve as a skylight, glazed cap, or open vent for
    a living roof. Common configurations use eight to sixteen rafters, with
    rafter length calculated from the building diameter minus the desired
    oculus size, plus overhang.

Reciprocal roofs are particularly well-suited to roundwood (unmilled
    poles) from locally sourced woodland thinnings. Species like locust, oak,
    larch, and Douglas fir provide excellent strength, and green woodworking
    techniques allow relatively easy cutting and shaping. Construction
    involves laying rafters one by one over a temporary center support post,
    with the structure becoming self-supporting once the final rafter closes
    the loop. Joinery is simpler than timber framing, relying on lapped
    notches secured with pegs, screws, or natural fiber lashings. The
    reciprocal frame pairs naturally with round buildings, earthbag walls,
    cob, and living roofs.

## Timber Frame
[[timber-frame-basics]]

Timber framing is a traditional building system using large timbers
    joined with wooden connections rather than nails or metal fasteners. The
    structural system organizes timbers into bents, which are cross-sectional
    assemblies spaced twelve to sixteen feet apart and connected by
    longitudinal plates and girders. Joinery relies on mortise and tenon
    connections in various configurations (through, blind, shouldered,
    dovetail, bridle, and birdsmouth joints) secured with hardwood pegs using
    the draw-boring technique for tight, lasting connections.

Timber framing has centuries of engineering precedent across European
    half-timber, Japanese post-and-beam, and New England barn traditions. The
    craft requires significant skill in layout, cutting, and joinery, but
    produces extremely strong and durable structures with many centuries-old
    examples still standing. Timber frames accommodate any rectangular
    footprint and work with diverse enclosure systems including straw bale,
    hempcrete, cob, light straw-clay, and structural insulated panels. The
    exposed timber aesthetic is a primary attraction, with oak, Douglas fir,
    eastern white pine, and reclaimed timber among the common material
    choices.

## Key Differences
| Aspect | Reciprocal Roof | Timber Frame |
|--------|-----------------|--------------|
| Geometry | Round or polygonal only | Rectangular (any shape) |
| Central Support | None (self-supporting loop) | Posts and columns
    required |
| Interior Space | Open center with oculus | Open bays defined by posts
    |
| Material Preference | Roundwood (unmilled poles) | Milled dimensional
    timber |
| Joinery Complexity | Simple (lapped notches, pegs) | Complex (mortise
    and tenon) |
| Skill Level Required | Moderate (beginner accessible) | High
    (specialized joinery skills) |
| Engineering Precedent | Limited (less standardized) | Extensive
    (centuries of data) |
| Aesthetic Character | Organic, sculptural, round | Classical,
    structured, rectilinear |
| Material Cost | Very low (local roundwood) | Moderate to high (milled
    timbers) |
| Wall Compatibility | Round buildings (earthbag, cob) | Rectangular
    (straw bale, SIPs, stick) |

## When to Choose a Reciprocal Roof
Choose a reciprocal roof when designing a round or polygonal building
    where an open, column-free interior with a dramatic central opening is
    desired. Reciprocal roofs are ideal for eco-village structures, garden
    pavilions, meditation spaces, yurts, greenhouses, and any natural building
    project using locally sourced roundwood. The simpler joinery and lower
    material cost make reciprocal roofs accessible to beginner builders
    working with green woodworking techniques. The central oculus provides
    natural light and ventilation, and the frame pairs beautifully with living
    roofs that benefit from the circular water-shedding geometry.

## When to Choose Timber Frame
Choose timber framing when building a rectilinear structure that
    demands proven structural performance, large clear spans, and the
    classical beauty of exposed timber joinery. Timber frames are the
    appropriate choice for homes, barns, and workshops where engineering
    rigor, durability, and design flexibility are priorities. The centuries of
    precedent provide confidence for code compliance and structural
    calculations. Timber frames pair with the widest variety of enclosure
    systems and can incorporate reclaimed timber for environmental and
    aesthetic benefits. The investment in skilled joinery produces a structure
    that can stand for generations.

## Hybrid Approaches
Creative builders combine elements of both systems. A timber frame
    rectilinear structure can incorporate a reciprocal roof tower or cupola,
    adding an organic focal point to an otherwise classical building.
    Conversely, a round reciprocal-roof building can use timber frame joinery
    for doors, windows, and connection points where the precision of mortise
    and tenon is advantageous. The living roof systems that pair naturally
    with reciprocal roofs can also be installed on timber frame structures
    with proper engineering, and roundwood reciprocal rafters can be used as
    secondary roof elements within a timber frame primary structure.
