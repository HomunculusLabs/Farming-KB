# Earthbag vs Cob Construction

Earthbag and cob are two of the most accessible [[query-how-do-i-choose-a-natural-building-method-for-my-climate]] methods
available to owner-builders. Both use earth as their primary material,
both require minimal manufactured inputs, and both can be learned through
workshops and self-study rather than formal engineering training. However,
they differ fundamentally in their construction process, structural behavior,
and the types of buildings they are best suited for. [[comparison-earthbag-construction-vs-cob-construction]]
fills polypropylene bags or tubes with moistened earth and lays them in
courses like oversized masonry, while cob mixes earth, sand, and straw into
a monolithic sculptable material built up by hand.

## Earthbag Construction

[[cob-building]]
Cob is one of the oldest building methods in human history, with cob
buildings in Devon, England dating back to the 15th century and still in
habitation today. The word "cob" comes from an Old English word meaning
"lump" or "rounded mass," which accurately describes the building process:
a mix of clay-rich subsoil, sand, and straw fibers is kneaded together
with water (traditionally by foot on a tarp) and formed into lumps that
are pressed onto the growing wall by hand.

Cob walls are monolithic — they have no joints, courses, or weak planes.
This gives them exceptional compressive strength and excellent resistance
to shear forces. Cob buildings have survived earthquakes that destroyed
conventional buildings, precisely because the monolithic wall acts as a
single unit rather than a collection of separate elements. The straw fibers
in the cob mix provide tensile strength, preventing cracking and
distributing stress throughout the wall mass.

The sculptability of cob is one of its most celebrated qualities. Cob can be
shaped into any form: curved walls, built-in furniture, niches, arches, and
sculptural elements are all possible without additional materials.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Earthbag | Cob |
|--------|----------|-----|
| Primary material | Bags + earth fill | Earth + sand + straw |
| Structural system | Courses of filled bags | Monolithic mass |
| Soil requirements | Very flexible | Needs 15-25% clay |
| Wall thickness | 300-450mm typical | 450-600mm typical |
| Learning curve | Moderate | Moderate |
| Speed of construction | Fast (bag filling) | Slow (hand-forming) |
| Sculptural freedom | Low | Very high |
| Seismic performance | Good (with barbed wire) | Excellent (monolithic) |
| Cost per square meter | Very low | Very low |
| Ideal climates | Arid to temperate | Temperate to mild |
| Waterproofing needs | High (bags degrade) | Moderate (render needed) |
| Foundation requirements | Rubble trench or reinforced | Rubble trench typical |
| Roof attachment | Ring beam or bond beam | Plate or ring beam |

## When to Choose Earthbag

Earthbag construction is the better choice when on-site soil is poor or
unpredictable, when building in seismic zones (the barbed wire reinforcement
provides ductility), or when the building design includes domes and vaults
that require precise curvature. Earthbag is also preferred for disaster-relief
construction and community projects where speed and material availability are
paramount. The method works well for retaining walls, root cellars, and
below-grade structures where the bag system provides excellent resistance to
lateral earth pressure.

Earthbag walls need a durable exterior render because the polypropylene bags
are vulnerable to UV degradation and physical damage. A lime or earthen
render applied over mesh is essential for long-term durability.

## When to Choose Cob

Cob is the better choice when artistic expression and sculptural freedom
are priorities, when the building site has suitable clay-rich soil, and
when the owner-builder wants the most hands-on, intimate building
experience possible. Cob construction is deeply satisfying as a process
— many cob builders describe it as meditative and connective — and the
resulting walls have a warmth and character that is difficult to replicate
with any other material.

Cob walls are thick and massive, providing excellent thermal mass for
[[comparison-passive-solar-vs-earth-sheltered]] heating and cooling. In temperate climates, cob buildings
stay cool in summer and warm in winter with minimal supplementary heating.
However, cob has limited insulation value (approximately R-0.5 to R-1.0 per
inch), so in very cold climates, additional insulation strategies may be
needed. Cob is also not ideal for very rainy climates unless protected by
wide roof overhangs, raised foundations, and breathable renders.

## Hybrid Approaches

Many natural builders combine earthbag and cob techniques in the same
building. A common hybrid uses earthbag walls for the lower courses
(foundation to window sill level) where moisture resistance and structural
strength are most critical, then transitions to cob for the upper walls
where sculptural detail and window and door framing are desired. Another
hybrid uses earthbag domes for structural roofs and cob walls for the
living spaces below.

A common hybrid uses earthbag for the first 1-3 courses as a
moisture-resistant base, then transitions to cob above. This eliminates
the need for a conventional concrete foundation and the transition is
straightforward: the bags are tamped and topped with cob that keys into
the irregular bag surface.
