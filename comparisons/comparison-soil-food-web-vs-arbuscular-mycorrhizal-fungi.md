# Soil Food Web vs Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi

## Overview
The soil food web describes the entire ecosystem of organisms in soil, from
bacteria and fungi through protozoa, nematodes, and higher predators, all
interacting through trophic relationships. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF)
are a specific group within this web that form symbiotic relationships with
plant roots, trading nutrients for carbon. The soil food web is the whole
system; AMF are one critically important component of it. Both frameworks are
essential for understanding soil health, but they operate at different scales.

## Soil Food Web
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) form the most widespread symbiosis on
Earth, associating with 80-90% of terrestrial plant species. Belonging to
the phylum Glomeromycota, AMF form intracellular structures called arbuscules
within root cortical cells where [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]] occurs. They are essential
for plant nutrition, soil health, and ecosystem function across virtually
all terrestrial environments.

AMF extend the effective root surface area by 100-1000 times through their
extraradical hyphal network, which extends 10-25 cm from root surfaces. They
dramatically improve phosphorus uptake (60-200% increase), nitrogen access,
water uptake during drought, and micronutrient absorption of zinc, copper,
and manganese. In return, plants provide 10-20% of net photosynthate as
carbon and essential lipids that AMF cannot synthesize themselves. AMF also
produce glomalin, a glycoprotein that binds soil particles into stable
aggregates, improving soil structure and water-holding capacity.

AMF are promoted by low to moderate soil phosphorus, minimal tillage, cover
crops, [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], and diverse rotations. They are suppressed by high
phosphorus fertilizer, conventional tillage, fallow periods, and fungicides.
Notable non-host families include Brassicaceae (cabbage, broccoli) and
Chenopodiaceae (spinach, beet), which do not form AM associations and
therefore cannot benefit from this symbiotic relationship.

## When to Focus on the Soil Food Web
A whole-system soil food web approach is appropriate when starting a new
garden or farm, rehabilitating degraded soil, or transitioning from
conventional to biological management. It provides the broadest foundation
for soil health by ensuring all trophic levels are represented and
functioning. This approach is essential when soil problems are complex or
poorly understood, since building diverse biology creates resilience against
multiple stressors simultaneously rather than targeting one issue.

## When to Focus on AMF
Targeted AMF management is appropriate when specific crops are known to be
mycorrhizal-dependent (onions, garlic, tomatoes, peppers, corn, beans), when
[[query-how-does-phosphorus-availability-change-across-soil-ph-levels]] is limiting, or when [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] is a priority.
It is also valuable in no-till systems where preserving existing mycorrhizal
networks is a primary goal and any soil disturbance would destroy years of
network development. AMF-focused management is simpler to implement since it
primarily involves minimizing soil disturbance, reducing phosphorus
fertilizer, and maintaining living roots through cover cropping.

## Hybrid Approaches
AMF are a component of the soil food web, so the two concepts are inherently
complementary. Building the overall soil food web through compost, mulch, and
diverse plantings creates conditions where AMF can thrive and establish
extensive hyphal networks. Conversely, promoting AMF through minimal tillage
and cover cropping benefits the broader web by maintaining fungal hyphal
pathways that support other soil organisms including beneficial bacteria and
predatory nematodes. The most effective soil management considers both the
specific symbiosis and the ecosystem that sustains it.
