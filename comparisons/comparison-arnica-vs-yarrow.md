# Arnica vs Yarrow

## Overview

Arnica (*[[arnica-montana]]*) and yarrow (*[[achillea-millefolium]]*) are two of
the most storied wound-healing herbs in the European phytotherapy
tradition. Both belong to the Asteraceae family and share a reputation
for treating bruises, swelling, and musculoskeletal pain, yet their
chemical profiles, safety windows, and practical applications differ
substantially. Arnica is a potent topical anti-inflammatory best known
for post-traumatic recovery, while yarrow is a versatile internal and
external remedy with hemostatic, digestive, and febrifuge properties.

Understanding the distinctions between these two herbs matters for
herbalists, homesteaders, and anyone building a home medicine kit.
Relying on arnica internally can be dangerous, whereas yarrow is
generally safe for internal use. Conversely, arnica outperforms yarrow
for acute bruising and postoperative swelling. Choosing the right herb
for the right situation prevents misuse and maximizes therapeutic
benefit.

## Arnica

[[yarrow-achillea-millefolium]]

Yarrow is a rhizomatous perennial native to temperate Europe and Asia,
widely naturalized across North America and other [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]]. It
forms dense clumps 30 to 90 cm tall with distinctive feathery, finely
dissected leaves and flat-topped clusters of small white to pink
flowers. The genus name honors Achilles, who legendarily used yarrow to
treat his soldiers' wounds at Troy.

Yarrow's phytochemistry is remarkably diverse. Key compounds include
achilleine (a hemostatic alkaloid), flavonoids such as apigenin and
luteolin, volatile oils like camphor and chamazulene, tannins,
sesquiterpene lactones, and coumarins. This breadth underlies yarrow's
wide-ranging medicinal actions: it stops bleeding, reduces
inflammation, relieves spasms, stimulates digestion, promotes sweating
during fevers, and exhibits antimicrobial activity.

Unlike arnica, yarrow is safe for both internal and external use.
[[classic-mushroom-recipes-traditional-preparations]] include teas, tinctures, poultices, and
salves. Yarrow tea is commonly used for feverish colds, menstrual
cramps, and digestive upset. Externally, fresh leaves or poultices are
applied to cuts, abrasions, and nosebleeds. Yarrow is also a
nutritive bitter, stimulating gastric secretions and bile flow.

Beyond medicine, yarrow is a valuable permaculture plant. It attracts
pollinators [[hemenway-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insects]], accumulates nutrients through deep
roots, and serves as a [[comparison-hyperaccumulator-vs-dynamic-accumulator]] in companion planting
schemes. Its [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] and hardiness across USDA zones 3 to 9
make it one of the easiest medicinal herbs to cultivate.

## When to Choose Arnica

Arnica is the clear choice for acute trauma involving bruising,
swelling, and soft-tissue inflammation. Athletes use arnica gel after
sprains, strains, and impact injuries, while post-surgical patients
apply it to reduce edema and accelerate recovery. It is also effective
for arthritic joint pain when applied topically.

Arnica should never be applied to broken skin or mucous membranes.
Individuals with asteraceae allergies should exercise caution due to
cross-reactivity with chamomile and ragweed. Pregnant and breastfeeding
women should avoid internal preparations entirely.

## When to Choose Yarrow

Yarrow is the better choice when a safe, versatile herb is needed for
both internal and external applications. For cuts and bleeding, fresh
yarrow leaves applied as a poultice are immediately effective. For colds
and fevers, yarrow tea promotes sweating and supports the body's natural
fever response. Menstrual cramps and digestive complaints respond well
to yarrow tincture taken internally.

Yarrow's ease of cultivation makes it the practical choice for
homesteaders and permaculture gardeners. Once established, it requires
minimal care and provides harvestable material year after year. Its role
as a companion plant and pollinator attractant adds value beyond
medicine.

## Combined Use

Arnica and yarrow complement each other well in a herbal first-aid kit.
Arnica handles bruising and inflammation while yarrow addresses bleeding,
fever, and digestive upset. A salve combining arnica-infused oil with
yarrow-infused oil serves as an excellent all-purpose topical remedy for
minor injuries. In permaculture design, growing yarrow as a garden staple
while sourcing arnica from reputable suppliers provides access to both
herbs' unique strengths.
