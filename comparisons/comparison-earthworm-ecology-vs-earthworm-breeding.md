# Earthworm Ecology vs Earthworm Breeding

## Overview
Earthworm ecology studies the role of worms in natural and garden soil
ecosystems, revealing why they are among the most important organisms in soil
health. Earthworm breeding is the deliberate cultivation of worms in managed
systems to produce compost, inoculate garden soil, and process organic waste.
Ecology tells you why worms matter and what conditions they need to thrive;
breeding tells you how to multiply them and deploy their products. Together,
they form a complete understanding of how to work with earthworms as the most
visible and important members of the soil food web.

Environmental conditions must be carefully managed. Room temperature is ideal.
The moisture balance is critical: soil should feel like a wrung-out sponge,
neither completely dry nor completely wet. Too much water makes worms pale and
can lead to drowning. Good oxygen supply is essential, requiring drainage
holes in the bottom of breeding containers. Worms must be protected from UV
light, which they are sensitive to.

The products of worm breeding serve multiple purposes. Worms and their eggs
can be scattered over new terraces and [[cervantes-breeding-program-design-goal-setting]]. The
ecological perspective reveals that worm presence is a reliable soil health
indicator: thriving worms mean the broader soil food web is functioning.
Ecology also teaches which practices harm worms: rototilling destroys burrows
and cuts worms into non-regenerating pieces, [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] without further intervention. The
breeder's job is to jumpstart the ecology; the ecology then sustains itself.
