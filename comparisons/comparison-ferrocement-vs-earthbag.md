## Ferrocement vs Earthbag Construction

Both ferrocement and earthbag construction are affordable, resource-efficient [[comparison-drip-irrigation-vs-rainwater-harvesting]] system where earthbag walls shelter a ferrocement tank, combining the tank's water-tightness with the building's thermal protection.

## Environmental Impact

The environmental footprint of each method diverges sharply at the cement question. Portland cement production accounts for roughly 8% of global CO₂ emissions. Ferrocement uses cement intensively — a typical small dome (5 m diameter) may require 2–4 cubic meters of mortar, translating to 800–1,600 kg of cement. Earthbag walls use little to no cement; the primary emissions come from manufacturing polypropylene bags and transporting barbed wire. If filled with on-site soil and plastered with local earthen materials, earthbag approaches a near-zero embodied-energy wall system. For builders in remote areas where cement must be trucked long distances, the transport emissions of ferrocement compound its already high material footprint.

However, ferrocement's longevity and low maintenance reduce lifecycle impacts — a well-built ferrocement tank or dome may outlast several generations without repair. Earthbag structures require ongoing plaster maintenance (every 5–15 years depending on climate and plaster quality), and bag degradation (even behind plaster) raises questions about very-long-term structural integrity that remain unanswered since the method is too young for centuries of data. Some builders use burlap or natural-fiber bags instead of polypropylene to reduce synthetic material use, but these degrade faster and must be plastered immediately.

## Speed of Construction

For a trained crew, ferrocement is faster once the armature is complete. Plastering a 30 m² dome shell can take 2–3 days with 4 workers. Total project time including armature construction, curing, and finishing: 3–5 weeks. The curing period (minimum 7 days, ideally 14) is a hard constraint — no subsequent work should load the shell until curing is complete.

Earthbag construction is slower per wall area but requires less setup. A crew of 4 can lay 30–40 bags per day, producing roughly 8–12 m² of wall. A small dome (20–25 m²) takes 3–5 days of bag work plus 2–3 days for plastering. Total project time: 2–4 weeks, with the caveat that no curing wait is required between wall completion and plastering. For rectilinear buildings with a conventional roof, earthbag walls can go up and be roofed immediately, allowing interior work to proceed without delay.

## Choosing Between Them

Choose **ferrocement** when you need thin, watertight, tensile-strong shells — water tanks, pools, and small domes where cement is available and you have (or can train) a skilled crew. It is the better choice for wet climates where breathability is less critical and where the speed of plaster application matters. Its longevity and low maintenance also make it attractive for infrastructure (tanks, cisterns, irrigation channels) where the cement investment pays back over decades.

Choose **earthbag** when you have abundant on-site soil, unskilled or volunteer labor, and want maximum thermal mass with minimal cement. It suits owner-builders, dry climates, and projects where material cost must stay extremely low. Earthbag is generally more earthquake-resilient in most configurations, owing to its mass, flexibility, and monolithic behavior. It also has a lower carbon footprint, which matters for builders prioritizing environmental impact.

Many projects combine both methods: earthbag walls with a ferrocement dome roof, or an earthbag root cellar with a ferrocement water storage tank. The techniques are complementary rather than competing, and each fills structural niches the other cannot.
