## Composting Toilets vs Humanure Composting

## Overview
Composting [[composting-toilets]]
Composting toilets encompass a range of manufactured and purpose-built systems that manage the
decomposition of human waste within the toilet unit itself. Three main types exist: batch systems
(alternating between two chambers or drums), continuous systems (waste moves gradually through a
single large chamber), and self-contained units (compact all-in-one designs). Commercial units
from manufacturers like Nature's Head, Sun-Mar, and Separett range from $900-2,500, while DIY
batch systems can be built for $200-500. All systems use carbon cover materials and manage
moisture, aeration, and sometimes heat to accelerate decomposition.

The strength of composting toilets is convenience and integration. They look and function more
like conventional toilets, making them easier to adopt for households with reluctant members.
Many units separate urine from feces (diverting systems), which dramatically reduces volume and
odor. Self-contained units require no outdoor composting infrastructure and can be installed in
spaces where outdoor piles are impractical (basements, tiny homes, boats). Some units include
ventilation fans, heaters, and agitators that automate much of the composting process.
NSF/ANSI 41 certification on commercial units provides regulatory acceptance in many
jurisdictions.

The drawbacks are significant: commercial units are expensive, mechanical components (fans,
heaters, agitators) can fail, capacity is limited for full-time households, and even the best
systems require periodic emptying of partially composted material. The compost produced inside
the unit is rarely fully finished — it typically needs secondary curing outdoors. Many units
require electricity for fans and heaters, undermining off-grid ambitions. The sealed environment
can create anaerobic pockets if not managed properly, leading to odor problems.
