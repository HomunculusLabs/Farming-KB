# Composting Systems Design Comparison

Composting systems range from simple open piles to enclosed bins, tumblers, and in-ground methods. The right system depends on your space, volume of organic waste, desired speed, and how much labor you want to invest. This comparison focuses on physical system designs (not biological methods — see [[hot-composting-vs-vermicompost-vs-bokashi]] for process comparisons).

On a homestead with [[duck-keeping]] and [[goat-keeping]], manure management makes a reliable composting system essential. The ideal homestead runs multiple systems simultaneously: a hot compost pile for bulk manure and yard waste, a vermicompost bin for kitchen scraps, and bokashi for pre-processing all food waste.

## System Design Comparison

| System | Space Needed | Speed | Labor | Rodent Resistance | Cost | Best For |
|--------|-------------|-------|-------|-------------------|------|----------|
| **Open pile** (Berkeley method) | Large (min 3x3x3 ft) | Fast (2-4 weeks active + cure) | High (turn every 2-3 days) | Poor (open access) | Free | Bulk yard waste, manure, large volumes |
| **Enclosed bin** (plastic/stationary) | Small (3-5 sq ft) | Moderate (2-6 months) | Low-Moderate (turn occasionally) | Good (sealed with lid) | $30-100 | Small yards, kitchen + yard waste |
| **Tumbler** (rotating drum) | Small (3-4 sq ft footprint) | Fast-Moderate (4-8 weeks) | Low (turn by crank) | Excellent (off-ground, sealed) | $80-250 | Easy turning, small spaces, moderate volume |
| **Wire bin** (circular mesh) | Medium (3x3 ft diameter) | Moderate (3-6 months) | Moderate (turn with fork) | Poor (open mesh) | $5-20 (DIY) | Fall leaves, yard waste, budget option |
| **Pallet bin** (3-4 pallets) | Medium (3-4x3-4 ft) | Moderate (3-6 months) | Moderate (turn with fork) | Moderate (pallet gaps) | Free-$10 | Homesteads with pallets, multi-bin setup |
| **Pit composting** | Small (hole in ground) | Slow (6-12 months) | Low (fill and forget) | Good (underground) | Free | Remote areas, minimal infrastructure |
| **Sheet composting** (lasagna) | As large as your garden bed | Slow (3-6 months to decompose) | Low (layer materials in place) | Good (no exposed food) | Free (using yard materials) | Building new garden beds, no-till |
| **Trench composting** | Linear (dig as needed) | Moderate (2-4 months in soil) | Low (dig, fill, cover) | Excellent (fully buried) | Free | Row-by-row garden integration, fall prep |

## Deep Comparison

### Speed and Volume

The **open pile** (Berkeley hot composting method) is the fastest system for bulk material. A properly built 3x3x3 ft pile with 25-30:1 C:N ratio and 50-60% moisture will reach 130-150F within 48 hours. Turning every 2-3 days sustains thermophilic temperatures and produces finished compost in 14-28 days, plus 2-4 weeks curing. The downside is labor intensity — each turn requires forking the entire pile.

**Tumblers** offer the easiest turning but have limited volume (typically 50-80 gallons). They work well for a household generating kitchen scraps plus moderate yard waste. The enclosed design retains heat and moisture well, but can become too wet if not balanced with enough browns. Speed is decent (4-8 weeks) but can't match a well-managed hot pile.

**Enclosed plastic bins** are the standard suburban solution. They moderate temperature, retain moisture, and look tidy. Speed depends on whether you actively turn the contents (faster) or passively fill and wait (slower, 6+ months). Ventilation holes provide limited aeration.

### Rodent and Pest Resistance

This is the critical factor for homesteads with animal manure. **Tumblers** and **trench composting** are the most rodent-proof designs. **Open piles** and **wire bins** attract rodents, especially if food scraps are not buried deep enough. **Pallet bins** with hardware cloth stapled to the inside offer moderate resistance.

For [[duck-keeping]] and [[goat-keeping]] manure, a hot pile or enclosed bin is strongly recommended — the thermophilic phase kills pathogens (131F+ for 3 days) and the enclosure keeps rodents out of nitrogen-rich material.

### Integration with Garden Beds

**Sheet composting** and **trench composting** are the most garden-integrated systems. Sheet composting (lasagna gardening) builds beds in place by layering browns and greens directly on the site — no turning, no transport. Trench composting digs waste into rows where crops will grow, decomposing in place and fertilizing the next crop. Both are excellent [[no-till-farming]] strategies.

### Multi-Bin Strategy

Most homesteads benefit from a 3-bin pallet system: one bin actively receiving material, one composting, one curing finished compost. This provides a continuous flow. Add a tumbler or enclosed bin near the kitchen for daily food scraps, and bokashi in the kitchen for pre-processing all waste including meat and dairy.

### Cold Composting (No-Turn Alternative)

For homesteaders who want the simplest possible approach, cold/slow composting requires no turning. Pile materials as they become available and wait 6-12 months. The trade-off: no reliable pathogen or weed seed kill, and slower decomposition. This works best with a pallet bin or enclosed bin that keeps materials contained while letting nature take its course.

### Carbon Sources for Each System

| Material | C:N Ratio | Best System | Notes |
|----------|-----------|-------------|-------|
| Dry leaves | 40-80:1 | Wire bin, pallet bin, sheet composting | Excellent, widely available |
| Straw | 80-100:1 | Open pile, sheet composting | Good structure, slower breakdown |
| Wood chips | 200-500:1 | Mulch paths, hugelkultur | Long-lasting; avoid in active compost |
| Shredded paper/cardboard | 150-200:1 | Enclosed bin, trench | Avoid glossy/coated paper |
| Sawdust | 300-500:1 | Sheet mulching (thin layers) | Mats easily; use sparingly |

## Choosing by Homestead Scale

| Scale | Recommended Setup | Reasoning |
|-------|------------------|-----------|
| Urban apartment | Bokashi bin + small vermicompost bin | Zero space; handles all kitchen waste |
| Suburban yard (0.25 ac) | Enclosed bin + tumblers + bokashi | Neat appearance; moderate volume |
| Small homestead (1-5 ac) | 3-bin pallet system + bokashi + vermicompost | Handles animal manure + kitchen + yard waste |
| Large homestead (5+ ac) | Multiple hot piles + windrows + sheet composting | Bulk manure management; field-scale capacity |

## Verdict

Don't use just one system — use the right tool for each waste stream. A **hot open pile or 3-bin pallet system** handles bulk manure and yard waste with pathogen kill. A **kitchen bokashi bin** pre-processes all food waste including meat and dairy. A **vermicompost bin** under the sink or in the garage produces castings for seed starting and [[cannabis-indoor-living-soil]]. **Sheet composting** builds new garden beds from scratch. For maximum simplicity on a small property, a **tumbler** handles moderate volumes with minimal effort.

## Scaling Tips

- Start with one system that matches your primary waste stream, then add complementary systems
- A 3-bin pallet system (build-finish-use) is the most cost-effective scale-up for homesteads
- Always position compost bins within 50 ft of the kitchen to encourage daily use
- Pit and trench composting are invisible — ideal for HOA-governed properties
