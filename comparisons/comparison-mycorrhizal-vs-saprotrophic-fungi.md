# Mycorrhizal Fungi vs Saprotrophic Fungi

## Overview
Mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi represent two fundamentally different
    13|    13|ecological strategies in soil ecosystems. Mycorrhizae form intimate symbiotic
    14|    14|partnerships with living plant roots, exchanging nutrients for carbohydrates,
    15|    15|while saprotrophs decompose dead organic matter and release mineralized
    16|    16|nutrients back into the soil food web. Together they form the biological
    17|    17|foundation of terrestrial nutrient cycling, and understanding both is essential
    18|    18|for managing soil biology in agriculture, forestry, mushroom cultivation, and
    19|    19|land restoration.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi
[[mycorrhizal-fungi]]

Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic associations with the roots of 80 to 95
    25|    25|percent of terrestrial plant species. In exchange for plant-derived
    26|    26|carbohydrates, the fungal hyphae extend far beyond the root zone to mine
    27|    27|phosphorus, water, and micronutrients from soil that roots alone cannot access.
    28|    28|The partnership is ancient, dating back over 400 million years, and is widely
    29|    29|considered one of the most important symbiotic relationships on Earth. Without
    30|    30|mycorrhizae, most plants cannot obtain optimal nutrition.

The two primary types relevant to most growers are arbuscular mycorrhizal
    33|    33|fungi (AMF) and ectomycorrhizae (ECM). AMF penetrate root cells and form
    34|    34|arbuscule structures for nutrient exchange, associating with most vegetables,
    35|    35|annuals, grasses, and legumes. ECM form a dense sheath around root tips of trees
    36|    36|like oaks, pines, and beeches, and often produce visible fruiting bodies such as
    37|    37|porcini, chanterelles, and truffles. AMF produce glomalin, a sticky glycoprotein
    38|    38|that contributes 27 to 30 percent of soil carbon and creates stable aggregates
    39|    39|that resist erosion.

Beyond nutrient exchange, mycorrhizal networks provide pathogen protection by
    42|    42|forming a physical web around roots and producing antibiotic compounds that
    43|    43|suppress soil-borne diseases like Phytophthora root rot and fusarium wilt. They
    44|    44|also facilitate inter-plant resource sharing through common mycelial networks,
    45|    45|connecting individuals of the same or different species. This network
    46|    46|connectivity makes mycorrhizae critical for ecosystem resilience, particularly
    47|    47|under drought stress, where hyphal networks extend effective root access area by
    48|    48|700 to 1,000 times.

Mycorrhizal populations are highly sensitive to soil disturbance.
    51|    51|Conventional tillage breaks hyphal networks, high-phosphorus synthetic
    52|    52|fertilizers suppress colonization because plants stop trading carbon when
    53|    53|phosphorus is abundant, and certain fungicides are directly toxic to
    54|    54|mycorrhizae. Building and maintaining mycorrhizal communities requires minimal
    55|    55|soil disturbance, appropriate nutrient management, and living root networks
    56|    56|maintained through cover crops or perennial plantings.

## Saprotrophic Fungi
[[saprotrophic-fungi]]

Saprotrophic fungi are the primary decomposers of dead organic matter in
    62|    62|terrestrial ecosystems. They obtain nutrients by secreting extracellular enzymes
    63|    63|into wood, leaf litter, dung, and soil organic matter, breaking down complex
    64|    64|polymers like cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin into simpler compounds that
    65|    65|they absorb through their hyphal membranes. This process of extracellular
    66|    66|digestion and nutrient mineralization is the foundation of nutrient cycling in
    67|    67|all terrestrial ecosystems, described by Dighton (1997) as the engine of
    68|    68|mineralization.

Saprotrophs follow predictable successional patterns on dead wood. Primary
    71|    71|colonizers, often Ascomycetes like Trichoderma and Hypoxylon, arrive within
    72|    72|weeks and modify substrate chemistry for secondary decomposers. White rot
    73|    73|basidiomycetes then degrade lignin and cellulose simultaneously using lignin
    74|    74|peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and laccase enzymes. Brown rot fungi employ
    75|    75|Fenton chemistry, generating hydroxyl radicals to depolymerize cellulose while
    76|    76|leaving lignin largely intact as a brown residue. Late-stage decomposers work on
    77|    77|recalcitrant residues and humus formation.

Different saprotrophs specialize on different substrates. White rot
    80|    80|basidiomycetes dominate hardwood decomposition using ligninase enzymes. Brown
    81|    81|rot fungi prefer softwoods and use oxidative chemistry. Ascomycetes and
    82|    82|early-succession basidiomycetes colonize leaf litter. Coprophilous fungi like
    83|    83|Coprinus specialize on dung. This substrate specialization means diverse woody
    84|    84|inputs in a garden or farm support a more diverse saprotrophic community, which
    85|    85|in turn mineralizes nutrients more completely and supports a healthier soil food
    86|    86|web.

The saprotrophic strategy, while seemingly wasteful because nutrients leak
    89|    89|from the mycelial network into surrounding soil, is precisely what makes these
    90|    90|fungi indispensable to all other soil life. That leaked nutrient fraction feeds
    91|    91|plants, bacteria, and other organisms. Without saprotrophic decomposition, dead
    92|    92|organic matter would accumulate indefinitely and nutrients would remain locked
    93|    93|in unavailable forms. Saprotrophs are also the primary fungi cultivated for
    94|    94|food: shiitake, oyster mushrooms, wine caps, and most other cultivated species
    95|    95|are saprotrophic.

## When to Choose Mycorrhizal Focus
Mycorrhizal management is most critical when establishing perennial systems
   121|   121|like orchards, food forests, pastures, and no-till gardens where root-fungal
   122|   122|partnerships can develop undisturbed over years. Inoculation with commercial AMF
   123|   123|products is worthwhile for transplanted crops in disturbed soils, and for
   124|   124|cannabis cultivation where phosphorus uptake and drought resilience directly
   125|   125|affect yield. Any system using minimal tillage and avoiding high-phosphorus
   126|   126|synthetic fertilizers will benefit from protecting and encouraging native
   127|   127|mycorrhizal populations. See [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]] for application
   128|   128|details and [[no-till-gardening-method]] for practices that protect mycorrhizal
   129|   129|networks.

## When to Choose Saprotrophic Focus
Saprotrophic fungi are the primary workers in composting systems, log-grown
   133|   133|mushroom cultivation, and organic matter decomposition in any soil. Wood chip
   134|   134|mulches, ramial chipped wood, and woody amendments feed saprotrophic communities
   135|   135|that break down lignin and build humus. When establishing new gardens on
   136|   136|compacted or low-organic-matter soil, encouraging saprotrophic decomposition
   137|   137|through compost and woody inputs is the fastest path to building soil structure.
   138|   138|Mushroom growers cultivating shiitake, oyster, or wine cap mushrooms are
   139|   139|directly managing saprotrophic species. See [[mushroom-cultivation-methods]] for
   140|   140|cultivation approaches.
