# Mulching vs Green Manure

## Overview
Mulching and green manuring are two foundational practices for building soil
[[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], suppressing weeds, and maintaining soil health. Mulch is dead
organic material laid on the soil surface as a physical barrier, while green
manure is a living crop grown specifically to be incorporated into the soil.
Both feed soil biology, retain moisture, and suppress weeds, but they operate
through different mechanisms, require different management, and suit different
situations in the [[cervantes-garden-calendar-checklist]].

## Mulching
[[green-manure-guide]]
Green manure is a crop grown specifically to be dug into the soil, adding
organic matter and nutrients during fallow periods. The practice involves
sowing a cover crop, allowing it to grow, then incorporating the green growth
into the soil before it becomes woody. [[jeavons-green-manures-cover-crops]] provide benefits that
surface mulch cannot: deep-rooting species like red clover and lupins bring
minerals from lower soil layers (over 7 feet deep) to the surface, and
nitrogen-fixing legumes (beans, clovers, lupins, winter tare) convert
atmospheric nitrogen into plant-available forms through root nodule bacteria.

Green manure crops cover the ground during periods when beds would otherwise
be bare, preventing nutrient leaching through winter drainage on light soils
and suppressing weed establishment. The soft green material rots down quickly
when dug in, releasing nutrients faster than surface mulch decomposition.
Different species serve different purposes: alfalfa provides deep rooting and
abundant organic matter; broad beans [[query-how-to-fix-nitrogen-toxicity-in-cannabis]] and produce edible beans;
red clover has an extensive root system and fixes nitrogen; lupins add
nitrogen and large amounts of phosphates; buckwheat attracts beneficial
insects; phacelia is fast-growing and soft; rye provides extensive roots and
winter cover.

The main limitation of green manure is that incorporation requires soil
disturbance. Digging or tilling the green manure crop into the soil disrupts
soil structure and earthworm burrows, conflicting with no-till and mulch-based
systems. The crop must be cut at the base and allowed to wilt for a few days
before incorporation, and should not be buried deeper than about 6 inches. If
the crop becomes woody before incorporation, decomposition will temporarily
rob soil nitrogen rather than adding it.

## Key Differences
| Aspect | Mulching | Green Manure |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| Material | Dead organic matter applied on surface | Living crop grown then incorporated |
| Soil disturbance | None (no-till compatible) | Requires digging or tilling to incorporate |
| [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] | None | Significant from legume species |
| Deep nutrient mining | Surface only | Deep roots access subsoil minerals |
| Speed of nutrient release | Slow (surface decomposition) | Fast (incorporation accelerates breakdown) |
| Seasonal timing | Year-round application | Grown during fallow periods between crops |
| Weed suppression | Physical barrier, ongoing | Competition during growth, then incorporation |
| Soil improvement | Water retention, temperature moderation | [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]], nitrogen addition, soil structure |
| Cost | Often free (leaves, chips) | Seed cost, minimal |
| Best For | No-till gardens, perennials, permanent beds | Rotational beds, winter cover, nitrogen addition |

## When to Choose Mulching
Mulching is the clear choice for no-till gardeners, perennial plantings (fruit
trees, berry bushes, ornamental beds), and any situation where soil
disturbance must be minimized. The year-round application flexibility means
mulch can be added at any time, and the physical barrier provides immediate
weed suppression without waiting for crop establishment. Mulch also protects
soil from erosion, compaction from rain impact, and temperature extremes in
ways green manure cannot match until the crop is fully established. For
gardeners transitioning to no-till methods, mulching is the primary tool for
smothering existing weeds and building soil without disturbance.

## When to Choose Green Manure
Green manure is most valuable when beds will be vacant for extended periods,
particularly over winter. The ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen through
legume species provides a fertility input that no amount of mulch can
replicate. Deep-rooting green manures like lupins and red clover access
minerals from depths beyond the reach of surface applications, bringing
phosphates and trace minerals to the root zone. For gardeners practicing crop
rotation, green manures fill the gap between crop families while improving
soil for the next planting.

## Hybrid Approaches
The most effective soil-building programs combine both practices. Grow a green
manure crop during fallow periods, then cut it at soil level (rather than
digging it in) and use the cut material as surface mulch. This no-till green
manure approach adds the nitrogen fixation and deep nutrient cycling of green
manures while maintaining the soil structure benefits of mulching. The cut
material decomposes on the surface, feeding earthworms and soil biology
without disturbance. This approach is central to many permaculture and no-till
farming systems.
