# Calendula vs Chamomile

Calendula (*Calendula officinalis*) and chamomile (*Matricaria chamomilla*, German chamomile, or *Chamaemelum nobile*, Roman chamomile) are two of the most beloved and widely used medicinal herbs in the Western herbal tradition. Both belong to the Asteraceae family, share bright daisy-like flowers, and are renowned for anti-inflammatory and skin-soothing properties. Despite these similarities, they have distinct profiles, uses, and growing requirements that make each uniquely valuable in the garden and apothecary.

## Key Differences

### Medicinal Profile

**Calendula excels at topical wound healing.** Its triterpenoid saponins (faradiol, arniidiol, calenduladiol) are among the most potent plant-derived wound-healing compounds known. Calendula stimulates fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, angiogenesis, and granulation tissue formation. Clinical trials support its use for surgical wounds, radiation dermatitis, diaper rash, and leg ulcers. Internal use focuses on lymphatic stimulation and mucosal healing.

**Chamomile excels at internal anti-inflammatory and calming effects.** German chamomile's chamazulene and α-bisabolol provide powerful anti-inflammatory action, while apigenin binds GABA-A receptors to produce anxiolytic (anti-anxiety) and mild sedative effects. Chamomile is the herb of choice for digestive spasms, insomnia, teething pain, and generalized anxiety. Topically, it is soothing but less directly wound-healing than calendula.

**Rule of thumb**: Reach for calendula on the skin, chamomile in the teacup.

### Growing Requirements

**Calendula is more forgiving.** It tolerates a wider range of soils, including heavy clay, and is more drought-tolerant once established. It blooms over a much longer season — from spring through fall with regular harvesting — providing months of color and harvest material.

**Chamomile demands better drainage.** German chamomile, in particular, struggles in heavy or waterlogged soils and prefers sandy, well-drained conditions. Roman chamomile tolerates a broader range but still requires good drainage. Neither chamomile species blooms as prolifically or for as long as calendula.

**Calendula produces dramatically more harvestable material.** A single calendula plant can yield hundreds of flowers over a season. Chamomile plants produce fewer flowers per plant, though the flowers are lighter and easier to dry in bulk.

### Companion Planting

**Calendula is the stronger companion plant.** Its nematicidal root exudates, pest-trapping properties, and long bloom season make it one of the most valuable companion plants in the vegetable garden. It actively protects neighboring crops from nematodes and attracts beneficial insects over a long period.

**Chamomile is a supportive but subtler companion.** German chamomile is said to improve the flavor and essential oil production of neighboring herbs and is a good general pollinator plant. Roman chamomile's low, spreading growth habit makes it an excellent living mulch and ground cover. Both chamomile species are reputed to revitalize sick plants when grown nearby — a claim from biodynamic tradition that has some anecdotal support.

### Flavor and Culinary Use

**Calendula** has a slightly peppery, tangy flavor with bitter undertones. Its primary culinary value is as a natural food coloring — the golden petals impart a saffron-like color to rice, soups, and butters. The flavor is pleasant in salads but less appealing as a standalone tea compared to chamomile.

**Chamomile** has a sweet, apple-like fragrance and flavor (the name derives from the Greek *chamaimēlon*, "earth apple"). German chamomile makes a more intensely flavored tea; Roman chamomile is sweeter and milder. Chamomile is one of the most consumed herbal teas worldwide and is widely used in baking, liqueurs (e.g., chamomile-infused honey and syrups), and desserts.

## Similarities

- Both are **Asteraceae family** members with daisy-like composite flowers
- Both are **anti-inflammatory** and **antioxidant** with strong clinical and traditional evidence
- Both are **gentle enough for children** and widely used in pediatric herbalism
- Both attract **pollinators and beneficial insects** to the garden
- Both are **easy to grow from seed** and require minimal care
- Both have **European monograph status** — recognized by the German Commission E and EMA as legitimate medicinal herbs
- Both make excellent **infused oils** for topical use
- Both are **safe for most people** with rare contraindications (Asteraceae allergy, pregnancy caution)

## When to Choose Which

### Choose Calendula When

- You need a **wound-healing salve** or topical skin treatment
- You want a **long-blooming, pest-repelling companion plant** for vegetables
- You need a **lymphatic support herb** for swollen glands or sluggish circulation
- You want **edible flowers for salads** or natural food coloring
- You're growing in **poor or heavy soil** that chamomile might not tolerate
- You want **maximum flower yield** for drying and herbal product-making
- You need a **trap crop** for aphids or a nematicidal intercrop

### Choose Chamomile When

- You need a **digestive calming tea** for spasms, bloating, or indigestion
- You want a **sleep aid or anxiolytic** herb
- You need an **anti-inflammatory for internal use** (GI tract, menstrual cramps)
- You want a **low-growing ground cover** (Roman chamomile)
- You're growing on **sandy, well-drained soil** where chamomile thrives
- You want a **sweetly fragrant tea herb** with broad popular appeal
- You're creating a **relaxing herbal bath blend** or bedtime ritual
- You want to make **chamomile-infused honey** or culinary preparations

### Why Not Both?

Calendula and chamomile are **highly complementary** and work well together in the garden and apothecary:

- **Garden**: Plant calendula as taller, pest-repelling companions in vegetable beds, and chamomile as lower-growing herbs near pathways or as a ground cover
- **Tea blends**: Combine for a synergistic anti-inflammatory, calming tea
- **Skin care**: Calendula-infused oil with chamomile essential oil creates a potent anti-inflammatory skin treatment
- **Salves**: A calendula-chamomile salve combines wound-healing with soothing properties — excellent for diaper rash, eczema, and radiation burns
- **Bath products**: Both flowers are beautiful and effective in bath teas and bath salts
