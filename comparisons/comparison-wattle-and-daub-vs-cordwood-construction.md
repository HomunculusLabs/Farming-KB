# Wattle-and-Daub vs Cordwood Construction
## Overview

Wattle-and-daub and cordwood construction are two of the most accessible
[[natural-building-techniques]] available to homesteaders and permaculture
practitioners. Both rely on locally sourced, low-cost materials and can be
built by hand without heavy equipment. Yet they differ fundamentally in their
approach: wattle-and-daub creates thin, lightweight infill panels within a
timber frame, while cordwood masonry builds thick, self-supporting walls from
short log sections laid in mortar.

Understanding the strengths and limitations of each method helps builders
choose the right technique for their climate, budget, skill level, and
aesthetic goals.
## Wattle-and-Daub

[[cordwood-construction]]

Cordwood construction (also called cordwood masonry or stackwall) builds walls
by laying short log sections — typically 16 to 24 inches long — in a bed of
mortar, similar to laying bricks. The exposed log ends create a distinctive,
attractive pattern on both interior and exterior faces. The technique combines
the insulation properties of wood with the thermal mass of masonry.

Cordwood walls are substantially thicker than wattle-and-daub, ranging from 16
to 24 inches. This thickness provides excellent insulation (R-16 to R-30
depending on wall depth and insulation fill) along with significant thermal
mass from the mortar joints. The combination makes cordwood particularly well-
suited to climates with large diurnal temperature swings.

The method works best with softwoods like cedar, pine, or spruce, which dry
quickly and shrink minimally. Logs must be debarked and dried to below 20%
moisture content before use — a preparation process that takes at least six
months. This long lead time is one of cordwood's main logistical challenges.

Cordwood can function as either a load-bearing wall system or infill within a
timber frame, giving builders flexibility in structural design. However, the
walls are extremely heavy (100-150 lbs per cubic foot) and require a
substantial foundation.
## Key Differences

| Aspect | Wattle-and-Daub | Cordwood |
|--------|----------------|----------|
| Wall thickness | 10-15 cm (4-6 in) | 40-60 cm (16-24 in) |
| Structural role | Infill only | Load-bearing or infill |
| Primary materials | Clay, sand, straw, withies | Logs, mortar (cement/lime) |
| Insulation (R-value) | R-2 to R-4 | R-16 to R-30 |
| Thermal mass | Moderate | High |
| Foundation needs | Light (timber sill on stone) | Heavy (concrete footing) |
| Material lead time | Days to weeks | 6+ months (wood drying) |
| Skill required | Low (weaving + plastering) | Moderate (masonry + leveling) |
| Cost | Very low | Low to moderate |
| Climate suitability | Temperate, moderate | Cold, continental, variable |
| Aesthetic | Smooth plastered walls | Exposed log-end patterns |
## When to Choose Wattle-and-Daub

Wattle-and-daub is the better choice for builders who need quick, inexpensive
walls in a temperate climate. It works particularly well as infill panels in
an existing timber-framed structure, such as a barn, shed, or garden
outbuilding. Because the materials are essentially free (subsoil, straw, and
pruned branches), the technique is ideal for budgets approaching zero.

The method also suits situations where thin walls are needed — interior
partitions, garden walls, or structures where maximizing floor area matters.
Its excellent breathability makes it a natural fit for humid climates where
condensation and mold are concerns. The smooth, plastered finish accepts lime
wash and natural pigments beautifully, allowing for creative expression in
surface treatments.

For permaculture practitioners, wattle-and-daub integrates seamlessly with on-
site material cycles: coppiced willow or hazel provides the wattle, and on-
site subsoil from pond or swale excavation supplies the daub. The entire wall
can return to the earth at end of life with zero waste.
## When to Choose Cordwood

Cordwood construction excels in cold climates where thick, well-insulated
walls are a priority. The high R-value and thermal mass of a 24-inch cordwood
wall outperform most conventional and many natural building alternatives,
making it one of the best choices for off-grid homes in harsh winter regions.

Choose cordwood when you have access to abundant wood — particularly softwood
slabwood, firewood culls, or storm-damaged trees. The technique transforms
low-value wood into high-value walls, making it economically attractive on
wooded properties. Cordwood also offers unique aesthetic appeal; the mosaic of
exposed log ends creates walls of remarkable beauty that need no additional
finish.

Cordwood is better suited for permanent, primary dwellings than for temporary
or seasonal structures. The time investment in log preparation and the
permanence of mortared walls favor long-term builds. If you are building your
forever home on a wooded property in a cold climate, cordwood deserves serious
consideration.
## Combined Approaches

These two techniques can complement each other within a single building
project. A common approach uses a timber frame with cordwood walls on the
windward (exposed) side for maximum insulation and wattle-and-daub panels on
the sheltered or interior side where thinner walls suffice. This hybrid
leverages the strengths of each material while managing cost and labor
effectively.

Another integration strategy uses cordwood for the lower wall courses (where
moisture resistance matters most) and wattle-and-daub for upper sections where
weight and thermal demands are lower. Both methods pair well with living
roofs, passive solar orientation, and other permaculture design strategies.
