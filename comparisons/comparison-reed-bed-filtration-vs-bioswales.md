# Reed Bed Filtration vs Bioswales

## Overview

Reed bed filtration and bioswales are both vegetated, nature-based water
treatment systems, but they serve fundamentally different purposes. Reed
beds are enclosed treatment cells that purify contaminated water through
subsurface flow across gravel substrates planted with reeds and aquatic
species. Bioswales are linear, surface-flow channels designed to slow,
filter, and infiltrate stormwater runoff as it moves across landscapes.
Understanding when to use each system is essential for effective
water management design.

## Reed Bed Filtration

[[reed-bed-filtration]]

Reed bed filtration uses constructed wetlands planted with species like
Phragmites, Scirpus, and Typha to treat water through biological,
physical, and chemical processes. Water flows horizontally through a
gravel or sand substrate below the surface, where dense populations of
aerobic bacteria in root zones break down organic pollutants. Anaerobic
zones deeper in the substrate support denitrification, converting
nitrate to nitrogen gas.

The system excels at treating greywater, sewage effluent, and
nutrient-rich water. Plants actively absorb dissolved nitrogen and
phosphorus, while biofilms on root surfaces serve as the primary
treatment engines. Reed beds handle varying water quality and flow
rates effectively and require minimal maintenance once established,
though they need three to five years to reach full maturity.

Reed beds operate as contained treatment systems with no standing water
visible in subsurface designs, which reduces mosquito habitat and odor.
They can be scaled from residential greywater systems to municipal
effluent treatment. The technique removes contaminants without chemicals
or energy-intensive mechanical systems, and simultaneously provides
wildlife habitat.

## Bioswales

[[bioswales]]

Bioswales are linear, vegetated channels engineered to convey, slow,
filter, and infiltrate stormwater runoff. Unlike conventional drainage
ditches that rapidly move water off-site, bioswales use engineered soil
media, dense vegetation, and graded contours to treat polluted runoff
through sedimentation, filtration, adsorption, and biological uptake as
water moves through the channel.

Designed with specific hydraulic characteristics, bioswales maintain a
longitudinal slope of one to five percent with side slopes of 3:1 or
flatter. Target flow velocity is 0.3 to 0.6 meters per second during
design storm events, with a minimum residence time of nine minutes for
effective treatment. They are a cornerstone of low-impact development
and green infrastructure, widely deployed in urban, agricultural, and
residential settings.

Plant selection emphasizes deep-rooted native grasses, sedges, and
perennials that stabilize soil and support rhizosphere microbial
communities. Species like switchgrass, soft rush, and blue flag iris
provide both water treatment and pollinator habitat. Agricultural
bioswales are typically wider and less engineered, positioned along
field margins at the base of slopes.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Reed Beds | Bioswales |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Primary Function | Water purification | Stormwater conveyance |
| Flow Type | Subsurface horizontal | Surface channel flow |
| Water Source | Greywater, effluent, sewage | Stormwater runoff |
| Scale | Point-source treatment | Linear landscape feature |
| Treatment Depth | Below ground in substrate | Surface and shallow soil |
| Flow Velocity | Very slow, controlled | 0.3-0.6 m/s target |
| Mosquito Risk | Low (subsurface) | Moderate (surface water) |
| Key Plants | Phragmites, Typha, Scirpus | Grasses, sedges, perennials |
| Maturity Time | 3-5 years | 1-2 growing seasons |
| Best For | Contaminated water treatment | Runoff management, infiltration |

## When to Choose Reed Bed Filtration

Choose reed beds when you have a specific water quality problem that
needs treatment. They are ideal for treating household greywater,
managing effluent from septic systems, or purifying nutrient-laden
water from livestock operations. Reed beds are the right choice when
water needs to be cleaned before release or reuse, rather than simply
slowed and infiltrated.

Reed beds are also preferred in situations where odor and mosquito
control matter, since subsurface flow designs keep water below ground.
They work well in confined spaces where a contained treatment cell is
more practical than a long linear channel. Residential natural swimming
pools often incorporate reed bed filtration as the primary water
purification mechanism.

## When to Choose Bioswales

Choose bioswales when managing stormwater runoff across a landscape.
They are the appropriate solution for parking lots, roadsides, field
margins, and anywhere that water flows across the surface during rain
events. Bioswales excel at capturing sediment, trapping pollutants, and
recharging groundwater through infiltration.

Bioswales are also preferred when you need a conveyance element in a
larger water management system. They move water from one point to
another while treating it, unlike reed beds which are destination
treatment systems. On farms, agricultural bioswales along field margins
trap sediment and nutrients before they reach waterways, providing
both erosion control and water quality benefits.

## Combined Use

Reed beds and bioswales work powerfully together in integrated water
management designs. A typical arrangement captures stormwater in a
bioswale network that slows, filters, and conveys runoff, then directs
concentrated flows into a reed bed for final polishing before
discharge or reuse. This treatment train approach leverages the
strengths of both systems: bioswales handle volume and conveyance,
while reed beds provide intensive biological treatment.

On homesteads, greywater from the house can flow into a reed bed for
purification, while stormwater from driveways and paths is managed
through bioswales that direct treated water to garden areas or
infiltration zones. The combination creates a comprehensive,
nature-based water management system.
