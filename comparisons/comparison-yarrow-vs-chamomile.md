## Yarrow vs Chamomile

## Overview

Yarrow ([[yarrow-plant-guide]]) and chamomile
([[chamomile-growing-guide]]) are two of the most widely used medicinal
herbs in the Asteraceae family. Both have millennia of recorded use in
European, Asian, and Indigenous herbal traditions. Despite sharing a plant
family and some overlapping properties, they serve distinctly different
primary roles in the herbal apothecary and the permaculture garden.

Yarrow is best known as a hemostatic wound herb and a powerful diaphoretic
for breaking fevers. Chamomile is celebrated as a gentle nervine sedative
and digestive relaxant, consumed worldwide as a calming tea. Understanding
their differences helps gardeners and herbalists choose the right plant for
each situation.

## Yarrow

[[yarrow-achillea-millefolium]]

Yarrow (Achillea millefolium) is a hardy perennial native to temperate
regions of the Northern Hemisphere. Named for the Greek hero Achilles, who
reportedly used it to treat battlefield wounds, yarrow has served as a
primary first-aid herb across cultures for over 3,000 years. Its finely
divided, feathery foliage and flat-topped clusters of small white (or pink,
red, and yellow in cultivars) daisy-like flowers make it easy to identify.

Medicinally, yarrow's key active compounds include achilleine (a hemostatic
alkaloid), chamazulene, camphor, eucalyptol, flavonoids, tannins, and
sesquiterpene lactones. Its primary actions are hemostatic (stops bleeding),
diaphoretic (promotes sweating to break fevers), anti-inflammatory,
antispasmodic, and digestive bitter. It is also antimicrobial and supports
peripheral circulation.

Beyond medicine, yarrow excels as a companion plant. It accumulates copper,
potassium, and phosphorus in its tissues, drawing these nutrients from deep
in the soil profile. Its nectar-rich flat flower heads attract ladybugs,
lacewings, hoverflies, and parasitic wasps, making it one of the best
insectary plants for integrated pest management. Hardy in USDA zones 3-9,
yarrow tolerates drought, poor soil, and neglect far better than chamomile.

## Chamomile

[[chamomile-cultivation-and-uses]]

Chamomile encompasses two primary cultivated species: German chamomile
(Matricaria chamomilla), an annual growing 2-3 feet tall, and Roman
chamomile (Chamaemelum nobile), a low perennial ground cover. The name
derives from the Greek khamaimelon ("earth apple"), referencing the
characteristic sweet, apple-like fragrance of the flowers.

Chamomile's key active compounds include apigenin (a flavonoid that binds
to GABA receptors, producing gentle sedation), chamazulene (anti-
inflammatory blue pigment), bisabolol (anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial),
and various flavonoids. Its primary actions are nervine sedative, digestive
antispasmodic, anti-inflammatory, and mildly antimicrobial. Chamomile tea
is among the most consumed herbal teas globally, prized for promoting
relaxation and sleep.

German chamomile is preferred for tea and tincture production due to higher
essential oil content. Roman chamomile serves as an aromatic lawn
alternative and ornamental ground cover. Both species attract pollinators
and beneficial insects, though not as aggressively as yarrow. Chamomile
prefers lighter, well-drained soils and consistent moisture.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Yarrow | Chamomile |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| Primary use | Wound care, fever | Calming, sleep, digestion |
| Key compound | Achilleine (hemostatic) | Apigenin (sedative) |
| Lifecycle | Hardy perennial | Annual (German) / perennial (Roman) |
| Height | 1-3 feet | 6-36 inches (species-dependent) |
| Hardiness | USDA 3-9, very tough | USDA 4-9, less drought-tolerant |
| Soil needs | Tolerates poor, dry soil | Prefers light, well-drained |
| Companion role | Top-tier insectary, nutrient accumulator | Moderate pollinator attractor |
| Tea flavor | Bitter, astringent | Sweet, apple-like, pleasant |
| Safety note | Avoid in pregnancy (uterine stimulant) | Very safe, caution with Asteraceae allergy |

## When to Choose Yarrow

Choose yarrow when your primary need is a first-aid wound herb or a garden
workhorse. If you want a plant that stops bleeding, reduces fever through
sweating, stimulates sluggish digestion with its bitter principle, and
attracts predatory insects to protect your vegetables, yarrow is the clear
choice. It is also ideal for difficult garden sites: dry banks, poor soils,
and areas where fussier herbs fail.

Permaculture designers should prioritize yarrow for food forest understories,
orchard borders, and insectary hedgerows. Its deep roots open compacted soil
and cycle nutrients, and its drought tolerance means it thrives without
irrigation once established. A single yarrow planting serves medicine,
ecology, and soil improvement simultaneously.

## When to Choose Chamomile

Choose chamomile when your primary need is a gentle calming herb for daily
use. If you want an evening tea that promotes sleep, a remedy for
intermittent digestive discomfort, or a soothing anti-inflammatory compress
for skin irritation, chamomile is superior to yarrow. Its pleasant flavor
makes it suitable for children, elderly individuals, and those who find
bitter herbs unpalatable.

Roman chamomile is additionally valuable as a living mulch and lawn
alternative in permaculture pathways and between raised beds. Its low,
creeping habit fills gaps beautifully, and walking on it releases the
soothing fragrance. German chamomile is the better choice for bulk tea and
tincture production due to higher flower yields and essential oil content.

## Combined Use in the Garden

Yarrow and chamomile complement each other beautifully in a medicinal herb
garden. Plant yarrow at the edges and in drier zones as an insectary anchor
and soil builder, while chamomile occupies slightly moister niches closer to
irrigation. Both belong to the Asteraceae family and attract similar
pollinators, creating a layered beneficial-insect habitat. In the apothecary,
yarrow addresses acute conditions (wounds, fevers, cramps) while chamomile
supports chronic, nervous-system complaints (anxiety, insomnia, mild GI
spasms). Together they cover a broad spectrum of common household ailments.
