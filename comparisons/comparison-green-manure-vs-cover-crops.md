# Green Manure vs Cover Crops

## Overview
Green manures and cover crops are closely related soil-building practices that are
frequently conflated but differ in purpose and management. Green manures are crops
grown specifically to be dug back into the soil while still green, adding organic
matter and nutrients through decomposition. Cover crops are plants grown primarily for
the benefit of the soil itself, including erosion control, weed suppression, nitrogen
fixation, compaction breaking, and nutrient scavenging, and may or may not be
incorporated into the soil. The distinction matters because green manure is a subset
of cover crop use: all green manures serve the soil, but not all cover crops become
green manures.

## Green Manure
[[green-manure-guide]]
Green manuring is the practice of growing crops specifically for incorporation into the
soil. The defining characteristic is intentional digging in: the crop is cut, wilted
briefly on the surface to reduce moisture content, and buried at approximately six
inches depth before it becomes woody, where it decomposes rapidly and releases nutrients
for the following crop. The technique is particularly valuable on light, sandy soils
prone to winter nutrient leaching, and on any soil that needs rapid organic matter
addition between cash crop rotations.

Crop selection follows rotation principles: legumes (clover, vetch, fava beans, field
peas, crimson clover) fix atmospheric nitrogen through rhizobium bacteria in their
root nodules, while non-legumes (winter rye, mustard, buckwheat, phacelia) add organic
matter and mine subsoil minerals with deep root systems. The practitioner must avoid
planting green manures from the same family as the preceding or following crop to
prevent disease carryover, a notable limitation with brassica green manures like
mustard that risk perpetuating clubroot. Dual-purpose crops like fava beans allow
harvesting some pods for food while still incorporating the bulk of the plant material
as green manure.

The strengths of green manuring are speed of organic matter addition, nitrogen fixation
from legume crops (40 to 200 pounds per acre depending on species), weed suppression
during growth, soil structure improvement from root penetration, and very low cost
with seed as the primary expense. The weaknesses include taking land out of production
during the green manure phase, the physical labor of digging in, the risk of nitrogen
robbery if woody material is incorporated (where decomposition microbes temporarily
tie up available nitrogen), and the delay between incorporation and nutrient
availability as decomposition completes.

## Cover Crops
[[cover-crop-guide-detailed]]
Cover cropping is a broader soil health strategy where plants are grown primarily for
their ecosystem services rather than harvest. Benefits include erosion prevention on
bare soil, weed suppression through competition and allelopathic chemicals, nitrogen
fixation from legume species, nutrient scavenging that prevents leaching of soluble
nutrients below the root zone, compaction breaking by deep taproots like daikon radish
and tillage radish, beneficial insect habitat and nectar sources, and biomass
accumulation for soil building. Cover crops may be terminated by tilling, roller-
crimping, mowing, or natural winter-kill without incorporation into the soil.

Modern cover cropping emphasizes multi-species mixes that outperform monocultures
through complementary root architectures (shallow fibrous roots alongside deep
taproots), diverse biomass types (grasses for carbon, legumes for nitrogen), and
synergistic biological interactions. A cold-climate fall mix might combine cereal rye
(erosion control, winter-hardy biomass), hairy vetch (nitrogen fixation, climbing
growth), crimson clover (nitrogen fixation, early spring bloom for pollinators), and
daikon radish (compaction breaking, nutrient scavenging from deep soil layers). Each
species occupies a different niche, creating a living mulch that protects and feeds
soil simultaneously.

Cover cropping is foundational to no-till and regenerative agriculture because it
provides the biomass needed for surface mulching and the biological stimulation that
replaces tillage as a weed management tool. The challenges are more complex than green
manuring: species selection requires understanding seasonal appropriateness, hardiness
zones, and termination timing for each species. Some species carry risks, such as
sorghum-sudangrass prussic acid toxicity to livestock and mustard clubroot potential
for brassica crops. Seed costs for multi-species mixes add up at farm scale, and
roller-crimpers or other no-till termination equipment may be needed.

## Key Differences
| Aspect | Green Manure | Cover Crops |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| Primary Purpose | Add organic matter and nutrients to soil | Provide multiple soil health services |
| Incorporation | Always dug in (defining characteristic) | May or may not be incorporated |
| Termination Method | Digging/tilling in | Crimping, mowing, winter-kill, or tilling |
| Species Selection | Focused on decomposability and nutrient content | Focused on ecosystem services |
| Complexity | Low to moderate | Moderate (multi-species, seasonal timing) |
| Soil Disturbance | Yes (digging in) | Can be zero-till (roller-crimping) |
| Best Systems | Traditional gardens, allotments | No-till, regenerative, broadacre |
| Nitrogen Fixation | Legumes only | Legumes in multi-species mixes |
| Erosion Control | Moderate (during growth) | Excellent (including winter cover) |
| Relationship | Subset of cover cropping use | Broader category encompassing green manure |

## When to Choose Green Manure
Green manuring is the simpler, more traditional approach best suited to small gardens
and allotments where the practitioner wants a straightforward method of adding organic
matter and nitrogen. It is ideal when there is a clear fallow period in the crop
rotation, when light soils need winter protection, and when the physical labor of
digging is acceptable. Green manuring works well in deep-bed and biointensive systems
where soil incorporation is already part of the management practice.

## When to Choose Cover Crops
Cover cropping is the superior choice for no-till systems, regenerative agriculture,
and any operation that wants to minimize soil disturbance. Multi-species cover crop
mixes provide more comprehensive soil benefits than any single green manure crop.
Cover crops suit larger-scale operations where mechanical termination (roller-crimping)
eliminates the need for hand-digging. They are essential for year-round soil coverage,
preventing erosion and maintaining biological activity through all seasons.

## Hybrid Approaches
The most effective approach combines both concepts: grow multi-species cover crops for
their full range of ecosystem services, then terminate and incorporate a portion as
green manure while leaving surface residue as mulch. In no-till systems, cover crops
are roller-crimped and left as surface mulch (no incorporation), while in traditional
systems, they may be partially incorporated. The cover crop-green manure spectrum is
continuous rather than binary, and the best practice adapts the approach to the
specific system, season, and soil needs.
