## Biogas Digesters vs Composting Toilets

## Overview

Biogas digesters and composting toilets represent two fundamentally different
approaches to managing human waste on the homestead or farm. A biogas
digester processes organic waste anaerobically to produce methane gas for
cooking and heating, while a composting toilet processes waste aerobically to
produce compost for soil building. Both systems close the nutrient loop that
conventional flush toilets break, but they operate through entirely different
biological mechanisms and serve different primary purposes.

## Biogas Digesters

The three main types are batch systems (dual-chamber designs where one
composts while the other fills), continuous systems (single large chambers
where waste moves gradually through the decomposition process), and dry
toilet or humanure systems (bucket-based collection with carbon cover
material). Each approach produces finished compost after 6 to 12 months
of decomposition, during which time pathogen levels drop dramatically.

Human excreta contains roughly 4.5 kilograms of nitrogen, 1.5 kilograms
of phosphorus, and 1 kilogram of potassium per person per year. Mollison
calculated that these nutrients could fertilize enough food to feed the
person who produced them, creating a genuine closed-loop nutrient cycle.
The mollison-sewage-biogas-systems page describes how community-scale
systems can offset substantial energy costs while simultaneously treating
sewage. The digestate liquid is a potent fertilizer that retains nitrogen
more effectively than [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]], ornamental plantings, and field crops. For
permaculture systems focused on closing nutrient loops, composting toilets
offer the most straightforward path from human waste back to productive
soil.

## Hybrid Approaches

The most sophisticated permaculture waste systems combine both technologies.
A biogas digester processes kitchen scraps, [[jadam-liquid-fertilizer-nutritional-perspective]], while a composting toilet handles
human excreta separately for compost production. This avoids pathogen
concerns of putting human waste in biogas systems while still capturing
energy from other organic waste streams.

Mollison described integrated multi-stage systems where sewage passes
through anaerobic ponds for biogas production, then facultative and
aerobic ponds for further treatment, ultimately producing clean water
for irrigation and algae for animal feed. These systems represent the
most complete realization of the permaculture principle that waste equals
food and every element should perform multiple functions.
