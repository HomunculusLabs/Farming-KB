## Beauveria bassiana vs Metarhizium anisopliae

## Overview

[[beauveria-bassiana]] and [[metarhizium-anisopliae]] are the two most widely
studied and commercially deployed entomopathogenic fungi in biological pest
control. Both are soil-dwelling ascomycetes that kill insects by penetrating
the cuticle, proliferating inside the hemocoel, and producing conidia on the
dead host. Despite their shared mode of action, they differ significantly in
host range, environmental persistence, production requirements, and optimal
use scenarios. Understanding these differences is essential for selecting the
right fungal biocontrol agent in permaculture and integrated pest management.

## Beauveria bassiana

[[beauveria-bassiana]] is a cosmopolitan fungus named after Agostino Bassi,
who first demonstrated that a microorganism could cause disease in animals.
It produces white fluffy growth on infected cadavers, earning it the name
"white muscardine fungus." B. bassiana has one of the broadest host ranges
among entomopathogenic fungi, attacking insects across Coleoptera, Lepidoptera,
Hemiptera, Diptera, and Hymenoptera.

The infection process begins when conidia attach to the insect cuticle,
germinate, and form penetration pegs that breach the exoskeleton using
cuticle-degrading enzymes including proteases, chitinases, and lipases. Once
inside the hemocoel, blastospores circulate in the hemolymph, overwhelming
immune defenses. After host death, the fungus emerges and sporulates on the
cadaver surface, producing conidia dispersed by wind, rain, or direct contact.

Isolates from tropical and subtropical regions show considerably more genetic
variation than those from temperate zones, suggesting tropical centers of
diversification. This diversity translates into a wide range of virulence
profiles, environmental tolerances, and host preferences. Commercial products
like BotaniGard and Naturalis target whiteflies, thrips, aphids, and beetles
in greenhouse and field settings.

## Metarhizium anisopliae

[[metarhizium-anisopliae]] was first described in 1879 by Metschnikoff and
is the oldest known entomopathogenic fungus used in biocontrol. It produces
green spore masses on cadavers, giving rise to the name "green muscardine
disease." The Metarhizium genus has undergone significant taxonomic revision,
with M. anisopliae now split into multiple species including M. robertsii and
M. brunneum based on multilocus phylogenetics.

Unlike B. bassiana's broad host range, many M. anisopliae strains show
narrower host specificity. This can be advantageous — preserving non-target
beneficial insects while targeting specific pests. Strain selection is
critical, as virulence varies significantly among isolates. Commercial
products like Met52 and Green Guard use proprietary high-virulence strains
selected for particular target pests.

M. anisopliae persists in soil for months to years, providing residual pest
control and reducing the need for repeated applications. This persistence
makes it especially valuable in permaculture systems where long-term pest
suppression is preferred over repeated interventions. Target pests include
termites, grasshoppers, locusts, whiteflies, and beetle larvae.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | B. bassiana | M. anisopliae |
|--------|-------------|---------------|
| Cadaver color | White sporulation | Green sporulation |
| Host range | Very broad (700+ species) | Narrow to moderate (varies by strain) |
| Persistence | Weeks to months | Months to years in soil |
| Optimal temp | 20-30°C | 20-30°C (some strains to 35°C) |
| Humidity need | High (≥90% RH ideal) | High (≥90% RH ideal) |
| Non-target risk | Moderate (broad range) | Lower (strain-specific) |
| Soil persistence | Moderate | Strong |
| Commercial names | BotaniGard, Naturalis | Met52, Green Guard |
| Best known for | Whiteflies, thrips, aphids | Termites, locusts, beetles |

## When to Choose Beauveria bassiana

Choose B. bassiana when dealing with diverse pest complexes in greenhouse or
indoor growing environments. Its broad host range means a single application
can suppress multiple pest species simultaneously — whiteflies alongside
thrips and aphids, for example. It is the preferred choice for cannabis and
 specialty crop growers facing mixed infestations where identifying a single
dominant pest is difficult. In tropical and subtropical climates, the greater
genetic diversity of local B. bassiana isolates offers better adaptation to
high temperature and humidity conditions.

B. bassiana also excels in foliar application scenarios where rapid infection
and kill are needed. Its relatively faster germination and infection cycle
under favorable humidity makes it effective against quickly reproducing pests
like aphids and whiteflies. However, its broader host range means greater
caution is needed when beneficial insects are present in the system.

## When to Choose Metarhizium anisopliae

Choose M. anisopliae for soil-dwelling pests, long-term pest suppression, and
situations where preserving beneficial insects is paramount. Its superior
soil persistence — lasting months to years — makes it ideal for permaculture
systems designed around low-input, long-cycle pest management. Apply it as a
soil drench for grub and beetle larvae control, or as a broadcast treatment
against termites and locusts.

The narrower host specificity of many M. anisopliae strains is a significant
advantage in polyculture systems where beneficial insects, pollinators, and
predatory arthropods must be preserved. By selecting a strain targeting the
specific pest, you minimize collateral damage to the beneficial insect
community. This aligns well with integrated pest management philosophies that
prioritize ecological balance over total pest elimination.

## Combined Use in IPM

Both fungi can be used together in a rotation program. Apply B. bassiana as a
foliar spray for above-ground pests during humid periods, while establishing
M. anisopliae in the soil for long-term grub and soil pest suppression. Avoid
tank-mixing them, as competition between the two species may reduce efficacy
of both. Alternate applications by two to four weeks to allow each fungus to
establish independently.
