# Kelp Meal vs Basalt Rock Dust

## Overview

Both kelp meal and basalt rock dust are valued organic soil amendments prized
for their broad-spectrum trace mineral content. While they share the goal of
restoring depleted soils, they originate from entirely different sources — one
from the ocean and one from volcanic rock — and they differ significantly in
their nutrient profiles, application methods, cost, and best use cases.

## Kelp Meal

[[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]].

Beyond mineral nutrition, kelp meal provides several unique biological benefits.
Its alginic acid content attracts beneficial bacteria and improves soil particle
aggregation, enhancing soil structure. Kelp also contains natural growth-promoting
hormones (cytokinins, auxins, and gibberellins) that can stimulate root
development and improve plant vigor when absorbed through foliage or roots.

Kelp meal is most economically used as a compost activator or as a targeted
amendment where trace element deficiencies are confirmed. Its relatively high
cost per kilogram limits large-scale field applications, but for home gardens,
container plants, and compost enrichment, it is an excellent choice. Fresh
seaweed gathered from beaches can serve as a lower-cost alternative in coastal
areas, though salt content must be managed.

## Basalt Rock Dust

Basalt dust is widely available and relatively inexpensive compared to kelp
meal, making it practical for large-scale agricultural use at rates of 500 to
2,000 kg per hectare. Its nutrient release spans multiple growing seasons,
with fine particles (<75 microns) becoming available within the first year and
coarse particles releasing over 3 to 10 years. See [[rock-dust-soil-remineralization]] for broader context on rock dust use.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Kelp Meal | Basalt Rock Dust |
|--------|-----------|------------------|
| **Source** | Marine seaweed | Volcanic rock |
| **Nitrogen** | 2.8% | None |
| **Phosphorus** | 0.2% | 0.5-2% (slow-release) |
| **Potassium** | 2.3% | 1-5% (slow-release) |
| **Silica** | Minimal | 45-52% (major benefit) |
| **Trace Elements** | 60-70 elements | Broad spectrum |
| **Growth Hormones** | Yes (cytokinins, auxins) | None |
| **Release Speed** | Moderate (weeks-months) | Slow (months-years) |
| **Cost per kg** | Higher | Lower |
| **Best Scale** | Home garden, compost | Field, orchard, garden |
| **pH Effect** | Neutral | Mild liming (raises pH) |
| **Soil Structure** | Alginate improves aggregation | Silicates improve CEC |

## When to Choose Kelp Meal

Kelp meal is the better choice when your soil needs immediate biological
stimulation along with trace minerals. Its nitrogen content makes it useful as
a light fertilizer, while its growth hormones support transplant establishment
and stress recovery. Use kelp meal as a compost activator to jump-start
microbial populations, as a top-dressing for container plants showing trace
element deficiency symptoms, or as a foliar spray ingredient for quick nutrient
uptake during the growing season. Gardeners near coastlines can supplement with
fresh seaweed for a lower-cost alternative. See [[azomite-mineral-amendment]] for comparison with another popular rock dust.

## Combined Use

Kelp meal and basalt rock dust are highly complementary and work well together.
Basalt provides the slow-release mineral foundation while kelp meal adds
nitrogen, growth hormones, and immediate biological stimulation. A practical
approach for home gardens is to apply basalt dust as a base amendment in fall
or early spring (2-4 kg per 10 m²) and supplement with kelp meal as a compost
activator or top-dressing during the growing season. In compost piles, adding
both basalt dust (2-5% by volume) and kelp meal creates a mineral-rich finished
compost that delivers both immediate and long-term benefits. See
