## Equipment Considerations
Pasteurization can be accomplished with relatively simple equipment. A 
55-gallon drum, a propane burner, and a wire mesh basket constitute a 
functional hot water bath pasteurizer for straw substrates. Steam 
pasteurization rooms require more infrastructure but are standard on 
commercial mushroom farms. The investment is moderate and the scale is 
flexible.
Sterilization demands a pressure vessel rated for at least 15 psi. For 
small-scale cultivation, a standard stovetop pressure cooker is adequate for 
quart jars and agar media. Larger operations require autoclaves or industrial 
pressure canners. The cost differential is significant, with pressure cookers 
starting around $50-100 while autoclaves run into thousands of dollars.

## Altitude Adjustments
Both processes require altitude corrections. At higher elevations, water 
boils at lower temperatures, reducing the effective heat treatment. For every 
1000 feet of elevation gain above sea level, the boiling point of water drops 
by approximately 2°F. At 5000 feet elevation, water boils at roughly 202°F, 
requiring an increase from 15 psi to approximately 20 psi to achieve the same 
250°F sterilization temperature. Pasteurization temperatures must similarly 
be adjusted upward at altitude to ensure effective microbial reduction.

## Common Mistakes
One of the most frequent errors in mushroom cultivation is pasteurizing a 
supplemented substrate when sterilization is required. Adding bran, grain, or 
other nitrogen sources to straw or sawdust and then only pasteurizing creates 
an ideal environment for rapid competitor mold colonization. Another common 
mistake is inadequate sterilization time for grain spawn. Because grain 
kernels contain internal cavities where bacteria and thermoresistant 
organisms can shelter, the minimum recommended time of 60 minutes at 15 psi 
should not be reduced. Shortcuts in sterilization are a primary cause of 
contaminated spawn jars.
