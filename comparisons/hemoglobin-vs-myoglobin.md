## Hemoglobin vs Myoglobin
Hemoglobin and myoglobin are related heme proteins that bind oxygen, but they solve different physiological problems.
Hemoglobin transports oxygen through the blood from respiratory surfaces to tissues.
Myoglobin stores and buffers oxygen inside muscle cells.
Both contain globin folds and iron-containing heme groups.
Their different quaternary structures produce different oxygen-binding behavior.
The contrast is a standard example of how protein architecture determines biological function.
## Short Answer
Hemoglobin is a tetrameric transport protein with cooperative oxygen binding.
Myoglobin is a monomeric storage protein with high oxygen affinity and no cooperativity.
Hemoglobin loads oxygen in the lung and unloads it in tissues.
Myoglobin holds oxygen inside skeletal and cardiac muscle until local oxygen pressure becomes very low.

## Structural Difference
Hemoglobin is composed of four globin chains, usually two alpha and two beta chains in adult humans.
Each chain carries one heme group, so the tetramer can bind four oxygen molecules.
The subunits communicate through their interfaces.
That communication is what makes cooperative binding possible.
Myoglobin is a single globin chain with one heme group.
Because it has only one binding site, one oxygen-binding event cannot increase affinity at another site.
Myoglobin therefore lacks quaternary allostery.
Its simpler structure makes it a local oxygen reservoir rather than a transport switch.
## Oxygen Binding Curves
Hemoglobin has a sigmoidal oxygen dissociation curve.
The first oxygen molecule binds relatively less easily than later oxygen molecules.
As oxygen binds, hemoglobin shifts from the T state toward the R state.
That transition increases affinity at remaining heme sites.
Myoglobin has a hyperbolic oxygen-binding curve.
It binds oxygen strongly at low oxygen pressures and becomes saturated quickly.
This high affinity means myoglobin does not release much oxygen under ordinary resting conditions.
It releases oxygen mainly when muscle oxygen pressure falls during intense work.
## Physiological Roles
Hemoglobin must perform two opposite tasks during every circulation cycle.
It must load oxygen efficiently in the lung or gill.
It must then unload a useful fraction of that oxygen in tissues.
Its moderate, adjustable affinity allows both tasks.
Myoglobin performs a more local task.
It keeps oxygen available near mitochondria in muscle.
It also smooths short-term changes in oxygen supply during contraction.
In diving mammals, unusually high myoglobin concentrations help muscles store oxygen during long dives.
In human muscle, myoglobin supports aerobic metabolism during periods of high demand.
## Regulation
Hemoglobin is strongly regulated by local chemistry.
Lower pH and higher carbon dioxide shift hemoglobin toward oxygen release through the Bohr effect.
2,3-BPG binds deoxygenated hemoglobin and stabilizes the low-affinity T state.
Heat also promotes oxygen unloading in active tissues.
These effects make hemoglobin sensitive to metabolic demand.
