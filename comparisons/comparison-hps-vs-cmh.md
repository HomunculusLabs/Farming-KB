# HPS vs CMH [[led-grow-lights]]

Choosing between HPS and CMH lighting is one of the most impactful decisions
for indoor cannabis cultivators. Both are high-intensity discharge (HID)
technologies that have proven themselves in [[cannabis-grow-room-setup]]s worldwide, but they
differ significantly in spectral output, efficiency, heat profile, and
versatility across [[cho-plant-growth-stage-treatments]]s. This comparison breaks down the strengths
and weaknesses of each to help you make the right choice for your operation.

## HPS (High Pressure Sodium)

[[hps-grow-lights]]

HPS (High Pressure Sodium) lamps are HID lights widely regarded as the most
efficient lighting for flowering cannabis. They produce yellow/orange/red
wavelengths (~589nm) that closely mimic the autumn sun, promoting dense bud
development and robust resin production. The 600W HPS is the most efficient
at 150 lumens/W, though wattages from 250W to 1000W are common. They have a
lifespan of 6,000–24,000 hours depending on model and usage patterns, are
widely available from countless manufacturers, and remain the commercial
standard for flowering rooms.

HPS strengths are concentrated in the flowering phase. The red-heavy spectrum
drives bud swelling, calyx development, and overall yield. Decades of commercial
cultivation data back HPS as a proven, reliable technology with predictable
results. Replacement bulbs are inexpensive and easy to source.

However, HPS has notable limitations. The narrow spectrum is poorly suited for
vegetative growth — plants under pure HPS tend to stretch with long internodal
spacing, necessitating MH (Metal Halide) supplementation during veg. HPS
fixtures generate significant heat, often requiring dedicated air-cooled
hoods, inline fans, and robust ventilation systems. Light intensity drops off
rapidly with distance from the canopy, meaning precise height management is
essential. Bulb output also degrades noticeably after 6,000–8,000 hours even
if the bulb still fires.

## CMH (Ceramic Metal Halide)

[[cmh-grow-lights]]

CMH (Ceramic Metal Halide), also marketed as LEC (Light Emitting Ceramic),
lamps use a ceramic arc tube to produce a broader, more balanced spectrum
across the PAR range (3000K–4000K). The 315W CMH covers a similar area to a
400W HPS with better full-cycle spectral quality and moderate UV output that
can enhance terpene and trichome production. This UV component is a meaningful
advantage — research suggests UV-B exposure increases cannabinoid and terpene
concentrations in cannabis flower.

CMH serves as a true single-lamp solution for both vegetative and flowering
phases. The balanced spectrum produces compact, healthy vegetative growth
without the stretching common under HPS, and transitions smoothly into
flowering without requiring a fixture swap. CMH generates less heat than HPS
(roughly 30–40% less at equivalent PAR output), offers 20,000+ hour lifespan
before significant degradation, and is simpler and cheaper than LED systems
while delivering comparable spectral quality.

Drawbacks of CMH include the requirement for a dedicated CMH ballast — these
are not interchangeable with HPS ballasts, which limits flexibility for growers
who already own HID infrastructure. CMH may produce slightly less dense buds
than HPS in pure flowering scenarios, as the spectrum spreads energy across
blue wavelengths rather than concentrating it in the red range. CMH still
generates meaningful heat requiring ventilation, and fixture selection is
more limited compared to the vast HPS market.

## Key Differences

| Factor | HPS | CMH |
|---|---|---|
| Approach | Red-heavy spectrum optimized for flowering | Balanced full-spectrum across PAR range |
| Cost | Lower initial cost per watt; widely available | Higher per-watt cost but fewer fixtures needed |
| Complexity | Similar HID setup; may need MH for veg | Similar HID setup; single lamp full-cycle |
| Best For | Flowering-heavy commercial operations | Full-cycle small-to-mid operations |
| Drawbacks | High heat, narrow spectrum, needs MH supplement | Dedicated ballast, slightly lower peak flowering density |
| Spectrum | ~589nm peak (yellow/orange/red) | Broad 3000K–4000K with UV-A |
| Heat Output | High; requires robust ventilation | Moderate; 30–40% less than equivalent HPS |
| Lifespan | 6,000–24,000 hours (replace at ~8K) | 20,000+ hours before significant degradation |
| UV Output | Minimal to none | Moderate UV-A for terpene enhancement |
| Vegetative Suitability | Poor without MH supplement | Excellent; compact growth from blue content |

## When to Choose HPS

HPS is the right call when your operation prioritizes flowering output above
all else. Choose HPS for commercial flowering rooms focused on maximum bud
density and yield per square foot, cost-sensitive operations where upfront
expense matters most, and growers who already run metal halide (MH) fixtures
for vegetative growth and want to complement them with a dedicated flowering
light. HPS remains unbeaten for raw flowering performance per dollar spent,
and its ubiquity means replacement parts and bulbs are always available.

## When to Choose CMH

CMH excels in scenarios where versatility and spectrum quality are priorities.
Choose CMH for small-to-mid operations wanting a single-lamp full-cycle
solution that eliminates the need for fixture swaps between veg and flower,
growers seeking UV benefits for enhanced terpene and trichome production,
and anyone wanting a middle ground between the flowering punch of HPS and the
spectrum quality of LED without the LED price premium. The balanced spectrum
produces healthier vegetative growth and high-quality flower with fewer
fixtures to manage.

## Hybrid Approaches

Some experienced growers combine both technologies for best-in-class results
at each growth stage: running CMH fixtures during the vegetative phase for
their balanced spectrum and UV output, then switching to HPS during flowering
to maximize bud density and overall yield. This approach captures the strengths
of each technology — CMH's full-cycle quality for building healthy plants, and
HPS's concentrated red output for driving flower production. The trade-off is
increased equipment cost, the need to manage two different ballast and fixture
types, and more complex environmental controls to handle the heat profile
change when switching lighting systems.
