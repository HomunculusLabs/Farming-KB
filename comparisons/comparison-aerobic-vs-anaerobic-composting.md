# Aerobic vs Anaerobic Composting

## Overview

Aerobic composting uses oxygen-loving microorganisms to decompose organic
matter at elevated temperatures, producing finished compost in weeks.
Anaerobic fermentation excludes oxygen, relying on different microbial
communities that work more slowly but preserve more nutrients. The choice
between these methods involves trade-offs in speed, nutrient retention,
labor requirements, and the type of soil amendment produced.

## Aerobic Composting

[[jadam-anaerobic-fermentation]]

Anaerobic composting excludes oxygen, relying on fermentative
microorganisms including photosynthetic bacteria, lactobacillus, and yeasts
to break down organic matter. JADAM's philosophy, developed by Youngsang
Cho, argues that anaerobic processes are universal in nature, from animal
digestion to kimchi production to leaf mold formation, and preserve
nutrients far better than aerobic methods because air, sunlight, and heat
are the three biggest nutrient destroyers in agriculture.

The practical JADAM approach, known as the gunnysack method, is radically
simple. Fill sacks with wild grass, leaves, food waste, and manure, wrap
tightly in plastic sheeting, and leave undisturbed for three months. No
turning, no carbon-to-nitrogen calculations, no moisture monitoring. JADAM
also simplifies liquid fertilizer production by adding plant materials and
a handful of leaf mold directly to water, relying on naturally present
anaerobic microorganisms to ferment the mixture. This zero-equipment
approach makes fertility production accessible to the poorest farmers.

Anaerobic methods produce amendments rich in preserved vitamins, amino
acids, organic acids, and plant growth hormones that aerobic composting
destroys through heat and oxidation. The process requires zero equipment,
no turning, no calculations, and minimal labor. However, the end product
differs significantly from aerobic compost. It is more acidic, has a
distinct fermented odor during production, takes longer to integrate into
soil biology, and may carry pathogens if the feedstock included manure. The
scientific evidence base for JADAM's claims is more limited than the
extensive research supporting aerobic composting.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Aerobic Composting | Anaerobic Composting |
|--------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| Oxygen | Required (turned piles) | Excluded (sealed containers) |
| Temperature | 130-160 degrees F | Ambient to mildly warm |
| Speed | 4-6 weeks to finished | 2-6 months |
| Nutrient Retention | Some loss (ammonia, vitamins) | Higher (vitamins, amino acids preserved) |
| Pathogen Kill | Yes (thermophilic phase) | Variable (depends on method) |
| Weed Seed Kill | Yes (131 degrees F+) | Partial (many survive) |
| Labor | High (turning, monitoring) | Very low (fill and forget) |
| Odor | Earthy when properly managed | Fermented, potentially strong |
| Input Range | Limited (no meat/dairy/oils) | Broad (accepts most organics) |
| Equipment | Pitchfork, thermometer | Sacks, plastic sheeting |
| End Product | Stable humus, neutral pH | Acidic, enzyme-rich ferment |
| Volume Reduction | 50-75% | Variable, less reduction |
| Scientific Evidence | Extensive | Growing, more limited |
| Skill Required | Moderate | Very low |

## When to Choose Aerobic Composting

Aerobic composting is the best choice when rapid turnaround is needed for
large volumes of organic material, when pathogen and weed seed destruction
is critical for biosecurity, and when producing stable, neutral-pH compost
for broad field application. It suits farms and homesteads with sufficient
material flow to build proper piles and the labor to manage them. Aerobic
composting is also preferable when the end product needs to be immediately
compatible with a wide range of plants without pH adjustment, and when
producing compost for sale or sharing where consistent quality is
important.

## When to Choose Anaerobic Composting

Anaerobic methods excel for ultra-low-cost operations where labor must be
minimized, following the JADAM philosophy of zero external inputs. They
suit small farms and homesteads that value nutrient preservation over
speed, and are ideal for producing liquid fertilizers and fermented plant
extracts that feed plants through both soil drenching and foliar
application. Anaerobic composting also works well in apartments and small
spaces where turning a large pile is impractical, and in any context where
the simplicity of fill-and-forget production outweighs the need for rapid,
pathogen-free output.

## Hybrid Approaches

Many practitioners use both methods strategically. Aerobic hot composting
handles bulk organic waste and produces stable soil amendment, while
anaerobic fermentation produces concentrated liquid fertilizers and
nutrient-dense extracts. Bokashi is essentially a hybrid system, using
anaerobic fermentation with effective microorganisms to pre-process food
waste including meat and dairy, then burying the fermented material in soil
where aerobic decomposition completes the process. JADAM combines anaerobic
fermentation with aerobic leaf mold production in a complete fertility
system that uses both pathways. Some gardeners start piles aerobically to
kill pathogens, then seal them for anaerobic finishing to preserve
remaining nutrients.
