# Arbuscular vs Ectomycorrhizal Fungi

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] (ECM) represent
the two dominant mycorrhizal symbioses on Earth. AMF (phylum Glomeromycota)
associate with 80-90% of plant species including most crops, forming
intracellular arbuscules within root cortical cells. ECM fungi (Basidiomycota
and Ascomycota, roughly 6,000-8,000 species) partner primarily with woody
plants, wrapping roots in a fungal mantle and extending hyphae between cells
as a Hartig net.

Both types trade plant carbon for soil nutrients, but their strategies for
nutrient acquisition, soil carbon dynamics, and stress mediation differ in ways
that matter for farmers, foresters, and ecologists. While AMF dominate
agricultural landscapes, ECM fungi govern nutrient cycling in the world's
great forests. Choosing between them is dictated by the plants being grown and
the ecosystem context rather than preference.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi

AMF are the workhorses of agricultural systems. Their extraradical hyphal
networks extend 10-25 cm from root surfaces, exploring soil volumes 100-1,000x
greater than roots alone. Hyphae are only 2-10 micrometers in diameter,
thinner than root hairs, penetrating micropores inaccessible to plant roots.
Key strengths include:

- **Phosphorus acquisition**: AMF increase P uptake by 60-200%, potentially
  replacing 30-50% of P fertilizer requirements
- **Water relations**: Hyphal networks improve water uptake during drought
- **Micronutrient delivery**: Enhanced uptake of zinc, copper, and manganese
- **Disease suppression**: Physical barrier and induced systemic resistance
  against soilborne pathogens including Pythium and Fusarium
- **Soil aggregation**: Glomalin binds soil particles and contributes up to
  27% of soil carbon in some systems
- **Broad host range**: Most crops, grasses, and many trees; notable non-hosts
  include Brassicaceae and Chenopodiaceae
- **Common networks**: AMF connect multiple plant species simultaneously

AMF are obligate symbionts sensitive to high soil phosphorus, tillage, and
fallow periods. Plants provide 10-20% of net photosynthate plus essential
fatty acids that AMF cannot synthesize. Spores are large (50-500 um),
thick-walled, and survive for years to decades. Arbuscules are short-lived
(4-15 days) and continuously replaced.

## Ectomycorrhizal Fungi

ECM fungi dominate in forest ecosystems, partnering with roughly 8,000 woody
plant species in boreal and temperate forests. Their anatomical structure
features a dense mantle sheath (3-7 hyphal layers) and intercellular Hartig
net. Where AMF penetrate inside root cells, ECM fungi remain outside. Key
capabilities include:

- **Organic nitrogen mining**: ECM enzymes break down proteins, peptides, and
  amino acids, accessing N unavailable to unaided roots
- **Enzyme production**: External mycelium secretes phosphatases and organic
  acids for nutrient mobilization from mineral and organic sources
- **Carbon storage**: Chitin and melanin in cell walls resist decomposition,
  promoting long-term soil carbon accumulation
- **Heavy metal tolerance**: Some ECM species protect hosts from toxic metals
- **Drought resilience**: Enhanced water uptake and stomatal modulation
- **Functional diversity**: Individual trees associate with dozens to hundreds
  of ECM species with different capabilities and stress tolerances
- **Common networks**: ECM hyphae interconnect trees in "wood-wide webs"

ECM fungi receive 10-30% of host net primary production as carbon, supporting
mycelial networks extending meters from root tips. Diversity spans Amanita,
Boletus, Cortinarius, Lactarius, Russula, and Pisolithus. Host specificity
varies from single-genus specialists to broad generalists.

## Key Differences

| Feature | AMF | ECM |
|---|---|---|
| **Fungal phylum** | Glomeromycota | Basidiomycota, Ascomycota |
| **Anatomical interface** | Intracellular arbuscules | Mantle + Hartig net between cells |
| **Host range** | Broad: 80-90% of plant species | Narrow: ~8,000 mostly woody species |
| **Primary nutrient strength** | Phosphorus (60-200% increase) | Organic nitrogen via enzymatic mining |
| **Soil exploration range** | 10-25 cm from root | Meters from root |
| **Carbon cost to plant** | 10-20% of net photosynthate | 10-30% of net primary production |
| **Soil carbon influence** | Glomalin; moderate C storage | Chitin/melanin; recalcitrant C pools |
| **Decomposition impact** | Neutral to positive | Gadgil effect: suppresses saprotrophs |
| **Spore dispersal** | Large thick-walled spores | Fruiting bodies (mushrooms, truffles) |
| **Commercial availability** | Widely available inoculants | Difficult to cultivate; limited products |
| **Key host families** | Solanaceae, Poaceae, Leguminosae | Pinaceae, Fagaceae, Betulaceae |
| **Non-host families** | Brassicaceae, Chenopodiaceae | Most herbaceous and crop species |
| **Drought role** | Hyphal water uptake | Water uptake + stomatal modulation |
| **Climate sensitivity** | Affected by tillage and P levels | Sensitive to N deposition; climate shifts |

## When to Choose AMF

- **Row crops and vegetables**: Tomatoes, peppers, corn, wheat, beans, onions,
  lettuce, and cucumbers are strong AMF hosts
- **Phosphorus-limited soils**: Most effective biological tool for P
  availability; strongest responses below 20-30 ppm P
- **Reducing fertilizer inputs**: AMF-rich soils require fewer inputs as
  hyphal networks mature and nutrient cycling intensifies
- **Dryland farming**: AMF-mediated drought tolerance is well documented
- **No-till systems**: Preserving hyphal networks amplifies AMF benefits
- **Soil restoration**: Glomalin improves aggregation faster than amendments
- **Cover crop rotations**: Maintaining host plants sustains AMF populations
- **Organic farming**: AMF inoculants approved and reduce soluble fertilizer
  dependence

## When to Choose ECM

- **Forestry and reforestation**: Pine, oak, birch, spruce, and beech
  seedlings benefit enormously from ECM inoculation
- **Organic-matter-rich soils**: ECM enzymes unlock organic N that AMF cannot
  access, especially valuable in forest soils
- **Carbon sequestration**: ECM stands accumulate more soil organic matter
  via the Gadgil effect
- **Heavy metal remediation**: Some ECM species sequester toxic metals
- **Mine reclamation**: ECM tree plantings standard on harsh substrates
- **Nursery seedling production**: ECM inoculation improves survival rates
- **Truffle cultivation**: ECM species (Tuber spp.) grown commercially

## Hybrid Approaches

In many systems, AMF and ECM coexist and complement each other. AMF dominate
in herbaceous root zones and upper soil horizons, while ECM excel in woody
root zones and access deeper soil layers.

- **Agroforestry**: AMF crops in understory, ECM trees overhead; distinct
  root zones reduce competition
- **Successional plantings**: Early species rely on AMF, late-successional
  dominants shift toward ECM
- **Woodlot edge management**: Diverse root environments support both types
- **Soil management for both**: Minimizing tillage, maintaining living roots,
  and avoiding excessive P support both types simultaneously

The most resilient plant communities leverage both symbioses. AMF excel at
rapid phosphorus delivery and soil stabilization, while ECM provide organic
nutrient access and long-term carbon storage. The practical question is not
"AMF or ECM?" but "how do I support the mycorrhizal type matching my plants?"
