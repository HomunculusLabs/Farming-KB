# Greenhouse vs Underground Walipini
## Overview
Both traditional above-ground greenhouses and underground walipinis extend the
growing season and protect crops from harsh weather, but they achieve this
through fundamentally different thermal strategies. A conventional greenhouse
relies on glazing and active or passive solar capture to trap heat within an
insulated envelope. A walipini digs into the earth, using geothermal insulation
and massive thermal mass to maintain stable temperatures with minimal
supplemental energy. Choosing between them depends on your climate, budget, site
conditions, target crops, and how much construction labor you are prepared to
invest.
## Greenhouse Design
A greenhouse is a freestanding or attached structure built above grade, framed
in galvanized steel, PVC, or timber, and covered with glazing such as
polyethylene film, twin-wall polycarbonate, or glass. Homestead-scale designs
range from simple high tunnels at $2-6 per square foot to fully equipped
freestanding greenhouses at $10-30 per square foot. A typical 20x48 ft high
tunnel costs $2,000-4,000 in DIY form.
Greenhouses offer excellent light exposure from all directions, flexible siting
on varied terrain, and straightforward construction that most homesteaders can
complete in a weekend to a few weeks. Ventilation is the primary design
challenge since overheating kills more plants than cold. Roll-up sides, ridge
vents, and exhaust fans manage airflow, while shade cloth handles summer heat.
Passive solar techniques like thermal mass water barrels (2-3 gallons per sq ft
of glazing), insulated north walls (R-10+), and foundation insulation reduce
heating costs. Supplemental options include compost heating, geothermal air
tubes, rocket mass heaters, or electric unit heaters. Despite these strategies,
most above-ground greenhouses in cold climates still require some supplemental
heat through deep winter.
The modular nature of greenhouses makes them easy to extend, retrofit, or
relocate. They integrate naturally with rainwater catchment, seed-starting
stations, vermicomposting bins, and cover cropping rotations.
## Underground Walipini
A walipini (from the Aymara word for "place of warmth") is an earth-sheltered
greenhouse excavated 6-8 feet below grade with a south-facing glazed roof. The
surrounding earth maintains a constant 50-60°F below the frost line, insulating
the growing space on the floor and two long walls. A thick, uninsulated north
wall built from rammed earth, stone, or concrete block serves as primary thermal
mass, absorbing daytime solar gain and radiating it back at night.
Temperature performance is impressive: winter daytime highs reach 60-80°F even
when outside air is below 20°F, and nighttime lows stay 10-25°F above ambient.
This stability comes from massive earthen thermal inertia that prevents rapid
temperature swings, something no above-ground structure at comparable cost can
replicate. Construction is significantly more labor-intensive than a conventional
greenhouse. It requires heavy excavation, retaining wall engineering, and
critically, comprehensive drainage infrastructure. A French drain around the
perimeter below floor level, a gravel base, and proper site grading are all
essential. Water infiltration is the most common cause of walipini failure; sites
with high water tables or poor drainage should be avoided.
Summer presents the opposite challenge: the same thermal mass that retains
winter heat resists cooling, making overheating a real risk. Adequate
ventilation through a top cupola vent, low south-side intake vents, and wide
doors is essential. High humidity and condensation on glazing are persistent
issues that require vigilant airflow management to prevent mold and fungal
disease.
## Key Differences
| Factor | Greenhouse | Underground Walipini |
|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| Thermal strategy | Glazing + insulation + heat | Earth sheltering + geothermal mass |
| Winter heating needs | Moderate to high | Minimal to none |
| Summer cooling | Straightforward ventilation | Challenging; mass resists cooling |
| Construction cost | $2-30/sq ft | $5-15/sq ft plus heavy labor |
| Construction effort | Low to moderate | High (dig, drainage, walls) |
| Light exposure | Full, all directions | South-facing only; north opaque |
| Humidity control | Manageable with ventilation | High humidity; condensation on glazing |
| Siting flexibility | High; many terrain options | Low; needs drainage, southern exposure |
| Lifespan | 5-20+ years by material | 20+ years if drainage is maintained |
| Expandability | Easy to extend or add bays | Difficult; size set at excavation |
| Flood risk | Low | High without proper drainage |
| Best climate fit | All zones with appropriate design | Cold, dry climates; low water table |
| Ideal crops | Full range including tall fruiting crops | Greens, roots, alliums, low growers |
## When to Choose a Greenhouse
Choose a traditional greenhouse when you need maximum flexibility in crop
selection, including tall fruiting crops like tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers
that demand full light exposure from multiple angles. Greenhouses are the right
call when your site has poor drainage, a seasonal high water table, shallow
bedrock, or uneven terrain that makes deep excavation impractical or expensive.
They suit homesteaders who want a faster build with manageable labor, or who
value the ability to relocate or reconfigure the structure over time. In mild
climates (zones 7-9), a well-designed high tunnel with passive solar features
may provide adequate winter production without the extreme thermal mass of a
walipini. Greenhouses also integrate seamlessly with rainwater catchment,
seed-starting stations, and composting systems.
## When to Choose a Walipini
Choose a walipini when reliable winter growing in a cold climate (zones 3-6)
is the primary goal and minimizing supplemental heating is a priority. The
earth's stable subterranean temperature provides a thermal buffer that no amount
of above-ground insulation can match at equivalent cost. A walipini can keep the
growing space above freezing through winters that would cripple an unheated
above-ground greenhouse.
Walipinis excel on homesteads with well-drained soil, a low water table, and an
unobstructed southern exposure free of shading from 9 AM to 3 PM even in
midwinter. They are compelling for off-grid or low-energy operations where fuel
or electricity for heating is scarce or expensive. If your primary winter crops
are leafy greens, root vegetables, and alliums, the walipini's limited light on
the north side is not a meaningful drawback since these crops thrive in the
conditions it provides.
## Hybrid Approaches
Many homesteaders combine elements of both systems:
- **Earth-bermed greenhouse:** An above-ground greenhouse with soil piled
  against the north and side walls. Adds thermal mass without full excavation.
- **Partially excavated high tunnel:** Digging 2-3 feet inside a high tunnel
  provides partial earth sheltering and warmer root-zone temperatures without
  the drainage challenges of a 6-8 foot walipini depth.
- **Walipini with attached greenhouse bay:** A walipini for winter greens
  connected to an above-ground bay for summer fruiting crops, sharing a wall
  and combined thermal mass.
- **Active thermal coupling:** Circulating water or air between a walipini's
  earth mass and an adjacent greenhouse to transfer stored geothermal heat to
  the above-ground structure during cold nights.
