# Greenhouse vs Underground Walipini
## Overview
Both traditional above-ground greenhouses and underground walipinis extend the
[[comparison-passive-solar-vs-earth-sheltered]] capture to trap heat within an
insulated envelope. A walipini digs into the earth, using geothermal insulation
and massive thermal mass to maintain stable temperatures with minimal
supplemental energy. Choosing between them depends on your climate, budget, site
conditions, target crops, and how much construction labor you are prepared to
invest.
## Greenhouse Design
A greenhouse is a freestanding or attached structure built above grade, framed
in galvanized steel, PVC, or timber, and covered with glazing such as
polyethylene film, twin-wall polycarbonate, or glass. Homestead-scale designs
range from simple high tunnels at $2-6 per square foot to fully equipped
freestanding greenhouses at $10-30 per square foot. A typical 20x48 ft high
tunnel costs $2,000-4,000 in DIY form.
Greenhouses offer excellent light exposure from all directions, flexible siting
on varied terrain, and straightforward construction that most homesteaders can
complete in a weekend to a few weeks. Ventilation is the primary design
challenge since overheating kills more plants than cold. Roll-up sides, ridge
vents, and exhaust fans manage airflow, while shade cloth handles summer heat.
Passive solar techniques like thermal mass water barrels (2-3 gallons per sq ft
of glazing), insulated north walls (R-10+), and foundation insulation reduce
heating costs. Supplemental options include compost heating, geothermal air
tubes, rocket mass heaters, or electric unit heaters. Despite these strategies,
most above-ground greenhouses in cold climates still require some supplemental
heat through deep winter.
The modular nature of greenhouses makes them easy to extend, retrofit, or
relocate. They integrate naturally with rainwater catchment, seed-starting
stations, vermicomposting bins, and [[cotter-growing-space-design-fruiting-rooms]] on the floor and two long walls. A thick, uninsulated north
wall built from rammed earth, stone, or concrete block serves as primary thermal
mass, absorbing daytime solar gain and radiating it back at night.
Temperature performance is impressive: winter daytime highs reach 60-80°F even
when outside air is below 20°F, and nighttime lows stay 10-25°F above ambient.
This stability comes from massive earthen thermal inertia that prevents rapid
temperature swings, something no above-ground structure at comparable cost can
replicate. Construction is significantly more labor-intensive than a conventional
greenhouse. It requires heavy excavation, retaining wall engineering, and
critically, comprehensive drainage infrastructure. A French drain around the
perimeter below floor level, a gravel base, and proper site grading are all
essential. Water infiltration is the most common cause of walipini failure; sites
with high water tables or poor drainage should be avoided.
Summer presents the opposite challenge: the same thermal mass that retains
winter heat resists cooling, making overheating a real risk. Adequate
ventilation through a top cupola vent, low south-side intake vents, and wide
doors is essential. High humidity and condensation on glazing are persistent
issues that require vigilant airflow management to prevent mold and fungal
disease.
