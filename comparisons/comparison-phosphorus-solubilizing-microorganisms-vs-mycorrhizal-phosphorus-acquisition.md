## Limitations and Failure Modes

### PSM Limitations
- **Survival**: Introduced PSMs often fail to establish due to competition with native microbiome; survival rates of 1–10% are common after 30 days
- **Soil buffering**: In heavily buffered calcareous soils, organic acids are rapidly neutralized before they can solubilize significant P
- **Regulation**: P solubilization genes are repressed by adequate available P — if soil P tests high, PSMs provide no benefit
- **Strain specificity**: P solubilization capacity varies enormously between strains; generic products may not match the soil conditions

### AMF Limitations
- **Host dependence**: AMF are obligate symbionts — they cannot be cultured without a living host plant, making inoculant production expensive
- **Crop incompatibility**: Brassicas (cabbage, broccoli, mustard) and Chenopodiaceae (spinach, beet) are non-mycorrhizal — AMF provide no benefit
- **P suppression at high fertility**: When soil P is abundant, plants suppress AMF colonization, reducing any potential benefit
- **Time to establishment**: AMF colonization requires 2–6 weeks to become functional; this timeline may not align with peak crop P demand
- **Inoculant quality**: Commercial AMF products vary enormously in spore viability (10–95%) and species composition — quality control is a significant industry problem

## When to Use Each Strategy

**Choose PSMs when:**
- Soil contains significant reserves of insoluble P (high soil test P but low available P)
- Applying rock phosphate as a P source (PSMs dramatically improve rock phosphate efficiency)
- Growing non-mycorrhizal crops (brassicas, spinach)
- Soil pH is neutral to slightly alkaline (PSMs solubilize Ca-P most effectively)
- Budget is limited (PSM products are typically cheaper than AMF inoculants)

**Choose AMF when:**
- Soil has low available P but good physical structure allowing hyphal exploration
- Growing mycorrhizal crops in low-input or organic systems
- Establishing perennial crops (orchards, vineyards, pastures) where long-term symbiosis provides cumulative benefits
- Soil is compacted or has fine pore structure inaccessible to roots
- Managing drought-prone sites (AMF improve water uptake alongside P)

**Use both when:**
- Soil has both insoluble P reserves AND low available P in the soil solution — PSMs solubilize P, AMF transport it
- Establishing living soil systems where diverse microbial communities are the goal
- Growing high-value crops where maximum nutrient efficiency justifies the cost of multiple inoculants
- Transitioning to no-till systems where preserved hyphal networks and active rhizosphere communities provide complementary P acquisition pathways

The most effective phosphorus management programs combine both strategies with soil health practices (cover crops, reduced tillage, organic matter management) that support the native P-cycling microbiome while supplementing with targeted biological inputs.
