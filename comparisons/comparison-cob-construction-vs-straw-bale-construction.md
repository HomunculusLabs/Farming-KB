## Cob vs Straw Bale Construction
## Overview
Cob and straw bale are two of the most accessible [[cob-building-basics-detailed]] for more detailed cob techniques. The walls are typically 60 to 90 centimeters thick at the
base, tapering to 40 to 50 centimeters at the top, and are sculpted by
hand into organic, flowing forms. Cob construction has been used for
centuries across Europe, Africa, the Middle East, and the Americas, with
surviving cob buildings in England and Yemen dating back over 500 years.
The thermal performance of cob is dominated by its high thermal mass.
The dense earthen walls absorb heat during the day and release it slowly
over 8 to 12 hours, moderating indoor temperature swings in both hot and
cold climates. This thermal lag makes cob particularly effective in
climates with significant diurnal temperature variation, where the mass
absorbs daytime heat and radiates it back during cool nights. Cob walls
also provide excellent sound insulation and a sense of solidity and
permanence that lightweight construction cannot match.
Cob's most celebrated quality is its sculptural versatility. Because the
material is applied wet and shaped by hand, it accommodates built-in
furniture, curved walls, niches, shelves, arches, and organic sculptural
elements that would be difficult or impossible with other building
methods. This artistic freedom allows buildings to be shaped to their
specific site, climate, and the personality of their builders. The
material requirements are simple: a clay-rich subsoil (often available
on site), sharp sand, straw, and water. Testing the soil mix is
straightforward and can be done with jar tests and squeeze tests before
beginning construction.
