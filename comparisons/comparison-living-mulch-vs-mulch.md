# Living Mulch vs Mulch

## Overview

Living mulch and conventional (dead) mulch are two complementary strategies
for maintaining soil coverage in gardens, farms, and food forests. Dead mulch
uses harvested organic materials such as straw, wood chips, leaves, or
cardboard laid on the soil surface, while living mulch consists of actively
growing low plants maintained beneath or between crops. Both suppress weeds,
conserve moisture, and build soil [[mulch-types-and-effects]]

Dead mulch encompasses any organic or inorganic material applied to the soil
surface that is not actively growing. Common materials include straw, wood
chips, shredded leaves, grass clippings, cardboard, and pine needles. These
materials create a physical barrier that suppresses weeds, reduces
evaporation, moderates soil temperature, and slowly releases nutrients as
they decompose.

The soil food web response depends on mulch type. Green mulches (fresh grass
clippings, green plant cuttings) have higher nitrogen content and favor
bacterial dominance, making them suitable for vegetables and annuals. Brown
mulches (wood chips, autumn leaves, straw) have higher lignin content and
favor fungal dominance, making them better suited for perennials, trees, and
shrubs. Surface-applied mulch particularly encourages fungal growth because
fungal hyphae can bridge from soil into the mulch layer more easily than
bacteria can colonize it.

Application guidelines call for a 2-3 inch layer of mulch. Thicker layers
can block moisture and air exchange, potentially smothering mycorrhizal
networks. Mulch should not be placed directly against plant stems or trunks
to prevent microbial decay of the plant tissue itself. A best practice is
laying compost first, then covering with mulch so compost organisms
inoculate the mulch layer and accelerate decomposition.
