# Duck Keeping vs Rabbit Husbandry

Ducks and rabbits are two of the most practical small livestock choices for
the homestead, yet they serve fundamentally different roles. Ducks produce eggs
and meat while providing [[comparison-pheromone-based-pest-control-vs-beneficial-insect-biocontrol]] and [[purdue-four-step-garden-fertilization-program]]. Rabbits are the
most space-efficient meat animal available, converting feed to protein with
remarkable efficiency.

## Primary Purpose

**Ducks** are waterfowl kept primarily for eggs. A good laying breed like the
Khaki Campbell produces 250-340 eggs per year of large, rich eggs that
outperform chicken eggs in baking quality and [[psilocybin-shelf-life-stability]]. Ducks also consume
slugs, snails, and insect pests, fertilize gardens with nitrogen-rich manure,
and can integrate into orchards and rice paddies as part of [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]]
management systems.

**Rabbits** are kept almost exclusively for meat. A single breeding trio (one
buck, two does) can yield 80-100+ lbs of lean meat per year in a footprint
smaller than a garden shed. Gestation is just 31 days with litter sizes of
6-12 kits, allowing 4-6 litters per doe annually. Their manure is a cold
fertilizer that can be applied directly to gardens without composting.

## Space and Infrastructure

Ducks require 3-4 sq ft per bird indoors plus outdoor access. Their greatest
demand is water — they need water deep enough to submerge their heads, and
bathing water such as kiddie pools. Ducks are messy with water, requiring
drainage planning and deep bedding.

Rabbits need far less space. A standard wire hutch of 24x30 inches houses a
single doe. A breeding trio fits in roughly 20 sq ft of cage space. No water
features needed — just clean water dispensers. **Winner for small spaces:
Rabbits**, decisively.

## Feed Efficiency

Both thrive on commercial pellets (16% protein for ducks, 16-18% for rabbits),
but their foraging profiles differ sharply. Ducks on good pasture can meet
30-50% of nutritional needs through foraging — slugs, snails, insects, and
aquatic vegetation. Rabbits supplement pellets with free-choice grass hay and
up to 20% fresh greens. Feed conversion favors rabbits for meat production:
roughly 4 lbs of feed per pound of gain versus more for ducks.

## Climate Tolerance

Ducks are cold-hardy with waterproof feathers and body fat. Their main
vulnerability is extreme heat with poor ventilation. Rabbits tolerate cold
well but are extremely heat-sensitive — above 85 degrees F they risk fatal
heat stroke, making them poorly suited to hot climates without dedicated
cooling infrastructure. **Winner for hot climates: Ducks**, by a wide margin.

## Reproduction and Production

Rabbits reproduce faster than any other homestead meat animal. A doe can
kindle 4-6 litters per year with 8-12 week grow-out. Ducks are seasonal layers
with peak production in spring and summer. Incubation takes 28 days (35 for
Muscovy). **Winner for meat volume: Rabbits. Winner for eggs: Ducks.**

## Products Beyond Meat

Duck eggs have higher fat and protein than chicken eggs, thicker shells, and
longer shelf life. Prized for baking and pastry. Rabbit pelts from heritage
breeds yield marketable fur. Angora rabbits produce wool fiber. Rabbit manure
(approx. 2.4% N, 1.4% P, 0.6% K) goes directly on gardens without composting.

## Noise Considerations

Ducks are moderately noisy — most breeds quack, with some being notably vocal.
Muscovies are largely silent. Rabbits are nearly silent, making them ideal for
urban and suburban homesteads where noise ordinances constrain livestock.

## Predator Vulnerability

Both face significant predator pressure. Ducks are exposed to hawks, foxes,
raccoons, and owls from both sky and ground. Rabbits in elevated hutches gain
protection but remain vulnerable to weasels and rats. Both require hardware
cloth (not chicken wire) and vigilant nighttime securing.

## Start-Up Costs

Rabbit start-up costs are among the lowest: a breeding trio costs $60-90,
hutches $150-250 total, monthly feed roughly $15-25. Ducks require more:
a flock of 4-6 costs $40-80 as started birds, coop and water infrastructure
$200-500, monthly feed $20-35.
