## Insectary Plants vs Trap Cropping

## Overview
Insectary plants and trap cropping are two [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] that prey on or parasitize pests. Trap
    cropping attracts the pests themselves, concentrating them on sacrificial
    plants away from the main crop. Both reduce pesticide dependence, but they
    operate on different ecological principles and timelines, making them
    complementary components of [[trap-cropping]]

Trap cropping uses sacrificial plants that are more attractive to
    specific pests than the main crop, concentrating pest populations where
    they can be monitored, managed, or destroyed. The push-pull strategy
    refines this approach by combining repellent plants near the main crop
    (push) with attractive trap crops at the perimeter (pull), creating a dual
    force directing pests away from valuable plants. Well-researched pairings
    include Blue Hubbard squash for squash bugs, nasturtiums and radishes for
    aphids, Indian mustard for cabbage worms and flea beetles, and dill and
    moonflowers for tomato hornworm.

Perimeter trap cropping surrounds the main crop with the trap species,
    while intercropping places trap plants within the crop rows. Timing is
    critical: trap crops should be planted two to three weeks before the main
    crop to establish attractiveness before pests arrive. Management includes
    regular monitoring, hand-picking of concentrated pests, application of
    insecticides only to trap plants, and destruction of severely infested
    trap crops before pests migrate. Dead-end trap crops like Chinese giant
    mustard for flea beetles or Datura for tomato hornworms prevent the pest
    from completing its life cycle, providing population reduction rather than
    mere diversion.

## Key Differences
| Aspect | Insectary Plants | Trap Cropping |
|--------|------------------|---------------|
| Target Organism | Beneficial insects (predators, parasitoids,
    pollinators) | Pest insects (attracted and concentrated) |
| Mechanism | Indirect (builds predator populations) | Direct (diverts
    pests from main crop) |
| Speed of Effect | Slow (builds over weeks to months) | Fast
    (immediate pest concentration) |
| Specificity | Broad (supports entire beneficial community) | Narrow
    (specific pest-crop pairings) |
| Maintenance | Low once established | Moderate to high (monitoring,
    management) |
| Pest Knowledge | General ecology understanding needed | Specific
    pest-crop pairing research needed |
| Co-Benefits | Pollination, biodiversity, windbreak, forage | Pest
    population data, reduced pesticide use |
| Risk | Some plants can become invasive | Trap crop becomes pest
    breeding ground if neglected |

## When to Choose Insectary Plants
Choose insectary plants when building long-term ecological resilience
    and supporting overall beneficial insect populations. Insectary plantings
    are the foundation of any organic or permaculture pest management
    strategy, providing pollination services alongside [[hemenway-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insects]] directly increases yields.
    Insectary hedgerows and interplantings require planning for year-round
    bloom but are relatively low-maintenance once established and provide
    aesthetic and ecological benefits beyond pest control.

## When to Choose Trap Cropping
Choose trap cropping when a specific, identified pest problem requires
    targeted intervention. Trap cropping provides visible, relatively fast
    results against known pests with well-researched plant pairings. It is
    ideal for vegetable gardens and small farms dealing with persistent pest
    pressure from species like squash bugs, flea beetles, or cabbage worms.
    Trap cropping works best as a precision tool within a broader integrated
    pest management program, where it addresses specific problems while
    insectary plants provide background [[companion-planting-reference]]
