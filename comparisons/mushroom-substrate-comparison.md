# Mushroom Substrate Comparison

Choosing the right substrate is the single most important factor in successful mushroom cultivation. Different substrates suit different species, skill levels, and growing environments. This comparison covers the major substrate categories used by home growers, synthesized from [[mushroom-substrates]], [[psilocybin-mushroom-cultivation]], and cultivation method pages.

The key tradeoff is contamination risk vs. yield. Unsupplemented substrates (straw, plain coir) are resistant to contamination but produce lower yields. Supplemented substrates (bran + sawdust) yield heavily but require sterilization. Grain spawn, while nutrient-rich, is virtually always used as inoculum rather than a direct fruiting substrate due to high contamination risk.

| Substrate | Prep Method | Pasteurization / Sterilization | Best Species | Yield Potential | Contamination Risk | Cost | Difficulty |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------------------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|------|-----------|

| **Wheat / Oats** (spawn) | Boil 15 min, drain | Sterilize 15-18 psi, 60-90 min | All species (as spawn) | N/A (inoculum) | Moderate-High | Low ($0.50-1/lb) | Intermediate |
| **Wheat straw** | Chop 2-4 in; soak or hydrate | Pasteurize 160F, 20-30 min (hot water) | Oyster, wine cap, panaeolus | Moderate (100-150% BE) | Low | Very low (often free) | Beginner |
| **Oat straw** | Chop; soak or hydrate | Pasteurize 160F, 20-30 min | Oyster, wine cap | Moderate (100-150% BE) | Low | Very low | Beginner |

## Key Metrics Explained

**Biological Efficiency (BE)**: Yield as a percentage of the dry substrate weight. 100% BE means 1 lb dry substrate produced 1 lb fresh mushrooms. Most home growers achieve 100-200% BE; commercial operations can hit 300%+ on supplemented sawdust.

**C:N Ratio and Contamination**: Higher C:N substrates (sawdust at 200-500:1, straw at 80-120:1) are more resistant to contamination because competitors have limited nitrogen. Supplementation (adding bran at 10-20%) narrows the ratio to 40-60:1, accelerating growth but also making the substrate more attractive to contaminants — hence the need for full sterilization rather than pasteurization.

**Moisture Content**: Target ranges vary — grain spawn at 49-54%, sawdust blocks at 55-65%, straw at 70-74%. The squeeze test works across all types: a handful squeezed firmly should produce a few drops but no stream.

## Pasteurization vs Sterilization Decision

Understanding when to pasteurize vs sterilize is critical. Pasteurization (160-170F) kills most competitors but leaves beneficial thermotolerant organisms. It is sufficient for low-nitrogen substrates like straw and plain coir — these don't contain enough accessible nitrogen for aggressive contaminants to colonize quickly. Sterilization (250F at 15 psi) kills everything, creating a blank slate. This is mandatory for supplemented substrates (bran + sawdust) because the added nitrogen would allow green molds and bacteria to outcompete mushroom mycelium if any spores survived.

Per Stamets, the practical rule: if you added bran, grain, or any nitrogen supplement, sterilize. If the substrate is plain straw, plain sawdust, or coir, pasteurize. For outdoor beds with fresh hardwood chips, no heat treatment is needed — natural fungal succession and larger substrate volume give mushroom mycelium time to establish before competitors.

## Species-Substrate Matching

Different mushroom species have evolved to decompose different materials. Matching species to substrate is essential:

- **Oyster mushrooms** (Pleurotus spp.) are the most adaptable — they grow on straw, sawdust, coffee grounds, paper, and agricultural waste. Best starter species.
- **Shiitake** (Lentinula edodes) requires hardwood (oak, alder, maple) — either logs or supplemented sawdust blocks at 4:1 ratio.
- **Wine cap** (Stropharia rugoso-annulata) thrives on fresh hardwood chips and straw in outdoor beds.
- **P. cubensis** grows well on grain spawn, CVG (coir/verm/gypsum), and composted substrates. The simplest method is [[pf-tek]] (BRF/verm).
- **Woodlovers** (P. cyanescens, P. azurescens) require fresh hardwood chips in outdoor beds — see [[outdoor-wood-lover-cultivation]].
- **Agaricus** species require fully composted substrate with a C:N of 17-25:1 — the most demanding preparation.

## Grain Spawn Comparison

All mushroom cultivation begins with grain spawn — the universal inoculum. The choice of grain affects colonization speed, number of inoculation points, and compatibility with different substrates.

| Grain | Kernel Size | Inoculation Points | Moisture Target | Sterilization Time | Best Paired With | Notes |
|-------|------------|-------------------|-----------------|-------------------|-----------------|-------|
| **Rye** | Medium | Good | 49-54% | 60-90 min at 15 psi | All substrates | Gold standard per Stamets; consistent size |
| **Wheat** | Slightly larger | Moderate | 49-54% | 60-90 min at 15 psi | Sawdust, compost | Widely available; affordable |
| **Oats** | Large | Fewer per volume | 50-55% | 60-90 min at 15 psi | Straw, outdoor beds | Cheapest option; holds moisture well |
| **Millet** | Very small | Excellent (many points) | 45-50% | 45-60 min at 15 psi | Sawdust blocks | Can dry out faster; monitor moisture |
| **Corn** | Large | Fewer | 50-55% | 90 min at 15 psi | Animal bedding + rice | Best carpophore size per Nwanze research |

Per Nwanze et al. (2005), no single grain is universally best — grain choice interacts with substrate medium. Corn spawn on nitrogen-rich media produced the heaviest mushrooms. Wheat performed best on sawdust. Rye remains the safest all-around choice.

## Verdict

For beginners, start with the CVG monotub method for [[psilocybin-mushroom-cultivation]] or pasteurized straw for oyster mushrooms — both are forgiving and require minimal equipment. Wheat straw costs nearly nothing and can be pasteurized with hot water. Coir-based substrates are the easiest indoor option thanks to the bucket tek method.

For advancing, move to supplemented sawdust blocks for gourmet species (shiitake, lion's mane, reishi). The yield jump from 100% to 200-300% BE justifies the pressure cooker investment. For outdoor/low-tech cultivation, fresh hardwood chips in beds are unbeatable — [[outdoor-mushroom-bed]] cultivation with wine cap or [[outdoor-wood-lover-cultivation]] with psilocybe species can produce for years from a single inoculation.

Grain spawn (rye, wheat, oats) is the universal inoculum — always prepare your own rather than buying pre-made, and always sterilize properly. Rye is the gold standard per Stamets, but wheat and oats are cheaper and widely available alternatives.

## Common Mistakes

- Over-supplementing sawdust (above 20% dry weight) increases contamination risk dramatically
- Skipping the field capacity test — substrates that are too wet are the #1 beginner killer
- Using treated or painted wood chips — always source clean, untreated hardwood
- Ignoring C:N ratios; wood-based substrates are already carbon-heavy and don't need extra browns
