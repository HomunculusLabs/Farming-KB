# Keyhole Garden vs Raised Bed

## Overview

Keyhole gardens and raised beds are both elevated growing systems that
improve soil drainage, reduce bending, and concentrate soil amendments
in a defined area. The key distinction is shape and internal structure:
keyhole gardens use a circular design with a central compost basket and a
notch for access, while raised beds are typically rectangular frames
filled with soil. Keyhole gardens originated in Africa through
humanitarian gardening programs and integrate composting directly into
the growing space. Raised beds have broader global adoption and come in
many variations including standard framed beds, hugelkultur mounds, and
wicking beds.

## Keyhole Gardens

[[keyhole-bed-design]]

A keyhole garden is a circular raised bed, typically 1.5 to 2 meters in
diameter, with a wedge-shaped notch cut into one side allowing the
gardener to reach the center. At the center sits a cylindrical compost
basket made from wire mesh, sticks, or other porous material. As kitchen
scraps and green waste decompose in the basket, nutrients and moisture
leach outward into the surrounding soil. This creates a self-fertilizing
growing system where compost tea percolates directly to plant roots. The
design was popularized by Send a Cow, a UK-based charity working in
Uganda, Lesotho, and other African nations, though similar concepts
exist in traditional gardening worldwide.

Keyhole gardens excel in arid and semi-arid climates because the
central compost basket acts as an internal irrigation and fertility
source. They require less water than conventional raised beds since
moisture wicks outward from the decomposing organic matter. The
circular shape maximizes the planting area relative to the footprint
and creates microclimates: heat-loving plants go on the sun-facing
side, shade-tolerant species on the opposite. Construction materials
are often scavenged — bricks, stones, corrugated metal, and even
plastic bottles — making them accessible in resource-limited settings.

The main limitations are size and shape constraints. A single keyhole
garden produces a modest harvest compared to a rectangular raised bed of
equivalent area because the central compost basket takes up planting
space. They are harder to extend or connect into larger systems, and
the circular layout can feel awkward when growing row crops or plants
that benefit from straight-line spacing. Reaching the far side of a
large keyhole garden from the notch can strain the back, negating one
of raised bed gardening's primary ergonomic benefits.

## Raised Beds

[[raised-bed-gardening]]

Raised beds are rectangular (occasionally square or L-shaped) frames
elevated above ground level, typically 0.15 to 0.6 meters tall and 0.9
to 1.5 meters wide. The width is calibrated so a gardener can reach the
center from either side without stepping on the soil. Materials include
untreated lumber, cedar, galvanized steel, concrete blocks, and natural
stone. They can be filled with native soil amended with compost, or
with a purpose-built mix of topsoil, compost, and aeration materials
like perlite or vermiculite. Raised beds warm faster in spring, drain
better than in-ground plots, and allow intensive planting through
square-foot or bio-intensive spacing methods.

The rectangular form is extremely versatile. Raised beds can be placed
in rows to create organized garden layouts, covered with hoops for
season extension, fitted with drip irrigation lines, or connected into
larger growing systems. They scale well — adding another bed is
straightforward, and beds can be arranged to follow contours or fill
available space. Crop rotation is simpler in rectangular beds because
families can move from one bed to the next each season. The long
straight edges also work well with trellises, netting, and row covers.

The weaknesses of standard raised beds are their dependence on external
inputs for fertility and their higher water needs compared to
self-fertilizing designs. Without integrated composting like a keyhole
basket, the gardener must top-dress or side-dress with compost and
fertilizers regularly. Raised beds also dry out faster than in-ground
gardens, requiring more frequent irrigation during hot weather. The
upfront cost of materials and soil can be significant, especially for
tall beds or large installations.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Keyhole Garden | Raised Bed |
|--------|---------------|------------|
| Shape | Circular with center basket | Rectangular frame |
| Composting | Built-in central basket | External composting |
| Water needs | Lower (self-watering) | Higher (drains freely) |
| Space efficiency | Moderate (basket takes area) | High (full bed is plantable) |
| Scalability | Individual units | Easily expandable in rows |
| Materials | Scavenged bricks, stone, wire | Lumber, steel, blocks |
| Best climate | Arid and semi-arid | Any climate |
| Crop rotation | Harder in single circle | Easy between beds |
| Ergonomics | Good if diameter is small | Excellent with proper width |
| Cost | Often very low | Moderate to high |

## When to Choose a Keyhole Garden

Choose a keyhole garden when working in a dry climate where water
conservation matters, when you want an integrated composting system that
feeds plants directly, or when materials are limited and you need a
garden that can be built from scavenged items. They are ideal for
small spaces like patios, school gardens, and community plots where a
single intensively managed bed provides enough production. The visual
appeal of the circular design is a bonus in ornamental edible
landscapes.

## When to Choose a Raised Bed

Choose raised beds when you need a scalable system that can grow with
your needs, when crop rotation between beds is a priority, or when
you want the clean look and easy maintenance of a rectangular grid.
Raised beds are better for row crops, trellised vegetables like
tomatoes and cucumbers, and any situation where you need to cover the
bed with netting or hoops. They work in any climate but particularly
shine in areas with poor native soil or drainage issues.

## Hybrid Approaches

Many gardeners combine elements of both designs. A rectangular raised
bed can incorporate a small central compost cage, gaining some
self-fertilizing benefit while retaining the row-friendly layout. Some
permaculture designs arrange multiple keyhole gardens in clusters,
creating a productive polyculture that mimics a larger raised-bed
system. The herb spiral, a permaculture staple, is essentially a
keyhole concept applied vertically.
