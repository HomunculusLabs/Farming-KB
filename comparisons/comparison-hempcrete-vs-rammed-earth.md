# Hempcrete vs Rammed Earth

## Overview

Hempcrete and rammed earth are two leading natural [[gadd-fungal-biodegradation-concrete-building-materials]] that
appeal to sustainable builders for different reasons. Hempcrete is a
lightweight, carbon-negative biocomposite of hemp hurd and lime binder,
used primarily as insulation and infill around a structural frame. Rammed
earth is a dense, monolithic wall system created by compacting damp earth
inside temporary formwork, valued for its extraordinary thermal mass and
structural capability. Choosing between them depends on climate, structural
requirements, material availability, and project goals.

## Hempcrete

[[rammed-earth]]

Rammed earth construction compacts damp earth — a mixture of clay, sand,
and gravel — between temporary formwork to create dense, monolithic walls.
The technique is over 7,000 years old, with surviving examples in the
Great Wall of China, North African kasbahs, and French pisé farmhouses.
Modern rammed earth uses the same principles with engineered formwork and
optional cement or lime stabilisation.

Rammed earth walls are typically 30-60 cm thick and are genuinely
load-bearing, capable of supporting multiple storeys without a separate
structural frame. The material's high density provides exceptional thermal
mass, absorbing heat during the day and releasing it at night. This 8-12
hour thermal lag dramatically reduces temperature fluctuations indoors.

The aesthetic appeal of rammed earth is distinctive: horizontal strata
lines from each compacted lift create natural banding patterns, and the
wall colour reflects the local earth. Walls can be left exposed internally
and externally, eliminating the need for additional finishes. When properly
constructed with good roof overhangs and raised foundations, rammed earth
buildings last for centuries.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Hempcrete | Rammed Earth |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| Structural role | Infill/insulation only | Load-bearing walls |
| Density | Very low (~300 kg/m³) | High (~1,800 kg/m³) |
| Carbon impact | Carbon-negative | Low embodied energy |
| Thermal property | Insulation (resists heat flow) | Thermal mass (stores and releases) |
| Primary binder | Lime (carbonates over time) | Clay (natural) or cement/lime |
| Material source | Hemp hurd + imported lime | On-site earth |
| Wall thickness | 300-400 mm (around frame) | 300-600 mm (monolithic) |
| Moisture behaviour | Highly breathable, regulates RH | Breathable but needs rain protection |
| Skill level | Moderate; mixing and casting | High; formwork and compaction |
| Cost of materials | Moderate (hemp hurd can be pricey) | Very low if local soil is suitable |
| Best climate | Temperate, cold, humid | Hot-dry, temperate, arid |

## When to Choose Hempcrete

Hempcrete excels in cold and [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]] where insulation is the
priority. Its low thermal conductivity keeps interiors warm in winter and
prevents overheating in summer. The material is ideal for renovations and
retrofits — hempcrete can be applied to the interior or exterior of
existing walls to improve insulation and [[mushroom-bed-moisture-management]].

Projects that prioritise [[exotic-plantations-soil-aggregates-and-carbon-sequestration]] should lean toward hempcrete.
Every cubic metre of hempcrete wall locks away roughly 30-40 kg of CO₂,
making it one of the most climate-positive building choices available.
It is also well-suited for people with chemical sensitivities, as the
material contains no synthetic components and naturally regulates indoor
humidity between 40-60%, inhibiting mold growth.

Hempcrete requires a separate structural system — typically a timber frame
— so it suits projects where [[preserving-wood-timber-framing]] is already planned. The material
is relatively forgiving to work with; mixing and placing hempcrete is less
technically demanding than rammed earth, though curing time is longer and
the building must be protected from rain during the weeks-long drying period.

## When to Choose Rammed Earth

Rammed earth is the clear choice when the building must be load-bearing
without a separate structural frame. In hot-dry climates, rammed earth's
thermal mass outperforms hempcrete's insulation: the dense walls absorb
daytime heat and radiate it back during cool nights, stabilising indoor
temperatures without mechanical systems.

When suitable soil is available on or near the building site, rammed earth
is dramatically cheaper than hempcrete. The primary material is literally
the ground beneath your feet, requiring only screening, moisture adjustment,
and compaction. This makes rammed earth one of the lowest-cost building
methods for rural and off-grid construction.
