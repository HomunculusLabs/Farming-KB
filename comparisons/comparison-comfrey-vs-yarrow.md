# Comfrey vs Yarrow

Comfrey and yarrow are both powerhouse permaculture plants, but they serve
different primary functions in the garden ecosystem. Comfrey is the biomass
champion and potassium accumulator, while yarrow excels at attracting beneficial
insects and providing medicinal value. Understanding their strengths helps you
place each plant where it delivers the most benefit.

## Overview

**Comfrey** ([[symphytum-officinale]] and hybrids) is a deep-rooted perennial with
a taproot that extends 6-10 feet into the subsoil. This extraordinary root
system mines potassium in very high concentrations, along with calcium,
magnesium, and iron, bringing these nutrients to the surface in its foliage.
The Bocking 14 cultivar is preferred in permaculture because it is sterile and
will not spread by seed, though it can still propagate aggressively from root
fragments.

Comfrey produces 3-5 cuts of biomass per season, yielding enormous quantities
of nutrient-rich green material. This biomass is used as comfrey tea (fermented
at 1:10 to 1:15 dilution), fresh mulch around hungry plants, or as a compost
activator that speeds decomposition. Comfrey is an excellent component in fruit
tree guilds, where its deep roots access nutrients beyond the reach of tree
roots and its biomass feeds the soil surface.

However, comfrey contains hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids, making it unsafe
for internal medicinal use, and its aggressive root spread requires careful
placement away from annual vegetable beds and pathways.

**Yarrow** (Achillea millefolium) is a hardy, drought-tolerant perennial that
thrives in poor soils and challenging conditions. Its flat flower heads attract
predatory insects including ladybugs, lacewings, and hoverflies, making it one
of the most valuable insectary plants in any permaculture system. As a dynamic
accumulator, yarrow draws up copper, potassium, and phosphorus from deep soil
layers, though its overall nutrient accumulation is modest compared to comfrey.

Yarrow has extensive medicinal applications as a hemostatic agent (stops
bleeding), diaphoretic (promotes sweating), and digestive tonic. It blooms from
June through September, providing consistent nectar and pollen for butterflies
and bees. Yarrow can become invasive in favorable conditions but is easier to
manage than comfrey due to its shallower root system.

## Comfrey: Detailed Uses

Comfrey's primary value lies in its exceptional biomass production. A single
mature comfrey plant can produce 4-5 pounds of leaf material per cutting, and
with 3-5 cuts per season that represents 12-25 pounds of nutrient-rich organic
matter per plant annually. This biomass has a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of
approximately 15:1, making it ideal for composting because it decomposes
quickly without tying up nitrogen.

Comfrey tea is made by packing comfrey leaves into a container, weighting them
down, and allowing them to ferment in water for 3-6 weeks. The resulting
concentrated liquid is diluted at 1:10 to 1:15 and used as a potassium-rich
liquid feed for fruiting and flowering plants. Some gardeners apply comfrey
leaves directly as mulch around tomatoes, potatoes, and fruit trees, where they
break down slowly and release nutrients at the soil surface.

In fruit tree guilds, comfrey serves multiple functions simultaneously. Its deep
taproot mines nutrients from below the root zone of fruit trees and cycles them
to the surface. Its broad leaves shade the soil, reducing moisture loss. Its
flowers attract pollinators during the brief blooming period. The biomass it
produces can be chopped and dropped directly in place as a nutrient-rich mulch.

## Yarrow: Detailed Uses

Yarrow's flat flower heads are specifically shaped to serve as landing platforms
for predatory insects. Ladybugs, lacewings, hoverflies, and parasitic wasps all
feed on yarrow nectar and pollen as adults while their larvae prey on aphids,
caterpillars, and other garden pests. Planting yarrow throughout vegetable
gardens and orchards creates a distributed insectary system that supports
biological pest control without chemical inputs.

Medicinally, yarrow is one of the most versatile Western herbs. The fresh or
dried aerial parts are used to stop bleeding from wounds, reduce fever through
diaphoresis, stimulate digestion, and ease menstrual cramps. The essential oil
contains chamazulene, which gives yarrow preparations their characteristic blue
color and contributes anti-inflammatory properties. Yarrow is safe for most
adults at proper dosages but should be avoided during pregnancy.

## When to Choose Comfrey

Comfrey is the clear choice when you need large quantities of nutrient-rich
biomass for mulching, composting, or making liquid fertilizer. Plant it around
fruit trees, along the edges of compost piles, or in dedicated chop-and-drop
zones. Its potassium-rich leaves are particularly beneficial for fruiting and
flowering plants. Choose Bocking 14 to eliminate seed spread, and plant it in
permanent locations where its aggressive root system will not interfere with
annual vegetable beds or pathways.

## When to Choose Yarrow

Yarrow is the better choice when your priorities include medicinal herb
production, pollinator and predatory insect support, or ground cover in poor
soils. It integrates beautifully into polyculture beds, herb spirals, meadow
plantings, and ornamental borders where its fern-like foliage and flat flower
clusters provide visual interest. Its drought tolerance makes it ideal for
xeriscaping and low-maintenance areas.

## Using Both Together

Comfrey and yarrow complement each other well in a diverse permaculture system.
Place comfrey in fruit tree guilds and biomass production zones for its
unmatched nutrient cycling. Scatter yarrow throughout vegetable gardens, herb
beds, and wildflower meadows for its insectary and medicinal functions.
Together they cover the full spectrum of dynamic accumulator, companion planting,
and medicinal roles that a productive homestead requires.

## Safety Considerations

Comfrey contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids that can cause serious liver damage if
ingested. Never use comfrey internally, and avoid using comfrey mulch on root
vegetables that may contact the soil surface. Yarrow is generally safe for
medicinal use but should be avoided by pregnant women and those with allergies
to plants in the Asteraceae family. Always research proper dosage and
preparation before using any medicinal herb.
