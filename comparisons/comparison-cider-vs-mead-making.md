## Cider vs Mead Making

Cider and mead are two of the oldest and most accessible fermented beverages a homesteader can produce. Both are simpler than [[query-how-do-i-choose-the-right-cannabis-strain-for-my-grow]] project for your season, available inputs, and patience level.

## Overview

**Hard cider** is fermented apple juice with a typical ABV of 5 to 8%. Its complexity comes from blending apple varieties with different balances of sugar, acid, and tannin. Cider has deep roots in rural European and American homesteading traditions, particularly in regions with abundant apple orchards. A well-made cider can rival wine in subtlety and depth.

**Mead** is fermented honey and water, often called honey wine. With a typical ABV of 8 to 18% depending on the recipe, mead is arguably the simplest fermented beverage to produce at home. It is also the oldest, with archaeological evidence dating back over 9,000 years. Mead's character is driven by honey variety, and it can be made in styles ranging from light and sessionable to rich and age-worthy.

## Ingredients and Raw Materials

|--------|-------|------|
| Primary ingredient | Apples, ideally a blend of sweets, sharps, bittersweets, and bittersharps | Honey, 2.5 to 3 lbs per gallon |
| Secondary inputs | Sugar or juice concentrate for gravity adjustment, acid for balance | Water, yeast nutrient, optional acid |
| Ingredient availability | Seasonal: autumn apple harvest | Year-round if honey stores are stocked |
| Cost per gallon | Moderate; depends on apple source and pressing equipment | High; honey is the most expensive fermentable sugar |

The biggest practical distinction is **seasonality**. Cider demands fresh apples in fall, making it a project tied to harvest season. Mead can be made any time you have honey, making it a more flexible year-round fermentation.

A quality cider blend typically combines 30 to 50% sweet apples (Golden Delicious, Gala, Fuji) for sugar and body, 10 to 30% sharp apples (Granny Smith, Bramley's Seedling) for acidity, and 20 to 40% bittersweet cider apples (Dabinett, Kingston Black) for tannin structure. A simple homestead fallback is 60% sweet, 30% tart, and 10% crabapple.

For mead, the honey variety drives the final flavor. Light honeys like clover, wildflower, and orange blossom produce clean, mild meads. Dark honeys like buckwheat and avocado create bold, earthy character. Varietal honeys such as tupelo, sage, and meadowfoam offer distinct floral profiles. Raw, unfiltered honey is strongly preferred over supermarket honey, which may be adulterated or heat-damaged. The honey-to-water ratio is the primary variable controlling final ABV and body: more honey means more alcohol and more residual sweetness potential.

Apples require a press and crusher or at minimum a juicer. Honey requires only a pot and a spoon. This gives mead a significant equipment advantage for beginners.

## Fermentation Science

Both fermentations rely on yeast converting sugar to ethanol and CO2, but the raw materials create very different fermentation environments.

**Cider juice** naturally contains nitrogen, vitamins, and minerals that support yeast health. Nutrient additions are optional rather than essential. Typical starting gravity is 1.045 to 1.065, producing 5 to 8% ABV. Target pH is 3.2 to 3.8. Fermentation completes in 2 to 4 weeks at 60 to 70 degrees F. Active bubbling typically begins within 24 to 72 hours. Fermentation is complete when gravity stabilizes between 0.995 and 1.005.

**Honey must** is nearly pure sugar with virtually no nitrogen, amino acids, or minerals. Yeast nutrient is not optional: it is essential. Without staggered nutrient additions (SNA), fermentation is likely to stall or produce hydrogen sulfide off-flavors. Starting gravity runs 1.080 to 1.120, producing 8 to 18% ABV. Primary fermentation takes 4 to 6 weeks at 65 to 70 degrees F. The basic recipe calls for heating water to 160 degrees F (never boiling), dissolving honey, cooling to 70 degrees F, and pitching yeast.

The higher sugar content of mead means more alcohol, more stress on the yeast, and a longer road to drinkability. Cider's lower gravity makes for faster turnaround and fewer fermentation problems.
