Canning preserves food by heat-processing it in sealed jars to destroy spoilage
organisms and create a sterile vacuum. Two methods exist: water bath canning for
high-acid foods (fruits, pickles, jams, tomatoes with added acid) and pressure
canning for low-acid foods (vegetables, meats, soups, beans) where botulism
risk requires temperatures above 240 degrees Fahrenheit. The result is
shelf-stable food that can be stored at room temperature for one to five years.

Canning's greatest strength is its versatility and the quality of food security
it provides. Nearly any food can be canned — vegetables, fruits, meats, soups,
stews, sauces, jams, and pickles. This makes it the most comprehensive single
preservation method for building a long-term food storage pantry. Properly canned
food is genuinely shelf-stable, requiring no refrigeration or special storage
conditions beyond a cool, dark cupboard. The science is well-established, and
with correct technique, canned food is safe indefinitely.

The drawbacks are serious when safety is compromised. Improper canning —
especially water-bath processing low-acid foods — creates a real risk of
botulism, a potentially fatal illness. The process is time-intensive, requiring
hours of preparation, processing, and cleanup. Equipment costs include a pressure
canner (essential, not optional), canning jars, lids, and specialized tools.
Single-use metal lids create ongoing cost, though reusable Tattler lids are
