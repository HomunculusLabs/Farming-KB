# Xeriscaping vs Drought Gardening

## Overview

Xeriscaping and drought gardening both address the challenge of gardening with limited water, but they approach the problem from fundamentally different angles. Xeriscaping is a **landscaping philosophy** that designs outdoor spaces around water conservation through plant selection, soil improvement, and efficient irrigation. Drought gardening, popularized by Steve Solomon, is a **food-production strategy** focused on [[query-best-mediterranean-herbs-for-beginner-herb-garden]] such as lavender, rosemary, and thyme. Rock gardens and stone elements are common features, reducing evaporation while adding visual texture and interest.

Soil improvement is central to xeriscaping. Incorporating 2-4 inches of compost increases water retention, while biochar can further improve moisture-holding capacity in sandy soils. Mulching at 3-4 inches depth suppresses weeds and dramatically reduces evaporation. In clay soils, coarse sand or expanded shale improves drainage and root penetration.

Efficient irrigation systems — drip irrigation (90% efficient versus 50% for sprinklers), soaker hoses, and buried olla pots — deliver water directly to root zones with minimal waste. Lawn alternatives like buffalo grass, blue grama, and creeping thyme replace water-intensive turf without sacrificing usable outdoor space.

Xeriscaping is primarily concerned with **ornamental and ecological value**: creating attractive, biodiverse outdoor spaces that require minimal water input. While it can include edible plants in oasis zones, food production is not its primary goal.

Regional plant selection is critical — species that thrive in the arid Southwest (agave, yucca, Palo verde) differ markedly from those suited to the Great Plains (little bluestem, coneflower, yarrow) or Mediterranean climates (manzanita, ceanothus, salvia). Choosing locally adapted plants is the single most important factor in xeriscaping success.

It is a long-term landscape investment that pays increasing dividends as plants mature and establish deep, self-sufficient root systems.

## Drought Gardening

Drought gardening is a food-growing methodology built around one central insight: **spacing determines water needs**. By placing vegetable plants much farther apart than conventional gardening recommends, each plant can access a far larger volume of soil moisture, develop deeper root systems, and become largely self-sufficient for water.

The approach relies on understanding soil moisture thresholds — specifically the concept of field capacity. Solomon identifies key thresholds:

- **70 percent of field capacity**: The ideal zone for vegetable growth
- **60 percent of field capacity**: The signal to irrigate
- **Below 50 percent**: Plants experience stress even without visible wilting

When irrigation is necessary, the method calls for deep, infrequent watering — replenishing to field capacity every 2-5 days depending on soil type. This avoids both nutrient leaching and shallow root development. The soil ball test (digging down 5-6 inches and squeezing) provides a practical way to assess moisture levels without instruments.

For situations where even minimal irrigation is unavailable, drought gardening recommends **[[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] corn gardening used four-foot centers with the adage "one for the worm, one for the crow, one to rot, and one to grow." This wide spacing allowed each plant to draw from a large soil volume, reducing or eliminating the need for irrigation.

Another practical tool is the **sparkle test** from John Jeavons: water with a fan nozzle until the bed surface sparkles, and when the shine lasts 1-10 seconds, enough water has been applied. Solomon also warns that consumer-grade sprinklers deliver 2-4 inches per hour — far more than most soils can absorb — making calibration with tin cans or gauges essential to avoid wasting water and leaching nutrients.

Drought gardening is unabashedly utilitarian: its purpose is producing calories and nutrients under water-constrained conditions, drawing on techniques that predate piped irrigation entirely.
