## Insectary Plants vs Trap Crops
Both insectary plants and trap crops are non-chemical strategies for managing pests, but they operate through fundamentally different mechanisms. Insectary plants recruit [[trap-cropping]]
Growing sacrificial crops that attract pests away from the main cash crop, concentrating them for monitoring and targeted management as part of an IPM strategy. Trap crops exploit the feeding and oviposition preferences of pests to create a decoy that intercepts them before they reach the harvest crop.
### How They Work
Pests preferentially feed on or lay eggs on the trap crop due to earlier maturity, stronger olfactory or visual attraction cues, or greater nutritional palatability. The trap crop acts as a biological magnet, drawing pest pressure away from the harvest crop and concentrating it in a known, manageable location where it can be monitored, treated, or destroyed without affecting the main crop.
The mechanism is direct: pests are physically drawn to the trap crop and away from the cash crop. Success depends on timing the trap crop's most attractive growth stage to coincide with peak pest emergence.
### Push-Pull Strategy
A refined approach that combines repellent plants near the main crop (push) with attractive trap crops on the perimeter (pull). This dual strategy maximizes pest diversion while protecting the cash crop from multiple angles. For example, interplanting garlic among roses (push) while surrounding the bed with nasturtiums (pull) creates a two-layered defense against aphids. The push-pull method is widely used in African maize farming to control stem borers and striga weed.
### Common Pairings
- **Nasturtiums** for aphids: black bean aphids strongly prefer nasturtium foliage over many vegetable crops
- **Radishes** for flea beetles: radish leaves emerge early and draw beetles away from young cole crops
- **Blue Hubbard squash** for squash bugs and vine borers: the most attractive cucurbit for these pests by a wide margin
- **Dill** for tomato hornworms: attracts hornworms away from tomato foliage where they cause defoliation
- **Mustard** for cabbage worms and diamondback moth larvae: a preferred brassica that draws these pests from broccoli and cabbage
- **Soybeans** for Mexican bean beetles: soybeans are strongly preferred over snap beans
- **Chervil** for slugs: draws slugs away from tender vegetable seedlings
- **Marigolds** for root-knot nematodes: French marigolds release compounds that repel or trap nematodes in the root zone
### Deployment Methods
- **Perimeter planting**: surround the main crop with a dense border of trap species to intercept pests moving into the field from field edges
- **Timed planting**: sow trap crops 2-3 weeks before the main crop so they reach vulnerable growth stages first, maximizing attractiveness when pests emerge
- **Row intercropping**: alternate rows of trap crop and main crop for even pest distribution and easy physical access for treatment or removal
- **Sacrificial percentage**: plant 5-10% of the field area in trap crop, balancing space use with pest diversion effectiveness
### Advantages
- Reduces pesticide applications on the main harvest crop, preserving crop quality and organic certification
- Provides early pest detection through concentrated scouting on trap plants, serving as a sentinel system
- Can be combined with biological controls (releasing beneficials onto the trap crop) for targeted suppression
- Low seed cost relative to the value of the crop protected
- Results are immediately visible and easy to evaluate within a single season
### Limitations
- Requires detailed knowledge of specific pest-crop preferences for effective pairing
- Demands regular monitoring to catch infestations on trap crops before pest populations build up
- Trap crop must be destroyed or treated before pests complete their life cycle and migrate to the main crop

## When to Choose Insectary Plants
Insectary plantings are the better choice in these situations:
- Pursuing long-term biological control programs where sustained pest suppression is the goal
- Pollinator support is needed alongside pest management (fruit farms, seed production)
- Organic farms and orchards where pesticide use is restricted or prohibited
- Diversified farming systems with permanent landscape features (hedgerows, buffer zones)
- Building resilient beneficial insect populations across multiple growing seasons
- When you want a set-and-forget approach that compounds in effectiveness year after year

### Practical Hybrid Layout
A simple field layout for combining both strategies: plant insectary hedgerows along field edges for permanent beneficial habitat, interplant insectary flowers every 50 ft as internal beneficial corridors, and dedicate a 5-10% perimeter strip to the appropriate trap crop for the primary pest of concern. Monitor the trap crop weekly and treat or remove it before pests complete their lifecycle.
