# Gabions vs Check Dams
Gabions and check dams are both earthwork structures designed to slow water
flow, capture sediment, and rehabilitate degraded landscapes. While they share
overlapping functions, their design, application, and complexity differ
significantly. Understanding these distinctions is essential for selecting the
right structure for your [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]] or [[permaculture-design]] framework, gabions must extend above the flood level of
the gully they protect, ensuring that water spills over rather than undermining
the structure. They are built in sequence from the outlet working upstream, with
each new structure placed where the water drops to the level of the previous
one downstream.
Gabions are permeable, allowing water to pass through while trapping sediment
behind them. Over time, this captured sediment creates productive silt fields
that can be planted with useful species. Construction uses low-cost local
materials: stone from the surrounding landscape and wire mesh cages. In areas
where stone is plentiful, solid stone weirs are preferred over gabion cages
because they are more permanent and require no wire that might corrode.
Gabion installations are integrated with [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]] and pioneer vegetation
planted on the captured sediments to stabilize the new land. The approach has
been proven at large scale, with 2,000 hectares of degraded land successfully
reclaimed in Cape Verde using gabion and stone weir systems.
**Check Dams** encompass five distinct types, each designed for different
functions and conditions. In the Mollison framework, these five types are:
diversion check dams, concrete flood check dams, silt check dams (0.5-2m
height), gabion check dams, and check dams integrated with ram pumps or water
wheels. Landscape placement follows a zoned approach with structures positioned
across catchment zones, transition zones, and revitalisation zones according
to the specific [[water-management]] needs of each zone.
Check dams allow normal low flows to pass through while retarding flood flows,
preventing downstream erosion and maximizing infiltration. They integrate
multiple functions including silt capture, water spreading across valley floors,
and [[fukuoka-three-dimensional-solar-energy-capture-rice-paddy]] through integrated water wheels or ram pumps. The system is
scalable from individual farm level to entire catchment management.
## Key Differences
| Aspect | Gabions | Check Dams |
|--------|---------|------------|
| Structure Type | Stone-filled wire mesh cages or weirs | Five types: diversion, concrete, silt, etc. |
| Materials | Local stone plus wire mesh or stone alone | Varies: brushwood, stone, concrete, gabion |
| Permeability | Permeable; water passes through | Varies by type; allows low flow, retards flood |
| Primary Function | Gully erosion control and sediment capture | Broad water management across landscapes |
| Design Complexity | Low to moderate; simple construction | Low to high depending on type selected |
| Scalability | Gully to watershed level | Farm to catchment level |
| Longevity | Wire may corrode; stone weirs permanent | Varies: brushwood temporary, concrete permanent |
| Maintenance | Moderate; check wire, refill gaps | Varies by type; concrete needs least |
| Best Terrain | Active gullies, eroded watercourses | Valleys, streams, graded landscapes |
| Multi-function | Primarily erosion and sediment control | Erosion, water spreading, energy capture |
## Gabion Design and Construction
Proper gabion construction begins with [[mollison-designers-design-client-interview-and-site-assessment-process]]. The gully profile,
flood levels, and available stone must be evaluated before construction begins.
Gabion cages are typically 1-2 meters wide, 1 meter tall, and extend across
the full width of the gully. The top of the completed gabion must sit above
the maximum observed flood level to prevent water from scouring beneath or
around the structure.
Stone filling is critical: stones should be hand-placed, not dumped, with
larger stones on the exterior faces and smaller stones filling interior voids.
This maximizes structural integrity and prevents the wire from bulging under
load. Gabions are built in a stepped sequence from the outlet upstream, with
each subsequent structure placed at the point where the water surface reaches
the elevation of the next downstream gabion during flood conditions.
Vetiver grass is planted on the upstream sediment deposit immediately after
gabion construction. The dense vetiver root network stabilizes the captured
silt and accelerates the creation of productive land. Additional pioneer
species follow as the silt field builds depth and [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] accumulates.
## Check Dam Types and Placement
The five check dam types serve different functions across the landscape.
Silt check dams are the simplest, built from brushwood, logs, or stone to
heights of 0.5-2 meters in upper catchment zones. They slow water velocity
and trap coarse sediment before it reaches downstream areas.
Concrete flood check dams are the most robust, designed to withstand extreme
flood events in transition zones where water concentrates. These require
engineered design and specialized construction but provide permanent, low-
maintenance protection. Diversion check dams redirect water from watercourses
into irrigation channels or spreading fields.
The most sophisticated type integrates mechanical systems: check dams paired
with ram pumps that use water flow energy to pump water uphill for storage or
irrigation, or water wheels that generate electricity or power mechanical
devices. These integrated systems transform erosion control structures into
productive assets.
## When to Choose Gabions
Gabions and stone weirs are the optimal choice for controlling active gully
erosion where water has carved deep channels into the landscape. They are most
effective where stone is readily available locally, keeping construction costs
minimal. Choose gabions when your primary goals are halting headward gully
erosion, capturing sediment to build productive land, and creating conditions
for revegetation of degraded watercourses.
## When to Choose Check Dams
Check dams are the appropriate choice for broader landscape water management
that goes beyond single-gully control. Select check dams when you need to
manage water flow across varied terrain types, integrate multiple functions
like irrigation and energy capture, or implement a zoned approach to watershed
management. The five-type system allows matching the structure to specific
conditions and needs of each zone.
## Integrated Design
The most effective erosion control designs often combine both approaches. Use
gabion check dams as one type within a broader check dam system, placing them
in active gullies while using simpler silt dams in headwater areas and
diversion dams for irrigation distribution. This integrated approach creates a
comprehensive water management system that addresses erosion at its source
while maximizing productive use of captured water and sediment.
## Construction Considerations
Gabion construction requires careful stone selection and packing to prevent
voids that weaken the structure. Wire mesh quality matters significantly;
heavier gauge wire extends lifespan considerably. Check dams require thorough
site assessment including flow calculations, sediment load estimates, and
downstream impact analysis. All structures should be designed for worst-case
flood events, not average flows. Professional engineering consultation is
recommended for concrete check dams and any structure protecting downstream
infrastructure.
