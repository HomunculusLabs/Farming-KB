# Companion Planting vs Crop Rotation

## Overview

Companion planting and crop rotation are foundational organic strategies
for maintaining plant health, managing pests, and preserving soil
fertility. Companion planting is a spatial technique that positions
beneficial plant combinations together in the same [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]], while
crop rotation is a temporal technique that sequences different crop
families across seasons and years. Together they form a comprehensive
approach to ecological garden and farm management that reduces dependence
on external inputs.

## Companion Planting

[[crop-rotation-benefits]]

Crop rotation is the practice of growing different crop families in
succession on the same piece of land across multiple growing seasons. By
alternating plant families with different nutrient requirements, growth
habits, and pest vulnerabilities, rotation prevents the buildup of
soil-borne diseases, breaks pest and weed cycles, and balances nutrient
demands on the soil. A legume phase adds atmospheric nitrogen, while a
heavy-feeding brassica phase draws down accumulated nutrients, creating a
[[fukuoka-natural-fertility-cycle]] cycle that reduces or eliminates the need for synthetic
fertilizers.

A basic four-year rotation might follow the sequence of legumes, followed
by leafy greens, then fruiting crops, and finally root crops. Each family
places different demands on the soil and hosts different pest organisms.
When the same crop is grown repeatedly in the same location, specialized
pathogens and pests build to damaging levels. Rotation disrupts these
cycles by removing the host plant before pest populations can escalate.
Soil-borne diseases such as clubroot in brassicas, nematodes in tomatoes,
and fusarium wilt in potatoes are all effectively managed through
well-designed rotation schedules.

Crop rotation operates across years and requires advance planning that
spans multiple growing seasons. It is most critical in intensive
agricultural systems where large areas are devoted to few crop species.
The benefits accumulate over time as soil health improves through
diversified root exudates, reduced disease pressure, and balanced
[[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]]. [[coleman-deep-organic-farming-philosophy]] standards typically require documented
crop rotation plans as a fundamental soil management practice.
