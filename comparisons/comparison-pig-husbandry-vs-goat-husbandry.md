## Pig Husbandry vs Goat Husbandry

## Overview

Pigs and goats are two of the most productive and popular homestead livestock
choices, yet they serve fundamentally different roles on a smallholding. Goats
are browsers that convert brush and weeds into milk, meat, and fiber, while
pigs are omnivorous ground-foragers prized for rapid meat production and their
ability to till and clear land. Choosing between them — or combining both —
depends on your land, goals, and management capacity.

## Pig Husbandry

[[goat-husbandry]]

Goats excel as versatile producers of milk, meat, and fiber. Dairy breeds like
Nigerian Dwarf, Nubian, and Alpine produce one to three gallons of milk daily
with butterfat content ideal for cheese-making. Meat breeds such as Boer and
Kiko grow quickly on browse, while fiber breeds like Angora provide mohair for
textile production. This multi-product output makes goats uniquely flexible for
smallholders who want daily returns rather than a single annual harvest.

As browsers rather than grazers, goats prefer woody plants, brush, and weeds
over [[mollison-designers-tropical-pasture-grass-species-and-establishment]]. This makes them ideal for clearing overgrown land,
maintaining hedgerows, and controlling invasive species like multiflora rose,
blackberry, and thistle. Their diet of rough browse means they require less
supplemental grain than pigs — lactating does may receive one pound of grain per
three pounds of milk produced, and dry animals can thrive on good hay and browse
alone. However, goats are susceptible to internal parasites, especially the
barber pole worm (Haemonchus contortus), which is the single greatest health
challenge in [[goat-keeping]].

Fencing must account for their reputation as escape artists. Woven wire at four
to five feet with electric strands at top and bottom is standard, and even then
goats will rub, lean, and climb to exploit any gap. Housing must be dry and
well-ventilated — goats detest rain and are prone to [[bloomfield-lycoperdonosis-puffball-spore-inhalation-respiratory-illness]] from
ammonia buildup. Unlike pigs, goats produce relatively modest manure that is
lower in nutrient density but easier to manage in small quantities.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Pigs | Goats |
|--------|------|-------|
| **Primary Products** | Meat, manure, land clearing | Milk, meat, fiber, brush clearing |
| **Feed Strategy** | Omnivorous, pasture + grain | Browser, hay + browse + minimal grain |
| **Growth Rate** | Market weight in 6-8 months | Meat kids ready in 6-9 months |
| **Feed Conversion** | 2.5-4.5:1 | 4-6:1 (meat), daily milk output |
| **Fencing Challenge** | Rooting under, pushing through | Climbing, squeezing through gaps |
| **Climate Sensitivity** | Heat-sensitive, need wallows | Rain/wind-sensitive, need dry shelter |
| **[[manure-quality-and-fertilizer-values]]** | Very high nutrient density | Moderate nutrient density |
| **Parasite Risk** | Moderate | High (barber pole worm) |
| **Breeding Cycle** | 2 litters/year, 8-12 piglets | 1-2 litters/year, 1-4 kids |
| **Handling Difficulty** | Strong, can be dangerous | Agile, can be stubborn |

## When to Choose Pigs

Pigs are the best choice when your primary goal is efficient meat production on
a moderate scale. A pair of pigs can yield 400-500 lbs of finished meat per
year from a relatively small paddock, and their feed [[energy-conversion-efficiency]]
means lower input costs per pound of protein compared to goats raised for meat
alone. Their manure production is a significant asset in garden-intensive
systems, providing large volumes of hot compost material. Pigs also excel at
land-clearing projects — a small herd can turn a brushy quarter-acre into
plantable ground in a single season, which is invaluable during farm
establishment. Choose pigs if you have sturdy fencing infrastructure, reliable
shade and water, and a plan for processing or selling 200+ lbs of meat at
slaughter time.

## When to Choose Goats

Goats are ideal when you want diversified daily production rather than a single
annual harvest. A milking doe provides a consistent daily return of milk for
drinking, cheesemaking, or soap production, while also producing kids that can
be sold or raised for meat. Their browsing habit makes them perfect for
managing overgrown or brushy land that other livestock cannot utilize, and they
thrive on steep, rocky terrain that would defeat pigs. Fiber-producing breeds
add yet another income stream. Goats are also better suited to very small
properties where space is limited — a pair of Nigerian Dwarf goats can be kept
on as little as a quarter-acre with supplemental hay. Choose goats if you want
daily engagement with your livestock, multiple product streams, and an animal
that thrives on marginal land.

## Integrated Systems

Many permaculture practitioners run pigs and goats together in complementary
roles. Goats clear brush from the upper story while pigs work the ground level,
rotating through paddocks in a sequence that maximizes forage utilization. Pig
manure enriches the soil for subsequent goat pasture, and both species benefit
from parasite-breaking rotations since their internal parasites are generally
host-specific. Sepp Holzer's systems in Austria demonstrate pigs and goats
sharing mountainous terrain, with each species accessing the vegetation and
microclimates best suited to their biology. The key to successful integration
is separate housing (goats need dry beds, pigs tolerate mud) and careful
fencing design that contains both species' different escape strategies.
