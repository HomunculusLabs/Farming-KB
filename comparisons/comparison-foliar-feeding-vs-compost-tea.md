# Foliar Feeding vs Compost Tea
Two powerful tools in the organic grower's toolkit that serve overlapping but
distinct purposes. Foliar feeding delivers nutrients directly through leaf
tissue for rapid uptake, while compost tea inoculates plants and soil with
concentrated beneficial microbiology. Understanding when and how to use each
method — and how they complement one another — is essential for building an
effective plant health program.
## Foliar Feeding
[[foliar-feeding]]
Spraying liquid nutrient solutions directly onto plant leaves, absorbed through
stomata and the waxy cuticle for rapid uptake. Nutrients enter primarily through
stomata (concentrated on leaf undersides) and the cuticle when applied as a fine
mist in early morning or late evening.
Effective sprays include kelp tea, fish emulsion combined with kelp, compost tea,
chelated iron, and calcium chloride. Each targets specific plant needs: kelp for
trace minerals and growth hormones, fish emulsion for nitrogen and phosphorus,
chelated iron for iron chlorosis, calcium chloride for blossom end rot prevention.
**Strengths:**
- Rapid correction within hours of application
- Effective for micronutrient deficiencies unavailable in soil pH conditions
  (e.g., iron chlorosis in alkaline soils)
- Useful during stress recovery, transplant shock, and periods of cool soil
  temperatures when root uptake is slow
- Targeted delivery treats specific deficiency symptoms directly on affected
  plants without broadcasting nutrients across the entire garden
- Low equipment requirement — a pump sprayer and soluble amendments are
  sufficient
**Limitations:**
- Supplements but cannot replace soil fertility
- Short-duration effect, typically 1–2 weeks per application
- Leaf burn risk if applied in full sun or at excessive concentrations
- Cannot supply large nutrient volumes needed for heavy-feeding crops
- Repeated applications needed throughout the growing season
- Coverage can be uneven on large or densely canopied plants
## Compost Tea (AACT)
[[compost-tea-aact]]
Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) is a liquid extract of quality compost
aerated for 18–24 hours to multiply beneficial microorganisms — bacteria,
fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. Developed and popularized by Dr. Elaine Ingham,
AACT concentrates the living biology found in finished compost into a liquid
form easily applied to plants and soil.
**Brewing Process:**
- Compost placed in a mesh bag in dechlorinated water
- Continuous aeration at minimum 0.05 CFM per gallon
- Food sources added: molasses, kelp meal, fish hydrolysate
- Brew time of 18–24 hours for peak microbial populations
**Tailoring by Crop Type:**
- Bacterial-dominated teas (molasses, worm castings) suit vegetables and
  annuals that prefer bacterial-dominated soils
- Fungal-dominated teas (humic acid, kelp, oatmeal) suit trees and perennials
  that thrive in fungal-dominated soil ecosystems
Applied as either a soil drench or foliar spray depending on the target
outcome. Soil drenches focus on root zone inoculation, while foliar
applications coat leaf surfaces with protective microbial communities.
**Strengths:**
- Rapidly applies concentrated beneficial biology to plant surfaces and
  root zones
- Suppresses foliar and soil diseases through competitive exclusion —
  beneficial organisms occupy niches that pathogens would otherwise exploit
- Customizable microbial populations for specific crop types and soil
  conditions
- Works faster than applying raw compost to soil
- Improves nutrient cycling when used as a soil drench by increasing
  microbial decomposition activity
- Can serve as a carrier for other amendments when brewed with additional
  nutrients
**Limitations:**
- Quality depends entirely on starting compost — garbage in, garbage out
- Must use within hours of brewing (biology peaks at 18–24 hours, then
  declines rapidly)
- Requires consistent aeration throughout the brew — anaerobic conditions
  produce harmful organisms like E. coli and Clostridium
- No standardization — results vary between batches and brewers
- Equipment investment needed for reliable brewing (air pump, brewer,
  food-grade ingredients, aeration stones)
- Potential for phytotoxicity if applied incorrectly or if tea goes
  anaerobic before use
## Key Differences
| Aspect | Foliar Feeding | Compost Tea (AACT) |
|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| Primary Function | Direct nutrient supplementation | Biological inoculation |
| What Gets Delivered | Soluble minerals and nutrients | Living microorganisms and metabolites |
| Application Method | Fine mist sprayed onto leaf surfaces | Soil drench or foliar spray |
| Duration of Effect | Short (1–2 weeks per application) | Medium (ongoing biological activity) |
| Speed of Results | Hours | Days to weeks for full effect |
| Soil vs Plant Focus | Plant-focused (foliar absorption) | Both soil and plant surfaces |
| Skill Required | Low to moderate | Moderate to high |
| Cost | Low (sprayer and solutions) | Moderate (brewer, compost, food sources) |
## When to Choose Foliar Feeding
- Emergency nutrient corrections when plants show visible deficiency symptoms
- Micronutrient deficiencies that are soil-unavailable due to pH lockout
- Transplant shock recovery to give stressed plants an immediate boost
- Early-season growth boosts when soil temperatures remain too cool for
  root uptake
- Targeted nutrient delivery to specific plants showing individual deficiencies
- Fruiting and flowering stages when plants have high phosphorus and potassium
  demand that soil alone may not meet quickly enough
## When to Choose Compost Tea
- Building and maintaining long-term soil biology and microbial diversity
- Disease suppression programs as part of an integrated pest management approach
- Organic growing systems seeking to replace synthetic chemical inputs with
  biological alternatives
- Inoculating new garden beds, potting mixes, or restored soil with beneficial
  organisms
- Regular maintenance sprays throughout the growing season for overall plant
  health and resilience
- Transitioning from conventional to organic management where soil biology
  has been depleted
## Using Both Together
Compost tea can be applied as a foliar spray, combining biology delivery with
some level of foliar feeding. Many experienced growers use both in integrated
programs — compost tea as a foundation treatment for soil health and disease
prevention, with targeted foliar feeds for specific nutrient corrections as
needed throughout the season.
A common integrated schedule: apply compost tea as a soil drench every 2–3
weeks for biological maintenance, use compost tea foliar sprays monthly for
disease prevention, and apply targeted foliar nutrient sprays as deficiency
symptoms appear or during key growth stages (early vegetative growth, pre-flower,
fruit development).
