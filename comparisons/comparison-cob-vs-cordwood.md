# Cob vs Cordwood

[[comparison-cob-building-vs-adobe-construction]] uses clay, sand, straw, and water mixed into monolithic
sculpted walls, offering ancient, extremely low-cost construction with great
design freedom. Cordwood construction lays debarked log ends crosswise in
mortar beds, combining thermal mass with insulation in a visually distinctive
wall system. Both are [[query-how-do-i-choose-a-natural-building-method-for-my-climate]] methods suited to owner-builders.

## Overview

### Cob Building

[[cordwood-construction]] uses short log sections, typically 12 to 24
inches long, laid crosswise in mortar beds to form walls. The log ends
create a distinctive pattern on both interior and exterior surfaces. The
mortar mix typically uses portland cement, lime, and sand with sawdust
added to improve insulation and reduce shrinkage cracking. Insulation can
be placed in the hollow center cavity between opposing log ends for
improved thermal performance.

Cordwood walls combine the thermal mass of the wood and mortar with the
insulation value of the log cavities, making them effective in colder
climates where pure thermal mass strategies like cob are insufficient. The
log-end aesthetic is warm and distinctive, and the technique is learnable
in a weekend workshop.

## Key Differences

| Feature | Cob | Cordwood |
|---|---|---|
| Primary materials | Clay/sand/straw mix | Short logs in mortar beds |
| Insulation value | Low: R-1 per inch, mass only | Moderate: R-1 to R-2.5 with fill |
| Climate suitability | Mild to moderate climates | Cold climates, mass plus insulation |
| Design freedom | Extreme, sculptural organic shapes | Log-end pattern, generally rectangular |
| Construction speed | Slow, layers must dry between courses | Moderate, individual log placement |
| Material sourcing | On-site clay and subsoil | Requires logs from forest or sawmill |
| Skill required | Intuitive, learnable by doing | Learnable in weekend workshop |
| Wall thickness | 18 to 24 inches monolithic | 12 to 24 inches depending on log length |
| Durability | 500+ years with proper details | Centuries with good mortar and protection |
| Best For | Sculptural design in mild climates | Cold-climate [[fukuoka-textdoc-energy-efficiency-agricultural-labor]] |

## When to Choose Cob

Choose cob for mild to moderate climates where thermal mass alone provides
adequate temperature regulation. Owner-built homes using on-site materials
benefit enormously from cob's near-zero material cost when suitable clay
subsoil is available. Sculptural structures such as garden walls, outdoor
ovens, benches, and sheds are ideal cob projects that build skills for
larger buildings. Permaculture homesteads appreciate cob's integration with
the landscape and its ability to incorporate recycled materials like glass
bottles for light features.

Cob performs best with a good roof overhang, a raised stone foundation that
keeps walls dry, and breathable lime or earthen plaster finishes. Hybrid
buildings can use cob on south-facing walls for [[comparison-passive-solar-vs-earth-sheltered]] mass and a
more insulating material on north-facing walls.

## When to Choose Cordwood

Choose cordwood for forested properties with available wood from thinning
operations, storm damage, or sawmill waste. Cold climates benefit from the
combination of thermal mass and insulation that cordwood provides, reducing
heating costs significantly. Owner-builders wanting distinctive log-end
aesthetics find cordwood delivers a warm, woodsy character that feels both
rugged and refined.

Cordwood requires careful attention to wood selection and preparation.
Softwoods with low shrinkage rates perform better than high-shrinkage
species. Logs should be debarked, dried to below 20 percent moisture
content, and coated with borate solution to prevent insect damage. The
mortar mix must be carefully proportioned to prevent cracking and ensure
good bond with the wood.

## Hybrid Approach

Many natural builders combine both techniques for optimal performance. A
common pattern uses cob walls on south-facing sides for thermal mass and
passive solar gain, with cordwood on north and east sides where insulation
is more important than heat absorption. Cob can also serve as interior
infill walls within a cordwood structure, providing curved partition walls,
built-in furniture, or sculptural elements.

The foundation and roof systems for both methods are similar, typically
rubble trench or reinforced concrete foundations with a good vapor barrier,
and [[comparison-bamboo-construction-vs-timber-frame-basics]] or living roof structures. This compatibility makes
combining them straightforward for builders comfortable with both materials.

## Bottom Line

Cob maximizes design freedom and minimizes material cost in mild climates
where thermal mass drives comfort. Cordwood delivers both mass and
insulation for cold-climate efficiency while creating a distinctive aesthetic.
Both are owner-builder friendly, and the most resilient natural buildings
often incorporate elements of each.
