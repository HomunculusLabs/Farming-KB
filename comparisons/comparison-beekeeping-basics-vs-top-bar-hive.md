## Beekeeping (Conventional Langstroth Approach)
[[top-bar-hive]]
Top bar hives are a horizontal hive design consisting of a single long
chamber (typically 40 to 48 inches) with wooden bars suspended across
the top. Bees build natural comb downward from each bar without the
constraint of pre-formed foundation, determining their own cell sizes
for worker brood, drone brood, and honey storage. The hive body
typically features sloped sides at approximately 30 degrees that follow
the natural shape of a honey bee cluster, and follower boards allow the
beekeeper to adjust the interior volume to match the colony's population
throughout the seasons.

The natural comb approach offers several significant advantages. Without
plastic foundation, bees build cells sized to their own specifications,
which research suggests may produce healthier bees with fewer varroa
mites due to a more natural ratio of drone to worker cells. The drone
comb that bees naturally build serves as a varroa monitoring tool, as
mites preferentially reproduce in drone brood and can be tracked by
examining drone cells. Natural comb also eliminates the concern about
chemical contamination from foundation wax, which can accumulate
agricultural pesticides and miticides over years of use.

Top bar hives eliminate the heavy lifting associated with Langstroth
supers because the beekeeper works from the back of the hive and handles
individual bars (each weighing 3 to 8 pounds) rather than stacked boxes.
This makes top bar beekeeping accessible to people with physical
limitations and reduces the risk of back injury. The hives can be built
from untreated lumber in a weekend using basic woodworking tools,
reducing startup costs. However, top bar hives generally produce less
honey per colony than Langstroth hives (typically 30 to 50 pounds versus
60 to 100 pounds), and the lack of standardized equipment means fewer
off-the-shelf management resources and no compatibility with commercial
honey extractors.
