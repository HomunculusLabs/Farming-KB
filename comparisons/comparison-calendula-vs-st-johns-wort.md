## Calendula vs St. John's Wort

## Overview

Calendula (*[[calendula-officinalis]]*) and St. John's Wort (*Hypericum 
perforatum*) represent two of European herbalism's most venerable medicinal 
plants, each with over 2,000 years of documented use. While both offer 
significant healing properties, they differ fundamentally in their primary 
applications—Calendula excelling as a topical wound healer and skin 
remedy, while St. John's Wort is renowned for its internal 
antidepressant and nervine effects. This comparison examines their 
distinct phytochemical profiles, cultivation requirements, medicinal 
applications, and safety considerations to help herbalists and gardeners 
understand when to choose one over the other.

## Calendula
[[st-johns-wort-guide]]

St. John's Wort (*Hypericum perforatum*) is a perennial herb native to 
Europe, western Asia, and North Africa, now naturalized throughout much 
of temperate North America. Named for flowering around St. John's Day (June 
24), it has been used medicinally for over 2,400 years and is one of the 
most clinically studied herbal remedies, particularly for mild to moderate 
depression. It also produces a beautiful red oil infusion valued in topical 
herbal medicine for nerve pain and wound healing.

St. John's Wort contains several active compounds: hypericin, hyperforin, 
pseudohypericin, flavonoids, and xanthones. Hyperforin is now believed 
to be the primary compound responsible for the antidepressant effect, 
acting as a reversible, non-competitive inhibitor of synaptic reuptake 
for serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, GABA, and L-glutamate. This gives 
it a broader pharmacological profile than most conventional antidepressants.

Over 50 randomized controlled trials and multiple systematic reviews 
support its efficacy for mild to moderate depression, showing it to be 
comparable to standard SSRIs with fewer side effects for many people. 
Topically, the famous red oil infusion (*Oleum Hyperici*) has been used 
for nerve pain, sciatica, muscle aches, and minor wounds since ancient 
Greek times.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Calendula | St. John's Wort |
|--------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Primary Use** | Topical wound healing, skin conditions | Internal depression/anxiety, nerve pain |
| **Key Compounds** | Triterpenes (faradiol), flavonoids, carotenoids | Hyperforin, hypericin, flavonoids, xanthones |
| **Plant Family** | Asteraceae | Hypericaceae |
| **Lifecycle** | Annual/short-lived perennial | Perennial |
| **Plant Part Used** | Flowers primarily | Flowering tops (flowers + leaves) |
| **Medicinal Strength** | Skin regeneration, anti-inflammatory | Antidepressant, nervine, antiviral |
| **Safety Profile** | Very safe, minimal contraindications | Significant drug interactions, photosensitivity |
| **Cultivation** | Easy annual, self-sows readily | Spreading perennial, can become invasive |
| **Harvest Time** | Throughout flowering season | Peak bloom (late June-early July) |

## When to Choose Calendula

Choose calendula when dealing with external skin conditions, wounds, or 
when seeking a gentle, multi-purpose herb for garden and medicine cabinet 
use. It is particularly indicated for:

- Slow-healing wounds, burns, or radiation dermatitis
- Diaper rash, eczema, or other inflammatory skin conditions
- General first aid applications in the home or clinic
- Companion planting in vegetable gardens to attract beneficial insects
- When safety is a primary concern (pregnancy, children, multiple 
  medications)

Calendula is the preferred choice for beginners due to its exceptional 
safety profile and versatility.

## When to Choose St. John's Wort

Choose St. John's Wort when addressing internal mood disorders, nerve 
pain, or viral conditions. It is particularly indicated for:

- Mild to moderate depression, especially with anxiety component
- Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) and winter depression
- Nerve pain, sciatica, or neuralgia (topically or internally)
- Viral infections, particularly enveloped viruses

St. John's Wort should be used with caution due to potent drug 
interactions and should only be used under professional guidance when 
taking conventional medications.

## Safety Considerations

**Calendula** has an exceptional safety profile with minimal 
contraindications. While allergies to plants in the Asteraceae family are 
possible, they are uncommon. Calendula is generally considered safe during 
pregnancy, breastfeeding, and for children. No significant drug interactions 
have been documented.

**St. John's Wort** requires careful consideration due to its significant 
drug interactions and side effects:

- **Drug Interactions**: Potent inducer of cytochrome P450 enzymes and 
  P-glycoprotein, reducing effectiveness of birth control pills, 
  antidepressants (risk of serotonin syndrome), blood thinners, HIV 
  medications, and many other drugs
- **Photosensitivity**: Increases sensitivity to UV radiation, requiring sun 
  protection during use
- **Serotonin Syndrome**: Risk when combined with other serotonergic drugs
- **Pregnancy**: Not recommended due to potential uterine stimulant effects
- **Withdrawal**: Discontinuation syndrome similar to conventional 
  antidepressants
