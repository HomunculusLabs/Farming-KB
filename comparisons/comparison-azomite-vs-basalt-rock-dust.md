# Azomite vs Basalt Rock Dust

## Overview

Azomite and basalt rock dust are two of the most popular mineral amendments
used in organic agriculture and permaculture for soil remineralization. Both
provide broad-spectrum trace minerals, but they differ significantly in
geological origin, mineral composition, sodium content, weathering rate,
and ideal use cases. Understanding these differences helps growers choose
the right amendment for their specific soil conditions and crop needs.

## Azomite

[[basalt-rock-dust-agriculture]]

Basalt rock dust is ground from basalt, the most common extrusive igneous
rock on Earth. Unlike Azomite's single-source deposit, basalt is
geologically abundant worldwide, making it widely available and often
locally sourced. Its mineral composition is balanced and well-suited to
agricultural use: silica (45-52%), alumina (12-18%), calcium oxide (8-12%),
magnesium oxide (5-12%), and iron oxide (8-14%) form the bulk, with useful
amounts of phosphorus (0.5-2%) and potassium (1-5%).

Basalt's weathering rate is moderate. Plagioclase feldspar and pyroxene
minerals dissolve gradually in the presence of carbonic acid from root
respiration, releasing calcium, magnesium, iron, and silica in plant-
available forms. An important co-benefit: basalt weathering consumes
atmospheric CO2 through enhanced rock weathering, making it a potential
carbon sequestration tool at scale. Application rates generally range
from 0.5-5 kg per square meter depending on soil deficiency severity.

Basalt's lower sodium content (typically under 0.5%) makes it safer for
sodium-sensitive soils and crops. Its broader geological availability
often translates to lower cost per tonne compared to the single-source
Azomite product, especially for broad-acre applications.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Azomite | Basalt Rock Dust |
|--------|---------|------------------|
| **Source** | Single Utah deposit | Globally abundant volcanic rock |
| **Silicon** | 30-35% | 45-52% |
| **Calcium** | 4-6% | 8-12% (as CaO) |
| **Magnesium** | 1-2% | 5-12% (as MgO) |
| **Iron** | 2-4% | 8-14% (as Fe2O3) |
| **Sodium** | 1-3% (elevated) | Under 0.5% (low) |
| **Phosphorus** | 0.1-0.3% (low) | 0.5-2% (moderate) |
| **Rare Earths** | Yes (unique) | Trace only |
| **Minerals Count** | 70+ | 50+ |
| **Weathering** | Moderate-slow | Moderate |
| **CO2 Capture** | Minimal | Significant potential |
| **OMRI Listed** | Yes | Varies by source |
| **Cost/Tonne** | Higher (niche) | Lower (commodity) |
| **Availability** | Single supplier | Multiple global sources |

## When to Choose Azomite

Azomite excels when the goal is maximum trace mineral diversity in a
single application. Its rare earth element content is unique among
agricultural amendments, and its 70+ mineral spectrum makes it ideal for
soils that have been heavily depleted through intensive cropping. If a
soil test reveals broad trace mineral deficiencies rather than specific
macronutrient shortages, Azomite's wide-spectrum approach fills many
gaps simultaneously. It is particularly popular among organic cannabis
cultivators, fruit growers seeking improved flavor and shelf life, and
home gardeners who value convenience of a single all-in-one amendment.

However, the elevated sodium content (1-3%) warrants caution in arid
regions or sodic soils. Avoid over-application in salt-sensitive growing
systems.

## When to Choose Basalt Rock Dust

Basalt rock dust is the better choice when magnesium, iron, or calcium
are specifically needed, or when cost-effective broad-acre remineralization
is the priority. Its higher silica content supports strong plant cell
walls and pest resistance, while the significant magnesium content
addresses one of the most common mineral deficiencies in leached or
weathered soils. For permaculture designers and regenerative agriculture
practitioners, basalt's carbon sequestration potential through enhanced
rock weathering adds a climate-benefit dimension absent from Azomite.

Basalt is also preferable in sodium-sensitive situations — arid-climate
soils, container growing, and hydroponic systems where sodium
accumulation is a concern. Its commodity pricing and local availability
make it practical for farm-scale applications where Azomite's cost would
be prohibitive.

## Combined Use

Many growers apply both amendments in complementary fashion: basalt at
higher rates (2-5 kg/m2) for baseline remineralization and pH buffering,
with Azomite at lower rates (0.2-0.5 kg/m2) as a trace-mineral booster.
This approach leverages basalt's macronutrient strength while capturing
Azomite's unique rare earth and trace element profile. A seasonal
rotation — basalt as a primary soil drench in spring, Azomite as a
lighter top-dress or foliar supplement during peak growth — can maximize
the benefits of both without overloading any single mineral pathway.
