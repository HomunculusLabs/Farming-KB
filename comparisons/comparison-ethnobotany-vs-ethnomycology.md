# Ethnobotany vs Ethnomycology
Ethnobotany and ethnomycology are sister disciplines within ethnobiology that study
the relationships between human cultures and the organisms they use — plants in the
former case, fungi in the latter. Both draw on anthropology, ecology, chemistry, and
linguistics, but they differ significantly in scope, history, and cultural weight.
## Definitions and Scope
**Ethnobotany** is the study of how people of a particular culture and region use
indigenous plants. This encompasses food crops, medicinal herbs, building materials,
fibers, dyes, poisons, and ritual plants. It is the broader and older of the two
fields, with roots tracing back to ancient Greek naturalists like Theophrastus and
Dioscorides, and was formalized as an academic discipline in the late 19th century
by John Harshberger. Modern ethnobotany spans paleoethnobotany (archaeological plant
remains), pharmacognosy (drug discovery from plants), anmedicinal-mushrooms
(intellectual property and biopiracy debates).
**Ethnomycology** is the study of the historical uses and sociocultural impact of
fungi. It encompasses edible and [[psilocybin-mushrooms]] to the Western public and launched ethnomycology
as a recognized pursuit. While mycological knowledge existed in every foraging
culture, the academic study of human-fungal relationships lagged behind ethnobotany
and remains comparatively niche. Giorgio Samorini has been a leading European voice,
documenting mushroom traditions across Africa and the Mediterranean.

In medicine, ethnobotany gave us artemisinin (sweet wormwood for malaria), quinine
(cinchona bark), vincristine (rosy periwinkle for cancer), and aspirin (willow
bark). Ethnomycology contributed penicillin (Penicillium), cyclosporine
(Tolypocladium for transplant immunosuppression), and statin drugs derived from
fungi. The untapped pharmacological potential of fungi — particularly endophytic
and soil-dwelling species — is considered one of the most promising frontiers in
drug discovery.
## Methodological Differences
Ethnobotanical fieldwork involves market surveys, herbarium voucher collection,
participatory plant identification walks, and interviews with healers. Plants are
visible and stationary, making documentation straightforward. Ethnomycological
fieldwork faces unique challenges: fungi are ephemeral, fruiting bodies appear and
disappear within days, many species are only identifiable microscopically, and
cultural knowledge about mushrooms is often closely guarded. Spore prints, DNA
barcoding, and seasonal repeated visits are essential. The invisibility of mycelial
networks means the full ecological role of culturally important fungi is often
underappreciated. Indigenous taxonomies reflect this asymmetry: plant folk genera
often map closely to scientific ones, while fungal taxonomies are shallower, with
many cultures grouping fungi as a liminal category associated with decay or the
spirit world. Non-descript mushrooms may be lumped into broad categories.
## Contemporary Relevance
Both fields are critical to biocultural conservation. Ethnobotany directly informs
efforts to protect crop wild relatives and recognize indigenous land stewardship.
The Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit Sharing is fundamentally an ethnobotanical
policy instrument. Ethnomycology supports preservation of wild mushroom habitats
and cultural contexts keeping fungal knowledge alive. Ethnobotany has been more
successfully integrated into conservation policy because plant-based ecosystem
services are well quantified. Ethnomycology is gaining ground as mycorrhizal
networks and fermentation industries receive more attention, and psychedelic
therapy research has renewed funding for ethnomycological study.
## When to Study Which
- **Ethnobotany** when your focus is on agriculture, herbal medicine, plant
  domestication, deforestation impacts, or indigenous land-use systems.
- **Ethnomycology** when your focus is on mushroom foraging traditions,
  fermentation heritage, psychoactive fungi in ritual contexts, or fungal ecology
  as it relates to cultural practice.
- **Both** when studying entheogenic traditions, traditional fermentation systems,
  or biocultural diversity where plants and fungi are intertwined.
