# Loquat vs Persimmon Cultivation

A detailed comparison of two outstanding winter/spring fruiting trees for home orchards, food forests, and permaculture systems. See also [[permplant-persimmon-diospyros-kaki-fruit-tree]].

## Quick Comparison Table

| Attribute | Loquat (*Eriobotrya japonica*) | Persimmon (*Diospyros kaki*) |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Rosaceae | Ebenaceae |
| USDA Zones | 8–10 | 7–10 |
| Mature Height | 10–25 ft (3–8 m) | 15–30 ft (5–10 m) |
| Chill Hours | 0–100 (very low) | 100–400 (moderate) |
| Harvest Season | Late winter – early spring | Late fall – early winter |
| Pollination | Mostly self-fertile | Depends on variety (some need male) |
| Soil pH | 6.0–7.5 (adaptable) | 6.0–7.5 |
| Water Needs | Moderate (drought-tolerant once established) | Low–moderate (quite drought-tolerant) |
| Lifespan | 20–50 years | 50–100+ years |
| First Fruit Age | 2–4 years from seed; 1–2 grafted | 3–5 years grafted |
| Sun Requirements | Full sun to partial shade | Full sun |

## Climate Adaptability

Loquats thrive in mild, frost-free or nearly frost-free climates. They tolerate brief dips to 10°F (−12°C) but flower and fruit suffer below 27°F (−3°C). Their low chill requirement makes them ideal for warm-winter subtropical zones where few other deciduous fruits succeed.

Persimmons are far more cold-hardy. Asian (Japanese) persimmons handle down to 0°F (−18°C), while American persimmons (*D. virginiana*) survive −25°F (−32°C). Persimmons need more chill hours to break dormancy properly, making them better suited to temperate or continental zones. Neither tree tolerates prolonged waterlogging.

## Soil and Site Requirements

Both trees are adaptable but perform best in well-drained loamy soils. Loquats tolerate heavier soils and slight salinity better than persimmons. Persimmons are unforgiving of poor drainage — root rot is a common failure cause. Both prefer a slightly acidic to neutral pH (6.0–7.5) and benefit from wind protection, especially persimmons when loaded with fruit.

## Planting and Establishment

**Loquats:** Plant in spring or fall. Dig a wide, shallow hole. Mulch heavily to retain moisture. Young trees may need staking in windy sites. Water deeply 2–3 times per week until established (6–12 months).

**Persimmons:** Plant bare-root in late winter while dormant. Container-grown trees can go in year-round. Avoid deep planting — keep the graft union well above soil level. Persimmons have sensitive taproots; handle carefully during transplant. Water regularly for the first year.

## Pruning and Training Differences

**Loquats:** Require minimal pruning. Train to an open center shape. Remove dead wood and crossing branches. Since they flower on current season's growth, prune immediately after harvest to avoid reducing the next crop. Thinning fruit clusters improves size and quality.

**Persimmons:** Also low-maintenance but benefit from scaffold training in early years. Prune during dormancy. Persimmons bear on current year's shoots, so moderate annual pruning encourages renewal wood. Remove suckers and water sprouts. Older trees may need restorative pruning.

## Fertilization Needs

**Loquats:** Respond well to a balanced NPK fertilizer applied 3× per year (spring, summer, fall). Micronutrient sprays (zinc, manganese) help in alkaline soils. Avoid excessive nitrogen, which promotes vegetative growth at the expense of fruit.

**Persimmons:** Light feeders — over-fertilization causes excessive drop of young fruit. Apply a low-nitrogen fertilizer once in early spring. Mature trees often need no supplemental feeding if grown in good soil with adequate organic mulch.

## Pest and Disease Profiles

**Loquats:** Fire blight is the most serious threat. Other issues include aphids, scale, and loquat scab. Birds are major pests of ripening fruit. Generally pest-resistant compared to other rosaceous fruits.

**Persimmons:** Remarkably pest-free. Occasional issues with scale, mealybugs, and persimmon psyllid. Fruit drop (related to over-fertilization or inconsistent watering) is more common than insect damage. Fungal leaf spot can occur in humid conditions.

## Harvest and Yield Comparison

| Factor | Loquat | Persimmon |
|---|---|---|
| Avg. yield (mature tree) | 50–100 lbs | 80–200 lbs |
| Fruit size | 1–2 oz | 2–8 oz (depending on variety) |
| Harvest window | 2–3 weeks | 2–4 weeks |
| Storage | Very short (3–5 days fresh) | Longer (2–4 weeks refrigerated) |
| Ripening | Tree-ripe (does not ripen off tree) | Many varieties can ripen off-tree |

Loquat yields are lower but arrive when almost nothing else is fruiting (Feb–Apr). Persimmons offer much larger harvests and store far better.

## Culinary Uses Comparison

**Loquats** are eaten fresh, canned, made into jams, jellies, and syrups. The slightly tart, aromatic flavor pairs well with citrus. Seeds are large relative to flesh, limiting processing efficiency. Loquat wine is a traditional product in Japan and China.

**Persimmons** shine both fresh and processed. Astringent varieties (Hachiya) are used for baking, puddings, and drying. Non-astringent types (Fuyu) are crisp and sweet like apples when firm. Dried persimmon (hoshigaki) is a prized delicacy. Persimmons are more versatile in the kitchen overall.

## Permaculture and Food Forest Roles

**Loquats** are excellent food forest understory trees. They tolerate partial shade, flower in autumn (rare bee forage), and provide early-spring fruit during a food gap. Their evergreen canopy offers year-round windbreak and privacy screening.

**Persimmons** serve as canopy or sub-canopy elements. Their late-season fruit provides critical calories when other crops are done. Persimmon leaves produce high-quality compost. American persimmons in particular are valuable wildlife plants, supporting birds and mammals through winter.

## Pros and Cons

### Loquat
- **Pros:** Low chill requirement, evergreen, ornamental, early spring harvest, few pests
- **Cons:** Short shelf life, frost-sensitive flowers, relatively small fruit, large seed-to-flesh ratio

### Persimmon
- **Pros:** Cold-hardy, long-lived, high yielding, few pests, excellent storage, versatile in kitchen
- **Cons:** Slow to bear, astringent varieties require softening, sensitive to poor drainage, can be messy when fruit drops

## When to Choose Loquat vs Persimmon

**Choose loquat if:** You live in zones 9–10 with mild winters, want an evergreen ornamental fruit tree, need early-spring fruit, or are building a subtropical food forest understory.

**Choose persimmon if:** You live in zones 7–9, want a long-lived productive tree, value high yields and good storage, or need a reliable late-season food source in a temperate food forest.

**Grow both if:** Your climate overlaps (zones 8–9). Together they provide nearly year-round interest — loquat fruit in early spring, persimmon fruit in late fall — with evergreen loquat and deciduous persimmon creating complementary canopy dynamics.

## Companion Planting Potential

Loquats and persimmons can absolutely be grown together. They share similar soil preferences and neither aggressively competes with the other. Key considerations:

- **Spacing:** Allow at least 12–15 ft between trees to prevent crowding at maturity.
- **Root zones:** Persimmons' deep taproots occupy a different soil profile than loquats' shallower, fibrous roots.
- **Succession harvest:** Planting both extends your fresh fruit season by 6–8 months in favorable climates.
- **Polyculture pairing:** Both work well underplanted with comfrey, daikon radish, and nitrogen-fixing groundcovers like white clover.
- **Caution:** Ensure the planting site has excellent drainage for the persimmon's sake.
