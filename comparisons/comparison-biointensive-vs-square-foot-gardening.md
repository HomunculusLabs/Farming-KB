     9|# Biointensive vs Square Foot Gardening
    10|
    11|## Overview
    12|
    13|Biointensive gardening and square foot gardening are two of the most popular small-scale
    14|intensive growing methods, both designed to maximize food production in minimal space using
    15|raised beds and close plant spacing. Biointensive gardening, developed by [[john-jeavons]] through
    16|Ecology Action, emphasizes closed-loop soil fertility through dedicated compost crops, deep
    17|soil preparation, and calorie-efficient food production. Square foot gardening, created by Mel
    18|Bartholomew, simplifies intensive gardening with a grid-based raised bed system using a
    19|proprietary soil mix and standardized plant spacing rules.
    20|
    21|## Biointensive Gardening
    22|
    23|[[biointensive-gardening-overview]]
    24|
    25|The biointensive method is built around eight interconnected principles: deep double-digging to
    26|loosen soil to a depth of 24 inches, close hexagonal spacing to create a continuous living
    27|canopy over the bed, composting with all crop residues and dedicated compost crops, carbon
    28|farming (growing specific crops for compost biomass production), calorie farming (growing
    29|calorie-dense root crops for dietary self-sufficiency), companion planting for pest control and
    30|nutrient sharing, open-pollinated seed saving for genetic diversity, and integrated pest
    31|management. The ambitious goal is to produce all compost materials and calories needed to
    32|sustain one person on as little as 4,000 square feet of growing area.
    33|
    34|Double-digging is the signature practice of biointensive gardening and the source of both its
    35|remarkable productivity and its reputation for being labor-intensive. The process involves
    36|removing the top 12 inches of soil from a trench, loosening the subsoil below another 12 inches
    37|with a digging fork, and replacing the topsoil amended with a 2-inch layer of mature compost.
    38|This creates a deeply aerated root zone that allows plant roots to penetrate much deeper than
    39|in conventionally prepared beds, dramatically improving drought resistance and nutrient access.
    40|However, double-digging requires 4-8 hours of intense physical labor per 100 square feet of
    41|bed, making it impractical for many home gardeners.
    42|
    43|A core philosophy of biointensive gardening is that at least 60% of the growing area should be
    44|devoted to compost crops (such as wheat, oats, rye, and fava beans) and calorie crops (such as
    45|potatoes, sweet potatoes, and parsnips) rather than vegetables. This focus on soil-building and
    46|staple calorie production creates a genuinely self-sufficient food system, but it means less
    47|space is available for the diverse vegetable harvests that most home gardeners expect. The
    48|method has been validated through decades of research at Ecology Action's mini-farm in Willits,
    49|California, and is widely taught in sustainable agriculture programs worldwide.
    50|
    51|## Square Foot Gardening
    52|
    53|[[square-foot-gardening]]
    54|
    55|Square foot gardening divides raised beds into a visible grid of one-foot squares, with each
    56|square planted with a specific number of plants based on their mature size. Mel Bartholomew's
    57|system assigns each plant type to a spacing category: extra-large plants like broccoli or
    58|cabbage (1 per square), large plants like lettuce or peppers (4 per square), medium plants like
    59|spinach or bush beans (9 per square), and small plants like carrots or radishes (16 per
    60|square). This grid system eliminates the need for thinning, row spacing calculations, and the
    61|guesswork of reading seed packet instructions.
    62|
    63|The growing medium, called "Mel's Mix," is a blend of one-third coarse vermiculite, one-third
    64|peat moss (or coconut coir as a sustainable alternative), and one-third blended compost from
    65|multiple sources. This soil-free mix provides excellent drainage, consistent moisture
    66|retention, and balanced nutrient availability without any of the compaction, weed seed banks,
    67|or soil-borne disease organisms found in native soil. While this eliminates many common
    68|gardening problems, it means that square foot gardens are not building or improving the native
    69|soil beneath them and remain dependent on purchased or imported ingredients.
    70|
    71|Raised beds in square foot gardening are typically 4 feet by 4 feet (16 square feet total) with
    72|6-inch-tall sides, placed on top of any surface including pavement, compacted clay, or even
    73|rooftops. This accessibility makes the method ideal for urban gardeners, apartment dwellers,
    74|renters, people with physical limitations, and anyone who wants a manageable garden requiring
    75|minimal maintenance. The weed-free growing medium and grid-based planting keep ongoing
    76|maintenance to roughly 5-10 minutes per week per bed.

    78|## Key Differences
    79|
    80|| Aspect | Biointensive | Square Foot Gardening |
    81||--------|-------------|----------------------|
    82|| Approach | Closed-loop soil fertility, deep tillage | Grid-based raised beds with imported soil mix |
    83|| Creator | John Jeavons (Ecology Action, 1970s) | Mel Bartholomew (1981) |
    84|| Soil Preparation | Double-digging to 24 inches with compost | 6-inch raised beds filled with Mel's Mix |
    85|| Soil Building | Central focus; 60% area for compost/calorie crops | No soil building; relies on imported materials |
    86|| Spacing System | Hexagonal close spacing based on canopy | Grid squares with fixed plant counts per foot |
    87|| Water Use | 60-80% less than conventional agriculture | Moderate; Mel's Mix retains moisture well |
    88|| Labor | Very high (especially double-digging) | Very low after initial bed construction |
    89|| Self-Sufficiency | High; designed for complete diet production | Low; depends on purchased soil components |
    90|| Skill Level | Advanced; significant learning curve | Beginner-friendly; minimal knowledge needed |
    91|| Start-Up Cost | Low (seeds, hand tools, compost) | Moderate to high (vermiculite, coir, compost, lumber) |
    92|| Scalability | Scales to full dietary self-sufficiency | Best for small supplemental gardens |
    93|| Best For | Homesteaders, serious food growers, sustainability | Beginners, urban gardeners, children |
    94|| Drawbacks | Extremely labor-intensive, less vegetable variety | Depends on imported materials, not self-sufficient |

    96|## Pros and Cons Summary
    97|
    98|Biointensive gardening offers genuine self-sufficiency, dramatic water savings, deep soil
    99|improvement, and the ability to grow a complete human diet in minimal space. The trade-off is
   100|the very high labor requirement, especially during initial bed preparation, and the reduced
   101|space available for diverse vegetable production since 60% must go to compost and calorie
   102|crops.
   103|
   104|Square foot gardening offers exceptional accessibility, simplicity, and low ongoing maintenance
   105|that makes it the most beginner-friendly gardening method available. The trade-off is
   106|dependency on purchased soil components, inability to improve native soil, and limited capacity
   107|for true self-sufficiency or large-scale food production.
   108|
   109|## When to Choose Biointensive
   110|
   111|Choose biointensive gardening if your goal is genuine food self-sufficiency, deep and lasting
   112|soil improvement, and minimal external inputs over time. The method is ideal for homesteaders,
   113|small-scale farmers, and dedicated gardeners willing to invest significant physical labor in
   114|exchange for long-term soil health and calorie independence. Biointensive is also the better
   115|choice for those who want to save their own seeds, grow their own compost materials, and create
   116|a truly closed-loop growing system that reduces dependence on purchased inputs of any kind.
   117|
   118|## When to Choose Square Foot Gardening
   119|
   120|Square foot gardening is the best choice for beginners, urban gardeners, and anyone who wants a
   121|productive garden without the physical demands of traditional methods. The system is
   122|particularly well-suited for educational settings such as schools, community gardens, and
   123|therapeutic horticulture programs because the grid provides a clear visual framework for
   124|learning about plant spacing and garden planning. It is also ideal for gardeners with physical
   125|limitations since the raised beds reduce bending and the small area minimizes walking and heavy
   126|lifting.
   127|
   128|## Hybrid Approaches
   129|
   130|Many experienced gardeners combine elements of both methods to leverage the strengths of each.
   131|Using biointensive hexagonal close spacing within square foot raised beds can increase planting
   132|density while avoiding the heavy labor of double-digging. Some gardeners use Mel's Mix for the
   133|first season to get started quickly, then gradually transition to biointensive compost-building
   134|practices as the beds mature. Another popular hybrid uses square foot grid organization within
   135|biointensive beds, gaining the visual management benefits without the dependency on imported
   136|soil mix components.
