# Biointensive vs Square Foot Gardening

## Overview

Biointensive gardening and square foot gardening are two of the most popular small-scale
intensive growing methods, both designed to maximize food production in minimal space using
raised beds and close plant spacing. Biointensive gardening, developed by [[john-jeavons]] through
Ecology Action, emphasizes closed-loop soil fertility through dedicated compost crops, deep
soil preparation, and calorie-efficient food production. Square foot gardening, created by Mel
Bartholomew, simplifies intensive gardening with a grid-based raised bed system using a
proprietary soil mix and standardized plant spacing rules.

## Biointensive Gardening

[[biointensive-gardening-overview]]

The biointensive method is built around eight interconnected principles: deep double-digging to
loosen soil to a depth of 24 inches, close hexagonal spacing to create a continuous living
canopy over the bed, composting with all crop residues and dedicated compost crops, carbon
farming (growing specific crops for compost biomass production), calorie farming (growing
calorie-dense root crops for dietary self-sufficiency), companion planting for pest control and
nutrient sharing, open-pollinated seed saving for genetic diversity, and integrated pest
management. The ambitious goal is to produce all compost materials and calories needed to
sustain one person on as little as 4,000 square feet of growing area.

Double-digging is the signature practice of biointensive gardening and the source of both its
remarkable productivity and its reputation for being labor-intensive. The process involves
removing the top 12 inches of soil from a trench, loosening the subsoil below another 12 inches
with a digging fork, and replacing the topsoil amended with a 2-inch layer of mature compost.
This creates a deeply aerated root zone that allows plant roots to penetrate much deeper than
in conventionally prepared beds, dramatically improving drought resistance and nutrient access.
However, double-digging requires 4-8 hours of intense physical labor per 100 square feet of
bed, making it impractical for many home gardeners.

A core philosophy of biointensive gardening is that at least 60% of the growing area should be
devoted to compost crops (such as wheat, oats, rye, and fava beans) and calorie crops (such as
potatoes, sweet potatoes, and parsnips) rather than vegetables. This focus on soil-building and
staple calorie production creates a genuinely self-sufficient food system, but it means less
space is available for the diverse vegetable harvests that most home gardeners expect. The
method has been validated through decades of research at Ecology Action's mini-farm in Willits,
California, and is widely taught in sustainable agriculture programs worldwide.

## Square Foot Gardening

[[square-foot-gardening]]

Square foot gardening divides raised beds into a visible grid of one-foot squares, with each
square planted with a specific number of plants based on their mature size. Mel Bartholomew's
system assigns each plant type to a spacing category: extra-large plants like broccoli or
cabbage (1 per square), large plants like lettuce or peppers (4 per square), medium plants like
spinach or bush beans (9 per square), and small plants like carrots or radishes (16 per
square). This grid system eliminates the need for thinning, row spacing calculations, and the
guesswork of reading seed packet instructions.

The growing medium, called "Mel's Mix," is a blend of one-third coarse vermiculite, one-third
peat moss (or coconut coir as a sustainable alternative), and one-third blended compost from
multiple sources. This soil-free mix provides excellent drainage, consistent moisture
retention, and balanced nutrient availability without any of the compaction, weed seed banks,
or soil-borne disease organisms found in native soil. While this eliminates many common
gardening problems, it means that square foot gardens are not building or improving the native
soil beneath them and remain dependent on purchased or imported ingredients.

Raised beds in square foot gardening are typically 4 feet by 4 feet (16 square feet total) with
6-inch-tall sides, placed on top of any surface including pavement, compacted clay, or even
rooftops. This accessibility makes the method ideal for urban gardeners, apartment dwellers,
renters, people with physical limitations, and anyone who wants a manageable garden requiring
minimal maintenance. The weed-free growing medium and grid-based planting keep ongoing
maintenance to roughly 5-10 minutes per week per bed.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Biointensive | Square Foot Gardening |
|--------|-------------|----------------------|
| Approach | Closed-loop soil fertility, deep tillage | Grid-based raised beds with imported soil mix |
| Creator | John Jeavons (Ecology Action, 1970s) | Mel Bartholomew (1981) |
| Soil Preparation | Double-digging to 24 inches with compost | 6-inch raised beds filled with Mel's Mix |
| Soil Building | Central focus; 60% area for compost/calorie crops | No soil building; relies on imported materials |
| Spacing System | Hexagonal close spacing based on canopy | Grid squares with fixed plant counts per foot |
| Water Use | 60-80% less than conventional agriculture | Moderate; Mel's Mix retains moisture well |
| Labor | Very high (especially double-digging) | Very low after initial bed construction |
| Self-Sufficiency | High; designed for complete diet production | Low; depends on purchased soil components |
| Skill Level | Advanced; significant learning curve | Beginner-friendly; minimal knowledge needed |
| Start-Up Cost | Low (seeds, hand tools, compost) | Moderate to high (vermiculite, coir, compost, lumber) |
| Scalability | Scales to full dietary self-sufficiency | Best for small supplemental gardens |
| Best For | Homesteaders, serious food growers, sustainability | Beginners, urban gardeners, children |
| Drawbacks | Extremely labor-intensive, less vegetable variety | Depends on imported materials, not self-sufficient |

## Pros and Cons Summary

Biointensive gardening offers genuine self-sufficiency, dramatic water savings, deep soil
improvement, and the ability to grow a complete human diet in minimal space. The trade-off is
the very high labor requirement, especially during initial bed preparation, and the reduced
space available for diverse vegetable production since 60% must go to compost and calorie
crops.

Square foot gardening offers exceptional accessibility, simplicity, and low ongoing maintenance
that makes it the most beginner-friendly gardening method available. The trade-off is
dependency on purchased soil components, inability to improve native soil, and limited capacity
for true self-sufficiency or large-scale food production.

## When to Choose Biointensive

Choose biointensive gardening if your goal is genuine food self-sufficiency, deep and lasting
soil improvement, and minimal external inputs over time. The method is ideal for homesteaders,
small-scale farmers, and dedicated gardeners willing to invest significant physical labor in
exchange for long-term soil health and calorie independence. Biointensive is also the better
choice for those who want to save their own seeds, grow their own compost materials, and create
a truly closed-loop growing system that reduces dependence on purchased inputs of any kind.

## When to Choose Square Foot Gardening

Square foot gardening is the best choice for beginners, urban gardeners, and anyone who wants a
productive garden without the physical demands of traditional methods. The system is
particularly well-suited for educational settings such as schools, community gardens, and
therapeutic horticulture programs because the grid provides a clear visual framework for
learning about plant spacing and garden planning. It is also ideal for gardeners with physical
limitations since the raised beds reduce bending and the small area minimizes walking and heavy
lifting.

## Hybrid Approaches

Many experienced gardeners combine elements of both methods to leverage the strengths of each.
Using biointensive hexagonal close spacing within square foot raised beds can increase planting
density while avoiding the heavy labor of double-digging. Some gardeners use Mel's Mix for the
first season to get started quickly, then gradually transition to biointensive compost-building
practices as the beds mature. Another popular hybrid uses square foot grid organization within
biointensive beds, gaining the visual management benefits without the dependency on imported
soil mix components.
