# Cicadas vs Locusts

## At a Glance

| Feature | Cicadas | Locusts |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Order** | Hemiptera (true bugs) | Orthoptera (grasshoppers & crickets) |
| **Family** | Cicadidae (~3,400 species) | Acrididae, swarming species (~20 species) |
| **Key behavior** | Long subterranean development; mass synchronized emergence | Density-dependent phase polyphenism; swarming |
| **Diet** | Xylem sap (plant roots as nymphs, tree sap as adults) | Herbivorous — leaves, stems, flowers, crops |
| **Life cycle** | 2–17 years (most spent underground as nymphs) | 2–6 months (multiple generations per year) |
| **Sound** | Tymbal organs; species-specific mating songs up to 120 dB | Stridulation (hind leg rubbing wing); chorusing during swarms |
| **Agricultural impact** | Minor — twig damage from oviposition; temporary nuisance | Catastrophic — can consume entire crop fields in hours |
| **Economic damage** | Negligible to moderate (nursery stock, young trees) | Billions of dollars globally during outbreak years |
| **Population strategy** | Predator satiation (numerical overwhelm, then disappear) | Swarm migration (gregarious phase seeks new food sources) |
| **Mobility** | Poor flyers; mostly stay near emergence area | Excellent flyers; swarms travel 100+ km/day |
| **Cultural perception** | Symbol of summer, rebirth, immortality | Symbol of plague, famine, divine punishment |

## Biology and Taxonomy

Cicadas and locusts belong to entirely different insect orders and share only superficial similarities — both are relatively large, conspicuous insects that can be extremely abundant.

**Cicadas** are hemipterans (true bugs) related to aphids, leafhoppers, and shield bugs. Their closest relatives among common insects are spittlebugs and treehoppers. They are defined by their piercing-sucking mouthparts adapted for xylem feeding and their unique tymbal sound-producing organs.

**Locusts** are a subset of grasshoppers in the order Orthoptera. They are not a distinct taxonomic group — rather, certain grasshopper species in the family Acrididae exhibit density-dependent **phase polyphenism**, switching between a solitary phase (behaving like typical grasshoppers) and a gregarious, swarming phase (becoming locusts). Key locust species include the Desert Locust (*Schistocerca gregaria*), the Migratory Locust (*Locusta migratoria*), the Australian Plague Locust (*Chortoicetes terminifera*), and the South American Locust (*Schistocerca cancellata*).

## Life Cycle Comparison

### Cicadas
- **Eggs**: 400–600 laid in plant twigs; hatch in 6–10 weeks.
- **Nymphs**: Burrow underground and feed on root xylem sap for 2–17 years (most of the life span).
- **Adults**: Emerge synchronously, live 2–6 weeks above ground; reproduce and die.

### Locusts
- **Eggs**: Laid in pods in bare soil; hatch in 10–65 days depending on temperature and species.
- **Nymphs** (hoppers): 5–6 instars over 3–10 weeks; can form marching bands of millions in gregarious phase.
- **Adults**: Mature in 2–4 weeks; can produce multiple generations per year. Gregarious adults form swarms that may persist for months and travel vast distances.

The contrast is stark: cicadas invest in **extreme duration** (years underground, brief above-ground adulthood), while locusts invest in **extreme proliferation** (rapid generation turnover, explosive population growth, long-distance migration).

## Population Dynamics: Predator Satiation vs. Swarm Migration

### Cicadas — Predator Satiation

Periodical cicadas (*Magicicada*) employ **predator satiation** as their primary survival strategy. By emerging in synchronized billions over a compressed 4–6 week window, they overwhelm every predator in the ecosystem. Birds, mammals, reptiles, and arthropods eat cicadas to the point of satiation, but enough survive to reproduce. Between emergences (13 or 17 years), predator populations decline to levels that cannot significantly impact the next brood. The long prime-number life cycles (13, 17) are hypothesized to minimize overlap with predator population cycles and potential hybridization with adjacent broods.

### Locusts — Swarm Migration

Locusts employ the opposite strategy. When population density crosses a threshold, individuals undergo a behavioral, physiological, and morphological transformation into the gregarious phase:
- **Behavioral change**: Become attracted to each other (rather than repelled), aggregate, and march/swarm together.
- **Morphological change**: Develop longer wings, different coloration, and larger body size.
- **Physiological change**: Increased metabolic rate, accelerated development, and enhanced flight muscle development.

Swarms are **not** satiation events — they are **resource acquisition events**. Locusts swarm precisely because they need to find new food. A single Desert Locust swarm (40–80 million individuals per km²) can consume the equivalent of food for 35,000 people in a single day. Swarms travel with prevailing winds, covering 100–200 km per day, and can remain active for weeks or months.

## Ecological Impact

### Nutrient Cycling
- **Cicadas**: Mass emergences deliver a massive pulse of nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon to surface soils as billions of adults die. This nutrient enrichment benefits understory plants and soil microbial communities for years after an emergence. The effect is similar to marine salmon runs depositing marine-derived nutrients in riparian forests.
- **Locusts**: Swarms remove enormous quantities of plant biomass, but most of this biomass is metabolized and lost as CO₂ and frass rather than being deposited as nutrient-rich litter. Locust defoliation can shift ecosystem composition toward less palatable plant species.

### Trophic Cascades
- **Cicadas**: Predator satiation during emergences temporarily depress populations of generalist predators (birds, mammals), which may release other herbivorous insects from predation pressure — a potential indirect effect on forest herbivory.
- **Locusts**: Defoliation removes habitat and food for other herbivores and can cascade through the food web. However, locusts themselves become a concentrated food resource during and after swarms, attracting birds and other predators that may follow swarm fronts.

## Agricultural Significance

| Aspect | Cicadas | Locusts |
|--------|---------|---------|
| **Primary crop damage** | Oviposition scars on young trees; flagging of branch tips | Complete defoliation of crops, pastures, rangeland |
| **Vulnerable crops** | Nursery stock, young orchard trees (<4 years) | All cereals, legumes, pastures, vegetables, fruit trees |
| **Economic scale** | Millions USD (localized, emergence-year only) | Billions USD (continental-scale, multi-year outbreaks) |
| **Management** | Netting, insecticide on high-value trees | Early warning systems, aerial spraying, biopesticides (*Metarhizium acridum*) |
| **Prediction** | Brood maps are well-known decades in advance | Complex forecasting using weather, soil moisture, and survey data |

Cicadas are primarily a **nuisance** in agricultural contexts, while locusts are a **crisis**. The FAO operates a permanent Desert Locust monitoring and response system, while cicada management is handled at the local level by individual growers.

## Cultural Perception

The cultural symbolism of these insects could hardly be more different:

- **Cicadas** are associated with summer beauty, music, rebirth, and spiritual transcendence. In ancient China, jade cicadas were burial amulets symbolizing resurrection. In Japan, their song evokes *mono no aware* — the bittersweet beauty of impermanence. In ancient Greece, they symbolized artistic devotion and immortality.

- **Locusts** are one of humanity's oldest symbols of catastrophe. They appear in the Bible as the eighth plague of Egypt (Exodus 10) and in the Book of Revelation as a symbol of apocalyptic destruction. The word "locust" derives from the Latin *locusta*, meaning "lobster" — originally applied to both creatures due to a perceived resemblance. In cultures across Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, locust swarms are associated with famine, economic ruin, and divine punishment.

## Summary

Cicadas and locusts represent two fundamentally different evolutionary solutions to the challenge of population survival. Cicadas opt for **temporal isolation** — disappearing for over a decade and emerging in overwhelming numbers that no predator can consume. Locusts opt for **spatial escape** — multiplying rapidly and migrating en masse to find new resources. Both strategies produce spectacular natural phenomena, but their impacts on human society diverge dramatically: one is a wonder of nature to be appreciated, the other is a threat to food security requiring coordinated international response.
