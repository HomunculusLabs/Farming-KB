# Light Straw Clay vs Hempcrete

Light straw clay (also called slip straw, light clay straw, or
Leichtlehm) and hempcrete are both natural, bio-based wall
infill materials designed to insulate [[comparison-bamboo-construction-vs-timber-frame-basics]] and other
structural systems. Both replace conventional fiberglass or foam
insulation with plant fiber composites that are breathable,
carbon-sequestering, and non-toxic. However, their composition,
performance profiles, and build processes differ substantially,
making each better suited to particular climates, budgets, and
builder experience levels.

## Material Composition

Light straw clay is made by coating chopped straw fibers with a
thin slip of clay and water, then packing the coated straw into
temporary formwork between structural members. The clay content
is low — typically just enough to coat each straw stalk — and the
resulting wall is mostly air and straw, with clay acting as the
binder that gives the assembly cohesion once dried. No lime is
used in traditional light straw clay, though some modern mixes
add a small percentage of lime or sand for improved durability.

Hempcrete combines hemp hurd (the woody core of industrial hemp
stalks) with a lime-based binder — typically a blend of hydrated
lime, natural hydraulic lime, and pozzolanic additives — and
water. The lime cures through carbonation over weeks to months,
gradually converting to limestone and locking the hemp fibers
into a solid, stone-like matrix. Hempcrete always contains lime;
it cannot be made with clay alone.

## Thermal Performance

Light straw clay walls typically achieve R-1.5 to R-2.5 per inch,
depending on straw density and packing. A common 8-inch wall
reaches R-12 to R-20, while a 12-inch wall can achieve R-18 to
R-30. The material has moderate thermal mass from the clay
binder, providing some temperature buffering.

Hempcrete offers R-2.1 to R-2.5 per inch, with a typical 12-inch
wall reaching R-25 to R-30. Its lime binder provides significant
thermal mass and thermal lag, moderating indoor temperature
swings more effectively than light straw clay. The thermal mass
benefit is particularly valuable in climates with large
diurnal temperature swings.

## Moisture Management

Both materials are highly hygroscopic and vapor-permeable. Light
straw clay absorbs excess humidity and releases it as conditions
dry, but must be protected from sustained liquid water — repeated
soaking can cause the straw to rot. Hempcrete is inherently mold
and rot resistant due to its high lime pH (12+). It absorbs up
to 10-15% moisture by weight without degradation, making it more
forgiving of minor detailing errors in humid climates.

## Structural Role and Fire Resistance

Neither material is load-bearing; both serve as infill within a
structural frame (typically timber frame). Light straw clay is
packed into formwork and dries in 2-6 weeks; hempcrete is cast or
sprayed and cures via lime carbonation over weeks to months.

Both materials offer good fire resistance when properly finished.
Hempcrete provides inherent non-combustibility (1-2 hour rating
even unplastered). Light straw clay achieves 1-hour ratings with
plaster on both sides. Hempcrete has a slight edge because its
resistance does not depend on finish quality.

## Carbon Sequestration

Light straw clay sequesters carbon through the straw fibers,
locking in approximately 25-40 kg of CO₂ per cubic meter. The
clay binder has minimal carbon impact. Since straw is an
agricultural byproduct, no additional cultivation is needed.

Hempcrete is one of the highest-carbon-sequestering building
materials available, locking in 110-165 kg of CO₂ per cubic meter
— including both the biogenic carbon in hemp and the ongoing
carbonation of the lime binder over decades. However, hemp
cultivation requires dedicated acreage, and lime production is
energy-intensive, partially offsetting the sequestration benefit.

## Cost and Availability

Light straw clay is one of the most affordable natural insulation
options. Straw is inexpensive or free as an agricultural
byproduct, and clay subsoil can often be sourced on-site or
purchased cheaply. No specialty binders or suppliers are needed,
making this material highly accessible to owner-builders on tight
budgets.

Hempcrete is significantly more expensive. Hemp hurd costs
$0.50-$2.00 per pound depending on source and shipping distance,
and lime binders add $15-$30 per bag. Total material costs run
20-40% higher than [[comparison-cob-construction-vs-straw-bale-construction]] and substantially more than light
straw clay. Hemp processing infrastructure is also less developed
in many regions, limiting availability and increasing shipping
