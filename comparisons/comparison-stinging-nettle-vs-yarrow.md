# Stinging Nettle vs Yarrow

## Overview

Stinging nettle (*Urtica dioica*) and yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*)
are two of the most versatile perennial plants in temperate
permaculture systems. Both serve as dynamic accumulators, medicinal
herbs, and valuable companion plants, yet they differ significantly
in growth habits, nutritional profiles, and primary ecological
functions. Understanding these differences helps gardeners decide
which plant best suits their needs — or how to combine both for
maximum benefit.

## Stinging Nettle

[[stinging-nettle]]

Stinging nettle is a tall (100–200 cm) herbaceous perennial in the
family Urticaceae, native to temperate regions worldwide. It spreads
aggressively through creeping rhizomes extending 2+ meters
horizontally, forming dense clonal colonies. Nettle thrives in
nitrogen-rich soils and is a classic ruderal species, commonly found
along riverbanks, forest edges, and disturbed ground.

Nettle is among the most nutritious wild edible plants. Young spring
shoots provide exceptional levels of vitamin A (~200% DV), vitamin C
(~370% DV), vitamin K (~500% DV), iron (41.8 mg per 100 g — higher
than spinach), calcium (481 mg — five times that of milk), and
potassium. Leaves must be cooked or dried to neutralize stinging
trichomes before consumption.

Medicinally, nettle is best known for treating benign prostatic
hyperplasia (BPH), allergic rhinitis, and arthritis. In permaculture,
nettle excels as a dynamic accumulator — its deep roots mine
nitrogen, iron, calcium, and potassium from subsoil. Nettle tea
(fermented leaf extract) is a popular organic foliar spray and soil
drench, providing readily available nutrients to garden plants.

## Yarrow

[[yarrow-achillea-millefolium]]

Yarrow is a compact (20–100 cm) herbaceous perennial in the family
Asteraceae, native across temperate Northern Hemisphere regions. It
features feathery bipinnate leaves and flat-topped clusters of small
white-to-pink flowers blooming June through September. Unlike
nettle's aggressive spread, yarrow forms manageable clumps via short
creeping rhizomes with a 3–5 year crown lifespan.

Yarrow's medicinal reputation stretches to ancient Greece, where
Achilles supposedly used it to treat soldiers' wounds. Key compounds
include achilleine (hemostatic alkaloid), azulene and chamazulene
(anti-inflammatory sesquiterpenes), apigenin and luteolin
(flavonoids), tannins, and camphor. Yarrow is primarily used as a
wound herb, digestive bitter, fever reducer (diaphoretic), and
menstrual regulator.

In permaculture, yarrow serves as an insectary plant — its flat
flower heads attract ladybugs, lacewings, hoverflies, and parasitic
wasps. As a dynamic accumulator, its deep taproot mines potassium,
phosphorus, and copper from subsoil. Feathery leaves decompose
rapidly, making yarrow an excellent compost activator and drought-
tolerant ground cover.

## Nutritional and Medicinal Comparison

The nutritional contrast is stark. Nettle is a nutritional powerhouse
with extraordinary iron, calcium, and vitamins A, C, and K — one of
the most nutrient-dense wild foods available. Yarrow, while edible,
is valued for bitter aromatic compounds rather than macronutrients.

Medicinally, nettle excels against chronic conditions (BPH, arthritis,
allergies), while yarrow suits acute situations: wound management,
fever reduction, digestive upset, and menstrual cramping. Yarrow's
hemostatic action (achilleine) makes it superior for stopping
bleeding; nettle's antihistamine properties make it superior for
allergic reactions.

## Permaculture Applications

These plants serve complementary roles. Nettle is the soil builder —
its nitrogen-rich biomass, deep mineral-mining roots, and compost tea
make it indispensable for nutrient cycling. Plant nettle near compost
piles, in chop-and-drop zones, or along nitrogen-hungry fruit trees.

Yarrow is the insectary champion. Its flat flower clusters attract
predatory insects with short mouthparts. Plant yarrow as a border
around vegetable beds or scatter throughout orchards for pest
management. Its drought tolerance suits dry marginal areas where
nettle would struggle.

## When to Choose Stinging Nettle

Choose nettle when soil fertility and nutrient cycling are the
priority. It suits food forests with nitrogen-demanding fruit trees,
liquid fertilizer production (nettle tea), and spring green
harvesting. Nettle also indicates nitrogen-rich soil. Be cautious
with placement, as aggressive rhizomatous spread can overwhelm
neighbors in small spaces.

## When to Choose Yarrow

Choose yarrow when attracting beneficial insects, managing pests
biologically, or adding drought-tolerant ground cover are the goals.
It suits borders, rock gardens, meadow plantings, and orchard
understories. Yarrow's compact size makes it appropriate for smaller
gardens where nettle would be invasive. It also provides reliable
mid-to-late-season nectar for pollinators.

## Growing Both Together

The most effective approach is growing both in a permaculture system.
Place nettle in fertility zones (near compost or chicken runs) and
yarrow in insectary borders throughout vegetable and orchard areas.
Both can be harvested for medicinal use — nettle for teas and
nutrient extracts, yarrow for wound care and digestive tinctures.
Together they form a foundation of the herbaceous layer in temperate
permaculture designs.
