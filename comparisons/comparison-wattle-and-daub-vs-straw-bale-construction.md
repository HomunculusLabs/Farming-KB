## Wattle and Daub vs Straw Bale Construction

## Overview

Wattle and daub and [[straw-bale-construction]] are two of humanity's oldest
[[mollison-designers-straw-bale-building-techniques]], both relying on locally sourced, renewable materials
to create insulated, breathable walls. Wattle and daub uses a woven
wooden lattice packed with earthy mortar, while straw bale construction
stacks baled straw and plasters it on both sides. They share a
commitment to low-embodied-energy building but differ dramatically in
wall thickness, construction speed, insulation values, and the skill
sets required.

## Wattle and Daub

[[coleman-rye-straw-mat-cold-frame-insulation]] into walls, then plasters both faces
with earthen or lime-based plaster. Walls are typically 450 to 600 mm
thick, producing deeply sculptural, rounded corners and deep window
sills. The technique was formalized in the late 19th century in the
Nebraska Sandhills, where settlers built homes from bales when timber
and stone were unavailable.

Straw bale walls offer exceptional thermal insulation, typically R-25
to R-35 depending on bale density and orientation, rivaling or
exceeding conventional fiberglass insulation. The thick walls also
provide excellent sound attenuation. Construction can be remarkably
fast — a small crew can stack and pin an entire house wall in a few
days. The bales serve as both structure and insulation simultaneously
in load-bearing designs, or as infill within a post-and-beam frame.

Key vulnerabilities include moisture: if bales get wet and cannot dry,
[[fungal-decay-and-nutrient-transport]] can compromise the wall rapidly. Proper detailing —
raised foundations, generous roof overhangs, breathable plasters, and
good site drainage — is essential. Straw bale walls also require more
square footage of floor plan due to wall thickness, and the bales must
be kept dry during construction.

## When to Choose Wattle and Daub

Wattle and daub is ideal when you have abundant on-site clay soil and
access to coppiceable wood. It works well in temperate and tropical
climates where thermal mass is more valuable than high insulation. The
technique suits smaller buildings, garden walls, and infill panels
within timber-framed structures. It is particularly well-suited to
homesteaders who want to minimize material transport costs and work
with materials they can harvest directly from their land.

The moderate wall thickness preserves interior floor space compared to
straw bale, and the high thermal mass helps stabilize indoor
temperatures in climates with large diurnal swings. Wattle and daub
pairs naturally with [[preserving-wood-timber-framing]], cob, and light earth construction
for hybrid building approaches.

## When to Choose Straw Bale

Straw bale construction shines [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]] where insulation is the
primary concern. The R-25 to R-35 wall performance drastically reduces
heating energy, and the thick walls create a profound sense of
shelter and solidity. It is ideal for owner-builders because the
stacking work requires no specialized skills and can involve community
volunteers in a "bale-raising" event.

Straw bale works best in dry or arid climates where moisture risk is
lower, though careful detailing makes it viable in humid regions too.
It suits larger homes and buildings where the wall thickness does not
significantly impact usable floor area. The technique is also
well-documented with extensive building codes and engineering
guidelines available in many jurisdictions.
