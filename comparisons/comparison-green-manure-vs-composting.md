# Green Manure vs Composting
Both green manure and composting build soil organic matter and fertility through
biological processes, but they differ fundamentally in approach, inputs, and where
the decomposition happens. Green manure grows fertility in-place on active fields;
composting concentrates and processes collected materials into a portable amendment.
Choosing between them depends on land availability, waste streams, timeline, and soil-building goals.
## Green Manure Crops
[[green-manure-crops]]
Growing plants specifically to be incorporated into soil while green, adding organic
matter, nitrogen, and biological activity. Rather than importing fertility, green
manure captures it from sunlight and atmospheric nitrogen. Three main categories
serve different soil-building purposes:
**Nitrogen-fixing legumes:**
- Hairy vetch fixes 90-200 lb N/acre, one of the most productive legume covers
- Field peas add both N and moderate biomass with good winter hardiness
- Fava beans produce deep taproots and large seeds for food plus cover
- Clovers (crimson, red, white) suit different climates and mowing regimes
- Cowpeas thrive in heat and fix nitrogen in summer cover crop windows
These convert atmospheric N2 into plant-available forms through rhizobium
symbiosis in root nodules. Inoculation with the correct rhizobium strain is
essential for maximum nitrogen fixation.
**Biomass-building grasses:**
- Cereal rye produces 4,000-8,000 lb DM/acre with extensive fibrous root systems
- Sorghum-sudangrass adds massive biomass and deep taproots for subsoil
  compaction breaking; can grow 8+ feet in warm conditions
- Oats provide quick fall biomass and winterkill in cold climates, leaving
  a mulched surface for no-till spring planting
**Biofumigant brassicas:**
- Mustard releases glucosinolates upon incorporation that hydrolyze into
  isothiocyanates, compounds toxic to soil pathogens like *Fusarium*,
  *Pythium*, *Verticillium*, and root-knot nematodes
- Daikon radish (tillage radish) penetrates compaction layers with thick
  taproots that decompose leaving channels for water infiltration
C:N ratio determines decomposition speed and nitrogen availability after
incorporation. Below 20:1 (young legumes at early bloom) releases N quickly
for the following crop with minimal immobilization. Above 30:1 (mature grasses
with lignified stems) temporarily immobilizes N as soil microbes consume
available nitrogen to break down carbon-rich tissue.
**Incorporation timing is critical:**
- Legumes: terminate at early bloom for peak N with moderate C:N ratio
- Grasses: terminate at boot stage before stems lignify heavily
- Brassicas: terminate at full flower for maximum glucosinolate production
**Advantages:** Builds soil organic matter and fertility without synthetic inputs,
breaks compaction with deep taproots and root channels, suppresses soil-borne
diseases through biofumigation and competitive exclusion, provides erosion control
on fallow or sloped ground, attracts beneficial insects with flowering species,
feeds soil microbiome with living root exudates during growth, scavenges residual
soil nutrients that might otherwise leach.
**Limitations:** Requires land out of production during the cover crop period
(weeks to months depending on species and purpose), incorporation typically
involves tillage which can disrupt soil structure and fungal networks, high C:N
grasses tie up nitrogen temporarily causing a 2-4 week decomposition delay before
the following crop can be planted, climate and season constrain which species
are viable, seed cost can be significant for large acreage.
## Composting
[[composting-methods-comparison]]
Breaking down organic materials through controlled microbial decomposition into
stable humus. Composting concentrates nutrients from diverse waste streams into
a consistent, storable soil amendment. Multiple methods suit different scales:
**Hot composting:**
- Aerobic thermophilic process reaching 130-160°F internally
- Completes in 1-3 months with proper management
- Kills pathogens, weed seeds, and most pesticide residues
- Requires minimum 3x3x3 ft piles for heat retention and insulation
- Needs proper C:N ratio (25-30:1), adequate moisture (50-60%), and
  frequent turning for oxygen supply
- Most labor-intensive method but produces highest volume efficiently
**Cold composting:**
- Passive piling of materials with minimal management
- 6-12 months to completion depending on materials and climate
- Does not reach temperatures needed to kill pathogens or weed seeds
- Layering greens and browns improves decomposition rate
- Lowest effort method; suitable for low-priority waste streams
**Vermicomposting:**
- Red wiggler worms (*Eisenia fetida*) process organic matter in
  contained bins at moderate temperatures (55-77°F)
- Ideal for small spaces, apartments, and indoor year-round use
- Produces highest quality castings with exceptional microbial diversity
- Limited to moderate volumes per unit space and effort
- Worms are sensitive to temperature extremes and excess moisture
**Bokashi:**
- Anaerobic fermentation using effective microorganisms (EM) inoculant
- Handles all food waste including meat, dairy, and cooked foods
- Two-week fermentation in sealed bucket, followed by soil burial
- Requires ongoing purchase of EM inoculant bran
- Pre-fermented material still needs soil incorporation to finish
**Sheet mulching (lasagna gardening):**
- Layering cardboard, compost, and organic matter directly in place
- Builds soil without turning; excellent for starting new beds
- Slow process (6-12 months for full breakdown) but very low labor
- Converts lawn or compacted ground to garden beds effectively
**Advantages:** Converts waste streams into valuable soil amendment, hot method
kills pathogens and weed seeds reliably, reduces landfill waste, flexible scale
from apartment to farm, produces consistent and concentrated product, can be
done anywhere regardless of growing season, finished compost is easy to store
and apply precisely where needed.
**Limitations:** Hot composting requires dedicated space and regular labor for
turning and monitoring moisture and temperature, cold composting does not destroy
pathogens or viable weed seeds, vermicomposting handles limited volume relative
to bin size, bokashi requires ongoing purchase of EM inoculant, all methods
require collecting and managing input materials over time.
## Key Differences
| Factor | Green Manure | Composting |
|--------|-------------|------------|
| Process Location | In-place, in the field | Off-site or designated area |
| Input Materials | Seeds, soil, sunlight | Kitchen/yard waste, manures |
| Time to Completion | Growing season + 2-4 week decomposition | 1-12 months depending on method |
| Labor Requirements | Low-moderate (plant, terminate, incorporate) | Low (cold) to high (hot, turning) |
| Space Needed | Full field or bed area | Small bin to large windrow |
| Pathogen/Weed Kill | Biofumigant brassicas only | Hot composting kills reliably |
| Nitrogen Dynamics | Legumes fix N; grasses may immobilize N | Finished compost provides slow-release N |
| Soil Structure Impact | Living roots actively improve structure | Applied amendment improves structure over time |
| Scalability | Directly proportional to acreage | Can concentrate nutrients from large areas |
| Seasonality | Tied to growing seasons | Can operate year-round indoors |
## When to Choose Green Manure
Active fields needing fertility building between cash crop rotations, breaking
compaction layers with deep-rooted species like daikon radish or sorghum,
suppressing soil-borne diseases through biofumigation with mustard, controlling
erosion on sloped or fallow ground during off-season, adding nitrogen-fixation
capacity to crop rotation, farms with rotation flexibility that can accommodate
cover crop windows, building soil organic matter through in-situ root exudates
and surface residue, scavenging excess nutrients from previous crops.
## When to Choose Composting
Processing kitchen and yard waste into useful product, creating concentrated soil
amendment for beds and containers, reliable pathogen and weed seed destruction is
required (hot composting), limited growing space makes dedicating beds to cover
crops impractical, urban or suburban settings with small yards, year-round soil
building regardless of growing season, need for consistent and storable amendment
that can be applied precisely, converting animal manures into stable product.
## Hybrid Approach
Green manure crops can be cut and composted rather than incorporated directly,
combining in-situ root benefits with controlled decomposition of above-ground
biomass. This avoids the tillage typically needed for incorporation while still
capturing the nutrients from the cover crop. Compost can be applied to fields
before green manure planting to boost establishment, microbial activity, and
early growth. Many diversified farms use both: cover crops build soil in active
fields while composting processes off-farm waste streams into targeted amendments
for high-value beds.
