# Timber Frame vs Wattle and Daub

## Overview

[[wattle-and-daub-construction]] represent two
distinct but complementary traditions in [[mollison-designers-lime-and-cement-natural-building]]. [[preserving-wood-timber-framing]] is
a structural system using large wooden posts, beams, and braces connected
through interlocking joinery — the skeleton of a building. Wattle and daub
is an infill technique that weaves wooden strips into a lattice and packs
it with an earthen mixture — the skin that fills between structural members.

These two methods have been paired together for millennia. English
half-timber houses, German Fachwerk, and Japanese nuriya walls all use
timber frames filled with wattle-and-daub panels. Understanding each
method on its own terms helps builders decide how to use them — whether
together, separately, or combined with other [[natural-building]].

## Timber Frame Construction

Timber framing uses large-dimension timbers (typically 6x6 to 8x12 inches)
joined with mortise-and-tenon, dovetail, and other wood-to-wood connections
secured by hardwood pegs. The frame carries all structural loads — roof
weight, wind, snow, and seismic forces — independent of any wall infill.

Key characteristics include exceptional durability (frames routinely last
300+ years), the ability to create large open interior spaces without
intermediate supports, and a distinctive aesthetic with exposed timbers.
Wood species selection matters greatly: oak is the traditional choice for
strength and rot resistance, while [[douglas-fir]] offers excellent
availability in western regions.

Timber framing requires significant skill in joinery cutting and frame
raising. The craft declined with stick framing in the mid-1800s but has
seen a strong revival since the 1970s. Green (fresh-cut) timber is often
used, which is easier to cut but shrinks as it dries — draw-bored pegs
compensate by pulling joints tight over time.

## Wattle and Daub Construction

Wattle and daub is one of humanity's oldest [[query-what-are-the-best-natural-building-methods]], with evidence
dating back over 6,000 years to the Neolithic period. The wattle consists
of upright stakes driven at 30-45 cm intervals, woven with flexible withies
(hazel, willow, or bamboo). The daub is an earthen mixture of clay subsoil
(40-60%), sand (20-30%), chopped straw (5-15%), and optionally animal dung
(5-10%), packed into and over the woven lattice.

Once dried, wattle-and-daub panels form solid, insulating walls with good
thermal mass. The technique requires no industrial inputs and can be
learned quickly. Many European examples from the 15th and 16th centuries
still stand today — a testament to the method's durability when protected
from prolonged moisture exposure.

Wattle and daub is strictly an infill material, not a structural system.
It requires a timber frame, post-and-beam structure, or stud wall to carry
loads. The panels provide enclosure, insulation, and thermal mass but
support no weight beyond their own.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Timber Frame | Wattle and Daub |
|--------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Role** | Structural skeleton | Infill panels |
| **Materials** | Large timbers, pegs | Stakes, withies, clay, sand, straw |
| **Skill Level** | Advanced joinery | Beginner-friendly |
| **Cost** | High (timber, tools) | Very low (local materials) |
| **Labor Time** | Weeks to months | Days per panel |
| **Lifespan** | 300+ years | 100+ years (if protected) |
| **Thermal Mass** | Low (wood) | High (earthen daub) |
| **Insulation** | Poor alone | Moderate |
| **Load-Bearing** | Yes (all loads) | No (infill only) |
| **Best Climate** | All climates | Temperate, dry |

## When to Choose Timber Frame

Choose timber framing when you need a durable, load-bearing structure that
will stand for centuries. It is the right choice for primary residences,
barns, and any building where structural independence from wall materials
matters. Timber frames pair well with any infill — straw bale, hempcrete,
cob, or wattle and daub — giving builders flexibility to change wall
systems over the building's life.

Timber framing is also ideal when you want large, open interior spaces
without posts interrupting the floor plan. The joinery tradition creates
buildings of exceptional beauty, with exposed timbers serving as both
structure and ornament. However, the skill requirement is significant: plan
to study joinery, invest in specialized tools (framing chisels, beam saws,
boring machines), and allow weeks or months for frame cutting and raising.

## When to Choose Wattle and Daub

Choose wattle and daub when you need low-cost, locally sourced wall panels
for an existing structure. The technique excels for small buildings, garden
walls, interior partitions, and repair of historic structures. The learning
curve is gentle — a motivated beginner can produce serviceable panels after
a day of practice.

Wattle and daub is also the right choice when site-harvested materials are
a priority. Hazel, willow, and bamboo can be coppiced sustainably, and
the clay subsoil often comes from the building site itself. This makes it
one of the most environmentally gentle building methods, with near-zero
embodied energy. In permaculture contexts, living willow structures can
even serve as both wattle source and windbreak.

## Using Both Together

The historical pairing of timber frames with wattle-and-daub infill remains
one of the most effective natural building approaches. The timber frame
provides the structural skeleton, while wattle-and-daub panels fill the
bays between posts and braces with insulating, breathable, thermally
massive walls. This combination dominated European building for centuries
and continues to produce comfortable, durable, beautiful structures.

Modern builders often add a lime plaster finish over the daub for weather
protection, and may incorporate additional insulation (light straw-clay or
hempcrete) in the wall cavity for colder climates. The timber frame's
flexibility means the infill strategy can vary from panel to panel — heavy
wattle and daub on the south side for thermal mass, lighter straw-clay on
the north for insulation.
