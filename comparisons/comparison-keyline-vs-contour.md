## Keyline Design vs Contour Line Water Management

## Overview
Keyline design and contour line water management are two approaches to
managing water movement across landscapes using topographic features. Keyline,
developed by P.A. Yeomans in 1950s Australia and adopted widely in permaculture
by Bill Mollison, uses a specific geometric reference line to systematically
redistribute water from valleys to ridges. Contour line management, central to
[[contour-line-water-management]]

Contour line water management, as practiced by Sepp Holzer, is an
observational approach that reads natural landscape patterns to identify where
water retention features should be placed. Rather than imposing a geometric
system onto the land, the practitioner studies how water has shaped the terrain
over millennia, identifies natural impermeable layers, and places water bodies
and swales where the landscape already indicates they belong. The emphasis is on
cooperation with existing natural patterns rather than engineering solutions
imposed from outside.

The approach begins with aerial observation or contour maps to understand the
broader landscape context: how water flows, where it accumulates, and what
natural formations exist. From this understanding, the practitioner identifies
sites for lakes, ponds, and swales that work with gravity and existing geology.
The principle is that water has already formed the landscape with impermeable
layers at certain depths, and working with these natural formations saves the
need for artificial waterproofing. A lake placed along a contour line where
natural clay layers exist will hold water without synthetic liners.

Holzer's contour approach extends beyond water retention to encompass

## Hybrid Approaches
Many permaculture designers combine elements of both systems to capitalize on
their complementary strengths. Keyline geometry provides the systematic
framework for broad-scale water planning and cultivation patterns, while
Holzer's observational approach refines placement of specific features like
ponds and terraces based on local conditions. A common integration is using
keyline patterns for cultivation and tree-planting layouts while using contour
observation to site individual water bodies where geology supports them. Both
systems share the fundamental principle that water should be spread and
infiltrated across the landscape rather than concentrated and discharged. Mark
Shepard's Restoration Agriculture explicitly combines keyline cultivation
