## Bamboo Construction vs Timber Frame

## Overview
Bamboo construction uses rapidly renewable bamboo grass as a structural
material, joined with lashings, bolts, and specialized connections. Timber
framing uses large-dimension wood timbers joined with traditional wooden
joinery (mortise and tenon, dovetails) secured by wooden pegs. Both create
beautiful, durable structures with exposed natural materials, but they differ
in material availability, joinery methods, climate suitability, renewal rate,
and cultural tradition. The choice between them depends heavily on local
resources, climate, building codes, and aesthetic preferences.

## Bamboo Construction
[[query-how-do-i-choose-a-natural-building-method-for-my-climate]] using large timbers joined
with wooden connections rather than nails or metal fasteners. The craft has
been practiced for thousands of years across European, Asian, and American
building traditions. Timber frames pair naturally with natural infill
materials like straw bales, light straw-clay, and hempcrete for walls that
provide insulation while the timber frame provides the structural skeleton.

Wood species selection prioritizes locally available timber: oak (traditional,
extremely strong), Douglas fir (strong, straight-grained), eastern white pine
(light, easy to work), hemlock (strong, rot resistant), and cedar (naturally
rot resistant). Timbers are specified by rough-sawn dimensions, with posts
typically 6x6 to 8x8 and beams 6x8 to 8x12. Green timber is easier to cut
but shrinks as it dries; air-dried timber (1+ year) is ideal for precision
joinery work that produces tight, long-lasting joints.

Essential joinery includes the mortise and tenon (the fundamental joint, with
through, blind, and shouldered variations), the dovetail (wedge-shaped for
tension connections), scarf joints (end-to-end for longer members), and the
birdsmouth (rafter-to-plate notch). All joints are secured with wooden pegs
(trunnels) rather than nails. The exposed timber frame becomes an
architectural feature, with timbers often left visible inside the building.
Timber frames declined with the introduction of stick framing in the mid-1800s
but have seen strong revival since the 1970s.
