## Overview

Fermented Plant Juice (FPJ) and Fermented Fruit Juice (FFJ) are two foundational bionutrient preparations in natural farming that share a common fermentation
method but differ in their source materials, the primary growth hormones or nutrients they deliver, and their ideal application windows across the crop cycle.
Both use crude sugar okorean-natural-farmingmotic extraction and microbial fermentation, yet they serve complementary roles that together form a core part of
the Korean Natural Farming and bionutrient systems pioneered by Han Kyu Cho and Gil Carandang.

Understanding the distinction between FPJ and FFJ is essential for practitioners who want to apply the right input at the right stage of plant development.
While the two preparations are often discussed together and can even be combined, they originate from fundamentally different plant parts and deliver different
classes of bioactive compounds. FPJ draws on the hormonal richness of vegetative growth, while FFJ taps the nutritional density of reproductive structures. This
comparison examines their preparation, composition, application, and strategic use.

## Fermented Plant Juice (FPJ)

[[bionutrients-lab-fermented-plant-juice-fpj]]

Fermented Plant Juice is produced from fast-growing plant materials — particularly young shoots, axillary buds, and vigorous vegetative parts of plants. The
core principle behind FPJ is that rapidly growing plants naturally concentrate growth hormones, including gibberellins (which promote stem elongation, seed
germination, and flowering), auxins (which stimulate root development, cell elongation, and apical dominance), and cytokinins (which promote cell division,
delay leaf senescence, and stimulate shoot formation). By fermenting these materials, those hormones are extracted into a bioavailable liquid form that can be
applied to crops to stimulate similar growth responses.

Typical FPJ source materials include kangkong (water spinach), kelp or seaweed, mugwort, and the axillary buds of cucurbits such as cucumber, squash, and
watermelon. Selection follows a simple logic: choose the plant whose growth habit matches the outcome you want to encourage. Kangkong, for example, is prized
because its extremely rapid vegetative growth signals high auxin and gibberellin content. Kelp provides the same rapid-growth signature from a marine
environment, plus trace elements not found in terrestrial plants. The key selection criteria are health and vigor of the source plant, preference for growing
tips and young shoots where hormone concentration is highest, and use of plants growing in their natural environment.

The general FPJ fermentation process involves chopping the plant material as thinly as possible to maximize surface area, mixing with one-third crude sugar or
molasses by weight, placing in a container with a 50 to 75 percent air gap, covering loosely to allow gas exchange, and fermenting for at least one week.
Readiness is indicated by mold formation and a sweet, sour, or alcoholic aroma. The liquid is then strained and diluted for application.

FPJ is most effective during the vegetative growth phase. It is applied as a foliar spray or soil drench at a standaindigenous-microorganisms20 to 1:500,
depending on the potency of the source material. Natural farmers commonly combine FPJ with beneficial indigenous microorganisms (BIM) and other bionutrients
such as fish amino acids and calcium phosphate for a synergistic effect that outperforms any single input alone.

## Fermented Fruit Juice (FFJ)

[[bionutrients-lab-fermented-fruit-juice-ffj]]

Fermented Fruit Juice is produced from ripe fruits using the same basic fermentation protocol: chopping the fruit, mixing with one-third crude sugar or molasses
by weight, and leaving to ferment with adequate air space in a loosely covered container. Where FPJ targets growth hormones extracted from vegetative plant
parts, FFJ targets the broader nutritional profile of the fruit itself, including enzymes (such as papain from papaya), vitamins (beta-carotene, vitamin C),
minerals, and especially potassium, which is critical for flowering and fruiting.

The model FFJ preparation uses papaya, valued for its proteolytic enzyme content and rich vitamin profile. The Banana-Squash-Papaya (BSP) fermented extract is
another widely used combination, noted for high potassium levels that support reproductive growth stages. In the Philippines, organic mango farmers use seaweed
extract alongside potassium-rich fruit ferments to induce flowering, achieving results comparable to conventional potassium nitrate applications but without
synthetic chemicals.

Natural farmers select fruits based on the growth stage they wish to promote: nitrogen-rich young green fruits (tomato, cucumber) for vegetative growth, and
potassium-rich fruits (banana, papaya, squash, mango) for flowering and fruiting. The Nutrioperiodism framework (Yasushi Inoue, 1930s) formalizes this approach,
mapping specific nutrients to specific developmental windows and providing a structured guide for input timing.

FFJ has broader multi-purpose applications than FPJ. Beyond foliar spray and sobokashich, FFJ can be added to compost piles to boost microbial activity, used as
an animal feed or water supplement, and incorporated into bokashi-style designer composts where potassium enrichment is the goal. The general potency hierarchy
— seed most potent, then fruit, then leaf — means seed-based ferments require a more dilute 1:1000 application rate versus the standard 1:500.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | FPJ | FFJ |
|--------|-----|-----|
| Source material | Fast-growing plants, shoots, axillary buds | Ripe fruits (papaya, banana, squash, mango) |
| Primary function | Growth hormones (auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins) | Enzymes, vitamins, minerals, potassium |
| Best growth stage | Vegetative growth phase | Flowering and fruiting phase |
| Selection logic | Match desired growth habit to source plant | Match fruit nutritional profile to crop need |
| Typical dilution | 1:20 to 1:500 | 1:500 standard; 1:1000 for seed-based |
| Application methods | Foliar spray, soil drench | Foliar spray, soil drench, compost additive, animal supplement |
| Multi-purpose use | Crop-focused | Crop, compost, and livestock applications |
| Key model recipe | Kangkong fermented extract | Papaya fermented extract; BSP combination |
| Growth hormones | High | Low to moderate |
| Potassium content | Low to moderate | High (especially banana and squash) |
| Enzyme content | Moderate | High (papain, amylase, etc.) |
| Availability | Year-round in tropics; seasonal in temperate | Seasonal, tied to fruiting periods |
| Sugar ratio | 1:3 sugar to plant material by weight | 1:3 sugar to fruit by weight |

## Preparation Similarities and Differences

Both FPJ and FFJ follow the same fundamental fermentation protocol, which makes them straightforward to learn as a pair. The shared steps include thin chopping
to maximize surface area, the one-third sugar ratio by weight, the 50 to 75 percent air gap requirement, loose covering for gas exchange, and a minimum one-week
fermentation period. Readiness indicators are identical: mold formation and a characteristic sweet-sour-alcoholic aroma. Both preparations benefit from the same
storage conditions in cool, dark locations that slow further fermentation.

The primary difference in preparation is material handling. FPJ materials are generally leafy and may require less chopping effort, while fruits with thick
skins or rinds may need more aggressive preparation. FPJ materials are typically harvested in the early morning when plant sugar content is highest, whereas FFJ
fruits should be fully ripe to maximize nutrient density. An important shared note: neither material should be washed before fermentation, as surface
microorganisms on the plant or fruit skin contribute to the fermentation process.

For materials with low moisture content in either category, adding enough water to moisten all materials before fermenting is recommended. This ensures the
sugar can dissolve and osmotic extraction can proceed effectively throughout the mixture.

## When to Choose FPJ

Choose FPJ when your crops are in the vegetative stage and you want to maximize leaf growth, stem elongation, root development, or overall vigor. FPJ is ideal
early in the season when seedlings and transplants need a hormonal boost to establish quickly and develop strong root systems. It is also the better choice when
your available local materials are predominantly leafy greens, herbs, or aquatic plants rather than fruits.

Because FPJ source plants are often available year-round in tropical and subtropical climates, it can serve as a reliable standby input whenever a rapid
vegetative response is needed. Farmers working with pastures, leafy vegetables, or crops in early establishment will find FPJ the more directly useful
preparation. The experimental approach encouraged in natural farming means that any locally abundant fast-growing plant can be tested as FPJ material, making it
accessible even in temperate regions where tropical fruits for FFJ may not be readily available.

## When to Choose FFJ

Choose FFJ when crops are transitioning into reproductive growth — budding, flowering, or fruit set — and potassium-rich nutrition will support that shift. FFJ
is the preferred input for fruit-bearing vegetables, orchard crops, and any situation where improving fruit quality, sweetness, or yield is the priority. Its
wider range of applications also makes it the better choice for farmers who want a single fermentation product that can serve crops, compost, and livestock
simultaneously.

The Nutrioperiodism framework provides guidance on selecting fruits for each growth stage: nitrogen-rich young green fruits during vegetative growth, balanced
mineral-rich fruits during the changeover period, and potassium-rich fruits like banana and papaya during flowering and fruiting. This targeted approach —
providing the right nutrient at the right developmental stage rather than blanket applications — is a core principle that distinguishes natural farming from
conventional input strategies. FFJ's versatility also makes it valuable for integrated farming systems where crop, compost, and animal health are managed as
interconnected components.

## Hybrid Approaches

Many natural farmers combine FPJ and FFJ rather than choosing one exclusively. The standard bionutrient formula includes both alongside BIM, fish amino acid,
brown rice vinegar, and OHN. This combination leverages FPJ's growth hormones for early vegetative push and FFJ's potassium and enzymes for the later
reproductive phase.

In practice, a farmer might apply FPJ-dominant sprays during weeks one through four of crop growth, then shift to FFJ-dominant sprays as flowering approaches.
The two inputs can also be tank-mixed in a single application for crops at the changeover stage between vegetative and reproductive growth, providing a balanced
nutrient and hormonal signal that smooths the transition. Some practitioners create blended ferments that combine both plant and fruit materials in a single
batch, though this sacrifices some of the precision of stage-specific targeting.

For farmers seeking a simplified approach, starting with FPJ for general vegetative health and adding FFJ only when flowering or fruiting support is needed can
reduce the number of concurrent fermentation batches while still capturing the benefits of both preparations. The key is observation: natural farming encourages
adjusting dilution rates, material selection, and application frequency based on plant response rather than rigid recipes.
