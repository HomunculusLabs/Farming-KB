# Constructed Wetlands vs Bioswales

Constructed wetlands and bioswales are two nature-based solutions for managing stormwater and treating polluted water. While both rely on vegetation, soil media, and biological processes, they differ significantly in design, capacity, and application. This comparison examines their strengths, limitations, and ideal use contexts.

## Overview

### Constructed Wetlands

Constructed wetlands are engineered systems designed to mimic natural wetland ecosystems. They use shallow pools, saturated substrates, and emergent aquatic vegetation to treat water through physical filtration, chemical adsorption, and microbial metabolism. Two primary types exist: **free-water surface (FWS)** wetlands, where water flows above the substrate, and **subsurface flow (SSF)** wetlands, where water moves through a gravel or sand bed beneath the surface. SSF systems are more common in urban settings because they avoid standing water and reduce mosquito habitat.

### Bioswales

Bioswales are linear, vegetated channels that convey and treat stormwater runoff along slopes, roadsides, or parking lot perimeters. Water flows through a graded channel filled with engineered soil media and planted with grasses, perennials, or shrubs. As runoff moves downslope, sediment settles, pollutants adsorb to soil particles, and plant roots uptake nutrients. Bioswales are primarily conveyance systems with incidental treatment, whereas constructed wetlands are dedicated treatment systems.

## Design and Construction

| Parameter | Constructed Wetlands | Bioswales |
|-----------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Typical depth** | 0.3–0.6 m (SSF); 0.15–0.5 m water (FWS) | 0.3–1.2 m total depth |
| **Slope** | Nearly flat (< 1%); relies on level spreading | 1–5% longitudinal slope |
| **Media** | Gravel, sand, or soil (SSF); native substrate (FWS) | Engineered bioretention soil mix (sand, compost, topsoil) |
| **Liner** | Often required (clay or geomembrane) | Rarely required |
| **Residence time** | 2–10 days | Minutes to hours |
| **Footprint** | Large (5–10% of contributing drainage area) | Narrow, linear (follows site contours) |

Constructed wetlands require careful grading to maintain uniform water distribution across the treatment bed. They typically include inlet structures, level spreaders, and outlet controls to regulate water depth. Bioswales are simpler to construct, following natural or cut slopes with check dams to slow flow and increase contact time.

## Water Treatment Performance

| Pollutant | Constructed Wetlands | Bioswales |
|-----------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Total suspended solids** | 70–90% removal | 60–85% removal |
| **Nitrogen (total)** | 40–70% removal | 20–50% removal |
| **Phosphorus (total)** | 50–80% removal | 40–70% removal |
| **Pathogens (E. coli)** | 70–99% removal | 50–80% removal |
| **Heavy metals** | 60–90% removal | 40–75% removal |
| **Hydrocarbons** | 70–95% removal | 50–80% removal |

Constructed wetlands consistently outperform bioswales across most pollutant categories due to their longer residence time, anaerobic zones that support denitrification, and greater biological activity. Bioswales provide effective first-flush treatment and sediment capture but have limited capacity for dissolved pollutant removal.

## Flow Capacity and Stormwater Handling

Bioswales are designed as **conveyance features** and can handle high-intensity storm events with peak flow rates managed through channel geometry and check dams. They are well-suited to capturing runoff from impervious surfaces and directing it to downstream systems. Constructed wetlands function as **storage and treatment features**, attenuating peak flows through ponding and slow release. They handle smaller, distributed flows more effectively than large storm surges and can be designed with forebays to capture sediment before it reaches the treatment zone.

## Vegetation Requirements

Constructed wetlands require emergent aquatic species tolerant of saturated or inundated conditions. Common choices include *[[phragmites-australis]]* (reed canary grass), *Scirpus* spp. (bulrushes), *Juncus* spp. (rushes), and *Iris versicolor* (blue flag iris). Plant density targets of 2–4 plants per square meter ensure adequate root-zone treatment.

Bioswales use a broader palette of upland and mesic species. Native grasses (*[[echinacea-purpurea]]*, *Solidago* spp.) are preferred for [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]], pollutant uptake, and [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]]. Trees and shrubs may be included along swale margins for canopy interception.

## Maintenance and Long-Term Operation

| Aspect | Constructed Wetlands | Bioswales |
|--------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Inspection frequency** | Monthly (first year); quarterly thereafter | After major storms; quarterly |
| **Vegetation management** | Harvest annually; control invasive species | Mow 2–4×/year; replant bare areas |
| **Sediment removal** | Every 5–15 years (forebay) | Every 3–10 years |
| **Media replacement** | Rarely needed if properly designed | Every 10–20 years |
| **Estimated annual cost** | $0.10–0.30/m²/year | $0.05–0.15/m²/year |

Constructed wetlands require more intensive monitoring of water levels, outlet structures, and vegetation health, but their longer design life (25–50 years) offsets higher maintenance costs. Bioswales need regular mowing and erosion repair but are generally lower maintenance overall.

## Space Requirements and Site Constraints

Space availability is often the deciding factor. Constructed wetlands demand significant flat or gently graded land, typically 5–10% of the contributing drainage area. They are impractical on constrained urban sites but excel where land is available, such as on the periphery of developments, in parkland, or in rural settings. Bioswales fit within linear corridors—road rights-of-way, parking lot medians, and property boundaries—making them adaptable to dense development patterns.

## Cost Comparison

| Cost Category | Constructed Wetlands | Bioswales |
|---------------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Installation (per m²)** | $30–$150 | $15–$60 |
| **Installation (per hectare of drainage)** | $15,000–$75,000 | $5,000–$25,000 |
| **Annual maintenance** | Moderate to high | Low to moderate |
| **Design life** | 25–50 years | 15–30 years |

Costs vary widely by region, media specifications, and site complexity. Constructed wetlands are more expensive per unit area but treat larger volumes more thoroughly. Bioswales offer a low-cost entry point for distributed stormwater management.

## Climate and Seasonal Considerations

Both systems are sensitive to climate. Constructed wetlands in cold climates require extended residence times to compensate for reduced microbial activity in winter; FWS wetlands may freeze, shifting treatment loads to SSF zones. In arid climates, supplemental water or drought-tolerant species are necessary during dry seasons. Bioswales are vulnerable to channel erosion during intense storms and may go dormant during extended dry periods, losing biological treatment capacity until vegetation recovers.

## Best Use Cases

**Choose constructed wetlands when:**
- Treating greywater, wastewater effluent, or agricultural runoff
- High nutrient or pathogen removal is required
- Sufficient land area is available
- Long residence time and storage are beneficial
- Regulatory standards demand consistent water quality

**Choose bioswales when:**
- Managing concentrated linear runoff (roads, parking lots)
- Space is limited to corridors or edges
- Primary goals are sediment capture and flow conveyance
- Budget constraints favor lower-cost solutions
- Rapid installation is needed

## Regulatory and Permitting Considerations

Constructed wetlands treating domestic wastewater or industrial effluent typically require permits under Clean Water Act Section 402 or equivalent regional frameworks. Design standards may prescribe minimum detention times, media specifications, and monitoring protocols. Stormwater-only constructed wetlands may qualify as best management practices (BMPs) under municipal separate storm sewer system (MS4) permits, with less stringent requirements. Bioswales are widely accepted as green infrastructure BMPs and often face a simpler permitting pathway, though jurisdictions increasingly require pretreatment or bypass structures to protect against high-flow events that could scour vegetation or overload the media.

## Complementary Use in Treatment Trains

The most effective stormwater strategies combine both systems in a **treatment train**. Bioswales serve as the first line of defense, capturing sediment, slowing flow, and providing initial pollutant removal. Pre-treated water then enters a constructed wetland for advanced nutrient and pathogen removal. This sequence protects the wetland from clogging, extends its service life, and maximizes overall treatment efficiency. A typical train might include: **source controls → bioswales → forebay → constructed wetland → receiving water body**.

## Summary

Constructed wetlands and bioswales address overlapping but distinct niches in stormwater and water treatment. Wetlands excel at thorough, multi-pollutant treatment with long residence times and are best suited where land is available and water quality targets are demanding. Bioswales provide cost-effective, space-efficient conveyance and first-flush treatment, making them ideal for distributed urban applications. The most robust designs leverage both in treatment trains, using bioswales for upstream protection and constructed wetlands for downstream polishing—achieving results neither system could deliver alone.
