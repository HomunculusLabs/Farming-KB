# Bokashi vs Hot Composting

## Overview

Bokashi and hot composting represent two fundamentally different approaches to
converting organic waste into soil-building amendments. Bokashi is an anaerobic
fermentation process that "pickles" food scraps with [[hot-composting-method]]

Hot composting is a managed aerobic process that harnesses thermophilic bacteria
to rapidly decompose organic materials at temperatures between 130 and 160
degrees Fahrenheit. By carefully balancing carbon-rich browns and nitrogen-rich
greens at a ratio of roughly 25 to 30:1, maintaining 50 to 60 percent moisture,
and turning the pile every three to five days, a well-managed hot compost system
produces finished compost in four to six weeks — compared to six to twelve
months for passive cold composting.

## When to Choose Hot Composting

Choose hot composting when you have ample space, generate large volumes of yard
and garden waste, and need finished compost quickly. Farms and larger homesteads
with significant green and brown material streams benefit from hot composting's
high throughput and rapid turnaround. Operations that must destroy pathogens —
such as those composting manure, diseased plant material, or invasive weed seeds
— require the thermophilic temperatures only hot composting provides. Hot
composting is also the better choice when you want a low-cost system that uses
only on-site materials without purchasing inoculants.

## Hybrid Approaches

Many homesteaders combine both methods effectively. Bokashi processes kitchen
food scraps (including proteins) in a compact indoor system, while hot composting
handles large volumes of yard waste, crop residues, and clean plant material
outdoors. The bokashi pre-compost can be buried directly into hot compost piles
to accelerate decomposition and add diverse organic matter. This division of
labor lets each method handle the waste streams it processes best, maximizing
total waste diversion while producing both liquid fertility (bokashi tea) and
bulk finished compost.
