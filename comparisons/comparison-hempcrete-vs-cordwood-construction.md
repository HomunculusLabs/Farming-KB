# Hempcrete vs Cordwood Construction

## Overview

Hempcrete and cordwood are two distinctive natural building methods that
prioritize sustainability, low embodied energy, and healthy indoor
environments. Both rely on plant-based materials combined with mineral
binders, yet they differ fundamentally in their structural approach,
material sourcing, and building process. Hempcrete uses hemp hurd mixed
with lime binder as a non-structural insulating infill, while cordwood
places short log segments in mortar to create thick, self-supporting
walls. Understanding their differences helps builders choose the right
system for climate, budget, and available materials.

## Hempcrete
[[hempcrete-and-hemp-lime-construction]]

Hempcrete is a biocomposite material made from the woody core of the
hemp plant (hurd or shiv) mixed with a lime-based binder and water. It
is not a structural material — it serves as insulating infill around a
timber frame or other load-bearing structure. Developed in France in the
1990s, hempcrete has gained prominence as one of the few building
materials that can genuinely claim to be carbon-negative. The hemp plant
sequesters CO2 during its rapid 100-to-120-day growth cycle, and the
lime binder continues absorbing carbon dioxide through carbonation for
decades after construction.

Hempcrete walls offer excellent thermal insulation (R-value roughly 2.1
per inch), outstanding moisture regulation, and high vapor permeability.
The material is lightweight — roughly one-seventh the density of
concrete — and creates a healthy indoor environment by buffering
humidity and resisting mold. Hempcrete is typically cast in place using
temporary formwork or sprayed onto structural frames, and it requires
several weeks to dry and begin hardening before plaster finishes can be
applied. The lime binder continues to carbonate and strengthen over
time, meaning a hempcrete wall actually improves with age.

## Cordwood Construction
[[cordwood-construction]]

Cordwood construction (also called cordwood masonry or stackwall) is a
building technique where short logs — typically 16 to 24 inches long —
are laid in a bed of mortar, log-end facing outward, to create thick
walls. The technique has been practiced for over a thousand years across
Europe and was widely used by pioneer settlers in North America during
the 1800s. It experienced a revival during the 1970s alternative
building movement and remains popular among homesteaders and natural
builders.

Cordwood is a DIY-friendly method that requires no special equipment or
advanced skills. The walls combine the insulating value of wood (R-1.0
per inch) with the thermal mass of mortar, producing thick walls with
high total R-values. Softwoods like cedar, pine, and spruce are
preferred because they dry faster and shrink less. Builders must
carefully debark, split, and season logs for at least six months before
use. The distinctive log-end patterns visible on interior and exterior
faces give cordwood buildings their signature aesthetic appeal.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Hempcrete | Cordwood |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Structural role** | Non-structural infill | Can be load-bearing |
| **Primary material** | Hemp hurd + lime binder | Short logs + mortar |
| **R-value per inch** | ~2.1 | ~1.0 (wood) + thermal mass |
| **Wall thickness** | 8–16 inches typical | 16–24 inches typical |
| **Carbon impact** | Carbon-negative | Low carbon, wood stores CO2 |
| **DIY difficulty** | Moderate (formwork skills) | Easy to moderate |
| **Material sourcing** | Hemp hurd may require shipping | Often locally sourced wood |
| **Moisture behavior** | Highly breathable, buffering | Breathable, needs good overhangs |
| **Finish required** | Lime plaster both sides | Optional; mortar joints exposed |
| **Cure time** | Weeks to months | Mortar sets in days |
| **Cost** | Moderate to high | Low to moderate |
| **Aesthetic** | Smooth plastered walls | Rustic log-end patterns |

## When to Choose Hempcrete

Hempcrete excels in projects where energy performance and indoor air
quality are top priorities. Its superior R-value per inch means thinner
walls achieve comparable insulation, saving floor space in compact
designs. The material's remarkable ability to buffer humidity makes it
ideal for damp or humid climates where condensation and mold are
concerns. Hempcrete is also the stronger choice when carbon impact is a
driving design factor — the combination of biological carbon
sequestration during hemp growth and ongoing lime carbonation creates a
genuinely carbon-negative wall assembly.

Hempcrete works best when paired with a structural frame — typically
timber — and is well-suited to new construction where formwork casting
or spraying can be planned into the build schedule. It is less suited to
remote or off-grid sites where shipping hemp hurd and lime binder would
be costly. The material's long cure time (weeks before plaster can be
applied) requires patience and scheduling flexibility.

## When to Choose Cordwood

Cordwood construction shines when builders have access to abundant wood
resources — ideally from their own land or nearby forestry operations.
The technique transforms short, otherwise low-value log sections into
beautiful, high-mass walls at very low cost. Its DIY accessibility
makes it one of the most approachable natural building methods for
owner-builders with basic carpentry skills and patience.

Cordwood is particularly well-suited to cold climates where thick walls
provide both insulation and significant thermal mass. The technique
works well for small to medium-sized buildings including cabins, studios,
and workshops. However, cordwood demands careful wood preparation —
logs must be debarked, split, and seasoned below 20% moisture content
for at least six months. Building with wet wood leads to shrinkage gaps
and mortar separation. Generous roof overhangs are essential to protect
the log ends from persistent moisture exposure.

## Decision Guide

| Your Situation | Best Choice |
|----------------|-------------|
| Hot, humid climate | Hempcrete (superior moisture buffering) |
| Cold climate with free wood | Cordwood (thick walls, low cost) |
| Prioritizing carbon footprint | Hempcrete (carbon-negative) |
| Owner-builder on tight budget | Cordwood (uses waste wood) |
| Urban or permitted build | Hempcrete (more code acceptance) |
| Off-grid remote site | Cordwood (local materials) |
| Seeking modern plastered aesthetic | Hempcrete |
| Seeking rustic handcrafted look | Cordwood |
| Need load-bearing walls without frame | Cordwood |
