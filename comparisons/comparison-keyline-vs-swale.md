## Keyline Design vs Swale Systems

## Overview

Keyline design and swale systems are two of the most widely used earthwork
strategies for managing water on agricultural and permaculture landscapes.

While both aim to capture, store, and redistribute water across a site, they
differ fundamentally in their design philosophy, scale of implementation, and
underlying mechanisms of [[david-holmgren]].

Both approaches recognize that water is the primary driver of landscape health
and that thoughtful management of water movement can reverse degradation, build
soil, increase biological productivity, and create lasting drought resilience.

| Integration | Roads, trees, buildings, subdivision | Food forests and tree crops |

## When to Choose Keyline Design

Choose keyline design when managing a large agricultural property where even
water distribution across hundreds of acres is the primary goal. It is the
superior choice when valley floors are waterlogged while ridge tops remain dry
and when deep soil compaction limits pasture productivity.

Keyline cultivation can be implemented gradually as part of normal farm
operations, making it practical for working farms that cannot take land out of
production for major earthworks installation.

## When to Choose Swale Systems

Choose swales when the goal is to establish tree crops, food forests, or
perennial plantings on sloped land where concentrated water infiltration at
specific locations is needed.

Swales are ideal for small properties, urban sites, and homesteads where
broadacre machinery is impractical. They are also the better choice for steep,
erosion-prone hillsides where concentrated runoff needs to be broken up.

## Hybrid Approaches

Many experienced permaculture designers combine elements of both systems. A
common hybrid uses keyline pattern cultivation for broad water redistribution
while installing swales at priority planting zones for intensive tree
establishment.

On degraded broadacre land, keyline ripping may be performed first to restore
deep infiltration capacity, followed by swale installation where intensive food
production is planned.
