# Composting Toilets vs Greywater Systems

## Overview
Composting toilets and greywater systems are complementary permaculture
technologies that together close the nutrient and water loops broken by
conventional waste infrastructure. Composting toilets process human waste
(blackwater) into safe compost, returning nutrients to the soil. Greywater
systems treat and reuse wastewater from sinks, showers, bathtubs, and washing
machines for landscape irrigation. Both technologies reduce pollution and
recover valuable resources, but they address different waste streams with
different biological processes and face different regulatory landscapes.

## Composting Toilets
[[greywater-systems-detailed]]
Greywater systems collect, treat, and reuse wastewater from household sources
other than toilets: sinks, showers, bathtubs, and washing machines. A well-
designed system reduces total household water consumption by 30 to 50% and
returns nutrient-laden water to the landscape for irrigation. Different
sources produce greywater of varying quality: bathroom sinks and showers
produce low-contaminant water that is easy to treat, while kitchen sinks
produce high-contaminant water with grease and food solids that many systems
exclude.

The branched drain system, developed by Art Ludwig, is the simplest and most
reliable approach. Wastewater flows by gravity through branching PVC pipes,
each terminating in a mulch basin filled with [[fukuoka-textdoc-sawdust-wood-chips-orchard-soil-acidification-experiment]]. No pumps, filters,
or electricity are needed. For sites without adequate slope, a surge tank with
pump distributes water to a larger area. [[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]] offer the
highest treatment level, using aquatic plants and gravel media to reduce BOD
by 85 to 95%, TSS by 80 to 90%, and nitrogen by 40 to 60%.

Product selection critically affects greywater quality. Plant-based
biodegradable soaps without boron, bleach, sodium-based water softeners,
petroleum-based surfactants, or artificial fragrances are essential. Boron
accumulates in soil and is toxic to plants; bleach kills beneficial soil
organisms; sodium softeners damage soil structure. Mulch basins are the heart
of most systems: shallow depressions filled with coarse wood chips that filter
particles, provide microbial treatment surfaces, and gradually release water
into the root zone.

Legal considerations vary enormously by jurisdiction. Some states actively
encourage greywater reuse, while others require permits, professional design,
or prohibit it entirely. Most jurisdictions require systems to be entirely
subsurface with minimum setbacks from property lines, wells, and surface
water. The simplest legal approach is a small branched drain system with no
pump.

## Key Differences
| Aspect | Composting Toilets | Greywater Systems |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Waste stream | Blackwater (feces, urine) | Greywater (sinks, showers, laundry) |
| Primary output | Compost/fertilizer | Irrigation water |
| Biological process | Aerobic decomposition | Soil and plant filtration |
| Pathogen risk | High initially; reduced over 6 to 12 months | Low (no fecal contamination) |
| Regulatory hurdles | Often significant (blackwater codes) | Increasingly permitted for simple systems |
| Space requirement | Small (indoor or small outdoor) | Moderate (mulch basins, wetland area) |
| Maintenance | Add cover material, manage moisture, harvest compost | Maintain mulch basins, clean filters, check pipes |
| Water savings | Eliminates flush water (13,000 liters/year/toilet) | Reduces total water use by 30 to 50% |
| Nutrient recovery | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium to soil | Nutrients in water to landscape plants |
| Integration | Closes nutrient loop (waste to fertilizer) | Closes water loop (wastewater to irrigation) |
| Best For | Off-grid living, [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] | Water conservation, landscape irrigation |

## When to Choose Composting Toilets
Composting toilets are essential for off-grid homes, rural homesteads, and
eco-villages where conventional sewer connections are unavailable. They are
also valuable for anyone who wants to close the nutrient cycle and convert
human waste into a resource. The simplest dry toilet systems require minimal
investment and can be implemented immediately. For permaculture designs aiming
for complete on-site waste cycling, composting toilets are a non-negotiable
component that eliminates the largest source of water pollution in most
households.

## When to Choose Greywater Systems
Greywater systems are appropriate for any household that wants to reduce water
consumption and irrigate landscape plants with otherwise wasted water. The
simplest branched drain systems require no electricity, no pumps, and minimal
maintenance, making them accessible to homeowners with basic plumbing skills.
Greywater is particularly valuable in drought-prone regions where every gallon
of water saved matters. In suburban and urban settings where composting
toilets face regulatory barriers, greywater reuse is often the more practical
first step toward sustainable waste management.

## Hybrid Approaches
The most complete waste management systems combine both technologies.
Composting toilets handle blackwater while greywater from sinks, showers, and
laundry irrigates [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]] and perennial plantings. Together, they
eliminate the need for conventional sewage infrastructure entirely and close
both the water and nutrient cycles on-site. The compost feeds the same trees
that the greywater irrigates, completing a closed-loop system that Bill
Mollison placed at the center of his permaculture waste-free design
philosophy.
