## Rabbit Husbandry vs Chicken Keeping

## Overview

Rabbits and chickens are the two most space-efficient livestock options for
homesteaders seeking meat production, but they occupy very different niches in a
homestead system. Chickens are dual-purpose producers (eggs and meat) with the
lowest barrier to entry of any livestock. Rabbits are specialized meat producers
with unmatched space efficiency and feed conversion, producing high-quality lean
protein in a fraction of the space required by any other meat animal. The choice
between them shapes daily management routines, processing requirements, and how
the homestead integrates livestock into its broader food production system.

The infrastructure for chicken keeping is well-developed and flexible. Coop
designs range from simple DIY structures to prefabricated units, and the deep
litter method reduces cleaning frequency while generating compost. Chicken
tractors add mobility, allowing birds to fertilize specific garden areas while
remaining protected from predators. Chickens integrate naturally into
[[permaculture-zone-0-house-design]] 2, cycling nutrients between garden, compost pile, and flock.
The knowledge base is enormous — breed selection, feeding, health management,
and housing are all well-documented with decades of community experience.

The challenges are familiar to any poultry keeper. Predator pressure is
relentless: raccoons, hawks, foxes, weasels, owls, and dogs all target
chickens, requiring secure housing and vigilance. [[pastured-egg-production]] declines after
the first year and annual molts cause six to twelve week laying pauses. Health
management includes monitoring for mites, lice, respiratory infections, egg
binding, and bumblefoot. Roosters (needed for flock reproduction) add noise
that may violate local ordinances. Feed costs represent the largest ongoing
expense, though supplementary foraging and food waste diversion reduce the bill.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Rabbits | Chickens |
|--------|---------|----------|
| Primary output | Meat (and pelts) | Eggs (primary), meat (secondary) |
| Space efficiency | Highest of any meat animal (garden-shed footprint) | Moderate (requires coop + run area) |
| Feed conversion | Excellent (3-4 lbs feed per lb gain) | Good for eggs (2-3 lbs feed per dozen eggs) |
| Reproduction rate | 4-6 litters/year, 6-12 kits per litter | ~300 eggs/year per hen; 21-day incubation |
| Time to harvest | 8-12 weeks | 12-20 weeks (meat breeds); hens kept for years (eggs) |
| Noise | Very quiet | Moderate (hens vocal, roosters loud) |
| Climate sensitivity | Heat-sensitive (85°F+ dangerous); cold-tolerant | Moderate (frostbite on combs); moderate [[coleman-hardy-winter-vegetables-cold-tolerance-mechanisms]] |
| Manure | Cold manure — apply directly to garden | Hot manure — requires composting |
| Secondary products | Pelts, Angora wool | Feathers, compost |
| Processing difficulty | Moderate (dispatch and dressing skill needed) | Low (eggs require no processing) |
| Daily management | Feeding, watering, ventilation checks | Feeding, watering, egg collection, coop maintenance |
| Predator vulnerability | High (rats, weasels, raccoons) | High (hawks, raccoons, foxes, weasels) |

## When to Choose Rabbits

Choose rabbits when your primary goal is efficient meat production in minimal
space. Urban and suburban homesteaders with limited room, noise restrictions, or
close neighbors benefit from rabbits' quiet operation and small footprint.
Homesteaders who want a steady supply of lean, mild-tasting meat with a
favorable feed conversion ratio will find rabbits outperform larger livestock on
every efficiency metric. Rabbit manure's direct-to-garden applicability makes
them ideal partners for intensive [[mollison-designers-chicken-tractors-egg-production]] offer
unique mobile soil management capabilities that rabbits cannot provide. Choose
chickens when you want the most versatile, community-supported, and
beginner-friendly livestock species.

## Hybrid Approaches

Many homesteaders keep both species in complementary roles. Chickens provide
daily eggs and compost turning while rabbits provide regular meat and cold
manure for direct garden application. The two species have different housing
requirements (rabbits need dry, well-ventilated hutches; chickens need secure
coops with roosts and nesting boxes) and should be housed separately to prevent
disease transmission. A typical homestead setup places chickens in Zone 2 near
the garden and compost area, with rabbits in a nearby but separate structure.
