# Companion Planting vs Polyculture

## Overview
Companion planting and polyculture both grow multiple plant species together
for mutual benefit, but they operate at different levels of complexity and
ecological integration. Companion planting focuses on specific pairwise or
small-group relationships between plants, leveraging known mechanisms like pest
deterrence through volatile compounds, physical support, nutrient sharing, and
attraction of beneficial insects. Polyculture takes a systems-level approach,
creating dynamic plant communities of many species that mimic natural ecosystems
where plants self-organize, fill ecological niches, and create conditions for
each other through succession and microclimate modification. Companion planting
is a technique within polyculture design, but the two are not synonymous and
serve different purposes.

## Companion Planting
[[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]]

Companion planting is the practice of growing specific plants together
because they provide each other measurable benefits based on known or
traditional ecological interactions. The mechanisms are diverse and
well-documented: basil releases volatile compounds that deter tomato hornworms
and aphids; marigold roots exude alpha-terthienyl that suppresses root-knot
nematodes; beans fix atmospheric nitrogen through rhizobial symbiosis, feeding
adjacent heavy feeders like corn; tall crops like corn provide trellising for
climbing beans; and flowering plants like dill and calendula attract parasitic
wasps and hoverflies that prey on crop pests.

The classic examples are accessible to any gardener. The Three Sisters
combines corn, beans, and squash in a system where each plant provides something
the others need: corn as support, beans as nitrogen fixer, and squash as living
mulch. Tomatoes and basil share similar growing requirements while basil deters
pests. Carrots and onions repel each other's respective pests (carrot rust fly
and onion fly) while occupying different root depths. Roses and garlic
demonstrate allium pest deterrence through sulfur compounds. Nasturtiums serve
as trap crops for aphids, drawing them away from main crops.

Companion planting is typically managed at the level of individual bed pairs
or small groupings within a conventional garden layout. It integrates easily
into raised beds, row gardens, and container arrangements without requiring
fundamental changes to garden design or management practices. The benefits are
real but often modest, and some traditional companion claims lack scientific
validation. The approach is best understood as a set of evidence-based plant
pairings and strategies that can be deployed within any garden framework, from
simple row gardens to complex permaculture designs. See
[[permaculture-plants-insectary-and-pest-repellent]] for pest-focused companion strategies.

## Polyculture
[[polyculture-design-hemenway]]

Polyculture design creates integrated plant communities of seven to twenty or
more species that function as a self-organizing ecological system. Developed for
temperate climates by Ianto Evans in the 1980s and drawing on indigenous farming
knowledge from Jajarkot, Nepal and other traditional cultures, polycultures aim
to replicate the dynamic complexity of natural plant communities. The plants
themselves tune their environment, creating microclimates, living mulch, and
nutrient cycling that goes far beyond simple pairwise benefits.

The design principles emphasize diversity across plant families to minimize
competition, seeding several varieties of each species to extend harvest
windows, including fast-growing shallow-rooted species for quick soil cover and
weed suppression, and harvesting whole plants early to prevent overcrowding.
Unlike companion planting, where specific pairs are selected for known benefits,
polyculture relies on the emergent properties of diversity itself: varied leaf
shapes minimize light competition, different root depths access different soil
resources, the thick planting creates living mulch that protects soil from
erosion and heat, and the sheer botanical diversity confuses pests searching for
large blocks of their preferred host plants.

Polyculture management is fundamentally different from companion planting.
Rather than placing known beneficial pairs and tending them individually, the
polyculture gardener observes the whole community daily and intervenes by
harvesting overcrowded plants, allowing reseeding of the best individuals, and
adjusting the species mix based on performance. The system evolves through the
season as a living ecosystem, with niches opening and closing as plants are
removed and new growth fills gaps. This requires more skill and attention but
produces a resilient, self-regulating food system that builds soil, reduces pest
pressure, and produces diverse harvests without external inputs. See
[[polyculture-design-hemenway]] for Ianto Evans's complete design guidelines.

## Key Differences
| Aspect | Companion Planting | Polyculture |
|--------|-------------------|-------------|
| Scope | Specific pairwise or small-group relationships | Whole-ecosystem
community design |
| Species count | 2-4 per grouping | 7-20+ per bed |
| Design basis | Known beneficial interactions | Ecological niche diversity
and succession |
| Management style | Pair-focused, low to moderate intensity |
Community-focused, daily observation |
| Self-organization | Low (human-designed pairs) | High (emergent ecological
properties) |
| Emergent properties | Few | Many (microclimates, living mulch, pest
confusion) |
| Skill requirement | Beginner-friendly | Intermediate to advanced |
| Predictability | High (known pair effects) | Moderate (system evolves
dynamically) |
| Integration ease | Drops into any existing garden layout | Requires
dedicated community beds |
| Output focus | Specific crop goals | Diverse, continuous harvest |
| Soil building | Indirect (some pair benefits) | Direct (living mulch,
diverse roots) |

## When to Choose Companion Planting
Companion planting is the right starting point for gardeners new to
multi-species growing. It requires no change to existing garden layout and
delivers measurable benefits with minimal additional complexity. Specific
companion pairings solve specific problems: marigolds for nematode suppression,
nasturtiums as aphid trap crops, alliums for general pest deterrence, beans for
nitrogen fixation near heavy feeders. Companion planting also integrates
seamlessly into conventional raised beds, row gardens, and container
arrangements. It is the most accessible entry point into ecological gardening
and provides immediate, understandable results that build confidence for more
complex designs later.

## When to Choose Polyculture
Polyculture is the better choice for experienced gardeners seeking maximum
yield diversity and ecological resilience from minimal space. It suits
homesteaders aiming for self-sufficiency, permaculture practitioners building
food forests, and anyone willing to invest daily observation in exchange for a
self-regulating food system. Polyculture beds produce food continuously over
long seasons from a single sowing, reduce pest pressure through ecological
confusion, and build soil through living mulch and diverse [[root-exudates]]. The
Jajarkot polyculture can feed a person for six to eight months from 20 to 30
square feet, demonstrating the extraordinary productivity possible with
ecological design.
