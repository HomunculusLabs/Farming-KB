# Companion Planting vs Polyculture

## Overview
Companion planting and polyculture both grow multiple plant species together
    13|for mutual benefit, but they operate at different levels of complexity and
    14|ecological integration. Companion planting focuses on specific pairwise or
    15|small-group relationships between plants, leveraging known mechanisms like pest
    16|deterrence through volatile compounds, physical support, nutrient sharing, and
    17|attraction of beneficial insects. Polyculture takes a systems-level approach,
    18|creating dynamic plant communities of many species that mimic natural ecosystems
    19|where plants self-organize, fill ecological niches, and create conditions for
    20|each other through succession and microclimate modification. Companion planting
    21|is a technique within polyculture design, but the two are not synonymous and
    22|serve different purposes.

## Companion Planting
[[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]]

Companion planting is the practice of growing specific plants together
    28|because they provide each other measurable benefits based on known or
    29|traditional ecological interactions. The mechanisms are diverse and
    30|well-documented: basil releases volatile compounds that deter tomato hornworms
    31|and aphids; marigold roots exude alpha-terthienyl that suppresses root-knot
    32|nematodes; beans fix atmospheric nitrogen through rhizobial symbiosis, feeding
    33|adjacent heavy feeders like corn; tall crops like corn provide trellising for
    34|climbing beans; and flowering plants like dill and calendula attract parasitic
    35|wasps and hoverflies that prey on crop pests.

The classic examples are accessible to any gardener. The Three Sisters
    38|combines corn, beans, and squash in a system where each plant provides something
    39|the others need: corn as support, beans as nitrogen fixer, and squash as living
    40|mulch. Tomatoes and basil share similar growing requirements while basil deters
    41|pests. Carrots and onions repel each other's respective pests (carrot rust fly
    42|and onion fly) while occupying different root depths. Roses and garlic
    43|demonstrate allium pest deterrence through sulfur compounds. Nasturtiums serve
    44|as trap crops for aphids, drawing them away from main crops.

Companion planting is typically managed at the level of individual bed pairs
    47|or small groupings within a conventional garden layout. It integrates easily
    48|into raised beds, row gardens, and container arrangements without requiring
    49|fundamental changes to garden design or management practices. The benefits are
    50|real but often modest, and some traditional companion claims lack scientific
    51|validation. The approach is best understood as a set of evidence-based plant
    52|pairings and strategies that can be deployed within any garden framework, from
    53|simple row gardens to complex permaculture designs. See
    54|[[permaculture-plants-insectary-and-pest-repellent]] for pest-focused companion strategies.

## Polyculture
[[polyculture-design-hemenway]]

Polyculture design creates integrated plant communities of seven to twenty or
    60|more species that function as a self-organizing ecological system. Developed for
    61|temperate climates by Ianto Evans in the 1980s and drawing on indigenous farming
    62|knowledge from Jajarkot, Nepal and other traditional cultures, polycultures aim
    63|to replicate the dynamic complexity of natural plant communities. The plants
    64|themselves tune their environment, creating microclimates, living mulch, and
    65|nutrient cycling that goes far beyond simple pairwise benefits.

The design principles emphasize diversity across plant families to minimize
    68|competition, seeding several varieties of each species to extend harvest
    69|windows, including fast-growing shallow-rooted species for quick soil cover and
    70|weed suppression, and harvesting whole plants early to prevent overcrowding.
    71|Unlike companion planting, where specific pairs are selected for known benefits,
    72|polyculture relies on the emergent properties of diversity itself: varied leaf
    73|shapes minimize light competition, different root depths access different soil
    74|resources, the thick planting creates living mulch that protects soil from
    75|erosion and heat, and the sheer botanical diversity confuses pests searching for
    76|large blocks of their preferred host plants.

Polyculture management is fundamentally different from companion planting.
    79|Rather than placing known beneficial pairs and tending them individually, the
    80|polyculture gardener observes the whole community daily and intervenes by
    81|harvesting overcrowded plants, allowing reseeding of the best individuals, and
    82|adjusting the species mix based on performance. The system evolves through the
    83|season as a living ecosystem, with niches opening and closing as plants are
    84|removed and new growth fills gaps. This requires more skill and attention but
    85|produces a resilient, self-regulating food system that builds soil, reduces pest
    86|pressure, and produces diverse harvests without external inputs. See
    87|[[polyculture-design-hemenway]] for Ianto Evans's complete design guidelines.

## Key Differences
| Aspect | Companion Planting | Polyculture |
|--------|-------------------|-------------|
| Scope | Specific pairwise or small-group relationships | Whole-ecosystem
    93|community design |
| Species count | 2-4 per grouping | 7-20+ per bed |
| Design basis | Known beneficial interactions | Ecological niche diversity
    96|and succession |
| Management style | Pair-focused, low to moderate intensity |
    98|Community-focused, daily observation |
| Self-organization | Low (human-designed pairs) | High (emergent ecological
   100|properties) |
| Emergent properties | Few | Many (microclimates, living mulch, pest
   102|confusion) |
| Skill requirement | Beginner-friendly | Intermediate to advanced |
| Predictability | High (known pair effects) | Moderate (system evolves
   105|dynamically) |
| Integration ease | Drops into any existing garden layout | Requires
   107|dedicated community beds |
| Output focus | Specific crop goals | Diverse, continuous harvest |
| Soil building | Indirect (some pair benefits) | Direct (living mulch,
   110|diverse roots) |

## When to Choose Companion Planting
Companion planting is the right starting point for gardeners new to
   114|multi-species growing. It requires no change to existing garden layout and
   115|delivers measurable benefits with minimal additional complexity. Specific
   116|companion pairings solve specific problems: marigolds for nematode suppression,
   117|nasturtiums as aphid trap crops, alliums for general pest deterrence, beans for
   118|nitrogen fixation near heavy feeders. Companion planting also integrates
   119|seamlessly into conventional raised beds, row gardens, and container
   120|arrangements. It is the most accessible entry point into ecological gardening
   121|and provides immediate, understandable results that build confidence for more
   122|complex designs later.

## When to Choose Polyculture
Polyculture is the better choice for experienced gardeners seeking maximum
   126|yield diversity and ecological resilience from minimal space. It suits
   127|homesteaders aiming for self-sufficiency, permaculture practitioners building
   128|food forests, and anyone willing to invest daily observation in exchange for a
   129|self-regulating food system. Polyculture beds produce food continuously over
   130|long seasons from a single sowing, reduce pest pressure through ecological
   131|confusion, and build soil through living mulch and diverse [[root-exudates]]. The
   132|Jajarkot polyculture can feed a person for six to eight months from 20 to 30
   133|square feet, demonstrating the extraordinary productivity possible with
   134|ecological design.
   140|target known pest problems while the overall diversity provides background
   141|protection. Nitrogen-fixing companions like beans and peas become structural
   142|elements in the polyculture's nutrient cycling. Conversely, polyculture
   143|principles like diverse root depths and canopy layers can improve simple
   144|companion pairings by adding a third or fourth species that fills an unoccupied
   145|niche, making the combination more resilient and productive than the original
   146|pair alone. The most effective gardens use companion planting as a tactical tool
   147|within a broader polyculture strategy.
