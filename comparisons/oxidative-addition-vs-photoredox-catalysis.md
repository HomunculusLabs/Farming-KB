## Oxidative Addition vs Photoredox Catalysis
Oxidative addition and photoredox catalysis are both central ideas in modern synthetic chemistry.
They are related because both move electrons to activate otherwise resistant chemical bonds.
They differ in scale: oxidative addition is an elementary step, while photoredox catalysis is a broader catalytic strategy.
Oxidative addition usually describes a metal complex inserting into a covalent bond.
Photoredox catalysis describes a light-excited catalyst performing single-electron transfer or energy-transfer events.
The two ideas meet most visibly in nickel-photoredox and other dual-catalytic reactions.
## Short Answer
Use oxidative addition when you are analyzing how a transition metal enters a bond and changes oxidation state.
Use photoredox catalysis when you are analyzing how absorbed light generates radicals, radical ions, or excited-state reactivity.
Oxidative addition is commonly a two-electron organometallic event.
Photoredox catalysis is commonly a one-electron photochemical cycle.
Oxidative addition is a step inside many cross-coupling mechanisms.
Photoredox catalysis can supply radicals or redox equivalents that feed into those mechanisms.
## Core Mechanistic Difference
In oxidative addition, a metal complex reacts with a substrate bond such as C-X, H-H, Si-H, B-H, H-X, or C-H.
The formal oxidation state of the metal increases, often by two units.
The coordination number of the metal usually increases because two new ligands are attached.
A simplified formula is M + X-Y to X-M-Y.
In photoredox catalysis, a photocatalyst absorbs light and enters an excited state.
That excited state can donate an electron, accept an electron, or transfer energy.
The catalyst is productive only if later steps return it to its ground-state form.
A simplified formula is PC plus light to excited PC, followed by electron transfer and catalyst regeneration.
## Electron Bookkeeping
Oxidative addition is usually taught with formal two-electron accounting.
A low-valent metal such as palladium(0), iridium(I), rhodium(I), or nickel(0) becomes more oxidized.
The substrate bond is broken and two metal-ligand bonds are formed.
