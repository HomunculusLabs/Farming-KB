# Companion Planting vs Green Manure

## Overview

Companion planting and green manure cover crops are two foundational
ecological gardening strategies, but they serve fundamentally different
primary purposes. Companion planting focuses on synergistic plant
relationships within an active [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] — pairing crops to
repel pests, attract pollinators, improve flavor, or share physical
space. Green manures are plants grown primarily to feed the soil
rather than the gardener, typically during fallow periods between
cash crops, and are terminated (incorporated or cut) to add organic
matter and nutrients. Understanding when each strategy excels helps
gardeners build more resilient, productive systems.

## Companion Planting

[[cover-crops-green-manures]]

Green manures are cover crops grown specifically to improve soil
health. They are typically planted during fallow periods — between
seasonal cash crops, over winter, or in unused beds — and then
incorporated into the soil or terminated as surface mulch. Leguminous
green manures like [[mushroom-compost]], hairy vetch, and field peas fix
atmospheric nitrogen through rhizobial symbiosis, contributing
70-200 lbs of nitrogen per acre. Non-legume options like winter
rye, buckwheat, and sorghum-sudangrass add massive amounts of
biomass, suppress weeds through allelopathy and shading, and
improve soil structure with their root systems.

Green manures address soil problems at a structural level. Deep-
rooted species like daikon radish and alfalfa break compacted layers
and create channels for water infiltration. Dense root networks from
annual ryegrass and cereal rye stabilize soil against erosion through
freeze-thaw cycles and heavy rain. The [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] returned to the
soil feeds microbial communities, improves water-holding capacity,
and gradually builds humus.

The primary trade-off with green manures is time and space. A bed
dedicated to a cover crop produces no harvest during that period,
which requires planning around seasonal gaps. Termination timing
matters — legumes should be incorporated at peak flowering for
maximum nitrogen, while grasses are best terminated before seed set
to prevent volunteer weeds.

## When to Choose Companion Planting

Companion planting is the right choice when pest pressure is your
primary concern and you want to maintain harvestable crop area.
It excels in intensive raised beds, square-foot gardens, and small
urban plots where dedicating space to non-harvested crops feels
wasteful. If you are growing a diverse polyculture with tomatoes,
peppers, brassicas, and herbs, strategic companion placement can
reduce or eliminate the need for organic sprays. Companion planting
also integrates seamlessly into food forests and perennial systems,
where herbs and flowers fill niche spaces between fruit trees and
berry bushes.

## When to Choose Green Manure

Green manure is the right strategy when soil health is the limiting
factor — depleted beds, compacted ground, or sandy soil lacking
organic matter. It is essential in larger-scale gardens and farms
where fallow periods between rotations are already part of the
management cycle. If your beds show signs of nutrient depletion
(yellowing leaves, stunted growth, poor water retention), a
leguminous green manure will address the root cause more
effectively than any companion arrangement. Green manures are also
the best choice for erosion-prone slopes and newly broken ground
that needs stabilization before intensive planting.

## Combining Both Strategies

The most productive gardens use both approaches in a layered system.
Green manure cover crops build the soil foundation during off-seasons,
while companion planting optimizes the in-season growing space.
Practical combinations include: planting a winter hairy vetch and
cereal rye green manure, terminating it in spring, then interplanting
tomatoes with basil and marigold companions into the mulched residue.
In annual rotations, buckwheat serves as both a summer green manure
(soil builder) and a [[mollison-designers-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insect-habitat]] attractor, blurring the line
between the two strategies. In food forests, perennial green manures
like white clover serve as living mulch beneath fruit trees while
companion herbs like comfrey and yarrow fill the herbaceous layer.
