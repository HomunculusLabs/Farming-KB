## Polyculture vs Intercropping

## Overview

interact dynamically, much like natural ecosystems. Pioneered for temperate
climates by Ianto Evans in the 1980s and drawing on indigenous knowledge from
Jajarkot, Nepal and other traditional cultures, polycultures go beyond simply
avoiding negative interactions between plants. In a true polyculture, the plants
themselves [[comparison-living-mulch-vs-cover-crops]], and [[holmgren-permaculture-twelve-design-principles-framework]] emphasize diversity across plant families, varied root
depths and canopy heights, overlapping harvest times, and continuous observation
and harvesting to prevent overcrowding. A well-designed polyculture includes
fast-growing shallow-rooted species that cover soil quickly (radishes, mustard
greens), slow-growing deep-rooted crops that access different resources
(parsnips, carrots), nitrogen fixers (bush beans, fava beans), insect-attracting
flowers (dill, calendula), and species harvested at different maturities. The
classic Ianto Evans seven-variety polyculture combines radishes, dill,
calendula, lettuce, parsnips, cabbage, and bush beans in about 20 square feet
per person.

Polyculture management is intensive and requires daily attention during peak
growth periods. Plants are harvested as they begin to crowd, with whole-plant
harvesting preferred to release space for slower companions. The system evolves
through the season as plants are removed and niches open, making it more akin to
managing a living ecosystem than tending rows of crops. The advanced Jajarkot
polyculture can yield food for six to eight months from a single major sowing of
20 to 30 square feet per person, demonstrating the extraordinary productivity
possible when ecological niches are fully occupied. Total yield per unit area
often exceeds monoculture, but the output is diverse rather than concentrated in
a single marketable crop.
