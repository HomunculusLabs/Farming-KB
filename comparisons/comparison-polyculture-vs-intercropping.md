# Polyculture vs Intercropping

## Overview
Polyculture and intercropping both grow multiple species together, but they
    13|    13|differ in [[mollison-permaculture-design-philosophy]], complexity, and management intensity. Intercropping
    14|    14|is a structured agricultural practice that arranges two or more crops in defined
    15|    15|spatial patterns like rows, strips, or relay sequences, typically managed at
    16|    16|field scale with equipment compatibility in mind. Polyculture is a more holistic
    17|    17|approach that creates dynamic, self-organizing [[faires-plant-communities-and-guilds]] mimicking
    18|    18|natural ecosystems, where plants fill [[mushroom-habitats-and-ecological-niches]] and create conditions
    19|    19|for each other through succession. The distinction lies in the degree of
    20|    20|integration and the role of ecological self-organization versus human-imposed
    21|    21|spatial design.

## Polyculture
[[polyculture-design-hemenway]]

Polyculture design creates plant communities of several to many species that
    27|    27|interact dynamically, much like natural ecosystems. Pioneered for temperate
    28|    28|climates by Ianto Evans in the 1980s and drawing on indigenous knowledge from
    29|    29|Jajarkot, Nepal and other traditional cultures, polycultures go beyond simply
    30|    30|avoiding negative interactions between plants. In a true polyculture, the plants
    31|    31|themselves create microclimates, living mulch, and nutrient cycling systems that
    32|    32|enhance the whole community's productivity through ecological self-organization.

The design principles emphasize diversity across plant families, varied root
    35|    35|depths and canopy heights, overlapping harvest times, and continuous observation
    36|    36|and harvesting to prevent overcrowding. A well-designed polyculture includes
    37|    37|fast-growing shallow-rooted species that cover soil quickly (radishes, mustard
    38|    38|greens), slow-growing deep-rooted crops that access different resources
    39|    39|(parsnips, carrots), nitrogen fixers (bush beans, fava beans), insect-attracting
    40|    40|flowers (dill, calendula), and species harvested at different maturities. The
    41|    41|classic Ianto Evans seven-variety polyculture combines radishes, dill,
    42|    42|calendula, lettuce, parsnips, cabbage, and bush beans in about 20 square feet
    43|    43|per person.

Polyculture management is intensive and requires daily attention during peak
    46|    46|growth periods. Plants are harvested as they begin to crowd, with whole-plant
    47|    47|harvesting preferred to release space for slower companions. The system evolves
    48|    48|through the season as plants are removed and niches open, making it more akin to
    49|    49|managing a living ecosystem than tending rows of crops. The advanced Jajarkot
    50|    50|polyculture can yield food for six to eight months from a single major sowing of
    51|    51|20 to 30 square feet per person, demonstrating the extraordinary productivity
    52|    52|possible when ecological niches are fully occupied. Total yield per unit area
    53|    53|often exceeds monoculture, but the output is diverse rather than concentrated in
    54|    54|a single marketable crop.

## Intercropping
[[intercropping-systems]]

Intercropping is a more structured agricultural practice that grows two or
    60|    60|more crops simultaneously in the same field using defined spatial arrangements.
    61|    61|The four main types are strip intercropping (wide alternating strips compatible
    62|    62|with machinery), relay cropping (a second crop planted into a standing crop
    63|    63|before harvest), row intercropping (alternating rows within beds), and mixed
    64|    64|intercropping (no distinct rows, essentially synonymous with polyculture at
    65|    65|field scale). Each type offers different balances of ecological benefit and
    66|    66|operational practicality.

Strip intercropping is particularly valued in commercial agriculture because
    69|    69|it maintains equipment-friendly row widths while creating ecological
    70|    70|interactions at strip borders that reduce pest movement and increase total
    71|    71|yield. Relay cropping maximizes use of the growing season by overlapping crop
    72|    72|cycles, eliminating fallow periods. Row intercropping works well in market
    73|    73|gardens where hand cultivation is the norm. The design logic is typically
    74|    74|pairwise: corn with beans for nitrogen fixation, carrots with onions for mutual
    75|    75|pest deterrence, wheat with clover for ground cover after grain harvest.

The intercropping approach is more predictable and easier to manage than
    78|    78|polyculture because each crop maintains a defined spatial relationship to its
    79|    79|companions. Planting, fertilizing, pest management, and harvesting can be
    80|    80|planned crop by crop, and the systems can be adapted to mechanized farming.
    81|    81|However, the ecological integration is shallower than polyculture. Intercropping
    82|    82|typically captures the obvious benefits of diversity (reduced pest pressure,
    83|    83|complementary nutrient use) without achieving the full self-organizing
    84|    84|resilience that emerges when many species interact dynamically in a shared
    85|    85|space.

## Key Differences
| Aspect | Polyculture | Intercropping |
|--------|------------|---------------|
| Design philosophy | Self-organizing ecosystem mimicry | Structured spatial
    91|    91|arrangements |
| Species count | Many (7-20+) | Typically 2-4 |
| Spatial arrangement | Mixed, no defined rows | Defined rows, strips, or
    94|    94|relay patterns |
| Management intensity | Very high, daily attention needed | Moderate,
    96|    96|seasonal management |
| Self-regulation | High, plants create conditions for each other | Low to
    98|    98|moderate, depends on design |
| Ecological depth | Deep, emergent properties | Shallow, known pairwise
   100|   100|benefits |
| Harvest pattern | Continuous, whole-plant harvest | Often crop-by-crop at
   102|   102|maturity |
| Scalability | Garden to small farm | Garden to broadacre agriculture |
| Mechanization compatibility | Low | Moderate to high (strip/relay types) |
| Market orientation | Diverse output for self-sufficiency | Can target
   106|   106|specific crop markets |
| Skill requirement | Advanced (ecological observation) | Intermediate (crop
   108|   108|planning) |

## When to Choose Polyculture
Polyculture is ideal for home gardens, homesteads, and small-scale market
   112|   112|farms where diverse output, self-sufficiency, and ecological resilience are
   113|   113|priorities over standardized production. It excels in intensive garden spaces
   114|   114|where maximizing yield per square foot of diverse food is the goal. Polyculture
   115|   115|is also the best choice for gardeners who enjoy daily observation and hands-on
   116|   116|management, as the system rewards close attention and evolves with the
   117|   117|gardener's understanding. Permaculture food forests and community gardens
   118|   118|benefit enormously from polyculture design. See [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]] for
   119|   119|specific beneficial pairings within polyculture designs.

## When to Choose Intercropping
Intercropping suits larger-scale operations where some mechanization is
   123|   123|needed but diversity benefits are still desired. Strip intercropping works well
   124|   124|on farms using standard equipment widths, while row intercropping is practical
   125|   125|for market gardens with defined beds. Relay cropping is valuable in shorter
   126|   126|growing seasons where maximizing time in production is critical. Intercropping
   127|   127|also provides a gentler introduction to multi-species growing for farmers
   128|   128|transitioning from monoculture, since the defined patterns make planning and
   129|   129|management more predictable than full polyculture. The Land Institute's
   130|   130|perennial grain intercropping research demonstrates the potential at field
   131|   131|scale.

Many successful farms layer both approaches to capture benefits at different
   135|   135|scales. A market garden might use strip intercropping for main cash crops while
   136|   136|maintaining intensive polyculture beds for household food production and CSA
   137|   137|diversity. Polyculture principles like diverse root depths and canopy layers can
   138|   138|inform intercropping design, making simple row combinations more ecologically
   139|   139|robust. The advanced Jajarkot polyculture demonstrates how intercropping
   140|   140|patterns (row-based legumes and alliums) can be nested within a broader
   141|   141|polyculture matrix of mixed greens and herbs. A farm might also use relay
   142|   142|intercropping to transition between seasonal polyculture plantings, maintaining
   143|   143|continuous ground cover and biological activity. See
   144|   144|[[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]] for more on how these systems relate.
