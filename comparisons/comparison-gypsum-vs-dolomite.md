# Gypsum vs Dolomite

## Overview

Gypsum (calcium sulfate) and dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate)
are two of the most widely used mineral amendments in agriculture,
horticulture, and mycology. Both supply calcium, but they behave
very differently in soil. Gypsum is pH-neutral and provides calcium
plus sulfur, while dolomite raises pH and supplies both calcium and
magnesium. Choosing the wrong one can throw soil chemistry out of
balance, tighten soil structure, or waste money on unnecessary pH
adjustment.

## Gypsum

[[growing-gourmet-gypsum-lime-ph-management]]

Gypsum is calcium sulfate dihydrate (CaSO₄·2H₂O). Unlike limestone
products, gypsum does not raise soil pH. Its primary role is supplying
calcium without affecting acidity, making it invaluable for alkaline
soils or situations where pH is already in the correct range.

In mushroom cultivation, gypsum improves substrate structure by
preventing grain kernels and sawdust particles from clumping. At
roughly 5% by weight, it keeps substrate loose and aerated, which
supports mycelial respiration and colonisation speed. Stoller (1962)
and Leatham and Stahlman (1989) demonstrated that gypsum stimulated
mycelial growth of shiitake in supplemented sawdust blocks.

In garden soils, gypsum helps flocculate clay particles. The sulfate
ions bind to clay, improving aggregation, drainage, and aeration.
Solomon recommends gypsum as one of three lime components in his
Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) blend, specifically for its ability
to deliver calcium without raising pH. It is also more soluble than
agricultural lime, making calcium available to plants more quickly.

## Dolomite

[[cervantes-dolomite-lime-ph-buffering]]

Dolomite is calcium magnesium carbonate (CaMg(CO₃)₂). It supplies
both calcium and magnesium in roughly equal proportions and raises
soil pH toward neutral. Cervantes describes fine dolomite lime as a
favourite pH stabiliser for cannabis growers, particularly in rainy
climates with acidic soils. Its pH of 7.0 means it can never raise
soil pH beyond neutral.

The standard application rate is one cup of fine dolomite lime per
cubic foot (roughly one ounce per gallon) of planting medium. For
garden beds, 1 to 2 pounds per cubic foot of soil is typical.
Dolomite also makes phosphates more available by neutralising
acidic conditions that lock up phosphorus.

However, dolomite carries a significant risk: over-application leads
to magnesium accumulation. Albrecht's research showed that excess
magnesium relative to calcium tightens soil structure, reduces
aeration, and impairs root growth. Solomon warns against using
dolomite exclusively year after year. If soil feels sticky or tight
despite adequate organic matter, excess magnesium from repeated
dolomite applications may be the culprit.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Gypsum | Dolomite |
|--------|--------|----------|
| Chemical formula | CaSO₄·2H₂O | CaMg(CO₃)₂ |
| pH effect | Neutral (no change) | Raises pH to neutral |
| Calcium supplied | Yes | Yes |
| Magnesium supplied | No | Yes (roughly equal to Ca) |
| Sulfur supplied | Yes | No |
| Clay soil benefit | Improves structure | Can tighten soil |
| Solubility | Moderate | Low (slow-release) |
| Primary role | Calcium + structure | Calcium + Mg + pH buffer |
| Over-use risk | Low | Mg accumulation |
| Best context | Alkaline soils, mushroom substrates | Acidic soils, Mg deficiency |

## When to Choose Gypsum

Choose gypsum when soil pH is already adequate or high and you need
to add calcium without pushing pH further into alkaline territory.
This is common in arid and semi-arid regions where soils tend toward
alkalinity. Gypsum is also the preferred choice for improving clay
soil structure — the sulfate ions help flocculate clay particles,
improving drainage and root penetration.

Mushroom cultivators should default to gypsum over dolomite for
substrate preparation. It prevents grain clumping, provides calcium
for metabolic processes, and offers mild pH buffering without the
pH spike that dolomite would introduce. In Solomon's COF recipe,
gypsum fills the niche of "calcium without pH change" that neither
agricultural lime nor dolomite can provide.

Gypsum is also the safer choice when you are uncertain about your
soil's current magnesium levels. Since it adds no magnesium, there
is no risk of creating the calcium-to-magnesium imbalance that
plagues gardens where dolomite has been applied heavily for years.

## When to Choose Dolomite

Choose dolomite when a soil test confirms both low pH and magnesium
deficiency. In rainy climates with leached, acidic soils, dolomite
addresses two problems at once: it raises pH and supplies the
magnesium that heavy rainfall has washed away. Cannabis growers in
particular benefit from dolomite's dual action, since magnesium
deficiency is common indoors, especially in fast-growing varieties.

Dolomite is also the right choice for preparing new garden beds in
acidic soil. Mixed thoroughly into the planting medium before the
season, it creates a stable pH environment and provides a slow-
release source of both calcium and magnesium. Cervantes recommends
fine dolomite flour (not coarse chips) for fastest availability.

The critical rule: use dolomite as part of a balanced lime program
rather than as the sole amendment. Solomon's approach of mixing
equal parts agricultural lime, gypsum, and dolomite in his COF
ensures that calcium, magnesium, and sulfur are all supplied in
balanced proportions without excessive pH shifts or magnesium
accumulation.
