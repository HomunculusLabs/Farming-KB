Cob provides almost no insulation but enormous thermal mass. A thick cob wall (18-24 inches) stabilizes indoor temperatures through thermal lag, absorbing heat during the day and releasing it at night. This works brilliantly in climates with large diurnal temperature swings (deserts, high plains) but poorly where temperatures remain consistently cold.

In cold, cloudy climates, papercrete is the better performer. In hot-dry climates with cool nights, cob excels.

## Strength, Waterproofing, and Durability

Cob has higher compressive strength and a proven track record of centuries-long durability (e.g., Devon, England cob houses from the 1500s). Papercrete strength is highly variable and depends on binder ratio, paper type, and curing conditions. Compressive strength typically ranges from 150-300 psi, adequate for single-story load-bearing but not for multi-story or heavily loaded structures.

Both materials are vulnerable to water damage. Papercrete can rot, soften, and lose structural integrity if saturated. Cob erodes from prolonged wetting. Both require protective finishes: lime wash, earthen plasters, or breathable siloxane sealants. Neither should be used below grade or in direct ground contact.

## Environmental Impact

Cob has the clear advantage. It uses locally sourced earth, minimal straw, and water. No industrial processing, no cement, no transportation of manufactured materials. End-of-life, cob returns to the earth with zero toxicity.

Papercrete diverts paper waste -- a genuine environmental benefit -- but relies on Portland cement or lime as a binder. Cement production accounts for roughly 8% of global CO2 emissions. Using lime instead reduces this impact, and some builders have experimented with natural binders (borax, clay, fly ash) with mixed results. Papercrete is greener than conventional concrete but less sustainable than cob.
