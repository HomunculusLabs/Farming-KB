# Fish Hydrolysate vs Fish Emulsion

## Overview

Fish hydrolysate and fish emulsion are both liquid mycorrhizal-inoculant-production-methods, nutrient profiles, biological activity, and effects on [[decomposition]], which also lowers the pH to 3.5–4.5 and produces the characteristic strong, unpleasant odor. Some manufacturers add small amounts of synthetic urea or ammonium sulfate to boost the nitrogen content on the guaranteed analysis label.

**Typical analysis:** 4–5% N, 2–3% P, 2–3% K, with a portion of the nitrogen in the form of ammonium and urea from added synthetic sources.

**Advantages:**
- Widely available at garden centers and big-box stores
- Inexpensive compared to hydrolysate
- Long shelf life (3–5 years) due to low pH and heat sterilization
- Higher immediately available nitrogen from ammonium and urea fractions
- Concentrated — a little goes a long way for quick nitrogen top-dressing

**Limitations:**
- Heat processing denatures proteins into simple ammonia and short-chain peptides, destroying the amino acid diversity that feeds soil biology
- Enzymes, vitamins, growth factors, and beneficial microorganisms are killed during cooking
- Added synthetic nitrogen sources make some emulsions unacceptable for strict organic certification
- Strong odor persists for days after application, making it impractical for indoor and urban growing
- The acidic pH can burn foliage if applied at too high a concentration or during hot weather
- Does not feed soil microbiome — provides mineral nutrition but minimal biological stimulation
- Phosphoric acid addition contributes phosphorus that may be unnecessary or undesirable in phosphorus-accumulating soils

## Fish Hydrolysate

Fish hydrolysate (also called fish amino acids or enzymatic fish digest) is produced by breaking down fish waste using proteolytic enzymes at low temperatures (typically 30–50°C). No cooking or acidification occurs. The enzymatic digestion cleaves proteins into their constituent amino acids and short peptides while preserving the native structure of vitamins, enzymes, hormones, and oils. The result is a complex, biologically active liquid that serves as both a fertilizer and a biostimulant.

**Typical analysis:** 2–5% N, 1–3% P, 1–3% K, with nitrogen primarily in the form of free amino acids and peptides.

**Advantages:**
- Full amino acid profile (all 20 proteinogenic amino acids plus free-form L-amino acids) directly feeds soil bacteria, fungi, and plants
- Preserved enzymes (proteases, lipases, amylases) enhance organic matter decomposition and [[fungal-lichen-stress-tolerance-and-poikilohydry]], and [[pgpr-mycorrhizal-inoculants-microplant-establishment]] without killing beneficial organisms

**Limitations:**
- Shorter shelf life (1–2 years) and often requires refrigeration after opening
- More expensive than emulsion due to enzyme costs and cold-chain logistics
- Less immediately available nitrogen — amino acids must be mineralized by soil microbes before plants can use them as a nitrogen source
- Can foam excessively in sprayer tanks due to protein content
- Less concentrated in some products, requiring larger application volumes

## Head-to-Head Comparison

| Feature | Fish Emulsion | Fish Hydrolysate |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| Processing temperature | 90–120°C (cooked) | 30–50°C (enzymatic) |
| Protein state | Denatured; mostly ammonia | Intact amino acids and peptides |
| Enzymes | Destroyed | Preserved |
| Vitamins | Mostly destroyed | Preserved |
| Fish oils | Removed during pressing | Retained |
| Odor | Strong, foul | Mild, earthy |
| Shelf life | 3–5 years | 1–2 years |
| pH | 3.5–4.5 (acidified) | 4.5–6.0 (natural) |
| Synthetic additives | Often contains urea | None |
| Organic certification | Varies by brand | Universally acceptable |
| Soil biology impact | Minimal stimulation | Strong stimulation |
| Foliar burn risk | Higher (acidic, ammonium) | Lower (buffered amino acids) |
| Cost | $8–15 per liter | $15–40 per liter |
| Immediate N availability | High (ammonium/urea) | Moderate (amino acid mineralization) |
| Compost tea compatible | No — kills microbes | Yes — feeds microbes |

## When to Choose Fish Emulsion

Fish emulsion is the pragmatic choice when you need a quick, cheap nitrogen boost and are less concerned about soil biology. Specific appropriate uses include:

- **Rapid nitrogen correction** in nitrogen-deficient plants showing chlorosis, where immediate ammonium uptake is needed
- **Large-scale row crops** where cost per acre is the dominant constraint and soil biology is already well-established
