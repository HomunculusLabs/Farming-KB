# Keyline vs Swale Design

## Overview

[[query-how-does-keyline-design-work-in-permaculture]] and swale design are two of the most influential water management
systems in permaculture and regenerative agriculture. Both aim to capture, distribute,
and infiltrate water across the landscape, but they differ significantly in approach.
Keyline uses cultivation patterns following specific topographic lines to spread water
from wet valleys to dry ridges without earthworks, while swales use constructed
channels on contour to physically intercept and pond surface runoff. Each has distinct
advantages depending on scale, topography, budget, and objectives.

## Keyline Design

[[swale-design]]

Swale design involves constructing level or near-level channels on contour to
intercept, slow, and infiltrate surface water runoff. Swales are dead-level hollows
built on contour that create temporary pools during and after rainfall. Unlike drains,
which carry water away, swales hold water on the land, allowing it to infiltrate into
the soil profile. Bill Mollison devoted significant attention to swales as the primary
means of rehydrating degraded landscapes and establishing tree crops on arid land.

A complete swale system consists of the berm (mound on the downslope side from
excavated material), the swale ditch (level channel on the upslope side), spillways
(level sections allowing safe overflow to the next swale below), and planting zones
on the berm and adjacent areas. Swale dimensions vary from 30 to 60 cm deep and 60 to
100 cm wide for home gardens, to 1 to 2 meters deep and 2 to 4 meters wide for
broadacre applications. Accurate contour identification using A-frame levels, laser
levels, or water tube levels is essential for construction.

Swales are typically planted with trees and shrubs on the berm, creating productive
growing zones in the moist soil above the water line. They are one of the most
important earthworking tools in permaculture for rehydrating degraded landscapes,
preventing erosion, and establishing tree crops on previously marginal land. Once
established and planted, swale systems require minimal maintenance.

## Key Differences

| Aspect | Keyline Design | Swale Design |
|--------|---------------|-------------|
| Primary Tool | Cultivation pattern (no earthworks) | Constructed earth channels on contour |
| Water Movement | Spreads from valleys to ridges | Intercepts and ponds on contour |
| Scale | Broadacre farms and ranches | Home gardens to broadacre |
| Construction Cost | Low (cultivation only) | Moderate to high (earthworks) |
| Topography Required | Valleys with defined keypoints | Any slope with identifiable contour |
| Maintenance | Periodic cultivation | Initial construction, then planted |
| Tree Integration | Trees above keyline contour | Trees planted on berm |
| Speed of Effect | Gradual improvement over seasons | Immediate water capture |
| Equipment Needed | Standard farm [[gottlieb-psilocybin-cultivation-equipment-and-facilities]] | Earthmoving equipment or [[garden-hand-tools-solomon]] |

## When to Choose Keyline Design

Keyline design is superior for broadacre agricultural properties where earthworks at
swale scale would be prohibitively expensive or disruptive. It is ideal for grazing
land, dryland cropping, and large properties where the goal is to improve soil
moisture distribution across hundreds of acres. Keyline cultivation can be
implemented with standard [[coleman-small-farm-equipment-and-tools]] and requires no permanent structures. The
system is particularly effective on land with defined valley-and-ridge topography where
the keyline and keypoint can be clearly identified. It works gradually through
cultivation and tree planting rather than through dramatic earthworks.

## When to Choose Swale Design

Swales are better when immediate, dramatic water capture is needed. They excel on
degraded, eroded, or arid land where establishing trees requires rapid soil
rehydration. Swales are ideal for food forests, orchards, and [[mollison-tree-crop-systems-permaculture]] where
the berm provides a productive planting zone. They are well suited to home gardens
and homesteads where the visual and functional benefits of terraced water-holding
earthworks are desirable. Swales provide immediate visible results after
construction, making them satisfying for demonstration sites and educational
properties.

## Hybrid Approaches

Keyline and swale design combine effectively on properties with varied topography.
Keyline cultivation can manage broadacre paddocks while swales are constructed in
intensive zones for tree crops and gardens. Swales can be constructed along the
keyline contour itself, using keyline topographic intelligence for optimal placement.
Tree belts planted above keylines can be integrated with swale systems, creating
complementary water management at different scales. On steep terrain, keyline
cultivation above the keypoint and swales below it can work together to manage the
full moisture profile of a property.
