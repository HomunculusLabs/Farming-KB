# Chaga vs Turkey Tail

## Overview

Chaga (Inonotus obliquus) and Turkey Tail (Trametes versicolor) are two of the
most widely used medicinal mushrooms in the world. Both are polypores rich in
beta-glucans and both have long histories in traditional medicine. Yet they
differ profoundly in biology, biochemistry, clinical evidence, and how they
are best used. This comparison helps you decide which mushroom fits your
health goals, or whether combining both is the optimal strategy.

## Chaga (Inonotus obliquus)

[[turkey-tail-mushroom-cancer-research]]

Turkey Tail is one of the most common mushrooms on Earth, found on dead
hardwood on every continent except Antarctica. Its colorful, fan-shaped
fruiting bodies with concentric zones of brown, tan, blue, and orange
resemble the fanned tail of a wild turkey. Unlike chaga, turkey tail is a
true saprotrophic decomposer that fruits readily on logs, stumps, and
supplemented sawdust substrates.

Turkey tail is the most extensively researched medicinal mushroom for immune
support and cancer adjunct therapy. Its two signature compounds — PSK
(polysaccharide-K, krestin) and PSP (polysaccharide-peptide) — are approved
pharmaceutical drugs in Japan and China respectively. PSK has been prescribed
alongside chemotherapy in Japan since 1977, with hundreds of clinical trials
demonstrating improved survival rates in gastric, colorectal, breast, and
lung cancers. Meta-analyses show 5-15% improvement in five-year survival.

Turkey tail's mechanisms are primarily immunomodulatory: activating dendritic
cells, enhancing T-cell proliferation, stimulating natural killer (NK) cell
function, and acting as a prebiotic that supports beneficial gut bacteria
producing short-chain fatty acids. It is easily cultivated on hardwood
sawdust and logs, making it one of the most accessible medicinal mushrooms
for home growers. For more on cultivation see
