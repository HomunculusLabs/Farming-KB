## Desert Gardening vs Drought Gardening

## Overview

Desert gardening and drought gardening both address the challenge of growing food with limited water, but they approach the problem from fundamentally different starting points. Desert gardening is a year-round discipline defined by a specific biome with permanent aridity — annual precipitation under 10-15 inches, intense solar radiation, alkaline soils, and extreme temperature swings. Drought gardening is a response strategy applicable in any climate during dry periods, relying on wide plant spacing and deep soil moisture access rather than irrigation infrastructure.

Where desert gardening asks "how do I engineer a food-producing system in a hostile environment?", drought gardening asks "how can I grow food with far less water than most gardeners assume is necessary?" Both challenge the assumption that abundant piped irrigation is essential for food production. Understanding their distinctions helps gardeners select the right approach and avoid applying temperate techniques to arid landscapes where they will fail.

## Climate Context

### Desert Gardening
Desert gardening operates in true arid environments where evapotranspiration far exceeds rainfall year-round. These regions face alkaline soils (pH 7.5-8.5+), UV levels up to 50% higher than at sea level, humidity regularly below 10% in summer afternoons, and diurnal temperature swings of 30-50 degrees F. Summer highs regularly exceed 100 degrees F while winter lows can drop below freezing. Frost is possible even in warm deserts during winter months.

The constraints are structural and permanent, not seasonal. A desert gardener cannot wait for a wet season to compensate; every drop of water must be actively managed through harvesting, storage, and efficient delivery systems.
