## No-Till vs Raised Bed

## Overview
No-till gardening and raised bed gardening address the same goal of productive vegetable
growing through fundamentally different approaches to soil management. No-till eliminates
all soil disturbance, relying on surface-applied [[raised-bed-gardening]]
Raised bed gardening elevates the growing surface above ground level, typically in
framed structures four to eight feet wide and eight to twelve inches deep. The soil
mix, often based on Mel's Mix (one-third compost, one-third peat moss or coir,
one-third vermiculite), provides ideal growing conditions independent of native soil
quality. Raised beds warm two to four weeks earlier in spring, drain better than
surrounding ground, eliminate compaction from foot traffic, and allow intensive spacing
that increases yields per square foot through concentrated root zones.

The method is highly accessible for beginners. Construction requires basic carpentry
or purchasing pre-made kits from garden centers. Filling beds is the primary upfront
cost, with a standard four-by-eight-by-ten-inch bed requiring approximately one cubic
yard of soil mix at roughly 50 to 150 dollars depending on ingredients. Seasonal
maintenance involves top-dressing with compost and occasional cover cropping during
fallow periods. Raised beds can be placed on pavement, rooftops, or over contaminated
soil, making them versatile for urban settings where ground soil is unavailable or
unsafe for food production.

The weaknesses of raised beds include initial material costs for frames and soil mix,
the need to periodically replace rotting wooden frames (every five to ten years),
limited root depth for deep-rooted crops unless beds are built tall (which compounds
cost exponentially), and dependence on imported soil mixes rather than building native
soil health. Raised beds create an artificial [[mollison-designers-community-garden-design-urban-permaculture]] |

## When to Choose No-Till
No-till gardening is the better choice for long-term soil health, ecological
sustainability, and scalability. It is ideal for gardeners who want to build and
improve their native soil over time, who value [[coleman-deep-organic-farming-philosophy]] systems that prioritize living
soil ecosystems over short-term convenience.

## When to Choose Raised Beds
Raised beds excel in situations where native soil is poor, contaminated, or
nonexistent, such as urban lots, paved surfaces, and rooftop gardens. They are ideal
for beginners who want a clean, organized, and immediately productive garden with
complete control over growing conditions. Raised beds provide accessibility benefits for
gardeners with mobility limitations and warm earlier in spring for extended growing
seasons. They are the pragmatic choice when speed of setup and predictability of
results matter more than long-term native soil improvement, such as in rental
properties or community garden plots.

## Hybrid Approaches

popular and arguably the best of both worlds. Raised beds can be managed without
tilling by top-dressing with compost and mulch rather than turning the soil each
