# Dynamic Accumulators vs Cover Crops

## Overview
Dynamic accumulators and cover crops are both strategies for building soil
fertility using living plants, but they differ in their primary mechanisms,
plant selection, and roles within a farm or garden system. Dynamic
accumulators are plants that concentrate specific nutrients in their tissues
through deep rooting or unique biochemical pathways, then make those
nutrients available when their biomass is harvested and recycled as mulch or
compost. Cover crops are crops grown primarily to protect and improve the
soil rather than for harvest, providing [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]], weed suppression, and [[comfrey-plant-guide]]), whose taproots can extend
several feet into the subsoil, mining potassium, calcium, and trace minerals
from horizons that shallow-rooted vegetables never reach. When comfrey
leaves are cut and used as mulch or added to compost, those accumulated
nutrients are deposited in the topsoil where food crops can access them.

Two types of dynamic accumulators exist. True bioaccumulators concentrate a
nutrient even when the soil is deficient in that element. Legumes are the
best-known example, fixing atmospheric nitrogen through rhizobial symbiosis
regardless of soil nitrogen levels. The second type, soil indicator
accumulators, thrive where certain minerals are abundant and reflect soil
chemistry in their tissue composition. Plants like dandelion, chicory, and
plantain fall into this category. Both types become useful when their
biomass is intentionally recycled.

The value of dynamic accumulators lies in their function as living
fertilizer pumps. They are typically perennial plants integrated into
permanent landscape features such as hedgerows, guild plantings, orchard
understories, and border beds. They are not usually grown as annual row
crops. Their harvest cycle is flexible; comfrey can be cut four to six
times per growing season, producing substantial biomass for mulching.

## Cover Crops
Cover crops are typically annual or short-lived plants grown during fallow
periods, between cash crops, or as interplantings. Their primary functions
include protecting bare soil from erosion and compaction, adding organic
matter, fixing nitrogen (when leguminous), suppressing weeds through
competition, breaking pest and disease cycles, and improving soil structure
through root penetration. Common cover crop species include crimson clover,
hairy vetch, winter rye, buckwheat, field peas, and daikon radish.

Cover crops are integrated into crop rotation plans. A typical pattern might
involve planting a legume cover crop after a heavy-feeding summer vegetable
crop, allowing it to grow through autumn, then terminating it in spring
before the next cash crop. The termination method, whether by mowing,
crimping, tilling, or herbicide, affects how much residue remains on the
soil surface and how quickly nutrients become available. No-till systems
using roller-crimpers are increasingly popular because they leave a thick
mat of residue that acts as both mulch and slow-release fertilizer.
