# Mealworm Farming vs Cricket Farming

Mealworms (*Tenebrio molitor*) and crickets (primarily *Acheta domesticus* and *Gryllodes sigillatus*) are the two most commercially established insect farming systems worldwide. Both offer compelling advantages as alternative protein sources, but they differ significantly in biology, economics, and operational requirements.

## Species Biology

| Aspect | Mealworms | Crickets |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| Species | *Tenebrio molitor* (darkling beetle larva) | *Acheta domesticus*, *Gryllodes sigillatus* |
| Lifecycle stage farmed | Larval stage (6-12 weeks) | Nymph to adult (6-8 weeks) |
| Final instar size | ~25-30 mm | ~20-25 mm |
| Metamorphosis type | Complete (holometabolous) | Incomplete (hemimetabolous) |
| Natural habitat | Stored grain, dark environments | Warm, humid tropical/subtropical |

Mealworms are the larval stage of the mealworm beetle and undergo complete metamorphosis. Crickets are farmed through their nymph and adult stages, with incomplete metamorphosis. This biological difference has practical implications: mealworm farming focuses on extending the larval growth phase, while cricket farming manages multiple life stages simultaneously.

## Nutritional Profile

Both species are nutritionally dense, though with notable differences in macronutrient ratios and micronutrient content.

### Protein and Fat

Mealworms provide approximately **50-55% protein** and **30-35% fat** on a dry matter basis. Crickets are slightly leaner, with **60-65% protein** and **15-20% fat** (dry matter). Cricket protein is often cited as more complete, with a higher digestibility score (DIAAS ~0.85-0.91) compared to mealworms (~0.76-0.85).

### Amino Acids and Micronutrients

Both species contain all nine essential amino acids. Crickets are particularly rich in leucine, lysine, and [[tryptophan]]. Mealworms have higher methionine and cysteine levels, making them complementary in amino acid profiles.

For micronutrients, crickets are notably rich in **iron (8-20 mg/100g dry)**, **zinc (12-16 mg/100g dry)**, and **B12**. Mealworms provide significant **copper, manganese, and vitamin E**. Both are excellent calcium sources when fed calcium-supplemented diets, which is critical for their use in animal feed.

## Space and Infrastructure Requirements

**Mealworm farming** requires relatively compact vertical shelving systems. Larvae are housed in shallow trays (5-10 cm depth) stacked vertically, making efficient use of warehouse space. Temperature control (25-28°C) and ventilation are the primary infrastructure needs. No special lighting is required—in fact, darkness is preferred.

**Cricket farming** demands more complex infrastructure. Crickets are highly mobile and require enclosed containers with vertical egg-laying substrates, proper [[stamets-cultivator-humidity-management-fogging-systems]] (60-80% RH), and temperature control (28-32°C). Crickets also exhibit cannibalism if overcrowded, requiring careful density management. Facilities need more headroom and specialized containment systems.

Overall, mealworm farming can be established in roughly **30-40% less floor space** per kilogram of protein output compared to cricket farming.

## Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR)

FCR is a critical metric for protein production efficiency:

- **Mealworms:** FCR of ~2.0:1 to 2.5:1 (dry feed to live weight)
- **Crickets:** FCR of ~1.5:1 to 1.7:1 (dry feed to live weight)

Crickets are more efficient feed converters, though estimates vary depending on feed substrate, moisture management, and accounting for water intake (crickets require standing water or hydrogel sources). Mealworms obtain moisture from their feed, simplify [[water-management]] ment.

## Growth Rate and Lifecycle

**Mealworms** have a longer farming cycle of **10-14 weeks** from egg to harvestable larva. Growth is steady but slow. They can be held at the desired harvest size by reducing temperature, offering flexibility in harvest timing.

**Crickets** reach harvest size in **6-8 weeks**. The faster lifecycle allows more harvest cycles per year (potentially 5-7 vs 3-4 for mealworms). However, cricket populations can crash rapidly if conditions deteriorate, while mealworm populations are more resilient.

## Startup Cost

| Cost Category | Mealworm Farm | Cricket Farm |
|---------------|---------------|--------------|
| Small-scale setup (< 100kg/mo) | $2,000-$5,000 | $3,000-$8,000 |
| Medium-scale setup (1-5 ton/yr) | $15,000-$40,000 | $25,000-$60,000 |
| Primary cost drivers | Trays, climate control | Enclosures, climate + [[cervantes-humidity-control-dehumidification]] |

Cricket farming typically requires **25-50% higher initial capital** due to more complex enclosure requirements, humidity systems, and the need for multiple rearing containers per production batch. Mealworm farming can scale incrementally by adding tray racks.

## Ease of Care

Mealworms are widely regarded as the easier species for beginners. They are slow-moving, non-vocal, require no water provisions, and tolerate wider temperature fluctuations. Crickets require active humidity management, regular egg harvesting, water provision, and are susceptible to cricket paralysis virus and fungal infections (e.g metarhizium zium* species).

**Winner for beginners: Mealworms.**

## Climate Tolerance

Mealworms tolerate a broader [[query-what-is-the-ideal-temperature-range-for-growing-cannabis]] (15-32°C) and are resilient to temporary fluctuations. Crickets require stricter temperature control (26-32°C) and high humidity, making them more energy-intensive to maintain in temperate climates. This gives mealworm farming a significant advantage in regions with variable climates or for operations seeking to minimize HVAC costs.

## Processing and Harvesting

**Mealworms** are harvested by sifting larvae from frass (waste) and substrate. They can be euthanized by freezing or blanching. Their soft exoskeleton and plump body make them easy to process into powder or flour.

**Crickets** require more complex harvesting—manual collection or mechanical sorting is needed to separate crickets from containers, egg cartons, and substrate. Their chitinous exoskeleton and legs create processing challenges for smooth-textured products. Dehydration yields a coarser powder than mealworm-derived flour.

## Regulatory Landscape

Both species have gained regulatory approval in several major markets:

- **European Union:** Mealworms (*T. molitor*) received Novel Food approval in 2021. Whole dried crickets (*A. domesticus*) received approval in 2022. Processed forms (powder, protein isolates) have additional applications under review.
- **United States:** Both are GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) for human consumption. The FDA regulates them under standard food safety frameworks.
- **Asia-Pacific:** Both species are widely consumed and face minimal regulatory barriers in Thailand, Vietnam, South Korea, and other markets with established entomophagy traditions.
- **Animal feed:** Both are approved for use in aquaculture feed (EU, US, several Asian markets). Approval for poultry and swine feed is expanding, with the EU authorizing insect-derived protein for poultry feed in 2021.

## Scalability

Cricket farming has seen more large-scale commercial investment (companies like Aspire Food Group, Protix, and Entomo Farms have built multi-thousand-ton facilities). However, the operational complexity and disease vulnerability of cricket farming pose scaling challenges. Mealworm farming, while less glamorous, scales more reliably due to simpler biology, lower infrastructure needs, and greater disease resistance. Several European companies (e.g., Ynsect, Protibute) have scaled mealworm production to industrial levels with automated harvesting systems.

## Taste and Culinary Use

**Mealworms** have a mild, nutty, slightly earthy flavor often compared to roasted nuts or mushrooms. They are popular in whole roasted form as snacks, as protein-enriched flour in baked goods, pasta, and protein bars. Their texture when cooked is pleasantly crunchy on the outside and soft inside.

**Crickets** have a slightly more pronounced umami flavor, sometimes described as earthy or shellfish-like. They are commonly used whole roasted as snacks, ground into flour for protein products, and are popular in cricket-based energy bars (e.g., Exo, Chapul). Cricket powder can have a slightly bitter aftertaste if over-processed due to chitin content.

Consumer acceptance studies generally show slightly higher preference for mealworm-based products among Western consumers unfamiliar with insect foods.

## Environmental Impact

Both insect farming systems dramatically outperform conventional livestock on environmental metrics:

| Metric (per kg protein) | Mealworms | Crickets | Beef (comparison) |
|------------------------|-----------|----------|-------------------|
| GHG emissions | 2.0-5.5 kg CO₂eq | 1.0-2.5 kg CO₂eq | 27-50 kg CO₂eq |
| Land use | ~5-10 m² | ~5-8 m² | 200-300 m² |
| Water use | ~10-15 L | ~8-12 L | 15,000-20,000 L |

Crickets have a slight edge in GHG emissions due to their superior FCR, but mealworms can be raised on organic waste substrates (spent grain, vegetable scraps) more readily, potentially closing the gap in net environmental impact. Both species produce frass that can be used as [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-solomon]], adding value to the farming system.
