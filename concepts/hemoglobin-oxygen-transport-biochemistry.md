## Overview

Hemoglobin (Hb) is a tetrameric metalloprotein responsible for transporting molecular oxygen (O2) from the lungs to peripheral tissues and facilitating the return transport of [[thc-heart-protective-mechanisms-myocardial-protection-cb1-nitric-oxide-blesching-2015]] [[brassinosteroid-mushroom-yield-dry-mass]] of mature erythrocytes and is the most abundant protein in the human body.

## Primary Structure and Globin Gene Families

The human hemoglobin molecule is a heterotetramer: two alpha-like chains and two beta-like chains, each associated with a prosthetic heme group. Adult hemoglobin (HbA) has the composition alpha-2-beta-2. Globin genes are organized in two clusters on separate chromosomes:

- **Alpha-globin cluster** (chromosome 16): zeta (embryonic), alpha-2, alpha-1, and the pseudogene psi-alpha.
- **Beta-globin cluster** (chromosome 11): epsilon (embryonic), G-gamma, A-gamma (fetal), delta (adult minor), and beta (adult major).

Developmental switching (hemoglobin ontogeny) progresses from embryonic (Hb Gower-1: zeta-2-epsilon-2) to fetal (HbF: alpha-2-gamma-2) to adult (HbA: alpha-2-beta-2, plus minor HbA2: alpha-2-delta-2). This switching is regulated by the BCL11A transcriptional repressor and the locus control region (LCR), a powerful upstream enhancer that coordinates temporal and tissue-specific globin expression.

## The Heme Group

Each globin chain enfolds one heme prosthetic group — a protoporphyrin IX ring coordinated to a ferrous iron (Fe2+) at its center. The iron sits in the plane of the porphyrin ring and makes six coordination bonds: four to the pyrrole nitrogen atoms of porphyrin, one to the proximal histidine (His F8, the 8th residue of the F helix), and one to the bound ligand (O2, CO, or H2O). The distal histidine (His E7) does not directly coordinate iron but hydrogen-bonds to bound O2, stabilizing it and sterically hindering entry of larger ligands.

The iron must remain in the Fe2+ (ferrous) state to bind O2. Oxidation to Fe3+ (ferric) produces methemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen. The erythrocyte enzyme NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase (methemoglobin reductase) reduces methemoglobin back to the functional ferrous form.

## Allosteric States: T and R Conformations

Hemoglobin exists in two principal quaternary conformations:

- **T (tense) state**: Low-affinity conformation stabilized by salt bridges between the C-terminal residues of the alpha-1-beta-2 and alpha-2-beta-1 interfaces. Deoxygenated hemoglobin preferentially occupies this state.
- **R (relaxed) state**: High-affinity conformation with broken salt bridges, rotated subunit interfaces, and the iron pulled into the plane of the porphyrin ring. Oxygenated hemoglobin occupies this state.

The transition between T and R states is the structural basis for cooperative oxygen binding. When O2 binds to one heme, the iron atom moves approximately 0.4 angstroms into the porphyrin plane, dragging the proximal histidine and the attached F helix. This shift is transmitted through the FG corner to the alpha-1-beta-2 interface, destabilizing the T-state salt bridges and promoting the R state in the remaining subunits — a classic example of an allosteric conformational cascade.

## Cooperative Oxygen Binding

Cooperativity means that binding of the first O2 molecule increases the affinity of the remaining sites. The oxygen dissociation curve (ODC) of hemoglobin is sigmoidal, described by the Hill equation: Y = pO2^n / (P50^n + pO2^n), where Y is fractional saturation, P50 is the partial pressure at 50% saturation (~26 mmHg for HbA), and n is the Hill coefficient (about 2.8 for HbA, compared to 1.0 for myoglobin).

A sigmoidal curve provides a physiological advantage: hemoglobin loads O2 efficiently in the high-pO2 environment of the lung alveoli (~100 mmHg) and unloads it readily in the lower pO2 of active tissues (~20-40 mmHg), with a steep middle region that allows large changes in O2 delivery for small changes in pO2.

## The Bohr Effect

The Bohr effect describes the pH and CO2 dependence of hemoglobin's oxygen affinity. In  tissues, increased CO2 production lowers pH (via carbonic anhydrase: CO2 + H2O -> H2CO3 -> H+ + HCO3-). Lower pH stabilizes the T state by protonating specific residues (notably His146-beta, the C-terminal His, and Val1-alpha), strengthening inter-subunit salt bridges. The result is a rightward shift of the ODC: decreased O2 affinity, promoting unloading where O2 is needed most.

The Bohr coefficient is approximately -0.48 for HbA (a drop of 0.1 pH unit increases P50 by ~3-4 mmHg). The reciprocal Haldane effect describes how deoxygenated hemoglobin binds CO2 and H+ more readily, facilitating CO2 transport back to the lungs.

## 2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) Regulation

2,3-BPG (formerly called 2,3-DPG) is the most significant allosteric effector of hemoglobin under physiological conditions. Produced by the Rapoport-Luebering shunt of glycolysis in erythrocytes, 2,3-BPG is an anionic molecule that binds in the central cavity of deoxygenated (T-state) hemoglobin, forming salt bridges with the N-terminal Val1 residues of both beta chains and with Lys82-beta and His143-beta.

This binding stabilizes the T state, lowering O2 affinity and right-shifting the ODC. Normal erythrocyte 2,3-BPG concentration is ~5 mM (approximately equimolar to hemoglobin tetramers). Key physiological adaptations include:

- **High altitude**: increased 2,3-BPG shifts the curve rightward, improving tissue O2 delivery despite lower ambient pO2.
- **Fetal hemoglobin (HbF)**: the gamma chains have Ser at position 143 instead of His, reducing 2,3-BPG binding affinity. This gives HbF a left-shifted curve (lower P50 of about 19 mmHg), enabling effective O2 extraction from maternal blood.
- **Anemia**: elevated 2,3-BPG compensates for reduced Hb concentration by enhancing O2 unloading per molecule.
- **Stored blood**: 2,3-BPG depletes over about 2 weeks of refrigeration, causing transfused blood to have temporarily reduced O2 delivery capacity.

## Myoglobin vs. Hemoglobin

Myoglobin is a monomeric oxygen-binding protein found in skeletal and cardiac muscle. Despite sharing the globin fold and a single heme group, myoglobin and hemoglobin have fundamentally different functional properties:

| Property | Myoglobin | Hemoglobin |
|---|---|---|
| Quaternary structure | Monomer | Heterotetramer |
| Molecular weight | ~17 kDa | ~64.5 kDa |
| O2 binding curve | Hyperbolic | Sigmoidal |
| Hill coefficient | 1.0 | ~2.8 |
| P50 | ~2-3 mmHg | ~26 mmHg |
| 2,3-BPG binding | No | Yes |
| Function | O2 storage in muscle | O2 transport in blood |

Myoglobin's high affinity (low P50) makes it an intracellular O2 reservoir and facilitates O2 diffusion from capillaries to mitochondria in working muscle. Myoglobin releases O2 only at very low pO2, such as during intense exercise when intracellular pO2 drops to ~5 mmHg or below.

## Carbon Monoxide and Nitric Oxide Binding

**Carbon monoxide (CO)** binds to hemoglobin with ~200-250 times the affinity of O2, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb). CO binds to Fe2+ in the same coordination site as O2 but forms a stronger bond due to better orbital overlap (back-bonding into CO's pi-star antibonding orbitals). Even at low atmospheric CO concentrations (e.g., 0.1%), significant COHb forms, displacing O2 and reducing O2-carrying capacity. Additionally, COHb left-shifts the ODC for the remaining heme sites, further impairing O2 unloading. CO poisoning is treated with 100% normobaric or hyperbaric oxygen to outcompete CO binding.

**Nitric oxide (NO)** binds to the heme iron with even higher affinity than CO, forming nitrosylhemoglobin. Under physiological conditions, hemoglobin also reacts with NO via the cysteine at beta-93 (Cys-beta-93) to form S-nitrosohemoglobin (SNO-Hb), which is thought to play a role in hypoxic vasodilation — a mechanism by which deoxygenated hemoglobin delivers NO bioactivity to tissues, matching blood flow to metabolic demand. This represents a dual physiological role: hemoglobin not only delivers O2 but also regulates its own distribution through NO signaling.
