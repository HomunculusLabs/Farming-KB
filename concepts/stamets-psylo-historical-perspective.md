# Historical Perspective on Psilocybin Mushrooms

The ceremonial use of psychoactive mushrooms stretches back at least seven
thousand years and probably extends to Paleolithic times. Present-day
understanding of historical psilocybin mushroom use in other civilizations
largely arises from the works of Blasius Reko, Richard E. Schultes, Roger
Heim, and R. Gordon Wasson. These ethnomycologists, the first of their kind,
not only studied the ethnographic origins of mushroom rituals but also used
the mushrooms and had personal knowledge of their power. Their work
supported the theory that modern-day mushroom cults are remnants of an
ancient religion practiced by the Aztec and Mayan civilizations.

## Aztec and Mesoamerican Mushroom Use

The discovery of mushroom motifs and mushroom stones in excavations of
Mayan temple ruins strongly underscores the important cultural role these
mushrooms played. One Psilocybe mushroom (P. mexicana) was so esteemed as
a holy sacrament as to be called teonanacatl ("god's flesh") in the Aztec
language.

In the sixteenth century, a Franciscan friar named Bernardino de Sahagun,
accompanying the expedition of the Spanish conqueror Hernando Cortes,
reported the ritualistic use of teonanacatl by the Aztec peoples. However,
misguided Catholic missionaries, carrying out their campaign against "pagan
idolatry," soon forced mushroom use into secrecy by persecuting those who
were caught using them. Christ subjugated native rites, religions, and
beliefs, artifacts and other motifs were viewed by the conquering Catholics
as tribute to pagan practices and were destroyed.

The widespread, planned destruction of this subculture nearly succeeded in
completely erasing the ancient and rich cultural heritage of Psilocybe use
in Mesoamerica. Approximately two hundred mushroom stones escaped
destruction, despite the concerted efforts of misguided Catholic
missionaries. The smaller mushroom stones were found with metates, mortar
and pestle sets presumably used for grinding the sacraments prior to use.

If it were not for the chronicling by Bernardino de Sahagun, who spent
fifty years studying native cultures, we would have no eyewitness reports.
Ironically, this priest from a foreign conquering nation remains our single
best source of knowledge of Psilocybe mushroom use by the Aztecs.

Sahagun described the Aztec mushroom ceremonies: mushrooms were eaten when
shell trumpets were blown. Participants ate no other food and only drank
chocolate during the night. When the effects took hold, they danced, then
wept. Some sat in their houses in contemplation. When the effects left
them, they consulted among themselves and told one another what they had
seen in visions.

The Aztec emperor Moctezuma held an annual feast called the "feast of the
revelations" in which the cognoscenti would eat green mushrooms.
Unfortunately, the text detailing this feast has never been recovered. The
original text, reportedly voluminous, may have been suppressed by the
church because of its controversial content.

Despite the persecution, the ancient ceremonies persisted in secret,
remaining hidden from outsiders until this century. From the confrontation
of two belief systems, a strange fusion of Christianity and mushroom ritual
evolved.

## Maria Sabina and Wasson's Discovery

Maria Sabina, the renowned Oaxacan shaman who was "discovered" by R.
Gordon Wasson's team, is a classic example of the syncretism between
Catholic and indigenous practices. Her mushroom veladas were permeated
with Catholic practices such as an altar to Christ, portraits of the Virgin
Mary, and other Christian elements.

When Wasson's "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" appeared in Life magazine on
May 13th, 1957, millions of Americans were introduced to the Psilocybe
mushrooms of Mexico. Roger Heim's excellent watercolors, accurate enough
for field identification, reached into the heartland of America. Wasson's
article introduced mushrooms to an entire generation of Americans during
the formative, pre-sixties social period.

Soon, fascination with psilocybin mushrooms could be measured by the
thousands of aficionados who made the trek to Mexico in search of Maria
Sabina and her magic mushrooms. Her sudden popularity was unexpected and
greatly regretted by Wasson. Were it not for the passion of Wasson and his
colleagues, these ritual remnants may have been lost forever.

When Maria Sabina gave R. Gordon Wasson thirteen pairs of fresh Psilocybe
caerulescens to ingest, he probably received well in excess of 50
milligrams of psilocybin, a dose dangerous for those not under the direct
guidance of a gifted shaman.

## The [[eleusinian-mysteries]]

The use of fungi as sacraments has historical precedence in European
cultures as well. Aristotle, Plato, Homer, and Sophocles all participated
in religious ceremonies in Greece at an unusual temple honoring Demeter,
the goddess of agriculture.

For over two millennia, thousands of pilgrims journeyed fourteen miles
from Athens to Eleusis, paying the equivalent of a month's wage for the
privilege of attending the annual ceremony. Inside the great telestrion,
pilgrims sat in rows on steps that descended to a hidden, central chamber
from which a fungal concoction was served. Under punishment of imprisonment
or death, no revelation of the ceremony's secrets could be mentioned. These
ceremonies, known as the Eleusinian mysteries, continued until repressed
in the early centuries of the Christian era.

In 1977, at a mushroom conference in western Washington, R. Gordon Wasson,
[[albert-hofmann]], and Carl Ruck first postulated that the Eleusinian
mysteries centered on the use of psychoactive fungi. Their papers were
later published in "The Road to Eleusis: Unveiling the Secret of the
Mysteries."

## Second-Generation Ethnomycologists

If Wasson and his colleagues are considered the first generation of
ethnomycologists, then [[jonathan-ott]], [[terence-mckenna]], [[andrew-weil]],
Christian Ratsch, [[jochen-gartz]], and Giorgio Samorini could be considered
the second generation. As the body of knowledge from this second generation
amasses, a broad foundation is being laid for future ethnomycologists.

The course of human history has been dramatically affected by the use of
psilocybin mushrooms and will continue to be for years to come. The
discovery of mushroom stones of Mesoamerica, Paleolithic cave drawings in
the Tassili-n-Ajer region of northern Algeria, and other archaeological
evidence all point to a deep and ancient relationship between humans and
psychoactive fungi.

## Tassili Cave Art and Ancient Africa

In northern Algeria, the Tassili plateau was once known as the "plateau of
the rivers." In the 1930s and 1940s, hundreds of Paleolithic drawings were
discovered in this region. The original artist lived seven thousand years
ago, when glaciers were rapidly receding. Cave art clearly communicates
that mushrooms were revered in an eco-spiritual context.

One Psilocybe species is documented from northern Algeria: P. mairei, a
species resembling the potent [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]. This group is found
seasonally in soils littered with wood debris. Do these few collections
represent the end of a bygone era when mushrooms were more prevalent?
Perhaps P. mairei is the same species that inspired the artist who drew
the mushroom figures in the Tassili cave.
