The use of fungi as sacraments has historical precedence in European
cultures as well. Aristotle, Plato, Homer, and Sophocles all participated
in religious ceremonies in Greece at an unusual temple honoring Demeter,
the goddess of agriculture.

For over two millennia, thousands of pilgrims journeyed fourteen miles
from Athens to Eleusis, paying the equivalent of a month's wage for the
privilege of attending the annual ceremony. Inside the great telestrion,
pilgrims sat in rows on steps that descended to a hidden, central chamber
from which a fungal concoction was served. Under punishment of imprisonment
or death, no revelation of the ceremony's secrets could be mentioned. These
ceremonies, known as the Eleusinian mysteries, continued until repressed
in the early centuries of the Christian era.

In 1977, at a mushroom conference in western Washington, R. Gordon Wasson,
gordon-wasson, and Carl Ruck first postulated that the Eleusinian
mysteries centered on the use of psychoactive fungi. Their papers were
later published in "The Road to Eleusis: Unveiling the Secret of the
Mysteries."

## Second-Generation Ethnomycologists

If Wasson and his colleagues are considered the first generation of
ethnomycologists, then [[terence-mckenna]], [[jochen-gartz]], and Giorgio Samorini could be considered
the second generation. As the body of knowledge from this second generation
amasses, a broad foundation is being laid for future ethnomycologists.

The course of human history has been dramatically affected by the use of
psilocybin mushrooms and will continue to be for years to come. The
discovery of mushroom stones of Mesoamerica, Paleolithic cave drawings in
the Tassili-n-Ajer region of northern Algeria, and other archaeological
evidence all point to a deep and ancient relationship between humans and
psychoactive fungi.

## Tassili Cave Art and Ancient Africa

In northern Algeria, the Tassili plateau was once known as the "plateau of
the rivers." In the 1930s and 1940s, hundreds of Paleolithic drawings were
discovered in this region. The original artist lived seven thousand years
ago, when glaciers were rapidly receding. Cave art clearly communicates
that mushrooms were revered in an eco-spiritual context.
