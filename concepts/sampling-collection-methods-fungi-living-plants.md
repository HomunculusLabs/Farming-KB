## Sampling and Collection Methods for Fungi on Living Plants

Effective sampling and collection of fungi on living plants requires understanding of ecological sampling theory and practical field techniques. Methods vary depending on host plant type, fungal guild (pathogens, obligate biotrophs, saprobes), and habitat. This page covers quantifying fungal abundance, accessing tree canopies, standard ground-based collection, and special circumstances that complicate fungal surveys.

## Sampling Species Abundance

Two primary metrics are used to quantify fungal abundance on plant hosts:

- **Incidence** — the percentage of diseased plants or plant parts infected by a given fungus. Incidence measures presence/absence at the plant-organ or whole-plant level and is useful for community-level surveys and comparisons across sites or seasons.
- **Severity** — the percentage of host tissue affected by disease. Severity provides a more granular measure of the extent of colonization and is the preferred metric for rusts and powdery mildews, where infection can be quantified across leaf surfaces.

The choice of metric and the unit of counting depend on the infection biology of the fungus:

- **Systemic infections** (e.g., *Tilletia* spp. causing smuts): each sorus is counted as a single infection event because the fungus has colonized the host systemically, and individual lesions are not independent.
- **Localized infections** (e.g., *Colletotrichum* spp. causing anthracnose): each discrete lesion is treated as a unique infection event, since each represents an independent colonization attempt by the pathogen.

### Determining Adequate Sample Sizes

Reliable estimates of fungal abundance require sufficient sample sizes. Pilot sampling is used to establish how many samples are needed:

1. Collect preliminary data and calculate the **mean** and **standard deviation (SD)** for the target metric at each sample size increment.
2. Plot mean and SD against sample size to produce a **stability curve**.
3. At least **5 measurement points** along the curve are needed to reliably determine where values stabilize.
4. The point at which the mean stabilizes and SD plateaus indicates the minimum adequate sample size.

This approach prevents both under-sampling (unreliable estimates) and over-sampling (wasted effort).

### Specialized Sampling Techniques for Trees

Valentine and Hilton (1977) developed a **randomized branch sampling technique** for *Quercus* (oak) that provides statistically unbiased estimates of branch-level characteristics, including fungal loads. This method uses probability proportional to size to select branches, allowing extrapolation from a manageable number of sampled branches to the entire tree crown.

## Tree Canopy Access Methods

Studying fungi in tree canopies presents significant logistical challenges. Several approaches have been developed, ranging from mechanical platforms to technical rope climbing.

### Platforms and Mechanical Systems

The **Canadian Forest Service** developed and deployed platforms on Vancouver Island using systems of ropes and pulleys to access canopy microhabitats. Such platforms allow multiple researchers to work simultaneously at height and can support heavier equipment than rope-based methods.

### Rope-Based Climbing Techniques

- **Wolf (1993)** described a method for tropical rain forest canopy access using an arrow shot over a high branch, followed by a nylon rope and mechanical ascenders (jumars). This technique is now widely used in tropical canopy research and requires relatively lightweight, portable equipment.
- **Denison et al. (1972)** detailed climbing techniques and criteria for branch system selection during canopy surveys. In their experience, a team of **3 experienced climbers** can rig a tall tree in approximately **1.5 days**, after which individual ascents take only about **20 minutes**.

### Systematic Canopy Sampling Schemes

#### Pike et al. (1975) — Douglas Fir

Pike et al. conducted systematic sampling on a **450-year-old [[douglas-fir]]** (*[[pseudotsuga-menziesii]]*), dividing the tree into **6 vertical zones**:

1. Base
2. Moist side of trunk
3. Dry side of trunk
4. Upper trunk
5. Branches >4 cm diameter
6. Branchlets <4 cm diameter

Their survey yielded **74 lichen species** and **32 bryophyte species**. Quadrats were placed at **5 m intervals** along the trunk, and branch sampling used the **cylindrat method** — sampling cylindrical segments of branches at standardized positions.

#### Lesica et al. (1991) — Grand Fir Forests

Lesica et al. sampled epiphytic communities in grand-fir (*[[abies-grandis]]*) forests using a structured plot design:

- **0.081-ha circular plots** centered on a **32-m reference line**
- **Three 30-m perpendicular lines** radiating from the reference line
- Sampling conducted in **3 vertical strata**: lower canopy, trunk, and ground
- **Trunk quadrats** measured **0.5 × 0.2 m** and were placed at **1.5 m height** on both the **north and south sides** of each tree to capture microclimatic variation

## Standard Ground-Based Collection Techniques

Proper handling of plant material in the field is critical for preserving fungal specimens and ensuring successful identification or culturing.

### Collection by Plant Part

- **Herbaceous plants** — press in newspaper in a standard plant press
- **Woody branches** — cut with pruning shears or extension poles for higher branches
- **Bark and cankers** — excise with knife or chisel to include margin between healthy and diseased tissue
- **Soft fruit** — place in perforated plastic bags inside a rigid container to prevent crushing
- **Large fruit** — section before drying to ensure thorough desiccation

### Packaging and Moisture Management

- **Plastic bags** — use in wet conditions to prevent additional moisture uptake
- **Paper bags** — preferred in dry weather to allow natural air circulation
- **Fleshy tissue for culturing** — wrap in newspaper or paper bags; **never use airtight plastic**, which promotes bacterial growth
- **Humid conditions** — include desiccant (e.g., silica gel) to prevent mold overgrowth
- **Dry climates** — zipper-lock bags with perforations retain slight moisture without suffocating specimens
- **Key principle**: slight desiccation is far preferable to excess humidity, which degrades fungal structures and promotes contaminants

## Special Circumstances in Fungal Sampling

Several situations require modified or specialized approaches beyond standard techniques.

### 1. Asymptomatic Obligate Parasites

Obligate biotrophic parasites (e.g., some downy mildews and rusts) may colonize healthy-looking host tissue with **no visible symptoms**. Detecting these fungi requires:

- Constructing **species-accumulation curves** for foliage, fruits, shoots, and stems
- Examining **50 leaves per branch** across a systematic sampling grid: **4 compass quadrants** (N, S, E, W) × **3 crown levels** (lower, middle, upper)
- Microscopic examination of asymptomatic tissue for latent infections

### 2. Secondary Saprobe Overgrowth

Primary pathogen colonies are frequently overgrown by fast-growing secondary saprobes, obscuring the target fungus. To recover the primary organism:

- Excise tissue from the **zone between healthy and diseased tissue** (the advancing margin), where the primary pathogen is most active and saprobes are least established
- **Flame-sterilize cutting tools between each cut** to prevent cross-contamination
- **Surface-sterilize** tissue pieces with **10% bleach** (typically 30–60 seconds)
- Plate onto **acidified [[malt-extract-agar]]** to suppress bacterial growth while permitting fungal isolation

### 3. Unculturable Obligate Parasites

Many obligate biotrophs (e.g., rusts, powdery mildews) cannot be maintained in pure culture. For these fungi:

- **Preserve specimens quickly** after collection to maintain structural integrity
- Make **fresh macroscopic and microscopic descriptions** while the material is still viable
- **Heat-kill** specimens (e.g., brief microwave treatment or hot-air drying) and then **dry thoroughly** for long-term storage in herbaria

### 4. Immature Fruiting Bodies

Fungal fruiting bodies encountered before full maturity may not possess diagnostic features. To complete development:

- Place specimens in a **plastic bag moist chamber** (sealed bag with slightly damp paper towel)
- Incubate at ambient temperature for **1–3 days**
- **Check daily** for spore maturation and structural development

### 5. Not All Host Plants Are Infected

Kranz found that only **28–52% of individual host plants** within a population actually harbor a given fungal species. This has important implications for sampling design:

- Surveys targeting presence/absence must sample enough individual plants to detect the fungus
- Failing to account for low infection rates leads to false-negative conclusions about fungal distribution

### 6. Rusts with Alternate Hosts

Many [[rust-fungi]] (Pucciniales) have complex life cycles requiring **two unrelated host species** (heteroecism). To fully document these fungi:

- Collect **all spore stages** (spermogonia, aecia, uredinia, telia) when present
- Collect **tissues of all ages** — young, mature, and senescent — as different stages may appear at different times
- Record both the primary and alternate host species and their spatial proximity
