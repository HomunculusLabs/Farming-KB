## Soap Making Guide

Cold process soap making is the core homestead soap craft, transforming fats and oils into soap through saponification -- a chemical reaction between triglycerides and sodium hydroxide (lye). The result is a solid, long-lasting bar of soap that can be customized with scents, colors, and additives from the homestead.

## Lye Safety

Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), commonly called lye, is a caustic alkali that requires strict [[mushroom-edibility-testing-safety-protocols]]:

- Always wear safety goggles, chemical-resistant gloves, and long sleeves
- Work in a well-ventilated area -- lye fumes are irritating
- Always add lye to water, never water to lye (prevents violent reactions)
- Use a dedicated lye pitcher -- never reuse for food
- Keep vinegar nearby to neutralize lye spills on skin
- Store lye in an airtight container, labeled, away from children and pets
- If lye contacts skin, flush with cool water for 15 minutes

Potassium hydroxide (KOH) is used for liquid soap making. This guide covers sodium hydroxide for solid bar soap.

## Understanding Saponification

Saponification converts oils and lye into soap and glycerin. Every oil has a saponification value (SAP value) -- the number of grams of lye needed to convert one gram of oil to soap. Use a soap calculator to determine exact lye amounts for your recipe. Never estimate lye quantities.

The reaction is exothermic. Raw soap reaches temperatures of 160-200 F during saponification. The soap is safe to use only after the lye has fully reacted, which takes 4-6 weeks (curing time).

## Oils and Their Properties

Different oils contribute different qualities to the finished soap:

- Coconut oil (72% lauric acid): Hardness, bubbly lather, cleansing. Use at 15-30% of total oils. Too much is drying.
- [[olive-cultivation-and-olive-oil-production]]: Gentle, moisturizing, mild lather. Can be used at 100% (Castile soap) but requires 6-12 month cure.
- Palm oil: Hardness, stable lather, economical. Often replaced with lard or tallow on homesteads.
- Lard/tallow: Hardness, creamy lather, economical. Traditional homestead soap base.
- Shea butter: Conditioning, creamy lather. Use at 5-15%.
- [[permaculture-castor-oil-plant]]: Rich, bubbly lather. Use at 3-8%. Too much makes soap sticky.
- Sunflower/safflower oil: Conditioning. Use at 10-20%.
- Hemp seed oil: Conditioning, silky feel. Use at 5-10% (expensive).

## Superfatting

Superfatting is adding more oil than the lye can convert, leaving free oil in the soap for extra conditioning. A 5% superfat is standard. This means the recipe includes 5% more oil than needed for full saponification. Some soapmakers add a luxury oil (shea butter, hemp seed) at trace for a more noticeable effect, but the mathematical superfat method is more reliable.

## Equipment

- Digital scale accurate to 0.1 oz or 1 gram
- Stainless steel or HDPE pitcher for lye solution
- Large stainless steel or enamel pot for oils
- Stick blender (immersion blender)
- Silicone spatula
- Soap molds (silicone, wooden, or repurposed containers)
- Thermometer (infrared or instant-read)
- Freezer paper or plastic wrap for lining molds

## Basic Recipe

A beginner-friendly recipe yielding approximately 2 pounds of soap:

- 16 oz olive oil
- 8 oz coconut oil
- 8 oz palm oil (or lard)
- 5.9 oz distilled water
- 2.3 oz sodium hydroxide (100% purity)
- 5% superfat

This produces a balanced bar with good lather and conditioning. Always verify lye amounts with a soap calculator before making any recipe.

## Step-by-Step Process

1. Prepare your workspace and put on safety gear
2. Weigh all oils and set aside
3. Weigh distilled water in the lye pitcher
4. Slowly add weighed lye to water, stirring gently. The solution will heat to ~180 F. Set aside to cool.
5. Heat oils gently until melted and combined (~110-120 F)
6. When both lye solution and oils reach 100-110 F, slowly pour lye into oils
7. Blend with stick blender, alternating short bursts and hand stirring
8. Bring to "trace" -- the point where drizzled soap leaves a visible trail on the surface (like thin pudding)
9. Add [[comparison-essential-oils-vs-neem-oil-vs-insecticidal-soap]], herbs, or other additives at light trace
10. Pour soap into prepared mold
11. Insulate the mold with towels for 24 hours (gel phase)
12. Unmold after 24-48 hours
13. Cut into bars and cure on a rack for 4-6 weeks

## Troubleshooting

- Soap seized (hardened instantly): Too much fragrance or certain essential oils (clove, cinnamon). Work quickly and pour at thinner trace.
- Soda ash (white powdery surface): Caused by sodium carbonate formation. Spray with rubbing alcohol or cover with plastic wrap during curing.
- Soft soap: Not enough hard oils, insufficient cure time, or too much superfat.
- Lye heavy soap (burning skin): Insufficient oil or incorrect SAP calculation. Discard or rebatch.
- DOS (dark orange spots): Rancidity from excess soft oils. Use antioxidants or reformulate.

## Homestead Additives

- Beeswax from [[top-bar-hive]] operations: 1-2% for hardness
- Calendula-infused oil from [[herbal-salves-and-balms]]: skin soothing properties
- Herbs from the garden: lavender buds, rosemary, chamomile
- Milk (goat milk from your herd): replace water with frozen milk for a creamy, gentle soap
- Honey: 1 tsp per pound of oils for extra lather and conditioning
- Oatmeal: ground colloidal oatmeal for exfoliation
