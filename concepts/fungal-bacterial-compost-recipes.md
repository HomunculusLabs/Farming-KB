## Fungal vs Bacterial Compost Recipes

The microbial makeup of [[compost]] is not random — it can be deliberately steered by choosing the right ingredients. Understanding how to manipulate compost for fungal or bacterial dominance is a core skill in working with the [[soil-food-web]].

## Why Microbial Balance Matters

Different plants thrive with different microbial communities. Trees, shrubs, and perennials generally prefer fungal-dominated soils, while annual vegetables, flowers, and lawns do best with bacterial dominance. By adjusting your compost recipe, you can produce an inoculant tailored to the plants you're growing.

## Manipulating Fungal vs Bacterial Dominance

The key lever is the ratio of **green materials** to **brown materials**:

- **Green materials** (grass clippings, fresh kitchen scraps, manure) — rich in simple sugars and nitrogen. These feed [[bacteria-and-microorganisms-in-mushroom-fruiting]] and promote bacterial dominance.
- **Brown materials** (dry leaves, wood chips, bark, cardboard) — rich in lignin, cellulose, and tannins (with some nitrogen). [[fungi-in-garden-ecosystems]] have the enzymes needed to break these complex compounds down. Only after fungi have begun decomposing browns can bacteria attack them.

**Rule of thumb:** More brown materials → more fungi. More green materials → more bacteria.

## Fungal Compost Recipe

| Ingredient | Proportion |
|---|---|
| Alfalfa meal | 5–10% |
| Fresh grass clippings | 45–50% |
| Brown leaves or small wood chips | 40–50% |

The higher proportion of woody/brown material selects for fungal decomposers. Use this compost around trees, shrubs, and perennial plantings.

## Bacterial Compost Recipe

| Ingredient | Proportion |
|---|---|
| Alfalfa meal | 25% |
| Green grass clippings | 50% |
| Brown leaves or bark | 25% |

The nitrogen-rich green materials fuel rapid bacterial growth. This compost is ideal for vegetables, annual flowers, and lawns.

## pH Effects of Microbial Dominance

The microbial community in your compost also influences pH:

- **Bacteria** tend to buffer pH around **7.0–7.5** (neutral to slightly alkaline).
- **Fungi** buffer pH around **5.5–7.0** (slightly acidic to neutral).

You want *some* fungi in all composts to prevent excessive alkalinity. More fungal material means lower overall pH. This is another reason to include at least some brown material even in bacterial composts.

## Practical Composting Guidelines

### Chemical Contamination

Inorganic fertilizers, pesticides, herbicides, miticides, and fungicides all kill members of the [[soil-food-web]]. Ensure all compost ingredients are free of these chemicals — they undermine the very organisms you're trying to cultivate.

### Material Size

Particle size affects aeration and decomposition rate:

- **Too fine** → pile compresses, goes anaerobic.
- **Too big** → excessive air diffusion, pile overheats.
- **Too large** → won't decompose fast enough.

Finding the right size requires experimentation with your specific materials.

### Pile Mass

- **Minimum size:** ~3.5 feet square (roughly 1 cubic meter) to generate and retain heat properly.
- **Maximum practical size:** ~6 feet wide and tall without mechanical turning equipment.

### Compost Setup Options

- **Pile on the ground** — simplest approach; allows direct soil contact for organism exchange.
- **Caged area** — fencing or chicken wire, 3 ft diameter, 4–5 ft high.
- **Pallet/screen base** — elevates the pile for better air circulation underneath.
- **Revolving bins** — convenient turning, but watch for moisture problems (easy to dry out or get saturated).

### Moisture Management

Layer green and brown materials 4–6 inches thick, moistening each layer as you build. Use the **squeeze test**: grab a handful of compost and squeeze — you should get just a few drops of water, no more. Don't let the pile dry out or become waterlogged.

### Temperature Monitoring

Keep the pile between **104°F and 150°F**. Below 104°F, decomposition slows significantly. Above 150°F, you begin killing beneficial organisms including [[actinomycetes]] and other thermophiles.

## "Compost for the Lazy"

A simple, low-effort approach:

1. Start with 3 cubic yards of brown leaves.
2. Add one 50-lb bag of alfalfa meal (or use equal volumes of grass clippings and leaves).
3. Shred leaves for faster bacterial action.
4. Layer: 4 inches of leaves, then 4 inches of alfalfa or grass, repeat.
5. Add sticks and branches throughout for air circulation.
6. Monitor temperature — keep it between 104°F and 150°F.

## Cool / Cold Composting

The easiest method: just pile organic matter and leave it. Trade-offs compared to hot composting:

- Takes **a year or more** vs. weeks to months for hot composting.
- Supports **higher populations** of worms, beetles, millipedes, and micro/macroarthropods.
- Greater overall biodiversity.

Keep a cold compost pile going at all times — the diversity it fosters benefits the entire garden ecosystem.

## Vermicompost

[[vermicompost]] is organic matter processed through earthworms. Key characteristics:

- **Always bacterially dominated** — there are few or no fungi in worm digestion.
- **No heat** — elevated temperatures kill worms, so vermicomposting is a cold process.
- Worms and their gut bacteria digest materials and produce castings coated with polysaccharides, carbohydrates, and simple proteins — perfect food for bacterial populations.

### Starting Materials for Vermicompost

- Food wastes (no fats or meats)
- Shredded paper and cardboard
- Leaves and grass clippings

### Vermicompost Tips

- Thermally compost weeds before adding them to the worm bin (to kill seeds).
- Shred brown materials to speed digestion.
- Keep the bin outdoors to allow arthropod activity, which supports a more complete [[soil-food-web]].

## Soil Inoculation with Compost

The ultimate goal of making targeted compost is inoculating your soil with the right microbial community:

- Apply **¼ to 1 inch** of the appropriate compost around plants.
- **Fungal compost** for trees, shrubs, and perennials.
- **Bacterial compost** for vegetables, flowers, and lawns.

### Timeline of Results

| Timeframe | Effect |
|---|---|
| ~6 months | New soil life evident in top 6–15 inches |
| ~1 year | Soil life extends to ~18 inches deep |

### Benefits of Compost Inoculation

- **Decompaction** — fungal hyphae and root growth break up compacted soil.
- **Aeration** — improved soil structure increases air exchange.
- **Water retention and drainage** — organic matter acts as a sponge while improving pore space.
- **Nutrient retention and availability** — microbial communities hold nutrients in plant-available forms, reducing leaching and the need for external inputs.
