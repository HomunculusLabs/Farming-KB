# Enokitake (Flammulina velutipes) Cultivation

Flammulina velutipes, commonly known as the Enoke or Winter Mushroom, is a
widely cultivated species prized for its long, slender stems and small caps.
In wild collections the stems are short and velvety, but in culture the
stems elongate dramatically, producing the distinctive enokitake form
familiar in Japanese cuisine. The species epithet velutipes refers to its
velvet-covered foot, while Flammulina derives from the Latin "flammeus"
for the yellowish-orange to reddish-orange cap coloration.

## Natural Habitat and Distribution

F. velutipes is common across the North American continent and in other
temperate to boreal regions worldwide. It thrives on woody tissue,
especially living trees, and is notably a cold-weather mushroom that
fruits in late autumn through winter. This cold tolerance makes it unique
among cultivated species and well-suited to unheated growing environments
during colder months.

## Strains

Many wild and domesticated strains are available from commercial and
private stocks. Japanese growers have led enokitake cultivation with two
popular commercial strains: "Maruei" and "Ebios." Strains vary in color
intensity, stem length, and yield potential. Selection should consider the
intended market and growing conditions.

## Growth Parameters

### Mycelial Characteristics

The mycelium ranges from linear to cottony, sometimes aerial. Notably,
F. velutipes tends to form mycelial "pellets" soon after colonizing a
substrate, a phenomenon that complicates [[stamets-cultivator-liquid-culture-propagation-methods]] techniques. Japanese
researchers found that adding 5% corn starch and 2% malt to a liquid
solution inhibits pellet formation.

### Spawn Medium

Sawdust and bran is the preferred spawn medium, as described in
[[stamets-spawn-making-and-grain-culture]]. One liter
(1000 ml) bottle of spawn inoculates 50-160 containers of 800 ml each.
Grain spawn is generally not recommended for this species.

### Fruiting Substrate

The recommended substrate is 80-90% hardwood sawdust and 10-20% rice bran
at 58-60% moisture content. A 4:1 volumetric ratio of sawdust to bran
(equivalent to a 10:1 mass ratio) is standard. Newly chipped sawdust holds
moisture poorly, so some Japanese growers age sawdust for several years
before use. Standard fruiting containers are quart mason jars or 800 ml
small-mouthed plastic bottles. Some growers experiment with bulk substrates
in trays. Sterilize at 250 degrees F (15 psi) for 1-2 hours.

### Spawn Run

- Relative Humidity: 90-100%
- Substrate Temperature: 72-77 degrees F
- Duration: 20-30 days standard; 12-13 days with in vitro inoculation
- CO2: 5,000-10,000 ppm
- Fresh Air Exchanges: 0 per hour
- Casing: None required

### Primordia Formation

- Relative Humidity: 85%
- Air Temperature: 50-55 degrees F (cold shock is essential)
- CO2: less than 1,000 ppm
- Fresh Air Exchanges: 4 per hour
- Light: None needed for initiation

### Cropping Cycle

- Relative Humidity: 85%
- Air Temperature: 50-55 degrees F
- Duration: 2-3 weeks
- CO2: less than 1,000 ppm
- Fresh Air Exchanges: 4 per hour
- Light: Natural daylight or grow-light on 12-hour on/off cycle
- Flushing Interval: 10 days

## Yield Potential and Nutrition

Average commercial yields are 160-220 grams per 800 ml bottle, with
maximum yields approaching 600 grams. The mushrooms are 92% water and 8%
dry matter. Protein content ranges from 18-31% (dry weight) with 107 mg
niacin per 100 grams dry weight. An anti-tumor antibiotic called
flammulin has been isolated from this species.

## Advanced Techniques

Shilo et al. (1974) found that fresh pieces of F. velutipes mixed
directly into liquid spawn and introduced into sawdust/bran medium
accelerated fruiting, halved the spawn run period, and nearly quadrupled
annual yields. Urayama (1972) discovered that cell-free extracts of fresh
F. velutipes mushrooms introduced to distantly related species caused
fruitbody formation, suggesting the presence of fruiting hormones.

## Genetic Characteristics

Basidia are tetrapolar (4-spored), forming haploid spores; the species
is bifactorially heterothallic. Single spore isolates can produce sterile
fruitbodies. Dikaryons grow faster and exhibit clamp connections. The
mycelium can produce oidia, self-sectioning chains of cells with spore-like
functions. See [[stamets-mushroom-genetics-mating-systems-reproduction]].
