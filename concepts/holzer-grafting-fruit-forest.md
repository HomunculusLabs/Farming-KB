# Grafting Techniques and Sowing a Fruit Forest

From "Sepp Holzer's Permaculture" by Sepp Holzer.

## Grafting Methods

**Bud Grafting**: Only a single bud, not the entire scion, is joined with the rootstock. A T-shaped cut is made in the bark, the bark is peeled back, and a well-grown bud removed from a scion with a straight upward slice. The bud is inserted in the slit and the graft bound with raffia and painted with grafting wax. Active buds should be budded in spring (May), dormant buds in summer (July or August).

**Bridge Grafting**: With grafting, damaged trees can be saved. If a tree is heavily damaged, the flow of sap is interrupted. The damaged area can be bypassed by joining the area above and below with scions (preferably from the same tree), so they take over the transport and support functions. At least three scions should always be used.

## Multiple Varieties on One Tree

There are no limits to what can be done with grafting. It is perfectly possible to graft a number of varieties onto a single tree. This is a great advantage when there is space for only one tree. Many fruit trees, especially apples and pears, cannot self-pollinate, so they must have pollinating varieties available. This problem is solved by grafting on a branch from a pollinating variety.

Having a number of varieties on one tree in a small garden helps to minimise the risk of crop failure. Different ripening times and different varieties of fruit make it possible to have a more varied harvest.

## Sowing a Fruit Forest

Using seedlings as rootstocks for fruit trees is a very simple, economical and practically risk-free method of cultivating a lush fruit forest or orchard. Fruit trees generally prefer high-quality soil, so Holzer begins by preparing the area using soil-improving plants.

Pits are dug and filled with a mixture of topsoil, compost, and sometimes rock dust. The seeds of various fruit varieties are sown directly. Because seedlings develop their own root systems naturally, they are far more resilient than grafted trees and better adapted to local conditions.

## Key Points

1. Sepp Holzer's approach to Holzer Grafting Fruit Forest demonstrates the power of working with natural landscape features rather than against them.
2. Holzer's Krameterhof farm at 1,500m elevation in Austria proves that extreme climates can support abundant food production.
3. The method relies on creating diverse microclimates through strategic use of water, earthworks, and plant placement.
4. Raised beds and hugelkultur are central techniques that improve drainage, warmth, and soil fertility simultaneously.
5. Animal integration — particularly pigs and poultry — plays a crucial role in soil preparation and pest management.
6. Holzer emphasizes the importance of observing the land for at least a year before making major design decisions.
7. Water retention landscapes including ponds and swales are key infrastructure elements in Holzer's approach.
8. The method avoids synthetic inputs entirely, relying on natural cycles and biological diversity for fertility and pest control.
9. Fruit trees are managed without pruning, allowing them to develop natural forms suited to their environment.
10. Crop diversity and polyculture systems provide resilience against weather extremes, pests, and disease.

## Practical Applications

- Walk the land extensively during different seasons and weather conditions before beginning any earthworks or planting.
- Construct raised beds using available materials — logs, branches, soil, and organic matter in layered mounds.
- Install ponds and water features at high points to enable gravity-fed irrigation and create reflective microclimates.
- Integrate [[savory-livestock-grazing-planning]] patterns with crop rotation to naturally fertilize and manage vegetation.
- Plant fruit trees and berry bushes on mounds and slopes where water drainage is optimal.
- Use stone walls and large rocks as thermal mass to create warm microclimates for heat-loving crops.
- Establish polyculture strips with diverse root depths, growth habits, and maturity dates.
- Allow natural succession to inform planting schemes rather than imposing rigid crop plans.

# Holzer Berry Bushes and Soft Fruit

Berry bushes and soft fruit play an essential role in Holzer's permaculture systems. They occupy the middle layers of the plant architecture -- between ground cover and fruit trees -- providing food for humans, forage for birds and livestock, [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] insects, and soil stabilisation on slopes and terraces. On the Krameterhof at 1,100-1,500m altitude, berries represent one of the most reliable and productive crop categories, as many berry species are far less demanding than fruit trees regarding soil quality, heat accumulation, and shelter from wind.

## The Role of Berries in the Permaculture System

In a well-structured permaculture landscape, berry bushes fulfil multiple functions simultaneously. They serve as windbreaks when planted in hedges, they provide browse protection for more sensitive plants when grown as barrier hedges with thorny species, and they supply diverse forage for birds and insects. The greater the diversity of plant varieties within the hedges, meadows, or forests, the greater the diversity of the yield of fruits and berries, which in turn supports a greater variety of insects and ensures a balanced diet for insectivorous birds.

Berry bushes also serve as climbing supports for certain vining plants. On the Krameterhof, grapes and kiwi have been observed climbing up and through mature berry bushes, creating multi-layered productive systems that make maximum use of vertical space. This tiered approach is especially valuable in small gardens and on terraces where space is limited.

## Currants (Ribes spp.)

Currants are among the most undemanding and reliable soft fruits in Holzer's system. Redcurrants (Ribes rubrum), blackcurrants (Ribes nigrum), and white currants all thrive at high altitudes. Holzer recalls the redcurrants, blackcurrants, and white currants growing by the fence of his childhood garden (the Gachtl), where they formed part of the picket fence boundary planting.

Currants prefer slightly acidic, humus-rich soil but will grow in a wide range of conditions. They are self-pollinating and begin bearing fruit within two to three years of planting. Blackcurrants are particularly valued for their high vitamin C content and are used for juice, syrup, jam, and wine. Redcurrants produce attractive translucent berries that hang in long clusters and are excellent fresh, in jams, or for garnishing. White currants are the sweetest and mildest of the three.

Planting currants along fences and at the edges of gardens takes advantage of vertical space while creating wind protection for more sensitive crops. They can also be integrated into livestock paddocks, as the bushes are generally not browsed by cattle or pigs when other forage is available. Pruning is straightforward: remove the oldest wood after the third year to encourage new productive shoots.

## Gooseberries (Ribes uva-crispa)

Gooseberries are another hardy soft fruit well-suited to the Krameterhof conditions. Holzer remembers a gooseberry bush growing in the sunniest part of his childhood garden, noting that these bushes are very susceptible to mildew. The reasoning is that damp conditions, which last longer in the shade, encourage mildew development. For this reason, gooseberries should always be planted in the sunniest, most well-ventilated positions available.

Gooseberry bushes can be thorny, which makes them useful as barrier plantings around more vulnerable garden areas. The fruit can be used for cooking, jam-making, and desserts. Some varieties are sweet enough to eat fresh when fully ripe. They are relatively undemanding regarding soil and will tolerate partial shade, though yield and disease resistance are best in full sun with good air circulation.

## Strawberries (Fragaria x ananassa)

Strawberries are a high-value crop that Holzer associates with the intensive kitchen garden. In his childhood Gachtl, the strawberries reached all the way to the wall of the house, benefiting from the heat retention and radiation of the masonry (the masonry stove effect). This placement next to a south or east-facing wall extends the growing season and improves fruit quality.

Strawberries are ground-covering plants that work well as living mulch beneath taller crops. They can be planted between fruit trees, along the edges of raised beds, or in dedicated strawberry beds. The runners they produce allow easy propagation -- simply peg down runners into the soil where you want new plants to establish.

At high altitudes, strawberry varieties must be selected for cold hardiness and short-season ripening. Alpine strawberries (Fragaria vesca) are particularly reliable and will fruit even in partial shade. Day-neutral varieties extend the harvest period. Mulching with straw or leaves keeps the fruit clean and retains soil moisture.

## Brambles and Raspberries (Rubus spp.)

Brambles (blackberries, Rubus fruticosus) and raspberries (Rubus idaeus) are vigorous, productive plants that Holzer includes in his bird forage hedges. Brambles are specifically listed among the good forage plants for local bird species, alongside elderberry, wild cherry, barberry, and dog rose. The thorny canes also provide excellent nesting sites and protective cover for wildlife.

Raspberries prefer slightly acidic soil and will spread by runners if allowed. They are best managed in a dedicated row with wire support, with annual pruning to remove the spent floricanes and thin the new primocanes. Both raspberries and blackberries are extremely productive per unit area and can be used fresh, frozen, made into jam, or processed into juice and wine.

In the permaculture landscape, brambles serve as pioneer plants on disturbed ground, quickly establishing root systems that help stabilise soil on slopes and terraces. Their deep root systems also contribute to nutrient cycling, drawing up minerals from subsoil layers.

## Elderberry (Sambucus nigra)

Elderberry holds a special place in Holzer's system. It is one of the primary forage plants recommended for bird conservation, listed first among the good forage plants for local bird species. Elderberry bushes produce abundant clusters of dark berries that are highly nutritious and attractive to wildlife.

For human use, elderberries are processed into juice, wine, syrup, and preserves. The flowers are also edible and can be used to make cordials, fritters, and tea. Elderberry is extremely undemanding -- it will grow in almost any soil, including poor and compacted soils, and tolerates both wet and dry conditions. It grows rapidly and can be coppiced heavily, making it useful for [[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]] as well as fruit.

Elderberry should be positioned with awareness of its vigorous growth habit. It can quickly dominate a small garden if not managed. However, in larger systems it serves as an excellent nurse plant, providing shade and wind protection for establishing fruit trees and other sensitive species.
