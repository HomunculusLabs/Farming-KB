## Stable Isotopes in Fungal Ecology — ¹⁵N and ¹³C as Trophic Indicators

## Overview

The [[gadd-saprotrophic-fungi-15n-13c-natural-abundance-isotopes]] of stable isotopes — particularly ¹⁵N and ¹³C — provides a powerful, indirect method for investigating [[fungal-ecology]] in situ, where direct observation of cryptic mycelia is often impossible. Isotope ratios in fungal sporocarps reflect the substrates utilized, the trophic strategy (saprotrophic vs. ectomycorrhizal), and the ecophysiology of individual species. The "saprotrophic–mycorrhizal divide" in isotope space has become a key tool for determining the functional roles of unknown fungal taxa.

## Stable Isotope Terminology

Isotope ratios are expressed in delta (δ) units (parts per thousand, ‰) relative to international standards:

- **δ¹⁵N**: Deviation from atmospheric N₂ standard. Range in ecosystems: ~−10‰ to +20‰.
- **δ¹³C**: Deviation from Vienna–PeeDee Belemnite standard. Biological materials have negative values; range in plants: −40‰ to −9‰.

δ values represent the average of all N and C in the tissue, but different chemical components (proteins, chitin, cellulose, lignin) can differ considerably in their isotope signatures.

## The Saprotrophic–Mycorrhizal Divide

### ¹⁵N Enrichment in ECM Fungi

A consistent and surprising finding across multiple studies:

- ECM fungi are **enriched in ¹⁵N** compared with their host plants by 2.4‰–6.4‰.
- This is paradoxical because >95% of root tips are typically colonized, meaning most N entering the host must pass through the fungi first.
- **Explanation**: Small discrimination against ¹⁵N during N transfer from fungus to host results in ¹⁵N accumulation within ECM mycelium.
- ECM species with high δ¹⁵N values tend to have **greater ability to degrade and utilize organic N sources**.

### ECM vs. Saprotrophic Fungi

| Parameter | Saprotrophic | ECM |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean δ¹⁵N** | 1.86‰ | 5.83‰ |
| **Mean δ¹³C** | −23.2‰ | −25.6‰ |
| **%N** | 4.35% | 3.96% |
| **%C** | 41.5% | 43.4% |

A joint plot of δ¹³C and δ¹⁵N usually results in **clear separation** between the two trophic groups. This "saprotrophic–mycorrhizal divide" has been used to determine the trophic status of fungi where this was unknown.

### Radiocarbon (¹⁴C) Evidence

Hobbie et al. (2002) used bomb-carbon (¹⁴C) dating:

- Saprotrophic sporocarps contained carbon photosynthetically fixed **>6 years previously**.
- ECM sporocarps contained carbon only **0–2 years old**.
- This provides very strong evidence for trophic status but is prohibitively expensive.

## Isotopic Fractionation During Metabolism

### Mechanisms

Isotopic fractionation occurs because heavier isotopes react more slowly and are more strongly bound in chemical reactions. Fungal species may differ in how they fractionate during uptake and metabolic transformation.

### ¹³C Enrichment in Wood-Decay Fungi

Kohzu et al. (1999) found consistent ¹³C enrichment of ~3.5‰ between wood substrate and fungal mycelium:

- Laboratory inoculation of *[[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]]* on wood blocks confirmed enrichment (ε = 3.5 ± 0.5‰).
- Wood δ¹³C remained remarkably constant (−26.3‰) even with 70% mass loss over 181 days.
- Respired CO₂ had δ¹³C similar to wood, suggesting respiration had little effect.
- **Explanation**: Enrichment relates to differences in cellular components — chitin (fungal cell walls) is enriched in ¹³C relative to woody substrates.

### Species-Specific Fractionation

Abraham & Hesse (2003) examined four fungal species (zygomycetes and ascomycetes):

- Differences found both in fractionation on uptake and in ¹³C enrichment of amino acids.
- Discriminant analysis of isotope ratios in four amino acids (Thr, Ile, Phe, Val) divided fungi into their respective groups.
- **Implication**: Could potentially be used to investigate selective feeding on fungal species by soil fungivores.
- **Complication**: If fungi differ markedly in fractionation, interpreting δ values concerning original substrates becomes difficult.

### Henn & Chapela (2000) vs. Hobbie et al. (2004)

- Henn & Chapela found taxon-specific fractionation related to distribution of heavier isotopes within sucrose molecules, suggesting limited usefulness of stable isotopes.
- Hobbie et al. found little influence of fractionation on δ values in closed systems; saprotroph–ECM differences were attributed to utilization of ¹³C-depleted agar (up to 45% of carbon).
- The Henn & Chapela results may be partly explained by unaccounted agar utilization.

## Terricolous vs. Lignicolous Saprotrophic Fungi

| Parameter | Terricolous | Lignicolous |
|---|---|---|
| **%N** | 5.87% | 3.25% |
| **δ¹⁵N** | 2.58‰ | 0.74‰ |
| **δ¹³C** | −23.23‰ | −23.06‰ |

- Terricolous fungi have higher N content and δ¹⁵N, reflecting utilization of more decomposed (¹⁵N-enriched) material.
- Wood-decomposing fungi show little enrichment (ε = 0.4‰) because low N concentrations in wood force extraction of **all available N** (closed system: no fractionation measurable).
- No consistent differences found between **white-rot** and **brown-rot** fungi, possibly masked by differences in woody substrate isotope composition.

## Notable Exceptions and Ambiguous Cases

- ***Agaricus*** species had high δ¹⁵N (5.56‰–9.92‰) despite being saprotrophic — likely reflects utilization of ¹⁵N-enriched substrates in mineral soil layers.
- ***Entoloma clypeatum***: High δ¹⁵N + low δ¹³C suggests mutualistic status despite being classified as saprotrophic.
- ***[[clavariadelphus-pistillaris]]***: Very high δ¹⁵N (11.52‰) + ambiguous δ¹³C suggests possible ECM status.
- ***[[fistulina-hepatica]]***: Lowest δ¹³C (−26.4‰) — may indicate access to phloem sugars from host oak.

## Environmental and Methodological Considerations

- **[[dighton-nitrogen-deposition-mycorrhizal-community-shifts]]**: Elevated N deposition significantly impacts average δ¹⁵N and narrows the range of potential values.
- **Soil horizon**: The horizon in which mycelium exists has significant influence on δ values.
- **Sporocarp vs. mycelium**: Most studies use sporocarps; whether these reflect mycelial values is assumed but not always verified.
- **Chemical components**: Proteins and amino acids can be enriched by up to 9‰ compared with cell wall chitin within the same fungus.
- **Site effects**: Taxonomic composition of the fungal community significantly impacts average isotope values at any given site.

## Future Applications

1. **Succession studies**: Determine whether late colonizers utilize mycelium of earlier species vs. wood components directly.
2. **Insect–fungus associations**: Analyse δ¹⁵N and δ¹³C in wood-boring beetle larvae, wood, and mycelium to determine the relative importance of feeding on decayed wood vs. fungal mycelium.
3. **Fungivore specificity**: Species-level isotope patterns could help investigate selective feeding by soil fungivores.
4. **Mycelial localization**: Combine mycelial mapping techniques with isotope analysis of organic material in specific soil layers.
