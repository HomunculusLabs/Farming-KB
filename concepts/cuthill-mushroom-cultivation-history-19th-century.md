## Economic Context

Mushrooms commanded high prices in the 19th-century London market. Cuthill
notes that thirty years before writing, one pottle (about a pound) could
realise 10 shillings, and even at the time of writing occasionally a
pound sold for 5 shillings in Covent Garden Market. He describes a
mushroom grown in May weighing eleven ounces, and another from July
1856 with a circumference of three feet six inches and weight of four
and a half pounds, valued at twenty shillings.

He valued artificially-grown bed mushrooms at three times the worth of
field mushrooms for any purpose, noting that properly made beds last
three to four times longer in bearing than field sources.

## Broader Significance

Cuthill placed mushrooms as "second only to beef and mutton" among
foods, and "the last and connecting link between the vegetable and the
animal creation." He advocated for cottagers to grow their own,
estimating savings of "a shilling or two week" during winter, and
noting that "mushrooms grow faster than pigs! and the mushrooms do not
eat anything; they only want a little attention."

The treatise sits within a broader Victorian tradition of practical
horticultural manuals published by Groombridge and Sons, which also
issued works by Shirley Hibberd on ferns, roses, and town gardening.
