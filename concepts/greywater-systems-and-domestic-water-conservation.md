# Greywater Systems and Domestic Water Conservation

## Overview

Domestic [[permaculture-design]] addresses the significant misuse of potable water for tasks like toilet flushing, which accounts for approximately 40% of indoor domestic water use. Permaculture approaches treat wastewater as a resource rather than a disposal problem, routing greywater from kitchens, bathrooms, and laundries to productive garden and tree systems.

## Toilet Water Conservation

### Handbasin-to-Toilet Diversion Systems

A simple but highly effective conservation strategy involves diverting handbasin wastewater directly into the toilet flush tank. In Australia, toilet units with moulded-in handbasins are commercially available (referenced as Figure 7.31 in Mollison's manual). This approach:

- Eliminates the use of clean potable water for flushing entirely
- The handbasin water volume approximately equals toilet flush requirements
- Holding tanks can contain several flushes, with excess draining to the toilet
- **Critical requirement**: Low-flush toilet bowls must be used, as standard bowls flush incompletely with reduced water volume, leading to heavy pathogenic bacteria buildup

This single intervention addresses 40% of domestic water misuse while encouraging hand cleanliness over the "false cleanliness" of excessive toilet flushing.

### Dry Toilets

Dry (composting) toilets are situationally appropriate but not universally recommended in [[bee-forage-systems-permaculture-design]]. They are specifically useful where:

- No methane (biogas) digestion system is in place
- Sewage is not used in plant production systems
- Soils do not suit septic tank installation
- Cities face critical water supply problems

Dry toilets are considered unnecessary on farms, in well-drained soils, or wherever sewage is productively used to generate methane through [[anaerobic-digestion-biogas-production]]. In permaculture thinking, dry toilets actually **reduce the potential uses** of sewage — just as compost is a reduction in the potential use of mulch. The preference is always to keep waste streams productive.

## Greywater Composition and Risks

Understanding what enters greywater is essential for safe routing. A typical powdered detergent or soap may contain:

- **Salts**: Sodium or potassium salts, polysulphates, silicates, sulphates, bicarbonates
- **Borates**: Problematic above 0.5 ppm in soils; above 1.0 ppm harmful to soil life and plants
- **Residual biocides**: Concentrated in animal fats — DDT, Dieldrin, Hexachlorophene from dairy cattle
- **Additives**: Resins (hardeners), scents, dyes, optical brighteners
- **Biological contaminants**: Faecal bacteria, viral and worm pathogens from showering and clothes washing

(Data source: [[kevin-handreck]], CSIRO Division of Soils, personal communication 1979)

## Soil and Plant Interactions

Most greywater contaminants can be processed by soil organisms, but two categories require special attention:

### Salinity Risks

Where the base water supply is already saline, sodium and potassium salts from detergents can:
- Deflocculate soil clays (destroy soil structure)
- Damage plant leaves at concentrations exceeding 1,000 ppm

### Boron Toxicity

Borates above 0.5 ppm create excessive boron concentrations in soils, and levels above 1.0 ppm are harmful to both soil life and plants.

## Routing Strategies

### Plain Soaps for Food Crops

Whenever possible, plain (unperfumed, unadditived) soaps should be used on food crop systems to minimise contaminant loading.

### Tree Systems for Complex Pollutants

More complex pollutants (biocides, brighteners, resins) should be routed to woody perennial tree systems rather than [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]] because:
- Woody perennials can cope better with pollutant loads
- Longer timeframes allow decomposition of long-term pollutants
- Trees are not directly consumed by humans
- Deep root systems provide greater soil filtration depth

## Arid and Sensitive Environments

In critical areas — especially arid or ecologically delicate environments — additional measures may be necessary:

1. **Special soaps**: Made from unpolluted oils with potash or sodium bases only
2. **Phytoremediation crops**: Water plants (aquatic species) that remove excess salts and contaminants before greywater reaches soils and streams

There is no blanket policy for [[greywater-management-and-dry-toilets]] — only site-specific strategies where yield can be gained and water simultaneously cleaned, provided the composition of both soils and soaps is understood.

## Key Principles

- Water is too valuable to waste on single-pass toilet flushing
- Wastewater composition must be understood before routing decisions are made
- Simpler soaps enable more productive reuse pathways
- Woody perennials provide a safer receiving environment than food crops
- Each site demands its own analysis — no one-size-fits-all solution exists
- The goal is to close the water loop: conserve, treat biologically, and redirect productively
