# Pattern Applications in Permaculture Design

## Overview

[[pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]] extends beyond philosophical frameworks into practical design applications. By observing and working with natural patterns — spirals, branches, nets, circles, and waves — designers can create systems that are more efficient, productive, and resilient than conventional rectilinear approaches. This concept covers specific pattern-based designs including herb spirals, circle-mulch grow pits, flowform water treatment systems, and flood-plain interceptors.

## Two Aspects of Patterning

Mollison identifies two complementary skills in pattern-based design:

1. **Pattern perception**: Recognizing the patterns that already exist in landscapes and understanding how they function
2. **Pattern imposition**: Applying pattern to sites to achieve specific ends — creating strategies, resolving problems, or producing local resources

Both skills are essential for sophisticated design. The first requires observation and analysis; the second requires creativity and understanding of how patterns translate into functional outcomes.

## Critique of Rectilinear Patterning

A bird's-eye view of centralized societies reveals strictly rectilinear networks of streets, farms, and property boundaries. Mollison argues that this approach patterns the earth to suit survey instruments rather than to serve human or environmental needs, resulting in:

- Landscapes that are neither aesthetically nor functionally satisfactory
- Streetscapes that resist sensible planning strategies
- Patterns that, once codified into law, are stubbornly defended despite their inadequacy

Rectilinear patterns are created by people and can be dismantled by people. The alternative is to pattern the land according to natural forms and ecological function.

## The Herb Spiral

The [[herb-spiral]], evolved by Mollison in 1978, is a practical demonstration of pattern applied to a kitchen-door garden. It is a three-dimensional earth construct that compactly coils a linear herb bed into a single mound.

### Physical Specifications

- **Base diameter**: 2 meters
- **Height**: 1 to 1.3 meters
- **Ramp length**: Exceeds 9 meters of plantable space
- **Irrigation**: One 2-meter sprinkler waters the entire spiral
- **Access**: All herbs on the spiral ramp are accessible without stepping on the planting area

### Microclimate Zones

The ascending spiral creates a gradient of conditions from bottom to top:

| Zone | Conditions | Suitable Herbs |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| Top / outer | Sunny, dry, well-drained | Thyme, sage, rosemary, oregano |
| Middle | Moderate sun, moderate moisture | Basil, tarragon, dill |
| Bottom / inner | Shaded, moist, cool | Mint, parsley, chives, coriander |

### Design Principles Demonstrated

- **Space compaction**: A 2-meter diameter circle replaces a 9+ meter linear bed
- **Water efficiency**: One sprinkler replaces multiple linear sprinklers
- **Microclimate creation**: The spiral form naturally creates diverse growing conditions
- **Accessibility**: All plants are reachable from a standing position on one side
- **[[edge-effect]]**: The spiral maximizes the edge between different microclimates, increasing productivity

### Variations

- **Balcony gardens**: Stepped walls, wall shelves, window boxes, or stacked pots in earth mounds for kitchens not at ground level
- **Roof gardens**: Adapted versions for rooftop growing spaces
- **Larger spirals**: Scaled-up versions for community gardens or market gardens

## Circle-Mulch Grow Pits

Pattern analysis applied to [[water-conservation]] yields the circle-mulch grow pit — a design that dramatically out-performs row cropping in both water use and production.

### Design

- **Central pit**: 60 cm wide and deep, filled with mulch
- **Planting shelf**: Surrounds the central pit
- **Spill bank**: Outer edge, creating total diameter of 1.2 meters
- **Perimeter**: 3.8 meters
- **Irrigation**: One low-pressure sprinkler (compared to three for a 3.8-meter straight row)

### How It Works

- **Central mulch pit**: Provides deep organic matter, moisture retention, and nutrient cycling
- **Self-shading**: Plants eventually overshade the center, preventing evaporation
- **Water concentration**: Circular form concentrates water in the mulch pit
- **Edge advantage**: The circular perimeter maximizes edge relative to area

### Scale Applications

- **Standard garden beds**: 1.2 meters (4 feet) across for vegetables and herbs
- **Banana circles**: 1.8 meters (6 feet) across
- **Coconut circles**: 1.8–3 meters (6–10 feet) across

### Performance

Circle-mulch systems out-produce row crops for approximately **one-third of the water use**. A series of such gardens greatly reduces:

- Path space needed between beds
- Total land area required
- Water consumption
- Mulch requirements (center pit serves as mulch source)
- Exposure to wind (circular form is naturally sheltered)

## Flood-Plain Interceptors

Patterned ground designed to direct flow and capture materials in flow represents a field-scale application of pattern design. These occur naturally as rock dykes or resistant rock strata and can be intentionally constructed using tree lines, embankments, or both combined.

### Design Elements

- **Embankments**: Direct floodwater flow to specific areas
- **Tree rows**: Poplar, willow, or tamarack lines that scour out river sand and create scour holes
- **Collector-deflectors**: Structures that bring mulch, firewood, and silt to the plain edge
- **Sub-surface options**: Elements can be placed below ground level

### Functions

- **Silt capture**: Floodwaters deposit nutrient-rich silt in targeted areas
- **Mulch collection**: Floating organic matter is captured for garden use
- **Firewood capture**: Driftwood and flood-deposited wood accumulate at interceptors
- **Water distribution**: Floodwaters are directed to areas where they benefit production
- **Sand scouring**: Tree rows help keep scour holes open for drainage
- **Partial flooding**: Controlled flooding of specific crop areas

### Ecological Basis

These patterns mirror natural stream dynamics where resistant rock strata create meanders, pools, and deposition zones. By working with rather than against flood dynamics, these interceptors turn a potentially destructive force into a productive resource.

## Flowform Water Treatment Systems

Flowform basins represent a sophisticated application of pattern understanding to water treatment and oxygenation. Developed at the Virbella Institute, Emerson College (Kent, UK), these systems are based on natural stream turbulence patterns and ancient Roman water cascade designs.

### Natural Basis

In nature, turbulence basins form in streams flowing over massive sandstones or mudstones. Roman hydrologists modelled these forms in pozzolanic cement. Leonardo da Vinci studied and portrayed these patterns in his drawings (documented by Popham, *The Drawings of Leonardo da Vinci*, 1946).

### Design and Construction

- **Materials**: Cast in concrete or fiberglass
- **Forms**: Can be elongate, truncate, symmetrical, asymmetrical, stepped in line, stacked like ladders, or spiraled
- **Arrangement**: Stacked in sets below sewage outfalls or above fish pond inlets

### Three Mixing Effects

1. **Vertical overturn (plunge)**: Fluid drops from one basin to another, creating vertical turbulence
2. **Figure-8 lateral flow**: Water courses around the basins in a figure-8 pattern, creating horizontal mixing
3. **Interaction pulse**: The lateral flow deflects the vertical drop, switching it from side to side in a regular rhythm

### Secondary Turbulence Patterns

Within these major turbulence patterns are distinct:

- **Vortices and counterflow**: Rotating water masses that mix air throughout the volume
- **Overfolds and cusps**: Edge effects that further mix air and water at basin margins and in the main flow stream

### Functions

- **Aeration**: Efficiently mixes air and water for oxygenation
- **Pollutant oxidation**: Oxidizes organic pollutants in sewage and organically polluted waters
- **Aquaculture support**: Oxygenates ponds for fish and aquatic species production
- **Aesthetic value**: The flowing water forms are visually pleasing
- **Educational value**: They serve as instructive hydrological pattern models

### Chappell's Analysis

Chappell (in *Landform Evolution in Australia*, ANU Press, 1978) further analysed these patterns using computer models and catastrophic theory for coastal uprush and backwash turbulence, confirming the efficiency of these natural forms.

## Broader Design Philosophy

### Art in the Service of Life

Mollison argues that art should serve functional and educational purposes, not merely decorative ones. The Flowform systems exemplify this principle — they are both beautiful and functional. The herb spiral is a sculptural form that also produces food.

### Pattern as Education

Tribal art, including Celtic and ancient engraving, has a pattern complexity that carried important meanings. Modern art has largely abandoned this educational function in favor of individualistic and decorative purposes. Permaculture challenges artists to:

- **Study and portray knowledge** in compact, memorable, transmissible form
- **Research and recreate** surviving art forms that retain their functional meaning
- **Re-integrate art** with science, society, and practical function

### Sacred vs. Decorative Art

Much of modern art has lost the sacred and educational function that characterized tribal art. Sacred calendars melted to bullion, tribal art hidden in museum storerooms, and meaningful patterns reduced to decorative motifs all represent a degradation of human knowledge systems. Permaculture seeks to restore the integration of art, function, and meaning.

## Key Takeaways

- Pattern perception (reading existing patterns) and pattern imposition (applying patterns to sites) are complementary design skills
- Rectilinear land patterning serves survey instruments, not human or environmental needs
- The herb spiral compacts 9+ meters of growing space into a 2-meter diameter circle with diverse microclimates
- Circle-mulch grow pits out-produce row crops using one-third the water
- Flood-plain interceptors turn destructive flood energy into productive resource capture
- Flowform basins efficiently aerate and treat water using natural turbulence patterns
- Art in permaculture should serve functional and educational purposes, not merely decorative ones
- Pattern-based designs are more efficient, productive, and resilient than conventional rectilinear approaches
