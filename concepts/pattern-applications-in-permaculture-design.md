# Pattern Applications in Permaculture Design

## Overview

Pattern analysis provides the foundational design language of permaculture. By understanding and applying natural patterns—spirals, branches, waves, nets, and scatters—designers can create systems that maximize efficiency, minimize waste, and work with natural energy flows. This concept covers specific applied pattern techniques for gardens, water systems, and landscape modification.

## The Herb Spiral

The herb spiral is one of the most elegant small-scale applications of pattern in permaculture. Evolved by Bill Mollison in 1978 as a kitchen-door design, it coils what would be a linear herb bed into a compact three-dimensional mound.

### Construction Specifications
- **Base diameter**: 2 meters
- **Height**: 1 to 1.3 meters
- **Planting space**: The ramp and walls exceed 9 meters of planting surface
- **Irrigation**: Watered with a single 2m sprinkler

### Microclimate Creation
The ascending spiral naturally creates a gradient of conditions:
- **Top, sunny, dry sites**: Oil-rich Mediterranean herbs (thyme, sage, rosemary, oregano)
- **Middle, moderate sites**: Transition-zone herbs (basil, tarragon)
- **Bottom, moist, shaded sites**: Leafy foliage herbs (mint, parsley, chives, coriander)

This gradient arises from the geometry itself—no complex engineering needed. The spiral form provides variable aspect (orientation to sun) and drainage simultaneously.

### Accessibility
All herbs on the spiral ramp are accessible from the outside without stepping on planted areas. The compact form places the entire herb garden directly at the kitchen door, reducing the distance between harvest and use to a few steps.

### Adaptations for Non-Ground-Level Kitchens
- Roof or balcony gardens with pot-herbs in stepped walls
- Wall shelves and window boxes
- Stacks of pots in earth mounds

## Circle-Mulch Grow Pits

### Design
A central mulch pit (60cm wide and deep) surrounded by a planting shelf and spill bank totaling 1.2m (4 feet) across.

### Water Efficiency
- **Perimeter**: 3.8m (12 feet)
- **Irrigation**: One low-pressure sprinkler versus three for a 3.8m straight row
- **Savings**: Approximately 60% reduction in water use

### Self-Shading Mechanism
Plants on the surrounding shelf eventually grow to overshade the central mulch pit, preventing evaporation from the decomposing organic matter at the center. This creates a positive feedback loop where the mulch retains moisture, feeds the plants, and the plants protect the mulch.

### Scale Applications
- **1.8m (6 feet) across**: Banana circles
- **1.8–3m (6–10 feet) across**: Coconut planting systems

Series of such gardens greatly reduce path space and land area needed for home gardens or orchards compared to row-crop arrangements.

## Flood-Plain Embankments and Flow Interceptors

Pattern analysis applied at the landscape scale reveals how structures placed on flood plains can harvest materials and direct water energy for productive purposes.

### Design Elements
- **Tree lines** (poplar, willow, tamarack) planted on flood plains
- **Embankments** or rock dykes combined with tree plantings
- These mimic natural rock dykes and resistant rock strata

### Functions
- **Material capture**: Floodwaters carry silt, mulch, and firewood; interceptor structures collect these resources
- **Flow direction**: Structures redirect water energy toward productive areas
- **Scouring**: Directed flow can scour river sand for construction use
- **Channel maintenance**: Deflectors keep channels open, allowing fish passage over shallows
- **Aeration**: Water flowing over structures becomes aerated

These are pattern impositions on landscape that occur naturally, but can be consciously designed and placed for specific beneficial effects near households or settlements.

## Flowform Water Treatment Systems

Flowforms are sculptural water-aerating devices developed at the Virbella Institute, Emerson College, UK, by artist-technicians. They represent a convergence of art, hydrology, and ecological engineering.

### Origin and Natural Model
- Natural occurrence: Shaped basins in streams flowing over massive sandstones or mudstones
- Historical precedent: Modelled in pozzolanic cement by Roman hydrologists in antiquity
- Modern development: Cast in concrete or fibreglass as artificial replicates

### Turbulence Mechanisms
Three distinct mixing effects operate within Flowform cascades:

1. **Vertical overturn (plunge)**: Fluid drops from one basin to another, entraining air
2. **Lateral figure-8 flow**: Water courses around the basin shapes in a figure-8 pattern
3. **Interaction rhythm**: Water circling the basins deflects the vertical drop flow, switching it side-to-side in a regular, rhythmic pulse

### Micro-Turbulence Features
Within these major patterns (portrayed by Leonardo da Vinci and analyzed through catastrophe theory and computer models):
- Distinct vortices and counterflow
- Overfolds and cusps
- Further air-water mixing at basin edges and in the main flow stream

### Practical Applications
- **Primary sewage treatment**: Oxidizing pollutants in wastewater
- **Organic pollution remediation**: Treating organically contaminated waters
- **Aquaculture pond oxygenation**: Dissolving oxygen for fish and aquatic species production
- **Aesthetic and educational function**: Pleasing sculptural forms that teach hydrological principles

### Configuration Flexibility
Flowforms can be arranged in various configurations:
- Elongate or truncate shapes
- Symmetrical or asymmetrical designs
- Stepped in line
- Stacked like ladders
- Spiralled to conserve space

## Designer's Checklist for Pattern Application

When designing with patterns, consider:

1. **Appropriate scale**: Patterned systems must be of appropriate size or order—small systems work for frost protection and water conservation in crops
2. **Space efficiency**: Minimize waste space using spiral, keyhole, and least-path systems; use clumped plantings and sophisticated interplants
3. **Edge effects**: Study and use edge effects for intercropping, plant guilds, pond production, and fail-safe species richness in variable climates
4. **Energy direction**: Use patterns to direct energies on site and lay out for zone, sector, slope, and orientation benefits—this approach alone creates the most energy savings
5. **Water conservation**: Circular and spiral patterns dramatically reduce irrigation requirements compared to linear row arrangements
6. **Material capture**: Design landscape features to capture flowing materials (silt, mulch, organic debris) rather than allowing them to pass through unused
