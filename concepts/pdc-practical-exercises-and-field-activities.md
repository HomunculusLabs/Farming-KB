# PDC Practical Exercises and Field Activities

The Williams PDC emphasizes that permaculture cannot be learned through rote memorization alone. Exercises and field activities form the experiential backbone of the course, bridging the gap between theoretical understanding and practical competence. The course treats exercises as opportunities for genuine discovery rather than busywork.

## Philosophy of Learning Exercises

Williams describes exercises using an axiom shared with students: the goal is to develop the ability to read patterns, think in systems, and observe rather than simply accumulate facts. Each exercise is designed to activate multiple permaculture principles simultaneously. The instructor is notably reluctant to give exercises that feel contrived, preferring activities that produce real information useful to actual design work.

Exercises serve three primary functions in the PDC:

1. **Pattern recognition training** -- building the capacity to see physical and behavioral patterns in both natural and built environments
2. **Skill development** -- hands-on practice with techniques like soil sampling, mapping, and measurement
3. **Site-specific data gathering** -- every exercise on a practice site produces data that feeds directly into the final design project

## Core Exercise Categories

### Observation and Pattern-Reading Exercises

- **Forest layer identification hike**: Students walk through a forest and identify the seven layers (canopy, understory, shrub, herbaceous, ground cover, root, and vertical/climber layers). They then observe succession stages and note which layer is dominant at the current successional phase.
- **Urban-as-forest walk**: Students walk through a city and attempt to read it using forest ecology concepts -- identifying canopy analogs, edge effects, microclimates, nutrient flows, and succession patterns in the built environment.
- **Neighborhood pattern walk**: Students observe physical and behavioral patterns in their own neighborhood, identifying invisible structures (work schedules, commuting patterns, social gathering spots) and their physical manifestations (traffic flows, commercial districts, park usage patterns).
- **Seasonal observation**: Williams notes that hardcore permaculture designers recommend observing a site for a full year, and the truly hardcore insist on three years. While a PDC cannot require this, exercises push students to observe sites under as many different conditions as possible -- during rain, after rain, morning, evening, day, and night.

### Measurement and Technical Exercises

- **Soil sampling and jar test**: Students take soil samples from around the practice site using a clear glass jar method. Soil is mixed with water and shaken vigorously; after settling, layers reveal sand, silt, and clay proportions measurable with a ruler. Students also perform the fist test -- squeezing soil into a ball to estimate clay content.
- **Infiltration rate testing**: Students build a homemade infiltrometer from a coffee can (both ends removed) driven one inch into the soil. They time how long one gallon of water takes to infiltrate, providing quantitative data for earthworks design.
- **Rain water catchment calculation**: Students measure their home roof area, research local average annual rainfall, and calculate total gallons collectible using the formula: (Square Feet x Inches of Rain x 600) / 1,000 = Gallons.
- **Solar altitude and azimuth research**: Students look up their solar altitude and azimuth for both summer and winter solstices, using sun charts available online or from regional energy offices.
- **Prevailing wind research**: Students identify prevailing summer and winter wind directions for their location through climate data research.

### Mapping and Documentation Exercises

- **Base map creation**: Students obtain or create a base map of their practice site from county offices or Google Earth. They then create working copies with analytical overlays using tracing paper or transparencies.
- **Watershed mapping**: Students find and analyze a map of their local watershed, tracing how water flows from mountains through their area to eventual discharge points.
- **Sector diagram construction**: Students create a sector analysis diagram centered on their site's Zone 0, plotting fire hazard direction, summer and winter wind sectors, solar azimuth sectors, noise sources, desirable and undesirable views, and any other forces flowing across the site.
- **Zone overlay mapping**: Students draw zone boundaries (0-5) on their base map, noting that in urban environments not all zones will be present and zone shapes will be irregular rather than concentric circles.

### Design and Research Exercises

- **Plant inventory and research**: Students purchase a book on local plants (especially those highlighting medicinal and edible species), then conduct a complete plant inventory from their practice site. This builds familiarity with native and appropriate species for design work.
- **Broad scale site context analysis**: Students analyze their site from global to local scale -- hemisphere, continent, country, region, bioregion, watershed, county, and finally the site itself. They identify nearby roads, towns, forests, potential toxin sources, and neighbor situations.
- **Invisible structures identification**: Students identify three invisible structures in their life (e.g., the 8-hour workday, seasonal shopping patterns, community governance structures), map their parallel physical structures, and analyze their environmental impact.
- **Design team organization**: Students practice organizing a design team using the organizational mandala, assigning design stewardship roles and specific analysis tasks to each team member.

### Practical Application Exercises

- **Biocompatible products research**: Students search their local area for biocompatible soaps, detergents, and hygiene products, committing to only putting biocompatible products down their drains from that point forward.
- **Freecycle community engagement**: Students join the Freecycle group in their area, experiencing the gift economy firsthand.
- **Greywater system opportunity assessment**: Students identify all domestic water systems in a hypothetical home and map potential greywater routing through the landscape.
- **Passive design assessment**: Students assess an existing structure for passive solar performance -- orientation, glazing percentage, thermal mass placement, insulation, natural ventilation potential, and landscaping microclimate effects.

## Site Visit and Field Trip Methodology

While the Williams PDC does not prescribe mandatory field trips, it strongly encourages visiting working permaculture sites, eco-villages, and natural areas. The recommended approach for any site visit includes:

1. **Pre-visit research**: Understand the climate, watershed, and general ecology of the site location before arriving
2. **Observation first**: Spend at least 30 minutes in silent observation before asking questions or taking notes
3. **Pattern documentation**: Sketch physical patterns, note behavioral patterns (animal movements, water flows, human usage), and identify sectors
4. **Interview the steward**: Ask the site manager about history, challenges, successes, and what they would do differently
5. **Comparative analysis**: After the visit, compare observations with your own practice site -- what patterns are similar, what is different, and why

## Exercise Documentation and the Student Portfolio

Every exercise in the Williams PDC is expected to produce documentation that feeds the final design project. Students are encouraged to maintain a portfolio containing:

- All soil test results with sample locations marked on maps
- Calculated rain water catchment potential with supporting data
- Climate research summaries including solar angles and wind patterns
- Completed base maps with all analytical overlays
- Plant inventories organized by zone and function
- Sector and zone analysis diagrams
- Site visit notes and comparative analyses
- Photographs of observations at different times and conditions

This portfolio approach ensures that exercises are not isolated learning events but form a cumulative body of site-specific knowledge. Williams emphasizes that the quality of the final design presentation depends directly on the thoroughness of the exercise-driven analysis that precedes it.
