# Partnership Society vs Dominator Culture

The partnership/dominator dichotomy is the central analytical framework of *Food of the Gods*. Drawing on and extending Riane Eisler's *The Chalice and the Blade* (1987), McKenna argues that human history is defined by the conflict between two fundamentally different models of social organization — one based on partnership, egalitarianism, and reverence for nature (linked to Goddess worship and mushroom ecstasy), and the other based on domination, hierarchy, and the suppression of both nature and the feminine (linked to male sky-god religion and intoxicating drugs like alcohol).

## The Partnership Model

Partnership societies, which McKenna associates with the Late Neolithic period and especially with cultures like Çatal Höyük, are characterized by:

- **Egalitarian social structure** — power distributed across genders, ages, and individuals rather than concentrated in a hierarchy
- **The Great Goddess as ultimate power** — not a subordinate consort but the supreme religious figure, representing fertility, nature, and the Earth itself (Gaia)
- **Non-proprietary male-female relations** — relationships not based on male ownership of women's reproductive capacity
- **Extended family child-rearing** — communal responsibility for offspring rather than the isolated nuclear family
- **Encouraged adolescent experimentation** — freedom for young people to explore sexuality and social roles
- **Boundary-dissolving group rituals** — communal ecstatic experiences, often involving psychoactive mushrooms, that reinforced social bonds
- **Gentle, maternal values** — nurturing, cooperation, and integration with natural cycles as guiding principles
- **Sacred relationship with nature** — the natural world understood as alive, intelligent, and worthy of reverence rather than exploitation
- **Art and craft as spiritual practice** — pottery, weaving, and other crafts infused with symbolic meaning reflecting partnership values

The archaeological record of Neolithic Europe and Anatolia provides what McKenna and Eisler consider evidence for these characteristics: the absence of fortifications and weapons in early Neolithic settlements, the predominance of female figurines in ritual contexts, burial practices that show minimal status differentiation, and art that celebrates fertility, animals, and the natural world rather than warfare and domination. ## The Vegetable Mind and Gaian Holism

McKenna's prescription for moving beyond the dominator model involves what he called the "vegetable mind" and "Gaian Holism" — a renewed awareness of the unity and balance of nature and humanity's position within that dynamic, evolving balance. This return to a perspective that places ego within the larger context of planetary life and evolution is "the essence of the [[mckenna-archaic-revival-four-part-structure-paradise-paradise-lost-hell-regained]]." He invoked Marshall McLuhan's insight that planetary human culture would be tribal in character, and argued that "the next great step toward a planetary holism is the partial merging of the technologically transformed human world with the Archaic matrix of vegetable intelligence that is the Transcendent Other."

> "Now our choice as a planetary culture is a simple one: Go green or die."

McKenna's concept of the vegetable mind draws on the idea that plants, as the primary producers and longest-lived organisms on Earth, embody a form of intelligence fundamentally different from — and in many ways superior to — the animal consciousness that dominator culture privileges. Plant intelligence operates on vastly longer timescales, maintains relationships with thousands of other species simultaneously, and does not rely on mobility or violence. The [[dighton-mycorrhizal-networks-carbon-transfer]] that connect forest trees — sometimes called the "wood wide web" — exemplify the kind of distributed, cooperative intelligence that McKenna saw as the model for a partnership society. By reconnecting with plant consciousness through the careful, reverent use of psychoactive plants, McKenna argued, humanity could recover the partnership orientation that was lost with the suppression of the [[mckenna-fall-of-partnership-society]] for McKenna's account of this transition. While Gimbutas's interpretation of the figurines as evidence of a unified goddess religion has been challenged — many archaeologists now read them as having diverse local meanings rather than representing a single pan-European cult — her documentation of Neolithic material culture remains invaluable, and her broader argument that gender relations in prehistoric Europe differed significantly from later patriarchal systems has gained renewed support.
### Modern Feminist Archaeology and Gender Studies
Feminist archaeologists have built on and critiqued Gimbutas's legacy. Scholars such as Lynn Meskell, Joan Gero, and Margaret Conkey have argued that while Gimbutas may have over-unified a complex archaeological record, she was correct to center gender as a fundamental category of archaeological analysis. The partnership model's emphasis on egalitarian social structures has found support in studies of contemporary foraging and horticultural societies, where anthropologists have documented that resource distribution, decision-making, and social prestige are often more egalitarian than the dominator model would predict. Eisler's framework has been applied to analyze everything from workplace dynamics to international relations, making the partnership/dominator dichotomy a widely recognized heuristic in gender studies and organizational psychology.
### Influence on Ecological and Environmental Thought
The partnership model has had perhaps its deepest influence on ecological thinking. The association between dominator culture and environmental destruction, and between partnership values and ecological stewardship, has become a central theme in ecofeminism, deep ecology, and the environmental humanities. The Gaia hypothesis — James Lovelock's proposal that Earth functions as a self-regulating system — resonates strongly with McKenna's vision of [[mckenna-archaic-revival]] for McKenna's prescription for recovering partnership values through renewed engagement with plant intelligence.
### Criticism and Revision
Academic critics have noted that the partnership/dominator framework risks romanticizing prehistoric cultures and oversimplifying a complex archaeological record. The assumption that goddess figurines necessarily indicate egalitarian social organization has been questioned, as has the sharp binary between "partnership" and "dominator" modes. Some scholars point out that prehistoric societies exhibited a wide range of social forms, and that the transition to hierarchy was likely gradual and regionally variable rather than the sudden cataclysm McKenna describes. The anthropologist Brian Hayden has argued that the very complexity of Neolithic ritual and art may indicate emerging elite classes rather than egalitarianism, and that competition and inequality may have been present in these societies even without the martial iconography of later periods. Despite these critiques, the framework's descriptive power as a tool for analyzing the relationship between consciousness, gender, ecology, and cultural organization remains influential. Even critics acknowledge that McKenna and Eisler identified a genuine historical pattern — the association between the rise of patriarchal hierarchy, sky-god religion, and the suppression of both feminine authority and nature-reverent spirituality — even if the specific mechanism McKenna proposed (psilocybin as the sole maintainer of partnership equilibrium) is considered speculative.
### Eisler's Ongoing Influence
Riane Eisler continued to develop the partnership model throughout her career, applying it to economics (*The Real Wealth of Nations*, 2007), education, and international relations. The partnership/dominator framework has been adopted by organizations working on gender equity, peacebuilding, and sustainable development. Eisler's argument that economic systems reflect underlying cultural values — that a dominator economy prioritizes extraction and accumulation while a partnership economy prioritizes care and sustainability — has resonated with movements for economic localization, cooperative business models, and care-centered social policy. The Dalai Lama endorsed Eisler's framework, calling *The Chalice and the Blade* one of the most important books of our time. McKenna's contribution was to ground Eisler's sociological model in a specific pharmacological mechanism — the presence or absence of psilocybin-containing mushrooms — connecting the partnership/dominator analysis to the broader history of human-plant relationships explored in [[mckenna-archaic-revival]]: that the controlled use of psilocybin and other entheogens in ceremonial, intentional settings can dissolve the ego-boundaries, competitive conditioning, and nature-alienation that dominator culture instills, and can replace them with the empathy, ecological awareness, and egalitarian sensibility that characterize partnership societies.

McKenna was careful to distinguish this proposal from naive nostalgia. He did not advocate a literal return to Neolithic social forms, but rather the recovery of a mode of consciousness — one characterized by what he called "the felt experience of the Gaian collectivity" — that could inform and transform modern institutions. The challenge, as he acknowledged, is that dominator culture has spent millennia developing institutional structures (religions, legal systems, economic arrangements) that reinforce dominator consciousness. Recovering partnership consciousness through plant sacraments is, in his view, a necessary but not sufficient condition for cultural transformation — it must be accompanied by parallel changes in social, economic, and political organization. The contemporary [[metzner-psycholytic-psychedelic-therapy-models]] movement, with its emphasis on set, setting, integration, and clinical containment, represents one model for how this recovery might proceed within — rather than in opposition to — modern institutional frameworks. The tension between these two approaches — the ceremonial-indigenous model and the clinical-scientific model — is itself a productive site for exploring how partnership consciousness might be recovered in forms appropriate to a globalized, technologically complex civilization. Both models share, at their core, the recognition that certain plants can facilitate direct experience of interconnectedness, and that this experience has transformative implications for how humans organize their societies and relate to the natural world.
