# Partnership Society vs Dominator Culture

The partnership/dominator dichotomy is the central analytical framework of *Food of the Gods*. Drawing on and extending Riane Eisler's *The Chalice and the Blade* (1987), McKenna argues that human history is defined by the conflict between two fundamentally different models of social organization — one based on partnership, egalitarianism, and reverence for nature (linked to Goddess worship and mushroom ecstasy), and the other based on domination, hierarchy, and the suppression of both nature and the feminine (linked to male sky-god religion and intoxicating drugs like alcohol).

## The Partnership Model

Partnership societies, which McKenna associates with the Late Neolithic period and especially with cultures like Çatal Höyük, are characterized by:

- **Egalitarian social structure** — power distributed across genders, ages, and individuals rather than concentrated in a hierarchy
- **The Great Goddess as ultimate power** — not a subordinate consort but the supreme religious figure, representing fertility, nature, and the Earth itself (Gaia)
- **Non-proprietary male-female relations** — relationships not based on male ownership of women's reproductive capacity
- **Extended family child-rearing** — communal responsibility for offspring rather than the isolated nuclear family
- **Encouraged adolescent experimentation** — freedom for young people to explore sexuality and social roles
- **Boundary-dissolving group rituals** — communal ecstatic experiences, often involving psychoactive mushrooms, that reinforced social bonds
- **Gentle, maternal values** — nurturing, cooperation, and integration with natural cycles as guiding principles
- **Sacred relationship with nature** — the natural world understood as alive, intelligent, and worthy of reverence rather than exploitation
- **Art and craft as spiritual practice** — pottery, weaving, and other crafts infused with symbolic meaning reflecting partnership values

The archaeological record of Neolithic Europe and Anatolia provides what McKenna and Eisler consider evidence for these characteristics: the absence of fortifications and weapons in early Neolithic settlements, the predominance of female figurines in ritual contexts, burial practices that show minimal status differentiation, and art that celebrates fertility, animals, and the natural world rather than warfare and domination. See [[mckenna-food-gods-tassili-cave-paintings]] for evidence of similar partnership values in North African prehistory.

## The Dominator Model

Dominator societies, which McKenna traces to the Indo-European invasions beginning around 4500 B.C., are characterized by:

- **Hierarchical power structures** — rigid social stratification with power concentrated at the top
- **Male sky-god religion** — replacement of the Earth Goddess with a distant, authoritarian male deity; the shift from "the Great Mother" to "God the Father"
- **Patriarchal sexual politics** — men choosing young partners, women bonding with older resource-controlling men for security
- **Possessiveness and property** — women, land, and resources treated as property to be owned and defended
- **War and conquest** — institutionalized violence as an instrument of policy
- **Intoxicating drugs** — alcohol replacing visionary hallucinogens as the socially preferred substance (McKenna notes that alcohol reinforces social hierarchy and competitiveness, while psychedelics dissolve it)
- **Suppression of the feminine** — systematic devaluation of women, nature, emotion, and the body

## Sexual Politics

McKenna provided detailed analysis of the sexual dimension of the partnership/dominator divide. In partnership societies, group sexual activities persisted in mystery religions (the rites of Dionysus, the Saturnalia) and were understood as sacred expressions of communal bonding and fertility. These were gradually suppressed as Christianity and other dominator religions pathologized the body and sexuality.

Citing Jean Baker Miller, McKenna argued that "the need to dominate stems from powerlessness, not power" — that dominator behavior arises from a fundamental sense of insecurity and disconnection, while partnership behavior flows from a sense of abundance and interconnectedness.

McKenna drew connections between the dominator model's sexual politics and the broader pattern of environmental destruction. He argued that the same impulse to dominate and control women's bodies — through rape, prostitution, reproductive control, and the pathologizing of female sexuality — mirrors the impulse to dominate and exploit nature. In both cases, the dominator model treats the "other" as a resource to be extracted rather than a partner to be respected. This ecofeminist analysis, while not original to McKenna, was powerfully integrated into his broader argument about the relationship between consciousness, pharmacology, and cultural organization. The shift from sacred sexuality to patriarchal control of reproduction paralleled the shift from mushroom ecstasy to alcohol intoxication — both represented the replacement of boundary-dissolving partnership consciousness with hierarchy-reinforcing dominator consciousness.

## What Maintained the Partnership Equilibrium?

McKenna's key extension of Eisler's framework was to ask a question she had not addressed: "What factor maintained the equilibrium of the partnership societies of the Late Neolithic and then faded, setting the stage for the emergence of the evolutionarily maladaptive dominator model?"

His answer was the **psilocybin mushroom**. McKenna proposed that the depth of a human group's relationship to the "gnosis of the Transcendent Other, the Gaian collectivity of organic life" determines the strength of its connection to the archetype of the Goddess and hence to the partnership style of social organization. The mushroom was the physical mediator of this connection — the "third and hidden member" of a shamanic trinity that also included the Great Goddess and her cattle.

The logic of McKenna's argument runs as follows: psilocybin-containing mushrooms (primarily *Psilocybe cubensis*, which grows on the dung of cattle and other ungulates) were readily available to the pastoral peoples of North Africa and the Near East during the Late Neolithic and Early Bronze Age. Regular communal use of these mushrooms in ritual contexts would have produced the boundary-dissolving, ego-suppressing, empathy-enhancing experiences that reinforce partnership social values. The mushrooms effectively functioned as a social technology — a chemical mechanism that maintained the partnership equilibrium by periodically dissolving the ego boundaries and competitive impulses that, left unchecked, lead to hierarchy and domination.

When the Indo-European invasions disrupted the pastoral mushroom-using cultures of the Old World, replacing Goddess worship with sky-god patriarchy and mushroom ecstasy with alcohol intoxication, the partnership equilibrium collapsed and the dominator model became ascendant. The ecological shift was also significant: as Indo-European pastoralists moved into regions where cattle dung was less abundant or where the climate was less favorable for psilocybin mushroom growth, the supply of the "Eucharist" that maintained partnership consciousness was interrupted. Climate change, migration, and cultural disruption all conspired to sever the human-mushroom relationship that had sustained partnership values for millennia.

## The Crucial Difference

McKenna argued that the mainstream of Western thought "ceased to be refreshed by the gnosis of the boundary-dissolving plant hallucinogens long before the close of the Minoan Era, circa 850 B.C." In Crete and nearby Greece, awareness of what he called "the vegetable Logos" continued as an esoteric and diminished presence until the Eleusinian Mysteries were finally suppressed by Christian barbarians in A.D. 268. "The consequence of that severed connection is the modern world — a planet dying under moral anesthesia."
### Minoan Crete as Partnership Culture
McKenna pointed to Minoan Crete as the last great flowering of partnership culture in the West. The Minoan civilization (c. 3000-1450 B.C.E.) was centered on palatial complexes that appear to have been ceremonial and redistributive rather than defensive — Knossos, Phaistos, and Mallia had no city walls. Minoan art prominently features women, snakes, dolphins, and botanical imagery, with no depictions of warfare or kings. The prominent role of women in Minoan religious life — priestesses, female figurines, and the famous Snake Goddess — suggests a society where feminine spiritual authority was central. McKenna argued that Minoan culture represented the last gasp of the partnership equilibrium in the Mediterranean before the Mycenaean Greeks (the first Indo-European speakers to establish themselves in the Aegean) imposed their hierarchical, martial, sky-god-oriented culture. The destruction of the Minoan civilization — whether by the Thera eruption, Mycenaean invasion, or both — marks, in McKenna's narrative, the definitive end of partnership culture in the Western world and the beginning of three millennia of unchallenged dominator ascendancy.

## Suppression of the Feminine and Natural Knowledge

The dominator pattern of suppressing the feminine extended to suppression of knowledge of the natural world. McKenna detailed how the late medieval Church, during the great witch burnings, wanted all magic and "derangement" attributed to the Devil, and therefore suppressed all knowledge of plants like Datura, deadly nightshade, and monkshood — the plants used in witches' flying ointments. The central role of plants in the nocturnal activities of practitioners of witchcraft was deliberately obscured, because a natural explanation for such phenomena "would have left the Devil with only a very modest significance, or none at all."
### The Witch Hunts as Anti-Ecological Campaign
McKenna interpreted the European witch hunts (roughly 1450-1750) not merely as religious persecution but as a systematic campaign to destroy the remaining folk knowledge of herbal medicine, midwifery, and plant-based spirituality. The predominantly female victims of the witch hunts — estimated at 40,000 to 100,000 executed — were often the community's healers and herbalists, the keepers of empirical knowledge about medicinal plants that had accumulated over centuries. Their elimination transferred authority over healing and health to the male-dominated medical establishment and the Church. This suppression of plant knowledge was, in McKenna's view, the final triumph of dominator culture over the partnership model's reverence for the natural world. The plants used in flying ointments — solanaceous nightshades containing tropane alkaloids — are now recognized as potent psychoactive substances, vindicating the traditional knowledge that the Inquisition sought to destroy. See [[mckenna-food-gods-fall-consciousness]] for McKenna's broader account of this suppression.

## The Vegetable Mind and Gaian Holism

McKenna's prescription for moving beyond the dominator model involves what he called the "vegetable mind" and "Gaian Holism" — a renewed awareness of the unity and balance of nature and humanity's position within that dynamic, evolving balance. This return to a perspective that places ego within the larger context of planetary life and evolution is "the essence of the Archaic Revival." He invoked Marshall McLuhan's insight that planetary human culture would be tribal in character, and argued that "the next great step toward a planetary holism is the partial merging of the technologically transformed human world with the Archaic matrix of vegetable intelligence that is the Transcendent Other."

> "Now our choice as a planetary culture is a simple one: Go green or die."

McKenna's concept of the vegetable mind draws on the idea that plants, as the primary producers and longest-lived organisms on Earth, embody a form of intelligence fundamentally different from — and in many ways superior to — the animal consciousness that dominator culture privileges. Plant intelligence operates on vastly longer timescales, maintains relationships with thousands of other species simultaneously, and does not rely on mobility or violence. The mycorrhizal networks that connect forest trees — sometimes called the "wood wide web" — exemplify the kind of distributed, cooperative intelligence that McKenna saw as the model for a partnership society. By reconnecting with plant consciousness through the careful, reverent use of psychoactive plants, McKenna argued, humanity could recover the partnership orientation that was lost with the suppression of the [[mckenna-food-gods-mesoamerican-mushrooms]] cults of antiquity.

## Contemporary Relevance

McKenna and Eisler's partnership/dominator framework continues to resonate across multiple academic and cultural fields, even as mainstream archaeology has revised many of the specific historical claims upon which it was built.
### Marija Gimbutas and the Archaeology of Old Europe
The archaeological foundation for the partnership model rests heavily on the work of Lithuanian-American archaeologist Marija Gimbutas (1921-1994). Gimbutas's analysis of Neolithic settlements in southeastern Europe — particularly her identification of thousands of female figurines, burial patterns, and the absence of fortifications — led her to argue that these cultures were matrifocal, peaceful, and goddess-worshipping. Her major works, *The Goddesses and Gods of Old Europe* (1974) and *The Civilization of the Goddess* (1991), proposed that these "Old European" cultures were overrun by patriarchal Indo-European invaders from the Pontic-Caspian steppe (the Kurgan hypothesis). See [[mckenna-fall-of-partnership-society]] for McKenna's account of this transition. While Gimbutas's interpretation of the figurines as evidence of a unified goddess religion has been challenged — many archaeologists now read them as having diverse local meanings rather than representing a single pan-European cult — her documentation of Neolithic material culture remains invaluable, and her broader argument that gender relations in prehistoric Europe differed significantly from later patriarchal systems has gained renewed support.
### Modern Feminist Archaeology and Gender Studies
Feminist archaeologists have built on and critiqued Gimbutas's legacy. Scholars such as Lynn Meskell, Joan Gero, and Margaret Conkey have argued that while Gimbutas may have over-unified a complex archaeological record, she was correct to center gender as a fundamental category of archaeological analysis. The partnership model's emphasis on egalitarian social structures has found support in studies of contemporary foraging and horticultural societies, where anthropologists have documented that resource distribution, decision-making, and social prestige are often more egalitarian than the dominator model would predict. Eisler's framework has been applied to analyze everything from workplace dynamics to international relations, making the partnership/dominator dichotomy a widely recognized heuristic in gender studies and organizational psychology.
### Influence on Ecological and Environmental Thought
The partnership model has had perhaps its deepest influence on ecological thinking. The association between dominator culture and environmental destruction, and between partnership values and ecological stewardship, has become a central theme in ecofeminism, deep ecology, and the environmental humanities. The Gaia hypothesis — James Lovelock's proposal that Earth functions as a self-regulating system — resonates strongly with McKenna's vision of [[mckenna-vegetable-mind-and-gaian-holism]] and the partnership model's emphasis on humanity's embeddedness in natural systems. The partnership framework has also informed permaculture design philosophy, regenerative agriculture, and the bioregionalism movement, all of which emphasize cooperation with natural processes rather than domination over them. The deep ecology movement, founded by Arne Naess, shares with McKenna and Eisler the fundamental premise that human well-being is inseparable from the well-being of the broader ecological community — a partnership premise in its essence. See [[mckenna-archaic-revival]] for McKenna's prescription for recovering partnership values through renewed engagement with plant intelligence.
### Criticism and Revision
Academic critics have noted that the partnership/dominator framework risks romanticizing prehistoric cultures and oversimplifying a complex archaeological record. The assumption that goddess figurines necessarily indicate egalitarian social organization has been questioned, as has the sharp binary between "partnership" and "dominator" modes. Some scholars point out that prehistoric societies exhibited a wide range of social forms, and that the transition to hierarchy was likely gradual and regionally variable rather than the sudden cataclysm McKenna describes. The anthropologist Brian Hayden has argued that the very complexity of Neolithic ritual and art may indicate emerging elite classes rather than egalitarianism, and that competition and inequality may have been present in these societies even without the martial iconography of later periods. Despite these critiques, the framework's descriptive power as a tool for analyzing the relationship between consciousness, gender, ecology, and cultural organization remains influential. Even critics acknowledge that McKenna and Eisler identified a genuine historical pattern — the association between the rise of patriarchal hierarchy, sky-god religion, and the suppression of both feminine authority and nature-reverent spirituality — even if the specific mechanism McKenna proposed (psilocybin as the sole maintainer of partnership equilibrium) is considered speculative.
### Eisler's Ongoing Influence
Riane Eisler continued to develop the partnership model throughout her career, applying it to economics (*The Real Wealth of Nations*, 2007), education, and international relations. The partnership/dominator framework has been adopted by organizations working on gender equity, peacebuilding, and sustainable development. Eisler's argument that economic systems reflect underlying cultural values — that a dominator economy prioritizes extraction and accumulation while a partnership economy prioritizes care and sustainability — has resonated with movements for economic localization, cooperative business models, and care-centered social policy. The Dalai Lama endorsed Eisler's framework, calling *The Chalice and the Blade* one of the most important books of our time. McKenna's contribution was to ground Eisler's sociological model in a specific pharmacological mechanism — the presence or absence of psilocybin-containing mushrooms — connecting the partnership/dominator analysis to the broader history of human-plant relationships explored in [[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-thesis]].
### Archaeological Evidence: Çatal Höyük and Beyond
The Neolithic settlement of Çatal Höyük (c. 7500-5700 B.C.E. in present-day Turkey) serves as the primary archaeological case study for the partnership model. Excavations by James Mellaart (1961-1965) and later by Ian Hodder (1993-present) revealed a large, apparently egalitarian town with no evidence of fortifications, no palaces, no elite burials, and no clear social hierarchy. The settlement's art is dominated by female figurines, bull imagery, and murals depicting vultures and geometric patterns — interpreted by Eisler and Gimbutas as evidence of goddess worship and partnership social organization. Hodder's more recent excavations have complicated this picture, suggesting that Çatal Höyük's social organization may have been more complex than initially assumed, with evidence of household-level inequality and potential symbolic violence. However, the absence of centralized authority, standing armies, and elite burial monuments remains striking and continues to distinguish Çatal Höyük from the clearly hierarchical societies that followed in the Bronze Age. The broader pattern across Neolithic Anatolia and the Balkans — settled agricultural communities without significant fortifications, with abundant female figurines and relatively egalitarian burial practices — provides circumstantial support for the existence of prolonged periods of partnership-style social organization before the rise of the first state-level hierarchies.

## Psychedelics and the Recovery of Partnership Consciousness

McKenna's argument is not merely historical — it carries a prescriptive dimension. If the loss of psychoactive plant sacraments enabled the dominator ascendancy, then the deliberate, reverent recovery of those sacraments could facilitate a return to partnership values. This is the pharmacological core of the [[mckenna-archaic-revival]]: that the controlled use of psilocybin and other entheogens in ceremonial, intentional settings can dissolve the ego-boundaries, competitive conditioning, and nature-alienation that dominator culture instills, and can replace them with the empathy, ecological awareness, and egalitarian sensibility that characterize partnership societies.

McKenna was careful to distinguish this proposal from naive nostalgia. He did not advocate a literal return to Neolithic social forms, but rather the recovery of a mode of consciousness — one characterized by what he called "the felt experience of the Gaian collectivity" — that could inform and transform modern institutions. The challenge, as he acknowledged, is that dominator culture has spent millennia developing institutional structures (religions, legal systems, economic arrangements) that reinforce dominator consciousness. Recovering partnership consciousness through plant sacraments is, in his view, a necessary but not sufficient condition for cultural transformation — it must be accompanied by parallel changes in social, economic, and political organization. The contemporary psychedelic therapy movement, with its emphasis on set, setting, integration, and clinical containment, represents one model for how this recovery might proceed within — rather than in opposition to — modern institutional frameworks. The tension between these two approaches — the ceremonial-indigenous model and the clinical-scientific model — is itself a productive site for exploring how partnership consciousness might be recovered in forms appropriate to a globalized, technologically complex civilization. Both models share, at their core, the recognition that certain plants can facilitate direct experience of interconnectedness, and that this experience has transformative implications for how humans organize their societies and relate to the natural world.
