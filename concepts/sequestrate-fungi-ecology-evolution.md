# [[sequestrate-fungi]] Ecology and Evolution

Sequestrate fungi are fungi that produce enclosed fruiting bodies
in which spores are not forcibly discharged into the air. This
diverse group includes truffles, false truffles, and related forms
found across the Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Zygomycota. The
sequestrate habit has evolved independently multiple times from
epigeous (above-ground) ancestors, representing one of the most
remarkable examples of convergent evolution in the fungal kingdom.

## Definition and Diversity

Sequestrate fungi share the characteristic of retaining spores
within an enclosed fruiting body rather than discharging them
ballistically. They occur in two phyla of the Kingdom Eumycota:
the Zygomycota (Endogonales, Glomales, Mucorales) and the
Dikaryomycota (Ascomycetes and Holobasidiomycetes). More than
80 genera are currently recognized, distributed across multiple
orders and families. Many genera do not fit neatly into described
families and are listed under uncertain status, and numerous
undescribed genera await formal description.

## Evolutionary Origins

Sequestrate fungi share common ancestry with or were derived from
epigeous, nonsequestrate forms of cup fungi and mushrooms. The
Basidiomycete order Boletales exemplifies these transitions
particularly well. In species of the mushroom genus Suillus,
spores are discharged forcibly from hymenium-lined tubes. The
related Gastrosuillus retains modified tubes that are contorted
and plugged so that spores cannot escape. Truncocolumella
represents further progression, with hymenium-lined chambers
enclosed in a peridium. Rhizopogon species lack chambers
entirely, with a gelatinous-solid gleba.

Similar morphological progressions occur in the Cortinariaceae
(Cortinarius to Thaxterogaster to Hymenogaster), the Russulaceae
(Russula to Macowanites to Gymnomyces), and the Tricholomataceae
(Laccaria to Podohydnangium to Hydnangium). In the Ascomycetes,
epigeous cup fungi such as Humaria are related to sequestrate
Geopora, which in turn connects to Stephensia and Phaeangium
with progressively reduced hymenial organization.

Molecular evidence indicates that such changes do not require
massive numbers of mutations. The sequestrate habit has arisen
independently in parallel evolutionary lines in different
geographic regions. Australia and North America provide a
striking comparison with independent host-specific lines
evolving on different continents.

## Ecology and Trophic Roles

Most sequestrate fungi are either ectomycorrhizal or saprobic,
inheriting these habits from their epigeous ancestors. They
usually associate with trees or shrubs and are most commonly
found in forests, woodlands, and shrublands. Ectomycorrhizal
sequestrate fungi are now known to abound in many tropical
forests, not just temperate to boreal zones as once thought.

Species diversity of all sequestrate groups can be relatively
high in the tropics but is generally highest in subtropical to
temperate forests. Diversity declines in boreal and polar
habitats and in alpine and arctic environments, even when
suitable hosts are present. In deserts, some sequestrate fungi
form specialized types of mycorrhizae with annual or perennial
hosts.

## Spore Dispersal and Mycophagy

Loss of forcible spore discharge must be accompanied by
adaptations for alternative dispersal. In most cases, the
dispersal agents are animals. Sequestrate fungi produce
aromatic compounds, including pheromones, that attract animals
when spores are mature. Each species produces its own array of
aromas, typically a combination of several compounds. Immature
sporocarps have little or no distinctive odor.

No sequestrate fungi are known to be toxic, reflecting their
dependence on mycophagy for spore dispersal. Mammals eat the
sporocarps; all sterile tissues are digested, but spores pass
through the digestive tract unharmed. Spore-containing feces
weather, and spores are washed into soil to contact receptive
host roots. Birds are important dispersal agents in New Zealand,
where bright-colored sequestrate fungi mimic fruits eaten by
birds.

## Desert Adaptations

Desert-dwelling sequestrate fungi are adapted for passive wind
dispersal. Species of Carbomyces, Terfezia, and Tirmania fruit
only in years with adequate rainfall. They produce succulent
ascomata composed of large, thin-walled cells. As ascomata dry,
the cells disintegrate into a spore-bearing powder that wind
blown sand abrades away. Some desert species such as Phaeangium
lefebvrei produce clusters of small ascomata that form visible
humps on the desert floor, attracting birds.

## Study Challenges

Sequestrate fungi are difficult to study because most fruit
hypogeously. In compacted soil, they may emerge or raise a
hump, but in typical forest soil they expand without providing
visual clues. Animal digs can indicate colonies. Raking near
animal digs often reveals additional specimens. In Europe,
dogs are trained to find commercially valuable truffles. The
cryptic nature of sequestrate fungi poses special problems for
inventory and monitoring of fungal diversity.
