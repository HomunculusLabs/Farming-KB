# Morel Cultivation Research Update

## Overview

Morels are among the most coveted mushrooms on the planet, but they are
extremely particular about their [[cannabis-growing-environment-setup]], making them difficult to
cultivate. Most cultivation manuals offer strategies as suggestion rather than
fact. Recently, however, some research success has been achieved on a
site-specific basis. Cotter emphasizes balancing broad claims with personal
observation and local testing.

## Ecology and Life Cycle

### Classification and Associations

Morels have been classified as both saprophytic and [[white-rot-fungi]] (shiitake, oyster), with a
growth rate rivaling some molds -- over 1 inch per day.

### Sclerotia Formation

Morel mycelium typically does not form fruitbodies without environmental
stressors. This prompts production of sclerotia -- resting "microtubers" that
serve as energy reserves while the mycelium waits for favorable water and
temperature conditions. Sclerotia typically form in summer and fall, then
develop into fruitbodies the following spring.

### Chill Hours

Morels need a certain number of "chill hours" (hours when soil at 4-10 inches
depth is below 32 degrees F / 0 degrees C) to induce development. Local weather
stations monitor soil temperatures with probes extending 8 feet underground.
"Low-chill-hour morels" exist, analogous to [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]] bred for warmer
climates. This chill hour requirement is probably necessary for successful
cultivation.

### Fire and Fruiting

Forest fires seem to encourage morel flushes in some regions, but the
phenomenon is site- and species-specific. East Coast [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] morel sites
do not show massive post-fire bounties. Different ecotypes evolve to fulfill
particular environmental niches.

## Sclerotia Research

Mycologist Thomas Volk found that morel mycelium inoculated on half-nutritive,
half-nonnutritive agar forms sclerotia only on the nonnutritive side. The
hyphae "find" nutritive agar and translocate nutrients back to the
nonnutritive side.

Cotter duplicated this work and added a gallery observation: when mycelium was
placed at the interface between nutritive and nonnutritive agar, sclerotia
formed almost immediately (2-3 days) on the nonnutritive side, compared to 7-10
days when mycelium traveled a long distance first. Placing mycelium directly at
the interface may cut a week off production time by changing inoculation
strategy.

## Strain Selection

### Importance of Local Strains

Unlike exclusively [[growing-gourmet-saprophytic-mushrooms-decomposers]], morels form complex biological and
chemical relationships with surrounding soil. Success depends heavily on the
local strain. Isolate strains from your own region. Cotter has cloned and taken
spore isolates from wild morel collections across the East Coast for ten years,
seeking "anomaly" strains that do not form direct associations with specific
tree hosts or their [[lowenfels-rhizosphere-bacteria-plant-interaction]].

### Collection Protocol

1. Collect morels close to maturity that are firm
2. Make a conical incision 2 inches into the soil, keeping the morel and soil
   plug intact
3. Separate fruitbody from soil plug, place each sample in a waxed-paper bag
   labeled with date, location, and sample number
4. Record: temperature, soil pH, distance and orientation from neighboring
   trees, tree size and species, understory vegetation, proximity to water
5. Keep samples cool but not frozen (freezing damages tissue)
6. Store fruitbodies under refrigeration; soil can be refrigerated or frozen

## Outdoor Cultivation

### Site Selection

Morels fruit within a narrow soil temperature window of approximately 48-56
degrees F (9-13 degrees C). If you have never seen or heard of morels collected
in your immediate area, a morel bed may be a waste of time.

### Spore Slurry Method

1. Bring 2 cups of water to a boil, pour into a clean jar, cool to room
   temperature
2. Place the head of one morel into the water, swirl for thirty seconds
3. Remove the morel (cook and eat it), cap the jar
4. Let sit at room temperature for twenty hours
5. Refrigerate for at least three weeks (can remain refrigerated for months)
6. Inspect weekly for darkening water, floating biomass, jelly-like biofilm, and
   strands of mycelium

## Primordial Hormone Extracts

Mushrooms produce hormones concentrated in the primordia that orchestrate
fruiting cycles. These can be harvested and applied as a mist to colonized
biomass to help induce fruiting:

1. Harvest primordia, clean away substrate debris
2. Surface-sterilize by swirling in ethanol for thirty seconds, rinse with
   distilled water (repeat three times)
3. Blend primordia with water in a sterilized blender
4. Vacuum-filter to remove particulates
5. Centrifuge at 10,000 xg for five minutes
6. Withdraw supernatant into a sterile syringe with syringe filter
7. Collect sterilized fluid and dilute to determined application rate
