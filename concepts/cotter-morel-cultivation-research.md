# Morel Cultivation Research Update

## Overview

Morels are among the most coveted mushrooms on the planet, but they are
extremely particular about their [[white-rot-fungi]] (shiitake, oyster), with a
growth rate rivaling some molds -- over 1 inch per day.

### Sclerotia Formation

Morel mycelium typically does not form fruitbodies without environmental
stressors. This prompts production of sclerotia -- resting "microtubers" that
serve as energy reserves while the mycelium waits for favorable water and
temperature conditions. Sclerotia typically form in summer and fall, then
develop into fruitbodies the following spring.

### Chill Hours

Morels need a certain number of "chill hours" (hours when soil at 4-10 inches
depth is below 32 degrees F / 0 degrees C) to induce development. Local weather
stations monitor soil temperatures with probes extending 8 feet underground.
"Low-chill-hour morels" exist, analogous to [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] morel sites
do not show massive post-fire bounties. Different ecotypes evolve to fulfill
particular environmental niches.

## Sclerotia Research

Mycologist Thomas Volk found that morel mycelium inoculated on half-nutritive,
half-nonnutritive agar forms sclerotia only on the nonnutritive side. The
hyphae "find" nutritive agar and translocate nutrients back to the
nonnutritive side.

Cotter duplicated this work and added a gallery observation: when mycelium was
placed at the interface between nutritive and nonnutritive agar, sclerotia
formed almost immediately (2-3 days) on the nonnutritive side, compared to 7-10
days when mycelium traveled a long distance first. Placing mycelium directly at
the interface may cut a week off production time by changing inoculation
strategy.

### Collection Protocol

1. Collect morels close to maturity that are firm
2. Make a conical incision 2 inches into the soil, keeping the morel and soil
   plug intact
3. Separate fruitbody from soil plug, place each sample in a waxed-paper bag
   labeled with date, location, and sample number
4. Record: temperature, soil pH, distance and orientation from neighboring
   trees, tree size and species, understory vegetation, proximity to water
5. Keep samples cool but not frozen (freezing damages tissue)
6. Store fruitbodies under refrigeration; soil can be refrigerated or frozen
