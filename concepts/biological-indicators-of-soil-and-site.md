# Biological Indicators of Soil and Site

## Overview

In any local area, the composition, shape, size, and distribution of plants give many clues to soil type, depth, and extrinsic factors. Rather than relying solely on laboratory soil analysis, [[permaculture]] practitioners learn to read the landscape through its biological indicators. The key factors that can be read from biological indicators include:

1. Soil depth
2. Water reserves
3. pH
4. Mineral status
5. Fire frequency
6. Frost
7. Drainage
8. Mineral deposits and rock type
9. Overgrazing and soil compaction
10. Animal (macrofauna) effects

## 1. Soil Depth

Shallow soils dry out quickly and hold few nutrients. A practical method for assessing soil depth is to observe a single tree species (e.g., [[acacia]], Prosopis, honey locust) across a range of sites. A "height and spread" estimate will reveal areas of deeper soils where the largest specimens grow. The same species will be dwarfish on shallow soils of the same derivation or rock type.

This technique works because tree growth is directly limited by available soil volume and water-holding capacity. Large, well-formed specimens indicate deep, well-developed soils.

## 2. Water Reserves

Deep-rooted trees that require reliable water — large nut trees and candlenuts (*Aleurites*) are excellent examples — occur naturally only in well-drained but water-conserving sites. They often indicate water-lines not associated with valleys and stand over springs or aquifer discharge areas.

In sandy landscapes, a great variety of deep-rooted shrubs and trees indicate where a clay base lies at 1-2 m depth. This is common on desert borders and hills in drylands. The rule of thumb: large tree stems reveal well-watered sites, small stems indicate drier sites.

These observations enable the practitioner to both create sites by water diversion and select optimal sites for large trees or shrubs.

## 3. pH Indicators

### Wild Plant Indicators

- **Sorrel (*Rumex*)** and **oxalis** in pastures may indicate compact or acid conditions.
- **Fen species** and **limestone flora** establish in alkaline areas.
- **Large snails and dense snail populations** occur only over alkaline soils or in alkaline water. No snails or only minute species are found in acid water (pH < 5.0).

### Cultivated Plant pH Preferences

**Acid-intolerant plants (prefer pH 4.5-6.0):**
Blueberry, chicory, chestnut, endive, potato, fennel, tea, shallot, coffee, watermelon, rhubarb.

**Acid-intolerant / alkali-tolerant plants (prefer pH 7.0-8.5):**
Cauliflower, cabbage, asparagus, green peas, bush beans, celery, leek, beet, onion, chard, parsnip, spinach.

**Acid-tolerant plants:**
Lupin, rye, oats, lucerne, broccoli, white clover.

### Practical Implications

Providing garden soils are mulched and a little lime is added to compost, all plants thrive in high-humus soils supplied with some lime at modest levels. Perennial species may need more care in site selection, or with mulch and compost in alkaline areas. Almost all pollutants and many fertilisers tend to make soils acid, as does continued cropping or over-grazing.

## 4. Fire Frequency

East-west ridges often reveal abrupt species changes at the ridge wherever fire occurs. Fire produces dry, scrabbly, summer-deciduous, thick-seeded species. Lack of fire develops broadleaf, winter-deciduous, small-seeded plants with thin seed capsules and deep litter fall.

### Reading Fire History

Cross-sectional cuts of trees reveal fire scars as gum pockets or charred sections. These can be counted to determine the "fire frequency" of a site. If tree stem sections are marked for directions before sampling, the direction of fires can also be determined.

## 5. Frost Indicators

Many species indicate frost-lines. It is a matter of observing local flora, or planting frost-susceptible species down a hill profile to measure frost intensity. Tomatoes, bananas, and potatoes are all frost-sensitive and will reveal frost-lines on hills in subtropics and deserts.

## 6. Drainage Indicators

- **Mosses, sundews, and fine-leaved heaths** indicate poorly-drained soils.
- **Large trees such as chestnuts** (which require 2 metres of well-drained soil) indicate good drainage.

These biological indicators assist survey before pits are dug or drainage measured.

## 7. Mineral Deposits

Plant systems over mineral deposits can indicate the presence of ores in underlying soil or rock. Research by Davidov (1979) documented these associations for Russian soils:

**Lead and copper-molybdate deposits** are indicated by leaves or humus from birch, cherry, honeysuckle, St. John's Wort, wormwood, juniper, and heather, which also reveal tungsten and tin concentrates.

### Specific Plant-Ore Associations

| Plant | Concentrates |
|-------|-------------|
| Rue of Violets | Zinc |
| Catchfly | Cobalt |
| Astragalus | Selenium |
| Milk Vetch | Selenium and uranium |
| Russian Thistle | Boron |
| Alyssum | Nickel |
| Honeysuckle | Silver and gold |
| Horsetail | Gold and silica |

### Implications

These associations have several important applications:

1. **Mine waste rehabilitation**: Selecting appropriate plant species for the biological concentration and removal of metallic soil pollutants like lead or uranium.
2. **Trace mineral sourcing**: Using concentrator plants to supply trace minerals for compost.
3. **Biological mining**: Some patents have been granted for mining gold deposits using banana or citrus plants deprived of common elements (potash, phosphate); their leaves then concentrate sparse gold deposits.
4. **Public health monitoring**: Oysters concentrate zinc (to 11% dry weight — an emetic dose), abalone concentrate cadmium, and several large fish concentrate mercury. Lead, cadmium, and mercury levels in fish or plants need careful monitoring.

## 8. Overgrazing and Soil Compaction

Overgrazing is indicated by high levels of grasshopper and pasture grub activity, and the presence of patches of poisonous, inedible, thorny, and unpalatable plants (e.g., Sodom apple, oxalis, capeweed).

The effect is a synthesis between changing soil conditions, plant stress, and heavy livestock selection of palatable species, favouring the survival and spread of spiny or inedible species.

### Recommended Approach

Rather than blaming weeds and seeking chemical solutions, a combined approach is more effective:

1. Utilise grasshoppers and grubs as a valuable dried-protein supplement for fish or food pellets.
2. Combine soil conditioning, slashing, and de-stocking or re-seeding to restore species balance.

## 9. Macrofaunal Effects

The site of a sea-bird rookery, a rabbit warren, a goose or eider ground nest, an owl pellet-pile, or the decay of a large carcass will cause sudden and often long-term vegetation change, as will termite mounds and harvester-ant colonies.

Once such sites are recorded and the plant assembly identified, similar sites can be located and recognised. The data can be used for conservation planning, soil drainage indication (rabbits choose good drainage), specific nutrient supply identification (guano on seabird rookeries), or as a way to establish tree clumps following natural indicators.

## 10. Seed Pelleting for Soil Rehabilitation

In pioneering soil rehabilitation or stabilisation, local soil deficiencies can be overcome through [[seed-pelleting]] — embedding seed in a capsule of substances that give it a good chance of establishment despite soil deficiencies.

### Process

1. **Seed pretreatment**: If seeds have thick coats, apply heat, cold, or scarification to break dormancy before pelleting.
2. **Inoculation**: Purchase and inoculate legume seed with appropriate microbial or fungal spores. Soak seed in inoculant solution, then dry. Mix dried seed with the primary coat.
3. **Pelleting**: Use a lime, clay layer, and a trace of fine rock flour, calcium, or phosphate mixed into a damp but plastic slurry around the seed. Extrude via a meat mincer with cutting blades removed to a shaker table or tray covered with dust on a slight incline.
4. **Dust coat**: Should incorporate a soil conditioning gel or polymer, a colloid-forming substance (fine graphite), a bird repellent (green dye), an insect repellent (powdered neem tree leaf or diatomaceous earth), and swelling clay such as bentonite.
5. **Drying**: Pellets are dried and scattered, drilled, or sown on sites to await rain.

The protected seed germinates when the pellet absorbs water, and the emerging root penetrates the enriched coating to establish in otherwise hostile soil conditions.
