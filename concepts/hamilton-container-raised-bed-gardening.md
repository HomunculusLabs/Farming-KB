they offer unique opportunities for creative planting and for catering to
specific plant needs.

## Choosing Containers

Almost any container can be used provided it has adequate drainage. Large
containers are better than small ones because they hold more moisture and
nutrients, reducing maintenance. Materials include terracotta, plastic,
wood, metal, and stone. Wooden containers and half barrels are attractive
and practical. Ensure drainage holes in the bottom and cover with broken
pot shards or gravel to prevent blockage.

## Compost for Containers

Use a good quality peat-free compost. For long-term plantings (shrubs,
perennials), add loam-based compost or garden soil to improve stability
and nutrient retention. For acid-loving plants like blueberries and
camellias, use ericaceous compost. Mix in perlite or vermiculite for
improved drainage.

## Herbs in Containers

Herbs are an obvious choice when space is limited. They are attractive
and provide fresh herbs conveniently close to the kitchen door. Stick to
shorter-growing types: thyme, sage, chives, rosemary, and parsley. Mint
must be confined in containers as it is extremely invasive.

## Vegetables in Containers

Most shorter-growing vegetables do well in containers. Mix them with a
few flowers to improve appearance and attract useful insects. A tub of
lettuce, onions, carrots, and marigolds is a good combination. Bush
tomatoes on their own look every bit as good as flowers.

## Strawberries in Containers

Strawberries are best grown in shallow containers. Traditional
strawberry barrels have a common problem: plants lower in the barrel
do not receive enough water. The solution is to insert a central
drainpipe filled with gravel before filling the barrel. This allows
water to reach all levels when you water through the pipe.

## Alpine Plants and Troughs

Alpine plants make interesting and eye-catching troughs, especially
useful for shallow containers. Almost any alpine will grow happily in
a trough provided there is good drainage. Use a free-draining compost
of equal parts loam and sharp sand, with plenty of drainage holes.

## Hanging Baskets

Hanging baskets brighten walls in summer and winter. The secret of
success is planting to create a complete ball of flowers -- use deep
wire or mesh baskets planted on top and through the sides.

For a summer basket, plant a central bushy plant (geranium or fuchsia)
surrounded by smaller plants (petunias, French marigolds, alyssum) and
trailing varieties (lobelia, ivy). Use coir or wool liners instead of
endangered sphagnum moss.

In winter, use frost-hardy plants: variegated pieris, winter heathers,
ivies, and evergreen honeysuckle. Bulbs like crocus and species tulips
can be added for spring color.

## Raised Beds

### For Acid-Loving Plants
If your soil is very chalky or alkaline and you want to grow acid-
loving plants (rhododendrons, azaleas, camellias, pieris), build a
raised bed from railroad ties. Fill with half acid soil and half peat-
substitute, or three parts peat-substitute to one part sharp sand.
Spread chipped [[hamilton-deep-bed-system]] (4 ft wide) doubles productive land by eliminating
unproductive paths between rows. Dig deeply, break up subsoil, and
