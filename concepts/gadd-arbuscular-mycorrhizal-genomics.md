# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Genomics

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi form one of the most ancient and
widespread symbiotic associations on Earth, dating back approximately
460 million years to the Ordovician period. The development of genomic
tools has opened new avenues for understanding the molecular basis of
this mutualistic relationship between fungi and plants. Genomic
approaches including expressed sequence tag (EST) databases,
transcriptional profiling, and reverse genetics are revealing the
complex signalling and developmental programs that underlie the AM
symbiosis, complementing the ecological understanding provided by

with roots. The fungus differentiates on the epidermis, forming an
appressorium through which it penetrates the root. Appressorium
formation occurs only on root surfaces, indicating a requirement for
specific plant-derived signals. Once inside the root, the fungus grows
through intercellular spaces to the inner cortex where it forms
arbuscules within cortical cells. Arbuscules are highly branched hyphae
enveloped in a plant-derived peri- arbuscular membrane. Nutrient
exchange takes place across the interface between the arbuscule and the
cortical cell, making the arbuscule central to symbiotic function. In
Arum-type associations, arbuscules develop by repeated dichotomous
branching of hyphal branches that penetrate cortical [[alpha-1-3-glucan-fungal-pathogen-cell-walls]].

## EST Resources and cDNA Libraries

Significant numbers of ESTs have been generated from mycorrhizal cDNA
libraries. The majority derive from Medicago truncatula in association
with [[root-exudates]], such as pyruvate carboxylase and mitochondrial
ATP/ADP translocase. During appressoria formation, nine G. mosseae genes
were identified with predicted roles in signalling, metabolism, translation,
defence, and stress, including a MAP3k-like protein kinase and a leucine
zipper transmembrane protein.

## Arbuscule-Associated Genes

Arbuscule development is accompanied by significant cortical cell alterations:
cytoskeletal rearrangement, vacuole fragmentation, [[clamp-connection-formation-and-nuclear-migration]] towards
the arbuscule, and a four-fold increase in plant plasma membrane length.
Genes expressed in arbuscule-containing cells include defence-related proteins
(chitinases, pathogenesis-related proteins), carbon and phosphate
transporters, cytoskeleton components, hormone-responsive proteins, and cell
wall-modifying enzymes.

The [[mycorrhiza-induced-resistance-defense-priming]] phosphate transporter
MtPT4 is located in the peri-arbuscular membrane and functions in phosphate
uptake. MtGST1, a glutathione-S-transferase, shows expression in both
arbuscule-containing and adjacent cells. MtXTH1, a xyloglucan endo-trans-
glycosylase/hydrolase, shows systemic expression throughout the root,
responding to a systemic signal.

## Cell Wall-Associated Proteins

Throughout the symbiosis, the AM fungus lives in the apoplastic compartment
of the root. Genes encoding cell-wall-associated proteins are prominently
expressed, including extensins, arabinogalactan proteins, cellulases, and
serine carboxypeptidases. These proteins likely function in modifying the
apoplastic interface, facilitating [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]], and regulating the
[[serotonin-psilocybin-structural-relationship-hallucinogenic-mechanism]] between fungal and plant cell surfaces.

## Reverse Genetics and Future Prospects

RNA interference has been demonstrated as a functional tool in M. truncatula
and Lotus japonicus, coupled with Agrobacterium rhizogenes-mediated root
transformation. This approach enables analysis of mycorrhiza-induced gene
function, including genes associated with arbuscule development and nutrient
transport. The completion of model plant genomes will facilitate rapid
promoter identification and characterization of spatial expression patterns.

The future of AM genomics lies in obtaining the complete genome sequence of
an AM fungus, which would provide unprecedented insight into the genetic
basis of obligate biotrophy and the molecular evolution of this ancient
symbiosis.
