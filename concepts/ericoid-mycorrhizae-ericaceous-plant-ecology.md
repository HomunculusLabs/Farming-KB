# Ericoid Mycorrhizae and Ericaceous Plant Ecology

[[ericoid-mycorrhizae]] represent one of the most specialized mycorrhizal associations in nature, formed between members of the Ericaceae plant family and a limited group of ascomycete fungi, most notably *[[hymenoscyphus-ericae]]* (syn. *Rhizoscyphus ericae*). These symbioses are particularly important in nutrient-poor, acidic habitats where ericaceous plants dominate, such as heathlands, bogs, and boreal forest understories.

## Ericoid Mycorrhizal Structure and Function

### Root Anatomy

The ericoid mycorrhizal root differs markedly from other [[mycorrhizal-types]]:

- Ericaceous plants produce very fine "hair roots" with limited secondary thickening
- The fungus forms a dense coiling hyphal complex within individual epidermal cells of the root
- Unlike ECM associations, there is no external fungal mantle or [[hartig-net]]
- The intracellular coils are the primary site of nutrient exchange between fungus and plant

The structure and function of the ericoid mycorrhizal root have been comprehensively described (Read, 1996), and the integrated development between roots and fungi follows distinct patterns compared to other mycorrhizal types (Peterson and Farquhar, 1994).

### The Ericoid Endophyte

*Hymenoscyphus ericae* is the primary fungal partner in ericoid mycorrhizae and possesses remarkable enzymatic capabilities:

- Extracellular proteinase activity enables degradation of complex nitrogen sources (Leake and Read, 1989)
- Proteinase production varies with extracellular pH, allowing function across different soil conditions (Leake and Read, 1990b)
- Chitin degradation capability allows the fungus to access chitin-nitrogen from soil microarthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls (Kerley and Read, 1995; Leake and Read, 1990a)
- Phosphodiesterase production enables utilization of DNA as a phosphorus source (Leake and Miles, 1996)
- The fungus can utilize nuclei as both phosphorus and nitrogen sources (Myers and Leake, 1996)

## Nitrogen Nutrition

### Peptide and Protein Utilization

Ericoid mycorrhizae are exceptionally efficient at accessing organic nitrogen:

- Peptides serve as nitrogen sources for the ericoid endophyte and for both mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal host plants (Bajwa and Read, 1985)
- The biology of mycorrhiza in the Ericaceae encompasses multiple nitrogen acquisition strategies across different species
- Chitin degradation by *H. ericae* and transfer of chitin-nitrogen to the host plant has been demonstrated (Kerley and Read, 1995)

### Phosphorus Nutrition

- Utilization of both inorganic and organic phosphate by ericoid endophytes has been studied in cranberry (*[[vaccinium-macrocarpon]]*) and rhododendron (*Rhododendron ponticum*) (Mitchell and Read, 1981)
- Phosphodiesters serve as mycorrhizal P sources through phosphodiesterase activity (Leake and Miles, 1996)
- Nuclei provide both P and N to *V. macrocarpon* through ericoid mycorrhiza (Myers and Leake, 1996)

## Multiple Mycorrhizal Associations

### Co-occurrence of Mycorrhizal Types

Some ericaceous plants can support multiple types of mycorrhizal associations simultaneously:

- Evidence suggests that some ericaceous plants can support ericoid, ecto-, and possibly arbuscular mycorrhizae (Dighton and Coleman, 1992; Smith et al., 1995)
- ECM and endomycorrhizal fungi can occur within the same root system (Chilvers et al., 1987)
- Mycorrhizae on ericaceous and pyrolaceous plants in northern California show complex patterns (Largent et al., 1980)

### Functional Significance of Multiple Partners

The ability to associate with different mycorrhizal types raises important ecological questions:

- Why should a plant need multiple mycorrhizal types?
- Does switching between mycorrhizal partners have ecosystem-level consequences?
- Tree species have been observed changing dominant mycorrhizal associates from arbuscular to ectomycorrhizal as they age
- These transitions may reflect changing nutrient demands or environmental conditions during plant development

## Blueberry Mycorrhizae

The mycorrhizal ecology of commercially important Ericaceae, particularly blueberries, has received research attention:

- Current knowledge of blueberry mycorrhizae and future research directions have been reviewed (Goulart et al., 1993)
- AM colonization of halophytes in central European salt marshes includes ericaceous species (Hildebrandt et al., 2001)
- Mycorrhizal infection of *Rhododendron maximum* in the southern Appalachians shows phosphorus relations linked to both ericoid and other mycorrhizal types (Dighton and Coleman, 1992)

## Ericaceous Plants in Disturbed and Restored Habitats

### Mediterranean Ecosystem Restoration

- Inoculation of woody legumes with selected AM fungi and rhizobia helps recover desertified Mediterranean ecosystems (Herrera et al., 1993)
- Mycorrhizal inoculation improves growth of Ethiopian acacias in non-sterile soil (Michelsen, 1993)
- AM fungi, phosphorus, and drought stress interact to affect growth of *Acacia nilotica* and *Leucaena leucocephala* seedlings (Michelsen and Rosendahl, 1990)

### Successional Dynamics

- Vegetation dynamics and AM associations in old-field successions of the western Italian Alps show patterns of mycorrhizal development (Barni and Siniscalco, 2000)
- Dune stabilization plants along the North Atlantic coast show field and greenhouse AM colonization (Gemma and Koske, 1997)

## Experimental Approaches

### Laboratory and Field Methods

A rich methodological literature supports ericoid mycorrhizal research:

- Experiments with ericoid mycorrhizae follow established protocols (Leake and Read, 1991)
- Comparative efficiency of different ECM fungi on radiata pine seedlings has been assessed (Chu-Chou and Grace, 1985)
- Coinoculation of Douglas fir with pairs of ECM fungi shows both synergistic and competitive effects (Parlade and Alvarez, 1993)
- Field use of 32P tracers to measure phosphate uptake by birch mycorrhizas provides in situ data (Dighton et al., 1990)

### Mycorrhizal Fungi in Terrestrial Habitats

The broader context of mycorrhizal fungi in terrestrial habitats has been reviewed (Leake and Read, 1997), and the role of mycorrhizae in ecosystems has been framed as nature's response to the "Law of the Minimum" (Read, 1991b).

## Climate and Environmental Interactions

### Drought and Endophytes

- Abiotic stresses and morphological plasticity in *Neotyphodium*-infected tall fescue demonstrate how fungal endophytes mediate plant responses to stress (Belesky and Malinowski, 2000)
- Effects of drought on perennial ryegrass genotypes with and without fungal endophytes show differential responses (Cheplick et al., 2000)

### CO₂ and Atmospheric Effects

- Effects of increased CO₂ concentration and temperature on phylloplane mycoflora of wheat flag leaves during ripening show atmospheric effects on fungal communities (Magan and Baxter, 1996)
- Lichen carbon gain under tropical conditions demonstrates water relations and CO₂ exchange patterns (Lange et al., 2000)

### Marine and Aquatic Fungi

- The role of fungi in marine ecosystems encompasses biodiversity conservation concerns (Hyde et al., 1998)
- A newly discovered role for aerobic fungi in anaerobic salt marsh soils expands our understanding of fungal habitat tolerance (Padgett and Celio, 1990)
- Occurrence and ecological significance of mycorrhizal symbiosis in aquatic plants has been documented (Khan, 1993; Khan and Belik, 1995)

## Heavy Metal Tolerance

Ericoid mycorrhizae play a role in plant tolerance to heavy metal contamination:

- Mycorrhizal infection in the Ericaceae contributes to heavy metal resistance (Bradley et al., 1982)
- Mycorrhizas and environmental stress interactions affect plant performance in contaminated soils (Colpaert and Van Tichelen, 1996)
