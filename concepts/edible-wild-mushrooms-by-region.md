
## Overview

Edible wild mushroom species vary significantly by region in North America. This guide covers the most sought-after edible species for major regions, organized by season. Always verify identification with multiple sources and an experienced forager before consuming any wild mushroom.

## Northeast (New England, Mid-Atlantic)

### Spring (April-June)
- **Morels (Morchella americana, M. esculentoides)**: Peak in May; under dead elms, ash, tulip poplar, and old apple trees; black morels appear first, followed by yellow and half-free morels
- **[[oyster-mushrooms]] ([[pleurotus-ostreatus]])**: On dead hardwood; available spring through fall
- **Dryad's saddle (Cerioporus squamosus)**: May on dead elm; only young specimens are edible; can be tough
- **Pheasant back ([[polyporus-umbellatus]])**: Spring on dead maple and oak

### Summer (June-August)
- **Chanterelles (Cantharellus cibarius)**: Late June through August; oak and beech forests
- **[[chicken-of-the-woods]] ([[laetiporus-sulphureus]])**: June onward; on oak and other hardwoods
- **Black trumpet (Craterellus fallax)**: Late summer in mixed hardwood forests
- **Boletes ([[boletus-edulis]], B. bicolor, B. pallidus)**: Mid-summer; under oaks and conifers

### Fall (September-November) -- Peak Season
- **Hen of the woods/Maitake (Grifola frondosa)**: September-October; base of oak trees; can be enormous (up to 50 lbs)
- **Honey mushroom (Armillaria mellea)**: October-November; on stumps and roots; must be thoroughly cooked
- **Lion's mane (Hericium erinaceus)**: Fall on dead/dying hardwood; beech and oak
- **Shaggy mane (Coprinus comatus)**: Lawns and disturbed ground; must be cooked immediately (deliquesces)
- **Meadow mushroom (Agaricus campestris)**: Fields and pastures; must distinguish from toxic Amanita species
- **King bolete (Boletus edulis)**: September-October under spruce and birch

### Winter (December-March)
- **Oyster mushrooms**: Winter flushes on hardwoods during warm spells
- **Enoki (Flammulina velutipes)**: Winter on elm; cultivated enoki is a different strain
- **Witch's butter (Tremella mesenterica)**: On dead hardwood after rain; edible but bland

## Southeast (Appalachians, Piedmont, Coastal Plain)

### Spring
- **Morels**: March-April in southern Appalachians; earlier than Northeast; tulip poplar and ash associates
- **Pheasant back**: Spring on hardwoods
- **Oyster mushrooms**: Begin in March on hardwoods

### Summer
- **Chanterelles**: June-August; abundant in Appalachian cove forests
- **Chicken of the woods**: Summer through fall; very common in the Southeast
- **Indigo milk cap (Lactarius indigo)**: Summer under oaks; stunning blue color; mild flavor
- **Coral mushrooms (Ramaria spp.)**: Summer in mixed forests; some species cause GI upset

### Fall -- Peak Season
- **Hen of the woods**: September-November; oak forests
- **Lion's mane**: Fall in Appalachian hardwood forests
- **Matsutake (Tricholoma murrillianum)**: October-November in piedmont; pine forests; highly prized
- **Wood blewit (Clitopilus prunulus)**: Late fall; leaf litter; pink spore print
- **Sweet tooth/Hedgehog (Hydnum repandum)**: Fall under oaks and conifers
- **Parasol mushroom (Macrolepiota procera)**: Open fields and roadsides; tall, distinctive

### Winter
- **Oyster mushrooms**: Winter flushes common in mild Southeast winters
- **Velvet foot (Flammulina velutipes)**: Winter on hardwoods

## Midwest (Great Lakes, Ohio Valley, Plains)

### Spring
- **Morels**: April-May; the Midwest is famous for morel hunting; old apple orchards, dead elms, ash, cottonwood; black, yellow, and gray morels
- **Dryad's saddle**: Spring on dead trees
- **Oyster mushrooms**: Spring on dead hardwoods

### Summer
- **Chanterelles**: July-August; oak-hickory forests
- **Chicken of the woods**: Summer on oak
- **King bolete**: Mid-summer under oaks and conifers in northern areas
- **Old man of the woods (Strobilomyces strobilaceus)**: Late summer in oak forests

### Fall -- Peak Season
- **Hen of the woods**: September-October; oak is the primary host
- **Honey mushroom**: October; abundant in the Midwest; must be well-cooked
- **Shaggy mane**: Roadsides and disturbed areas
- **Puffballs (Calvatia gigantea, Lycoperdon spp.)**: Fields and lawns; slice open to verify pure white interior (no gills)
- **Bear's head tooth fungus (Hericium coralloides)**: Fall on dead/dying hardwood
- **Beefsteak polypore (Fistulina hepatica)**: Fall on oak; sour, acidic flavor

### Winter
- Limited foraging; oyster mushrooms on warm days
- Enoki on dead elm in southern Midwest

## West (Rocky Mountains, Southwest, Great Basin)

### Spring
- **Morels**: May-June; conifer burn sites (1-3 years post-fire); river corridors with cottonwood
- **Oyster mushrooms**: Spring on cottonwood and aspen

### Summer
- **Chanterelles**: July-August in mountain conifer forests
- **King bolete (Boletus edulis var. pinophilus)**: August-September under spruce and fir; highly prized
- **Cauliflower mushroom (Sparassis crispa)**: Late summer at base of conifers; harvest sustainably
- **Lobster mushroom (Hypomyces lactifluorum)**: Parasitizes Russula and Lactarius; August-September; bright orange-red

### Fall
- **Matsutake (Tricholoma magnivelare)**: September-November; under ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir; commercially important
- **Hedgehog mushroom (Hydnum umbilicatum)**: Fall under conifers
- **Coral mushrooms (Ramaria spp.)**: Late summer-fall in conifer forests
- **Wood ear (Auricularia americana)**: Fall on dead hardwoods and elderberry; rehydrates well
- **Turkey tail (Trametes versicolor)**: Technically edible when young; better for medicinal tea

### Winter
- Very limited; some oyster mushrooms on warm days
- Velvet foot on hardwoods in mild canyon bottoms

## Pacific Northwest (Washington, Oregon, Northern California)

### Spring
- **Morels**: April-May; burn sites are legendary (see "burn morels"); also river corridors with cottonwood
- **Oyster mushrooms**: Spring on alder and bigleaf maple
- **Angel wings (Pleurocybella porrigens)**: Fall on conifer logs; exercise caution (some reports of toxicity in vulnerable individuals)

### Summer
- **Chanterelles**: July-August; begin earlier than most regions
- **King bolete (Boletus edulis)**: Late summer under Douglas-fir; the Pacific Northwest is famous for boletes
- **Cauliflower mushroom**: Late summer under Sitka spruce and Douglas-fir
- **Golden chanterelle (Cantharellus formosus)**: The Pacific golden chanterelle; Oregon state mushroom

### Fall -- World-Class Foraging
- **Chanterelles**: October-December; massive fruitings in coastal forests
- **Matsutake (Tricholoma murrillianum)**: October-November under Douglas-fir; major commercial harvest
- **Porcini/King bolete**: September-November; abundant in Cascade and Coast Range forests
- **Lobster mushroom**: Fall in mixed forests
- **Coral mushrooms**: Fall under conifers
- **Yellowfoot (Craterellus tubaeformis)**: Late fall-winter in conifer duff
- **Hedgehog mushroom**: Fall under Sitka spruce and hemlock
- **Candy cap (Lactarius rubidus)**: November-December; maple forests; famous for desserts (maple-curry flavor when dried)
- **Wood ear**: Fall and winter on alder
- **Matsutake**: See also the "matsutake corridor" of the Cascade Range

### Winter
- **Yellowfoot chanterelles**: December-February; the Pacific Northwest has the best winter foraging in North America
- **Oyster mushrooms**: Winter on alder
- **Enoki**: Winter on alder and cottonwood
- **Black trumpet (Craterellus cornucopioides)**: Winter in coastal forests

## Dangerous Look-alikes by Region

### Amanita Species (Nationwide)
- **Death cap (Amanita phalloides)**: West Coast, expanding eastward; looks like edible [[paddy-straw-mushroom]]
- **Destroying angel (A. virosa, A. bisporigera)**: White mushroom in the button stage resembles meadow mushroom and puffballs
- Always check for: white gills, volva (cup at base), ring on stem, growing from volva in soil
- Rule: never eat any white-gilled mushroom unless you are absolutely certain

### Galerina marginata (Nationwide)
- Looks similar to honey mushroom and some Psilocybe species
- Contains the same deadly amatoxins as Amanita phalloides
- Key difference: Galerina has a brown spore print (honey mushroom has white)
- Growing on wood is NOT a safety indicator

### Jack O'Lantern (Omphalotus olearius)
- Orange mushroom that resembles chanterelles
- Key differences: grows in clusters on wood (chanterelles on soil), has true gills (chanterelles have blunt ridges), is bioluminescent
- Causes severe GI distress
