# Banks, Benches, and Terracing

Banks, benches, and terraces are three fundamental earthwork techniques used in [[green-manure-crops]] improve soil structure and infiltration while their roots bind the soil.

### Conditions Leading to Terrace Failure

Despite their potential stability, terraces can and do fail under certain conditions:

- **Unstable soils**: Sandy, highly organic, or dispersive soils lack the cohesion needed to maintain steep terrace faces. These soils require additional stabilisation measures such as rock facing, geotextiles, or very gentle berm angles.
- **Hydraulic pressure**: If water accumulates behind a terrace bund or wall faster than it can drain away, the resulting hydrostatic pressure can cause catastrophic failure. Proper drainage through and around terraces is non-negotiable.
- **Unstable bund or wall face**: Bunds that are too steep, made of poor-quality material, or insufficiently compacted will slump or collapse. Wall faces must be designed for the specific soil and water conditions of the site.
- **Too-large annual crop terraces**: Very large terrace platforms dedicated to annual crops can be problematic because annual crops do not provide year-round root reinforcement. When the crop is harvested and the soil is bare, the terrace is at its most vulnerable. Smaller terraces with permanent vegetated edges are more resilient.

### Retaining Walls in Terrace Systems

In situations where the slope is too steep or the soil too unstable for earth bunds alone, retaining walls may be necessary. Walls should be designed to allow drainage through them (weep holes, gravel backfill) and should be sized appropriately for the soil pressure they will bear. In permaculture design, retaining walls should be considered a last resort due to their cost and the energy required for construction; living or vegetated solutions should be attempted first.

### Steep Terrace Systems

On very steep slopes, terrace systems must be carefully designed with narrow platforms, robust structural support, and integrated drainage. The steeper the slope, the more critical water management becomes. On extreme slopes, terracing may need to be combined with other stabilisation measures such as rock gabions, anchored geogrids, or intensive tree planting on the terrace faces and risers.

## Integration in Permaculture Design

Banks, benches, and terraces are rarely used in isolation. An integrated design might use banks along contour lines for water harvesting, benched roads for access across a slope, and terraces for intensive food production — all working together to stabilise the landscape and maximise productivity. The choice between these techniques depends on slope angle, soil type, water availability, intended land use, and available resources. In all cases, vegetation is the primary long-term stabilising agent, and earthworks should be planted and protected as soon as possible after construction.
