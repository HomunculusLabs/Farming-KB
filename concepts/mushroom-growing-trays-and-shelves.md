# Mushroom Growing Trays and Shelves

The physical infrastructure for holding substrate during colonization and fruiting is a defining feature of [[mushroom-cultivation-in-kenya]] systems. Trays and shelves represent the two primary approaches for mushroom-composting|Agaricus and [[growing-gourmet-tree-species-guide-mushroom-cultivation]] production, each with distinct advantages in terms of labor efficiency, space utilization, and crop management.

This content draws from Paul Stamets' The Mushroom Cultivator, which describes commercial tray and shelf systems in detail.

## Tray Systems

Tray cultivation is the traditional method for Agaricus production and remains the most common commercial system worldwide. The basic concept involves filling standardized containers with compost, spawning, casing, and fruiting the mushrooms directly in the trays.

Standard tray dimensions vary but typically measure 4-6 feet long, 2 feet wide, and 6-8 inches deep. Wooden trays have been largely replaced by aluminum or plastic for durability and ease of cleaning. Each tray holds a specific volume of compost, allowing precise calculation of spawn rates, supplement amounts, and expected yields.

Commercial tray farms use automated systems for filling, spawning, and casing that can complete all three operations in less time and with fewer workers than manual methods. Automated tray lines are a significant capital investment but dramatically reduce labor costs at scale.

## Shelf Systems

Shelf cultivation eliminates the individual container, instead filling compost directly onto permanent shelf structures within the growing room. Shelves are typically tiered at 18-24 inch vertical intervals, with each tier serving as a growing bed.

The advantages of shelf systems include better space utilization (no gaps between trays), lower equipment costs (no trays to purchase or maintain), and more compost per square foot of growing room floor space. Shelves also eliminate the labor of moving trays between rooms.

The disadvantages include more difficult environmental control (compost depth and mass affect temperature distribution), more challenging cleaning between crops, and less flexibility in crop scheduling. With shelf systems, the entire room must typically be emptied, cleaned, and refilled as a unit, whereas trays can be rotated through rooms individually.

## Phase II on Trays vs. Shelves

The [[stamets-phase-ii-composting]] process can be conducted with either trays or shelves, but the procedures differ.

For trays, Phase II is carried out with trays stacked in the growing room with 15 cm spacing between them. The trays are tightly placed within the room to fully utilize compost heat. During Phase II and spawn running, this tight stacking promotes heat retention. After spawning, wooden spacers are inserted between trays to allow for air circulation and mycelial growth.

For shelves, Phase II is conducted directly on the shelf tiers. The compost depth must be carefully managed — depths over 5 inches create insufficient air circulation in the core, while depths under 5 inches may lack sufficient mass for proper heat generation during pasteurization.

The key to successful Phase II in either system is managing the heat generated by microbial activity. The compost-to-air temperature differential drives the pasteurization process. The greater this differential, the faster ammonia is fixed by thermophilic organisms.

## Bulk Room Systems

An alternative to both trays and shelves is the bulk room system, where compost is filled in a single large mass on the floor of the Phase II room, then spawned and moved to trays or shelves after conditioning is complete.

This approach allows more precise control of Phase II conditions because the large compost mass generates consistent, uniform heat. After Phase II, the conditioned compost is distributed to trays or shelves for spawning and fruiting.

Bulk systems are particularly advantageous for growers who use forced-air composting tunnels for Phase II, which has become the industry standard in large-scale operations.

## Environmental Considerations

The choice between trays and shelves affects environmental management in several ways.

Air circulation differs significantly. Trays with spacers allow air movement on all surfaces, promoting even temperature and CO2 distribution. Shelves with deep compost beds may develop temperature gradients from the core to the surface, requiring more sophisticated air handling systems.

Temperature management is more challenging with shelves because the large compost mass retains and generates more heat. Cooling capacity must be adequate to prevent overheating during spawn run, which can kill the mycelium.

CO2 management is affected by the ratio of compost surface area to room volume. Trays with spacing provide more surface area per unit volume, making CO2 control easier. Shelves with tight spacing require more aggressive ventilation.

## Hybrid Systems

Many commercial operations use hybrid approaches that combine elements of both systems. A common pattern is to use bulk rooms for Phase II and spawn run, then transfer spawned compost to trays for the fruiting phase. This provides the environmental control advantages of bulk composting with the flexibility of tray-based fruiting.

Another hybrid approach uses shelving for spawn run and trays for fruiting, allowing the grower to move colonized substrate to different rooms optimized for each phase of the crop cycle.

## Small-Scale Adaptations

For home and small-scale cultivators, tray systems are generally more practical than shelves. Standard plastic storage containers or custom-built wooden trays can be used at any scale. The key principles remain the same: consistent compost depth, adequate drainage, and proper environmental control.

[[mushroom-grow-room-design]] can be set up with simple shelving units holding standard-sized trays, allowing the cultivator to practice crop rotation and experiment with different [[dikaryon-formation-mushroom-strain-genetics]] and [[mushroom-substrate-comparison]] strategies on a manageable scale.

## Cleaning and Sanitation

Between crops, all surfaces that contact substrate must be thoroughly cleaned and sanitized. For trays, this means washing each tray individually with a detergent solution, rinsing, and applying a disinfectant. For shelves, the entire structure must be cleaned in place, which is more labor-intensive.

Steam cleaning is effective for both systems and has the advantage of reaching into cracks and crevices that manual cleaning may miss. Some operations use chemical disinfectants, though these must be thoroughly rinsed before the next crop cycle to avoid residue effects on mushroom mycelium.
