## Winter Salad Temperature Thresholds

Understanding how temperature affects winter salad crops in protected cultivation is essential for successful year-round harvesting. Temperature is not a simple binary — it operates through multiple dimensions that collectively determine crop health, growth rate, quality, and survival. [[eliot-coleman]] provides a comprehensive temperature scale and framework for managing winter salad production.

## The Temperature Scale for Protected Winter Cultivation

The following scale defines the key temperature thresholds that govern winter salad crop performance:

| Temperature | Significance |
|---|---|
| **115°F** | Upper lethal limit — plants die |
| **110°F** | Extreme heat stress |
| **100°F** | Severe heat stress |
| **95°F** | Maximum rate of photosynthesis; sustained exposure is damaging |
| **90°F** | High temperature stress zone |
| **80°F** | Warm but manageable |
| **75°F** | Optimum photosynthesis and plant growth |
| **70°F** | Excellent growing conditions |
| **60°F** | Good growing conditions |
| **55°F** | Typical daytime greenhouse temperature during the coldest winter months |
| **50°F** | Cool but productive |
| **40°F** | Slow growth; cold-tolerant crops still functioning |
| **35°F** | Ideal minimum greenhouse air temperature — crops do not freeze |
| **30°F** | Approaching freezing threshold |
| **20°F** | Cold but survivable for hardy crops under protection |
| **15°F** | Lower practical limit for quality harvest of most winter salad crops |
| **10°F** | Severe cold — only the hardiest crops survive with multiple protection layers |
| **0°F** | Extreme cold — multi-layer protection essential for any crop survival |

## The Four Factors of Temperature Stress

Coleman identifies four distinct dimensions of cold exposure that affect winter salad crops. Understanding each factor is critical because crops respond differently to each, and management strategies must address all four:

### 1. Rate of Temperature Drop

How quickly the temperature falls matters enormously. A gradual cooling over hours allows plants to activate their cold-hardiness mechanisms — accumulating sugars, modifying cell membranes, producing antifreeze proteins. A sudden temperature crash (e.g., when removing a row cover on a windy day) can cause cellular damage before these protective responses can engage.

**Management implications:**
- Avoid sudden exposure of protected crops to cold air
- Ventilate gradually rather than abruptly on sunny winter mornings
- Time row cover removal for the warmest part of the day
- Consider the thermal mass of the growing environment — heavy soil beds moderate temperature swings

### 2. Depth of Temperature Drop

The absolute minimum temperature reached determines whether the crop freezes. This is the most critical factor because freezing causes ice crystal formation in plant cells, which is physically destructive. The entire [[winter-harvest-system]] is designed around preventing the crop from reaching 32°F, even when outside temperatures drop far below that.

**Management implications:**
- Multiple layers of protection (greenhouse + inner layer + row cover) create cumulative insulation
- Each protection layer provides approximately 4-8°F of temperature differential from outside conditions
- The goal is to maintain the crop environment at or above 35°F regardless of outside conditions
- Monitor interior temperatures during cold snaps to verify protection adequacy

### 3. Duration of Temperature Drop

How long the temperature remains at stressful levels affects cumulative damage. Brief dips to near-freezing (a few hours overnight) are far less damaging than sustained periods at the same temperature. Plants can recover from short-duration stress if their core temperature returns to growing conditions during daylight hours.

**Management implications:**
- Winter days, even short ones, provide a recovery period when solar gain warms the protected environment
- Extended cold spells (multiple days and nights below freezing) require careful monitoring
- Crop quality may decline during extended cold periods but typically rebounds when warmer conditions return
- Planning harvest timing around warm spells yields the best quality

### 4. Frequency of Temperature Drop

Repeated freeze-thaw cycles are more damaging than a single sustained cold period. Each cycle stresses the plant's cellular structures and depletes its protective sugar reserves. This is why maintaining a stable above-freezing environment is more important than achieving warm growing conditions.

**Management implications:**
- Consistency of protection matters more than maximum insulation
- Avoid practices that cause daily temperature cycling near the freezing point
- Stable microclimates produce better quality than fluctuating ones
- If heating is used, maintaining a consistent minimum temperature is more valuable than occasional warm periods

## The 55°F Daytime Benchmark

During the coldest months (December through February at Coleman's Maine location), the typical daytime greenhouse temperature reaches approximately 55°F. This is well below the 75°F optimum for photosynthesis and growth, which explains why winter-harvested crops grow very slowly — sometimes imperceptibly — during the deepest cold.

However, 55°F is sufficient for:
- **Maintaining crop quality**: Crops hold in prime condition without bolting or becoming bitter
- **Slow, continuous growth**: Even minimal growth means continuous fresh harvest is possible
- **Sugar accumulation**: Cool temperatures promote sugar production in cold-hardy crops, enhancing flavor
- **Pest and disease suppression**: Most pests and diseases are inactive at these temperatures

The practical implication is that winter harvesting is primarily about **crop holding** — maintaining plants at harvestable quality — with growth as a secondary benefit. Crops are planted in fall to reach full size before the coldest weather, then held for progressive harvest through winter.

## The 35°F Minimum Threshold

Maintaining the greenhouse environment at or above 35°F is the critical management target. At this temperature, even the most cold-sensitive salad crops in the winter mix will not freeze. This threshold is achievable in an unheated structure through:
- **Solar gain**: Even weak winter sun warms the enclosed air during daylight
- **Thermal mass**: Soil and water within the structure absorb and slowly release heat
- **Multiple protection layers**: Each layer adds insulation value
- **Proper closing time**: Closing structures before the temperature drops in late afternoon traps accumulated warmth

The difference between 35°F and 32°F may seem small, but it is the difference between a living, harvestable crop and frozen, damaged tissue.

## The 15°F Lower Harvest Limit

While crops can survive temperatures below 35°F under multiple layers of protection, the practical lower limit for quality harvest of most winter salad crops is approximately 15°F. Below this temperature:
- Outer leaves may become damaged even with protection
- Harvesting causes physical damage to cold-stressed tissue
- Crop quality and shelf life decline significantly
- Recovery time after harvest extends substantially

In Coleman's experience, the winter harvest system works reliably when outside temperatures stay above 15°F with the standard three-layer protection system (greenhouse + inner layer + row cover). Colder events are manageable but may require temporary suspension of harvesting.

## Summer Temperature Considerations

The same protected structures used for winter production can become excessively hot in summer. Key summer thresholds include:
- **95°F maximum photosynthesis**: Above this, plants experience heat stress despite adequate light and water
- **Greenhouse ventilation**: Adequate ventilation (roll-up sides, roof vents, end walls) is essential to prevent overheating
- **Soil solarization**: In very hot climates, growers can close greenhouses during peak summer to solarize soil, using solar energy to kill soilborne pathogens and weed seeds
