# Fungal Detergent and Surfactant Biodegradation

Synthetic surfactants and detergents are among the most widely produced
and discharged organic chemicals worldwide, entering aquatic and
terrestrial environments through domestic and industrial wastewater. While
bacterial communities in activated sludge systems handle much of the
biological degradation of these compounds, fungi contribute important
complementary capabilities, particularly for recalcitrant surfactant
structures and under conditions unfavorable to bacteria. Singh's
treatment of fungal surfactant biodegradation connects to the broader
scope of [[mycoremediation-pahs]] and [[singh-wood-rot-fungi-structural-timber-decay]].

## Major Surfactant Classes in the Environment

### Linear Alkylbenzene Sulfonates (LAS)
LAS are the most widely used anionic surfactants, found in laundry
detergents, dishwashing liquids, and industrial cleaners. They consist
of a linear alkyl chain (typically C10 to C13) attached to a benzene ring
with a sulfonate group. LAS are moderately biodegradable under aerobic
conditions, with primary degradation (removal of the alkyl chain)
occurring faster than complete mineralization of the benzene sulfonate
ring. Their environmental concentrations in surface waters typically
range from micrograms to low milligrams per liter.

### Alkyl Ethoxylates and Alcohol Ethoxylates
Nonionic surfactants including alkyl phenol ethoxylates (APEOs) and
alcohol ethoxylates (AEs) are the second major class. APEOs such as
nonylphenol ethoxylates (NPEOs) are of particular concern because their
degradation intermediates, especially nonylphenol and octylphenol, are
endocrine-disrupting compounds with potent estrogenic activity. APEOs are
being phased out in many countries and replaced by AEs, which degrade to
less toxic fatty alcohols and polyethylene glycols.

### Other Surfactant Types
Cationic surfactants (quaternary ammonium compounds, or quats) are used
as disinfectants and fabric softeners. Amphoteric surfactants such as
betaines find applications in personal care products. Biosurfactants
including rhamnolipids, sophorolipids, and surfactin are microbial-derived
alternatives with generally higher biodegradability and lower toxicity
than their synthetic counterparts.

## [[fungal-enzyme-systems]] for Surfactant Degradation

### Cytochrome P450 Monooxygenases
Fungal cytochrome P450 enzymes play a central role in the initial
oxidation of alkyl chains in LAS and alcohol ethoxylates. These
heme-thiolate enzymes introduce hydroxyl groups into terminal and
subterminal carbon positions, initiating the pathway toward beta-oxidation
and chain shortening. The broad substrate specificity of fungal P450s
allows them to attack diverse alkyl chain lengths and branching patterns
that may resist bacterial degradation.

### Peroxidases and Laccases
White rot fungal peroxidases, including lignin peroxidase, manganese
peroxidase, and laccase, contribute to the degradation of the aromatic
portions of surfactants. These enzymes can oxidize the benzene ring of
LAS, cleave ether linkages in ethoxylate chains, and mineralize phenolic
degradation intermediates. Laccase from [[trametes-versicolor]] has been
shown to degrade nonylphenol and other endocrine-disrupting intermediates
of APEO degradation, offering a promising approach for detoxifying these
persistent metabolites.

### Esterases and Lipases
Fungal esterases and lipases hydrolyze ester bonds in surfactant molecules,
breaking complex structures into simpler, more biodegradable fragments.
These enzymes are particularly effective against ester-linked surfactants
and contribute to the primary degradation step that makes surfactant
molecules more accessible to other enzyme systems.

## Key Fungal Genera in Surfactant Biodegradation

Several fungal genera have demonstrated significant surfactant
degradation capabilities. White rot basidiomycetes including Trametes,
Phanerochaete, and Pleurotus species degrade LAS, NPEOs, and their
metabolites through their ligninolytic enzyme systems. Ascomycetes such
as Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Fusarium species are effective
degraders of alkyl chains through P450 and beta-oxidation pathways.
Cunninghamella species are notable for their ability to transform and
degrade quaternary ammonium compounds.

## Biosurfactants from Fungi

Beyond degrading synthetic surfactants, fungi produce biosurfactants that
can enhance [[pah-bioremediation]] of hydrophobic pollutants. Yeasts such as
Candida bombicola (now Starmerella bombicola) produce sophorolipids,
glycolipid biosurfactants with excellent surface-active properties.
Pseudozyma species produce mannosylerythritol lipids (MELs). These fungal
biosurfactants can emulsify hydrocarbons, increase their bioavailability
for microbial degradation, and have commercial applications as eco-friendly
detergent ingredients.

## Wastewater Treatment Applications

Fungal surfactant degradation is applied in several wastewater treatment
contexts. Fungal biofilters using immobilized white rot fungi on wood
chips or other carrier materials treat surfactant-containing effluents.
Fungal sequencing batch reactors have been tested for textile wastewater
containing both dyes and surfactants. The tolerance of many fungi to
extreme pH and high surfactant concentrations gives them advantages over
bacterial systems in industrial effluent treatment.

## Factors Affecting Fungal Surfactant Biodegradation

Degradation efficiency depends on surfactant concentration (high levels
can be toxic to fungi), temperature, pH, oxygen availability, and the
presence of co-contaminants. Mixed fungal-bacterial consortia often
outperform pure fungal cultures because bacteria efficiently handle
primary degradation products while fungi attack the more recalcitrant
structures. Nutrient availability, especially nitrogen and phosphorus,
influences enzyme production and overall degradation rates.

## Environmental Fate and Monitoring

Environmental monitoring of surfactant degradation typically involves
measuring parent compound concentrations and key metabolites using HPLC,
GC-MS, or LC-MS techniques. Biodegradation tests following OECD
guidelines (e.g., OECD 301, 306) assess ready and inherent
biodegradability. Molecular methods targeting fungal functional genes
(such as laccase and P450 genes) in environmental samples provide
insights into the role of indigenous fungal communities in surfactant
removal in natural systems.
