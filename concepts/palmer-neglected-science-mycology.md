# Palmer on the Neglected Science of Mycology in America

In his 1877 article "A Neglected Science," published in the Boston Daily
Globe, Palmer lamented the state of American mycology with particular
eloquence. While American botanists had classified nearly every green
herb on the continent, and collectors regularly documented new species
of beetles, birds, and fish, the fungi — "a race of beings about which
the schools tell us nothing" — remained almost entirely unstudied.

## The State of American Knowledge

Palmer wrote that when a child kicked a fungus over, "we call them
toadstools. When the cook broils them, they are mushrooms. This is the
limit of popular knowledge." He contrasted this ignorance with the
intensive study given to other branches of natural history: Amherst
vied with Harvard in the race for the longest name and clearest type,
while the vast kingdom of fungi lay untouched beneath everyone's feet.

## The Few American Authorities

Palmer could identify only a handful of American experts. The leading
English authorities — Cooke, Berkeley, Smith, Badham, and Hussey — had
no real American counterparts. In this country, Dr. Curtis and Mr.
Ravenal, both of South Carolina, were "worthy the name of experts."
Mr. Charles James Sprague of Boston had made valuable contributions to
the Boston Library of the Natural History Society through original
correspondence and foreign works. Mr. Charles Frost of Vermont was "a
living authority in this science."

Beyond these few, Palmer found that American literature showed "the
ingenuity of professional writers in weaving the web of other men's
thoughts and researches on this subject into diverse forms for the
magazines." Original contribution was almost nonexistent.

## Medical Ignorance

Palmer was particularly struck by the ignorance of the medical
profession. A prominent physician wrote to him in 1877: "If you can
give me the name of an author treating of the subject, or refer me to
an article, you will confer a great favor and receive my heartiest
thanks. I must plead to a lamentable ignorance, which I regret to find
I share with all medical men with whom I have conversed on the
subject."

Dr. D. G. Brinton of Philadelphia copied one of Palmer's articles with
the introduction: "The subject is so important, and the information on
it in the text-books so vague, that this cannot fail to be welcome to
many readers."

## Botanical Limitations

Even professional botanists offered little practical help. Dr. Curtis,
despite his courage in using many edible fungi, expressed surprise that
a mushroom which would liquify could be eaten. Mr. Sprague admitted he
ate only canned mushrooms. Professor Farlow of Harvard was an authority
on microscopic fungi but had published nothing on those of larger
growth. Professor Peck of the University of New York produced a long
series of reports naming varieties without discussing their edible or
poisonous qualities. Mr. Frost of Vermont did the same — in both cases,
"the classification serving to perpetuate the circumstances under which
some plant was handed to the gentlemen."

## Chemistry's Failure to Help

Palmer even turned to chemistry for assistance. He carried a vial
containing a black fluid resulting from the deliquescence of a fungus
to S. Dana Hayes, one of Boston's best-known assayers. Hayes told him
that someone had deceived him, as "nothing was known in nature which
would thus liquify." After watching the process himself, the
gentleman "cheerfully acknowledged his error."

## The Need for Popular Education

Palmer proposed a practical solution: the use of chromolithographs to
illustrate edible and poisonous species at natural size and proper
color. He suggested that "a little book, with explanations in popular
language, might accompany the pictures. One glance at such a work would
acquaint the ordinary mortal with the chances of death or life by
toadstools more surely than volumes of technical description."

This vision eventually materialized in his collaboration with Louis
Prang, the famous chromolithographer, though not without difficulty.
The published charts were, Palmer noted, "the first attempt in America
to popularize the fungi."

## Palmer's Assessment After Fifteen Years

Looking back in 1893 from the perspective of sixteen years of study,
Palmer acknowledged some progress. "Whatever I may have done has been
accomplished in the moments of forest recreation at intervals in a busy
life; its most valuable fruit is in the number now interested compared
with those who cared anything about it fifteen years ago." Yet he still
considered the general ignorance "nothing less than a disgrace," noting
that "not probably twelve men in America" could tell the average person
much more about mushrooms than they already knew.

## The Field's Promise

Despite his frustrations, Palmer saw enormous potential in the neglected
field. "With this limitation there is no field in nature that offers
such opportunity for new and important discoveries." The great need was
for "study, experiment, and analysis of the properties and nature of
non-microscopic fungi as esculents, poisons, or medicines" — work that
must be "systematic, and, above all, original, to be of any value."

## No American Work on Esculent Fungi

Palmer stressed that no American work on edible fungi had ever been
issued. There had been several patient students who had classified and
arranged this natural kingdom, but "their labors have been so
exclusively technical and scientific as to be of little benefit to the
mass of the people." The late Dr. M. A. Curtis of South Carolina had
left in manuscript a work on edible fungi illustrated with forty
colored species drawn by Rev. Charles J. Curtis, but "this book has
never found a publisher." This absence of accessible American
literature was, in Palmer's view, the fundamental barrier to wider
knowledge and safer use of fungi as food.
