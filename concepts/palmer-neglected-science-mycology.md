## Palmer on the Neglected Science of Mycology in America
In his 1877 article "A Neglected Science," published in the Boston Daily
Globe, Palmer lamented the state of [[mckenna-the-kingdom-of-fungi]]
lay untouched beneath everyone"s feet.

## The Few American Authorities

Palmer could identify only a handful of American experts. The leading
English authorities -- Cooke, Berkeley, Smith, Badham, and Hussey -- had no
real American counterparts. In this country, Dr. Curtis and Mr. Ravenal,
both of South Carolina, were "worthy the name of experts." Mr. Charles
James Sprague of Boston had made valuable contributions to the Boston Library
of the Natural History Society through original correspondence and foreign
works. Mr. Charles Frost of Vermont was "a living authority in this
science."
Beyond these few, Palmer found that American literature showed "the
ingenuity of professional writers in weaving the web of other men"s
thoughts and researches on this subject into diverse forms for the
magazines." Original contribution was almost nonexistent.

## Medical Ignorance

Palmer was particularly struck by the ignorance of the medical profession. A
prominent physician wrote to him in 1877: "If you can give me the name of an
author treating of the subject, or refer me to an article, you will confer a
great favor and receive my heartiest thanks. I must plead to a lamentable
ignorance, which I regret to find I share with all medical men with whom I
have conversed on the subject."
Dr. D. G. Brinton of Philadelphia copied one of Palmer"s articles with the
introduction: "The subject is so important, and the information on it in the
text-books so vague, that this cannot fail to be welcome to many readers."

## Botanical Limitations

Even professional botanists offered little practical help. Dr. Curtis,
despite his courage in using many edible fungi, expressed surprise that a
mushroom which would liquify could be eaten. Mr. Sprague admitted he ate
only canned mushrooms. Professor Farlow of Harvard was an authority on
microscopic fungi but had published nothing on those of larger growth.
Professor Peck of the University of New York produced a long series of
reports naming varieties without discussing their edible or poisonous
qualities. Mr. Frost of Vermont did the same -- in both cases, "the
classification serving to perpetuate the circumstances under which some plant
was handed to the gentlemen."

## Chemistry"s Failure to Help

Palmer even turned to chemistry for assistance. He carried a vial containing
a black fluid resulting from the deliquescence of a fungus to S. Dana Hayes,
one of Boston's best-known assayers. Hayes told him that someone had deceived
him, as "nothing was known in nature which would thus liquify." After
watching the process himself, the gentleman "cheerfully acknowledged his
error."

## The Need for Popular Education

Palmer proposed a practical solution: the use of chromolithographs to
illustrate edible and poisonous species at natural size and proper color. He
suggested that "a little book, with explanations in popular language, might
accompany the pictures. One glance at such a work would acquaint the ordinary
mortal with the chances of death or life by toadstools more surely than
volumes of technical description."
This vision eventually materialized in his collaboration with Louis Prang,
the famous chromolithographer, though not without difficulty. The published
charts were, Palmer noted, "the first attempt in America to popularize the
fungi."
