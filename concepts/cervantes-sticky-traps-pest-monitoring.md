# Sticky Traps and Physical Pest Monitoring for Cannabis

**Source:** Marijuana Horticulture: The Indoor/Outdoor Medical Grower's Bible
-- Jorge Cervantes

## Yellow Sticky Traps

Yellow sticky traps are used to monitor pest populations as well as to
control small flying insects. Sticky traps such as Tanglefoot resins can
be smeared on attractive yellow or red cards to simulate ripe fruit. When
pests land on the cards, they are stuck permanently.

### How They Work
The yellow or red color attracts flying insects that mistake the cards for
ripe fruit or flowers. The sticky coating (Tanglefoot or similar resin)
traps them on contact. This provides both population monitoring and
physical control.

### Target Pests
Yellow sticky traps help monitor and contain several key cannabis pests:
- Fungus gnats (primary target for monitoring)
- Thrips (helps control populations)
- Winged aphids (often the first to enter a garden)
- Whiteflies
- Other small flying insects get stuck haphazardly

### Placement Strategy
Install yellow sticky traps near the base of several plants and near the
tops of other plants. This dual placement monitors both ground-level and
canopy-level pest invasions. Winged aphids are often the first invaders
to enter a garden, so traps placed strategically will provide early
warning of infestations.

## Tanglefoot Barrier Traps

### Application
Smear Tanglefoot around the edges of pots, base of stems, and at the end
of drying lines to form an impenetrable barrier-trap against mites and
insects. This simple precaution helps keep mites isolated. However,
resourceful spider mites can spin a web above the barrier. The marauding
mites also ride air currents created by fans from plant to plant.

### What It Controls
Helps contain spider mites and non-flying insects within bounds. The
barrier traps are particularly useful for isolating infested plants from
healthy ones.

### Persistence
Tanglefoot is persistent until it is wiped off or completely fouled with
insect bodies.

### Forms
Sticky, thick paint. Not toxic to mammals or insects; trapped insects and
mites starve to death.

### Safety
Wear gloves. The sticky substance is very difficult to remove from skin.

## Black-Light Traps

Black-light traps catch egg-laying moths and other flying insects, most
of which are not plant pests. Cervantes cautions that light and fan traps
attract many insects including beneficials, and their use may do more
harm than good.

## Sex-Lure Traps

Sex-lure traps exude specific insect pheromones (sexual scents of females
ready to mate). These traps are most effective to monitor insect
populations for large farms but less practical for small indoor gardens.

## Water Sprays for Pest Control

A cold jet of water, preferably with a pH between 6 and 7, blasts
insects, spider mites, and their eggs off leaves and often kills them.
This simple physical method can be effective for small infestations and
is completely non-toxic.

## The Role of Monitoring in IPM

Cervantes outlines a logical progression of insect control:

1. Prevention (cleanliness, new soil, disease-resistant plants,
   climate control, companion planting)
2. Manual Removal (fingers, sponges)
3. Organic Sprays (pyrethrum, neem, insecticidal soap)
4. Natural Predators (beneficial insects)
5. Chemicals (last resort)

Monitoring with sticky traps fits into the prevention stage, providing
early detection that allows growers to intervene before infestations
become severe. A 30X battery-powered magnifying scope makes insects and
diseases easier to identify.

## Spreader-Stickers for Enhanced Control

Spreader-stickers improve and promote wetting and increase sticking and
absorption through foliage. They are especially important when plants
develop a waxy coating of resin. Spreader-stickers also impair insects'
respiration mechanisms and function as pesticides themselves.

## Homemade Pest Sprays

Cervantes also describes homemade sprays made from strong, hot-tasting,
smelly substances and desiccating powders or liquids. These can control
aphids, thrips, spider mites, scale, and many others. Make spray
concentrates by mixing repellent substances with a little water in a
blender, then strain through a nylon stocking before diluting with water.

Always test a new spray on a single plant and wait a few days to learn
the outcome before applying to all plants.
