## Identifying Poisonous Mushrooms

## The Danger of Amateur Mushroom Eating

[[julius-auboineau-palmer]] Jr. devoted considerable energy to warning the American public about the lethal risks of eating mushrooms without proper species-specific knowledge. His warnings were not theoretical — they were grounded in documented fatalities, case reports from physicians, and his own extensive experience.

As a self-described "professed fungus eater" who used various species of toadstools for food daily, gathering them fresh in summer and drying his surplus stock for winter use, Palmer spoke with the authority of deep [[palmer-mushroom-cooking-and-personal-experience]] tempered by genuine caution.

He identified several factors that make amateur mushroom eating especially perilous:

- **Most people do not know the taste of edible fungus**, so a novel but poisonous flavor may attract them momentarily — the indulgence in which experiment will cost them life itself
- **The lines of distinction between some fungal varieties are so closely drawn** that an amateur may cook three mushrooms believing them identical — one delightful, one deadly
- **No antidote to fungus poison has ever been discovered** — once the toxin is ingested, physicians are largely powerless to intervene
- **Fatal doses can be remarkably small** — in one case Palmer documented, no more than two tablespoonfuls of mushroom stew were eaten at the table, yet three people died

Palmer"s position was nuanced. He was not an alarmist who believed all wild mushrooms were dangerous. He personally had tested perhaps a hundred varieties on his own stomach, including some long considered poisonous, and had never been deceived. But he recognized that the consequences of a single mistake were so severe — "sure death" — that no one should presume to eat mushrooms without devoting sufficient time to study.

## The Fairy-Ring Champignon Hazard

Palmer provided a specific and instructive example of how easily even warned individuals can make dangerous mistakes. The fairy-ring champignon (*[[marasmius-oreades]]*) is a very common mushroom, easily recognized by experienced foragers, and of rare excellence as an edible species.

It grows in the characteristic rings on lawns and pastures that give it its common name, and its small size and uniform cream color make it relatively straightforward to identify. Yet Palmer reported: "I have never received them gathered by friends without detecting specimens of totally different species."

These were not strangers to mycology — they were friends who had been specifically warned by Palmer himself of the distinguishing marks of the esculent and the doubtful fungus. Despite his explicit instructions, they still gathered look-alikes alongside the intended species.

This example powerfully illustrates why general knowledge and even explicit warnings are insufficient. The similarities between [[classification-of-edible-and-poisonous-mushroom-families]] species growing in the same habitat can fool even those who have been taught what to look for. Only disciplined, species-specific recognition developed through extended study can provide reliable protection.

## Dr. F.S. Cordier: The French Authority

Rather than relying solely on his own testimony, Palmer quoted extensively from **Dr. F.S. Cordier**, a member of many French scientific societies, whose illustrated work published in Paris in 1876 was described as "by far the most complete work on the subject in the world."

Cordier"s analysis, illustrated by chromo-lithographs of the highest quality, systematically demolished every proposed universal test for distinguishing poisonous from [[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]]. His catalogue of false rules included the widespread belief that one should mistrust species exhibiting any of the following characteristics:

- Species growing in shady woods, dark and humid places, caverns, and subterranean vaults
- Fungi with flesh that is soft and watery rather than firm and substantial
- Those with acrid, astringent, acid, or saline taste
- Those with brilliant red, green, or blue coloration
- Those with no odor, or an odor different from the [[classification-of-cultivated-mushroom-species]]
- Those secreting a milky fluid when cut or broken
- Those that change color when broken or exposed to air
- Those that turn brown or black with age
- Those with viscous or slimy surfaces
- Those with flabby or fibrous flesh rather than firm and brittle
- Those not attacked by snails, insects, or worms

Conversely, the same authorities claimed safety for fungi exhibiting the opposite characteristics: those growing in open spots and meadows; those with pleasant mushroom-like odor; those with firm, brittle flesh; those with rosy or violet coloring; and those attacked by insects or sought by mammalian animals.

## Cordier's Systematic Refutation

Cordier methodically demolished every single characteristic named above, both the danger signs and the safety indicators. For each trait, he cited specific botanical species as counterexamples — approximately fifty varieties named individually, most of which were also found in New England, with hundreds more available as further examples.

His conclusion was categorical and unambiguous:

> "No one of these characteristics is exclusively the property either of esculent mushrooms or of poisonous mushrooms."

To accept such traits as adequate means of distinguishing edible from poisonous mushrooms would be, on the one hand, to reject perfectly safe esculent varieties as noxious species, and on the other hand, to mistake "species preeminently poisonous" for harmless food.

Cordier went further, declaring that assigning general practical traits to distinguish poisonous mushrooms in a way obvious to everybody appeared to him "impossible." The French mycologist"s authoritative voice lent tremendous weight to Palmer's own arguments, demonstrating that the problem was recognized by the foremost European experts as well as American students.

## The Silver Spoon Myth

Perhaps the most persistent and dangerous folk test is the **silver spoon method**. The belief held that if a silver spoon or gold ring turned black when used to stir cooking mushrooms, the fungi were poisonous; if the metal remained bright, they were safe to eat.

This test was widely recommended by physicians, cooks, and household manuals throughout the 19th century, and its persistence speaks to the deep human desire for a simple, mechanical solution to the complex problem of [[mushroom-identification]].

Cordier condemned this practice in the strongest possible terms: "This is an error against which one cannot protest too strongly." He reported personally testing the procedure with *[[agaricus-bulbosus]]* and many other poisonous mushrooms — they never blackened silver or gold.

Palmer later provided the scientific explanation for why the test sometimes appears to work and why it is nonetheless so dangerously misleading. Mushrooms decay very rapidly. In a short time, a fair, solid fungus becomes a mass of maggots that honeycomb its tissue.

On a warm day, these cells become charged with the vapor of decomposition. When such spoiled mushrooms are boiled, sulphuretted hydrogen and other noxious gases are liberated. These gases react chemically with the silver, causing discoloration.

The silver test therefore detects **spoilage and decomposition**, not intrinsic toxicity. A fresh, deadly Amanita — perfectly sound and free from decay — would leave the silver bright, giving a false assurance of safety.

While discarding spoiled mushrooms based on the silver test does no harm, the fatal error lies in the inverse conclusion: that all fungus which discolors silver is poisonous, and that which leaves it bright is edible. This reasoning is "in dangerous error."

## The Onion and Bulrush Test Myths

The naturalist **Necker** had advanced the idea that a white onion cooked with mushrooms would turn bluish or brown if the mushrooms were poisonous, while remaining white if harmless. Similarly, some authorities claimed that the pith of the bulrush would blacken under the same conditions with poisonous species.

Cordier dismissed both tests in a single devastating sentence: "Unfortunately these assertions are not confirmed by experience." Palmer"s own position was equally definitive — no kitchen test of any kind could substitute for species-specific knowledge.

## The Amanita Family: The Primary Killer

Palmer identified the **Amanita** family — particularly *Agaricus bulbosus* and its relatives — as responsible for all known fatal mushroom poisonings in his experience and in the reports he received from physicians across the northeastern United States.

He had personally found ten varieties of this mushroom in the vicinity of Boston alone, making them common and widely distributed in the American landscape. These fungi contained what Palmer described as a subtle alkaloid "not to be detected by smell or taste, yet surer than arsenic in its fatal consequences to human life."

The poison was entirely invisible to the senses — there was no warning bitterness, no unusual odor, no discoloration to alert the eater. By the time symptoms appeared — typically hours after the meal — the toxin had already done its damage.

The only potentially effective treatment Palmer mentioned was the **hypodermic injection of atropia** (atropine) or some other alkaloid from the nightshade family — a measure described as still in its infancy. Beyond that experimental treatment, physicians were "absolutely powerless to save life, however prompt and energetic the treatment."

## Species-Specific Knowledge: The Only Safe Approach

Cordier"s ultimate conclusion, which Palmer fully endorsed, was unequivocal and remains the foundation of mycological safety to this day:

> "The true, perhaps the sole means of avoiding mistakes, when we wish to use mushrooms as food, is to learn to know the characteristics which are peculiar to each species by itself, or, in other terms, to have in the memory or under the eye the exact description of the species and to attain this in all the ages and under all the forms that they can assume."

There is no shortcut. No universal test, no folk remedy, no simple rule of color, habitat, or odor can replace the disciplined study of individual species. Each mushroom must be known on its own terms — its cap shape and color, its gill structure, its stem characteristics, its spore print, its habitat and season, its associations with particular trees or soil types.

Palmer expressed frustration that despite such authoritative testimony from both sides of the Atlantic, the public would continue to ask how to "tell a mushroom from a toadstool," and that unqualified individuals would continue to volunteer answers that trained students declared impossible.

## Charles James Sprague"s Additional Caution

Palmer added the testimony of his colleague **[[charles-james-sprague]]**, a Boston mycologist of rare attainments, who never allowed any fungi to be eaten in his house except the common cultivated mushroom.

Sprague believed that intermediate forms existed among the Agarics — transitional species occupying the taxonomic ground between clearly edible and clearly poisonous types — which no expert could identify with certainty. This observation reinforced the case for extreme caution even among those with considerable mycological knowledge.

## Palmer's Own Balanced Position

Despite his strong warnings, Palmer was not an alarmist. He personally had tested perhaps a hundred varieties on his own stomach, including some long considered poisonous by other authorities, and had never been deceived. He believed the majority of toadstools were "just as safe esculents as the common mushroom."

His critique was directed at the mycological establishment itself: most mycologists of his era pursued what he called "the useless phantom of identifying microscopic fungi" rather than making practical knowledge of edible species available to the public. The science had become so absorbed in the technical that it had abandoned its potential to serve ordinary people.

Palmer positioned himself as a bridge between the laboratory and the kitchen — someone who could translate rigorous botanical knowledge into practical guidance for safe and enjoyable mushroom eating.
