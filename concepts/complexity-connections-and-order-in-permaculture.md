# Complexity, Connections, and Order in Permaculture

[[bill-mollison]] devotes significant attention to these themes because a designer who misunderstands complexity and order will inevitably create systems that require constant energy input and still underperform.

## Complexity in Ecological Systems

Complexity in nature is not random; it arises from the interaction of many elements across varied conditions.

- **Flat or invariant sites** (salt-pans, featureless plains) tend toward simplified ecosystems because fewer niches exist
- **Varied sites** — hills, valleys, ridgelines, coastlines — support greater species diversity because topography creates microclimates, varied soils, and multiple edge zones
- Marshes, swamps, tidal flats, and deserts are individually less diverse than hill-and-valley landscapes, yet each is rich in its own adapted species; their combined total is enormous
- **Simple plant associations** can support highly complex and productive animal communities — the mangrove system is a prime example, where a narrow range of tree species supports mobile species occupying every niche from underground burrows to the canopy, with activity schedules spanning low tide to high tide

### The Square of Elements

Waddington (1977) observed that complexity in a system rises roughly as the **square of the number of elements** present. This means adding even a few well-chosen species to a design can dramatically increase the web of possible connections and interactions.

## Connections: Designing for Essentials, Trusting the Details

Every element in a [[dodo]], Darwin's finches, and Galápagos tortoises all arose from this process
- Unique island species often perform **functions not easily found in continental ecologies** — a reminder that local adaptation generates irreplaceable complexity

## Quality of Complexity

Not all complexity is equal. Mollison draws a critical distinction:

- **Benign cooperative complexity** — elements working together for mutual benefit — is useful and productive
- **Competitive or inharmonious complexity** — elements in conflict, wasting energy on competition — is destructive
- The designer's task is to assemble systems where complexity trends toward cooperation, not competition

## Order Versus Disorder

Perhaps the most counterintuitive section in the Designer's Manual concerns what "order" actually means.

### True Order vs. Superficial Tidiness

- **True order** is defined functionally: things working beneficially together, with **yield as the acid test** — an ordered system produces a surplus of energy and resources
- Order arises from **ground conditions** — soil, water, climate, existing biology — not from abstract energy theory
- Entropy is a **result of the framework**, not of complexity itself; a well-structured complex system resists entropy

### The Disorder of Neatness

Mollison is blunt: in natural systems, **neatness, tidiness, uniformity, and straightness are energy-maintained disorder**. A clipped lawn, a bare-tilled field, a monoculture row — these require continuous energy input to maintain and produce nothing beyond their own existence.

> *"The seemingly-wild garden of a New Guinea villager is beautifully ordered; the clipped lawns of the pseudo-aristocrat are nature in wild disarray."*

### The Principle of Disorder

- **Order and harmony produce energy** for other uses — they are net-positive
- **Disorder consumes energy** to no useful end — it is net-negative
- A [[permaculture]] designer seeks to create systems where order is self-maintaining and generates surplus energy, rather than systems that demand energy inputs merely to sustain their structure

## Permitted Functions vs. Forced Functions

A key practical distinction in [[permaculture-design]] is between what an element does naturally (permitted functions) and what we compel it to do (forced functions).

### Permitted Functions

- When an element's **natural behaviors** happen to benefit other parts of the system, those are permitted functions
- Chickens scratching manure into the soil, ducks eating snails in a rice paddy, bees pollinating while collecting nectar — all permitted
- Permitted functions are **low-stress, self-maintaining, and resilient**

### Forced Functions

- When we **compel** an element to perform additional tasks beyond its natural range, we force functions onto it
- A cow providing milk and raising a calf is operating within permitted functions; asking that same cow to also plough, haul water, and tread a corn mill forces too many functions
- Forced functions create **stress, reduce yields, and risk system collapse** when the element fails

### The Human Analogy

Mollison extends this to people:

- **Too few functions** (a 300-egg chicken confined to a cage, a typist reduced to keying data) — stultifying and wasteful of human potential
- **Too many functions** (the overburdened cow, the sole farmer doing everything) — stressful and unsustainable
- Permaculture seeks the **middle path**: each element performs several functions, all within its natural capacity

## The Principle of Stress and Harmony

This principle synthesizes the above themes:

- **Stress** arises when elements are forced beyond their natural capacities — too many functions, too much competition, insufficient resources
- **Harmony** arises when elements function within their capacities in cooperative relationships — each supported by and supporting the others
- A harmonious system is productive, self-regulating, and generates surplus; a stressed system degrades, requires external inputs, and eventually collapses

## Key Takeaways for Designers

1. **Place elements where their natural functions serve the system** — don't try to wire them together artificially
2. **Design for essentials, trust the details** — components will make connections you didn't plan
3. **Aim for cooperative complexity** — more connections are good only if they are beneficial
4. **Judge order by yield, not appearance** — wild-looking systems may be deeply ordered
5. **Respect the limits of each element** — permitted functions are sustainable; forced functions invite collapse
6. **Minimize disorder** — every element of your design that requires energy input to maintain, without producing useful yield, is a liability
