# Yeast Biology

Yeasts are single-celled fungi that reproduce primarily by budding (or fission). Though they represent only a small fraction of fungal diversity, yeasts have an outsized impact on human civilization through fermentation, food production, biotechnology, and medicine.

## What Is Yeast

Yeast is not a taxonomic group but a growth form. Any fungus that exists predominantly as a single-celled organism (unicellular) and reproduces by budding or fission can be called a yeast. Multiple fungal lineages have independently evolved the yeast form — it is a case of convergent evolution, not monophyly.

### Key Features

- **Unicellular**: Individual cells are visible under a microscope (5-10 micrometers diameter)
- **Budding reproduction**: Daughter cell emerges as a small bud from the parent cell surface
- **Facultative anaerobes**: Most yeasts can ferment sugars in the absence of oxygen (fermentation) and respire in its presence
- **Rapid growth**: Doubling time of 90-120 minutes under ideal conditions
- **Robust**: Tolerate a wide range of pH (2.5-8.5), temperature (0-45°C), osmotic pressure, and ethanol concentration

## Saccharomyces cerevisiae: The Model Yeast

### Overview

S. cerevisiae (baker's yeast / brewer's yeast) is the most studied and most important yeast species:

- **Phylum**: [[ascomycota]]
- **Genome**: 12.1 Mb, ~6,275 genes on 16 chromosomes
- **First eukaryotic genome fully sequenced** (1996) — landmark achievement
- **Haploid or diploid**: Can exist in both states, unlike most fungi which are predominantly haploid
- **GRAS status**: Generally Recognized As Safe for food use

### Life Cycle

1. **Haploid vegetative growth**: Single set of chromosomes (n=16). Can be either mating type a or alpha
2. **Mating**: Cells of opposite mating type (a x alpha) fuse to form a diploid (2n)
3. **Diploid vegetative growth**: Diploid cells grow and divide by budding
4. **Sporulation**: Under nutrient starvation, diploids undergo meiosis to produce four haploid ascospores inside an ascus
5. **Germination**: Spores germinate into haploid cells, completing the cycle

### Budding Process

1. A small protuberance (bud) forms at a specific site on the mother cell surface
2. The site is determined by cortical markers (Bud1/Bud2 system) — yeast bud in specific patterns (axial or bipolar)
3. The nucleus migrates toward the bud, and mitosis occurs with one spindle pole entering the bud
4. Cytokinesis: a chitin ring (bud scar) forms at the base of the bud
5. The daughter cell separates, leaving a visible bud scar on the mother cell
6. Mother cells accumulate bud scars (visible with Calcofluor White stain) — each scar represents one division

## Fermentation Biology

### The Science of Fermentation

Yeast fermentation is the metabolic process by which yeast converts sugars into ethanol, CO2, and other byproducts:

- **Glycolysis**: Glucose is broken down to pyruvate, producing 2 ATP and 2 NADH
- **Fermentation**: Pyruvate is decarboxylated to acetaldehyde (releasing CO2), then reduced to ethanol (regenerating NAD+)
- **Products per molecule of glucose**: 2 ethanol + 2 CO2 + 2 ATP
- **Byproducts**: Glycerol, acetic acid, succinic acid, fusel alcohols (higher alcohols), esters, aldehydes — these contribute to flavor and aroma in fermented foods

### Crabtree Effect

S. cerevisiae exhibits the Crabtree effect — in the presence of excess glucose, it ferments even when oxygen is available (aerobic fermentation):

- Fermentation produces ATP faster than respiration (though less efficiently per glucose molecule)
- This "wasteful" metabolism is an advantage in competitive, sugar-rich environments (rotting fruit)
- Maximum ethanol tolerance: ~15-18% ABV (ethanol becomes toxic to the yeast above this)

### Pasteur Effect vs. Crabtree Effect

- **Pasteur effect** (most organisms): Respiration is preferred when oxygen is available; fermentation only under anaerobic conditions
- **Crabtree effect** (S. cerevisiae): Fermentation occurs even with oxygen present, given sufficient glucose concentration
- **Custers effect** (some yeasts): The opposite — ferment only under anaerobic conditions

## Beyond Saccharomyces: Yeast Diversity

### Major Yeast Genera

| Genus | Key Characteristics | Uses |
|-------|-------------------|------|
| Saccharomyces | Crabtree-positive, robust fermentation | Beer, wine, bread, bioethanol |
| Candida | Opportunistic pathogen, dimorphic | Medical significance, C. albicans infections |
| Pichia (Komagataella) | Methylotrophic — uses methanol | Recombinant protein production (Pichia pastoris) |
| Kluyveromyces | Ferments lactose | Dairy fermentation, kefir |
| Schizosaccharomyces | Fission yeast, divides by splitting | Research model (S. pombe), African millet beer |
| Brettanomyces (Dekkera) | Produces acetic acid, "funky" flavors | Belgian lambic beers, wine spoilage |
| Torulaspora | Clean fermentation, ester production | Wine, bread, bioethanol |
| Yarrowia | Oleaginous (lipid-accumulating) | Omega-3 production (Y. lipolytica) |
| Malassezia | Lipophilic, skin-associated | Component of human skin microbiome; dandruff |

### Wild Yeasts

Wild yeasts are found everywhere in nature — on fruit surfaces, in soil, on tree bark, in flower nectar, and in the air:

- **Fruit surfaces**: The natural habitat of S. cerevisiae. Oak trees and their associated fruits (figs, grapes, berries) are major reservoirs
- **Tree exudates**: Slime fluxes on trees harbor diverse yeast communities
- **Soil and leaf surfaces**: Debaryomyces, Cryptococcus, Rhodotorula
- **Flower nectar**: Metschnikowia reukaufii and other nectar specialists
- **Insect associations**: Many yeasts are transmitted by insects, especially wasps, bees, and Drosophila

### Spontaneous Fermentation

Traditional fermentation methods rely on wild yeasts rather than commercial cultures:

- **Lambic beer**: Fermented by wild yeasts and bacteria (Brettanomyces, Pediococcus) in the Senne Valley, Belgium. No added yeast — relies on airborne microflora
- **Natural wine**: Fermented by indigenous grape yeasts (Hanseniaspora, Candida, then Saccharomyces)
- **Sourdough bread**: Maintained by wild yeast (often Kazachstania exigua, Pichia kudriavzevii) and [[lactobacillus]] bacteria in a starter culture
- **Wild fermentation**: Sandor Katz's philosophy — "wild fermentation" as a broader cultural practice

## Industrial Uses of Yeast

### Food and Beverage

- **Bread**: S. cerevisiae produces CO2 for leavening. Baker's yeast strains selected for rapid CO2 production
- **Beer**: Ale yeasts (S. cerevisiae, top-fermenting) vs. lager yeasts (S. pastorianus, bottom-fermenting, a hybrid of S. cerevisiae and S. eubayanus)
- **Wine**: Multiple Saccharomyces strains available. Different strains produce different ester profiles, affecting flavor
- **Distilled spirits**: Yeast strains optimized for high ethanol tolerance and fast fermentation (rum, whiskey, vodka)
- **Cheese**: [[debaryomyces-hansenii]] contributes to rind development on many cheeses
- **Soy sauce and miso**: Zygosaccharomyces rouxii is the primary yeast in soy sauce fermentation

### Biotechnology

- **Recombinant protein production**: Pichia pastoris (Komagataella phaffii) produces human insulin, hepatitis B vaccine, and many other pharmaceuticals
- **Bioethanol**: S. cerevisiae engineered for higher ethanol tolerance and cellulose degradation for second-generation biofuels
- **Single-cell protein**: Yeast biomass as a protein source for animal feed and human nutrition
- **Bioremediation**: Some yeasts can accumulate heavy metals (Candida, Rhodotorula)

### Nutritional Yeast

- Inactive S. cerevisiae grown on molasses as a nutritional supplement
- **High in B vitamins**: Often fortified with B12 (not naturally present)
- **Complete protein**: Contains all essential amino acids
- **Cheesy, nutty flavor**: Popular as a cheese substitute in vegan cooking
- **Deactivated**: Grown specifically for food, not active fermentation
- **Glutamic acid**: Naturally contains MSG-like umami flavor
- **Not the same as brewer's yeast** (which has a bitter taste from hops residue)
