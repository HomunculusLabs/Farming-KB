# Acidifying Pollutants and Saprotrophic Fungal Communities

Saprotrophic fungi, which decompose dead organic matter and drive nutrient cycling in terrestrial ecosystems, are affected by acidifying pollutants through multiple mechanisms distinct from those affecting mycorrhizal fungi. The sulfur-containing pollutants that constitute acid rain produce toxic solubility products that directly inhibit fungal growth and decomposition, while nitrogen deposition indirectly alters competitive dynamics within fungal communities. Additionally, changes in saprotrophic fungal communities cascade through soil food webs, affecting faunal communities that depend on fungi as food resources.

## Sulfite Toxicity: Direct Effects on Fungal Growth

Sulfur-containing pollutants generate toxic solubility products that directly affect saprotrophic fungi. The chemistry of sulfur toxicity depends on soil pH:

- At low soil pH (2–7): bisulfite (HSO₃⁻) is the predominant toxic species
- At higher pH (7+): sulfite (SO₃²⁻) predominates (Dursun et al., 1996a)

Sulfur solubility products have been used in the brewing and food industries for sterilization, confirming their potent antifungal properties. Dursun et al. (1996a) and Boddy et al. (1996) demonstrated that sulfite at environmentally realistic concentrations of 12.5–100 μM had significant negative effects on:

- Mycelial growth and respiration of *Mycena galopus*, *Phoma exigua*, *Cladosporium cladosporioides*, and *Aureobasidium pullulans*
- Spore germination of these species (though spores were more resistant than mycelia)
- Decomposition of Sitka spruce leaf litter in pure culture

The differential sensitivity of mycelia versus spores suggests that established fungal populations may be more vulnerable to sulfite exposure than dispersal stages, potentially affecting the balance between colonization and persistence in polluted environments.

## Differential Species Responses to SO2

Dursun et al. (1996b) fumigated leaf litters with 40 nl l⁻¹ SO2 and found differential effects on respiration among fungal species and leaf litter substrates:

- **Mycena galopus**: SO2 reduced respiration on leaf litters, but the reduction was only statistically significant for Sitka spruce (*Picea sitchensis*) and, after 10 weeks, hazel (*Corylus avellana*). Little reduction occurred on Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*) and ash (*Fraxinus excelsior*).
- **Other species**: *Phoma exigua*, *Cladosporium cladosporioides*, and *Aureobasidium pullulans* showed no significant respiration reduction on any leaf litter species at this SO2 concentration.

The greatest effect was on Sitka spruce litter decomposition, where respiration of *Mycena galopus* was reduced by over half. This substrate-specific response — where the same fungus responds differently to SO2 depending on the leaf litter it is decomposing — highlights the complexity of predicting pollutant effects and suggests that litter quality mediates fungal sensitivity to atmospheric pollution.

## Effects on Leaf Litter Decomposition

The reduction in fungal respiration and growth in the presence of sulfur dioxide translates to reduced decomposition rates. Prescott and Parkinson (1985) and Wookey and Ineson (1991a,b) independently showed that sulfur dioxide fumigation reduced leaf litter decomposition. Newsham et al. (1992a,b) investigated SO2 fumigation at 10–30 nl l⁻¹ on saprotrophic fungal communities across multiple leaf litter species:

- **Species reduced by fumigation**: *Cladosporium* spp., *Epicoccum nigrum*, *Fusarium* spp., and *Phoma exigua*
- **Species increased by fumigation**: *Coniothyrium quercinum*, *Cylindrocarpon ortosporum*, and *Penicillium* spp.

Notably, Newsham et al. found no change in the leaf litter niches occupied by fungi despite community composition shifts, suggesting functional redundancy — the replacing species fill similar ecological roles. However, the documented reduction in overall decomposition rates despite functional redundancy suggests that species identity does matter for decomposition efficiency, even when niches remain occupied.

## Nitrogen Addition and Decomposition Stimulation

Paradoxically, acidifying pollutants containing nitrogen can stimulate fungal activity and decomposition under certain conditions. In a mesocosm experiment exposing plants and leaf litter to ozone and acid mist (2:1 mixture of H2SO4:HNO3), Shaw (1996) found that metabolically active (fluorescein diacetate-stained) fungal hyphae in leaf litter were significantly more abundant in acid-misted than control systems. This stimulation was attributed to the nitrogen component of the acid mist, which would help decompose high C:N litter resources (Garrett, 1963; Killham et al., 1983; Shaw and Johnston, 1993).

This dual effect — sulfur components inhibiting while nitrogen components stimulating fungal activity — complicates predictions about the net impact of mixed acidifying pollutants on decomposition processes. The balance between inhibition and stimulation likely depends on the chemical composition of the pollutant mixture, the C:N ratio of the litter being decomposed, and the composition of the fungal community.

## Liming as Remediation

Experimental field-scale liming has been investigated as a remediation strategy for acidified forest soils. Veerkamp et al. (1997) studied lignicolous (wood-inhabiting) fungal communities in a limed Scots pine forest on acidic soils and found that liming:

- Increased the total number of fungal species
- Shifted the community toward species characteristic of deciduous woodlands rather than coniferous woodlands
- Increased abundance of *Amphinema byssoides*, *Hyphodontia breviseta*, *Hypochnicium geogenium*, and *Sistotrema octosporum*
- Decreased abundance of *Trechispora farinacea*

The rationale for this community shift is that elevated soil pH increases nitrogen availability and stimulates decomposition of the high C:N ratio woody material. This makes coniferous wood — which is normally low resource quality for decomposers — more similar to the higher resource quality wood of angiosperms. The liming effect thus appears to be mediated primarily through changes in nutrient availability and resource quality rather than through direct pH effects on fungal physiology.

## Effects on Fungal-Faunal Interactions

Changes in fungal community structure and biomass caused by acidifying pollutants propagate through soil food webs to affect faunal communities. The evidence comes from multiple experimental approaches:

**Nematode trophic shifts**: Ruess et al. (1993) correlated changes in nematode trophic groups to changes in the bacteria:fungi ratio of forest soils subjected to experimental applications of sulfuric, nitric, and oxalic acids. Using the maturity index (Bongers, 1990), they showed a shift from bacterial-feeding to fungal-feeding nematodes with acid addition, indicating that bacteria are more sensitive to soil pH changes and associated metal toxicity than fungi.

**Three-year acid precipitation experiment**: Ruess et al. (1996) applied equimolar sulfuric acid and ammonium nitrate to a Sitka spruce plantation in Scotland. Soil acidity increased from pH 5.0 to 4.0 in the upper 2 cm, inducing:

- Significant increase in fungal biomass (measured by soil ergosterol content)
- Increased ectomycorrhizal colonization of roots
- Elevated nematode populations
- Reduction in omnivorous and predatory nematodes (*Filenchus* spp. and *Aporcelaimellus obtusicaudatus*)
- Increase in fungal-feeding nematodes (particularly *Aphelenchoides* spp.)

**Two-year pine forest study**: Esher et al. (1992) found no effects of acid precipitation (pH 4.3 and 3.6) on saprotrophic microbial communities (using dilution plate counts), nematode numbers, or arbuscular mycorrhizal spore counts in southern US pine forests. However, they documented significant reductions in fine root length, dry mass, and ectomycorrhizal short roots of both loblolly pine (*Pinus taeda*) and longleaf pine (*Pinus palustris*). Numbers of oribatid, prostigmatid, and astigmatid mites increased with increasing soil acidity, possibly reflecting changes in fungal food resource availability.

The consistent finding across these studies is that acidification shifts soil food web structure, generally favoring fungal-based energy channels over bacterial-based channels, and altering the balance between trophic groups within fungal-feeding faunal communities.

## Phylloplane Fungal Communities

The phylloplane — the surface of living leaves — presents a large surface area for fungal colonization and atmospheric pollutant interception. Phylloplane fungi derive nutrition from wax cuticles, leaf exudates, and nutrients arriving via wet or dry deposition. Magan et al. (1995) investigated SO2 and O3 effects on phylloplane fungal communities of Scots pine, Sitka spruce, and Norway spruce over three years:

- SO2 markedly reduced total phylloplane fungal population on Sitka spruce needles
- O3 caused an increase in fungi on Scots pine (recorded as increased fluorescein diacetate-stained hyphae)
- Community composition changes differed among tree species, pollutants, and detection methods (serial dilution vs. direct plating)

Specific community shifts included increased *Sclerophoma pythiophila* on Sitka spruce under SO2, and increased *Epicoccum nigrum*, *Cladosporium* spp., *Aureobasidium pullulans*, and *Rhizosphaera kalkhoffii* under O3, with responses varying by tree species.

In cereal crops, Magan and McLeod (1991) demonstrated significant reduction in pink and white yeasts on barley flag leaves under elevated SO2, while *Cladosporium* spp. — which are weakly parasitic — increased. This suggests that on leaves with less surface wax protection, SO2 may increase susceptibility to pathogenic fungal invasion by reducing the protective saprotrophic yeast community.

## Aquatic Fungal Communities

In aquatic ecosystems, the impact of acidifying pollutants appears less severe, probably due to the large dilution factor in water bodies. Bärlocher (1992a,b) reported that aquatic fungal communities show relatively little response to acidification, though a reduction in water pH from 6 to 4 did decrease fungal growth on alder leaves. The buffering capacity of aquatic systems and the dilution of atmospheric pollutants in water appear to provide some protection for aquatic fungi.

## Conclusions

Acidifying pollutants affect saprotrophic fungal communities through direct toxicity (sulfite inhibition of growth and respiration), indirect nutrient-mediated effects (nitrogen stimulation of decomposition), and community-level shifts in competitive dynamics. The responses are highly species-specific, substrate-dependent, and vary with the chemical composition of the pollutant mixture. These fungal community changes propagate through soil food webs, altering faunal community structure and trophic interactions. The net effect on ecosystem-level processes such as decomposition and nutrient cycling is difficult to predict due to these complex, often opposing mechanisms, but the evidence clearly demonstrates that acidifying pollutants fundamentally alter the structure and function of saprotrophic fungal communities.
