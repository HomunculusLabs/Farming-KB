## Identification Warnings

The variety commonly found in western Washington has a movable
membranous annulus that can degrade into an annular zone, bringing
its overall appearance very close to that of other Conocybes.
Since there is potential for misidentification, collectors could

dung can all help distinguish P. filaris from active species.
Collectors should always make spore prints and verify spore color
before consuming any small, conic-capped mushroom.

## Prevention of Poisoning

The most effective strategy for avoiding amatoxin poisoning from
P. filaris is strict adherence to identification protocols. Every
mushroom intended for consumption should be positively identified
to species level, not just to genus. Key steps include making a
spore print (P. filaris produces rusty brown, not purplish brown
spores), checking for a bluing reaction (absent in P. filaris),
and noting the substrate (wood chips versus grass or dung for
many active species). When in doubt, the mushroom should not be
consumed. The consequences of misidentification with P. filaris
are severe and potentially irreversible without prompt medical
treatment.

## Comparison with Conocybe Species

The taxonomic transfer from Conocybe to Pholiotina reflects
evolving understanding of Bolbitiaceae systematics. Many field
guides and older references still list this species as Conocybe
filaris. The morphological similarity to other Conocybe species
is high, and the membranous annulus is the primary feature that
sets P. filaris apart from many annulate Conocybes. Collectors
familiar with Conocybe species should be aware that P. filaris
represents one of the most dangerous members of this group and
warrants particular attention during any identification process.
