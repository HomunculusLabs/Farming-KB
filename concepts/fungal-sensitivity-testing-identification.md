# Fungal Sensitivity Testing and Identification Fungal sensitivity testing evaluates the growth response of fungal isolates to antifungal compounds, environmental parameters, or selective agents. In taxonomic and ecological mycology, sensitivity profiles serve as supplementary characters for distinguishing morphologically similar species and assessing functional diversity within fungal communities. ## Principles Sensitivity testing in mycology operates on the same principles as antibiotic susceptibility testing in bacteriology. A standardized inoculum of the test fungus is exposed to a gradient of the test compound on solid or liquid media, and growth is measured relative to an untreated control. Results are typically expressed as: - **Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)**: The lowest concentration that completely prevents visible growth - **EC50**: The concentration that reduces growth by 50% relative to the control - **Growth rate ratios**: Linear extension or biomass accumulation expressed as a proportion of the control In contrast to clinical mycology (where sensitivity testing guides antifungal therapy), ecological and taxonomic sensitivity testing uses antifungal compounds as tools to probe fungal physiology, characterize species boundaries, and understand community dynamics. ## Selective Agents in Taxonomic Mycology Several antifungal compounds are used routinely in fungal identification and isolation: ### Benomyl (Methyl 1-(butylcarbamoyl)-2-benzimidazolecarbamate) Benomyl sensitivity is one of the most widely used taxonomic characters in fungal systematics. It distinguishes between major groups within the Zygomycota and among mitosporic fungi: - **Sensitive**: Most Ascomycota and Basidiomycota are inhibited by low concentrations (0.5-2.0 µg/mL) - **Resistant**: Many Zygomycota (Mucorales) and some Oomycota grow readily at concentrations that completely inhibit other groups - **Intermediate responses**: Can indicate hybrid or intermediate taxa Benomyl has been particularly valuable in distinguishing [[fungal-mycoparasitism-and-biocontrol-interactions]], sensitivity testing evaluates the ability of potential biocontrol agents to inhibit target pathogens. Trichoderma species, for example, are screened for their sensitivity to host metabolites and vice versa. The sensitivity profile of a biocontrol candidate influences its compatibility with other management practices and its efficacy against specific pathogens.

### Environmental Stress Profiling
Sensitivity to temperature extremes, pH, water activity, and osmotic potential characterizes the ecological amplitude of fungal species. This is particularly relevant for [[fungal-biodiversity-in-extreme-environments]], where tolerance thresholds define niche boundaries.

## Methods

### Agar Dilution Method
1. Prepare a series of [[query-how-to-make-agar-plates-for-mushroom-cultivation]] containing graded concentrations of the test compound
2. Spot-inoculate each plate with a standardized inoculum (typically a 5mm agar plug from the colony margin or a spore suspension of defined concentration)
3. Incubate at appropriate temperature
4. Measure colony diameter at regular intervals
5. Calculate growth rate (mm/day) or percentage inhibition relative to control

### Disc Diffusion Method
1. Prepare lawn plates by spreading a spore suspension evenly across the agar surface
2. Place paper discs impregnated with the test compound on the surface
3. Measure zones of inhibition after incubation
4. This method is less precise than agar dilution but faster and suitable for screening

### Microtiter Broth Method
1. Prepare two-fold serial dilutions of the test compound in liquid medium in 96-well microtiter plates
2. Inoculate each well with a standardized spore suspension
3. Measure optical density (turbidity) or [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] (resazurin reduction) after incubation
4. This method allows high-throughput screening and precise MIC determination
