# Mycorrhizal Dispersal and Soil Food Web Research

The dispersal of mycorrhizal fungal propagules and their integration into soil food webs represents a critical but often underappreciated component of ecosystem dynamics. Research in this area spans mycorrhizal spore ecology, the role of fauna as dispersal vectors, grazing effects on mycorrhizal function, and the broader implications for plant community structure and ecosystem productivity.

## Vertebrate Dispersal of Mycorrhizal Spores

Small mammals play an essential role in dispersing mycorrhizal fungal spores, particularly those of hypogeous (truffle-like) fungi that rely entirely on animal vectors. Trappe and Maser (1976) demonstrated that spores of the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus *Glomus macrocarpus* and the hypogeous ectomycorrhizal fungus *Hymenogaster* could survive passage through the guts of small mammals including the Oregon vole (*Microtus oregoni*) and the chickaree (*Tamiasciurus douglasi*), and subsequently form functional mycorrhizal associations.

Kotter and Farentinos (1984) showed that a variety of ectomycorrhizal fungal spores survived passage through the gut of the tassel-eared squirrel (*Sciurus aberti*) and could develop mycorrhizal associations with ponderosa pine. Cazares and Trappe (1994) extended this work by demonstrating that mycophagy of both hypogeous and epigeous [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] results in the deposition of viable spores in animal feces. Mangan and Adler (2002) documented seasonal dispersal of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] by spiny rats in a neotropical forest, revealing temporal patterns in dispersal efficacy.

## Ant-Mycorrhizal Interactions

Ants serve as both dispersal agents and ecosystem engineers that influence mycorrhizal fungal communities. Friese and Allen (1993) studied the interaction of harvester ants with vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in a patchy semi-arid environment, showing that mound structure affected fungal dispersion and establishment. Snyder and Friese (2001) conducted a broader survey of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal root inoculum associated with harvester ant nests across the western United States, finding that ant nests serve as significant reservoirs of mycorrhizal inoculum. MacMahon et al. (2000) reviewed the community influences of harvester ants (*Pogonomyrmex* spp.), highlighting their role as ecosystem engineers that modify soil conditions favorable for mycorrhizal establishment.

## Collembolan Grazing on Mycorrhizal Fungi

Collembola (springtails) are among the most studied mycorrhizal grazers. Their feeding on extraradical mycorrhizal hyphae can significantly impact mycorrhizal function and plant nutrition. Warnock et al. (1982) demonstrated that the springtail *[[folsomia-candida]]* influenced the mycorrhizal association of leek (*Allium porrum*) with the vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal endophyte *Glomus fasciculatus*.

Klironomos and Ursic (1998) showed density-dependent grazing on the extraradical hyphal network of the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus *Glomus intraradices* by *F. candida*, finding that grazing intensity directly affected hyphal network integrity. Klironomos et al. (1999) further demonstrated the reproductive significance of feeding on both saprobic and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi by *F. candida*, revealing that collembolan fitness is linked to their fungal food sources.

Schultz (1991) characterized the grazing preferences of two collembolan species, *Folsomia candida* and *Proisotoma minuta*, for ectomycorrhizal fungi. Thimm and Larink (1995) similarly examined grazing preferences of collembola for endomycorrhizal fungi. Harris and Boerner (1990) showed effects of belowground grazing by collembola on growth, mycorrhizal infection, and phosphorus uptake of *Geranium robertianum*.

Lussenhop (1992) reviewed mechanisms of microarthropod-microbial interactions in soil, and Lussenhop (1996) demonstrated that collembola serve as mediators of microbial symbiont effects upon soybean. Bakonyi et al. (2002) documented density-dependent regulation of arbuscular mycorrhiza by collembola, while Klironomos and Moutoglis (1999) showed that collembola influenced the colonization of nonmycorrhizal plants by mycorrhizal neighbors.

## Mycorrhizal Function Under Grazing Pressure

The question of how important faunal grazing is to mycorrhizal function in natural ecosystems remains debated. Many studies demonstrating detrimental effects of grazing on mycorrhizae have been conducted under laboratory or greenhouse conditions at unrealistically high animal densities. Setälä (2000) investigated reciprocal interactions between Scots pine and [[soil-food-web-structure]] in the presence and absence of ectomycorrhiza, providing a more realistic assessment of these interactions under field conditions.

Finlay (1985) studied interactions between soil microarthropods and endomycorrhizal associations of higher plants, concluding that while grazing can damage hyphal networks, moderate grazing may stimulate compensatory growth. Fitter (1985) examined the functioning of vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizas under field conditions, finding that field-level effects of grazing are often less dramatic than laboratory results suggest.

## Aphid-Fungal Interactions in Canopies

Aboveground fungal-faunal interactions also affect ecosystem nutrient dynamics. Stadler and Müller (1996) demonstrated that aphid honeydew affected the phyllosphere microflora of *Picea abies*. Stadler et al. (1998) linked aphid ecology with nutrient fluxes in coniferous forests, while Stadler and Michalzik (1999) showed the impact of spruce aphids on nutrient flows in the canopy of Norway spruce. These aboveground interactions create feedback loops connecting faunal activity, fungal growth, and nutrient cycling.

## Soil Food Web Theory

The broader theoretical framework for understanding fungal-faunal interactions in soil has been developed by several researchers. Moore and de Ruiter (2000) examined invertebrates in detrital food webs along gradients of productivity. Wardle et al. (1998) reviewed trophic relationships in the soil microfood-web and attempted to predict responses to changing global environments. Wall and Moore (1999) explored interactions underground, emphasizing soil biodiversity, mutualism, and ecosystem processes. Anderson and Ineson (1984) examined interactions between microorganisms and soil invertebrates in nutrient flux pathways of forest ecosystems.

## Implications for Plant Community Structure

The dispersal and grazing of mycorrhizal fungi by fauna has direct implications for plant community composition. If mycorrhizal inoculum is patchily distributed — as it typically is due to the defecation patterns of small mammals — the availability of mycorrhizal partners will vary across microhabitats. This creates heterogeneous conditions where mycorrhizal-dependent plant species may thrive in some patches while non-mycorrhizal species dominate others. The selective grazing of collembola on particular mycorrhizal fungal species further modifies this landscape, potentially favoring certain plant-mycorrhizal combinations over others.

## Knowledge Gaps and Future Directions

Key unresolved questions include: the relative importance of faunal grazing on mycorrhizal function under realistic field densities; the degree to which selective grazing alters plant community composition in natural ecosystems; and how climate change may modify these interactions by altering faunal activity patterns, fungal growth rates, and the phenology of both partners.
