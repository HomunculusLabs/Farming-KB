# Farm Strategies for Yield Increase

[[water-management]], soil development, and integration of multiple production systems.

## Water Storage (12-20% of Landscape)

Dedicating 12-20% of the landscape to water storage produces multiple interacting benefits:

1. **Direct product increase** — water is more productive per unit area than land; fish are more efficient at food conversion than cattle, producing animal protein
2. **Indirect land product increase** through irrigation and enhanced nutrient quality from fish manure applied to remaining land
3. **Interaction effects** — ducks on water increase yields in and around ponds through pest and weed control plus manuring
4. **Microclimatic buffering** — water bodies moderate temperature extremes for surrounding areas

## Land Forming

Reshaping land contours creates fundamental improvements to production capacity:

1. **Product increase** through even irrigation — eliminating both dry areas and waterlogging
2. **Land stability** through reduction of soil loss from water runoff and salting
3. **Energy savings** as gravity flow replaces pumped water (site-dependent)
4. **Water recycling** becomes feasible with controlled contour and drainage

## Soil Development

Investment in soil pays compound returns across all other strategies:

1. **Product increase** due to deeper root penetration in developed soils
2. **Water infiltration** achieving zero runoff through improved absorption capacity
3. **Microclimate buffering** within the soil profile itself
4. **Nutrient supply** from increased biological activity and organic matter

## Windbreaks and Shelter Systems (20-30% of Landscape)

Allocating 20-30% of landscape to windbreaks and shelterbelts produces effects far exceeding their footprint:

1. **Shelter effects** increasing plant yields, animal protein production, and microclimate buffering both above and below ground
2. **Increased carrying capacity** through shrub and tree fodder systems
3. **Nutrient savings** recycled via legumes and deep-rooted trees accessing subsoil minerals
4. **Intrinsic forest products** including nectar for honey, seeds, and firewood from fallen timber
5. **Pest predator habitat** providing insect and bird refugia that reduce crop damage
6. **Wildlife corridors** connecting habitat patches across the landscape

## Farm Forestry (Not Industrial Forestry)

Integrated farm forestry differs fundamentally from industrial monoculture forestry:

1. **Precipitation increase** due to night condensation and enhanced water penetration
2. **Product superiority** of perennials over annuals in bulk, energy savings, and length of productive yield period
3. **Rainfall increase** due to trees planted cross-wind, intercepting moisture
4. **Reduced stockfeed costs** through selected self-forage browse providing drought-proof feed and some medicinal qualities
5. **Reduced infrastructure costs** through on-farm durable timber for fence posts and construction
6. **Reduced animal losses** due to shelter from shivering, sweating, and exposure
7. **Increased crop production** in sheltered areas downwind of tree belts
8. **Increased carcass weight** as cattle graze all day on shaded pasture instead of sheltering from sun
9. **Reduced evaporation** from ponds due to wind reduction over water surfaces

## Market and Process Strategies

Yield increases must be matched with intelligent marketing and processing:

1. **Specialty crop selection** for premium markets (e.g., fresh herbs near restaurant concentrations)
2. **Direct marketing** through self-pick, mail order, direct dispatch, and roadside sales
3. **Value-added processing** converting raw products to higher-order products (e.g., seed to oil, crude eucalyptus oil to fractions)
4. **On-farm fuel processing** saving purchase costs plus generating saleable surplus

## Social and Financial Strategies

Cooperative approaches multiply the effectiveness of individual farm strategies:

1. **Farm-link strategy** — urban groups contracting to buy specific produce, providing market stability
2. **Education income** from field days and educational courses
3. **Agri-tourism** through guest houses, holiday farms, and urban visitor rental
4. **Direct investment** by city people in particular farms
5. **Local financial institutions** — credit unions and banks that recycle money locally
6. **Equipment pooling** with neighbors; coordinated sowing and reaping schedules (capital saved up to 90%)
7. **Labor exchange** with neighboring farms
8. **Produce and marketing cooperatives** for collective bargaining power

## No-Tillage Benefits

Low or no-tillage farming provides multiple simultaneous advantages:

- **Energy savings** from reduced tillage operations
- **Soil conservation** preserving structure and organic matter
- **[[norm-sims]] (Weekly Times, 5 Jan 1983) demonstrated the compound effect of multiple strategies applied together:

- **Land-forming**: "We expect to double production over the next few years, using half the irrigation water" — a four-fold benefit. "Salinity is reduced." In severe drought: "Pasture production has never looked better and water is available." Irrigation time reduced from six days to two.
- **Grazing rotation**: 26 paddocks grazed in a 21-day rotation, averaging 1.6 hectares each with central trough water-points for cattle.

His specific strategies included laser levelling of fields for even irrigation, water reticulation, water storage and recycling, grazing rotation of 21 days, central access road, concentrated ration crops, and reduced pasture area for optimal watering regime.

The primary increases in crop yield come not from varietal selections — a fiction promoted by agricultural companies, seed patent holders, and extension officers — but from attention to site design and development, followed by wise enterprise selection to suit the modified site, concurrently with marketing and processing strategy. These permanent or durable strategies are often not in the commercial interest to encourage, as the continuous benefit accrues to the farmer alone while reducing the role of middlemen and traders.

Benefits, like wastes, must be returned or recycled to keep any system functioning. Accumulations of unused benefits are predictive of collapse at production level, and then throughout all tiers of the system.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

## Research Directions
Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.
Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.
