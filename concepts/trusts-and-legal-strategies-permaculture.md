# Trusts and Legal Strategies for Permaculture

Trusts in the public interest are the legal basis on which churches, universities, schools, research establishments, hospitals, public services, aid programmes, and charities rest. In [[permaculture]], trusts provide the legal and financial framework for community land ownership, [[ethical-investment-and-earth-banking]], and sustainable development. Understanding trust structures is essential for anyone seeking to create lasting, community-owned permaculture enterprises.

## The Role of Trusts in Society

Few people realize how many and how varied are the trusts that serve them. About 18 to 20% of businesses may be non-profit trusts owned or operated by the charitable trusts that benefit from them. It is quite possible — even sensible — to completely replace the bureaucracy of public services with a series of locally administered trusts.

Holland (in particular) largely supplants expensive paid public services (burdened with heavy salary and capital costs, and liable to inaction, self-interest, and executive inefficiency) with publicly formed trusts called "stichtings." In the case of any small country, such trusts can run all public operations, and the "government" becomes simply a way of conveying tax capital back to the regions via local trusts. However, trusts can also self-fund via non-profit businesses to become foundations, fully equipped with their own income sources.

## How Trusts Work

Trusts are formed to conduct businesses and trade, giving away their profits annually to named beneficiaries. If the beneficiaries are individuals, such gifts are taxed as private income. If the beneficiaries are charitable trusts or churches, the gift is not only not taxable but can be tax deductible to any giver. Trading, or "unit discretionary," trusts are also known as non-profit corporations (not to be confused with for-profit organizations).

Many large companies set up and fund non-profit organizations or charitable trusts as a means to reduce taxable income, carry out educational services, or obtain public goodwill. Some businesses tithe to worthy trusts they believe in — a tithe is usually a tenth of income, but in practice ranges from 5 to 15%.

Trusts are usually formed, operated, and staffed by people (often initially volunteers) motivated to perform one or other public duty, or who seek to assist a defined or special group in need. Such trusts often have names including the words: church, foundation, institute, communion, school, congregation, charity, bureau, trust, or even company.

## Legal Structure of a Trust

Legally, a trust body consists of a **trustee** and a **trust deed**, registered with the public company registrar. There are many good reasons to make the trustee a private company:

- Directors of such companies need to be few in number (3 or 4 are enough)
- Directors can appoint others if one dies or resigns
- Directors can be anonymous
- A company does not die, unlike its directors
- A small group of trustees can act quickly and decisively without reference to a cumbersome and often uninvolved "board of directors"

It is wise to restrict directorships of a trustee company to those who are very active in trust affairs, and preferably live close to each other in one region.

## Setting Up a Trust

Should any person wish to set up a trust, the very first thing to do is to closely define:

1. The purposes of the trust
2. The group to whom it will apply (e.g., "all the citizens of Australia" or "those suffering from spina bifida")
3. Instruct a lawyer to draw up the trust deed and register this and the trustee company

It is usually possible to buy a copy of the trust deeds of other ethical organizations and use these as a model for a local trust, reducing legal costs. Some law societies service ethical trusts at no charge for their time. A careful search for the right lawyer is essential — corporate lawyers are often knowledgeable about trusts. Many lawyers give bad advice to groups, setting up litigious or cumbersome systems, giving endless trouble and necessitating agreement among many people.

## The Charitable Trust / Trading Trust Pair

It is very wise for any charitable trust to establish a non-profit trading (business) trust to help finance its activities. This creates a two-trust system:

**Trust A (Charitable Trust):** Holds all real property which is not at risk — land, buildings, copyrights, leases, and important equipment that is clear of debt. This trust takes no risks and does not trade. It only accepts volunteer or contract labour, and can pay employees. It is involved in areas of public good.

**Trust B (Non-Profit Trading Trust):** Accepts volunteer or contract labour, pays employees, involves itself in areas of normal business risk, and can rent or lease real property from Trust A. Profits go to beneficiaries — wages and costs can be paid to staff or volunteers. The main beneficiary is Trust A.

Any other charity or trust can be funded by this arrangement. The trading trust can refund costs to volunteers, pay wages, and gift profits to the charity or to any other charity.

## Trust Deeds and Duration

The trust deeds state not only the purposes of the trusts but also the "will" of the trust. This usually leaves its assets to an allied trust if this trust completes operations, closes down, or fails from lack of interest or funds. The trust deed gives an estimate of the duration of the trust. If intended to be "forever," the legal statement is likely to be on the lines of "until 21 years after the death of the last descendant of Ming emperors" or some such legally indefinable period.

## Associations

Any trust can have (unregistered, no cost) an association of volunteers, aides, or clients who can publish a newsletter and generally assist the trust in its affairs. This provides a low-barrier entry point for community involvement.

## Distributed Trusts vs. Hierarchical Systems

Trusts are durable, efficient, easy to administer, and of great public service. As so few dedicated people can operate a trust effectively, it is far better to set up many local trusts than to risk the power-centered inefficiencies of a monstrous hierarchical system, such as some religious sects and foundations have become. These are essentially fossilized and no longer of relevance to ordinary people.

Every dissenter or group of dissenters should therefore set up trusts to promulgate their own views, or form an independent trust in a cooperative network of like trusts. France is estimated to have 100,000 public interest groups, each with its own areas of interest and subscribers, with about 10,000 forming up annually — while others fade out as their work becomes redundant or completed.

## Other Legal Structures

Other simple legal structures necessary to complete the picture — companies, cooperatives, credit unions, public investment trusts, and so on — are all well-outlined in company law, have excellent support services, and are routine arrangements. A good accountant to lay out the bookkeeping and give advice is necessary, as is an efficient office manager to communicate with the trust's target population.

## The Permaculture Trust Network

Many small independent but cooperative Permaculture Institutes and allied groups exist which have associated non-profit trusts operating businesses to fund them. In this way, many trusts are independent of gifts or grants and become self-reliant for funds. This model of self-funding through ethical enterprise is central to the permaculture approach to [[informal-and-formal-community-economics]] and legal organization.
