# Order Cantharellales

## Overview

Cantharellales is an order of basidiomycete fungi within the class Agaricomycetes that includes some of the most highly prized edible mushrooms (chanterelles) as well as ecologically critical mycorrhizal partners and plant pathogens. The order is morphologically diverse, encompassing fungi with forked ridges, true gills, teeth, and smooth hymenophores, as well as microscopic "resupinate" crusts that form essential mycorrhizal relationships with orchids and forest trees. Molecular phylogenetics has united these disparate forms into a well-supported monophyletic order.

## Taxonomic History

The order Cantharellales was historically a small group centered on the genus Cantharellus (chanterelles). Early classification placed chanterelles near the gilled mushrooms (Agaricales) due to their above-ground, mushroom-like fruiting bodies. The application of molecular systematics, particularly the multi-gene analyses of Hibbett et al. (2007) and the comprehensive phylogeny of Buyck et al. (2014) and Binder et al. (2013), dramatically expanded the order to include genera previously scattered across multiple orders.

Key molecular findings included:
- The genus *Craterellus* (trumpet chanterelles) is nested within Cantharellales
- The tooth fungus genus *Hydnum* (hedgehog mushrooms) belongs in Cantharellales
- The important orchid mycorrhizal genus *Tulasnella* is a member
- The plant pathogenic genus *Ceratobasidium* (cause of rice sheath blight and other crop diseases) is included
- The economically damaging *Thanatephorus cucumeris* (Rhizoctonia solani teleomorph) is a cantharelloid fungus

## Morphological Features

### Hymenophore Diversity
Cantharellales exhibit remarkable hymenophore variation:

**Blanched ridges (cantharelloid)**: *Cantharellus* species have shallow, blunt, forking ridges on the hymenophore that resemble blunt gills but are structurally distinct. These ridges are typically decurrent (running down the stipe), cross-veined, and blunt-edged — never sharp or blade-like. This morphology is intermediate between gills and pores and is the hallmark of the order's type genus.

**True gills (lamellate)**: Some Cantharellales have well-developed lamellae, including *Arrhenia*, *Lichenomphalia*, and certain *Craterellus* species. These gills are often thick, waxy, and widely spaced compared to those of Agaricales.

**Teeth (odontoid)**: *Hydnum* species produce a tooth-like hymenophore with spines or teeth hanging vertically from the underside of the pileus. *Hydnum repandum* (sweet tooth, hedgehog mushroom) is a prized edible with pale teeth. This morphology converges with similar forms in Hericiales and some Polyporales.

**Smooth to wrinkled**: Resupinate (crust-forming) genera like *Tulasnella*, *Ceratobasidium*, and *Thanatephorus* have smooth, tuberculate, or merulioid hymenophores. These are often visible only as thin crusts on substrate surfaces or are entirely microscopic.

**Tubular (cyphelloid)**: Some genera produce cup-shaped or tubular fruiting bodies, particularly in the *Craterellus* alliance.

### Macroscopic Characters of Cantharellus
True chanterelles (*Cantharellus* sensu stricto) share these features:
- **Pileus**: convex to funnel-shaped, often with an irregular, wavy margin
- **Color**: typically yellow to orange (egg yolk to deep apricot), some species white
- **Hymenophore**: blunt, forking ridges that are decurrent and cross-veined
- **Flesh**: firm, pale yellow to white, with a fruity, apricot-like odor
- **Stipe**: solid, tapering downward, often paler than the pileus
- **Spore print**: white to pale yellow
- **Ecology**: obligately ectomycorrhizal with forest trees

### Spore Characteristics
Cantharellales produce basidiospores that are typically:
- Smooth or ornamented (echinulate in some *Tulasnella*)
- Non-amyloid or weakly amyloid
- Elongated to ellipsoidal, sometimes cylindrical
- Hyaline (transparent)
- Variable in size across genera

## Major Families and Genera

### Cantharellaceae
Cantharellaceae contains the classic chanterelles:

**Cantharellus**: The type genus, with approximately 90 described species worldwide. The genus has been substantially revised by molecular work, with many new species described from Africa, Asia, and the Americas. Major groups include:
- *C. cibarius* group: golden chanterelle complex, widespread in temperate Northern Hemisphere forests, associated with oaks, beeches, spruces, and other trees
- *C. lateritius* group: smooth chanterelle, widespread in eastern North America and parts of Asia
- *C. minor* and allies: small tropical chanterelles from Africa and Asia
- *C. floridulus*: East Asian species
- *C. subalbidus*: white chanterelle from the Pacific Northwest of North America

**Craterellus**: Trumpet chanterelles and black trumpets:
- *C. tubaeformis* (yellowfoot): funnel-shaped, yellow-brown, autumn fruiting in conifer forests. Very common in northern Europe and North America. Prized edible with excellent flavor.
- *C. cornucopioides* (black trumpet, horn of plenty): dark gray to black, trumpet-shaped, with a smoky, rich flavor. Considered by many to equal or surpass golden chanterelles in culinary quality.
- *C. cinereus* (ashen chanterelle): gray to brown, related to black trumpet

### Hydnaceae
Hydnaceae includes the tooth fungi of the genus *Hydnum*:
- *H. repandum* (hedgehog mushroom, sweet tooth): pale buff to orange, with soft, conical teeth. Excellent edible found in association with various trees in temperate forests worldwide.
- *H. umbilicatum*: similar to H. repandum but with a depressed center (umbilicate pileus)
- *H. rufescens*: reddish hedgehog mushroom, often considered a variety or subspecies of H. repandum
- *H. albidum*: white hedgehog mushroom, less common

The genus *Sistotrema* is also placed in Hydnaceae. Sistotrema species are mostly microscopic resupinate crusts that are important ectomycorrhizal partners of forest trees, particularly in the Ericaceae (heath family).

### Tulasnellaceae
Tulasnellaceae is one of the most ecologically important families in the order, despite its members being largely microscopic:

**Tulasnella**: These fungi form the essential mycorrhizal partner for the majority of orchid species worldwide. Orchid seeds are microscopic and contain virtually no energy reserves; they require infection by an appropriate Tulasnella species (or sometimes other fungi) to germinate and develop into a protocorm. This obligate mycorrhizal relationship has driven orchid evolution, ecology, and conservation challenges. *Tulasnella* species are cosmopolitan and highly host-specific, with different orchid species requiring different Tulasnella strains.

### Ceratobasidiaceae
Ceratobasidiaceae contains important plant pathogens and mycorrhizal fungi:

**Ceratobasidium**: Causes rice sheath blight, damping-off diseases, and root rot in multiple crops.

**Thanatephorus**: *T. cucumeris* is the teleomorph of *Rhizoctonia solani*, one of the most destructive plant pathogens worldwide. Rhizoctonia affects hundreds of plant species across all major crop families, with economic impacts estimated in billions of dollars annually.

Some Ceratobasidiaceae species also form mycorrhizal associations with orchids and mycoheterotrophs.

### Botryobasidiaceae
A smaller family of resupinate fungi with diverse ecological roles, including wood decomposition and potentially mycorrhizal associations.

## Ecological Roles

### Ectomycorrhizal Symbiosis
Cantharellus and Hydnum species are obligate ectomycorrhizal fungi, forming mutualistic associations primarily with trees in forest ecosystems. Key partnerships include:
- *Cantharellus cibarius* with Quercus (oak), Fagus (beech), Picea (spruce), and Betula (birch)
- *Cantharellus lateritius* with various hardwoods in North America and Asia
- *Hydnum repandum* with diverse temperate forest trees
- *Sistotrema* species with Ericaceae (heaths, heathers) and some tree species

These mycorrhizae facilitate nutrient exchange, with the fungus providing phosphorus, nitrogen, and trace minerals to the host tree while receiving carbohydrates. Chanterelle fruiting is an indicator of healthy forest soil conditions.

### Orchid Mycorrhiza
Tulasnella species are the primary mycorrhizal partners for most of the approximately 28,000 described orchid species. This relationship is:
- **Obligate for the orchid**: seeds cannot germinate without fungal infection
- **Specific**: different orchid species require different Tulasnella strains
- **Temporal**: the fungus infects orchid seeds and protocorms, and often persists in mature plants
- **Critical for conservation**: orchid reintroduction programs require identification of appropriate Tulasnella partners

### Plant Pathogenesis
Ceratobasidiaceae includes some of the most damaging plant pathogens in agriculture and forestry. Rhizoctonia solani (Thanatephorus cucumeris) affects virtually all crop species and is particularly damaging in warm, humid conditions. Control relies on crop rotation, resistant varieties, fungicides, and biological control agents.

### Saprotrophy
Some Cantharellales are saprotrophic, decomposing leaf litter, wood, and other organic matter. *Craterellus fallax* and related species have mixed nutritional strategies, showing both saprotrophic and mycorrhizal capabilities.

## Edibility and Culinary Importance

Cantharellales contains some of the most commercially valuable wild-harvested mushrooms:

**Golden chanterelle (Cantharellus cibarius group)**: Among the top three most commercially important wild mushrooms globally, along with porcini and matsutake. Global trade exceeds $1 billion annually. The distinctive fruity, apricot-like aroma and peppery flavor make it a prized ingredient in fine cuisine.

**Black trumpet (Craterellus cornucopioides)**: Highly valued in French and European cuisine for its intense, truffle-like flavor when dried. Particularly important in regions where it is abundant (France, Spain, Pacific Northwest).

**Yellowfoot (Craterellus tubaeformis)**: Widely harvested commercially in Scandinavia, Eastern Europe, and the Pacific Northwest. Often used in soups and sauces.

**Hedgehog mushroom (Hydnum repandum)**: Excellent edible, firm texture and mild, nutty flavor. Considered a safe species for beginning foragers due to its distinctive toothed hymenophore.

All commercially important Cantharellales are wild-harvested; reliable cultivation methods remain elusive. Attempts to cultivate chanterelles have been largely unsuccessful due to their obligate mycorrhizal requirement, though some progress has been made with nursery inoculation of seedlings.
