# Thermal Composting — Process Standards and Microbial Biology

## Overview

Thermal composting uses the heat generated by microbial metabolism to create conditions that kill pathogens, weed seeds, and unwanted organisms while preserving beneficial microbial diversity. When done correctly, thermal composting produces a biologically rich amendment that serves as the foundation for compost tea brewing and soil food web restoration. Ingham emphasizes that thermal compost and worm (vermi-) compost can produce equally good results—neither is inherently superior—but each requires proper management.

## Thermal vs. Worm Compost

A common question is whether thermal compost is better than worm compost. Ingham's answer is clear: **equally good compost can be made from either process**. The comparison:

| Aspect | Thermal Compost | Worm Compost |
|--------|----------------|--------------|
| Pathogen kill mechanism | Heat (55°C+) | Worm digestion |
| Can be fungal-dominated | Yes | Yes |
| Can be bacterial-dominated | Yes | Yes |
| Risk of anaerobic conditions | Yes, if poorly managed | Yes, if poorly managed |
| [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] | Can have excellent populations | Can have excellent populations |
| Key requirement | Proper [[cervantes-reservoir-temperature-management]] | Adequate worm populations |

The more important consideration is finding a source of high-quality compost—regardless of process—that has the biology needed for your intended application. A 50:50 or 25:75 mix of thermal and worm composts works well.

## Temperature Requirements

### Pathogen Kill Standards

For thermal compost to meet food safety standards:

- **Minimum temperature**: 55°C (131°F) or higher for at least 3 days
- **Maximum temperature**: Not greater than 70°C (158°F)
- **Coverage**: Temperature must reach 55°C in ALL parts of the compost pile
- **Turning requirement**: Compost must be turned every time it approaches 68-70°C to maintain adequate air throughout the pile

Temperatures above 70°C begin killing beneficial organisms, including the bacteria and fungi that make compost biologically valuable. The narrow window between pathogen kill (55°C) and beneficial organism kill (~70°C) requires careful monitoring and management.

### Temperature Monitoring

- Daily temperature readings are required for each batch
- Multiple probe locations needed to verify uniform heating throughout the pile
- Temperature data should be submitted for regulatory compliance
- Each turning event should be recorded with date and temperature readings

## Moisture Requirements

Optimal moisture content for thermal composting: **45-75%**

- Below 45%: Microbial activity slows dramatically; decomposition stalls
- Above 75%: Anaerobic conditions develop; beneficial organisms are lost; foul odors result
- Ideal range: 50-60% for most feedstock combinations

The squeeze test provides a practical field assessment: compost should feel damp but not drip water when a handful is squeezed firmly.

## Oxygen Requirements

Maintaining aerobic conditions is critical throughout the composting process:

- **Minimum dissolved O₂**: 5.5 ppm
- **Daily CO₂ or O₂ monitoring** should be recorded
- Pleasant smell indicates aerobic conditions; foul odors signal anaerobic zones
- Turning frequency depends on oxygen monitoring data

When oxygen levels drop below threshold, [[ingham-anaerobic-organisms-role-soil]] produce toxic metabolites (alcohols, phenols, organic acids) that can be phytotoxic and harm beneficial [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]].

## Aerobic Habitat for Beneficial Organisms

The goal of thermal composting is not just decomposition—it is producing compost with the right biological community. Requirements for maintaining beneficial bacteria and fungi include:

1. **Adequate oxygen**: 5.5+ ppm dissolved gases throughout the cycle
2. **Proper moisture**: 45-75% throughout the process
3. **Appropriate temperature**: 55-70°C for pathogen kill, but not exceeding 70°C to preserve beneficials
4. **Pleasant smell**: Indicates aerobic conditions are maintained
5. **Required organism set**: The compost must contain the specific organisms needed to compete with, inhibit, and consume target diseases or pests

## Turning Strategy

Turning serves multiple purposes: redistributes oxygen throughout the pile, equalizes temperature by moving hot exterior material to the cooler center, breaks up anaerobic zones, and mixes materials to ensure all feedstock reaches proper temperatures. Turn compost every time the temperature approaches 68-70°C to prevent overheating and maintain aerobic conditions.

## Feedstock Considerations

### Starting Materials

- All starting materials must be documented for each batch
- Feedstock quality directly affects compost biology
- A mix of green (nitrogen-rich) and brown (carbon-rich) materials provides balanced nutrition for decomposer organisms

### Paper and Cardboard

Paper and cardboard are cellulose-based materials that help support fungal growth. Most modern inks are soy-based and safe for composting. Cardboard has longer fibers and is less refined than paper. Shredding paper/cardboard accelerates decomposition, while layering cardboard as sheets can help block sunlight from weed seeds.

### Mushroom Compost

Mushroom compost (spent substrate) requires careful evaluation—it is typically high in peat, sometimes high in sawdust, salts, and antibiotics, and has a wide C:N ratio. It may immobilize nitrogen, causing plant stunting. Inoculation with diverse beneficial fungi and addition of fungal foods (fish hydrolysate, oatmeal, seed meals) is recommended.

### Wood Chips

Wood chips require nitrogen sources and microbial inoculation. Combined with worm castings, microorganisms rapidly immobilize nitrogen from urea. Weekly compost tea applications to wood chip piles accelerate decomposition. The combination of carbon (wood) + nitrogen + biology creates productive compost quickly.

## Compost Quality for Tea Brewing

The quality of compost directly determines the quality of compost tea. Key considerations:

- **Better compost = less compost needed**: High-quality compost with diverse, active biology requires smaller volumes per brew
- **Amount varies by machine**: Compost volume requirements depend on brewer design (bag type, aeration method, pump type)
- **Machine-specific calibration**: Consult manufacturer recommendations based on their testing data
- **Bag type matters**: Single-layer nylon bags typically need less compost than woven/pressed fabric bags or baskets with tiny holes

## Finished Compost Standards

A mature, high-quality compost should meet these benchmarks:

| Parameter | Target Range |
|-----------|-------------|
| Total bacteria | Adequate for intended use |
| Active bacteria | <10% activity indicates mature compost |
| Total fungi | Matched to plant requirements |
| Active fungi | <10% activity in mature compost |
| Flagellates | 25,000+/g dry weight |
| Amoebae | 25,000+/g dry weight |
| Ciliates | 50-100/g dry weight (higher = anaerobic issue) |
| [[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]] | 20-100/g dry weight |
| Root-feeding nematodes | Zero |
| Fungal:bacterial ratio | Matched to plant successional stage |

## Record-Keeping Requirements

For each batch of thermal compost, document:

1. Starting materials used
2. Daily temperature readings (multiple probe locations)
3. Turning times and dates
4. End moisture content
5. Daily CO₂ or O₂ data
6. Chemistry and pathogen testing results meeting applicable standards
7. Intended use and matching of fungal:bacterial ratio to plant requirements

## Key Principle

Making good compost is an art because each batch of starting materials is different. The composter must adjust the process for differences in feedstock composition, particle size, moisture content, and ambient conditions. The biological content—not just chemical analysis—determines compost quality and its suitability for compost tea production.
