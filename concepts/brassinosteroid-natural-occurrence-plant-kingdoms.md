# Brassinosteroid Natural Occurrence Across Plant Kingdoms

Brassinosteroids are distributed across an impressively broad range of
plant lineages, reflecting ancient [[brassinosteroid-cross-kingdom-growth-regulation-plants-fungi]]. As noted by Gartz, Adam and
Vorbrodt (1990), approximately 30 brassinosteroid members had been
identified at the time, detected in higher plants including Angiospermae
and Gymnospermae as well as in a green alga. This broad phylogenetic
distribution provides context for understanding why brassinosteroids
might affect [[ingham-natural-growth-regulators-soil-microbes]], distinct from auxins, gibberellins,
cytokinins, [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]] and
cell division, processes most active in meristematic and reproductive
tissues.

## Distribution in Gymnospermae

Detection of brassinosteroids in Gymnospermae, including conifers such
as pine, spruce, and fir, significantly expanded the understood
phylogenetic range. Gymnosperms diverged from angiosperms approximately
300 million years ago, and brassinosteroid presence in both lineages
indicates the biosynthetic pathway is at least this old.

Brassinosteroids have been isolated from gymnosperm pollen, needles, and
young shoots. The compounds found are generally similar in structure to
angiosperm brassinosteroids, though some gymnosperm-specific
modifications have been noted. This structural conservation across such a
deep evolutionary split suggests strong functional constraints.

## Occurrence in Green Algae

Perhaps the most phylogenetically significant finding referenced in the
Gartz study is brassinosteroid detection in a green alga. Green algae
(Chlorophyta) share a common ancestor with land plants from
approximately 500 million years ago. Their presence in both green algae
and land plants suggests the biosynthetic pathway originated in their
shared algal ancestor and has been conserved throughout plant evolution.
