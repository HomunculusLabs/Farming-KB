## The Pharmacology of Sugar

As with all stimulants, ingestion of sugar is followed by a brief
euphoric "rush," which is itself followed by depression and guilt. The
cycle parallels that of other addictive substances. Janice K. Phelps
described sugar addicts as people addicted to one of the most powerful
substances to be found anywhere, the refined sugars. Their addiction is
a real, harmful, highly damaging health problem, just as debilitating as
addiction to any other substance.

When the chemical is not supplied, sugar addicts suffer identifiable
withdrawal symptoms. The process of feeding physiological hunger with a
chemical is destructive to the body, and the point may be reached when
supplying the chemical becomes as painful as withdrawing from it. The
cycle of chemical dependence becomes both entrenched and intolerable.

## Sugar and the Spice Trade

When the thirst for variety was slaked by massive and continuous
importation of spices, dyes, and flavorings, the infrastructure that had
been put in place turned its attention to fulfilling other cravings. The
production and shipping of sugar, chocolate, tea, and coffee, and of
distilled alcohol, all of which are drugs, became the engine of global
commerce.

Our present global trading system was created to cater to people's
inherent need for variety and stimulation. It did this with a single-
minded intensity that brooked no interference from the church or the
state. Neither moral scruples nor physical barriers were able to stand
in its way. Any "spice" or drug, no matter how restricted its
traditional area of use, can now be identified and produced or
synthesized for rapid export and sale to hungry markets anywhere on the
globe.

## Sugar and Slavery

The Spanish planted sugarcane in their Caribbean holdings, and they can
claim the dubious distinction of introducing slavery into the New World
for the purpose of producing sugar. Until 1550 the only sugar imported
from the Western hemisphere consisted of a few loaves brought as proof
of the possibility of production, or as mere curiosities. The plantings
in West Atlantic islands and the New World had no effect on production,
distribution, or prices until the latter half of the sixteenth century,
and only became dominant from about 1650.

The distortion and dehumanizing of human institutions and human lives
caused by sugar cultivation eclipses even the devastation wrought by
modern drug epidemics. The triangular trade pattern that developed, with
manufactured goods from Europe to Africa, enslaved people from Africa to
the Caribbean, and sugar from the Caribbean to Europe, defined the
economic structure of the Atlantic world for three centuries.

## Sugar and the Dominator Style

McKenna links sugar to what he calls the "dominator style" of culture.
Sugar's cheap sweetness fed the growing urban working class of
industrializing Europe, providing calories without nutrition and
creating dependence without awareness. Sugar exemplifies the dominator
approach to the natural world: the extraction of a single desired
compound (sweetness) from a complex whole, the destruction of the
ecological and [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-polydrug-use-social-context]] in which it naturally occurs, and the
commodification of the result for mass consumption.

The sugar plantation system, with its enslaved labor force and
monocultural agriculture, represents the dominator model of relationship
to nature in its purest form. It is the antithesis of the partnership
model that McKenna associates with hallucinogenic plant use in tribal
societies.

## The Broader Pattern

The sugar story illustrates McKenna's central thesis: that the drugs a
society chooses determine the character of that society. A society
fueled by sugar, caffeine, alcohol, and tobacco will develop different
social forms, values, and self-images than one that uses hallucinogenic
plants within a shamanic framework. The transition from medieval spice
hunger to modern sugar addiction represents a shift from diversity to
uniformity, from variety to monotony, from exploration to exploitation.
