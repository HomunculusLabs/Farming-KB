# Mycorrhizal Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic relationships with plant roots — the fungi get carbohydrates from the plant, and the plant gets water, phosphorus, and other nutrients in return. 80-95% of all terrestrial plants form mycorrhizae. Without them, plants cannot obtain optimal nutrition.

## Two Types

### Endomycorrhizae (Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi — AMF)
- **Penetrate** root cells and extend hyphae outward into soil
- Associated with most vegetables, annuals, grasses, legumes, shrubs
- Form arbuscules (tree-like structures) inside root cells for nutrient exchange
- Produce **glomalin** — a sticky glycoprotein that contributes 27-30% of soil carbon and builds soil structure
- No above-ground fruiting body (no mushrooms)
- Reproduce via spores in soil (50-400 micrometers)
- **This is the type relevant to cannabis cultivation**

### Ectomycorrhizae
- Form dense sheath **around** root surfaces, do not penetrate cells
- Associated with hardwoods, conifers, oaks, pines, beeches
- Often produce visible mushrooms (porcini, chanterelles, truffles)
- Important for forestry and orchards

## What Mycorrhizae Do for Plants

### Nutrient Mining
- **Phosphorus**: Major function — produce acids that dissolve chemically locked-up phosphorus and transport it to roots
- **Other nutrients**: Free up copper, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iron
- **Water**: Extend root access area 700-1000x; crucial during drought
- Can connect different plants and share resources between them

### Plant Protection
- Form physical web around roots, blocking pathogen access
- Produce antibiotics that suppress soil-borne diseases
- Help plants resist root rot (Phytophthora), fusarium wilt
- Increase resistance to environmental stress (drought, heavy metals, salinity)

### Soil Building
- Glomalin (from AMF) glues soil particles together, creating stable aggregates
- Hyphae leave microscopic tunnels (10 micrometers) when they die — channels for air and water
- Continuously add carbon to soil as hyphae grow and die

## What Kills Mycorrhizae

- **Tillage**: Breaks hyphal networks (biggest killer)
- **Synthetic phosphorus fertilizer**: Plant stops feeding fungi, fungi die off
- **Fungicides**: Obviously kill fungi (including beneficial ones)
- **Pesticides**: Many kill or weaken mycorrhizal fungi
- **Soil compaction**: Crushes hyphal networks
- **Bare fallow soil**: No host plant = no food for fungi = they die
- **Excessive nitrogen**: Reduces plant dependence on fungal partners

## How to Encourage Mycorrhizae

- Practice [[no-till-gardening-method]] — never rototill living soil beds
- Keep living roots in the soil at all times ([[cover-cropping-guide]])
- Use minimal phosphorus — only what plants need
- Avoid fungicides entirely
- Apply mycorrhizal inoculant at planting/transplanting
- Mulch with woody materials (fungal food)
- Use [[compost-tea-aact]] with fungal food (kelp, humic acids)
- Keep soil moist but well-drained

## Inoculation

- **Powder spore inoculants**: Mix into backfill soil or root zone at planting
- **Liquid inoculants**: Drench roots before transplanting
- **Compost inoculants**: Use compost from mature, undisturbed soils
- Apply spores directly to root zone or seed — fungi must contact roots to colonize
- Once established, mycorrhizae persist as long as host plants are present
- Re-inoculate after tilling, major disturbance, or if phosphorus fertilizer was overused

## For Cannabis in Living Soil

- Endomycorrhizae are essential for cannabis root systems
- Inoculate at clone/seed planting — young roots colonize most easily
- Avoid synthetic P boosters during veg (especially "bloom boosters" with high P)
- Keep soil covered with mulch layer for fungal habitat
- Fungal-dominated [[compost-tea-aact]] supports mycorrhizal maintenance
- Mycorrhizal networks can connect cannabis plants, sharing nutrients

## Deep Science: Evolution and Mechanisms

### Ancient Origins (Symbiotic Fungi, Varma & Kharkwal)
- Mycorrhizae are **400-460 million years old** — they existed when the first plants colonized land (Remy et al. 1994)
- Fossil arbuscules found in *Aglaophyton major* (Early Devonian) are morphologically identical to living AMF
- A.B. Frank first coined the term "mycorrhiza" in 1885 and hypothesized the mutualistic nutrition exchange — this was controversial for 120+ years but is now conclusively proven
- About **80% of all land plants** have AMF symbiosis (phylum Glomeromycota)

### Nutrient Absorption Mechanisms
- Mycorrhizal hyphae are **much smaller in diameter** than the finest root, exploring a greater soil volume
- Fungal cell membrane chemistry differs from plants — they access phosphate ions immobilized in basic-pH soils that plant roots cannot reach
- Absorption is both **physical** (surface area) and **chemical** (membrane transport capabilities)
- Under phosphorus limitation, AMF directly influence plant community development, nutrient uptake, water relations, and above-ground productivity

### Bioprotection
- Some mycorrhizal fungi **secrete antibiotics** that protect hosts from parasitic fungi and bacteria
- AMF act as bioprotectants against pathogens and toxic stresses (heavy metals, salinity)
- Biological nitrogen fixation by Rhizobium in legumes can be **enhanced by co-infection with AMF** (Xavier and Germida 2002)

### Role in Ecosystem Sustainability
- Mycorrhizal networks are **critical for ecosystem restoration** — degraded soils often lack mycorrhizal inoculum
- In sustainable agriculture, AMF reduce dependency on phosphorus fertilizers by 25-60%
- Fungal networks create **"common mycorrhizal networks" (CMNs)** that connect multiple plants, enabling resource sharing and signaling between individuals
- These networks may transmit **chemical warning signals** about pathogen attack between connected plants
- Mycoremediation leverages mycorrhizal fungi to **clean contaminated soils** — they can sequester heavy metals and degrade organic pollutants
