# Arid Land Fertilization and Pollution Risks

## Overview

Fertilization practices in arid lands require a fundamentally different approach from those in humid temperate zones. The combination of fragile soils, limited water, extreme temperatures, and slow organic matter breakdown means that misapplication of fertilizers or pollutants has far more severe and longer-lasting consequences than in more forgiving environments. This page covers practical fertilization strategies, innovative planting techniques, and the critical dangers of chemical pollution in desert ecosystems.

## Fertilization Philosophy for Arid Areas

### The Humus-First Approach

Fertilisers—apart from humus and limited animal manures—should be used sparingly in arid environments. The reasoning is clear:

- **Excessive green growth** subjects trees in particular to drought stress when water is limited
- **Salt accumulation** from synthetic fertilizers compounds existing soil salinity
- **Nutrient leaching** is minimal in arid soils but what little leaching occurs carries pollutants directly to groundwater with no biological buffering

The primary fertility strategy should always be building humus through mulching, composting, and green manuring, with mineral supplements used only where soil and leaf tissue analysis confirms specific deficiencies.

## Practical Fertilization Techniques

### The Plastic Bag Method for Valuable Trees

Magg (CSIRO Horticultural Science) suggests a novel method for establishing valuable trees in desert soils:

- **Bury two plastic bags** in the planting hole, provided infiltration is good
- Bags contain **0.5 kg of phosphate** plus slow-release pelleted fertiliser
- **Pin holes** in the bags allow gradual release over years
- These last the **entire life of the tree**

This method provides a sustained nutrient supply at root level without surface salt accumulation or loss to runoff.

### Compost and Mulch Systems

Gardeners in arid areas have had consistently good results from a layered approach:

1. **Surface layer**: Shredded bark, manure, and leaf nutrients as mulch
2. **Below mulch**: Compost layer for nutrient release and microbial activity
3. **pH amendment**: Sulphur added if pH is high

### Pit Composting Systems

An excellent system where salt in water is not a major problem combines:

- **Permanent compost pit** with continuous mulch layers above
- **Drip irrigation** from a small sprinkler or watering jar

The pit maintains moisture and biological activity at depth, while the mulch surface prevents evaporation and moderates soil temperature.

### Dune Fertilization

In sandy dune environments, specific amendments give good results for tree establishment:

- **Phosphate**: Essential for root development in nutrient-poor sands
- **Ammonia or guano**: Provides nitrogen in readily available form
- **Zinc**: Often deficient in calcareous dune sands
- **Ammonium sulphate**: Combines nitrogen and sulphur, both often needed

### Sewage and Wastewater Use

Sewage waters have given good results with fuelwood trees throughout arid regions:

- **Salt content must be checked**: Water gains approximately 300 ppm in salts as it passes through towns
- **Sewage lagoon walls** are excellent tree sites, as are raised islands within lagoons
- **Shallows** grow lotus and water-lily
- **Bird-carried seeds**: Lagoons and waterholes attract birds that distribute seeds through defecation and regurgitation

### Nutrients Already Present in Arid Soils

Most people do not add potash to desert soils because these minerals may already be plentiful:

- Sodium, chlorine, carbonates, calcium, boron, and magnesium are naturally abundant in many arid soils
- The best practice is to get **thorough analyses of both soils and leaf** before and after growing rather than applying standard fertilizer regimes

## The Effects of Poisons in Deserts

### Why Poisons Are More Dangerous in Arid Lands

All persistent biocides should be sensibly banned from drylands. The reasoning is critical:

- **Missing aquatic plants**: It is aquatic plants that break down most biocides most effectively—and these are absent in deserts
- **Slow water movement**: Water movement in soils is slow, and deep sands do little to offset or neutralize poisons
- **No dilution**: Unlike in humid regions with abundant rainfall and active soil biology, poisons persist and accumulate
- **Long-distance travel**: Herbicides used on lawns seep slowly to sandy watercourses, travel down them for miles, and move up and down on groundwater, killing thousands of plants over time

### Case Study: California Groundwater Crisis

California is described as being in "very deep trouble" with its groundwater supplies after a 40-year chemical application period. Many Californians die from these poisons in their drinking water. The few nearby swamps and lagoons that could help biologically break down contaminants are themselves being destroyed.

### Case Study: Alice Springs, Australia

Trees near Alice Springs are being killed where lawns are developed along with their associated chemical treatments. The poisons move through sandy soils into the root zones of established native vegetation.

### Case Study: Israeli Sewage Pollution

In Israel, sewage and agricultural pollution rises through sandy soils during summer. Nitrates can be included in bore water pumped for domestic use. Winter rains carry pollutants downward, requiring careful management to keep them clear of reservoir water. As many desert waters already have high levels of dissolved salts, any additional nitrate stress may cause kidney malfunction.

### Aboriginal Health Impact

Some **47% of Aboriginal outstation people** using bore water suffer kidney damage, consequent high blood pressure, and excessive intercellular water (a form of dropsy). This demonstrates the direct human health consequences of poor water quality management in arid areas.

## Sewage Disposal in Arid Lands

The position of settlement and the disposal of sewage must be carefully assessed in arid lands (including arid islands). Key considerations:

1. **Seasonal pollutant movement**: Summer heat drives pollutants upward through sandy soils; winter rains carry them downward
2. **Bore water contamination**: Nitrates from sewage can enter domestic bore water supplies
3. **Kidney stress**: Combined nitrate and salt loads in water can cause cumulative kidney damage
4. **Testing requirements**: Water supplies in arid areas must be tested for nitrates, particularly where children consume bore water and garden leaf products

## The Desert Valley Profile

Understanding the soil and vegetation zones within a desert valley provides a template for intelligent placement of settlements and agriculture:

### Zone Structure (from plateau to watercourse)

| Zone | Characteristics | Vegetation Potential |
|---|---|---|
| **A - Hard lateric/ironstone cap** | Skeletal soils, long-lived woody plants | Hardy woody plants if introduced |
| **B - Scree slopes** | Loose rock, rapid drainage | Bunch grasses, hardy plants, some large trees |
| **C - Outwash plains** | Sandy clays, fast runoff | Trees if swales are installed |
| **D - Sandy infiltration and mud-out areas** | Mixed sediments, mammal and reptile shelters | Diverse plantings possible |
| **E - River silts** | Fertile deposition, many burrowing organisms | Large trees with deep roots: figs, casuarina, cottonwoods (North America), redgums (Australia) |
| **F - Sandy riverbed (wadi)** | Seasonal water flow | Dense large trees and vines if ungrazed |

### Settlement Placement Principles

- **Locate settlements in alkaline waterway areas** to take advantage of water runoff
- **Test bore water thoroughly** for fluorine, sodium, nitrates, and radioactives
- **Position sewage disposal** with understanding of seasonal pollutant movement
- **Plant along favorable corridors** extending from settlement cores

## Natural Remedies as Priority

The overarching principle for desert pest and weed management is clear: **we should out-think, not poison out, any species that promise to trouble us**. Most weeds provide biomass or mulch, and natural biological controls should always be preferred over chemical interventions in these fragile ecosystems.

## Key Principles Summary

1. **Humus is the foundation**: All fertility strategies begin with organic matter building
2. **Test, do not guess**: Soil and leaf tissue analysis before and after growing
3. **Spare application**: Less is more with fertilizers in arid environments
4. **Ban persistent biocides**: No chemical controls where aquatic systems cannot break them down
5. **Protect water quality**: Bore water testing and careful sewage placement are non-negotiable
6. **Use wastewater wisely**: Sewage supports fuelwood trees but requires salt monitoring
7. **Small, careful systems**: Assess total system capacity before expanding
8. **Natural pest management**: Out-think problems rather than poison them
