# Fungal Interactions with Insects

Fungi have evolved intimate associations with diverse insect groups, ranging from mutualistic symbioses to parasitic relationships. These interactions represent some of the most complex and ecologically significant fungus-animal relationships.

[[ant-termite-fungal-symbiosis]] [[fungal-beetle-symbiosis]] [[fungal-relationships-with-insects]]

## Fungus-Growing Ants

The ant-fungus association alone is responsible for an estimated 20% of the photosynthate turnover in the Neotropics, and some specific associations rank among the most important causes of crop loss in Latin America.

The fungus-culturing habit was probably acquired by ants only once in evolutionary time. All ants able to culture fungi belong to a single tribe (Attini) in the family Myrmicidae. The broadest treatment of the ants is by Holder and Wilson (1990), although molecular studies have since expanded understanding.

[[ant-termite-fungal-symbiosis]]

## Lower vs Higher Attines

Associations between attine ant species and their fungi differ, ranging from relatively loose associations in lower attines (Mycocepurus, Mycetophylax, Mycetarotes, Mycetosoritis, Sericomyrmex) to fixed clonal associations in higher attines (Atta, Acromyrmex, Apterostigma).

Because fungi are transferred asexually from one generation to the next in the higher attines, sampling in a given location or country may produce very low diversity of ant-associated fungi. Regulatory mechanisms fine-tuning the relationships between ants and fungi may be most diverse in the otherwise taxonomically least diverse fungi of the higher attines.

[[ant-termite-fungal-symbiosis]]

## Attine Fungal Symbionts

The only applicable binomial is Leucoagaricus gongylophorus, referring to the fungus cultivated by higher attines. That fungus is most probably a single species in which all representatives may be clonal descendants of a common ancestor.

Fungi associated with lower attines eventually produce basidiocarps in culture, consistent with Leucoagaricus or Leucocoprinus species. Each basidiocarp reported appears distinct at the species level. Until all fungi are better known, a conservative approach is to refer to each isolate by the ant species name, location, and date.

Attine nests are maintained under axenic or quasi-axenic conditions, formed mostly of mycelium of a single basidiomycete strain. Two types of non-host fungi occur: endophytic xylariaceous species brought in with leaf substrata, and specialized parasites such as Escovopsis.

## Culturing Attine Fungi

The fungi cultivated by attine ants are saprobes that grow well on general media such as PDA and MEA. Isolation from nests in the field usually fails because cultures are highly likely to be contaminated with faster-growing fungi.

If ant workers are kept together with nest material in a moist container for about 3 days, they reconstitute quasi-axenic conditions, greatly reducing contamination risk. Clonally derived fungi of higher attines and Apterostigma cannot withstand temperatures below 4 degrees Celsius, and no cryopreservation regimen has been developed.

## Fungus-Growing Termites

Termite gardening symbioses are restricted to old-world higher termites, primarily the tribe Macrotermitinae. These termites construct a comb of fecal pellets on which the fungus grows. The fungal associates are species of Termitomyces (Lepiotaceae), the same basidiomycete family associated with most attine ants.

Most Termitomyces species form basidiocarps annually at the onset of the rainy season. Fungal inoculum of some species is dispersed in the guts of alates. Xylaria is regularly associated with combs but quickly overgrows Termitomyces when combs are removed from nests.

## Wood Wasps and Fungi

Several genera of resupinate aphyllophoralean wood-decaying basidiomycetes have specific interactions with wood wasps of the family Siricidae. The wasps require fungi for nutrition, carrying oidia in mycangia (hypopleural organs).

Amylosterium areolatum associated with Sirex noctilio has been introduced into New Zealand and Australia, where it has become a serious pathogen of Pinus radiata. Only certain species of Amylosterium and Cerrena unicolor have been found associated with wood wasps.

## Bark Beetle-Associated Fungi

Bark beetles carry stain fungi that can interact with wood wasp-associated fungi, causing die back when both are present in trees. The spore-dispersal interactions between bark beetles and their fungal associates represent another dimension of insect-fungus coevolution.

These associations range from mutualistic to pathogenic, with beetles serving as vectors for fungal dispersal while fungi may improve host quality for beetle larvae or contribute to tree killing.

[[fungal-beetle-symbiosis]]

## Parasitic Fungi of Insects

Many fungi are obligate parasites of insects, including the well-known genus Cordyceps and related hypocrealean genera. Noda and colleagues (1995) found planthopper-associated fungi specifically related to Cordyceps.

These entomopathogenic fungi regulate insect populations and represent potential biological control agents. Their diversity is substantial but poorly documented, especially in tropical regions.

## Endosymbiotic Fungi

Some insects harbor yeastlike endosymbiotic fungi within their bodies. Planthoppers may be infected with yeastlike symbionts that play nutritional roles. These intimate associations blur the line between parasitism and mutualism.

The diversity of insect-associated fungi, including endosymbionts, is likely vastly underestimated due to the difficulty of detecting and culturing these organisms.

## Ecological Significance

Insect-fungus interactions are ecologically significant at multiple scales. Ant and termite fungus gardens process enormous quantities of plant material. Entomopathogenic fungi regulate insect populations. Wood-associated fungi transmitted by insects drive decomposition and nutrient cycling.

The coevolutionary dynamics of these associations have produced remarkable specialization and represent a frontier for fungal biodiversity discovery.
