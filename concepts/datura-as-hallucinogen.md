# Datura as Hallucinogen

Datura species contain potent tropane alkaloids — primarily scopolamine, hyoscyamine, and atropine — that produce a distinctive deliriant state rather than classic psychedelic effects. Schultes's "Golden Guide to Hallucinogenic Plants" documents the extensive use of Datura in both Old World and New World cultures, noting that "the hallucinogenic properties of Datura have been thoroughly exploited, particularly in the New World."

## The Genus Datura

### Taxonomy

- **Family**: Solanaceae (nightshade family).
- **Genus**: *Datura* (sometimes placed in *Brugmansia* for woody, tree-like species).
- **Key species**: *Datura stramonium* (jimsonweed), *D. inoxia* (downy thornapple), *D. metel* (Hindu datura), *D. candida* (angel's trumpet), *D. aurea* (golden angel's trumpet).

### Plant Description

Datura plants are coarse, erect annuals or short-lived perennials with large trumpet-shaped flowers and spiny seed capsules:

- **Leaves**: Large, coarsely toothed, with a strong, distinctive odor when crushed.
- **Flowers**: Trumpet-shaped, 5–20 cm long, typically white, purple, or yellow. Open in the evening and emit a sweet fragrance.
- **Fruit**: Spiny, egg-shaped capsule containing numerous kidney-shaped seeds.
- **Habitat**: Widely distributed in temperate and subtropical regions; often grows as a weed in disturbed soils, roadsides, and cultivated fields.
- **All parts toxic**: Leaves, seeds, flowers, and stems all contain tropane alkaloids.

## The Tropane Alkaloids

### Scopolamine (Hyoscine)

- **Chemical name**: L-(-)-scopolamine.
- **Classification**: Tropane alkaloid, anticholinergic.
- **Mechanism**: Competitive antagonist at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (M1–M5), blocking parasympathetic nervous system signaling.
- **Effects**: Amnesia, sedation, delirium, mydriasis (pupil dilation), dry mouth, tachycardia.
- **Medical use**: Used as an antiemetic (scopolamine patches for motion sickness), antispasmodic, and pre-anesthetic.

### Hyoscyamine and Atropine

- **Atropine** is the racemic mixture of L- and D-hyoscyamine.
- Similar anticholinergic mechanism to scopolamine but with different receptor subtype affinities.
- Atropine is widely used in medicine (pupil dilation for eye exams, heart rate regulation, organophosphate poisoning treatment).
- Hyoscyamine is the more pharmacologically active enantiomer.

### Toxicity

Datura poisoning is a significant medical concern:

- The difference between a psychoactive dose and a lethal dose is narrow and unpredictable.
- Alkaloid content varies dramatically between plants, parts, and growing conditions.
- Seeds are the most concentrated source; a handful can be fatal.
- Symptoms of poisoning overlap with the desired hallucinogenic effects, making safe dosing extremely difficult.

## The Deliriant Experience

Datura produces a fundamentally different experience from classic psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, mescaline):

### Characteristics of Delirium

- **True hallucinations** — Users cannot distinguish hallucinations from reality. Unlike psychedelics, where the user typically knows they are under the influence, Datura produces a state where the hallucinations are experienced as completely real.
- **Amnesia** — Users typically have little or no memory of the experience afterward.
- **Confusion and disorientation** — Profound cognitive impairment; users may engage in dangerous behaviors (walking into traffic, attempting to fly) with no awareness.
- **Inability to communicate** — Users often speak to nonexistent people or objects and cannot be reasoned with.
- **Duration** — Effects can last 24–72 hours, far longer than classic psychedelics.

### Physical Effects

- **Anticholinergic syndrome**: dry mouth (xerostomia), dry skin, flushed face, dilated pupils (mydriasis) with light sensitivity, rapid heartbeat (tachycardia), urinary retention, elevated body temperature (hyperthermia), constipation.
- **Dangerous complications**: Seizures, coma, cardiac arrhythmias, rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown), and death in severe cases.

## Cultural Use — New World

### Mexico and the Southwest

Schultes documents extensive Datura use in Mexican indigenous cultures:

- **Divination and prophecy** — Datura was used to foresee the future and receive divine messages.
- **Ritual curing** — Healers (curanderos) used Datura to diagnose illness through visionary states.
- **Aztec use** — The Aztecs called Datura *tolguaca* or *teomatlatl* and used it in religious ceremonies.
- **Tarascan use** — The Tarascan people of Michoacán used Datura in divination rituals.
- **Zuni and other Pueblo peoples** — Southwestern US tribes used Datura as an analgesic and in initiation ceremonies.

### Tree Daturas (Brugmansia)

Schultes describes tree daturas (*Brugmansia* spp.) as important hallucinogens in South America:

- **Culebra borrachera** (*Brugmansia arborea* or *B. sanguinea*) — Used in Colombia and Ecuador. Schultes notes it as one of the more potent Datura preparations.
- **Use contexts**: Shamanic healing, communication with ancestors, and ritual intoxication.
- **Preparation**: Leaves, flowers, and seeds brewed into teas or used as poultices.
- **Added to ayahuasca** — In some traditions, Brugmansia leaves are added to ayahuasca brews to intensify the visionary experience.

## Cultural Use — Old World

### India

Datura has deep roots in Indian culture:

- **Religious significance** — Associated with Shiva; offerings of Datura flowers are made at Shiva temples.
- **Ayurvedic medicine** — Used as an antispasmodic, analgesic, and treatment for asthma (despite its dangers).
- **Thuggee use** — Historical accounts (possibly exaggerated) claim Datura was used by the Thuggee to incapacitate victims.

### Europe

- **Witchcraft and folklore** — Datura was one of the plants believed to be used in witches' flying ointments. Tropane alkaloids absorbed through the skin could produce sensations of flying and animal transformation.
- **Medieval medicine** — Used as a painkiller and sedative, though the narrow therapeutic window made it dangerous.
- **Oracle of Delphi** — Some scholars have suggested that Datura or related tropane-containing plants may have been involved in the Pythian priestess's trances, though this theory remains debated.

## Modern Warnings

The extreme danger of Datura cannot be overstated:

- **Unpredictable potency** — Alkaloid concentration varies 10-fold or more between individual plants.
- **No safe dose** — There is no reliably safe recreational dose; hospitalization from Datura poisoning is common in emergency departments worldwide.
- **Dangerous behavior** — The deliriant state leads users to engage in life-threatening activities they are unaware of.
- **Lasting effects** — Some users report cognitive impairment, memory problems, and psychological disturbances lasting weeks or months after use.
