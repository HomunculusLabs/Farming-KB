# Food Fermentation Guide

Lacto-fermentation is the process of preserving food through the action of lactic acid bacteria (LAB), primarily Lactobacillus species. These bacteria convert sugars into lactic acid, lowering pH below 4.6, which inhibits pathogenic organisms and preserves the food. Fermentation is one of the oldest food preservation methods and is central to homestead food security and nutrition.

## The Science of Lacto-Fermentation

Lactic acid bacteria are naturally present on the surfaces of vegetables and in the environment. In a salt brine, these beneficial organisms outcompete spoilage organisms. The process occurs in stages:

1. Initial phase (0-3 days): Aerobic bacteria and yeasts consume available oxygen. Leuconostoc species produce CO2.
2. Active fermentation (3-10 days): Lactobacillus species dominate, producing lactic acid. CO2 production is vigorous -- brine may bubble and foam.
3. Stable phase (10-21+ days): Lactic acid concentration peaks. Fermentation slows as available sugars are consumed.
4. Storage: At proper acidity (pH below 4.6), the food is stable at cool temperatures for months.

## Salt Brine Fundamentals

Salt concentration is the most critical variable in vegetable fermentation:

- 2% brine (20g salt per liter of water): Standard for most vegetables. Mild flavor, good texture retention.
- 3.5% brine (35g salt per liter): Better preservation, firmer texture. Good for cucumbers and peppers.
- 5% brine (50g salt per liter): Strong preservation. Used for long-term storage and very hot climates.

Use non-iodized salt -- sea salt, kosher salt, or pickling salt. Iodine can inhibit fermentation. Avoid salts with anti-caking additives.

To make brine: Dissolve salt in non-chlorinated water. If using tap water, let it sit uncovered for 24 hours or boil and cool to dissipate chlorine. Spring water or well water is ideal.

## Equipment

- Fermentation vessels: Glass jars with airlock lids, crocks, or dedicated fermentation vessels
- Weights: Glass fermentation weights, boiled stones, or a ziplock bag filled with brine
- Airlocks: Allow CO2 to escape while preventing oxygen ingress
- Non-metal utensils: Wood or plastic for packing vegetables
- Kitchen scale: For precise salt measurement
- Thermometer: For monitoring fermentation temperature

## Sauerkraut

Sauerkraut is the most accessible fermented food -- cabbage, salt, and time.

Ingredients:
- 5 lbs green cabbage (1 large head)
- 3 tablespoons non-iodized salt (approximately 2% by weight)
- Optional: caraway seeds, juniper berries, grated carrot

Process:
1. Remove outer leaves of cabbage. Reserve one clean leaf.
2. Slice cabbage thinly (1-2mm) using a knife or mandoline.
3. Weigh cabbage and calculate salt (2% of cabbage weight).
4. Combine cabbage and salt in a large bowl. Massage and squeeze for 5-10 minutes until juice releases and cabbage becomes limp.
5. Pack cabbage tightly into fermentation vessel, pressing down to eliminate air pockets and submerge cabbage in its own juice.
6. Place reserved leaf on top to hold shredded cabbage under brine.
7. Add weight to keep everything submerged.
8. Seal with airlock lid or cover with cloth.
9. Ferment at 65-72 F for 3-4 weeks.
10. Transfer to cold storage (refrigerator or root cellar). Flavor improves with age.

## Kimchi

Kimchi is a Korean fermented vegetable preparation with more complex seasoning than sauerkraut.

Ingredients:
- 2 lbs napa cabbage
- 1 daikon radish, julienned
- 4 green onions, chopped
- 3 tablespoons Korean chili flakes (gochugaru)
- 1 tablespoon minced garlic
- 1 tablespoon minced ginger
- 1 tablespoon fish sauce or soy sauce
- 1.5 tablespoons salt

Process:
1. Quarter cabbage, salt generously, and let sit for 2 hours to draw out moisture.
2. Rinse cabbage and squeeze dry.
3. Mix remaining ingredients into a paste.
4. Coat cabbage quarters and radish with paste.
5. Pack tightly into jar, pressing to release air.
6. Add brine if needed to submerge vegetables.
7. Ferment at 65-72 F for 5-10 days (taste at day 5).
8. Transfer to cold storage.

## Other Fermentable Foods

- Pickles: Whole or sliced cucumbers in 3.5% brine with dill, garlic, and peppercorns
- Hot sauce: Fermented chili peppers blended with vinegar
- Fermented garlic: Whole cloves in 2% brine
- Fermented beets: Cubed beets in 2% brine
- Cortido: Latin American fermented cabbage with oregano and onions
- Curtido: Salvadoran cabbage slaw with carrots and onion
- Ginger beer: Fermented ginger, sugar, and water (uses ginger bug starter)

## Safety

Lacto-fermentation is inherently safe when basic principles are followed:

- Keep vegetables submerged below brine at all times -- exposure to air risks mold
- Use proper salt concentration (minimum 2%)
- Maintain temperature between 60-75 F
- Discard if you see fuzzy mold (not kahm yeast, which is a white film and harmless)
- Off-putting smells (rotten, putrid) indicate spoilage -- discard
- The finished product should smell tangy and sour, not rotten
- Fermented vegetables that have gone soft and slimy should be discarded

## Troubleshooting

- Kahm yeast (white film on surface): Harmless, skim off. Reduce headspace to minimize.
- Mold (fuzzy, colored growth): Discard the batch.
- Soft vegetables: Too little salt, temperature too high, or fermentation too long.
- Hollow pickles: Cucumbers were too large or brine too weak.
- No bubbles: Fermentation may be slow -- wait up to 7 days before worrying.
