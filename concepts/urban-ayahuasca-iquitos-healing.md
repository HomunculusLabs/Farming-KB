# Urban Ayahuasca Healing in Iquitos

Marlene Dobkin de Rios documented the practice of urban ayahuasca curing in the eastern Peruvian city of Iquitos, focusing on the slum district of Belén. Her work, published in [[hallucinogens-and-shamanism-harner-anthology]] (1973), revealed how traditional Amazonian shamanic healing adapted to an urban mestizo context, with significant implications for understanding the therapeutic mechanisms of hallucinogen-assisted healing.

## Setting: The Belén Slum

Iquitos is a city of approximately 100,000 inhabitants in the Peruvian Amazon, accessible only by air or river. Belén, the port district, is a slum area characterized by extreme poverty, overcrowding, and poor sanitation. Its population of mixed Indian-white ancestry (mestizos) lives in houses built on stilts over the Itaya River, subject to seasonal flooding.

When modern Western medicine fails to provide a cure — as it often does given the cost and limited availability of formal medical consultation — slum-dwellers ascribe illness to supernatural causation. This ascription draws on both native American and European beliefs concerning witchcraft as a source of illness, leading them to seek the services of an ayahuasquero, a mestizo shaman who uses ayahuasca.

## The Ayahuasquero

Dobkin de Rios contacted ten healers in the Iquitos area, working most intensively with four primary informants. The ayahuasquero prepares ayahuasca from Banisteriopsis caapi and B. rusbyana, serving as both diagnostician and therapist. Patients and the curer partake of the hallucinogenic drink together in group sessions, typically lasting through the night.

The curer uses the trance state to determine the cause of each patient's illness. Sickness is typically ascribed to a specific bewitcher in each case — a practice Dobkin de Rios identifies as a therapeutically effective means of transforming the generalized and incapacitating anxiety of the slum-dwellers into "solid fear that is placed squarely on the shoulders of some evildoer."

## Disease Categories

The urban ayahuasqueros work within a framework of culturally defined disease categories that blend indigenous and European elements:

Susto (fright sickness) results from a traumatic experience that causes the soul to leave the body.

Daño (harm) is illness caused by the deliberate malevolent action of a sorcerer.

Pulsario refers to a sense of pulsation or throbbing in the body, attributed to supernatural intervention.

Mal de ojo (evil eye) is caused by the envious gaze of another person.

These categories provide meaningful explanatory frameworks for patients whose suffering cannot be addressed by available biomedical resources.

## Mechanisms of Healing

Dobkin de Rios identified several therapeutic mechanisms at work in the urban ayahuasca healing sessions:

Doctrinal compliance plays a central role: the patient accepts the healer's interpretation of the cause of illness and the prescribed treatment, creating a coherent narrative that makes suffering meaningful. Unlike suggestion, which operates on a conscious level, doctrinal compliance seems to be an unconscious process which occurs in both magical and modern therapy procedures.

Abreaction occurs as the hallucinogenic experience allows patients to release repressed emotional material within a culturally sanctioned framework. The generalized, immobilizing anxiety present in the sociocultural milieu is changed into solid fear placed on the shoulders of some evil-doer by a healer whose presentation of self includes an omnipotent stance.

Suggestion operates powerfully in the heightened suggestibility of the drug state. The hallucinations of the group session and the guidance provided by the shaman reinforce the patient's belief in the reality of the shaman's power and information.

## Magical Conditioning

Before allowing some patients to take the purge, a healer will spend up to two weeks exorcising the "evil" afflicting them. This preparatory period is necessary because patients are drawn from both transitional Indian groups and middle-income segments of the community, and magical beliefs among this latter segment function close to and at times in competition with rational ones.

Patients' expectations, learned either in childhood or reinforced during these preliminary sessions, that they will be visited by a boa or other snake, as well as their belief in the curative prediction of success anticipated by that apparition's appearance, provide them with reassurance that healing is indeed occurring.

## Ayahuasca Visions and Healing

One major pattern of informants' visions are reports of similar forest creatures such as boa constrictors and viperous snakes, which often appear before a man or woman taking ayahuasca. Another frequent vision was that of the person responsible for bewitching them. Others would report a panorama of activity in which some man or woman would express his innermost thoughts and feelings toward the patient, such as sexual desire, vengeance, or hate.

These fearsome creatures, which in any part of the world might be considered part of a "bad trip" phenomenon, are rather cleverly utilized by healers to help them in their curing. Believed to be the reincarnated spirit of the vine itself, such mental productions are believed to bring messages of healing and assurance to the patient.

During a successful session, one can observe patients spontaneously breaking out into song, accompanying the healer or his apprentices. The physiological effects of hallucinogenic use, ranging from states of euphoria to great anguish, probably have universal distribution, but it is quite interesting to note how such "bad trips" can be minimized or controlled by healing techniques when the presence of a belief system sets such activity within an ongoing magical framework.

## The Diagnostic and Revelatory Function

The use of ayahuasca to heal does not include a conceptualization of the hallucinogen as a curative agent, per se. Rather, the vine is seen to operate as a powerful means to a desired end — it gives the healer entry into the culturally important area of disease causality, enabling him to identify the nature of the illness from which a person is suffering and then to deflect or neutralize the evil magic which is deemed responsible for illness.

A selection process takes place so that curers accept patients only if they believe they will have a good chance of success. Simple illnesses are rarely treated with the drug, but herbs, plants, and store-bought medicines are prescribed by the healer for these types of affliction. Nor are psychotic patients given ayahuasca.

## Comparison with Western Psychotherapy

Dobkin de Rios contrasted the role of ayahuasca in the Iquitos context with the use of related drugs in Western psychotherapy, where their role is primarily "to open areas of repressed and painful memories." In the urban Amazonian setting, the hallucinogenic experience operates within a comprehensive cosmological and social framework that provides meaning, community support, and a clear path from diagnosis to cure.

The hallucinogen, ayahuasca, is used most effectively in healing those illnesses believed to be magical in origin. The particular visual hallucinations are put to use by the healer to determine the magical cause of illness as well as to neutralize evil magic. The importance of the forest setting, and the widespread knowledge, awareness, and familiarity of most people with the drug, the expectations of what will happen and the great respect for and remembered cures of drug healers, point up the importance of cultural variables.

## Historical Context

Marlene Dobkin de Rios's documentation of urban ayahuasca healing in Iquitos, published in 1973, was among the first ethnographic accounts to examine the adaptation of indigenous shamanic practices to an urban mestizo context. The Belén district of Iquitos, accessible only by air or river, represented a unique setting where traditional Amazonian healing practices confronted the challenges of urban poverty, inadequate biomedical resources, and cultural hybridity.

The blending of indigenous and European supernatural beliefs in the Iquitos healing system — combining native concepts of soul loss (susto) and witchcraft (daño) with European notions of the evil eye (mal de ojo) — reflects the complex cultural history of the Peruvian Amazon, where indigenous, colonial, and modern influences have intermixed for centuries. This syncretism parallels the religious blending observed in other contexts, such as the Christian elements in Campa ayahuasca ceremonies documented in [[campa-ayahuasca-ceremony]].

Dobkin de Rios's identification of specific therapeutic mechanisms — doctrinal compliance, abreaction, suggestion, and magical conditioning — was informed by both anthropological and psychoanalytic theory, and her framework anticipated many of the concepts later developed in the field of psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy.

## Modern Relevance

Iquitos has since become a major center of ayahuasca tourism, with dozens of retreat centers catering to international visitors seeking healing and spiritual experience. The tensions between traditional healing practices and commercial exploitation that Dobkin de Rios observed in their early stages have only intensified, raising important ethical questions discussed in [[ayahuasca-tourism-ethics]].

The therapeutic mechanisms identified in the Iquitos context — particularly the transformation of generalized anxiety into specific, actionable fear through the identification of a bewitcher — have direct parallels in modern psychotherapeutic approaches. The concept of "externalization," in which patients attribute their suffering to an external cause that can be confronted and resolved, is a recognized therapeutic technique in cognitive-behavioral and narrative therapies.

Contemporary clinical research on ayahuasca, including studies of its potential for treating depression, anxiety, and addiction, has increasingly recognized the importance of the ceremonial context and the healer-patient relationship — factors that the Iquitos model demonstrates are essential to therapeutic outcomes. The [[beta-carboline-chemistry]] provides the biochemical foundation, but the cultural framework documented by Dobkin de Rios is what translates that biochemistry into healing.

## Merged: Transcendent Other

# Transcendent Other

The "Transcendent Other" is Terence McKenna's term for the ontologically autonomous, seemingly intelligent presence encountered during high-dose [[dmt-dimethyltryptamine-experience-guide]] and [[philosophical-implications-of-psychedelics]] experiences. The concept is developed most fully in Food of the Gods as the experiential core that gives the mushroom experience its religious and evolutionary significance.

## Definition

McKenna defines the Transcendent Other as "what one encounters on powerful hallucinogens. It is the crucible of the Mystery of our being, both as a species and as individuals. The Transcendent Other is Nature without her cheerfully reassuring mask of ordinary space, time, and causality."

From one perspective, the Transcendent Other is "nature correctly perceived to be alive and intelligent." From another, it is "the awesomely unfamiliar union of all the senses with memory of the past and anticipation of the future." McKenna insists this is "only a label, not an explanation."

## The Encounter

Those who report contact with the Transcendent Other describe a range of experiences: entities that seem to possess agency and intention, machine-elf or jester-like beings that communicate through visual and auditory modalities, and landscapes of impossible complexity that respond to the observer's thoughts. The encounter is consistently described as more real than everyday reality — hyper-real, not unreal.

McKenna argued that the consistency of these reports across cultures and historical periods suggests the phenomenon is not merely subjective hallucination but points to something genuinely transpersonal. The same basic encounter has been reported by Amazonian shamans, modern psychonauts, and participants in clinical research.

## Mechanism: Information Transfer Across Species

McKenna frames the encounter as an instance of interspecies information transfer. If hallucinogens function as "interspecies chemical messengers" (exopheromones), then the dynamic between primate and hallucinogenic plant becomes one of information transfer from one species to another:

"Where plant hallucinogens do not occur, such transfers of information take place with great slowness, but in the presence of hallucinogens a culture is quickly introduced to ever more novel information, sensory input, and behavior and thus is bootstrapped to higher and higher states of self-reflection."

The Transcendent Other is thus both the content of the experience and the evolutionary mechanism by which the experience transforms human cognition. Each encounter introduces novel information that expands the cultural and cognitive horizon.

## Exopheromone Hypothesis

McKenna proposes that psychoactive plant compounds may function as exopheromones — chemical messengers that act across species lines. Some exopheromones may "allow a small group of individuals to affect a community or an entire biome." This extends the concept of nature as "an organism whose interconnected components act upon and communicate with one another through the release of chemical signals."

This framing draws on chemical ecology suggesting that plant secondary metabolites may not be "secondary" at all but are in a "dynamic state, fluctuating in both concentration and in their rate of metabolic decay." The role of these compounds "makes it clear that they are essential to the life and the survival strategy of the organism."
