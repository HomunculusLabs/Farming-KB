# Levels and Levelling Tools

## Overview

Accurate levelling is essential in [[permaculture-earthworks]] to ensure that spillways function, drains flow correctly, dam walls are level, and house sites are truly horizontal. While sophisticated instruments exist, many highly effective levelling tools can be handmade from readily available materials. Thousands of kilometres of drains have been surveyed and levels set using very simple tools, often by people with minimal training.

## The Water Principle

The most ancient and reliable level is the surface of water itself. Water always finds its own level, and this principle underlies all levelling instruments. One can always check a horizontal against the sea horizon, or verify that a drain or gutter works by using a small amount of water to flow down it. Even sophisticated instruments like the theodolite use a bubble level to set up the base plate and check the telescope before proceeding with tilt or swivel measurements.

## The Hose ("Bunyip") Level

The hose level is one of the most practical and widely-used levelling tools for permaculture applications. It can survey drains across many metres or even kilometres, level a house site from corner to corner, or set a wall or spillway at a predetermined level.

### Materials

- 20 metres (65 feet) or so of clear plastic hose, approximately 10 mm (1/2 inch) diameter
- 2 corks or stoppers that fit into the ends of the tube, each with a breather hole
- 2 small cork balls that freely fit inside the tube below the corks (optional)
- 2 stakes (2 metres or 6.5 feet), marked with centimetres or inches; dressmakers' tapes glued or screwed on serve well
- 4 clamps or tape to connect the hose to the stakes

### Setup

1. Uncork one end and fill the entire hose with water
2. Ensure all air bubbles are completely removed (only certain with a fully clear hose)
3. Place both stakes together, tops level, on a piece of level concrete or wood
4. Keeping stakes vertical, mark the water level in the clear tubes by adjusting the measuring strips until water level (or both cork floats) reads precisely the same measurement on both stakes
5. Recork the tubes

### Field Use

One person walks past the other alternately, driving a stake every 6 or 15 metres (20 or 50 feet) to guide diggers or machine operators. The rise or fall of drains is set by allowing one cork ball to be an inch or so lower than the other at the next peg, moving the pole up or downhill to achieve this. Stakes must be kept vertical when measuring.

### Accuracy

A fall of 1 cm in 5 m equals 1:500, which is ample for most [[diversion-drains-and-spreader-banks]] carrying run-off to dams. Dead level lines can also be run across landscape, and dam walls checked for level. A 12-kilometre drain flowing gently at 1:2000 in sand was reportedly laid in not by a skilled surveyor but by two women hired for a week and trained in 30 minutes on a hose level.

## The Plane Table

The plane table is a sturdy tripod that holds a U-shaped piece of plastic tube or glass, corked and de-bubbled as for the hose level, but capable of being swivelled to sight in all directions. This makes it useful for across-valley measurements where the hose level cannot reach.

### Construction

Lacking tubing, a simple alternative uses a bowl of water on which floats a piece of painted wood with two nails of equal length as sights. Although the bowl version may require tapping one nail in a bit for adjustment, the tube version is self-levelling.

### Checking Accuracy

1. Set up two marked stakes with visible white masking tape a little uphill from the standing point
2. Drive the tops to level using the table
3. From position B, check that the level shows new level marks at exactly the same distance below the stake tops

### Applications

- Setting siphons across valleys
- Determining where a dam outlet will send water by pipe and gravity flow
- Checking whether a distant hill is higher than a shower head at home
- Any across-valley levelling where direct measurement is impossible

## The "A" Frame Level

The A-frame is one of the simplest and oldest levelling tools, used for centuries to lay out contours and drains at set slopes or flow rates. It requires no water and can be constructed on-site from basic materials.

### Construction

1. Two wooden or metal legs, nailed or welded firmly together at the apex
2. A cross-piece fixed about two-thirds down the length of the legs (welded, nailed, and glued)
3. A plumb bob or weight hanging from a cord at the centre of the apex
4. A mark made on the cross-piece where the cord touches
5. The frame is reversed, and another mark made where the cord touches the cross-piece
6. The midpoint between the two marks is the true level point — where the cord hangs when the feet are level

### Dimensions

About 2 metres wide. The calibration ensures accuracy regardless of minor construction imperfections.

### Field Use

A person swivels the frame about one leg and sets the second leg where the cord hangs vertical (at the marked midpoint). A peg is put in temporarily, then the frame pivots about the new peg to find the next point. Many metres of drain have been set using A-frames, often made on the spot from lumber, cord, and a weight.

## The Long Inclinometer

[[ralph-long]] of Australia devised a tractor-mounted level (bubble level) that can be fitted to any vehicle on farms. Called the Long Inclinometer, it has been successfully used to cut drains using the tractor itself as a check on levels.

## Key Principles

- **Levelling need only be done once if done right** — accuracy at the planning stage saves enormous cost in rework
- Simple tools are sufficient for most farm and residential earthworks
- When in real doubt, purchase a dumpy level or theodolite and read the instructions
- Professional surveyors (whose real job is mapping) can be hired for complex or precision work, but most earthworks do not require their services
- The water principle is the foundation of all levelling — if in doubt, use water itself to verify
