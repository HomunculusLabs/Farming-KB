## Overview

Microcolonial fungi (MCF) are a specialized group of melanized fungi that colonize rock surfaces and subsurfaces in some of the harshest terrestrial environments on Earth. These organisms form distinctive spherical or hemispherical clusters on and within rock substrates, playing a significant role in biological weathering and mineral cycling. Their ability to survive extreme desiccation, UV radiation, and temperature fluctuations makes them key colonizers of bare rock in arid and semi-arid regions.

Research into MCF has expanded considerably in recent decades, driven by interest in astrobiology, geomicrobiology, and the fundamental processes of soil formation. As discussed by Gadd et al. in Chapter 5, the capacity of fungi to interact with and transform mineral substrates places them among the most important biological agents of geological change. The diverse strategies fungi employ — from acid production to physical penetration — enable them to colonize and weather virtually every rock type.

## Morphology and Growth Form

Microcolonial fungi are characterized by their compact, globular growth habit. Unlike the spreading filamentous mycelia typical of most fungi, MCF produce densely packed clusters of cells that appear as dark, melanized microcolonies on rock surfaces. These colonies typically range from tens to hundreds of micrometers in diameter and may be visible to the naked eye as tiny black specks on rock faces. The colony morphology is highly conserved across diverse phylogenetic lineages, suggesting convergent evolution driven by the shared selective pressures of the rock surface niche.

### Moniliform Hyphae

A distinctive feature of many microcolonial fungi is the presence of **moniliform hyphae** — hyphal structures that resemble a string of beads. In moniliform growth, the hyphae develop a series of swollen, roughly spherical cells connected by narrow constrictions. This morphology is thought to be an adaptation to desiccating conditions, as each swollen cell can act as an independent survival unit with its own reserve of cytoplasmic resources. When conditions become favorable, these cells can resume growth and establish new colonies.

The moniliform habit has been documented in genera such as *Coniosporium*, *Sarcinomyces*, and *Trimmatostroma*, among others. The irregular swelling pattern provides both structural resilience against mechanical stress and a compartmentalized architecture that limits damage propagation when individual cells are compromised by environmental extremes.

### Melanin Pigmentation

The melanin pigmentation of MCF provides critical protection against intense ultraviolet radiation, a major stressor in the exposed rock habitats these fungi inhabit. The pigment also contributes to desiccation tolerance by reducing cell wall permeability and scavenging [[gadd-fungal-bioweathering-rock-mineral-interactions]] through metal ion binding and redox chemistry. Some melanized rock fungi can survive years of desiccation and remain viable, reactivating metabolism within hours of rehydration. This remarkable resilience makes MCF among the most stress-tolerant eukaryotic organisms known.

## Colonization Strategies

Microcolonial fungi employ different strategies depending on their position relative to the rock substrate, reflecting a gradient of exposure and protection:

### Epiliths

Epilithic fungi colonize the exposed surface of rocks, growing directly on the rock face and remaining fully exposed to atmospheric conditions. They are subject to the greatest extremes of temperature and UV exposure but benefit from relatively easy access to airborne nutrients and moisture from dew or occasional precipitation.

Epilithic communities are often the first to establish on freshly exposed rock surfaces, including recently deglaciated terrain, volcanic lava flows, and anthropogenic stone structures. Their position at the rock–atmosphere interface makes them particularly effective at intercepting nutrient inputs from the air and from runoff.

### Endoliths

Endolithic fungi penetrate into the porous fabric of the rock itself, growing within cracks, crevices, and pore spaces below the surface. This niche offers partial protection from UV radiation and reduced evaporative water loss compared to fully exposed surfaces.

The endolithic zone may extend several millimeters into the rock, following natural fractures and grain boundaries. Colonization often begins at pre-existing microfractures that provide entry points for [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]. The vesicular texture of volcanic rocks provides abundant pore spaces ideal for cryptoendolithic growth. Carbonate rocks such as limestone and marble also support diverse fungal communities that exploit the calcium content, often producing extensive oxalate encrustations as metabolic byproducts.

## Preferred Environments

Desert and semi-arid environments represent the primary habitats where microcolonial fungi reach their greatest ecological prominence. Well-studied regions include the Negev Desert, the Antarctic Dry Valleys, the Colorado Plateau, the Atacama Desert, and the Sahara. In these water-limited environments, rock surfaces and interiors provide critical microhabitats where trace moisture can persist long enough to support intermittent [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] of colonized rocks reveals distinctive dissolution features: shallow circular pits at sites of hyphal attachment, groove-like channels following hyphal paths along mineral grain boundaries, and generalized surface roughening from diffuse acid attack.

These features are direct evidence of biologically mediated mineral dissolution and represent the first stages of soil formation from bare rock. The combination of physical wedging and chemical dissolution creates a positive feedback loop: as weathering progresses, more pore space becomes available for further colonization, and increased surface area accelerates subsequent chemical attack.

### Biogenic Oxalate Crusts

One of the most visible products of fungal-rock interaction is the formation of **biogenic oxalate crusts**. Oxalic acid secreted by the fungi reacts with calcium and other metal cations present in the rock matrix to produce insoluble metal oxalate minerals. [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-and-carbonate-precipitation]], occurring as the monohydrate whewellite and the dihydrate weddellite, is the most common product. Oxalates of magnesium, iron, copper, zinc, and other metals have also been documented.

These oxalate crusts accumulate as distinctive white, grey, or yellowish surface coatings on colonized rocks, sometimes reaching several hundred micrometers in thickness. They represent a significant carbon sink and play an important role in the long-term biogeochemical cycling of carbon and metals. The oxalate minerals can persist long after the fungal colony has died, serving as a geological record of past biological activity. Fossilized oxalate encrustations have been identified on ancient rocks and stonework.

Biogenic oxalate formation also causes significant biodeterioration of cultural heritage materials, including stone buildings, monuments, and artworks. Understanding oxalate crust formation mechanisms is therefore important for conservation science as well as geomicrobiology.

### Alkaline Rock Susceptibility

Not all rocks are equally susceptible to fungal weathering. **Alkaline rocks** — including limestone, marble, and other calcareous substrates — tend to be more readily colonized and weathered by microcolonial fungi compared to acidic or siliceous rocks. The higher pH and calcium content of alkaline rocks may create more favorable conditions for fungal growth and provide a direct substrate for oxalate formation.

The relative solubility of calcium carbonate in weak organic acids makes these rocks particularly vulnerable to oxalic and other fungal acids, accelerating pitting and etching. Silicate rocks, while generally more resistant, are still subject to fungal weathering through acid-promoted hydrolysis of Si-O bonds and through siderophore production that destabilizes mineral crystal lattices. Feldspar minerals in granite, for example, can be significantly altered by prolonged fungal colonization, with potassium, sodium, and calcium being selectively leached.
