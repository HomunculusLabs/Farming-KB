## Underground and Earth-Sheltered Housing

Underground and earth-sheltered housing represents one of the most energy-efficient and climate-resilient building strategies available, particularly suited to desert and [[fungal-diversity-arid-environments]]. Drawing on centuries of traditional practice across multiple continents, these structures leverage the [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]] of earth to maintain stable, comfortable interior temperatures with minimal energy input.

## Principles

Earth-sheltered housing works by surrounding living spaces with the insulating and thermal-buffering mass of soil and rock. The ground acts as a vast thermal battery — absorbing heat during the day and releasing it at night — resulting in interior temperatures that remain remarkably constant regardless of extreme exterior fluctuations. In central Australian deserts, for example, underground dwellings experience temperature fluctuation of only about 5°C, maintaining a fairly constant ~25°C year-round.

Key advantages include:

- **Thermal stability** — minimal heating or cooling required
- **Silence** — earth walls and ceilings absorb sound completely
- **Low maintenance** — sealed stone surfaces require little upkeep
- **Dust-free interiors** — when properly sealed with appropriate compounds
- **Cost efficiency** — as of 1985, underground homes cost roughly 50% of equivalent surface dwellings, even when fully sealed and tiled
- **Flood safety** — elevated earth-covered structures are protected from flooding

## Construction Methods: The Coober Pedy Model

Coober Pedy, South Australia, provides a well-documented modern example of underground housing construction using machinery-scale techniques:

1. **Site preparation** — A bulldozer cuts a 3–6 metre cliff face into suitable rock or sediment
2. **Drilling** — Large-bore drills (1–1.5 m diameter, known as "Caldweld" drills) cut corridors and rooms into the rock face
3. **Spoil removal** — Large blowers and vacuum pumps extract spoil during the drilling process
4. **Hand finishing** — Rooms are hand-trimmed with axes to achieve desired dimensions and shapes
5. **Services installation** — Electrical conduit and plumbing are laid before floor pouring
6. **Floor sealing** — Floors are sealed with poured concrete
7. **Wall treatment** — Bondcrete® or polyvinylacetate sealer is sprayed onto stone walls to prevent dust and stabilise surfaces

The result is a clean, stable living space carved directly into the earth with modern amenities.

## Ventilation and Health Considerations

### Radon Gas

A critical concern in underground construction is radon gas, which accumulates in volcanic sediments and rhyolite formations. Radon is a radioactive, colourless, odourless gas that poses serious long-term health risks. Mitigation strategies include:

- **[[mollison-designers-solar-chimneys-and-passive-cooling-in-desert-housing]]** — provide positive ventilation by drawing air upward through thermal convection
- **Continuous airflow systems** — ensure radon cannot accumulate to dangerous levels
- **[[coleman-greenhouse-orientation-site-selection-wind-protection-coleman]]** — avoid known radon-bearing geological formations where possible

### Natural Ventilation Design

Traditional earth-sheltered designs incorporate clever passive ventilation:

- **Hot caves** — feature an overhanging entry that traps hot up-slope air, making them suitable for drying fruits and providing livestock winter storage
- **Cold caves** — have their entry positioned in a dip in the hillside so that cold night air pools downsloping into the cave; a sill diverts water away from the entrance
- **Courtyard drainage** — cliff sites are selected so that courtyard drainage can exit via the cave opening, providing natural airflow

## Lighting Underground Spaces

Deep underground complexes require artificial or redirected natural lighting:

- **Fresnel lenses** — concentrate and direct sunlight into interior spaces
- **Mirrors** — reflect and channel light around corners into deeper rooms
- **Light guides** — fibre-optic or reflective tube systems pipe daylight from the surface into underground chambers

These technologies make it possible to illuminate even deep complexes without relying solely on electric lighting during daytime hours.

## Traditional and Regional Examples

Earth-sheltered and underground housing has evolved independently across many cultures and climates:

### North African Courtyard Pits

- Large pits approximately 20 × 20 metres are excavated in the ground
- Individual cave rooms are cut into the walls of the pit
- The open courtyard at the base provides a cool, protected outdoor living space
- The pit design shields inhabitants from wind, sun, and dust

### Canary Islands

- Only the building facade is visible from the surface
- The remainder of the structure extends underground
- Facades are shaded by grape trellises, adding both food production and cooling shade

### Pueblo Housing (Great Basin, North America)

- Compact structures sheltered in notches at the bases of cliffs
- Constructed from mud brick
- Design takes advantage of natural rock overhangs for additional protection

### Surface Housing with Earth Sheltering

Not all earth-sheltered housing is fully underground. Surface variants include:

- **Earth-banked structures** — Concrete walls with earth banks built up to the eaves, duplicating cave thermal conditions while maintaining surface access
- **Hill-stepped houses** — Hillsides are compacted by bulldozer and houses are built with mud walls extending from half-caves carved into the slope
- **"Turkey"s nest" dam houses** — An above-ground dam structure that, when roofed, becomes a cool, flood-safe dwelling

## Storage Applications

Underground and earth-sheltered spaces are exceptionally well-suited for storage due to their stable [[greg-green-temperature-and-humidity-control]]. Appropriate uses include:

- [[growing-root-vegetables-guide]] and other foodstuffs
- Books, films, and archives
- Machine parts and tools
- Dried fruits
- Livestock winter shelter

## Global Distribution

Examples of underground and earth-sheltered housing can be found across a wide range of countries and climates, demonstrating the universality of the approach:

- **China** — yaodong (cave dwellings) carved into [[loess]] cliffs
- **Iran** — kara-kal (underground settlements) in desert regions
- **Turkey** — rock-cut dwellings in Cappadocia
- **Australia** — Coober Pedy opal-mining town
- **North Africa** — courtyard pit housing
- **Canary Islands** — facade-only surface structures with underground interiors
