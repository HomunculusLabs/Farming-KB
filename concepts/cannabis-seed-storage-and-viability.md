# Cannabis Seed Storage and Viability

Proper seed storage is essential for preserving the genetic potential of cannabis genetics over time. Whether preserving seeds from a prized strain, maintaining a breeding program, or simply storing purchased seeds for future grows, understanding the factors that affect seed viability allows growers to maintain high germination rates for months or years.

[[cannabis-seed-banks-and-genetics]]

## Factors Affecting Seed Viability

Seed viability is determined by four primary environmental factors: temperature, humidity, light exposure, and oxygen levels. Controlling these factors allows seeds to remain viable for extended periods, while neglecting them causes rapid deterioration.

Temperature is the most critical factor. Seeds stored at room temperature (70 degrees Fahrenheit, 21 degrees Celsius) begin losing viability within months. Refrigeration (35-40 degrees Fahrenheit, 2-4 degrees Celsius) significantly extends storage life. Freezing (0 degrees Fahrenheit, -18 degrees Celsius or below) provides the longest-term storage, with properly prepared seeds potentially remaining viable for years.

Humidity must be kept low for seed preservation. Seeds exposed to high humidity absorb moisture, which can trigger premature germination or promote fungal growth. The ideal relative humidity for seed storage is below 30%. Seeds stored in humid conditions may swell, crack, or develop mold within weeks.

Light exposure accelerates seed degradation. Seeds should always be stored in complete darkness. Ultraviolet light from sunlight or artificial sources damages seed cells and reduces viability.Opaque containers are essential for long-term storage.

Oxygen exposure contributes to seed aging through oxidative processes. While complete oxygen exclusion is difficult to achieve without specialized equipment, storing seeds in airtight containers reduces oxygen exposure and extends viability. Some commercial seed banks flush storage containers with inert gases such as nitrogen or argon to displace oxygen.

## Short-Term Storage (Up to 6 Months)

For seeds that will be used within six months, a simple approach is sufficient. Seeds should be placed in an airtight container such as a film canister, small glass jar, or dedicated seed storage vial. Adding a small desiccant packet (silica gel) to the container helps maintain low humidity.

The sealed container should be stored in a cool, dark place such as a refrigerator crisper drawer, a basement, or a dark cupboard. Avoid storing seeds near heat sources, in direct sunlight, or in areas with temperature fluctuations.

Seeds stored this way typically maintain germination rates above 80% for up to six months, though some deterioration begins even under good conditions.

## Long-Term Storage (6 Months to 5+ Years)

For long-term preservation, more careful preparation is needed. Seeds should be dried to the appropriate moisture content before freezing. If seeds are placed in a freezer while still moist, ice crystal formation can damage cellular structures and kill the seed.

To prepare seeds for freezing, place them in a container with a desiccant packet for several days at room temperature. This allows the seeds to gradually dry to the appropriate moisture level. Then seal the container and move it to the freezer.

Film canisters are a traditional and effective storage container for cannabis seeds. They are opaque, airtight, and small enough to store conveniently. Label the container with the strain name, date, and any other relevant information.

Seeds stored in a freezer at -18 degrees Celsius can remain viable for several years. However, the odds of maintaining viability decrease over time, and germination rates of frozen seeds are generally lower than those of fresh seeds even under ideal conditions. Most growers report reliable germination from frozen seeds stored for up to two years, with decreasing success rates beyond that point.

## Assessing Seed Viability

Several indicators help predict whether stored seeds will germinate successfully.

Healthy cannabis seeds are dark in color, typically brown, gray, or black. Light-colored, pale, or whitish seeds are often immature and have lower germination rates. However, some strains naturally produce lighter-colored seeds, so color alone is not definitive.

Viable seeds have a hard, durable outer shell. Seeds that crush easily between the fingers or appear shriveled and cracked are less likely to germinate. Gently squeezing a seed between thumb and forefinger should reveal firm resistance from a viable seed.

The float test is a common but imperfect method for assessing viability. Seeds are placed in a glass of water; those that sink are generally considered viable while those that float are considered non-viable. However, some viable seeds may float due to trapped air, and some non-viable seeds may sink. The test provides a rough indication but should not be the sole criterion for discarding seeds.

The only definitive test of viability is attempted germination. If stored seeds consistently fail to germinate using reliable germination methods, they have likely lost viability.

## Reviving Stored Seeds

Seeds that have been stored in a refrigerator should be allowed to come to room temperature before the container is opened. Opening a cold container in warm, humid room air causes condensation inside the container, which can introduce moisture to the seeds.

Seeds stored in a freezer require a more gradual warming process. Move the sealed container from the freezer to the refrigerator for 24 hours, then to room temperature for another 24 hours before opening. This gradual temperature change prevents condensation and thermal shock to the seeds.

Once the seeds have reached room temperature and the container has been opened, proceed with the preferred germination method. Seeds that have been stored for extended periods may benefit from a pre-soak in water for 12-24 hours to rehydrate them before planting.

Older seeds may have thicker, harder shells that resist water penetration. Some growers gently scarify the shell with fine sandpaper or nick it with a nail file before germination to help water penetrate. This technique requires care to avoid damaging the embryo inside.

## Seed Aging and Genetic Drift

Over time, even properly stored seeds undergo gradual genetic degradation. This is not genetic drift in the evolutionary sense but rather a deterioration of cellular integrity that can affect the vigor and uniformity of the resulting plants.

Seeds from the same batch may show varying germination rates after extended storage. Some seeds will germinate normally while others from the same batch may fail, germinate weakly, or produce less vigorous seedlings. This variability increases with storage duration.

For breeding programs and serious genetic preservation, regular germination testing of stored seeds is recommended. Growing out stored seeds periodically allows the breeder to assess viability and, if needed, produce fresh seeds from the resulting plants to maintain the genetic line.

## Handling and Labeling

Seeds should be handled with clean, dry hands or tweezers to avoid introducing moisture or contaminants. The natural oils from fingerprints can affect seed viability over time.

Proper labeling is essential for any collection of stored seeds. Each container should be labeled with the strain name, breeder or source, date of acquisition, and date of storage. For breeding programs, additional information about the cross, generation, and expected characteristics should be recorded.

A seed inventory system, whether a simple spreadsheet or a dedicated database, helps track what genetics are available, where they are stored, and how long they have been in storage. This is particularly important for large collections or breeding programs with many different lines.
