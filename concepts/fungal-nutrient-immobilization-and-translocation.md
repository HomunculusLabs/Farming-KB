# Fungal Nutrient Immobilization and Translocation

Fungal nutrient immobilization and translocation are fundamental processes by which fungi regulate the flow of energy and elements through ecosystems. As decomposers and symbionts, fungi sequester mineral nutrients into their biomass (immobilization) and move them across space within their mycelial networks (translocation). These processes create temporal and spatial heterogeneity in nutrient availability, with profound consequences for plant nutrition, soil fertility, and ecosystem productivity.

## The Immobilization Process

During the course of decomposition, mineral nutrients are sequestered by decomposer organisms through incorporation into their biomass. The residence time of these elements in microbial tissue is equivalent to the turnover time or lifespan of that organism. While immobilized, nutrients are not present in soluble form in the soil solution but are bound within fungal structures, making them temporarily unavailable to plants and other organisms.

Fungi differ from bacteria in important ways regarding immobilization. Fungi are larger organisms with lower rates of turnover, particularly among the long-lived Basidiomycotina. While bacterial turnover times range from hours to days, some higher fungi may have hyphal turnover times of weeks or years. This means that fungi can serve as important long-term accumulators of materials, thereby effecting temporal changes in the availability of nutrients in the environment.

The fungal mycelium is a sessile system of indeterminate growth. As mycelia grow, there is continuous incorporation of new carbon and mineral nutrients into the biomass of the advancing hyphal front and into more proximal biomass by translocation and immobilization. While the fungal mycelium is alive and active, much of this material remains bound to structural components or sequestered in the cytoplasm.

## Nutrient Concentration in Fungal Tissue

Fungal biomass typically contains significantly higher concentrations of nutrients than the substrates on which it grows. Stark (1972) demonstrated that hyphae had 193 to 272 percent greater nitrogen content and 104 to 223 percent greater phosphorus content than the pine needle litter on which they were found, indicating substantial immobilization of these elements into fungal biomass.

Clinton and colleagues (1999) measured nutrient concentrations in fungal fruit bodies and underlying forest floor substrates in a Nothofagus forest. All elements other than calcium were more concentrated in fungal tissue than in forest floor material. Nitrogen in forest floor mushrooms was 35 mg per kilogram compared to 8.7 mg per kilogram in the forest floor substrate. Phosphorus was 4 mg per kilogram in mushrooms versus 1.0 mg per kilogram in the substrate.

## The C:Nutrient Ratio and Immobilization Dynamics

The carbon-to-nutrient ratio of the resource being decomposed strongly influences the direction and magnitude of nutrient immobilization. Where the carbon-to-nutrient ratio of a resource is very high, as in wood, the model of Swift and colleagues (1979) proposes initial immobilization and the import of free nutrients into organic form (fungal thallus) during the initial stages of decomposition.

This immobilization phase continues until the fungal resource carbon-to-nutrient content is equivalent to that of the fungus itself. Only after the fungal biomass has reached its stoichiometric equilibrium does net mineralization begin, releasing excess nutrients back into the soil solution. This means that during the early stages of wood decomposition, fungi can compete with plants for available soil nutrients, potentially reducing plant growth in the vicinity of decomposing woody debris.

## Fungal Biomass as a Nutrient Pool

Fogel and Hunt (1983) demonstrated the quantitative importance of fungal biomass as a nutrient pool in a temperate Douglas fir forest ecosystem. Their allocation analysis showed that fungi constituted approximately 2 percent of the total standing stock of organic matter, with mycorrhizae accounting for 6 percent of total tree standing stock. For all nutrients except calcium, roots and mycorrhizae contained greater stocks than the forest floor fungal community.

The return of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium by mycorrhizae to soil was about 83 to 87 percent of the total tree return, and 25 to 51 percent of the calcium and magnesium return. These figures underscore the central role of fungal biomass as both a sink and a source of nutrients in forest ecosystems.

## Translocation Within Mycelial Networks

The movement of resources between areas of high availability (sources) and areas of low availability or sites of demand (sinks) within the fungal mycelium is known as translocation. This capacity, described by Jennings (1976, 1982) and reviewed by Cairney (1992) and Boddy (1999), is a defining attribute of fungal mycelia.

Resource translocation can occur over distances ranging from millimeters to tens of meters, depending on the fungal species and the degree of differentiation of its mycelial structures. Simple mycelia may translocate materials over short distances through cytoplasmic streaming and diffusion. Differentiated structures such as rhizomorphs and cords enable long-distance bulk flow of water and dissolved nutrients.

This ability to move resources internally has several important consequences. It allows fungi to maintain growth fronts in nutrient-poor areas by subsidizing them with resources captured elsewhere. It enables the reallocation of resources from senescing to growing parts of the mycelium. And it permits the accumulation of resources at sites of fruiting body formation, which act as strong sinks for internally translocated carbon and nutrients.

## Retranslocation from Dying Biomass

Upon the death of more proximal parts of the mycelium, materials incorporated into biomass may either be retranslocated from dying to living components or released into the environment through decomposition and mineralization processes. The duration of incorporation of material into biomass, the immobilization phase, is therefore not only determined by the lifespan of individual hyphae but also by the capacity of the fungus to recover resources from senescing tissue.

The ability to retranslocate resources from dying mycelial sectors is thought to be one reason why some fungi can maintain persistent mycelial networks in soil. Rather than losing all invested resources when a hyphal sector dies, the fungus can recover a significant fraction of the carbon and nutrients, reallocating them to support continued growth elsewhere.

## Ecosystem-Level Consequences

Fungal immobilization and translocation have several ecosystem-level consequences. First, they create spatial heterogeneity in nutrient availability, with zones of depletion around actively growing hyphae and zones of enrichment around decomposing hyphal biomass and fruiting bodies. Second, they introduce temporal dynamics into nutrient cycling, with nutrients being temporarily locked in fungal biomass and released on timescales of weeks to years.

Third, fungal translocation connects otherwise isolated nutrient pools, linking organic matter decomposition in one location with plant nutrient demand in another. This is particularly important in mycorrhizal systems, where the fungal partner can absorb nutrients from organic matter some distance from the root and transport them back to the host plant. Fourth, the temporary immobilization of nutrients in fungal biomass can buffer ecosystems against nutrient loss, particularly during periods of high leaching potential.

## Interaction with Decomposition Kinetics

The process of decomposition is governed by the production of enzymes, which are in turn regulated by the availability of nitrogen and phosphorus. Where nutrient elements are less available, fungi expend greater amounts of energy to produce enzymes to sequester nutrients from organic sources. This feedback between nutrient availability, enzyme production, and immobilization creates complex dynamics that influence decomposition rates.

Sinsabaugh and colleagues (1993) developed models that contain both fungal microbial and soil nutrient controls over the expression of decomposition enzymes. Their work on standardized wood substrates showed that the rate of immobilization of total nitrogen into decomposing wood ranged from 43 to 139 micrograms per gram at the time when 80 percent mass loss was achieved, demonstrating the magnitude of fungal nutrient demands during decomposition.

## Phosphorus Translocation

Phosphorus translocation is particularly important in cord-forming fungi that decompose wood, a substrate that is typically phosphorus-deficient. Phosphorus absorbed from soil or mineral particles can be transported through mycelial cords to sites of active wood decomposition, where it supports the production of phosphatase enzymes and other phosphorus-requiring metabolic processes. This phosphorus mining capability allows fungi to decompose wood that would otherwise be limited by phosphorus availability.

Wells and Boddy (1995) demonstrated that translocation of phosphorus in mycelial cords is temperature-dependent, with greater rates of movement at higher temperatures. Fungi can serve as vectors for phosphorus movement between spatially separated nutrient pools, such as from mineral soil to decomposing wood, or from mycorrhizal networks to host plants.

## Carbon Translocation

Carbon translocation in fungi occurs primarily in the form of soluble carbohydrates (trehalose, mannitol, glucose) and, to a lesser extent, as amino acids. In cord-forming fungi, carbon translocation from established resource bases supports the exploration and colonization of new resources. When multiple resources are connected by a mycelial network, carbon can be redistributed among them, with the direction and magnitude of flow depending on the relative resource quality and the metabolic demands of different parts of the network.

In mycorrhizal networks, carbon translocation from host plants to fungal symbionts represents a major belowground carbon flux. Common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) can interconnect multiple plants, allowing carbon to move from dominant to subordinate individuals, from shaded to illuminated plants, or from adult to seedling plants.

## Translocation and Competition

The ability to translocate nutrients gives fungi a significant competitive advantage in patchy environments. Fungi with extensive, well-connected mycelial networks can import nutrients from distant resource bases to support local competition, effectively subsidizing their competitive efforts in nutrient-poor patches. This resource subsidy mechanism means that the outcome of competitive interactions at a local scale may be determined by the resource status of the entire mycelial network, not just the local nutrient supply.

## Practical Implications

In practical forestry, competition for essential nutrients between fungi decomposing woody residues and growth by second-rotation tree crops is a significant concern. To alleviate this competition, burning protocols have been established to remove woody debris and the nutrients it contains. A greater understanding of fungal immobilization dynamics could lead to more rational use of post-harvest residues to provide sustainable forestry without nutrient loss from burning and without the need for exogenous fertilizers.

The decomposition of fruit body material is faster than that of surrounding forest floor material, providing localized spots of high rates of mineral nutrient release. The duration of immobilization of nutrients into fungal hyphae before translocation to the fruit body is an important aspect of the control fungi exert on the rates and timing of nutrient release in ecosystems.
