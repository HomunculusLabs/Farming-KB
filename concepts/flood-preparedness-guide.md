# Flood Preparedness Guide

Floods are the most common and costly natural disaster in the United States. Homesteads near rivers, streams, low-lying areas, or in regions with intense rainfall are at elevated risk. Even areas far from waterways can experience flash flooding, storm surge, or localized flooding from overwhelmed drainage. This guide covers flood risk assessment, protection measures, evacuation, and recovery.

## Flood Risk Assessment

Understanding your flood risk is the first step in preparation.

### Risk Factors
- **Flood zone classification:** Check FEMA flood maps (floodsmart.gov) for your property zone. Zones A and V are high risk; Zone X is moderate risk; Zones B and C are lower risk.
- **Proximity to water:** Rivers, streams, creeks, and drainage ditches within 1 mile
- **Topography:** Low-lying areas, natural drainage paths, valleys, and floodplains
- **Upstream development:** New construction upstream increases runoff volume
- **Soil type:** Clay soils drain slowly; sandy soils drain faster but may erode
- **Historical flooding:** Has the property or area flooded before? If so, it will likely flood again
- **Climate trends:** Increasing extreme rainfall events in many regions
- **Dam or levee proximity:** Failure of upstream dams or levees creates catastrophic flash flooding

### Key Terms
- **Flood watch:** Conditions are favorable for flooding — prepare
- **Flood warning:** Flooding is occurring or imminent — take action
- **Flash flood warning:** Sudden, violent flooding — move to higher ground immediately
- **100-year flood:** A flood with a 1% chance of occurring in any given year (not once per century — can happen multiple times in one year)

## Flood Protection Measures

### Property-Level Protection

**Elevation:**
- The most effective long-term flood protection
- Raise structures above the base flood elevation (BFE) for your area
- Elevate utilities (electrical panel, HVAC, water heater) above flood level
- Elevate fuel tanks and propane storage

**Flood barriers:**
- Sandbags: Place sandbags in a pyramid pattern, 3 bags high minimum, staggered like bricks. Fill sandbags 1/2 to 2/3 full. Stack against doors, garage openings, and low wall sections. One sandbag protects approximately 1 square foot of wall at 1 foot high.
- Flood barriers: Commercial flood barriers (FloodSax, AquaDam) expand when wet and provide faster deployment than sandbags
- Water-inflated cofferdams: For protecting larger areas around buildings
- Portable flood gates: Metal or plastic panels that mount to pre-installed tracks on doorways

**Drainage management:**
- Keep ditches, culverts, and drainage swales clear of debris
- Grade your property to direct water away from structures
- Install French drains around foundations
- Ensure gutters and downspouts direct water well away from the foundation (minimum 10 feet)
- Consider a sump pump with battery backup in basements or crawlspaces

**Waterproofing:**
- Apply waterproof sealant to foundation walls
- Install backflow prevention valves on sewer lines to prevent floodwater from backing up into the house
- Use water-resistant building materials (concrete, tile, closed-cell foam insulation) in flood-prone areas
- Raise electrical outlets and switches above projected flood levels

### Sandbagging Guide

**Materials needed:**
- Sand or soil (1 cubic yard fills approximately 100 sandbags)
- Empty sandbags (polypropylene, 14x26 inches)
- Shovels
- Gloves

**Filling:**
- Fold the empty bag open
- Fill 1/2 to 2/3 full — overfilled bags don't shape well
- Do not tie the bag — fold the open end under the filled portion

**Stacking:**
- Place first layer lengthwise, parallel to the water flow
- Place second layer crosswise on top of first
- Place third layer lengthwise on top
- Each layer offsets the joints below (like running bond in brickwork)
- Tamp each layer firmly
- Slope the wall toward the water (pyramid shape)
- Minimum height: 3 layers (about 2 feet)
- Place plastic sheeting between the water and the sandbag wall for additional protection

## Evacuation Planning

### Know Your Routes
- Identify at least two evacuation routes to higher ground
- Routes should go perpendicular to the floodplain — not along rivers or streams
- Know the location of designated shelters
- Account for flooded roads — never drive through flooded roadways

### Critical Safety Rules
- **Turn around, don't drown:** Never walk, swim, or drive through floodwater. Six inches of moving water can knock you down. One foot of water can float a vehicle. Two feet of water can carry away most vehicles.
- Never drive around barricades
- Never camp or park along streams or washes during rain events
- If trapped in a building by rising water, go to the highest floor — not the attic unless you have roof access
- If caught outdoors, move to higher ground immediately

### Go Bag for Flooding

In addition to standard emergency supplies, include:
- Water purification supplies (filter, tablets, bleach)
- Rubber boots and waterproof waders
- Heavy-duty plastic sheeting and duct tape
- Life jackets (PFDs) for every family member
- Rope and rescue line
- Waterproof bags for electronics and documents
- Mosquito repellent (standing floodwater breeds mosquitoes rapidly)
- Extra food that does not require cooking

## Protecting Livestock

- Move livestock to higher ground before floodwaters rise — do not wait until roads are flooded
- Pre-identify high-ground holding areas on your property
- Ensure livestock trailers are accessible and road-ready
- Do not tie animals — they need freedom to seek higher ground
- Provide access to elevated feed and fresh water
- After flooding, check animals for injuries, foot rot, respiratory illness, and waterborne diseases
- Contaminated floodwater can carry bacteria, chemicals, and debris — do not allow animals to drink it

## Recovery After Flooding

### Safety Before Re-entry
- Wait for official clearance from authorities
- Check for structural damage — foundations, walls, floors
- Do not enter buildings with standing water if electricity is still connected
- Wear PPE: rubber boots, gloves, N95 mask, safety glasses
- Assume floodwater is contaminated with sewage, chemicals, and pathogens

### Cleanup
- Pump out standing water as soon as possible (generator-powered pump if electricity is out)
- Remove all wet materials (carpet, drywall, insulation, furniture) within 24-48 hours to prevent mold
- Disinfect all surfaces with a solution of 1 cup bleach per gallon of water
- Run fans and dehumidifiers continuously
- Document all damage with photos and video before cleanup for insurance
- Discard all food that contacted floodwater (including canned goods if the can is dented, rusted, or swollen)
- Test well water before drinking — wells are commonly contaminated during flooding. Boil or treat water until tested safe.

### Insurance
- Standard homeowner's insurance does NOT cover flooding — you need a separate flood insurance policy through FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP)
- There is typically a 30-day waiting period for new flood insurance policies to take effect
- Document all losses with receipts, photos, and serial numbers
- File claims as soon as possible — delays can complicate reimbursement

## Long-Term Flood Mitigation

- Work with local agencies on watershed management and drainage improvements
- Consider relocating structures if flooding is frequent and severe
