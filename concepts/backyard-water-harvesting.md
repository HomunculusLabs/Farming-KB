# Backyard Water Harvesting

In an ecologically designed garden, water is not an externally caused event -- it is designed in, naturally present, naturally abundant. The cheapest place to store water is in the soil. A well-designed garden doesn't require constant irrigation; it spontaneously cycles toward lush and vibrant growth, even when the gardener is absent.

## The Fivefold Path to Water Wisdom

The [[williams-pdc-ecological-design-principles]] principle states that each function should be supported by many elements. A garden that captures, holds, and recycles water through multiple complementary strategies can survive months between waterings. The five complementary techniques are:

### 1. Building Organically Rich Soil

Soil with as little as 2 percent [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] can reduce irrigation needs by 75 percent compared to poor soils with less than 1 percent organic matter. Humus acts as a sponge, swelling to hold several times its weight in water. One foot of rich, moist soil blanketing a backyard holds as much water as a 3-inch-deep lake the same size. The key is building organic matter through compost, mulch, and the techniques of [[cover-crop-multifunction-design]].

Rich soil is stingy with water: a rainstorm must first saturate the soil before a single drop trickles away as runoff. Unlike pond water, moisture held in soil doesn't evaporate easily. As Hemenway observes, three quarts of dry soil hold about one quart of water — but soil enriched with humus swells to hold several times its own weight. The practical implication is that every inch of topsoil gained is equivalent to adding a significant water reservoir to your property.

This is why the phrase "Rivers come from the soil" is so apt: rivers are fed not just by surface runoff but by humic earth slowly seeping stored water into waterways over weeks and months. Building soil is building a water reservoir.

### 2. Contouring the Landscape

Sculpting the land to catch and hold water includes several techniques. See [[permaculture-earthworks-and-water-harvesting]] for detailed guidance.

**Swales**: A shallow trench laid dead level along the land's contours, one to several feet across and about a foot deep. Earth dug from the swale is piled on the downhill side as a berm. The cross-section is roughly S-shaped. Surface water runs in, spreads along the length, and slowly percolates into the soil. This underground water seeps downhill, forming a lens of moisture held together by hydrostatic tension that aids plant growth for tens of feet downslope.

**Basins and dishes**: Circular depressions, even gentle ones invisible to the eye, collect rainwater and let it soak in. Every tree can be planted in a small basin to capture and hold water directly at its root zone.

**Fishscale swales**: Small, overlapping basins on slopes that catch and hold water like overlapping fish scales.

**Urban swales**: In a backyard setting, swales don't need to be large or industrial-looking. Small, delicate, eye-pleasing curves softened by plant silhouettes work beautifully. Even a modest 6-inch-deep swale along a property line can significantly reduce runoff and build soil moisture. See [[drought-tolerant-planting-design]] and [[forest-garden-seven-layers]] model is the ultimate expression of dense planting for water conservation.

### 5. Mulching Deeply

A thick layer of mulch slows evaporation, cools soil, adds fertility, boosts soil life, and smothers weeds. Deep mulching and rich soil work together: mulch feeds the soil organisms that create the humus that holds the water. [[permaculture-dynamic-accumulator-plants]] and permaculture-water-strategies for how these techniques interlock at larger scales.

## Greywater Systems

Household greywater from sinks, showers, and laundry can be directed into mulch basins to irrigate landscape plants. A simple drain-to-mulch-basin system uses no pumps or filters — greywater flows from the house to a mulch-filled basin where plants use the water and nutrients. This turns a waste product into a resource. See [[graywater-systems]] and graywater-systems|graywater for design details.

## Backyard Wetlands

A backyard wetland created at the lowest point of the property serves dual purposes: purifying water that passes through it and providing habitat for wildlife. Even a small [[comparison-constructed-wetland-vs-backyard-pond]] can process greywater, capture runoff, and create a biodiversity hotspot that supports frogs, dragonflies, birds, and [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]]. See [[permaculture-pond-construction]] for guidance.

## Ponds and Wetlands

Small ponds serve multiple functions: they store water, provide habitat for wildlife and beneficial insects, create a firebreak, reflect light into shaded areas, and offer a beautiful focal point. Even tiny pools or puddles support dragonflies and other insect predators. See [[query-how-to-set-up-a-backyard-pond]].

## Rainwater Catchment

Rain barrels and cisterns capture roof runoff for later use. While soil storage is cheaper and more efficient for large volumes, tanks provide water for precise delivery during dry spells and can be sized to bridge gaps between rainfall events. See [[water-storage-techniques]] for design options.
