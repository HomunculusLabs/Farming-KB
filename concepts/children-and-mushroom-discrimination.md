## Children and Mushroom Discrimination

## Overview

Children possess a natural affinity for mushrooms and, under certain conditions,
demonstrate a remarkable ability to distinguish between edible and poisonous
species. However, this instinctive discrimination is not infallible and can
lead to tragic consequences. This concept explores the evidence for children's
palate preferences, their capacity for fungal identification, and the critical
limitations of relying on instinct alone.

## The Child's Palate: An Unperverted Appetite

### Challenging the Acquired-Taste Myth

A persistent misconception holds that the taste for mushrooms must be acquired
through repeated exposure. [[julius-auboineau-palmer]] argued forcefully against
this notion, asserting that children possess "unperverted appetites" that are
naturally receptive to the flavor of properly prepared mushrooms. The problem,
in his view, lay not with the mushrooms themselves but with how cookbooks of
the era instructed their preparation.

### The Problem of Over-Seasoning

Nineteenth-century cookbooks routinely disguised mushrooms beneath heavy
seasonings — excessive spices, garlic, and hot herbs — effectively masking the
fungus's own delicate flavor. When mushrooms were sent to families entirely
unaccustomed to eating them, the results were revealing: children consistently
enjoyed them, suggesting that the supposed need to "acquire" a taste for
mushrooms was an artifact of poor culinary practice rather than any inherent
unpalatability.

## Case Studies in Children's Mushroom Preferences

### The Fairy-Ring Champignon (*Marasmius oreades*)

The fairy-ring champignon is a small, leathery toadstool that grows in rich
grassy lawns. It is cream-colored with a distinctive musk flavor and possesses
the remarkable property of being preservable indefinitely through drying — the
dried specimens expand again when soaked in water, making them a practical
storehouse food.

Palmer recorded a telling anecdote involving an 18-month-old infant who was
served mushrooms prepared from this species. The child showed "great delight"
with the dish, licking the gravy plate entirely clean — a vivid illustration of
the natural appeal mushrooms hold for very young palates.

### The Elm-Tree Mushroom (*Agaricus ulmarius*)

The elm-tree mushroom is a large white fungus that appears on elm trees in
autumn. It emits a fresh, tonic smell that Palmer likened to broiled or fried
fish, so much so that his friends nicknamed it the "tree-fish." In another
compelling anecdote, a 21-month-old child, upon being served this mushroom,
immediately identified it as "fish" — recognizing the similarity in flavor and
aroma without any prompting or prior exposure.

## Children's Capacity for Species Discrimination

### A Promising Young Scholar

Palmer described a 10-year-old girl as his "most promising scholar" in the
study of mycology. This child demonstrated an extraordinary ability to
distinguish between two visually similar but critically different genera:

- **Russulas** — Edible mushrooms that are crisp and brittle in texture,
  typically found growing in woods near trees. Those free from acridity are
  considered safe for consumption.

- **Amanitas** — Deadly poisonous mushrooms that bear a deceptively similar
  appearance to certain Russulas, sharing pure white gills and stems along
  with corresponding color shades. They are nearly tasteless yet lethally
  noxious.

### The Role of Instinct vs. Identification

What made this child's ability particularly striking was her method of
discrimination. She did not rely on the standard identifying marks that
mycologists emphasize — the volva (cup at the base), the ring (annulus on the
stem), or surface warts — but instead recognized the mushrooms by what Palmer
described as "instinct," much as any person instinctively distinguishes a pear
from an apple. Her recognition was characterized as "unerring."

This observation raises an interesting question about the nature of fungal
identification: whether some individuals possess a sensory capacity — perhaps
involving subtle differences in odor, texture, or visual impression — that
operates below the level of conscious analytical identification.

## The Fatal Limitation: The Linden Tragedy

### A Cautionary Account

Despite the encouraging anecdotes about children's discrimination abilities,
Palmer related a tragic case from Linden, New Jersey, that serves as a sobering
counterpoint. An entire family was poisoned after consuming wild-gathered
mushrooms. The children in this household had gathered mushrooms on three or
four prior occasions and were described as being fond of them — suggesting
they had developed some familiarity with fungal foraging. Nevertheless, they
were unable to detect the danger on this fatal occasion. A 4-year-old girl and
a 12-month-old boy died as a result.

### The Moral Lesson

Palmer drew a clear and urgent moral from this tragedy: while children can
indeed learn to discriminate between some mushroom species, they **cannot**
detect the fatal poison that "hides in beautiful guise." The visual similarity
between edible and deadly species — particularly the deadly Amanitas — is
sufficient to deceive even experienced and enthusiastic young foragers.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Children naturally enjoy mushrooms** when properly prepared without
   excessive seasoning, contradicting the acquired-taste hypothesis.

2. **Children can demonstrate impressive discrimination** between visually
   similar fungal species, sometimes operating on instinct rather than learned
   identification marks.

3. **Instinct is not a reliable safeguard** against poisoning. The tragic
   Linden case proves that prior successful foraging experience provides no
   guarantee of safety.

4. **Supervision and expert verification remain essential** whenever children
   interact with wild mushrooms, regardless of the child's apparent skill or
   enthusiasm.
