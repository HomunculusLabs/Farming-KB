# Dissolved Oxygen Management in Compost Tea Brewing

## The Critical Measurement
The measure of oxygen you want to use is oxygen concentration, not
percent dissolved oxygen. You need above 6 ppm, or 6 mg O2 per
liter. At sea level, at 72 degrees F, the typical oxygen
concentration is around 9 to 9.5 mg oxygen per liter. Streams with
90% oxygen saturation or above are considered healthy. Based on
that, you need around 8 ppm at 72 degrees F to achieve 90% oxygen
saturation.

## Maximum Oxygen Concentration

The concentration of oxygen in water can only be 16.1 mg O2 per
liter maximum, which occurs at sea level at freezing temperatures.
As temperature increases, oxygen concentration drops. Near 100
degrees at sea level, oxygen drops into the anaerobic range for
most aerobic organisms. As you go up in elevation, oxygen
concentration also decreases. At 2000 feet above sea level, at
room temperature, maximum oxygen will be about 8.8 mg O2 per liter.

## Fine vs. Coarse Bubbles

Fine bubbles are really hard on the [[aact-fungal-dominant-tea]] and seem to break them up
and shatter them. If you suspend fine bubbles in the water by over-
aerating, you end up with problems. Too much air and not enough
water, and the organisms will be killed because the environment
becomes too oxidizing. The bigger bubbles seem to do a better job of
extracting the organisms from the compost and keeping things as
aerated as possible. Additional testing on fine versus coarse bubble
situations is needed, but coarse bubbles are generally preferred.

## Different Oxygen Measurement Scales

Total dissolved gases: nitrogen makes up about 75% of the atmosphere,
oxygen about 22%, CO2 about 3%. As oxygen is used up by respiring
organisms, O2 drops and CO2 increases. When O2 drops below 15% to
16%, or CO2 increases to 9% to 10%, things are getting anaerobic.

Dissolved oxygen versus CO2: the two important gases sum to 100%.
Oxygen is usually in the 95% to 99.9% range. When oxygen drops to
the 70% to 75% range, conditions are getting anaerobic.

## Fish Tanks vs. Tea Brewers

Compost tea brewers are different from fish tanks. Fish do not grow
as fast as [[aact-bacterial-dominant-tea]]. When you feed bacteria well, they can use up
all the oxygen in any amount of water within minutes. The aeration
rate needs to be high enough to offset bacterial and fungal growth.
Not too much, not too little. The optimal aeration rate depends on
how much food you add, the compost you use, and the water
temperature.

## Detecting Anaerobic Conditions

Use your nose to detect bad smells, but production of these gases
may not reach a level detectable unless accumulated. Fill half a
plastic bottle with tea, seal it, and incubate overnight at ambient
temperature. Open carefully and smell. If it smells bad, you are
not aerating enough. If the tea smells acceptable, there is most
likely enough aeration and not too much food in the recipe.

## The DO Probe as a Management Tool

While many people dislike the dissolved oxygen probe, it becomes a
money saver and a stern taskmaster. It does not lie and tells you
the truth all the time. You have to calibrate it properly, but that
is like learning how fast to drive your tractor through the field
in order to apply fertilizer at the correct rate. The DO probe
prevents costly mistakes in tea production.

## Factors Affecting Oxygen Levels

Several factors influence dissolved oxygen in your brew: water
temperature (warmer water holds less oxygen), elevation (higher
elevation means less oxygen), the amount and type of food added
(more food means more microbial growth means more oxygen demand),
the quality of compost used, and the ambient air pressure. All of
these must be considered when setting up your aeration system.

## Temperature Considerations

As temperature increases, organisms grow faster and use more oxygen,
while at the same time the water holds less dissolved oxygen. This
double effect makes warm-weather brewing particularly challenging.
If water temperature approaches 100 degrees F, even at sea level,
oxygen will drop into the anaerobic range regardless of aeration.
Brewing in the morning or using shade can help manage temperature.

## Maintaining Aerobic Conditions

Aerate sufficiently as determined by the machine manufacturer. Use
a tea maker where the amounts of compost, food, and organism growth
at different temperatures and elevations have been studied and the
information on how to alter food resources or aeration rate is
provided. Measure oxygen during brewing, or use a proven system
with established protocols for your conditions.

## The Anaerobic Threshold

Below approximately 6 mg O2 per liter (6 ppm), conditions shift
toward [[ingham-anaerobic-vs-aerobic-compost-tea]]. Different organisms have different oxygen
requirements, but 6 ppm is a practical minimum for maintaining
the full diversity of beneficial organisms. Aerobic fungi are
particularly sensitive and begin to be outcompeted by anaerobic
bacteria when levels drop below this threshold. [[aact-nematode-protozoa-management-ingham]] are also oxygen-dependent and will be lost if the
brew goes anaerobic.

## Oxygen Demand During Brewing

The oxygen demand in a compost tea brew changes over time. When
food is first added, [[aact-bacterial-dominant-tea]] begin to grow rapidly, increasing
oxygen consumption. This creates the characteristic oxygen dip
early in the brew cycle. As the easily metabolized foods are
consumed, oxygen demand stabilizes. Understanding this pattern
helps brewers anticipate when oxygen levels are most likely to
drop and adjust [[aact-aeration-oxygen-management-ingham]] accordingly. The initial food addition
and its composition are the primary factors determining the
magnitude of the oxygen dip. See also [[aact-brewing-ingredients-ingham]].

## Oxygen Probes and Calibration

Dissolved oxygen probes require regular calibration to provide
accurate readings. Calibration should be done according to the
manufacturer's instructions, using a zero-oxygen solution and a
saturated-oxygen solution. Probe maintenance includes keeping the
membrane clean and replacing it on schedule. A poorly maintained
probe gives misleading readings that can lead to incorrect
management decisions. Despite maintenance requirements, the DO
probe remains the most reliable tool for real-time monitoring.
