# Super-Guild Design

A super-guild is an interconnected landscape-scale assemblage where multiple individual [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]], each based on a different type of tree, act as subunits of a larger, multi-tree community. This concept, developed in [[polyculture-design-hemenway]]'s work, represents the next level of complexity beyond individual guilds like the [[apple-centered-guild-design]] or the [[gaias-garden-three-sisters-guild]].

## Why Super-Guilds

If the only trees in a yard are fruit-bearing varieties, all pruned into open form and uniformly blooming, the landscape will be visually boring and will lack the biodiversity that is possible and necessary for a thriving [[gaias-garden-backyard-ecosystem]]. Also, any orchard acts as a beacon to summon fruit-chomping pests. A monoculture of fruit trees, no matter how diverse the understory, still presents a target-rich environment for specialized pests.

By combining [[hemenway-guild-design-roles-and-assembly]], [[gaias-garden-living-plant-systems-insectary]], and other [[gaias-garden-multipurpose-plants]], we can weave together trees of diverse uses to create a more deeply connected community. Think of the various guilds as organs in a body, combining to form a healthy and long-lived organism capable of much more elaborate behaviors than the components alone. Super-guilds alter [[gaias-garden-net-and-pan-water-harvesting]], attract new species, change the look and feel of the landscape, and restore it to health.

## The Buffer Plant Strategy

The key technique for assembling super-guilds is using buffer plants to link otherwise incompatible species. For example, if you planted a walnut guild next to an apple guild, the apple tree would suffer from juglone poisoning -- black walnut roots secrete juglone, a chemical compound toxic to many plants including apples. Rather than keeping incompatible species far apart, buffer trees can fit between and link them, following [[gaias-garden-stacking-functions-detailed]] principles to add new benefits at the same time.

Bill Mollison, in Permaculture: A Designers Manual, elaborates on the value of buffer plants. What makes a good buffer tree:

1. **Compatibility**: Buffer trees must be compatible with the trees being linked. A tree harmed by juglone is not a good choice for a walnut buffer. Research the allelopathic properties of all species in your design.
2. **Positive interactions**: Buffer trees should have a beneficial effect on one or both guilds being linked. [[comfrey-multifunctional-guild-plant]], mulch-producers, and bird- or insect-attracting plants are ideal candidates.
3. **Multiple functions**: Species offering food, animal forage, lumber, or other useful products should be preferred. Each buffer tree should serve at least three functions to justify its space in the design.

## Example Buffer Plants

Species that connect walnut and apple guilds while satisfying multiple criteria:

### Mulberry Trees (Morus spp.)
Unharmed by walnut exudations, mulberries come in many attractive cultivars (white, Russian, Illinois, Olivett). Some homeowners complain that mulberry fruit creates a gooey, staining mess, but intelligent placement -- avoiding the driveway and play areas -- minimizes this drawback. In a healthy [[gaias-garden-beneficial-birds-wildlife]], abundant wildlife will greedily consume the berries. Mulberries are quickly gobbled up by domestic fowl as well, so [[gaias-garden-chicken-tractors]] or [[gaias-garden-duck-integration]] in the yard will clean up any dropped fruit. Mulberries also provide excellent forage for silkworms, timber, and shade.

### Nitrogen-Fixing Trees and Large Shrubs
Most nitrogen fixers tolerate walnut toxins. Black locusts work well, having attractive foliage, generous blossoms that bees love, and rot-resistant timber for fence posts. Other attractive options include:

- **Acacia** (Acacia spp.) -- fast-growing, drought-tolerant, with edible flowers in some species
- **Red or black alder** (Alnus spp.) -- excellent for riparian areas, fast-growing
- **Golden-chain tree** (Laburnum spp.) -- stunning yellow flower cascades
- **Silk tree** (Albizia julibrissin) -- tropical-looking pink flowers, ferny foliage
- **Mountain mahogany** (Cercocarpus spp.) -- drought-hardy, attractive bark
- **Russian olive** (Elaeagnus angustifolia) -- nitrogen-fixing, silvery foliage, edible fruit
- **Wax myrtle** (Myrica spp.) -- nitrogen-fixing, aromatic foliage, bird habitat

## The Four-Tree Super-Guild

A combination of fruit, nut, and buffer trees -- for example, apple, walnut, mulberry, and a nitrogen fixer -- creates an array that maximizes beneficial interactions and minimizes the negative:

- A nitrogen fixer boosts growth of all three other trees by adding soil fertility through its root-associated bacteria
- Mulberries buffer apples from walnut toxins, and research indicates apples actually profit from mulberry companionship
- The combined guilds offer a liberal assortment of food that will not bore human palates
- Blossoms, fruit, and shelter attract many species of [[gaias-garden-beneficial-birds-wildlife]]
- The diversity of tree species disrupts pest cycles that target specific fruit types

## Scaling to the Suburban Yard

Ten to twenty medium-sized trees can be tucked into a typical suburban quarter-acre without shrouding the inhabitants in a gloomy forest. Two iterations of the four-tree super-guild gives eight trees, leaving ample space for [[gaias-garden-keyhole-beds-and-mandala-gardens]], herb-spiral|herb spirals, vegetables, flowers, and open space for recreation.

The nitrogen-fixing tree and mulberry are underplanted with useful shrubs and flowers such as [[gaias-garden-multipurpose-plants]] and [[gaias-garden-living-plant-systems-insectary]]. Rows of [[gaias-garden-berry-bushes-guide]] and flowering shrubs fill the alleys between trees, and any open space can hold vegetables and flowers. This layered approach creates a [[gaias-garden-food-forest-case-study-jerome-osentowski]].

## Connecting Repeated Super-Guilds

When multiple super-guilds are planted across a property, they can be further interconnected with:

- **Berry hedgerows** between guilds for bird habitat, privacy screening, and fruit production. Choose species that fruit at different times for extended harvest.
- **Ground-cover guilds** of nitrogen fixers and [[comparison-perennial-vegetables-vs-cover-crops]] beneath all trees. White clover, creeping thyme, and oregano serve as living mulch while providing harvests.
- **[[gaias-garden-living-plant-systems-insectary]]** of flowering plants along pathways between guilds, ensuring pollinators and predatory insects can move freely through the landscape.
- **[[gaias-garden-water-harvesting-swales-and-greywater]]** that pass through multiple guilds, distributing captured rainwater across the entire super-guild system.

## Design Process for Super-Guilds

1. Select your primary tree species (fruit, nut, etc.) based on your climate zone, soil conditions, and personal preferences
2. Design individual guilds around each tree using [[hemenway-guild-design-roles-and-assembly]] -- include nitrogen fixers, insectary plants, nutrient accumulators, mulch producers, and root crops
3. Identify compatibility conflicts between adjacent guilds (allelopathy, shade competition, root competition)
4. Choose buffer plants that bridge conflicts while adding new functions -- prioritize species with three or more roles
5. Add understory layers of shrubs, herbs, and ground covers that fill gaps and connect the canopy trees
6. Connect guilds with hedgerows, swales, and pathways that serve as both functional infrastructure and additional habitat corridors
7. Observe and adjust over time as the system matures and species interactions become clear

## Potential Pitfalls

[[hemenway-guild-design-roles-and-assembly]]. Not every combination will work as planned. Some considerations:

- Allelopathic effects may extend further than expected, especially in sandy soils where juglone travels readily
- Shade competition may reduce yields of sun-loving fruit trees if canopy trees are too closely spaced
- Root competition can be severe, especially during drought years; maintain adequate spacing between trees
- Pest attraction is real -- diverse plantings attract more insects overall, including some pests. The key is that predator populations also increase to maintain balance
- Not every guild will look beautiful in its early years. Young guilds often look messy and wild compared to conventional orchards. Patience is required as the system matures.

## Super-Guilds and the Food Forest

A super-guild is essentially the design methodology behind a [[hemenway-food-forest-design]]. When guilds are interconnected with hedgerows, ground covers, swales, and [[gaias-garden-beneficial-birds-wildlife]], the result is a multi-layered, self-maintaining ecosystem that produces food, fiber, medicine, and habitat. The food forest is the ultimate expression of the super-guild concept, scaled from individual tree guilds to an entire landscape.

The transition from simple guilds to super-guilds to food forests represents increasing levels of [[gaias-garden-stacking-functions-detailed]] and interconnected beneficial relationships. Each level of complexity yields more total output with less total maintenance -- the hallmark of mature ecological design.
