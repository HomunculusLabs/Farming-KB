### Nitrogen-Fixing Trees and Large Shrubs
Most nitrogen fixers tolerate walnut toxins. Black locusts work well, having attractive foliage, generous blossoms that bees love, and rot-resistant timber for fence posts. Other attractive options include:

- **Acacia** (Acacia spp.) -- fast-growing, drought-tolerant, with edible flowers in some species
- **Red or black alder** (Alnus spp.) -- excellent for riparian areas, fast-growing
- **Golden-chain tree** (Laburnum spp.) -- stunning yellow flower cascades
- **Silk tree** (Albizia julibrissin) -- tropical-looking pink flowers, ferny foliage
- **Mountain mahogany** (Cercocarpus spp.) -- drought-hardy, attractive bark
- **Russian olive** (Elaeagnus angustifolia) -- nitrogen-fixing, silvery foliage, edible fruit
- **Wax myrtle** (Myrica spp.) -- nitrogen-fixing, aromatic foliage, bird habitat

## The Four-Tree Super-Guild

A combination of fruit, nut, and buffer trees -- for example, apple, walnut, mulberry, and a nitrogen fixer -- creates an array that maximizes beneficial interactions and minimizes the negative:

- A nitrogen fixer boosts growth of all three other trees by adding soil fertility through its root-associated bacteria
- Mulberries buffer apples from walnut toxins, and research indicates apples actually profit from mulberry companionship
- The combined guilds offer a liberal assortment of food that will not bore human palates
- Blossoms, fruit, and shelter attract many species of [[gaias-garden-keyhole-beds-and-mandala-gardens]], herb-spiral|herb spirals, vegetables, flowers, and open space for recreation.
