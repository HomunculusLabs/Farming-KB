# Alley Cropping and Contour Hedgerow Systems

Alley cropping, also known as hedgerow intercropping, is an agroforestry
practice that integrates rows of perennial woody plants with annual or
perennial crops in the alleys between them. Mollison treats alley cropping as
a bridge between broadscale agriculture and permanent food forest systems,
offering a transition pathway that maintains crop production while building
soil, controlling erosion, and establishing the perennial framework of a
future [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]]. The
technique integrates closely with [[mollison-designers-keyline-pattern-plow-technique]] methods and [[mollison-pdc-swale-design-water-harvesting]].

## Principles of Alley Cropping Design

Alley cropping arranges trees or shrubs in parallel rows along the contour
with crop alleys between them. The design follows several key principles:

- **Contour alignment**: Hedgerow rows follow the land contour precisely to
  intercept water flow, reduce runoff velocity, and maximise water infiltration.
  This is the same principle underlying [[mollison-designers-swale-spacing-and-contour-interval]] design.
- **Competition management**: Tree rows and crop alleys must be managed to
  minimise competition for light, water, and nutrients. Root pruning, shoot
  pruning, and species selection all contribute to balancing tree and crop
  productivity.
- **Multiple functions**: Hedgerow species are selected for nitrogen fixation,
  mulch production, fodder, timber, or fruit production, not just soil
  conservation. Every element performs multiple functions per the core
  permaculture principle.
- **Progressive perennialisation**: Over time, tree rows can be widened or
  additional species added, gradually converting the system from annual crop
  dominance to perennial polyculture. See
  [[mollison-succession-planting]].

## Contour Hedgerow Species Selection

Mollison provides detailed guidance on selecting hedgerow species for
different climates and functions:

### Nitrogen-Fixing Hedgerow Species for the Humid Tropics
These species form the backbone of tropical alley cropping systems:
- **Leucaena leucocephala**: Fast-growing nitrogen fixer; excellent fodder
  and mulch; deep taproot. Susceptible to psyllid insect in some regions.
  Coppices readily. Up to 500 kg N/ha/year fixation.
- **Gliricidia sepium**: Widely adapted nitrogen fixer; produces abundant
  leafy biomass for mulch; good fodder for livestock; easy to establish from
  cuttings. See [[mollison-designers-coppicing-management-of-legume-trees]].
- **Calliandra calothyrsus**: High-altitude tropical species tolerating
  acidic soils; excellent fodder and mulch producer; fixes 100-200 kg N/ha/yr.
- **Sesbania sesban**: Fast-establishing short-lived species for the first
  years of system establishment; excellent green manure; tolerates waterlogging.
- **Flemingia macrophylla**: Shade-tolerant understory nitrogen fixer; good
  for adding to existing systems; produces persistent mulch.

### Hedgerow Species for Temperate Climates
Temperate alley cropping uses different species adapted to cold winters and
deciduous growth patterns:
- **Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus proliferus)**: Fast-growing evergreen nitrogen
  fixer; excellent poultry and livestock fodder; drought-tolerant once
  established. Requires well-drained soils and frost protection when young.
- **Black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia)**: Extremely hardy nitrogen fixer;
  produces durable timber and excellent firewood; foliage is good fodder
  (contains some anti-nutritional factors but palatable to livestock).
  See [[mollison-designers-fuelwood-forestry-coppice-pollard]].
- **Hazel (Corylus avellana)**: Produces nuts, provides coppice wood, and
  forms effective windbreak hedges. Not nitrogen-fixing but compatible with
  interplanted nitrogen fixers.
- **Elaeagnus species**: Nitrogen-fixing shrubs producing edible fruit;
  autumn-olive (E. umbellata) and goumi (E. multiflora) are most common.
- **Alder (Alnus spp.)**: Nitrogen-fixing trees suited to moist sites; fast
  growth; good for riparian buffer strips and wetter portions of the landscape.

### Multi-Function Hedgerow Assemblies
Mollison recommends mixing multiple species in each hedgerow row rather than
planting single-species rows. A typical tropical hedgerow assembly might
include:
- Alternate planting of Leucaena and Gliricidia at 25 cm spacing within the
  row.
- Occasional fruit or timber trees (mango, citrus, jackfruit) at wider
  spacing within the hedgerow, forming a future overstorey.
- Ground-level plantings of sweet potato or cover crop beneath the hedgerow
  to maximise productivity of the row itself.

## Alley Width and Hedgerow Spacing

The spacing between hedgerow rows determines the width of crop alleys and
must balance crop light requirements with hedgerow productivity:

- **Close spacing (3-5 metres between rows)**: Suitable for shade-tolerant
  crops (taro, sweet potato, ginger, turmeric) and regions with high
  rainfall where hedgerows are aggressively pruned. Maximises hedgerow
  biomass production per hectare.
- **Medium spacing (6-10 metres between rows)**: The most common arrangement.
  Suitable for maize, beans, cassava, and other moderate-height crops.
  Allows reasonable crop yields while building soil through hedgerow mulch.
- **Wide spacing (12-20 metres between rows)**: Suitable for mechanised crop
  production or light-demanding crops. Hedgerows function primarily as soil
  conservation and windbreak elements rather than intensive mulch producers.

Hedgerow row width is typically 1-2 metres, with trees planted at 25-50 cm
within the row depending on species. This gives a hedgerow-to-alley ratio
that shifts from 1:2 in close spacing to 1:10 in wide spacing.

## Management Practices

### Pruning and Biomass Harvest
Regular pruning is essential to manage competition between hedgerows and
alley crops. Mollison specifies:

- **Pruning height**: Cut hedgerows to 50-100 cm height during the crop
  growing season to minimise shading. Allow regrowth to 1.5-2 m after crop
  harvest.
- **Pruning frequency**: 2-4 times per year in the humid tropics; 1-2 times
  per year in temperate zones. Time pruning to coincide with crop planting
  for maximum mulch benefit.
- **Biomass application**: Pruned material is laid as mulch in the adjacent
  crop alley. Apply 2-5 tonnes of fresh material per hectare per pruning.
  This represents the primary nutrient input to the cropping system.
- **Leaf versus stem**: Leafy material decomposes quickly and releases
  nutrients; woody stems decompose slowly and build soil structure. Both are
  valuable; manage the mix based on soil needs.

### Root Pruning
Tree roots extending into crop alleys compete with crops for water and
  nutrients. Mollison recommends:
- Shallow trenching along the hedgerow edge once or twice per year to cut
  invading roots.
- [[mollison-designers-keyline-pattern-plow-technique]] cultivation
  between hedgerows to sever shallow roots without deep soil disturbance.
- Selection of species with naturally deep root systems that minimise surface
  root competition.

### Crop Rotation in Alleys
The crop alley supports rotational annual cropping while the soil is being
built by hedgerow mulch:
- Year 1: Nitrogen-demanding grain crop (maize, sorghum) benefiting from
  fresh hedgerow mulch nitrogen release.
