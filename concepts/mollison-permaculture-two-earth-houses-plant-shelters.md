### Research on Vines as Insulation

Holgar Wishart (N.S.W. I.T., in conversation) notes that wind passing over and around solar heaters is the main cause of their inefficiency. For this reason they should be encased inside the roof of the building, lying on the roof insulation and protected by a glass skylight about 50 mm above them, rather than exposed to the winds.

Stephen Lesiuk (in conversation, May 1978) measured the gain or loss of heat over bare and vine-covered brick walls in spring, to obtain data on the effects of vines on heat loss and gain. His findings are as yet to be published, but briefly, ivy on brick walls suppressed the entry of about 70% of summer heat excess, and prevented the escape of about 30% of heat from the house at night. Dozens of brick-cavity buildings could benefit from this simple biological insulation.

### Designing for Thermal Efficiency

Solar heaters positioned externally suffer greatly from wind effects on their efficiency. The permaculture approach is to integrate them within the building structure itself, lying beneath roof insulation with a glass skylight above. This not only protects the collectors from wind but uses the roof structure itself as part of the thermal system.

For vine-covered walls, the selection of species matters: evergreen, waxy-leaved climbers provide year-round insulation. Deciduous vines allow winter sun penetration while shading in summer. The mass of the root systems in the soil also contributes to the thermal buffer zone around foundations.

## Sun Traps and Wind Protection

Structures breasting the wind should be like a vessel breasting the sea, either "easy" in entry like a sharp-bowed boat, or permeable, like a raft, or both. A U-shaped suntrap, curved and permeable back to the cold winds, and facing north (in the southern hemisphere), can be adapted for every site. If a local site features salty and strong easterlies, then the U should be higher and longer to the east than to the west. If mornings are sunny and clear, and evenings cloudy, or the sun sets early behind a western hill, the U should be swung towards the NE to gain most sun. On very hostile, dry, windy, shoreline, or cold sites, a whole series of interlinked sun traps may be the only sensible way to plant.

In wind-protection nets, the corner junctions give squarer fields than do centre junctions. Similarly, sun screens may be curved and tapered to admit low and early light, based on the known path of the sun.

## Shadehouses and Glasshouse Integration

Water tanks, often regarded as old-fashioned, can be vine-covered in the shadehouse as a cool air/water block. Buildings designed with shade areas allow cool air to be drawn into all courtyard buildings, and give a refuge for teachers and children in extreme summer heat. Even the dripping of water helps, as does the sight of ferns in a droughted landscape.

In really cool climates, trellis at right-angles to the walls decreases cool wind and forms warm air pockets to the north, and by arranging hot-water usage to the north side of the house, a double benefit is arrived at:

1. Solar ponds can be filled either outside or inside the glasshouse for hot water provision
2. Heated water from sinks, showers, and baths can be released into a cooling-off tank inside the glasshouse
