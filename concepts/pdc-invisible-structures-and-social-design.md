# PDC Invisible Structures and Social Design

The Williams PDC devotes significant attention to "invisible structures" -- the non-physical forces that shape human behavior and ecological outcomes. Williams argues that invisible structures are as important to [[permaculture-design]] as physical structures like soil, water, and plants, yet they are frequently overlooked by designers who focus exclusively on the tangible.

## Defining Invisible Structures

Invisible structures are forces that govern the behavior of both the natural and human-made world. They are called "invisible" because you cannot point at a discrete thing and identify them, yet they manifest through physical structures and shape every aspect of design outcomes.

### Characteristics of Invisible Structures

- Every invisible structure has a concurrent physical structure
- Every physical structure creates an invisible structure around it (like ripples of cause and effect)
- Placing a physical structure in a design will create an invisible structure around it
- Humans count as physical structures (they are animal systems), so every human creates invisible structures around themselves
- Invisible structures are easiest to represent with charts, diagrams, lists, and schedules

### Examples of Invisible Structures

Williams provides numerous examples drawn from both nature and human society:

**Natural invisible structures:**
- The seasons -- you cannot point at "Spring" as a discrete object, yet seasons manifest through snow, wind, falling leaves, and temperature changes
- Animal migration patterns
- Plant flowering and fruiting cycles
- Predator-prey population dynamics

**Human-made invisible structures:**
- The 8-hour workday -- its concurrent physical structure is the freeway system designed to absorb commuter traffic ebbs and flows
- Economic systems (market, barter, gift, local production, skill sharing)
- Community governance structures
- Cultural traditions and social norms
- Legal and regulatory frameworks (zoning ordinances, building codes)
- Educational systems

## The Designer's Challenge

The permaculture designer faces a dual challenge with invisible structures:

### Reading Invisible Structures

Just as the designer must read physical patterns (microclimates, water flows, soil types), they must also read invisible structures. This requires:
- **Pattern understanding skills**: The ability to identify behavioral patterns and their causes
- **Observation skills extended to human systems**: Not just observing where water flows, but where people flow, when they gather, what motivates their movements
- **Systems thinking**: Understanding how invisible structures interact with each other and with physical structures

Williams compares reading invisible structures to Neo reading the code of the Matrix -- it requires a "zen-like" ability to see the actual structures operating in the world.

### Designing Invisible Structures

The designer must be able to:
1. Identify existing invisible structures on the site and understand their ecological role
2. Assess which invisible structures will be beneficial to the new design
3. Determine which must be changed or removed
4. Design new invisible structures that will positively impact the design
5. Ensure new invisible structures and their necessary physical structures are feasible

### The Ripple Effect

Williams emphasizes a critical design principle: placing a physical structure in a design will create an invisible structure around it. This means every design decision has social and behavioral consequences beyond its physical function. Examples:
- Placing a garden path in a certain location creates predictable foot traffic patterns
- Installing a community gathering space creates patterns of social interaction
- Designating a food production area creates patterns of daily and seasonal activity
- Building a greywater system creates patterns of water-conscious behavior

## The Five Economic Streams

Williams presents a framework of five economic input streams that [[permaculture]] designers should integrate into their designs and livelihoods:

### 1. Market Economy
Goods and services traded for legal currency. This is the economy everyone thinks of first -- having a job, earning money, buying things. Williams does not reject the market economy but insists it should not be the sole economic input.

### 2. Barter Economy
Direct trade of goods and services without currency. Often a "tit-for-tat" exchange, though what constitutes "equal" value is subjective. One person's junk is another person's treasure.

### 3. Gift Economy
Giving from abundance without expectation of return. Distinct from barter in its ethical nature -- things are simply given, never asked for. Williams describes this as taking something you have in abundance and giving it to someone else. The Freecycle network is given as a practical exercise in experiencing the gift economy.

### 4. Locally Produced Natural Resources
Food, water, medicine, and building materials produced on-site through [[permaculture]] systems. When your design yields the necessities of life directly, you reduce dependence on external economic systems. Grow as much food as possible, harvest water, create local infrastructures that support needs.

### 5. Skill Shares
People gathering to share skills and knowledge -- essentially teaching and passing on practical ability. With skills, one can acquire the necessities of life directly through one's own capability.

### Integration Principle

[[permaculture]] Principle #5 ([[integrate-rather-than-segregate]]) applies directly to economics. A person pursuing right livelihood should integrate all five streams. Each should be used as much as possible without being dependent on any single one. The goal is maximum resilience through diversification.

## Right Livelihood

Williams connects invisible structures to the Buddhist concept of right livelihood -- the idea that how we earn our living is an ethical choice with ecological consequences.

### Redefining Livelihood

The true definition of livelihood is "securing the necessities of life," not "earning money." While money is the primary means of acquiring necessities in modern systems, there are many more ways to acquire them. [[permaculture]] design can create systems that directly generate the necessities of life.

### The Abundance Hierarchy

Williams outlines a priority sequence for [[permaculture]] livelihood:
1. Ensure the health of the earth
2. Take care of personal needs
3. Generate abundance
4. Create stores for lean times
5. Share abundance with others through the gift economy

The more immediate needs your system can provide directly, the less money you need to survive. This reduces dependence on the market economy and increases personal and ecological resilience.

## Community Governance and Organization

Williams describes how invisible structures shape community life, drawing on the Organizational Mandala concept for community design:

### The Stewardship Council
A governance body where representatives from different community "lobes" (departments or functional areas) each give a brief report about their area. This structure:
- Ensures all aspects of community function are visible and connected
- Creates accountability through regular reporting
- Enables coordination between different functional areas
- Prevents any single area from becoming isolated or neglected

### Design Team Application

The community governance structure directly translates to design team organization:
- Each team member is a "lobe" representing a design element area
- Sub-circles within each lobe represent specific analytical and design tasks
- All tasks must be assigned to someone
- Regular meetings ensure integration and coordination
- A central coordinator (or mutual trust) keeps everything aligned

## Social Habits and Design Response

The client interview should uncover social patterns that affect design:
- How many people will the design serve?
- What are social event patterns that cause "spikes" in impact and resource consumption?
- How "hands on" does the client want to be in daily management?
- What are the client's social habits regarding hosting, entertaining, and community engagement?

These social patterns (invisible structures) create concurrent physical structure requirements:
- Entertaining may require outdoor gathering spaces sized for expected group sizes
- Social events may create temporary surges in water use, waste generation, and parking needs
