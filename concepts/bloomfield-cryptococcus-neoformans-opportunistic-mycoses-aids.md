## Infection Pathway

When Cryptococcus cells or spores are inhaled, they are usually removed by the
lifelong river of mucus that cleanses the deepest recesses of the lungs, and are then
carried by way of the throat into the acid bath of the stomach. This mucociliary
clearance system effectively eliminates most encounters. However, when critical immune
defenses are damaged, as in AIDS patients, Cryptococcus travels from the lungs to the
nervous system. In the brain it forms abscesses called cryptococcomas, leading to
debilitating headaches, and eventually to blindness, dementia, and death.

## Diagnosis

Cryptococcosis is diagnosed by collecting cerebrospinal fluid from a lumbar puncture.
The clear fluid circulating through the ventricles of the brain is spread on culture
plates, where colonies of Cryptococcus appear within a day or two. Swifter diagnosis
can be performed by direct [[ergosterol]], a lipid molecule in the plasma membrane that controls
membrane fluidity, analogous to cholesterol in animal cells. Amphotericin causes the
plasma membrane to perforate, and the debilitated cell leaks salts and sugars vital
to its survival.

## The Ergosterol Target

Ergosterol is one of the few unique drug targets present in fungi but absent in
humans, which seems like a compelling advantage for amphotericin B. Unfortunately,
at high doses over prolonged periods, the drug causes side effects ranging from chills
and vomiting to anemia and kidney damage. These disadvantages must be weighed against
the palliative nature of chemotherapy for patients with advanced brain infections,
where the aim shifts from cure to symptom relief.
