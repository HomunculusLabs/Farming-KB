# Rabbit and Small Poultry Keeping

Small livestock — rabbits, pigeons, and quail — are especially practical for urban and suburban homesteaders because they are legal in most places (including where chickens are banned), quiet, and require minimal space. In the [[permaculture-zones-system]], they fit best in Zone 1 near the house or at the edge of Zone 2 where their waste can feed the garden and [[faires-composting-and-vermiculture]].

## Rabbits

In a permaculture system, rabbits are not pets — they provide meat, manure, and fur. Unlike other animals, they can never be allowed to roam gardens freely because they will eat everything and multiply uncontrollably.

### Housing

- Hutch with mesh floor so droppings fall through for easy cleanup
- Wire mesh no larger than half an inch
- Place a worm bin underneath for vermicompost — eliminates cleanup and creates fertilizer
- Keep clean, dry, and sheltered from weather
- Rabbits are especially practical for urban homesteaders because hutches are legal in most places

### Diet

Rabbits eat grass, leaves, twigs, hay, vegetables, and kitchen scraps. They need a constant supply of fresh water. In the greenhouse during winter, rabbits provide both heat and manure for the garden.

### Meat Breeds

Any rabbit breed can be eaten, but meat breeds grow much larger:
- California — large, fast-growing, calm temperament
- New Zealand — most common meat breed, excellent feed conversion
- Champagne d'Argent — silver-gray, good meat-to-bone ratio
- Florida White — smaller but efficient, good for limited space

### Breeding

- Small breeds should not breed until 6 months old; large breeds not until 9 months
- Keep males and females separate at all times except for supervised breeding
- Put the doe into the male's cage (not the reverse) so he notices her
- Try again about 8 hours later to ensure successful mating
- Once pregnant, provide a nesting box: low-sided wood box with tall back and roof awning
- Add soft material — hay, down, or clean straw
- The doe will pull her own fur out to make a nest; if she doesn't, gently pull small bits from all over her body a couple of days before expected birth
- Gestation is approximately 30 days
- After babies are weaned, separate males and females — they become territorial and need their own cages
- Siblings should not mate, but mother-son or cousin breeding is common practice
- Don't breed rabbits with defects or illness; choose pairs promoting the best traits
- Typical schedule: breed a doe every 6 weeks, wean at approximately 5 weeks
- With intensive breeding, potential yield is 300 pounds of meat per year

### Common Breeding Problems

- Does sometimes don't give birth in the nesting box — move babies back if found elsewhere (they may be too cold but try to warm them)
- Does may eat their young (caused by stress, poor nutrition, or disturbance during birth)
- Does may not feed their young or may accidentally step on them
- Hand-feeding babies every two hours is possible but rarely successful — since the doe can have another litter soon, it may not be worth the effort

## Pigeons

Pigeons provide squabs (young pigeons, considered a gourmet dish) and eggs. A breeding pair produces 12 squabs per year. Pigeons are easy keepers — they mate for life and need minimal care.

### Housing

- Coop at least 6 x 8 feet (1.8 x 2.5 meters), 7 feet (2 meters) tall — walk-in design
- Each breeding pair needs an elevated nesting box attached to the wall, filled with straw or hay
- Constant supply of fresh water
- Can be designed to sit off the ground (like a rabbit hutch) to deter possums and weasels
- At least three breeding pairs recommended — they are social animals

### Management

- Pigeons can fly free during the day — they eat seeds and grain from the garden, fertilize everything, and return to the coop at night
- This is common practice but carries higher predator risk
- Secure them inside at night
- Sex determination: males are noisy, busy strutting; females are very quiet and sit still
- Eventually replace first breeding pairs with younger pairs

### Breeding

- Pigeons mate for life — put a breeding pair together and they settle in
- Both parents take turns incubating eggs (18 days)
- Parents feed squabs regurgitated food
- Butcher squabs at 28 days, before they start to fly
- Two squabs make a meal for one person

### Meat Breeds

- Cropper — large, distinctive crop
- White King — most common meat breed
- Silver King — good size and temperament

## Quail

Quail are exceptionally space-efficient — up to 6 can be raised per square foot (though more space is better for [[homesteading]]). They eat insects (not plants), so they can live in the greenhouse without damaging crops. They are raised more often for eggs than meat, since they are smaller than pigeons but lay more prolifically.

### Housing

- Smaller cages than pigeons
- Can live in the greenhouse — they eat insects and don't damage plants
- Need help hatching eggs (poor brooders, like chickens)

### Egg Production

- About 200 eggs per year under natural light (nearly one per day)
- With supplemental lighting in winter, 300+ eggs per year
- More often raised for eggs than for meat

### Breeding

- Coturnix (Japanese) quail: eggs hatch in 18 days
- Eastern Bobwhite quail: eggs hatch in 23 days
- Butcher at 6 weeks

### Meat Breeds

- Coturnix (Japanese) — most common, fast-growing
- Eastern Bobwhite — larger, native to North America

### Legal Considerations

Quail are considered wildlife in many places. Check local regulations — you may need a game bird license. Pigeons, quail, and rabbits are sometimes allowed in urban locations where chickens are banned, although not necessarily in the quantities desired for serious production.

## Butchering Squab and Quail

1. Take the bird from the nest in the morning before it eats
2. Cut off the head and hang to bleed out (same as chicken)
3. Carefully pull feathers out without scalding (unlike chickens)
4. Remove feet and discard
5. Cut body from vent to breastbone, remove organs
6. Save gizzard, heart, and liver
7. Rinse with cold water
8. Refrigerate or freeze as soon as possible

## Integration Notes

- Rabbits: Best in Zone 1 near the house; manure drops directly into worm bins below the hutch
- Pigeons and quail: Can free-range during day (eating garden pests and weed seeds, fertilizing), return to coop at night
- All three provide manure for garden fertilization
- All three are legal in most urban areas where chickens are banned
- Worm bins under rabbit hutches create a closed-loop system: rabbit manure feeds worms, worm castings fertilize garden, garden scraps feed rabbits
- In winter, rabbits kept in the greenhouse provide body heat alongside compost heating
