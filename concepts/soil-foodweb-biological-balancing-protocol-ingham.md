## Soil Foodweb Biological Balancing Protocol

## Overview

Elaine Ingham's soil foodweb balancing protocol provides a systematic, step-by-step approach to restoring healthy soil biology. Rather than treating symptoms with chemical inputs, the protocol diagnoses which groups of soil organisms are deficient or excessive and prescribes specific biological amendments to restore balance. The underlying principle is that healthy soils contain a complete and balanced community of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms — and that when this community is intact, plants naturally resist disease, cycle nutrients efficiently, and build soil structure. The protocol is built around twelve sequential steps that address each major group of soil organisms.

## The Foundation: Soil Foodweb Testing

Before any corrective action is taken, the current state of soil biology must be assessed. Ingham recommends sending soil samples to a qualified laboratory (such as Soil Foodweb Inc.) that can measure:

- Total and active bacterial biomass
- Total and active fungal biomass
- Protozoa numbers (flagellates, amoebae, ciliates)
- Nematode numbers and [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] percentage
- Microarthropod and earthworm presence

Without this baseline data, any amendments are guesses. The test results define which steps of the protocol are needed.

## The Twelve-Step Protocol

### Steps One Through Three: Bacterial Balance

**Assessment**: Compare total and active bacterial biomass to desired ranges for the specific crop type (annual vegetables, perennial pastures, orchards, etc. each have different target ranges).

**If bacteria are too low**:
- Add compost (thermal or worm compost depending on fungal needs)
- Apply [[gadd-soil-biomass-fungal-dominance-elemental-cycles]].

### Steps Four and Five: Fungal Balance

**Assessment**: Compare total and active fungal biomass to desired ranges.

**If fungi are too low**:
- Add fungal foods: humic acids, fulvic acids, fish hydrolysate, kelp meal
- Apply fungal-dominated compost tea
- Add woody mulch (selects for fungi by spatially separating carbon from soil nutrients)
- Reduce tillage ([[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-inoculum-production-best-practices]] (spores) must be placed directly next to roots or on/around seeds
- VAM fungi cannot be effectively delivered through compost tea because they are obligate root symbionts
- Direct placement is essential — spores broadcast on the soil surface will not effectively colonize roots

**If colonization is 15–40%**:
- The system is building; adding humic acid as fungal food may be sufficient to support continued colonization
- Avoid practices that damage [[lowenfels-aerobic-anaerobic-soil-conditions]]
