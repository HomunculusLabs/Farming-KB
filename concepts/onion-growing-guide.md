## Onion Growing Guide

Onions (Allium cepa) are a foundational kitchen staple with specific day-length requirements that determine bulb formation. This guide covers day-length types, planting methods (sets, seeds, transplants), growing care, curing, and storage.

## Day-Length Types

Onions bulb in response to daylight hours. Choosing the correct type for your latitude is the most important factor in onion success.

| Type | Daylight Required | Latitude Range | Planting Season | Examples |
|------|------------------|----------------|-----------------|----------|
| Short-day | 10-12 hours | Southern US (zones 7-10) | Fall/winter | Granex (Vidalia), Texas 1015, Red Burgundy |
| Long-day | 14-16 hours | Northern US/Canada (zones 3-6) | Early spring | Walla Walla, Copra, Yellow Stuttgarter, Red Wing |
| Day-neutral | ~12-14 hours | Most US zones (5-9) | Spring | Candy, Red Candy Apple, Super Star |

### Key Rule
- Planting the wrong day-length type for your latitude = onions that grow large tops but never form bulbs (they bolt to seed instead).
- If you live above 35° latitude, grow long-day or day-neutral onions.
- If you live below 35° latitude, grow short-day onions.
- When in doubt, day-neutral varieties are the most adaptable.

## Planting Methods

### Sets (Small, Dry Bulbs)
- **What**: Miniature dormant bulbs (3/4 inch or smaller), grown from seed the previous year.
- **Pros**: Easiest method, fast to mature, reliable.
- **Cons**: Limited variety selection, smaller final bulbs, higher bolt rate.
- **Planting**: Push into soil with root plate down, tip just at soil surface.
- **Spacing**: 2-4 inches apart in rows 12-18 inches apart.
- **Best for**: Beginners, gardeners who want a simple reliable crop.
- **Note**: Sets larger than 3/4 inch are more likely to bolt. Plant larger sets closer together and harvest as green onions/scallions.

### Transplants (Bare-Root or Plug)
- **What**: Young onion plants grown from seed in a nursery, 8-12 weeks old.
- **Pros**: Wider variety selection, larger bulbs, lower bolt rate than sets.
- **Cons**: More expensive, need careful handling.
- **Planting**: Trim tops to 4 inches and roots to 2 inches. Plant 1 inch deep.
- **Spacing**: 4-6 inches apart in rows 12-18 inches apart.
- **Best for**: Gardeners wanting specific varieties and maximum bulb size.

### Seeds
- **What**: Direct-sow or start indoors 10-12 weeks before transplanting.
- **Pros**: Largest variety selection, most economical, strongest plants.
- **Cons**: Slowest method, requires planning, onions are slow growers from seed.
- **Indoor starting**: Sow 1/4 inch deep in flats, 1/2 inch apart. Grow at 60-65°F. Transplant when pencil-thick.
- **Direct sowing**: Sow 1/4 inch deep in early spring (as soon as soil can be worked). Thin to 4-6 inches.
- **Best for**: Maximum control, unusual varieties, dedicated growers.

## Soil Preparation

- Onions need loose, well-drained, fertile soil with pH 6.0-7.0.
- Work 2-3 inches of compost into the bed before planting.
- Onions are heavy nitrogen feeders — the more nitrogen available, the larger the bulbs.
- Add phosphorus (bone meal) for root/bulb development.
- Raised beds are ideal — loose soil allows bulbs to expand freely.

## Growing Care

### Watering
- Onions have shallow root systems — consistent moisture is critical.
- 1-1.5 inches per week.
- Keep soil evenly moist but not waterlogged.
- Mulch with straw to retain moisture and suppress weeds.
- Reduce watering when bulbs approach full size (last 2-3 weeks before harvest).

### Fertilizing
- Onions are heavy nitrogen feeders.
- Side-dress with nitrogen-rich fertilizer (blood meal, composted manure, fish emulsion) every 2-3 weeks until bulbs begin to swell.
- Stop nitrogen when bulbs are 2/3 their final size — excess N delays maturity.
- Apply phosphorus and potassium at planting (bone meal, kelp meal).

### Weeding
- Onions cannot compete with weeds — their foliage is narrow and doesn't shade the soil.
- Weed carefully by hand or with a hoe — onion roots are shallow and easily disturbed.
- Mulch heavily to suppress weeds.

### Bolting (Going to Seed)
- Onions bolt when exposed to temperature fluctuations (cold snap after warm period) or when planted from large sets.
- Bolted onions produce a flower stalk — the bulb becomes unusable for storage (hollow core).
- If bolting occurs: cut off the flower stalk immediately; use the onion promptly (won't store well).
- Prevention: use transplants or seeds (not large sets), choose appropriate day-length type, mulch to stabilize soil temperature.

## Harvesting

### Timing
- Harvest when approximately 50% of the tops have fallen over and turned brown.
- Neck should feel soft and papery.
- Do NOT wait for all tops to fall — this invites disease and rot.
- Typical harvest: mid-summer for spring-planted onions.

### How to Harvest
- Pull bulbs gently by hand or loosen with a fork.
- Do NOT step on or drop bulbs — bruising reduces storage life.
- Shake off loose soil — do NOT wash or cut tops yet.
- Leave bulbs on the ground in the sun for 1-2 days to dry (if weather is dry and warm).
- If rain threatens, move to a covered, well-ventilated area.

## Curing

- Cure onions in a warm (75-80°F), dry, well-ventilated area for 2-4 weeks.
- Spread in a single layer on screens, racks, or newspaper.
- Do NOT pile onions — airflow is essential.
- Ideal humidity: 60-70% (too humid = rot; too dry = excessive shrinkage).
- Curing is complete when the neck is completely dry and papery, outer skin rustles, and there is no moisture at the neck.
- After curing, trim roots to 1/2 inch and stems to 1-2 inches.

## Storage

- Storage conditions: cool (35-45°F), dry (60-70% humidity), dark, well-ventilated.
- **Do NOT refrigerate** — cold + humidity causes sprouting and rot.
- Mesh bags, nylon stockings, or slatted crates allow airflow.
- Store in a cool basement, root cellar, or unheated garage.
- **Do NOT store near potatoes** — potatoes release moisture and ethylene gas that cause onions to rot and sprout.

### Storage Life by Variety

| Type | Storage Life | Best Varieties |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| Short-day | 1-3 months | Granex, Texas Legend |
| Long-day (storage) | 6-10 months | Copra, Yellow Stuttgarter, Patterson, Red Wing |
| Long-day (sweet) | 2-3 months | Walla Walla, Ailsa Craig, Candy |
| Day-neutral | 3-5 months | Candy, Super Star |

### General Rule
- Thicker, papery skins = longer storage.
- Sweet/mild onions (thin skins) do NOT store well — eat within 1-2 months.
- Pungent, firm onions with multiple dry layers store longest.

## Common Problems

| Problem | Cause | Prevention |
|---------|-------|------------|
| Small bulbs | Wrong day-length type, insufficient nitrogen, crowding | Choose correct type, fertilize, proper spacing |
| Thick necks (won't cure) | Excess nitrogen late in season, too much water | Stop N when bulbs are 2/3 mature, reduce watering |
