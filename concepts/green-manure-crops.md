# Green Manure Crops

Green manure crops are plants grown specifically to be incorporated into the soil while still green, adding organic matter, nitrogen, and biological activity. Unlike cover crops that may be terminated on the surface, green manures are typically tilled or dug in. They are a cornerstone of organic fertility management.

## Nitrogen Fixers

Leguminous green manures convert atmospheric nitrogen (N2) into plant-available forms through Rhizobium bacteria housed in root nodules. The nitrogen contribution depends on species, growing conditions, and termination timing.

### Top Nitrogen-Fixing Green Manures

- **Field Peas (Pisum sativum)**: Fix 70-150 lb N/acre. Cool-season, rapid growth, easy to incorporate. Best sown in early spring or late summer. Terminate at early pod formation for maximum N.
- **Hairy Vetch (Vicia villosa)**: Fix 90-200 lb N/acre. The highest N fixer commonly available. Requires inoculation with Vicia-specific Rhizobium. Combine with cereal rye for structural support.
- **Fava Beans (Vicia faba)**: Fix 100-200 lb N/acre. Deep taproot brings up subsoil nutrients. Large seeds make planting easy. Edible when young. Cold-tolerant.
- **Clovers** (Red, Crimson, White): Fix 50-130 lb N/acre. Red clover is a biennial providing long-term N release. Crimson clover is an annual with faster turnover. White clover can serve as a living mulch.
- **Cowpeas (Vigna unguiculata)**: Fix 100-200 lb N/acre. Warm-season, drought-tolerant. Ideal summer green manure in the South. Combine with sorghum for a C:N balanced mix.

### Maximizing Nitrogen Fixation

- Always inoculate legume seed with the correct Rhizobium strain if the legume has not been grown in that field within 3 years
- Ensure adequate soil phosphorus and molybdenum for nodulation
- Avoid excess nitrogen fertilizer, which suppresses nodulation
- Terminate at peak biomass but before seed set for maximum N return
- Allow 2-4 weeks between incorporation and planting the next crop for decomposition

## Biomass Builders

Non-leguminous green manures add carbon and organic matter, feeding soil microbes and improving soil structure. They have a higher carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratio, which slows decomposition and builds long-term humus.

### Grass-Based Green Manures

- **Cereal Rye (Secale cereale)**: Produces 4,000-8,000 lb dry matter/acre. Extremely vigorous root system. The gold standard for biomass production. C:N ratio of 30-40:1.
- **Oats (Avena sativa)**: Fast-growing, produces 2,000-4,000 lb DM/acre. Convenient because it winter-kills, eliminating termination work. C:N ratio of 25-30:1.
- **Annual Ryegrass (Lolium multiflorum)**: Deep-rooted (up to 3 feet), excellent for breaking compaction. Produces good biomass but can become weedy. C:N ratio of 25-35:1.
- **Sorghum-Sudangrass (Sorghum bicolor x S. sudanense)**: The biomass champion at 6,000-12,000 lb DM/acre. Warm-season. Allelopathic root exudates suppress soil pests. C:N ratio of 35-45:1.

### C:N Ratio Considerations

When incorporating green manures, the C:N ratio determines how quickly nutrients become available:

| C:N Ratio | Decomposition Speed | Nitrogen Availability |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Below 20:1 | Rapid (weeks) | N released quickly |
| 20-30:1 | Moderate (months) | Balanced release |
| Above 30:1 | Slow (months) | N temporarily immobilized |

Legume-green manure mixes (C:N 15-25:1) release nitrogen faster than pure grass green manures (C:N 30-45:1). Balance species in mixes for optimal nutrient timing.

## Biofumigants

Certain brassica species release glucosinolate compounds that break down into isothiocyanates — volatile gases toxic to soil-borne pathogens, nematodes, and weed seeds. This process, called biofumigation, is a natural alternative to chemical soil fumigation.

### Biofumigant Green Manures

- **Mustard (Sinapis alba, Brassica juncea)**: The strongest biofumigant. High glucosinolate content. Incorporate at full flower for maximum compound concentration. Effective against Fusarium, Pythium, and root-knot nematodes. See [[insect-allies]].
- **Daikon Radish (Raphanus sativus)**: Moderate biofumigant properties plus deep taproot for compaction relief. See [[cover-cropping-guide]] for planting details.
- **Rapeseed / Canola (Brassica napus)**: Good biomass production with moderate glucosinolate levels. Effective biofumigant when incorporated properly.

### Biofumigation Protocol

1. Grow the brassica crop to full maturity (flowering stage) for maximum glucosinolate accumulation
2. Finely chop the above-ground biomass with a flail mower or weed whacker — more surface area = more gas release
3. Incorporate immediately into the top 4-6 inches of soil using a rototiller or spading fork
4. Compact the soil surface slightly (roll or tamp) to trap the volatile gases
5. Seal the surface with a tarp or water and wait 2-4 weeks before planting
6. Soil temperature must be above 50°F for effective biofumigant gas production

Biofumigation is most effective against soil-borne diseases and nematodes when done in mid-summer with warm soil temperatures. It is less effective in cool soils.

## Incorporation Timing

When and how to incorporate green manures significantly impacts nutrient availability and soil biology.

### Optimal Incorporation Stages

| Crop Type | Best Stage to Incorporate | Reason |
|-----------|--------------------------|--------|
| Legumes | Early bloom to early pod | Peak N content, tissues still succulent |
| Grasses | Boot stage to early heading | Good biomass, still decomposing readily |
| Brassicas | Full flower | Maximum glucosinolate content |
| Buckwheat | 4-6 weeks, before seed set | Prevents reseeding, peak biomass |

### Decomposition Timeline After Incorporation

- **Week 1-2**: Rapid microbial activity, visible residue breakdown begins
- **Week 3-4**: Significant nutrient release, especially from legumes
- **Week 5-8**: Most nitrogen from legume green manures is plant-available
- **Month 2-4**: Grass residues break down, humus formation begins
- **Month 4-12**: Long-term humus stabilization, slow nutrient release

### Incorporation Methods

- **Tillage**: Fastest decomposition. Rototilling incorporates residue uniformly. Tradeoff: disrupts soil structure and mycorrhizal networks. Best for biofumigant incorporation.
- **Solarization over incorporated residue**: Incorporate, cover with clear plastic for 4-8 weeks in summer. Accelerates decomposition and kills pathogens. Combines biofumigation with heat.
- **Sheet mulching on top of mowed residue**: Mow green manure low, then layer cardboard/compost on top. Slower decomposition but preserves soil structure. Works in [[no-till-gardening-method]] systems.

## Green Manure Selection Guide by Goal

### Primary Goal: Add Nitrogen

Use legumes alone or as the dominant species. Best options: hairy vetch, field peas, fava beans, cowpeas.

### Primary Goal: Build Soil Organic Matter

Use high-biomass grasses. Best options: cereal rye, sorghum-sudangrass, annual ryegrass.

### Primary Goal: Suppress Soil-Borne Disease

Use brassica biofumigants. Best options: mustard, daikon radish.

### Primary Goal: Break Compaction

Use deep-rooted species. Best options: daikon radish, annual ryegrass, sorghum-sudangrass.

### Primary Goal: Attract Beneficial Insects

Use flowering species. Best options: buckwheat, crimson clover, phacelia. See [[insect-allies]].

### Primary Goal: Erosion Control

Use fast-establishing, dense-rooting species. Best options: cereal rye, annual ryegrass, crimson clover.

## Combinations and Mixes

Balanced green manure mixes combine nitrogen fixers with biomass builders:

- **Classic Soil Builder**: 60% cereal rye + 30% hairy vetch + 10% daikon radish
- **Summer Powerhouse**: 50% cowpeas + 30% sorghum-sudangrass + 20% buckwheat
- **Quick Spring Fix**: 50% field peas + 50% oats
- **Biofumigant Boost**: 50% mustard + 25% field peas + 25% oats
