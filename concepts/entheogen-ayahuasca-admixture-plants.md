## Other Additive Plants

The diversity of plants added to ayahuasca is remarkably broad and
includes even ferns. Several of these plants are known to be active
themselves and to alter effectively the properties of the basic
drink. The practice of adding admixture plants has been documented
across dozens of indigenous groups throughout the western Amazon
basin, from Colombia and Ecuador in the north to Bolivia in the
south. Among the many additives documented in the ethnographic
literature are:

- Prestonia species (formerly Haemadictyon), considered essential
  to the success of the drink by Indians of the Rio Negro region
- Nicotiana species (tobacco), widely used as an additive and
  purificatory substance
- Various unidentified plants known by indigenous names such as
  chuchupawa, chuchupano, and cha'i
- Plants of the genus Altenanthera, reported from the Rio
  Putumayo region

The Amahuaca Indians use several additional plants in their
Banisteriopsis brew, including chuchupawa, cha'i, and chuchupano.
Cha'i is sometimes drunk first, before taking ayahuasca, to increase
the hallucinations. This practice of sequential administration of
different plant preparations reveals a nuanced understanding of
pharmacological timing and synergistic effects.

## Implications for Ethnopharmacology

The ayahuasca admixture tradition demonstrates that Amazonian
indigenous peoples possess a sophisticated empirical knowledge of
plant chemistry and pharmacological synergy. The discovery that
DMT-containing plants are consistently added to MAOI-containing
vines suggests a deep understanding, achieved through centuries of
experimentation, of the pharmacological mechanism by which orally
ingested DMT becomes psychoactive. This knowledge was arrived at
without any modern understanding of enzyme inhibition or
neurotransmitter chemistry, representing a remarkable achievement
of indigenous science. The admixture tradition also highlights
the importance of biodiversity to indigenous pharmacopoeias, as the
loss of any single plant species could disrupt the delicate balance
of a multi-plant preparation that has been refined over generations
of traditional use.
