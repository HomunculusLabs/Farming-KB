# Ayahuasca Admixture Plants

One of the most remarkable features of [[ayahuasca-botany-and-preparation]] pharmacology is
the practice of adding secondary plants to the Banisteriopsis brew.
Indigenous Amazonian peoples have developed an extraordinarily
diverse pharmacopoeia of admixture plants that modify, intensify,
or direct the visionary experience. These additives represent a
sophisticated empirical understanding of plant chemistry that is only
now being fully appreciated by modern pharmacology.

## The Core Brew and Its Limitations

The common denominator in all drinks called yage, caapi, or ayahuasca
is Banisteriopsis, typically B. caapi or B. inebrians. The primary
alkaloids of these vines -- harmine, harmaline, and d-tetrahydroharmine
-- are beta-carboline derivatives that function as monoamine oxidase
inhibitors (MAOIs). While these compounds produce visionary effects
on their own, it is commonly believed among indigenous peoples that
adding one or more additional plants to the mixture provides added
strength and alters the character of the experience.

Typically, only one other plant is added to the brew, though the
specific additive varies widely from tribe to tribe and region to
region. The biochemistry of complete Banisteriopsis brews embodying
two or more plants is not well known, as there exists little if any
information on the alkaloid constituents of such multi-plant
preparations.

## [[psychotria-viridis]] and Chacruna

The most widely documented ayahuasca additive is Psychotria viridis,
known as chacruna in Peru. This 20-foot forest treelet belongs to the
coffee family, Rubiaceae. Its leaves have been found to contain the
powerfully hallucinogenic alkaloid N,N-dimethyltryptamine ([[dmt-and-ayahuasca-history]]).
Psychotria viridis is added to the brew over a wide area including
Amazonian Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru, and is cultivated
specifically for this purpose by many indigenous groups.

Among the Cashinahua of eastern Peru, P. viridis is called nai kawa.
The Shipibo-Conibo of the Rio Ucayali add the leaves of a botanically
unidentified plant called cawa, which presumably is the Psychotria of
the linguistically related Cashinahua. The Cofan Indians of eastern
Ecuador also use Psychotria viridis as a regular admixture, and its
identity as the DMT-containing component of ayahuasca was confirmed
through chemical analysis in the late 1960s.

## Banisteriopsis rusbyana

The leaves (but not the bark) of a third species of Banisteriopsis,
B. rusbyana, are often added to the preparation "to lengthen and
brighten the visions." Called oco-yaje in the westernmost Amazon
region of Colombia and Ecuador, this liana is cultivated specifically
for its leaves alongside B. caapi and B. inebrians. Chemical analysis
has confirmed that B. rusbyana contains significant amounts of the
fast-acting hallucinogenic alkaloid DMT, functioning similarly to
Psychotria viridis as a source of visionary tryptamines that become
orally active through the MAO inhibition provided by the primary
Banisteriopsis vine.

## Solanaceous Additives

Several plants from the nightshade family, Solanaceae, are also added
to [[ayahuasca-preparation]]s. These include Datura suaveolens, whose
tropane alkaloids (scopolamine, atropine, and hyoscyamine) add a
deliriant dimension to the experience, and species of Brunfelsia.
Both of these plants are hallucinogenic in their own right and
significantly alter the character of the ayahuasca experience when
included in the brew. Their addition may produce more intense but
less controllable visionary states, and they increase the potential
danger of the preparation.

## Other Additive Plants

The diversity of plants added to ayahuasca is remarkably broad and
includes even ferns. Several of these plants are known to be active
themselves and to alter effectively the properties of the basic
drink. The practice of adding admixture plants has been documented
across dozens of indigenous groups throughout the western Amazon
basin, from Colombia and Ecuador in the north to Bolivia in the
south. Among the many additives documented in the ethnographic
literature are:

- Prestonia species (formerly Haemadictyon), considered essential
  to the success of the drink by Indians of the Rio Negro region
- Nicotiana species (tobacco), widely used as an additive and
  purificatory substance
- Various unidentified plants known by indigenous names such as
  chuchupawa, chuchupano, and cha'i
- Plants of the genus Altenanthera, reported from the Rio
  Putumayo region

The Amahuaca Indians use several additional plants in their
Banisteriopsis brew, including chuchupawa, cha'i, and chuchupano.
Cha'i is sometimes drunk first, before taking ayahuasca, to increase
the hallucinations. This practice of sequential administration of
different plant preparations reveals a nuanced understanding of
pharmacological timing and synergistic effects.

## Implications for Ethnopharmacology

The ayahuasca admixture tradition demonstrates that Amazonian
indigenous peoples possess a sophisticated empirical knowledge of
plant chemistry and pharmacological synergy. The discovery that
DMT-containing plants are consistently added to MAOI-containing
vines suggests a deep understanding, achieved through centuries of
experimentation, of the pharmacological mechanism by which orally
ingested DMT becomes psychoactive. This knowledge was arrived at
without any modern understanding of enzyme inhibition or
neurotransmitter chemistry, representing a remarkable achievement
of indigenous science. The admixture tradition also highlights
the importance of biodiversity to indigenous pharmacopoeias, as the
loss of any single plant species could disrupt the delicate balance
of a multi-plant preparation that has been refined over generations
of traditional use.
