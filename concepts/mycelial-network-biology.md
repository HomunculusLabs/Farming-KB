 Hyphal Structure and Growth
Individual hyphae are tubular cells typically one to ten micrometers
in diameter, growing by extension at the apex. Their structure is
adapted for invasive exploration of solid substrates and efficient
absorption of nutrients from the surrounding environment.
- The **apical vesicle cluster** (Spitzenkorper) concentrates
  vesicles at the growing tip that deliver cell wall materials
  and enzymes, directing polarized growth and maintaining hyphal
  tip shape during extension.
- **Septa** are cross-walls dividing hyphae into compartments,
  with pores allowing cytoplasmic streaming between cells,
  maintaining connectivity throughout the mycelial network.
- Cell walls are composed primarily of chitin, beta-glucans,
  and glycoproteins, providing rigidity for mechanical support
  and selective permeability for nutrient exchange.
- Growth occurs via tip extension at rates of 0.1 to 1.0
  millimeters per hour depending on species, temperature, and
  nutrients, with branching creating a dense mycelial mat.
- See [[phytochrome-ecological-function-dusk-dawn-red-far-red-light-switch]]. The distinction
between rhizomorphic and cottony growth forms is particularly important.
- **Rhizomorphic (linear) mycelium** forms organized, rope-like
  strands that grow rapidly and colonize aggressively, indicating
  vigor and often correlating with higher cultivation yields.
- **Cottony (tomentose) mycelium** appears as fluffy, aerial
  growth that is slower to colonize. It may indicate suboptimal
  genetics, poor nutrition, or contamination susceptibility.
- Strain selection for rhizomorphic growth is a key
  consideration in [[environmental-sensing-filamentous-fungi-read]], and reproductive
potential. These patterns emerge from simple hyphal branching and
fusion rules without central coordination, representing biological
self-organization at a fundamental level.
- Networks develop **foraging fronts** that explore new territory
  while maintaining connections to colonized areas through robust
  cord-like hyphal bundles called rhizomorphs in some species.
- Hyphal **anastomosis** (fusion between compatible hyphae)
  creates interconnected networks that redistribute resources
  from nutrient-rich zones to growing fronts, functioning as
  an integrated physiological unit.
- The architecture described in [[biological-transport-networks]] balancing exploration efficiency against
  maintenance costs, suggesting natural selection has optimized
  these systems over hundreds of millions of years.
## Nutrient Transport and Cytoplasmic Streaming
Mycelium moves nutrients, water, and signaling molecules through
cytoplasmic streaming, the bulk flow of cytoplasm within hyphae
driven by motor proteins along the cytoskeletal framework.
- Reallocation of resources from nutrient-rich zones to growing
  fronts allows sustained exploration of new territory even
  when local supplies are depleted at the colony margin.
- Rapid distribution of enzymes and metabolites enables
  coordinated responses to environmental changes such as
  upregulation of [[fungal-extracellular-enzyme-production]] when new
  substrates are encountered.
- Translocation allows nutrients from decomposing material
