# Fungal Bioreactor Types for Pollutant Removal

Updated: 2026-04-18

Various bioreactor configurations have been developed for fungal treatment of industrial effluents and contaminated waters. The choice of reactor type depends on the nature of the pollutant, the fungal species used, whether the fungus grows as pellets or filaments, and the required treatment scale. Bioreactors for fungal wastewater treatment (also called mycoreactors) exploit both the metabolic activity of living fungal biomass and the extracellular enzyme systems of [[fungal-ligninolytic-enzyme-systems-overview]].

## Stirred-Tank Bioreactors

Stirred tanks are the most common bioreactors for submerged cultivation. They provide good mixing and aeration but can damage filamentous fungal mycelium through shear forces. They are well-suited for yeast and pellet-forming fungi. Advantages include well-established design, easy monitoring, and scalability. Disadvantages include high energy consumption for stirring and potential damage to mycelial structures.

For filamentous fungi, stirred-tank reactors are most effective when operated to promote pellet formation rather than free mycelial growth. Pellet morphology is controlled by adjusting spore inoculum concentration, agitation rate, and medium composition. Compact pellets with diameters of 1-3 mm provide optimal mass transfer while maintaining high biomass density. These design considerations are central to [[mycoremediation-bioreactor-design]].

## Packed-Bed Bioreactors

Packed-bed reactors contain immobilized fungal biomass on a solid support (celite, polyurethane foam, porous ceramic, wood chips, or other carriers). Effluent flows through the packed bed and contacts the immobilized fungi. Clogging is a common problem when treating effluents containing agricultural wastes or high solids content, because of excessive fungal growth and fluffy biofilm formation. Clogging can be controlled by phosphate limitation of the fungus.

Packed-bed reactors are particularly effective for treatment of dilute effluents where high hydraulic retention times are acceptable. The immobilized biomass provides long-term stability, and the absence of mechanical agitation reduces energy costs. However, mass transfer limitations can occur within the biofilm, particularly for oxygen supply to interior cells. The use of [[fungal-immobilization-bioreactor-systems]] in packed beds has been demonstrated for dye decolorization, phenol removal, and pulp mill effluent treatment.

## Fluidized-Bed Bioreactors

Fluidized-bed reactors suspend solid carriers with attached fungal biomass in an upflowing liquid stream. This configuration avoids clogging problems of packed beds and provides good mass transfer. Solids are kept in suspension by the upward flow velocity. A 500-mL fluidized-bed bioreactor has been employed for enzyme production by white rot fungi. Three-phase (gas-liquid-solid) fluidized beds are used for aerobic and anaerobic wastewater treatment and cultivation of immobilized and pellet-forming microorganisms.

The fluidized-bed design offers excellent gas-liquid-solid contact, uniform biomass distribution, and reduced pressure drop compared to packed beds. Particle size and density must be carefully matched to the upward flow velocity to maintain stable fluidization without elutriation of biomass carriers. Temperature control is easier in fluidized beds than in packed beds due to the continuous mixing. These reactors are discussed further in [[fungal-immobilization-bioreactor-systems]].

## Airlift Bioreactors

Airlift tower loop reactors use air injection to create circulation of liquid and suspended biomass. They require no mechanical stirring, reducing energy costs and shear damage to mycelia. Airlift reactors have been used extensively for fungal treatment of starch-processing wastewater. A non-aseptic airlift bioreactor with Aspergillus oryzae removed 95% COD, 93% BOD, and 98% suspended solids from starch wastewater. Rhizopus oligosporus in a 45-L airlift bioreactor achieved 96% COD reduction. The reactor can be operated without sterile conditions, significantly reducing costs.

The draft tube in airlift reactors creates differential gas holdup between the riser and downcomer sections, driving liquid circulation. This provides gentle but effective mixing that preserves mycelial integrity while maintaining adequate oxygen transfer. Airlift reactors are particularly suited to fungal wastewater treatment because they combine low shear with good aeration, which aligns with the oxygen demands of aerobic white rot fungi.

## Membrane Bioreactors (MBRs)

Membrane bioreactors employ ultrafiltration or microfiltration membranes for complete retention of biomass. The MBR Bio-Filt uses an FM module for ultrafiltration in a modular multitank design. Membrane applications in aerobic and anaerobic schemes provide complete biomass retention, high treatment efficiency, and small footprint. They are particularly useful for retaining slow-growing white rot fungi that would otherwise be washed out of continuous systems.

MBRs solve the critical problem of biomass retention for slow-growing organisms in continuous treatment. White rot fungi typically have doubling times of 12-24 hours, much slower than bacteria used in conventional activated sludge. Without membrane retention, fungal biomass would be washed out of continuous reactors at hydraulic retention times shorter than the fungal generation time. MBRs also produce clarified effluent suitable for direct discharge or reuse. The combination of MBRs with [[mycoremediation-techniques]] represents a promising approach for industrial-scale fungal wastewater treatment.

## Rotating Biological Contactors (RBCs)

RBCs consist of partially submerged rotating discs covered with attached biofilm. As the discs rotate through the effluent, the biofilm alternately contacts the wastewater and the atmosphere, providing both pollutant exposure and aeration. They have been used for pulping effluent treatment with white rot fungi. RBCs provide good oxygen transfer without mechanical aeration.

RBCs are well-suited to fungal treatment because the attached growth mode mimics the natural growth habit of wood-rotting fungi on solid substrates. The periodic exposure to air during rotation supports the high oxygen demand of ligninolytic enzyme production. RBCs have been successfully demonstrated for [[fungal-treatment-of-industrial-wastewaters]] including pulp mill effluents and dye-containing wastewaters.

## Bubble Column Bioreactors

Bubble columns use air sparging at the bottom to provide mixing and oxygenation without mechanical agitation. However, sedimentation of solids can occur, limiting their use to wastewaters with low suspended solids content.

## Rotating Drum Bioreactors

Used for solid-state fermentation where fungal biomass grows on solid substrates. The drum rotates to mix the substrate, providing aeration and uniform moisture distribution. These are common in fungal enzyme production and solid-state treatment of contaminated soils or agricultural wastes.

Rotating drum bioreactors are particularly valuable for [[fungal-treatment-of-pulp-mill-effluents]] when combined with solid substrate pretreatment. They enable uniform colonization of solid carriers and provide controlled aeration without the shear forces that would damage mycelial networks. Scale-up considerations include maintaining adequate oxygen transfer at larger drum diameters and preventing excessive heat buildup from metabolic activity.

## Continuous vs. Batch Operation

Both batch and continuous methods can be effective. On theoretical grounds, batch processes are more likely to achieve complete treatment and may have kinetic advantages (Royer et al., 1985; Knapp et al., 1997). For non-toxic effluents, decolorization may approximate Michaelis-Menten kinetics. For toxic effluents where toxic components are degraded by the fungus, continuous treatment may be advantageous. Repeated batch (fed-batch) operation allows extended use of fungal biomass over weeks to months, demonstrating commercial viability.

## Pellet-Forming Fungi in Bioreactors

The formation and structure of mycelial pellets significantly affect bioreactor performance:

- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium pellets**: Only the outer 100-200 micrometer layer of pellets assimilates glucose. Nutrient transfer limitations in pellet interiors reduce overall metabolic efficiency.
- **Formation mechanisms**: Two mechanisms have been proposed -- coagulating (two-step: spore aggregation then germination) and noncoagulating (single spore germination).
- **Advantages**: Pelleted suspensions are not viscous and are easier to handle in bioreactors.
- **Disadvantages**: Nutrient transfer limitations in pellet interiors can reduce metabolite production. Tightly compact pellets develop hollow centers due to autolysis.

## Process Monitoring and Control

Effective operation of fungal bioreactors requires monitoring of key parameters:

- **Physical and chemical parameters**: Temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, redox potential, and nutrient concentrations must be controlled within optimal ranges for fungal growth and enzyme production.
- **Mathematical modeling**: Mathematical process models can predict the efficiency of contaminant removal, aiding in design optimization.
- **Molecular analysis**: PCR amplification of DNA from bioreactor samples allows analysis of the metabolic and genetic diversity of fungi during operation.
- **Knowledge-based control**: Knowledge-based control systems have been applied to fed-batch fungal cultivations, including qualitative process state identification and supervisory control.

## Applications by Contaminant Type

Different bioreactor configurations are suited to different treatment objectives:

- **Olive mill wastewater**: Multiple reactor types tested including repeated-batch systems with Pleurotus species and packed-bed reactors with Trametes.
- **Phenol and chlorophenol removal**: Immobilized enzyme bioreactors using MnP from Lentinula edodes and co-immobilized P. chrysosporium cells and enzymes.
- **PCB removal**: A bench-scale upflow tubular column bioreactor removed 77% of PCP from adsorbent-contaminated streams.
- **Pulp and paper mill effluent**: Bench-scale upflow column bioreactors used for paper industry wastewater treatment.

## Scale-Up and Industrial Implementation

Scaling fungal bioreactors from laboratory to industrial scale requires careful consideration of hydrodynamic similarity, oxygen transfer efficiency, heat removal capacity, and mixing intensity. The unique challenges of fungal systems -- including mycelial morphology changes, pellet formation, and attachment to reactor surfaces -- must be addressed in the scale-up design. Industrial-scale airlift bioreactors (up to 120 m3) have been successfully modeled for fungal wastewater treatment, demonstrating the feasibility of large-scale implementation.

The selection of an appropriate bioreactor configuration depends on multiple factors:

- **Pollutant type and concentration** -- high-strength wastes may require batch treatment, while dilute streams suit continuous operation

- **Fungal growth form** -- pellet-forming species work well in stirred tanks and fluidized beds, while filamentous species prefer attached-growth reactors

- **Oxygen demand** -- ligninolytic enzyme production requires high oxygen transfer, favoring airlift and RBC designs

- **Treatment scale** -- laboratory studies typically use stirred tanks, while pilot and industrial scale favor low-energy designs such as packed beds and RBCs

- **Biomass retention** -- membrane reactors and packed beds retain slow-growing fungi, while stirred tanks require continuous inoculation

These considerations are integral to [[bioremediation-ecosystem-restoration]] for selecting and optimizing fungal treatment systems.

## Hybrid and Emerging Bioreactor Designs

Recent innovations in bioreactor design for fungal pollutant removal include hybrid systems that combine multiple reactor types to leverage their respective advantages:

- **Sequential reactor systems** -- combining an airlift reactor for biomass production with a packed-bed reactor for pollutant degradation

- **Trickling filter-airlift combinations** -- using trickling filters for primary treatment followed by airlift reactors for polishing

- **Membrane-aerated biofilm reactors (MABRs)** -- supplying oxygen through gas-permeable membranes directly to the biofilm, improving oxygen transfer efficiency

- **Solar-powered bioreactors** -- integrating photovoltaic power for remote-site applications, reducing operating costs

These emerging designs reflect the ongoing evolution of [[mycoremediation-bioreactor-design]] toward more efficient and sustainable treatment systems.
