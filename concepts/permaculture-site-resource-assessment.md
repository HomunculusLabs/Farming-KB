## Overview

[[permaculture]] site resource assessment is the critical first step in any design process. Before placing a single element on a property, the designer must thoroughly catalog what the land offers, what forces act upon it, and what opportunities exist beyond the property boundary. [[bill-mollison]] emphasizes that **observation** is the primary design tool — the designer's job is to read the landscape before attempting to write upon it.

## Cataloging On-Site Resources

A complete resource inventory begins with three fundamental categories:

### Earth Resources

- **Soil types** and their depth, structure, and mineral content
- **Clay deposits** suitable for building, pottery, or pond sealing
- **Rock and stone** for construction, walls, and thermal mass
- **Minerals** that may have commercial or functional value
- **Topography** — slopes, ridges, valleys, and drainage patterns

### Plant Resources

- **Existing vegetation** — native species, useful exotics, and weeds indicating soil conditions
- **Timber and pole wood** from forest management for construction and fencing
- **[[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]]** potential for mulch, [[compost]], and fuel
- **Forest management** is essential: forests can produce continuous yields of firewood, [[gadd-fungal-biodegradation-concrete-building-materials]], and animal fodder while improving soil and water cycles

### Energy Resources

- **Sun** — aspect, solar access hours, microclimate warmth
- **Wind** — prevailing directions, velocity, and seasonal patterns
- **Water** — springs, streams, runoff, and groundwater availability
- **Geothermal** or thermal mass opportunities in rock outcrops
- **Slope and aspect** — north/south orientation and grade determine solar gain and drainage

### Water as a Resource

Mollison treats water as a **salable mineral resource** — it has quantifiable commercial value. When acquiring property, the designer should assess water rights, flow rates, and storage potential. Water is simultaneously the most critical and most frequently underestimated resource on any site.

## Extrinsic Resources

Resources beyond the property boundary are equally important to identify:

- **Market demands** — what can be produced and sold locally or regionally
- **Waste products** from nearby industry or urban areas that can serve as inputs (manure, organic matter, green waste)
- **Unfulfilled needs** in the surrounding community that the site could address
- **Social infrastructure** — neighbors, skills, and cooperative potential

## Securing Resource Rights

Mollison advises designers to actively **take out rights to resources** on and near the property:

- **Wind power** — register or lease wind rights before others claim them
- **Clay and mineral extraction** — establish legal rights to valuable earth resources
- **Water rights** — ensure legal access to surface and groundwater
- **Timber and foraging rights** on adjacent public or common land where possible

These rights are assets that appreciate over time and protect the system's long-term viability.

## Property Boundaries and Initial Site Analysis

The first physical task is to **establish property boundaries** precisely. Walk the boundary lines, understand the legal description, and note where fences, roads, and natural features intersect. Initial site analysis involves mapping all the resource layers identified above onto a single base map.

**Observation over time** is essential. A single visit reveals only a snapshot; the designer needs to understand seasonal patterns — where water flows in winter, where shade falls in summer, where frost pockets form, where animals move. Repeated visits across different weather conditions and times of day build the deep site literacy that enables sound design decisions.

## Two Design Positions

Mollison distinguishes two fundamentally different starting conditions:

### Retrofit Design

Working with an **existing property** that already has structures, roads, and established patterns. The designer must assess what works, what must change, and how to incrementally improve the system without catastrophic disruption. Most real-world permaculture work is retrofit.

### Greenfield Design

Starting from a **bare or undeveloped site** where the designer has maximum freedom to place elements optimally. This is the ideal teaching scenario but rarely the practical reality.

## Access Road Design

Access roads should follow **keyline principles** — running along contour or with a gentle grade (approximately 1:20 fall) to distribute water across the landscape rather than concentrating it in erosion channels. Keyline roads double as water-harvesting swales and provide access to all zones of the property.

## House Site Selection

The house site is the most permanent and consequential decision. [[cannabis-breeding-traits-selection-criteria-clarke]] include:

- **Solar access** — maximum winter sun exposure
- **Protection from wind** — avoid exposed ridges and wind tunnels
- **Drainage** — well-drained elevated positions, never in frost pockets or flood zones
- **Views and outlook** — psychological benefit of a pleasant prospect
- **Proximity to water** — close enough for practical use, far enough to avoid flooding
- **Access** — reasonable road access without excessive grading

## Type-One Errors

Mollison warns against **type-one errors** — irreversible mistakes that doom the entire system:

- **Siting on ridges** — exposed to wind, lightning, and fire with no water retention
- **Wind tunnels** — positioning structures in natural airflow corridors that accelerate fire and chill
- **Fire funnels** — narrow valleys or saddles where fire moves with catastrophic speed
- **Siting in the bush** — placing the house within dense vegetation creates extreme fire risk and eliminates solar access, garden space, and ventilation

### The Conservationist's Tragedy

Mollison describes the pattern of well-meaning people buying **wildlife-rich scrub** and placing their house within it. They then defend the bush around their home — exactly the vegetation that threatens them with fire, blocks their light, and harbors vermin. The result is a conflict between the desire to preserve nature and the reality of living safely within it. The solution is to site the house in a cleared, designed zone and preserve the bush elsewhere.

## Alternative Intensive Approaches

For small properties or urban situations, Mollison proposes highly intensive strategies:

- **Glasshouses and polytunnels** — extending growing seasons and creating controlled microclimates
- **Enclosed eighth-acre plots** — entire food systems designed within intensive, fenced, and irrigated small areas that can out-produce extensive acreage

These intensive systems demonstrate that [[permaculture-principles]] scale down as effectively as they scale up — the design logic remains identical regardless of area.

## Zone One: The Core of Daily Attention

**Zone one** is the area immediately surrounding the house — the garden of daily visitation. It contains the most intensively managed elements: culinary herbs, salad greens, frequently harvested vegetables, seedling nurseries, and small livestock. Everything in zone one is within a few steps of the kitchen door.

### Zone One Pond Systems

A small **pond in zone one** serves multiple functions: irrigation water, microclimate moderation, [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] species, and aesthetic value. Mollison specifically recommends stocking ponds with **stratified tadpole populations** — different frog species breed at different times of year, ensuring continuous predation on mosquito larvae and other insect pests. This [[beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]] eliminates the need for chemical interventions.

## Key Principles

- **Observe before designing** — let the land teach you what it needs
- **Secure resource rights early** — they are the foundation of long-term abundance
- **Avoid type-one errors** — permanent structures demand permanent decisions
- **Start from the house and work outward** — zone one is the anchor of the entire system
- **Design for redundancy** — every element should serve multiple functions
- **Think in systems** — no element exists in isolation; everything connects
