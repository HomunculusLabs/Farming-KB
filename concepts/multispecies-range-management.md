# Multispecies Range Management

Multispecies range management is a [[permaculture]] strategy that leverages the complementary feeding behaviours of multiple animal species to achieve far greater overall production from rangeland than any single-species approach could deliver. Rather than dedicating range to cattle alone, a judicious polyculture of grazers and browsers operating at different heights and niches transforms rangeland into a highly productive, resilient protein-producing system.

## Browse Equivalents and Cow Browse Days

Graziers conventionally measure range productivity in **cow browse days** — the number of days a single cow can forage on a given unit area. This metric provides a useful baseline for comparing the productive capacity of different species on the same land.

Mollison provides a striking equivalence table for one European cow browse year:

| Species | Individuals Equivalent to 1 Cow Year |
|---|---|
| European cow | 1 |
| Sheep | 6 |
| Kangaroo | 18 |
| Antelope jackrabbit | 164 |
| Blacktailed jackrabbit | 266 |
| Ground squirrel | 385 |

These figures reveal that many smaller animals can be supported on the same forage base as a single cow, and because they feed at different browse levels, they are not directly competing. This stratification of the grazing niche — from ground-level foragers like squirrels to mid-level browsers like sheep to the taller kangaroo — is the foundational principle of polyculture grazing.

## Protein Conversion Advantages of Smaller Species

Raw forage consumption is only part of the productivity equation. The critical metric for food systems is **assimilable protein yield** — how much of what an animal eats is converted into usable protein for human consumption. Here, smaller species dramatically outperform cattle.

- **Kangaroo**: 58% assimilable protein
- **Cattle**: 35% assimilable protein

The 23-percentage-point advantage means that per unit of forage consumed, kangaroos produce roughly 66% more usable protein than cattle. When combined with the fact that smaller species can be **continually harvested** rather than requiring the long growth cycles of cattle, the cumulative protein advantage becomes even more significant. Jackrabbits, with their rapid reproduction rates, can similarly be harvested on a rolling basis, converting range forage into protein far more efficiently than a herd of cows.

From a purely protein-production standpoint, well-managed kangaroo or jackrabbit systems are substantially more productive per hectare than cattle ranching, especially in arid and semi-arid environments where these species are naturally adapted.

## Species Balance and Range Protection

The stability of [[rangeland-ecology-and-multispecies-grazing]] systems depends on **population balance**. Any single species that is not harvested — or whose natural predators have been removed — will expand unchecked until it damages the rangeland beyond recovery. This is one of the central failures of single-species ranching: without predation pressure or managed harvesting, herbivore populations grow until they exceed the [[fukuoka-textdoc-land-requirements-per-diet-type-carrying-capacity]] of the land.

In a well-designed multispecies system, each species constrains the others indirectly through competitive browsing at different levels, while human management (harvesting, culling, or predator reintroduction) keeps populations within sustainable bounds. The removal of even one balancing element — whether a predator or a competing browser — can trigger cascading ecological damage.

## Insect Plagues from Range Depletion

When overgrazing degrades rangeland vegetation, the consequences extend well beyond the loss of plant cover. Stoddart (1955) documented a **direct correlation between range depletion and grasshopper outbreaks** in Canadian rangelands. The mechanism is straightforward: as perennial grasses are destroyed by overgrazing, bare soil expands, microclimates shift, and the conditions that favour insect population explosions are created.

Rangelands infested with grasshoppers, plague locusts, or Mormon crickets can lose up to **67% of their browse** to insect consumption — a devastating secondary impact that compounds the original damage from overgrazing. This creates a feedback loop: degraded range produces insect plagues, which further degrade the range, which invites more plagues.

## Integrated Insect Control Through Insectivores

Permaculture treats insect plagues not as a catastrophe but as a **resource to be harvested**. Insects are themselves an excellent protein source, and a range of domesticated species can efficiently convert plague insects into usable food:

- **Fish** in farm dams and ponds adjacent to rangeland
- **Guinea fowl** — aggressive insect foragers well-suited to range conditions
- **Turkeys** — large-bodied insectivores that thrive on open range

These species serve a dual function: they provide pest control by consuming grasshoppers and locusts, and they convert those insects into high-quality protein for human use. The system transforms a problem (insect plague) into a solution (increased protein harvest).

The nutritional value of harvested insects is substantial. In Botswana, the drought-season harvest of locusts was calculated to have **four times the food value by weight** of stored grains, making locust collection a genuinely significant food strategy in marginal environments.

## Water Infiltration and Vegetation Cover

Vegetation cover is the single most important factor determining whether rainwater infiltrates into the soil or runs off the surface carrying topsoil with it. Mollison cites specific infiltration data:

| Vegetation Cover | Water Infiltration Rate |
|---|---|
| 5% cover | 1.2% |
| 15% cover | 1.3% |
| 25% cover | 1.7% |
| 35% cover | 2.4% |

The relationship is non-linear: as cover increases from sparse (5%) to moderate (35%), infiltration doubles. Below a critical threshold, almost all rainfall is lost to runoff. A minimum of **65–70% plant cover** is required to effectively [[query-how-do-i-garden-on-a-slope-and-prevent-erosion]]. This has profound implications for grazing management: any stocking rate that reduces cover below this threshold guarantees soil loss.

## Soil Pore Space and Trampling Damage

Livestock trampling progressively compacts soil, destroying the pore spaces that allow water infiltration and root penetration. The degradation follows a clear gradient:

| Grazing Intensity | Soil Pore Space | Rain Infiltration Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Slight grazing | 68.1% | 44%/hr |
| Overgrazed | 51.1% | 21.6%/hr |
| Depleted | 46.5% | 8.2%/hr |

Overgrazing reduces pore space by 17 percentage points and cuts infiltration by more than half. Fully depleted rangeland retains less than half the pore space of lightly grazed land and accepts rainwater at only one-fifth the rate. This compaction is largely irreversible on human timescales, making prevention far more important than remediation.

## Runoff and Erosion

When water cannot infiltrate, it becomes runoff — and runoff is the primary agent of topsoil loss. Runoff water may carry **6–10% silt by volume**, meaning that every significant rainfall event on degraded range physically removes measurable quantities of the soil resource. Over years and decades, this translates into metres of soil loss from hillslopes and the silting of waterways and dams downstream.

The relationship between [[gadd-mycelial-network-resilience-grazing-pressure]], soil compaction, reduced infiltration, and accelerated erosion forms the central destructive cycle that multispecies range management is designed to break.

## Fencing Strategy: Valley to Ridge

The physical design of fencing on rangeland has a significant effect on erosion patterns, particularly from cattle. Mollison recommends that fencing should **fall from valleys to ridges** — meaning fences run perpendicular to the slope, with paddock boundaries extending from the valley floor up to the ridge line.

This arrangement prevents cattle from concentrating along watercourses (where they cause bank erosion and water pollution) and instead distributes their grazing pressure more evenly across the slope. By controlling where animals can move, valley-to-ridge fencing ensures that valley floors — the most productive and ecologically sensitive zones — are not trampled into bare ground while ridgeland is underutilised.

The primary function of rangelands, Mollison emphasises, is not meat production per se but the **preservation of upland soils and water** for more intensive use downstream. Grazing is the means by which this preservation is funded, not the end purpose of the land.

## Game Species Management

In integrated permaculture systems, wild game species are managed alongside domestic livestock, adding both diversity and resilience. Management techniques include:

- **Drift traps** — funneling animals into enclosed areas using natural [[desert-landscape-features-and-erosion-landforms]]
- **Yard traps at waterholes** — capturing animals at concentrated watering points
- **Baited box traps** — targeted trapping of specific species
- **Cage traps and set nets** — for smaller game species
- **Knockout drugs** — chemical immobilisation for safe capture and marking

Population monitoring relies on **marking techniques** including dyes, collars, and ear tags, which allow managers to estimate population sizes and track demographic trends. In fully integrated systems, as many as **40–80 species** may be farmed or managed simultaneously, creating a diverse and resilient protein production system that mimics the complexity of natural ecosystems.

## Design Principles Summary

1. **Species diversity** across browse levels maximises total range productivity
2. **Smaller species** offer superior protein [[energy-conversion-efficiency]]
3. **Population balance** through harvesting or predation prevents range degradation
4. **Insect plagues** are converted into protein via integrated insectivores
5. **Vegetation cover above 65–70%** is non-negotiable for erosion prevention
6. **Fencing from valley to ridge** protects watercourses and distributes grazing
7. **Soil and water preservation** is the primary function of rangeland
8. **Game species integration** adds resilience and up to 80 managed species
