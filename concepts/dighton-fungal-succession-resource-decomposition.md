# Fungal Succession During Resource Decomposition

The decomposition of organic resources by fungi is not a static process
but a dynamic succession of species adapted to different stages of
[[singh-basidiomycete-fungi-in-petroleum-hydrocarbon-cleanup]] including Marasmius and Collybia. The fourth stage
features entry of soil arthropods and invasion by [[dynamic-accumulator-bracken-fern-potash]]
(Pteridium aquilinum) litter. Changes progressed from lesion-forming
Rhizographus and Aureobasidium on standing dead litter, through
colonization by basidiomycetes related to cellulose and lignin loss.
The consequential decrease in C:N ratio from approximately 200:1 to
30:1 accompanied this succession, reflecting the shift from
carbohydrate-rich to more recalcitrant resources.

## The Swift Model of Succession

Swift et al. (1979) modeled changes from "sugar" fungi to
basidiomycetes in relation to resource changes and climatic stresses.
During initial decomposition, carbohydrate components serve as energy
sources until the C:nutrient ratio approaches that of the decomposer
organism (around 15:1 for P and 6:1 for N in fungi). Only then does net
conversion of organic to inorganic nutrient (net mineralization) occur.

Initial resource structure is chemically heterogeneous, supporting
variety of fungal species. As decomposition proceeds, recalcitrant
chemicals remain that can be degraded only by fungi producing necessary
enzymes. Diversity thus decreases. From these studies, windows of
opportunity for decomposition can be determined and decomposition rates
mapped.

## Changes with Ecosystem Succession

During seral succession from herbaceous to forest ecosystems, dominant
plant species and forms change, altering the diversity and complexity of
resources entering the decomposer system. Initial seral stages receive
high-quality resources consisting mainly of cellulose with high C:N
ratio and low lignin content. Following forest canopy closure, woody
resources and more recalcitrant leaf litters dominate with high lignin
content and low C:N ratios, decomposing at slower rates.

Along with changes in dominant fungal species during ecosystem
succession, the degree of interaction between fungi and animals
increases. More intimate associations develop between fungi and fauna in
exploitation of recalcitrant plant residues. The cascade of
decomposition is thus related to colonization of individual resource
units.
