# Mushroom Cooking and Culinary Uses

Palmer's "About Mushrooms" (1894) contains detailed practical advice on
preparing and cooking [[edible-wild-mushrooms-by-region]], grounded in his personal experience
of eating them "three times a day" during the season. He was sharply
critical of prevailing cook-book practices that disguised the natural
flavor of fungi.

## The Two Proper Cooking Methods

Palmer insisted there are only two correct ways to cook mushrooms:

### 1. Broiling

"By far the majority are best broiled on a fine-wire gridiron."

- Sprinkle with salt
- If the species is well known as esculent, add red pepper
- Butter as the fire browns them
- Best for: honey-colored mushroom (Agaricus melleus), elm-tree
  mushroom (Agaricus ulmarius), and any others that taste raw of
  grass or trees

### 2. Stewing in Milk

"Stew them in milk, exactly as you would make an oyster stew."

- Best for: meadow mushroom ([[agaricus-arvensis]]), and softer species
- The milk tempers strong flavors and creates a rich, creamy base
- This method was considered particularly suitable for American
  palates unaccustomed to strong fungal flavors

Palmer specifically praised the fairy ring mushroom ([[marasmius-oreades]])
as versatile, noting it can be kept "any length of time by drying; it
expands again the moment it is put in water"

## Nutritional Character

Palmer's nutritional observations reflect the limited scientific
understanding of his era but capture genuine insights:

- Mushrooms "furnish a natural alkali which in some systems is greatly
  needed"
- "No better substitute for meat than fungus can be found"
- "Neither its odor when cooking, nor the gravy it makes, resembles
  any form of vegetable food. This is but natural, since the plant
  absorbs oxygen from the air, after the manner of animals"
- "In taste, character, and perhaps in their value to the human
  economy, they occupy a place between animal and vegetable food,
  approximating to the former more than to the latter"

These observations presage modern nutritional science confirming that
mushrooms are indeed nutritionally unique, providing umami compounds,
B vitamins, and bioavailable minerals in concentrations uncommon among
plant foods.

## International Culinary Context

Palmer noted that while Americans considered mushrooms valueless, other
nations used them extensively:

- Chinese, Russians, Germans, French, and Italians all had robust
  mushroom culinary traditions
- Delicious Boleti had been imported to American markets by European
  immigrants
- Italians in America were observed "making a harvest" of Boleti in
  local forests, using the same varieties they ate at home
- The Clavaria family was described as "more delicate food than
  cauliflower, nicer in soup or stew than vermicelli"

This cross-cultural comparison was central to Palmer's mission of
convincing Americans that mushrooms were not poisonous curiosities but
valuable, delicious food worthy of culinary attention.

## Freshness Requirements

Palmer was adamant about the importance of freshness:

- Mushrooms must be eaten in "their freshest state"
- The common mushroom "in a partly decomposed state often generates
  hydrogen gas in such quantity as to produce nausea and vomiting"
- They "change very rapidly after gathering"
- "None but young specimens with substance too solid to admit of
  fermentation should ever be eaten"

These guidelines remain sound advice for modern foragers. Proper
identification and freshness assessment are the two most important
safety considerations in wild mushroom consumption.

## Species-Specific Recommendations

Palmer provided cooking guidance tailored to individual species:

- Meadow mushroom ([[agaricus-arvensis]]): best stewed in milk, with
  bread to dip in the broth
- Honey-colored mushroom: best broiled with butter and salt
- Elm-tree mushroom: best broiled, robust flavor stands up to high
  heat
- Fairy ring mushroom ([[marasmius-oreades]]): versatile, dries well
  for winter use, good in soups and stews
- Boleti: excellent when properly prepared, with flesh that is firm
  and meaty when fresh

He cautioned that some species required careful preparation to remove
bitter or astringent qualities, and that overcooking was a common error
that ruined texture and flavor alike.

## Preparation Techniques

Palmer described several preparation methods that deserve attention:

- Peeling was recommended for only the largest mushrooms; smaller
  specimens were simply wiped clean with a damp cloth
- Cutting rather than tearing was preferred for even cooking
- Salt was applied at the start of cooking to draw out moisture and
  concentrate flavor
- Butter was the preferred cooking fat; Palmer considered other fats
  inferior for [[magic-mushroom-preparation-and-consumption-methods]]
- Lemon juice was sometimes added to prevent discoloration in species
  that browned rapidly when cut

## Palmer's Critique of Contemporary Cooking

Palmer was deeply critical of how American cookbooks treated mushrooms
in his era. He argued that elaborate preparations with cream sauces,
heavy spicing, and prolonged cooking destroyed the delicate natural
flavor that made each species unique.
