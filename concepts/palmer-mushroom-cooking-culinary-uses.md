# Mushroom Cooking and Culinary Uses

Palmer's *About Mushrooms* (1894) contains detailed practical advice on preparing and cooking wild mushrooms, grounded in his personal experience of eating them "three times a day" during the season. He was sharply critical of prevailing cook-book practices that disguised the natural flavor of fungi.

## The Two Proper Cooking Methods

Palmer insisted there are only two correct ways to cook mushrooms:

### 1. Broiling

"By far the majority are best broiled on a fine-wire gridiron."

- Sprinkle with salt
- If the species is well known as esculent, add red pepper
- Butter as the fire browns them
- Best for: honey-colored mushroom (*Agaricus melleus*), elm-tree mushroom (*Agaricus ulmarius*), and any others that taste raw of grass or trees

### 2. Stewing in Milk

"Stew them in milk, exactly as you would make an oyster stew."

- Best for: meadow mushroom (*[[agaricus-arvensis]]*), coprinus (*[[marasmius-oreades]]*) can be kept "any length of time by drying; it expands again the moment it is put in water"

## Nutritional Character

- Mushrooms "furnish a natural alkali which in some systems is greatly needed"
- "No better substitute for meat than fungus can be found"
- "Neither its odor when cooking, nor the gravy it makes, resembles any form of vegetable food. This is but natural, since the plant absorbs oxygen from the air, after the manner of animals"
- "In taste, character, and perhaps in their value to the human economy, they occupy a place between animal and vegetable food, approximating to the former more than to the latter"

## International Culinary Context

Palmer noted that while Americans considered mushrooms valueless, other nations used them extensively:
- Chinese, Russians, Germans, French, and Italians
- Delicious Boleti had been imported to American markets
- Italians in America were observed "making a harvest" of Boleti in local forests, using the same varieties they ate at home
- The Clavaria family was described as "more delicate food than cauliflower, nicer in soup or stew than vermicelli"

## Freshness Requirements

- Mushrooms must be eaten in "their freshest state"
- The common mushroom "in a partly decomposed state often generates hydrogen gas in such quantity as to produce nausea and vomiting"
- They "change very rapidly after gathering"
- "None but young specimens with substance too solid to admit of fermentation should ever be eaten"
