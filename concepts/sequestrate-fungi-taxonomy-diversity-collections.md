# Sequestrate Fungi — Taxonomy, Diversity, Herbarium Collections, and Species Richness

[[sequestrate-fungi]] are a polyphyletic assemblage characterized by enclosed, hypogeous or semi-hypogeous fruiting bodies that do not actively discharge spores. They rely on animal vectors for dispersal and are predominantly ectomycorrhizal.

## Taxonomy, Diversity, and Distribution

Sequestrate fungi span two phyla: **Zygomycota** (class Zygomycetes) and **Dikaryomycota** (classes Ascomycetes and Holobasidiomycetes). Family assignments have shifted substantially with DNA-based phylogenetic studies; many genera remain of uncertain placement and numerous undescribed genera await recognition.

## Herbarium Collections

Study began in Italy, where pre-Linnaean botanists were interested in truffles. **Carlo Vittadini** (1831, 1842) laid the foundations of modern generic concepts; his collections and those of **Oreste Mattirolo** are at the Botanical Institute, University of Torino.

## Live Culture Collections

Most sequestrate fungi grow poorly or not at all in culture — especially Ascomycetes, Zygomycetes, Russulaceae, and Cortinariaceae. *Rhizopogon* is a notable exception, growing well and being easy to isolate. Saprobic species are probably more culturable than mycorrhizal ones. Only two major collections exist: the **U.S. Forest Service PNW Research Station** (Oregon) and the **CSIRO Division of Forestry** (Australia). Cultures without voucher specimens have questionable identity due to the lack of reliable keys.

## Patterns of Species Richness and Endemism

### Exploration Status and Biogeographic Gradients

Well-explored regions: temperate Europe, western North America, New Zealand, temperate/tropical Australia, North African/west Asian deserts. The rest of the world is unexplored or scantily explored.

- Species richness is **greatest at subtropical to middle latitudes** in ectomycorrhizal forests, declining northward into boreal zones in the Northern Hemisphere. Southern Hemisphere forests are insufficiently explored for comparison.
- North America and Australia are richer than Europe, mirroring ectomycorrhizal host patterns. N–S mountain ranges in NA and Australia allowed ice-age migrations; Europe's E–W ranges (Alps, Pyrenees) impeded them — especially significant for animal-dispersed fungi.

### Habitat Correlates

- **Ectomycorrhizal host presence** is the strongest predictor of sequestrate fungal diversity; greater host genus diversity contributes.
- Moist forests > dry or very wet forests. Richness is reduced in rain forests and strongly xeric environments.
- *Pseudotsuga* forests (Pacific NW) and *Eucalyptus* forests (eastern Australia) are exceptionally rich.
- Mature/old-growth Pacific NW stands can harbor **100+ species**, far exceeding younger stands.

### Endemism

Endemism is common, driven by host specificity, animal dispersal dependence, narrow habitat requirements, and geographic barriers (oceans, deserts, mountains). Pines, Douglas fir, spruce, true firs, oaks, and *Eucalyptus* each harbor numerous host-specific species. However, **local endemism may be overestimated** — *Cazia flexiascus* was initially known from SW Oregon, then found 80 km north and later 1500 km south in S California.

## Global Assessment

No large geographic region has been adequately assessed. Stand-level assessments began only in the last 20 years (Oregon, Washington, northern California). An Australian effort over 13 years (20 trips, <400 person-days) yielded ~7,500 collections representing ~600 species — approximately **90% undescribed** — underscoring how profoundly sequestrate fungal diversity remains unknown.
