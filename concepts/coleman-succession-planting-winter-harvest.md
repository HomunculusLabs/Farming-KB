# Coleman Succession Planting Strategy for Winter Harvest

## Overview

Succession planting is one of the three foundational components of Coleman's winter harvest system, alongside cold-hardy vegetables and [[coleman-protected-cultivation]]. The concept is simple—sowing vegetables more than once during a season to provide continuous harvest—but the execution for [[coleman-beet-greens-cold-house-winter-production]] is fundamentally different from spring and summer succession planting. Understanding these differences is essential for anyone adapting Coleman's system to their own climate and operation. (see [[coleman-greenhouse-summer-crops]]).

## What Is Succession Planting?

### General Definition

Succession planting means sowing the same crop (or different crops) at multiple dates throughout a season to maintain a continuous supply of harvestable produce. Rather than planting everything at once and harvesting it all within a short window, succession planting staggers the maturity dates so that fresh vegetables are always available. (see [[coleman-weed-control]]).

### The Winter Difference

In spring and summer succession planting:
- Day length is increasing, so growth accelerates over time
- Temperatures are rising, supporting faster metabolism
- Later plantings catch up quickly because conditions improve
- The timing pressure is relatively low

In fall and winter succession planting, the situation is reversed:
- Day length is decreasing, so growth slows progressively
- Temperatures are dropping, further reducing growth rates
- Each successive planting takes longer to reach maturity
- Precise sowing dates are critical and highly crop-specific

## The "Second Spring" Concept

### Beginning Date

Coleman designates August 1 as the start of the "second spring"—the beginning of the planting season for winter harvest crops. This is counterintuitive for growers accustomed to thinking of August as mid-summer, but it reflects the biological reality that winter crops need to be well-established before the shortest days arrive.

### The Logic

Winter crops sown on August 1 experience:
- Approximately 14 hours of daylight for photosynthesis
- Warm soil temperatures that support rapid germination and early growth
- Two to three months of declining but still adequate day length
- Enough time to reach near-maturity before the 10-hour day threshold

### The 10-Hour Day Threshold

Coleman identifies the 10-hour day length as a critical milestone. Once days drop below 10 hours (which occurs around mid-November at his Maine latitude):
- Plant growth nearly ceases
- Existing crops are maintained in a dormant but harvestable state
- No new planting will reach harvestable size until days lengthen again in late January

This means all the growth that sustains the winter harvest must occur between August 1 and mid-November—a window of approximately 3.5 months.

## Crop-Specific Sowing Windows

### Fast-Maturing Crops (30-40 days)

Crops like radishes, arugula, and baby greens can be sown later in the fall because they require less time to reach maturity:
- Final sowing dates: mid-September to early October
- These crops provide harvests during the shoulder seasons (fall [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]])
- They may not produce through the depths of winter

### Medium-Maturing Crops (45-60 days)

Crops like lettuce, spinach, tatsoi, and mizuna need earlier sowing:
- Sowing window: August through mid-September
- These are the workhorse crops of the winter harvest
- Cut-and-come-again harvesting extends their productive period

### Slow-Maturing Crops (60-90+ days)

Crops like carrots, leeks, chicory, and endive require the earliest sowing:
- Sowing window: August 1 through late August
- These crops form the backbone of the mid-winter harvest
- They must be fully sized before the 10-hour day threshold

## The Never-Unplanted Bed Philosophy

### 24-Hour Turnaround

Coleman's goal is to never leave a greenhouse bed unplanted. Within 24 hours of harvesting a crop:
1. Crop residues are removed
2. The soil is re-prepared (light cultivation, compost amendment if needed)
3. The bed is replanted with the next succession
