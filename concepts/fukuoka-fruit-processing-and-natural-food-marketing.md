[[masanobu-fukuoka]] offered a scathing critique of the Japanese citrus industry's post-harvest processing system and the economics
of natural food distribution. His observations reveal how consumer preferences for appearance drive a cycle of chemical dependency,
economic hardship for farmers, and nutritional degradation. This critique extends to the broader failure of [[conventional-vs-organic-vs-regenerative|commercial agriculture]] and the paradoxes of the natural food market.

## The Mandarin Orange Processing Pipeline

Fukuoka traced what happens to Unshu mandarin oranges between harvest and market. The process involves five or six chemical
treatments beyond the fertilizers and sprays used during growth.

First, colour-accelerating chemicals are sprayed on trees so the fruit becomes fully coloured one week earlier. Whether the fruit is
sold a week before or after October 10th, the price either doubles or falls by half, so the farmer applies the chemicals. Second,
artificial sweeteners are applied because early-shipped fruit is not sweet enough. Although general chemical sweeteners are
prohibited, the specific spray used on citrus trees has not been specifically outlawed and almost everybody is using it.

Third, the fruit goes to a ripening room for gas treatment after harvest. Fourth, each piece is sent rolling several hundred yards
down a long conveyor to separate sizes. Bruising is common; the larger the sorting centre, the longer the fruit is bounced and
tumbled. Fifth, the mandarin oranges are washed and sprayed with preservatives. Sixth, a colouring agent is brushed on. Finally, a
paraffin wax solution is applied and the fruit polished to a glossy shine. "Nowadays fruit is really run through the mill." All of
this occurs because the consumer wants produce that looks "just a little more attractive."

## The Economic Trap of Incremental Adoption

Fukuoka had opposed introducing the American citrus processing system to Japan when he worked at the Yokohama Customs Office forty
years earlier. He predicted exactly what happened: once one co-op adopted a new process like waxing, higher profits attracted
imitators. Within two to three years the practice spread nationwide, competition drove prices down, and all that remained for
farmers was the burden of extra work and added costs.

This pattern is universal. One farm household takes up a new process and profits from the extra care and attention. Other co-ops
notice and adopt it. Fruit that is not treated no longer brings a high price. The competition brings prices down, and the farmer is
left with hard work and added costs. Now he must apply the wax.

The "good ones" selected for market average only 25 to 50 percent of the total crop. Even some of these are rejected by the co-op.
After working in the orchard all day, loading boxes, and carrying them to the sorting shed, the farmer works until eleven or twelve
at night picking over fruit one by one, keeping only those of perfect size and shape. Rejected fruit is sold for about half price to
be squeezed for juice. Net profit reaches two or three cents per pound at best.

## Out-of-Season Production and Its True Cost

Hothouse mandarins grown for summer shipment, when the fruit naturally ripens in late fall, fetch prices ten to twenty times higher
than seasonal fruit. Instead of the usual 10 to 15 cents per pound, outrageous prices of $0.80 to $1.75 per pound are paid. The
farmer invests thousands in equipment, fuel, and extra labour to meet this artificial demand.

Fukuoka asked: "If you ask how important it is for human beings to have this fruit a month earlier, the truth is that it is not
important at all, and money is not the only price paid for such indulgence." The same dynamic applies to hothouse vegetables. The
eggplants shipped in winter have no vitamins and the cucumbers no flavour because certain of the sun's rays cannot penetrate the
vinyl and glass enclosures. The technician's response is not to question whether people need winter eggplants but to research
artificial lighting systems, devoting entire careers to producing a hothouse eggplant with vitamins. Naturally, since such great
efforts have gone into producing this eggplant, and the vegetable is said to be high in nutritional value, it is tagged at an even
higher price and sells well. "If it is profitable, and if you can sell it, there can't be anything wrong with it."

## The Freshness Paradox

Fukuoka challenged the assumption that shiny, plump produce equals quality. Biologically, slightly shrivelled fruit is holding
respiration and energy consumption at the lowest possible level. Like a person in meditation, its metabolism, respiration, and
caloric consumption reach extremely low levels. Even if it fasts, the energy within is conserved. The food value is preserved
longest in this state.

It is a mistake to maintain mere appearance, as when shopkeepers sprinkle water on vegetables repeatedly. The vegetables look fresh
but their flavour and nutritional value deteriorate. Food that is not fresh can be sold because it looks fresh. Speaking
biologically, fruit in a slightly shrivelled state is the optimal state for preservation.

## Natural Food Marketing Experience

Fukuoka shipped 5,000 to 6,500 pounds of rice and 400 thirty-five-pound cartons of mandarin oranges to natural food stores annually.
The first year brought complaints about variable fruit size, dirty exterior, and sometimes shrivelled skin. He began packing in
cartons lettered "natural mandarins."

His core principle: "Since natural food can be produced with the least expense and effort, I reason that it should be sold at the
cheapest price." His fruit was the lowest priced in the Tokyo area while also being rated the most delicious by shopkeepers. This
year he had been asked to ship two or three times as much as before. The fruit was free of chemicals and tasted good.

## The Natural Food Pricing Problem

Fukuoka was furious when a Tokyo merchant sold his honey and mountain eggs at extravagant prices. He immediately stopped all
shipments, suspecting the merchant would also mix his rice with other rice to increase weight. His position was clear: "If a high
price is charged for natural food, it means that the merchant is taking excessive profits. Furthermore, if natural foods are
expensive, they become luxury foods and only rich people are able to afford them."

The consumer bias that natural food must be expensive works against widespread adoption. One retailer remarked that no one would buy
natural produce unless it was priced high. Fukuoka insisted the opposite: natural food should be available locally at a reasonable
price. "If the consumer will only adjust to the idea that low prices do not mean that the food is not natural, then everyone will
begin thinking in the right direction."

## The Economic Case for Natural Farming

The economics favour natural farming because expenses are lower. Growing fruit without chemicals, fertilizer, or cultivation costs
less, and the farmer's net profit is therefore higher. Fukuoka packed fruit practically unsorted, shipped it, and got to bed early.
Other farmers in his neighbourhood, working very hard with chemicals, barely broke even.

Chemical fruit growers had been driven into an extremely tight economic pinch, making natural food production more attractive. No
matter how hard the average farmer works applying chemicals, colouring, waxing, and so on, he can only sell his fruit for a price
that barely covers expenses. The feeling was growing that there was nothing strange about natural food production, and farmers were
ready for change.

## Commercial Agriculture Will Fail

Fukuoka opposed commercial agriculture from the beginning. In Japanese agriculture, fertilizer, feed, equipment, and chemicals are
purchased at prices fixed abroad, while selling prices are also fixed. The farmer's income is at the mercy of forces beyond his
control. "The farmer would do much better by growing the food he needs without thinking about making money."

He described commercial vegetables as "a watery chemical concoction of nitrogen, phosphorous, and potash, with a little help from
the seed." Commercial chicken eggs are "nothing more than a mixture of synthetic feed, chemicals, and hormones... a man-made
synthetic in the shape of an egg." The farmer who produces such things, he called a manufacturer, not a farmer. In olden times,
agriculture was said to be closer to the source of things than trade or manufacturing, and the farmer was "the cupbearer of the
gods."

## Local Chickens vs. Commercial Layers

The Japanese farmer who produces white leghorn eggs works within a system where the birds are cooped up in small cages, their feet
never touching the ground, pumped full of antibiotics and fed imported formula feed. After one year the leghorn is exhausted and
cast aside. Meanwhile, two local hens and one rooster released on the mountainside became twenty-four birds in one year. When it
seemed that few eggs were being laid, the local birds were busy raising chickens. The local birds scratch freely, feed on seeds and
insects, and lay delicious natural eggs.

## The Co-op and Government Policy

The Agricultural Co-op and government agencies are structurally unable to advocate for change. If crops were grown without
chemicals, fertilizer, or machinery, the giant chemical companies would become unnecessary and the government's Agricultural Co-op
Agency would collapse. To do away with machinery and chemicals would bring about a complete change in economic and social
structures.

At a pollution conference, when Fukuoka spoke out about this structural conflict, the chairman told him, "Mr. Fukuoka, you are
upsetting the conference with your remarks," and shut his mouth for him. The fundamental problem was that government agencies have
no intention of stopping pollution. All aspects of the food pollution problem must be solved simultaneously, which is impossible
when each party addresses only its own narrow interest.

## The Farmer's Spiritual Decline

Fukuoka connects the economic trap to a deeper spiritual impoverishment. The farmer who once spent January through March hunting
rabbits in the hills has seen his New Year's holiday shrink to three days. In the old days, even in a small village, twenty or
thirty people had composed haiku and presented them as offerings at the village shrine. Now there is no one in the village with
enough time to write poetry. During the cold winter months, only a few villagers can find time to sneak out for a day or two to go
after rabbits.

This is what Fukuoka means when he says that agriculture has become poor and weak spiritually; it is concerning itself only with
material development. The human economics that value money and a high standard of living above all else have brought about a sick
environment and a sick agriculture.
