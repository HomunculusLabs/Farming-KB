## Cloning from Flowering Plants (Related to Re-Vegging)

Cervantes covers cloning from flowering females, which is closely related to re-vegging:

- "You can clone a favorite flowering plant, but it is difficult"
- "Clones take longer to root, and results are not always the best"
- "Powerful flowering hormones must be reversed, and rooting hormone signals must be sent"
- "Now is the time to give plants 24 hours of light to signal them to grow"
- "Cut clones from the lower green branch tips"
- "Cut a one to two-inch-long (3-5 cm) stem. Trim off flowers and lower leaves"
- "Keep two or three green leaves"
- "The earlier in the flowering stage cuttings are taken, the more rapid the rooting and the re-vegetation rate"
- Clones with yellowed leaves have "survival chances [that] diminish exponentially"

## The Rejuvenation Scenario

Cervantes provides a detailed practical example:

1. A grower produces a beautiful crop of named females
2. Instead of starting from seed, he leaves "a few leaves and buds on the harvested stubby branches"
3. He induces vegetative growth "with 18-hour days and 6-hour nights the day after harvesting"
4. A month later, he takes "many clones from these original favorite females"
5. The original mothers are induced to flower "a month after the clones were taken"
6. Original harvest: January 1st. Second harvest: April 1st (3 months later)
7. The second harvest weighed less, and buds were smaller
8. "Taking clones from rejuvenated plants also diffuses hormones and severely stresses plants"

## Nutritional Requirements During Re-Vegging

Cervantes recommends "an increased dose of high-nitrogen fertilizer to promote green, leafy growth." The plant needs nitrogen to rebuild its vegetative structure. During re-vegetation, the plant shifts from flowering nutrients (higher phosphorus and potassium) back to vegetative nutrients (higher nitrogen).

## Challenges and Limitations

- Second harvests typically produce less weight and smaller buds than the first
- Plants are often root-bound from the first cycle, which limits second-cycle growth
