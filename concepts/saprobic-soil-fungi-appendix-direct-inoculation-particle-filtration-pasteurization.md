# Saprobic Soil Fungi — Appendix: Direct Inoculation, Particle Filtration, and Ethanol Pasteurization

## Soil Collection (Appendix)

Proper soil collection is the foundation of any fungal isolation study. Recommended sample sizes are 10–30 g of soil per individual sample, with 6–20 samples collected per site to capture adequate diversity across microhabitats. The soil-coring tube must be sterilized in 70% ethanol between sampling locations to prevent cross-contamination between sites.

Soil is collected either from an exposed soil profile or from the mineral soil surface; in both cases, any overlying litter and humus layer should be removed before sampling to ensure that the mineral soil itself is the material collected. Each sample is placed in a labeled sterile bag or vial, transported in an ice chest or cooler, and refrigerated upon arrival at the laboratory. Samples should be plated within 4 days of collection or frozen for long-term storage of 6–9 months (Christensen 1969).

**Regulatory considerations:** Phytosanitary permits are required for foreign soils entering many countries. In the United States, USDA/APHIS regulations govern the import, transport, and disposal of foreign soil materials. Proper storage and autoclave-mediated disposal are mandatory to prevent accidental introduction of exotic fungal species into native ecosystems.

## Tools, Equipment, and Work Area

A well-organized work area is essential for efficient fungal isolation and minimizes contamination risk:

- **Work surfaces:** A clean bench or laminar flow hood is used for routine media pouring and seeding. A class-2 biosafety hood is required when handling foreign soils to contain potential pathogens and meet regulatory biosafety requirements.
- **Incubation:** A refrigerated incubator maintains the cool temperatures (15–20°C) preferred by many soil fungi. Temperature consistency is important for reproducible isolation results.
- **Media preparation:** Measuring and weighing equipment, an autoclave for sterilization, glassware (flasks, bottles, Petri dishes), pipettes, and peristaltic pumps for dispensing media evenly across plates.
- **Microscopy:** A bright-field compound microscope combined with a high-quality dissecting microscope (50–120× magnification). Epistage and substage lighting provide optimal illumination for observing colony morphology, hyphal characteristics, and spore features.
- **Dissection tools:** Insect pins and hypodermic needles (26–28 gauge) are essential for hyphal tip transfers. Tools are flame-sterilized between uses; pins are disinfected by dipping in 70–95% ethanol followed by stabbing into sterile agar to cool and remove residual solvent.
- **Ergonomics:** A comfortable workspace with adjustable seating, good lighting, and identification guides within easy reach reduces fatigue during long isolation sessions and improves accuracy.

## Direct Inoculation Protocols

### Drechsler's Method

Drechsler's method is the simplest and most widely used direct inoculation technique. A few milligrams of fresh, finely divided soil or humus are sprinkled directly onto the surface of water agar, cornmeal agar, or carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) agar. Plates are incubated at 15–20°C; antibacterial antibiotics may be added optionally to suppress bacterial growth.

Emerging hyphal tips are transferred daily to fresh media to obtain pure cultures. Mitosporic and zygomycetous species frequently sporulate under these minimal-nutrient conditions, facilitating identification. Moist-spored conidia are picked up by touching them with a fine needle; dry-spored conidia are transferred using a needle moistened with sterile glycerol or 0.2% agar solution to ensure adhesion.

### Soil Swabbing and Stamping

Developed by Hunter-Cevera and Belt (1992), these methods work particularly well with low propagule densities, such as those found in desert soils and other arid environments where direct sprinkling may yield too few colonies.

**Swabbing:** Pulverized or sifted soil is spread thinly in the lid of a sterile Petri dish. A cotton swab is touched to the soil surface and then streaked across the surface of DRBC, CMC, water agar, or malt-cyclosporin agar plates. This transfers a small, manageable number of propagules to each plate.

**Stamping:** A cotton ball or felt pad is pressed lightly onto the soil surface, then used to stamp the entire surface of agar in 6–10 plates in clockwise succession. The first 2–3 plates typically receive an overwhelming inoculum, but later plates receive progressively fewer propagules, yielding manageable colony densities suitable for isolation. Plates are incubated at 15–20°C, reviewed daily, and hyphal tips are transferred as colonies emerge.

### Warcup Soil Plates

Warcup's method (1950; Parkinson 1994) embeds soil particles within a thin agar layer, providing a semi-natural growth environment. Molten medium cooled to approximately 45°C is prepared. A small quantity (0.2–5.0 mg) of finely pulverized soil is placed in a sterile Petri dish and dispersed with 0.5 ml of sterile water or a few sterile sand grains. The soil is then overlaid with 8–10 ml of molten medium while the dish is gently agitated to distribute particles evenly throughout the agar.

The resulting thin agar layer facilitates colony dissection from beneath the surface. Young submerged colonies are excised by cutting agar blocks from below, which can then be transferred to fresh media. Plates are incubated at 15–20°C, reviewed daily, and hyphal tips are transferred to fresh media as colonies develop.

### Suspension Plating

Suspension plating follows a simplified 10-step protocol suitable for systematic soil surveys:

1. Collect and prepare soil samples as described above
2. Perform preliminary plating to estimate propagule density for each sample
3. Prepare a dilution series based on density estimates to achieve 20–50 colonies per plate
4. Sterilize isolation medium by autoclaving
5. Prepare sterile water blanks for serial dilution
6. Weigh soil aliquots (typically 1 g) and add to dilution blanks; vortex or shake thoroughly
7. Spread 1 ml of the final suspension across 3–6 plates using a sterile glass spreader
8. Incubate plates in darkness at 15–20°C
9. Isolate individual colonies as they emerge, transferring hyphal tips to fresh media
10. Prepare tube slants (PDA, malt-extract agar, or Czapek yeast extract agar) for sorting; sort isolates after 7–10 days of growth; lyophilize representative isolates for long-term preservation

## Particle Filtration

Particle filtration isolates fungi from specific soil particle size fractions, improving the recovery of fungi associated with organic debris and mineral particles that may be missed by dilution-based methods.

### Apparatus

The apparatus consists of a micro-sieve set (Bel-Art Products) featuring a stack of 4–5 interchangeable polypropylene cylinders (51 mm internal diameter). Three meshes are arranged in descending order: a brass 1 mm mesh (excludes large fragments), a polypropylene 210 μm mesh, and a polypropylene 105 μm mesh. Polypropylene inserts are supported by a brass 2 mm mesh underneath, and pressure from interlocking cylinders secures the entire assembly. The assembled device is held under a faucet by a burette clamp mounted on a ring-stand, allowing hands-free washing.

### Protocol

The 10-step particle filtration protocol proceeds as follows:

1. Collect and prepare soil samples as described in the soil collection section
2. Disinfect the filtration device thoroughly using ethanol, bleach, or peracetic acid between uses
3. Pulverize air-dried soils to break up aggregates and release adhering propagules
4. Load soil onto the uppermost brass prescreen (1 mm mesh)
5. Wash for 10 minutes with a gentle 4–6 L water spray to separate particles by size
6. Separate the cylinder sections and recover each mesh fraction individually
7. Transfer the 105 μm filter with retained particles into a 50 ml polystyrene centrifuge tube
8. Wash particles with 50 ml sterile water to remove residual fine sediment
9. Dilute to a 20:1 ratio of water to particles (with 0.2% agar or CMC added as a wetting agent)
10. Pipette 0.1 ml aliquots onto 5–10 plates; incubate and isolate emerging colonies

### Troubleshooting

- **Mesh clogging:** Overloaded meshes may clog during washing; occasional gentle stirring of the soil on the prescreen helps maintain flow and ensures complete separation.
- **Vacuum assistance:** A vacuum pump with a side-armed flask (2–4 L) can be used to trap wash water and speed processing.
- **Effluent disposal:** All effluent must be autoclaved before discarding to comply with biosafety regulations.
- **Incomplete washing:** Insufficient washing produces stray colonies not associated with particles, complicating interpretation. Fine-textured soils (e.g., clays) may wash through the finest mesh entirely, reducing recovery from that fraction.

## Ethanol and Phenol Pasteurization

### Overview

Partial sterilization methods — including steam, dry heat, and chemical treatments — selectively favor the recovery of ascomycete fungi from soil. These methods suppress rapidly growing zygomycetes and common mitosporic fungi while permitting hardier ascomycete propagules (ascospores, sclerotia, cleistothecia) to survive, germinate, and grow on isolation media.

### Treatment Variants

The standard treatment employs **60% ethanol**, although **2% phenol** and **5% acetic acid** serve as effective alternatives. All three agents partially sterilize soil, preferentially permitting ascomycete recovery while reducing competition from fast-growing taxa.

### Taxa Recovered

Ethanol pasteurization markedly increases isolation of coprophilous ascomycetes including *Chaetomium*, *Gelasinospora*, *Thelebolus*, *Sordaria*, *Ascobolus*, *Saccobolus*, and *Sporormiella*. It also enhances recovery of ascosporic and sclerotial Eurotiales, as well as cleistothecial and perithecial ascomycetes that are typically outcompeted on non-treated soil plates. These taxa are often underrepresented in standard isolation studies using untreated soil.

### Soil-Specific Considerations

Fine-textured soils are sterilized more effectively by chemical treatments due to greater surface area contact between the biocide and soil particles. Coarse sands may require longer exposure times. Organic matter — particularly fibrous roots and plant debris — can shield fungal propagules from the biocide, reducing treatment efficacy. Soils high in organic content may benefit from longer treatment durations or mild pre-screening to remove large debris.

### Protocol

The 7-step ethanol pasteurization protocol:

1. Collect and prepare soil samples; prepare isolation media and hold at 45°C
2. Pulverize 5–10 g of air-dried soil to a fine, homogeneous powder
3. Add 20–30 ml of 60% ethanol to the soil in a sterile container
4. Shake vigorously for 5 minutes, then let stand undisturbed for 5 minutes
5. Decant the ethanol supernatant and rinse the soil twice with sterile water
6. Pipette 0.5–1.0 ml of the treated soil suspension into each of 5 Petri dishes
7. Overlay each dish with 10–15 ml of molten medium; incubate at 15–20°C and isolate colonies as they emerge

A **control treatment** using sterile water instead of ethanol should always be run in parallel to confirm that the pasteurization treatment is genuinely increasing ascomycete recovery relative to untreated soil, and to quantify the suppression of non-target taxa.
