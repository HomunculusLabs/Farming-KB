## Lyophilization (Freeze-Drying)

The most widely used method for long-term preservation. Developed for fungi by Alexander and colleagues in the 1960s.

### Protocol
1. Grow cultures in liquid medium (static or shake) or on [[mycelium]] or spores
3. Suspend in sterile skim milk (10%) or other protectant (sucrose, inositol, honey, serum)
4. Transfer to lyophilization ampoules
5. Freeze at -40C to -80C
6. Place under vacuum (0.05-0.1 mm Hg) for 24-48 hours
7. Seal ampoules under vacuum
8. Store at 4C in the dark

### Best Suited For
- Sporulating fungi (spores survive well)
- Yeasts
- Many mitosporic ascomycetes

### Not Suitable For
- Oomycetes (generally do not survive)
- Nonsporulating basidiomycetes (limited success)
- Zygomycetes with large, delicate spores
- Some fungi with melanized hyphae

### Viability
- Many fungi remain viable for 20-30 years
- Some for >50 years
- Failures occur; periodic testing recommended

## Liquid Nitrogen Storage (Cryopreservation)

Storage at or below -139C stops all metabolic activity and ice crystal growth. Recommended for fungi that cannot be lyophilized.

### Advantages
- Prevention of genetic variability in distributed stocks
- Time-saving; reduced labor vs. maintaining living stocks
- Eliminates need for repeated pathogenicity tests
- Prevention of culture loss from contamination
- Best long-term availability assurance

### Disadvantages
- High cost of apparatus and liquid nitrogen (replenish every 2 days)
- Space requirements for refrigeration units
- Need for constant surveillance
- Shipping requires growing cultures first (cannot ship frozen)

### Cryoprotectants
Two types:
- **Penetrating agents:** Glycerol, DMSO (pass through cell membrane; protect intra- and extracellularly)
- **Nonpenetrating agents:** Sucrose, lactose, glucose, mannitol, sorbitol, dextran, hydroxyethyl starch (protect externally)

Glycerol and DMSO are most effective for fungi. Typical concentrations: 5-10% DMSO or 10-20% glycerol.

### Protocols
- **Protocol A:** Agar plugs in cryovials with cryoprotectant; control-rate freezing
- **Protocol B:** Spore/mycelial suspensions from agar surfaces with 10% glycerol or 5% DMSO
- **Protocol C:** Liquid-culture mycelium macerated; mixed with 20% glycerol or 10% DMSO

### Revival
Rapidly thaw at 37C water bath; place on appropriate agar plates; check viability after 2-7 days.

### Recommended For
- Dictyostelids
- Amoebae
- Zygomycetes including Entomophthorales
- Oomycetes
- Phytopathogenic fungi
- Yeasts
- Nonsporulating ascomycetes
- Higher basidiomycetes (mycelial cultures)
- Genetic stocks
- Dangerous human pathogens

## Freezing (-20C to -80C)

### Simple Freezing
- Agar slants in screw-cap bottles/tubes placed directly in freezer
- Failure rate for mitosporic ascomycetes, zygomycetes, yeasts at -20C: 5.1% after 5 years
- Failure rate for medically important fungi fungi at -70C: 2.3% over 6 months to 13 years
- Vigorously growing/sporulating cultures survive better

### Disk Method (Ito)
Six 6-mm disks from vigorously growing cultures placed in cryotubes with 10% glycerol; preserved up to 5 years at -80C.

**Important:** Do not subject cultures to repeated freezing/thawing cycles — significantly reduces viability.

## Silica Gel Drying

Developed by Perkins (1962) for Neurospora. Sporulating fungi in skim milk on silica gel remain viable 4-5 years; dictyostelid spores up to 11 years.

### Protocol A (Sporulating strains)
1. Tubes partially filled with 6-22 mesh silica gel (sterilized at 180C for 90 min)
2. Spores suspended in 10% skim milk, cooled to 4C
3. Add spore suspension to silica gel (0.5 ml/4 g)
4. Store with loose caps 1-2 weeks at room temperature
5. Check viability; tighten caps; store at 4C in sealed container

### Protocol B (Nonsporulating strains)
1. Scrape aerial hyphae/mycelia from agar; transfer to tube with 0.5 ml water
2. Grind to homogenate; add 0.5 ml sterile skim milk
3. Pipette over silica gel; agitate; ice bath 15 minutes
4. Seal with Parafilm; store at 5C or -20C

### Not Suitable For
- Pythium and Phytophthora species (do not survive)
- Fungi that do not produce resistant spores

## Organic Substrata

Fungi can be stored on dried organic materials:
- Cereal grains (wheat, rice, sorghum)
- Plant parts (stem pieces, seed pods)
- Filter paper
- Insect tissue (for entomopathogens)

Dried substrata can then be frozen. Advantages: similar to natural conditions; may maintain pathogenicity and sporulation ability.

## Oil Overlay Method

Mineral oil overlay on agar slants: simple, mushroom growing house, effective for short-to-medium term. Not recommended for long-term preservation (>5 years).

## Choosing a Method

| Method | Cost | Effort | Duration | Best For |
|--------|------|--------|----------|----------|
| Lyophilization | Moderate | High | 20-30+ years | Sporulating fungi, yeasts |
| Liquid N2 | High | Moderate | Indefinite | Nonsporulating, valuable stocks |
| Freezing -80C | Low | Low | 5-10 years | General use |
| Silica gel | Low | Low | 4-11 years | Genetic stocks, sporulating fungi |
| Oil overlay | Very low | Very low | 1-5 years | Short-term backup |
