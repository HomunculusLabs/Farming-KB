## The Taxonomic Revolution

Until the mid-nineteenth century, the study of fungi was in disarray. The old folk wisdom about how to
distinguish "mushrooms" from "toadstools" was gradually replaced by positive identification of species --
or, more commonly, by a blanket avoidance of wild mushrooms altogether. The Swedish mycologist Elias Magnus

In The Seven Sisters, Cooke argued for the moral equivalence of the world's regional drug preferences. He
built the book upon the conceit that the Queen of Sleep, in order to fend off the political ambitions of her
seven jealous sisters, assigned each a narcotic drug. "Morphina" took Tartary and morphine, for example,
and "Amanita" (fly-agaric) took Siberia. Each racial group naturally utilised the local narcotic to which it
was drawn -- an idea he almost certainly plagiarised from James Johnston's earlier best-seller The Chemistry
of Common Life (1855).

Cooke devoted space to the [[1799-liberty-cap-discovery]]. Cooke realised this must have been caused by a mushroom similar in its
intoxicating properties to fly-agaric, writing that "if future generations do not deem it desirable to
indulge in a narcotic of this kind for the purposes of producing pleasurable sensations, or to smother the
carking cares of life, yet they may learn more than we do at present know of the peculiar characteristics
which distinguish this [mushroom] from all the others." The implication was plain: future generations would
indeed find local intoxicating mushrooms desirable.

Mushrooms appeared "tortuously in storybook illustrations as the preferred seating of gnomes." Their sudden
appearance, ephemerality, association with rot and decay, grotesque shapes, and sheer otherness meant they
stirred and troubled the Victorian imagination. Charles Darwin's prudish granddaughter was said to collect
and secretly burn stinkhorn fungi (Phallus impudicus) lest they corrupt passing children.

## The Mushroom as Symbol of Decay

For some writers, mushrooms symbolised decay and mortality. Percy Bysshe Shelley in The Sensitive Plant
(1820) compared the mushroom's stalk to "a murderer's stake / Where rags of loose flesh yet tremble on
high, / Infecting the winds that wander by." Both Charles Dickens in Dombey and Son and Edgar Allan Poe in
The Fall of the House of Usher employed mushrooms as outward symbols of human decline. It was common to find
wild mushroom patches brutally trampled and kicked, as if their offensive presence would somehow drag
observers into ruin. Their cold, bloated fleshiness was an unwelcome reminder of mortality and death.

## The Mushroom in Imaginative Literature

Other writers responded to the mushroom's otherworldly qualities. Jules Verne and H. G. Wells imagined
forests of giant mushrooms growing at the centre of the earth and on the moon respectively, as if no other
plant could capture the strangeness of these imagined places. The Belgian sculptor Carsten Holler
specialises in making accurate models of mushrooms, including an installation in which giant, human-sized
fly-agarics appear to grow downwards from the ceiling.

The Victorian era thus bequeathed to later generations the full ambivalence that still characterises our
relationship with fungi -- revulsion and fascination in equal measure. The fact that certain species are
hallucinogenic simply accords with all our expectations of what a mushroom should be: strange, uncertain,
dark and disquieting, ruled by apparently supernatural forces, producing in us desire and loathing in equal
measure.

## Collective Amnesia and the Liberty Cap

A peculiar feature of this period is that the Liberty Cap's listing as a poisonous species gradually
disappeared from guidebooks until by the 1940s it was wholly absent. The respected mycologist John Ramsbottom
listed it as "suspicious" in 1923, but omitted it from his Poisonous Fungi of 1945. Worthington Smith
similarly omitted it from his 1910 guide. Mordecai Cooke removed it from his 1904 Plain and Easy Account. It
was, as Letcher puts it, "as if a collective amnesia" had descended. The increasing incidence of
"poisoning" episodes throughout the nineteenth century did not represent a Victorian mushroom craze, but was
simply a product of the better record-keeping of the emerging institutions of medicine and science.
