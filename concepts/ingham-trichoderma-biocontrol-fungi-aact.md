# Ingham — Trichoderma and Biocontrol Fungi in AACT

Updated: 2026-04-18

Trichoderma and Gliocladium species are among the most widely studied and commercially available biocontrol fungi. Dr. Elaine Ingham discusses their use within the context of [[cervantes-compost-tea-organic-brewing]] and provides important caveats about how these organisms function, how they differ from mycorrhizal fungi, and what their limitations are. This page covers Ingham's perspective on adding specific biocontrol fungal inoculants to compost tea.

## Trichoderma as a Biocontrol Agent

Trichoderma species are saprophytic fungi that can function as parasites of other fungi -- a relationship called mycoparasitism. They produce enzymes that break down the cell walls of certain pathogenic fungi.

### How Trichoderma Works

Ingham clarifies an important distinction about Trichoderma's mechanism of action:

- Trichoderma produces **enzymes** that digest the cell walls of target fungi -- this has been demonstrated with photographic evidence in the microbiological literature
- Claims that Trichoderma produces **toxins** to kill other fungi have "NEVER been substantiated"
- Trichoderma is correctly classified as a **parasite** of other fungi, not a predator
- There is "no data to support" claims of Trichoderma being a fungal predator

This distinction matters because it affects how we think about biocontrol. Enzymatic cell wall digestion is a specific, targeted interaction. It does not produce the broad-spectrum toxins that anaerobic organisms create, and it does not harm non-target organisms.

### Gliocladium

Gliocladium species function similarly to Trichoderma as mycoparasitic biocontrol agents. Both genera can be added to compost tea and will germinate and grow if adequate fungal foods are present.

## Adding Trichoderma to Compost Tea

### Timing of Addition

Unlike many compost tea ingredients that are added at the beginning of the brew, Trichoderma spores can be added at the start of the brew cycle. The spores will germinate and begin to grow during the brewing process, so "they are active and begin to perform their function immediately on addition to the soil."

### T-22 Product

Ingham specifically addresses T-22 (a commercial Trichoderma harzianum product):

- T-22 can be added at the beginning of the brew cycle
- The fungus does not go through its entire life cycle in the tea brewer
- Once applied to soil, the fungi will colonize and reproduce in the soil environment
- Placement around the seed can help protect seedlings from disease fungi
- However, Trichoderma "does not follow along the root the way a mycorrhizal fungus does" -- its protection is limited to the zone of application

### Minimum Fungal Biomass Requirement

Trichoderma and Gliocladium perform best when there is already a minimum level of fungal biomass present in the tea. Ingham references the SFI compost tea report's "desired range" for fungal biomass as the target. Starting with a tea that already has adequate fungi provides the competitive environment in which Trichoderma can function effectively.

### Fungal Food Requirements

For Trichoderma spores to germinate and grow in tea, adequate fungal foods must be present. These can come from:

- The compost itself (if it contains fungal food resources)
- Added fungal foods during brewing (see [[aact-brewing-ingredients-ingham]])

Ingham provides a general guideline: "a teaspoon per 5 gallons, a cup in 50 gallons" for fungal food additions.

## Trichoderma vs Mycorrhizal Fungi

A critical distinction that Ingham emphasizes is that Trichoderma is NOT a substitute for mycorrhizal fungi. The two serve entirely different functions:

### Trichoderma
- Saprophytic -- lives on dead organic matter
- Mycoparasitic -- attacks other fungi through enzymatic cell wall digestion
- Does NOT follow root growth
- Provides localized protection around the point of application
- Does not improve nutrient uptake or soil structure
- Does not form long-term associations with plant roots

### Mycorrhizal Fungi
- Mutualistic -- forms a beneficial association with living plant roots
- Extends the root system's effective reach for nutrient and water uptake
- Follows root growth and colonizes new root tissue as the plant grows
- Builds soil structure through [[ingham-glomalin-biological-soil-glues]] production
- Cannot survive the tea brewing process -- must be added just before application
- Provides ongoing, systemic benefits to the plant

Ingham's practical advice: "Trichoderma does not follow along the root the way a mycorrhizal fungus does. Thus, with Trichoderma, you only get protection in the area where you place the inoculum."

## Label Compliance Considerations

Ingham notes a regulatory issue with adding commercial biocontrol products to compost tea:

- It is prohibited by law to suggest using any product in a way not listed on its label
- Product manufacturers typically do not list compost tea application on their labels
- This means formal recommendations for adding specific commercial products to tea cannot be made
- However, the general biology of Trichoderma germination and growth in aerated compost tea can be discussed

## When Trichoderma Is Most Useful

Based on Ingham's discussion, Trichoderma is most appropriate when:

- Specific soilborne fungal diseases have been identified (Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, Pythium)
- The compost tea already has adequate baseline fungal biomass
- Fungal foods are available to support Trichoderma growth
- Seed treatment or transplant drench is needed for localized protection
- Mycorrhizal fungi are not yet established or the crop does not form mycorrhizal associations

## When Trichoderma Is NOT the Best Approach

Trichoderma is not a substitute for:

- Building overall fungal biomass in the soil food web
- Establishing mycorrhizal associations for nutrient uptake
- Creating the competitive exclusion environment that [[ingham-aact-disease-suppression]] provides
- Addressing the root causes of disease (compaction, anaerobic conditions, lack of organic matter)

Ingham's overall philosophy is that broad-spectrum biological approaches (full soil food web restoration) are more effective and sustainable than relying on specific biocontrol agents. Trichoderma can be a useful tool within a broader biological management program, but it should not be the sole strategy.

## Practical Protocol

1. Ensure compost has adequate fungal biomass before brewing (thick white strands visible)
2. Add fungal foods (malt, soy sauce, humic acids) to the brew at approximately 1 teaspoon per 5 gallons
3. Add Trichoderma spores at the beginning of the brew cycle if using a commercial inoculant
4. For mycorrhizal spores, add them just before application -- NOT during brewing
5. Apply tea promptly after brewing to maintain aerobic conditions
6. Use Trichoderma for targeted seed treatment or transplant protection, not as a standalone disease management strategy
7. Combine with overall soil food web restoration for best long-term results
