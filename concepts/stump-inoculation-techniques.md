# Stump Inoculation Techniques

Stump inoculation is the practice of introducing beneficial mushroom mycelium into tree stumps after logging or tree removal. Rather than leaving stumps as waste or burning them, stump inoculation converts them into productive [[mushroom-cultivation-for-food-and-medicine]] while simultaneously competing with pathogenic fungi.

Paul Stamets developed comprehensive techniques for stump inoculation as part of his [[mycoforestry]] toolkit, arguing that every stump represents an opportunity for both food production and ecological restoration.

## Why Inoculate Stumps

Tree stumps represent enormous volumes of wood that would otherwise decompose slowly or become reservoirs for parasitic fungi. Active inoculation achieves multiple objectives simultaneously:

- [[mushroom-cultivation-for-food-and-medicine]] are produced for years
- Pathogenic fungi like Armillaria are outcompeted by fast-colonizing beneficial species
- Decomposition is accelerated, returning nutrients to the soil food chain faster
- The stump becomes part of a [[mycorrhizal-nutrient-transport]]
- Wood debris is retained on-site rather than burned or exported, preserving the nutritional bank

Stumps can host multiple species simultaneously. Oyster mushrooms and honey mushrooms have been observed fruiting from the same stump, suggesting oysters -- classic saprophytes -- can compete against honey mushrooms, which have a dual parasitic then saprophytic nature.

## Plug Spawn Method

The most straightforward method uses plug spawn -- wooden dowels colonized with mushroom mycelium:

- The procedure is similar to [[mushroom-cultivation-inoculation-methods]] but adapted for stumps
- The cut face is a huge wound allowing entry of competitors, so spacing must be close
- Inoculate with no more than 4 inches of spacing across the stump face surface
- The best place is the inside of the peripheral edge, into the sapwood
- Sapwood has the highest moisture and nutrient content for mycelial establishment

Stumps with fine fissure cracks (called checks) running through them are more quickly colonized than those without. Mycelium placed directly into these cracks is protected from drying and competition.

## Rope Spawn Method

Rope spawn involves hemp rope fully colonized with mycelium in a controlled environment:

- The rope is wrapped around the stump, especially in girdled areas with bark removed
- This provides continuous mycelial contact around the entire circumference
- Ensures colonization from multiple entry points simultaneously
- Cauliflower mushrooms (Sparassis crispa) have been successfully fruited from rope inoculation
- Works well for Hypholoma, Stropharia, Agrocybe, Pleurotus, and Ganoderma species

## Sawdust Spawn Sandwich

Sawdust spawn layered into the cut face in a "sandwich" arrangement:

- Alternating layers of sawdust spawn and fresh wood chips or sawdust
- Provides extensive contact between mycelium and wood substrate
- Maximizes surface area for colonization on large stumps
- Particularly effective where plug spawn alone would leave coverage gaps

## Quadruple Inoculation Method (QIM)

For mycorestoration-ecology|mycorestoration projects, Stamets recommends combining all methods simultaneously. The Quadruple Inoculation Method uses four approaches that together provide comprehensive coverage:

1. **Spored oils**: Applied during cutting via chain saw bar oil for immediate inoculation
2. **Rope spawn**: Girdle and wrap stumps with mycelium-colonized hemp rope
3. **Plug spawn**: Drill and inoculate stumps and logs at regular intervals
4. **Sawdust spawn**: Layer in a fungal sandwich on the cut face for maximum contact

Combined methods do a better job than single methods used alone. Using a quartet is more likely to result in complete colonization, especially when [[mycoforestry]] goals include competing with disease fungi.

## Species Selection for Stumps

Different mushroom species have different affinities for stump substrates:

- **Oyster mushrooms** (Pleurotus ostreatus): Aggressive colonizers, prolific fruiting, strong competition
- **Shiitake** (Lentinula edodes): Not recommended for stump inoculation by plug method
- **Reishi** (Ganoderma lucidum): Excellent stump species with high medicinal value
- **Wood lovers** (Hypholoma capnoides): Can fruit from the same stump for more than 10 years
- **Turkey tail** (Trametes versicolor): Strong competitor with well-documented medicinal properties
- **Cauliflower mushroom** (Sparassis crispa): Responds well to rope spawn on conifer stumps
- **Maitake** (Grifola frondosa): Can be inoculated into hardwood stumps, logs, or standing trees

## Disease Prevention Through Stump Inoculation

When a fungal disease such as Armillaria is sweeping through a forest, inoculating stumps with competitive saprophytes can slow the spread:

- By occupying the niche with beneficial species, pathogenic colonization is reduced
- Species like Hypholoma, Psilocybe, Trametes, Ganoderma, and Sparassis compete effectively
- Targets include Armillaria species and Heterobasidion annosum
- This converts potential disease reservoirs into productive assets
- The approach is a form of mycorestoration -- using fungi to heal damaged ecosystems

## Tools Required

For serious stump inoculation work the following tools are needed:

- Cordless drill or modified grinder with backup batteries or a small generator
- 5/16-inch (8 mm) drill bits for plug spawn holes
- Chain saw and safety gear for initial cutting and girdling
- Hammer and 2- to 4-inch flat-head nails for securing rope spawn
- Supply of rope spawn, plug spawn, sawdust spawn, and [[mycoforestry-spored-oil-techniques]]

## Best Practices

Tips for successful stump inoculation based on field experience:

- Inoculate as soon as possible after cutting, before competitors arrive
- Focus on sapwood rather than heartwood for best colonization rates
- Cover inoculation sites with wax or tape to prevent drying in arid climates
- Mark inoculated stumps for monitoring and future harvest
- Keep records of species used, dates, and methods for learning from results
- Multiple species on one stump can increase total productivity over time

## Long-Term Productivity

A mycelial raft established on a stump can produce mushrooms for a decade before being exhausted. The clustered wood lover (Hypholoma capnoides) has fruited from the same stump for more than 10 years, providing years of delicious fungi from a single inoculation event.

This long-term productivity makes stump inoculation one of the highest-return investments in [[mycoforestry-practices]] -- converting waste wood into years of food and medicine while building soil for future tree generations.
