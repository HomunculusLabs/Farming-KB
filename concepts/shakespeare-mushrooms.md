## Overview

William Shakespeare made several notable references to mushrooms and toadstools in his plays, drawing on the rich folklore and cultural associations of fungi in Elizabethan England. These references range from the magical to the derogatory, reflecting the complex and often contradictory attitudes toward mushrooms in early modern English culture.

***

## The Tempest: "Midnight Mushrumps"

Shakespeare's most explicit mushroom reference appears in The Tempest (c. 1610-1611), where Prospero describes the spirits under his command:

> "demy-puppets ... whose pastime is to make midnight mushrumps"

This reference places mushrooms within the magical framework of the play — the mushroom as a product of enchantment, appearing mysteriously in the dark. The word "mushrumps" (an archaic variant of "mushrooms") links fungal growth with the supernatural transformations that drive the play's plot. In this context, mushrooms symbolize the sudden, unexplained phenomena of Prospero's island.

Importantly, Shakespeare was breaking with tradition here. While he gave mushrooms a magical quality, the more common usage in 16th and 17th century England was to hurl the word "mushroom" as an insult — a derogatory metaphor for the emerging mercantile middle class.

***

## Hamlet: Decay and Mortality

In Hamlet, references to fungal growth on corpses and decaying matter evoke the play's central themes of mortality and corruption. The idea of bodies decomposing and nourishing fungal growth served as a powerful metaphor for the physical reality of death that Hamlet obsessively contemplates. Mushrooms, as agents of decomposition, symbolize the inevitable breakdown of all earthly things.

***

## The Insult Tradition

### "Mushroom Man"

In Shakespeare's time, "mushroom" was commonly used as a term of abuse. The word carried multiple negative connotations:
- Sudden, unexpected appearance (like the overnight emergence of fungi)
- Lack of substantial roots (implying superficiality or newness)
- Inherent association with dung and decay
- Rapid decay (suggesting impermanence and worthlessness)

### John Taylor's Satire (1639)

The poet John Taylor (1580-1653) exemplified this tradition:

> "Consider this thou new made Mushroom man,
> Thy Life's a Blast, a Bubble, and a Span;
> And thou with all thy Gorgeous trappings gay,
> Art but a Mouldring lumpe of guilded Clay."

This verse captures the essential critique: the "mushroom man" may appear impressive but is fundamentally insubstantial and doomed to rapid decay.

### Anti-Middle-Class Sentiment

The mushroom insult was particularly directed at the emerging mercantile middle class — people who had recently acquired wealth and status but who, in the eyes of the established aristocracy, lacked legitimate roots and substance. The mushroom metaphor perfectly captured this anxiety about social mobility: sudden appearance, no deep roots, and inevitable decay.

***

## Fairy Rings in Shakespeare

Kipling's Puck of Pook's Hill (1906) drew on the Shakespearean tradition when Puck was accidentally summoned in a fairy ring. Shakespeare's enchanted view of mushrooms placed them in the realm of the supernatural — objects of wonder and mystery rather than fear or disgust.

The connection between Shakespeare and [[fairy-rings-folklore-and-science]] reflects the broader Elizabethan fascination with the boundary between the natural and supernatural worlds. Mushrooms, appearing overnight as if by magic, naturally belonged to this liminal space.

***

## Broader Context

Shakespeare's mushroom references must be understood within the context of English [[wasson-mycophobia-mycophilia-theory]]. Unlike the cultures of Eastern Europe or Mesoamerica (see [[aztec-mushroom-feasts-and-revelations]]), England had no tradition of deliberate mushroom consumption beyond a few easily identified species. The mushroom in English culture was primarily a symbol of:
- Danger and poison
- Decay and mortality
- The uncanny and supernatural
- Superficiality and impermanence
