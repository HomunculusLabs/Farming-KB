# Fodder Trees for Livestock Integration

Fodder trees are multi-purpose woody perennials that produce leaves, pods, or branches suitable for feeding livestock. They represent a cornerstone of [[permaculture-designers-manual-animal-forage-systems]], providing drought-resilient feed, shade, windbreaks, soil improvement, and often human food simultaneously.

## Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus palmensis)

Previously known as tree lucerne (Cytisus proliferus), tagasaste is a nitrogen-fixing legume tree reaching 6-10 meters and living over 30 years. It originated in dry Mediterranean-type climates but grows well in cool temperate areas, withstanding light frosts.

**Growing:** Easy to grow from seed -- scarify or pour boiling water over seeds and soak. Tolerant of poor soils, drought, and wind. For best results, fertilize with trace elements and lop branches regularly (by hand or browsing) to encourage bushy foliage. Seed can be direct-drilled into pasture, but plants should be protected from stock for up to 3 years, or stock allowed in for brief grazing periods only. If sheep ringbark young trees, cut to the ground to encourage regrowth from the base; this forms thickets more resistant to damage.

**Uses:** Foliage is an important protein-rich fodder for stock during drought and at the end of summer when pasture is scarce. Many small white flowers provide excellent bee forage. Chickens eat the seeds. Functions as a windbreak hedge. Serves as a nurse plant surrounding frost-sensitive trees in early years. Excellent cut mulch -- can be lopped 3-4 times in summer. See [[comparison-sheet-mulching-vs-chop-and-drop]] for related techniques.

## Leucaena (Leucaena leucocephala)

A fast-growing leguminous tropical tree reaching 10-20 meters, though it can be kept manageable if [[permaculture-animal-integration]]. Does best on well-drained soils.

**Toxicity concern:** Contains mimosine, which may cause toxicity in stock if overfed. The low-mimosine variety L. leucocephala var. Cunninghamii is preferred. CSIRO scientists have also isolated a microbial culture that cattle can use to break down the toxic substance in their stomachs. As long as leucaena is kept to 30-40% of the diet, there are no adverse effects even from normal varieties.

**Uses:** Excellent high-quality forage (both leaves and pods) for cattle, sheep, and goats -- palatable and nutritious. Can be cut-and-carried or stock allowed to browse directly. Useful for revegetating tropical hillslopes prone to erosion. Excellent coppicing for firewood; good timber. Rich in organic matter for use as mulch in alley cropping. Fixes nitrogen in the soil. Used extensively as living fence and hedge species in West Africa and India.

## Mesquite (Prosopis juliflora, P. tamarugo)

Leguminous spreading shrubs and small trees reaching 10-15 meters. Adapted to arid climates; totally drought-resistant and extremely salt-tolerant. Grown from saline desert to semi-desert zones.

**Production:** P. juliflora (honey mesquite) yields up to 50 tons of pods per hectare, reaching production in 3-5 years. Other species include P. alba, P. nigra, P. pallida, and P. chilensis. Caution: easily becomes rampant and weedy -- plan for management.

**Uses:** Major fodder trees of drylands for stock and poultry. The 14cm-long pods are high in sugar with moderate protein. Pods can be made into a syrup (traditional practice in Peru). Provides bee forage. Coppices easily for firewood. The pods are so productive that mesquite stands can sustain significant livestock numbers even in areas where grass is scarce.

## Honey Locust (Gleditsia triacanthos)

Deciduous tree ranging from 6-40 meters; very thorny when young, though thornless cultivars (G. triacanthos inermis) have been developed. Trees have an open canopy that allows clovers and pasture to be grown underneath. Frost- and drought-hardy; prefers temperate climates with hot summers and cold winters. Tolerates most soils.

**Production:** Yields up to 110 kg of pods per tree at years 8-9. At 86 trees per hectare, pod production is equivalent to 10 tons per hectare of oat crop. Pods should be gathered as soon as they fall in mid-autumn. Seed requires scarification or boiling water treatment. Select high-yielding, thornless cultivars for ease of management.

Note: Although a legume tree, nitrogen-fixing nodules have not been observed in the roots. The open canopy makes it ideal for silvopasture systems where pasture grows beneath the trees. The pods are high in sugar and readily eaten by cattle, sheep, goats, and pigs.

## Moringa (Moringa oleifera)

Also called the horseradish or drumstick tree. Small tree to 10 meters; propagated by cuttings. Tropical and fast-growing. One of the most nutritious plants on earth -- leaves contain 25% protein, high levels of vitamins A and C, calcium, iron, and potassium.

**Human uses:** Tender pods eaten as vegetables. Flowers and young leaves eaten raw or cooked. Fried seeds. Roots used as a condiment (similar to horseradish). Moringa leaf powder is increasingly sold as a nutritional supplement.

**Livestock uses:** Twigs and leaves lopped for stock fodder. Moringa leaf meal is increasingly used in poultry and cattle feed formulations due to its exceptional protein content. Studies have shown improved weight gain and milk production when moringa is included in rations at 20-40% of dry matter intake.

## Hot, Dry Climate Fodder Trees

Large trees suited to agroforestry in arid regions include bottle trees (Brachychiton spp.), which have deep taproots that do not compete with crops or pastures. They can be easily coppiced. Leaves serve as fodder, especially as drought rations for sheep and cattle, though they lack phosphorus and must be supplemented with stock licks. In extreme drought, bottle trees (B. rupestris) can be cut down completely to feed the soft inner pith to cattle -- these must be replanted afterward.

Other arid-zone fodder trees include acacia species, carob (Ceratonia siliqua), and mesquite (already described above). These species share the characteristics of drought tolerance, deep taproots, and pod or leaf production suitable for livestock.

## Mulberry (Morus spp.)

Deciduous dome-shaped trees 10-20 meters, growing from temperate to subtropical climates. Main species are black mulberry (M. nigra), red mulberry (M. rubra), and white mulberry (M. alba). Can be grown in full sun but also shade-tolerant. Easily grown from seed or cuttings.

**Uses:** Edible berries -- M. nigra and M. rubra have superior fruit. M. alba is fast-growing with a shorter fruiting season; leaves are used as silkworm food in China. Excellent trees for poultry and pig forage as fruits are numerous and fall easily to the ground. Leaves can also be fed to cattle. Useful wood for fenceposts and farm tools.

## Alley Cropping with Fodder Trees

Alley cropping (also called hedgerow intercropping) is a system where rows of fodder trees are planted with crop alleys between them. The trees are periodically coppiced and the cut foliage is used as mulch or fodder. Benefits include:

- **Soil improvement** -- nitrogen fixation and leaf mulch build organic matter in the alleys
- **Shade regulation** -- trees provide dappled shade that reduces heat stress on crops
- **Wind protection** -- hedgerow rows reduce wind speed and evaporation
- **Multiple yields** -- fodder, mulch, firewood, and crop production from the same land area
- **Erosion control** -- tree roots stabilize soil on slopes

Common alley cropping species include leucaena, gliricidia, calliandra, and sesbania in the tropics, and tagasaste and honey locust in temperate regions.

## Design Considerations

When integrating fodder trees into livestock systems:

- Plant a diversity of species to spread risk and extend the leafing and flowering season
- Protect young trees from browsing until established (3+ years for most species)
- Use thornless cultivars where animals will graze beneath
- Combine with understory pasture for maximum land-use efficiency
- Plan for coppicing rotations to maintain productivity -- typically every 6-12 months for tropical species, annually for temperate ones
- Consider the open-canopy vs. closed-canopy habit when designing silvopasture layouts
- Account for toxicity -- research each species for potential livestock health issues
- Site trees to maximize shade and shelter for livestock during extreme weather

## Fodder Tree Yield Comparison

A rough comparison of fodder tree productivity:

| Species | Height | Annual Leaf Yield | Key Advantage |
|---------|--------|-------------------|---------------|
| Tagasaste | 6-10m | High (3-4 cuts/yr) | Cold-tolerant, long-lived |
| Leucaena | 10-20m | Very high | Fast growth, high protein |
| Mesquite | 10-15m | High (pods) | Extreme drought tolerance |
| Honey Locust | 6-40m | Moderate (pods) | Open canopy for pasture |
| Moringa | 10m | Very high | Exceptional nutrition |
| Mulberry | 10-20m | High (fruit + leaves) | Multi-use, shade-tolerant |

Select species based on climate, soil conditions, livestock type, and intended management intensity.

## Merged: Fodder Trees Permaculture Systems
Fodder trees provide a perennial source of livestock feed, reducing the need for imported feed, preventing soil erosion, and contributing nitrogen to the system through biological fixation. They are a critical component of [[permaculture-livestock-forage-systems]] and [[permaculture-tree-crop-systems]], offering multiple yields from a single planting. They provide leaf protein for livestock, fix atmospheric nitrogen, offer wind protection, prevent soil erosion on slopes, supply firewood and timber, create [[permaculture-wildlife-corridors]], and can serve as nurse crops for establishing more valuable tree species. Many fodder trees are also pioneer species that can establish on degraded land, making them valuable tools for [[permaculture-water-harvesting-earthworks]] and land rehabilitation projects. In the wild it grows as a companion to Eucalyptus spp, other Acacia spp, Atriplex spp, Maireana spp, Eragrostis spp, Triodia spp, and Aristidia spp, demonstrating the [[permaculture-plant-guilds]] concept in natural plant communities.
