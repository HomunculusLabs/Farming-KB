## Wood Decay and Ecosystem Carbon

Dighton (2003) examines wood decay by fungi as a major pathway in ecosystem
carbon cycling, emphasizing the distinct decay strategies of white rot and
brown rot fungi, the long residence time of woody carbon, nutrient
immobilization during decay, and the functional importance of cord-forming
basidiomycetes in forest carbon and nutrient dynamics. Wood represents a
substantially different substrate from leaf litter due to its high C:N ratio,
lignin content, and physical structure.

## Wood as a Carbon Pool

Woody material represents a substantial and long-lived carbon pool in forest
ecosystems. In a second-growth Douglas fir forest (Fogel and Hunt, 1983),
tree biomass constitutes 78-79% of total standing stock organic matter, with
soil organic matter at 17%, forest floor litter at 4%, and fungi at
approximately 2%. Of the tree standing stock, boles account for 64-66%,
branches 7-8%, foliage 4%, nonmycorrhizal roots 17-18%, and mycorrhizae 6%.

Much deadwood remains in the canopy prior to recruitment to the forest floor.
This standing dead material supports a different fungal community than
ground-level wood. Boddy and Rayner (1983) identified 12 basidiomycete
species dominating oak wood canopies, with distinct pioneer (Phellinus
ferreus, Stereum gausapatum), secondary (Phlebia adiata, [[stereum-hirsutum]]) species.

## White Rot and Brown Rot Strategies

Two primary decay strategies exist among wood-decaying basidiomycetes:

**White rot fungi** degrade both cellulose and lignin simultaneously or
selectively. They employ lignin peroxidase, [[energy-efficient-house-design]], rapidly releasing cellulose-derived carbon while leaving a
lignin-rich residue.

**Aquatic analogues**: In stream ecosystems, where true white and brown rot
fungi are absent, lignolytic [[aquatic-hyphomycetes-and-ingoldian-spores]] have been isolated from wood (Shearer, 1992).
