# Wood Decay and Ecosystem Carbon

Dighton (2003) examines wood decay by fungi as a major pathway in ecosystem
carbon cycling, emphasizing the distinct decay strategies of white rot and
brown rot fungi, the long residence time of woody carbon, nutrient
immobilization during decay, and the functional importance of cord-forming
basidiomycetes in forest carbon and nutrient dynamics. Wood represents a
substantially different substrate from leaf litter due to its high C:N ratio,
lignin content, and physical structure.

## Wood as a Carbon Pool

Woody material represents a substantial and long-lived carbon pool in forest
ecosystems. In a second-growth Douglas fir forest (Fogel and Hunt, 1983),
tree biomass constitutes 78-79% of total standing stock organic matter, with
soil organic matter at 17%, forest floor litter at 4%, and fungi at
approximately 2%. Of the tree standing stock, boles account for 64-66%,
branches 7-8%, foliage 4%, nonmycorrhizal roots 17-18%, and mycorrhizae 6%.

Much deadwood remains in the canopy prior to recruitment to the forest floor.
This standing dead material supports a different fungal community than
ground-level wood. Boddy and Rayner (1983) identified 12 basidiomycete
species dominating oak wood canopies, with distinct pioneer (Phellinus
ferreus, Stereum gausapatum), secondary (Phlebia adiata, [[coriolus-versicolor]]),
and fauna-associated (Hyphoderma setigerum, [[stereum-hirsutum]]) species.

## White Rot and Brown Rot Strategies

Two primary decay strategies exist among wood-decaying basidiomycetes:

**White rot fungi** degrade both cellulose and lignin simultaneously or
selectively. They employ lignin peroxidase, [[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]], and laccase
enzymes to break down the lignin polymer, exposing cellulose fibers for
further degradation. Examples include [[energy-efficient-house-design]], rapidly releasing cellulose-derived carbon while leaving a
lignin-rich residue.

**Aquatic analogues**: In stream ecosystems, where true white and brown rot
fungi are absent, lignolytic [[freshwater-fungi-aquatic-hyphomycetes-ascomycetes]] (Tricladium, Anguillospora,
Dendrospora) fulfill analogous roles. Approximately one-third (86 species) of
[[aquatic-hyphomycetes-and-ingoldian-spores]] have been isolated from wood (Shearer, 1992).

## Enzyme Systems in Wood Decomposition

Sinsabaugh et al. (1993) studied [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]] in wood decomposition,
finding that most are derived from fungal activity:

- **Lignocellulases**: Production did not differ significantly across locations
  in temperate forest ecosystems, suggesting consistent functional capacity.
- **[[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]] enzymes**: Acid phosphatase and N-acetylglucosaminase
  showed high spatial variability, reflecting edaphic (soil condition)
  controls.
- **Enzyme regulation**: Production is governed by [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]]. Where
  N and P are less available, fungi expend more energy on enzyme production to
  sequester nutrients from organic sources.

Cellulose becomes unavailable when the cellulose:lignin ratio falls below 0.5
(Linkins et al., 1984). Temperature positively influences cellulase activity.
These findings led Sinsabaugh et al. (1993) to develop a model containing
both fungal (microbial) and soil nutrient controls over enzyme expression.

## Nutrient Dynamics During Wood Decay

Wood has extremely high C:N ratios (294:1 to 327:1) compared to herbaceous
litter (15:1 to 160:1), requiring substantial nutrient import for
decomposition to proceed. The Swift et al. (1979) model predicts initial
[[dighton-fungal-nutrient-immobilization-turnover]] until the wood's C:nutrient ratio approaches that of
the fungus. At 80% mass loss, Sinsabaugh et al. (1993) measured N
immobilization of 43-139 mg/g wood and P immobilization of 2.2-4.4 mg/g wood.
The spatial variability of these parameters was much greater than for
lignocellulase activity.

Longer-lived basidiomycetes have greater potential for nutrient accumulation
than ephemeral molds due to their lower turnover rates and higher biomass
investment. Unlike bacteria, fungi are larger organisms with lower turnover,
particularly in the long-lived Basidiomycotina.
