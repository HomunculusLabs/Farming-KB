# Cannabis and Anxiety Research (General)

**Source:** Uwe Blesching, The Cannabis Health Index (2017)

## Condition Overview

Anxiety is a normal reaction to stress. Chronic anxiety affects ~20% of the US population. Five types defined by Western medicine:

- **GAD:** chronic worry about anticipated events

- **OCD:** compulsive rituals providing sense of control

- **Panic disorder:** acute attacks with rapid heart rate, hyperventilation, feelings of doom

- **PTSD:** re-experiencing trauma, avoidance, hypervigilance

- **Social anxiety:** anticipation of humiliation

Standard treatments: anti-anxiety drugs, antidepressants, psychological intervention.

## Cannabis Research Findings

**Dose-dependent effects:** Too little = sub-optimal; too much = increases anxiety. Therapeutic window is critical.

**Key studies:**

- 2005 meta-analysis (Lilly Research): Cannabis modulates anxiety and mood states

- 2005 international study: HU210 (synthetic cannabinoid) promoted hippocampal neurogenesis -- produces anxiolytic and antidepressant-like effects. Most illegal substances decrease hippocampal neurogenesis; cannabinoids increase it

- 2009 Boston (775 HIV/AIDS patients, 10 locations): Cannabis considered more effective than standard medications for anxiety, depression, diarrhea, fatigue, neuropathy (but less effective for nausea)

- 2010 Sao Paulo review: CBD has therapeutic potential as antipsychotic, anxiolytic, and antidepressant; THC as adjuvant in schizophrenia treatment

## Strain-Specific Considerations

- Both CB1 and CB2 pathways modulate anxiety, individually and in combination

- **Indicas and indica-dominant hybrids preferred** for anxiety -- lower THC:CBD ratio

- Sedating, relaxing, grounding effects of indicas preferred by anxiety patients

- HU210 has higher affinity for CB1

## Pharmacological Mechanisms

The anxiolytic (anti-anxiety) effects of cannabinoids operate through multiple pathways that distinguish them from conventional anxiolytics like benzodiazepines and SSRIs.

- **CB1 receptor-mediated anxiolysis:** CB1 receptor activation in the amygdala (the brain's fear center) reduces fear responses and threat processing. At low-to-moderate doses, THC activates CB1 receptors and produces anxiolytic effects. At high doses, the same mechanism can produce anxiety and paranoia, reflecting the classic biphasic dose-response curve.

- **CBD's multi-receptor anxiolysis:** CBD reduces anxiety through 5-HT1A (serotonin) receptor activation, which is the same pathway targeted by buspirone. CBD also enhances endocannabinoid signaling by inhibiting FAAH (the enzyme that breaks down anandamide), effectively boosting the body's natural anxiolytic compounds.

- **Hippocampal neurogenesis:** The 2005 international study showed that HU210 promotes hippocampal neurogenesis -- the birth of new neurons in a brain region critical for emotional regulation and memory. Chronic stress, a primary driver of anxiety, shrinks the hippocampus. Cannabinoid-induced neurogenesis reverses this damage, providing a structural rather than merely symptomatic treatment for anxiety.

- **HPA axis modulation:** The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis controls the stress response. CBD normalizes HPA axis hyperactivity by reducing cortisol release, which is chronically elevated in anxiety disorders.

- **Endocannabinoid deficiency hypothesis:** Clinical evidence suggests that some individuals with anxiety disorders may have reduced endocannabinoid tone (lower anandamide levels). Cannabis supplementation may correct this deficiency, particularly CBD which preserves anandamide.

- **GABA modulation:** Cannabinoids enhance GABAergic transmission in the amygdala, similar to benzodiazepines but through a different mechanism that may carry lower dependence and withdrawal risks.

## Clinical Evidence

The clinical evidence for cannabis in anxiety is moderate, with a CHI value of 14.

- **Hippocampal neurogenesis (2005, International):** The discovery that cannabinoids promote hippocampal neurogenesis is a paradigm-shifting finding. Unlike benzodiazepines, which merely suppress anxiety symptoms, cannabinoids appear to actively rebuild brain structures damaged by chronic stress. Most recreational drugs decrease hippocampal neurogenesis; cannabinoids uniquely increase it.

- **Patient-reported effectiveness (2009, Boston):** In the largest comparative effectiveness study, 775 HIV/AIDS patients across 10 locations rated cannabis as more effective than standard medications for anxiety. This real-world evidence from a large, diverse population carries significant weight.

- **CBD's broad therapeutic profile (2010, Sao Paulo):** The comprehensive review establishing CBD as antipsychotic, anxiolytic, and antidepressant positions it as a uniquely versatile cannabinoid for treating the common anxiety-depression comorbidity.

- **Biphasic dose response:** The consistently observed pattern -- low doses reduce anxiety while high doses increase it -- is well-documented across multiple studies and is critical for clinical guidance. This effect appears to be CB1-mediated and may reflect differential activation of anxiolytic vs. anxiogenic circuits in the amygdala.

- **Acute vs. chronic effects:** While acute THC administration at moderate doses is anxiolytic, chronic heavy use is associated with increased anxiety risk. This suggests that intermittent, moderate dosing may be more effective than continuous high-dose use.

## Dosing Considerations

- **CBD is the first-line cannabinoid for anxiety:** CBD 10-25 mg per dose, 1-3 times daily, provides reliable anxiolysis without the dose-dependent anxiety risk of THC. Start with 10 mg and titrate upward.

- **THC dose window for anxiety:** If using THC, stay within the 2.5-7.5 mg range per dose. Below 2.5 mg may be sub-therapeutic; above 7.5-10 mg significantly increases the risk of paradoxical anxiety and paranoia.

- **CBD:THC ratio:** A 5:1 to 10:1 CBD:THC ratio is recommended for anxiety patients. CBD buffers THC's anxiogenic potential at higher doses while both cannabinoids contribute to anxiolysis through different pathways.

- **Strain selection:** Indica and indica-dominant hybrids are preferred due to their higher myrcene content (sedating terpene) and lower THC:CBD ratios. Strains high in linalool (lavender-scented) and beta-caryophyllene (CB2 agonist terpene) provide additional anxiolytic effects.

- **Acute anxiety/panic:** Sublingual CBD 25-50 mg for rapid onset during acute anxiety episodes. Onset within 15-30 minutes sublingually vs. 1-2 hours orally.

- **Chronic anxiety maintenance:** CBD 15-25 mg twice daily (morning and evening) provides sustained anxiolytic coverage. Consistent dosing is more important than high doses.

## Safety and Interactions

- **Benzodiazepine interaction:** Combining cannabis with benzodiazepines (Xanax, Valium, Klonopin) can cause excessive sedation and cognitive impairment. Patients transitioning from benzodiazepines to cannabis should taper benzodiazepines gradually under medical supervision, as abrupt discontinuation can cause seizures.

- **SSRI augmentation:** CBD can be safely combined with SSRIs and may enhance their anxiolytic effect. However, CBD inhibits CYP2C19, which metabolizes several SSRIs (citalopram, escitalopram), potentially increasing blood levels. Monitor for increased SSRI side effects.

- **Paradoxical anxiety with THC:** Approximately 10-20% of individuals experience increased anxiety with THC, even at moderate doses. These individuals should use CBD-only preparations. This response may be genetically determined.

- **Cannabis withdrawal anxiety:** Abrupt cessation of regular cannabis use can cause rebound anxiety lasting 1-2 weeks. This should not be misinterpreted as evidence that cannabis causes anxiety -- it reflects the neuroadaptation of discontinuing any effective anxiolytic.

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** Cannabis use during pregnancy is associated with adverse developmental outcomes. Anxiety treatment during pregnancy should be discussed with a healthcare provider.

- **Driving impairment:** Even at anxiolytic doses, cannabis can impair driving ability. Patients should wait at least 4 hours after using cannabis before driving.

## Mind-Body Perspective

"In the depths of anxiety reside the keys to our imagination and access to the power of our creativity." Anxious people can imagine everything that can go wrong -- and often produce the very thing feared. The task: take back the power of imagination and direct it toward positive outcomes.
