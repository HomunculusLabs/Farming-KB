# Canker Fungi and Plant Galls

Canker fungi and gall-inducing organisms represent a major class of plant pathogenic fungi
that cause localized, often conspicuous deformations and necroses on their hosts. Cankers
are discrete, necrotic lesions on stems, branches, or twigs, while galls are abnormal
growths of host tissue stimulated by the presence of the parasite. Together, these
fungi and fungus-like organisms cause significant economic and ecological damage across
agricultural, forestry, and natural ecosystems worldwide.

## Foliar Pathogens

Fungal foliar pathogens are recognized in the field by characteristic symptoms including
water-soaked areas, chlorosis (yellowing), and necrosis (tissue death). These symptoms
alone are insufficient for species-level identification, which requires compound
microscopy of reproductive fruiting structures. Key diagnostic structures include:

- **Acervuli** — subcuticular or subepidermal cushion-shaped fruiting bodies producing
  conidia, typical of anthracnose fungi
- **Pycnidia** — flask-shaped asexual fruiting bodies that exude conidial cirrhi
- **Perithecia** — globose or flask-shaped sexual fruiting bodies containing asci
- **Apothecia** — open, cup- or disc-shaped ascocarps

Accurate identification demands sectioning of infected tissue and careful examination
of spore morphology, septation, and the architecture of these reproductive structures.

## Parasitic Fungi on Grasses

### Temperate Grass Parasites

Temperate grasses harbor a diverse assemblage of parasitic fungi spanning multiple
taxonomic groups. Ascomycete genera include *Gaeumannomyces* (notably *G. graminis*,
cause of take-all disease), *Gibberella* (sexual stage of *Fusarium*), *Monographella*,
*Phyllachora* (producing tar-spot stromata on leaf surfaces), and *Pleospora*.

Hyphomycete parasites on temperate grasses are dominated by dematiaceous and
hyaline genera including *Alternaria*, *Cladosporium*, *Drechslera* (leaf spot and
net blotch pathogens), and *Fusarium*. Coelomycete representatives include
*Ascochyta* and *Colletotrichum* (anthracnose agents), both producing acervuli
or pycnidia within leaf lesions.

### Tropical Grass Parasites

Tropical grass systems support a distinct parasitic mycota. Key genera include
*Cercospora* (cercospora leaf spots), *Magnaporthe*/*Pyricularia* (the rice blast
pathogen complex, one of the most devastating cereal diseases globally),
*Rhynchosporium* (leaf scald of barley and other grasses), and *Sphaerulina*.
The *Magnaporthe oryzae*/*Pyricularia oryzae* complex alone infects over 50 grass
species and is a model organism for studying fungal plant pathogenicity.

## Needle-Cast Fungi

Conifer needle-cast diseases are caused by a suite of ascomycetes that infect,
kill, and ultimately cast needles. Major genera include:

- *Lophodermium* — one of the most widespread conifer needle pathogens, producing
  characteristic black hysterothecia on shed needles
- *Lophodermella* — causes needle cast and needle drop in pines, with similar
  lifecycle strategies to *Lophodermium*
- *Elytroderma* — causes needle cast and witches'-broom deformations
- *Hypodermella* — infects needles of various conifers, forming subepidermal
  fruiting bodies
- *Lirula* — an obligate biotroph that produces distinctive setose hysterothecia
  on discolored needles

These fungi are critical in forest health, causing growth loss, reduced timber
quality, and in severe cases, tree mortality.

## Canker Types

Canker fungi are classified by the morphology and development pattern of the
canker they produce:

### 1. Woody Stem Perennial (Target) Cankers

Target cankers are characterized by concentric zones of callus and necrotic
tissue, giving a bull's-eye appearance in cross-section. The pathogen kills a
zone of bark and cambium annually, followed by host wound response that forms
a callus ridge, which is then re-invaded the following season. Key genera:

- *Nectria* — cosmopolitan canker pathogens, including *N. cinnabarina* (coral
  spot) and *N. ditissima* (European canker of apple)
- *Ceratocystis* — includes *C. fagacearum* (oak wilt), vectored by bark beetles,
  and *C. wagenerii* (black-stain root disease)

### 2. Woody Stem Diffuse Cankers

Diffuse cankers lack concentric callus ridges; instead, necrosis spreads rapidly
without host containment, often girdling and killing the stem. Key genera:

- *Valsa* — common on deciduous trees; *V. ceratosperma* causes perennial canker
  of stone fruits
- *Diaporthe* (sexual stage of *Phomopsis*) — causes cankers, dieback, and fruit
  rot across diverse woody hosts
- *Cryptosporella* — associated with cankers on hardwood trees
- *Cryphonectria* — notably *C. parasitica* (chestnut blight), which devastated
  American chestnut; hypovirulence strains provide a biological control model

### 3. Twig and Shoot Blights

These fungi cause rapid necrosis of young shoots and twigs, often progressing to
larger branches. Genera include *Melanconis*, *Sclerophoma*, *Cytospora* (sexual
stage *Valsa*), and *Diplodia* (notably *D. sapinea*/*D. pinea*, a major pathogen
of pines worldwide).

## Gall-Associated Fungi

Several fungi induce gall formation on their hosts through complex manipulation
of host developmental pathways:

- *Endocronartium harknessii* — causes western gall rust on pine, producing
  spherical galls on branches and stems that release basidiospores
- *Gymnosporangium* — [[rust-fungi]] with obligate alternation between juniper
  (telial host) and Rosaceae (aecial host), causing conspicuous galls on
  juniper twigs (cedar-apple rust complex)
- *Apiosporina morbosa* — the causal agent of black knot of *Prunus*, producing
  characteristic black, cylindrical gall formations on branches
- *Phomopsis* — induces gall-like swellings on various woody hosts
- *Cyttaria* — produces large, striking orange fruiting bodies in galls on
  *Nothofagus* (southern beech) in South America and Australasia

## Synchytrium (Chytridiales)

The genus *Synchytrium* (order Chytridiales) comprises approximately 120 species
of obligate biotrophs that cause galls on an exceptionally broad range of hosts
spanning algae, mosses, ferns, and flowering plants — collectively infecting more
than 1,350 host species. These chytrids are among the most ancient lineages of
gall-inducing organisms. Infection begins with zoospore encystment on the host
surface, followed by penetration and establishment of a resting sporangium that
induces hypertrophy and hyperplasia of surrounding host cells. *Synchytrium
endobioticum* is a notorious species causing potato wart disease, subject to
strict quarantine regulations worldwide.

## Plasmodiophoromycetes

The Plasmodiophoromycetes are a group of approximately 15 genera of obligate
endoparasites traditionally studied by mycologists but now classified within
the protist supergroup Rhizaria. These organisms exist as multinucleate
protoplasts (plasmodia) within host cells, lacking cell walls during their
vegetative phase. Reproduction involves biflagellate zoospores and the
formation of resting structures called cystosori (aggregate resting spores).

Notable genera include:

- *Plasmodiophora brassicae* — cause of clubroot disease in crucifers, one of
  the most economically important diseases of brassica crops
- *Spongospora subterranea* — causes powdery scab of potato and serves as a
  vector for potato mop-top virus
- *Polymyxa* species — root parasites that act as vectors for several important
  plant viruses (e.g., beet necrotic yellow vein virus, wheat spindle streak
  mosaic virus)

## Fungi on Fruits, Nuts, and Flowers

### Blossom Blights

Blossom blight pathogens infect flowers, often progressing into fruit and
structural tissues. Major agents include:

- *Botrytis cinerea* — gray mold, one of the most polyphagous fungal pathogens,
  affecting hundreds of plant species; causes blossom blight, fruit rot, and
  damping-off
- *Monilinia* species — brown rot of stone fruits (*M. fructicola*, *M. laxa*)
  and blossom blight of pome fruits (*M. fructigena*)

### Fruit Rots and Scabs

Fruit rotting fungi cause extensive post-harvest losses and pre-harvest fruit
degradation. Scab diseases produce corky, superficial lesions:

- *Elsinoë* — causes anthracnose and scab diseases on diverse hosts including
  citrus (*E. fawcettii*, citrus scab), grape, and avocado
- *Venturia* — notably *V. inaequalis* (apple scab) and *V. pirina* (pear
  scab), among the most economically significant diseases of pome fruits

## Ecological and Economic Significance

Canker fungi and gall-inducing organisms collectively represent one of the
largest categories of plant disease agents. Their impact spans natural forest
ecosystems (where they drive gap dynamics and succession), agricultural systems
(causing billions of dollars in annual losses), and managed landscapes (nursery,
ornamental, and urban forestry contexts). Understanding their taxonomy, biology,
and host interactions remains central to plant pathology and fungal ecology.
