# Mycorrhizal Inoculum Production

Methods for producing your own arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal fungal inoculum, from field harvesting to greenhouse pot culturing. Based on Jeff Lowenfels' *Teaming with Fungi*. See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for the overview of mycorrhizal types and [[inoculation-methods]] for mushroom spawn production.

## Why Make Your Own Inoculum

Commercial inoculants are available, but producing your own ensures:
- **Strain adaptation** to local soil conditions and host plants
- **Lower cost** for large-scale applications (orchards, food forests, farms)
- **Higher diversity** of fungal species than commercial single-strain products
- **Independence** from supply chains

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Inoculum

### Rodale Method (Field Production)

The Rodale Institute developed a reliable field-scale method:

1. Four months before predicted last frost, germinate bahiagrass (*Paspalum notatum*) in vermiculite
2. Three months before last frost, transplant seedlings into conical plastic pots (drainage holes plugged) with pathogen-free growing media
3. After last frost, set up inoculum production area by covering growing beds with weed barrier fabric to prevent contamination
4. During growing season, weed and water as needed — mycorrhizae will colonize the roots over months
5. The following spring, harvest inoculum: remove dead plant material, shake growing media free of roots. The media contains spores, hyphae, and root fragments — all viable propagules

### Greenhouse Pot Culture Recipe

For controlled production of specific fungal strains:

1. Sterilize plastic pots in dishwasher with bleach-based detergent, or wash with 10% bleach solution
2. Soak host plant seeds 5 minutes in 1:9 bleach:water solution, then rinse thoroughly
3. Place seeds between damp paper towels in a sealed plastic bag to pre-germinate
4. Plant in 6-10 inch sterile pots with pathogen-free growing media (vermiculite, sand, or commercial mix)
5. Mix commercial inoculum into media at 1:20 ratio (inoculum:media). Roll seeds in mix or sprinkle over seeds. Plant 3-6 seeds per pot
6. Feed host plants periodically with low-phosphorus fertilizer (P number of 6 or less): alfalfa meal (2-1-3), fish emulsion (5-2-2), soybean meal (7-2-1), compost tea, or diluted fertilizer
7. After ~6 weeks, mycorrhizae begin forming. Sample roots to test colonization rates
8. At ~14 weeks, stop watering to dry out the media (stimulates sporulation)
9. At ~16 weeks, gently pull plants, shake off media (contains spores and hyphal fragments) into container. Cut roots into small pieces (contain vesicles and hyphae). Store cool and dry — viable ~1 year

### Host Plants for Pot Culture

Successful host plants include corn, onion, strawberry, peanut, sorghum, big bluestem, pencilflower, bahiagrass, and kudzu. Bahiagrass is the preferred commercial host (C4 grass, highly mycorrhizae-dependent).

### Field Culturing

1. Start with any commercial soil mix without added fertilizers or manures
2. Sterilize at 260°F (121°C) for ~90 minutes (autoclave or oven — oven creates unpleasant odors)
3. After 1-2 weeks, mix 1 part field-collected soil with 3 parts sterilized soil
4. Choose collection site: previously productive field with rich organic matter and high mycorrhizal diversity
5. Have field soil tested for propagules before use

**Strawberry field culture method:** Transplant strawberry plant from field to pot of sterile soil, or establish a runner in potted field soil then transplant to sterile soil. Nurture until mature. Remove plants, save soil clinging to roots, chop roots. Use resulting products as inoculum.

### Pot Culture vs Field Culture

**Pot culturing is usually far more successful.** The overcrowding of fungal hyphae in the confined pot encourages spore production. Fungi and roots grow until the pot constrains them; unable to expand hyphal branches, the fungi channel carbon energy into sporulation.

## Ectomycorrhizal Inoculum

Ectomycorrhizal fungi are easier to produce if you have the appropriate host plant:

### Soil Transfer Method

Studies show soil containing ectomycorrhizal fungi from one location can successfully associate with host trees at another location — even across continents (New Zealand, India, Italy, Wisconsin, Alaskan tundra soils all worked in one plot). Both conifers and deciduous trees respond.

### Fruiting Body Method

1. Collect mushrooms of known ectomycorrhizal species (boletes, lactarius, amanita, tricholoma, etc.)
2. Make spore prints: remove stem, place cap spore-side down on paper/glass for 24 hours
3. **Easiest method:** Cut fruiting bodies into small pieces and mix directly into potting soil
4. Alternatively, spray seeds with spore suspensions or add propagules adjacent to roots
5. You can't use too much — a few spores are sufficient, more is fine
6. This is excellent for growing single strains or creating custom mixes

## Harvesting Spores from the Field

Four methods for collecting arbuscular mycorrhizal spores:

1. **Direct soil isolation** — extract spores from soil samples (moderate success rate)
2. **Root fragment collection** — dig up roots with visible colonization, chop and use as inoculum
3. **Sporocarp collection** — find and collect any fruiting bodies of AMF-associated fungi
4. **Trap culture** — plant host seedlings in field soil, let mycorrhizae colonize, then harvest

### Wet-Sieving Method

The standard lab technique for isolating spores from soil: suspend soil in water, pour through nested sieves of decreasing mesh size (commonly 250μm, 100μm, 50μm). Spores collect on the finest sieves and can be identified under microscope.

### Staining for Microscopy

To visualize arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization in roots: clear root tissue with KOH, stain with trypan blue or acid fuchsin, mount on slides. Arbuscules, vesicles, and hyphal coils become visible. This is the standard method for determining colonization percentage.
