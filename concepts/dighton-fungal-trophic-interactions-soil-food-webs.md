# Fungal Trophic Interactions and Soil Food Webs

Fungi occupy central positions in soil food webs, connecting primary
production and decomposition to higher trophic levels. Nutrient
transformations among ecosystem components result from trophic
interactions within food webs, and the transformations carried out by
saprotrophic decomposers use energy and nutrients from dead organic
matter. Fungi play important and direct roles within food webs by being
consumed by fungal grazers, and as dead organisms, by saprotrophic
activities of bacteria and fauna.

## Fungi as Food Resources

The accumulation of nutrients into fungal biomass makes fungi excellent
food sources for animals. Fruiting bodies are ideal food sources for
grazing animals, supporting secondary production (grazer biomass) in
ecosystems. In forest ecosystems, the surface litter layer supports
saprotrophic fungal communities whose fruiting structures (mushrooms)
are food for grazing animals, connecting decomposition to higher trophic
levels.

Reindeer and moose in boreal ecosystems depend on arboreal lichen
("old man's beard") as winter food. Caribou and reindeer rely on
terrestrial lichens that are resistant to trampling effects. In
Svalbard, lichen biomass may become severely depleted due to intense
grazing pressure, low growth rates, and indirect effects of reindeer
trampling.

## Fungal Grazing Effects on Decomposition

Grazing of fungal hyphae by soil animals affects both colonization of
litter resources and rates of nutrient mineralization. Newell (1984a,b)
showed that collembola grazing altered the vertical distribution of
competing mycelia of Mycena galopus and Marasmius androcaceous in a
spruce forest floor. Preferential grazing of Marasmius restricted its
growth to lower depths, allowing Mycena to dominate in the A soil
horizon.

Coleman et al. (1990) showed that reduction in microbial predators in
forest ecosystems with high soil fauna densities led to increased
decomposition through relief of grazing pressure. In contrast, in
agricultural soils with low fauna densities, faunal reduction decreased
decomposition, suggesting synergistic interactions between fungi and
soil fauna in those systems.

Beare et al. (1992) compared tilled and untilled agricultural soils
and found that exclusion of fungivorous arthropods reduced litter dry
mass loss by only 5%, but significantly altered nitrogen dynamics in
no-till surface litter. Saprotrophic fungi were responsible for up to
86% of net nitrogen immobilization (1.8 g per square meter) into
surface litters when fungivorous microarthropods were excluded.

## Mycorrhizal Grazing

Faunal grazing on arbuscular mycorrhizal extraradical hyphae has been
shown to reduce the efficiency of the mycorrhizal symbiosis for the
host plant. This effect is likely more important in agroecosystems,
where the fungivorous nematode Aphelenchoides spp. can reach high
densities. The trophic interactions between forest floor fungi and
fungivorous fauna require more research to understand implications of
changing fungal communities under anthropogenic stress.

## Dung Fungi and Coprophilous Communities

Animal dung forms specific organic resources selecting for particular
fungal groups. Richardson (2001) showed highly significant differences
in fungal community structure among dung of six animal species. These
coprophilous fungi are often specific to their niche but provide food
for a variety of fungivorous animals and are significant in ecosystem
function. Marine wood-boring isopods demonstrate the nutritional
importance of fungi: the gribble Limnoria tripunctata has increased
longevity when feeding on fungus-colonized wood and is incapable of
reproduction without marine fungi in its diet, likely due to enhanced
availability of proteins, amino acids, and vitamins.

## Pollution Effects on Trophic Interactions

Acid rain treatment induced significant changes in nematode trophic
groups, with reduction in omnivorous and predatory nematodes and
increase in fungal feeders (Aphelenchoides spp.). Pollutants can be
transferred up food webs by grazing of mycelium or fruiting structures.
The presence of pollutant chemicals within fungal mycelium can change
the chemical and physical state of the pollutant, making it more or less
toxic to fungal consumers.

Fruiting body surveys revealed that some mycorrhizal fungi fruited
more abundantly under pollution stress, despite reduced mycorrhizal
root colonization. This apparent contradiction suggests that pollutant
effects on fungal community structure may be more complex than simple
biomass reduction, affecting the balance between vegetative growth and
reproductive allocation.

## Energy Flow Through Fungal Channels

Fungi mediate energy flow from dead organic matter through multiple
channels: direct consumption of fruiting bodies, grazing on mycelium,
consumption of spores, and consumption of animals that have fed on
fungi. Each channel represents a different rate of energy and nutrient
transfer, with mycelial grazing representing the most continuous pathway
and fruiting body consumption representing a pulsed resource. The
fungal decomposer pathway is particularly important in forest ecosystems
where fungal biomass often exceeds bacterial biomass.
