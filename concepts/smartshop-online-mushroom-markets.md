# Smartshop and Online Mushroom Markets

## Overview

The commercial market for hallucinogenic mushrooms in Europe emerged and expanded rapidly during the late 1990s and early 2000s, driven primarily by smartshops in the Netherlands and the UK. The internet subsequently became a major distribution channel, enabling international sales and information dissemination. Understanding these market structures is key to analyzing the emerging trend in mushroom use and the effectiveness of legal responses.

## Smartshops: The Dutch Model

A **smartshop** is a retail establishment found predominantly in the Netherlands that specializes in psychoactive herbal substances that are legal, alongside vitamins, mineral supplements, and other health products. Many also sell new synthetic drugs that have not yet been placed under control.

### Market Scale

- An estimated 120 to 150 smartshops operate in the Netherlands (exact numbers unknown)
- They are concentrated in Amsterdam
- Smartshops also exist in some coffee shops and headshops
- "Paddo cabs" in some areas offer home delivery of hallucinogenic mushrooms
- Grow shops (supplying home cannabis cultivators) also sometimes stock mushrooms
- According to the Dutch Risk Assessment Report, magic mushrooms constituted approximately **50% of smartshop turnover**

### Product Types Sold

Smartshops and similar retailers sell a range of hallucinogenic mushroom products:

- **Fresh mushrooms:** Most commonly [[Psilocybe cubensis]] varieties, sold by weight (typically 30 gram portions)
- **Dried mushrooms:** For longer storage and later use
- **Sclerotia (truffles):** Especially [[Psilocybe mexicana]], sold as "philosopher's stones"
- **Growkits:** Complete cultivation kits including colonized substrate, humidity bags, and instructions
- **Spore prints and spore syringes:** For home cultivation on a substrate of nutrients
- **Spawn bags:** Sterile bags containing hardwood chips inoculated with mushroom mycelium

## The UK Retail Market

Prior to the legal change in July 2005, an estimated 300 shops and market stalls across the UK sold hallucinogenic mushrooms. The UK Treasury collected sales tax on mushroom sales estimated at up to 175,000 GBP annually on a turnover of approximately 1 million GBP per annum.

A 2004 Mixmag survey of UK clubbers found that 54.6% had purchased their most recent batch of mushrooms from a club, shop, or market stall, 16% obtained them from a friend, and 4.2% purchased them via the internet.

## Brand Names and User Terminology

Hallucinogenic mushrooms are known by a wide variety of names that often reflect regional slang, marketing strategies, and cultural associations:

**English common names:** magic mushrooms, shrooms, mush, mushies, boomers, psilocybes, cubes, liberty caps, caps, philosopher's stone, sacred mushrooms, teonanacatl (Aztec: "divine flesh"), Mexican mushrooms, moon children

**European language equivalents:**
- Dutch: paddos
- German: Zauberpilze, Psychoaktive Pilze
- French: champignons hallucinogenes, champis
- Hungarian: varazsgombák, pszilocibingombák
- Italian: funghi magici
- Polish: magiczne grzybki
- Swedish: magiska svampar, psykedeliska svampar
- Czech: magicke houby
- Spanish: hongos alucinogenos

The late 1990s commercialization through smartshops introduced new marketing labels. Mushrooms were sold under the general umbrella of **"Herbal highs"** to align with the consumer trend toward natural and organic products. Other products sold under this label included herbal ecstasy and [[Salvia divinorum]].

## Online Internet Shops

### Market Snapshot (January 2006)

A systematic search using Google and AltaVista with English keywords ("magic mushrooms shop," "buy magic mushrooms," "psilocybe cubensis shop," "buy psilocybe cubensis") identified **39 distinct online shops** selling hallucinogenic mushroom products.

### Geographic Distribution

- **82%** of identified online shops were based in the Netherlands
- Remaining 18% included four Austrian shops (selling growkits and spore prints), two German sites, one UK site linked to a Dutch shop, and one Polish site (primarily selling cannabis products)

Website statistics from one Dutch online shop showed visitor origins: Netherlands 73%, Belgium 14%, Germany 2%, France 2%.

### Products and Pricing

| Product | Quantity | Price Range (EUR) |
|---------|----------|-------------------|
| Fresh Psilocybe cubensis (Mexican) | 30 g | 8.50 to 45.28 |
| Fresh Copelandia cyanescens | 10 g | 13.00 to 17.50 |
| Psilocybe mexicana truffles | 15 g | 10.00 to 17.50 |
| Spawn bag (Psilocybe azurescens) | 1 bag | 24.00 to 25.00 |
| Growkits | 1 kit | 25.00 to 100.00 |
| Spore prints | 1 print/syringe | 9.00 to 28.00 |

The non-profit organization **Free Spore Ring Europe** (fsre.org) distributed spore prints of various strains for approximately 1 EUR per print.

### International Shipping Practices

Most online shops offered international shipping but many excluded countries where sales were prohibited. Sites commonly advised customers to verify local legal status before ordering. Many shops provided multi-language versions (primarily English, French, and German) to attract foreign customers.

## Information Quality from Retailers

A snapshot of 21 online shops revealed significant variability and bias in the information provided:

- **Majority** warned against use with medication and/or in combination with alcohol or other drugs
- Only **two thirds** cautioned against use by people with depression or psychosis
- About **half** provided dosage information and safe use practices (empty stomach, drink liquids, consume in a quiet safe environment)
- Most sites described sought-after positive effects
- **Vast majority** excluded information on possible negative side effects
- Only a **minority** explicitly mentioned nausea and sickness
- Information was generally biased toward positive effects

Dutch smartshop product labels often included warnings about use by minors, during pregnancy, with medication, by those with mental illness, or when driving. Some labels notably included UK drug helpline numbers, suggesting deliberate targeting of UK customers.

## Impact of Legal Changes on Markets

Following the UK ban in July 2005:
- UK internet sites stopped selling hallucinogenic mushrooms
- Some sites claimed to be "sold out" while posting notices about impending legal changes
- Online headshops began switching to legal alternatives: [[Amanita muscaria]] (fly agaric), [[Amanita pantherina]] (panther cap), and Purple Ohms capsules (containing LSA from Hawaiian Baby Woodrose)
- A 2005 Mixmag survey found 67% of respondents reported reduced availability post-ban

This pattern of market substitution, where prohibition of one substance drives retailers to market potentially more dangerous alternatives, is a recurring feature of drug control policy.

## Wild Mushroom Foraging

Evidence on the extent of wild foraging is sparse but exists. User websites provide identification guides and location sharing. Qualitative reports from Poland, Ireland, and the Czech Republic document seasonal foraging for wild [[Psilocybe semilanceata]]. However, the risk of misidentifying poisonous species limits the popularity of this approach.
