## Mushroom Poisoning Incidents and Remedies

Julius A. Palmer"s writings provide some of the earliest documented accounts
of mushroom poisoning in America, along with practical remedies derived from
[[palmer-mushroom-cooking-and-personal-experience]]. His cases illustrate several distinct mechanisms of fungal
toxicity and challenge common assumptions about what makes a mushroom
dangerous.

## Case 1: Fermentation Poisoning from *Boletus subtomentosus*

Palmer described multiple cases of illness from a favorite boletus — the
olive-red boletus (*Boletus subtomentosus*, also called *B. subtomentosus*
in Peck's catalogue). The critical insight was that the mushroom itself was
not inherently poisonous; rather, the poisoning resulted from a chemical
change in its substance when the spongy flesh became waterlogged.

This mushroom normally shows a marked acid reaction under litmus paper. When
heavily charged with water from dew or rain, the spongy substance enters a
state of acidulous fermentation, producing a brilliant blue color when
pressed, cut, or broken. Three cases of illness from this cause were brought
to Palmer's notice almost simultaneously. Symptoms in all cases were limited to
excessive purging and vomiting, similar to what Worthington G. Smith
experienced in his experiments.

**Lesson:** Not that the mushroom is unfit for food, but that only young
specimens with substance too solid to admit of fermentation should ever be
eaten. This case established an important principle that edibility is not an
inherent property of a species but can depend on the condition and age of the
specimen.

## Case 2: Cross-Contamination from *Coprinus deliquescens*

Palmer was once poisoned not by eating a poisonous species, but by consuming
[[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]] that had been contaminated. Common mushrooms were sent to him
in a box containing a large number of *Coprinus deliquescens* (inky cap)
specimens that were in a state of decomposition. The deliquescent coprinus
naturally turns to ink independent of any fluid, and the manuscript of Palmer's
article was actually written with the result of such deliquescence.

The symptoms appeared rapidly after swallowing the edible mushrooms: dizziness,
nausea, purging, perspiration, and alternate cold spells — resembling the
effects of using tobacco for the first time. All symptoms passed within an
hour, demonstrating the rapid effect of mushroom toxins on the human system.

Palmer noted that his "engines refused to reverse action" — meaning that once
the symptoms began, vomiting could not be voluntarily induced, making the
poison particularly insidious.

## Case 3: Potash-F forced Mushrooms

A gardener sent Palmer a basket of horse mushrooms that had been forced in
growth by the use of potash. When cooked, they behaved like pieces of soap,
and the cooking liquor resembled strong lye. While no one was poisoned (the
unnatural appearance and behavior prevented consumption), this incident
illustrated how growing medium and [[gourmet-medicinal-mushroom-oyster-cultivation-methods]] can affect fungal
chemistry.

## Case 4: Environmental Contamination

Palmer found specimens of *Boletus aestivis* so strongly impregnated with the
odor of decayed fish as to be unfit for food. Similarly, specimens of *Boletus
pachypus* communicated their strong nutty flavor without direct contact to
potatoes placed beside them in his refrigerator. The shaggy mane (*Coprinus
comatus*) was delicious when found in lawns but flavorless and potentially
deleterious when collected from dust-heaps, brick-yards, or city offal.

These cases illustrated what Palmer called "the extremely susceptible character
of mushrooms" — their tendency to absorb and concentrate compounds from their
[[cannabis-growing-environment-setup]].

## Palmer"s Recommended Remedy

Palmer prescribed a specific remedy for all cases of suspected mushroom
poisoning: two wineglasses of equal parts whiskey and sweet-oil (olive oil).
This neutralized the poison from the *Coprinus deliquescens* contamination,
and Palmer was "no worse for the experience" within a few hours.

He recommended this remedy over emetics in all cases. The whiskey could be
omitted or substituted with vinegar, but sweet-oil should be taken liberally.
Palmer theorized that the Italians ate many dangerous fungi with impunity
because fresh specimens" properties were modified by sweet-oil, and preserved
specimens were neutralized by pickle. Either element rendered harmless the
peculiar alkali to which mushrooms owe their noxious qualities.

This theory — that fungal toxicity is mediated by alkaline compounds that can
be neutralized by acids (vinegar) or fats (olive oil) — was one of Palmer's
more notable contributions to understanding mushroom poisoning mechanisms.

## The Taste Warning System

Palmer emphasized that many non-poisonous mushrooms simply do not agree with
the human system but are not deadly poisons. He cited the example of a Harvard
professor who mistook *[[boletus-pachypus]]* for an edible species and ate it
freely despite its blue-staining reaction and exclusion from English
mycological lists of esculents. Neither the professor nor his friends required
"the services of the undertaker."

The critical distinction was between digestive disturbance (nausea, vomiting,
purging from species that are merely unpalatable or in poor condition) and
genuine systemic toxicity (the potentially fatal effects of *Amanita*
species containing amanitine).

## The Alkali Theory of Fungal Toxicity

Berkeley had proposed that mushrooms might become poisonous through an excess
of alkali. Palmer initially appeared to endorse this idea but later carefully
considered and rejected it in a subsequent article for the Boston Medical and
Surgical Journal. The potash-forced mushroom incident may have influenced
this reconsideration, as the potash-grown mushrooms showed alkaline properties
but were not consumed, leaving the question open.

Palmer did, however, maintain that many mushrooms owed their noxious qualities
to "a superfluity" of alkaline compounds, which explained why acidic substances
(vinegar) and fats (olive oil) could neutralize their harmful effects.

## The Amanitine Problem

The most serious form of mushroom poisoning — from amanitine-containing
Amanita species — was distinct from all the cases Palmer personally
experienced. Amanitine absorption by the human system caused symptoms
fundamentally different from the digestive distress of spoiled or fermented
fungi, and the purgative or emetic treatments that were effective for
decomposition poisoning were useless or even aggravating for amanitine
poisoning.

This distinction was one of Palmer"s most important contributions: recognizing
that "poisonous mushroom" was not a single category but encompassed several
fundamentally different toxic mechanisms requiring different treatments.
