# Fungal Decomposition Processes

## Overview

Fungal decomposition is one of the most critical ecosystem processes, converting dead plant and animal remains into mineral nutrients that sustain primary production. As Forsyth and Miyata (1984) described: "under the silent, relentless chemical jaws of the fungi, the debris of the forest floor quickly disappears." Fungi accomplish this through extracellular enzyme secretion, a fundamentally different strategy from animal digestion where enzymes act within a gut.

## Mechanism of Decomposition

Saprotrophic fungi release exoenzymes into their environment to break down complex organic molecules in dead plant and animal remains. The degradative products are then absorbed through the fungal hyphal surface. This reliance on extracellular enzymatic activity is less efficient than internal digestion, meaning some mineralized nutrients escape absorption and enter the soil solution as available fertility.

The rate of decomposition depends on three main factors:
- **Resource quality**: Chemical composition of the substrate (C:N ratio, lignin:N ratio, polyphenol content)
- **Edaphic factors**: Available moisture and temperature
- **Colonization**: Arrival of appropriate saprotrophic organisms with matching enzymatic competence

## Resource Quality Controls

The carbon:nitrogen and lignin:nitrogen ratios are primary determinants of decomposition resistance. High C:N or lignin:N ratios reduce decomposition rates. Vanlauwe et al. (1997) showed that the (lignin + polyphenol):N ratio was the strongest predictor of both litter mass loss and nitrogen mineralization rates. Secondary plant metabolites like polyphenols and tannins also inhibit microbial decomposition.

## Fungal Succession During Decomposition

Decomposition is not performed by a single organism. A succession of fungi colonizes resources as their chemical composition changes:

1. **Stage 1 (Sugar fungi)**: Fast-colonizing species (Lophodermium, Ceuthospora, Lophodermella) utilize readily available soluble carbohydrates from freshly fallen litter
2. **Stage 2 (Microfungi expansion)**: Verticicladium and other decomposing microfungi invade as simple sugars are depleted
3. **Stage 3 (Basidiomycete dominance)**: Marasmius and Collybia species degrade cellulose and lignin, reducing C:N ratios from ~200:1 toward 30:1
4. **Stage 4 (Mycorrhizal invasion)**: Mycorrhizal fungi enter as the resource becomes increasingly humified

This succession follows a trajectory from r-selected (opportunistic, fast-growing) to K-selected (competitive, slow-growing with greater enzymatic diversity) strategies.

## Environmental Controls

Temperature positively influences extracellular cellulase activity. Cellulose becomes unavailable for microbial use when the cellulose:lignin ratio drops below 0.5. In wet tropical forests, up to 7% of leaf litter never reaches the forest floor but is trapped and decomposed in the canopy by fungal rhizomorphs (Marasmius, Marasmiellus), creating an aerial "soil" that releases nutrients through stemflow and throughfall.

## Decomposition and Soil Profile Development

Where decomposition is slow, organic matter accumulates as peat. Where decomposition is rapid (agricultural soils, grasslands), mineral soil profiles develop with low organic matter. Forest ecosystems develop distinct Ao (undecomposed litter) and A (decomposing organic matter) horizons where fungal activity is greatest. Grassland systems receive more below-ground organic input, resulting in organic matter more intimately mixed with mineral soil.

The pattern of decomposition determines whether ecosystems function as carbon sinks or sources. In boreal forests and peatlands, slow decomposition leads to massive carbon accumulation. In tropical forests, rapid decomposition returns carbon to the atmosphere quickly, but the continuous growing season maintains high primary production. Understanding fungal decomposition is thus central to predicting ecosystem responses to global change.

## Fungal vs. Bacterial Decomposition

Fungi differ from bacteria in several key aspects of decomposition:

- **Growth form**: Filamentous hyphae allow fungi to bridge between resource patches and translocate nutrients across distances. Bacteria are confined to the microsite where they are located.
- **Enzymatic capability**: Fungi produce a wider range of extracellular enzymes, particularly lignin-degrading peroxidases and laccases that bacteria generally lack.
- **Carbon use efficiency**: Fungi convert approximately 30-40% of decomposed carbon into biomass (vs. 20-40% for bacteria), with the remainder respired as CO2.
- **pH tolerance**: Fungi tolerate acidic conditions better than bacteria, allowing them to dominate in coniferous forest soils.
- **Resource quality preference**: Fungi outcompete bacteria on low-quality (high C:N) resources because of their lower nitrogen requirements per unit carbon assimilated.

## Humification and Soil Organic Matter

The end product of fungal decomposition is humus -- a complex, dark-colored organic material resistant to further microbial attack. Fungal cell wall components (chitin, melanin) and secondary metabolites contribute significantly to humus formation. The recalcitrance of these fungal residues means they persist in soil for years to decades, forming stable soil organic matter pools. This fungal contribution to humus is especially important in forest soils where fungal biomass dominates the microbial community.

## Decomposition in Aquatic and Wetland Ecosystems

Fungal decomposition extends beyond terrestrial systems. In freshwater streams, aquatic hyphomycetes (aero-aquatic fungi) decompose leaf litter, playing a role analogous to terrestrial decomposers. # Aquatic and Marine Fungal Ecology

Fungi play important but often overlooked roles in aquatic and marine ecosystems. Approximately 600 fungal species are associated with aquatic environments, functioning as saprotrophs, pathogens of both plants and animals, and critical intermediaries in energy flow through stream food webs. Despite their significance, the diversity and functional roles of aquatic and marine fungi remain considerably less studied than their terrestrial counterparts.

## Fungi in Freshwater Stream Ecosystems

In stream ecosystems, aquatic hyphomycetes are central to leaf litter decomposition. Bärlocher and Kendrick (1975) established that these fungi serve as intermediaries in energy flow, conditioning leaf material to make it more palatable for faunal grazing. The fungal degradation process alters leaf chemistry, increases food value, and softens tissue structure for invertebrate shredders.

The amphipod Gammarus grows faster per unit of food ingested when feeding on leaves colonized by fungi such as Humicola, Anguillospora, Clavariopsis, Tricladium, or Fusarium compared to uncolonized elm or maple leaves. This demonstrates that fungal conditioning significantly improves the nutritional quality of detritus for aquatic consumers.

Hieber and Gessner (2002) quantified decomposition in alder and willow leaves in streams. The shredder community accounted for 64% and 51% of leaf mass loss respectively, fungi for 15% and 18%, and bacteria for only 7% and 9%. Critically, fungi composed 95-99% of the total microbial biomass on decaying leaves, indicating their dominant role in the initial stages of aquatic decomposition.

Despite composing only 1-5% of detrital biomass, fungal biochemical modification of leaves substantially enhances nutrient availability. The mechanism for improved animal growth appears to be that fungal attack improves the availability of essential nutrients, particularly nitrogen, and provides readily assimilable energy sources that are otherwise locked in complex plant polymers.

## Shredder-Fungal Interactions

Leaf-shredding invertebrates including Amphipoda, Isopoda, Diptera, Plecoptera, and Trichoptera interact closely with aquatic fungi. While Tipula larvae do not select fungal-colonized leaves, other shredder species actively prefer them. Gammarus pseudolimnaeus selectively feeds on leaves colonized by certain fungal species over others, growing better on leaves colonized by four specific fungal species compared to six others.

There is evidence of synergistic benefits when animals consume fungal-colonized leaf litter. Suberkropp (1992) noted that the amphipod Gammarus and the isopod Asellus grew better on leaves colonized by fungi than when fed the fungi alone or the leaf litter alone, suggesting that the combined substrate provides superior nutrition to either component independently.

Trichopteran larvae show significantly improved growth rates on fungal-colonized versus uncolonized leaves. The fungal contribution to respiration accounts for 1-57% of total carbon respired from decomposing leaves in aquatic systems, underscoring the metabolic importance of fungi in stream energetics.

## Grazing Effects on Aquatic Fungal Communities

In contrast to terrestrial ecosystems, evidence for faunal grazing effects on aquatic fungal community structure is limited. Suberkropp (1992) found that leaves in fine mesh bags (excluding macroinvertebrates) supported more diverse fungal communities with lower mass-specific sporulation rates than leaves in coarse mesh bags (allowing grazer access).

This pattern suggests several possible mechanisms: reduced fungal biomass under higher grazing intensity, stimulation of sporulation under a more stressed environment, or selective feeding by fauna combined with differential resistance to grazing within the fungal community. The net result appears to be reduced fungal diversity in more heavily grazed conditions, though the ecological implications of this remain poorly understood.

## Marine and Estuarine Fungal Ecology

In marine and estuarine ecosystems, fungal information is more limited but growing. Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer (1979) documented 107 fungal species from decomposing wood in marine habitats, including 73 Ascomycotina, two Basidiomycotina, and 29 mitosporic species. The low resource quality of wood in marine environments encourages tight linkages between fungi and fauna for decomposition, mirroring terrestrial wood decomposition patterns.

Wood-boring marine mollusks preferentially settle and feed on wood previously colonized and partially decomposed by fungi, rather than invading fresh wood. The association has become so specialized that the wood-boring crustacean Limnoria tripunctata (the gribble) has increased longevity when feeding on fungus-colonized wood and is incapable of reproduction on any substrate unless marine fungi are included in its diet. This nutritional dependency likely stems from enhanced availability of proteins, essential amino acids, and vitamins provided by fungal colonization.

In salt marsh ecosystems, fungal biomass in decomposing material sustains invertebrate herbivore populations. The amphipod Ulorchestia spartinophila grows best and produces the most offspring when fed on decaying leaves with high fungal biomass, indicating that fungal biomass directly supports faunal productivity in estuarine food webs.

## Fungal Pathogens in Marine Environments

Fungi are major pathogens of marine animals across multiple taxonomic groups. Mitosporic fungi cause disease in crustaceans and damage corals and juvenile clams. Norton et al. (1994) isolated dematiaceous fungi including Exserohilum rostratum and Curvularia species from mantle and shell tissues of juvenile Tridacna crocea clams, where they were associated with declining health.

Oomycetes are highly destructive pathogens in finfish, mollusks, and shellfish. Many marine algae are susceptible to chytrid and oomycete pathogens, though the impact on host production and fitness remains inadequately investigated. The role of fungi as plant pathogens in aquatic ecosystems appears minor compared to their pathogenicity toward animals, though this assessment may reflect research bias rather than ecological reality.

Fungi have been identified as pathogens of fish, turtles, tadpoles, adult anurans, and numerous invertebrates in marine and freshwater environments. There are suggestions that certain fungi may serve as biocontrol agents for mosquito populations, analogous to the use of entomopathogenic fungi in terrestrial pest management.

## Gut Symbionts in Aquatic Animals

As in terrestrial ecosystems, aquatic animals harbor gut fungi to assist in food digestion. Cafaro (2000) showed that Patagonian intertidal isopods contain trichomycetes of the genus Palavascia. Termites similarly maintain Termitomyces cultures in their guts for the same purpose. In aquatic ecosystems, trichomycetes also serve as endosymbionts in black fly larval guts, with Beard and Adler (2002) demonstrating significant seasonal and site-specific variation in fungal abundance.

New recruits to black fly populations rapidly become colonized by Harpella melusinae, suggesting strong dependence on this fungus as a gut symbiont. The rapid colonization indicates an obligate relationship, and the seasonal variation in abundance likely reflects environmental conditions affecting both the host insect and the fungal symbiont.
