# [[fungal-decomposition]] Processes

## Overview

Fungal decomposition is one of the most critical ecosystem processes, converting dead plant and animal remains into mineral nutrients that sustain primary production. As Forsyth and Miyata (1984) described: "under the silent, relentless chemical jaws of the fungi, the debris of the forest floor quickly disappears." Fungi accomplish this through extracellular enzyme secretion, a fundamentally different strategy from animal digestion where enzymes act within a gut.

## Mechanism of Decomposition

[[saprotrophic-fungi]] release exoenzymes into their environment to break down complex organic molecules in dead plant and animal remains. The degradative products are then absorbed through the fungal hyphal surface. This reliance on extracellular enzymatic activity is less efficient than internal digestion, meaning some mineralized nutrients escape absorption and enter the soil solution as available fertility.

The rate of decomposition depends on three main factors:
- **Resource quality**: Chemical composition of the substrate (C:N ratio, lignin:N ratio, polyphenol content)
- **Edaphic factors**: Available moisture and temperature
- **Colonization**: Arrival of appropriate saprotrophic organisms with matching enzymatic competence

## Resource Quality Controls

The carbon:nitrogen and lignin:nitrogen ratios are primary determinants of decomposition resistance. High C:N or lignin:N ratios reduce decomposition rates. Vanlauwe et al. (1997) showed that the (lignin + polyphenol):N ratio was the strongest predictor of both litter mass loss and nitrogen mineralization rates. Secondary plant metabolites like polyphenols and tannins also inhibit microbial decomposition.

## Fungal Succession During Decomposition

Decomposition is not performed by a single organism. A succession of fungi colonizes resources as their chemical composition changes:

1. **Stage 1 (Sugar fungi)**: Fast-colonizing species (Lophodermium, Ceuthospora, Lophodermella) utilize readily available soluble carbohydrates from freshly fallen litter
2. **Stage 2 (Microfungi expansion)**: Verticicladium and other decomposing microfungi invade as simple sugars are depleted
3. **Stage 3 (Basidiomycete dominance)**: Marasmius and Collybia species degrade cellulose and lignin, reducing C:N ratios from ~200:1 toward 30:1
4. **Stage 4 (Mycorrhizal invasion)**: [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] enter as the resource becomes increasingly humified

This succession follows a trajectory from r-selected (opportunistic, fast-growing) to K-selected (competitive, slow-growing with greater enzymatic diversity) strategies.

## Environmental Controls

Temperature positively influences extracellular cellulase activity. Cellulose becomes unavailable for microbial use when the cellulose:lignin ratio drops below 0.5. In wet tropical forests, up to 7% of leaf litter never reaches the forest floor but is trapped and decomposed in the canopy by fungal rhizomorphs (Marasmius, Marasmiellus), creating an aerial "soil" that releases nutrients through stemflow and throughfall.

## Decomposition and Soil Profile Development

Where decomposition is slow, organic matter accumulates as peat. Where decomposition is rapid (agricultural soils, grasslands), mineral soil profiles develop with low organic matter. Forest ecosystems develop distinct Ao (undecomposed litter) and A (decomposing organic matter) horizons where fungal activity is greatest. Grassland systems receive more below-ground organic input, resulting in organic matter more intimately mixed with mineral soil.

The pattern of decomposition determines whether ecosystems function as carbon sinks or sources. In boreal forests and peatlands, slow decomposition leads to massive carbon accumulation. In tropical forests, rapid decomposition returns carbon to the atmosphere quickly, but the continuous growing season maintains high primary production.

## Fungal vs. Bacterial Decomposition

Fungi differ from bacteria in several key aspects of decomposition:
- **Growth form**: Filamentous hyphae allow fungi to bridge between resource patches and translocate nutrients across distances
- **Enzymatic capability**: Fungi produce a wider range of extracellular enzymes, particularly lignin-degrading peroxidases and laccases
- **Carbon use efficiency**: Fungi convert approximately 30-40% of decomposed carbon into biomass (vs. 20-40% for bacteria)
- **pH tolerance**: Fungi tolerate acidic conditions better than bacteria, allowing them to dominate in coniferous forest soils
- **Resource quality preference**: Fungi outcompete bacteria on low-quality (high C:N) resources because of their lower nitrogen requirements

## Humification and Soil Organic Matter

The end product of fungal decomposition is humus -- a complex, dark-colored organic material resistant to further microbial attack. Fungal cell wall components (chitin, melanin) and secondary metabolites contribute significantly to humus formation. The recalcitrance of these fungal residues means they persist in soil for years to decades, forming stable soil organic matter pools. This fungal contribution to humus is especially important in forest soils where fungal biomass dominates the microbial community.

## Aquatic Fungal Decomposition

Fungal decomposition extends beyond terrestrial systems. In freshwater streams, aquatic hyphomycetes decompose leaf litter, playing a role analogous to terrestrial decomposers. Hieber and Gessner (2002) quantified decomposition in alder and willow leaves in streams, finding that fungi composed 95-99% of total microbial biomass on decaying leaves, indicating their dominant role in initial aquatic decomposition stages.

In marine ecosystems, Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer (1979) documented 107 fungal species from decomposing wood in marine habitats. The wood-boring crustacean Limnoria tripunctata (the gribble) is incapable of reproduction on any substrate unless marine fungi are included in its diet, demonstrating a remarkable nutritional dependency on fungal colonization for protein, essential amino acids, and vitamins.

## Climate Change and Decomposition Dynamics

Global climate change is altering fungal decomposition patterns worldwide. Experimental warming studies consistently show increased decomposition rates in temperate and boreal ecosystems, where temperature is the primary limiting factor. However, in tropical and arid systems where moisture constrains decomposition, altered precipitation patterns may have a stronger influence than warming. These shifts in decomposition rates have significant implications for global carbon cycling, as faster decomposition releases more CO2 to the atmosphere while slower decomposition promotes carbon storage in soils and peatlands.

## Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling

Fungal decomposition plays a central role in closing nutrient loops within ecosystems. As fungi break down organic matter, they release nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients in forms available for plant uptake. The timing and rate of nutrient release during decomposition are critical for plant productivity. In forest ecosystems, fungal decomposition of leaf litter and woody debris supplies the majority of plant-available nutrients. Mycorrhizal fungi further enhance this cycling by directly transferring mineral nutrients from decomposing organic matter to their host plants, bypassing the soil solution entirely.
