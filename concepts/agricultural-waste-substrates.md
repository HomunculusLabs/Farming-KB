# Agricultural Waste Substrates for Mushroom Cultivation

Many wood-decomposing mushrooms can be grown on alternative substrates
that do not contain wood. Cereal straws, corn stalks, sugar cane
bagasse, coffee pulp, banana fronds, seed hulls, and a wide variety of
other agricultural waste products serve as viable substrates for
mushroom cultivation. As sources for hardwood by-products become scarce
due to deforestation, these alternative substrates are in increasing
demand. The more hearty and adaptive Pleurotus (Oyster) species are
the best examples of mushrooms that have evolved on wood but readily
produce on agricultural waste products.

## Alternative Fruiting Formula

A basic wood-free formula for cultivating wood-decomposing mushrooms
consists of the following components, mixed in dry form and wetted to
70-75% moisture content:

- 100 lbs (45.5 kg) ground corn cobs, peanut shells, chopped sugar
  cane bagasse, tea leaves, coffee, banana material, straw, etc.
- 10 lbs (4.6 kg) rice bran or approximately 2.5 lbs extracted
  [[growing-gourmet-substrate-materials-wood-types]]. When nitrogen supplements like rice bran are
added, simple pasteurization may not adequately treat the substrate,
and full sterilization is called for. Without supplementation,
pasteurization usually suffices. The mixture must be loaded into bags
and immediately heat-treated; sitting overnight without sterilization
allows contaminants to proliferate, rendering the mixture unsuitable.

## Wheat Straw as a Substrate

Wheat straw is the most accessible and forgiving substrate for small
to mid-size cultivators. It is readily available, inexpensive, and
needs only a quick run through a shredder before use. Wheat, rye,
oat, and sorghum straws are all suitable. Straw accepts a limited
number of contaminants while selectively favoring [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]]. Growing Oyster

## Hot Water Bath Pasteurization

The simplest method for preparing straw is the hot water bath. Straw
is chopped into 1 to 4 inch lengths and stuffed into a wire basket.
The basket is submerged in a cauldron of 160-180 degrees F (71-82
degrees C) water for 1 to 2 hours, heated by a portable propane gas
burner. A probe thermometer inserted deep into the mass monitors
temperature. After submersion for at least one hour and no longer
than two, the basket is lifted out, the straw is drained and laid
out to cool, and grain spawn is broadcast over the surface and mixed
throughout. Hands must be washed every 15 minutes during inoculation
to limit cross-contamination.

After two soakings, the hot water must be discarded. The discolored
water, known as "straw tea," becomes toxic to mushroom mycelium after
the third soaking. Interestingly, this tea is also toxic to most
vegetation and can be used as a natural herbicide.
