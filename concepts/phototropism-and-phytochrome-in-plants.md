# Phototropism and Phytochrome in Plants

## Overview

Phototropism is the directional growth response of plants toward or away from light. It represents one of the most fundamental sensory capabilities of plants, enabling them to orient their growth for maximum photosynthetic efficiency.

The discovery of phototropism and the identification of the responsible photoreceptors form a cornerstone of plant biology with profound implications for understanding how plants perceive and respond to their environment.

Plants do not "see" in the way animals do, yet they possess sophisticated light-detection systems that rival human vision in their sensitivity to different wavelengths and light intensities. A plant knows if you come near it, whether you are wearing blue or red, and whether its pot has been moved across the room.

While plants cannot form images, they can detect light across a broader range of the electromagnetic spectrum than humans, including ultraviolet and infrared wavelengths.

## Human Vision as a Point of Comparison

Human vision relies on photoreceptor proteins in the retina. Rod cells (approximately 125 million per eye) are sensitive to all visible light and enable night vision but without colour discrimination. Cone cells (approximately 6 million per eye) come in three types, each responsive to different wavelengths: red, green, and blue.

These photoreceptors contain specific chemicals (rhodopsin in rods, photopsins in cones) that absorb light energy and transmit signals to the brain for processing into coherent images.

Plants lack a brain and nervous system, yet they possess photoreceptor proteins that absorb light of specific wavelengths and trigger biochemical cascades that alter growth patterns. This can be considered a legitimate form of vision, albeit without image formation.

## Darwin's Experiments on Phototropism

Charles Darwin and his son Francis conducted pioneering experiments on phototropism in their 1880 book *The Power of Movement in Plants*.

Darwin wrote that "there are extremely few [plants], of which some part does not bend towards lateral light." Their classic experiment used canary grass (Phalaris canariensis) seedlings grown in total darkness.

When exposed to an extremely dim gas lamp positioned twelve feet away (so dim the Darwins themselves could not see the seedlings or a pencil line on paper), the seedlings curved toward the light within three hours.

The curving consistently occurred approximately two centimeters below the shoot tip, leading the Darwins to investigate which part of the plant perceived the light. They systematically tested five conditions:

- **Intact seedling**: bent toward light normally, confirming conditions supported phototropism
- **Tip removed**: no bending response, demonstrating the tip was necessary for light perception
- **Tip covered with lightproof cap**: no bending, confirming the tip must receive light directly
- **Tip covered with clear glass cap**: bent normally, confirming glass-transmitted light was sufficient
- **Middle section covered with lightproof tube**: bent normally, confirming the bending region did not need direct light

This elegantly demonstrated that the "eyes" of the plant reside at the shoot tip, while the bending response occurs in the midsection. The tip perceives the light direction and sends a mobile signal to the stem to trigger differential cell elongation.

## Blue Light and Phototropism

In 1864, Julius von Sachs discovered that blue light is the primary colour inducing phototropism. Plants are generally insensitive to other colours of the visible spectrum for this directional response.

The photoreceptors responsible are called phototropins, which contain flavin chromophores that absorb blue light (approximately 450 nm). Upon activation, phototropins trigger an auxin redistribution mechanism: auxin accumulates on the shaded side of the stem, promoting cell elongation there and causing the plant to bend toward the light.

Plants also detect ultraviolet light (which causes sunburn in humans) and infrared light (which produces heat), expanding their light perception beyond the human visible range.

Plants can determine light direction, intensity, and duration, and can even detect whether neighbouring vegetation is shading them by sensing changes in the red to far-red light ratio.

## Photoperiodism: Measuring Night Length

### Maryland Mammoth Tobacco

In 1906, farmers in southern Maryland noticed a tobacco strain that grew continuously without flowering, reaching 4.5 metres in height with nearly a hundred leaves. Named Maryland Mammoth, it only stopped growing when frost arrived, making it useless for seed production.

Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of Agriculture discovered in 1918 that the plant was measuring day length. When exposed to artificially shorter days by moving plants to a dark shed each afternoon, Maryland Mammoth flowered normally.

This phenomenon, called [[cannabis-photoperiodism-and-light-cycles]], demonstrated that plants measure light duration to regulate flowering:

- **Short-day plants**: flower only when nights exceed a critical length (e.g., chrysanthemums, soybeans)
- **Long-day plants**: flower only when nights are shorter than a critical length (e.g., irises, barley)

### Plants Measure Darkness, Not Daylight

Scientists around World War II discovered that plants actually measure the length of continuous darkness, not daylight. A brief flash of light during the night can prevent short-day plants from flowering or induce long-day plants to flower.

This discovery revolutionised commercial floriculture. Chrysanthemum farmers could keep plants vegetative by turning on lights briefly each night throughout autumn, then stop two weeks before Mother's Day to trigger simultaneous flowering for optimal market timing.

### The Colour Sensitivity of Photoperiodism

Scientists discovered that plants respond only to red light flashes during the night for photoperiodic regulation. Blue or green flashes have no effect on flowering timing, but even a few seconds of red light is sufficient.

This means plants use different photoreceptor systems for different purposes: blue light (via phototropins) to determine which direction to bend, and red/far-red light (via phytochrome) to measure the length of the night.

## Phytochrome: The Light-Activated Switch

### Discovery

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues made the remarkable discovery that red light and far-red light have opposing effects on plant flowering.

A pulse of red light (approximately 660 nm) during the night promotes flowering in long-day plants, but a subsequent pulse of far-red light (approximately 730 nm) cancels this effect completely.

The cancellation is fully reversible: red activates, far-red deactivates, red reactivates, and so on. Only a few seconds of either colour is sufficient. The plant effectively "remembers" the last colour of light it perceived.

Warren L. Butler and colleagues identified a single photoreceptor responsible for both effects, naming it [[chamovitz-phytochrome-red-far-red-switch-photoperiodism]] ("plant colour").

### Molecular Mechanism

Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms:

- **Pr (P-red)**: the inactive form that absorbs red light (~660 nm) and converts to Pfr
- **Pfr (P-far-red)**: the biologically active form that absorbs far-red light (~730 nm) and converts back to Pr

In nature, sunlight is enriched in red wavelengths while shade light (filtered through leaves) is enriched in far-red. The last light at dusk is enriched in far-red wavelengths, converting phytochrome to its inactive Pr form and signalling the plant that day has ended.

Morning red light converts it back to active Pfr, signalling dawn. The plant measures the duration of continuous darkness by tracking how long phytochrome has been in its inactive state.

### Site of Perception for Photoperiodism

Unlike phototropism (perceived at the shoot tip), photoperiodism is perceived primarily through phytochrome in the leaves. Illuminating any single leaf is sufficient to regulate flowering throughout the entire plant.

When all leaves are removed, leaving only stem and apex, the plant becomes insensitive to light flashes for flowering. Phytochrome in the leaves receives light cues and initiates a mobile signal (now known to include the protein FLOWERING LOCUS T, or FT) that propagates through the phloem to the shoot apex to induce the floral transition.

## Ecological and Agricultural Significance

The phytochrome system allows plants to detect not just light quality but also the ratio of red to far-red light. Plants growing in shade receive light filtered through other leaves that is depleted in red and enriched in far-red.

This triggers shade avoidance responses including stem elongation, accelerated flowering, and reduced branching, all aimed at growing out of the shaded zone. Understanding these responses has practical applications in agriculture and horticulture.

Commercial applications of phytochrome manipulation include:

- Greenhouse flower production (controlling chrysanthemum flowering schedules)
- Photoperiod control in cannabis cultivation for flowering initiation
- Seed germination management, as many seeds require red light to break dormancy
- Lighting design for indoor plant growth, where far-red supplementation can modify plant architecture
