# [[sphaerobolus-stellatus]], the Artillery Fungus, and Spore Launch

[[sphaerobolus-stellatus]] is a gasteromycete fungus that grows on wood mulch and
dung, and has earned its common name, the artillery fungus, through a
remarkable spore discharge mechanism. It shoots a black ball of spores
from a fruiting body that operates as a tiny trampoline, launching its
projectile with enough force to soil the paintwork of cars parked near
mulched flower beds. The spore balls stick to smooth surfaces with
incredible tenacity, and even when removed by vigorous cleaning, spots
remain in the paint. The intended targets of Sphaerobolus are grass
blades rather than automobiles, but the fungus's ballistic capabilities
make it one of the most mechanically impressive organisms in the fungal
kingdom.

## Gasteromycetes: The Stomach Fungi

The gasteromycetes are defined by the fact that their spores form
inside the fruiting body rather than on gills or other fertile surfaces
exposed to the air. Their scientific name refers to this developmental
feature: gastero (stomach) and mycetes (fungi). They seem to have
evolved from different kinds of ancient fungi that produced conventional
umbrella-shaped mushrooms, and as such are regarded as a ragbag of
species rather than a natural grouping. This makes them analogous to a
collection of tools gathered for convenience rather than a set that
shares a common design philosophy.

Other members of the gasteromycetes include puffballs, earthstars, and
bird's nest fungi. The bird's nest fungi produce fruiting bodies shaped
like tiny cups containing egg-like packets of spores. Raindrops falling
into the cups splash the spore packets outward, and they are carried by
animals as they pass through their digestive systems, later deposited
in warm manure. Like bird's nest fungi, [[sphaerobolus-stellatus]] is adapted for
passage through a herbivore gut, linking its dispersal strategy to the
grazing habits of mammals.

## The Trampoline Mechanism

The fruiting body of [[sphaerobolus-stellatus]] begins as a small spherical
structure, roughly the size of a pinhead, that develops on decaying
wood or herbivore dung. Inside, a single spore mass called the gleba
forms at the center, surrounded by a series of tissue layers. As the
fruiting body matures, the outer layer splits open to form a cup-shaped
structure, while the inner layers remain under increasing tension.

When the fruiting body is fully mature, the innermost layer, called the
peridium, suddenly inverts, ejecting the gleba like a ball from a
catapult. The entire sequence takes place in a fraction of a second and
can propel the spore mass a distance of several meters, a remarkable
achievement for an organism only a few millimeters in diameter. The
force generated by the inversion is comparable to the discharge of
[[ballistospore-discharge-mechanism-mushrooms]] mechanisms in other basidiomycetes, but
concentrated into a single explosive event rather than the continuous
stream of spore release seen in gilled mushrooms.

## Ballistospore Discharge: A Universal Fungal Mechanism

The discharge mechanism of [[sphaerobolus-stellatus]] is a spectacular example of
the broader phenomenon of ballistospore discharge that unites the
basidiomycetes. In gilled mushrooms, each spore sits on a
four-pronged basidium. A droplet of fluid, called Buller's drop, forms
at the base of the spore by condensation. As the drop grows, it
eventually contacts a second droplet on the spore surface, and the
sudden merging of these two bodies of water generates sufficient force
to catapult the spore horizontally away from the gill. After this
millisecond journey, gravity assumes control and the spore falls
between the gills, where it is swept away by air currents.

Mushrooms actually cool during spore release, by as much as 5 degrees
Celsius on warm days, through an evaporative mechanism. The spore
surface is sugary and differs from the surrounding gill tissues. Water
vapor streams from most of the mushroom's tissues, but a little
condenses on the spore, hydrating those sugars and making Buller's drop
appear. Cooling promotes this condensation by slowing water molecules,
encouraging them to occupy a condensed liquid state. If the mushroom
warmed, even slightly, water would evaporate from the spore surface,
the drops would never expand, and spores would remain trapped on the
gills.

## Ecological Impact and Nuisance

In its natural habitat, [[sphaerobolus-stellatus]] performs an important
ecological role by dispersing spores onto vegetation where they can be
ingested by herbivores. The spore mass, called the gleba, is coated
with a sticky substance that adheres to plant surfaces. When a grazing
animal consumes contaminated foliage, the spores pass through its
digestive system unharmed and are deposited in dung, where the next
generation of fruiting bodies develops. This dispersal strategy links
the fungus intimately to the grazing habits of mammals and the
distribution of dung in pastureland.

In suburban and urban settings, however, the fungus has become a
significant nuisance. Mulched flower beds provide ideal growing
conditions, and the artillery fungus readily colonizes wood chips.
Homeowners who park cars near mulched areas may find their vehicles
speckled with tiny black spots that are nearly impossible to remove
without damaging the paint. The spore masses are adhesive and
water-resistant, making them exceptionally persistent once attached to
surfaces. The problem is compounded by the fact that Sphaerobolus can
shoot its spore masses distances of several meters, giving it an
effective range that encompasses most residential driveways.

## Wind Tunnel Insights

Research on spore dispersal has revealed that mushrooms are adapted to
maximize the effectiveness of air currents around their caps. The
mushroom stem supports the cap above the boundary layer of still air
that hugs the ground surface, exposing the gills to moving air that
carries spores away. At high wind speeds the fruiting body can be
wrenched from its attachment, but under normal conditions the cap and
stem are highly responsive to gravity and orient themselves to provide
unimpeded pathways for the free fall of discharged spores. Airflow
studies in wind tunnels have shown that the mushroom shape creates
specific patterns of air movement that enhance spore removal from the
gill surfaces.
