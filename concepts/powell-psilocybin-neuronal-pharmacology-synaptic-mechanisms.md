## The Neuron as Information Processor

The brain consists of individual information-processing nerve cells
called neurons. The human brain contains approximately 13 billion
neurons, comparable to the number of stars in our galaxy and more than
twice the number of people alive on the planet. These neurons are the
essential wetware of the brain and, massed together with other cells that
provide support and energy, they form the grey matter within our skulls.

Structurally, the neuron has four main components: dendrites, the [[soma]] or
cell body, the axon, and terminal fibres. Powell offers a memorable
analogy: imagine a tree suspended in mid-air, where the roots are the
dendrites, the lower bulbous trunk is the soma, the long thin upper trunk
is the axon, and the topmost branches are the terminal fibres. All 13
billion neurons in the brain are essentially constructed in this way.

## Neuronal Firing and Information Transmission

Neurons transmit informational impulses in an orderly, well-defined
manner. The dendrites receive signals from other neurons, which travel to
the soma where they are integrated. The singular result of this
integration is then passed along the axon to the terminal fibres. Neurons
operate by generating electrochemical impulses that travel as rapidly
moving waves of electrical excitation.

A crucial feature is that neurons can either fire or not fire; they
cannot half-fire. This binary operating principle is similar to computer
bits that store information by being either on or off. Neurons thus
appear to operate digitally, with no room for doubt or indecision. The
concept of threshold is crucial: if a sufficient number of excitatory
impulses are received, the firing threshold is met and an impulse is
passed along the axon. If the threshold is not met, no impulse is sent.

## Excitatory and Inhibitory Neurons

Neurons can be either excitatory or inhibitory. Excitatory neurons, when
they fire, tend to cause excitation in other neurons they connect with,
adding to the chances of the next neuron firing as well. Inhibitory
neurons, when they fire, decrease the chances of the next neuron in line
firing. The balance between excitatory and inhibitory signals across
billions of neurons determines the overall state of the brain at any
given moment.

Each individual neuron can potentially pass impulses to as many as
10,000 other neurons and can receive as many as 50,000 potential impulses
from other neurons. The informational storage capacity of the brain has
been calculated as comparable to the content of all the books ever
written. It is this bewildering capacity to process and store information
that makes the human mind as rich and complex as it appears to be.

## The Synapse: Where Psychoactive Substances Operate

The synapse is the junction between two neurons where they communicate,
and it is arguably the most interesting feature of neuronal activity for
understanding psychoactive substances. All of the most powerful
psychoactive drugs act by mimicking the brain's own chemical substances
employed at synaptic sites. Neurons do not contact one another directly;
instead, there is an intervening gap called the synaptic cleft.

The chemicals employed at the neuronal synapse are called
neurotransmitters, the chemical agents that allow neurons to transmit
their electrochemical impulses to one another. When an impulse reaches
the terminal fibres, it causes a neurotransmitter substance to be
released into the synaptic cleft. These molecules bind to special
receptors on the surface of the receiving neuron's dendrites, and the
original impulse is regenerated in that neuron.
