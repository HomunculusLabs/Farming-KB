## Coppice Management

### For Bark
- Start coppice at appropriate height for the species
- Harvest on schedule for bark products

### For Timber
- Allow poles to reach structural diameter before harvest
- Black locust is the premier coppice timber tree

### For Basketry
- Start coppice below ground level
- Use striped willow; bury billets in wet sites in rows
- They shoot up and can be coppiced repeatedly
- Tasmanian basket makers use half-acre patches for five generations

### For Forage
- Start coppice above cow level
- A quarter acre can carry cattle through droughts
- Either cut and throw over, or turn cattle in depending on observed damage

## Stopping Unwanted Coppicing

To keep a stump from coppicing:
1. Throw something over the stump to exclude light (a piece of carpet works)
2. Cut a hole in the stump
3. Put road salt in the hole
4. The stump will die without resprouting

## Tea-Tree as a Multi-Product Pole

Tea-tree deserves special mention:
- Grows very thick; you can hardly get your hand between stems
- Weaves well for baskets and lobster traps
- High-value oil in the leaves (distill the heads when cut)
- Long-lasting in fences: 30-40 years per stick
- Sells at 30 cents per stick (historical price)
- Don't put butts in the ground; use sticks between black locust posts
- Place rocks under them; they hang indefinitely
- In 5 years, ready for another cut
- Oil from heads gives double value to the harvest

## Forest Management Strategy

In a permaculture forest design:
1. Identify existing pole stands with few large trees -- keep as pole forest
2. Look for dying young trees -- these are firewood
3. Identify areas promising for future plank timber -- manage differently
4. Install dry distillation for processing dead and dying trees (see [[permaculture-dry-distillation-wood]] from waste wood
