## Soil Moisture Monitoring for Irrigation Management

## Why Monitor Soil Moisture?

Soil moisture monitoring is a cornerstone of modern irrigation management. Rather than irrigating on a fixed schedule or visual
estimates, sensors provide real-time data on the water status within the [[cervantes-root-zone-heating]]. This allows growers to apply water only when crops
actually need it, reducing water waste, energy costs, and [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

## Soil Water Content vs. Soil Water Potential

Two fundamental measurements describe soil water status, and understanding the difference between them is essential for proper sensor
selection and data interpretation.

**Volumetric water content (VWC)** is the volume of water per unit volume of soil, expressed as a percentage or cm³/cm³. It tells you
how much water is present but not how tightly it is held or how available it is to plant roots. VWC sensors include capacitance
probes, TDR probes, and neutron [[bulk-substrate-field-capacity]] the potential is near zero; at the
permanent wilting point it reaches about -15 bars (-1,500 kPa), beyond which plants cannot extract water. Tensiometers and granular
matrix sensors measure potential directly.

Both measurements are valuable. VWC is intuitive and directly comparable across sites, while potential tells you whether the water
present is actually available to the crop.

## Key Soil Water Thresholds

Three reference points define the plant-available water range in any soil:

- **[[mollison-designers-soil-water-storage-and-field-capacity]] (FC):** Water content remaining after gravity drainage ceases (typically 1–3 days after saturation). Corresponds to roughly -0.33 bars (-33 kPa) matric potential in most soils.
- **Permanent Wilting Point (PWP):** Water content at which plants wilt and cannot recover, corresponding to approximately -15 bars (-1,500 kPa) matric potential.
- **Available Water Capacity (AWC):** The difference between FC and PWP, expressed in mm of water per unit depth. Sandy soils may hold only 50–80 mm/m, while loams and clay loams hold 150–250 mm/m. AWC determines the soil water reservoir size that buffers between irrigation events or rainfall.

## Sensor Types

### Tensiometers

Tensiometers measure soil water potential directly through a water-filled tube with a porous ceramic cup at the tip. As soil dries,
water is drawn out, creating a vacuum measured by a gauge. Accurate in the wet range (0 to -80 cb) but lose continuity in drier
conditions. Best suited for high-value crops irrigated frequently at relatively high moisture levels.

### Capacitance / FDR Probes

Frequency-domain reflectometry (FDR) probes such as those from Decagon (now METER Group) and Acclima measure the dielectric
permittivity of the soil, which correlates strongly with VWC. They can be installed at multiple depths in a single access tube,
providing a full root-zone profile. Well suited to both research and production agriculture across a wide range of moisture conditions.

### TDR Probes

Time-domain reflectometry probes send an electromagnetic pulse along waveguides embedded in the soil and measure the travel time,
proportional to the dielectric constant and thus VWC. Highly accurate and widely used in research, though commercial systems tend to
be more expensive than FDR.

### Neutron Moisture Meters

Neutron probes measure soil moisture by emitting fast neutrons thermalized by hydrogen atoms (primarily in water). They require an
access tube and a radioactive materials license. Extremely accurate and capable of deep measurements, but regulatory burden and cost
limit use mostly to research stations.

### Gypsum Blocks and Granular Matrix Sensors

Low-cost sensors measuring electrical resistance between two electrodes embedded in a porous medium ([[gypsum]] or granular fill).
Resistance increases as the medium dries. Inexpensive and simple but limited in accuracy and lifespan — gypsum blocks dissolve over
time in saline soils.

## Sensor Placement and Installation Depth

Proper sensor placement is critical for representative data. Place sensors in the active root zone at depths matching the majority of
the root mass — typically 15 cm, 30 cm, and 60 cm for row crops, or deeper for orchards and vineyards. Install multiple stations per
field to account for spatial variability in soil type, slope, and irrigation uniformity. Ensure good soil-sensor contact during
installation; for access-tube sensors, maintain a tight seal to prevent water channeling along the tube wall.

## Data Logging and Telemetry

Modern systems integrate data loggers that record sensor readings at configurable intervals (typically every 15–60 minutes) and
transmit data via cellular, Wi-Fi, or radio to cloud platforms, enabling real-time monitoring from any device. Many systems support
automated alerts — for example, notifying when soil moisture drops below a management-defined threshold.
