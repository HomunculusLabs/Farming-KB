# Perennialising Annual Vegetables and Cool Climate Garden Techniques

Mollison presents a collection of practical techniques for extending the life
of conventionally annual vegetables, effectively converting them into
perennial or semi-perennial crops. These methods reduce the need for annual
seed starting and can significantly lower garden maintenance while providing
continuous harvests. The chunk also covers trellis systems and small fruit
management specific to cool mesothermal climates.

## Perennialising Techniques by Crop

### Carrots

Cut a disc off the top of a harvested carrot, sprout it on soft wet paper in
a shallow tray, and replant. The top will regrow new foliage and eventually
produce seed. Mollison reports one gardener who claimed to have been eating
the same carrots for 9 years using this technique. While this is likely an
exaggeration, the principle is sound: many root vegetables can be maintained
through successive regrowth cycles if the crown is preserved.

### Leeks

Two approaches for extending leek production:

1. Let one in four plants go to seed and dry off, then remove the small
   bulbils from the base and replant them. These bulbils grow into full-sized
   leeks without the need for seed sowing.

2. When harvesting, cut the leek off 2 cm below ground level rather than
   pulling the entire plant. The central shoot quickly regrows, producing a
   second (and sometimes third) harvest from the same root system. There is
   also a variety of perennial bunching leek (called pearl onions) that
   naturally multiplies.

### Lettuce

Pick single leaves from the outer rosette rather than harvesting the whole
head. Allow some plants to go to seed and scatter seed naturally. The
Mignonette variety is a particularly good self-seeder, as are some Italian
varieties. This creates a perpetual lettuce bed that needs only occasional
thinning.

### Cabbage

After harvesting the main head, cut the stem 8 cm above the ground, then
deeply crosscut the stem (making an X-shaped cut through the top). Each
quarter of the stem regrows a small secondary cabbage. While these secondary
heads are smaller, they extend the harvest period and can be produced with
no additional planting.

### Self-Seeding Crops

Garden fennel, chard, parsley, parsnip, and many other biennial plants will
self-seed prolifically if allowed to go to flower. Judicious thinning,
weeding, and transplanting of volunteer seedlings is all that is needed to
maintain productive beds. This approach mimics natural plant succession and
requires minimal intervention once established.

## Seed-Saving for Umbellifers

When saving seed from any umbelliferous plant (carrots, parsnips, fennel,
celery, dill, coriander), collect the strong, mature seed from the terminal
panicle only. Terminal seed germinates best of all. Side panicles contain
immature and small seeds, and can be pruned or rejected. This selective
harvesting improves overall germination rates and seedling vigour.

## Trellis Systems for Cool Climates

Trellis crop in mesothermal climates is important. Many peas, beans, and
cucurbits will trellis, as will varieties of tomatoes. However, trellis over
the garden is inappropriate in cool humid climates (unlike in deserts where
overhead shade is beneficial). The preferred trellis configurations are:

- Vertical zigzag trellis
- Inverted "V" trellis
- House wall and garden fence trellis

All should be aligned north/south where possible for even sun effects on both
sides. This orientation ensures that neither side of the trellis is
permanently shaded, maximising [[photosynthetic-efficiency]] and allowing a
greater diversity of crops to share the structure.

## Small Fruit Integration

Small fruit such as currants, gooseberry, raspberry, or trellis blackberry
is a feature of cool-area gardens. Six to fifteen plants of 7-8 species
yields a great deal of fresh or frozen food of high vitamin C value. Many
such rows can act as windbreak within the garden, serving a dual function of
crop production and microclimate modification.

Rows of berry bushes planted on the windward edge of the garden provide
shelter for more sensitive vegetables and herbs while producing a significant
food crop. This integration of shelter and production is a hallmark of
permaculture design in cool climates.

## Cool Climate Garden Design Principles

The garden layout in cool areas follows the same general principles as
tropical gardens but with important modifications:

- Omit any over-shaded areas or tree-crop interplant, as light levels can be
  low in cloudy weather and light saturation is rarely a problem
- Mulch is appropriate for most crops, but pull mulch from spring carrot
  seedbeds to let the soil warm up early
- A small glasshouse for seedling trays and a series of cloches for early
  transplants are invaluable
- Even potatoes can be sprouted on old hessian bags indoors, or in any open
  shadehouse, and transplanted complete with leaves and roots by cutting up
  the bags into squares
