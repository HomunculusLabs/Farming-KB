# Mushroom Identification Resources

A guide to safely identifying wild mushrooms using morphological features, microscopy, DNA barcoding, and regional field guides. See also [[mushroom-identification]] for basic ID features and [[mushroom-species-guide]] for commonly cultivated species.

## Key Morphological Features

### Cap (Pileus)
Size, shape (convex, flat, umbonate, funnel), surface texture (smooth, scaly, viscid, dry), color changes with age, margin characteristics. The cap alone is never sufficient for identification.

### Gills/Pores/Tubes
Gill attachment (free, adnate, decurrent, sinuate), spacing (close, distant), color, bruising reaction. Some species have pores (boletes) or teeth (hydnoids) instead of gills. Spore-producing surface is critical for genus-level identification.

### Stem (Stipe)
Presence/absence of ring (annulus), volva (cup at base), surface texture, bruising color. The base is particularly important — Amanita species have a sac-like volva that may be underground.

### Veil and Ring
Partial veil remnants (annulus on stem), universal veil remnants (volva at base, warts on cap). These features distinguish many dangerous species.

### Spore Print
Made by placing the cap gills-down on paper for 4-24 hours. Color categories: white, cream, pink, brown, rusty-brown, purple-brown, black. Essential first step in narrowing identification. Psilocybe species typically have dark purple-brown to black spore prints.

### Flesh and Bruising
Color of cut flesh, bruising reactions (blue in psilocybin-containing species, red in certain boletes). Odor and taste (when safe to test) provide additional characters.

### Habitat and Substrate
Growing on wood, soil, dung, leaf litter, or grass. Tree associations (mycorrhizal partners) are crucial for boletes, chanterelles, and Amanita. Season and geographic location narrow possibilities.

## Dangerous Lookalikes

| Edible Species | Dangerous Lookalike | Key Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Paddy straw mushroom | Death cap (Amanita phalloides) | Volva at base, white gills |
| Honey mushroom (Armillaria) | Galerina marginata | Galerina has rusty-brown spore print, ring |
| Chanterelle | Jack-o'-lantern (Omphalotus) | True chanterelles have forked ridges, not true gills |
| Morel | False morel (Gyromitra) | True morels are hollow; false morels have chambered caps |
| King bolete | Satan's bolete | Satan's bolete has red pore surface and blue bruising |

## Poisonous Fungi Overview

From [[john-ramsbottom]] (1945) and Carolina Mushrooms sources:

- **Amanita species (amatoxins):** Death cap (A. phalloides), destroying angel (A. virosa, A. bisporigera). Cause delayed onset (6-24h) liver/kidney failure. Fatality rate 10-60% without treatment. No reliable antidote; silibinin may help.
- **Galerina species:** Contain amatoxins identical to Amanita. G. marginata grows on wood like honey mushrooms — critical lookalike.
- **Cortinarius species:** Some contain orellanine (causes delayed kidney failure). Web-like partial veil (cortina) is characteristic.
- **Gyromitra esculenta:** Contains gyromitrin (converted to monomethylhydrazine). Causes vomiting, seizures, hemolysis.
- **Clitocybe/Inocybe species:** Contain muscarine. Cause sweating, salivation, tear production, and respiratory distress.
- **Coprinus atramentarius:** Contains coprine, which causes severe reaction with alcohol (disulfiram-like effect) for up to 72 hours after ingestion.

## Ramsbottom's Extended Species Descriptions (1945)

### Amanita muscaria (Fly Agaric) — Detailed

Ramsbottom provides the most thorough early ethnographic account of A. muscaria use. Key points not in general references:

- **Koryak use**: Consumed by tribes of northeast Siberia to produce "excessive emotion" on ceremonial occasions. The stimulant is excreted by kidneys, enabling reuse — outsiders reportedly drank the urine of those who had consumed the fungus to share the experience.
- **Viking berserker theory**: According to Scandinavian tradition, Vikings ate A. muscaria before battle. This remains debated but is extensively documented in Ramsbottom.
- **Insecticidal use**: The name "muscaria" refers to its use in milk to kill flies (first noted by Albertus Magnus, 13th century). In Poland and Czechoslovakia, sugar solution replaced milk; in Romania, caps were placed on windowsills.
- **Toxicology**: Three identified alkaloids — muscarine (small amounts, not the primary active), muscaridine/mycetoatropine (central nervous system effects similar to atropine), and choline (gastrointestinal symptoms).
- **Prognosis**: Never causes death in healthy people. Symptoms: delirium and hallucinations 1-3 hours post-ingestion, sometimes with intestinal disturbance, followed by intense stupor and complete amnesia on waking.
- **Potency variation**: Considerable variation in poison content; price on the Siberian steppes was 3-4 reindeer per specimen, suggesting high potency in some populations.
- **Poison location**: Resides principally in the skin of the cap.

### Amanita pantherina (Panther Cap)

- Brown cap with numerous small white warts; confused with edible A. rubescens but flesh does NOT redden on cutting
- Radish smell, mild taste
- Symptoms similar to A. muscaria but "apparently more serious"
- Bulbous base surrounded by membranous border with scaly "bracelets" above (volva remnants)

### Lepiota helveola and L. cristata (Poisonous Lepiotas)

- Small brownish-pink species, rare in Britain
- Cause symptoms resembling mild A. phalloides poisoning
- Ramsbottom advises treating all small Lepiota species as potentially dangerous

### Additional Ramsbottom Safety Principles

1. **No universal test exists** — Silver spoon, cap peeling, and animal testing all fail as reliable safety indicators
2. **Delayed symptom onset is the most dangerous sign** — Symptoms beginning 6+ hours after ingestion suggest amatoxin poisoning (medical emergency)
3. **Cooking does not destroy amatoxins** — The deadliest fungal toxins survive all cooking temperatures
4. **Volva presence = extreme caution** — All Amanita with a volva should be treated as potentially lethal
5. **Avoid all LBMs** — "Little Brown Mushrooms" include many deadly species requiring expert identification

## Microscopy Basics

- **Spore size/shape:** Measured in micrometers (um). Length, width, and Q ratio (length/width). Shape categories: elliptical, spherical, amygdaliform, fusiform.
- **Spore ornamentation:** Smooth, warty, ridged, reticulate. Requires oil immersion at 1000x magnification.
- **Basidia:** Number of sterigmata (2 or 4), presence/absence of clamp connections at hyphal septa.
- **Chemical tests:** Melzer's reagent (amyloid/iodine reaction), KOH (color changes on cap surface or flesh).

## DNA Barcoding

- **ITS region:** Internal Transcribed Spacer is the standard fungal barcode region. Enables species-level identification from small tissue samples.
- **BLAST matching:** Compare sequences against GenBank or UNITE databases for identification.
- **Limitations:** Database coverage is incomplete for many genera. Environmental sequences may not match described species.

## Recommended Field Guides by Region

- **North America:** "Mushrooms Demystified" (David Arora), "National Audubon Society Field Guide to North American Mushrooms," "Mushrooms of the Northeastern United States and Eastern Canada"
- **Europe:** "Mushrooms" (Roger Phillips), "The Mushrooms and Toadstools of Great Britain and Europe"
- **Australia/New Zealand:** "A Field Guide to Australian Fungi" (Bruce Fuhrer), "Magic Mushrooms of Australia & New Zealand" ([[john-w-allen]])
- **Mexico/Central America:** Guzman's monograph of Psilocybe, "Field Guide to the Mushrooms of Mexico"
- **General reference:** "Mushrooms of the World" (Benjamin, 1995) as referenced in Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard

## Safety Principles

1. Never eat any mushroom unless you are 100% certain of identification
2. Learn the dangerous species in your region before learning edibles
3. When in doubt, throw it out
4. No single test (silver spoon, peeling cap, animal consumption) reliably identifies safety
5. Spore print is mandatory, not optional
6. Keep a detailed record of habitat, substrate, and associated trees
