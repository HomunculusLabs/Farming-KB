# Estimating Fungal Biodiversity: Methods and Challenges

## Overview

Fungal biodiversity remains one of the most poorly quantified domains in biology. As documented in *[[biodiversity-of-fungi-biodiversity-patterns-ecosystems]]* (Mueller, Bills, and Foster), current estimates suggest that only 5–10% of fungal species on Earth have been described, meaning that between 1.35 million and 2.7 million species remain undiscovered. This gap is far larger than for most other organismal groups and presents unique methodological challenges because fungi cannot be surveyed using standard approaches developed for plants or animals.

## The Scale of Undiscovered Fungal Diversity

### Current Knowledge

The number of described fungal species stands at approximately 74,000–120,000, depending on how "fungi" are defined (whether to include slime molds, chromists like oomycetes, and various fungus-like organisms). The most commonly cited working hypothesis, proposed by Hawksworth in 1991, estimates 1.5 million total fungal species globally. More recent evidence from tropical studies and molecular investigations suggests this figure may be conservative.

### Why the Gap Is So Large

1. **Cryptic lifestyle**: Most fungi live as microscopic mycelium hidden in soil, wood, or host tissue, visible only when fruiting (which may last hours to days).
2. **Ephemeral fruiting**: Many species fruit only sporadically — some once every several years.
3. **Microscopic diversity**: A significant proportion are known only from cultures or molecular detections and have never been observed in nature.
4. **Undersampled habitats**: [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]].
5. **Taxonomic expertise shortage**: Declining numbers of trained mycological taxonomists create a bottleneck in species description.

## Methods for Estimating Fungal Diversity

### Fruiting Body Surveys

Systematic collection and identification of sporocarps from a defined area. **Strengths**: voucher specimens, ecological data, low cost. **Limitations**: detects only fungi fruiting during survey, biased toward conspicuous macrofungi, weather-dependent, requires taxonomic expertise.

### DNA Metabarcoding

Extracting DNA from environmental samples and amplifying fungal barcode regions (typically ITS2) via high-throughput sequencing. **Strengths**: detects all fungi including non-fruiting and microscopic taxa, quantitative, rapid. **Limitations**: cannot distinguish living from dormant/dead DNA, PCR bias, incomplete reference databases, no ecological context.

### Culture-Based Methods

Isolating fungi from environmental samples into pure culture. **Strengths**: living material for study, morphological ID via sporulation, genome sequencing. **Limitations**: biased toward fast-growing opportunists, many fungi unculturable, medium-dependent results.

### Direct Microscopy

Examining substrates under compound/dissecting microscopes. **Strengths**: reveals fungi in ecological context, essential for microfungi on surfaces and in litter. **Limitations**: limited to morphologically distinctive taxa, species-level ID often impossible, time-consuming.

## Site-Based Diversity Estimation

### The All Taxa Biodiversity Inventory (ATBI) Model

The ATBI concept, developed for the Guanacaste Conservation Area in Costa Rica, proposed a comprehensive inventory of all species within a defined area. For fungi, this would require:

- Multiple years of systematic fruiting body collection (minimum 5 years to account for variation in fruiting patterns)
- Culture-based isolation from multiple substrate types (soil, leaf litter, wood, leaves, roots)
- DNA metabarcoding of bulk environmental samples
- Specialist teams for different fungal groups (agarics, polypores, ascomycetes on wood, leaf-inhabiting fungi, soil fungi, lichens, yeasts)
- Seasonal sampling to capture temporal variation

### Expected Species Richness

Based on existing data from well-surveyed temperate sites, the relationship between fungal and plant diversity is approximately 6:1 (six fungal species per plant species). A temperate forest with 400 native plant species might support 2,400–3,000 fungal species. - Sampling methods (fruiting body collection, culturing, DNA analysis)
- Effort levels (number of visits, person-hours, number of samples)
- Identification criteria (morphological vs. molecular, species concepts)
- Taxonomic scope (which fungal groups are included)
- Habitat [[compost-tea-definitions-and-classification]] schemes

### The Species Concept Problem

[[fungal-species-concepts-estimates]] remain contentious. Different researchers apply different criteria (morphological, biological, phylogenetic, ecological), and these can lead to dramatically different species counts for the same group of organisms. Molecular data has revealed that many morphologically defined "species" are actually species complexes containing multiple genetically distinct lineages.

## Future Directions

### High-Throughput Sequencing

As sequencing costs continue to decrease, comprehensive DNA-based fungal surveys will become increasingly feasible. The development of standardized primer sets, reference databases, and analytical pipelines will improve comparability between studies.

### Citizen Science

Platforms like iNaturalist and Mushroom Observer are generating increasingly valuable fungal occurrence data. While identification quality varies, the sheer volume of observations provides distributional data that would be impossible for professional mycologists to collect alone.

### Machine Learning

Automated image recognition systems are being developed for fungal identification from photographs. While still imperfect, these tools can significantly accelerate the triage of collected specimens and may eventually provide real-time species identification in the field.
