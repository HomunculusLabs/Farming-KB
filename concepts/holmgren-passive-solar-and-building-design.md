## Passive Solar as Foundational Energy Strategy

Passive solar design is listed among the five important sources of energy
currently poorly used but commonly available for building personal and
local self-reliance. At Melliodora, Holmgren's mud-brick passive solar
house is charged by every sunny winter day, providing overnight warmth.
This exemplifies the principle of catching and storing energy from the
sun through building design rather than mechanical systems.

A passive solar house uses the high-embodied energy in glass to capture
solar energy and store it in thermal mass. Holmgren emphasises that
heating and cooking provided with renewable energy (passive solar and
wood) represent small scale relative to the centralised energies of gas
and electricity grid systems.

## Thermal Mass and Climate Response

The selection of building materials and design strategies must respond to
bioregional climate. Materials that are permeable to water vapour, have
some thermal mass, and provide high insulation qualities make the best
combination in cold climates. The colder the climate, the greater the
benefits from the reduced surface-to-volume ratio of compact and
integrated buildings.

Lightweight houses on poles, and even exploded designs with rooms as
separate buildings, are suitable in humid tropical and subtropical
climates where cooling air movement is essential and termites are a
problem. In south-eastern Australia, such designs are expensive to
insulate and heat and almost impossible to make safe against bushfires.

## Compact Design and Integration

At Melliodora, the house includes a greenhouse and garage/workshop within
a single building envelope. This integration maximises thermal efficiency
and minimises external surface area. Compact two-storey buildings make
sense in cities, but in rural areas large lightweight metal roofs over
single-storey buildings are more useful for rainwater collection.

The great thermal stability of underground buildings has to be balanced
against engineering complexity and damp-proofing costs. In continental
climates, that balance is favourable. The touted insulation value of
straw bale walls is of greatest benefit in extreme climates, but must be
matched with top-quality roof insulation.

## House Size and Sustainability

In the ecological housing movement there was early optimism about houses
with no adverse environmental impacts that capture all their own energy
from the sun. This has been tempered by acceptance that, in general, a
small house is an ecological house. Beyond obvious material costs, larger
houses create systemic problems:

- Higher debt, leading to unsustainable lifestyles
- More space encourages purchase of more consumer goods
- More space encourages indoor lifestyles with less time in nature and
  community
- Higher heating, cleaning and maintenance costs

## The Aesthetics of Descent

Holmgren argues that aesthetic appreciation has a legitimate place in
permaculture. The creative tension between function and aesthetics is
analogous to that between materialism and spirituality. In the
transition from growth to descent, much of the task is finding
appropriate patterns. Over time, bioregional aesthetics will emerge as
distilled design wisdom attuned to local conditions.

## Renewable Energy Sources in Building

Holmgren identifies five important sources of energy currently poorly
used but commonly available for building self-reliance: solar energy
(including passive solar design), wind energy, biomass for fuel and
materials, and run-off water. At Melliodora, all five sources have been
used to establish and maintain the system. The particular mix is unique
to the site and situation.

The slogan "the problem is the solution" helps recognise opportunities
to harvest site-specific energy sources. Occasional gully floods provide
nutrients and sediment; hot drying winds are an opportunity to dry fruit;
blackberries and other weeds serve as mulch and animal fodder. These
examples reflect how ecosystems develop in response to diverse and
irregular energy availability.

## Frugality in Building

One resident's savings on electricity and gas for her new passive solar
house gave her the money for an investment in productive land. This
illustrates how energy-efficient design creates financial capacity for
further sustainable investment -- a virtuous cycle.

Holmgren cautions against prizing design novelty over proven solutions.
He notes that most solutions for keeping out the rain have already been
worked out. His experience with an octagonal house in Tasmania
demonstrated that unconventional roof geometries involve not only more
time and skill but often greater waste in off-cuts of rectangular
materials.
