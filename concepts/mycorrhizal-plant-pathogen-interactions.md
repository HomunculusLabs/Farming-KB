# Mycorrhizal–Plant Pathogen Interactions

## Overview

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] play a significant role in defending host plants against root-infecting pathogenic fungi and nematodes. This protective interaction occurs at the soil–root interface, where mycorrhizal fungi and pathogenic organisms compete for space and resources. Both [[arbuscular-mycorrhizae]] (AM) and ectomycorrhizae (ECM) provide biocontrol functions, though through different mechanisms.

## Phylloplane Pathogens and Environmental Stress

Plant pathogenic fungi attacking above-ground tissues are influenced by leaf surface (phylloplane) microbial communities. The ability of saprotrophic bacteria and fungi on the leaf surface to exclude pathogens depends on plant phenology and the timing of pathogen arrival. If the pathogen colonizes before leaf expansion or before a saprotrophic community develops, biocontrol by the resident community is limited.

Environmental conditions significantly alter phylloplane fungal communities. Atmospheric SO₂ and elevated CO₂ can modify the community structure of both saprotrophic and pathogenic fungi on wheat flag leaves (Magan and Baxter, 1996). Climate change predictions indicate increased risks of fungal pathogen outbreaks and greater severity on crop plants due to rising temperatures and reduced rainfall (Jahn et al., 1996; Luo et al., 1995). Phytophthora-induced oak decline in Europe is also predicted to intensify with climate change (Brasier, 1996).

## Emerging Plant Pathogens

A range of emerging fungal, viral, and bacterial pathogens threaten major crops worldwide (Moffat, 2001). Notable fungal threats include:

- **[[late-blight]]** (*Phytophthora infestans*) on potato and tomato — spreading worldwide
- **Downy mildew** on corn and sorghum — expanding from Southeast Asia
- **Soybean rust** — spreading from Southeast Asia and Russia
- **Karnal bunt** on wheat — present in Pakistan, India, Nepal, Mexico, and the US
- **Rice blast** — prevalent across Asia

Genetic manipulation of plant species is being pursued to increase disease resistance against these emerging threats. The race to promote natural resistance and engineer artificial resistance through gene manipulation is ongoing.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizae and Disease Prevention

### Meta-Analysis Findings

A meta-analysis by Borowicz (2001) reviewing studies from 1970 to early 1998 found that approximately 50% of published studies demonstrated that arbuscular mycorrhizae afforded some degree of protection against plant pathogenic fungi and nematodes. However, the review noted important limitations:

- Most studies were performed on economically important crop species in low-phosphate soil conditions
- Experiments were primarily conducted in greenhouses or microplots
- Much broader studies are needed before extrapolating to agricultural or natural ecosystems

The pathogenic fungi typically reduced growth of the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus, but this reciprocal suppression occurred in only 16% of reported cases.

### Mechanisms of Protection

The protective effect of arbuscular mycorrhizae appears to operate primarily through:

1. **Improved host plant nutrition** — Enhanced phosphorus uptake and general nutrient status make the plant more resistant to pathogen attack (Dehne, 1982; Smith, 1988; Volpin et al., 1994)
2. **Altered host physiology** — Changes in root exudate chemistry and defense signaling pathways
3. **Reduced nematode performance** — Lower fertility and egg production, reduced root penetrability, and enhanced phosphorus content of the host plant (Roncadori, 1997)

Unlike ectomycorrhizae, there is little physical protection afforded by AM fungi, as less fungal tissue exists at the root surface.

### Case Study: Peanut Pod Rot Protection

*Glomus mosseae* provided significant protection against two pod rot pathogens, *Fusarium solani* and *Rhizoctonia solani*, in peanut plants (Abdalla and Abdel-Fattah, 2000). Key findings:

- Mycorrhizal inoculation increased plant growth and yield
- Pathogens reduced shoot and root dry weights, pod number, and seed weight
- Mycorrhizal plants had significantly higher growth and biomass than non-mycorrhizal plants, even in the presence of pathogens
- Propagule numbers of each pathogen isolated from plant parts were significantly lower in mycorrhizal compared to non-mycorrhizal plants
- The mycorrhizal fungus reduced pathogen fecundity, not just plant susceptibility

### Case Study: Nematode Suppression

*Glomus intraradices* significantly reduced the reproductive capacity of the burrowing nematode *[[radopholus-similis]]* on carrot root tissue (Elsen et al., 2001). The mycorrhizal fungus suppressed nematode populations by approximately 50%, with statistically significant reductions in male and female nematodes within root tissue and females in the growth medium.

### Hemiparasitic Plant Interactions

The relationship between AM fungi and root hemiparasitic plants is complex (Salonen et al., 2001):

- AM colonization of annual grass (*Poa annua*) had little effect on plant growth, while the hemiparasite (*Odontites vulgaris*) significantly reduced host biomass
- AM status of *P. annua* did not affect the parasitic plant's biomass or flower production
- AM colonization of clover (*[[trifolium-pratense]]*) greatly increased host biomass but hemiparasite (*Rhinanthus serotinus*) infection had no effect
- The AM fungi actually *increased* the hemiparasite's biomass and flower production on clover hosts
- Enhancement of parasite performance depends on the degree of growth promotion afforded to the host by the mycorrhizae

## The Pathogen–Mycorrhizae Balance

A delicate balance exists between beneficial mycorrhizal fungi and detrimental pathogenic fungi. Newsham et al. (1994) demonstrated this in experiments with the annual grass *Vulpia ciliata*:

- The fungicide perchloraz did not affect AM abundance or plant performance
- The fungicide benomyl significantly reduced AM colonization but did not significantly lower plant growth or phosphorus inflow
- Benomyl *did* increase plant fecundity (seed number), likely because it simultaneously reduced weak root pathogens (*Fusarium oxysporum* and *Embellisia chlamydospora*)
- The lack of mycorrhizal response to reduced colonization was offset by improved growth when the plant was relieved of pathogen stress

This suggests that fungi previously thought to be non-pathogenic (causing no visible symptoms) may still impose a measurable stress on host plants. The balance between mycorrhizae and pathogens could shift with plant phenology, edaphic conditions, or environmental changes, potentially converting from beneficial to detrimental effects on plant growth.

## Ectomycorrhizae as Biocontrol Agents

### Protective Mechanisms

Ectomycorrhizal fungi provide protection against root pathogens through two primary mechanisms:

1. **Chemical (antibiotic) interference** — Production of antifungal compounds that inhibit pathogen growth (Fravel, 1988; Chakravarty and Hwang, 1991; Duchesne, 1994)
2. **Physical defense** — A fungal sheath (mantle) enveloping colonized short roots creates a physical barrier to pathogen entry (Marx, 1973)

### Evidence from Nursery Studies

The biocontrol effect of ECM fungi has been demonstrated primarily in nursery conditions:

- *Laccaria laccata* suppressed *Fusarium oxysporum* on Douglas fir seedlings (Sylvia and Sinclair, 1983)
- *L. laccata* protected *Pinus banksiana* seedlings from *Fusarium* damping-off (Chakravarty and Hwang, 1991)
- Four ECM fungi (*Laccaria laccata*, *[[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]]*, *H. sinapizans*, and *[[paxillus-involutus]]*) protected chestnut seedlings from *Phytophthora cambivora* and *P. cinnamomi* (Branzanti et al., 1999)
- After five months of pathogen challenge, mycorrhizal plants showed no signs of infection and had increased growth and biomass

### Knowledge Gaps in Natural Ecosystems

Most evidence for mycorrhizal biocontrol comes from agricultural monocultures or forest nurseries — both artificial, low-diversity systems. The role of these interactions in maintaining plant communities in natural ecosystems remains largely speculative. Critical unanswered questions include:

- How do these interactions affect [[leake-mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration-plant-community-composition]] and fitness?
- Has coevolution produced a balance between mycorrhizal and pathogenic fungi?
- Could significant alteration of the fungal community disrupt ecosystem stability?
