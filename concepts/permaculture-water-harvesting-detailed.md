# Permaculture Water Harvesting Detailed

updated: 2026-04-18

Water harvesting in permaculture encompasses the full cycle of capturing, storing, and distributing water from precipitation, run-off, groundwater, and biological sources. Chapter 7 of Mollison's Designers' Manual treats water as the primary selection factor for land use and presents comprehensive strategies for regional intervention in the water cycle.

## Water as Primary Selection Factor

The primary selection factor when choosing cropland should be adequate, well-distributed, and reliable rainfall -- minimum 80 cm (31 inches) annually. Equally important is the ability to hold water in clay or clay-loam storages. Any stream flow within property boundaries is a bonus. All other factors (soil type, present uses, market potential, access, forested areas) are secondary to water availability.

## Regional Intervention in the Water Cycle

Mollison presents multiple strategies for increasing local water availability:

### Cloud Seeding

Silver iodide or tea leaf dust can seed cumulus and cumulonimbus clouds to initiate local precipitation. Effects spread for hundreds of square kilometres and persist for months. Initial precipitation increases of up to 30% have been recorded, averaging 19% in Australia. However, ground storage systems must be developed before cloud-seeding to cope with extra water.

### Orographic and Forest Effects

Cross-wind ridges of even modest height (3-20 m) planted with tree condensers can induce cloud formation and rain through streamline compression. Forested catchments exceeding 6-10 m in height are credited with up to 40% of rainfall in Sweden and Australia. Deforestation depletes rainfall, streamflow, and cloud formation -- effects never costed against wood-chipping operations.

### Soil Storages

Soil conditioning (ripping followed by tree plantation) increases soil water-holding capacity by factors of 70-85%. Areas of 85% run-off can be converted to zero overland flow through soil conditioning, swales, and water spreading to forests. Soils contain many times the water of open storages or streams, so increasing soil water increases throughflow, baseflow, and plant-available water.

## Catchment Strategies

### Infiltration via Earthworks

Pitting, swales, and water spreading are the main aids to getting fresh water to deeper storages. Diversion of surface flow to sand basins, dune fields, swamps, and soakage beds ensures resident water reserves for crops and trees, and longer-term storages for dry seasons.

Diversion drains with associated valves, slides, cross-walls, and intakes enable effective water harvesting and fast distribution in rolling lowlands, hill country, and drylands. They can recharge sand basins and swales from otherwise wasted overland flow and damp wildfires.

### Tank Storages

Water captured off roof areas, roads, and paved surfaces serves both drinking and garden use. Roof water is least polluted and most easily treated. Absorption beds handle muddy or polluted run-off from roads and parking areas.

### Pond and Dam Storages

In humid areas, water storages can occupy up to 20% of the landscape with great benefit. Dams and ponds serve as long-term water and wildlife reserves while providing fish and aquatic protein. Many Australian farms are now drought-proof due to Keyline or similar water conservation systems.

### Biological Storages

Great forests and biological water storages in fruits and nuts (like coconut) form the basis for life proliferation where no free water exists. On atolls and arid islands of free-draining sands, biological reserves are the main water reserves -- often overlooked. Cactus, palms, and agaves have specific water-storage tissues.

In the local microclimate, water in vegetation greatly moderates heat and cold extremes, and both releases to and absorbs water from passing air streams.

## Distribution Systems

Gravity-fed systems are preferred wherever possible. Slope allows unpowered flow of water from source to use point. The keyline system uses the natural keypoint to distribute stored water to lower fields by gravity. Where no slope exists, towers for water tanks can be raised.

Thermosiphon effects use slope for hot water circulation without pumps -- hot air or water collectors placed below their storages circulate naturally.

## Water Purification

Mollison covers purification of polluted waters through biological systems: constructed wetlands, reed beds, and marsh treatment systems that process sewage and greywater through plant and microbial action. Natural swimming pools use biological filtration rather than chemicals.

## Key Design Principles

- There is plenty of water for the world if we define the ways in which we store and use it carefully
- Soil treatments should precede tree planting over every area used by contemporary agriculture
- Trees are essential to prevent waterlogging and soil salting in the long term
- The value of land must be assessed on its yield of potable water
- Every property should aim for drought-proofing through multiple storage types
