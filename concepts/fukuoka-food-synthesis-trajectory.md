### Stage 2: Scientific Agriculture with Chemicals

The second stage begins with the industrial revolution and the rise of what Fukuoka calls "[[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]], pesticides, mechanized plowing, and monoculture cropping. The soil was reconceived as an inert substrate — a mere physical support for plant roots — rather than a living ecosystem. Nutrients were reduced to N-P-K numbers. Weeds and insects became enemies to be exterminated. The farmer became an engineer, and nature became a problem to be solved.

Fukuoka saw this stage as the beginning of genuine alienation. The farmer no longer needed to understand the land intuitively. Knowledge was transferred from the field to the laboratory, from the peasant to the agronomist. Food began to lose its connection to place and season.

### Stage 3: Hothouse and Hydroponic Agriculture

The third stage intensifies the removal from natural conditions. In hothouse agriculture, crops are grown indoors under artificial light, with temperature, humidity, and nutrient delivery precisely controlled by technology. Hydroponic systems eliminate soil entirely, delivering mineral solutions directly to roots suspended in water or inert media.

For Fukuoka, this stage represents a critical turning point. The last vestige of natural relationship — the soil itself — has been removed. Plants are no longer growing in an ecosystem; they are being manufactured in a factory-like environment. The food produced may look identical to field-grown produce, but for Fukuoka, it is fundamentally different in its essence. It is food that has never known the sun, the rain, or the living earth.

### Stage 4: Total Chemical Synthesis

The final stage of the trajectory, as Fukuoka envisions it, is the total chemical synthesis of food. In this scenario, food is no longer grown at all — it is assembled from base chemical compounds in laboratories. Proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, and minerals are combined to produce nutritionally complete but entirely artificial food substances.

Fukuoka recognized that this stage had not yet been fully realized in his time, but he saw it as the inevitable endpoint of the logic that had been set in motion. If food is nothing more than a collection of chemical nutrients, and if growing food in soil is merely an inefficient way of assembling those nutrients, then the rational conclusion is to bypass biological processes entirely and synthesize food directly.

The logic is inescapable once the initial premise — that food is merely chemistry — is accepted. Every intermediate stage is already a compromise between that premise and the practical difficulties of implementation. As technology advances, the compromises are stripped away one by one.

He warned that once this stage is reached, the relationship between humans and the earth is severed beyond repair. There would be no practical reason to maintain farms, forests, or any of the living systems that currently sustain human life. The entire biosphere becomes, from a purely instrumental perspective, expendable. This is not science fiction for Fukuoka — it is the logical terminus of a civilization that has already accepted the first three stages without meaningful resistance.
