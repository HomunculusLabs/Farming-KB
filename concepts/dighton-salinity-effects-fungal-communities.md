# Salinity Effects on Fungal Communities

## Overview

Soil salinity is a significant environmental stressor that affects
fungal community composition, mycorrhizal colonization, and ecosystem
function. Salinization arises naturally in coastal and arid regions,
and is exacerbated by irrigation practices in agricultural systems.
Understanding how fungi respond to saline conditions is critical for
managing [[biodiversity-fungi-soil-fungal-communities]],
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-network-visualization-anastomosis]] function, and
the [[fungal-ecosystem-roles]] of fungi in
salt-affected environments including salt marshes, mangroves, and
irrigated agroecosystems.

## Sources of Soil Salinity

Salinity in soils originates from several natural and anthropogenic
processes. In coastal ecosystems, saltwater intrusion and tidal
flooding create naturally saline conditions. In arid and semiarid
regions, high evaporation rates concentrate salts in the soil profile.
In agroecosystems, irrigation is a major driver of localized salinity
increases. As Dighton notes, where irrigation plays a significant part
in ecosystem management, the evaporation of water often leaves
localized increases in soil salinity, which can fundamentally alter the
fungal communities present.

## Effects on Mycorrhizal Fungi

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Responses

Juniper and Abbott (1993) demonstrated that increased soil salinity
can reduce the germination of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-network-visualization-anastomosis]] spores and reduce extraradical hyphal growth. Plants
growing in saline soils thus have a reduced mycorrhizal component,
which is probably detrimental to their growth and survival. The impact
of salinity on mycorrhizal colonization is, however, a matter of
degree.

Some degree of tolerance of arbuscular mycorrhizae to salinity has
been observed (Sengupta and Chaudhuri, 1990), although reductions in
mycorrhizal development have been shown to occur with increasing
salinity (Semones and Young, 1995; Baker et al., 1995; Johnson-Green
et al., 2001). Johnson-Green et al. (2001) suggest that although
mycorrhizal function is reduced in highly saline soils, mycorrhizae
could still be of benefit in the revegetation of salt-degraded lands.

### Phosphorus-Salinity Interactions

The interaction between salinity and phosphorus availability is an
important consideration for mycorrhizal function in saline
environments. Studies have examined the effects of phosphorus
availability and salinity on mycorrhizal colonization, with results
showing that phosphorus addition can modify the response of
mycorrhizal plants to saline conditions. The mechanisms involve
complex interactions between soil chemistry, plant physiology, and
fungal tolerance thresholds.

## Fungal Communities in Saline Ecosystems

### Salt Marsh Ecosystems

Salt marsh fungal communities are distinct from those of terrestrial
ecosystems and are dominated by specific ascomycete and mitosporic
fungi adapted to fluctuating salinity and tidal inundation. Fungal
biomass in decaying salt marsh vegetation is substantial, reaching 60
to 85 mg per gram of organic mass, considerably higher than in
mangrove systems. The fungal biomass in these decomposing materials is
important for sustaining invertebrate herbivore populations, including
amphipods that grow best when fed on decaying leaves containing high
fungal biomass.

Different plant species dominate salt marshes at different salinity
levels, with Spartina alterniflora being a dominant grass in
high-salinity marshes and Spartina cynosuroides dominating in
lower-salinity areas. The fungal communities associated with these
different plant hosts differ accordingly.

### Mangrove Ecosystems

Mangrove swamps are the tropical equivalent of salt marsh habitats.
The mangrove fungal community consists almost exclusively of
saprotrophs, with some 23 species of ascomycete, 17 mitosporic
species, and two basidiomycetes identified (Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer,
1979). Despite the similar saline environment, the composition of
mangrove fungal communities is distinct from those of salt marsh
ecosystems (Newell, 1996). Notably, fungal biomass in decaying
mangrove leaves is much lower than in salt marsh vegetation (less than
1 mg per gram compared to 60 to 85 mg per gram).

## Fungal Adaptations to Salinity

Fungi exhibit several strategies for coping with saline conditions.
These include the accumulation of compatible solutes, modification of
cell wall composition, and alteration of enzyme production patterns.
Fungi that grow in saline environments serve as models for
understanding ecophysiological adaptation to osmotic stress. The
presence of marine oomycetes (e.g., Halophytophthora) in mangrove
systems represents an additional fungal-like group adapted to saline
conditions, though these organisms do not contain ergosterol and are
thus not captured by standard fungal biomass measurements.

## Broader Ecosystem Implications

The effects of salinity on fungal communities cascade through
ecosystem processes. Reduced mycorrhizal colonization limits plant
access to nutrients, particularly [[fungal-role-in-nitrogen-cycling]]
and nitrogen. Alterations in fungal decomposer communities affect
rates of organic matter breakdown and nutrient mineralization. In
agricultural contexts, understanding salinity effects on mycorrhizae
is essential for developing strategies to maintain soil productivity
in irrigated systems and to restore salt-degraded lands.

The relationships between soil salinity, mycorrhizal function, and
plant community composition highlight the importance of fungal
communities as mediators of ecosystem responses to environmental
stressors. Changes in salinity regimes, whether from sea level rise,
irrigation practices, or climate-driven changes in evaporation, will
have significant consequences for fungal-mediated ecosystem processes.
