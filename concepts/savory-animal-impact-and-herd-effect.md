# Animal Impact and Herd Effect

Animal impact is one of the most powerful and least understood tools in
Holistic Resource Management. It encompasses all the physical effects
that large herbivores have on land beyond simple removal of forage,
including trampling, dung deposition, urine concentration, soil
compaction, and the breaking of soil surfaces. Understanding the
distinction between animal impact and herd effect, and how to manage
both through timing, is central to reversing [[desertification]] in brittle
environments.

## The Discovery of Animal Impact

Savory's recognition of animal impact's importance came through years
of field observation, initially as a tracker in Zimbabwe's civil war.
He noticed vast differences between land where wildlife herded
naturally, where people herded domestic stock, and where stock was
fenced without herding. Compared to areas without any large animals,
the differences were startling: where animals were present, plants were
green and growing; without them, plants were grey and dying unless
burned.

The degree of animal disturbance showed a proportionately positive
impact on the health of plants and soils. Areas heavily churned by
animals showed better soil structure, more rapid plant establishment,
and higher overall community complexity than areas with little or no
disturbance.

## Two Modes of Animal Behavior

Savory identified two fundamentally different modes of behavior in
herding animals that produce very different effects on land:

When feeding, herding animals spread out and walk gently and slowly.
They place their hooves beside coarse plants rather than on top of
them. They place their full weight on their hooves, compacting soil
below the surface but hardly disturbing the surface itself. While
feeding, they have remarkably little impact on plants and soil other
than forage removal and mild compaction.

When moving as a herd, whether from predators, migration, being driven,
or competing for supplements, the animals behave entirely differently.
They bunch together, kick up dust, and step recklessly. Even very
coarse plants containing old ungrazed material are trampled down,
providing cover for the soil surface. Trotting and galloping hooves
chip and break the soil as if a gardener had worked it over.

## Herd Effect Defined

The term "herd effect" refers specifically to the excited movement of
crowded, hooved animals when under threat or attack by predators, in
full migration, being driven, or competing for concentrated food
sources. Herd effect is difficult to quantify but easy to identify:
soil surfaces are broken, coarse plants are laid flat, dust rises, and
the ground is left in a state of aggressive disturbance.

This distinction is critical because grazing or walking animals place
hooves carefully and barely break the soil surface. When herd effect
occurs, the same animals break down coarse plants, raise dust, chip
soil surfaces, and open the soil to aeration. The difference in
ecological effect is enormous.

## The Role of Predators

In natural ecosystems, predators played a crucial role in maintaining
herd effect. The constant threat of predation kept herding animals
bunched and moving, ensuring periodic high-impact disturbance. When
predators were removed and livestock were fenced in by humans without
herding, this natural pattern was disrupted. Animals became calm,
localized, and scattered, producing only the gentle feeding-mode impact.

On Savory's game reserve in Zimbabwe, where lions, leopards, hyenas,
cheetahs, and wild dogs maintained natural predation pressure, the
same wildlife species behaved quite differently than on nearby ranches
where predators had been eliminated. The animals had become localized
and calm, providing insufficient disturbance for soil health.

## Impact on Succession

Periodic high animal impact promotes the successional process in
brittle environments. It breaks algal and lichen crusts that can retard
succession for thousands of years. It chips capped soil surfaces,
allowing water infiltration and seed establishment. It lays old plant
material on the ground as mulch, initiating biological decay. It
creates the disturbed conditions necessary for new seedlings to
establish.

Without adequate large animal impact, plant spacings enlarge and soil
becomes exposed. Some mobile organisms like termites build structures
over bare soil to reach dung or leaf fall, but this alone cannot
sustain a good mineral cycle. Where plant spacings are wide and soil
exposed, few forces exist to decompose old material or hold it against
wind and water.

## Recovery from Extreme Impact

Trampling carried to extremes can cause temporary or even long-lasting
damage, but when the time factor is controlled, most environments have
astounding resilience. Savory documented a case where two thousand
cattle concentrating at a fence corner during a heavy downpour created
a severe quagmire. Eighteen months later, the site could no longer be
distinguished from surrounding paddocks. In many cases, temporarily
overtrampled areas look much better after a season.

The continuous trampling seen around gates, water points, and feed
troughs does not allow recovery and is often cited as evidence against
trampling in general. However, this represents a failure of management
rather than a failure of the tool. Properly timed, concentrated impact
followed by adequate recovery produces beneficial results.

## Application to Cropland

Savory also envisioned applications of animal impact to cropland,
particularly in brittle areas where conventional row-cropping practices
cause rapid soil deterioration. Livestock could be introduced in high
concentrations for very short periods to speed cycling and decay of
crop residues while improving surface crumb structure and porosity.

## Watershed Restoration

The greatest immediate benefit from animal impact lies in the restoration
and maintenance of brittle watersheds. Irrigable and arable land accounts
for only about ten percent of most countries. The water on which cities,
mining, irrigation, and industry depend comes from largely brittle land
comprising nearly all the rest. No mechanical or technological means can
replace animal impact across such vast areas.
