# Natural Farming Rice Barley Clover Method

## Overview

Fukuoka's "direct-seeded, no-tillage, winter grain/rice succession in a clover
cover" is the practical centerpiece of his natural farming system. This method
produces consistent yields of close to 22 bushels (1,300 pounds) of winter grain
and 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre without plowing, [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]],
compost, pesticides, or any of the practices conventional farmers consider
essential. Fukuoka describes growing grain this way as very easy and
straightforward — the labor of one or two people is more than enough to grow
crops on a quarter-acre. His goal was to eventually take in 33 bushels per
quarter-acre.

## The Seasonal Cycle

### Autumn: Broadcasting Over Ripening Rice

The cycle begins in autumn. Fukuoka simply broadcasts clover and winter grain
(barley or wheat) over the ripening heads of rice before the fall harvest. The
clover seed and grain seed fall among the standing rice plants, settling into
the moist soil surface. This timing is critical — the rice plants provide shade
and protection for the newly broadcast seeds, and the residual moisture from the
maturing rice creates ideal germination conditions. No tillage is needed because
the seeds are simply dropped onto the soil surface.

### Autumn: Rice Harvest

Later, he harvests the rice while treading on the young shoots of winter grain.
The treading actually presses the winter grain seeds more firmly into contact
with the soil, improving germination. After leaving the rice to dry for three
days, he threshs it then scatters the straw uncut over the entire field. If
chicken droppings are on hand, he scatters these over the straw. The uncut rice
straw serves as mulch, retaining moisture, suppressing weeds, and gradually
decomposing to feed the soil microorganisms.

### Winter: Sowing Rice in Clay Pellets

Next, he forms clay pellets containing seed rice and scatters the pellets over
the straw before the New Year. The clay protects the rice seeds from birds and
rodents during the winter months and prevents premature germination. With the
winter [[jeavons-grain-growing-mini-farms]] and the rice seed sown, there is now nothing left to do
until the harvesting of the winter grain. The field requires no irrigation, no
weeding, no fertilization — only patience through the winter months while the
clover covers the ground and the winter grain grows tall.

### Late Spring: Winter Grain Harvest and Field Flooding

In late May, while harvesting the winter grain, he notices the clover growing
luxuriantly at his feet and the small shoots that have emerged from the rice
seed in the clay pellets. After harvesting, drying, and threshing the winter
grain, he scatters all of the straw uncut over the field. This second layer of
straw mulch continues the cycle of soil building and weed suppression.

He then floods the field for four to five days to weaken the clover and give the
rice shoots a chance to break through the cover of clover. This brief flooding
is the only water management intervention in the entire cycle. The flooding does
not kill the clover — it merely checks its vigorous growth long enough for the
rice seedlings to establish themselves above the clover canopy.

### Summer: Minimal Irrigation

In June and July, he leaves the field unirrigated, and in August he runs water
through the drainage ditches once every week or ten days. This minimal water
management stands in stark contrast to conventional [[fukuoka-natural-rice-farming-steps]], which maintains
continuous flooding throughout the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. The intermittent drainage
allows the soil to breathe and maintains the health of soil microorganisms that
would be killed by prolonged flooding.

## The Role of Clover

White clover serves as the living foundation of the entire system. It functions
simultaneously as a nitrogen-fixing ground cover, a weed suppressant, a soil
builder, and a mulch crop. The clover grows throughout the fall and winter,
protecting the soil surface and adding nitrogen through its root nodules. In
spring, its luxuriant growth shades the soil and prevents weed germination. The
brief flooding in May temporarily weakens the clover without killing it, allowing
rice seedlings to emerge. After the rice is harvested in autumn, the clover
quickly recovers and the cycle begins again.

## Yields and Productivity

With this method, Fukuoka consistently achieved yields of 22 bushels per
quarter-acre for both rice and winter grain. He noted that there are generally
about 250 heads of grain per square yard, with an average of 200 grains per
head, giving a potential harvest of some 33 bushels for every quarter-acre. Those
who see the sturdy heads of rice rising from the field marvel at the strength
and vigor of the plants and their large yields. No matter that there are insect
pests here — as long as their natural enemies are also present, a natural balance
asserts itself. The yield varies from year to year, but consistently meets or
exceeds the average for conventionally farmed fields in the same region.

## Why This Method Works

Fukuoka explains the success of his method as the symbiosis of [[fukuoka-rice-and-barley-natural-farming]] or
wheat in clover. The key insight is that nature, not man, grows the crops. By
eliminating the practices that create the need for further intervention —
tillage, which hardens the soil; flooding, which rots roots; fertilizers, which
weaken plants — the natural productivity of the land is allowed to express
itself fully. Human effort is reduced to sowing seed and spreading straw because
that is all that is needed when working with nature rather than against it.

## Contrast with Conventional Methods

Were Fukuoka to say that all his method of farming boils down to is the symbiosis
of rice and barley or wheat in clover, he would probably be reproached: "If
that's all there is to growing rice, then farmers wouldn't be out there working
so hard in their fields." Yet, that is all there is to it. Such being the case,
the only conclusion possible is that there must be something drastically wrong
with farming practices that require so much unnecessary labor. Scientists are
always saying, "Let's try this, let's try that," and agriculture becomes swept
up in all of this fiddling around. Fukuoka took the opposite tack, eliminating
unnecessary practices, expenditures, and labor by telling himself, "I don't need
to do this, I don't need to do that."
