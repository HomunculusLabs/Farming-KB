The keypoint dam is a foundational concept in [[permaculture-earthworks]]
for humid landscapes. Developed from P.A. Yeomans' keyline system,
the keypoint represents the point in a valley profile where convex
landforms transition to concave. It is the highest point at which
mechanical tools can work practical water diversion on a hillside.

## Locating the Keypoint

Raindrops travel across the landscape by crossing contours at right
angles, following the shortest path between contour lines. At the
valley head where creeks converge, contours begin to spread apart.
That spreading point is the keypoint. On a contour map, you can
identify it by finding where contour lines start to diverge after
running closely together in the upper valley.

Having found the keypoint, the entire landscape can be treated as if
it were a roof and a tank. Water falling on hilltops runs off along
these predictable paths. The keyline starts to fall from one in 1,000
to one in 2,000 below contour, creating a very gentle gradient that
water can follow without erosion.

## The Diversion Drain

From the keypoint, a shelf or groove is carved around the hillside.
This is essentially a gutter for the landscape. The line lifts about
one foot at every 2,000 feet of distance. Water that was previously
running to unknown destinations is now collected and directed to the
keypoint dam. No matter where water was going before, it now reaches
the storage point.

The diversion drain can also serve as a road system. Existing roads
across properties often already perform this function, diverting
water around the landscape without the owner realizing it. Permaculture
design uses the keyline road system intentionally to collect and
direct snowmelt and rainfall from forested upper slopes.

## The Keypoint Dam

At the keypoint itself, a small dam is constructed. This is the
highest point in the valley profile where economical water storage
is possible. It requires a fair amount of earth but gathers all water
from the hilltop down to that point. Water can be released from the
dam through a siphon or lock-pipe system into a contour drain,
allowing irrigation of any area below.

For large dams holding five to six million gallons, a sliding gate
or lock-pipe about 18 inches square is installed in the dam wall.
Water flows out at walking pace when the gate is opened. The most
restful irrigation method uses two people with two flags, pegging
alternately so water soaks one section before flowing to the next.

One person can water hundreds of acres this way with no effort at
all. For fire control, walking backwards with the flag douses the
whole hillside. No pumps are needed; the system operates entirely
by gravity.

## Saddle Dams on Ridge Tops

Beyond valley dams, saddle dams sit in the low points of ridge tops.
These mark weaknesses in the landscape where rivers begin forming on
both sides of the ridge. By building walls on either side of the
saddle, very large and very high water storages can be created.
These are the highest water storages possible on any property.

Saddle dams fill faster than lower dams because they intercept water
first during rainfall events. They buffer erosion by absorbing the
initial shock of storm water. Even after rain stops, they continue
feeding the water table, providing moderating effects on downstream
flow and reducing flood risk. The authorites in Australia encourage
building as many small dams as possible for this reason.

## Contour Dams

Contour dams are shallow dams with large surface areas, placed on
the least sloping sites available. They can perch on knolls where
the land dwindles out. The flatter the floor that is flooded, the
more water obtained per dollar spent. This principle applies whether
on open fields, ridges, or valley floors.

Spillways are always contoured away from the dam wall and graded to
produce sheet flow. The spilldown area is often planted with shrubs.
These low dams pose minimal flood risk because they overflow gently
and the resulting flood spreads only a short distance.

## Mini-Terraces for Small Properties

For clients on slopes with no uphill catchment, mini-terraces
provide productive garden space. Terraces are designed about waist
high, two feet wide at the top and three to four feet at the base.
Each tier has a mulched walkway at its base. No more than three or
four tiers are recommended, each no longer than about forty feet.

## Practical Considerations

The depth of diversion ditches depends on dam size. A 5,000 gallon
dam serving a small market garden needs only a small ditch controlled
by a spade. A lake-sized storage requires a large lock pipe and a
ditch half the size of a room. It is possible to make the ditch
itself into a lake by widening it wherever easy, creating a
broad waterway that doubles as irrigation storage.

On sandy soils, even with only one foot of fall per 2,000 feet,
very little sand shifts in these ditches. An eight-mile ditch was
successfully run through sand in northeast Tasmania during a dry
spell and functioned on light misty rain alone. Rocks in the path
are easily worked around: the ditch drops down the rock face,
picks up at the bottom, and continues. If rocks are large, a small
six-inch-deep channel may suffice.

Soil type determines cultivation limits. Sandy soils should not be
cultivated beyond a 19-degree slope. Clay may tolerate up to 20
degrees occasionally. Local soils offices can provide guidance.

## High Country Water Points: Dieu-Ponds

Dieu-ponds are hand-dug catchments dotting the British landscape,
traditionally constructed by monasteries. Sited where a mini-catchment
or cup-shaped depression exists in the hill, they are clay tamped
but not machine compacted. Material removed is laid on the
catchment area to increase runoff.

The secret to their function is that they taper inward. As water
evaporates, the surface area decreases, so they always retain some
water and rarely dry out completely. Two or three people can dig one
in a day. They range from three to twenty feet in diameter and
provide eternal water supplies for stock in normally humid uplands.
