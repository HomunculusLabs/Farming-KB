## Fukuoka's Natural Orchard and Semi-Wild Vegetable Methods

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] applied his [[natural-farming-principles]] not only to rice and winter grain but also to citrus orchards (12.5 acres) and semi-wild vegetable gardens on the hillsides of Iyo, Ehime Prefecture, Japan.

## Orchard Establishment and Soil Building

After WWII, Fukuoka took over surrounding abandoned hillsides and cleared them by hand. The process of restoring barren red clay slopes:

1. **Initial condition:** Hard red clay, "so hard you could not stick a shovel into it," previously exhausted by potato farming and abandoned
2. **Planting:** Dug holes in contour lines and planted citrus seedlings among resprouting forest trees, pampas grass, cogon grass, and bracken
3. **Windbreaks:** Cut most pine sprouts but allowed some to grow back as windbreaks
4. **Ground cover:** Cut back thicket growth and sowed white clover and alfalfa — took several years before they could take hold on the barren ground
5. **Nitrogen-fixing trees:** Planted Morishima acacia (from Australia) — grows faster than any tree Fukuoka had seen, sends deep roots in months, reaches telephone pole height in 6–7 years. Rhizobium bacteria in roots fertilize deep soil strata. 6–10 trees per quarter acre eliminates need to haul organic matter
6. **Soil loosening:** Planted Japanese radish (daikon) — roots penetrate deeply, add organic matter, open channels for air and water. Reseeds itself easily after one sowing
7. **Result:** After 25 years, hard red clay became loose, dark-colored, rich with earthworms and organic matter to a depth of 4+ inches

**Key lesson:** Deep cultivation and burying organic matter in trenches (the recommended post-war technique) is "physically exhausting and, as far as improving the soil is concerned, just plain useless." Trenches caved in, and buried straw/ferns produced negligible humus after 2–3 years.

## Orchard Pest Management Without Chemicals

### Natural Form Prevents Pests
Trees allowed to follow their natural form from the beginning bear fruit every year with no need for pruning. A citrus tree grows with a single central trunk and branches spreading alternately, like cedar or pine. When seedlings were growing beneath resprouted forest trees, there was **no evidence of damaging insects**. Only after cutting away the thicket and making the land "more like an orchard" did insect problems appear.

### The Morishima Acacia Ecosystem
The acacia creates a self-regulating pest control system:
- Aphids feed on the acacia"s year-round new buds → multiply rapidly
- Ladybirds feed on aphids → multiply rapidly
- When aphids are consumed, ladybirds climb down to citrus trees and feed on mites, arrowhead scales, and cottony-cushion scales

### Minimal Intervention
For mites and scales that do appear: dilute machine oil emulsion 200–400× and spray lightly in midsummer. **Critical:** this only works if no organic phosphorous pesticide was used in June or July, as those chemicals also kill predators. Fukuoka does not advocate introducing foreign predator species.

## Semi-Wild Vegetable Growing

Fukuoka grows vegetables among weeds and clover in the spaces between citrus trees on the mountainside — "just toss out the seeds and let the vegetables grow up with the weeds."

### Timing is Everything
- **Spring vegetables:** Sow when winter weeds are dying back, just before summer weeds sprout
- **Fall sowing:** Sow when summer grasses are fading and winter weeds have not yet appeared
- **Best conditions:** Wait for rain likely to last several days; cut a swath in weed cover, scatter seeds, lay cut weeds back over as mulch

### Key Techniques
- **Scatter, don"t row-plant:** Beetles and insects walk in straight lines; chickens spot cleared patches and scratch. Random scattering avoids both threats
- **Clay pellets** for hard-to-germinate species (spinach, carrots) — soak seeds 1–2 days, wrap in clay
- **Sow thickly** for competitive species: radish, turnips, leafy greens will outcompete weeds
- **Self-seeding perennials:** Garlic, Japanese pearl onions, Chinese leeks come up year after year once established
- **Legumes** (cowpeas, kidney beans) are easy and high-yielding; early germination is essential for peas, azuki, soybeans
- **Transplant weaklings:** Tomatoes and eggplants can't compete with weeds when young — start in beds, transplant, let tomatoes run along ground (roots grow from stem nodes)
- **Cucumbers, melons, squash:** Creeping varieties best; lay bamboo or branches for fruit to rest above ground
- **Potatoes and taros:** Very strong; leave a few in ground at harvest for perpetual reseeding. If soil is hard, grow radish first to break it up
- **White clover as living mulch:** Sows thickly enough to smother mugwort and crabgrass; enriches soil, keeps ground moist and aerated. Once established, lasts 5–6 years without resowing

### Why Semi-Wild Works
When various herbs and vegetables are mixed together and grown among natural vegetation, damage by insects and diseases is minimal. The varied weed growth indicates soil conditions and nutrient availability. Vegetables grown this way have a unique, richer flavor — closer to their wild predecessors.

### What Doesn't Work
- Trying to use improved techniques or maximize yields leads to failure (usually from insects or diseases)
- Row planting or furrow sowing attracts pests walking in straight lines
- Over-managing defeats the purpose

## The Stem Borer Paradox

While at the Kochi Testing Station, Fukuoka conducted stem borer experiments: one field sprayed with insecticide, another left untreated. **The untreated field with many withered stalks had the higher yield.** The stem borers attacked weaker plants, producing a natural thinning effect — remaining plants grew stronger, sent up more grain-bearing stalks, and produced more grains per head. When stalk density is too great and insects don't thin the excess, the harvest is actually lower.

This result was checked as an "experimental discrepancy" and discarded. Only half of agricultural chemical test results showing positive effects are published; negative yield results are rarely reported.

## Natural vs Chemical Food Quality

Fukuoka"s observations on food quality:

- **Eggs:** Caged leghorn yolks are soft, watery, pale yellow. Free-range mountain chicken yolks are firm, resilient, bright orange — "a real egg, just like in the old days"
- **Vegetables:** Semi-wild vegetables have a distinctly richer, more subtle flavor than chemically grown ones — they receive a more balanced diet of nutrients from diverse weed communities
- **Shriveling is preservation:** Fruit/vegetables in slightly shriveled state have lowest respiration and energy consumption — like a person meditating. Maintaining mere appearance of freshness (sprinkling water) actually accelerates deterioration of flavor and nutrition
- **Food and medicine are one:** Wild vegetables and edible herbs are both nutritious and medicinal. Chemically grown vegetables may be eaten as food but cannot serve as medicine
- **The seven herbs of spring** (watercress, shepherd"s purse, wild turnip, cottonweed, chickweed, wild radish, bee nettle) calm the spirit. Shepherd's purse is best for calming restless feelings and curing children's tantrums

## Economics of Natural Food

Fukuoka's economic analysis:
- Natural food production involves the least expense and effort → should be sold at the cheapest price
- His unsorted, unwaxed mandarins were the lowest-priced in Tokyo yet rated most delicious
- Chemical farmers work extremely hard but profit less than 5¢/lb after sorting, waxing, colouring, and preservative treatment
- Only 25–50% of fruit meets co-op quality standards; rejected fruit sold at half price for juice
- Commercial agriculture is unstable: input prices (fertilizer, feed, chemicals) are fixed abroad; selling prices are also fixed — the farmer's income is at the mercy of forces beyond his control
- "If you plant one grain of rice, it becomes more than one thousand grains. One row of turnips makes enough pickles for the entire winter" — self-sufficiency is more reliable than profit-seeking

## Critique of Modern Agriculture and Research

- **Specialization blindness:** Each researcher sees only one factor; none grasp the whole
- **Research serves itself:** Professor Tsuno studies plant metabolism, writes books, gets doctorates — but explicitly leaves applications to Fukuoka, saying "I"m going to stick to research"
- **The Green Revolution** depends on weak "improved" varieties that require 8–10 chemical applications per season, burning soil clean of microorganisms and making crops dependent on external nutrients
- **Pollution compounds:** Solutions to pollution create second- and third-generation problems more difficult than the original. The proposed solution to Inland Sea red tide (pumping Pacific water through a pipe across Shikoku) would require nuclear power plants, uranium processing, massive concrete — each step creating new pollution
- **Technology serves commerce, not farmers:** A manufacturer rejected Fukuoka"s simple seeding tool ($3.50 value) saying "If we made a gadget like that, the farmers might start thinking they didn"t need the tractors we sell for thousands of dollars"
