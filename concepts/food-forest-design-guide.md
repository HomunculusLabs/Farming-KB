# Food Forest Design and Layers **Updated:** 2026-04-18 A food forest (or forest garden) is a multi-layered perennial polyculture that mimics the structure and function of a natural forest while producing food, fuel, fiber, medicine, and habitat. It is one of the most productive and resilient systems in permaculture design. ## Concept Food forests emulate natural woodland ecosystems by filling every ecological niche with useful species. Instead of a single crop in neat rows, a food forest stacks multiple layers of plants from canopy to ground, creating a self-maintaining system where each element supports the others. The result is a garden that, once established, requires less maintenance than a conventional vegetable plot while producing a diverse and continuous harvest. ## The Seven Layers of a Food Forest ### 1. Canopy / Overstory Layer (30-60+ feet) Large trees that form the uppermost canopy: - Standard fruit and nut trees: walnut, pecan, chestnut, hickory, oak (for acorns) - Timber trees: black locust (also nitrogen-fixing), pine, cedar - Provide shade, wind protection, leaf litter, and habitat - In small gardens, this layer may be absent or represented by a single large tree ### 2. Understory / Sub-Canopy Layer (15-30 feet) Smaller trees below the main canopy: - Dwarf and semi-dwarf fruit trees: apple, pear, plum, cherry, peach, apricot - Nut trees: filbert/hazelnut, almond, heartnut - Useful trees: pawpaw, persimmon, mulberry, serviceberry, elderberry - This is typically the primary food-producing layer ### 3. Shrub Layer (3-15 feet) Woody plants filling the space between trees: - Berry bushes: blueberry, raspberry, blackberry, currant, gooseberry, elderberry - Ornamental/functional shrubs: seaberry (nitrogen fixer), goumi, Nanking cherry - Bamboo (for shoots, poles, windbreaks) - Provides food, wildlife habitat, and wind protection ### 4. Herbaceous Layer (ground level to 3 feet) Non-woody plants: - Perennial vegetables: asparagus, rhubarb, globe artichoke, Good King Henry, sorrel - Culinary herbs: oregano, thyme, sage, mint, chives, fennel - Medicinal herbs: echinacea, yarrow, comfrey, valerian - Insectary plants: dill, yarrow, bee balm, calendula ### 5. Ground Cover / Rhizosphere Layer Low-growing plants that protect the soil surface: - Creeping plants: clover, creeping thyme, vinca, wintergreen - Strawberries, lingonberries, cranberries - Sweet woodruff, ajuga, bishop's weed - Suppress weeds, retain moisture, build soil organic matter ### 6. Root Layer Edible and useful roots in the soil: - Root crops: Jerusalem artichoke, horseradish, Chinese artichoke (crosne) - Dynamic accumulators with deep taproots: comfrey, dandelion, chicory, burdock - These draw nutrients from deep soil layers and make them available to shallow-rooted plants ### 7. Vine Layer Climbing plants using vertical space: - Edible vines: kiwi, grape, hardy kiwi, passionflower, hops - Bean vines (runner beans, perennial beans) - Cucurbits: squash, melons, cucumbers (annual vines) - Trained onto trees, trellises, or arbors ## Additional Layers ### Mycelial / Fungal Network - Mushrooms and fungi throughout all layers - Decompose organic matter, cycle nutrients, connect plant root systems - Edible species: shiitake, oyster, wine cap, chanterelle (in appropriate conditions) ### Aquatic Layer (if water features present) - Duckweed, watercress, water chestnut - Edible and ornamental aquatic plants in ponds or swales ## Design Principles ### Succession - Start with pioneer species that prepare the soil (nitrogen fixers, fast-growers) - Gradually replace with longer-lived productive species as the system matures - A food forest takes 5-15+ years to reach full productivity ### Pattern and Placement - Use a keyhole or mandala pattern for access to dense plantings - Place the most frequently harvested plants closest to pathways - Consider sun angles and shade patterns through the seasons - Plant tall species to the north (in northern hemisphere), short species to the south ### Species Selection - Choose species suited to your climate, soil, and microclimate - Select disease-resistant varieties when possible - Include nitrogen-fixing species throughout all layers - Prioritize perennial edibles over annuals for long-term productivity ### Edge Effect - Maximize edges between different plant communities - Edges are the most productive zones in a food forest - Use meandering paths and varied plant groupings to increase edge length ## Establishment 1. **Design phase:** Map existing conditions, sun patterns, water flow, soil types 2. **Earthworks:** Install swales, ponds, and access paths first 3. **Plant canopy trees:** These take longest to establish 4. **Install nitrogen fixers:** Throughout all layers to build soil 5. **Add shrubs and herbaceous layers:** Fill in from understory to ground 6. **Sheet mulch:** Suppress weeds and build soil before planting 7. **Integrate animals:** Chickens, ducks, or geese for pest control and fertility 8. **Observe and adapt:** Adjust species placement as the system evolves ## Maintenance - Annual pruning of fruit trees and shrubs - Chop-and-drop mulching with comfrey and other biomass plants - Harvesting (which also functions as pruning and thinning) - Occasional replanting of failed individuals - Path maintenance and weed control at edges - Watering during establishment (reduced as system matures) ## See Also - [[food-forest-design]]
