## European Distribution System Critique

updated: 2026-04-18

Masanobu Fukuoka observed a striking example of how the distribution
system exploits both farmers and consumers while traveling through Europe
in the 1980s. He witnessed this directly while near Venice, reading
contradictory reports in Austrian and Italian newspapers about fruit
prices.

## The Price Manipulation Mechanism

The Viennese paper reported that fruit in Vienna was extremely expensive
because Italian farmers were limiting production. Yet when Fukuoka had
crossed into Italy earlier, he saw heaps of ripe cherries and pears
picked and left to rot in the orchards. The Italian farmers told him
they had been instructed to dump their fruit because prices in the
cities were too low.

The middlemen, positioned at the core of the distribution system,
manipulate both sides simultaneously. They tell consumers in the cities
that produce is expensive because farmers do not grow enough. At the
same time, they tell farmers that fruit is not selling well in the
towns and instruct them to produce and ship only high-grade products.

The result: consumers in cities eat expensive fruit while farmers sell
their produce at low prices. This is the tragedy of a system where
merchants exist solely to buy small quantities of high-grade products
cheaply and sell them dearly.

## Violence Born of Misinformation

While Fukuoka was still in Europe, he heard news of how a truck carrying
Italian wine into France was attacked by French farmers. This violence
was stirred by misinformation spread through the distribution system.

The loss of regional food varieties — each adapted to local conditions and cultural preferences — represented to Fukuoka a deeper impoverishment than any economic measure could capture. A food system that can deliver the same standardized product across a continent has, in his view, sacrificed the ecological and cultural intelligence encoded in local food traditions.

Transportation itself was a target: moving food thousands of miles required refrigeration, packaging, fuel, and infrastructure — all of which added costs borne not by the merchants but by the environment and by communities whose local food economies were undermined. Fukuoka noted that Italian farmers growing produce for northern European markets were caught in a cost squeeze: they had to invest in packing, cooling, and shipping while receiving prices dictated by distant buyers who had no relationship with the land or the producers.

## Fukuoka's Proposed Alternatives

Fukuoka's alternative was not a policy reform but a structural transformation: communities should grow their own food, or obtain it from nearby farms with which they have direct relationships. He envisioned a decentralized food system where the distance between producer and consumer is measured in walking or cycling distance rather than shipping routes. This was not nostalgia for pre-industrial life but a practical observation: when food is grown and eaten locally, the middlemen disappear, quality improves, waste decreases, and the ecological cost of distribution approaches zero.
