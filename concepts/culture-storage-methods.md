# Culture Storage Methods

Proper storage of [[brassinosteroids-in-fungal-cultures]] is essential for preserving [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]], maintaining strain vigor, and ensuring the long-term viability of a cultivation program. Multiple storage methods exist, each with different capacity for duration, complexity, and equipment requirements. The best practice is to maintain cultures in multiple forms at multiple locations to guard against catastrophic loss.

## The Importance of Backups

Cultures represent irreplaceable genetic material — often the only copies of specific mycelium on the planet. Backups protect against:

- **Contamination** of primary cultures
- **Spillage or accidental loss**
- **Power outages, fire, or natural disaster** that could destroy years of collected and cultured fungi

Best practices for backup management:
- Store backup cultures at **multiple locations** (e.g., with friends or family in their refrigerator crisper drawers)
- Keep only the **purest forms** of each fungus in storage — if a culture is not clean enough for production, continue purifying it before committing to storage
- Use small tubes that take up minimal space for off-site storage

## Agar Plates (Short-Term)

The simplest storage method — sealed with tape or Parafilm and refrigerated. Properly stored plates can last for months, sometimes up to a year.

**Best practices:**
- Store sealed plates inside plastic ziplock bags for extra protection against drying and for cleanliness when transporting between fridge and laminar hood
- Be aware that alcohol dissolves [[permanent-marker]] labels — plan labeling accordingly
- Monitor periodically for dehydration or contamination

**Limitations:** Agar plates are the most vulnerable to drying out and the shortest-term storage option.

## Submerged Agar Cubes (Medium-Term)

A significant improvement over plain agar plates that extends viability well beyond one year:

1. Take a fully colonized agar plate and slice the agar into small cubes
2. Transfer cubes into a test tube **half filled with sterile water**
3. Ensure cubes remain fully submerged
4. Refrigerate the tube

To retrieve culture, use a sterilized tool to fish out one cube, cap the tube, and return to cold storage. The water protects and hydrates the mycelium, dramatically slowing [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]].

**Mineral oil variant:** Sterilized mineral oil can replace water with even higher success rates. However, oil must be rinsed away in sterile water before the culture can be used.

## Agar Slants (Short-to-Medium-Term)

A classic mycological storage technique that maximizes agar surface area within a test tube:

### Preparation

1. Mix agar formula with warm water
2. Fill test tubes **one-third full**
3. Place upright in a rack and sterilize for 20–30 minutes
4. After autoclaving, transfer to laminar station and **tilt the rack** to create a long sloped surface of gelled agar
5. Allow to cool and gel at the slant

### Use

- Transfer cultures by cutting a small wedge of mycelium from a purified plate
- Once adequately colonized, store tubes in the refrigerator
- Viability: up to a year, sometimes longer

**Tip:** Maintain a stock of blank, pre-poured slants ready for instant backup use, avoiding the need to fire up the sterilizer for small batches.

## Colonized Grain (Medium-to-Long-Term)

Many species store successfully for years on colonized grain — one of the most practical long-term methods:

### Method

1. Submerge colonized grain in **sterile water** in a large tube or jar
2. Refrigerate

One 50 ml centrifuge tube of colonized grain can last a year, with individual grains retrieved as needed using a sterilized tool. This saves many steps and critical time compared to reviving agar-based cultures.

**Advantages:**
- Water coating **insulates cold-sensitive species** from cold damage
- Excellent for tropical species like paddy straw (*[[volvariella-volvacea]]*)
- [[grain-spawn]] retrieved this way can be used directly to prepare liquid cultures for bulk spawning

**Mineral oil variant:** Autoclaved mineral oil can replace water for especially difficult-to-store species, providing additional protection against cold damage and dehydration.

**Optimal storage temperature for tropical species:** 50–60°F (10–16°C), which is warm enough to prevent cold death but cool enough to slow metabolic activity significantly. A root cellar or dedicated warm zone in a walk-in cooler works well.

## Freezing (Long-Term / Indefinite)

Freezing can preserve cultures almost indefinitely, but requires precise execution. Without a cryoprotectant, ice crystal formation ruptures mycelial cells.

### Cryoprotectant: Glycerol

Glycerol (glycerin) at **5% concentration** prevents cell rupture during freezing by interfering with ice crystal formation. This is chemistry, not approximate cooking — follow guidelines precisely.

### Method 1: Agar Cubes or Grain in Glycerol Solution

1. **Sterilize water** and refrigerate
2. Fill test tubes half full with chilled sterile water
3. Add glycerol to achieve **5% solution** — agitate to distribute
4. Drop in colonized agar cubes or grain
5. **Keep caps loose** to allow for expansion during freezing
6. Place tubes (upright in a rack) in a sterile autoclavable bag with filter patch, seal, and freeze
7. Once frozen, remove bag, open in front of [[laminar-flow-hood]], **screw caps tight**, seal with Parafilm
8. Place in ziplock bags and return to freezer

**Critical:** Chill the [[liquid-culture]] before adding glycerol — adding glycerol to warm culture can damage mycelium of some species.

### Method 2: Liquid Culture Slurry (Professional Standard)

1. Make a slurry of colonized agar and sterilized water
2. Transfer to liquid broth containing **10–20% of the original antibiotic/nutrient formula**
3. Agitate on a plate stirrer with a sterile magnetic stirring bar for **1–3 days** — this allows mycelium to recover from blender damage
4. **Pre-chill** the mixture (refrigerator or ice bath)
5. Add glycerol to 5% solution (e.g., 95 ml culture slurry + 5 ml glycerol)
6. Transfer to sterile storage tubes and freeze as described above

### Testing Frozen Cultures for Viability

Before committing an entire collection to frozen storage, test each species:

1. Prepare identical liquid culture sets of a single strain and freeze them
2. Remove one tube monthly, thaw in a **warm water bath (77–86°F / 25–30°C) for 2–5 minutes** to allow acclimation
3. Transfer to a fresh agar plate and incubate
4. Evaluate recovery speed and growth quality compared to the original pre-freeze culture
5. If first transfer looks unhealthy, try a second transfer to fresh agar
6. Optionally verify identity and purity by examining **[[basidiomycete-sexual-reproduction-and-clamp-connections]]** under a microscope (a small "elbow" near septa that confirms a basidiomycete and not a contaminant mold)

Continue monthly testing to determine the maximum interval each species can withstand freezing without damage.

## Cold-Sensitive Species

Species that die below 50°F (10°C) — even for as little as one week — require special storage approaches:

### Spore Storage
- Take a spore print on sterilized aluminum foil, using a disinfected metal grill to keep the cap from touching the foil
- Keep spores dry and cool — generally viable for **at least one year**
- **[[comparison-mushroom-liquid-culture-vs-spore-syringe]] method:** Flood the print with sterile water from a syringe, draw the water back, and store the spore solution under refrigeration for up to **two years**
- When needed, inoculate a single spot on an agar plate (don't swirl) to allow spores to germinate and outrun competitors

### Moderate-Temperature Grain Storage
- Prepare grain spawn using **larger grains** (e.g., milo)
- Store at **50–60°F (10–16°C)** — warm enough to prevent cold death, cool enough to slow metabolism
- Viability: up to one year
- A dedicated zone in a walk-in cooler entrance (like a root cellar) provides ideal conditions
