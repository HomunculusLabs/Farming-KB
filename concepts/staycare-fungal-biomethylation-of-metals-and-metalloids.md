# Fungal Biomethylation of Metals and Metalloids

## Overview

Biological methylation (biomethylation) of metalloids by filamentous fungi
and yeasts is a significant process in environmental biogeochemistry. This
transformation frequently results in the volatilization of metals and
metalloids, modifying their mobility and toxicity in contaminated
environments. The process has been demonstrated across diverse fungal taxa
and has practical applications in the bioremediation of contaminated land
and water. Fungi can transform metals, metalloids, and organometallic
compounds by reduction, methylation, and dealkylation, processes of both
environmental and biotechnological importance since transformation may
modify the mobility and toxicity of these elements.

## Biochemical Mechanisms

The biochemical pathway for the fungal production of trimethylarsine from
arsenite was first suggested by Challenger in 1945, establishing the
foundational understanding of metalloid biomethylation. The pathway
involves the transfer of methyl groups as carbonium ions (CH3+) mediated
by S-adenosylmethionine (SAM). This SAM-dependent transmethylation is the
primary mechanism by which [[gliocladium-roseum]]ganic metalloid species into
organometallic derivatives with altered physical and chemical properties.

## Arsenic Methylation

Arsenic has received extensive attention in fungal biomethylation research.
Several fungal species, including Gliocladium roseum, Candida humicola,
and Penicillium spp., can convert monomethylarsonic acid to
trimethylarsine. The sequential methylation pathway progresses from
arsenite through mono-, di-, and trimethylated intermediates, ultimately
producing volatile trimethylarsine gas. This volatilization process
effectively removes arsenic from the immediate environment, converting it
to a form that can be dispersed or captured. The Challenger pathway remains
the reference model for understanding arsenic biomethylation in fungi,
although additional enzymatic routes may exist.

## Selenium Methylation

Selenium biomethylation by fungi has been demonstrated for both inorganic
forms: selenite (SeO3(2-)) and selenate (SeO4(2-)). These are converted
to methyl derivatives such as dimethylselenide ((CH3)2Se) and
dimethyldiselenide ((CH3)2Se2). Inorganic forms of selenium appear to be
methylated more rapidly than organic forms, such as selenium-containing
amino acids. The mechanism for selenium methylation appears similar to
that for arsenic, involving SAM-mediated transfer of methyl groups.

Selenium methylation results in volatilization, a process that has been
used to remove selenium from the San Joaquin Valley and Kesterson
Reservoir in California. The approach involves evaporation pond management
and primary pond operation: incoming selenium-contaminated drainage water
is evaporated to dryness and the process repeated until sediment selenium
concentration approaches 100 mg/kg dry weight. The volatilization process
is then optimized using parameters such as carbon source, moisture,
temperature, and aeration until selenium concentrations fall to acceptable
limits.

## Mercury Biomethylation

Mercury biomethylation by fungal species has been reported, representing
an important pathway in the environmental cycling of this toxic metal.
The production of methylmercury compounds is of particular concern due to
their high bioaccumulation potential and neurotoxicity. While bacterial
mercury methylation has been more extensively studied, fungal species
contribute to mercury transformation in soil and sediment environments.
Understanding fungal mercury biomethylation is critical for assessing the
full scope of mercury risk in contaminated ecosystems.

## Tellurium Biomethylation

Evidence exists for the production of dimethyltelluride ((CH3)2Te) and
dimethylditelluride ((CH3)2Te2) from both tellurite (TeO3(2-)) and
tellurate (TeO4(2-)) by Penicillium spp. In Fusarium spp., volatile
tellurium production occurs over the entire growing period, although
amounts are extremely small, indicating this process may not be an
important detoxification mechanism. Tellurite reduction to elemental
tellurium(0) results in characteristic black or dark grey colonies, with
transmission electron microscopy revealing deposition of large black
granules, apparently in vacuoles, corresponding to the reduction of
tellurite to elemental tellurium.

## Reduction Transformations

In addition to methylation, fungi can reduce metalloid oxyanions, such as
selenite and tellurite, to amorphous elemental selenium or tellurium
respectively. This reduction results in immobilization and
detoxification, providing a complementary mechanism to volatilization.
The dual capacity for both reductive immobilization and methylative
volatilization gives fungi versatile strategies for metalloid
detoxification. Reduction of uranium(VI) to uranium(IV) has also been
demonstrated in fungal systems, contributing to uranium immobilization.

## Environmental Significance

Methylated metalloid derivatives are frequently volatile and less toxic
than their inorganic precursors, making biomethylation an attractive
bioremediation strategy. The ability of fungi to transform metals and
metalloids has been utilized in the bioremediation of contaminated land
and water. Selenium methylation through volatilization has proven
particularly effective, with documented field applications demonstrating
significant selenium removal from contaminated sites.

## Comparison with Bacterial Biomethylation

Bacteria also perform biomethylation of metalloids, and in many cases the
biochemical pathways are similar to those in fungi. However, fungal
biomethylation may offer advantages in certain environments due to the
robust nature of fungal mycelia, their tolerance to extreme conditions,
and their ability to grow in low-nutrient environments. The filamentous
growth form of fungi allows them to explore large volumes of soil,
potentially accessing metalloid contaminants that are not available to
single-celled organisms.

## Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

There are few detailed studies on fungal biomethylation of metals and
metalloids beyond arsenic, selenium, mercury, and tellurium. The full
range of elements susceptible to fungal biomethylation remains to be
characterized. The ecological significance of fungal biomethylation in

rates of methylation under field conditions and the fate of volatile
organometallic products after release into the atmosphere.

## Practical Applications

Fungal biomethylation can be integrated into [[bioremediation-strategies]]
for sites contaminated with metalloids. The volatilization approach is
most developed for selenium, where pond-based systems have demonstrated
effective selenium removal. For other metalloids, optimization of
environmental conditions (carbon source, moisture, temperature, and
aeration) can enhance biomethylation rates. Future research directions
include the identification of novel fungal species with enhanced
methylation capabilities and the development of genetically improved
strains for bioremediation applications.
