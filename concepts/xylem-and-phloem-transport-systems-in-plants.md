## Xylem and Phloem Transport Systems in Plants

Plants have two parallel vascular transport systems that work in fundamentally different ways. The xylem moves water and dissolved nutrients upward (unidirectional), while the phloem transports sugars, amino acids, and [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

### Tree Rings

As some plants grow, the oldest xylem no longer functions. New xylem cells at the pericycle replace it. Layers of old xylem make the rings in trees. Ring width varies because large xylem cells form in wet spring conditions and thinner tubes develop as temperatures rise and transpiration increases.

### Loading the Xylem

Before water and dissolved nutrients can enter xylem vessels, they must be "loaded" from the symplastic pathway into the dead xylem. This happens through xylem parenchyma cells (living cells next to the tracheids and vessels), whose membranes are full of aquaporins, ion carriers, ion channels, and ion pumps.

The journey: root hair -> epidermis -> cortex -> endodermis (Casparian strip forces symplastic pathway) -> near xylem -> diffuses into apoplastic space -> enters dead xylem vessels -> pulled upward by transpiration.

## The Phloem: The Plant's Distribution Network

### Structure

Phloem sieve tubes are atypical cells: they lack nuclei, vacuoles, and plastids, with only limited mitochondria. This minimal internal structure allows sap to flow more freely. Each sieve tube has:

- **Sieve plates**: Perforated end walls connecting individual tubes. When a plant is damaged, these become clogged with proteins (P-proteins), blocking the damaged portion to prevent sap loss.
- **Companion cell**: Produced by the same mother cell as the sieve tube, the companion cell has a full complement of organelles. It controls the activities of the sieve tube through numerous plasmodesmata connections. It has many mitochondria to produce energy for both cells.
- **Storage cells**: Associated with phloem throughout the plant, extending from leaf veins to roots.

### Source-to-Sink Transport

Phloem flow is bidirectional and driven by pressure:

1. **Source**: An area of high sugar concentration (leaves in summer, roots in spring). Sugars are actively loaded into phloem, creating osmotic pressure.
