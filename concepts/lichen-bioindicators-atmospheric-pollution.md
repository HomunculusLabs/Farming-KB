## Lichen Bioindicators and Fungal Heavy Metal Interactions

## Overview

Fungi occupy a central role in controlling pollutant dynamics in terrestrial
ecosystems. Lichens serve as sensitive bioindicators of atmospheric pollution
(SO₂ and O₃), while free-living fungi mediate heavy metal cycling through
biosorption, bioaccumulation, and biotransformation.

## Lichens as Bioindicators of Sulfur Dioxide Pollution

### Species-Specific SO₂ Tolerance

Different lichen species exhibit markedly different tolerances to atmospheric
SO₂, making community composition a reliable indicator of ambient
concentrations. Tolerance spans more than an order of magnitude:

- **[[lecanora-conizaeoides]]** — most tolerant, surviving at up to
  **150 mg/m³** SO₂. Became dominant in industrial Europe as sensitive
  species were eliminated.
- **[[hypogymnia-physodes]]** — moderately tolerant, persisting at intermediate
  SO₂ levels.
- **Ramalina fastigiata** — least tolerant, declining at concentrations as
  low as **10 mg/m³** SO₂.

This gradient allows construction of lichen diversity scales correlating with
measured SO₂, providing cost-effective biological monitoring.

### Substrate Effects on Sensitivity

Bark substrate significantly modifies lichen SO₂ sensitivity. Key factors
include bark pH, water-holding capacity, and nutrient content across host
trees: **oak** (*Quercus*) offers intermediate buffering; **poplar**
(*Populus*) has higher pH and nutrient-rich bark; **elm** (*Ulmus*) supports
distinct assemblages. The same species may show different tolerance thresholds
depending on the tree it colonizes.

### Cyanobacterial Lichens: Differential Sensitivity

Cyanolichens display a distinct pollution sensitivity pattern:
- **Acid rain** — highly sensitive; nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterial partners
  (e.g., *Nostoc*) are inhibited by low pH, and gelatinous sheaths are more
  permeable to acidic solutions.
- **Heavy metals** — more tolerant than green-algal lichens; extracellular
  polysaccharide sheaths chelate metal ions, providing protection.

This dual profile makes cyanolichens useful discriminators between areas
dominated by acid rain versus metal deposition.

## Lichens as Bioindicators of Ozone Pollution

Ground-level ozone damages lichen communities through discoloration, reduced
photosynthetic efficiency, and thallus necrosis. A hierarchy of sensitivity
exists, with foliose and fruticose forms generally more susceptible than
crustose forms, enabling ozone-specific bioindicator scales.

## Recovery of Lichen Communities After Pollution Controls

In the greater London area, long-term monitoring after SO₂ emission reductions
has documented:
- Recolonization of previously lichen-desert zones by sensitive species.
- Successional progression from *Lecanora conizaeoides*-dominated communities
  toward diverse assemblages including fruticose and foliose species.
- Gradual increase in species richness and cover over decades.

This recovery validates biological monitoring as a long-term air quality
assessment strategy.

## Fungi and Heavy Metal Dynamics

### Biosorption by Fungal Mycelium

Both live and dead fungal mycelium adsorb heavy metals from solution.
*Rhizopus arrhizus* is an extensively studied biosorbent demonstrating uptake
of **Cu, Ag, Zn, U, Co, and Th**. Dead mycelium retains substantial capacity,
indicating primarily physicochemical cell wall binding rather than metabolic
dependence — making fungal biomass practical for effluent treatment.

### Heavy Metal Effects on Saprotrophy and Decomposition

Metal contamination impairs fungal decomposition:
- **Differential sensitivity** — fungi are more sensitive than bacteria,
  shifting communities toward bacterial dominance.
- **Enzyme inhibition** — metals suppress cellulases, lignin-degrading enzymes
  (laccase, peroxidase), and phosphatases by binding active sites or disrupting
  protein structure.
- **Reduced decomposition** — lower fungal biomass and enzyme activity cause
  measurable reductions in decomposition rates, leading to litter accumulation
  and altered nutrient cycling.

### Metal Accumulation in Basidiomycete Fruit Bodies

Basidiomycetes concentrate metals in fruit bodies far exceeding substrate
levels:
- **Agaricus species** — accumulate Cd, Hg, and Pb, raising food safety
  concerns for wild-harvested mushrooms from contaminated areas.
- **Armillaria rhizomorphs** — melanized shoestring structures concentrate
  **Al, Zn, Fe, Cu, and Pb** during translocation, with concentrations
  exceeding surrounding soil levels.

### Arsenic Transformation in Fungi

Fungi transform inorganic arsenic through biomethylation:
- Inorganic As(III)/As(V) is methylated to **mono-methylarsonic acid (MMA)**
  and **dimethylarsinic acid (DMA)**, generally reducing toxicity and
  increasing mobility.
- **Arsenobetaine** — a non-toxic organoarsenical discovered in various
  fungal species, indicating more complex arsenic metabolism than previously
  appreciated.
- Fungal methylation contributes to the terrestrial arsenic cycle, influencing
  speciation and trophic transfer in food webs.
