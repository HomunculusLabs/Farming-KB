# Fukuoka Principle of Non-Causality in Nature

[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s principle of non-causality holds that [[fukuoka-nature-no-cause-and-effect]] do not exist in nature. Nature is a unified whole — a circle or sphere without beginning or end — and any attempt to isolate a single cause from an infinite web of interrelationships is fundamentally misguided. This principle emerges from Fukuoka's broader [[cho-natural-farming-philosophy]].

## The Limits of Analytical Knowledge

The [[fukuoka-limits-scientific-method-agriculture]] proceeds by a familiar sequence: observe, hypothesize, experiment, and accept the result as truth. This process has yielded extraordinary technological achievements, and its authority is rarely questioned in modern society.

Yet this entire process begins with discrimination — the act of breaking down, separating, and analyzing. Because the starting point is one of fragmentation, the truths arrived at can never be absolute and universal. The method itself guarantees that its conclusions will be partial.

Scientific knowledge is, by its very nature, fragmented and incomplete. It isolates one variable at a time, holds others constant, and draws conclusions about the behavior of a system from the behavior of its parts. No amount of incomplete knowledge, assembled together, can ever form a complete whole. The gaps between fragments remain gaps, no matter how many fragments are collected.

Man believes that dissection enables broad generalizations about how nature works. In reality, dissection breaks nature into ever-smaller fragments, reducing wholeness to greater imperfection. The more finely something is divided for study, the further it drifts from the truth of the living whole. Each act of separation severs connections that were essential to the thing being studied.

Fukuoka illustrates this with the example of soil analysis. A scientist divides a soil sample into its chemical components — nitrogen (N), potassium (K), phosphorus (P), calcium (Ca), manganese (Mn) — studies their absorption pathways, grows wheat in pots under [[query-how-to-fix-magnesium-deficiency-in-cannabis]], and supplements it. This he calls "scientific truth." The experiment is repeatable, the results are measurable, and the conclusion seems solid.

But was the lack a true deficiency? On what basis is that judgment made? The wheat was grown in a pot — an artificial environment bearing little resemblance to a living field. Science treats symptoms first: "If there is bleeding, it stops the bleeding." The deeper question of why the imbalance arose in the first place goes unasked. The symptom is corrected while the condition that produced it remains untouched.

Behind every identified cause, there is a second cause, a third cause, a main cause, a fundamental cause, an underlying cause, and countless [[allegro-biblical-references-to-mushrooms-and-fungi]] break the sawdust down further into cellulose and lignin
- The soil arrives at a state of equilibrium — neither acidic nor basic

The entire system self-regulates through a succession of microbial activity. Each stage creates the conditions for the next stage, and the final result is balance. No intervention was required. The experts' prediction was not wrong exactly — organic acids were indeed produced and acidity did rise — but it was radically incomplete. They saw one moment in a dynamic process and mistook it for the whole story.

"The decision to counteract acidity by applying lime only addresses the situation at a particular moment in time." The human intervention is based on a snapshot of a living process, mistaking a transient state for a permanent condition. The expert sees acidity and prescribes lime, unaware that the acidity is already on its way to resolving itself through the very processes that produced it.

"Nonintervention is the wisest course of action."

The soil teaches patience. It teaches that what appears to be a problem at one moment may be the beginning of a solution at the next. The farmer who intervenes interrupts a process he does not understand. The farmer who waits allows understanding to reveal itself — or, more precisely, allows the system to resolve itself without the need for understanding at all.

## Rice Blast Disease and the Infinite Chain of Causality

Rice blast disease provides an even deeper illustration of the infinite chain of causality. The common belief is that rice blast is caused by bacteria, and the conventional response is to dispel those bacteria with copper or mercury agents. The treatment is aggressive and effective in its narrow aim — the bacteria are killed — but the disease returns, season after season, because the conditions that produced susceptibility have not changed.

In reality, rice blast emerges from many interrelated factors: high temperature, heavy rainfall, excess nitrogen in the soil, flooded paddy conditions during high temperatures that weaken roots, and the use of low-resistance varieties. The disease is not the result of a single pathogen but a convergence of conditions within a living system. Remove one factor and the others remain; address one cause and another takes its place.

Even if one identifies excess nitrogen as a contributing factor, how does it relate to bacterial attack? The relationship is far from straightforward:

- If sufficient sunlight is available, photosynthesis speeds up, nitrogen is assimilated as protein, and the plant remains healthy
- If conditions are cloudy or planting is too dense, insufficient light and [[carbon-dioxide-retention-jar-lid-mushroom-cultivation]] slow photosynthesis, excess nitrogen remains unassimilated, and susceptibility increases

So excess nitrogen may or may not be the cause. It could equally be insufficient sunlight, insufficient carbon dioxide, or insufficient starch in the leaves. The nitrogen is only a problem in certain conditions — conditions that themselves have causes, which have further causes, extending back without end.

To understand these relationships fully, one would need to understand photosynthesis completely — a process that science has not fully unlocked despite decades of research. And photosynthesis itself depends on soil conditions, weather, [[fukuoka-microbial-ecology-decomposition]], and the health of the entire plant, each of which depends on further conditions still. The web of interdependence has no edge.

Rotting roots contribute to susceptibility, but scientists cannot convincingly explain why roots rot in the first place. Sometimes the problem is attributed to a "weak strain," yet no one can define what "weak" truly means in the context of the plant's total environment. The term is a label, not an explanation — a way of hiding ignorance behind a word.

"We gradually fail to understand even those causes that appeared clear at first, and completely lose sight of the true cause."

## The Rice Plant's Perspective

Fukuoka asks us to consider rice blast from the rice plant's own standpoint. Rice blast may not be a disease at all in the conventional sense. It may be a protective mechanism — a means of halting excessive growth and restoring balance between the surface growth of the plant and its underground root system.

It may be nature's way of preventing overly dense growth, thereby aiding photosynthesis and assuring full seed production. What humans label a "disease" may serve an essential function within the living system — a regulatory process that maintains the health of the whole at the expense of a part.

"Rice blast disease is not the final effect, but merely one stage in the constant flux of nature. It is both a cause as well as an effect."

This reframing dissolves the linear cause-and-effect model entirely. The disease is not an endpoint produced by prior causes. It is a moment within a continuous process — simultaneously shaped by conditions and shaping conditions in return. To call it an "effect" of something is to freeze a dynamic reality into a static category. To call it a "cause" of something else is equally reductive. It simply is — a movement within the whole.

## The Principle of Non-Causality

Fukuoka's principle of non-causality can be stated directly:

"When there is no front or back, no beginning or end, but only what resembles a circle or sphere, one could say that there is unity of cause and effect, but one could just as well claim that cause and effect do not exist."

"This is my principle of non-causality."

Nature is not a chain of events. It is a sphere — whole, self-contained, without direction or hierarchy. To impose linear causality on such a system is to impose a human abstraction on a reality that does not recognize it. The circle has no starting point; every point is simultaneously origin and destination. One could say that cause and effect are unified in the circle, but that formulation still preserves the concepts. Fukuoka goes further: the concepts themselves do not apply.

"To science, which examines this wheel of causality in parts and at close quarters, cause and effect exist." The scientific method, by its very nature, isolates variables and searches for discrete causes. Within that narrow frame, causality appears real and actionable. "To the scientific mind, there most certainly is a way to combat rice blast bacteria."

But this is an illusion born of limited perspective. The closer you look at a fragment, the more real the fragment appears — and the less real the whole becomes. Science sees the spoke of the wheel and calls it a line. It does not see that the spoke is part of a circle, and that the circle has no beginning.

"Yet when man perceives rice disease as a nuisance and takes the scientific approach of controlling it with a powerful bactericide, he proceeds from his first error that causality exists to subsequent errors."

Each intervention, based on the false premise that a single cause has been identified and can be corrected, generates new disturbances. The bactericide kills bacteria but also disrupts soil ecology, weakens the plant's natural defenses, and creates conditions for future problems.

"From his futile efforts he incurs further toil and misery."

Fukuoka's conclusion is radical and uncompromising:

"Nature has neither beginning nor end, before nor after, cause nor effect. Causality does not exist."

This is not a statement about the limits of human knowledge — as if cause and effect exist but we simply cannot trace them. It is a statement about the nature of reality itself. Causality is not hidden; it is absent. The circle is not a chain that has been bent into a loop — it was never a chain at all.

## Implications

The principle of non-causality has profound implications for agriculture, ecology, and human action more broadly. If cause and effect do not exist in nature, then the entire project of controlling nature through intervention is built on a false foundation. The wise course is not to identify and correct supposed causes but to align oneself with the natural order — to practice nonintervention and allow nature's self-regulating processes to unfold.

This does not mean doing nothing. It means acting in harmony with nature's wholeness rather than against its perceived fragments. It means recognizing that every problem identified through analysis is itself an artifact of analysis, and that the truest response to any natural phenomenon is trust in the integrity of the whole.

The principle of non-causality is, finally, an invitation — to see nature as it is, whole and undivided, rather than as we have made it in our efforts to control it.
