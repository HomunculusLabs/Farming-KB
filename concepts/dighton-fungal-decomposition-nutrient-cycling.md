# Fungal Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling

## Overview

Fungal decomposition is a central ecosystem process through which organic matter is broken down and nutrients are recycled back into bioavailable forms. Saprotrophic fungi are the primary decomposers of recalcitrant plant litter, particularly lignin and cellulose, owing to their extracellular enzymatic capabilities. This process is not isolated -- fungi interact with soil fauna, bacteria, and actinomycetes in a complex decomposer community (Dighton, 1997).

## Extracellular Enzymatic Degradation

Fungi secrete a suite of extracellular enzymes that enable them to degrade complex plant polymers:

- **Cellulases** break down cellulose into glucose units
- **Ligninases** (laccases, peroxidases) degrade lignin -- a capacity largely restricted to basidiomycete white-rot fungi
- **Phosphatases** release phosphate from [[mycoremediation-white-rot-fungi-lignin-degradation-enzymes]].

## Nutrient Immobilization and C:N Dynamics

During decomposition, the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of litter declines from approximately 200:1 to 30:1 (Frankland, 1992). A period of **nutrient immobilization** occurs when decomposer fungi sequester nitrogen into their biomass. The duration of this immobilization period equals the fungal turnover time.

Basidiomycetes have a greater capacity for nutrient accumulation than bacteria due to:
- Longer lifespan
- Larger individual biomass
- Greater [[bloomfield-stinkhorns-phallus-impudicus-osmotic-fruiting]]* translocates **13%** of added phosphorus
- Maximum P translocation rate: **7,225 nmol P/cm²/day**
- Translocation possible over **75 cm** between resource patches (Wells and Boddy, 1990, 1995a)

Cords only form in unsterile soil -- the triggering stimulus is derived from other soil organisms, suggesting an ecological signaling mechanism.

## Ecosystem Significance

Fungal decomposition mediates:
- **Carbon flux** from litter to soil organic matter and atmospheric CO₂
- **Nutrient mineralization** making N, P, and other elements plant-available
- **Soil organic matter formation** through the accumulation of fungal necromass
- **Rock weathering** through organic acid secretion, contributing to long-term soil development
