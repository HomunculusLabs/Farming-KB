# Philosophy of Mu and the Buddhist Roots of Natural Farming

Masanobu Fukuoka explicitly identified natural farming as "a Buddhist way of farming that originates in the philosophy of Mu, or nothingness, and returns to a do-nothing nature." The concept of Mu — a
term drawn from Zen Buddhist tradition meaning emptiness, nothingness, or the negation of dualistic thinking — is not merely a decorative metaphor in Fukuoka's work but the philosophical foundation
upon which his entire agricultural method rests. Understanding Mu is essential to understanding why Fukuoka rejected not only chemical agriculture but also organic farming, scientific research, and
even the ordinary exercise of human intellect as guides to proper land stewardship.

## The Moment of Awakening

Fukuoka described the origin of his method in a single moment of insight. As a young man working as a plant pathologist at the Yokohama Customs Bureau research laboratory, he had been grappling with
the meaning of life and humanity. One night, "the truth came to me in a flash." He saw that "nature is an astounding thing that cannot be named." In that instant, he understood the principle of
nothingness, of Mu.

This experience bore practical fruit. It led him to the conviction that man should live only in accordance with nature and has no need to do anything. Yet it also produced a profound personal crisis.
He found that one person cannot live alone — one either lives in association with people or in communion with nature. He discovered to his despair that people were no longer truly human, and nature no
longer truly natural. The noble road that rises above the world of relativity was too steep for him.

## Mu as Agricultural Philosophy

Fukuoka traced the concept of Mu to the traditional farming village. He observed that farmers of old possessed an intuitive understanding of life and death that freed them from anguish and grief. They
had no need for learning. They joked that agonizing over life and death and wandering through ideological thickets in search of truth were the pastimes of idle city youth. Farmers preferred to live
common lives without knowledge or learning.

This did not mean the farming village was without philosophy. On the contrary, it had a very important philosophy, embodied in the principle that "philosophy is unnecessary." The farming village was a
society of philosophers without a need for philosophy. It was none other than the philosophy of Mu — which teaches that all is unnecessary — that gave the farmer his enduring strength.

Fukuoka explicitly connected this to the Taoist vision of Lao-tzu's villages, whose inhabitants were unaware that the Great Way of man lay in living independently and self-sufficiently, yet they knew
this in their hearts. To the remark that "any fool can farm," Fukuoka had the farmers reply: "A fool cannot be a true farmer." There was no need for philosophy in the farming village. It was the urban
intellectual who pondered human existence, who went in search of truth and questioned the purpose of life. The farmer never learned to question his existence because life was never so empty and void
as to bring him to contemplate it.

## Discriminating vs. Nondiscriminating Knowledge

At the core of Fukuoka's philosophy is the distinction between two modes of understanding: discriminating knowledge and nondiscriminating knowledge. Discriminating knowledge is the analytical,
categorizing, reductionist mode of thought that characterizes Western science. It breaks nature into components, classifies organisms, isolates variables, and draws causal conclusions. Through this
lens, nature appears as a collection of separate objects to be studied and manipulated.

Nondiscriminating knowledge, by contrast, is the intuitive, holistic mode of perception that grasps nature as an indivisible whole. It does not analyze or classify; it simply sees. Fukuoka argued that
"nature as perceived by man through discriminating knowledge is a falsehood." Man can never truly know even a single leaf or a single handful of earth. Unable to fully comprehend plant life and soil,
he sees these only through the filter of human intellect.

Although man may seek to return to the bosom of nature or use it to his advantage, he only touches one tiny part of nature — a dead portion at that — and has no affinity with the main body of living
nature. He is, in effect, merely toying with delusions. Man is but an arrogant fool who vainly believes that he knows all of nature and can achieve anything he sets his mind to. Seeing neither the
logic nor order inherent in nature, he has selfishly appropriated it to his own ends and destroyed it.

## The Critique of Scientific Agriculture

Fukuoka's philosophical position generated a comprehensive critique of scientific agriculture. The scientist envisions nature as something that can be understood and improved through human
intervention. He analyzes soil chemistry, identifies nutrient requirements, develops targeted fertilizers, breeds resistant varieties, and designs pest control strategies. Each step appears rational
and progressive, yet the cumulative effect, in Fukuoka's view, is destruction.

The problem is not that science fails to produce results within its narrow framework — it demonstrably does. The problem is that these results are local, temporary, and achieved at the cost of
disrupting the larger natural order that science cannot perceive. More than seventy percent of the "big three" chemical fertilizers is concentrated sulfuric acid, which acidifies the soil, suppresses
and kills soil microorganisms, and disrupts the soil in ways that may one day spell catastrophe for Japanese agriculture. Some 1.8 million tons of sulfuric acid are dumped onto Japanese farmlands each
year in the form of fertilizer.

Fukuoka did not claim that fertilizers are totally worthless, but rather that there is no need to apply chemical fertilizers. Scientific technology for applying fertilizers is basically pointless for
the same reason. He included organic composting in this critique: the composting of organic materials is "fundamentally useless" — all the trouble taken during preparation accelerates decomposition by
at most ten to twenty percent.

## "Do-Nothing" as Active Non-Intervention

The concept of "do-nothing" farming requires careful interpretation. Fukuoka did not advocate literal inaction or laziness. He practiced what he called "do-nothing" farming for over thirty years and
consistently harvested close to 22 bushels each of winter grain and rice per quarter-acre — better-than-average yields.

"Do-nothing" means eliminating unnecessary practices, expenditures, and labor. Fukuoka's approach was the inverse of the scientific method: where science adds techniques, he subtracted them. He told
himself, "I don't need to do this, I don't need to do that." After thirty years of this process of elimination, he had reduced his labor to essentially just sowing seed and spreading straw. Human
effort is unnecessary because nature, not man, grows the rice and wheat.

Every time someone says "this is useful" or "that has value" or "one ought to do such-and-such," it is because man has created the preconditions that give the whatever-it-is its value. Humans create
situations in which, without something they never needed in the first place, they are lost. And to escape this predicament, they make what appear to be new discoveries, which they then herald as
progress.

## The Illusion of Cause and Effect

Fukuoka's most radical philosophical claim concerns the nature of causality. He argued that "in nature, there is no cause and effect." This does not mean that events are random; it means that the
relationships between events in nature are so complex, so interwoven, and so far beyond human comprehension that the very concept of linear cause and effect is a fiction imposed by the discriminating
mind.

When scientists try to determine why deserts spread, for example, they trace causes back through an endless chain of interwoven events and factors that is beyond man's powers of comprehension. Even if
they identified the first plant to die in a desertifying landscape, they still could not determine whether to begin by planting the first type of vegetation to disappear or the last to survive. The
reason: in nature, there is no cause and effect.

This philosophical position has direct agricultural implications. If there is no simple cause-and-effect relationship between, say, fertilizer application and crop yield, then the entire edifice of
agronomic science rests on a false premise. Fukuoka did not deny that applying fertilizer makes crops grow taller; he denied that this growth represents genuine improvement or that the causal
relationship is what scientists claim it to be.

## The Village Philosophy and Its Disappearance

Fukuoka mourned the disappearance of the traditional farming village culture that embodied the philosophy of Mu. Not long ago, one could hear the woodsman sing a woodcutter's song, voices roll over
the paddy fields during transplanting, and drums surge through the village after the fall harvest. Over the past twenty years, chain saws replaced hand saws, mechanical plows and transplanters raced
over fields, and vegetables were grown in vinyl houses ranged like factories.

The disappearance of the sunken hearth from farming homes extinguished the light of ancient farming village culture. Fireside discussions vanished, and with them, the village philosophy. Fukuoka saw
this as a fall from a true way of life to one that is false — people rushing about in a frenzy to shorten time and widen space, and in so doing losing both. The farmer may have thought modern
developments would make his job easier, but they freed him from the land and now he works harder than ever at other jobs, wearing away his body and mind.

## Toward a "Do-Nothing" Age

Fukuoka envisioned a future age of consolidation and convergence, in which humanity would establish a new way of life and a spiritual culture founded on communion with nature. To achieve a humanity
and society founded on non-action, man must look back over the past and rediscover the truth that was lost. The young people living in his orchard carried with them the hope of someday resolving the
great problems of the world that cannot be solved by science and reason — "mere dreams perhaps, but these hold the key to the future."

Natural farming is based on a nature free of human meddling and intervention. It strives to restore nature from the destruction wrought by human knowledge and action, and to resurrect a humanity
divorced from God. All begins by relinquishing human knowledge.
