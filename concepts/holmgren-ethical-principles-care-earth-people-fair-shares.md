# Holmgren Ethical Principles: Care for Earth, People, and Fair Shares

## Ethics as Design Constraints

Ethics are the moral principles guiding action toward good outcomes and away from
bad ones. In Holmgren's framework, ethics act as constraints on survival instincts
and self-interest that drive human behavior. They are culturally evolved mechanisms
for more enlightened self-interest — a more inclusive view of who and what
constitutes "us" and a longer-term understanding of consequences.

The greater the power of civilization (due to energy availability) and the greater
the concentration of power within society, the more critical ethics become in
ensuring long-term cultural and biological survival. This ecologically functional
view makes ethics central to developing a culture for energy descent.

## The Three Ethical Principles

Since the development of the [[permaculture-design]] Course, three broad maxims have
been taught as the ethical foundation:

1. **Care for the Earth**
2. **Care for People**
3. **Set limits to consumption and reproduction, and redistribute surplus**

These were distilled from research into community ethics adopted by older religious
and cooperative groups. The third principle — and even the second — can be seen as
derived from the first. Holmgren notes that these principles are common to all
indigenous tribal peoples and reflect cultures that have existed in relative
balance with their environments for far longer than recent experiments in
civilization.

## Philosophical Context

Holmgren locates permaculture ethics within a broader philosophical landscape
spanning reductionist science, systems theory, dialectical materialism, and
spiritual traditions. He argues that many values held precious in human thinking
developed under energetically novel conditions of recent centuries — conditions
likely to be dysfunctional in a world of limited and declining energy.

The philosophy of individualism, for example, should be seen as an outcome of
material well-being rather than its source. Beliefs and values that developed with
a rising energy base are likely to be destructive in a declining-energy world.

Permaculture is described as integrated design science — applied science
concerned with improving long-term material well-being through wholistic
integration of utilitarian values, using an ecological perspective that sees a
broader canvas of utility than reductionist econometric perspectives.

## Care for the Earth: Multiple Scales

### Global: The Gaia Hypothesis
The Gaia hypothesis of James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis provides a whole-system
science showing Earth as a self-organized system. Four billion years of
evolutionary history demonstrate that if humanity seriously affects fundamental
life-support systems, we will be "neutralized" by co-evolutionary mechanisms such
as climate change or disease. Care for the Earth is not merely ethical restraint
but self-preservation.

### Grounded: Living Soil
In the most practical sense, Care for the Earth means caring for living soil as
the source of terrestrial life. The state of soils is the best measure of future
societal health and well-being. This builds on the scientific and ethical
traditions of the organic agriculture movements. How to care for soil remains
controversial, but careless use can rapidly destroy its capacity to support life.

### Local: Stewardship of Place
At the scale of the horizon, Care for the Earth means looking after home, place,
or territory — as understood by indigenous cultures and through bioregionalism.
This acknowledges individual and collective responsibility for particular natural
resources about which we have some understanding and power.

Wendell Berry's critique of planetary stewardship is relevant here: the notion
of managing the entire planet reflects arrogant disconnection from nature and
belief in our own power, rather than genuine care.

## Permaculture as Design Science

Bill Mollison described permaculture as integrated design science. This places it
firmly within the culture of science while drawing together strategies from modern
and traditional cultures. Permaculture seeks wholistic integration of utilitarian
values through an ecological perspective that sees a much broader canvas of utility
than the reductionist econometric perspectives dominating modern society.

## Spiritual Dimensions

Although permaculture can be seen as essentially materialist and scientific, it
depends on an ecological perspective that has universal spiritual dimensions.
Holmgren notes that the more we understand the world through systems thinking
and ecology, the more we see wisdom in spiritual perspectives and traditions.

The union of materialism and spirituality is occurring along both creative and
destructive pathways. Holmgren places permaculture between inclusive ecological
spirituality and emerging holistic science, distinct from both fundamentalist
religion and fundamentalist reductionism.

## Beyond Sustainability

The subtitle of Holmgren's book — "Principles & Pathways Beyond Sustainability"
— signals that sustainability itself is an insufficient goal. A sustainable
system merely maintains existing conditions, while permaculture aims for systems
that actively regenerate and improve over time. The design principles are
pathways to a future that is not just sustained but enhanced.

This reframing is critical: sustainability implies a steady state that may be
neither desirable nor achievable. Energy descent demands systems that can adapt,
self-organize, and provide increasing value even as available energy declines.

## Ethics in Practice

The ethical principles function as first principles for design:

- **Care for the Earth** constrains designs that degrade natural systems
- **Care for People** constrains designs that harm human communities
- **Fair Shares** constrains designs that concentrate wealth or resources

Every design decision can be tested against these three principles. A design that
violates any one of them is fundamentally flawed, regardless of its technical
elegance or short-term productivity.
