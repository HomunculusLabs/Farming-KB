# Organic Weed Control

There is no such thing as an organic weedkiller, and whatever may be
claimed about the safety of chemical weedkillers, there is always danger
in their use. Prolonged use of chemicals has a very damaging effect on
soil organisms. At a horticultural research station, during an
experiment on soils, it was noticed that in the soil on land that had
been regularly treated with a paraquat/diquat mixture there were no
earthworms. Most other beneficial soil organisms were either reduced in
numbers or not present.

## The Principles of Weed Control

Whether you are trying to clear a new garden of weeds or deal with their
habitual menace in an established one, the basic principles to remember
are:

- **Light deprivation**: All green plants must have access to sunshine
  to survive. There are a variety of light-deprivation measures that
  can be used by the organic gardener to control weeds
- **Constant vigilance**: Remove weeds as soon as you see them. Regular
  hoeing will deny persistent weeds a foothold
- **Never let weeds flower or seed**: Cutting them down and digging out
  the roots takes a moment, while coping with the hundreds of seedlings
  they may disperse is a time-consuming job

## Clearing Uncultivated Ground

If you are starting a brand new garden or taking over a weed-infested
one, the first stage in weed control is to make the ground as clean as
possible right from the start. Begin by digging over the whole site and
removing as much as you possibly can. If the ground is infested with
pernicious weeds like couch grass or ground elder, you should not expect
to be totally successful the first time because any tiny piece of root
left in the soil may multiply to form a massive root again.

### Planting a Cleaning Crop
Areas planned for ornamental borders or vegetable plots should be
planted for the first year with a "cleaning crop." None is better than
potatoes, which will not only help clean the soil of weeds but will pay
for themselves. Potatoes have two great virtues as a cleaning crop:
their cultivation entails turning over the soil three times in the year,
and they grow a dense canopy of leaves that excludes light from any
weeds that may try to compete.

### Dealing with Persistent Weeds
Weeds with tap roots, such as dandelions, can be remarkably persistent.
As an experiment, a dock root was once nailed to a shed door and left
for two years to suffer scorching sun, drying winds, and frost. When
planted again two years later it flourished as if the rest had done it
good. There is only one way with these perennials and that is to dig
them out and put them in your regular garbage, not your [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]].

### Black Plastic Sheeting
One of the most effective mulches for eradicating perennial weeds is
black plastic sheeting. When the plastic is laid over the soil, no
light at all can reach the leaves and the weeds will die. They will try
to work their way to the sides, so keep a special lookout at the edges
of the sheeting and be ready with the hoe.

### Organic Mulches
In ornamental borders, the most effective and attractive method of
keeping weeds out is to provide competition in the form of ground cover
plants that create a canopy over the soil. Bark chippings, gravel, and
other organic materials also serve well.

### Paper and Biodegradable Materials
Of the biodegradable materials available, use tough brown paper rather
than biodegradable plastic. The brown paper is used in exactly the same
way as plastic — simply roll it out onto cultivated and watered soil.
It eventually breaks down and can be dug into the soil.

## Preventing Weeds from Spreading

There are two sources of nuisance: weed seeds flying over the fence and
settling on your land, and weeds with creeping roots coming underneath
the fence. The first problem is solved by asking your neighbors for
permission to cut down any weeds before they seed. To cope with weeds
that creep under the fence, install a barrier of heavy-gauge plastic
that runs deep into the soil to discourage encroaching roots.
