## Water Lilies and Floating Plants

Close to the water's edge, in soaked soils, there will be fly catching
plants. Further out, you can have plants which root, come up, and float on
top, including water lilies. The best way to plant those is to take a bag
of manuring materials, tuck it into a good old tire, punch the tire in
two or three places, and push it out with the water lily root buried in
the bag inside the tire.

Beyond six to nine foot depth, the only thing we are much interested in is
a continuation of fern or duckweed on the surface. The dreaded water
hyacinth can be used, and used well, in restricted locations to clear up
pollution. In warm climates, this plant is bad in large slow rivers.

## Algae and Pond Fertility

We are interested in algae bloom. When a white painted disc disappears at
a depth of about two and a half feet, obliterated in a soft green water,
you have a really well-manured pond. If the disk blacks out at about a
foot, the pond is over-manured. If the disk can be seen at five feet, the
pond is not well-manured. For shrimps, prawns, yabbies, fish, or whatever,
we want a well-manured pond with a soft green bloom.

Certain fish browse algae. Even the rainbow trout has gill rakers that
enable it to collect algae and zooplankton. A good way to fertilize ponds
is to strew bales of hay around the edge, both to seal ponds and to get
diatoms working. Diatoms like hay. You can feed little fish by having a
bowl of hay and water, and dipping the water out and feeding it to the
fish.

## Mussels and Water Quality

Mussels don't hurt plants, nor do shrimps or prawns. Mussels pump nitrogen
and phosphorus into the mud. They will filter about 200 gallons of water
per day per mussel. The mussel draws from the water all of the little
living forms and particle and shoots them out and buries them. Of all
plants, animals, seeds, anything, the mussels are the superior phosphorus
fixer.
