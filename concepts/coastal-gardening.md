# Coastal Gardening
## Overview
Coastal gardening presents unique challenges including salt spray, persistent wind, sandy nutrient-poor soils, and occasional flooding from storm surge and tides. Successful coastal gardens work with these conditions by selecting salt-tolerant species, creating windbreaks, building soil rapidly, and designing for storm resilience. Coastal gardening connects to [[pollinator-conservation|conservation]] of dune and shoreline ecosystems and [[soil-erosion-control]] on vulnerable coastal land.
## Coastal Challenges
### Salt Spray
Salt spray is the primary challenge for coastal gardens:
- Wind carries salt-laden mist inland 300-1,000+ feet from the shoreline
- Salt deposits on foliage cause leaf burn, stunting, and dieback
- Salt accumulates in soil over time, raising salinity to levels toxic to many plants
- Storm events can deposit significant salt loads far inland
- Salt spray damage is worst on the windward side of plantings and structures
### Wind Exposure
Coastal winds are stronger and more persistent than inland:
- Prevailing onshore winds create constant drying stress
- Storm winds can exceed 70-100 mph; even summer sea breezes run 15-25 mph
- Wind causes mechanical damage (broken branches, torn leaves)
- Wind accelerates evapotranspiration, increasing water needs
- Sandy, shallow-rooted plants are easily uprooted
- Pollination is disrupted by constant wind
### Sandy Soils
Coastal soils are predominantly sandy:
- **Rapid drainage**: Water moves through sand quickly; irrigation must be frequent
- **Low nutrient retention**: Sand holds little cation exchange capacity; nutrients leach rapidly
- **Low organic matter**: Decomposition is rapid and sand dilutes organic inputs
- **Low water-holding capacity**: Sandy soil holds only 0.5-1 inch of water per foot of depth
- **pH variability**: Coastal sands range from acidic (under pine) to alkaline (shell deposits)
### Storm and Flood Risk
Coastal gardens face periodic extreme events:
- Hurricane and tropical storm surge can inundate gardens with saltwater
- Nor'easters cause prolonged flooding and erosion
- Sea level rise is gradually increasing flood frequency
- Storm waves can deposit sand, debris, and salt water far inland
## Salt Tolerance in Plants
### Halophytes vs. Glycophytes
Plants vary enormously in salt tolerance:
- **Halophytes**: True salt-tolerant plants that thrive in saline conditions (salt marsh plants, sea oats, glasswort)
- **Moderately tolerant**: Can withstand occasional salt spray but not saline soil (many coastal natives)
- **Sensitive**: Damaged by even light salt spray; require protection (most vegetables and ornamentals)
### Salt Tolerance Mechanisms
Salt-tolerant plants employ several strategies:
- **Salt exclusion**: Root membranes block salt uptake; some species excrete salt through specialized glands
- **Salt compartmentalization**: Salt is sequestered in vacuoles away from metabolic processes
- **Succulence**: Water-storing tissues dilute internal salt concentrations
- **Leaf adaptations**: Thick, waxy cuticles, small leaf surfaces, and salt-excreting glands reduce salt damage
## Wind Protection
### Windbreak Design
Strategic windbreaks are essential for coastal gardens:
- **First row (seaward)**: Dense, salt-tolerant evergreens (Juniperus virginiana, Pinus rigida) planted 10-15 feet apart
- **Second row**: Deciduous trees and large shrubs for additional filtering
- **Third row**: Smaller shrubs and perennials that benefit from wind reduction
- **Permeability**: Ideal windbreak is 40-60% permeable; solid barriers create damaging turbulence downwind
- **Distance**: Windbreak reduces wind speed to 50% at 5x the windbreak height; plan garden placement accordingly
### Living Windbreaks
Salt-tolerant species for coastal windbreaks:
- **Eastern red cedar (Juniperus virginiana)**: Extremely salt-tolerant, evergreen, drought-tolerant
- **Pitch pine (Pinus rigida)**: Native to coastal plain; salt and wind tolerant
- **Bayberry (Morella pensylvanica)**: Salt-tolerant shrub; fixes nitrogen via associated bacteria
- **Beach plum (Prunus maritima)**: Salt-tolerant fruiting shrub; produces edible plums
- **Russian olive (Elaeagnus angustifolia)**: Very salt-tolerant; nitrogen fixer (invasive in some areas—check locally)
- **Seagrape (Coccoloba uvifera)**: Tropical/subtropical; excellent coastal windbreak
- **Coco plum (Chrysobalanus icaco)**: Tropical; salt-tolerant evergreen hedge
### Structural Wind Protection
- **Fences and walls**: Wooden slat fences (50% permeability) or masonry walls on windward side
- **Berms**: Earth mounds planted with salt-tolerant groundcovers deflect wind upward
- **Trellises and lattice**: Provide filtered wind protection while supporting climbing plants
- **Temporary screens**: Burlap or shade cloth screens for young plantings until windbreaks mature
## Salt-Spray Resistant Plants
### Salt-Tolerant Trees
| Tree | Salt Tolerance | Notes |
|------|---------------|-------|
| Live oak (Quercus virginiana) | High | Evergreen; classic coastal shade tree |
| Southern magnolia | High | Evergreen; large, showy flowers |
| Crape myrtle | Moderate | Summer flowers; attractive bark |
| Sycamore | Moderate | Fast-growing; large tree |
| Bald cypress | High | Deciduous conifer; tolerates flooding |
| Date palm | High | Tropical/subtropical; edible fruit |
### Salt-Tolerant Shrubs
- **Oleander**: Extremely salt-tolerant; all parts are toxic to humans and livestock
- **Lantana**: Salt-tolerant; excellent butterfly plant; invasive in some areas
- **Silver dollar plant (Lunaria annua)**: Ornamental; moderate salt tolerance
- **Saltbush (Atriplex spp.)**: True halophyte; silvery foliage
- **Yucca**: Extremely salt and drought tolerant; architectural form
- **Agave**: Salt-tolerant succulent; multiple species available
### Salt-Tolerant Perennials and Groundcovers
- **Sea oats (Uniola paniculata)**: Primary dune stabilizer; protected in many coastal areas
- **Beach morning glory (Ipomoea pes-caprae)**: Dune stabilizer; purple flowers
- **Daylily (Hemerocallis)**: Surprisingly salt-tolerant; tough and adaptable
- **Liriope**: Grass-like groundcover; tolerates salt and shade
- **Sedum**: Succulent groundcover; drought and salt tolerant
- **Blanket flower (Gaillardia)**: Native wildflower; salt and drought tolerant
- **Sea lavender (Limonium)**: Salt marsh native; excellent cut flower
- **Mugwort (Artemisia)**: Aromatic; salt-tolerant; medicinal uses
### Vegetables with Some Salt Tolerance
Most vegetables are salt-sensitive, but a few tolerate mild salt spray:
- **Asparagus**: Moderate salt tolerance; perennial
- **Swiss chard**: Moderate tolerance; harvest outer leaves
- **Kale**: Some salt tolerance; best with wind protection
- **Beets**: Moderate tolerance; edible leaves and roots
- **Potatoes**: Moderate tolerance; mulch heavily to prevent salt splash
- **Rosemary**: Herbs in the mint family (rosemary, thyme, oregano) handle salt spray
- **Strawberries**: Some varieties tolerate light salt if protected by windbreak
## Sandy Soil Management
### Building Organic Matter
Sandy coastal soils need constant organic matter additions:
- **Compost**: Apply 2-4 inches annually; mix into top 6 inches of soil
- **Green manure cover crops**: Plant in fallow areas; crimson clover, winter rye, field peas
- **Mulch**: Maintain 3-4 inch layer of organic mulch at all times
- **Biochar**: Incorporate to improve nutrient retention in sand
- **Sheet composting**: Layer organic materials directly on garden beds and let them decompose in place
### Nutrient Management
Nutrients leach quickly from sandy soils:
- **Split applications**: Apply fertilizer in small doses every 2-3 weeks rather than large single applications
- **Slow-release organic fertilizers**: Alfalfa meal, feather meal, bone meal release nutrients gradually
- **Foliar feeding**: Liquid kelp, fish emulsion, compost tea provide quick nutrient boosts
- **Trace elements**: Sandy soils are often deficient in iron, manganese, zinc, and boron
- **Monitor pH**: Sandy coastal soils may be acidic or alkaline depending on parent material
### Water Management
- **Frequent, shallow irrigation**: Water 2-3 times per week in sandy soil; deep watering wastes water as it passes below root zone
- **Drip irrigation**: Delivers water directly to root zones; reduces evaporation and salt splash
- **Mulch**: Essential for moisture retention in sandy soil
- **Rain barrels**: Capture rainwater for irrigation; rainwater is salt-free
- **Swales and berms**: Slow runoff and allow infiltration; see [[swale-design-construction|swales]] for design
## Dune and Shoreline Conservation
Coastal gardeners have a responsibility to protect fragile dune and shoreline ecosystems:
- Do not remove native dune vegetation (sea oats, beach grass, beach morning glory)
- Use boardwalks or stepping stones rather than paths across dunes
- Avoid planting invasive species that can escape and degrade natural areas
- Participate in [[pollinator-conservation|conservation]] efforts like dune restoration and beach grass planting
- Design gardens to reduce runoff that could carry nutrients or sediment to coastal waters
- See [[riparian-zone-restoration]] for related stream and shoreline restoration practices
## Storm Resilience Design
- Use flexible ties for staking trees rather than rigid supports
- Plant in groups to provide mutual wind protection
- Keep trees well-pruned to reduce wind resistance
- Store rainwater for post-storm irrigation (salt-contaminated wells may be unusable)
- Choose plants that resprout after damage rather than those killed by defoliation
- Elevate garden beds to prevent saltwater inundation during minor flooding events
- Have a plan to flush soil with fresh water after saltwater flooding
