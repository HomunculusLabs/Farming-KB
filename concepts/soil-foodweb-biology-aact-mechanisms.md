# Soil Foodweb Biology and AACT Mechanisms

The soil foodweb is the interconnected community of organisms that inhabit soil — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms — and their interactions with each other and with plants. Understanding the soil foodweb is essential for understanding why [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) works, how to use it effectively, and how to manage soil health without reliance on chemical inputs. This concept explores the biological mechanisms through which AACT improves plant health, suppresses disease, and restores soil function.

## Establishing a Healthy Foodweb

### Assessing Existing Biology

Before applying AACT, it is critical to understand what biology already exists in the soil. Different soils have vastly different microbial communities depending on their history, management, and environmental conditions. The first step in any biological soil management programme is to establish a baseline through direct microscopic examination of soil samples.

Key questions to answer include:

- What is the total biomass of bacteria and fungi?
- What is the ratio of bacteria to fungi?
- Are protozoa (flagellates, amoebae, ciliates) present?
- Are beneficial nematodes present? What types?
- Are mycorrhizal fungi colonising plant roots?

### Balancing the Foodweb

Different plant types require different foodweb structures:

- **Annual crops and vegetables** generally perform best with a **bacteria-dominated** foodweb. These plants evolved in successional, disturbed environments where bacterial decomposition is the dominant nutrient cycling pathway.

- **Perennial crops, trees, and pastures** generally perform best with a **fungi-dominated** foodweb. Perennials evolved in later successional stages where fungal decomposition dominates, providing a slow, steady release of nutrients.

- **Most agricultural soils** have been driven toward bacterial dominance through tillage, fertiliser use, and pesticide applications. Many of these soils are actually over-dominated by bacteria and need fungal inoculation to support perennial crops or healthy pasture systems.

### Balancing Biology and Chemistry

Biological and chemical soil factors are intimately linked. Microorganisms are affected by soil pH, nutrient availability, salinity, compaction, and the presence of chemical contaminants. Conversely, microorganisms affect soil chemistry by cycling nutrients, producing acids and chelates, and building soil organic matter.

Ingham emphasises that **biology comes first** — if the soil foodweb is not functioning, chemical amendments will not be effective in the long term. Fertiliser applied to biologically inactive soil is prone to leaching, volatilisation, or immobilisation. The biological approach is to first restore the foodweb, then adjust chemistry as needed to support the desired biological community.

### Monitoring Over Time

Soil biology is dynamic and responds to management practices, seasonal changes, and environmental conditions. Regular monitoring — ideally quarterly — allows practitioners to track trends and adjust management accordingly. Key metrics to monitor include total bacterial and fungal biomass, the bacterial:fungal ratio, protozoan and nematode populations, and mycorrhizal colonisation rates.

## Typical Application Rates and Timing

### Compost

Compost is the foundation of biological soil management. Typical application rates depend on the current state of soil biology and the desired foodweb structure:

- **Initial restoration**: 5–15 tons per acre of high-quality, biologically active compost, incorporated into the top few inches of soil.
- **Maintenance**: 1–5 tons per acre annually, applied as a surface mulch or lightly incorporated.

The key factor is not the total amount of organic matter but the **biological quality** of the compost — the diversity and activity of the microbial community it contains.

### Compost Tea

AACT is typically applied as a soil drench or foliar spray:

- **Soil drench**: 15–30 gallons per acre (undiluted), applied to the root zone. Soil drenches directly inoculate the soil foodweb.
- **Foliar spray**: 15–30 gallons per acre (diluted with water to appropriate concentration), applied to leaf surfaces. Foliar applications coat leaf surfaces with beneficial organisms that occupy ecological niches and prevent pathogen establishment.

Timing of applications should align with plant growth stages and environmental conditions:

- **Pre-planting**: Soil drench to establish biology in the root zone.
- **Early growth**: Foliar applications to protect emerging leaves.
- **Pre-flowering**: Foliar applications to protect flowers and fruit set.
- **Post-harvest**: Soil drench to feed the soil foodweb during fallow periods.

## Can Anaerobic Brews Be Beneficial?

This is one of the most debated questions in the compost tea community. Ingham's position is nuanced:

**Strictly aerobic teas** (AACT, maintained above 6 mg/L dissolved oxygen) are the most reliable and predictable way to produce beneficial microbial inoculants. They consistently support aerobic, beneficial organisms and suppress anaerobic pathogens.

**Anaerobic or partially anaerobic brews** may contain beneficial metabolites (antibiotics, enzymes, growth factors) produced by facultative or anaerobic organisms, but they also risk containing phytotoxic compounds (alcohols, organic acids, hydrogen sulfide) that can harm plants and beneficial soil organisms. The problem is one of quality control — without rigorous testing, there is no way to know whether an anaerobic brew will be beneficial or harmful.

**Practical recommendation**: Stick with AACT (actively aerated) for consistent, predictable results. If anaerobic brews are used, they should be treated as experimental and applied with caution, ideally with laboratory testing to verify their composition.

## Mechanisms of Impact from AACT Applications

### AACT Does Not Kill Anything

This is perhaps the most important principle to understand about how AACT works. AACT does not contain antimicrobial compounds that directly kill pathogens. Instead, it works through **ecological mechanisms** — competitive exclusion, niche occupation, and food resource competition. This distinction is critical because it means AACT works in harmony with natural processes rather than against them.

### How AACT Reduces Disease-Causing Organisms

AACT reduces disease pressure through multiple, overlapping mechanisms:

#### 1. Competitive Exclusion

Beneficial organisms introduced by AACT occupy the same ecological niches that pathogens would otherwise exploit. On leaf surfaces, beneficial bacteria and fungi cover the available surface area, leaving no room for pathogen spores to germinate and establish. In the root zone, beneficial organisms form a protective biofilm around roots that prevents pathogen access.

#### 2. Food Resource Competition

All microorganisms require food resources (carbon compounds, nitrogen, minerals) to grow and reproduce. By introducing large populations of beneficial organisms, AACT creates competition for these resources. Since the beneficial organisms are already established and actively feeding, they outcompete pathogen organisms that arrive later, effectively starving them.

#### 3. Physical Interference

Some beneficial organisms physically interfere with pathogens. Certain bacteria produce antibiotics that inhibit pathogen growth. Trichoderma fungi, common in compost teas, are known to parasitise pathogenic fungi. Protozoa feed on pathogenic bacteria.

### Microbes Improve Nutrient Uptake

Perhaps the most underappreciated benefit of AACT is its effect on plant nutrition. The soil foodweb is the primary mechanism by which plants obtain nutrients in natural systems:

- **Bacteria** decompose simple organic compounds and retain nutrients in their biomass.
- **Fungi** decompose complex organic compounds (lignin, cellulose) and retain nutrients in their mycelial networks.
- **Protozoa** (flagellates and amoebae) consume bacteria and excrete excess nitrogen as ammonium — directly feeding plant roots.
- **Beneficial nematodes** consume fungi and bacteria, also excreting plant-available nutrients.
- **Mycorrhizal fungi** form symbiotic associations with plant roots, extending the effective root surface area by hundreds of times and accessing phosphorus and other nutrients that are otherwise unavailable to plants.

By inoculating the soil with these organisms, AACT restores the natural nutrient cycling pathways that chemical fertilisers bypass — but with the critical difference that natural cycling provides a steady, balanced supply of nutrients matched to plant demand.

### Pathogenic Fungi

Pathogenic fungi such as *Pythium*, *Phytophthora*, *Rhizoctonia*, *Fusarium*, and *Verticillium* are major causes of plant disease. These organisms are present in most soils but only cause disease when conditions favour them — typically when the soil foodweb is depleted and plants are stressed.

AACT addresses pathogenic fungi indirectly by:

1. Restoring the beneficial fungal community that competes with pathogens.
2. Feeding protozoa and nematodes that consume pathogenic fungi and bacteria.
3. Improving plant health through better nutrition, making plants less susceptible to infection.
4. Improving soil structure (through fungal hyphae and glomalin), which creates better drainage and aeration — conditions that favour beneficial organisms over pathogens.

### Saprophytic Fungi

Saprophytic fungi decompose dead organic matter and are essential for nutrient cycling and soil structure formation. They are the primary decomposers of lignin and cellulose — the tough structural compounds in woody material, leaves, and stems. A healthy population of saprophytic fungi is essential for:

- Breaking down organic matter and making nutrients available.
- Building soil structure through hyphal aggregation and glomalin production.
- Suppressing pathogenic fungi through competition and antibiosis.
- Supporting mycorrhizal networks by providing a continuous food source.

### Calcium and Fungi

Calcium plays a critical role in fungal growth and soil structure. Adequate calcium levels:

- Promote fungal hyphal growth and extension.
- Support the production of glomalin by mycorrhizal fungi.
- Improve soil structure by promoting flocculation (the binding of soil particles into aggregates).
- Reduce compaction by maintaining open soil structure.

Ingham emphasises that calcium availability is often the limiting factor for fungal growth in agricultural soils. Addressing calcium deficiencies can dramatically improve the effectiveness of AACT applications by creating conditions that favour fungal establishment and activity.
