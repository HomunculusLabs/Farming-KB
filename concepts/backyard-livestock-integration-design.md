# Backyard Livestock Integration Design

Updated: 2026-04-18

Integrating small livestock into the backyard garden creates multiple closed cycles: animals convert kitchen scraps and garden waste into meat, eggs, and fertilizer while performing useful garden labor including weeding, tilling, pest control, and pollination. This approach extends the ecological garden into the animal kingdom, completing nature's cycles of nutrient flow.

## Benefits of Garden Animals

Animals intelligently raised in the garden provide multiple functions:
- **Fertilize:** Manure and urine cycle nutrients back to soil
- **Till:** Scratch and burrow, incorporating organic matter
- **Clip grass and weed:** Graze on unwanted vegetation
- **Eat scraps:** Process kitchen waste more efficiently than composting alone
- **Hunt insects and slugs:** Natural pest control
- **Warn of intruders:** Alarm behavior (except rabbits and guinea pigs)
- **Warm greenhouses:** Body heat in winter
- **Boost plant growth:** CO2 from breath enhances greenhouse photosynthesis
- **Companionship:** Educational and entertainment value

Cycling wastes through animals captures more stored energy than direct composting because the animal converts waste to food (meat, eggs) while still producing useful fertilizer. A hen processing kitchen scraps provides eggs, insect control, weed management, and nearly as much fertilizer as composting the scraps directly.

## Suitable Animals for Backyard Integration

### Chickens
The most versatile backyard animal. A laying hen needs 4 square feet of space; a broiler needs 2 square feet. A hen needs about 80 pounds of grain per year (requiring ~1,000 sq ft to grow). Rather than growing all feed, supplement with multifunctional garden plants (see table below). Breeds vary by purpose: layers for eggs, broilers for meat, dual-purpose breeds for both.

### Ducks
Gentler on plants than chickens; don't scratch much, so they need less supervision in the garden. Khaki Campbells and Indian Runners are good egg layers; Pekins and Improved Mallards for meat. Muscovies (South American) are very quiet -- good for urban/suburban settings.

### Rabbits
Voracious vegetarians that quickly eliminate weeds. Need wire-bottomed tractor (they burrow). Best integrated with worm bins: elevated wire-bottomed hutch over worm bin processes manure into compost. Five breeding rabbits (3 litters x 5 kits/year) yield ample family meat. California and New Zealand breeds for meat; French Angoras for wool.

### Pigeons
Require minimal space and feed. Useful for raising squab (young pigeons). Can be housed in small lofts.

### Quail
Very space-efficient. Coturnix quail mature in 6 weeks and lay prolifically. Require minimal infrastructure.

### Turkeys
Larger than chickens but similar management. Heritage breeds forage well; broad-breasted varieties less mobile.

### Guinea Pigs
Popular in Latin American permaculture systems. Efficient converters of green forage to protein. Quiet and easy to manage.

## Animal Tractors

The key tool for integrating animals with gardens is the animal tractor: a bottomless mobile pen that concentrates animal labor in specific areas.

### Chicken Tractor Design
- Bottomless pen on wheels, 4 feet wide x 8 feet long x 2 feet high
- Open wood-frame box covered with 1-inch chicken wire (poultry netting)
- Roofed with plastic panels
- Wheels or skids at one end
- Door for access; food and water containers hang from roof
- Optional perches projecting from sides
- Capacity: 8 laying hens or 16 broilers in 32 sq ft

### Three Methods for Building Soil with Tractors

**1. Rotation Method:**
- Each morning, wheel pen to an unused garden bed
- Withhold feed for 1 hour to encourage birds to eat bed vegetation
- Birds weed, till, and manure all day
- Next morning, move to next fresh spot
- Sprinkle mulch on completed beds
- Sow cover crop (buckwheat, winter rye, vetch) on completed beds; return chickens when 4 inches high
- Requires garden 2x needed size so beds can go fallow

**2. Sheet Mulching with Chickens:**
- Leave tractor in one spot for several days
- Add ~1 inch of mulch daily; chickens work it and add manure
- When mulch is ~4 inches deep, move to new spot
- Plant in soil pockets or top layer of potting soil

**3. Deep Mulch Method:**
- Leave tractor in one place; add ~1 inch mulch daily
- After ~5 weeks (broiler grow-out period), have thick raised bed
- Works on slopes: creates level terraced beds
- Risk: predators may dig under stationary pen (mitigate with buried chicken wire perimeter)

### Duck Integration
Ducks patrol gardens for slugs and insects. Many plants suitable for chickens also work for ducks. Best managed in free-range rather than tractor systems since they don't scratch.

### Rabbit-Worm Bin Integration
- Wire-bottomed rabbit hutch elevated on posts
- Open wooden or plastic bin (18-30 inches deep) below to catch droppings
- Bin filled with shredded newspaper + peat moss or shredded dry leaves (6 inches deep)
- When bin is full, add 100-300 red worms, cover, set aside
- Place second bin under hutch
- By the time second bin fills, first bin contains beautiful worm compost
- Worm bins also process kitchen scraps
- Virtually odorless; can be used indoors; ideal for winter composting

## Greenhouse Animal Integration

When small animals are housed in or adjoining a greenhouse:
- Body heat warms the structure in winter
- CO2 from breath boosts plant growth (photosynthesis enhancement)
- Less tangible: entertainment and educational value
- Animals benefit from winter shelter

## Poultry Forage Plants

### Trees
Black locust (pods), fruit trees (fallen fruit), mulberry (fruits), nut trees (cracked nuts), oak (acorns, high protein), pistachio (nuts), honey locust (pods)

### Shrubs
Autumn olive, barberry, boxthorn, buffaloberry, coffeeberry, currant, elderberry, hackberry, hawthorn, manzanita, privet, Russian olive, serviceberry, Siberian pea shrub (edible pods)

### Herbaceous Plants
Alfalfa, buckwheat, chickweed, chicory, cleavers, clover, comfrey, fennel, pigweed, rye, shepherd's purse, Swiss chard

### Seed Crops
Amaranth, barley, corn, millet, oats, quinoa, sunflower, wheat

## Managing Neighbors and Regulations

- Dogs and cats require expensive feed and intense care, destroy garden property, yet are socially accepted
- Many other animals provide more tangible benefits with less trouble
- Supplying neighbors with eggs or meat can quiet concerns
- Check local zoning regulations before acquiring livestock
