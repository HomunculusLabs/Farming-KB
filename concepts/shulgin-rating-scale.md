## The Shulgin Rating Scale

**Source:** PiHKAL (Part 2), Shulgin Rating Scale

**Updated:** 2026-04-19

## Overview

The Shulgin Rating Scale is a five-level system devised by Alexander Shulgin for quantifying the subjective intensity of a psychedelic drug experience. Used throughout [[shulgin-dosage-guidelines]] that accompany each compound entry in PiHKAL.

## The Scale Levels

### PLUS / MINUS (+/-)

"The level of effectiveness of a drug that indicates a threshold action. If a higher dosage produces a greater response, then the plus/minus (+/-) was valid. If a higher dosage produces nothing, then this was a false positive."

The +/- level represents the bare minimum detectable effect — a hint that something is happening, but insufficient to confirm pharmacological activity without further testing.

This level is particularly important in the [[psychedelic-dose-response]]. For compounds with steep dose-response characteristics, this transition can occur with a very small increase in dosage. Shulgin"s careful documentation of this transition for each compound was a key contribution to the field of [[psilocybin-and-mystical-experience]], which distinguishes between ordinary [[shulgin-dosage-guidelines]].

The scale also appears in TiHKAL (tryptamine entries) and has been widely adopted by the broader [[adverse-effects-and-harm-reduction-psilocybin]] communities as a standard communication tool.

## Critiques and Limitations

The Shulgin Rating Scale has some recognized limitations:

- It is inherently subjective and dependent on the reporter's prior experience
- The gap between +++ and ++++ is not well-defined operationally
- It does not capture valence (positive vs. negative quality of experience)
- Individual variation in sensitivity means the same dose can produce different scale levels in different people
- The scale was designed for phenethylamines and tryptamines; its applicability to other classes (e.g., cannabinoids, dissociatives) is less established

Despite these limitations, the scale remains the most widely recognized system for communicating [[shulgin-dosage-guidelines]]

## Shulgin on Animal Models and Psychopharmacology

**Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Overview

Throughout PiHKAL, Alexander Shulgin expressed deep and articulate skepticism about the value of animal models for studying psychedelic drugs. His commentary reveals a fundamental philosophical position about consciousness, scientific methodology, and the limits of reductionist pharmacology.

## The Fundamental Problem of Animal Screening

In the commentary on TMA-5, Shulgin outlined the logical structure of the problem:

"There are two questions that must be answered by the pharmacologist who is faced with a new drug that he suspects might be a psychedelic. There is the qualitative question: is the drug a psychedelic? And there is the quantitative question: how potent is it?"

The standard approach involves administering known psychedelics to animals, identifying behavioral responses common to all, and then testing whether unknown compounds produce the same responses. Shulgin noted the critical weakness: "Since there is little logic in the argument that animals can experience, let alone reveal, altered states of consciousness or fantasy fugues or colored imagery, the investigator must look for objective signs."

## The Bootstrap Problem

The entire discipline of animal psychopharmacology depends on accepting that the initial reference compounds are "indeed psychedelic materials. And these inputs can only come from human trials." This creates an irreducible dependency on human experience to validate the animal screening system. Without human data establishing the reference set, animal models have no anchor.

Shulgin illustrated this with TMA-5: "The statement that it is psychedelic has actually been published in reviews solely on the basis of the above two studies; the potency has been put at some ten times that of mescaline." But these conclusions, published in authoritative reviews, were based solely on animal data of uncertain validity.

## Specific Examples of Misleading Animal Data

### The DOIP Red Herring

A particularly colorful example involved the isopropyl isomer DOIP. An "olive-faced M.D., Ph.D." published animal behavioral responses that he attributed to DOIP. However, "what he had studied could only have been DOPR since DOIP, at that time, had not yet been synthesized either by me, or by either of the other two active synthesists of that moment." The researcher had apparently tested the wrong compound. Shulgin applied a French phrase: "Il a foutu le camp."
