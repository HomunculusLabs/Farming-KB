## The Position

The view of consciousness as a fundamental property of reality holds that conscious
experience is not merely an emergent byproduct of complex neural computation but
is instead a basic feature of the universe, comparable to mass, charge, or
spacetime. This position stands in contrast to the mainstream materialist
assumption that consciousness arises solely from physical processes in the brain.

Simon G. Powell articulates a version of this position in which information
reveals itself as the fundamental stuff of Nature, with consciousness itself
representing a particular pattern of information embodied within the neuronal
substrate of the brain. In this framework, consciousness is not an anomaly in an
otherwise insentient universe but a natural expression of the informational
fabric of reality.

The implications of this view for understanding psychedelics are profound. If
consciousness is fundamental rather than incidental, then the expanded states of
awareness produced by entheogenic substances are not aberrations or tricks played
on the brain but represent a more direct encounter with the fundamental nature of
reality.

Powell argues that if information is the basic currency of reality and
consciousness is a particular pattern of information processing, then conscious
experience has genuine ontological significance. Chalmers, who formulated the "hard problem of consciousness," has argued that panpsychism offers a more promising framework than physicalism for explaining subjective experience. The related position of panentheism — that everything exists within a universal consciousness — has been advocated by process philosophers like Alfred North Whitehead and finds expression in some interpretations of Eastern philosophical traditions. ## The Hard Problem and Fine-Tuning Arguments

A key argument for consciousness as fundamental comes from what is sometimes
called the hard problem of consciousness: the difficulty of explaining how
subjective experience arises from purely physical processes. If consciousness
cannot be adequately reduced to neural correlates, then alternative frameworks
that treat consciousness as fundamental become more plausible.

The position also draws support from the fine-tuning argument applied to
consciousness. The fundamental constants of Nature have the precise values needed
to allow life and consciousness to emerge. If consciousness is a fundamental
property rather than an accident, this fine-tuning may reflect the universe's
inherent tendency toward self-awareness rather than mere coincidence.

Critics of consciousness-as-fundamental point out that it may be unfalsifiable

faces its own serious difficulties, particularly the explanatory gap between

The integrated information theory (IIT) of consciousness, proposed by Giulio Tononi, attempts to bridge this gap by providing a mathematical framework for quantifying consciousness as a fundamental property of systems with high integrated information. While IIT remains controversial, it represents one of the most rigorous attempts to develop a scientific framework compatible with consciousness-as-fundamental. The theory predicts that consciousness is not unique to biological brains but exists in any system with sufficiently high integrated information — a prediction that, if confirmed, would have profound implications for our understanding of both consciousness and the nature of reality.
