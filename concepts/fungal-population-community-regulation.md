## Overview

Fungi play a central role in regulating populations and structuring communities across all ecosystem types. Population and community regulation occurs through both the promotion and reduction of growth, fitness, and reproductive potential of organisms. When fungal effects are asymmetric across species — benefiting some while harming others — they can alter competitive hierarchies and reshape community composition over successive generations.

The mechanisms by which fungi regulate biological communities are diverse, ranging from direct nutritional mutualisms (mycorrhizae) and pathogenic suppression to indirect effects mediated through decomposition, nutrient cycling, and trophic interactions. The following ecosystem services provided by fungi illustrate their regulatory breadth:

| Ecosystem Service | Fungal Functional Group |
|---|---|
| Soil formation (rock dissolution, particle binding) | Lichens, Saprotrophs, Mycorrhizae |
| Soil fertility (decomposition, nutrient mineralization) | Saprotrophs, Ericoid and Ectomycorrhizae |
| Primary production (nutrient accessibility, plant defense) | Mycorrhizae, Endophytes |
| Plant community structure | Mycorrhizae, Pathogens |
| Secondary production (food source) | Saprotrophs, Mycorrhizae |
| Population/biomass regulation | Pathogens |
| Carbon sequestration and storage | Mycorrhizae, Saprotrophs |
| Modification of pollutants | Saprotrophs, Mycorrhizae |

## Mycorrhizae and Plant Succession

### The Role of Nutrient Availability

Pedersen and Sylvia (1996) identified nutrient availability as a major determinant of plant colonization success during seral succession. In this context, the ability of plants to associate with mycorrhizal fungi provides a significant competitive advantage by enhancing nutrient sequestration from limited soil resources.

The dispersal of hypogeous fungal spores by rodents is a critical mechanism for delivering mycorrhizal inoculum to newly exposed surfaces. However, this distribution is rarely random. Small mammals defecate in middens, concentrating spores at active feeding sites and creating a patchy distribution of mycorrhizal inoculum potential across the landscape. This spatial heterogeneity directly influences which plant species can successfully establish in each microhabitat.

M.F. Allen (1991) suggested that mycorrhizae increase plant species diversity during colonization of new areas. The patchy distribution of spores allows both mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal plant species to co-establish. During primary colonization on Mount Saint Helens, mycorrhizal inoculum potential varied from zero to abundant within distances of only centimeters (Allen and MacMahon, 1985). Vegetation colonizing the volcanic substrate consisted entirely of mycorrhizal species, both arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal forms.

### Contrasting Successional Patterns

Allen and Allen (1990) documented contrasting patterns across ecosystems:

- **Mount Saint Helens**: Exclusively mycorrhizal vegetation (both AM and ECM) colonized the disturbed landscape
- **Galapagos Islands**: A mixture of arbuscular mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal plants established, with distribution related to soil nutrient content — non-mycorrhizal species in fertile lowland soils, mycorrhizal species in poorer rocky soils

From these studies, Allen and Allen (1990) hypothesized patterns of mycorrhizal dependence in developing ecosystems relative to nutrient and water availability.

### Glacier Forefront Succession

The Lyman Glacier forefront provides a well-studied model of primary succession. Trappe and Maser (1976) showed that spores of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus *Glomus macrocarpus* and the hypogeous ectomycorrhizal fungus *Hymenogaster* survived passage through the guts of small mammals (Oregon vole, *Microtus oregoni*; chickaree, *Tamiasciurus douglasi*), assisting in the colonization of bare ground.

Cazares and Trappe (1994) demonstrated that mycophagy of both hypogeous and epigeous mycorrhizal fungi results in deposition of viable spores in feces. At the Lyman Glacier, Jumpponen et al. (1999) identified "safe sites" — concave surfaces of coarse rocky particles ideal for trapping tree seeds and protecting them from desiccation. These sites also served as foraging foci for small mammals, concentrating mycorrhizal spore-laden feces and further enhancing seedling survival.

### Dark-Septate Endophytes in Early Succession

Jumpponen et al. (1998) demonstrated that the dark-septate mycorrhizal fungus *[[phialocephala-fortinii]]* significantly enhanced growth of lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*), an early colonizer of the glacier forefront, but only when nitrogen was added. However, total plant phosphorus was significantly enhanced by the mycorrhiza even without added nitrogen, demonstrating that different nutrients respond differently to fungal colonization depending on environmental conditions.

### Ectomycorrhizal Succession

The succession of ectomycorrhizal species during primary succession is supported by findings at the Lyman Glacier forefront. Across different successional stages, Jumpponen et al. (1999, 2002) identified 68 ectomycorrhizal species belonging to 25 genera, with no single species occurring on all three successional sites. Ectomycorrhizal species diversity increased to a maximum where tree canopies started to overlap, corresponding with findings from other studies (Dighton et al., 1986; Last et al., 1987; Visser, 1995).

This diversity peak at canopy closure may relate to both the paucity of available phosphorus (Dighton and Harrison, 1990) and an increasing proportion of nutrients locked in organic forms. The increased diversity allows greater expression of mycorrhizal function to utilize mixed available resources. Conn and Dighton (2000) showed that [[biodiversity-fungi-ectomycorrhizal-diversity]] on different tree litters reflects appropriate enzyme functions — where phosphorus is immobilized, the mycorrhizal community contained a greater proportion of acid phosphatase-producing types.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Succession

The succession of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] on herbaceous plant roots is less obvious than ectomycorrhizal succession, but different AMF species have contrasting effects on host performance. Hart et al. (2001) proposed two hypotheses:

1. **Driver hypothesis**: Mycorrhizal fungi are the driving force, determining [[leake-mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration-plant-community-composition]] through interspecific differences in colonization and persistence potential
2. **Passenger hypothesis**: Changes in mycorrhizal communities are dependent on plant and environmental conditions; the mycorrhizae are essentially passengers along for the ride

### The Mycelial Network Effect

The importance of maintaining a continuous mycelial mat has been demonstrated by Amaranthus and Perry (1989). When Douglas fir was planted into partially cleared sites where mycorrhizal roots were maintained on remaining trees, survival was approximately 90%. In totally cleared areas, survival after 2 years was only 50%. The existing mycelial network provided ecosystem stability through carbon [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]] between connected plants, allowing new recruits to access larger resource pools.

### Saprotrophic Influences on Establishment

Even saprotrophic fungi can influence plant establishment. Osonubi et al. (1990) showed that inoculation of *[[gmelina-arborea]]* seeds with *Chaetomium bostrychodes* improved seed germination, likely through enzyme production that assists in seed stratification or scarification.

## Mycorrhizae and Plant Fitness

Beyond improving growth, [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] enhance overall plant fitness, providing competitive advantages when effects are asymmetric across species.

Sanders et al. (1995) showed that arbuscular mycorrhizal plants had improved phosphate nutrition, leading to significant increases in flower bud and seed production beyond what would be expected from vegetative growth enhancement alone. Koide et al. (1988), Stanley et al. (1993), Lewis and Koide (1990), Bryla and Koide (1990), and Koide and Lu (1992) all documented increased reproductive potential, including improved offspring vigor through enhanced seedling germination, leaf area, root:shoot ratio, and root enzyme production.

Heppell et al. (1998) demonstrated that offspring of arbuscular mycorrhizal-infected *Abutilon theophrasti* were significantly larger than those of non-mycorrhizal parents. Under high-density conditions, mycorrhizal offspring improved even more due to effects of early self-thinning. This advantage was transferred to the next generation in terms of total seed production.

### Plant Family Responses to Mycorrhizae

Francis and Read (1994, 1995) proposed a continuum of plant responses to arbuscular mycorrhizal infection, from mutualism to antagonism:

**Positive (Mutualism)**: Asteraceae, Ericaceae, Fabaceae, Liliaceae, Pinaceae, Plantaginaceae, Ranunculaceae

**Neutral to Commensal**: Burmanniaceae, Gentianaceae, Monotroaceae, Orchidaceae, Triuridaceae, Gramineae, Caryophyllaceae, Resedaceae, Scrophulariaceae

**Negative (Antagonism)**: Boraginaceae, Brassicaceae, Chenopodiaceae, Polygonaceae

This variation in plant response invokes differences in competitive fitness among plant groups, determining plant community structure under given environmental conditions. The effect is most beneficial to K-selected plant species and adverse to ruderals (Francis and Read, 1995). Janos (1980) noted that these asymmetric mycorrhizal effects can be a significant factor determining plant species composition in tropical forests.
