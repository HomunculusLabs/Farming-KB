# Fungal Ecology -- Saprotrophs and Decomposers

## Overview

Saprotrophic fungi are the primary decomposers of organic matter in terrestrial ecosystems. They play an indispensable role in [[query-how-does-nitrogen-cycling-work-in-a-permaculture-system]], soil formation and stabilization, and the maintenance of soil fertility.

## Decomposer Roles and Succession

Saprotrophic fungi function as primary, secondary, and tertiary decomposers in the often complex process of degrading organic matter. Decomposition typically follows a successional sequence in which different fungal groups dominate at different stages:

- **Primary decomposers**: Colonize fresh litter and organic matter, utilizing readily available sugars, amino acids, and simple polysaccharides (starch, pectin). These include many fast-growing fungal taxa that exploit the initial pulse of nutrients.
- **Secondary decomposers**: Degrade more recalcitrant compounds including cellulose and hemicellulose, becoming dominant as the more labile resources are depleted.
- **Tertiary decomposers**: Specialize in the most resistant materials -- lignin, humic substances, and heavily melanized organic matter. White-rot basidiomycetes are the principal tertiary decomposers of wood.

This successional pattern operates on multiple scales: within individual substrate particles (leaf litter fragments, wood chips), across vertical soil profiles (from fresh litter to mineral soil), and across landscape-level substrate availability gradients. Species composition and propagule density clearly change through the sequence of fragmentation, decomposition, and humification of plant detritus.

## Wood Decay Fungi

Wood-inhabiting macrofungi represent one of the best-studied groups of saprotrophic fungi. Different groups of wood-inhabiting fungi use quite different volumes of substratum (resource bases) for the production of sporocarps. Wood substrata range from tiny twigs to large trunks, and all sizes must be sampled if a majority of fungal species within a study area are to be found.

### White Rot

White-rot fungi (primarily basidiomycetes) are unique in their ability to degrade all major components of wood -- cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. They produce [[lowenfels-humus-formation-benefits]].

### Decay Class Progression

Wood decomposition proceeds through recognizable stages that can be classified into decay classes: hard and not decayed; hard but decayed (discolored or not); softened and punky (white rot); and well decayed, humus-like (brown rot). The species composition of wood-inhabiting fungi changes predictably through these decay stages, with different species dominating at different points in the decomposition trajectory. In a 30-year chronosequence study on beech logs in Denmark, Heilmann-Clausen (2001) showed that the correlated factors of log age, decay stage, and soil contact, together with microenvironmental variables such as sun exposure, moss cover, and soil moisture, were the strongest correlates of community structure.

## Leaf Litter Decomposition

Leaf litter decomposition is a major pathway of carbon and nutrient flux in terrestrial ecosystems. Fungi are the dominant decomposers of leaf litter in most forest and grassland environments. The litter layer supports a distinct fungal community that is typically more species-rich than the underlying mineral soil, especially in humid tropical forests where the litter-soil interface may be abrupt and the two layers support markedly different mycotas.

Key factors influencing leaf litter [[coprophilous-fungi-taxonomic-groups-distribution]] of fungi are encountered in soils, including members of the Saprolegniales, Pythiales, Mucorales, Eurotiales, Microascales, Hypocreales, Sordariales, Onygenales, Leotiales, Pezizales, and a plethora of mitosporic fungi (primarily anamorphs of ascomycete or basidiomycete species). Basidiomycetes, described as "the missing link in soil mycology" more than 50 years ago, are still underrepresented in soil surveys due to methodological biases in culture-based techniques.

## Ecological Patterns and Biodiversity

Mycodiversity exhibits distinct patterns in both space and time, although such fungal patterns remain largely unexplored. Key questions include:

- How do species-area curves of wood-inhabiting fungi vary across environments?
- Does fungal species richness follow a latitudinal gradient?
- How do natural and anthropogenic disturbances (hurricanes, fires, logging) perturb species composition and richness?
- How does sporocarp production vary within and between years?

Sampling studies have shown that species-substrata curves rarely reach an asymptote, indicating that additional sampling would continue to reveal new species. Turnover in fruiting agaric species is high in both temperate and tropical forests, requiring weekly or biweekly sampling intervals, while corticioid and polypore fungi may be sampled at approximately 2-month intervals.
