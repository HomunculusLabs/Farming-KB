# Barley Varieties for Brewing

Barley ([[hordeum-vulgare]]) is the primary grain used in beer production worldwide. Not all barley is suitable for brewing -- malting barley differs from feed or food barley in key characteristics. Understanding barley varieties helps brewers make informed decisions about [[mushroom-cultivator-grain-selection-moisture]] and helps growers [[query-how-do-i-choose-the-right-cannabis-strain-for-my-grow]] varieties for their conditions.

## Malting Barley vs Feed Barley

Malting barley must meet specific quality criteria that feed barley does not:

- High germination rate (above 95%)
- Plump kernels with uniform size
- Low protein content (typically 9.5-12%)
- Moderate to high enzyme potential
- Low moisture content at harvest (below 13%)
- Free from disease, mold, and pesticide residue

High protein is the most common disqualifier for malting barley. Protein above 12% can cause haziness, reduced extract yield, and off-flavors in finished beer. Maltsters pay a premium for barley that meets malting specifications, and the standards are strict.

## Two-Row vs Six-Row Barley

The most fundamental distinction in brewing barley is between two-row (Hordeum distichon) and six-row (Hordeum vulgare) varieties. This refers to the arrangement of fertile florets on the barley head.

### Two-Row Barley

Two-row barley has two rows of kernels on each ear. It is the dominant type in European brewing and in most craft brewing worldwide.

Advantages of two-row barley:

- Larger, plumper kernels with higher starch content
- Lower protein content (typically 9.5-11.5%)
- Higher extract yield per pound of grain
- More uniform kernel size for consistent malting
- Richer, maltier flavor

Major two-row varieties include Maris Otter (the classic English malting barley), Golden Promise (Scottish), Metcalfe, Copeland, and Conlon. European varieties include Scarlett, Concerto, and Laureate.

### Six-Row Barley

Six-row barley has six rows of kernels on each ear, though the kernels are smaller. It was historically dominant in American brewing because it grows well in the [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] climate and has higher enzyme content.

Advantages of six-row barley:

- Higher diastatic power (enzyme content), useful when brewing with adjuncts like corn or rice
- Better adaptation to hot, dry conditions
- Higher protein content provides more nutrients for yeast (but can be excessive)

Six-row varieties include Larker, Morex, Excel, and Robust. These are primarily grown in the Upper Midwest of the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]].

## Major European Malting Barley Varieties

### United Kingdom

- Maris Otter: The benchmark English malting barley since 1966. Produces a rich, biscuity malt character that defines classic English ales. Lower yield than modern varieties but prized by craft brewers for its flavor.
- Golden Promise: A Scottish variety known for producing excellent malt with a clean, sweet flavor. Used in many premium Scottish ales and some English beers.
- Pearl: A modern, high-yielding variety with good malting quality. Increasingly popular as a replacement for Maris Otter in commercial brewing.
- Concerto: A high-yielding variety with good enzyme potential and consistent malting quality.

### Continental Europe

- Scarlett: A widely grown European variety with good disease resistance and reliable malting quality
- Power: A high-yielding variety with excellent enzyme potential
- Sebastian: German variety with good malting characteristics
- Odyssey: Modern variety with good agronomic performance

## North American Malting Barley Varieties

- Metcalfe: Two-row variety widely grown in the Canadian Prairies, popular for craft brewing
- Copeland: Two-row variety with good enzyme potential, widely used in North American craft brewing
- Conlon: Two-row variety with good agronomic performance in the Northern Plains
- Harrington: An older two-row variety still valued by some craft maltsters
- AC Metcalfe: Canadian two-row with excellent malting quality
- CDC Copeland: Improved version of the original Copeland

## Specialty and Heritage Barley Varieties

The craft malting movement has revived interest in heritage and specialty barley varieties that offer unique flavor characteristics:

- Bere barley: An ancient six-row landrace from Scotland and the Orkney Islands. Produces a distinctive, earthy, nutty malt. Used in traditional Scottish ales and Bere bannock bread.
- Hana malt barley: Japanese variety used in premium sake and beer production
- Hooded barley: A distinctive form of barley with altered spike morphology, sometimes used for specialty malts
- Himalayan barley (naked barley): Grown in the high Himalayas, used in both food and traditional [[comparison-mead-vs-kvass-fermented-beverages]]
