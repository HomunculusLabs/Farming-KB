# Organic Composting Methods

Every garden must have a compost heap. This is the ideal way to return as much [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]]** is vital. Without it, anaerobic bacteria develop and turn material into a stinking slime worse than useless on the garden. The container should allow air circulation. Never pack contents down too much. Mix fine material (grass cuttings, small weeds) with larger weeds, shredded newspaper, or straw.

**Nitrogen** fuels the bacteria. Ideally use animal manure. Alternatives include organic compost activators, dried sewage sludge, kelp meal, or dried blood. A fine dusting every 12in (30cm) of compost is sufficient.

**Lime** keeps the compost "sweet" by neutralizing acidity. A slightly heavier dusting than the nitrogen activator every 12in (30cm) is recommended. On chalky soils, consider omitting lime.

**Water** is essential. Green material usually provides enough, but in hot summers edges may dry out. Straw must be wetted before adding. Cover the heap in winter to prevent it becoming too wet.

**Heat** dramatically speeds decomposition. In summer, usable compost is ready in 2-3 months; in winter the process slows considerably.

## What Not to Compost

- Material infected with persistent diseases (clubroot, blight) -- always burn
- Top growth of main crop potatoes (blight spore risk)
- Woody prunings (too slow to rot; use a chipper instead)
- Cooked kitchen scraps (attract vermin)
- Roots of pernicious weeds: couch grass, ground elder, bindweed, creeping buttercup
- Weed seeds -- heap heat rarely kills all seeds

## Compost Containers

The advantage of a container is that compost rots right up to the edges. In an open heap, edges dry out and the whole thing must be turned 2-3 times. The most useful type is a solid-sided wooden box with removable front panels, allowing easy access. Alternatives include plastic bins, wire-and-post bins, brick-built bins, and plastic barrels with drilled holes.

## Building Up a Heap

Start with a 6in (15cm) layer of coarse material (horse manure, straw, or large weeds) for air flow at the bottom. Add material until 6in deep. Sprinkle compost activator or nitrogen fertilizer. Continue alternating layers: organic material, activator, lime, until the bin is full. Cover with old carpet (breathable) or black plastic.

## Managing the Heap

Good compost is brown and crumbly with a sweet smell like fall woods. In practice it is often variable with semi-rotted fibrous material -- this is fine and will still improve soil. Grass cuttings must always be mixed with coarser material. Newspaper should be cut into 1in strips (about 1 part newspaper to 4 parts grass cuttings). Kitchen scraps should have nothing cooked on them. Natural fiber clothing can be composted if cut into strips.

From each bin, expect two good binfuls in a hot year (early summer and late fall), possibly a third in spring.

## Application Rates

At least two 2-gallon (9 liter) buckets of well-rotted compost or manure per square yard/meter in the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

## Covering the Heap

You may need to cover the compost heap with plastic sheeting in winter — not only to keep heat in but also to prevent the compost from getting too wet. A piece of old carpet is preferable to plastic because it "breathes," allowing more air into the heap. Black plastic weighted at the edges also works well and retains more heat.

## Composting Hard Materials

Woody material such as prunings from shrubs and trees should not be composted directly — they take too long to rot. Instead, use a chipper or shredder to reduce woody material to small pieces before adding to the heap. Shredded wood makes an excellent carbon-rich layer when mixed with nitrogen-rich green material.

## Sheet Composting (No-Turn Method)

An alternative to the traditional heap is [[sheet-composting]], where organic material is spread directly on the soil surface in thin layers and left to decompose in place. This approach requires no turning or bin construction and feeds [[the-soil-food-web]] directly. See [[ingham-carbon-nitrogen-ratios-compost-tea]], moisture, and aeration. It kills most weed seeds, pathogens, and pests, and produces finished compost in 2-3 months. Requires regular turning and sufficient volume (at least 3x3x3 feet).

**Cold composting** is the simpler approach of piling material and letting it decompose slowly over 6-12 months. It does not reach temperatures high enough to kill weed seeds or pathogens, but requires minimal effort. Most home gardeners use a combination of both methods.

## Using Finished Compost

- Apply to vegetable beds before planting — dig in or use as a surface mulch
- Top-dress lawns in spring and fall by raking sieved compost into the turf
- Mix with soil for potting and seed composts
- Use as a mulch around established plants (1-2 inch layer)
- Side-dress heavy-feeding crops (tomatoes, corn, brassicas) during the growing season
- Compost improves both sandy soils (water retention) and clay soils (drainage and structure)

## Common Problems and Solutions

- **Slimy, smelly compost** — Too much green material, too little air, or too wet. Add dry carbon material (straw, shredded paper), turn the heap, and ensure drainage
