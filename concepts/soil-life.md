# Soil Life

Soil is not simply a substrate for growing plants. It is a living
ecosystem, teeming with billions of organisms per teaspoon. The health
of every plant depends on the health of this underground community. In
fact, soil organisms are the first recyclers, responsible for breaking
down dead plant and animal matter into forms that living plants can
absorb. Without soil life, there would be no topsoil, no fertile land,
no food.

## The Soil Food Web

The soil food web describes the community of organisms living in soil.
At the base are decomposers: bacteria and fungi that break down
organic matter. These are fed upon by larger organisms: protozoa,
nematodes, microarthropods (mites and springtails), and earthworms.
Above these are predators: centipedes, ground beetles, spiders, and
other insects. Each level feeds the next, cycling nutrients through
the soil in an intricate web of relationships.

Bacteria-dominated soils are characteristic of disturbed or
agricultural soils and favor annual plants and grasses. Fungi-dominated
soils are found in forests and mature ecosystems and favor perennials,
shrubs, and trees. Ecological gardeners aim to shift their soil toward
fungal dominance by minimizing tilling and adding woody organic matter.

## Key Soil Organisms

### Earthworms
Earthworms are perhaps the most visible and appreciated soil organisms.
They aerate soil as they burrow, create channels for water infiltration,
and their castings (vermicompost) are rich in plant-available nutrients.
Earthworms need organic matter to thrive; a bare, chemical-fed soil
supports few worms. Charles Darwin spent 39 years studying earthworms
and called them the most important creatures on earth. Darwin's final book was
devoted entirely to their role in soil formation.

### Mycorrhizal Fungi
Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic relationships with plant roots. The
fungal threads extend far beyond the root zone, acting as extensions
of the root system to gather water and nutrients (especially
phosphorus) that the plant could not reach alone. In exchange, the
plant feeds the fungus sugars from photosynthesis. Most forest trees
and many garden plants depend on mycorrhizae. Tilling destroys these
delicate fungal networks.

### Bacteria
Soil bacteria are the most numerous organisms in soil, with billions in
a single teaspoon. They decompose simple organic compounds, fix
nitrogen from the air, and make nutrients available to plants. Different
bacterial species thrive at different pH levels and temperatures, so a
diverse soil supports diverse bacterial communities.

### Other Important Organisms
Protozoa eat bacteria and release nitrogen in plant-available form.
Beneficial nematodes prey on root-feeding nematodes and other pests.
Springtails and mites break down organic matter and help control
fungi. Ants aerate soil and bring organic material underground.

## How Soil Life Builds Fertility

Soil organisms perform several critical functions:
- **Decomposition**: Breaking down dead plant and animal material
- **Nutrient cycling**: Converting organic nutrients into mineral
  forms that plants can absorb
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Converting atmospheric nitrogen into
  plant-available form (performed by rhizobia and other bacteria)
- **Disease suppression**: Outcompeting or preying on pathogens
- **Soil structure**: Creating aggregates that improve porosity,
  water-holding capacity, and resistance to erosion

## Protecting Soil Life

The practices that support soil life are fundamental to [[building-soil]]
and ecological gardening. Key practices include:
- Minimize tilling, which destroys fungal networks and soil structure
- Keep soil covered with mulch or living plants at all times
- Add organic matter regularly (compost, leaf litter, green manures)
- Avoid synthetic fertilizers and pesticides, which harm beneficial
  organisms
- Use [[cover-crops]] to feed soil life between plantings
- Practice [[sheet-mulching]] to build soil without disturbance

When we feed the soil, the soil feeds our plants. Chemical fertilizers
bypass the soil food web, directly feeding plants but starving the
organisms that create long-term fertility. This creates dependency, as
the soil loses its ability to cycle nutrients naturally.

## The Living Skin of the Earth

Topsoil is the living skin of the earth, and it is being lost at
alarming rates. It takes nature 500 to 1,000 years to build one inch
of topsoil, yet conventional farming can lose that much in a few
decades. By understanding and nurturing soil life, ecological gardeners
can actually build topsoil rather than lose it, creating productive,
resilient landscapes that improve over time.

The lesson is clear: healthy plants need healthy soil. Chemical
fertilizers give a quick boost but ultimately impoverish the soil food
web. Building soil life through organic methods creates a self-
sustaining system where nutrients cycle naturally, diseases are held in
check, and plants receive exactly what they need through their
symbiotic relationships with soil organisms.
