# Lichenicolous Fungi and Basidiomycete Mycoparasites

Lichenicolous (lichen-inhabiting) fungi and basidiomycete mycoparasites represent a vast yet poorly understood component of fungal biodiversity. These organisms grow on or within lichens and other fungi, establishing parasitic, commensalistic, or sometimes saprotrophic relationships with their hosts. Their study reveals extraordinary ecological specialization and evolutionary adaptation, with many genera and species still awaiting formal description. They are found across nearly all major fungal lineages, but are especially diverse within the Ascomycota and, to a lesser extent, the Basidiomycota.

## Ascomycete Families Hosting Fungicolous and Lichenicolous Species

### Nectriaceae

The **Nectriaceae** harbor numerous fungicolous species, many of which remain undescribed. Within this family, **Cosmospora** is especially notable for its diversity of fungicolous species that colonize carbonized perithecia and other fungal fruiting structures. These small, brightly colored perithecial fungi are frequently encountered on dead fungal substrata in tropical and temperate regions. Additionally, [[lichenicolous-fungi]] within **Sordariales** demonstrate the breadth of habitats exploited by ascomycetes growing on other fungi, blurring the line between fungicolous and lichenicolous lifestyles.

### Clavicipitaceae

Members of the **Clavicipitaceae** are well known for their entomopathogenic and fungicolous habits. **Cordyceps** species parasitizing hypogeous fungi, particularly **Elaphomyces**, represent a remarkable trophic interaction between two major fungal phyla. These cross-phylum interactions have been documented across diverse forest ecosystems worldwide. The genus **Lecanicillium** within this clade has gained prominence in biological control due to its capacity to parasitize insect pests and plant-pathogenic fungi, illustrating the applied significance of mycoparasitic clavicipitaceous fungi.

### Niessliaceae

The **Niessliaceae** contribute several genera to the fungicolous and lichenicolous fungal inventory. **Monocillium** species are frequently isolated as fungicolous associates, often growing on decaying fungal hyphae or fruiting bodies. Lichenicolous species of **Niesslia** add to the family's ecological breadth, colonizing lichen thalli and apothecia with subtle, often overlooked infections. The fungicolous and lichenicolous members of Niessliaceae illustrate how easily these ecological niches are overlooked without targeted microscopic examination.

## Dothideomycetes: Dothideales and Pleosporales

### Tubeufiaceae and Meliolaceous Associations

Within **Dothideales** and **Pleosporales**, the **Tubeufiaceae** are remarkable for their fungicolous tendencies, particularly on meliolaceous and phyllachoraceous ascomycetes, as well as on [[rust-fungi]]. These associations represent complex three-way interactions between host plants, their biotrophic fungal colonizers, and the fungicolous tubeufiaceous secondary parasites.

### Dacampiaceae and Lichenicolous Dothideomycetes

The **Dacampiaceae** include several lichenicolous genera that have been increasingly recognized through focused surveys. Key lichenicolous genera within this group include:

- **Lichenopeltella** — forming characteristic pycnidia on lichen surfaces
- **Buelliella** — parasitizing various foliose and crustose lichens
- **Cercidospora** — an ecologically diverse genus with many lichenicolous representatives

These genera highlight the repeated, independent evolution of lichenicolous lifestyles across the Dothideomycetes.

## Lichenicolous Fungi in Major Lichen-Forming Orders

### Arthoniales

The **Arthoniales** contain some of the most conspicuous lichenicolous fungi, many of which are themselves lichenized or have lichenized ancestors. Important genera include:

- **Arthonia** — by far the most species-rich lichenicolous genus, with hosts spanning dozens of lichen families
- **Lecanographa** — forming distinctive dark, script-like fruiting bodies on crustose lichens
- **Opegrapha** — occupying an intermediate position between lichenized and lichenicolous lifestyles
- **Plectocarpon** — gall-forming parasites that induce conspicuous deformations in their hosts

### Caliciales

Within **Caliciales**, **Chaenothecopsis** is the predominant lichenicolous genus. Species of **Chaenothecopsis** are commonly found on the apothecia and thalli of diverse lichen groups, often occupying very narrow ecological niches. **Microcalicium** represents another mazaediate genus occasionally reported in lichenicolous contexts.

### Lecanorales

The **Lecanorales** harbor several important lichenicolous taxa:

- **Toninia** — some species of which are lichenicolous on other crustose lichens
- **Buellia** — a few species exhibit lichenicolous tendencies, especially on physciaceous hosts
- **Phacopsis** — a highly specialized genus parasitizing members of **Parmeliaceae**, frequently causing chlorosis and tissue necrosis in the host thallus

### Ostropales

**Odontotrema** and **Nanostictis** represent lichenicolous elements within **Ostropales**, though the group as a whole remains incompletely surveyed for fungicolous diversity.

These genera typically form small, inconspicuous apothecia that are easily overlooked in field collections.

### Verrucariales

The **Verrucariales** include numerous lichenicolous taxa, many associated with crustose lichens on rock surfaces:

- **Verrucaria** — several species are themselves parasitic lichens, complicating the definition of lichenicolous versus lichenized
- **Adelococcus** — forming minute perithecia on lichen thalli
- **Merismatium** — a genus with characteristic multiseptate ascospores, growing on stictic-acid-containing lichens
- **Norrlinia** — a rarely collected, poorly known lichenicolous genus
- **Stigmidium** — one of the most speciose lichenicolous genera, with hosts across many lichen families, often producing black, setose perithecia

The Verrucariales as a whole are among the most important orders for lichenicolous fungal diversity, yet many species remain cryptic and difficult to identify without molecular data.

## Genera Incertae Sedis

Several important lichenicolous genera remain of uncertain systematic placement:

- **Abrothallus** — a widespread and conspicuous genus parasitizing **Parmeliaceae**, forming distinctive brown to black apothecia with prominent brown setae; it is among the most commonly collected lichenicolous fungi worldwide
- **Dactylospora** — found on diverse lichen hosts, this genus requires taxonomic revision to clarify its phylogenetic affinities

## Anamorphic Conidial Ascomycetes as Fungicolous Species

Mitosporic (anamorphic) fungi contribute substantially to fungicolous diversity, though they are frequently undercollected and underreported:

- **Acremonium** — a large genus encompassing many fungicolous species; **Acremonium** sect. **Lichenoidea** groups species specifically adapted to lichen substrata
- **Verticillium** — several species are mycoparasitic on other fungi, including some lichenicolous representatives
- **Lecanicillium** — as noted under Clavicipitaceae, this genus bridges the teleomorph-anamorph divide with significant fungicolous and entomopathogenic capabilities
- **Chalara** — typically associated with dead plant material but occasionally encountered as fungicolous colonizers

These anamorphic taxa are often identified only through culture-based methods, suggesting that environmental DNA surveys would likely reveal substantially greater diversity. Improved culture-independent sampling approaches, including metagenomics and high-throughput sequencing of lichen surfaces, are expected to uncover substantial hidden diversity among these conidial fungi.

## Basidiomycota: Mycoparasitism Overview

### Urediniomycetes

The **Urediniomycetes** contain several lineages with mycoparasitic tendencies. The **Platygloeomycetidae** are of particular interest, possessing specialized structures known as **lenticular bodies** or **colacosomes** — membrane-bound organelles involved in adhesion to and penetration of host fungal hyphae. Key orders include:

- **Atractiellales** — containing species with poorly known mycoparasitic potential
- **Platygloeales** — some members of which associate with **Cystobasidiaceae** in complex interactions
- **Cryptomycocolacales** — an enigmatic, rarely collected order with obligate mycoparasitic members
- **Heterogastridiales** — representing another poorly understood mycoparasitic lineage within the rust fungi

### Ustilaginomycetes

The **Ustilaginomycetes** include genera with demonstrated mycoparasitic activity, particularly among yeast-like forms.

- **Tilletiopsis** — yeast-like species capable of parasitizing powdery mildews and other plant-pathogenic fungi, with biocontrol potential
- **Pseudozyma** — anamorphic ustilaginomycetous yeasts, some of which exhibit antifungal activity and have been investigated as biological control agents

## Ecological and Taxonomic Significance

Lichenicolous fungi and basidiomycete mycoparasites are critical yet understudied components of fungal ecosystems. Their ecological roles range from benign commensalism to aggressive parasitism capable of killing host lichens or fungi. Many species are highly host-specific, making them valuable indicators of lichen community health and composition. Despite their ecological importance, the majority of lichenicolous and fungicolous species remain undescribed, representing one of the most significant gaps in our understanding of global fungal biodiversity. Molecular phylogenetic studies continue to reveal unexpected diversity and novel lineages within these groups, underscoring the need for comprehensive taxonomic surveys and revised classification frameworks. Future research integrating morphological study, culture work, molecular systematics, and metagenomic approaches will be essential to document the full extent of lichenicolous and mycoparasitic fungal diversity and to elucidate the evolutionary origins of these fascinating ecological strategies.
