# Water Design Checklist and Dam Construction

## Overview

Water design is a foundational element of any permaculture property. Bill Mollison emphasizes that every property must have its water resources identified, analyzed for quality and quantity, and sites reserved for tanks, swales, or dams. Gravity-fed systems should be prioritized wherever slope permits, and plant lists should be developed for mature plants and trees that can grow without irrigation.

## Key Design Principles

### Water Source Identification

On any property, the designer must:

- **Identify all sources of water** — springs, streams, runoff, rainfall, and groundwater
- **Analyze water quality and quantity** — test for contaminants, mineral content, and seasonal variation
- **Reserve sites for storage** — tanks, swales, and dams should be sited before other development
- **Use slope to advantage** — raise tanks or use elevation to enable gravity flow to use points

### Soil Analysis for Dam Construction

Soil samples are critical for identifying sites suited to earth-dam construction. A minimum of 40% clay content is required for dam walls. The "jar test" is a practical field method:

1. Collect soil samples from several typical sites
2. Mix and place a cup of soil in a tall jar filled nearly to the top with water
3. Shake vigorously and let settle over 1–7 days
4. Observe the layered fractions: coarse sand (settles first), fine sand, silt, and clay (may remain suspended for up to a week)
5. Estimate the proportion of each fraction by volume

### Arid vs. Humid Area Strategies

Where **evaporation exceeds precipitation** (arid areas), all water runoff must be infiltrated to soil storages through:

- **Rip-lines** — shallow channels that break compacted soil surfaces
- **Swales** — level trenches on contour that capture and infiltrate runoff
- **Pits** — deep infiltration points for concentrated flow
- **Sand-field soakages** — engineered sand beds that accept and distribute water

In **humid areas**, open-surface dams can be used more freely since evaporation losses are lower relative to precipitation.

## Water Pathways and Greywater

Water use in houses should be economical, with greywater directed to gardens through filtration beds, forests, or communal effluent treatment schemes. Mollison advocates for maximum use of waste water through:

- **Methane digestion** of sewage for biogas production
- **Plant production** using nutrient-rich effluent
- **Irrigation** of orchards and food forests after appropriate treatment

## Dam Construction Guidelines

### Critical Safety Considerations

- **Get good advice and supervise construction** of all dams
- **Do not impede normal stream flow** or fish migration
- **Site houses out of the way** in case of dam failure
- **Provide adequate stable spillway flow** designed for worst-case rain intensity

### Swale and Earth Storage Management

All earth storages, particularly swales, must be planted with trees to:

- Remove infiltrated water (preventing waterlogging)
- Prevent salting problems in arid areas through transpiration

### Keyline Systems

A sequence of primary valleys may enable a **Keyline system** to be established for:

- Downhill fire control
- Irrigation distribution across the landscape

The Keyline system, developed by P.A. Yeomans, uses the natural topography of land to manage water flow from ridges to valleys, maximizing infiltration and minimizing erosion.

## Natural Swimming Pools

Innovative pool designers filter natural pools below a base pebble bed, using pebbles as algal and bacterial cleaners. Water is then cycled through a reed-bed to remove excess nutrients before cascading back, freshly oxygenated, into the pool. Benefits include:

- **Chemical-free water** — no chlorine needed
- **Ecological habitat** — tame fish, crayfish, rock ledges, over-arching ferns
- **Multi-functionality** — fire-fighting reserves, heat sources for heat pumps, fire barriers, emergency water supplies
- **Solar-powered recycling** — photovoltaic pumps can maintain circulation
- **Elimination of servicing** — self-maintaining biological systems replace endless chemical treatments

## Cloud Seeding Considerations

Before recommending cloud-seeding, ensure that:

- The area to be affected is warned
- Dams and swales are designed to cope with any increase (up to 30%) in rainfall

## Forested Ridges and Windbreaks

- **Design for forested ridges** and maximize forest on strategic uplands
- **Do not lend skills to high-country deforestation** or any deforestation
- **Windbreaks and in-crop trees** are essential to reduce water loss in croplands

## Soil Formation and Topsoil Loss

Topsoil builds at approximately 2–4 tonnes per hectare per year as uncompacted soil. However, conventional cropping and tillage remove soil at rates of 40–500 tonnes per year. In the worst cases, 2,000 years of soil can be lost in a single erosion season from one sequence of flood or strong wind over cultivated soils.

### Where Soils Are Conserved or Increased

1. **In uncut forest** — the natural soil-building ecosystem
2. **Under the quiet water of lakes and ponds** — sediment deposition
3. **In prairies and meadows** of permanent plants — deep root systems stabilize soil
4. **Where plants are grown with mulched or non-tillage systems** — protecting soil structure

## Soil Formation Processes

Soils are either **residual** (resting in place over their parent rock) or **transported** by water, ice, gravity, and wind. Key formation factors include:

- **Rock type** — determines mineral content
- **Climate** — temperature and rainfall drive weathering rates
- **Topography/landform** — affects drainage, erosion, and deposition

Water plays a central role in rock breakdown:

- Combines with felspars in rock to swell and fracture them
- Hydrates felspars to kaolin, clay, and potassium carbonate
- Atmospheric oxygen dissolves in rain to oxidize iron minerals (pyrites), forming haematite and sulphuric acid
- Sulphuric acid dissolves metals, making water an effective rock decomposer

## Soil as a Living System

Soil is not merely dirt — it is a complex, living system composed of:

- **Minerals** — mainly silica, iron oxides, aluminium oxides, and complex minerals
- **Soil waters** — fresh or saline, with dissolved minerals and gases
- **Gases** — from the atmosphere, rock breakdown, and the earth's interior
- **Life forms** — from bacterial and fungal spores to roots and burrowing animals
- **Once-living remains** — humus, decayed and fossil organic material

Soils rarely extend much below 1–2 meters, and are more often a living system only 6–12 cm deep. The soil mantle covering the earth is as thin as the shine on the skin of an orange.
