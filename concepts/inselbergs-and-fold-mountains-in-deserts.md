# Inselbergs and Fold Mountains in Deserts

Inselbergs (rock domes) and fold mountains are two of the most extensive and productive landform types in arid regions. While simpler than scarp and wadi systems, inselbergs offer concentrated runoff collection, and fold mountains present the most complex and water-rich opportunities for desert settlement and forestry.

## Residual Domes and Inselbergs

Inselbergs are massive isolated domes of granite or metamorphosed sandstone that rise steeply from the desert plain. Great examples include Uluru (Ayers Rock) in Australia and many similar massifs worldwide. Compared with wadi and scarp systems, they are relatively simple:

- They rarely develop deep caves (though some rock shelters exist).
- They rarely have pronounced valley entries.
- They usually dip steeply into loose sandy soils around the base.

Despite this simplicity, inselbergs are very solid runoff areas, some covering many tens of hectares. Their design potential rests on the runoff-to-field ratio and the microenvironments created at their margins.

### Water Harvesting from Inselbergs

The fundamental strategy is to allocate approximately 1 hectare of field to every 20 hectares of bare rock catchment. Base floods from rainfall on the dome can be led via fluming (open channels) to walled fields, supporting both crops and fruit trees or palms. Specific techniques include:

- **Concrete or stone gutters** on bare domes to collect and direct runoff.
- **Rock slab gutters** or concreted drains to serve household water needs.
- **Small tanks** with access ramps for wildlife (pigeon, quail, small mammals, frogs), thatched to retard evaporation and exclude large animals.
- **Shallow mulch-filled valleys** on the massif itself, where soils are mainly [[humus]] and can support trees, yams, and other vegetation.

Occasionally, steep and shaded clefts on inselbergs present sites for rock dams, but for the most part, partly sealed tanks and infiltration basins must suffice. Larger systems (100 hectares or more) have been occupied in historical times by careful and conservative peoples, usually as base camps for long-range foraging after rains. Each has a special character that calls for very sensitive and restricted occupancy — developing and protecting natural resources rather than attempting extensive systems.

### Small Rock Slabs

Very small rock outcrops (1–10 hectares) are useful for a gutter and tank system, a hectare or two of trees, and a wayside house. Many have been guttered using rock walls (slab on edge) or concreted drains to serve household water needs or, historically, steam train water supplies in remote locations. Many more could support wildlife ramp-tanks for desert birds and small mammals.

## Fold Mountains

Fold mountains are the most extensive features in many deserts, with characteristics combining those of inselbergs and scarps but generally far more complex than either. They arise from tectonic forces that create alternating structural features.

### Geological Structure

Folded strata in deserts create two primary landforms:

- **Synclines** (down-flexed sediments): These may erode to great canoe shapes, with a few river gorges cutting their way out.
- **Anticlines** (up-flexed sediments): These may form great whaleback ridges, but over time a river forms on their spines and produces a long valley in the hills, faced on each side by cliffs or steep tilted sediments.

Eventually, a typical basin and range topography evolves from these folded series. Fold mountains commonly occur in large series, with correspondingly large catchment areas.

### Water Storage Opportunities

A dam at the exit of a river from fold mountain ranges can create a large ovoid freshwater lake in the interior. The permanence of this water depends on the ratio of runoff area to storage area — this must be 20–30:1 in deserts. Therefore, modest and deep storages are more likely to hold water year-round than large shallow storages. Such dams require careful hydrological assessment and engineering.

Key advantages of fold mountain dams:

- Wherever massive ranges back the fold mountains (as in Colorado, Nevada, and near Mt. Ararat in Turkey), foothill dams will hold water from a combination of snow melt and rain runoff.
- Few settlements exist in deserts, so dams can be sited well away from flood paths, whereas in well-settled areas large dams tend to be built above existing valley settlements with frequently disastrous consequences during war or earthquake.

### Modest Dam Systems

A great many more modest dams are available in fold systems than large-scale reservoirs:

- **Narrow rock-walled exit valleys** can be dammed simply.
- **Foothill dams** capture exit waters via diversion structures.
- **Complex freshwater systems** can be led off low-slope wadi-like streams as they flow to the plains.
- **Permanent rockholes and streams** are characteristic of fold systems, owing to their large series and corresponding catchment sizes.

A vigorous day of reconnaissance around potential village sites usually reveals many possible storages wherever ranges are sufficiently large to shed useful volumes of water.

### Palisades and Rock Strata

All fold mountains of sedimentary or metamorphic rock show more or less marked palisades of harder rock series running along the slopes like sub-contours. Wherever vegetation exists, it is far taller, greener, and denser on the upslope side of such palisades, because runoff is slowed and forced to infiltrate there. Some palisades are wide-spaced, well on contour, project well above the surface, and develop striking tree-lines.

Wherever series of palisades are a feature, a horizontal bore will often tap sweet water trapped in the rock strata. Unlike qanats, such water can be sealed off and used only as needed, and regulated to control the amount taken from the aquifer.

### Headwater Stream Diversion

A shallow, fast dry-stream system typically develops from the upper foothill regions of folds — often stream sequences only 4–15 metres wide with banks 0.5–2 metres high. The bed is usually rock-filled. As in wadis, flood periods are violent and sudden when they occur.

The strategy for these violent flows is not to dam them directly (which would fill with boulders and silt in a few rains) but to bleed off the torrent and divert it to a more peaceful contour flow, and thence to a dam. An oversized, wide, shallow contour trench allows silt to settle out before reaching the dam, and the trench itself provides a planting site and a silt trap that is easily cleaned.
