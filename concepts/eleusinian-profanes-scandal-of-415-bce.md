# Eleusinian Profanes Scandal of 415 BCE

**Source:** The Road to Eleusis, Chapter III — Carl A. P. Ruck

## Overview

In 415 BCE, on the eve of the disastrous Sicilian Expedition, a scandal erupted in Athens when Alcibiades and his associates were accused of "profaning" the [[lesser-and-greater-eleusinian-mysteries]] by parodying the sacred rites in a private home. The scandal led to Alcibiades' political destruction and became one of the most notorious episodes in Athenian history.

## The Accusation

Alcibiades, then Athens' most prominent and controversial politician, was accused along with his friend Andocides of performing a mock version of the Eleusinian initiation in a private house. The specific charges included:

- Parodying the [[eleusinian-telesterion-architecture-and-ritual]] ceremonies in a domestic setting

- Revealing or mimicking the sacred words and actions normally performed by the [[eleusinian-hierophantic-families-eumolpidae-kerykes]]

- Performing the rites outside the proper ritual context

The accusations emerged just as the Athenian fleet was about to depart for Sicily, and the political timing was almost certainly not coincidental.

## The Severity of the Offense

The extreme severity of the response — Alcibiades faced the death penalty — reveals the profound importance the Athenians attached to the Mysteries. Profaning the rites was not mere sacrilege but a direct threat to the civic and spiritual order.

The scandal demonstrates that the Mysteries were not "just" religious ceremonies but were central to Athenian identity. Participation was essentially universal among citizens, and the secrecy surrounding the rites was considered a civic obligation. Breaking this secrecy was akin to treason.

## The Pharmacological Implication

From the perspective of the [[wasson-hofmann-ruck-collaboration]], the scandal takes on additional significance. If the [[kykeon-composition-and-ergot-alkaloid-hypothesis]] contained a psychoactive substance, then the "profanation" was not merely a symbolic parody but an attempt to replicate the Eleusinian experience outside its carefully controlled ritual context — analogous to the [[psychedelic-secrecy-comparative-eleusis-mesoamerica]] after Wasson's publication.

The Athenians' horrified response would then reflect an intuitive understanding that the Mysteries' power depended on the precise combination of [[set-and-setting-eleusinian-initiation-methodology]] — and that casual replication would be not merely offensive but dangerous.

## Andocides' Testimony

Andocides, who turned informant to save himself, provided the most detailed surviving account of the affair. His testimony (in the speech "On the Mysteries") describes the mock ceremonies but carefully avoids revealing the actual content of the genuine rites — demonstrating that even the profaners maintained some respect for the oath of silence.

## Political Context

The scandal must be understood within the context of Athens' Peloponnesian War crisis:

- Athens was deeply divided between war and peace factions

- Alcibiades was the leading advocate of the Sicilian expedition

- His opponents (including Nicias) may have used the scandal to eliminate a political rival

- The episode contributed to the paralysis of Athenian democracy during a critical military moment

## Alcibiades' Flight and Downfall

Rather than face trial in Athens, Alcibiades fled to Sparta, where he provided military advice to Athens' enemies — advice that proved devastating to the Athenian war effort. His defection was directly caused by the profanation charges, making the scandal one of the most consequential episodes of the Peloponnesian War.

The Athenians condemned Alcibiades to death in absentia and officially cursed him. His remarkable later career — including a return to Athenian favor, further exile, and eventual assassination — made him one of the most colorful and controversial figures in classical history. But the profanation charges permanently associated his name with sacrilege.

## The Trial and Punishment of Accomplices

Several of Alcibiades' alleged accomplices were tried and convicted. Andocides himself was imprisoned but escaped execution by turning informant. Other defendants were executed or exiled. The severity of the punishments — including execution for what might seem to modern eyes a prank — underscores the sacred status of the Mysteries in Athenian society.

Notably, the [[eleusinian-hierophantic-families-eumolpidae-kerykes]] themselves pressed the charges aggressively, demonstrating their institutional power and their determination to protect the secrecy of the rites even during a time of political crisis.

## Legacy

The scandal became a cautionary tale about the consequences of profaning sacred knowledge. It also inadvertently provides some of the best evidence for the power and importance of the Eleusinian Mysteries — the extreme reaction of the Athenian state demonstrates that what happened at Eleusis was considered genuinely transformative and dangerous.

The [[the-profanation-of-415-bc-and-evidence-for-eleusinian-psychoactivity]] remains a key piece of evidence for scholars arguing that the Eleusinian Mysteries involved more than symbolic ritual. The disproportionate response suggests that the Athenians themselves understood the rites as involving genuinely powerful — and potentially dangerous — forces.
