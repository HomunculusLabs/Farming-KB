# Iron, Manganese, and Zinc Micronutrient Management

Zinc, iron, and manganese are the three most
common micronutrients found deficient in cannabis
gardens. Deficiencies of all three plague many more
grow rooms than most growers realize. Often
deficiencies of all three occur concurrently,
especially when the soil or water pH is above 6.5.
All three share the same initial symptom:
interveinal chlorosis of young leaves. Cervantes
provides detailed identification and treatment
guidance for each.

## Chelation and Availability

A chelate (Greek for claw) is an organic molecule
that forms a claw-like bond with free electrically
charged metal particles. This keeps metal ions such
as zinc, iron, and manganese soluble in water and
suppresses reactions with other materials. Roots
take in chelated metals in a stable, soluble form
that is used immediately.

Different chelate types work at different pH
ranges:
- DTPA is most effective in a pH below 6.5
- EDDHA is effective up to a pH of 8
- EDTA chelate is slow to cause leaf burn

Chelates decompose rapidly in low levels of
ultraviolet light, including light from HID bulbs
and sunlight. Keep chelates out of light to
protect them from rapid decomposition. Natural
chelates such as humic acid and citric acid can
be added to organic soil mixes.

## Zinc (Zn) - Mobile

Zinc is the most common micronutrient found
deficient in arid climates and alkaline soils.
Zinc works with manganese and magnesium to
promote enzyme functions, helps form chlorophyll,
and is essential for stem growth and sugar and
protein production. Deficiencies are most common
in soils with a pH of 7 or more.

### Deficiency Symptoms

Younger leaves exhibit interveinal chlorosis, and
new leaves and growing tips develop small, thin
blades that contort and wrinkle. Leaf tips and
margins discolor and burn. Flower buds also
contort into odd shapes, turn crispy dry, and are
often hard. A lack of zinc stunts overall growth
including buds.

### Treatment

Flush the growing medium with a diluted mix of a
complete fertilizer containing chelated trace
elements, including zinc, iron, and manganese. Or
add a quality hydroponic micronutrient mix
containing chelated trace elements.

### Toxicity

Zinc is extremely toxic in excess. Severely toxic
plants die quickly. Excess zinc interferes with
iron's ability to function properly and causes an
iron deficiency.

## Manganese (Mn) - Immobile

Manganese is engaged in the oxidation-reduction
process associated with photosynthetic electron
transport. It activates many enzymes and plays a
fundamental part in the chloroplast membrane
system. Manganese assists nitrogen utilization
along with iron in chlorophyll production.

### Deficiency Symptoms

Young leaves show symptoms first: yellowing
between veins (interveinal chlorosis) while veins
remain green. Symptoms spread from younger to
older leaves as deficiency progresses. Necrotic
(dead) spots develop on severely affected leaves,
which become pale and fall off. Overall plant
growth is stunted. Severe deficiency looks like
severe magnesium deficiency.

### Treatment

Lower the pH, leach the soil, and add a complete
chelated micronutrient formula. Toxicity from low
pH can cause toxic manganese intake, which causes
iron and zinc deficiency.

## Iron (Fe) - Immobile

Iron is fundamental to enzyme systems and electron
transport during photosynthesis, respiration, and
chlorophyll production. Iron deficiencies are
common when the pH is above 6.5 and uncommon below
6.5. Acidic soils normally contain adequate iron.

### Deficiency Symptoms

Young leaves are unable to draw immobile iron from
older leaves. The first symptoms appear on smaller
leaves as veins remain green and areas between
veins turn yellow, starting at the petiole end.
Leaf edges can turn upward as deficiency progresses.
Leaves fall off in severe cases. Iron deficiency
is sometimes traced to an excess of copper.

### Treatment

Lower soil pH to 6.5 or less. Avoid fertilizers
with excessive manganese, zinc, and copper which
inhibit iron uptake. High phosphorus competes with
iron uptake. Improve drainage; excessively wet soil
holds little oxygen to spur iron uptake. Apply
chelated iron in liquid form to the root zone.
Leaves should green up in four or five days.
