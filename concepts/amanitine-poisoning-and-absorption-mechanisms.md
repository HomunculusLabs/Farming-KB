## Amanitine Poisoning and Absorption Mechanisms

## Overview

[[amanitine]] (also known as muscarine, named by Schmiedeberg and Koppe) is the deadly alkaloid found in mushrooms of the *Amanita* genus. It is one of the most powerful and insidious poisons in the natural world — tasteless, odorless, and capable of killing hours after ingestion, long after the victim believes they have escaped harm. Palmer"s 1879 documentation of this poison represents some of the earliest systematic study of fungal toxicology in English.

## Nomenclature of the Poison

The deadly principle in *Amanita* species has been given several names by different researchers:

- **Amanitine** — the name used by Dr. Letellier, probably derived from study of multiple *Amanita* species
- **Muscarine** — the designation given by German chemists Schmiedeberg and Koppe, isolated from *Amanita muscarius*
- **Bulbosine** — Dr. Boudier's name for the alkaloid he obtained from *[[amanita-bulbosa]]*

Palmer considered "Amanitine" the best general term, as it encompasses all variants and includes the others. The effects of all three isolates are nearly identical in their action on the human system.

## Three Modes of Fungal Poisoning

Palmer classified [[amanita-toxins-and-mushroom-poisoning]] into three distinct categories:

### 1. Indigestion from Physical Properties
Hard, tough varieties of fungi, or the common mushroom (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*) in a partly decomposed state, can generate hydrogen gas in sufficient quantity to produce nausea and vomiting. This is mechanical, not chemical poisoning, and passes through natural channels.

### 2. Acrid or Nauseous Elements
Many fungi contain irritating compounds that produce immediate symptoms. Mild Boleti can be too slimy for food. Russulas include very acrid varieties. *[[polyporus-squamosus]]* and others are bitter, with the taste of leaves and grass. These cause prompt rejection by the digestive system and are rarely dangerous.

### 3. Amanitine — The Silent Killer
The third and most dangerous category involves the subtle alkaloid found in the *Amanita* family. This poison has no taste, no smell, and no indication of its presence whatsoever. It passes harmlessly through the stomach mixed with other food. From eight to fifteen hours after ingestion, dizziness, nausea, and purging begin, followed by fainting, delirium, and death.

## Case Reports

### Case 1: Sautry, France
Two persons made a meal from *Amanita bulbosa* (under which the French include *A. verna*, *A. phalloides*, and *A. mappa*). Beyond initial vertigo in one case, no ill effects were felt until three and eight o"clock the following morning. One patient even slept calmly after the vertigo passed. Once attacked, the usual symptoms followed, and within three days both were dead. The physician tried coffee, tea, antimony, ether, whites of eggs, laudanum, milk, leeches, ice, and emollient fomentations. He reported: "None of the remedies were followed by the least sign of amelioration." Only ice internally and emollients externally provided any simple alleviation.

### Case 2: October 1876
Four persons ate the fatal meal at four o"clock in the afternoon. About midnight, all who had partaken were attacked. The first death occurred Thursday at ten a.m.; the second on Friday morning. One woman recovered, experiencing only severe diarrhea — she had put vinegar on the toadstool before eating, which may have neutralized the alkaloid.

### Case 3: Recent Case with Children
Two children died within thirty-six hours. The father experienced severe delirium, jumping from a second-story window. The attack came on about eight hours after ingestion. In all cases, up to the actual attack, no person felt the worse for the fatal meal.

## Palmer's Theory of Absorption

Palmer proposed that the alkaloid enters the system by absorption through the villi (the lining of the intestinal canal). He argued that were every vestige of the mushroom removed from the stomach, the system might still be so charged with poison as to refuse to rally. The poison is absorbed independently of the physical fungus material, meaning that expelling the mushroom does not expel the poison.

### Cross-Contamination Between Species
Palmer observed that mushrooms can absorb each other's properties — either with or without direct contact. If edible and noxious toadstools are gathered together, the former will absorb the properties of the latter. Their "virus" exercises the same power of permeation through the human system that yeast (itself a true fungus) has over dough.

He documented a [[palmer-mushroom-cooking-and-personal-experience]] where [[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]] and edible ones had lain together in a box for twelve hours. After rejecting the noxious specimens and eating the washed edible ones, he experienced violent perspiration, vertigo, trembling, chills, nausea, purging, and tenesmus — all within thirty minutes. Since the substance could not have reached the bowels that quickly, he concluded the venom had permeated the entire system through the harmless mushrooms.

## Dermal and Inhalation Absorption

Palmer demonstrated that absorption can occur through the skin and respiratory system:

- An *Amanita* held in a closed hand can produce all symptoms of poisoning, even to convulsions
- While perspiring from a long walk, he carried a bunch of *Amanitas* in a warm hand protected only by paper wrapper. A fearful nausea followed, with all symptoms of seasickness
- Merely opening a box containing *Amanita* specimens and smelling them a few times produced, three hours later, an attack of vomiting and stomach oppression. The whites of his eyes became livid, and a leaden facial color persisted until noon the following day
- Chewing a piece the size of a half-dime and ejecting the substance but swallowing saliva produced throat constriction, vertigo, and sea-sickness-like disgust. Seventeen hours later, vomiting and tenesmus followed

## Diagnostic Significance

These observations are of great clinical importance. The time elapsed between a suspected mushroom meal and the onset of symptoms indicates the type of poisoning:

- **Short delay** (immediate to a few hours): suggests acrid or irritant elements — good prognosis
- **Long delay** (eight hours or more): indicates Amanitine poisoning — grave prognosis, as the system has absorbed the poison

Physicians should always inquire carefully about the elapsed time, as this determines both the nature of the poison and the appropriate treatment strategy.
