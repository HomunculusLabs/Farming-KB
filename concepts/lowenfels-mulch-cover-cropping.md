# Mulch and Cover Cropping

## Overview

Mulch is any material placed on the soil surface to protect and improve it. In
the context of the soil food web, organic mulches serve as both food for soil
organisms and a tool for managing the fungal:bacterial (F:B) ratio of the soil.
Mulch is one of the three primary tools of soil food web gardening (along with
compost and compost tea). Proper mulching is critical because it determines
which types of microbes thrive, which in turn determines what nutrients are
available and what plants will succeed.

## What Is Organic Mulch?

Organic mulch is any formerly living material placed on the soil surface:
leaves, straw, wood chips, bark, grass clippings, shredded newspaper, pine
needles, hay, and compost. These materials feed soil organisms as they
decompose, build soil structure, moderate soil temperature, retain moisture,
and suppress weeds. The key principle: mulch feeds the soil food web, and the
soil food web feeds the plants.

## Bacterial vs. Fungal Mulch

The type of mulch determines whether bacteria or fungi will dominate:

**Bacterial mulches** -- Fresh, green materials with high nitrogen content:
grass clippings, green leafy material, young plant residues. These decompose
quickly and favor bacterial growth. Best for vegetable gardens, annual beds,
and lawns where bacterial soils are preferred. Green mulches should be applied
in thin layers (1-2 inches) to avoid matting and anaerobic conditions.

**Fungal mulches** -- Aged, brown materials with high carbon content: wood
chips, bark, fall leaves, straw, pine needles, shredded newspaper. These
decompose slowly and favor fungal growth. Best for trees, shrubs, perennials,
and other plants that prefer fungal soils. Coarse, dry mulches are
particularly effective at promoting fungal activity.

## Surface vs. Incorporated

How mulch is applied matters as much as what mulch is used:

- **Surface application** -- Mulch left on the soil surface favors fungi.
  Fungi can grow hyphae from the soil surface into the mulch, decomposing it
  without disturbance. This is the preferred method for trees and shrubs.

- **Incorporation (mixing into soil)** -- Mulch that is dug or tilled into
  the soil favors bacteria. The physical mixing breaks up fungal hyphae and
  distributes organic matter throughout the soil where bacteria can access it.
  This is acceptable for vegetable gardens where bacterial soils are desired.

## Moisture and Decomposition Rate

Wet, ground-up mulch decomposes faster and favors bacterial colonization.
Water activates bacterial populations and speeds the breakdown of organic
material. Coarse, dry mulches decompose more slowly and favor fungal
colonization. If the goal is fungal dominance, use coarse materials and keep
them relatively dry on the surface. If the goal is bacterial dominance, use
finely ground materials and ensure adequate moisture.

## Nitrogen Tie-Up

When mulches with high carbon:nitrogen ratios (wood chips, straw, sawdust)
are incorporated into soil, soil microbes use available nitrogen to break down
the carbon. This temporarily reduces the nitrogen available to plants -- called
"nitrogen tie-up" or "nitrogen robbery." The effect is temporary; once
decomposition is well underway, nitrogen is released back to the soil. Surface
application of high-carbon mulch minimizes nitrogen tie-up because only the
interface layer contacts the soil, keeping the decomposition zone shallow.

## Mulch and Protozoa

Mulch provides ideal habitat for protozoa. The moist layer between mulch and
soil surface is where protozoa thrive, feeding on bacteria that colonize the
decomposing material. A healthy protozoa population means efficient nitrogen
cycling: protozoa eat bacteria and release ammonium for plant uptake. This is
one reason why mulched gardens tend to be more productive -- the protozoa layer
under the mulch is a nitrogen factory.

## Weed Suppression

A 2-3 inch layer of organic mulch effectively suppresses weed seed
germination by blocking light from reaching the soil surface. Most weed seeds
require light to germinate. Mulch also creates a physical barrier that prevents
existing weed seeds from reaching the surface. For no-till gardens, mulch is
the primary weed control strategy, replacing cultivation and herbicides
entirely.

## Cover Cropping

Cover crops are living mulches -- plants grown specifically to protect and
improve soil. While the book does not dedicate a chapter to cover crops, they
fit naturally into the soil food web framework:

- **Legume cover crops** (clover, vetch, peas) fix atmospheric nitrogen
  through Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules, adding nitrogen to the soil
  food web when the cover crop is terminated.

- **Grass cover crops** (rye, oats, annual ryegrass) produce dense root
  systems that feed bacterial populations and build soil structure through
  root exudates and root turnover.

- **Brassica cover crops** (radishes, mustards) produce deep taproots that
  break compaction and bring minerals from deep soil layers to the surface.

- **Mixed cover crops** combine multiple species for maximum soil food web
  diversity, providing food sources for bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and
  nematodes simultaneously.

## The Soil Food Web Rules for Mulching

From the book's 19 Soil Food Web Gardening Rules:

- Rule 6: Aged, brown materials promote fungi; fresh, green materials
  promote bacteria.
- Rule 7: Surface mulch promotes fungi; incorporated mulch promotes
  bacteria.
- Rule 8: Wet, ground-up mulch promotes faster bacterial colonization.
- Rule 9: Coarse, dry mulches promote fungal activity.
