# Set and Setting Model in Psychedelic Therapy

## Overview

The "set and setting" hypothesis is the foundational principle of psychedelic
therapy, first articulated by Timothy Leary, George Litwin, and Ralph Metzner
in 1963. It holds that the primary determinants of a [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]] are
the internal *set* (intention, expectation, motivation) and the external
*setting* (context, environment, guide presence). The drug itself plays the role
of a catalyst. This model distinguishes hallucinogens from all other
psychoactive drugs — including stimulants, depressants, and narcotics — where
pharmacological action is paramount and set/setting play a minor role.

## Historical Development

### Psychotomimetic to Psycholytic

When LSD's properties were discovered at Sandoz labs in the 1940s, it was
initially characterized as "psychotomimetic" (mimicking psychosis). Military
agencies explored it as a weapon for "maximum confusion, disorientation or
embarrassment." Ironically, LSD's capacity to tap into mystical potentials
ruined its applicability as a weapon — rather than producing predictable
submission, it released subjects into "awesome worlds of cosmic consciousness."

The first Sandoz research described LSD as producing "seelische Auflockerung"
(psychic loosening or opening) — the psycholytic concept that became the
dominant European model. In [[european-psycholytic-therapy-1960s-1970s]], neurotic patients received
LSD in gradually increasing doses during analytic sessions using a Freudian
framework. The rationale was that through "psycholysis" — the loosening of
defenses — patients would gain awareness of unconscious emotional dynamics.

Stanislav Grof, working within this model, discovered that deeper psychic
openings occurred at higher doses, revealing birth and prebirth memories. He
named these patterns "[[metzner-grof-perinatal-matrices-and-transpersonal-realms]]."

### The Psychedelic Model

The psychedelic model was developed by Humphrey Osmond, working with Abram
Hoffer in Canada on LSD treatment of alcoholism. Osmond coined the term
"psychedelic" in correspondence with Aldous Huxley. Psychedelic therapy
typically involved one or a small number of high-dose sessions producing vivid
hallucinatory imagery leading to insight and transformation.

At Harvard, Leary, Frank Barron, and Richard Alpert shifted focus from
treatment to exploring psychedelic possibilities for "normals" — graduate
students, artists, musicians, and writers — introducing the concept of
"[[mckenna-consciousness-expansion-and-drug-war]]" contrasted with the contracted awareness of
addictions and obsessions.

### Empathogens: The Phenethylamine Extension

Alexander Shulgin's discovery of phenethylamines (MDA, MDMA, 2-CB) extended the
field. These substances expand awareness primarily at the emotional/heart level
with minimal perceptual changes. MDMA was used with "impressive success in
psychotherapy," facilitating relationship communication and healing trauma.
The term "empathogens" ("generating a state of empathy") was proposed to
distinguish this class from classical hallucinogens.

## Five Shared Principles

Despite theoretical differences, psycholytic and psychedelic approaches share
fundamental features that distinguish them from other psychoactive drug use:

1. **Expanded consciousness**: Psychotherapy with hallucinogens invariably
   produces a profoundly expanded state where individuals can gain therapeutic
   insight and may question fundamental self-concepts and views of reality.

2. **Set and setting as primary**: The drug is a catalyst; internal intention
   and external context are the true determinants. This model extends to
   non-drug altered states — hypnosis, meditation, drumming, fasting, sensory
   isolation.

3. **Amplifier and microscope analogies**: The drug functions as a nonspecific
   amplifier of psychic contents (lowering sensory thresholds, "cleansing the
   doors of perception") and as a microscope for psychology — opening hidden
   realms of the mind to direct, repeatable, verifiable observation.

4. **Experienced therapist essential**: Personal psychedelic experience is
   considered "an essential prerequisite of effective [[metzner-psychedelic-psychotherapy-clinical-history]]."
   Without it, communication with a client in a psychedelic state is "severely
   limited." This implies a role for psychedelic experience in therapist training.

5. **Transpersonal access**: Mystical and spiritual experiences occur with
   psychedelics, posing both challenge and promise to psychology. Grof named
   these "transpersonal" realms and "holotropic" (seeking the whole) states.

## Evolutionary Remembering

Leary, stimulated by Aldous Huxley, Huston Smith, and Alan Watts, recognized
that the fundamental mystical vision in psychedelic states is an "evolutionary
remembering" — reconnecting with biological and cosmological evolution. The
experience goes beyond personal and cultural developmental issues; "the
language of mystics and shamans in our time was basically going to be the
scientific language of evolution."

## The Tibetan Buddhist Connection

Leary adapted the Tibetan Buddhist *Bardo Thödol* as a guidebook for
psychedelic experience (*The Psychedelic Experience*, 1964, co-authored with
Metzner and Alpert). This was followed by adaptations of the Taoist *Tao Te
Ching*. Both source traditions contained strong shamanic elements involving
connection with spirits of nature through visionary states — suggesting a deep
structural resonance between Eastern contemplative traditions and psychedelic
experience.

## Distinction from Conventional Psychopharmacology

Psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy is fundamentally different from conventional
psychiatric drug use (tranquilizers, antidepressants) where the patient takes a
pill and goes home. Key distinctions include:
- The experience is conducted in a controlled, supportive setting
- A guide or therapist is present throughout
- The drug serves as a catalyst for a therapeutic process, not as a chronic
  medication
- Set and setting are actively managed rather than left to chance
- The therapist's own experience with the substance is considered relevant

## Legacy and Revival

The history of psychedelic therapy was summarized by Grinspoon and Bakalar in
*Psychedelics Reconsidered* (1979/1997) and by Jay Stevens in *Storming Heaven*
(1987). After decades of suppression following the criminalization of these
substances, the late 1990s and early 2000s saw renewed research interest. The
set-and-setting model remains the theoretical foundation for modern clinical
trials of psilocybin, MDMA, and other psychedelics for depression, PTSD,
addiction, and end-of-life anxiety.

The fundamental insight — that context, intention, and the therapeutic
relationship matter more than pharmacology — distinguishes psychedelic therapy
from conventional pharmacotherapy and aligns it with psychotherapy, shamanic
healing, and contemplative practice.
