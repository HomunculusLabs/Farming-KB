    10|# Mushroom Spoilage, Freshness, and Safety Testing
    11|
    12|Julius Auboineau Palmer, one of America's earliest mycological writers working
    13|in the 1870s and 1880s, documented extensive personal experience with mushroom
    14|poisoning, spoilage, and the critical importance of freshness. His work reveals
    15|that many cases historically attributed to inherent [[mushroom-toxicity-identification-warnings]] actually
    16|result from consuming specimens that have undergone chemical changes through
    17|[[decomposition]], water absorption, or environmental contamination.
    18|
    19|## The Chemistry of Spoilage in Edible Species
    20|
    21|Palmer discovered that edible mushrooms can become toxic through chemical
    22|changes in their substance during storage or after periods of heavy rain. The
    23|specific case involved Boletus sub-tomentosus (the olive-red boletus), a
    24|favorite edible species that he had eaten by the bushel from the same location.
    25|
    26|Under normal conditions, this mushroom shows a marked acid reaction under
    27|litmus paper and is perfectly wholesome. However, when heavily charged with
    28|water from dew or rain, its spongy substance enters a state of acidulous
    29|fermentation.
    30|
    31|In this fermented state, the mushroom exhibits brilliant blue coloring when
    32|pressed, cut, or broken, and causes illness characterized by excessive purging
    33|and vomiting. The symptoms were not unlike those exhibited in a person using
    34|tobacco for the first time: dizziness, nausea, perspiration, and alternate
    35|cold spells, all passing within an hour.
    36|
    37|The key insight is that the mushroom was not inherently poisonous but became
    38|dangerous through fermentation. Only young specimens with substance too solid
    39|to admit of fermentation should ever be eaten. This principle applies broadly
    40|to many fungal species, particularly those with spongy or water-absorbent
    41|textures.
    42|
    43|## Environmental Contamination of Normally Safe Species
    44|
    45|Palmer documented several instructive cases where growing conditions rendered
    46|normally edible mushrooms unfit for consumption:
    47|
    48|Horse mushrooms forced with potash by a gardener cooked like pieces of soap,
    49|and the liquor resembled strong lye, making them obviously inedible. Boletus
    50|aestivalis specimens were found impregnated so strongly with the odor of
    51|decayed fish as to be unfit for food, despite being a delicious species under
    52|normal conditions.
    53|
    54|Perhaps most remarkably, Boletus pachypus communicated its strong nutty flavor
    55|to nearby potatoes placed beside it in a refrigerator, without any direct
    56|contact, demonstrating how readily mushrooms absorb and transmit environmental
    57|substances.
    58|
    59|The maned mushroom (Coprinus comatus) from lawns was delicious, while
    60|specimens from dust-heaps, brick-yards, or city offal had no flavor and would
    61|prove deleterious in large quantities.
    62|
    63|These observations demonstrate the extremely susceptible character of mushrooms.
    64|They function essentially as biological sponges, absorbing and concentrating
    65|chemicals from their environment. The location of collection is therefore as
    66|important as the species identification when evaluating safety.
    67|
    68|## Cross-Contamination During Storage
    69|
    70|One of Palmer's most instructive poisoning cases involved cross-contamination
    71|rather than misidentification. He was poisoned by common edible mushrooms that
    72|had been shipped in a box containing Coprinus deliquescens in a state of
    73|active decomposition.
    74|
    75|The deliquescing mushrooms were literally turning to ink, and the edible
    76|mushrooms absorbed toxins from contact with the decomposing material. Symptoms
    77|appeared almost immediately after swallowing the edible mushrooms, demonstrating
    78|how quickly mushroom-derived toxins can affect the human system.
    79|
    80|Palmer's remedy was two wineglasses each of whiskey and sweet-oil in equal
    81|parts, which neutralized the poison within a few hours. He attributed the
    82|rapid recovery to the oil absorbing the toxic compounds.
    83|
    84|This case illustrates the importance of storing mushrooms separately and
    85|inspecting all specimens for signs of decomposition before consumption. Even
    86|edible species in a clean box can become dangerous if contaminated by a
    87|spoiling neighbor.
    88|
    89|## The Blue Matter and Its Significance
    90|
    91|Palmer's associate, chemist Paul Casamajor, found the blue matter that appears
    92|when certain boleti are bruised to be soluble, but was totally unable to give a
    93|reason for its appearance when the bruised substance of the fungus is exposed
    94|to air.
    95|
    96|This blueing reaction, now understood to involve the oxidation of variegatic
    97|and xerocomic acids, was a mystery to nineteenth-century science but served as
    98|an important warning sign. Mushrooms that blue intensely when cut, particularly
    99|when combined with a waterlogged or fermented condition, should be treated with
   100|suspicion.
   101|
   102|The blueing itself is not necessarily harmful, but it indicates chemical
   103|changes occurring in the mushroom tissue that may signal the production of
   104|secondary compounds not present in the fresh, healthy specimen. Palmer learned
   105|to use blueing as one indicator among many in assessing mushroom safety.
   106|
   107|## Palmer's Broiling Test for Safety
   108|
   109|After tracing a specimen to its family through careful taxonomic study, Palmer
   110|recommended a graduated safety test. He would broil a small piece on an empty
   111|stomach with salt and butter (no pepper), then increase the quantity in
   112|successive experiments over time.
   113|
   114|Usually, the non-edible fungus discloses its character through warning signs
   115|during this process. Nauseous slime weeping from the stem as it heats, a
   116|grassy and disagreeable odor arising during cooking, an unpleasant taste that
   117|discourages taking another mouthful, and a stinging sensation affecting the
   118|tongue, throat, and tonsils all serve as indicators.
   119|
   120|Palmer tested perhaps forty varieties of toadstools using this method. Of
   121|these, he ate regularly more than half, and was daily making additions to his
   122|bill of fare. However, he cautioned strongly against carelessness and
   123|emphasized that reckless disregard of consequences is not bravery.
   124|
   125|## The Oil Neutralization Principle
   126|
   127|Palmer believed that the peculiar alkali to which mushrooms owe their noxious
   128|qualities can be rendered harmless by either sweet-oil or pickle (vinegar). He
   129|noted that Italians eat many dangerous fungi with impunity because their
   130|properties are changed by sweet-oil when fresh, or neutralized by pickle when
   131|preserved.
   132|
   133|He prescribed sweet-oil liberally in all cases of suspected poisoning rather
   134|than the use of emetics. His reasoning was that emetics increase stress on an
   135|already distressed system, while oil absorbs and neutralizes the alkaline
   136|toxins. He recommended omitting the whiskey if desired, or substituting vinegar
   137|for spirits, but always taking sweet-oil generously.
