## The Mycelium as a Transport System

Fungal mycelia are not simply collections of independent hyphae growing
outward from a food source. They are integrated, networked systems capable
of:
- Moving nutrients from resource-rich zones to resource-poor zones
- Coordinating growth direction toward new resources
- Shifting internal [[fungal-mycelial-foraging-heterogeneous-environments]] like soil, where nutrients are distributed in
patches rather than uniformly.

## Vacuolar Transport at the Cellular Level

Research by Bebber, Tlalka, and colleagues examined nutrient transport
within individual hyphae using fluorescent dyes and confocal microscopy.
The vacuolar system — the network of membrane-bound compartments within
hyphae — plays a central role in nitrogen transport.

### Vacuolar Organization Types
Three distinct categories of vacuolar organization were identified along
the length of hyphae:
1. **Large, discrete vacuoles** — found in regions distal from the growing
   tip, acting as storage compartments
2. **Mixed vacuolar structures** — intermediate regions with both large
   vacuoles and tubular connections
3. **Tubular vacuolar network** — near the growing tip, forming a complex
   reticulate network of longitudinal tubes and small vesicles

### Diffusion as Transport Mechanism
[[fluorescence-recovery-after-photobleaching]] photobleaching (FRAP) experiments showed that
diffusion is sufficient to explain observed transport within the vacuolar
system. Fick"s first law was used to calculate effective diffusion
coefficients for each compartment type. Monte Carlo simulations of 1,000
in silico hyphae determined mean diffusion parameters.

### Transport Distance Limitations
An unbranched hypha with a continuous tubular vacuole system could sustain
tip growth over approximately 12-24 mm through diffusion alone. In a
maximally branched system, diffusion alone operates over only a few
millimetres. This suggests the vacuolar system acts as a regulatory organ,
coordinating tip growth and branching by modulating translocation capacity.

### Resource-Directed Transport
When damp cellulosic resources (filter paper) were added to growing colonies,
a marked change occurred:
- Nitrogen allocation shifted toward the new resource
- Growth became tightly focused on the resource location
- The response was sustained and directional

Glass-fibre "resources" produced more variable, transient responses,
suggesting that fungi can distinguish between resource quality.

## Pulsatile Transport Component

In addition to steady-state translocation, a strong pulsatile component was
discovered associated with rapid [[nutrient-movement-through-soil-to-plant-roots]] corded mycelial
systems. Fourier analysis of time-series data from discrete regions revealed:

- Both assimilatory (central) and foraging (peripheral) mycelium pulse
- The two regions pulse out of phase with each other
- Pulsatile transport is superimposed on net translocation patterns
- The pulses may represent a mechanism for coordinating resource
  distribution across the network

This pulsatile behavior suggests that fungal mycelia have a level of
internal coordination analogous to a primitive circulatory system, with
rhythmic nutrient flows moving between different functional regions of the
colony.

## Analytical Framework

### Quantitative Parameters
The AIB distribution pattern is characterized by three parameters:
- **Center of mass displacement** (CMDAIB) — how far the nutrient
  center has shifted from the inoculum
- **Angular concentration** (ConcDAIB) — how focused the nutrient
  distribution is in a particular direction
- **Bait alignment** — how well the nutrient distribution vector aligns
  with a resource location

### Image Analysis
Colony area is estimated from scintillation images using contrast-limited
adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) and automated grey-scale thresholding
algorithms. This automated segmentation showed very good correspondence with
visible colony boundaries when validated against bright-field images.

## Ecological Implications

The ability to image [[pulsatile-nutrient-transport-in-fungal-mycelia]] real-time across intact mycelial
networks has profound implications for understanding fungal ecology:

1. **Resource foraging strategy**: Fungi do not grow randomly — they
   dynamically redirect growth and internal resources toward detected
   nutrient patches
2. **Network integration**: The mycelium functions as a single coordinated
   organism, not a collection of independent hyphae
3. **Competitive ability**: Species with more efficient internal transport
   can dominate resource-poor environments by importing nutrients from
   distant resource-rich zones
4. **[[fungal-decomposition-ecology]]**: Understanding transport helps predict how
   fungi partition carbon and nitrogen during decomposition, affecting
   nutrient cycling rates
