# Coastal Ground Covers and Hedges in Permaculture

Coastal environments present extreme challenges for plant growth: salt spray, sandy soils, strong winds, and occasional inundation by seawater. Selecting appropriate ground covers, hedge plants, and coastal trees is critical for establishing productive permaculture systems in these environments. Many coastal plants serve multiple functions including sand stabilisation, [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]], edible [[query-how-do-i-prune-brambles-for-maximum-fruit-production]], and honey forage.

## Coastal Ground Covers

### Sea Celery (*Apium prostratum*)
A small, squat herb growing on coastal headlands and dunes, margins of brackish swamps, and on saline stretches of river banks throughout southern Australia. It bears a striking resemblance to *[[apium-graveolens]]*, the wild ancestor to celery from Europe and Asia. The plant was used as a parsley substitute to allay scurvy in the eighteenth century. Although the stems are too small to use as celery, it has potential to be bred as a vegetable. Related species *Apium annuum* and *Apium insulare* are also worthy of trial.

### Bearberry / Kinnikinik (*Arctostaphylos uva-ursi*)
A trailing or creeping shrub (rarely exceeding 75 cm) of the northern temperate region, hardy to frost. The red berries are fairly bland and mealy, usually eaten cooked, preserved, or made into jams, jellies, marmalades, sauces, and pastes. The dried leaves are used as tea in some parts of Russia. An excellent ground cover for stabilising sandy slopes.

### African Daisy (*Arctotis stoechadifolia*)
This bushy, creeping perennial (to 25 cm) is native to South Africa. It is half-hardy to frost and is an extremely good sand binder. In sand it builds [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] effectively. It survives hot, dry summers and has proven to stabilise heavy soils such as dam walls — a valuable multi-function plant.

### Sea Pink (*Armeria maritima*)
A hardy, perennial, creeping, frost-hardy native shrub of Eurasia that readily covers sand banks.

### Saltwort / Beachwort (*Batis maritima*)
A spreading, prostrate shrub with creeping stems usually found in mangrove swamps and salt marshes. Native to coastal regions, it tolerates regular inundation by saltwater.

### Beaded Glasswort / Chicken Claws (*Sarcocornia quinqueflora*)
This species forms extensive colonies on tidal mudflats. It has succulent, jointed leafless stems rarely growing taller than 50 cm. The fleshy stem absorbs the flavour of pickles, but the woody centre within each stem is a nuisance. The English native *Sarcocornia stricta* is a preferred plant for culinary use.

### Sea Purslane (*Sesuvium portulacastrum*)
A fleshy-leaved, shrubby herb native to beaches and mudflats of northern Australia and New South Wales. The leaves are reported to make an excellent vegetable and pickling ingredient. Highly tolerant of salt spray and sandy conditions.

### Sea Blight (*Suaeda australis*)
A fleshy-leaved plant native to most of the Australian coast. It is a shrubby herb (15–30 cm) of saltflats, inland saltpans, and sandbanks behind mangroves. Early settlers used the plant as a vegetable and in pickles.

### Creeping Ragweed (*Ambrosia hispida*)
A good sand binder for coastal dune stabilisation.

### Heather (*Calluna vulgaris*)
This frost-hardy, evergreen shrub to 600 mm is native to Europe. When grown in masses it is effective as erosion control on coastal slopes.

### Pigface / Karkalla (*Carpobrotus* spp.)
Native of southern Australia with thick, fleshy, triangular leaves (often reddish), purple flowers, and purplish-red fruits. Common creepers of sand dunes, beaches, and headlands. *Carpobrotus rossii* and *Carpobrotus modestus* extend well inland.

One of Australia's tastiest fruits, tasting somewhat like salty apples. The leaves were often eaten cooked by Aboriginal people to salt their meat. The dried fruit was also eaten. In Queensland, the mucilaginous juice from the leaves treats midge bites and stings from Portuguese man-of-war. The related [[cannabis-strain-swazi-landrace-south-african-sativa-oner]] species contains mesembrine, which has a physiological action similar to (but weaker than) cocaine. The juice of both species is used traditionally to soothe burns and scalds.

### Warrigal Cabbage / New Zealand Spinach (*Tetragonia tetragonioides*)
A shrubby, creeping herb common to sheltered beaches, saltmarshes, arid woodlands, and plains, extending into central Australia. Also native to the coast of New Zealand. This is the only plant native to Australia to be cultivated internationally as a vegetable. The leaves are cooked as for spinach, and the succulent young leaves can be eaten raw in salads.

### Finger Rot / Spurge Nettle (*Bivonea stimulosa*)
A shrub to 1 m native to Florida, covered with stinging hairs. Note: not recommended for edible landscapes but has value in coastal barrier plantings.

## Coastal Hedge Plants

### Natal Plum (*Carissa grandiflora*, syn. *C. macrocarpa*)
A thorny plant with edible fruit — ideal for defensive hedges in coastal locations where livestock exclusion is needed.

### Sea Grape (*Coccoloba uvifera*)
A large bush or small tree (to 6 m) with scarlet-coloured foliage. Native to Florida and the Caribbean, it is an extremely hardy tree able to withstand wind and salt spray, and can grow with its roots partly in the sea. However, it is fairly tender to frost.

Sea grape is dioecious. The fruit is reddish purple to off-white and may be eaten out of hand, and used in jellies, jams, drinks, syrups, and wines.

## Coastal Shrubs

### Swan River Cypress Pine (*Actinostrobus pyramidalis*)
A small ornamental conifer to 3 m, noted for its tolerance of salty land.

### Ruby Saltbush (*Enchylaena tomentosa*)
Also known as barrier saltbush or Sturt's saltbush. A small shrub (to 1 m) found throughout arid Australia in nearly all habitats. Well adapted to saline and marshy conditions.

### Groundsel Bush (*Baccharis halimifolia*)
Also called silverling or salt bush. A frost-hardy, deciduous shrub from 1–3 m. Provides pollen in autumn.

## Design Principles for Coastal Plantings

1. **Zonation**: Plant in bands from the most exposed (front line) to most sheltered positions, matching species tolerance to exposure levels
2. **Sand stabilisation first**: Establish ground covers like pigface, African daisy, and bearberry before planting larger species
3. **Windbreaks**: Use coastal hedge plants to create sheltered microclimates for more sensitive species
4. **Edible stacking**: Many coastal plants produce edible fruit (sea grape, pigface, natal plum) or edible greens (warrigal cabbage, sea celery)
5. **Melliferous species**: Incorporate honey-producing species (tea tree, melaleuca, hakea) for pollinator support
6. **Defensive barriers**: Thorny species (natal plum, gorse) serve dual roles as livestock barriers and wind protection
