# PDC Implementation Planning and Management Schedules

A [[permaculture-design]] is only as good as its implementation. The Williams PDC teaches that moving from design concept to installed reality requires careful implementation planning, phasing, and ongoing management schedules. Williams addresses implementation through the lens of ecological succession, resource availability, and the client's capacity for involvement.

## Design from Patterns to Details: The Implementation Philosophy

Permaculture Principle #4 -- design from patterns to details -- applies directly to implementation planning. Williams teaches that implementation should follow the natural pattern of ecological succession rather than attempting to install the final mature system all at once.

### The Succession Question

A critical question in the client interview is whether the client is willing to have a design that "naturally progresses through the stages of ecological succession" or wants the design to "jump to the end." This distinction fundamentally shapes the implementation plan:

- **Progressive succession approach**: Installation follows natural pioneer-to-climax patterns. Pioneer species establish first, building soil and creating conditions for more complex species. This approach costs less initially, requires more time, and produces more resilient systems.
- **Jump-to-end approach**: Mature systems are installed immediately with imported soil, large plants, and intensive earthworks. This costs more, requires heavy initial investment, and may produce less resilient systems because the ecological foundation has not had time to develop naturally.

Williams clearly favors the progressive approach, noting that the permaculture designer should understand that nature tells us the rate at which systems can develop, and we should consciously stay within that ecological discipline.

## Phased Implementation Strategy

Williams does not prescribe a rigid phasing system but implies a logical sequence through the design process structure:

### Phase 1: Earthworks and Water Infrastructure

Water is the first priority. Before any planting or building, the [[water-management]] infrastructure must be in place:
- Install swales, berms, and infiltration pits on contour
- Set up rainwater catchment cisterns and distribution systems
- Establish [[permaculture-designers-manual-greywater-systems]]
- Create or modify water courses for controlled flow
- Ensure all earthworks have overflow routes and emergency spillways

Williams emphasizes that every drop of rain falling on the site should be accounted for. The formula (Square Feet x Inches of Rain x 600) / 1,000 = Gallons guides cistern sizing, while infiltration rate testing determines earthworks capacity.

### Phase 2: Structures and Infrastructure

Once water management is established, structures can be built:
- Primary structures (home, outbuildings) using locally appropriate materials
- Perimeter systems (fencing, windbreaks, privacy screens)
- Access infrastructure (paths, roads, parking)
- Utility systems (solar, greywater plumbing, [[composting-toilets]])

Material selection follows the principle of starting on-site and expanding outward: first look for on-site resources, then search the immediate local area, and only source from further away when local options are exhausted.

### Phase 3: Zone 1 Installation

Daily-use systems are installed next because they receive the most attention and are most critical to daily life:
- Annual vegetable gardens
- Herb gardens near the kitchen
- Culinary herb spirals
- Cold frames and season extension
- [[composting-systems]]
- Outdoor living spaces

### Phase 4: Zone 2 Installation

Weekly-visit systems come next:
- Fruit trees and berry bushes
- Chicken coops and animal systems
- Beehives
- Food preservation areas
- Larger composting systems
- Pump houses and water distribution points

### Phase 5: Zone 3 and Beyond

Less frequently visited systems are installed last:
- Orchard systems (larger scale)
- Field crops and grains
- Pasture and grazing systems
- Timber and forestry systems
- Wildcrafting areas

### Ongoing: Monitoring and Adaptation

Implementation is never truly complete. Williams emphasizes the permaculture principle of applying self-regulation and accepting feedback. The designer and client must continuously monitor system performance and adapt accordingly.

## Resource and Budget Planning

### The Five Economic Streams in Implementation

Williams describes five economic input streams that can fund implementation:
1. **Market economy**: Conventional currency from jobs, sales, or savings
2. **Barter economy**: Direct trade of goods and services
3. **Gift economy**: Giving from abundance and receiving from others' abundance
4. **Locally produced natural resources**: Food, water, materials produced on-site
5. **Skill shares**: Trading knowledge and labor

A robust implementation plan draws on multiple streams rather than depending on any single one. The more the system produces its own resources (stream 4), the less external funding (stream 1) is needed.

### Budget Consciousness

The client interview establishes the budget and timeframe, which directly constrains the implementation plan. Williams recommends:
- Prioritize elements that reduce ongoing costs (water systems, food production, energy capture)
- Start with the highest-impact, lowest-cost interventions
- Use [[appropriate-technology]] -- the simplest solution that effectively meets the need
- Consider salvage and reclaimed materials before purchasing new
- Factor in maintenance costs, not just installation costs

## Maintenance Schedules

While Williams does not provide detailed maintenance calendar templates, the course material implies maintenance requirements for each system category:

### Daily Maintenance
- Harvesting from Zone 1 gardens
- Feeding and checking on animals in Zone 1-2
- Monitoring greywater system flow
- Brief observation walk around the property

### Weekly Maintenance
- Harvesting from Zone 2 systems
- Compost pile management (turning, adding materials)
- Water system checks (cistern levels, filter cleaning)
- Animal system management beyond daily feeding
- Pest and disease monitoring in gardens

### Monthly Maintenance
- Orchard inspection and management
- Earthworks inspection (checking for erosion, overflow function)
- Water quality testing
- Seasonal planting transitions
- Zone 3 crop management

### Seasonal Maintenance
- Pruning (dormant season for most trees)
- Major planting and transplanting
- Cover crop rotation
- Rainwater system preparation (pre-rain cleaning of gutters and first-flush diverters)
- Structure maintenance (roof inspection, painting, repairs)
- Mulching and soil amendment application

### Annual Maintenance
- Comprehensive earthworks inspection and repair
- Tree health assessment
- Soil testing and amendment planning
- System performance review and design adaptation
- Client interview update (have needs changed?)
