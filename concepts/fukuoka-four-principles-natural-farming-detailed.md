# [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]]: Detailed Account

updated: 2026-04-18

The Theory of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-method]] by [[masanobu-fukuoka]] presents four
foundational principles that define his entire approach to agriculture.
These principles emerged from thirty years of experimentation and
refinement, beginning in 1938 when Fukuoka returned to his father's farm.

## The Origin Story

Fukuoka's journey began with a moment of spiritual enlightenment at age
25 while working at the Yokohama Customs Inspection Division. He
experienced a revelation that "human knowledge was worthless" and
resigned the next day. He wandered Japan preaching the concept of
non-usefulness, was dismissed as eccentric, and eventually returned to
his father's citrus farm in Ehime Prefecture.

His first attempt at natural farming was a disaster. When his father
entrusted him with the mandarin orange orchard, Fukuoka simply abandoned
the trees to nature. The branches became intertwined, insects attacked,
and the entire orchard withered. "This is abandonment, not natural
farming," he realized.

He then spent eight years as Head Researcher of Disease and Insect
Control at the Kochi Prefecture Testing Station, all while secretly
pondering the relationship between scientific and natural agriculture.
After the war, he returned to farming and spent thirty years moving
toward [[fukuoka-road-do-nothing-farming]].

## Principle One: No Cultivation

For centuries, farmers have assumed that the plough is essential for
growing crops. However, non-cultivation is fundamental to natural
farming. The earth cultivates itself naturally by means of the
penetration of plant roots and the activity of microorganisms, small
animals, and earthworms.

Historical evidence supports this: a thousand years ago, agriculture
was practiced in Japan without ploughing. Shallow cultivation was
introduced only 300-400 years ago during the Tokugawa Era, and deep
ploughing came with Western agriculture. Fukuoka predicted that the
next generation would return to the non-cultivation method.

When the soil is cultivated, the natural environment is altered beyond
recognition. Very strong weeds such as crabgrass and docks sometimes
come to dominate the vegetation. When these weeds take hold, the farmer
is faced with a nearly impossible task of weeding each year. Very
often, the land is abandoned.

## Principle Two: No Chemical Fertilizer or Prepared Compost

People interfere with nature and, try as they may, they cannot heal the
resulting wounds. Their careless farming practices drain the soil of
essential nutrients and the result is yearly depletion of the land. If
left to itself, the soil maintains its fertility naturally, in accordance
with the orderly cycle of plant and animal life.

Fukuoka challenged soil fertility experts with a question: "If a field
is left to itself, will the soil's fertility increase or will it become
depleted?" After reflection, even the specialists admitted that nature,
left to itself, fertility increases. Organic remains of plants and
animals accumulate and are decomposed on the surface by bacteria and
fungi. With the movement of rainwater, the nutrients are taken deep
into the soil to become food for microorganisms, earthworms, and other
small animals. Plant roots reach to the lower soil strata and draw the
nutrients back up to the surface.

In practice, Fukuoka used a leguminous cover of white clover, returned
all threshed straw to the fields, and added a little poultry manure.
For many years he let ducks loose in the fields -- ten ducks supplied
all the manure necessary for a quarter acre and also helped control
weeds.

## Principle Three: No Weeding by Tillage or Herbicides

Weeds play their part in building soil fertility and in balancing the
biological community. As a fundamental principle, weeds should be
controlled, not eliminated. Straw mulch, a ground cover of white clover
interplanted with the crops, and temporary flooding provide effective
weed control.

Key insights about weeds: As soon as cultivation is discontinued, the
number of weeds decreases sharply and the varieties change. If seeds are
sown while the preceding crop is still ripening, those seeds germinate
ahead of the weeds. Winter weeds sprout only after the rice has been
harvested, but by that time the winter grain already has a head start.
Timing the seeding so there is no interval between succeeding crops
gives the grain a great advantage over the weeds.

The farmer who tries to control weeds by cultivating the soil is,
quite literally, sowing the seeds of his own misfortune. Cultivation
stirs up deep-buried seeds and gives fast-growing varieties an
advantage.

## Principle Four: No Dependence on Chemicals

From the time that weak plants developed because of such unnatural
practices as ploughing and fertilizing, disease and insect imbalance
became a great problem in agriculture. Nature, left alone, is in
perfect balance. Harmful insects and plant diseases are always present,
but do not occur in nature to an extent which requires the use of
poisonous chemicals. The sensible approach to disease and insect control
is to grow sturdy crops in a healthy environment.

Fukuoka illustrated this with the rice leafhopper investigation. A
professor from the Kochi Prefecture Testing Centre came to investigate
why leafhoppers were scarce in Fukuoka's fields. He found that while
harmful insects were few, their natural predators were far more numerous
than in sprayed fields. He acknowledged that if the method were widely
adopted, the leafhopper problem could be solved. Then he got in his
car and drove home. Nothing changed.

## The Rice/Barley Annual Cycle

The practical application of these four principles is demonstrated in
Fukuoka's rice/winter-grain succession:

Early October: White clover and winter grain seeds are broadcast among
ripening rice stalks. The clover and barley or rye sprout and grow an
inch or two before rice harvest.

Mid-November to mid-December: Rice seeds enclosed in clay pellets are
broadcast among the young barley or rye plants. A thin layer of chicken
manure is spread over the field.

May: Winter grain is harvested. After threshing, all straw is scattered
over the field.

June-July: Water is allowed to stand for a week or ten days, weakening
weeds and clover while allowing rice to sprout. Rainwater alone is
sufficient.

August: Fresh water runs through the field about once a week.

Autumn: The harvest is at hand, and the cycle begins again.

It takes only an hour or two for one farmer to sow seeds and spread
straw across a quarter acre. "It has taken me over thirty years to reach
this simplicity."
