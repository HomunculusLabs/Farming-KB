# The Doi Garment and the Culture of Clothing in Natural Farming

## Overview

During his [[fukuoka-european-travels-natural-farming]] documented in *The Road Back to Nature*, Masanobu Fukuoka wore
traditional Japanese geta (wooden sandals) and a garment called the doi throughout his tour.
This experience led him to develop a comprehensive philosophy connecting the garments farmers
wear to their relationship with the land, with God, and with cultural identity. He argued that
modern synthetic clothing was not merely uncomfortable but represented a fundamental alienation
from nature that extended to farming itself.

## The Problem with Modern Farmer's Clothing

Fukuoka observed that Japanese farmers after World War II had adopted Western clothing made of
nylon and other synthetic materials, deeply unsuited to agricultural work.

The fabric did not breathe, making the wearer hot and stuffy during physical labor.

Although the close fit seemed convenient, the clothing was tight and tended to cause stiff
shoulders, possibly related to static electricity buildup in synthetic fibers.

Modern rubber raincoats kept rain out but trapped humidity inside, leaving the farmer drenched
in sweat — worse than being wet from rain alone.

Such clothing could not be worn during heavy agricultural work without significant discomfort.

Fukuoka was amazed that farmers tolerated this discomfort and wondered why no one had
addressed the problem.

## Traditional Japanese Farming Attire

In contrast, traditional Japanese farming clothing was designed around the actual needs of
agricultural work.

Navy blue cotton clothing was simple, airy, and breathable.

Sedge hats and straw raincoats made of hemp palm provided effective protection from sun and
rain while allowing ventilation.

Raincoats made of hemp palm were considered the best of all, though they had become too
expensive by the 1980s.

The jika-tabi — a heavy cloth split-toe shoe — was one of the finest inventions of Japanese
clothing design, serving the farmer well and even worn with pride by some American carpenters.

Straw sandals made of bamboo bark remained superior for actual field work.

## The Invention of the Doi

A few years before his European tour, a new garment called the doi was designed in response to
the lack of proper farmer's clothing.

The doi consisted of a loose-fitting upper half similar to the Japanese haori (formal jacket)
and baggy work pants resembling the skirt-like hakama.

Made of navy blue cotton fabric, it was practical for farm work yet presentable for formal
occasions.

Fukuoka received a complimentary set and wore it regularly from that point forward.

The doi represented a synthesis of traditional Japanese garment design with contemporary
practicality — a garment that could serve as both work clothes and proper dress before God.

## Geta, Monpe, and the European Tour

Fukuoka departed for Europe wearing the doi, geta, and traditional monpe (work pants).

His choice was partly rebellion against the ways of the world and partly mischievousness, but
it turned out to be a great success everywhere.

He was sometimes mistaken for a martial arts master, an artist, or a gangster boss.

While looking at a tourist brochure on the airplane, he noticed the traditional Bulgarian
peasant dress was identical to that of Japan — suggesting a deep commonality in agricultural
clothing across cultures shaped by practical farming demands rather than fashion.

## Clothing as Proper Dress Before God

Fukuoka's reflections went beyond practicality into the spiritual dimension of farming.

He argued that the farmer works in the service of God, and the farmer's struggle with scythe
and hoe must be identical in spirit to the samurai holding his sword.

Therefore, the farmer's work clothes must be proper dress before God.

The haori and hakama — proper dress for gentlemen — probably originated in the plainer farmer's
denchi (work vest) and monpe.

The doi restored this connection in contemporary form, bridging formal and work clothing.

## The Minimalist Clothing Vision

Fukuoka's ultimate vision for clothing was radically minimalist.

Because man is an animal born naked, simple diet and dress would suffice.

One bowl and one robe — the traveling robe of a Zen monk — would do well.

For Japan, he proposed men need wear only indigo-dyed cotton from Tokushima, women only light
blue jindaifuku. Two colors throughout the year.

All the Japanese ever had to do was live in houses of wood, earth, and paper, wear the doi,
and drink tea. The revival of a people held in high esteem begins with the geta.

## The Confusion of Modern Fashion

Japanese fashion designers had become caught up in creating unusual new attire, driven by the
same restlessness characterizing the broader Japanese confusion over diet and lifestyle.

The Japanese had lost their traditional aesthetic anchors and were adrift in synthetic
materials and imported fashions serving neither comfort, beauty, nor spiritual purpose.

If Japanese women traveled abroad in kimono rather than Western outfits, Fukuoka believed they
would be most happily received — traditional clothing carried cultural distinctiveness that
commanded respect and curiosity.

## Implications for Farming Communities

When farmers adopted clothing of urban professionals, they implicitly accepted a hierarchy
placing farming below other occupations.

When they wore distinctive farming garments, they affirmed the dignity of agricultural work.

The doi was Fukuoka's attempt to create a garment bridging these worlds: practical for field
work, dignified for formal occasions, rooted in tradition but adapted to contemporary life.
