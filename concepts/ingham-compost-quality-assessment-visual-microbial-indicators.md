# Compost Quality Assessment: Visual and Microbial Indicators

## Overview

The quality of compost used as starter material for [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]]
Compost Tea (AACT) is the single most important factor determining brew
success. Ingham's field guide devotes extensive attention to compost
quality because no brewing skill can compensate for poor starting
compost. High-quality compost must contain diverse, active communities of
beneficial bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes that multiply during
brewing and are delivered to soil or plant surfaces through the tea.

## Thermal vs. Vermicompost

Ingham distinguishes between [[aact-vermicompost-vs-thermal-compost-ingham]] and vermicompost as tea
starters. Both can produce excellent tea but have different microbial
profiles. Thermal compost tends to have more balanced bacterial-to-fungal
ratios and can be managed to favor either group. Vermicompost is
typically more bacterial-dominated but contains significant protozoa and
[[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]] from worm processing.

The choice depends on target crops. For fungal-dominated systems
(perennials, trees), thermal compost with high fungal biomass is
preferred. For annual vegetables, either type works well.

## Visual Assessment

### Color and Texture

Good compost is dark brown to black with crumbly, friable texture and
no recognizable feedstock pieces. Light-colored material indicates
incomplete decomposition. Compost with large undecomposed chunks reduces
microbial colonization surface area and may introduce anaerobic pockets
into tea.

### Smell

Properly finished compost has a pleasant, earthy forest-floor aroma from
geosmin produced by actinobacteria. Offensive odors indicate problems:
sour or vinegar-like smells suggest anaerobic conditions; ammonia
indicates incomplete [[fungal-role-in-nitrogen-cycling]]; putrid or sulfurous smells
indicate severe anaerobic conditions with hydrogen sulfide. Compost with
any offensive odor should not be used for tea brewing.

### Moisture and Temperature

Compost for tea should be 40–60% moisture — like a wrung-out sponge.
Too-dry compost has reduced microbial activity; too-wet compost may be
anaerobic. Finished compost must be at [[jadam-ambient-temperature-principle]]. Warm compost
indicates incomplete succession and unstable material unsuitable for tea.

## Microbial Assessment

### Direct Microscopy

Ingham strongly advocates direct microscopy at 400x magnification as the
primary quality assessment method. Brewers directly observe and count
organisms, getting immediate results on diversity, activity, and
proportions. For bacteria, good compost should have at least 10–15%
active organisms showing visible movement. For fungi, hyphal diameter
and color distinguish beneficial [[saprophytic-fungi]] (wide, light-colored)
from potential pathogens (narrow, dark, septate).

Ingham is critical of standard plate count methods because they detect
only 0.1–10% of organisms present. Most compost microorganisms cannot
be cultured on artificial media. Plate counts systematically undercount
fungi and overcount fast-growing bacteria. She reserves plate counts
for specific pathogen screening (E. coli, Salmonella) for food safety.

### Organism Diversity Targets

Ingham provides numerical targets for compost quality. Good compost
should contain 100 million to 1 billion bacterial cells per gram, with
10–15% active. Fungal hyphal length should be 2–10 meters per gram for
vegetable crops and 10–100+ meters per gram for perennials and trees.
Protozoa should be at least 10,000–100,000 per gram. Beneficial
nematodes should be detectable. Diverse [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] indicate
mature compost food webs with functional [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]].
