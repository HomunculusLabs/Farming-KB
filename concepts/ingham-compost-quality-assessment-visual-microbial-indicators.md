# Compost Quality Assessment: Visual and Microbial Indicators

## Overview

The quality of compost used as starter material for [[aact-vermicompost-vs-thermal-compost-ingham]] and vermicompost as tea
starters. Both can produce excellent tea but have different microbial
profiles. Thermal compost tends to have more balanced bacterial-to-fungal
ratios and can be managed to favor either group. Vermicompost is
typically more bacterial-dominated but contains significant protozoa and
[[fungal-role-in-nitrogen-cycling]]; putrid or sulfurous smells
indicate severe anaerobic conditions with hydrogen sulfide. Compost with
any offensive odor should not be used for tea brewing.

### Moisture and Temperature

Compost for tea should be 40–60% moisture — like a wrung-out sponge.
Too-dry compost has reduced microbial activity; too-wet compost may be
anaerobic. Finished compost must be at [[saprophytic-fungi]] (wide, light-colored)
from potential pathogens (narrow, dark, septate).

Ingham is critical of standard plate count methods because they detect
only 0.1–10% of organisms present. Most compost microorganisms cannot
be cultured on artificial media. Plate counts systematically undercount
fungi and overcount fast-growing bacteria. She reserves plate counts
for specific pathogen screening (E. coli, Salmonella) for food safety.

### Organism Diversity Targets

Ingham provides numerical targets for compost quality. Good compost
should contain 100 million to 1 billion bacterial cells per gram, with
10–15% active. Fungal hyphal length should be 2–10 meters per gram for
vegetable crops and 10–100+ meters per gram for perennials and trees.
Protozoa should be at least 10,000–100,000 per gram. Beneficial
nematodes should be detectable. Diverse [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]].
