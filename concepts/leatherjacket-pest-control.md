# Leatherjacket Pest Control

## Identification

Leatherjackets are unmistakable in appearance — they are white, fat, and very ugly.
They are in fact the larvae of the crane fly (commonly known as "daddy longlegs").
The adult crane fly resembles a large mosquito but does not bite; its sole purpose is reproduction.
Females lay eggs in grass or moist soil during late summer and autumn, and the larvae hatch within one to two weeks.
Leatherjackets grow through four instar stages over several months, reaching lengths of up to 4cm by late spring before pupating in early summer.

The name "leatherjacket" refers to their tough, leathery grey-brown skin, which provides some protection against predators and environmental conditions.
They have no distinct head capsule visible from above, and their legs are small and located near the head end.
These features distinguish them from other common soil larvae like wireworms and vine weevil grubs.
The body is cylindrical and slightly tapered toward the head, with a tough, segmented appearance when viewed up close.
When disturbed, leatherjackets curl into a characteristic C-shape, which can help distinguish them from other soil grubs.

## Behavior and Damage

- Found just below the surface of the soil
- Nibble away at the roots of just about any plant
- Sometimes come to the surface on warm nights
- Can completely eat through the stem of a plant at ground level overnight

Leatherjacket damage is most severe in late winter and early spring when the larvae are at their largest and most voracious.
They feed primarily on grass roots, which makes them a significant pest in lawns and pastures, but they readily attack the roots of vegetables, ornamental plants, and seedlings as well.
Lawns suffering leatherjacket infestation develop yellow patches that can be pulled up easily because the root system has been destroyed underneath.
In severe cases, birds — particularly crows, rooks, and starlings — will tear up turf to feed on the larvae, causing secondary damage that can be more visible than the root feeding itself.

The larvae are most active during mild, damp conditions.
During prolonged dry spells or cold winter weather, they burrow deeper into the soil and become temporarily dormant.
This behaviour means that damage often appears suddenly after warm spring rains when the larvae return to the root zone to feed aggressively before pupation.
Seedlings and young transplants are especially vulnerable because their root systems are small and can be destroyed entirely by a single leatherjacket.
Newly sown lawns are particularly at risk, as the young grass roots offer little resistance to larval feeding.

## Life Cycle

Understanding the leatherjacket life cycle is key to effective control.
Adult crane flies emerge from pupae in the soil during July to September, depending on the region and weather.
After mating, each female deposits 200-300 eggs in the soil or among grass blades.
The eggs hatch within two weeks, and the small larvae begin feeding immediately on root hairs and organic matter.
They overwinter in the soil and resume feeding in spring.
By May or June, they stop feeding, pupate in the soil, and emerge as adults to complete the cycle.
There is one generation per year in most temperate regions.

Adult crane flies are weak fliers and are commonly seen resting on walls and windows in late summer.
Their brief adult lifespan — typically only a few days — is devoted entirely to mating and egg-laying.
The adults do not feed, which means that controlling the flying adults is not an effective pest management strategy.
All control efforts should focus on the soil-dwelling larval stage.

## Control Methods

### Physical Removal (Primary Method)

You will generally find leatherjackets when digging or hoeing.
Because of their white color, they are easy to see and squash.
This manual action is normally all that is needed to control their numbers.
Regular cultivation of garden beds in autumn and early spring exposes leatherjackets to birds and desiccation.
Forking over affected lawn areas in autumn can also bring large numbers to the surface.

A useful technique is to lay a black plastic sheet or tarp over watered grass overnight.
The warmth and moisture drive leatherjackets to the surface where they can be collected in the morning.
This method is particularly effective in early autumn when larvae are still small and feeding near the surface.
Another approach is to water an affected area heavily in the evening and cover it with an old carpet or cardboard, then check underneath the following morning.
The leatherjackets will have migrated to the surface and can be collected and disposed of.

### Encourage Natural Predators

Ground beetles eat leatherjackets.
Encourage them by growing ground cover plants, which provide habitat for predatory beetles.
Rove beetles (Staphylinidae) are particularly effective leatherjacket predators and are attracted to compost-rich soils with plenty of surface debris.
Creating beetle banks — strips of undisturbed, tussocky grass — along field margins provides overwintering habitat for predatory beetles and other beneficial insects.

Birds are also significant predators of leatherjackets, particularly in early spring.
Encouraging bird activity in the garden through bird feeders, nesting boxes, and hedgerow planting can help keep leatherjacket populations in check.
Hedgehogs consume large numbers of leatherjackets when foraging at night and are valuable allies in organic pest management.
A healthy garden ecosystem with diverse predator populations will naturally suppress leatherjacket numbers below damaging levels.

### Biological Control

Nematode-based biological controls are commercially available and highly effective against leatherjackets.
The parasitic nematode Steinernema feltiae is applied to moist soil during September or October when the leatherjackets are still small and near the surface.
The nematodes enter the leatherjacket's body through natural openings and release bacteria that kill the larva within days.
This method is safe for other soil organisms and can reduce leatherjacket populations by 50-80% when applied correctly.
Soil temperature must be above 12°C for the nematodes to be active, making timing critical.

The nematode product is typically supplied as a powder or gel that is mixed with water and applied with a watering can or sprayer.
The soil should be kept moist for two weeks after application to allow the nematodes to actively seek out and infect leatherjacket larvae.
This biological approach is widely used by organic golf courses and sports turf managers as well as home gardeners.
It is completely safe for children, pets, and wildlife, and leaves no harmful residues in the soil.

### Cultural Practices

Avoid excessive use of nitrogen fertiliser on lawns, as soft, lush grass is more attractive to egg-laying crane flies and more vulnerable to larval damage.
Aerating compacted lawns improves drainage and makes the soil environment less favourable for leatherjacket survival.
Removing thatch build-up also reduces the moist, protected habitat that crane fly larvae favour during their development.
Encouraging diverse plantings in borders and beds reduces the attractiveness of the area to crane flies, which prefer to lay eggs in monoculture grass.

### Wireworms

- Found in newly turned soil; can be trapped using rows of wheat (attract wireworms, then dig up and burn), old potatoes or carrots spitted on sticks and buried (dig up periodically and destroy worms), or split cabbage stalks pushed 2-3in into the ground near affected plants

### Ants

- Rarely harm plants directly but carry aphids between plants and protect them from ladybugs and hoverfly larvae (ants feed on the honeydew excreted by aphids)
- Control: Sugar and borax bait — mix equal parts powdered sugar and borax, place on wood/stone near ant activity, cover from rain. Ants carry the poison back to the nest; since ants eat their own droppings, the entire colony is destroyed

## Economic Significance

Leatherjackets are a major agricultural pest in parts of Europe, particularly the UK and Ireland.
In grassland farming, they can reduce pasture productivity by 10-20% in outbreak years.
Grass reseeding is particularly vulnerable because the young grass plants lack the root mass to withstand heavy feeding.
Modern integrated pest management strategies combine monitoring (soil sampling in autumn to assess larval populations), cultural controls, biological nematode applications, and targeted chemical intervention only when economic thresholds are exceeded.
