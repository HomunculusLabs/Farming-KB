## Overview
Cucumbers are warm-season vines that produce prolifically when given proper support, consistent moisture, and warm temperatures. They are among the most productive plants per square foot in the home garden. This guide covers trellising, pruning, [[succession-planting]], pickling varieties, and pest management.
## Varieties
### Slicing Cucumbers
- Fresh eating, salads, sandwiches
- Thick skin, crisp flesh, larger size
- Varieties: Marketmore 76, Straight Eight, Diva (parthenocarpic, no pollination needed), Suyo Long (Asian, burpless), Lemon (round, mild)
### Pickling Cucumbers
- Smaller, blockier shape with thin skin
- Firm flesh that holds up to brining
- Varieties: Boston Pickling, National Pickling, Picklebush (bush type), Salt and Pepper, Homemade Pickles
- Harvest small (2-4 inches) for gherkins, 4-6 inches for dill pickles
### Specialty
- **English/Hothouse**: Long, thin, seedless, burpless. Varieties: Telegraph, Pepinex
- **Armenian**: Pale green, ribbed, actually a melon. Grows very long (18-24 inches)
- **Lemon**: Round, yellow, mild flavor
- **Asian/Burpless**: Long, tender, less bitter. Varieties: Suyo Long, Tasty Jade
- **Mini/Snacking**: Small, no peeling needed. Varieties: Muncher, Spacemaster
### Bush vs Vining
- **Bush types**: Compact, 2-3 feet, no trellis needed. Good for containers and small gardens. Varieties: Picklebush, Spacemaster, Bush Pickle
- **Vining types**: 6-10+ feet, require trellis for best results. Higher yield per plant. Most common type
## Trellising
Trellising cucumbers is strongly recommended for better fruit quality, disease prevention, and space efficiency.
### Benefits of Trellising
- Straighter, cleaner fruit (no soil contact)
- Better air circulation reduces disease
- Easier to spot and harvest fruit
- Saves garden space — grow vertically
- Reduces slug and ground pest damage
- Fruit hangs freely, preventing yellowing on ground contact side
### Trellis Systems
- **A-frame trellis**: Two panels of wire mesh hinged at top. Plant on both sides. Sturdy and reusable
- **Cattle panel trellis**: Cattle panels attached to T-posts. Strong, tall, durable. Best for heavy vining types
- **String trellis**: Strings from overhead support down to plants. Common in greenhouses. Train vines by twisting around strings
- **Bamboo teepee**: Simple but may not support heavy fruit loads
- **Netting**: Nylon or polypropylene netting stretched between posts. Cost-effective for large plantings
### Training Vines
- Guide initial vines onto the trellis by gently tying or weaving
- Once established, cucumbers climb readily with their tendrils
- Remove any vines growing away from the trellis
- Support heavy fruit with slings made from old pantyhose or netting if needed
## Pruning
Pruning cucumbers improves air circulation, directs energy to fruit production, and extends the harvest.
### How to Prune
- Remove the first set of lateral shoots from the lowest 2-3 leaf nodes
- This directs energy to the main vine and opens the base for air circulation
- Allow subsequent laterals to grow and fruit
- Remove yellow or damaged leaves throughout the season
- Cut off any leaves showing signs of disease immediately
### Benefits
- Earlier and larger fruit on the main vine
- Reduced powdery mildew due to better airflow
- Easier to see and harvest fruit
- Extends productive life of the plant
## Succession Planting
Cucumbers are productive but have a defined lifespan — succession planting extends the harvest.
### Schedule
- First sowing: After last frost, when soil is at least 65F
- Second sowing: 3-4 weeks after first sowing
- Third sowing: 3-4 weeks after second (optional in long-season areas)
- Stop sowing 10-12 weeks before first fall frost
- Total of 2-4 successions per season in most temperate zones
### Tips
- Vining types have a longer productive window (8-10 weeks) than bush types (5-6 weeks)
- Later successions often face more pest and disease pressure
- In hot climates, mid-summer plantings may struggle — choose heat-tolerant varieties
## Planting and Care
### Growing Care
- Full sun (6-8+ hours), rich well-draining soil pH 6.0-6.8
- Work in 2-3 inches of compost; cucumbers are heavy feeders
- Direct sow 1 inch deep, 6-12 inches apart at trellis base
- Germination: 5-10 days at 70F+; thin to 12-18 inches
- Drip irrigation at the base is ideal (keeps foliage dry)
- Consistent moisture critical — drought causes bitter fruit; mulch heavily
- Side-dress with compost or balanced fertilizer when vines run and at first fruit
## Harvesting
- Harvest frequently to keep plants productive
- Slicing cucumbers: pick at 6-8 inches
- Pickling: pick at 2-6 inches depending on desired pickle size
- Harvest daily or every other day at peak production
- Cut fruit from the vine rather than pulling (prevents vine damage)
- Overripe fruit left on the vine signals the plant to stop producing
## Pickling Varieties in Detail
### Characteristics of Good Picklers
- Thin skin absorbs brine
- Firm, crisp flesh holds texture in vinegar
- Small seed cavity
- Blocky shape fits jars efficiently
- Consistent size for uniform packing
### Top Pickling Varieties
- **Homemade Pickles**: Reliable, prolific, disease-resistant
- **Boston Pickling**: Heirloom, 1850s origin, great all-around pickler
- **National Pickling**: Developed by the USDA specifically for pickling
- **Salt and Pepper**: White with black stripes, attractive in jars
- **Sumter**: Compact, early, good for small gardens
### Harvest for Pickling
- Pick at 2-4 inches for sweet gherkins, 4-6 inches for dill spears
- Harvest every 2-3 days; pick before seeds become large
## Cucumber Beetle Control
Cucumber beetles are the primary pest of cucumbers and can devastate plantings.
### Types
- **Striped cucumber beetle**: Yellow with black stripes, most common east of Rockies
- **Spotted cucumber beetle**: Yellow with black spots, also feeds on other crops
- Both transmit bacterial wilt, which kills vines rapidly
### Damage
- Adults chew holes in leaves, flowers, and fruit
- Larvae feed on roots and underground stems
- Most damaging: they transmit bacterial wilt (Erwinia tracheiphila)
- Bacterial wilt causes vines to wilt suddenly and die — no cure once infected
### Management Strategies
1. **Row covers**: Exclude beetles from transplant until flowering
2. **Kaolin clay (Surround WP)**: Sprayed on foliage as a deterrent barrier
3. **Sticky traps**: Yellow sticky traps catch adults; place near plant base
4. **Delayed planting**: Miss peak beetle emergence by planting 2-3 weeks late
5. **Neem oil**: Repels beetles when applied regularly
6. **Companion planting**: Radishes, nasturtiums, and marigolds may help
7. **Clean up**: Remove crop residue in fall to reduce overwintering beetles
### Disease Resistance
- Marketmore 76, County Fair, and Little Leaf show tolerance to bacterial wilt
- Choose powdery mildew resistant varieties (marked PM) for late summer
## Common Diseases
### Powdery Mildew
- White coating on leaves in late summer; reduces photosynthesis
- Prevention: air circulation, resistant varieties, neem oil or potassium bicarbonate sprays
### Downy Mildew
- Yellow patches on upper leaf surface, gray fuzz underneath
- Favored by cool, wet conditions; remove affected leaves immediately
### Bacterial Wilt
- Vines wilt suddenly; transmitted by cucumber beetles
- No treatment once infected — prevent by controlling beetles
