## Fungal Unit and Community Characterization

## Overview

No individual, population, or species assemblage exists in ecological isolation — all organisms are integrated within a complex ecological whole whose higher-order dynamics (biodiversity) arise from interactions among abiotic and biotic components (Putman 1994). The ability to detect patterns of fungal biodiversity and the mechanisms that shape them is influenced by the approaches used to evaluate the ecological structure of fungal communities. Key parameters defining a community and important for assessing biodiversity include species composition along with its functional and genetic correlates; the types and intensities of interspecific interactions and how they regulate species densities and occurrences; and the dynamics of those attributes over time and space or as they change due to past or present human activities.

Most mycologists focus on only one (or at best two) aspects of communities, thereby providing an incomplete assessment of fungal biodiversity. A comprehensive treatment requires addressing all aspects of community organization and dynamics.

## The Fungal Unit

### The Problem of Defining the Fungal Individual

Most fungi (excluding yeasts and some zoosporic taxa) consist of filaments (hyphae) that grow by tip extension through deposition of cell-wall material from a growing tip. As tips expand and produce new growing points, a network termed the **mycelium** develops. Established fungal mycelia are capable of essentially unlimited growth and persistence. This **indeterminate body structure** differs fundamentally from the determinant body plan of most animals and many plant species, making protocols and assumptions for estimating fungal biodiversity inherently different from those used for most macroorganisms.

Mycelia can expand to occupy a heterogeneous suite of microenvironments or macroenvironments, with distant segments remaining interconnected to facilitate intercellular communication. Because fungi are heterotrophic, they convert organic material and nutrients into biomass at spatial scales ranging from several millimeters to entire landscapes (Smith et al. 1992; Anderson et al. 1994).

### Three Perspectives on "Individual"

The term "individual" can be applied in a **numerical**, **genetic**, or **ecological** context, depending on the focal organism and question of interest (Andrews 1991):

- **Numerical perspective**: An individual is a countable unit of a particular species (e.g., a tree, a mushroom, or a colony that develops from substratum placed on an agar plate). Cooke and Rayner (1984) define individuals as discrete, functionally independent units, but the level of cellular aggregation fulfilling that criterion for fungi and other microorganisms is not clear. For example, isolates of a single species obtained from decomposing leaves at spatially disjunctive locations on the forest floor may represent the mycelium of a single individual or the mycelia of several individuals. Counting colonies on an isolation plate — whether obtained from a 1-mm² piece of substratum or from a dilution series — does not represent the same biological reality as counting insects within a defined area.

- **Genetic perspective**: An individual can be defined as a single cell or collection of cells exhibiting the same genotype — a **genet** (sensu Kays and Harper 1974). For animals like a squirrel, the numerical and genetic individual coincide. For fungi, however, the number of discrete countable units is *not* the same as the number of genets (Andrews 1991). Clonal organisms capable of asexual growth produce **ramets** (Harper 1977) — countable units that, although members of a specific genet, are capable of essentially independent growth. Sporocarps of one basidiomycete species on a forest floor can represent multiple ramets of a single genet or ramets from multiple genets. Only molecular or isozymic analyses can untangle this genetic structure, but that approach is not generally practical.

- **Ecological perspective**: The presence or absence of species within an area can be used to assess some aspects of biodiversity; other comparisons, however, require quantification of operational individuals for each taxon. The operational definition for an individual will differ for different taxonomic or ecological groups of fungi.

### Operational Definitions Are Essential

Because the concept of "individual" as used for animals and plants does not apply to fungi, each investigator must establish a clearly stated, **operational definition** of the fungal unit relevant to the taxon of interest. This definition must facilitate unambiguous comparisons among ecosystems and must remain consistent throughout the spatial and temporal domain of the investigation. It is critical that biomass, density, or frequency of occurrence be based on the same unit of collection and obtained in the same manner throughout. Population and community metrics are designed to accommodate data that are not based solely on the delineation of individuals, so the investigator must establish a consistent unit by which presence-absence, biomass, density, or frequency can be estimated over space and time.

## Characterization of Communities

### Collection Effort Curves

Many, if not most, investigations of populations and communities describe patterns rather than test *a priori* hypotheses. They rely on the collection of presence-absence, biomass, density, or frequency of occurrence data for a suite of species. When estimating total species richness of an area or domain, a random (or stratified random) series of plots should be placed within the inference space to determine the number of samples required.

As the number of samples increases, the total number of species identified increases but eventually attains a **plateau** (asymptote). The number of samples needed to reach this plateau depends on:

- Environmental heterogeneity of the area of interest
- Dispersion patterns of the focal species
- Fruiting phenology of the focal species

**Collector's curves** should be generated for each treatment type. Because even different sample units of the same type can differ in heterogeneity and species composition, collector's curves should be generated for multiple replicates. To be conservative, sample sizes should be chosen *beyond* the minimal effort corresponding to the plateau. Alternatively, assuming a logistic relationship between richness and effort allows statistical parameter estimation and graphical determination of a conservative sample size. Human and financial resources are often too limited to enable complete discovery of species richness at a site, so quantitative ecologists have developed methods to estimate total richness from modest sampling (Colwell and Coddington 1994).

Three mathematical relations describe how species richness (S) increases with effort (A):

| Model | Equation | Behavior |
|-------|----------|----------|
| Logistic | Approaches asymptote | Plateau reached |
| Power (Arrhenius 1921) | S = CA^z | Monotonic increase |
| Exponential (Gleason 1922) | S = C + z·ln A | Monotonic increase |

Power and exponential models are most appropriate for heterogeneous landscapes where increases in effort continually reveal new species. Alternative methods such as **rarefaction analysis** (Magurran 1988; Colwell and Coddington 1994) facilitate comparison of species richness among areas as if based on a standardized sample size.

### Sampling Design

#### Plot Size and Shape

The most effective size and shape for a sampling plot depend on the total time required to erect and characterize individual plots as well as the variability of resultant data. The investigator should have some *a priori* knowledge of appropriate plot size and shape so that the range of sizes evaluated is not excessive. A growing body of evidence (Kolasa and Pickett 1991; Waide et al. 1999; Gross et al. 2000) shows that ecological patterns and processes are **scale-dependent**, so considerable caution is required when comparing studies based on plots of different sizes. The spatial scale at which data are collected should be related to the spatial scales at which causative mechanisms are thought to operate.

**Circular plots** are delineated easily and quickly (center pole + freely swinging radius line) in grasslands, shrublands, and forests. Their key advantage is **minimizing edge effects** that can strongly bias estimates of density.

**Rectangular (elongated) plots** (Krebs 1989) provide more accurate estimates of species composition than circular or square plots of equal area. They are especially suited for assessing environmental gradients when the long axis is oriented parallel to the underlying gradient (Cox 1996), because rectangular quadrats more effectively detect habitat heterogeneity and accurately estimate the patchy distribution of organisms within a sampling scheme. A long quadrat potentially covers more patches than a circular one of the same area.

#### Sample Size Determination

The number of sampling units required to provide accurate estimates of species richness or density depends on:

- Characteristics of the fungal assemblage
- Objectives of the investigation
- Magnitude of differences considered biologically relevant

In practice, sample size often represents the upper limit of expendable effort — whether the number of plots assessed for macrofungi fruiting on a forest floor or the time necessary to culture and identify isolates on agar medium. Regardless, the investigator must avoid capricious decisions and always expose the criteria on which sample size was determined. Power analysis should be employed where possible.

### Frequency of Occurrence

Although the individual is usually the smallest biological unit for quantitative population and community indices, because of the fungal growth form, data must be tied to a consistent operational unit. Frequencies of occurrence can be calculated in two ways:

**Raw frequency** (can sum to >100% when multiple species occur per sample unit):

```
Frequency (%) = (sample units with species / total sample units examined) × 100
```

For example, if 50 1-mm × 1-mm particles of organic soil matter are plated onto agar medium, the maximum frequency for any species is 50/50 = 100%. Note that the sum of frequencies of all species can exceed 100%.

**Relative frequency** (standardized, sums to 100%):

```
Relative Frequency (%) = (number of isolates for each species / total number of isolates) × 100
```

Dividing each species' raw frequency by the sum of all frequencies yields **relative frequency**, a straightforward standardization. Investigators should report average frequency and **standard error of the mean** for each species when sampling is replicated. Standard error provides an estimate of variation associated with the mean frequency. The variability among species in frequency often conveys more insight into ecological mechanisms responsible for occurrence patterns than do the means alone.

### Scale-Dependence and Causative Mechanisms

The spatial scale at which data are collected should be explicitly stated and related to the spatial scales at which causative mechanisms are thought to operate. Effects of environmental variation on species density and numerical species richness are scale-dependent — the importance of different causal mechanisms depends on the scale of data collection (Waide et al. 1999; Gross et al. 2000). Critical implications include:

- Patterns observed at one scale may not hold at another
- Comparisons among studies with different plot sizes must be made cautiously
- Most ecological studies of fungi cover species densities as a consequence of sampling design, although this is rarely stated explicitly
- Future research should always include an explicit definition of scale as well as the attribute of richness being evaluated
