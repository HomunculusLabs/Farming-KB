# Mycological Landscaping and Gardening with Fungi

Paul Stamets advocates for integrating fungi into garden and landscape design, arguing that "mycogardening" should be as fundamental to landscaping as choosing plant species. By thoughtfully matching mycelia with compatible plants and conditions, gardeners can create self-sustaining fungal ecosystems that improve soil, filter water, and support biodiversity.

## The Concept of Mycogardening

Mycorestoration practices can be implemented at the garden scale through "mycogardening" -- the intentional cultivation of [[biodiversity-of-fungi-soil-fungal-communities-agriculture]] in designed landscapes. The early introduction of primary saprophytes, which are among the first organisms to rejuvenate the food chain, can determine the course of biological communities through thoughtfully matching mycelia with compatible plants, insects, and other organisms.

## The Rhododendron Garden Model

Stamets documents a 40-year case study of a rhododendron garden lovingly cared for by an elderly couple. Each year, they distributed wood chips around the plants, building pathways and for general landscaping. The wood chips supported thriving communities of Hypholomas, Psilocybes, and other species. For more than four decades, this annual replenishment sustained a rich mycoflora. However, when the elderly owners could no longer physically replenish the wood chips, there was a sudden transition in the mycoflora -- the previously prominent species became scarce. This demonstrates that mycological landscapes must be actively replenished with carrier materials and sometimes recharged with spawn to preserve saprophytic mushroom communities. The lesson is clear: fungal landscapes are not self-sustaining without periodic input of woody substrates.

## Wood Chip Bed Construction

Stamets' standard method for outdoor mycelial beds:

1. Obtain wood chips from tree-trimming operations or recycling centers
2. Spread chips in a layer approximately 1 foot deep
3. Inoculate with mushroom spawn (garden giants, oyster mushrooms, or other species)
4. Rake spawn into the wood chips
5. Ensure contact with soil and moisture
6. Maintain shade to prevent drying

Key parameters:
- Chips should be no smaller than 3/8 inch
- Pile no more than a foot deep to avoid suffocating aerobic fungi
- Wood must be in contact with ground moisture
- Shade helps maintain humidity

## Companion Planting with Mushrooms

Stamets recommends specific fungal companions for landscape settings:

- **Garden giants** (Stropharia rugosoannulata): ideal for garden beds, pathways, and mycoremediation-stamets-approach.
- **Turkey tails** ([[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]]): multibeneficial polypores that fight bacteria, prevent blights, and break down toxic chemicals like PCBs and dioxins.
- **Woodlovers** (Hypholoma species): colonize wood chips in pathways and garden borders. [[growing-gourmet-hypholoma-capnoides-cultivation]] is edible, while H. fasciculare is beautiful though poisonous.
- **Reishi** ([[stamets-reishi-ganoderma-lucidum-cultivation]]): for medicinal landscapes, reishi can be cultivated on stumps and buried logs. See [[permaculture-zones-and-sectors]]:

- **Zone 1** (immediate surroundings): [[comparison-chaga-vs-turkey-tail-medicinal-mushrooms-comprehensive]] like reishi on stumps near the house; small oyster mushroom buckets for kitchen waste decomposition.
- **Zone 2** (home garden): garden giant beds in pathways and between garden rows; [[soil-food-web-structure]] enhancement.
- **Zone 4** (managed forest): wood chip trails inoculated with Hypholoma and Stropharia; [[mycorestoration-ecology]].
