# HPLC and TLC Analysis for Psilocybin and Psilocin Quantification

## Overview

Bigwood and Beug (1982) employed reversed-phase high-performance liquid
chromatography (HPLC) as the primary quantification method for psilocybin
and psilocin [[cap-and-stem-alkaloid-distribution-in-psilocybe-cubensis-strains]] tissue, with thin-layer chromatography
(TLC) as a qualitative confirmation technique. Their analytical protocol
established a reliable workflow for measuring [[hplc-analysis-of-tryptamine-alkaloids]] in
fungal tissue that has been widely referenced in subsequent studies.

## Sample Preparation and Extraction

The extraction procedure was described in the authors' companion paper
(Beug and Bigwood, 1982) on [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] species:

- **Harvest and preservation:** Mushrooms were immediately freeze-dried
  after harvest, sealed in plastic, and stored at -5 C until analysis.
  Voucher specimens were deposited at the University of Washington
  Herbarium (WTU).

- **Freeze-drying rationale:** Immediate freeze-drying preserves alkaloid
  content by halting [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]]. Psilocybin is relatively
  stable when dry, but psilocin is prone to oxidation. Rapid drying
  and cold storage minimize post-harvest alkaloid loss.

- **Tissue preparation:** Dried mushroom tissue was prepared for
  extraction (specific extraction solvent and protocol detailed in
  the companion paper on 20 Pacific Northwest species).

## Reversed-Phase HPLC Protocol

The HPLC analysis used a reversed-phase configuration with specific
instrumentation:

- **Column:** Reversed-phase column (C18-bonded silica is standard
  for tryptamine separation — the exact column specification was not
  detailed in the 1982 paper but reversed-phase C18 was the standard).

- **Quantification:** Hewlett-Packard 8380A reporting integrator-plotter.
  This instrument integrated peak areas and generated quantitative
  reports from the chromatographic output.

- **Calibration standards:** Calibrated against reference standards
  from the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA). This ensured
  traceability to certified reference materials.

- **Linear range:** A linear relationship (10% repeatability) between
  concentration and peak area was established from 0.2 to 3 ug total
  psilocybin or psilocin. This range covers the concentrations
  typically found in P. cubensis tissue.

- **Detection limit:** Approximately 0.01 ug psilocybin or psilocin.
  This sensitivity is sufficient to detect the trace psilocin levels
  found in early flushes of P. cubensis.

## TLC Qualitative Confirmation

HPLC results were qualitatively confirmed using thin-layer chromatography
with a specific solvent system:

- **Mobile phase:** Butanol-acetic acid-water (12:3:5 by volume).
  This solvent system is well-established for separating indole
  alkaloids including psilocybin and psilocin.

- **Purpose:** TLC confirmation ensured that HPLC peaks attributed to
  psilocybin and psilocin were correctly identified and not co-eluting
  contaminants or degradation products.

- **Complementary role:** TLC provided separation by polarity on a
  silica stationary phase, using a different separation mechanism
  than HPLC. Agreement between the two methods increased confidence
  in compound identification.

## Analytical Results Summary

The combined HPLC-TLC approach yielded several key findings:

- **Psilocybin:** Levels varied unpredictably from flush to flush,
  ranging from 3.2 to 13.3 mg/g dry weight across three minicultures.
  No consistent upward or downward trend across flushes.

- **Psilocin:** Generally absent in the first flush (0-0.5 mg/g),
  reached maximum by the fourth flush (1.7-2.9 mg/g), then appeared
  to decline. The psilocin-to-psilocybin ratio was typically 1:2 or
  less.

- **Detection reliability:** The 0.01 ug detection limit was critical
  for accurately reporting psilocin levels in early flushes where
  values were at or near zero. The 10% repeatability within the
  linear range ensured flush-to-flush comparisons were meaningful.

## Cap vs. Stem Distribution

The analytical method was sensitive enough to quantify alkaloid
distribution between cap and stem tissue:

- **M.R. strain:** Caps contained 6.7 mg/g psilocybin, stems 4.2 mg/g.
  Psilocin was 0 mg/g in caps, 0.35 mg/g in stems.
- **Equadorian strain:** Caps 7.6 mg/g psilocybin, stems 4.7 mg/g.
  Psilocin 0 mg/g in caps, 0.4 mg/g in stems.
- **Amazon strain:** Caps and stems equal at 5.7 mg/g psilocybin.
  Psilocin 0.1 mg/g in caps, 0 mg/g in stems.

These distributions would be difficult to detect with less sensitive
or less specific [[fungal-siderophore-detection-analytical-methods]].

## Street Sample Analysis

The same HPLC protocol was applied to five street samples of P. cubensis
(purchased dried, frozen until analysis). Results showed:

- Highly variable psilocybin levels: 0.7 to 6.2 mg/g (nearly 10-fold
  range). Two samples had only 0.7 mg/g — among the lowest reported
  values for cultivated P. cubensis.
- Consistently low psilocin: 0 to 0.3 mg/g across all five samples.
- This variability (10-fold) exceeded the controlled-culture
  variability (4-fold), highlighting the unpredictable potency of
  uncontrolled cultivation and distribution.

## Method Strengths and Limitations

**Strengths:**
- NIDA-certified calibration standards ensure accuracy.
- Dual-method confirmation (HPLC + TLC) reduces misidentification risk.
- 0.01 ug detection limit enables trace-level psilocin quantification.
- 10% repeatability within the linear range allows reliable comparisons.

**Limitations:**
- Dried tissue only — the method does not address alkaloid content
  in fresh mushrooms or changes during the drying process.
- Total alkaloid content — cap and stem were analyzed separately in
  some experiments but the standard protocol used whole fruiting
  bodies.
- Companion analytes — baeocystin, norbaeocystin, and other minor
  tryptamine alkaloids were not quantified in this study (but were
  addressed in Repke et al. 1977).
