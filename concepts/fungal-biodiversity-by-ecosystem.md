## Aquatic Ecosystems

Freshwater and marine habitats harbor specialized fungal communities: freshwater [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] and Europe show striking similarities in their principal saprobic fungal species. This suggests fungal communities are primarily shaped by substrate and microclimate rather than geographic distance.

Soil fungal similarity is an outstanding indicator of environmental similarity. In general, a strong positive correlation often exists between vascular plant diversity and soil-fungal diversity. The compositions of soil fungal communities are regulated strongly by climatic and [[lowenfels-humus-formation-benefits]] and accumulation on large limbs in forest canopy trees. Fungal species composition in those specialized soils may be quite different from those in mineral soils because of the lack of mineral input, extreme fluctuations in water content, distinctive microfauna, and the generally exposed environment in the canopy.

## Successional Patterns Across Ecosystems

Species richness in soil fungal communities increases through both primary and secondary succession in plant communities. Disturbance — whether natural (fire, windthrow) or anthropogenic (clear-cutting, agriculture) — dramatically reshapes fungal communities. Structural change in the community (an increase in equitability as principal species diminish) is a different parameter from species richness and confuses the matter of total species present unless specifically analyzed.

In the Wisconsin survey program spanning more than 20 years across 107 sites, northern upland conifer-hardwood forests yielded more than 476 taxa from 8,061 isolates — the highest diversity of any vegetation type. Southern wet-mesic hardwood forests yielded more than 210 taxa. Prairies and sand barrens showed lower diversity (>111 and >18 taxa respectively), reflecting the relationship between vegetation complexity and fungal richness.

## Monitoring and Baseline Data

Rigorous fungal surveys produce data that help answer fundamental scientific questions: What do fungal distribution data tell us about the effects of historical events such as glaciations, orogenies, and plate tectonics? What levels of host, substratum, habitat, and environmental specificity do fungi exhibit, and how and why? How do fungi influence the success or failure of land management practices? Fungi are a large and important component of programs monitoring ecosystem health, including the Forest Ecosystem Management Assessment Team program for old-growth forests in the Pacific Northwest, where 225 species of old-growth-associated macrofungi are legally required to be surveyed and managed.
