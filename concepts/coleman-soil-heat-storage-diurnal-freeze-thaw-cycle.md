# Soil Heat Storage and the Diurnal Freeze-Thaw Cycle

## Overview

In Eliot Coleman's unheated winter harvest system, the soil serves as the
primary [[phase-change-materials-thermal-energy-storage]] storage medium — a "thermal battery" that captures solar
energy during the day and releases it slowly at night. Understanding and
managing this diurnal (daily) freeze-thaw cycle is the central operational
challenge of [[coleman-aphid-management-winter-greenhouse-nitrogen-flushing]] farming. The entire system's viability depends
on the soil absorbing enough heat during daylight hours to keep the root zone
of cold-hardy crops from remaining frozen for extended periods.

## The Soil as Thermal Battery

Unlike heated greenhouses that rely on fossil fuel or electric heat to
maintain air temperature, the winter harvest system stores energy in the
ground itself. Dark, organically rich soil absorbs solar radiation through
the [[coleman-greenhouse-covering-materials-comparison]] and row cover layers. This energy is conducted
downward and stored in the soil mass. At night, when the air temperature
drops well below freezing, the stored soil heat migrates upward, keeping the
immediate root zone and soil surface warmer than the surrounding air. The
effect is modest — the soil surface may still freeze — but it is sufficient
because the crops are adapted to tolerate light freezing.

## The Diurnal Freeze-Thaw Pattern

On a typical winter day in the [[coleman-unheated-greenhouse-design]], a predictable cycle
occurs:

1. **Night** — Air temperatures inside the greenhouse drop below freezing.
   The soil surface freezes lightly, perhaps to a depth of a quarter to half
   an inch. The crops themselves freeze solid, their leaves becoming stiff
   and brittle to the touch.

2. **Morning** — As soon as sunlight penetrates the greenhouse covering,
   typically by 9:00 or 10:00 AM even in midwinter, the soil surface begins
   to thaw. The dark soil absorbs solar energy rapidly, and the thin frozen
   layer melts from above and below simultaneously.

3. **Afternoon** — By midday, the soil surface is fully thawed and may reach
   temperatures significantly above ambient. The crops thaw and become
   harvestable. Root activity resumes, and slow metabolic processes continue.

4. **Evening** — As the sun declines and eventually sets, the soil begins to
   radiate stored heat upward. The surface gradually refreezes as night
   temperatures drop.

This cycle repeats every day throughout the winter. The crops experience
freezing as a routine, expected condition — not as a traumatic event.

## When the Soil Fails to Thaw

There are conditions under which the diurnal cycle breaks down and the soil
fails to thaw during the day. This occurs when extreme overnight cold (well
below normal winter lows) is followed by a heavily overcast day that prevents
adequate solar energy from reaching the soil. In such cases, the soil surface
remains frozen throughout the day, and the crops stay in a frozen state for
24 hours or longer.

Coleman reports that this occurs only one or two days per winter in his
USDA Zone 5 location in Maine. When it does happen, the impact is minimal
because the crops are adapted to survive extended freezing. The concern is
not that the plants will die, but that an extended frozen period means no
harvesting is possible until the next thaw. In practice, the rarity of these
events means they are a minor logistical inconvenience rather than a serious
crop threat.

## Daytime Highs and Nighttime Warmth

There is a direct and critical relationship between the maximum daytime
temperature achieved inside the greenhouse and the minimum nighttime
temperature at the soil surface. Higher daytime maxima translate directly
into more soil heat storage, which in turn means warmer soil at night and
earlier thawing the following morning. This relationship is the engine that
drives the entire system.

## The 24-Hour Average Temperature Hypothesis

Drawing on plant physiology research, Coleman observes that what matters most
for cold-hardy crops is not the absolute minimum temperature but the 24-hour
average temperature. Plants that can tolerate a nighttime low of 10°F may
thrive if the daytime high reaches 60°F, yielding a reasonable 24-hour
average. The same plant might struggle if both day and night temperatures
hovered around 30°F, even though the nighttime low is much less extreme. This
insight supports the strategy of maximizing daytime solar gain even at the
cost of wider diurnal temperature swings.

## Allowing Higher Daytime Maxima (85-95°F)

One of the more surprising recommendations in Coleman's system is to allow
greenhouse daytime temperatures to rise quite high — to 85°F or even 95°F —
during sunny winter days. In a heated greenhouse, such temperatures would be
considered dangerously excessive and would trigger ventilation. But in the
unheated winter harvest system, these high daytime temperatures are desirable
because they maximize soil heat storage. The crops tolerate the heat because
it is brief (a few hours in the afternoon) and because cold-hardy vegetables
are more stressed by cold than by moderate warmth. The stored heat then
sustains the plants through the following night.

## Moistening Soil Surface for Young Seedlings

For young seedlings that have not yet developed deep root systems, the
standard diurnal cycle may not provide enough warmth at the root zone. In
these cases, Coleman recommends moistening the soil surface in the evening.
Water has a high specific heat capacity, and a moist soil surface freezes at
0°C (32°F) rather than dropping to lower temperatures. The phase change from
liquid to ice releases latent heat, moderating the temperature at the soil
surface and protecting shallow roots. This technique is especially valuable
for late-fall plantings and early-spring transplants.

## The Late-February Transition: Cold to Warm Concerns

Around late February, the dynamic of the winter harvest system shifts
fundamentally. As day length increases and solar angles steepen, the
greenhouse begins to accumulate more heat than the system can shed. The
operator's concern transitions from "will the soil thaw today?" to "are the
crops getting too warm?" Excessive warmth triggers premature bolting,
bitterness in salad greens, and disease pressure from high humidity. This
transition requires active management — venting the greenhouse earlier in
the morning and removing the inner layer of row covers progressively.

## The March 15 Deadline

By March 15, Coleman recommends that all row covers be completely folded back
and removed. The risk of cold damage has diminished sufficiently by this date,
and the plants benefit from full light exposure and the natural temperature
moderation of the greenhouse alone. Continuing to use row covers past this
point risks creating excessively warm, humid conditions that favor disease
and reduce crop quality. The March 15 date is not arbitrary — it aligns with
the point at which day length and solar intensity have increased enough to
make passive protection unnecessary and potentially harmful.
