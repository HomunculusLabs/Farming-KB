# Cannabis as an Antiemetic for Chemotherapy-Induced Nausea

## Overview

Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) remains one of the most
distressing side effects of cancer treatment, affecting up to 80% of
patients. Cannabis and its cannabinoids, particularly THC, have been studied
since the 1970s for antiemetic properties. The Cannabis Health Index (CHI)
by Uwe Blesching systematically reviews this evidence across dozens of
clinical studies.

## Historical Context

Cancer patients in the 1970s reported that smoking marijuana relieved
chemotherapy nausea when conventional antiemetics failed. These anecdotal
reports prompted the first controlled trials, demonstrating that oral THC
(dronabinol/Marinol) effectively reduced CINV. This led to FDA approval of
dronabinol in 1985, one of the first cannabinoid pharmaceuticals approved
in the United States.

## Pharmacological Mechanism

The antiemetic effect is mediated primarily through CB1 receptors in the
brainstem — the dorsal vagal complex, nucleus tractus solitarius, and area
postrema (the vomiting center). CB1 activation inhibits release of serotonin
and substance P, key neurotransmitters in the emetic reflex. This mechanism
is distinct from 5-HT3 receptor antagonists (ondansetron), suggesting
potential synergistic benefits when combined with conventional antiemetics.

THC is the primary antiemetic cannabinoid. CBD may contribute through
serotonin receptor modulation (5-HT1A) and anti-inflammatory pathways. The
entourage effect of whole-plant cannabis may enhance efficacy beyond
isolated THC.

## Clinical Evidence

### Dronabinol (Synthetic THC)
Multiple RCTs demonstrate oral dronabinol is superior to placebo and
approximately equivalent to prochlorperazine for CINV. A meta-analysis of
30 studies with over 1,300 patients showed significant cannabinoid
antiemetic efficacy, particularly for patients refractory to conventional
antiemetics.

### Nabilone
Nabilone shows efficacy comparable to dronabinol. Some studies suggest it
may be more effective for delayed-onset nausea, which is often more
difficult to treat.

### Whole-Plant Cannabis
Patient surveys consistently indicate preference for whole-plant cannabis
over synthetics. Inhaled cannabis provides faster onset of relief than oral
preparations — critical for breakthrough nausea despite prophylactic
treatment.

### Combination Therapy
Evidence supports cannabinoids as adjuncts to standard regimens. Studies
comparing ondansetron plus dronabinol versus ondansetron alone show mixed
results, but cannabinoids appear valuable for refractory nausea.

## CINV Classification and Cannabis Response

- **Anticipatory nausea**: Anxiolytic properties of cannabis may help,
  but evidence is limited.

- **Acute nausea** (within 24 hours): Strongest evidence base, with
  multiple RCTs supporting efficacy.

- **Delayed nausea** (24-72 hours): Some evidence supports nabilone.

## Advantages Over Conventional Antiemetics

1. **Multi-symptom relief**: Simultaneously addresses nausea, pain,
   appetite loss, insomnia, and anxiety.

2. **Novel mechanism**: Patients failing 5-HT3 or NK1 antagonists may
   respond to CB1-mediated pathway.

3. **Appetite stimulation**: Promotes nutrition, unlike conventional
   antiemetics.

4. **Patient preference**: Many prefer botanical cannabis over synthetics.

## Limitations and Considerations

### Route of Administration
Oral cannabinoids have delayed onset (30-60 min) and variable absorption.
Inhaled cannabis provides rapid relief but raises respiratory concerns.
Sublingual tinctures and oromucosal sprays offer intermediate onset.

### Psychoactive Effects
THC produces euphoria, dysphoria, anxiety, and cognitive impairment.
CBD may mitigate some effects. Patient education is essential.

### Tolerance
Regular users may develop tolerance, requiring dose escalation. Drug
holidays or rotation may maintain efficacy.

### Regulatory Barriers
Cannabis remains Schedule I under U.S. federal law, limiting research
access. Only synthetic cannabinoids are available by prescription in all
states; botanical access varies by jurisdiction.

## CHI Assessment

For CINV, the evidence base is among the strongest in all medical cannabis
research, with multiple RCTs, systematic reviews, and decades of clinical
experience supporting cannabinoid antiemetic efficacy.
