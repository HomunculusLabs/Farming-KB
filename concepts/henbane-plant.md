# Henbane Plant in Plants of the Gods

Schultes and Hofmann document henbane (Hyoscyamus niger and H. albus) as one
of the most important hallucinogenic plants of European history, feared and
revered from classical antiquity through the Middle Ages as a principal
ingredient of witches' brews and oracular preparations.

## Botanical Identity

Two species are recognized in the ethnobotanical record: Black Henbane
(Hyoscyamus niger L.) and Yellow Henbane (Hyoscyamus albus L.), both members
of the Solanaceae (Nightshade Family). Henbane is a coarse annual or
biennial herb bearing conspicuous leaves and dirty yellow, funnel-shaped
flowers marked with a purple or brownish network. Indian Henbane or Egyptian
Henbane (H. muticus) is also noted.

See also: [[henbane-hyoscyamus-niger-ethnobotany]]

## Classical Antiquity

Hyoscyamus has been known and feared from earliest classical periods, when it
was recognized that there were several kinds and that the black variety was
the most potent, capable of causing insanity. The ancient Egyptians recorded
their knowledge of Henbane in the Ebers Papyrus, written in 1500 BC. Homer
described magic drinks with effects indicative of Henbane as a major
ingredient.

In ancient Greece, Henbane served as a poison, to mimic insanity, and to
enable man to prophesy. It has been suggested that the priestesses at the
Oracle of Delphi made their prophetic utterances while intoxicated with the
smoke from Henbane seeds.

## Medieval Witchcraft

During the Middle Ages, Henbane was an ingredient of the witches' brews and
ointments. In ancient Greece and Rome, reports of "magic drinks" indicate
that Henbane frequently served as an ingredient. Henbane is best known as an
ingredient of the ointments applied by witches and as a flying ointment.
Witches were often given a drink of Henbane so they could fall into a deep
sleep and dream.

## Effects of Intoxication

Those who consume Henbane feel a pressure in the head, a sensation as if
something were pressing on the brain. The intoxication induced by Henbane
differs markedly from other classical hallucinogens. The effects are
characterized by an intoxication followed by narcosis, in which
hallucinations occur during the transition state between consciousness and
sleep. The user remembers nothing experienced during the intoxication,
losing all sense of reality and falling into a deep sleep resembling
alcoholic delirium.

## Chemistry

Henbane contains relatively high concentrations of tropane alkaloids,
primarily atropine, hyoscyamine, and scopolamine; other bases occur in
trace amounts. It is apparently scopolamine, not atropine or hyoscyamine,
that produces the hallucinogenic effects. The difference between Henbane and
its solanaceous relatives Belladonna and Mandrake is one of relative
concentration of these alkaloids. Belladonna contains little scopolamine, but
this alkaloid is the main component of Mandrake and especially of Henbane.

Atropine has served chemists as a model for the synthesis of several
hallucinogenic compounds. The extraordinary reputation of these solanaceous
plants is due primarily to the bizarre psychoactivity they possess.

## Ancient Medicine

The Great (Dioscorides) reported that Henbane was employed in classical
medicine. The medicinal properties of Henbane have been recognized for
millennia. Egyptian Henbane (H. muticus) has been used in similar ways. In
the Middle Ages, Henbane was valued for its medicinal as well as its
psychoactive properties. Preparations of Henbane seeds were used to produce a
soothing smoke, and the plant was employed in various therapeutic contexts.

## Ritual and Oracle Use

The association of Henbane with prophecy and oracular practice extends deep
into antiquity. The suggestion that the priestesses at Delphi prophesied
under the influence of Henbane connects this plant to one of the most
important religious institutions of classical Greece. The smoke of Henbane
seeds was employed in various ritual contexts to induce visionary states
and communication with the divine.

## Comparative Solanaceous Context

Henbane belongs to the group of four solanaceous plants that Schultes and
Hofmann designate as the "hexing herbs": Henbane (Hyoscyamus), Belladonna
(Atropa belladonna), Mandrake (Mandragora officinarum), and Datura. All four
species have long histories of use as hallucinogens and magic plants
connected with sorcery, witchcraft, and superstition. Their similarity in
effects is the result of similarity in chemical constitution.
