# Soil Nematodes

## Overview

Nematodes are nonsegmented, blind roundworms found in virtually every soil environment on Earth. Their name derives from the Greek word *nema*, meaning "thread" — a fitting description of their slender, cylindrical bodies. On average, soil nematodes measure approximately 2 millimeters in length and just 50 micrometers in diameter, making them invisible to the naked eye yet overwhelmingly abundant.

Despite their diminutive size, nematodes are the **second most dominant form of animal life on Earth**, surpassed only by arthropods. Scientists have identified more than 20,000 species of nematodes to date, but some estimates suggest the true total could reach as high as 1 million species. Their [[lichen-biology-and-ecological-significance]] in soil food webs is profound, serving simultaneously as predators, prey, decomposers, and nutrient cyclers.

## Abundance in Soil

A single teaspoon of healthy soil typically contains **40 to 50 nematodes**. The composition of this population follows a characteristic pattern:

- ~20 bacteria-eating (bacterivorous) nematodes
- ~20 fungal-feeding (fungivorous) nematodes
- A small number of predatory nematodes
- A small number of plant-parasitic nematodes

This balance is a hallmark of a well-functioning soil ecosystem. When the ratios shift dramatically — for example, a spike in plant-parasitic species — it can signal underlying soil health problems such as nutrient imbalances, compaction, or disruption of the microbial community.

Conversely, a healthy ratio dominated by bacterivores and fungivores, with a smaller but consistent predatory population, indicates a mature and stable [[soil-food-web]] where energy flows efficiently from decomposing organic matter through microbes and nematodes to higher trophic levels.

## Classification by Feeding Habits

Nematodes are classified primarily by their **eating habits** and the **specialized mouthparts** they have evolved. These morphological adaptations determine what each nematode can consume and how it interacts with the broader soil food web.

### Bacterivores

Bacterivorous nematodes possess a simple **hollow tube** as a mouthpart, optimized for sucking up bacteria suspended in soil water films. A single bacterivore can consume multitudes of bacteria per hour, making them one of the most efficient bacterial grazers in the soil ecosystem. By feeding on bacteria, they regulate bacterial populations and prevent any single bacterial group from dominating the soil pore space.

Their high metabolic rate and rapid reproductive cycle mean bacterivore populations can respond quickly to flushes of bacterial growth — such as those following rain events, root exudation pulses, or the incorporation of fresh organic matter into soil.

### Fungivores

Fungivorous nematodes are equipped with a **stylet** — a hardened, needle-like structure — that allows them to pierce the tough **chitin cell walls** of [[fungal-hyphae]]. Once the stylet penetrates the hyphal wall, the nematode withdraws the fungal cytoplasm as food. Fungivores play an important role in controlling fungal biomass and cycling the nutrients stored within fungal networks.

In no-till and fungal-dominated soils, fungivorous nematodes become proportionally more important, grazing on the extensive mycorrhizal and saprophytic hyphal networks that characterize these systems. Their feeding activity releases nutrients locked within fungal biomass back into plant-available forms.

### Plant Parasites

Plant-parasitic nematodes also use a stylet to feed, but their target is living plant tissue — specifically **root tissue**. They are responsible for significant agricultural damage worldwide and are broadly categorized by their feeding strategy:

- **Ectoparasites** — Remain outside the root while inserting their stylet into root cells to feed
- **Endoparasites** — Burrow inside the root tissue, feeding from within

The damage they cause manifests in several characteristic forms:

- **Root lesions** — Localized areas of dead or damaged root tissue caused by migratory endoparasites
- **Cysts** — Swollen, egg-containing structures formed on roots by sedentary endoparasites (e.g., *Heterodera* and *Globodera* species)
- **Root knots** — Gall-like swellings on roots induced by root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne* species), which disrupt water and nutrient uptake

While plant-parasitic nematodes are often viewed purely as pests, it is important to note that in balanced soils their populations are naturally suppressed by predatory nematodes, [[nematophagous-fungi]], and other soil organisms, keeping their impact minimal.

### Predatory Nematodes

Predatory nematodes are the apex micro-predators of [[the-soil-food-web]]. Their diet is remarkably broad:

- Protozoa
- Algae
- Insect larvae (grubs)
- Weevils and wasps
- Slugs
- Other nematodes (including plant parasites)

One of their most critical ecological roles is **preventing overgrazing**. By consuming bacterivores and fungivores, predatory nematodes keep microbial grazers in check, which in turn prevents the excessive depletion of bacterial and fungal populations that perform essential decomposition and nutrient-holding functions.

### Omnivores

Some nematode species are generalist feeders that consume virtually anything they can overpower or puncture. These omnivores add resilience to the soil food web, able to switch food sources depending on what is most available in their immediate environment.

## Nutrient Cycling and Mineralization

Nematodes are powerful agents of **mineralization** — the process of converting organic nitrogen compounds into plant-available inorganic forms. When nematodes consume bacteria or fungi, they assimilate a portion of the nitrogen in their food but excrete the surplus as **ammonium (NH₄⁺)**, a form directly usable by plants.

A key advantage nematodes have over protozoa in this role is their **lower nitrogen requirement**. Because their bodies need less nitrogen per unit of biomass than protozoa, nematodes release a greater proportion of consumed nitrogen back into the soil. This makes them especially efficient at making nutrients available to plant roots.

The mineralization pathway through nematodes is sometimes called the **microbial loop** shortcut: plants invest carbon into [[root-exudates]] → bacteria and fungi multiply → nematodes graze the microbes → excess nitrogen is excreted as ammonium → plant roots absorb it. This cycle can operate on timescales of hours to days, far faster than passive decomposition alone.

## Soil Porosity Requirements

Nematodes move through the thin water films that coat soil particles, relying on the interconnected pore spaces of well-structured soil. **Soil compaction is one of the most significant threats to nematode populations.** When soil is compressed — by heavy machinery, foot traffic, or natural settling — the pore networks collapse, restricting nematode movement and cutting them off from food sources and mates.

Maintaining adequate soil porosity through practices like [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]], cover cropping, and organic matter addition is essential for supporting healthy nematode communities. Soils with high organic matter content tend to have more stable aggregates and a wider range of pore sizes, providing ideal habitat for diverse nematode populations.

## Dispersal and Transport

Nematodes themselves are mobile, but they also serve as **vectors for bacteria and fungi**. Microorganisms frequently hitch rides on the cuticle (outer skin) of nematodes as they move through the soil. This phoretic relationship benefits the microbes by providing transport to new nutrient-rich locations, and it benefits the soil ecosystem by accelerating the colonization of organic matter and root zones by [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]].

This transport function means that nematodes indirectly influence where bacterial and fungal communities establish and thrive, effectively seeding new soil microsites with beneficial organisms as they migrate through the soil profile.

## Food Location Mechanisms

Nematodes are remarkably effective at locating food despite their blindness and lack of complex sensory organs. They employ two primary sensing strategies:

1. **Temperature gradient sensing** — Nematodes can detect minute temperature differences in the soil, which often correspond to areas of high microbial activity (decomposing organic matter generates heat)
2. **Chemical sensing** — Nematodes perceive chemical cues — [[chamovitz-plant-scent-communication-volatile-organic-compounds]], amino acids, and other metabolites — released by their prey

Their movement toward food sources has been described as **"heat-seeking missile behavior"** — they orient themselves along chemical and thermal gradients with impressive precision, adjusting their sinusoidal movement pattern to home in on their target.

## Fungal Predators of Nematodes

The predator-prey dynamic in soil is not one-directional. Several species of **fungi have evolved specialized mechanisms to trap and consume nematodes**, forming a natural biological control against nematode overpopulation. These nematophagous fungi employ remarkable strategies:

- **Constricting rings** — Some fungi form three-celled ring structures on their hyphae. When a nematode crawls through the ring, the cells rapidly inflate, constricting and trapping the nematode within seconds
- **Chemical attractants** — Predatory fungi release volatile and soluble compounds that mimic the chemical signals of nematode food sources, luring nematodes toward the fungal traps

This biological arms race between nematodes and fungi represents one of the most fascinating antagonistic relationships in soil ecology and is the basis for several [[beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]] products used in sustainable agriculture.

## Ecological Significance

Soil nematodes occupy a **central position in the soil food web**. They link the microbial loop (bacteria and fungi) to higher trophic levels (microarthropods, insects), regulate microbial [[fukuoka-textdoc-microclimate-mite-scale-population-dynamics]], drive nutrient mineralization, and serve as bioindicators of soil health. Their diversity, abundance, and feeding group ratios provide valuable diagnostic information about the condition and functioning of soil ecosystems.

> **Key principle:** In healthy soil, the nematode community is diverse and balanced. A shift toward dominance by any single feeding group — especially plant parasites — indicates disturbance. The goal of soil management is not to eliminate nematodes but to foster the conditions that support a diverse, self-regulating nematode community.

## Summary of Feeding Groups

| Feeding Group | Mouthpart | Primary Diet | Ecological Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bacterivores | Hollow tube | Bacteria | Regulate bacterial populations, mineralize nitrogen |
| Fungivores | Stylet | Fungal hyphae | Control fungal biomass, cycle fungal nutrients |
| Plant parasites | Stylet | Root tissue | Can cause crop damage; suppressed by predators in healthy soil |
| Predators | Teeth/grasping structures | Other nematodes, protozoa, insects | Top-down regulation, prevent overgrazing of microbes |
| Omnivores | Variable | Mixed diet | Food web resilience, opportunistic feeding |
