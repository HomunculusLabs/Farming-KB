# Permaculture Site Analysis

Site analysis is the essential first step in any permaculture design. Before placing a single element, the designer must thoroughly understand the land — its strengths, limitations, patterns, and potentials. This process aligns with the principle "observe and interact" and creates the foundation for "design from patterns to details."

## Sector Mapping

Sector analysis maps the external energies and influences that affect a site. These are forces the designer cannot control but can work with or redirect.

### Common Sectors
- **Sun sectors** — path of the sun across seasons, shade patterns, solar gain zones
- **Wind sectors** — prevailing wind directions, cold winter winds, cooling summer breezes
- **Water sectors** — runoff patterns, flood risk, groundwater flow, spring locations
- **Fire sectors** — wildfire risk directions, fuel loads, defensible space needs
- **Wildlife sectors** — animal movement corridors, pest entry points, beneficial insect pathways
- **Noise and pollution** — roads, industry, neighbors that affect site quality
- **Views** — desirable and undesirable sight lines

The result is typically an overlay map showing wedge-shaped sectors radiating from the house or central point. Designs then place elements to either harvest beneficial energies (sun, summer breeze) or buffer against harmful ones (cold wind, fire, noise).

## Slope Analysis

Understanding the landform is critical for water management, access, and planting decisions.

### Slope Categories
- **0-3%** — nearly flat; water drainage may be slow; suitable for buildings, gardens, ponds
- **3-8%** — gentle slope; easy to work; good for swales, orchards, grazing
- **8-15%** — moderate slope; requires terracing or contour planting; good for orchards on contour
- **15-30%** — steep; erosion risk is high; best kept in permanent vegetation, trees, or pasture
- **30%+** — very steep; avoid cultivation; maintain forest cover; use for timber, wildlife habitat

### Key Slope Considerations
- Place water-harvesting earthworks (swales, berms) on contour
- Position access roads on the ridgeline, not in the valley
- Use gravity for water distribution where possible — place storage tanks uphill from use points
- Cold air drains downhill; frost pockets form in low points

## Soil Survey

A thorough soil assessment informs plant selection, earthwork design, and amendment strategies.

### Assessment Methods
- **Visual inspection** — color, structure, root penetration depth, worm activity
- **Texture test** — ribbon test for clay content, jar test for sand/silt/clay proportions
- **Drainage test** — dig a hole, fill with water, observe infiltration rate
- **pH testing** — simple kit or meter; most crops prefer 6.0-7.0
- **Soil profile** — dig a pit to examine layers (topsoil, subsoil, parent material, bedrock)
- **Lab analysis** — for detailed nutrient levels, organic matter percentage, CEC

### Soil Mapping
Create a soil map showing different soil types across the site. Note areas of compaction, erosion, waterlogging, or contamination. This map directly influences [[water-management]] earthwork placement and plant community selection.

## Water Flow Analysis

Water is often the first design priority in permaculture. Understanding how water moves across the landscape determines where to place swales, ponds, diversion drains, and planting areas.

### Mapping Water Flow
- Observe the site during and after rain — where does water flow, pool, or erode?
- Mark existing drainage lines, wet areas, springs, and seeps
- Calculate approximate catchment area and runoff volumes
- Identify the highest and lowest points on the property

### Water Priorities
1. Slow water down (swales, berms, vegetation)
2. Spread it across the landscape (contour earthworks)
3. Sink it into the soil (deep-rooted plants, organic matter)
4. Store it (ponds, tanks, soil moisture)

## Microclimate Assessment

Microclimates are small areas with conditions different from the general climate. Identifying them creates opportunities for species that would otherwise not thrive.

### Common Microclimates
- **Frost pockets** — low areas where cold air accumulates
- **Heat sinks** — south-facing walls, rocks, or buildings that absorb and radiate heat
- **Wind tunnels** — gaps between structures or trees that channel wind
- **Shade zones** — under canopy, north side of structures
- **Moisture zones** — near water features, springs, or drainage lines
- **Dry zones** — under eaves, near windward edges

### Using Microclimates
Plant tender species in warm microclimates (against south walls, near thermal mass). Use frost pockets for hardy natives or as water storage. Place humidity-loving plants near ponds or in shade corridors.

## Access and Movement

Map how people, animals, and equipment move across the site. Identify existing paths, desire lines (where people naturally walk), and planned routes. Good access design reduces erosion, saves energy, and makes the system pleasant to use.

### Zone Planning
Zone planning is the permaculture method of placing elements based on frequency of use:
- **Zone 0** — the house, home center
- **Zone 1** — daily visits: herbs, salad greens, seedlings, small animals
- **Zone 2** — frequent visits: chickens, ducks, vegetables, fruit trees
- **Zone 3** — occasional visits: main crops, orchards, pastures, goats
- **Zone 4** — infrequent visits: forage, timber, wildlife corridor
- **Zone 5** — unmanaged: wilderness, biodiversity reserve

## Documentation

A complete site analysis package typically includes:
- Base map (scale drawing of property boundaries and features)
- Topographic/contour map
- Sector overlay map
- Soil map
- Water flow map
- Microclimate map
- Zone plan
- Existing vegetation inventory
- Photographs from multiple vantage points
