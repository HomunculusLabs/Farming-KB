## Integration Rather Than Segregation

Closely related to stacking functions is the principle of integrating
rather than segregating. Monocropping, the practice of using only one
type of plant in a specific area, is an example of segregation.
Polycropping, using several different plant species together, is an
example of integration. In a well-integrated system, each element
effects and supports every other element. Together they form a web of
mutually beneficial relationships.

When you integrate elements, the system becomes more robust. If you
mess with one element in the system, all other elements are affected
in a cascading web of connections. This is by design: it means the
system is alive and responsive. A polyculture system incorporating
trees, shrubs, ground covers, root crops, and climbing plants creates
a complete nutrient cycle, modifies microclimates, and supports
complex food webs that resist [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] course teaches you how
to connect plants ecologically to other elements in the system. The
culture, the infrastructure, and the technology that supports those
plants are equally important design considerations.

Each element should be connected to everything else in as many
mutually beneficial relationships as possible. This diversity of
connections increases efficiency and places value on elements that
might formerly have been seen as annoyances. Every 'problem' can be
turned into an advantage. Weeds are Stage 1 plants (pioneers) that
can be turned into mulch. A big immovable rock becomes part of a
supporting wall for a structure.

## Producing No Waste Through Integration

Nature has no concept of waste. Everything is used and then reused in
an endless cycle. When a bear deposits manure in the forest, it
becomes nutrients that feed plants, which in turn feed the bear. When
the bear dies, its body decomposes and supports life for its progeny.
Waste is arguably the only true human invention. From a Permaculture
perspective, waste is a [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]] issue.

Every material, after its first function is done, can be reused,
recycled, or repurposed into something beneficial. The only limit to
its potential is the imagination and skill of the designer. With
some forethought, designers could create products that, when their
initial use is over, are feasible to recycle or reuse. Producing no
waste is a design issue, a resource management issue, and a choice.
