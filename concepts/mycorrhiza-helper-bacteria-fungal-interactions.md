# Mycorrhiza Helper Bacteria and Fungal-Bacterial Interactions

## Overview

Mycorrhiza Helper Bacteria (MHB) are soil bacteria that specifically promote
the establishment and functioning of mycorrhizal symbioses. First defined by
Garbaye (1994), MHB represent a critical component of the [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] fungi. Bacterial species colonise the
surfaces of fungal hyphae and mycorrhizal roots, and some reside within
fungal structures. The best-studied MHB include Streptomyces spp.,
Pseudomonas spp., Paenibacillus spp., and Bacillus spp.

## Streptomyces AcH 505: A Model MHB

Streptomyces sp. AcH 505, isolated from the hyphosphere of a Norway spruce
(Picea abies) stand, has emerged as a model system for studying MHB
interactions:

  - Significantly promotes mycelial growth and mycorrhization rate of
    the ectomycorrhizal fungus [[streptomycete-mycorrhization-helper-bacteria]] effect.
Three experimental approaches are used:

  - Growing bacteria next to the fungus on agar, allowing diffusible
    molecules to interact
  - Using a cellophane membrane below fungal material, restricting fungal
    growth into the agar while allowing passage of small soluble metabolites
    and volatiles
  - Cross-walled Petri dishes that permit only [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] to pass
    between compartments

### Influence of Media and Growth Conditions

Media composition critically influences interaction outcomes. The mutualistic
interaction between Streptomyces AcH 505 and Amanita muscaria shifts to
antagonism when cultivated on ISP2 medium instead of MMN medium. Similarly,
bacteria may compensate for poor fungal growth under suboptimal conditions
but may not further increase the maximum growth of faster-growing isolates
under optimal conditions.

The growth rate of the mycelium also affects bacterial influence. The strain
of Amanita muscaria growing most slowly on MMN medium was most responsive
to helper streptomycetes, suggesting that bacteria may compensate for poor
growth conditions rather than enhance already-optimal growth.
