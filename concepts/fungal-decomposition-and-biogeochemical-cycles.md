# Fungal Decomposition and Biogeochemical Cycles

Fungi play fundamental roles in biogeochemical cycling — the transformation and movement of chemical elements through the biosphere. The chapter by Geoffrey Gadd, Euan Burford, Marina Fomina, and Karrie Melville in *Fungi in the Environment* (Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, 2007) presents a comprehensive "geomycological" perspective on how fungi transform minerals, mobilize and immobilize elements, and influence the long-term chemistry of terrestrial environments.

## Geomycology Defined

Geomycology is the study of the roles of fungi in geological processes, including mineral weathering, mineral formation (biomineralization), and the cycling of major and trace elements. While bacterial geomicrobiology has been studied extensively, the geomycological contributions of fungi — despite their often larger biomass in soil — have received less attention until recently.

Fungi are uniquely suited to geomycological roles because of their filamentous growth habit, which allows them to physically penetrate rock and mineral substrates; their production of powerful organic acids and chelating agents, which chemically dissolve minerals; and their ability to accumulate metals and other elements at concentrations far exceeding their metabolic needs.

## Organic Matter Decomposition

### The Decomposer Role

Saprotrophic fungi are the primary decomposers of lignin and cellulose — the two most abundant organic polymers on Earth. While bacteria can decompose cellulose, only a relatively small group of fungi (primarily Basidiomycota) possess the enzymatic machinery to efficiently break down lignin, the complex aromatic polymer that gives wood its structural rigidity.

The lignin-degrading enzymes produced by white-rot and brown-rot fungi include:

- **Laccases:** Multi-copper oxidases that oxidize phenolic compounds in lignin
- **Manganese peroxidases:** Oxidize Mn²⁺ to Mn³⁺, which then acts as a diffusible oxidant
- **Lignin peroxidases:** Directly oxidize non-phenolic lignin structures
- **Peroxidases and oxidases:** Generate [[cervantes-hydrogen-peroxide-sterilization]] used by other [[singh-ligninolytic-enzymes-fungal-bioremediation]]

This enzymatic capability makes fungi essential for carbon cycling in forest ecosystems, where lignin constitutes 20–30% of woody biomass and would otherwise accumulate indefinitely.

### Decomposition Processes

Fungal decomposition of organic matter involves several sequential stages:

1. **Colonization:** Fungal hyphae physically contact the substrate, often preceded by spore germination triggered by chemical signals from the organic material
2. **Enzyme secretion:** Extracellular enzymes are produced and diffuse into the substrate, breaking down complex polymers into smaller molecules
3. **Absorption:** The breakdown products are absorbed through the hyphal cell wall and membrane
4. **Metabolism:** Absorbed compounds are metabolized for energy and biosynthesis, with waste products (CO₂, water, minerals) released back to the environment
5. **Mineralization:** Organic nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur are converted to inorganic forms available for plant uptake

## Mineral Weathering

### Mechanisms of Fungal Weathering

Fungi accelerate the breakdown of rock-forming minerals through both mechanical and chemical mechanisms:

**Mechanical weathering:** Hyphal tips exert turgor pressure as they grow, physically forcing apart mineral grains and extending into cracks and fissures in rock. This penetration is assisted by the secretion of mucilaginous polysaccharides that lubricate the hyphal surface and help wedge apart mineral particles.

**Chemical weathering:** Fungi secrete organic acids (oxalic, citric, gluconic) that dissolve minerals through acid hydrolysis and chelation. Oxalic acid is particularly important — it forms stable complexes with calcium, iron, and aluminum, effectively removing these cations from mineral structures and accelerating dissolution.

Fungal siderophores — high-affinity iron-chelating compounds — also contribute to mineral weathering by extracting iron from silicate and oxide minerals. This iron-scavenging ability is especially important in iron-limited environments.

### Minerals Subjected to Fungal Weathering

Fungi have been documented weathering a wide range of rock-forming minerals:

- **Silicates:** Feldspars, micas, pyroxenes, and amphiboles are dissolved by organic acid secretion, releasing potassium, calcium, magnesium, and other cations
- **Phosphates:** Apatite and other phosphate minerals are solubilized, making phosphorus available for biological uptake
- **Carbonates:** Calcite and dolomite are readily dissolved by organic acids, contributing to calcium cycling
- **Iron and aluminum oxides:** These relatively insoluble minerals are attacked by siderophores and organic acids, releasing iron and aluminum

## Metal Transformations

### Metal Accumulation and Biosorption

Fungi can accumulate metals from solution through several mechanisms:

- **Biosorption:** Metals bind to cell wall components (chitin, glucans, melanin) without entering the cell. This passive process can remove large quantities of metals from solution and is the basis for fungal bioremediation technologies
- **Bioaccumulation:** Metals are actively transported into the cell and may be sequestered in vacuoles or bound to intracellular proteins such as metallothioneins
- **Precipitation:** Fungal metabolic activities can change environmental conditions (pH, redox potential) causing metals to precipitate as insoluble compounds

### Metal Transformations

Fungi can change the chemical form (speciation) of metals, altering their mobility and toxicity:

- **Oxidation/reduction:** Some fungi oxidize manganese(II) to manganese(III/IV) oxides, or reduce chromium(VI) to less toxic chromium(III)
- **Methylation:** Fungi can methylate arsenic, mercury, and other elements, producing volatile species that enter the atmosphere
- **Complexation:** Organic acids and siderophores form soluble complexes with metals, increasing their mobility in soil and water

### Biomineral Formation

Fungi can induce the formation of secondary minerals through their metabolic activities:

- **[[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]]:** Many fungi precipitate calcium oxalate crystals in and around their mycelium. These crystals can persist in soil and contribute to calcium cycling and soil structure
- **Metal oxalates:** Similar processes can form oxalate minerals with other metals (iron, copper, zinc)
- **Carbonate precipitation:** Some fungi induce calcium carbonate precipitation through urease activity or pH modification

## Nutrient Cycling

### Carbon Cycle

Fungi mediate carbon flow through ecosystems by decomposing organic matter and transferring carbon into fungal biomass and soil organic matter. Fungal cell walls contain chitin and glucans that decompose more slowly than bacterial cell wall components, contributing to relatively stable soil carbon pools.

### Nitrogen Cycle

Fungi participate in nitrogen cycling through:

- **Protein decomposition:** Fungal proteases break down proteins and peptides, releasing amino acids and ammonium
- **Nitrification:** Some fungi can oxidize ammonium to nitrite, though bacterial nitrification is generally more important
- **Denitrification:** Under anaerobic conditions, certain fungi can reduce nitrate to nitrogen gas, contributing to nitrogen loss from ecosystems
- **Nitrogen immobilization:** Fungi assimilate inorganic nitrogen into biomass, temporarily removing it from the plant-available pool

### Phosphorus Cycle

Fungi are critical for [[mollison-phosphorus-cycling-and-conservation-in-permaculture-systems]] because they can solubilize insoluble phosphates through organic acid secretion and access organic phosphorus through phosphatase enzyme production. Mycorrhizal fungi are particularly important, extending the effective phosphorus acquisition zone of plant roots by orders of magnitude.

### Sulfur Cycle

Fungi decompose sulfur-containing organic compounds (sulfonates, sulfates, and amino acids) and can oxidize elemental sulfur and sulfide minerals. Some fungi can also reduce sulfate under anaerobic conditions.

## Environmental Applications

The geomycological capabilities of fungi have practical applications in environmental management:

- **Bioremediation:** Using fungi to remove metals, radionuclides, and organic pollutants from contaminated soil and water
- **Biomining:** Employing fungi to solubilize valuable metals from low-grade ores and waste materials
- **Soil restoration:** Applying fungi to accelerate soil formation on degraded or newly exposed substrates
- **Carbon sequestration:** Managing fungal communities to enhance long-term soil carbon storage
