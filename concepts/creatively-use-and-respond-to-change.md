# Creatively Use and Respond to Change

Creatively Use and Respond to Change is the twelfth and final principle of [[permaculture-design]], as articulated by [[david-holmgren]]. It encompasses two complementary threads: designing to make deliberate and cooperative use of change, and creatively responding or adapting to large-scale system change that is beyond our control or influence.

## Core Philosophy

While stability is important in Permaculture, evolutionary change is essential. Permaculture concerns the durability of natural living systems and human culture, but this durability paradoxically depends on flexibility and change. Many traditions hold that within the greatest stability lie the seeds of change. Modern science confirms this — at the cellular and atomic level, the apparently solid and permanent is a seething mass of energy and change.

The butterfly, as a transformation of a caterpillar, symbolizes adaptive change that is uplifting rather than threatening. Understanding impermanence and continuous change must be integrated into daily consciousness.

## Scale-Dependent Nature of Change

The apparent illusion of stability, permanence, and sustainability is resolved by recognizing the scale-dependent nature of change (Principle 7: [[design-from-patterns-to-details]]). In any particular system, small-scale, fast, short-lived changes of elements contribute to higher-order system stability. A contextual and systemic sense of the dynamic balance between stability and change contributes to design that is evolutionary rather than random.

The proverb "vision is not seeing things as they are but as they will be" emphasizes that understanding change goes far beyond projecting statistical trend lines.

## Top-Down Managerial Change

When intervening in systems over which we have substantial design or management influence — gardens, farms, businesses, families — we can make use of change in ways that reflect our power and relationship to the system. This is top-down control. However, our hierarchical relationships with co-participants and the degree to which we are engaged in interactive exchange determine how much top-down control is possible or appropriate.

### Permaculture Design as Top-Down Change

The Permaculture approach starts by dealing with concrete and mundane aspects of material existence through self-audit processes. By making small changes, confidence grows to tackle larger ones. A rational audit of material needs and processes can be a fast track to dealing with abstract questions of motivation, value, and meaning.

In the garden, practitioners are free to explore and experiment with top-down change processes because they can exercise great relative power when they choose. The mysteries of soil and nature's complexity challenge hubristic control, while seasonal harvest and ease of management reward appropriate action.

### The Problem of Over-Intervention

The general problem with top-down control is doing too much too quickly. A little change goes a long way if used carefully. Identifying where limited resources and power can gain the most leverage is more important than rushing around trying to make everything just right. [[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s "do-nothing" [[natural-farming]] suggests observing first and thinking hard before changing anything.

Fukuoka's approach and Mollison's "self-maintaining Permaculture Garden of Eden" should not be misinterpreted as passive consumption of a "natural" drip feed. The two responses — over-energetic and slothful — come to the fore in most people at different times. Permaculture involves the transition from dependent consumer to responsible producer.

## Resilience to Change from Above

Sometimes we must respond to change driven by forces beyond our control or comprehension. Gaia delivers change through tectonic forces, weather events, climatic change, species invasions, and disease. Governments and global corporations make decisions individuals cannot significantly affect. How much these forces affect our lives depends on our independence from, or resilience and adaptability to, specific or general change.

### Flexibility

When dealing with powerful external forces, flexibility becomes more important than resistance and rigidity. Large, long-lived trees occupying fertile alluvial flats are replaced by pliable trees and shrubs along stream banks and islands, where destructive floods make rigidity a disadvantage. This flexibility produces a dynamic stability more like riding a bike than the inert stability of a concrete slab.

In the face of extreme and unpredictable change, human behavior is driven toward opportunistic responses — from the homeless person living by hustling and handouts to the pastoralist who sows a crop on a floodplain to use rare flood fertility.

### Entropy as Constant Source of Change

Beyond precipitous change, there are predictable and inexorable changes due to entropy — the tendency to disorder. These lead to ageing and gradual decay in all living systems, especially affecting the built environment.

## Built Environment: Renewability vs. Durability

Learning to see change in the built environment as an organic process is important as energy declines. Two strategies exist: building things to last (durable), or using cheap and abundant renewable options that adapt to entropy's effects.

### Stewart Brand's Six S's of Buildings

From most permanent to most ephemeral:
1. **Site** — the location itself
2. **Structure** — the foundation and frame
3. **Skin** — the exterior cladding
4. **Services** — wiring, plumbing, HVAC
5. **Space plan** — interior layout
6. **Stuff** — furniture and movable items

By not embedding ephemeral aspects in permanent structure, flexibility is maintained for current and future users. This contrasts with the concept of buildings as fixed sculptural art pieces.

### When Renewable Approaches Excel

Renewable structures are favored when:
- A low-input renewable resource can grow replacement materials faster than decay demands replacement
- Replacement can be done without disrupting other system elements
- Novel design conditions and high uncertainty reduce the value of durable solutions
- The solution addresses only an ephemeral or occasional need

## Ecological Models of Succession

### Classic Succession Model

Based on observations of moist, temperate landscapes, following disturbance, bare ground is colonized by herbaceous weeds, leading to grassland, then pioneer shrubs, fast-growing forest trees, and finally slow-growing, long-lived climax trees. Changes progress from low to high biomass, low to high soil humus, low to high resilience, and competitive to cooperative relationships.

Agricultural land uses correspond to succession stages:
- **Crop agriculture** ≈ herbaceous weed phase
- **Pastures** ≈ managed grassland
- **Marginal/abandoned land** ≈ pioneer shrub and fast-growing trees
- **Traditional managed forests** ≈ near-climax state

### Initial Floristic Composition Model

This model suggests disturbance leads to simultaneous regeneration of all species, with short-lived ones dying out over time. This describes most Australian ecosystems, where fire or disturbance produces thick regeneration of seedling eucalypts, wattles, and understorey species, but diversity decreases over time as ephemerals disappear.

## Practical Succession Applications

Understanding successional dynamics is essential for working with plants and land. Common problems arising from succession ignorance include:
- Designing gardens at full capacity without accounting for system changes
- Planting large trees at wide spacings where they struggle with competing grass and damaging wind
- Abandoning species damaged by frost or wind when a more mature system would protect them
- Assuming new weeds signal invasive takeover when they may decline as perennials establish

The role of animals in steering succession is often ignored or misunderstood. Grazing animals can maintain thistles and other unwanted species if not managed appropriately.
