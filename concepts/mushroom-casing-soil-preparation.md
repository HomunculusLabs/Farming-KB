# Mushroom Casing Soil Preparation

Casing is a non-nutritive layer applied to fully colonized substrate to create
the microclimate necessary for mushroom fruitbody (pin) formation. The casing
soil itself provides no nutrition to the fungus; instead, it creates a humid,
structurally supportive surface with appropriate water-holding capacity and gas
exchange properties.

## Purpose of Casing

Casing layers serve several critical functions in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]:

1. **Moisture reservoir** — holds water that slowly evaporates, maintaining
   high humidity at the substrate surface
2. **[[holzer-climate-zone-microclimate-creation]]** — provides the specific humidity, temperature, and
   gas exchange conditions that trigger pinning ([[growing-gourmet-primordia-formation-pinning]])
3. **Physical support** — gives developing mushrooms a stable base
4. **Contamination barrier** — helps protect the colonized substrate from
   airborne contaminants during the vulnerable fruiting phase

## Standard Casing Recipe

The classic casing soil recipe uses three ingredients in dry form:

| Ingredient | Parts (by volume) | Function |
|------------|-------------------|----------|
| Peat moss | 10 | Water retention, structure |
| Vermiculite | 5 | Aeration, water retention |
| Limestone (Marl) | 2 | pH buffering |

### Peat Moss

Peat moss is the primary component, providing excellent water retention and a
loose, friable texture. The moisture content of peat varies between sources
and seasons, which affects the final water content of the casing mix.

### Vermiculite

Vermiculite is an expanded mica mineral that improves aeration and adds water-
holding capacity. It prevents the casing from becoming compacted and helps
maintain an open structure for gas exchange.

### Limestone (Marl)

Limestone buffers the pH of the casing. Most mushrooms prefer a slightly
alkaline casing (pH 7.0–7.5), which also helps suppress bacterial competitors
that thrive in acidic conditions. Agricultural lime or ground limestone
(marl) is used rather than hydrated lime, which can burn mycelium if applied
incorrectly.

## Preparation Method

1. **Mix dry ingredients** — combine peat, vermiculite, and limestone in the
   specified ratio in a large container
2. **Add water gradually** — while stirring continuously, add water until the
   mix reaches field capacity (holds maximum water without dripping when
   squeezed)
3. **Check consistency** — the casing should form a ball when squeezed but
   break apart easily when tapped. It should not be muddy or soupy
4. **Adjust if needed** — if too wet, add more dry ingredients; if too dry,
   add water sparingly

## Sterilization

The casing soil is sterilized before use to eliminate competing organisms:

- **Method**: [[pressure-cooker-sterilisation-fungal-substrate]] or autoclave at 15 PSI
- **Duration**: 1 hour
- **Containers**: Oven bags (nylon), autoclave bags (polypropylene), or jars
- **Cooling**: Must cool to room temperature before application

### The Sterilization Debate

Some authors advise against sterilizing casing soil, arguing that beneficial
microorganisms (particularly bacteria) are killed, which can reduce yields. The
theory is that certain bacteria in the casing stimulate pinning and suppress
molds. However, many experienced cultivators report **only bad experiences
with untreated or pasteurized casing soils**, particularly in non-commercial
settings where contamination is a greater risk. Sterilization provides more
consistent and reliable results for small-scale cultivation.

Pasteurization (heating to 70–80°C for 1–2 hours) is a middle ground that
kills most competitors while preserving some beneficial microbes. This can be
effective but is less reliable than full sterilization for hobbyist setups.

## Application to Colonized Substrate

When the grass seed substrate is fully colonized, it is cased as follows:

1. **Transfer grain** — the colonized grain is shaken loose from the jar and
   spread evenly in a fruiting tray (1-litre disposable plastic trays work
   well)
2. **Break up lumps** — any large aggregates of grain are broken up using
   the clean rim of the jar
3. **Level the surface** — the grain bed is made as even as possible
4. **Apply casing** — a layer of casing soil approximately **1.5–2.0 cm**
   thick is applied over the grain
5. **Texture the surface** — the casing surface should be kept both level and
   rough (with small valleys and hills). This uneven texture creates
   microclimates that promote even pinning
6. **Cover** — the cased tray is covered with foil and placed at 20–25°C

## Post-Casing Colonization

Within a few days of casing, mycelium will begin growing through the casing
soil. The timing of exposure to [[fruiting-conditions]] depends on the strain:

- **Easy-fruiting strains** (e.g., [[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]] "Ecuador"): may be
  ready as soon as mycelium is visible on the surface
- **Harder-to-fruit strains**: benefit from waiting until the mycelium has
  more fully colonized the casing layer

## Moisture Management

Maintaining proper casing moisture is one of the most important and
challenging aspects of fruiting:

- **Daily misting** — the casing surface should be misted daily and never
  allowed to dry out
- **Post-flush watering** — increase watering immediately after picking a
  flush, as maturing mushrooms extract significant moisture from the casing
- **Avoid overwatering** — waterlogged casing promotes bacterial bloom and
  prevents proper gas exchange
- **Develop intuition** — explicit watering instructions are difficult because
   requirements vary with strain, environment, and flush number

## Casing and Contamination

A well-prepared casing layer acts as a contamination buffer, but it is also
vulnerable. Dead pinheads (aborts) on the casing surface should be removed
promptly, as they serve as nutrient sources for mold colonization. If mold
appears on the casing surface, it can sometimes be controlled by applying a
thin layer of damp, sterilized casing soil or a salt paste to the affected
area, though heavily contaminated trays should be discarded.
