# Mushroom Casing Soil Preparation and Application

Casing is a non-nutritive layer applied to fully colonized substrate to trigger
and support the fruiting (mushroom production) phase of fungal cultivation.
The casing layer creates a microclimate at the substrate surface that provides
the humidity, gas exchange, and moisture retention conditions necessary for
primordia (pin) formation and subsequent mushroom development. Unlike the
nutritious substrate below, the casing itself provides no food to the fungus --
its role is entirely environmental.

## Casing Soil Recipe

The recommended recipe for casing soil consists of three ingredients mixed in
specific proportions:

- **10 parts peat** -- The primary component, providing water retention and
  structure. Peat moss holds large volumes of water while maintaining air
  spaces essential for gas exchange at the mycelial surface.
- **5 parts vermiculite** -- An expanded mineral that improves aeration and
  moisture distribution within the casing layer. Vermiculite prevents the
  peat from compacting and helps buffer moisture fluctuations.
- **2 parts limestone (marl)** -- Raises the pH of the naturally acidic peat
  to a range more favorable for mushroom formation while less hospitable to
  competing organisms. The [[peat-calcium-carbonate-perlite-casing-psilocybe-cubensis-miniculture]] also provides calcium ions that
  support [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]]. The authors state they have had
"only bad experiences with untreated or pasteurised casing soils."

The sterilization approach prioritizes [[ingham-aact-beauveria-fruit-fly-beneficial-bacteria-inoculant]] such as *Pseudomonas putida* in the casing layer
help stimulate fruiting and suppress competitor molds. However, the source
authors found that the risk of contamination from incompletely treated casing
outweighed any potential benefits from microbial activity.

## Application Technique

When the grass seed substrate is fully colonized, it is shaken loose and
poured into a 1-litre disposable plastic tray. Any lumps in the colonized
seed are broken up using the clean rim of the jar to create an even surface.
The casing layer is applied at a depth of 1.5 to 2.0 cm using a large spoon
and fork.

The casing surface should be both level and rough -- maintaining small
valleys and hills across the surface. This microtopography creates varied
microenvironments that support pin formation across the entire surface area.
A perfectly smooth casing surface can result in uneven pinning.

After casing, the tray is covered with aluminum foil and placed in a clean
location at 20-25°C. Within a few days, mycelium will become visible growing
through the casing soil. The timing of exposure to air and light to initiate
fruiting depends on the strain -- some fruit earlier and more readily than
others.

## Moisture Management During Fruiting

The casing layer must never be allowed to dry out. Daily misting is required
to maintain surface moisture. After each flush is harvested, watering should
be increased because maturing mushrooms pull substantial moisture from the
casing soil during development. The exact watering regime develops with
experience and depends on environmental conditions, strain, and flush number.

Dead pinheads (aborts) should be removed promptly from the casing surface.
These failed primordia can become sites for mold colonization if left in
place. Keeping the casing surface clean is one of the most important
maintenance tasks during the fruiting phase.
