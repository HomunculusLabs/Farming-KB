# Straw-Based Oyster Mushroom Cultivation

Straw is the most accessible and economical substrate
for oyster mushroom cultivation, making it the
preferred choice for beginners and small-scale growers
worldwide. Wheat straw, oat straw, and rice straw are
all suitable substrates, though wheat straw is most
commonly used due to its wide availability, favorable
physical structure, and consistent performance across
multiple flushes. Straw cultivation leverages the
natural ability of Pleurotus species to efficiently
decompose lignocellulosic agricultural residues with
minimal processing requirements.

## Straw Preparation

### Sourcing and Storage
Clean straw from fields without excessive mold or
moisture is ideal for mushroom cultivation. Straw
should be stored dry in a well-ventilated area
protected from rain and groundwater. Baled straw that
has been exposed to rain or stored in damp conditions
often has high microbial loads that increase
contamination risk even after proper pasteurization.
Fresh straw from the current harvest season is
preferred over aged material, as excessive aging
reduces nutritional value and increases pre-existing
microbial contamination.

### Chopping and Shredding
Straw should be chopped into 3-8 cm lengths to increase
surface area for mycelial colonization and improve
water absorption during the hydration step. A weed
whacker in a garbage can, a wood chipper, or a
dedicated straw chopper can accomplish this
efficiently. Finer shredding (2-5 cm) improves
colonization speed and yield but increases labor
requirements. For small-scale batches, manual cutting
with scissors or a machete is feasible but tedious for
volumes exceeding a few kilograms of material.

### Hydration
Straw is hydrated by soaking in clean water for 12-24
hours. The hydrated straw should reach 65-70% moisture
content for optimal mycelial growth. After soaking,
straw is drained and allowed to drip-dry until it
passes the squeeze test: a handful squeezed firmly
should release only a few drops of water. Excess
moisture leads to anaerobic conditions within the
substrate and promotes bacterial contamination during
the colonization phase.

## Pasteurization Methods

### Hot Water Pasteurization (Lime Method)
The most common home cultivation method involves
soaking chopped straw in hot water (65-80 degrees C)
for 60-90 minutes. A 55-gallon drum with a propane
burner is the standard setup for this approach. Adding
hydrated lime (calcium hydroxide) at 1-2% by dry weight
raises the pH to 10-12, creating alkaline conditions
that suppress most competitors while being well
tolerated by Pleurotus mycelium. After pasteurization,
the straw is drained thoroughly and cooled to below 30
degrees C before inoculation.

### Steam Pasteurization
Steam pasteurization in a dedicated chamber or
converted barrel reaches the entire straw mass more
uniformly than hot water immersion. Straw is loaded
into a mesh basket or wire cage and steam is introduced
for 1-2 hours at 80-90 degrees C. This method uses less
water than immersion pasteurization and produces more
consistent results across batches. Commercial
operations use purpose-built steam pasteurization rooms
that can process hundreds of kilograms of substrate per
batch with minimal labor input.

### Cold Water Lime Pasteurization
A low-energy alternative involves soaking straw in cold
water with hydrated lime (pH 12+) for 12-24 hours. This
method requires no heating equipment and is widely used
in tropical regions where energy costs are prohibitive.
Results are somewhat less consistent than heat
pasteurization, but the method is effective for oyster
mushrooms which tolerate high pH conditions well
compared to other cultivated species.

## Inoculation and Bagging

After cooling to below 30 degrees C, pasteurized straw
is mixed with grain spawn at a 10-15% rate by weight.
Thorough mixing ensures even distribution of mycelium
throughout the substrate mass. The inoculated straw is
packed firmly (but not so tightly as to exclude air)
into polypropylene bags or polyethylene tubing, then
sealed. Bags typically hold 2-5 kg of substrate. Filter
patches or micropore tape provide necessary gas
exchange for respiration during colonization.

## Colonization and Fruiting

Bags are incubated at 22-26 degrees C in darkness or
low light conditions for 14-21 days. Once fully
colonized (solid white mycelium visible throughout),
bags are moved to the fruiting area. Holes 2-4 cm in
diameter are cut in the bags at intervals, and
mushrooms form at these openings where mycelium is
exposed to fresh air and indirect light. Biological
efficiency typically reaches 50-100% over 2-3 flushes,
with the first flush producing 60-70% of total yield.
