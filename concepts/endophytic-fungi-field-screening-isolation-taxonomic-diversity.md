# Endophytic Fungi — Field Screening, Isolation Procedures, and Taxonomic Diversity

## Overview

Endophytic fungi live asymptomatically within plant tissues and represent a remarkably diverse group spanning multiple ascomycete orders. Their detection requires systematic field screening, careful isolation protocols, and detailed taxonomic knowledge. This concept page covers the practical methodologies for field screening and laboratory isolation of grass and woody perennial endophytes, followed by a comprehensive survey of their taxonomic diversity and ecological distribution.

## Screening Field Populations

### Specimen Collection

Field screening for endophytic fungi typically follows an initial herbarium assessment to identify potentially infected plant species. Once target taxa are identified, living plants are obtained directly from collection sites. Collection can be random across a population, or alternatively, potentially infected individuals may be identified in situ using a field microscope for preliminary examination (Clark et al. 1983).

Collected plant material should be transported on ice to preserve tissue integrity, or alternatively frozen and thawed later for processing (Clark et al. 1983). Suitable living tissues include culms, rhizomes, and leaf sheaths — the primary organs where clavicipitaceous endophytes are known to colonize.

### Staining and Microscopic Examination

Living plant tissues resist uptake of nonacidified aniline blue, making acidified aniline blue the more effective stain for detecting endophytic mycelium in tissue sections (Bacon and White 1994). Rose bengal stain is also a viable alternative (Saha et al. 1988).

**Acidified aniline blue preparation (Bacon and White 1994):**
1. Dissolve 0.1 g aniline blue in 100 mL sterile distilled water, mix thoroughly.
2. Add 50 mL lactic acid (85%), mix again.
3. The prepared stain is stable at room temperature for months.

**Culm and rhizome examination:** Split tissues longitudinally and scrape moist inner tissue directly into a drop of acidified aniline blue on a microscope slide. Macerate gently and apply brief heat to improve staining. Blot excess stain, remoisten with a drop of sterile distilled water, and examine at 40× magnification.

**Non-flowering plants:** For specimens lacking inflorescences, use leaf sheaths near the crown that show minimal pigmentation. Cut the upper epidermis laterally and peel it back to expose approximately 5 mm of mesophyll tissue. Apply stain to the exposed area and examine under the microscope.

## Isolation Procedures

### Isolation from Leaf and Stem Tissues

After positive field screening, laboratory isolation is performed to confirm clavicipitaceous identity and obtain pure cultures:

1. Cut young culm or leaf sheath tissues into 3–5 mm segments.
2. Agitate segments in 50% bleach solution for 15 minutes.
3. At 2–3 minute intervals, remove 2–3 pieces and rinse them vigorously in sterile distilled water.
4. Press surface-sterilized tissue pieces into potato dextrose agar (PDA).
5. Seal plates with Parafilm and incubate at room temperature for 3–4 weeks.

Rapidly growing fungi appearing during the first 2 weeks should be discarded as contaminants or saprobes. Endophyte colonies — typically white to off-white — become visible after 2–4 weeks.

### Seed Isolation Protocol

Endophytes transmitted vertically through seeds require a modified isolation approach:

1. **Deglume seeds** by rubbing inflorescences between hands and collecting freed seeds.
2. Place 30–40 seeds in a 250 mL beaker and cover with 50% bleach solution; agitate for 15–20 minutes.
3. Decant the bleach and add 100 mL sterile distilled water; agitate for 5 minutes.
4. Remove seeds with sterile forceps and press individually into PDA.
5. Prepare approximately 20 plates with 3 seeds per plate; seal with Parafilm.

As with tissue isolation, discard rapidly growing fungi in the first 2 weeks. Endophyte colonies typically emerge after 2–4 weeks of incubation at room temperature.

## Taxonomic Status, Diversity, and Distribution

### Endophytic Balansieae (Clavicipitaceae)

The tribe Balansieae within the Clavicipitaceae contains two endophyte-bearing genera: *Epichloë* and *Balansia* (Diehl 1950; White 1993, 1994a). These represent the most extensively studied group of grass endophytes due to their significant ecological and agronomic impacts.

#### Balansia

Several *Balansia* species are endophytic. Their stromata, which bear conidia and/or perithecia, develop on different host organs depending on species:

- **Host inflorescences:** *B. claviceps*, *B. obtecta*
- **Culms at nodes:** *B. aristidae*, *B. nigricans*, *B. strangulans*, *B. gaduae*
- **Leaves:** *B. epichloë*, *B. henningsiana*

*Balansia* stromata are white, purple, or brown in color. Ascomata are black, stipitate to flattened in morphology. Conidia are filamentous, and anamorphs belong to the genus *Ephelis*. Asci are cylindrical with thick refractive tips, and filamentous multiseptate ascospores disarticulate to form 1-septate cylindrical units. Notably, endophytic mycelium occurs in leaf and culm tissue but does not enter ovaries and seeds (White and Owens 1992).

#### Epichloë

*Epichloë* species produce white conidial stromata with perithecia on the host inflorescence meristem. A characteristic feature is that stromata surround part of a host leaf emerging from the stroma apex — a morphology consistent throughout the genus (Leuchtmann et al. 1994; White 1994b). During perithecia development, stromata turn yellow to orange. Asci are cylindrical with a thick refractive tip, and ascospores are filamentous and hyaline. Mycelia are endophytic in leaves, culms, rhizomes, and seeds of many grasses.

#### Neotyphodium

*Neotyphodium* (formerly *Acremonium* sect. *Albo-lanosa*; reclassified by Glenn et al. 1996) maintains a consistently close phylogenetic relationship to *Epichloë* (White 1987; Schardl et al. 1991; Moon et al. 2000). Molecular evidence indicates that *Neotyphodium* evolved from *Epichloë* ancestors through loss of the *Epichloë* (sexual) stage, combined with interspecific hybridization events (Schardl and Leuchtmann 1999; Moon et al. 2000; Schardl and Wilkinson 2000).

These endophytes are common in cool-season grasses. Colonies are white with a cottony or feltlike texture (White and Morgan-Jones 1987). Conidiogenous cells project laterally from hyphae, producing reniform to subulate conidia. Under a dissecting microscope, the conidium lies crosswise at the cell apex, forming a characteristic T-shape. Well-documented species include *N. coenophialum* (in tall fescue) and *E. uncinatum* (in *Festuca pratensis*). *N. typhinum* encompasses the conidial state of several *Epichloë* species (White 1992).

### Nonclavicipitaceous Seed-Transmitted Grass Endophytes

Beyond the Clavicipitaceae, several nonclavicipitaceous fungi are transmitted through grass seeds:

- ***Pseudocercosporella trichachnicola*** in *Trichachne insularis* (a warm-season grass; White et al. 1990): Hyphae are intercellular, longitudinally oriented, and unbranched within leaf sheaths, culms, and seeds. No external (reproductive) stage is known, and its ecological impact remains unclear. *T. insularis* has been reported as toxic (White and Halisky 1992).

- **Gliocladium-like and Phialophora-like "P-endophytes"** (An et al. 1993; Siegel et al. 1995): Isolated from stems and seeds of festucoid grasses (Latch et al. 1984). These remain sterile on PDA, resembling clavicipitaceous endophytes, but produce a bright-yellow pigment on starch-milk agar that distinguishes them (Bacon and White 1994). Sporulation is induced by refrigeration at 4–5°C for 6–10 weeks. Conidiogenous cells are clavate with a single apical locus, borne singly or in clusters of 2–3 on short lateral hyaline conidiophores. Conidia are ovate to ellipsoidal and hyaline.

### Nonsystemic Grass Endophytes

Relatively few graminaceous hosts have been systematically surveyed for nonsystemic endophytes. The dominant nonsystemic colonists tend to be familiar epiphytes (*Alternaria alternata*, *Cladosporium* species, *Epicoccum purpurascens*) or typical grass pathogens:

- **Barley (New Zealand):** *Didymella phleina*, *Alternaria* species, *Stemphyllium botryosum* (Riesen and Close 1987).
- **Winter wheat (Switzerland):** *Phaeosphaeria* (*Stagonospora*) *nodorum* was the most common of 196 endophytic colonists recovered (Riesen and Sieber 1985).
- **Rice and maize:** *A. alternata*, *Arthrinium*, *C. tenuissimum*, and *E. purpurascens* are dominant (Fisher and Petrini 1992; Fisher et al. 1992).

Latent pathogens frequently encountered include *Phoma sorghina*, *Fusarium equiseti*, *F. oxysporum*, *F. graminearum*, and *Ustilago* species. Leslie et al. (1990) documented universal infection of maize and near-universal infection of sorghum by *Fusarium* section *Liseola* (primarily *F. moniliforme*). Rice and maize are frequently colonized concurrently by several *Fusarium* species. Importantly, the fungal species recovered from live tissue differ from those found in plant debris or soil, suggesting selective host-endophyte interactions. *F. moniliforme* produces intercellular hyphae distributed throughout the host plant (Bacon and Hinton 1996). *Juncus* species in Oregon exhibit unique internal colonization patterns by *A. alternata*, *C. cladosporioides*, *Drechslera*, and *S. innumerosa* (Cabral et al. 1993).

### Endophytes of Woody Perennials

The endophytic habit has evolved independently numerous times and is represented across various Ascomycete orders. A large proportion of woody perennial endophytes belong to the inoperculate Discomycetes.

- **Rhytismatales** (Livsey and Minter 1994) and **Phacidiaceae/Hemiphacidiaceae** (Leotiales): An endophytic habit similar to that of *Rhabdocline parkeri* may be widespread within these groups.
- ***Fabrella tsugae***: Fruits in late winter on the oldest *Tsuga* needles, coincident with needle senescence.
- ***Lophodermium***: Conspicuous on senescent or fallen conifer needles and recognizable in culture. Among the most common endophytic isolates from *Abies*, *Picea*, and *Pinus*.
- ***Rhytisma*** (e.g., *R. punctata* on *Acer*) and ***Coccomyces***: Occupy similar niches in broad-leaved trees and shrubs, fruiting on leaves at senescence. Ascospore release coincides with bud opening — a strategy characteristic of "latent pathogens."
- **Anamorphs of *Lophodermium* and *Coccomyces***: Frequently isolated from healthy leaves of *Mahonia nervosa* (Petrini et al. 1982), confirming the endophytic phase precedes pathogenic fruiting on senescent tissue.

## Summary

Endophytic fungal research integrates field-based screening, rigorous laboratory isolation, and careful taxonomic placement. Acidified aniline blue staining of culm, rhizome, or leaf sheath tissues remains the standard field detection method, followed by surface-sterilization and culture on potato dextrose agar. The clavicipitaceous endophytes — *Balansia*, *Epichloë*, and *Neotyphodium* — are the best-characterized systemic grass symbionts, with well-documented ecological roles in alkaloid-mediated herbivore deterrence and stress tolerance. Nonclavicipitaceous seed-transmitted endophytes (e.g., *Pseudocercosporella* and P-endophytes) and nonsystemic colonists (dominated by *Alternaria*, *Cladosporium*, *Fusarium*, and *Phaeosphaeria*) broaden the diversity picture. In woody perennials, inoperculate Discomycetes — particularly *Lophodermium*, *Rhytisma*, and *Coccomyces* — represent a widespread endophytic-to-latent-pathogen continuum, fruiting only upon host tissue senescence. The repeated independent evolution of the endophytic habit across Ascomycete orders underscores its ecological significance and warrants continued investigation across under-sampled host lineages and geographic regions.
