# Polypore Fungi of Eastern North American Forests

Polypores, also known as bracket fungi or shelf fungi, are a
morphologically diverse group of basidiomycete fungi that produce their
spores on the inner surfaces of tubes arranged as a pore surface on the
underside of their fruiting bodies. They are among the most important
decomposers in forest ecosystems, playing essential roles in carbon
cycling, nutrient turnover, and forest stand dynamics.

## Biology and Ecology

Polypores are predominantly wood-decay fungi, breaking down the
structural polymers of woody tissues: cellulose, hemicellulose, and
lignin. They are classified by their decay strategy into brown rot
fungi, which primarily degrade cellulose and hemicellulose while leaving
lignin largely intact (producing a crumbly, brown decayed wood), and
white rot fungi, which can degrade all three components including the
recalcitrant lignin polymer (producing a lighter, bleached, fibrous
decay). Some species are intermediate, exhibiting a selective delignification
pattern.

Wood-decay polypores are further categorized by their host condition
preferences. Primary decayers colonize dead wood of recently killed
trees, while secondary decayers succeed initial colonizers. Some species
are wound parasites that attack living trees through injuries, and a
few are true heart rot pathogens that colonize the living heartwood of
standing trees, eventually leading to structural failure.
