# Water Purification and Contaminants

Water quality is one of the most critical design considerations in any [[permaculture]]
system. Understanding the sources, nature, and interactions of water contaminants
is essential for designing effective purification strategies, managing household
wastewater, and protecting soil and plant health. This page covers greywater
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems|systems]], common water pollutants, their complex interactions, and treatment
approaches as described in *Permaculture: A Designers Manual*.

## Greywater Systems

Greywater — wastewater from handbasins, showers, baths, and laundry — represents
a significant reusable resource. Unlike blackwater (toilet waste), greywater
contains relatively low pathogen loads and can be directed to gardens, orchards,
or treatment wetlands when properly managed.

### Composition of Greywater

Greywater composition varies with household habits and cleaning products used:

- **Organic matter**: Skin cells, hair, food residues, soap scum, and oils
- **Surfactants**: From soaps, shampoos, and detergents — can affect soil
  structure and root function
- **Nutrients**: Nitrogen and phosphorus from body wastes and cleaning products
- **Microorganisms**: Low levels of bacteria from skin contact; generally
  non-pathogenic but requiring treatment before reuse on food crops
- **Suspended solids**: Fine particles that clog irrigation if not filtered
- **Salts**: Sodium and ions from water softeners and detergent additives,
  accumulating in soils with repeated application

### Diversion and Reuse Strategies

- **Handbasin-to-toilet diversion**: Redirecting handbasin outflow directly to
  toilet cisterns uses each litre twice — once for washing, once for flushing.
- **Low-flush toilets**: Reducing flush volume from 9–12 litres to 4–6 litres
  (or less with dual-flush) cuts household water demand dramatically.
- **Dry toilets ([[composting-toilets]])**: Eliminate water for sewage transport
  entirely. Aerobic decomposition with carbon-rich bulking materials (sawdust,
  straw) produces stable humus for non-food gardens after adequate curing.

## Chemicals in Detergents and Soaps

The chemicals in greywater directly affect soil biology and plant health. Product
selection is a design decision that determines whether greywater can be safely
integrated into biological systems.

### Harmful Substances to Avoid

- **Phosphates**: Act as excessive fertilisers in soil, cause algal blooms in
  waterways, and disrupt nutrient balance, favouring weeds over desired [[biological-water-purification-with-plants|plants]].
- **Boron (borates)**: Toxic to many [[permaculture-plants-aquatic-systems-water-plants|plants]] at low concentrations; accumulates
  in soils causing leaf tip burn, stunted growth, and eventual plant death.
- **Chlorine bleaches and disinfectants**: Sterilise soil biology on contact,
  killing beneficial bacteria, fungi, and soil invertebrates.
- **Synthetic surfactants**: Persist in the environment, damage soil structure
  and aquatic organisms, and interfere with root function.
- **Optical brighteners and artificial fragrances**: Non-biodegradable compounds
  that accumulate in soils and groundwater over time.

### Preferable Alternatives

- Pure soap (fats + alkali, no synthetic additives)
- Soap nuts or other plant-based saponins
- Biodegradable, phosphate-free and boron-free detergents
- Baking soda and vinegar for household cleaning

## Problem Contaminants in Drinking Water

### Turbidity

Suspended particles — clay, silt, organic matter, and microorganisms — cloud water
and interfere with disinfection by shielding pathogens from UV light and chemical
treatment. High turbidity also indicates erosion and catchment degradation.

### Bacterial and Organic Pollution

Pathogenic bacteria (*E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*), viruses, and
parasites enter water from human and animal waste. Organic pollution from
decomposing matter reduces dissolved oxygen, creating anoxic conditions that
kill aquatic life and further degrade water quality.

### Metallic Pollutants

Heavy metals — lead, mercury, cadmium, chromium, arsenic — enter water through
industrial discharge, mining, agricultural chemicals, and pipe corrosion. These
cumulative toxins bioaccumulate in food chains, causing neurological damage,
organ failure, and cancer.

### Biocides

Pesticides, herbicides, and fungicides wash into waterways and persist in the
environment. Even at sub-lethal concentrations they disrupt endocrine systems
in wildlife and humans.

### Excessive Fertiliser

Nitrogen and phosphorus from agricultural runoff cause eutrophication — nutrient
over-enrichment triggering explosive algal growth. Decomposing algae strip oxygen
from water, creating dead zones. Nitrates in drinking water cause
methemoglobinemia (blue baby syndrome) in infants.

### Acids

Acid rain (sulphuric and nitric acids from industrial emissions) lowers water
pH, mobilises toxic metals from geological deposits, damages aquatic ecosystems,
and reduces agricultural productivity.

## Interactions Between Contaminants

Contaminants rarely act in isolation. Their interactions amplify threats in
non-obvious ways:

- **Acid rain dissolving metals**: Acidic water passing through soils and rock
  dramatically increases dissolution of aluminium, lead, and copper into
  groundwater. A safe water source at neutral pH becomes dangerously contaminated
  when acidified — and acidification may originate far from the affected source.
- **Mercury conversion**: Anaerobic bacteria convert inorganic mercury from
  industrial emissions into methylmercury — a far more toxic organic form that
  bioaccumulates through aquatic food chains. Fish consumption causes severe
  neurological damage, especially to developing foetuses and children. Low levels
  of inorganic mercury become serious threats under these microbial conditions.
- **Asbestos pipes**: Older asbestos-cement pipes release fibres as they age,
  especially in acidic conditions that accelerate degradation. While inhalation
  is the primary risk pathway, ingestion concerns have driven phased replacement
  of these pipes in many countries.
- **Aluminium in water**: Aluminium enters supplies from natural geological
  sources (accelerated by acidification dissolving aluminium-bearing minerals)
  and from aluminium sulphate (alum) used in municipal water treatment. Elevated
  aluminium has been linked to neurological conditions, creating a tension:
  aluminium coagulants effectively remove turbidity but may introduce their own
  health concern if not carefully dosed.

## Water Treatment: Ferric and Aluminium Sulphate

Coagulation with ferric sulphate or aluminium sulphate is standard treatment for
removing turbidity and suspended solids. These chemicals cause fine particles to
flocculate into larger masses that settle out or can be filtered.

- **Ferric sulphate** is preferred in permaculture-compatible systems because it
  does not introduce aluminium. It is effective across a wider pH range (4.5–11)
  and produces denser, faster-settling floc. The iron-rich sludge can sometimes
  be applied to iron-deficient soils, turning waste into a resource.
- **Aluminium sulphate** is widely used municipally but requires careful dose
  management to avoid residual aluminium. It works best in a narrow pH window
