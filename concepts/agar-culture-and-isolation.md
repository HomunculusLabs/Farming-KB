## What Is Agar?

Agar is a gelatinous substance derived from red algae. It serves as a solid
growth medium when combined with nutrients and water. Agar melts at 185°F
(85°C) and solidifies at approximately 100-110°F (38-43°C), allowing it to
be sterilized as a liquid and poured into plates where it sets into a firm
surface for mycelium growth.

## Agar Recipes

 [[malt-extract-agar]]Malt Extract Agar (MEA)

The standard, most widely used recipe:
- 10g light malt extract (DME)
- 10g agar agar powder
- 500ml water

Provides balanced nutrition suitable for most mushroom species. Simple,
reliable, and inexpensive.

### Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA)

A classic mycological medium:
- 200g sliced potatoes (boiled in 500ml water for 30 minutes, broth saved)
- 10g dextrose (or substitute with 10g light malt extract)
- 10g agar agar powder
- Water to 500ml total

Rich in starches and sugars, promoting vigorous mycelial growth. Slightly
more labor-intensive than MEA.

### Dog Food Agar (DFA)

20g dog food kibble, 10g agar, 500ml water. Boil kibble 20 minutes, strain,
add agar. Produces very fast, rhizomorphic growth. Good for reviving old
cultures.

### Yeast Malt Agar (YMA)

3g yeast extract, 10g malt extract, 15g agar, 500ml water. Enhanced nutrition
for demanding species like *Ganoderma*.

## Preparation and Sterilization

1. Mix dry ingredients with water in a flask or bottle.
2. Heat with stirring until agar fully dissolves (microwave or stovetop).
3. Pour into media bottles or jars (no more than 2/3 full).
4. Sterilize at 15 psi for 20-30 minutes in a pressure cooker.
5. Cool to ~120°F (49°C), then pour ~20-25ml per 90mm plate in a still air
   box or laminar flow hood.
6. Allow to solidify with lids slightly ajar to release condensation.
7. Seal and store at room temperature; use within 2-4 weeks or refrigerate
   (sealed in bags) for up to 2 months. Dehydrated plates should be discarded.

[[inoculation-methods]]oculation Methods

### Spore Swab or Print

Spores from a print or swab are streaked across the agar surface using a
sterile loop or inoculation needle. Germination typically occurs within 3-10
days depending on species and spore viability. Each spore produces a
genetically unique mycelium, resulting in a multispore culture with genetic
variability.

### Tissue Culture

A small piece of inner tissue from a fresh fruiting body is placed on the agar
surface, capturing the exact parent genetics (clonal propagation). Sterilize
the outer surface, tear (not cut) the mushroom open, transfer a rice-grain
piece of inner tissue with flame-sterilized tweezers, seal, and incubate.

### Liquid Culture Transfer

A small amount of liquid culture is dropped onto the agar surface. This is
primarily used to test liquid culture for contamination or to isolate sectors
from a known culture. Liquid culture should not be the sole method of storage
due to risk of senescence and contamination over time.

## Isolation Techniques

### Sectoring

When mycelium grows on agar, it often displays sectors — areas of visibly
different morphology. Fast-growing, ropey (rhizomorphic) sectors are generally
more vigorous fruiters than slow, cottony (tomentose) ones. Isolate a sector
by cutting a wedge from the leading edge and transferring to a fresh plate.
Repeat 2-4 times for a clean, isolated strain.

 [[contamination-identification]]on Identification and Elimination

Common agar contaminants and their appearance:

- **Bacteria**: Milky, glossy, iridescent colonies on the agar surface.
  Often smell sour or rotten. Mycelium may grow around but not through
  bacterial colonies.
- **Mold** (Aspergillus, Penicillium, Trichoderma): Fast-growing, colored
  colonies (green, blue-green, black). Produce spores that can spread rapidly.
  Affected plates should be discarded in a sealed bag.
- **Yeast**: Creamy, raised, circular colonies. Often white or pinkish.
  Slower growing than molds but persistent.

## Storage and Preservation

### Slant Cultures

Agar is poured into small tubes at an angle, creating a slanted surface.
Inoculated slants are sealed and stored at refrigeration temperature
(35-39°F / 2-4°C), where mycelium growth slows to near-zero. Slants preserve
viable cultures for 1-3 years. Master slants serve as the "seed bank" and are
never opened for routine use; they inoculate working slants for production,
preventing loss of the original genetics.

## Agar-to-Grain Transfer

Once a clean isolate is established, it is expanded to grain spawn. A full agar
plate or wedge is transferred to a sterilized grain jar in a still air box or
laminar flow hood. The mycelium colonizes the grain over 7-21 days depending
on species and grain type.

## Equipment

- **Petri dishes**: 90mm disposable plastic or reusable glass. Pre-sterilized
  disposable plates eliminate a preparation step.
- **Still air box (SAB)**: A clear plastic container with two arm holes.
