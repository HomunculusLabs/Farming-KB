# Green Manures and Cover Crops

Green manures are cover crops grown specifically to improve soil
fertility. In conventional organic gardening, these crops — typically
legumes such as vetch, clover, alfalfa, beans, or peas — are grown
until the plants reach 10% to 50% flower, then dug directly into the
soil. In the GROW BIOINTENSIVE method, green manure crops serve a
different but equally important role, with important modifications
to the traditional approach.

## Traditional Green Manuring

The traditional practice involves growing nitrogen-rich legumes and
incorporating them into the soil at the flowering stage. At this
point, a maximum of nitrogen has been fixed in the root nodules. The
key indicator of successful nitrogen fixation is visible when you
cut a nodule in half with a fingernail: if the inside is pink,
the nodules have fixed nitrogen effectively.

### Benefits of the Traditional Approach

- Brings nitrogen into the soil without purchasing fertilizer
- Roots loosen the soil and improve structure
- Eventually decompose into [[jeavons-humus-and-soil-microbiology]]
  beneath the earth
- Can bring unworked soil into better condition

### Special Case: Fava Beans for Tomatoes

Fava beans are exceptionally good for green manuring when tomatoes
will follow. Their decomposed bodies help eradicate tomato wilt
organisms from the soil. However, caution is needed: some people of
Mediterranean descent are fatally allergic to fava beans, and people
on certain medications may experience the same reaction.

## The Biointensive Modification

While traditional green manuring has value, Jeavons recommends using
cover crops as [[jeavons-composting-methods]] rather
than digging them directly into the soil. The roots still provide
their beneficial soil effects even when the above-ground biomass is
removed for composting.

### Why Composting Is Preferred Over Digging In

1. **Rapid decomposition with humus depletion**: Due to their high
   nitrogen content, green manures decompose rapidly when dug in and
   can actually deplete some of the soil's existing humus
2. **Lost production time**: The land is not producing food crops
   during the cover crop growth period plus the 1-month decomposition
   period
3. **Lower carbon yield**: Green manures generally produce only about
   one-quarter the carbon that dedicated carbonaceous compost crops do
4. **Carbon is the limiting element**: Carbon in the form of humus is
   the most limiting and essential element in maintaining sustainable
   [[jeavons-soil-fertility]] — it serves as food for
   microbial life and holds minerals so they cannot easily leach out

## The Compost Crop Advantage

In the GROW BIOINTENSIVE system, using cover crops as compost
materials offers several advantages:

- The growing process puts nitrogen into the soil through the roots
- Above-ground material goes into the compost pile, building long-term
  humus reserves
- Food crops can be grown simultaneously in other beds
- The composting process stabilizes nutrients and creates a more
  balanced soil amendment

## Sustainable Soil Fertility Crops

Jeavons emphasizes the importance of growing what he calls
"carbon-and-calorie crops" — [[jeavons-tree-cane-crops]] that feed both the soil and the farmer. Examples include
corn, millet, wheat, oats, barley, cereal rye, and amaranth. These
crops produce large amounts of carbonaceous material for the compost
pile while also providing nutritious food.

## Legumes in the Mini-Farm System

Legumes serve dual purposes in the GROW BIOINTENSIVE system:

- **Heavy givers in crop rotation**: After harvesting
  [[jeavons-crop-rotation-detail]], legumes restore
  nitrogen to the soil through their symbiotic relationship with
  rhizobium bacteria
- **Compost crop material**: When harvested at the right stage, the
  above-ground biomass contributes valuable nitrogen-rich material
  to the compost pile
- **Soil improvers**: Their root systems break up compacted soil and
  the roots themselves become humus

### Common Biointensive Legumes

- **Fava beans** — exceptional for nitrogen fixation and tomato wilt
  suppression; may yield the greatest amount of organic matter
- **Alfalfa** — deep-rooted perennial that brings up subsoil
  nutrients; a fun nitrogen-fixing legume to raise
- **Clover** (medium red) — beautiful red flowers; can be grown
  between trees to increase soil friability, nitrogen, and organic
  matter content
- **Vetch** — excellent winter cover crop; provides good nitrogen
  contribution
- **Peas and beans** — edible nitrogen fixers that produce food while
  improving soil

## Integration with Backyard Composting

The key advantage of the small-scale GROW BIOINTENSIVE method is that
backyard composting is easily feasible. When you use compost crops
without digging in the crop residues, the growing process puts
nitrogen into the soil and makes it possible to grow heavy nitrogen
feeders like corn and tomatoes. The plant residues become valuable
compost material, closing the nutrient loop that makes the entire
system sustainable.

## Carbon Efficiency Comparison

The distinction between green manures and dedicated carbon crops is
critical. Green manures produce roughly 1/4 the carbon of carbonaceous
compost crops per unit area. Since carbon — as humus — is the most
limiting factor in sustainable fertility, dedicating space to
high-carbon compost crops is more efficient than relying solely on
green manures. The optimal approach combines legumes for nitrogen
fixation with carbon crops for humus building, cycling both through
the [[jeavons-composting-methods]] before returning them
to the soil.
