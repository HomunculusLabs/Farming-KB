# Permaculture Seed Saving Networks

Seed saving networks are community-based systems for preserving, sharing, and breeding open-pollinated plant varieties. In permaculture, seed sovereignty — the right and ability to save, exchange, and breed seeds — is fundamental to self-reliance, regional adaptation, and the preservation of genetic diversity against corporate monoculture.

## Why Seed Saving Matters

- **Genetic diversity** — preserving a wide gene pool protects against crop failure, disease, and climate variability
- **Regional adaptation** — seeds saved locally year after year become better adapted to local climate, soil, and pest pressures
- **Self-reliance** — reduces dependence on commercial seed companies, many of which sell F1 hybrids that do not breed true
- **Cost reduction** — seeds are free once the initial stock is acquired
- **Food sovereignty** — communities control their own food supply chain from seed to plate
- **Cultural preservation** — heirloom varieties carry cultural history, flavor, and nutritional qualities

## Open-Pollinated vs Hybrid Seeds

### Open-Pollinated (OP) Varieties
- Breed true from saved seed — offspring resemble parents
- Genetic diversity within the variety provides adaptability
- Can be selected and improved by the grower over generations
- Essential for seed saving networks

### F1 Hybrids
- First generation cross of two inbred parent lines
- Often exhibit hybrid vigor (uniform, high-yielding)
- Do NOT breed true — saved seed produces unpredictable offspring
- Cannot be reliably saved or shared
- Intellectual property restrictions may prohibit saving

### Genetically Modified (GM) Seeds
- Engineered for specific traits (herbicide resistance, Bt toxin production)
- Patented — illegal to save in most jurisdictions
- Pose contamination risk to OP varieties through cross-pollination
- Antithetical to seed sovereignty

## Seed Saving Basics

### Isolation Distances
To maintain varietal purity, prevent cross-pollination between different varieties of the same species:
- **Self-pollinating** (tomatoes, lettuce, peas, beans): 10-20 feet minimum
- **Insect-pollinated** (squash, cucumbers, brassicas, carrots): 0.25-1 mile minimum
- **Wind-pollinated** (corn, spinach, beets): 0.5-2 miles minimum

Where isolation distance is impractical, use hand-pollination, caging, or staggered flowering times.

### Seed Selection
Select seeds from the best plants — not just the best fruit. Consider:
- Disease resistance
- Flavor and texture
- Yield
- Earliness or lateness (for extending the season)
- Growth habit (bush vs. vining, compact vs. sprawling)
- Local adaptation traits (drought tolerance, cold hardiness, pest resistance)

Save from multiple plants (minimum 20-50 for outcrossing species) to maintain genetic diversity.

### Harvesting and Processing
- Allow seeds to fully mature on the plant
- Harvest on dry days
- Dry seeds to 10-15% moisture content before storage
- [[coleman-quick-hoops-low-cost-season-extension-structures-design]] by wet fermentation (tomatoes, cucumbers), dry threshing (beans, grains), or winnowing (brassicas, lettuce)
- Store in cool, dark, dry conditions — sealed jars in the refrigerator or freezer for longest viability

### Viability
- Most vegetable seeds remain viable 2-5 years under proper storage
- Alliums and parsnips lose viability quickly (1-2 years)
- Some seeds (lettuce, tomatoes) can last 5-10+ years
- Test germination rate before planting by sprouting 10 seeds on moist paper towel

## Seed Libraries

Seed libraries are community collections where members can borrow seeds, grow plants, and return seeds from their harvest at the end of the season.

### How They Work
1. Library stocks open-pollinated seeds donated by members or purchased from seed companies
2. Members "check out" seeds for planting
3. Members grow plants and save seeds from the healthiest individuals
4. Members return a portion of saved seeds to the library
5. The library maintains the collection and provides education on seed saving techniques

### Hosting Models
- **Public libraries** — many public libraries host seed collections as a community service
- **Community centers** — attached to existing community gathering spaces
- **Farms and gardens** — hosted at community gardens, farms, or [[permaculture-community-resilience]] hubs
- **Online platforms** — digital catalogs connecting savers across regions

### Challenges
- Maintaining varietal purity as seeds are grown by many different people
- Ensuring returned seeds are properly identified, dried, and stored
- Legal issues in some jurisdictions (seed laws originally written for commercial seed sales)
- Volunteer burnout — seed libraries depend on ongoing community engagement

## Community Seed Exchanges

Seed swaps are events where gardeners gather to exchange seeds, knowledge, and stories. They are often seasonal (early spring) and may include:

- Seed sharing tables organized by crop type
- Seed saving workshops and demonstrations
- Variety tastings of saved produce
- Guest speakers on topics like regional adaptation, heirloom preservation, and breeding

## Regional Adaptation

One of the most powerful outcomes of seed saving networks is regional adaptation. Over 5-10 generations of selection, seeds become:
- Better adapted to local temperature extremes
- More resistant to local pest and disease pressures
- Synchronized with local growing seasons (day length, rainfall patterns)
- More productive under local soil conditions

This is a form of participatory plant breeding — the community collectively selects for traits that commercial breeding programs may not prioritize.

## Legal Considerations

Seed laws vary by jurisdiction. Some regions restrict the sale of untested seeds but allow non-commercial sharing. Organizations like the Seed Savers Exchange in the US have advocated for exemptions for community seed sharing. Before starting a formal seed library or exchange, research local seed regulations.

## Building a Network

Steps to start a seed saving network:
1. Gather interested gardeners and farmers
2. Identify which crops and varieties are priorities for your region
3. Establish a seed library or exchange mechanism
4. Provide education on seed saving techniques
5. Create a catalog or database of available varieties
6. Track growing results and share feedback
7. Celebrate successes — seed fairs, tastings, garden tours
8. Connect with other networks for variety exchange and knowledge sharing
