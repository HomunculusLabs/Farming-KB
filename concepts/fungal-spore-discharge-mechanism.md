## The Basidium and Its Spores

The spore-producing surface of a mushroom gill is called the **hymenium**.
Under a microscope, this surface is covered with specialized cells called
**basidia** — four-pronged crowns shaped like miniature cow udders, each
bearing a single basidiospore on each spike (or "teat"). The spores are
borne aloft on slender stalks called **sterigmata**.

When a basidiospore matures, it is ready for discharge. The process is
passive until the final moment — the fungus relies entirely on physical
forces rather than muscular or chemical propulsion.

## Buller's Drop Formation

The [[basidiospore-discharge-mechanism]] proceeds through a precise sequence:

### 1. Secretion and Condensation

Sugars and other molecules seep from the interior of the spore and
accumulate on its surface. The space between the mushroom gills is
saturated with water vapor evaporating from the mushroom's tissues. This
humid atmosphere causes water to condense onto the spore surface at the
sites where sugars have accumulated.

### 2. Two-Site Condensation

At the base of each spore is a small hump called the **hilar appendix**.
Water that collects here forms a spherical droplet that is held away from
the rest of the spore. This creates two separate condensation sites:
- A thin film covering most of the spore surface.
- A growing spherical droplet (Buller's drop) on the hilar appendix.

### 3. Center of Mass Shift

As Buller's drop grows, it pulls the spore's center of mass toward its
base. This is analogous to how your center of mass shifts toward a heavy
bucket if someone hands you one. The spore is now unbalanced, with most
of its weight concentrated at the base near the drop.

### 4. Drop Coalescence

Water continues to accumulate until the two condensation regions — the
drop on the hilar appendix and the film on the spore surface — become
large enough to make contact. When this happens, [[bloomfield-buller-drop-surface-tension-spore-catapult-basidiospore-discharge]] causes
the droplet to snap from the hump and merge with the fluid on the rest
of the spore surface. This is similar to two raindrops running down a
windowpane that suddenly snap together when they touch.

### 5. Momentum Transfer

The sudden merger of the two fluid masses shifts the center of mass
violently from the base of the spore to its center. This momentum
transfer, occurring in approximately **one millionth of a second**, propels
the spore from its sterigma. The spore is launched horizontally from the
gill surface.

### 6. Gravity Takes Over

After the millisecond horizontal journey — just far enough to clear the
neighboring gill — gravity assumes control. The spore turns abruptly and
accelerates straight down between the gills. Once below the cap, air
currents sweep the spore away. If observed in correct lighting, a dusty
plume of basidiospores is visible swirling from an active mushroom cap.
