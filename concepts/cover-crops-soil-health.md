# Cover Crops and Soil Health

Updated: 2026-04-18

Cover crops are plants grown primarily to benefit the soil rather than for
harvest. In the context of the [soil food web
approach, cover crops serve multiple critical functions: they maintain living
roots in the soil (feeding the microbial community through
[root exudates), protect soil from erosion, add
organic matter, and can be selected to shift the
[fungal-to-bacterial ratio.

## Why Cover Crops Matter

When soil is left bare between crops, the soil food web loses its primary
energy source -- plant root exudates. Without exudates, the bacteria and fungi
that depend on them die back, protozoa and nematodes lose their food source,
and nutrient cycling shuts down. Cover crops keep the food web active by
maintaining living roots in the soil year-round.

## Legume Cover Crops (Nitrogen Fixers)

Legumes form symbiotic relationships with Rhizobium bacteria that live in
root nodules and fix atmospheric nitrogen. This is microbial nitrogen fixation
-- the bacteria convert atmospheric N2 into ammonium that the plant can use.
Legume cover crops include:

- **Clover** (white, red, crimson) -- Excellent for lawns and between rows;
  indicates soil food web activity when it appears naturally
- **Vetch** (hairy vetch, common vetch) -- Vigorous nitrogen fixer, good for
  heavy soils; provides extensive root mass for fungal networks
- **Peas** (field peas) -- Quick-growing cool-season legume
- **Beans** (fava beans, field beans) -- Good cool-season nitrogen fixers
- **Alfalfa** -- Deep-rooted perennial; brings up nutrients from subsoil
- **Lupine** -- Nitrogen fixer that also helps condition acidic soils

Legume cover crops prefer [bacterially dominated soils and contribute nitrogen that subsequent crops can use.

## Grass Cover Crops

Grasses provide abundant root mass and root exudates that feed soil bacteria.
They are excellent for building soil structure and preventing erosion:

- **Winter rye** -- Hardiest of winter cover crops; extensive root system;
  allelopathic properties help suppress weeds
- **Oats** -- Quick-growing fall cover; winter-kills in cold climates leaving
  easy-to-manage residue
- **Annual ryegrass** -- Fast germination; good for erosion control
- **Barley** -- Good for nutrient scavenging; adapts to many soil types

## Brassica Cover Crops

- **Radish** (daikon, tillage radish) -- Deep taproot breaks compacted soil
  layers, creating channels for water and air movement
- **Mustard** -- Biofumigant properties; releases compounds that suppress
  soil-borne pathogens when incorporated

## Broadleaf Non-Legume Cover Crops

- **Buckwheat** -- Fast summer cover; attracts beneficial insects; mines
  phosphorus from soil; prefers bacterial dominance
- **Sunflower** -- Deep taproot; good for breaking compacted layers

## Selecting Cover Crops for Your Soil Food Web

The choice of cover crop should match the needs of the subsequent crop:

- **Before vegetables** (which prefer bacterial soils): Use grass/legume mixes
  or buckwheat to maintain bacterial dominance
- **Before perennials or trees** (which prefer fungal soils): Use longer-lived
  covers like vetch or clover that support fungal network development
- **For compacted soil**: Use deep-rooted species like radish, sunflower, or
  alfalfa
- **For low-nitrogen soil**: Include legumes in the mix
- **For weed suppression**: Use fast-growing, dense species like winter rye
  or buckwheat

## Termination

Cover crops can be terminated by:

- Cutting at soil level and leaving residue as [mulch
  (preserves no-till benefits and fungal networks)
- Crimping (for mechanical no-till systems)
- Incorporating into soil (shifts toward bacterial dominance but disrupts
  fungal networks)

The no-till approach of cutting and leaving residue on the surface is
preferred because it preserves [mycorrhizal networks and continues feeding the soil food web as the residue decomposes.
