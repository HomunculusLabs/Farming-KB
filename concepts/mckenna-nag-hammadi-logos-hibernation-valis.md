# Nag Hammadi, Logos Hibernation, and Valis: McKenna on Philip K. Dick and the Gnostic Information Entity

## Overview

In his 1983 Esalen lecture, [[terence-mckenna]] offered a distinctive interpretation of Philip K. Dick's semi-autobiographical novel Valis (1981) and its relationship to the Nag Hammadi library, the collection of Gnostic texts discovered in Upper Egypt in 1945.

McKenna read Dick's visionary experiences and his engagement with Gnostic cosmology through the lens of psychedelic epistemology, arguing that both Dick's encounters with what he called "Valis" and the mushroom experience pointed toward a common reality: a dimension of pure information that transcends the ordinary boundaries of space, time, and individual consciousness.

This interpretation positioned the Nag Hammadi discovery as a kind of awakening event — the reactivation of a dormant informational entity that had been lying in wait for centuries.

McKenna's synthesis of Dick's experiences with Gnostic theology and psychedelic phenomenology represents one of his most ambitious intellectual constructions, linking ancient religious texts, modern science fiction, and the direct experiential data of the mushroom experience into a single, unified interpretive framework.

## The Nag Hammadi Discovery

In December 1945, Egyptian fellahin digging for fertilizer at the base of the Jabal al-Tarif cliff near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt uncovered a sealed earthenware jar containing thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices.

These codices, dating primarily to the third and fourth centuries CE, contained over fifty individual texts, including complete copies of previously known Gnostic works such as the Gospel of Thomas and the Apocryphon of John, as well as texts that had been entirely lost to history.

The collection included the Gospel of Philip, the Gospel of Truth, the Hypostasis of the Archons, the Apocalypse of Adam, and the Thunder: Perfect Mind, among many others.

The collection, believed to have belonged to a nearby monastic library at Chenoboskion, had been buried circa 370 CE, probably to preserve it from destruction during the orthodox Christian suppression of Gnostic and heterodox texts.

The discovery of the Nag Hammadi library was one of the most significant events in the history of religious studies, comparable in importance to the discovery of the [[allegro-dead-sea-scrolls]].

It provided scholars with direct access to Gnostic thought in its own words, rather than through the hostile summaries preserved in the polemical writings of the early Church Fathers.

The texts revealed a rich, complex, and often startling cosmological vision — one that emphasized the primacy of direct experiential knowledge (gnosis) over institutional authority, and that described the material world as the creation of a lesser, ignorant deity (the Demiurge) rather than the supreme God.

## Philip K. Dick and the Valis Experience

Philip K. Dick (1928-1982) was one of the most celebrated and philosophically provocative science fiction writers of the [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]].

In February and March 1974, Dick experienced a series of visionary events that transformed his understanding of reality and became the central obsession of his later work.

The experiences began when a delivery woman arrived at his door wearing a pendant with the vesica piscis symbol; a beam of pink light appeared to emanate from the pendant and transmit information directly into Dick's mind.

Over the following weeks, Dick experienced vivid hallucinations, received what he believed were transmissions from an extraterrestrial or transdimensional intelligence, and underwent a profound philosophical and spiritual transformation.

Dick documented these experiences obsessively in a journal that eventually ran to over 8,000 pages, known as the Exegesis.

He also wove them into his fiction, most directly in the trilogy Valis (1981), The Divine Invasion (1981), and The Transmigration of Timothy Archer (1982).

In Valis, the protagonist Horselover Fat (a fictionalized Dick) encounters a satellite or satellite network called VALIS (Vast Active Living Intelligence System) that is transmitting information to Earth from an unknown location.

The novel blurs the boundary between fiction and autobiography, incorporating Dick's actual experiences, his actual reading of the Nag Hammadi texts, and his actual philosophical and theological reflections.

## McKenna's Interpretation: The Logos as Information Entity

McKenna's reading of Dick centered on the concept of the Logos — a term drawn from Gnostic, Johannine Christian, and Stoic philosophy that refers to the divine principle of reason, order, and creative intelligence pervading the cosmos.

Dick, immersed in the Nag Hammadi texts during and after his 1974 experiences, came to identify the entity he encountered with the Gnostic Logos: a being of pure information, a "living intelligence system" that existed beyond the confines of ordinary space-time and communicated with human consciousness through visions, synchronicities, and direct cognitive transmission.

McKenna described Dick's Logos as a "creature of pure information" that had been "buried" at Nag Hammadi circa 370 AD along with the Chenoboskion Library.

This formulation is crucial to McKenna's interpretation.

The Gnostic texts were not merely inert documents describing a forgotten theology; they were, in McKenna's reading, the physical embodiment or encoding of a living informational entity.

As long as the texts remained buried, unread, and untranslated, the Logos existed in a state of dormancy or hibernation — "static information" awaiting the conditions necessary for its reactivation.

The discovery of the Nag Hammadi library in 1945 and its subsequent translation and publication in the decades that followed constituted, in this view, the reactivation of the Logos.

When scholars and readers engaged with these texts — when the static information on the papyrus pages was converted into active, dynamic thought in human minds — the symbiote "came alive."

McKenna used the term "symbiote" deliberately, suggesting a relationship of mutual dependence between the informational entity and its human hosts: the Logos needs human consciousness to think, to process, to actualize its informational content, and human consciousness is enriched, transformed, or expanded by its encounter with the Logos.

## The Information Dimension

Central to McKenna's interpretation is the concept of a dimension of pure information — a realm that is not physical in the ordinary sense but is nonetheless real, accessible, and capable of interacting with human consciousness.

This informational dimension is, in McKenna's framework, the same reality that psychedelic explorers encounter under the influence of [[dmt-tryptamine-hallucinogens]].

The mushroom experience, the DMT flash, the ayahuasca vision: these are not merely subjective distortions of ordinary perception but genuine encounters with an informational hyperspace that exists independently of any individual mind.

McKenna mapped several apparently disparate phenomena onto this single informational continuum: the mushroom experience (direct encounter with a transdimensional intelligence or "teaching entity"), the Gnostic Logos (a divine informational principle accessible through gnosis), the Valis entity (an extraterrestrial or transdimensional information system), the world of dreams (a nightly excursion into the same informational dimension), the [[mckenna-quantum-mechanical-correlates-hallucinogenesis]] foundation of being (the level of reality at which information and matter are indistinguishable), the human future (a teleological attractor pulling consciousness toward greater informational integration), and the after-death state (the dissolution of the individual ego into the informational hyperspace).

All of these, McKenna argued, are different aspects or manifestations of the same underlying reality — a reality that is fundamentally informational in nature and that can be accessed through a variety of experiential modalities.

The continuity between the Gnostic descriptions of the Pleroma (the divine fullness) and the phenomenological descriptions of the DMT experience is, in McKenna's view, too striking to be coincidental.

## Dick, the Nag Hammadi Library, and Gnostic Cosmology

Dick's engagement with the Nag Hammadi texts was not merely scholarly; it was experiential and deeply personal.

He read the Gnostic creation myths — the story of Sophia's fall, the creation of the Demiurge Yaldabaoth, the imprisonment of divine sparks in material bodies — as literal descriptions of the human condition and of his own visionary experiences.

The Gnostic distinction between the true, unknowable God and the false creator deity of the material world resonated powerfully with Dick's sense that ordinary reality was somehow artificial, deceptive, or illusory — a theme that pervades his earlier fiction as well.

McKenna noted that Dick's experience paralleled the classic shamanic pattern: an initiatory crisis (the 1974 visions), the reception of knowledge from a transhuman source (the Valis transmissions), the discovery of a [[christus-eros-osiris-mckenna-mythological-framework-suppression-psychedelic-knowledge]] for interpreting the experience (the Gnostic texts), and the assumption of a prophetic or teaching role (Dick's Exegesis and late novels).

This pattern, McKenna argued, is the same pattern exhibited by individuals who undergo transformative psychedelic experiences and subsequently encounter what they perceive as transdimensional intelligences.

The convergence of ancient Gnostic cosmology, modern visionary experience, and psychedelic phenomenology suggested to McKenna that all three are describing the same underlying reality — a reality that is fundamentally informational in nature and that can be accessed through a variety of experiential modalities.

Dick himself seemed to recognize this convergence, explicitly identifying the Valis entity with the Gnostic Logos and with the divine mind described in the Nag Hammadi texts.

## The Symbiote Hypothesis

McKenna's use of the term "symbiote" to describe the Logos/Valis entity is one of the most distinctive and provocative elements of his interpretation.

The symbiote hypothesis suggests that the informational entity is not a passive object of contemplation but an active participant in a relationship of mutual exchange with human consciousness.

It needs human minds to process, elaborate, and actualize its informational content; in return, it provides human minds with access to knowledge, perspectives, and experiential dimensions that would otherwise be inaccessible.

This framing draws on the biological concept of symbiosis — a close, prolonged association between organisms of different species that benefits both parties.

Just as mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots, exchanging minerals for carbohydrates, so too does the Logos form a symbiotic association with human consciousness, exchanging informational content for the processing power of human cognition.

McKenna saw this symbiotic relationship as fundamental to the history of human culture: the great religious, philosophical, and artistic traditions are, in this view, the products of humanity's symbiotic relationship with transdimensional informational entities.

The symbiote hypothesis also implies that the relationship is not merely historical but ongoing: the informational entities with which humanity has been in symbiotic contact since prehistoric times continue to be accessible through the same experiential modalities — psychedelic states, visionary experiences, meditative absorption — that have always served as the primary channels of communication between the human mind and the transdimensional information field.

This framing has profound implications for how we understand the history of religion, the nature of artistic inspiration, and the persistent human intuition that consciousness is not an isolated phenomenon confined to individual brains but participates in a larger, shared informational reality.
