# Fungal Metal Redox Transformations

Beyond passive biosorption, fungi actively transform metals through oxidation-reduction reactions, methylation-demethylation, precipitation, and organic acid-mediated dissolution. These biogeochemical transformations alter metal mobility, toxicity, and bioavailability -- processes critical to both environmental contamination and remediation.

## Oxidation-Reduction Reactions

**Chromium:** Fungi reduce highly toxic Cr(VI) to less toxic Cr(III). Rhizopus nigricans dead biomass biosorbs Cr(VI) at 212 mg/g (APTS-treated) and 160 mg/g (PEI-treated). Reduction mechanisms involve cell surface electron transfer and intracellular enzymatic reduction. Chromium reduction by yeasts may involve oxidation-reduction reactions, interaction with cellular organelles, and formation of protein-DNA and Cr-DNA adducts.

**Manganese:** Fungi oxidize Mn(II) to Mn(III) and Mn(IV). MnP produces Mn(III) as a diffusible oxidant that extends the oxidative reach of the fungal enzyme system beyond the hyphal surface. Mn(III) chelated by organic acids (oxalate, malonate) acts as a key mediator in lignin and pollutant degradation. Some fungi also precipitate manganese as Mn(IV) oxides on hyphal surfaces.

**Iron:** Fungi reduce Fe(III) to Fe(II) through organic acid production and enzymatic mechanisms. Neurospora crassa mutants showed significant Fe(II) pickup capacities. Iron redox cycling affects availability of both iron and other metals whose behavior is coupled to iron chemistry.

**Selenium and tellurium:** Filamentous fungi reduce selenium oxyanions [Se(IV), Se(VI)] and tellurite [Te(IV)]. Reduced selenium accumulates intracellularly or is volatilized. Reduction products and cellular location influence tolerance mechanisms. Baker's yeast demonstrates different biosorption capacities for different oxidation states of selenium and antimony, confirming that oxidation state affects biosorption behavior.

**Mercury:** Organomercurial compounds are detoxified through a two-step pathway:
1. Organomercury lyase cleaves the carbon-mercury bond, releasing Hg(II)
2. Mercuric reductase reduces Hg(II) to volatile elemental mercury (Hg0)

This system parallels bacterial mercury resistance (mer) operon mechanisms.

## Methylation and Demethylation

Fungi methylate arsenic, selenium, and other metalloids:
- Arsenic methylation produces mono-, di-, and trimethylated arsenic species
- Methylation can increase or decrease toxicity depending on the element and product
- Volatile methylated forms (e.g., dimethyl selenide) enable element removal from contaminated sites
- Demethylation of methylmercury and other organometallic compounds reduces bioaccumulation potential

The methylation of metalloids by fungi has particular relevance for environmental biotechnology because volatile methylated products can be captured from the headspace of bioreactors, offering a pathway for simultaneous removal and recovery of toxic elements. The [[lichenized-fungi-biology-ecology-diversity]] provides the foundation for understanding these tolerance mechanisms. Cell wall composition varies significantly among fungal taxa, which partly explains the widely different metal tolerance and biosorption capacities observed across species. Melanized fungi (those with dark-pigmented cell walls) generally show higher metal tolerance and biosorption capacity than non-melanized species, due to the metal-binding properties of melanin and its contribution to cell wall structural integrity.

## Glomalin as Metal Stabilizer

Glomalin, an insoluble glycoprotein produced by VAM fungi, sequesters potentially toxic elements:
- Extracted from polluted soils containing Cu, Cd, and Pb
- Glomalin from Gigaspora rosea hyphae sequestered up to 28 mg Cu/g in vitro
- Can act as a biostabilizer for long-term remediation of polluted soils
- Persists in soil for years, providing durable metal immobilization

The discovery of glomalin by Sara Wright in 1996 transformed understanding of soil carbon dynamics. ## Applications in Bioremediation and Bioleaching

The metal-transforming capabilities of fungi are exploited in several bioremediation and bioleaching applications. In bioleaching of low-grade ores, fungi such as Aspergillus niger and Penicillium species produce organic acids that solubilize metals from mineral matrices, offering a low-energy alternative to conventional metallurgical extraction. This approach has been applied to recovery of copper, nickel, cobalt, and rare earth elements from mine tailings and electronic waste.

In remediation of chromium-contaminated sites, fungal Cr(VI) reduction offers an in-situ treatment option. The reduction of highly soluble and toxic Cr(VI) to relatively insoluble Cr(III) dramatically reduces chromium mobility and bioavailability. Constructed wetlands incorporating Cr(VI)-reducing fungi have been proposed for passive treatment of chromium-bearing effluents from tanneries and electroplating operations. The [[fungal-immobilization-bioreactor-systems]] for metal-contaminated wastewater. A system that exploits both the biosorptive capacity of the fungal cell wall and the redox-transforming capability of extracellular enzymes can achieve more complete metal removal and immobilization than either mechanism alone. Dead fungal biomass provides high biosorption capacity without the maintenance requirements of live cultures, while live cultures supply continuous enzyme production for ongoing redox cycling. Hybrid systems using killed biomass pretreated with heat or chemical crosslinking agents, combined with enzyme addition from fungal culture supernatants, represent a promising engineering approach.
