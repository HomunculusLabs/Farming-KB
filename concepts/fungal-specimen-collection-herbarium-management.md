# Fungal Specimen Collection and Herbarium Management

Preservation of voucher specimens resulting from biodiversity surveys is extremely important for scientific studies. Voucher specimens and their accompanying field notes document the existence of a fungus at a given place and time and provide the raw data from which [[fungal-species-concept-and-taxonomy]] are constructed.

## Herbarium Functions

Herbaria serve as conservatories of voucher specimens. Early fungal herbaria (e.g., those of E. Fries and C. H. Persoon) contained one or very few collections of a wide range of taxa. Modern herbaria now strive to obtain specimens representing all developmental stages and geographic ranges, reflecting the incorporation of evolutionary concepts into systematic theory.

Herbaria contain the historical information necessary to examine changes in species compositions and abundances, fruiting patterns, host associations, and effects of human activities. Specimens also provide materials for chemical and [[molecular-methods-fungal-taxonomy-and-diversity]].

Major fungal herbaria worldwide hold hundreds of thousands to millions of specimens. These collections are increasingly being digitized, with high-resolution photography and database records making specimen data accessible to researchers globally. Online portals such as MycoPortal and GBIF (Global Biodiversity Information Facility) aggregate herbarium records for broad-scale analyses of fungal distribution and phenology.

## Collection Acquisition Policies

Herbaria evaluate specimens based on six key questions:

1. **Specimen quality:** Is material dried properly, sufficient, uncontaminated, and undamaged?

2. **Documentation:** Do data adequately document the specimen (locality, habitat, collector, date)?

3. **Representation:** Is the taxon already represented in the collection?

4. **Expert identification:** Has the specimen been identified by a specialist?

5. **Scientific value:** Has the specimen been cited in a publication or designated as a voucher?

6. **Legal compliance:** Do copies of appropriate collection, export, and import permits accompany the specimen?

## Field Collection Protocols

Effective field collection begins with careful planning and thorough documentation. Each specimen should be assigned a unique collection number linked to detailed field notes. Key field protocols include:

- Recording precise GPS coordinates and elevation at each collection site

- Photographing specimens in situ before collection, showing habitat context

- Noting the substratum (soil, wood, dung, leaf litter, living plant tissue)

- Recording associated organisms, especially host species for plant pathogens and parasites

- Making spore prints in the field when possible, using contrasting paper or foil

- Collecting multiple specimens when a species is encountered, to serve as duplicates for exchange

## Specimen Processing

Fungal specimens differ fundamentally from plant specimens. Traditional plant pressing methods are ill-suited to fungi. Modern mycologists have recognized intrinsic differences and adjusted preparation techniques accordingly.

### Drying Methods

Prompt and thorough drying is essential to prevent mold growth and insect damage. Air-drying with good ventilation at moderate temperatures (30-40°C) works for most macrofungi. For fleshy species, a food dehydrator set to low heat can accelerate drying without degrading tissues excessively. Freeze-drying produces the highest quality specimens but requires specialized equipment.

### Packeting, Boxing, and Mounting

- 9.3-cm packets made by folding 21.6-cm × 27.9-cm paper are standard

- Fragile specimens are placed in small card boxes or polythene bags with reclosable tops

- Collector's name, collection number, and herbarium number are written on each container

### Associated Information

Essential data include: (1) country and state/province; (2) scientific name of the organism; (3) taxonomic authority; (4) date of collection; (5) collector's name and collection number; (6) detailed locality; (7) habitat and substratum; (8) elevation; (9) host information; (10) specimen description; (11) associated organisms.

## Specimen Maintenance

### Pest Control

Insect hormone attractants should be carried out as routine herbarium activity. Before returned loans are shipped, they should be treated for insect contamination. Herbarium facilities must maintain appropriate temperature and humidity controls.

Regular monitoring with sticky traps and visual inspection helps detect pest infestations early. Fungal herbaria are particularly susceptible to dermestid beetles and psocids, which can rapidly destroy poorly protected collections. Integrated pest management (IPM) strategies that combine environmental controls, monitoring, and targeted treatments are now standard practice.

### Destructive Sampling

Strict protocols govern removal of material for DNA analysis:

1. No material may be removed without permission

2. Permission is granted on a specimen-by-specimen basis

3. Only a portion of any collection may be sampled

4. Molecular data must be submitted to a database (e.g., GenBank)

5. Storage locations of extracts must be provided to the herbarium

6. The extraction procedure used must be documented

7. The herbarium must be acknowledged in publications

Destructive sampling for [[dna-barcoding-fungal-identification]] has become one of the most common reasons for accessing herbarium specimens. Standardized protocols have been developed to minimize damage while maximizing the quality and quantity of extracted DNA, even from century-old specimens.

## Digital Documentation and Imaging

Modern herbarium management increasingly relies on digital infrastructure. High-resolution imaging systems capture specimen morphology, while databases track loans, accessions, and annotations. Digital collections complement physical specimens and greatly facilitate remote research, education, and conservation planning.
