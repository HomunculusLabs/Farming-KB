## Summary
Chlorophyllum molybdites presents one of the most puzzling cases in mycology: a
mushroom that is simultaneously regarded as poisonous, edible, and potentially
neurotropic depending on the source. In Africa, the Yoruba people call it "a jegba
ariwo-orun" — "eat and hear voices from heaven" — suggesting psychoactive use.
Yet most mycological literature classifies it as a poisonous species responsible
for widespread gastrointestinal illness. This contradiction illuminates the deep
challenges of cross-cultural mycological classification and the limitations of
applying Western toxicological frameworks to [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] worldwide.

## The Poisonous Mushroom Consensus

The majority of mycological literature classifies C. molybdites as a poisonous
mushroom. Lincoff (1981), Guzmán (1977a), Portugal et al. (1992), and Duffy and
Vergeer (1977) all report it as toxic, primarily causing severe gastrointestinal
symptoms including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal cramps. Cases of
poisoning have been documented in the U.S.A., Argentina, the Philippines, and
East Africa.

The toxic principle is not fully characterized but is believed to involve
gastrointestinal irritants rather than amatoxins or other lethal mushroom toxins.
While the poisoning is unpleasant and can be severe, fatalities are rare, which
may partly explain why some communities continue to consume it.

## The Edible Mushroom Perspective

Despite the prevailing toxic classification, some authorities have argued that
C. molybdites is edible. Heim (1978) considered it an edible fungus in Africa,
where it is widely consumed. Pegler (1977, 1983) noted the considerable confusion
about whether this species is toxic or edible, suggesting that the answer may not
be straightforward. Singer (1969) stated that "apparently not all forms or races
are poisonous," indicating the possibility of intraspecific variation in toxicity.

This variation could be geographical (different populations producing different
levels of toxins), ecological (growing conditions affecting toxin production),
genetic (distinct chemotypes within what is currently classified as a single
species), or related to [[cultural-evolution-and-psychoactive-substances]], or the mushroom could be confused with
a psychoactive look-alike species.

## Cross-Cultural Confusion and Species Misidentification

The C. molybdites paradox is representative of a broader pattern in ethnomycology:
local names and uses often do not map cleanly onto Western taxonomic species.
Indigenous peoples may apply a single name to multiple mushroom species that share
a habitat, appearance, or cultural function. Conversely, what mycologists classify
as a single species may encompass multiple locally distinguished "folk taxa."
