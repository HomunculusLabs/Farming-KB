# Permaculture Fire Control (Design Course)

**Source:** Permaculture Design Course, Bill Mollison (1981, Pamphlet VII)
**Updated:** 2026-04-18




## Fire Periodicity

Fire has a periodicity specific to the site, depending on two factors: (1) the rate that fuel accumulates on site, and (2) the amount of moisture contained on site. Any ridge top is far more fire prone than valley systems. Vegetation on ridge tops may even be fire-dependent, with species that germinate after fire. Sun-facing sites are more fire prone than shaded slopes.

With 30–40 inches of rainfall, a catastrophic fire occurs about every 25–30 years. The cycle can be changed: the less a site burns, the less likely it is to burn again (more humus, more moisture). The more it burns, the more likely it is to burn again soon, as fire removes moisture-retaining humus and creates fire-prone litter build-up. Natural 30-year fire cycles can collapse to 8–10 year cycles once burning becomes frequent.




## Defense Sequences

The primary strategy is to **reduce fuel** by creating non-fuel surfaces (roads, ponds), constructing swales, pit mulching, and reducing fuel by browsing or grazing.

### House Protection
- Only 100 feet of non-fuel systems between house and forest needed
- Select fire-resistant species: high ash content, high water content, low total bulk, dense growth
- Ice plants and thick-leaf evergreens with fast-decomposing litter are ideal
- **Do NOT use** eucalypts, pines, or other fire weeds with high volatile oil content

### Sprinkler Systems
- Primary protection: good sprinklers downhill, turned on before fire arrives
- Roof sprinklers (monsoon type) are critical — most houses ignite from ash accumulation in gutters, not direct flame contact
- Use gravity-fed systems (independent of public water supply)
- The safest houses in fire are **wooden** (13–15% higher survival than stone or brick) — wood doesn't transmit heat through the fabric

### Fire Shadows
- The real danger of fire is **radiation** (400 feet before fire, hair catches alight; 200 feet, body fat catches)
- Create fire shadows with earth banks and high-water-content trees (willows, poplars) that throw steam clouds
- A Coprosma hedge is so effective you don't even feel the fire behind it

### Fire Shelters
- Dig T-shaped or L-shaped pits, earth them over — clients can wait underground
- Backhoe-dug bank shelters can be built in minutes and are the critical survival factor
- Position shelter away from the fire side, on the slope, at the back of the house
- **Never jump into water during fire** — no oxygen left, you will faint and drown

### Fuel Reduction
- Chickens break up ground litter, mix it with oxygen for breakdown
- Short grazers (sheep, wallaby) on fire side reduce standing litter to one inch
- Mulches are not a fire risk (at most half-inch surface smolder)
- Work from valleys upward with plantings of low fuel vegetation




## Housing Design for Fire
- Specify fire hardware mesh screens to block large particles
- Use rolled-under roof sections with gutters below to prevent leaf/ash/snow accumulation
- White painted wooden houses reflect radiation
- Store fuels and explosives away from living situations, in separate dispersed sheds
- Never place clients at head of converging valleys or where ridges converge




## Broader Pattern

Greece was once wet rainforest with enormous oaks and beeches. It became a skeleton through repeated burning. The Mediterranean and much of North Africa have reached terminal fire degradation — the dirt is burned, plants burned, rocks slip downhill, and nothing is left to burn. The task is to reverse this process by re-establishing moisture-holding systems.
