# Succession and Ecological Evolution in Permaculture

## Overview

Ecological succession is the process by which biological communities evolve and transform over time, progressing from simple pioneer assemblages to complex, stable climax ecosystems. In permaculture, understanding succession is fundamental — designers work with, accelerate, and guide natural successional processes rather than fighting them. Rather than planting a final system directly, [[bill-mollison]] stage plantings to mimic and compress the natural timeline of ecological development.

## The Stages of Succession

### Pioneer Species (Phase 1)

Pioneer species are the first colonizers of disturbed or bare land. They are typically fast-growing, short-lived, nitrogen-fixing, and tolerant of poor soils and full sun exposure. In permaculture, these are often called "nurse crops" or "first aid plants." Common pioneers include:

- **Leguminous plants** — clover, vetch, lupines, acacia, tagasaste
- **Weedy annuals** — dandelion, plantain, thistle, dock
- **Grasses** — rye, oats, barley, Bermuda grass
- **Fast-growing trees** — alder, birch, poplar, willow, casuarina

These species break up compacted soil, fix atmospheric nitrogen, accumulate organic matter, moderate temperature extremes, and create the microclimatic conditions that later successional species need to establish.

### Establishment Phase (Phase 2)

As pioneers modify the site, longer-lived species begin to colonize. Shrubs and small trees establish, creating vertical structure and more diverse habitats. This phase typically includes:

- **Fruit-bearing shrubs** — currants, berries, guava, feijoa
- **Medium-sized nitrogen fixers** — Siberian pea shrub, carob, mesquite
- **Perennial herbs** — comfrey, artichoke, rhubarb
- **Climbers and ground covers** — passionfruit, kiwi, sweet potato

Soil organic matter increases significantly during this phase. The soil food web becomes more complex, with fungi beginning to dominate over bacteria as woody materials accumulate.

### Climax Community (Phase 3)

The mature ecosystem is a diverse, multi-layered polyculture that is largely self-maintaining. [[mollison-lawn-reform-and-alternatives-in-permaculture-design]], this is often a food forest or forest garden system:

- **Canopy trees** — oak, chestnut, pecan, avocado, mango
- **Understory trees** — citrus, pawpaw, persimmon, loquat
- **Shrub layer** — berry bushes, coffee, cacao
- **Herbaceous layer** — [[comparison-perennial-vegetables-vs-cover-crops]], herbs
- **Root layer** — root crops, tubers
- **Ground cover** — living mulches, creeping plants
- **Vine layer** — climbers on trellises or trees

## Accelerating Succession

One of the core strategies in permaculture is to compress the timeline of succession. Nature may take centuries to move from bare ground to mature forest; the permaculture designer can achieve similar results in decades or even years.

### Keyline Design and Earthworks

water harvesting and earthworks provide the water foundation that accelerates all biological growth. Swales, ponds, and contour planting slow water movement, increase infiltration, and create moist microclimates that allow later-successional species to establish sooner.

### Sheet Mulching

Layering cardboard, compost, manure, and mulch over existing vegetation creates instant organic soil profiles. This technique bypasses the pioneer weed phase and allows direct planting of food crops into rich, weed-free beds.

### Nurse Planting

Planting fast-growing nitrogen fixers alongside slower-growing food trees provides shade, wind protection, and nitrogen to the desired species. Once the food trees are established, the nurse plants can be chopped and dropped as mulch or removed entirely.

### Chicken Tractors and Animal Systems

animal systems in permaculture such as chicken tractors accelerate succession by clearing ground, adding manure, and controlling pests in concentrated areas. The disturbed ground is then ready for immediate planting, skipping the weed-pioneer phase.

### Pre-planting with Pioneers

Deliberately planting pioneer species in zones intended for later development builds soil rapidly. After 2-5 years, the pioneer material is slashed and used as mulch, and the desired climax species are planted into the enriched, moderated site.

## Using Succession as a Design Tool

### Temporal Stacking

Just as [[zone-and-sector-analysis]] organizes space, succession organizes time. A well-designed permaculture site has multiple succession stages happening simultaneously in different zones, creating a continuous yield stream as the overall system matures.

### The concept of "Fast Tracking"

Mollison emphasizes that designers can read the landscape to determine which successional phase a site is in, and then design interventions that move it toward a productive climax state. This might mean:

1. **Observing existing pioneer species** to understand soil conditions
2. **Identifying the target climax community** appropriate to the climate
3. **Installing earthworks and water systems** as the foundation
4. **Planting the full succession sequence at once**, with pioneers as nurse crops
5. **Managing the transition** by pruning, chop-and-drop, and selective removal

### Edge Effect and Succession

edge effect and ecotone design is closely related to succession — the boundaries between successional stages are where the greatest diversity and productivity occur. Designers create and maintain these edges for maximum yield.

### Nutrient Accumulation

Pioneer plants mine nutrients from deep soil layers and bring them to the surface through leaf fall. Deep-rooted [[comparison-dynamic-accumulators-vs-cover-crops]] like comfrey, dandelion, and chicory extract minerals from subsoil and deposit them in the topsoil as their leaves decompose. This nutrient pumping is a critical function that the permaculture designer leverages by interplanting accumulators with food crops.

### Soil Structure Development

The root systems of successive plant communities [[query-how-do-fungi-improve-soil-structure]] progressively. Grasses create fine, fibrous root mats. Shrubs develop deeper, coarser roots that penetrate compacted layers. Trees create extensive root channels that allow water and air to penetrate deep into the soil profile. Mycorrhizal networks, which develop strongly in later successional stages, extend the effective root zone of all plants in the community.

## Applying Succession in Practice

A practical example: on a degraded, compacted pasture, the designer might first sow a mix of clover, daikon radish, and oats. Within a year, the soil is loosened, nitrogen is fixed, and organic matter has begun accumulating. In year two, fruit tree seedlings are planted with comfrey and tagasaste as nurse plants. By year five, the tagasaste is being managed as a chop-and-drop mulch source, the fruit trees are beginning to bear, and berry shrubs fill the understory. By year ten, the system resembles a young food forest with multiple yields and minimal external inputs.

## Disturbance and Retrogression

Succession can be reversed through disturbance — fire, overgrazing, clearing, or soil erosion. Permaculture designs include strategies to prevent retrogression:

- Fire-resistant plantings and green firebreaks
- [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]] rather than continuous grazing
- Soil conservation measures on slopes
- Maintaining ground cover at all times
- Designing for redundancy so that no single disturbance can collapse the system

Understanding both forward succession and retrogression allows the designer to maintain productive systems indefinitely, creating [[permaculture-design-principles]] in action — stable, self-reliant, and yielding. The key insight from Mollison is that succession is not something to wait for passively but a process to be actively designed, directed, and harvested at every stage.
