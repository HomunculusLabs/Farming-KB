# Mollison: Earth Resources from Excavation

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 9, Section 9.9)

## The Philosophy of Earth Resources

Whenever earth is moved, it seldom reveals a uniform composition. In fact, most excavation reveals materials already sorted by nature, and of specific use. To mix them up is to set back the clock a few thousand years. The supervising designer or property owner will do well to follow every excavator (animal or technological) and put aside useful materials.

It would not be uncommon for excavation to pay for itself on any one of the useful factors listed below, and for the client to benefit long after the water storage is built, from materials carefully put aside during excavation. If you are wise, you never leave the site when earth is being moved: it is very expensive to bring machines back for fine touches, to adjust the work, or to sort out mixed materials.

## Topsoil

Topsoil is the often dark, root-filled living surface of the earth. It is to be carefully stripped off, piled aside, and later returned over other material onto banks, over padi or fields, and even below water in ponds. Topsoil can be very thin and rare, usually no more than 6 to 18 cm deep. Where deeper, it can be spread on poorer soils to help them produce, or pocketed for trees in poor soil areas.

## Peats

Excavation in marshes, bogs, or lowlands may reveal 1 to 9 m (3 to 30 feet) of semi-compacted fibrous plant material. Most can be stockpiled, otherwise about 0.6 m or so returned to the surface as for topsoil. The peat stockpile is most valuable for:
- Mixing with sands and loams for making even more topsoil.
- Fine growing medium in nurseries.
- Insulator in buildings.
- Only in desperation as a fuel, for those who burn peat are near the base of life on earth.

Peatlands throughout the world now urgently need preservation as threatened habitats of unique vegetation. Peat preserves timber, animals, and treasure-troves such as hoards of acorns and firkins of beech butter from the forests which preceded the bogs.

## Clay

Good clay is very useful stuff. A depth can extend 0.3 to 6 m (1 to 20 feet). This resource can be stockpiled, preferably covered with plastic sheets. Uses include:
- Dried and baked brick.
- Dam and pond sealing.
- Pots shaped and fired.
- Cricket pitches (some types).
- Fine porcelain or special filters and insulators (other types).

Clay compositions:
- Fireclay: 58 to 75 percent silica, 25 to 36 percent alumina, 0.25 to 2 percent iron oxide.
- Bricks: 95 percent silica, no alumina, and less than 2 percent lime.

## Sand

Clean sand may make grinding powder or silica chips. Yellowish sand has enough calcium to counter acidity. Fine black sand may be of use in casting metals or as material for a black-sand solar collector. White sand reflects light like snow and can reflect heat to houses and walls.

Fat sand (containing some clay, less than 20 percent) makes ovens and mortar. Sharp sand (mostly silica) makes glass and cement, or can be mixed with peat to grow trees or other plants in nurseries (the best use). Sieved sand gives special sizes for grit or polish in mortars and grinders.

## Gravels

- Heaped up: good roads, drains, and soft sun-bathing patches.
- Angular gravel: good concrete.
- Smooth gravel: good water filters or enzyme columns.
- Mulch for trees, as are cinder and crushed pumice.
- 19 mm-size angular gravels are the best for heat stores, where air from solar attics or glasshouses is blown into a heat store wall or tank.
- Heat stoves can be placed under floors to heat gravel beds.

## Shingle

Shingle is good under gravels on roads, for drains, and coarse filters. It is an excellent mulch for condensing water in deserts or dry places, a refuge for snails and decomposers, and a filter bed for swimming pools au naturel.

## Slate

Floors, tablets, billiard tables, roofs. Any rock that can be split can be utilised for walls and houses, roof areas, garden paths, and floor tiles.

## Boulders

Boulders serve as coarse mulch, wildlife refuge, and walling and windbreak material which gains and radiates heat. Some boulders are good as pounders, others excellent mortar and pestles, weights, anchors, and ballast in boats.

## Tracks, Signs, and Fossils

Like snow and windblown sand, newly moved earth and puddled clays reveal a section of earth life, making an imprint of secretive and nocturnal birds and mammals. After rain, it is a good time to take a notebook and observe footprints, scrapes, and tracks. A list of species obtained this way adds to basic information resources and may warn of troubles from ground foragers.

Under the earth lie the mute stories of prior life: shells, tools, shards, ashes, bones that can be dated. Buried trees give growth rings telling past climate, air quality, and accurate burial dates. Today's disposal pits may well become tomorrow's mines.
