# Sidney Cohen and Oscar Janiger: Hollywood Psychedelic Therapy

## Overview

In the early 1960s, a major center for [[psychedelic-therapy-and-research]]
exploration emerged in Southern California, distinct from both the
[[european-psycholytic-therapy-1960s-1970s]] model and the Harvard [[mckenna-consciousness-expansion-and-drug-war]]
project. Led by psychiatrist Sidney Cohen and physician Oscar Janiger,
this program provided psychedelic experiences to clients in the
Hollywood film, arts, and media community.

As Metzner noted in his 1998 review, this Southern California work
"brought considerable publicity and notoriety to psychedelics" and
occupied a unique position in the landscape of early psychedelic
research. The Hollywood connection gave these sessions a cultural reach
that far exceeded what was possible in academic or clinical settings.

## Sidney Cohen: Clinical Research and Evolving Perspective

Sidney Cohen was a prominent psychiatrist and one of the first American
researchers to seriously study LSD's [[metzner-ayahuasca-therapeutic-potential]] in a
systematic clinical setting. His career trajectory mirrors the broader
arc of psychedelic research in America, from initial optimism through
growing concern to eventual advocacy for strict regulation.

In the early 1960s, Cohen's Southern California practice attracted
creative professionals from the Hollywood entertainment industry,
including actors, writers, musicians, directors, and visual artists.
He provided carefully structured sessions with attention to set and
setting, maintaining scientific rigor while exploring the experiential
dimensions of the psychedelic state.

The Hollywood connection was culturally significant for several reasons.
Artists who had transformative experiences naturally wove elements of
those experiences into their creative work, introducing psychedelic
consciousness into mainstream American culture through film, music,
literature, and visual art. The association with Hollywood also brought
media attention that both popularized and sensationalized psychedelics.

This media dynamic created a network of influential advocates who would
later help shape the counterculture of the 1960s and beyond. A single
transformative LSD experience by a prominent actor or musician could
reach millions through their subsequent creative work and public
statements about the experience.

Cohen's later career took a notably different direction. After
conducting extensive surveys of psychedelic use and adverse reactions,
he became a prominent scientific voice calling for caution. His 1960
paper documented side effects and complications associated with LSD use,
drawing on data from hundreds of clinical cases and becoming influential
in shaping both medical and public opinion about the risks of LSD.

## Oscar Janiger: The Largest LSD Creativity Study

Oscar Janiger was a physician who conducted one of the most extensive
studies of LSD's effects on creativity ever attempted. Based in Los
Angeles, Janiger administered LSD to over 1,000 participants between
1954 and 1962, making his study one of the largest clinical
investigations of LSD in the history of psychedelic research.

Janiger's approach differed from the European psycholytic model. Rather
than using LSD for psychoanalytic exploration of neurotic patterns, he
was interested in the drug's capacity to enhance creativity, alter
perception, and provide meaningful personal experiences. He gave
participants creative tasks during sessions and studied how the
psychedelic state affected their creative output and process.

Many participants reported lasting positive changes in their creative
abilities, breakthroughs in artistic blocks, new directions in their
work, and a deepened connection to their [[allegro-womans-part-in-the-creative-process]]. Participants
frequently noted that LSD allowed them to see their work from entirely
new perspectives and to break free from habitual patterns of thinking.

However, this massive dataset was never published in full in the
peer-reviewed literature of the time. This represents a significant
lost opportunity, as data from over a thousand sessions could have
provided invaluable insights into the relationship between altered
states of consciousness and creative cognition.
