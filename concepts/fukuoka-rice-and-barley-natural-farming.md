## Fukuoka's Natural Farming of Rice and Barley

Masanobu Fukuoka developed a revolutionary no-till rice and barley cultivation method he called "direct-seeded, no-tillage, winter grain/rice succession in a clover cover." This system eliminates ploughing, [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]], pesticides, and most of the labor traditionally associated with rice production, while achieving yields of approximately 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre.

### The Five Principles

Fukuoka's natural farming rests on five foundational principles:
1. **No tillage** — never plough or turn the soil
2. **No fertilizer** — no chemical or prepared [[hamilton-organic-fertilizers-and-plant-nutrition]]
3. **No pesticides or herbicides** — no chemicals for pest or weed control
4. **No weeding** — or minimal weeding; weeds managed by natural competition and mulching
5. **No pruning** — of fruit trees (in his broader system)

### The Annual Calendar

**Autumn (before rice harvest)**: White clover and winter grain (barley or wheat) are broadcast over the ripening heads of rice before the fall harvest. The rice supports the germinating clover and grain, and the clover begins to establish as a living ground cover.

**After rice harvest**: Rice is harvested while treading on the young shoots of winter grain. After leaving the rice to dry for three days, it is threshed and the straw is scattered uncut over the entire field as mulch.

**Winter**: Seed rice is formed into clay pellets (to protect from birds and rodents) and scattered over the straw before the New Year. With the winter grain growing and the rice seed sown, there is nothing left to do until harvesting the winter grain.

**Late May**: The winter grain (barley) is harvested, dried, and threshed. All straw is scattered uncut over the field as mulch. The field is then flooded for 4-5 days to weaken the clover and give the rice shoots a chance to break through the cover. After flooding, the field is drained and allowed to dry. The clover recovers and continues to fix nitrogen.

**The cycle repeats**: No ploughing, no chemical inputs, no weeding.

### Key Innovations

**Clover as living mulch**: White clover (Trifolium repens) serves multiple functions simultaneously — it fixes nitrogen (replacing fertilizer), suppresses weeds, protects soil from erosion, retains moisture, and provides [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] insects. Periodic flooding temporarily weakens it to allow rice emergence.

**Straw mulch**: Returning all straw to the field (both rice and barley straw) replaces organic matter, feeds soil organisms, suppresses weeds, and maintains soil structure. Fukuoka states that "compost does not have to be prepared if rice straw is applied to the fields half a year before the rice is planted."

**Clay pellet seed coating**: Seed rice is encased in clay pellets before broadcasting. This protects seeds from birds, rodents, and drying out, dramatically improving germination rates without the need for transplanting seedlings in a nursery.

**No-till succession**: Rice and winter grain grow in succession on the same field without ploughing. The soil is never disturbed, preserving its structure, microbial communities, and organic matter.

### The Philosophy

Fukuoka argues that modern agriculture's heavy inputs — prolonged [[fukuoka-puddling-summer-flooding-soil-death-rice-cultivation]], chemical fertilizers, pesticides, ploughing — actually weaken rice plants and create disease susceptibility. "Healthy rice plants have no need for the plow or chemicals." The problem is that farmers create the conditions for disease through their interventions, then apply more interventions to fix the problems they created.

He emphasizes that "the labor of one or two persons" is sufficient for his method on a quarter-acre, compared to the intensive labor requirements of conventional rice farming. The key insight is that "nature, not man, grows the rice and wheat."

### Yields

Fukuoka reports yields of approximately 590 kg (1,300 pounds) of winter grain and 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre, comparable to or exceeding conventional yields in his region. He developed a strain called "Happy Hill" rice that he described as one of the highest-yielding strains in the world.

### Criticisms and Limitations

- The system was developed for a specific climate (temperate Japan) and may require adaptation for other regions
- Clay pellet production is labor-intensive if done by hand
- Flood management requires some skill and observation
- [[query-how-to-transition-from-conventional-to-no-till-farming]] to natural farming may involve temporary yield reductions
- Fukuoka acknowledges that "natural farming will never be perfected" — it requires constant observation and responsiveness to local conditions
