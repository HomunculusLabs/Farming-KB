# Precipitation Types and Dew-Fog Collection

Precipitation is one of the most critical climatic factors in [[table-mountain]], South Africa**: 329 cm (128 inches) per year from fog drip alone
- **Lanai, Hawaii**: 127 cm (50 inches) per year from fog drip

In such areas, even field crops may thrive without irrigation. On sea-facing slopes, bare rock and new soil surfaces are colonized with lichens and mosses, while rainforest develops on richer soils.

### Fog Forests
Much of New Zealand experiences upslope fog precipitation, where dense forests develop naturally unless cleared. The irregular canopy of such forests acts as excellent fog condensers. Even without visible fog, trees condense considerable moisture on sea-facing slopes when night winds move in off warm seas and encounter cool leaf surfaces.

In the humid air of fog forests, giant trees may accumulate so much moisture that evapotranspiration becomes ineffective. More large limbs may fall in still foggy air than in high winds (which snap dry branches rather than living, water-laden limbs). Permanent condensation fogs cloak the tops of high oceanic islands, supporting hanging mosses and epiphytes.

## Design Implications

For permaculture designers, the key strategies derived from understanding precipitation types include:

1. **Site for fog capture** — use existing vegetation and topography to intercept fog on seaward slopes
2. **Build dew condensers** — stone piles, mesh fences, and scattered plantings to supplement water in dry areas
3. **Size water storage for extremes, not averages** — use flood records and intensity data
4. **Select species for actual water availability** — not rainfall averages, which may mislead
5. **Preserve cloud forests** — they are irreplaceable natural water-harvesting systems
