# Precipitation Types and Dew-Fog Collection

Precipitation is one of the most critical climatic factors in [[permaculture-design]]. Understanding the different mechanisms that deliver water to a site — rainfall, dew, fog, and condensation — enables designers to maximize water availability even in arid and semi-arid regions where conventional rainfall alone is insufficient.

## Two Basic Inputs to Precipitation

There are two fundamental categories of water input to any landscape:

1. **Water falling from clouds** — rainfall, snow, and hail
2. **Condensation** — water condensed or trapped from clear air or fogs by cool surfaces

While rainfall is the only widely and reliably measured form, condensation can be critically important on seaward slopes and at higher altitudes where cloud forests develop. World average rainfall is approximately 86 cm (34 inches) per year, but this average is a poor guide to actual site conditions.

## Rainfall Classification

Areas receiving 50 cm (20 inches) or less of rainfall annually are considered **semi-arid**, while 25 cm (10 inches) or less defines **arid and desert** conditions. However, local seasonal aridity can occur in any climate due to long-term cycles, jetstream fluctuations, or oceanic current changes. Broad-scale deforestation can also induce long-term increases in aridity.

### Distribution Over Averages

Rainfall averages are best used as broad indicators rather than definitive limits. Far more useful is understanding:

- **Expected distribution** of rainfall across seasons
- **Intensity data** — critical for sizing road culverts, dam spillways, and storage capacity
- **Extreme events** — 100-year flood records indicate true placement limits for plant systems, fences, and buildings

As precipitation rises, available light decreases. In extremely cloudy or fog-bound humid climates, light becomes the limiting factor for some plants. At the dry end of the spectrum (50 cm mean rainfall), evaporation in excess of precipitation becomes the limiting factor, determining arid-land storage strategies.

## Rainfall by Process

Rainfall is distinguished by the physical process causing it:

### Orographic Rainfall
Air cools as it rises over mountains or hills, causing moisture to condense and fall. This is the dominant rainfall mechanism on windward mountain slopes and creates dramatic rainfall gradients across short distances.

### Cyclonic or Frontal Rainfall
Cool and warm air masses override each other in the polar circulation system. This produces the cyclonic or spiral rains that characterize mid-latitude climates, arriving in sequences of warm and cold fronts.

### Convective Rainfall
Columns of hot air rise from deserts or warm ocean surfaces into cooler upper atmosphere layers. This process creates the characteristic mosaic rainfall pattern of desert regions, where isolated thunderstorms may drench one area while leaving nearby areas completely dry.

## Dew

Dew is a common result of clear nights, rapid radiation heat loss, and a moist air mass over coasts and hills. It occurs more frequently in clear-sky deserts than in cloudy areas. A slight wind speed (1-5 km/h) assists dew deposition, while both still air and strong winds reduce it.

### Key Characteristics
- **Optimal height**: Deposition is greatest 3-100 cm above ground level
- **Highest deposition**: Over dry ground (which cools more rapidly at night)
- **Lower deposition**: Over wet earth (which chills less quickly)

Dew must be distinguished from **guttation** (water exuded from leaf tips) and **distillation** (rising ground vapour condensing on leaves on cloudy nights). Guttation water clings to leaf tips; dew covers the whole leaf surface.

### Dew Significance in Deserts
In deserts, the 4-5 cm (1-2 inches) of dew per year can be significant as an accessory to trickle irrigation. Dew can be captured through:

1. **Stone mulch piles** — loosely stacked stones less than 1 m high where low night winds cool rock surfaces and dew accumulates to dampen ground below. In the Negev desert, each stone mound can water one tree.
2. **Large radiation traps** — as on Lanzarote in the Canary Islands, where cinder-covered pits grow one grape vine per hole
3. **Free-standing shrubs** — the most efficient dew collectors are scattered shrubs 1-2 m (3-6 feet) tall. Discrete spacing of desert plants may trap 40% more dew than solid vegetation canopies
4. **Metallic mesh fences** — 1 m high fences serve as initial condensers, with shrubs grown along the fence drip-line, then the fence moved onward

## Fog

Fog forms where warm water vapour enters cool air, or where cold ground chills an airstream. Chang (1968) identified three types:

### 1. Radiation Ground Fog
On clear nights, hollows and plateaus cool rapidly and fog forms in patterns similar to winter frosts.

### 2. Advection Fog
Cold offshore currents condense moisture in warm sea airstreams. These coastal fogs plague many coasts including Newfoundland and northwest Europe.

### 3. Upslope or Orographic Fog
Warm, humid airstreams carried up hill slopes condense as air cools with altitude.

### Fog Drip — A Major Water Source
Unlike dew, fogs can deliver enormous quantities of water:

- **[[table-mountain]], South Africa**: 329 cm (128 inches) per year from fog drip alone
- **Lanai, Hawaii**: 127 cm (50 inches) per year from fog drip

In such areas, even field crops may thrive without irrigation. On sea-facing slopes, bare rock and new soil surfaces are colonized with lichens and mosses, while rainforest develops on richer soils.

### Fog Forests
Much of New Zealand experiences upslope fog precipitation, where dense forests develop naturally unless cleared. The irregular canopy of such forests acts as excellent fog condensers. Even without visible fog, trees condense considerable moisture on sea-facing slopes when night winds move in off warm seas and encounter cool leaf surfaces.

In the humid air of fog forests, giant trees may accumulate so much moisture that evapotranspiration becomes ineffective. More large limbs may fall in still foggy air than in high winds (which snap dry branches rather than living, water-laden limbs). Permanent condensation fogs cloak the tops of high oceanic islands, supporting hanging mosses and epiphytes.

## Design Implications

For permaculture designers, the key strategies derived from understanding precipitation types include:

1. **Site for fog capture** — use existing vegetation and topography to intercept fog on seaward slopes
2. **Build dew condensers** — stone piles, mesh fences, and scattered plantings to supplement water in dry areas
3. **Size water storage for extremes, not averages** — use flood records and intensity data
4. **Select species for actual water availability** — not rainfall averages, which may mislead
5. **Preserve cloud forests** — they are irreplaceable natural water-harvesting systems
