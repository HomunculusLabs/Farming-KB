## Overview

Mycelial networks represent one of the most remarkable and underappreciated biological systems on Earth. Described by mycologist Paul Stamets as "Earth"s natural internet," these vast underground networks of fungal threads connect plants, trees, and microorganisms across entire ecosystems. The concept of the "Wood Wide Web" captures the idea that forests are not collections of isolated organisms but interconnected communication and resource-sharing networks mediated by fungi.

## Common Mycorrhizal Networks (CMNs)

The most ecologically significant mycelial networks are **mycorrhizal networks**—symbiotic associations between fungi and plant roots. Approximately 90% of all land plants form [[glomeromycota-arbuscular-mycorrhizae]] (AM):** The most common type, formed by Glomeromycota fungi with most herbaceous plants and many trees. Fungal hyphae penetrate root cortical cells, forming tree-like arbuscules that facilitate nutrient exchange.
- **Ectomycorrhizae (ECM):** Formed primarily by Basidiomycota and Ascomycota with woody plants, especially in temperate and boreal forests. Hyphae form a sheath around root tips without penetrating cells.
- **[[chamovitz-plant-scent-communication-volatile-organic-compounds]] (VOCs) and other signaling molecules. Research has shown that these signals can travel through CMNs to warn neighboring, connected plants.

Neighboring plants that receive these signals may then activate their own defense mechanisms before the attacker reaches them. This network-mediated early warning system provides a significant survival advantage, effectively creating a distributed immune system for the plant community.

## Resource Allocation and Decision-Making

Mycelial networks demonstrate a remarkable capacity for **resource allocation and decision-making** that rivals simple nervous systems. Slime molds, though not true fungi, provide compelling evidence of this intelligence:

### Physarum polycephalum and the Tokyo Rail Network

In a famous experiment, researchers at the University of Tokyo placed oat flakes at locations corresponding to major cities around Tokyo and introduced *Physarum polycephalum* slime mold. The organism grew to connect all food sources, and over 26 hours, it refined its network to closely mirror Tokyo's existing rail system—one of the most efficient transportation networks ever designed by human engineers.

The slime mold optimized for multiple objectives simultaneously: efficiency (shortest paths), redundancy (alternative routes if one connection is severed), and resilience (cost-effectiveness). This feat is particularly impressive because the slime mold has no brain or central nervous system—the optimization emerges from the distributed decision-making of the entire organism.

## Parallels Between Mycelial and Neural Networks

Stamets and other researchers have drawn explicit parallels between mycelial networks and neural networks in animal brains:

| Neural Network Feature | Mycelial Network Parallel |
|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Synapses | Cross-walls (septa) with pores |
| Action potentials | Cytoplasmic streaming and ion waves |
| Neurotransmitters | Chemical signals and metabolites |
| Axon bundles | Rhizomorphs and mycelial cords |
| Learning (synaptic plasticity) | Network remodeling based on resource availability |
| Memory (stored patterns) | Persistent mycelial pathways |

Both systems solve the problem of processing information and distributing resources across a spatially distributed network. Both exhibit emergent properties that cannot be predicted from studying individual components in isolation. Stamets argues that this is not merely analogy—mycelial networks may represent a form of biological intelligence that predates neural networks by hundreds of millions of years.
