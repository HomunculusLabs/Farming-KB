# Fukuoka Natural Way of Farming - Theory of Natural Farming

Masanobu Fukuoka's "The Natural Way of Farming" presents a comprehensive philosophical and practical [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]], proposing instead a method of farming based on non-intervention and harmony with nature. The book extends far beyond agriculture into a fundamental critique of Western epistemology, industrial civilization, and humanity's relationship with the natural world.

## Five Principles of Natural Farming

Fukuoka's practice rests on five foundational principles, which he maintained for over fifty years without once doubting their universal applicability:

1. **No tillage** -- Plants have always grown by direct seeding without tillage. Microorganisms and small animals act as nature's tillers. Plowing kills soil biology and creates hardpan that then "requires" further plowing.
2. **No fertilizer** -- [[cultivator-compost-preparation-phase-methods]] depletes the land from which organic material is taken.
3. **No pesticides** -- Nature strikes a balance among insects, plants, and microorganisms. Pesticides kill natural predators (spiders, dragonflies, frogs) faster than pests, creating the very conditions that make pesticides appear necessary.
4. **No weeding** -- Nature does not arbitrarily designate one plant a weed. Clover and other ground covers serve beneficial functions in the ecosystem.
5. **No pruning** -- A tree grows best in its natural habitat; branches do not tangle, sunlight falls on every leaf, and the tree bears fully each year without pruning.

## The Philosophy of Mu (Nothingness)

Natural farming originates in the philosophy of "Mu," or nothingness -- the Eastern teaching that all is unnecessary. Fukuoka describes it as a "Buddhist way of farming" that is "methodless," "unmoving," "boundless and yielding," leaving "the soil, the plants, and the insects to themselves."

The core insight is that human knowledge and action are the source of agriculture's problems, not its solutions. "All begins by relinquishing human knowledge." Nature is "neither living nor dead" in the scientific sense; notions of pest versus predator, good versus bad, are distinctions invented by man that are alien to nature.

## Critique of Scientific Agriculture

Fukuoka argues that scientific farming is centrifugal and divergent -- it breaks away from nature through the expansion of human wants, becoming ever more complex, expensive, and labor-intensive. Natural farming is convergent and centripetal -- it returns to nature by eliminating unnecessary practices.

Key critiques include:

- **Chemical fertilizers** only appear to increase yields because they restore productivity after soil has been killed by plowing and chemicals. On naturally fertile soil, they may actually reduce productivity.
- **Pesticides** destroy the natural balance of predators and prey. One application kills tens of thousands of young spiders per square yard while leaving the target pests largely unaffected.
- **Mechanization** has not made farming easier -- it has driven farmers from their land and enslaved them to equipment. Farmers must work additional jobs to pay for machinery.
- **High-yield technologies** are "no more than glorified attempts to stave off reductions in productivity" that human intervention itself has caused.
- **Organic farming**, despite its popularity, is "just another type of scientific farming" -- it still involves moving materials, processing, and treating, producing only "local and temporal gains" that may be destructive from a broader perspective.

## Energy Efficiency

Fukuoka provides striking energy calculations. Natural farming produces 130 pounds of rice (200,000 kilocalories) per man-day of labor without outside material inputs -- about 100 times a farmer's daily caloric intake. Traditional farming with draft animals requires 5-10 times more energy input. Light mechanization doubles this again, and large-scale mechanization doubles it yet again. Scientific agriculture thus requires up to 50 times the energy expenditure per unit of food produced compared to natural farming.

## Nature is Unknowable

A central philosophical argument is that man cannot know nature. Nature is an "organically interwoven community" that "cannot be dissected or resolved into its parts." Once broken down, "it dies." Scientists examine "a dead and fragmented nature" and claim to "know" it, but "what they are examining is no longer what they think it to be."

Fukuoka illustrates this with the example of forest decline: logging leads to monoculture planting, which changes soil characteristics, which affects plants and animals, which alters air quality and temperature, which affects microorganisms, in an "unending chain of interwoven events" where no true first cause can be identified. "In nature, there is no cause and effect."

## The "Do-Nothing" Movement

Fukuoka frames natural farming as part of a broader "do-nothing" movement -- a shift from the age of aggressive material expansion to one of consolidation and convergence. This is not passivity but active simplification: eliminating unnecessary practices, expenditures, and labor. After thirty years, Fukuoka reduced his farming to "essentially just sowing seed and spreading straw."

His method: "direct-seeded, no-tillage, winter grain/rice succession in a clover cover." Broadcast clover and winter grain over ripening rice, harvest rice while treading on young grain shoots, scatter uncut straw, form clay pellets with rice seed, and wait. "Human effort is unnecessary because nature, not man, grows the rice and wheat."

## Critique of Modern Civilization

Fukuoka extends his analysis beyond agriculture to civilization itself:

- **Dietary decline**: Agriculture has become "the manufacture of petroleum-derived foods." Transition from field cultivation to hothouse to hydroponic to total chemical synthesis represents progressive alienation from nature.
- **Livestock inefficiency**: It takes 10,000 square yards of land to support one person on beef versus 200 square yards on grains. Raising cattle "impoverishes the soil and denudes the earth."
