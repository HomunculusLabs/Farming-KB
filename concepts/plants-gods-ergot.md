# Ergot

## Overview

Ergot ([[eleusinian-mysteries]] of ancient Greece, and its contribution to
modern medicine. Most importantly, the ergoline alkaloids derived from
Ergot are the chemical basis of lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), the
most potent hallucinogen known. Ergot alkaloids or derivatives of them
are the basis of important medicines used today in obstetrics, internal
medicine, and psychiatry.

## Botanical Description

Claviceps purpurea (Fr.) Tulasne belongs to the Clavicipitaceae family
and is distributed throughout temperate zones of Europe, northern
Africa, Asia, and North America. Ergot is a fungal disease of certain
grasses and sedges, primarily of rye. The term "Ergot" refers to the
sclerotium or fruiting body of an ascomycete or sac fungus.

The spur is a purplish or black, curved, club-shaped growth 0.5-2.5
in. (1-6 cm) long, which parasitically replaces the endosperm of the
rye kernel. The fungus produces psychoactive and toxic alkaloids. There
are two distinct periods in the life cycle of this fungus: an active
and a dormant stage. The Ergot spur represents the dormant stage. When
the spur falls to the ground, the Ergot sprouts globular heads called
ascocarps from which grow asci, each with threadlike ascospores that are
disseminated when the asci rupture.

## Traditional and Indigenous Use

It appears that Ergot has never been utilized purposefully as a
hallucinogen in medieval Europe. However, it has recently been
convincingly argued that Ergot played a role in the Eleusinian mysteries
of ancient Greece, the most important religious initiation ceremony of
the classical world. A Greek lecythus (sacramental vessel, 450-425
B.C.) depicts Triptolemus holding the Eleusinian grain, a grass
probably infected with Ergot, while Demeter or Persephone pours a sacred
libation, presumably prepared from the infected grain.

Ergot was employed extensively as a medicine by midwives in cases of
difficult childbirth during the Middle Ages. It induced contractions of
involuntary muscles and was a strong vasoconstrictor, making it a
valuable if dangerous obstetric tool.

## Chemical Constituents

Ergoline alkaloids, mainly derivatives of lysergic acid, are the
pharmacologically active constituents of Ergot:

- **Ergotamine**: A major ergot alkaloid used medicinally for migraine
  treatment. It is a partial agonist at serotonin, dopamine, and
  adrenergic receptors.
- **Ergometrine** (ergonovine): Used in obstetrics to control
  postpartum hemorrhage by inducing uterine contractions.
- **Lysergic acid amide** (LSA, ergine): Found in the seeds of
  Morning Glory species (Ololiuqui), chemically related to the ergot
  alkaloids and possessing mild psychedelic properties.
- **Lysergic acid hydroxymethylamide**: Another psychoactive ergoline
  found in Morning Glory seeds.
- **Lysergic acid diethylamide** (LSD): A synthetic derivative of
  ergot, the most potent hallucinogen known, first synthesized by
  gordon-wasson in 1938 from ergotamine.

The psychoactive constituents of Morning Glory seeds (Ololiuqui) are
found only in this unrelated group of fungi containing Ergot, making
the chemical relationship between these two important hallucinogenic
traditions a matter of great chemo-taxonomic interest.

## Pharmacology and Effects

Ergot alkaloids interact with multiple neurotransmitter systems,
including serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine receptors. Their
pharmacological effects include:

- Vasoconstriction and vasospasm
- Uterine contraction (used in obstetrics)
- Hallucinogenic activity (at higher doses)
- Nausea, vomiting, and gastrointestinal distress

Ergotism, the disease caused by consuming Ergot-contaminated grain,
manifests in two forms: convulsive ergotism (St. Anthony's fire),
characterized by hallucinations, convulsions, and psychotic behavior;
and gangrenous ergotism, characterized by vasoconstriction leading to
limb gangrene. Both forms were responsible for devastating epidemics
throughout European history.

## Cultural and Historical Significance

When accidentally ground up with rye flour during the Middle Ages,
Ergot poisoned whole districts with ergotism. These mass poisonings
became known as St. Anthony's fire, named after the order of monks who
treated the afflicted. Some historians have suggested that ergotism
epidemics may have contributed to episodes of mass hysteria, including
the Salem witch trials, though this theory remains debated.

The Eleusinian mysteries, celebrated for nearly 2,000 years at
Eleusis near Athens, were the most important religious initiation
ceremony of the classical world. Participants consumed a sacred
beverage called the kykeon, which recent scholarship argues may have
been prepared from Ergot-infected barley. The mysteries promised
revelations about the afterlife and were considered essential for a
blessed existence after death.

## Modern Research and Legal Status

Ergot-derived alkaloids remain essential in modern medicine. Ergotamine
is used for migraine treatment, and ergometrine for postpartum
hemorrhage. LSD, the synthetic derivative first discovered by Albert
Hofmann in 1943, has seen renewed research interest for treating
cluster headaches, anxiety, depression, and end-of-life distress.
Claviceps purpurea itself is not controlled, but its alkaloids and
derivatives are variously scheduled depending on their specific
pharmacological properties.
