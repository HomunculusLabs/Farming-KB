# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi — Biology, Host Relationships, and Phylogeny

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi form one of the most widespread and ecologically significant mutualistic symbioses on Earth. Classified within the order **Glomales** (Morton and Benny 1990)—formerly placed in Endogonales sensu Gerdemann and Trappe (1974)—these fungi are defined by their capacity to colonize plant roots and form highly branched **arbuscule** structures that interface directly with the root cell plasmalemma. All fungi within this group share this defining feature, which serves as the primary site of nutrient exchange between fungus and host.

## Taxonomic Classification and Systematics

The order Glomales was formally established by Morton and Benny (1990), reorganizing taxa previously assigned to Endogonales by Gerdemann and Trappe (1974). This reclassification reflected fundamental differences in spore morphology, mycelial structure, and symbiotic behavior that distinguished [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] from other zygomycete-like organisms. The phylogenetic framework, based on [[culturing-macrofungi-describing-morphological-characters]] of the mycelium and reproductive spores (Morton 1990a; Morton and Benny 1990; Morton and Redecker 2001), recognizes two major suborders: **Glomineae** and **Gigasporineae**.

## Mycelial Structure and Growth

AM fungi possess a **coenocytic mycelium**—multinucleate hyphae lacking regular cross-walls (septa)—consistent with the broader Zygomycota lineage. Septa form only with advancing age or in response to wounding, a trait shared with other zygomycetes. The coenocytic condition facilitates rapid cytoplasmic streaming and resource distribution across the hyphal network, which is critical for maintaining the extensive external mycelium that extends into the soil from colonized roots.

## Reproduction

### Clonal Reproduction via Mitospores

Most evidence, including genetic analyses by Rosendahl and Taylor (1997), indicates that AM fungi reproduce primarily through **clonal reproduction via mitospores**. Sexual reproduction has been reported in only one instance: Tommerup and Sivasithamparam (1990) claimed sexual reproduction in *[[gigaspora-decipiens]]*, but this finding has not been broadly corroborated and remains controversial within the field. Despite the absence of confirmed sexual recombination, genetic variation is expected in these asexual organisms as a result of local and regional selection pressures (Morton 1990b, 1999).

### Sporulation Dynamics

New sporulation typically begins **4–8 weeks** after the onset of colonization and plateaus at **12–16 weeks** in greenhouse pot cultures (Morton et al. 1993). Field sporulation is considerably more variable and fluctuates with seasonal conditions and host phenology (Gemma et al. 1989; An et al. 1993). Importantly, sporulation is **not** a response to nutrient deprivation or stress. Instead, spore formation begins only after a threshold of mycorrhizal biomass has been achieved (Gazey et al. 1992; Franke and Morton 1994). A dramatic increase in sporulation occurs after root growth ceases but the root continues to function as a carbon sink, redirecting photosynthate to the fungal partner.

## Distribution and Habitat

AM fungi are extraordinarily widespread. They are found commonly in agricultural and horticultural crops (Abbott and Gazey 1994), though their impacts on crop performance can be highly variable under field conditions (Jeffries and Dodd 1991). Beyond soil habitats, these fungi have been documented in aerial epiphytes (Gemma and Koske 1995) and within vegetative plant debris (Koske and Gemma 1990), demonstrating remarkable ecological versatility.

## Relationships with Host Plants

### The Symbiotic Interface

The most significant practical obstacle in AM fungal research is the **inability to culture these fungi apart from their plant hosts**. The symbiosis is obligate for the fungus. Within the root, the plant provides carbon to the fungus through the arbuscule-plasmalemma interface, while the fungus receives a protected site within root cortical cells.

### Nutrient Exchange

External hyphae extending from the mycorrhizal root dramatically improve **phosphorus (P) acquisition** in low-P soils (Safir 1987; Smith and Gianinazzi-Pearson 1988; Smith and Read 1997). The extensive hyphal network explores a far greater soil volume than roots alone, effectively scavenging poorly mobile phosphate ions. However, when soil P concentrations exceed host requirements, the AM symbiosis becomes **inhibited** and can even shift to a **pathogenic** relationship (Modjo and Hendrix 1986), underscoring the conditional nature of this mutualism.

### Colonization Process

Mycorrhizal colonization is initiated by hyphae emerging from an already-colonized root or from germinating spores. The fungus spreads within the root through hyphal extension and branching. During colonization, two types of specialized structures may develop:

- **Vesicles**: Contain lipids and serve as carbon storage organs within root cells.
- **Auxiliary cells**: More transitory structures whose storage function is tied to specific developmental events.

### Infectivity Differences Between Suborders

Infectivity—the capacity of different fungal propagules to initiate new mycorrhizal associations—varies significantly by suborder (Biermann and Linderman 1983):

| Suborder | Mycorrhizal Roots | Detached Hyphae | Spores | Auxiliary Cells |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Glomineae** | Highly infective | Highly infective | Highly infective | — |
| **Gigasporineae** | Infective | **Not infective** | Infective | **Not infective** |

This distinction has important implications for the spread and persistence of AM fungi in different ecosystems and is a key diagnostic character separating the two lineages.

### Host Specificity and Fungal Communities

Host specificity in AM fungi is **minimal or absent under controlled culture conditions** (Smith and Gianinazzi-Pearson 1988; Brundrett 1991; Smith and Read 1997). The International Culture Collection of [[vam-vesicular-arbuscular-mycorrhizae]] [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] (INVAM) has established more than 200 fungal accessions from single spores, many of which can colonize a broad range of host species. However, fungal communities in nature are **rarely composed of a single species** (Morton 1999); multiple AM fungal taxa typically co-occur within the same root system and rhizosphere.

### Non-Mycorrhizal Plants

Not all plant taxa form AM associations. Non-AM plant orders have been catalogued by Tester et al. (1987). Nonmycorrhizal taxa are generally assumed to have **evolved away from the symbiosis** (Trappe 1987), losing the capacity for mycorrhizal colonization. Chemically induced mycorrhiza-resistant mutants in otherwise mycorrhizal species display activated defense mechanisms (Peterson and Bradbury 1995), suggesting that the symbiosis involves active suppression of plant immune responses. Notably, some plant genera can support both AM and [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] simultaneously, as demonstrated in *Eucalyptus* (Lapeyrie and Chilvers 1985).

## The Mycorrhizosphere

The term **"mycorrhizosphere"** describes the complex zone of microbial interactions in the soil volume surrounding mycorrhizal roots. Rambelli (1973) first documented the dramatic influence that external AM hyphae exert on root-associated and soil microorganisms. These interactions extend far beyond the classic rhizosphere effect, as the extensive hyphal network creates additional surfaces and exudation zones that shape microbial community composition. The mycorrhizosphere has significant effects on the establishment and spread of mycorrhizae (Bagyaraj 1984), with both stimulatory and inhibitory microorganisms influencing fungal colonization success.

## Phylogeny

### Morphological Basis

The phylogenetic classification of Glomales rests primarily on morphological characters of the mycelium and reproductive spores (Morton 1990a; Morton and Benny 1990; Morton and Redecker 2001). Key diagnostic features include spore wall structure, mode of spore formation, hyphal attachment morphology, and the presence or absence of vesicles and auxiliary cells.

### Two Suborders

The order is divided into two suborders (Figure 15.1):

1. **Glomineae** — Characterized by the formation of vesicles within roots and by highly infective propagules (mycorrhizal roots, detached hyphae, and spores). Species in this suborder typically produce spores singly or in clusters, often with complex wall layers.

2. **Gigasporineae** — Distinguished by the formation of auxiliary cells on external hyphae and by the lack of vesicles. A critical difference is that auxiliary cells and detached hyphae are **not infective**, restricting new colonization to intact mycorrhizal roots and germinating spores. Spores are typically borne singly on sporogenous cells.

The phylogenetic separation of these two suborders reflects deep divergence in life-history strategy, with Glomineae species possessing greater propagule versatility and Gigasporineae species relying more heavily on spore-based dispersal.

## Key Research Challenges

Several fundamental questions remain unresolved in AM fungal biology. The obligate biotrophic nature of these fungi—requiring a living host for growth and reproduction—severely limits experimental approaches and has historically hampered efforts to develop axenic cultures. The apparent absence of sexual reproduction in most taxa raises questions about how genetic diversity is generated and maintained across populations, particularly given the evidence for substantial genotypic variation (Morton 1990b, 1999). Additionally, understanding the functional consequences of co-occurring AM fungal communities within individual root systems remains an active area of investigation, with implications for both ecological theory and agricultural management.
