# Mycorrhizal Inoculum Reduction Techniques

Mycorrhizal inoculum reduction techniques are methods used to decrease or eliminate indigenous arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungal populations from soil to establish non-mycorrhizal control treatments. Such controls are essential for evaluating the true contribution of specific AM fungi or inoculants to plant growth and nutrient uptake. The challenge lies in reducing mycorrhizal inoculum without substantially altering soil physical, chemical, or biological properties.

## Why Inoculum Reduction Is Needed

Given the ubiquitous presence of AM fungi in most soils, indigenous soil inoculum must be controlled (reduced or eliminated) to obtain a true non-mycorrhizal control for analysis of AM effects in natural substrates. Without adequate controls, it is impossible to distinguish the effects of introduced AM fungi from those of the native community. The interaction between AM fungi and plant communities depends on fungal species composition, plant community structure, soil conditions, and climatic factors.

### The Problem of Indigenous Inoculum

Indigenous [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] are present in virtually all undisturbed soils, making it extremely difficult to establish truly non-mycorrhizal conditions for experimental purposes. Even small amounts of residual inoculum can lead to significant colonization over the course of an experiment, confounding results. This challenge is particularly acute in field experiments, where complete sterilization is impractical.

### Importance for Experimental Design

The validity of mycorrhizal research depends critically on the quality of non-mycorrhizal controls. Poor inoculum reduction can lead to underestimation of mycorrhizal benefits, as indigenous fungi partially colonize control plants. Conversely, methods that severely alter soil chemistry may create artificial conditions that overestimate inoculant effectiveness. Standardized protocols for inoculum reduction are therefore essential for comparability across studies.

## Soil Solarization

Solarization involves covering soil with clear plastic film to trap solar radiation, typically for 4-6 weeks during the hottest part of the year. The process increases average daily soil temperatures by 6-10 degrees C at 5-20 cm depth. Indigenous AM fungal populations can be reduced to zero after 2-4 weeks, though Glomus intraradices showed tolerance. The effect varies by AM fungal species, inoculum form (hyphae are more sensitive than spores), density, host crop, and duration. Solarization also increases availability of mineral nutrients.

### Limitations of Solarization

The effectiveness of solarization is highly dependent on climate and season, limiting its application in temperate regions or during cooler months. Even in tropical and subtropical areas, soil moisture content, cloud cover, and soil depth influence the temperature achieved. Solarization may not eliminate deep soil propagules, and recolonization from untreated soil layers or adjacent areas can occur rapidly after plastic removal.

## Steam Sterilization

Autoclaving at 121 degrees C at 1.1 atm for 20-30 minutes is widely used to sterilize soil. The method is efficient at eliminating indigenous AM fungi, but may alter soil structure and physicochemical properties. Nutrient release following steam sterilization (particularly NH4+, extractable P, K, Ca, Mg, Cu) can affect growth of non-mycorrhizal control plants. Nitrification is inhibited due to elimination of nitrifying bacteria, while net mineralization of NH4+ increases. A second cycle after 24 hours may be needed for resistant spores.

## Pasteurization

Pasteurization applies moderate heat (typically 60-80 degrees C for 2-4 hours) to soil, resulting in less chemical change than full sterilization. Heating soil to 60 degrees C for 4 hours had no significant effect on soil nutrient concentrations but reduced AM colonization of Plantago lanceolata roots by less than 1%. At 80 degrees C for 2 hours, only extractable NH4+-N and NO3--N increased, with no changes to P, Mg, K, or pH. Arbuscules in roots were reduced to trace numbers.

### Advantages Over Full Sterilization

Pasteurization offers a middle ground between complete sterilization and untreated soil. By preserving some soil microbial communities while eliminating AM fungi, pasteurization may produce control conditions that more closely reflect natural soil biology. This approach is particularly relevant for studies investigating [[mycorrhizal-inoculum-production]] and comparing different inoculant formulations under semi-natural conditions.

## Gamma Irradiation

Gamma irradiation is highly effective as a biocide with relatively minor impacts on soil chemical and physical properties, including little effect on particle size or aggregate stability. An irradiation dose of 3 kGy may be sufficient to eliminate AM fungi infectivity. The method does not induce sample radioactivity, making handling safe. Short-term increases in temperature and nutrient release can occur, including NH4+-N, organic N, Mn, soluble C, exchangeable S and P.

### Practical Considerations

Gamma irradiation requires access to specialized facilities (typically cobalt-60 or cesium-137 sources), which limits its accessibility for many researchers. The method is most practical for small soil volumes used in controlled environment experiments. Cost considerations and regulatory requirements for radioactive source handling further constrain its routine use in mycorrhizal research.

## Chemical Methods

Various gaseous chemicals have been used to fumigate soil for AM fungi elimination. Methyl bromide was extremely effective but is being phased out due to ozone-depleting effects. Ethylene oxide and propylene oxide can increase soil pH and produce growth-hindering residues. Chloroform fumigation can reduce AM colonization by more than 99%. Benomyl (carbendazin) is considered the most appropriate chemical method for field-scale non-mycorrhizal controls, showing no ecologically significant effects on soil pH or nutrient contents.

### Field Applications of Chemical Methods

Benomyl application is particularly useful for establishing non-mycorrhizal plots in [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]] research. Regular application throughout the growing season can maintain low colonization levels while preserving most other soil properties. However, the emergence of benomyl-resistant fungal species and increasing regulatory restrictions on fungicide use have prompted researchers to explore alternative approaches.

## Soil Disturbance

Soil disturbance physically disrupts the hyphal network, which is the main source of inoculum when host plants are present. Hyphal networks are more rapid and efficient at initiating colonization than spores, which are considered long-term propagules. Disruption of the hyphal network can lead to differences in AM colonization of almost 50% in pot trials and 20% under field conditions. Deep ploughing (beyond 15 cm) hinders mycorrhiza formation by reducing propagule density in the rooting zone.

## Crop Rotation as a Management Tool

Cultivation of non-mycorrhizal crops (Chenopodiaceae and Brassicaceae families) in rotation reduces mycorrhizal propagules. Growing non-AM hosts such as sugar beet or canola reduces AM colonization of subsequent crops even without alteration to soil phosphorus availability. Bare-fallow reduces mycorrhizal propagules by approximately 40%, while growing a non-mycorrhizal host reduces them by 13%. This represents a sustainable approach to inoculum management without chemical inputs.

## Comparison of Methods

No single method is ideal for every application. Soil sterilization methods are effective but alter soil properties. Chemical methods may leave toxic residues. Physical methods like soil disturbance and crop rotation are least disruptive but require longer timeframes. The choice depends on experimental design, available facilities, soil volume requiring treatment, and whether the goal is complete elimination or partial reduction of inoculum. Combining methods may achieve better results than any single approach.

### Integrating with Mycorrhizal Research

Selecting the appropriate inoculum reduction method is critical for the validity of [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-techniques]] experiments. Researchers must carefully consider the trade-offs between completeness of inoculum removal and preservation of soil properties. The development of molecular methods for quantifying residual AM fungal propagules has improved the ability to assess the effectiveness of reduction treatments and ensure robust experimental controls.
