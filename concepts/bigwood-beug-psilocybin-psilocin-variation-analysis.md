# Bigwood and Beug Psilocybin and Psilocin Variation Analysis

Bigwood and Beug's 1982 study documenting flush-by-flush variation in psilocybin and psilocin levels in cultivated Psilocybe cubensis, demonstrating that potency varies by a factor of four under controlled conditions and tenfold among street samples. Their finding that psilocin is typically absent in the first flush but reaches maximum by the fourth flush has important implications for dosage predictability and biosynthetic understanding. See also [[gartz-tryptamine-enrichment-psilocybin-biosynthesis]], [[psilocybin-mushroom-potency-comparison-by-species]], [[psilocybin-mushroom-chemistry]], and [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway]].

## Experimental Design

### Cultivation Method

- Strain: Amazonian Psilocybe cubensis from spore print collected near Pucallpa, Peru (Repke et al., 1977)
- Substrate: rye-grain medium (San Antonio, 1971; adapted to "miniculture")
- 10g rye grain + 15ml water in wide-mouth half-pint (~250ml) jars, autoclaved
- Inoculated under sterile conditions with agar mycelium culture
- Shaken every 4 days for 28 days to distribute mycelium
- Cased with 2 parts peat : 1 part calcium carbonate : 2 parts perlite/vermiculite (~2cm deep)
- Watered with 1ml sterile water every 2 days via syringe
- First flush 4-5 weeks after inoculation (~2 weeks after casing)
- Continued fruiting for 20+ weeks; average 2.7g dry weight per miniculture
- Mushrooms freeze-dried, sealed in plastic, stored at -5 C

### Analytical Method

- Reversed-phase HPLC with Hewlett-Packard 8380A reporting integrator
- Calibrated against NIDA standards
- Linear relationship (10% repeatability) between concentration and peak area from 0.2 to 3 mcg total
- Detection limit: ~0.01 mcg psilocybin or psilocin
- Qualitative confirmation by TLC (butanol:acetic acid:water, 12:3:5)

### Key Patterns

**Psilocybin:**
- No consistent upward or downward trend across flushes
- Varied unpredictably from flush to flush
- Generally similar levels in first and last flushes
- Overall variation of approximately 4x under controlled conditions

**Psilocin:**
- Generally absent (zero) in first one or two flushes
- Reached maximum by the fourth flush
- Appeared to start declining after flush 4 (limited data as 5 flushes was typically the maximum)
- MC1 was unusual in having 0.5 mg/g psilocin in flush 1

## Cap vs Stem Distribution

Analysis of three separate strains (first flush only):

Strain      | Cap Psilocybin (mg/g) | Cap Psilocin | Stem Psilocybin | Stem Psilocin
M.R.        | 6.7                    | 0            | 4.2             | 0.35
Equadorian  | 7.6                    | 0            | 4.7             | 0.4
Amazon      | 5.7                    | 0.1          | 5.7             | 0

- Caps generally contained roughly twice as much psilocybin as stems (M.R. and Equadorian)
- The Amazon strain had equal cap/stem psilocybin distribution
- The small amount of psilocin present was entirely in the stems (M.R. and Equadorian)
- Amazon strain had a trace of psilocin in the cap but none in the stem
- This suggests tissue-specific differences in enzyme expression or alkaloid transport

## Street Sample Variability

Five street samples (rye-grown, flush number unknown):

Sample | Psilocybin (mg/g) | Psilocin
1      | 5.6               | 0
2      | 6.2               | 0
3      | 0.7               | 0.3
4      | 0.7               | 0.3
5      | 1.3               | 0.3

- Psilocybin varied over a factor of ten (0.7 to 6.2 mg/g)
- Consistently low psilocin levels (suggesting early-flush harvest or degradation)
- This 10x variability among presumably similar material underscores the unpredictability of wild-market mushroom potency

## Biosynthetic Implications

The flush-dependent psilocin pattern suggests a developmental regulation of the [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway]]:

- Psilocybin biosynthesis appears constitutive (present from flush 1)
- Psilocin accumulation may depend on enzyme maturation or cofactor availability that develops over successive flushes
- Psilocin is the dephosphorylated form of psilocybin; its absence in early flushes could indicate active phosphatase activity is low initially, or that psilocybin production outpaces dephosphorylation
- Alternatively, the pathway may prioritize psilocybin production in early flushes and shift toward psilocin accumulation later

## Dosage Predictability

Bigwood and Beug concluded that users have no way to predict psilocybin and psilocin content from dry weight alone:

- 4x variation under rigidly controlled conditions
- 10x variation among street samples
- Subjective experience variation stems not only from set and setting but from genuine dosage differences
- This finding remains highly relevant to harm reduction and [[lsd-pharmacology]]
- The unpredictable potency underscores the importance of starting with low doses, especially with material from unknown sources or flush numbers
- The factor-of-four variation under controlled conditions suggests that even careful cultivators cannot precisely predict potency from appearance alone
- Repke et al. (1977) traced the Amazon strain used in this study to a spore print from near Pucallpa, Peru, providing geographic provenance for the genetic background

## Connection to Gartz (1989)

Gartz cited Bigwood and Beug as reference 2 and reproduced their general finding of low psilocin in early flushes. His tryptamine enrichment experiments dramatically amplified psilocin production across all flushes, suggesting the pathway is substrate-limited at the tryptamine step under normal conditions. The Bigwood and Beug control data (without tryptamine) served as the baseline against which Gartz's enriched results were compared. Together, these two studies provide the most detailed picture of flush-by-flush alkaloid dynamics in P. cubensis available in the literature, and remain the primary references for understanding potency variation in cultivated psilocybin mushrooms.
