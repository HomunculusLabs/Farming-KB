# Night Interruption Photoperiodism: Plants Measure Darkness, Not Daylight

One of the most surprising discoveries in plant photobiology is that plants do not
measure the length of the day at all — they measure the length of the continuous
period of darkness. This finding, discovered through night-interruption
experiments around the time of World War II, fundamentally changed our
understanding of how plants perceive and respond to seasonal changes in [[photoperiodism-plants-measure-night-length]].

## Practical Applications

The night-interruption technique has been widely adopted in commercial
agriculture and horticulture. By controlling the duration of uninterrupted
darkness, growers can manipulate flowering in photoperiod-sensitive crops. The
most dramatic example is chrysanthemum production, where night-interruption
lighting allows year-round flowering of a species that naturally blooms only in
autumn.

## Night Interruption in Research

The night-interruption technique remains an important experimental tool in plant
biology research. By precisely controlling the timing and duration of light breaks
during the dark period, researchers can dissect the molecular mechanisms of the
photoperiodic response. Experiments have shown that the timing of the light break
within the dark period matters: interruption during the middle of the night is
most effective for altering flowering, while interruption near the beginning or
end of the dark period is less effective. This timing sensitivity maps onto the
circadian regulation of photoperiodic gene expression.

## The Far-Red Reversal

An additional layer of sophistication was added when scientists discovered that
far-red light could reverse the effect of a red light night interruption. If a
red flash is followed immediately by a far-red flash, the night-interruption
effect is cancelled. This reversibility, identical to that seen in phytochrome
experiments, confirmed that phytochrome is the molecular basis for night-length
measurement. It also demonstrated the precision of the plant's light-sensing:
a few seconds of the wrong wavelength at the right time can completely negate
hours of photoperiodic signaling.

## Ecological Significance

In natural environments, the night-interruption phenomenon helps explain how
plants respond to moonlight and lightning. Full moonlight contains some red
wavelengths but at very low intensity. Some plants are sensitive enough to detect
moonlight as a weak signal, though this sensitivity varies by species. Lightning
flashes, which contain broad-spectrum light including red wavelengths, could
theoretically interrupt the night period. However, the extremely brief duration
of most lightning (milliseconds) is insufficient to activate phytochrome
significantly, so plants have evolved to be insensitive to such transient
disturbances.
