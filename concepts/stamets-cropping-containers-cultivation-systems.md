# Stamets Cropping Containers and Cultivation Systems

In Chapter 19 of *Growing Gourmet and Medicinal Mushrooms*, Paul Stamets provides a
comprehensive survey of cropping container systems for mushroom cultivation. The choice
of container depends upon three key variables: the mushroom species being grown, the
cultivator's skill level, and the equipment and facility available. The traditional
Button mushroom (Agaricus) industry developed around tray systems, but many exotic
species require fundamentally different approaches due to their evolutionary adaptations.

## The Species-Container Compatibility Problem

Most Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.) evolved on the vertical surfaces of trees and
readily form eccentrically attached stems. These species perform poorly on the
horizontal trays designed for the Agaricus industry. Because Oyster mushrooms require
healthy exposure to light, the darkened, dense-packed tray system produces unnatural-
looking, trumpet-shaped mushrooms with elongated stems and underdeveloped caps. Many
Oyster strains perform far better in columns, vertical racks, or bags where light
reaches the fruiting surface more evenly and air circulation is improved.

## Tray Culture — The Traditional Method

Tray culture is the traditional method of mushroom cropping, first developed by the
Button mushroom industry. Trays range from small 2 ft. × 3 ft. × 6 in. deep units
(handled by one person) to large 6 ft. × 10 ft. × 12 in. deep units moved by electric
or propane-powered forklifts. Originally constructed of treated or rot-resistant wood,
modern trays use polycarbonate and steel with obvious advantages in durability and
sanitation. Both types are designed to stack without additional structural supports.

Trays allow dense filling of growing rooms (up to 25% of volume). The main advantage
is in handling efficiency — substrate mass-filling, transporting, cropping, and dumping
are all streamlined. The Dutch currently use tray culture for Button, Shiitake, Oyster,
and other species simultaneously. Tray culture easily accepts a casing layer (peat moss
buffered with calcium carbonate), which is essential for Button mushroom production.

### The Brooke-Webster Technique

In North America, tray culture for Oyster mushrooms was perfected by Davel Brooke-
Webster (1987). This method uses perforated plastic covering over the surface of trays.
Holes (1-2 inches diameter) are punched evenly through an 8-foot roll of plastic before
application. The plastic sheeting is stretched over trays directly after inoculation of
Oyster spawn into pasteurized wheat straw. The plastic barrier prevents approximately
98% of evaporation while allowing fruiting at designated hole sites.

The key to success lies with strains that form bouquets of mushrooms site-specifically
at the holes. Ideally, primordial clusters hosting multiple mushrooms form at each
locus. An advantage is that bouquets of equal weight are produced simultaneously on the
same trays widely used by the Button mushroom industry. A disadvantage is that equal
light exposure across tightly stacked trays is difficult to achieve. When lighting is
insufficient, stems elongate while caps remain underdeveloped.

## Vertical Wall Culture

In the progression of cultivation techniques, the Agaricus tray was modified for growing
Oyster mushrooms by turning it vertically so mushrooms could fruit from both faces.
Vertical surfaces are typically screened with tight wire or plastic mesh. Perforated
plastic positioned between substrate and wire mesh allows mushroom formation and
development while retaining moisture. A plastic curtain can envelope the container until
fruiting time.

Racks with a breadth of 12-16 inches support full flushes without becoming anaerobic
at the core — a problem seen when racks exceed 20 inches in breadth. Individual 4 ft. ×
4 ft. to 4 ft. × 8 ft. rack frames can be stacked to construct continuous mushroom
walls, with one side hinged for easy filling.

## Slanted Wall or "A-Frame" Culture

Slanted walls are constructed by stacking bags of inoculated substrate to build sloped
faces. The advantage is higher density of fill within a given growing room space and
easier harvesting. The disadvantage is that mushrooms form on only one plane — the
outwardly exposed surface. In comparison, blocks spaced apart give rise to fruitings on
five planes (four sides plus the top), especially with Shiitake and Oyster mushrooms.

Many cultivators find that walls composed of individually wrapped blocks limit cross-
contamination from infected units. Isolation of blocks has distinct advantages in both
yield enhancement and contamination containment.

## Bag Culture — The Versatile Standard

Plastic bags have become the logical choice for inexpensive, portable, and disposable
containers. High temperature-tolerant polypropylene bags are used for wood-based
substrates requiring sterilization. After cooling and inoculation, sterilized substrates
are filled directly into heat-sensitive polyethylene bags. Mushrooms fruit from the top
or sides through punched holes.

For wood decomposers, a biodegradable, heat-tolerant, and breathable plastic is sorely
needed. "Cellophane," a wood cellulose-based material used in the early 1900s, has some
desired features (heat tolerance, gas porosity) but lacks durability. Despite this
shortfall, one appealing aspect is that mushroom mycelium digests cellophane during use.

### Bag Culture Technique

Opaque black bags encourage photosensitive strains to form only where holes allow light
exposure. Clear bags stimulate maximum primordia populations but often cause formation
all over the surface. Many cultivators overcome this by using select Oyster strains
that localize primordial clusters exactly at puncture sites. By punching dozens of
holes with stainless steel four-bladed arrowheads, large bouquets of mushrooms are
encouraged. The enlarging mushrooms push the plastic flaps open naturally.

For the Button mushroom industry, bag culture remains standard: inoculated compost is
filled into bags topped with a casing layer. Individual bags are grouped on horizontal
shelves, and by preventing contact between mycelium and wooden shelves, contamination
from wood-loving Trichoderma and Botrytis molds is minimized.

## Column Culture — Natural-Looking Oyster Production

Growing Oyster mushrooms in columns produces natural-looking fruitbodies. Having evolved
on vertical tree surfaces, Oyster mushrooms with their off-centered stems grow out
horizontally at first, then turn and grow upright at maturity (negative geotropism).
This produces highly desirable clusters or "bouquets" of Oyster mushrooms.

Advantages of column culture include: many young mushrooms form from a common site,
allowing 1/4 to 1 lb. clusters to be picked without trimming; yields of succulent
young mushrooms are maximized while spore load is minimized; harvesting is far faster
than picking individually; and clusters store better under refrigeration than single
mushrooms. Of the many variations, one popular method uses perforated plastic sleeves
stuffed with pasteurized straw, hung vertically from overhead supports.

## Bottle Culture

Bottle culture is particularly well-suited for species that benefit from a controlled
microenvironment at the substrate surface. Originally developed in Asia for Enoki
(Flammulina velutipes) cultivation, bottle culture allows precise environmental control
over each individual unit. The narrow neck of the bottle creates a high-CO2 environment
that promotes the characteristic long-stemmed morphology prized in Enoki mushrooms.
After colonization, the neck is opened to introduce fresh air, triggering fruiting.

Bottle culture is labor-intensive for substrate filling and cleaning but offers superior
contamination control and uniform product quality. It is most economically viable for
high-value species where premium presentation justifies the additional handling cost.
