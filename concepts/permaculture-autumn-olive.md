Autumn Olive (Elaeagnus umbellata) is a hardy nitrogen-fixing small tree producing small, nutritious fruit. Despite its versatility, it is considered invasive in parts of North America. It fits well within [[permaculture-pioneer-species]] strategies and [[permaculture-tree-crop-systems]] as a nitrogen-fixing support tree.

## Botanical Description

Autumn Olive is a deciduous shrub or small tree typically growing 10-20 feet (3-6 m) tall with a similar spread. It has silvery-green foliage and produces abundant small, red-silver berries in autumn. The plant can live 30-50 years and forms an extensive root system with nitrogen-fixing nodules from symbiotic actinorhizal bacteria.

## Uses

- Bare soil erosion control

- Edible fruit (fresh, dried, or jam)

- Bee attraction (good forage)

- Windbreak

- Leguminous nitrogen fixation — improves soil

- Wildlife habitat and food source

- [[salt-tolerant-plant-guide]] — useful in coastal and roadside plantings

## Growing Conditions

- Grows in most soil conditions including poor, sandy, and gravelly soils

- Hardy and drought tolerant once established

- USDA zones 3-8

- Tolerates soil pH from 4.5 to 7.5

- Full sun to partial shade

- Small tree form, reaching 10-20 feet tall

## Propagation

Autumn Olive is most easily propagated from seed, which requires cold stratification for 60-90 days. Softwood cuttings taken in summer can also root successfully. The plant spreads readily by bird-dispersed seed, which contributes to its invasive reputation. Germination rates are typically high once stratified.

## Harvesting

The small, nutritious fruit can be eaten fresh, dried, or turned into jam. The red-silver berries are high in lycopene — one of the highest concentrations found in any fruit, containing up to 17 times more lycopene than tomatoes. Berries ripen from September through October and can be harvested by shaking branches over a tarp. Yield per mature bush can reach 15-25 pounds of fruit.

## Nutrition and Culinary Uses

The fruit is tart-sweet with a pleasant flavor when fully ripe. It makes excellent jam, fruit leather, and sauces. The high lycopene content makes it nutritionally significant as an antioxidant source. Dried berries can be stored for winter use. Some cultivars have been selected for larger, sweeter fruit.

## Companion Planting and Guild Role

As a [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]], Autumn Olive serves as a support tree in fruit tree guilds. It provides dappled shade for understory plants, fixes nitrogen for neighboring trees, and attracts pollinators. In a [[backyard-orchard-design]], it can be planted on the north side of fruit trees to provide wind protection and nitrogen.

## Pruning and Maintenance

Prune in late winter to maintain shape and remove dead wood. The plant responds well to coppicing and can be cut back to the ground to regenerate. Regular pruning helps keep the plant manageable and encourages new, productive growth.

## Caution: Invasive Potential

In North America, Autumn Olive is often considered an invasive plant or weed. Once established, it continues to grow back from roots and can displace other species. It should only be grown in places where it already grows naturally, chiefly in Eastern North America. Always check local regulations before planting.

## Pests and Diseases

Autumn Olive is remarkably pest-resistant and disease-free. No significant insect pests or diseases affect it in North America, which partly explains its invasive success. Birds are the primary dispersal agents.

## Varieties and Selection

Several improved cultivars have been developed for larger and sweeter fruit, including 'Cardinal', 'Ruby', and 'Brilliant Rose'. These selected varieties produce berries up to twice the size of wild types and are worth seeking out for food production. Goumi (Elaeagnus multiflora) is a related species with similar traits but less invasive potential.

## Role in [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]]

In food forest design, Autumn Olive serves as a nurse tree and nitrogen fixer in the canopy or understory layers. Its ability to thrive on degraded soils makes it valuable for site rehabilitation. In swale plantings alongside [[faires-swales-and-water-conservation]], it helps stabilize banks while providing food and forage.
