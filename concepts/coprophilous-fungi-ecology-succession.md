## Environmental Factors

The presence of an essential nutrient is more important than external environmental factors in determining fungal community composition (Wicklow 1981). Key environmental influences include:

- **Temperature**: Wicklow and Moore (1974) demonstrated significant effects on fungal succession. Certain species dominate under colder early spring conditions while others replace them in warmer months.

- **Light**: Some taxa are positively phototropic (e.g., Ascobolus species and some Podospora species; Morinaga et al. 1980).

- **Moisture**: Self-evident influence, with Kuthubutheen and Webster (1986) and Wicklow (1992) documenting its effects on community composition.

## Nutrient Cycling Role

Coprophilous fungi are very important in ecosystems because they break down substrata and recycle micronutrients. The rapid germination and growth of coprophilous fungi is advantageous for colonization of fresh dung (Harper and Webster 1964), and speed of germination and growth rates directly influence competitive success and thus fungal succession.

## Arthropod Interactions

Arthropods and worms contribute to the fragmentation process of dung and thus influence the mycobiota. The role of insects in dispersal has often been overlooked (Stevenson and Dindal 1987). Mites also play a significant role, ingesting and then disseminating certain myxomycete spores (Keller and Smith 1978). The interplay between fungal and arthropod communities on dung represents a complex food web that shapes the ultimate composition of the coprophilous fungal community.

## Spore Discharge and Dispersal

Many coprophilous ascomycetes have evolved remarkably efficient spore discharge mechanisms. Spores are forcibly ejected from asci and must adhere to nearby vegetation where they will later be consumed by herbivores. Species like Sphaerobolus stellatus (the artillery fungus) have taken this further, launching spore masses several meters to escape the dung substrate entirely — though this adaptation sometimes misfires, leading to spores adhering to buildings and vehicles.

## Connections to Broader Fungal Succession

Dung succession is one of several well-studied models of [[fungal-chitin-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]]. What makes dung succession particularly instructive is the well-defined temporal sequence and the clear relationship between substrate chemistry and community assembly.

Frankland (1998) noted that while the traditional hypothesis explains succession through sequential substrate utilization, competitive interactions and other factors also play significant roles. ## Key Points

- Dung-inhabiting fungi play crucial roles in nutrient cycling and decomposition

- Coprophilous species show remarkable specialization to their substrate

- These fungi are often among the first colonizers of fresh dung deposits

- Many coprophilous species have complex life cycles involving multiple spore types

- Their study reveals important patterns in fungal ecology and succession

- Competition, antagonism, and chemical ecology shape community assembly on dung
