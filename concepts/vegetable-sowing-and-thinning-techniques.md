## Vegetable Sowing and Thinning Techniques

Proper sowing and thinning technique is one of the most underappreciated skills in vegetable gardening. Most garden instruction books provide spacing recommendations but skip the practical details of how to actually achieve that spacing from seed. The method of progressive thinning described here is essential for getting the most vigorous, productive plants from every sowing.

## Sowing Depth Guide

Seed size determines sowing depth. Getting this right is fundamental:

| Seed Size | Examples | Depth | Notes |
|-----------|----------|-------|-------|
| Tiny | Celery, basil, sorrel, most herbs | Barely covered | Scatter into soil crevices, press gently, restore capillarity |
| Small | Brassicas, carrot, parsley, fennel | ~1/2 inch (1.25 cm) | Standard row sowing |
| Medium-small | Spinach, beet, chard, radish, oxalis | ~3/4 inch (2 cm) | Slightly deeper |
| Large | Peas, beans, corn, cucurbits | ~1 inch (2.5 cm) | Can go deeper in sandy soil |

### The Capillarity Principle

After sowing tiny seeds into tiny cracks and crevices of newly raked soil, press the earth down gently — either with your hand or the back of a spade, much as you would roll a large area like a lawn. This essential step **restores capillarity**, ensuring moisture can rise from below to reach the seeds.

### Tiny Seeds Indoors

Fine seeds should only be directly seeded outdoors in mild temperatures, or the rows must be shaded temporarily until they sprout.

## Progressive Thinning Method

This is one of the most important techniques in the book. The concept of "thin to X inches" needs to be understood as a progressive process, not a single action.

### Why Progressive Thinning?

In the home garden, seed distribution is never uniform. Clumps of seedlings inevitably emerge in some places while gaps appear in others. Simply thinning to final spacing immediately would leave you with too few plants and an uneven stand.

### The Step-by-Step Process

**Example**: Growing looseleaf lettuce that needs to be 12 inches (30 cm) apart, sown in drills.

#### Step 1: Immediate Post-Emergence Thinning
- Right after emergence, thin dense seedling clusters so survivors don"t quite touch
- This eliminates severe competition immediately

#### Step 2: Light Competition Thinning
- When initial thin density starts to compete for light (seedlings lean away from each other)
- Thin again so they don't quite touch
- About a week after Step 1

#### Step 3: Second Spacing Thinning
- A week or ten days later, when they're bumping again
- Take out every other plant
- At this point they're about 3 inches (8 cm) apart

#### Step 4: Salad Thinning
- When these 3-inch plants are touching, cut off every other one
- These thinnings are salad material — nothing wasted

#### Step 5: Final Spacing
- The survivors are now properly spaced to reach maturity
- ~12 inches (30 cm) apart in this example

### The Benefits

1. **Maximum survival**: Multiple thinning rounds ensure you always have enough plants, even if some are lost to pests, disease, or weather
2. **No waste**: Every thinning provides edible material
3. **Even spacing**: Progressive thinning naturally corrects for uneven germination
4. **Strongest plants survive**: Each thinning round removes the weakest, leaving the most vigorous
5. **Insurance**: By the time you reach final spacing, you've accounted for all losses

## Key Terminology

### "In Clusters"
Means a few seeds are sown in a cluster. Used in raised beds or wide raised rows. The clusters are at fixed positions (e.g., 18 × 50 cm), and each cluster is later thinned to one or two plants.

### "In Drills"
Seeds set in the bottom of a shallow furrow. Sometimes seeds are sown in hills (mounded soil) within drills to be sown in highly fertile conditions.

### "In Hills"
Individual mounds of soil, often enriched with extra fertilizer, where 3-6 seeds are sown and later thinned to one plant.

## Fertility and Spacing

### The Three Levels

The book describes three levels of soil fertility:
- **Low-demand vegetables**: Grow adequately in minimally fertile soil
- **Medium-demand vegetables**: Need moderate fertility with [[compost]] or COF
- **High-demand vegetables**: Require the most fertile soil possible

### Don't Forget

These are **minimums** — all vegetables grow much better when given soil more fertile than the bare minimum.

### Fertility Affects Spacing

Higher soil fertility can sometimes allow slightly tighter spacing because each plant has more nutrients available. However, exceeding recommended spacing usually causes more problems (poor air circulation, disease, competition for light) than it solves.

## General Sowing Tips

1. **Don't rush the season**: Sowing too early is the single biggest cause of trouble. Two weeks later often means the difference between struggle and success
2. **Sow more than you need**: Plant for losses — thinning is easier than resowing
3. **Water after sowing**: Unless rain is imminent, water thoroughly after sowing to ensure good seed-to-soil contact
4. **Mark your rows**: Use a string line or stake to ensure straight rows — this makes weeding much easier
5. **Label everything**: Especially important when sowing multiple varieties on different dates
6. **Keep records**: Note what worked and what didn't — your garden is unique

## Transplanting

When using transplants rather than direct sowing:

- **Harden off**: Gradually expose greenhouse-grown seedlings to outdoor conditions over 7-10 days
- **Don't plant too deep or too shallow**: Follow species-specific recommendations
- **Water immediately after transplanting**: Ensures good root-to-soil contact
- **Protect from wind and sun** for the first few days after setting out
- **Consider row covers**: For early plantings, row covers provide both frost protection and pest exclusion
