# Mescalero Apache Shaman Personality and Psychopathology

## Overview

The study of Mescalero Apache shaman personality represents one of the most detailed psychoanalytic investigations of shamanism in anthropological literature. Conducted primarily by L. B. Boyer and colleagues during the mid-20th century, this research used Rorschach projective tests and clinical psychoanalytic interviews to distinguish between genuine shamans, pseudoshamans, and the general Apache population on the Mescalero Indian Reservation in New Mexico.

## Shaman vs. Pseudoshaman Personality Profiles

Boyer, Klopfer, Brawer, and Kawai (1964) conducted comparative Rorschach studies that revealed striking personality differences between genuine shamans and pseudoshamans among the Mescalero Apache:

### Genuine Shamans

Genuine shamans displayed personality configurations characterized by:

- **Creative characteristics** and a high degree of reality testing potential
- **Capacity to regress in the service of the ego** (in the Kris sense) — meaning they could access altered states of consciousness while maintaining the ability to function effectively upon return
- **Hysterical signs** present, but combined with keener awareness of peculiarities and more selective theoretical interest
- When viewed through Devereux's framework of the "ideal psychological normal," shamans more nearly approached normality than their culture mates
- Ability to use imposture convincingly — a crucial social skill in shamanic performance

### Pseudoshamans

Pseudoshamans, by contrast, showed markedly different profiles:

- They were **not hysterical** in the clinical sense
- Had **variable degrees of reality testing potential**
- Possessed **impoverished personalities** overall
- Were generally scorned by the Apache community
- Could not use imposture convincingly (as noted by Klopfer's indirect data)
- Employed solely by the most suggestible individuals in the community
- Both pseudoshamans who used peyote confessed while intoxicated that they doubted their own claims of supernatural power and consciously sought to deceive others

## Devereux's Framework of Psychiatric Anthropology

George Devereux (1956) proposed that shamans must be considered seriously neurotic or psychotic when compared with the hypothetical psychological normal. This stance has been frequently misunderstood. The key insight is not that shamans are broken individuals, but that the standards of "normality" themselves are culturally constructed. Boyer's position has been similarly mischaracterized — Handelman (1968), for instance, incorrectly inferred that Boyer considered shamans to be autocultural deviants.

Boyer (1969) later reviewed the broader cross-cultural literature on shamanism and concluded that shamans worldwide share personality configurations similar to those exhibited by the Apache practitioners he studied.

## The Peyote Ban Among the Mescalero

Historical and modern data provide partial answers to why the Mescalero Apache abandoned the use of peyote (*Lophophora williamsii*) in shamanistic rituals and today forbid its use entirely.

### Psychoanalytic Explanation

Apache child-rearing practices engendered considerable hostility. Aggression was — and is — addressed institutionally toward outsiders, witches, ghosts, and cultural bogies, in an attempt to produce individual repression of hostile impulses originally directed toward familial and societal members. This effort was more effective aboriginally but was never strikingly successful.

The use of peyote in camps introduced a **foreign element** into Apache shamanistic procedures: the simultaneous assumption of authority by more than one practitioner. Each shaman vied for supremacy of power and status. The physiopsychological effects of the hallucinogen reduced the efficacy of their repression of hostilities from socialization experiences. Drug-induced regression resulted in releasing aggression in its earlier, childish form — directly toward parent and sibling surrogates. Bloodshed and feuds occurred, leading the Apache to wisely ban the peyote camps.

### Projection of Evil Onto Peyote

The ascription of the quality of evil to peyote (power) represented a **basic deviation** from the Apache conceptualization of power without intrinsic properties of good or evil. This was intended to deny the presence of intragroup hostility. Peyote was thereafter proscribed for shamans and employed by possessors of supernatural power solely in witchcraft rituals, alongside owl power and love magic practices.

Notably, only peyote and owl power have been considered evil in themselves among the Mescalero, Chiricahuas, and Lipans. In each instance, murderous wishes are projected onto the power in question.

### Dual Fears

The Mescalero Apache fear the use of peyote for two stated reasons:

1. **It has an evil power** which will drive them to do evil — intrapersonal asocial tendencies are projected onto the peyote. Sexual transgressions arouse little overt anxiety among these Apaches except when intergenerational incest has occurred, but they fear their poorly controlled aggressive impulses.
2. **It causes hallucinations** — reducing their capacity to perceive and judge external reality accurately. The Apaches may displace their fear of loss of control over destructive urges onto fear of loss of control of perceptual accuracy.

## Exceptions: Illicit Peyote Users

### Powerful Shamans Who Defied the Ban

Two shamans continued to use peyote in illicit practices despite the prohibition:

- **Black Eyes**: Intoxicated, he frequently bragged that he was a witch and once flaunted peyote buttons before the psychoanalytic author.
- **Ancient One**: Said to have killed many individuals, both tribal enemies and Apaches, sometimes by means that appeared to require supernatural intervention. His own children hesitated to whisper their conviction that he was a witch.

These men likely deemed themselves so strong that they were above social sanctions, continuing to use peyote to demonstrate contempt for fellow Mescaleros and for material purposes — demanding greater recompense and commanding greater respect by performing rituals conceptualized as illegitimate.

### Alcohol vs. Peyote

A significant question arises: why was alcohol use socially permissible while peyote was proscribed, given that alcohol also produces hallucinosis and violent behavior? The answer appears to lie in the **cultural context** of use. Peyote was incorporated into the shamanistic ritual complex from the time of its introduction, where group situations fostered conflict centering on the varying powers controlled by particular individuals. Aggression released during drinking parties, by contrast, was channeled outside the personally mediated world of the supernatural. Alcohol consumption was never culturally acceptable in ceremonial contexts.

## Cross-Cultural Implications

Boyer's research demonstrated that the Apache findings align with shamanic personality profiles across cultures. The capacity for controlled regression, creative expression, and effective reality testing — rather than psychopathology per se — appear to be the distinguishing features of genuine shamanic practitioners. The Mescalero case also illustrates how cultural belief systems and social dynamics can transform the meaning and acceptability of psychoactive substances within a society, turning a once-sacred plant into an object of fear and prohibition.
