# Ergot Fungi and Ergotism in Human History

Ergot fungi, primarily [[escovopsis-parasitic-fungi-attine-ant-gardens]] that infect
the grains of grasses and cereals, particularly rye. The disease they cause,
ergotism, has been one of the most devastating afflictions in human history,
responsible for countless deaths and episodes of mass hysteria. The dark
sclerotia (hard, compact fungal masses) that replace grain kernels in
infected ears of rye contain a complex mixture of potent alkaloids that
affect the nervous system, circulatory system, and uterus.

## The Fungus and Its Life Cycle

Claviceps purpurea infects grasses when its spores land on the stigmas of
open flowers. The fungus germinates and grows into the developing ovary,
replacing the grain with a dark, hardened mass called a sclerotium. This
sclerotium, known as ergot, falls to the ground at harvest and overwinters.
In spring, the sclerotium germinates to produce stalked structures that
release ascospores, initiating new infections on the next season's crop.

The entire life cycle is tightly synchronized with the development of the
host plant. Infection occurs only during the brief window when the grass
flowers are open and receptive. This specificity means that ergot is most
prevalent in years when cool, wet weather coincides with the flowering
period of the host crop.

## Ergot Alkaloids

Ergot sclerotia contain a complex mixture of alkaloid compounds, including
ergotamine, ergocristine, ergocryptine, and ergometrine. These compounds
are derivatives of [[lsd-d-lysergic-acid-diethylamide]] (LSD), one of the most potent [[bloomfield-salem-witch-trials-ergot-poisoning-caporael-hypothesis]],
including hallucinations, convulsions, and bizarre behavior, may have been
caused by ergot-contaminated rye. This hypothesis remains debated but
illustrates the profound social consequences that ergotism could produce.
