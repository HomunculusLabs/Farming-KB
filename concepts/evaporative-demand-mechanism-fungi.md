## Evaporative Demand Mechanism in Fungi

Evaporative demand—the rate at which water evaporates from a surface—is a central concept in understanding how fungi orient their growth. In mushrooms, differential evaporative demand across the surface of the stipe (stem) is the leading proposed mechanism for anemotropic (wind-directed) and phototropic (light-directed) growth responses. This concept unifies multiple tropic responses under a single physical framework.

- **More humid side**: Cells on the side with lower evaporative demand retain more water, allowing greater wall loosening and cell elongation.
- **Net curvature**: The side with greater cell elongation determines the direction of bending. The stipe curves toward the side with higher evaporative demand (the drier side).

This mechanism can explain both anemotropism (bending toward wind) and certain aspects of phototropism (bending toward illuminated surfaces), since both wind and light increase evaporative demand.

## The Four Factors of Evaporative Demand

Badham (1982) identified evaporative demand as a function of at least four interacting [[windward-leeward-boundary-layer-redistribution-stipe-curvature-badham-1982]] around the mushroom stipe:

- In still air, the boundary layer (a thin film of relatively still, humid air) is symmetric around the stipe.
- When wind blows across the stipe, the boundary layer becomes thinner on the windward side and thicker on the leeward side.
- The thinner boundary layer on the windward side means water vapor is carried away more efficiently, increasing evaporative demand.
- The thicker boundary layer on the leeward side traps water vapor near the surface, reducing evaporative demand.
- This asymmetry drives differential cell expansion and stipe curvature toward the wind.

The relationship between wind speed and evaporative demand is nonlinear. At very low wind speeds, the effect is minimal. At moderate speeds (such as the 9 ± 3 cm/sec used in Badham's wind tunnel experiments), the boundary layer effect is pronounced. At very high wind speeds, mechanical forces may dominate over evaporative effects.

### 2. Humidity

Ambient humidity directly modulates the evaporative demand gradient:

- At high humidity (near saturation, such as the 94 ± 3% RH in Badham's experiments), the absolute evaporative demand is reduced but the differential between windward and leeward sides persists.
- At low humidity, the overall evaporative demand increases dramatically, potentially accelerating the rate of curvature.
- Very high humidity (near 100%) would minimize the differential, potentially reducing or eliminating the tropic response.
- Humidity interacts with wind: in still, humid air, there is no significant evaporative demand differential and no curvature. In moving, dry air, the differential is maximized.

### 3. Light

Light influences evaporative demand through two distinct mechanisms:

- **Thermal effect**: Light heats the illuminated surface of the mushroom, increasing the temperature and therefore the [[gadd-spitzenkorper-vesicle-supply-centre-hyphal-tip-growth-direction]]. This effect is separate from the physical evaporative mechanism.
