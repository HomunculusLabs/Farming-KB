## Advantages of Fungi Over Bacteria for Pesticide Bioremediation

Fungi offer several advantages for pesticide mycoremediation-pesticides|bioremediation (Table 8.1 from source):

- Use inexpensive lignocellulosic material as nutrient source (unavailable to bacteria), giving competitive advantage
- Tolerant of relatively high pollutant concentrations through extracellular degradation
- Mycelial growth maximizes physical/mechanical and enzymic contact with the environment
- Able to survive with multiple different xenobiotics simultaneously
- Degrade chemical mixtures through non-specific free-radical-based mechanisms
- No preconditioning or enrichment required for a particular pollutant
- Wide tolerance of environmental conditions (oxygen, temperature, pH, moisture)
- Degradation rate proportional to pollutant concentration (solubility less important)

## Organochlorine Insecticides

### DDT
Phanerochaete chrysosporium degrades DDT (1,1,1-trichloro-2,2-bis(4-chlorophenyl)ethane) under nitrogen-limited conditions. Bumpus and Aust (1987) demonstrated this degradation, and subsequent work showed that P. chrysosporium also degrades DDE (the persistent DDT metabolite). Trichoderma koningii was shown to degrade dieldrin to carbon dioxide.

### Lindane and Endosulfan
White rot fungi have demonstrated ability to transform the organochlorine insecticides lindane (gamma-HCH) and endosulfan, though degradation pathways are less well characterized than for DDT.

## Organophosphorus Insecticides

### Fenitrothion
Trichoderma viride can use fenitrothion as an energy source, degrading it through initial cleavage of the P-O-aryl bond. The enzyme involved is an arylphosphatase.

### Parathion and Chlorpyrifos
Both pure and mixed cultures of soilborne fungi can biodegrade chlorpyrifos. Gliocladium virens shows lack of sensitivity toward several organophosphates and is both ecologically competent and an excellent biocontrol agent, making it a unique candidate for bioremediation.

## Synthetic Pyrethroids

Pyrethroids show varying persistence in soil depending on structure. Permethrin is among the synthetic pyrethroids that can be degraded by fungal action, though specific pathways vary by compound.

## Chlorophenoxy Herbicides

### 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T
P. chrysosporium degrades 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) and 2,4,5-trichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4,5-T) through peroxidase-catalyzed reactions. Methylation of intermediate quinones appears to serve as a mechanism for regenerating substrates susceptible to further peroxidase attack.

## Triazine Herbicides

### Atrazine and Simazine
- Pure cultures of Aspergillus, Fusarium, Penicillium, Rhizopus, and Trichoderma can use atrazine and simazine as sole carbon or nitrogen sources
- Common degradative mechanisms include dealkylation, deamination, and hydrolytic dechlorination
- P. chrysosporium biotransforms atrazine to hydroxylated and N-dealkylated metabolites
- Nine mycorrhizal fungi demonstrated atrazine degradation through incorporation of herbicide carbon into fungal tissue
- Approximately 30% of atrazine was immobilized as bound residue in P. chrysosporium soil treatments

## Phenylurea Herbicides

### Isoproturon, Diuron, Chlortoluron
Many micromycete strains degrade phenylurea herbicides. Bjerkandera adusta and Oxyosporus sp. proved the most effective degraders. The white rot fungus that degrades linuron also showed broad substrate specificity for phenylureas.

## Polychlorinated Biphenyls

### PCB Degradation
White rot fungi degrade PCBs through their ligninolytic system, preferentially attacking lesser-chlorinated congeners:
- P. chrysosporium degrades Aroclor 1242, 1254, and 1260
- T. versicolor benefits from low Triton X-100 surfactant concentrations that increase bioavailability
- 14 of 21 tested ectomycorrhizal fungi degraded PCBs, with effectiveness comparable to or exceeding P. chrysosporium
- Mixed populations of white rot fungi with methylotrophic yeasts showed substantial PCB degradation

## Dioxins and Furans

P. sordida and P. chrysosporium degraded mixtures of chlorinated PCDDs and PCDFs with no clear structural dependence, suggesting a free-radical process. Cunninghamella sp. oxidized and hydroxylated dibenzo-p-dioxin and dibenzofuran through P450 monooxygenases.

## Genetic Engineering Prospects

Catabolic genes responsible for degrading several pesticides have been identified, isolated, and cloned into fungi. Gliocladium virens has an effective genetic transformation system for introducing foreign genes. Optimization of degradative gene expression in organisms like P. chrysosporium holds promise but remains largely unrealized.

# Trichothecene Mycotoxins and Biological Warfare

Trichothecene mycotoxins are a class of fungal poisons that have attracted serious attention as potential biological weapons. Unlike most fungal toxins, whose effects develop too slowly for military use, trichothecenes can act immediately upon contact, causing burning, blistering, and tissue damage that can be rapidly fatal.

## Chemistry and Effects

Trichothecenes are produced by several genera of fungi, most notably [[mycological-terms-glossary]] and various Fusarium species. They are organic compounds classified as sesquiterpenes, with a characteristic 12,13-epoxytrichothec-9-ene ring structure.

The trichothecene family includes more than 150 related compounds, classified into four groups (A through D) based on their chemical substituents. Group A trichothecenes, including T-2 toxin, are generally more toxic than Group B compounds like deoxynivalenol (vomitoxin). This structural diversity has implications for both their natural occurrence and their potential as weapons.

When dispersed as an aerosol and inhaled, trichothecenes cause a severe form of the symptoms associated with black mold spore exposure: coughing, breathing difficulties, and a burning sensation in the lungs. Contact with eyes causes tearing and blurring of vision.

Ingestion produces nausea and repeated vomiting, followed by bloody diarrhea. The most potent trichothecene mycotoxins, including T-2 toxin and diacetoxyscirpenol, are lethal at doses of just a few milligrams. Lower doses do not produce immediately obvious effects, but within a few days, patients become anemic, platelets become scarce, and plunging white blood cell counts indicate immune system collapse.

There are no specific antidotes for trichothecene poisoning. Soap and water will remove mycotoxins from skin, but only if used immediately after exposure. Trichothecenes are among the biological and chemical warfare agents that can be treated using the special decontamination kits issued to U.S. military personnel.

The 12,13-epoxy ring is essential for toxicity, and the presence of this ring across all trichothecene variants explains the similarity of their biological effects despite structural differences elsewhere in the molecule.

## Yellow Rain Allegations

Reputable investigators have concluded that trichothecenes were used by the Soviet Union to kill thousands of anticommunist guerrillas in Laos and Cambodia in the 1970s, and against members of the opposition to their invasion of Afghanistan in the 1980s.

A yellow crystalline deposit appears when trichothecenes are purified from fungal cultures, which may account for the color of the putative weapon dubbed "yellow rain." Despite compelling testimony from some victims, a number of investigations have concluded that droplets of yellow rain did not originate from aircraft but were deposited by swarms of defecating bees.

The grounds for favoring trichothecene delivery in Afghanistan during the 1980s are similarly murky, although more than 3,000 deaths in the region have been linked to an unidentified chemical or biological weapon. The debate over yellow rain remains one of the most contentious episodes in the history of biological weapons investigation.

The controversy illustrates the difficulty of attributing biological effects to specific agents in conflict zones where multiple environmental hazards coexist. Bee feces and trichothecene-contaminated aerosols produce superficially similar deposits, making forensic analysis exceptionally challenging.

## Gulf War Connections

Trichothecenes have been considered as a possible cause of some of the devastating illnesses contracted by American servicemen who served in the Persian Gulf in 1991. Declassified documents from the U.S. Department of Defense report that Iraqi scientists were working with mycotoxins in the 1980s, establishing the capacity to stockpile these compounds.

The production of mycotoxins is an incomparably simpler project than developing nuclear munitions. The techniques are well within the grasp of anyone with a Ph.D. in microbiology or mycology, provided appropriate strains have been acquired and a modern laboratory is available.

Western intelligence agencies are familiar with some biological warfare specialists in the Middle East because they earned degrees from universities in Europe and the United States. This dissemination of expertise represents a significant proliferation concern.

## Delivery Methods

The obvious delivery method would involve aircraft carrying tanks of purified toxins. Even something as primitive as a crop-duster could be frighteningly effective. Trichothecenes cause burning and blistering of the skin, with damage sufficiently extensive that whole patches of tissue are shed before the agonized victim dies.

For inhalation exposure, the effects are rapid and devastating. The combination of ease of production, stability in storage, and rapid onset of symptoms makes trichothecenes a continuing concern for biodefense planning.

The stability of trichothecenes under various environmental conditions enhances their potential as weapons. Unlike many biological agents that degrade quickly when exposed to sunlight or heat, trichothecenes are relatively stable chemicals that can persist in the environment for extended periods.

## Natural Occurrence

In nature, trichothecenes are produced as secondary metabolites by fungi competing with other microorganisms. Their production in water-damaged buildings by Stachybotrys chartarum causes the indoor air quality problems that have generated widespread public concern.

Trichothecene contamination of agricultural crops is a significant global problem. Fusarium species produce deoxynivalenol (DON) and other trichothecenes in cereal grains, particularly wheat, barley, and maize. The FDA has established advisory levels for DON in food products, reflecting the chronic health risks of low-level exposure.

The same compounds that have been weaponized occur naturally in the damp walls of homes, representing one of the more unsettling intersections between mycology and human affairs.
