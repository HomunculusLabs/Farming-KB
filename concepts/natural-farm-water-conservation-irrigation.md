# Water Conservation and Irrigation

Water is the most important element on any piece of land. No other factor
impacts so many other things or is affected by so many elements. How much
water you have depends on rainfall, soil drainage, existing plants, people
and animals using water, and the kinds of plants you want to grow. In a
[[natural-farm-permaculture-ethics-principles]] system, the goal is to harvest,
store, conserve, and reuse water as efficiently as possible.

## Core Water Strategy

The first step is deciding where water comes from and devising a way to store
it, utilizing gravity to move it where needed. The second step is using
species that need less water in places difficult to irrigate. Water is also a
responsibility: use harvested water for as many tasks as possible, be super
efficient, grow life through gardens and aquaculture, generate electricity,
conserve and reuse water, and recycle it as many times as you can.

## Water Sources

There are three primary water sources available on most land:

- **Rain:** Surface runoff or groundwater collection
- **Springs:** Groundwater emerging naturally from the earth
- **Streams:** Permanent or temporary surface water flows

These sources are diverted through various means to storage, irrigation
systems, and swales. Gentle slopes and drains lead water from streams,
valleys, roofs, and roads that collect rainfall and send it to where it is
needed, taking advantage of the free, energy-efficient power of gravity.

## Swales

A swale is a long, shallow ditch about three to five feet across that stops
and channels the flow of water into the soil. Unlike a regular ditch that
directs water elsewhere, a swale helps water get absorbed directly into the
soil. Swales lie across the land, especially across slopes, and water forced
into the soil is soaked up by trees planted along the swales. They can be
filled with rock, gravel, or gypsum for even better water penetration.

Swales are perhaps the most effective method of water conservation in both
dry and humid climates. They work on steep slopes or flat prairies and can
even be implemented in urban areas to capture road and roof runoff.

### Building Swales

1. Dig the swale a few feet deep. It will catch water in a pool that
   gradually soaks in. If overflowing, widen it or improve drainage.

2. Two or more swales are always put together along the contour of the
   slope, with soil loosened to help water penetrate.

3. The spacing between swales depends on annual rainfall: 60 feet apart
   for 5 inches of rain, 50 feet for 10 inches, 40 feet for 20 inches,
   30 feet for 30 inches, and 20 feet for 40 inches or more.

4. After a couple of soaking rainfalls, plant trees directly behind the
   dirt mound. In wet climates, plant heavily between swales; in dry
   climates, plant only the edges.

5. It takes three to ten years for trees to fully mature and begin shading,
   mulching, and absorbing salts from the soil. Trees soak up water runoff,
   prevent waterlogging and evaporation, produce food, and improve the
   microclimate of the land.

## Diversion Drains

Unlike a swale, which stops water and forces it into the soil, a diversion
drain carries water away. It can direct water into a swale, pond, or
irrigation system. Drains directed to swales need not be waterproof, but
drains sending water to a dam should be built of rock or concrete. Installing
a spill gate gives control over water flow for flood management or crop
irrigation.

## Dry Bed Management

In desert areas where small creeks can quickly become dangerous after brief
rainfall, stream braiding spreads flow into many smaller channels across the
landscape, irrigating the entire area while preventing flooding. Start at the
head of the stream, dig a small pool, and branch off with diversion channels
in a diamond pattern, with small pools at each intersection.

## Rainwater Catchment

Every 1,000 square feet of roof surface gathers 600 gallons of water per
inch of rain. Metal roofing is best because it is relatively clean. Install
gutters with a leaf screen. For drinking water, direct through a roof washer
that diverts the first gallon per 100 square feet into a separate tank or
garden to keep dirt out. Cover tank inlets with mosquito screens and install
overflow valves directed to the garden.

### Water Storage Tanks

A single person should store at least 1,000 gallons of water; a family needs
a minimum of 2,500 gallons. Tanks can be made from concrete, compacted clay,
metal, or plastered dirt. Position dams or tanks at the top of a hill for
gravity-fed distribution. A large tank set on a hilltop can serve as a
building foundation, with the roof collecting rainwater and a pond below
acting as a firewall and drought reservoir.

## Irrigation Design

Permaculture irrigation is half species choice and half terrain. Only a small
part involves piping, pumps, or equipment. Gravity is used to direct water
into gardens or to individual plants. Species are chosen for traits that make
them function well in their specific locations. The barn roof is harvested
for rainwater stored in a large tank and, ideally, gravity-fed to gardens.

### Desert Irrigation

With careful planning, enough water exists to grow food in the desert. All
graywater should be recycled to a wetland marsh. Use buried drip hoses at
least six inches under mulch or soil, or make your own with earthenware pots
(ollas) sunk into the ground, bottles with holes, or pipes filled with
gravel. In orchards, use mist sprinklers only in shady tree areas to avoid
evaporation. Water only in early morning, late evening, or at night. Mulch
and swales are the secret to successful [[desert-gardening]].

## Water Purification

Ground water is often contaminated with parasites like Giardia and
agricultural chemicals. The most effective homemade purification is
distillation: boiling water to make steam, which is then condensed back to
liquid, minus impurities. This removes heavy metals, poisons, bacteria,
viruses, nitrates, and fluoride. A slow sand filter is simpler but slightly
less effective: a tank with pebbles, gravel, coarse sand, and fine sand
through which water flows, leaving impurities behind. It requires no energy
and no pressure.
