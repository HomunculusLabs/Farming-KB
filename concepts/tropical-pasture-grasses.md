# Tropical Pasture Grasses

Tropical pasture grasses form the foundation of livestock systems in warm climates, providing forage for cattle, sheep, and goats across a wide range of rainfall zones from 40 cm to over 300 cm annually. Effective grassland management requires selecting species matched to local soil, rainfall, and temperature conditions, and integrating them with leguminous companions for nitrogen fixation and sustained productivity.

## Grassland Management Strategies

Four fundamental strategies guide productive tropical grassland management:

1. **Addition of forage species** — Grass legumes or trees providing foliage, food sugars, seed carbohydrates, or fruits
2. **Environmental changes** — Water storage, soil structure improvement, irrigation, windbreaks and shelter, key fertiliser or trace elements, and plant species maintenance by slashing or light cultivation
3. **Conservation of forage** — Rotational or periodic rests from grazing, hays and silages, protein or urea supplements and molasses in drought, and stocking rates kept below worst-case conditions
4. **Stock management** — Well-planned buying and selling timed to seasons, calving or buying in animals in spring or early summer, selling or culling dry cows in autumn to lessen winter feed demand

An ideal tropical system combines a mixture of legumes with selected grass species plus some storable forage. Most grazing systems can extend under palms, between large tree crops, or as a complex with belts of forage tree legumes.

## Major Tropical Grass Species

### Guinea Grass (*Panicum maximum*)

The first choice for subtropical and tropical pastures. A bunch grass for warm areas receiving more than 90–300 cm of rain. Drought resistant but yields best in humid areas. Frost sensitive.

**Key characteristics:**
- Shade tolerant; often yields well under thin-crowned tree crops
- Growth maintained in cloudy summer-autumn regimes
- Grazed down to 15–20 cm
- Combines well with twining legumes that climb on stiff stems
- Suits rotational grazing with 12–18 fields
- Interspersed with sugar-pod trees and tree fruit forages
- Drilled to 6 mm depth to establish

### Kikuyu Grass (*Pennisetum clandestinum*)

Cold tolerant, grown from cool to tropical areas. Valuable for cooler uplands and thinner soils with good autumn growth.

**Key characteristics:**
- Prefers light soils and red loams
- Can be sown as runners or seed
- Excellent for water spillways and erosion control
- Few legumes tolerate the tight sward, so nitrogen-fixing trees (*Acacia*, *Leucaena*, *Prosopis*, *Albizia*) are essential
- *Desmodium*, *Glycine*, and white clover can be sod-drilled in autumn-cut areas
- Valuable on rocky knolls in combination with leguminous tree pioneers

### Makarikari Grass (*Panicum coloratum*)

Bunch and spreading types suited to 40–90 cm rainfall. Exceptionally adaptable to difficult soils.

**Key characteristics:**
- Tolerates alluvial fans, flood plains, black clay soils, red earths, and even semi-caked salted soils
- Needs a year after seeding to establish, suiting rotational systems
- Lucerne interplant can succeed in irrigated areas
- Drilled at 1.3 cm depth, 2–3 kg/ha, or planted from rooted cuttings
- Valuable for winter-green feed and drought resistance
- Suits long-rotation grazing in open savannah with *Acacia* and sugar-pod trees
- On black clay soils, purple pigeon grass (*Setaria porphyrantha*) may germinate better

### Elephant or Banna Grass (*Pennisetum purpureum*)

Best on deep alluvial or coarse flats above 110 cm rainfall (alluvium) or 90 cm (coastal).

**Key characteristics:**
- Reaches 2.5–4.5 m high but can be grazed to 1 m or cut to 15 cm for forage and mulch
- Needs a vigorous legume interplant such as *Leucaena*
- Compatible with forage legumes: *Calopogon*, *Centrosema*, *Glycine* (in high rainfall tropics)
- Can be set out as windbreak by burying hard slim pieces of 4–5 nodes horizontally at 8–10 cm depth in summer
- Permanent plots well-suited to feeding selected stall-fed dairy cattle
- Can carry 7 milk cows/ha if cut and hand-fed with *Leucaena* and sugar pods
- Accessory plots of bean trees, coconut, banana, and palms for diet variation and bedding are ideal

### Para Grass (*Brachiaria mutica*)

For warm areas of low frost intensity, particularly valuable for swampy soils and at soaks, dams, and waterholes.

**Key characteristics:**
- Provides good soil structure due to fibrous shallow roots
- Compatible with forage legumes: phasey bean, greenleaf *Desmodium*, *Centrosema*
- Ideal as a fenced-out reserve food for drought and for finishing animals for sale in poor seasons
- Planted from cuttings at 2 x 2 m spacing or seeded if available
- Can reach 4 m in one summer
- Do not plant where clogging of channels could be a problem or where other crops are to be grown

### Sorghums (*Sorghum almum*, silk sorghum, Sudan grass *S. halapense*)

Annual, biennial, or persistent from seed. Most useful as broadcast-sown pioneers.

**Key characteristics:**
- Effective at 50–90 cm rainfall
- Uses: pioneers with perennials, mulch in orchard strips, emergency dry-season fodder, garden mulch source
- Surface-sown for easy establishment
- Easily grazed out if not managed
- Provide birdseed, forage, and help control weeds
- Particularly useful in early establishment phases

## Establishing Perennial Grass Swards

Establishing perennial grasses on weedy or eroded areas is a one to three year process:

1. **Choose a land-forming system** — Swaling, interception banks, or pitting; establish dams for irrigation above good soil types
2. **Sow pioneer grass** — Molasses grass, *Sorghum almum*, or silk sorghum mixed with sawdust into slashed weeds; or drill selected grass and legumes after slashing
3. **Burn molasses grass** (or drill selected perennials and broadcast *Sorghum almum*)
4. **Plant leguminous trees** — Along swales through the area at 30–100 metre strip spacing; allow 2–3 years with light grazing to year 3
5. **Commence managed rotational grazing** — Drill or broadcast forage legumes into established grasses; 15–18 fields are necessary for rotation

## Soil and Mineral Management

At every stage of pasture establishment:
- Soil analysis and minimal mineral fertiliser amendments may be necessary
- With intensive grazing, sulphur and potash dressings are desirable
- Each soil type, location, rainfall area, slope type, and main crop needs individual assessment and planning

## Orchard Integration

The leaf swards valued by graziers also suit green-crop cover for orchardists where regular slashing for easy fruit harvest is practiced. Soils under slashed pastures are of excellent structure, and erosion is effectively stopped. It is essential that the orchard crop is well established using manures before twining legumes are planted.
