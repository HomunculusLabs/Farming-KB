# Comfrey Tea Liquid Fertilizer

## Overview

Comfrey tea is a liquid [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-solomon]] produced by steeping comfrey leaves (Symphytum officinale or Symphytum x uplandicum) in water. The resulting liquid is rich in potassium, nitrogen, and trace minerals, making it one of the most effective and widely used homemade [[solomon-liquid-fertilizers-and-foliar-feeding]] in [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]] and permaculture. Comfrey earns its reputation as a "[[nutrient-dynamic-accumulator-reference]]" due to its deep taproot system (extending 2-3 meters), which mines minerals from subsoil layers that are inaccessible to shallower-rooted crops. These accumulated nutrients are concentrated in the leaves, where they can be harvested and converted into liquid feed through fermentation.

## Nutritional Profile of Comfrey

Comfrey leaves contain an exceptional concentration of plant nutrients, particularly potassium:

- **Potassium (K)**: 3-7% on a dry weight basis — comparable to commercial potassium fertilizers. Potassium promotes flowering, fruiting, disease resistance, and overall plant vigor
- **Nitrogen (N)**: 1.5-3% — significant nitrogen content that supports [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]]. Most of the nitrogen is in protein form rather than nitrate, providing slower release
- **Phosphorus (P)**: 0.3-0.8% — moderate phosphorus content for root development and energy transfer
- **Calcium (Ca)**: 2-3% — important for cell wall structure and soil pH buffering
- **Trace minerals**: Comfrey accumulates iron, magnesium, manganese, copper, zinc, boron, and selenium from deep soil layers. These trace elements are often deficient in surface soils after years of cultivation

The NPK ratio of comfrey leaves is approximately 3-1.5-5 (N-P-K), making it one of the few organic sources that is significantly higher in potassium than nitrogen. This profile makes comfrey tea particularly valuable as a feed for fruiting and flowering crops rather than leafy green vegetables.

## Comfrey Species for Fertilizer Production

Two main species are cultivated for fertilizer production:

- **Symphytum x uplandicum (Russian comfrey, Bocking 14)**: The preferred variety for fertilizer production. Bocking 14 is a sterile hybrid that does not set seed, preventing the invasive spread that plagues common comfrey. It produces large, vigorous leaf growth and can be harvested 4-6 times per [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. Developed by Lawrence Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association in the 1950s
- **Symphytum officinale (common comfrey)**: The wild species, native to Europe. It sets viable seed and can become invasive in garden settings. While equally useful for fertilizer production, it is less preferred due to its weedy tendency. Common comfrey is also the source of pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) that raise safety concerns for internal use

Other Bocking cultivars (1, 2, 4, 7, etc.) were developed by HDRA for various purposes, but Bocking 14 remains the standard for organic fertilizer production due to its high yield, sterility, and nutrient content.

## Methods of Making Comfrey Tea

### Method 1: Aerated Comfrey Tea (Quick Method)

This method produces a nutrient-rich liquid in 2-4 weeks with minimal odor:

1. Fill a container (bucket or barrel) loosely with freshly harvested comfrey leaves
2. Fill the container with water (rainwater preferred), ensuring all leaves are submerged
3. Stir daily to introduce oxygen and prevent anaerobic conditions
4. Optionally, add a small handful of compost or garden soil as a microbial inoculant
5. After 2-4 weeks, when the liquid is dark brown, strain through a coarse sieve
6. Dilute 1:10 with water before applying to plants
7. The remaining leaf residue can be added to the compost pile

This aerobic method is preferred by most gardeners because it produces less odor and preserves more of the nitrogen content. Regular stirring is essential — neglected aerobic brews quickly become anaerobic.

### Method 2: Anaerobic Comfrey Tea (Traditional Method)

The traditional method produces a more concentrated but significantly more odorous product:

1. Pack comfrey leaves tightly into a container (a bucket with a lid or a drainage-tapped barrel)
2. Do NOT add water — the leaves release their own liquid under pressure as they decompose
3. Place a weight on top to compress the leaves and keep them submerged in their own juices
4. Cover tightly to exclude air and contain odors
5. After 4-6 weeks, a dark, concentrated liquid collects at the bottom
6. Drain off the liquid (this is the comfrey concentrate)
7. Dilute 1:15 to 1:20 with water before applying

This method produces a stronger, more concentrated product but generates a smell comparable to raw sewage. It must be done in a remote area of the garden or in a sealed container. The concentrate can be stored in sealed bottles for several months.

### Method 3: Comfrey Leaf Meal (Dry Method)

For gardeners who prefer to avoid the fermentation process entirely:

1. Harvest comfrey leaves and dry them in a well-ventilated area out of direct sunlight
2. Once completely crisp, crumble the dried leaves into small pieces or powder them
3. Store the dried leaf meal in airtight containers in a cool, dark place
4. To use as liquid feed, steep 2-3 handfuls of dried leaf meal in 10 liters of water for 24-48 hours
5. Strain and apply directly (no dilution needed for this method)

This method avoids odor entirely and provides a shelf-stable product that can be used year-round. The nutrient content of dried comfrey is concentrated compared to fresh material.

## Application Guidelines

- **Tomatoes and peppers**: Begin feeding at first flower set, every 10-14 days at 1:10 dilution. The high potassium content promotes fruit development and flavor
- **Fruit trees and berries**: Apply as a soil drench around the drip line in spring and early summer. Comfrey tea supports fruit sizing and sweetness
- **Potatoes**: Apply as a liquid feed during the tuber-bulking phase for improved yield and storage quality
- **Flowering ornamentals**: Regular feeding promotes abundant, long-lasting blooms
- **Avoid on leafy greens**: The high potassium relative to nitrogen is not ideal for lettuce, spinach, and other leafy vegetables that benefit from nitrogen-dominant feeding

## Comfrey Patch Establishment

A productive comfrey patch requires minimal maintenance once established:

- **Planting**: Root cuttings (5-8 cm sections) are planted 5 cm deep in prepared soil, spaced 60-90 cm apart. Crown divisions can also be used
- **Soil preference**: Comfrey tolerates a wide range of soil types but performs best in deep, moisture-retentive soils. It grows in full sun to partial shade
- **First year**: Allow plants to establish without harvesting. Remove flowers to direct energy to root and leaf development
- **Subsequent years**: Begin harvesting when plants reach 60 cm in height. Cut 5-10 cm above ground level. Plants typically recover in 4-6 weeks for the next harvest
- **Yield**: A well-established comfrey patch can produce 3-5 kg of fresh leaves per plant per harvest, with 4-6 harvests per growing season
- **Lifespan**: Comfrey plants are extremely long-lived. A single planting can remain productive for 20+ years

## Comfrey Tea vs. Nettle Tea

Comfrey tea and nettle tea are the two most popular homemade liquid fertilizers, and they serve complementary purposes:

- **Comfrey tea**: Higher in potassium and calcium. Best for fruiting, flowering, and fruit-bearing crops. Better suited for mid-to-late season feeding when plants are setting and maturing fruit
- **Nettle tea**: Higher in nitrogen and iron. Best for vegetative growth, leafy greens, and early-season feeding when plants are building foliage. Also acts as a mild insecticide when applied as a foliar spray

Many organic gardeners maintain both a comfrey patch and a nettle patch to produce both feeds, applying nettle tea early in the season and switching to comfrey tea as plants transition to flowering and fruiting.

## Safety Considerations

Comfrey contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs), which are hepatotoxic (liver-damaging) when consumed internally. While the risk from external application to plants is negligible (PAs are not taken up by crop plants in meaningful quantities), gardeners should be aware of this toxicity when handling comfrey. Wear gloves when harvesting and processing large quantities. Comfrey should never be used as a feed supplement for livestock or poultry. The PAs are concentrated in the roots; leaves contain lower levels but should still not be ingested.

## Comfrey as a Mulch and Compost Accelerator

Beyond liquid tea production, comfrey leaves serve as a valuable direct mulch and compost ingredient. Fresh comfrey leaves placed as a surface mulch around heavy-feeding crops (tomatoes, squash, potatoes) slowly release nutrients as they decompose, providing a sustained low-dose feed throughout the growing season. The leaves break down relatively quickly (2-4 weeks as mulch), leaving a nutrient-rich organic layer on the soil surface. When added to compost piles, comfrey acts as a natural compost accelerator — its high nitrogen and moisture content activates the composting process and raises internal temperatures, significantly speeding decomposition of carbon-rich materials. A layer of comfrey leaves every 15-30 cm in a compost pile can reduce overall composting time by several weeks.

## Scaling Comfrey Production for Larger Gardens

For gardens or small farms requiring larger quantities of comfrey tea, several scaling strategies are effective:

- **Dedicated comfrey beds**: Establish rows of Bocking 14 spaced 75 cm apart with 60 cm between plants within rows. A 3-meter row of 5 plants produces enough leaf material for weekly applications to a 50-square-meter garden
- **Comfrey barrels**: Use 200-liter barrels with drainage taps for anaerobic comfrey concentrate production. Fill with packed comfrey leaves, weight down, and drain concentrate as it accumulates. One barrel produces approximately 20-30 liters of concentrate per batch
- **Continuous brewing systems**: Set up multiple brewing containers on a staggered schedule so that fresh comfrey tea is always available. With 3-4 containers started at weekly intervals, a continuous supply is maintained throughout the growing season
- **Comfrey and comfrey only**: For maximum fertilizer output, dedicate the entire comfrey patch to leaf production. Remove flower stalks as they appear to redirect energy from seed production to leaf growth
