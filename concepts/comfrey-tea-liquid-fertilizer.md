## Overview

Comfrey tea is a liquid [[solomon-liquid-fertilizers-and-foliar-feeding]] in [[nutrient-dynamic-accumulator-reference]]" due to its deep taproot system (extending 2-3 meters), which mines minerals from subsoil layers that are inaccessible to shallower-rooted crops. These accumulated nutrients are concentrated in the leaves, where they can be harvested and converted into liquid feed through fermentation.

## Nutritional Profile of Comfrey

Comfrey leaves contain an exceptional concentration of plant nutrients, particularly potassium:

- **Potassium (K)**: 3-7% on a dry weight basis — comparable to commercial potassium fertilizers. Potassium promotes flowering, fruiting, disease resistance, and overall plant vigor
- **Nitrogen (N)**: 1.5-3% — significant nitrogen content that supports [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. Developed by Lawrence Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association in the 1950s
- **Symphytum officinale (common comfrey)**: The wild species, native to Europe. It sets viable seed and can become invasive in garden settings. While equally useful for fertilizer production, it is less preferred due to its weedy tendency. Common comfrey is also the source of pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) that raise safety concerns for internal use

Other Bocking cultivars (1, 2, 4, 7, etc.) were developed by HDRA for various purposes, but Bocking 14 remains the standard for organic fertilizer production due to its high yield, sterility, and nutrient content.

## Methods of Making Comfrey Tea

### Method 1: Aerated Comfrey Tea (Quick Method)

This method produces a nutrient-rich liquid in 2-4 weeks with minimal odor:

1. Fill a container (bucket or barrel) loosely with freshly harvested comfrey leaves
2. Fill the container with water (rainwater preferred), ensuring all leaves are submerged
3. Stir daily to introduce oxygen and prevent anaerobic conditions
4. Optionally, add a small handful of compost or garden soil as a microbial inoculant
5. After 2-4 weeks, when the liquid is dark brown, strain through a coarse sieve
6. Dilute 1:10 with water before applying to plants
7. The remaining leaf residue can be added to the compost pile

This aerobic method is preferred by most gardeners because it produces less odor and preserves more of the nitrogen content. Regular stirring is essential — neglected aerobic brews quickly become anaerobic.

### Method 2: Anaerobic Comfrey Tea (Traditional Method)

The traditional method produces a more concentrated but significantly more odorous product:

1. Pack comfrey leaves tightly into a container (a bucket with a lid or a drainage-tapped barrel)
2. Do NOT add water — the leaves release their own liquid under pressure as they decompose
3. Place a weight on top to compress the leaves and keep them submerged in their own juices
4. Cover tightly to exclude air and contain odors
5. After 4-6 weeks, a dark, concentrated liquid collects at the bottom
6. Drain off the liquid (this is the comfrey concentrate)
7. Dilute 1:15 to 1:20 with water before applying

This method produces a stronger, more concentrated product but generates a smell comparable to raw sewage. It must be done in a remote area of the garden or in a sealed container. The concentrate can be stored in sealed bottles for several months.

### Method 3: Comfrey Leaf Meal (Dry Method)

For gardeners who prefer to avoid the fermentation process entirely:

1. Harvest comfrey leaves and dry them in a well-ventilated area out of direct sunlight
2. Once completely crisp, crumble the dried leaves into small pieces or powder them
3. Store the dried leaf meal in airtight containers in a cool, dark place
4. To use as liquid feed, steep 2-3 handfuls of dried leaf meal in 10 liters of water for 24-48 hours
5. Strain and apply directly (no dilution needed for this method)

This method avoids odor entirely and provides a shelf-stable product that can be used year-round. The nutrient content of dried comfrey is concentrated compared to fresh material.

## Application Guidelines

- **Tomatoes and peppers**: Begin feeding at first flower set, every 10-14 days at 1:10 dilution. The high potassium content promotes fruit development and flavor
- **Fruit trees and berries**: Apply as a soil drench around the drip line in spring and early summer. Comfrey tea supports fruit sizing and sweetness
- **Potatoes**: Apply as a liquid feed during the tuber-bulking phase for improved yield and storage quality
- **Flowering ornamentals**: Regular feeding promotes abundant, long-lasting blooms
- **Avoid on leafy greens**: The high potassium relative to nitrogen is not ideal for lettuce, spinach, and other leafy vegetables that benefit from nitrogen-dominant feeding

## Comfrey Patch Establishment

A productive comfrey patch requires minimal maintenance once established:

- **Planting**: Root cuttings (5-8 cm sections) are planted 5 cm deep in prepared soil, spaced 60-90 cm apart. Crown divisions can also be used
- **Soil preference**: Comfrey tolerates a wide range of soil types but performs best in deep, moisture-retentive soils. It grows in full sun to partial shade
- **First year**: Allow plants to establish without harvesting. Remove flowers to direct energy to root and leaf development
- **Subsequent years**: Begin harvesting when plants reach 60 cm in height. Cut 5-10 cm above ground level. Plants typically recover in 4-6 weeks for the next harvest
- **Yield**: A well-established comfrey patch can produce 3-5 kg of fresh leaves per plant per harvest, with 4-6 harvests per growing season
- **Lifespan**: Comfrey plants are extremely long-lived. A single planting can remain productive for 20+ years
