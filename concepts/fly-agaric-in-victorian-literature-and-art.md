## From Nursery to Commerce

By the end of the century, the Victorian love of fairies had dwindled, and with it the mushrooms with which they had become inexorably associated were banished to the domain of the nursery. The fly-agaric found its way into children"s books, Christmas cards (especially in Germany and Eastern Europe), and other ephemera. ## Alice"s Adventures in Wonderland and the Mushroom: The Scholarly Debate

The question of whether Lewis Carroll intended the mushroom scene in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865) as a pharmacological reference has generated considerable scholarly debate. The scene in question — Chapter V, "Advice from a Caterpillar" — depicts Alice encountering a blue hookah-smoking caterpillar perched atop an enormous mushroom. The caterpillar instructs Alice that one side of the mushroom will make her grow taller and the other will make her grow shorter. After experimenting, Alice finds that eating from the right side shrinks her to nine inches and eating from the left restores her normal size.

The pharmacological reading was most influentially advanced by the ethnomycologist R. Gordon Wasson in his landmark book Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality (1968). The caterpillar himself may owe as much to Victorian entomological interest as to any drug reference; Carroll was an enthusiastic amateur naturalist who collected insects.

The debate remains unresolved, and perhaps unresolvable. What is certain is that Carroll's mushroom scene has become one of the most enduring images in English literature, and that the fly-agaric's distinctive red-and-white appearance has ensured its permanent association with Wonderland in the popular imagination. The 1951 Disney animated adaptation and the 2010 Tim Burton film both feature prominently the mushroom scene, cementing the visual connection between Alice and the fly-agaric for new generations.

## Fly-Agaric in Pre-Raphaelite and Symbolist Art

While the fairy painters of mid-Victorian Britain used mushrooms primarily as scenic props for their diminutive subjects, the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood and later Symbolist painters engaged more directly with the fly-agaric's uncanny associations. The Pre-Raphaelites, founded in 1848 by William Holman Hunt, John Everett Millais, and Dante Gabriel Rossetti, sought to return to the vivid colours, complex compositions, and intense detail of Quattrocento Italian art. Their fascination with medievalism, folklore, and the supernatural brought them into contact with mushroom imagery in ways that went beyond mere decoration.

Dante Gabriel Rossetti, the most bohemian of the Pre-Raphaelites, incorporated fungal imagery into several works that explored themes of enchantment and altered consciousness. His painting Proserpine (1874), depicting the classical goddess of the underworld trapped between the realms of life and death, evokes the fly-agaric's liminal status as a substance that transports the user between ordinary and extraordinary states of awareness. While Rossetti did not depict the mushroom directly in this painting, his extensive correspondence reveals a fascination with folklore that included mushroom lore.

The late-Victorian Symbolist movement, which drew heavily on the Pre-Raphaelite legacy, engaged even more explicitly with the psychoactive associations of the fly-agaric. Symbolist artists sought to express ideas and emotions through metaphor and suggestion rather than direct representation, and the mushroom — with its dual identity as both a mundane organism and a gateway to visionary experience — was a potent Symbolist motif. The French Symbolist Odilon Redon, whose work was widely known in Britain through exhibitions and reproductions, created numerous works featuring fungi in dreamlike, otherworldly landscapes. Redon's lithographs and pastels of the 1880s and 1890s often placed enlarged mushrooms in barren, lunar landscapes, suggesting realms of consciousness beyond the ordinary.

In Britain, the painter and illustrator Walter Crane — associated with the Arts and Crafts movement but deeply influenced by Symbolism — included fly-agaric-like mushrooms in his illustrations for children's books and fairy tales. Crane's The Baby's Own Aesop (1887) and his illustrations for Spenser's The Faerie Queene both feature prominent mushroom imagery that bridges the gap between Victorian fairy painting and the emerging Symbolist sensibility. Crane's mushrooms function as thresholds between worlds, visual metaphors for the transition from the mundane to the marvellous.

Aubrey Beardsley, working in the final decade of the century, pushed the mushroom's symbolic potential further into the realm of the decadent and the erotic. Beardsley's illustrations for Oscar Wilde's Salome (1894) include vegetal forms that scholars have identified as fungal, their organic curves suggesting both growth and decay, fertility and corruption. Beardsley's work exemplifies the late-Victorian tendency to invest the mushroom with complex, often contradictory meanings that went far beyond its simple fairy-tale associations.

## The Fly-Agaric and the Gothic Imagination

Beyond the realms of fairy painting and Symbolist aesthetics, the fly-agaric also found its way into the darker current of Victorian Gothic literature and art, where its poisonous qualities and mind-altering effects served as metaphors for states of psychological disturbance and transgression. The mushroom's dual nature — at once beautiful and deadly, natural and supernatural — made it an ideal symbol for Gothic writers exploring themes of altered perception, forbidden knowledge, and the fragility of rational consciousness.

The most significant Gothic engagement with the fly-agaric came indirectly through the broader Victorian fascination with poisonous plants and fungi. The popular "poison fictions" of the period, including works by Mary Elizabeth Braddon and Wilkie Collins, often featured botanical poisons as instruments of crime, and the public's morbid fascination with toxic plants was fed by a steady stream of newspaper reports on accidental and deliberate poisonings.
