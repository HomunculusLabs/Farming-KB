# Fly-Agaric in Victorian Literature and Art

The [[amanita-muscaria-cultural-history]] (Amanita muscaria) exercised a profound influence on victorian-mycology|Victorian literature, art and popular imagination, despite — or perhaps because of — the fact that nobody in Britain was actually eating it. The mushroom's lurid appearance, its association with [[siberian-fly-agaric-shamanism-urine-recycling]], and its genuinely bizarre psychoactive effects made it a potent catalyst for creative and speculative minds throughout the nineteenth century.

## The Siberian Travelogues

The cultural history of the fly-agaric in the West properly begins with the travelogues emerging from Siberia. In 1736, the Swedish colonel Philip von Strahlenberg published his Description of the North and Eastern Parts of Europe and Asia, describing how Koryak tribespeople got "drunk" upon an unspecified mushroom. Most scandalously, poorer tribespeople would drink the mushroom-tainted urine of wealthier community members who could afford the fungi directly.

This account was seized upon by Oliver Goldsmith, who popularised it in his Citizen of the World (1762), imagining what would happen were the custom introduced in London. Goldsmith's satirical vision of a willing chain of condescension — with lords drinking from priests, priests from lords, and a "simple squire drinking it double distilled from the urine of knighthood" — ensured the fly-agaric story circulated widely.

## M. C. Cooke and the Popular Science Bridge

The fly-agaric's effects entered Victorian popular consciousness most significantly through the writings of Mordecai Cubitt Cooke. In his Plain and Easy Account of the British Fungi (1862), Cooke described the Siberian mushroom experience: macropsia (altered perception of size), where "a straw lying in the road becomes a formidable object" and users would leap "sufficient to clear a barrel of ale." This charmingly written guide was designed for a popular audience and probably did more than any other book to circulate the fly-agaric story in Britain.

## Lewis Carroll and Alice's Mushroom

The perception-altering effects of the fly-agaric, circulating through Victorian society, almost certainly influenced the Reverend Charles Dodgson (Lewis Carroll). The famous mushroom scene in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland — where Alice meets a hookah-smoking caterpillar atop a mushroom, one side of which makes her grow taller and the other smaller — draws directly upon the macropsia described in the Siberian accounts.

However, Letcher argues that this reading of Alice as a knowing pharmacological odyssey is unlikely. There is no record of Carroll using mushrooms or other drugs himself. An array of psychoactive drugs, including opium, were freely available in Victorian society, and consuming them carried nothing like the stigma it does today. Carroll was an insomniac with persistent migraines, opposed to smoking tobacco, and drank only in moderation — he makes an improbable pioneer of illicit drug-taking.

## Victorian Fairy Painting

The generalised Victorian obsession with fairies gave rise to a series of fairy paintings by artists including Joseph Noel Paton, John Anster Fitzgerald, Richard Doyle and Richard Dadd (who spent much of his life in Broadmoor asylum). Mushrooms appeared in many of these paintings, not only to suggest the diminutive size of the fairies but to act as signifiers for the eldritch net, the otherworldly realm fairies were supposed to inhabit.

In Fitzgerald's Fairy Feast of 1859, a group of fairies dine around a mushroom table. Doyle's watercolour, painted between 1870 and 1880, is titled Elves Playing Leapfrog over Red Toadstools. On the Victorian stage, fairies appeared in everything from pantomimes to Shakespeare — in the Princess Theatre's 1856 production of A Midsummer Night's Dream, Puck rode onto the stage atop a mechanical mushroom.

Not one of these examples was inspired by first-hand experience with the fly-agaric. As Kingsley noted, people had yet to acquire the taste. In the case of Dadd and other otherworldly painters, the visions can most definitely be attributed to imagination bolstered by that true Victorian staple, opium.

## Kingsley, Wells and the Literary Tradition

Charles Kingsley included an episode of unwitting fly-agaric intoxication in his 1866 novel Hereward the Wake. A character uses juice from "scarlet toadstools" to loosen the tongues of Hereward's men, who grow "merry-mad" and are terrified by the prospect of stepping over a gutter they think to be a vast gulf.

More extraordinary is H. G. Wells's short story The Purple Pileus (1897), in which a henpecked husband's attempted suicide by eating poisonous mushrooms has quite the opposite effect — it transforms him, bringing about a wholly life-changing invigoration. The story is a remarkable fictional treatment of psychedelic transformation predating the modern psychedelic era by half a century.

## From Nursery to Commerce

By the end of the century, the Victorian love of fairies had dwindled, and with it the mushrooms with which they had become inexorably associated were banished to the domain of the nursery. The fly-agaric found its way into children's books, Christmas cards (especially in Germany and Eastern Europe), and other ephemera. Cartoon fly-agarics litter the forest floor in Walt Disney's Snow White and performed a "Danse Chinois" in Fantasia. From there they entered the enchanted forests of Enid Blyton, forever conjoined with the suburban garden gnome.

## Alice's Adventures in Wonderland and the Mushroom: The Scholarly Debate

The question of whether Lewis Carroll intended the mushroom scene in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865) as a pharmacological reference has generated considerable scholarly debate. The scene in question — Chapter V, "Advice from a Caterpillar" — depicts Alice encountering a blue hookah-smoking caterpillar perched atop an enormous mushroom. The caterpillar instructs Alice that one side of the mushroom will make her grow taller and the other will make her grow shorter. After experimenting, Alice finds that eating from the right side shrinks her to nine inches and eating from the left restores her normal size.

The pharmacological reading was most influentially advanced by the ethnomycologist R. Gordon Wasson in his landmark book Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality (1968). Wasson argued that the Amanita muscaria was the identity of the mysterious Vedic soma, and further suggested that Carroll's mushroom was a deliberate reference to the fly-agaric's well-documented effects on perceived body size — the macropsia and micropsia that Cooke had popularised. This interpretation gained further traction in the 1970s and 1980s as psychedelic culture became mainstream.

However, several scholars have pushed back against this reading. As Andy Letcher notes in Shroom: A Cultural History of the Magic Mushroom, there is no direct evidence that Carroll ever consumed fly-agaric or any other psychedelic substance. Carroll's diaries and letters are exhaustive and reveal a man who was, by Victorian standards, notably temperate: he opposed tobacco smoking, drank wine only sparingly, and used laudanum (tincture of opium) only occasionally for migraines, as was common practice. The pharmacological interpretation also overlooks the fact that Carroll was a mathematician and logician, and the mushroom scene can be read as an allegory for the relative nature of scale — a theme that recurs throughout the book in its sustained parody of Victorian mathematics education.

An alternative literary interpretation situates the mushroom within the broader tradition of Victorian fairy lore. In English folk tradition, toadstools were associated with fairy rings and the diminutive world of the fair folk. The mushroom as a site of transformation between scales — between the human world and the fairy world — aligns with Carroll's broader themes of perspective and the arbitrariness of size. The caterpillar himself may owe as much to Victorian entomological interest as to any drug reference; Carroll was an enthusiastic amateur naturalist who collected insects.

The debate remains unresolved, and perhaps unresolvable. What is certain is that Carroll's mushroom scene has become one of the most enduring images in English literature, and that the fly-agaric's distinctive red-and-white appearance has ensured its permanent association with Wonderland in the popular imagination. The 1951 Disney animated adaptation and the 2010 Tim Burton film both feature prominently the mushroom scene, cementing the visual connection between Alice and the fly-agaric for new generations.

## Fly-Agaric in Pre-Raphaelite and Symbolist Art

While the fairy painters of mid-Victorian Britain used mushrooms primarily as scenic props for their diminutive subjects, the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood and later Symbolist painters engaged more directly with the fly-agaric's uncanny associations. The Pre-Raphaelites, founded in 1848 by William Holman Hunt, John Everett Millais, and Dante Gabriel Rossetti, sought to return to the vivid colours, complex compositions, and intense detail of Quattrocento Italian art. Their fascination with medievalism, folklore, and the supernatural brought them into contact with mushroom imagery in ways that went beyond mere decoration.

Dante Gabriel Rossetti, the most bohemian of the Pre-Raphaelites, incorporated fungal imagery into several works that explored themes of enchantment and altered consciousness. His painting Proserpine (1874), depicting the classical goddess of the underworld trapped between the realms of life and death, evokes the fly-agaric's liminal status as a substance that transports the user between ordinary and extraordinary states of awareness. While Rossetti did not depict the mushroom directly in this painting, his extensive correspondence reveals a fascination with folklore that included mushroom lore.

The late-Victorian Symbolist movement, which drew heavily on the Pre-Raphaelite legacy, engaged even more explicitly with the psychoactive associations of the fly-agaric. Symbolist artists sought to express ideas and emotions through metaphor and suggestion rather than direct representation, and the mushroom — with its dual identity as both a mundane organism and a gateway to visionary experience — was a potent Symbolist motif. The French Symbolist Odilon Redon, whose work was widely known in Britain through exhibitions and reproductions, created numerous works featuring fungi in dreamlike, otherworldly landscapes. Redon's lithographs and pastels of the 1880s and 1890s often placed enlarged mushrooms in barren, lunar landscapes, suggesting realms of consciousness beyond the ordinary.

In Britain, the painter and illustrator Walter Crane — associated with the Arts and Crafts movement but deeply influenced by Symbolism — included fly-agaric-like mushrooms in his illustrations for children's books and fairy tales. Crane's The Baby's Own Aesop (1887) and his illustrations for Spenser's The Faerie Queene both feature prominent mushroom imagery that bridges the gap between Victorian fairy painting and the emerging Symbolist sensibility. Crane's mushrooms function as thresholds between worlds, visual metaphors for the transition from the mundane to the marvellous.

Aubrey Beardsley, working in the final decade of the century, pushed the mushroom's symbolic potential further into the realm of the decadent and the erotic. Beardsley's illustrations for Oscar Wilde's Salome (1894) include vegetal forms that scholars have identified as fungal, their organic curves suggesting both growth and decay, fertility and corruption. Beardsley's work exemplifies the late-Victorian tendency to invest the mushroom with complex, often contradictory meanings that went far beyond its simple fairy-tale associations.

## The Fly-Agaric and the Gothic Imagination

Beyond the realms of fairy painting and Symbolist aesthetics, the fly-agaric also found its way into the darker current of Victorian Gothic literature and art, where its poisonous qualities and mind-altering effects served as metaphors for states of psychological disturbance and transgression. The mushroom's dual nature — at once beautiful and deadly, natural and supernatural — made it an ideal symbol for Gothic writers exploring themes of altered perception, forbidden knowledge, and the fragility of rational consciousness.

The most significant Gothic engagement with the fly-agaric came indirectly through the broader Victorian fascination with poisonous plants and fungi. The popular "poison fictions" of the period, including works by Mary Elizabeth Braddon and Wilkie Collins, often featured botanical poisons as instruments of crime, and the public's morbid fascination with toxic plants was fed by a steady stream of newspaper reports on accidental and deliberate poisonings. While the fly-agaric was rarely the specific poison in these fictions — it is only mildly toxic compared to the truly deadly Amanita phalloides — its lurid appearance and psychoactive effects made it a frequent point of reference in discussions of fungal toxicity.

The artist Richard Dadd, whose fairy paintings place him among the most important Victorian interpreters of the otherworldly, represents a unique case in which the fly-agaric's associations with altered states took on a deeply personal dimension. Dadd, who murdered his father in 1843 and spent the remaining forty-two years of his life in Bethlem and Broadmoor psychiatric hospitals, produced a body of work that is widely regarded as among the most psychologically intense of the Victorian era. While there is no evidence that Dadd's illness was caused by fly-agaric consumption, his paintings — with their obsessive detail, distorted perspectives, and visionary imagery — have been cited by later commentators as bearing a striking resemblance to the visual effects of psychedelic experience.

The Gothic dimension of the fly-agaric's Victorian reception highlights the ambiguity that surrounded the mushroom throughout the period. It was simultaneously an object of scientific curiosity, a children's illustration, a literary symbol of enchantment, and a marker of psychological disturbance. This multiplicity of meanings ensured that the fly-agaric remained one of the most culturally resonant organisms in the Victorian imagination, long after its actual psychoactive properties had been relegated to the footnotes of ethnographic literature.

## The Fly-Agaric in Victorian Children's Literature and Ephemera

As the Victorian era drew to a close, the fly-agaric underwent its final cultural transformation, passing from the domains of science, art, and literature into the world of childhood. This transition was neither sudden nor simple, but rather the culmination of decades of cultural association between mushrooms, fairies, and the miniature world. The mushroom's shift from a symbol of altered consciousness to a benign decorative motif for children represents one of the most striking examples of cultural sanitisation in the history of Victorian popular culture.

The process began with the fairy paintings of the 1850s and 1860s, which were widely reproduced as prints and engravings for domestic display. As the appetite for fairy painting diminished among adult audiences, publishers discovered that the same imagery — gossamer-winged fairies, enchanted forests, and, inevitably, red-and-white mushrooms — appealed enormously to children. By the 1880s and 1890s, a flourishing industry of illustrated children's books had emerged, many of which drew heavily on the visual vocabulary established by the fairy painters.

The German and Eastern European tradition of depicting fly-agarics on Christmas cards and New Year's postcards reinforced the mushroom's association with childhood wonder and festive magic. The connection between the fly-agaric and Santa Claus — which some scholars have linked to Siberian shamanic traditions involving the mushroom, though this interpretation remains controversial — ensured that the mushroom maintained its visibility in popular culture long after the Victorians themselves had lost interest in its pharmacological properties.

By the turn of the twentieth century, the fly-agaric had been thoroughly domesticated. It appeared in Beatrix Potter's illustrations, in the flower fairy books of Cicely Mary Barker, and in the enchanted forests of Disney animation. Its journey from Siberian shamanic tool to Victorian literary symbol to children's book illustration is a remarkable case study in the cultural life of an organism whose psychoactive properties remained largely unknown to the very people who surrounded themselves with its image.

## The Fly-Agaric in Victorian Science and Popular Botany

The Victorian period witnessed an explosion of popular interest in natural history, and the fly-agaric occupied a distinctive position within this broader botanical culture. As the mycological establishment gradually distinguished itself from mainstream botany — a process that culminated in the founding of the British Mycological Society in 1896 — the fly-agaric served as a bridge between scientific knowledge and popular fascination, a fungus that was simultaneously an object of serious study and a source of wonder and speculation.

The publication of the first comprehensive guides to British fungi in the mid-Victorian period brought the fly-agaric to a wide audience of amateur naturalists. Miles Joseph Berkeley's Outlines of British Fungology (1860) and Mordecai Cubitt Cooke's various popular guides provided detailed descriptions of the Amanita muscaria, including its distinctive morphology, its habitats, and its toxic properties. These guides were written in an accessible, engaging style that made mycology — previously a rather dry branch of botany — into a popular pastime. The fly-agaric, with its vivid colouration and dramatic appearance, was invariably one of the first species described and illustrated, ensuring its prominence in the public imagination.

The Victorian fashion for collecting and preserving natural history specimens also contributed to the fly-agaric's cultural visibility. Seaweed albums, butterfly collections, and herbaria were standard features of middle-class drawing rooms, and the more adventurous collector might include dried fungi among their specimens. The fly-agaric, with its bright red cap, retained its colour even when dried, making it a particularly attractive addition to fungal collections. This material engagement with the mushroom — handling it, studying its structure, preserving it for display — created an intimate familiarity that complemented its literary and artistic representations.

## The Ethnographic Dimension: Victorian Armchair Anthropology

The Victorian fascination with the fly-agaric was not confined to Britain's domestic culture; it extended outward through the vast network of colonial ethnographic inquiry that characterised the period. The reports of Siberian mushroom use that had entered European consciousness through von Strahlenberg and Goldsmith in the eighteenth century were supplemented throughout the nineteenth by a growing body of ethnographic literature documenting the use of psychoactive fungi among indigenous peoples across the world.

Victorian anthropologists and explorers — working within the intellectual framework of cultural evolutionism that dominated the discipline in this period — typically interpreted indigenous mushroom use through the lens of "primitive superstition." The Siberian Koryak and Chukchi practices of Amanita muscaria consumption were described in the ethnographic literature as evidence of the irrational, childlike mentality that cultural evolutionists attributed to "savage" peoples. The urine-recycling practice, in particular, was treated as a mark of extreme cultural degradation, a source of amused contempt rather than serious ethnographic inquiry.

Nevertheless, the ethnographic literature preserved important details about indigenous mushroom practices that would later prove valuable to serious researchers. The accounts published in the Journal of the Anthropological Institute and other learned societies documented the methods of preparation, the social context of consumption, the effects reported by users, and the ritual frameworks within which mushroom use was embedded. While the interpretive framework was often patronising and inaccurate, the descriptive data was frequently reliable and would later be reinterpreted by more sympathetic scholars, most notably R. Gordon Wasson in the mid-twentieth century.

The Victorian ethnographic engagement with psychoactive fungi thus represents an ambivalent legacy: a repository of valuable information about indigenous practices, filtered through a colonial ideology that prevented its authors from understanding the full significance of what they were documenting. It was not until the dissolution of the evolutionary paradigm in the early twentieth century that the ethnographic study of psychoactive plants and fungi could begin to be approached with the cultural sensitivity and phenomenological seriousness that the subject demands.
