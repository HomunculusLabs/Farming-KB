## JADAM Soil Management — The Middle Way and Root Health

## Overview

JADAM's approach to soil management combines philosophical principles with practical techniques. The Middle Way—the Buddhist concept of balanced moderation—guides decisions about inputs, while careful attention to root health provides direct feedback on soil conditions.

## The Middle Way in Farming

### Definition

"The way of life, the way of farming, and the way of nature all come together as one. A true farmer is qualified as a sage. Walking the path of farming is no different from walking the path of a noble life."

JADAM explicitly connects agricultural practice to spiritual practice. The farmer who cultivates balanced conditions in the field is simultaneously cultivating wisdom in themselves.

### Practical Application

"A farmer who walks the Middle Way will be always mindful and not become attached to something good; s/he will also not blindly reject something labeled bad."

The Middle Way farmer:
- Does not over-apply fertilizers, even "good" organic ones
- Does not panic at the sight of pests but seeks balance
- Does not chase the latest agricultural trend but maintains proven practices
- Ages with increasing wisdom rather than decreasing yields

### The Danger of Good Things

"When s/he comes across a new fertilizer that everybody hails as full of nutrients, s/he will not lose caution of the potential danger of overusing it. This wise farmer is aware that too much of any 'good' thing will be 'bad.'"

## Empathy with Plants

### Put Yourself in the Crop's Shoes

"Look at the fields with a loving eye and think with common sense. What do you expect from crop growth when the roots are tortured with heat? Put yourself in the crops' shoes (or roots, should I say). Remember that 'I and others are one.'"

JADAM advocates for a radical empathy with cultivated plants: the farmer should imagine the plant's experience of its growing conditions and adjust management accordingly.

### The Desperate Struggle of Plants

"Do you not see the desperate struggle of plants? Farming has lost its direction. Soil is suffering from compaction, suffocation and contamination. Roots are left to grow in hideous conditions."

The language of suffering and desperation reflects JADAM's view that modern farming practices—compaction from heavy machinery, suffocation from chemical inputs, contamination from pesticides—create conditions in which plants struggle to survive.

### The Inviting Disease Paradox

"Strangely, it is almost as if current farming practices are inviting disease so that the business can sell more products." Cho identifies a structural perverse incentive: the practices that create plant stress also create the conditions for pest and disease outbreaks, which are then "treated" with purchased products.

## Roots as Soil Indicators

### The Root Principle

"Roots represent the soil condition." This is JADAM's key diagnostic principle: by observing root growth, the farmer can assess soil health without laboratory tests.

### Early Root Settlement

"Early root settlement determines the success of harvest. Early root settlement is very, very important." Getting roots established quickly after planting is the single most important factor in crop success, according to JADAM.

### Soil Temperature Management

"What do you expect from crop growth when the roots are tortured with heat?" JADAM identifies soil temperature as a critical but often overlooked factor. Mulching with hay or plant materials moderates soil temperature, suppresses weeds, and retains moisture.

## Antibiotic Contamination

### Livestock Manure Risk

"Soil-grown crops with compost containing antibiotics accumulate the chemical within their tissue. Root vegetables such as potatoes, carrots, and radish that come into direct contact with soil were far more vulnerable to antibiotics contamination."

JADAM highlights a hidden risk of conventional livestock manure: antibiotics administered to factory-farmed animals pass through into the manure and are taken up by crops, creating a pathway for antibiotic resistance genes to enter the human food chain.

### Wholesome Inputs

"Food going into my mouth determines my health; manure going into the soil determines the health of the crops. Eat whole foods for your body and apply wholesome fertilizers for the crops."

The parallel between human nutrition and crop nutrition encapsulates JADAM's holistic approach: the quality of inputs determines the quality of outputs, whether for humans or plants.

## The Commercialization Critique

### Research Serving Corporations

"Methodology (and products) too easily fall into the hands of the corporations. Instead of serving the farmers, researches will be benefiting the corporations. Microorganism is a good example. It is an essential input in organic farming yet it is completely commercialized."

JADAM argues that agricultural research has been captured by corporate interests that profit from selling inputs rather than from improving farming outcomes. The commercialization of microorganism inoculants—a resource that can be produced on-farm for free—is a prime example.

## Microorganisms as Aquatic Animals

### The Water Connection

"Microorganisms cannot travel without the help of water. They are somewhat like fish. However strong a microorganism might be, over 95% of its body is water and it is like an aquatic animal. It cannot simply move around inside dry soil."

This observation has direct practical implications: microorganism solutions must be applied with sufficient water to allow the organisms to "swim" through the soil to the root zone. Dry soil is effectively a desert for beneficial microbes.
