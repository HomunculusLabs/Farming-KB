# Manure Nutrient Variability

## Overview

One of the most common gardening mistakes is assuming all manure has similar nutritional value. In reality, the [[solomon-manure-quality-variable-nutrient-content-horse-steer-chicken]] of manure varies enormously — even within the same animal species. Solomon illustrates this with a telling comparison: horse manure can range from 0.5% nitrogen to over 1.3%, depending on the quality of feed the horse received. This variability makes manure an unreliable sole source of plant nutrition, which is why Solomon recommends using it primarily for [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] and relying on COF for actual nutrient supply.

## Why Manure Quality Varies So Much

### 1. Animal Diet Quality
The single biggest factor in manure quality is what the animal ate. Animals fed high-protein, mineral-rich diets produce manure far superior to those subsisting on poor forage:
- **Racehorses**: Fed like endurance athletes with premium hay, oilseed meals, and abundant vitamin-rich supplements — their manure is potent fertilizer
- **Pasture horses**: Fed low-quality local grass hay — their manure is essentially bedding material with minimal fertilizing value
- The same variability applies to cattle, sheep, and poultry

### 2. Bedding Material Contamination
It is nearly impossible to know how much bedding material (straw, sawdust, wood shavings) was mixed into manure samples. Bedding dilutes nutrient concentration and, when fresh, can actually tie up soil nitrogen as it decomposes (the carbon-nitrogen imbalance problem).

### 3. Storage and Handling
- **Fresh vs. composted**: Fresh manure contains readily [[plant-available-nitrogen-ammonium-nitrate]] but also weed seeds and potential pathogens. Composting reduces volume, kills pathogens and weed seeds, but also loses some nitrogen to volatilization.
- **Exposure to rain**: Nutrients leach from exposed manure piles, particularly potassium.
- **Age**: Older, well-rotted manure has lower NPK but more stable humus.
- **Urine content**: Urine contains a significant portion of the total nitrogen and potassium excreted by animals. Manure collected without urine (common in some stable setups) is substantially weaker.

### 4. Animal Species Differences

| Dairy cow | 0.5–0.7 | 0.2–0.3 | 0.4–0.5 |
| Steer | 0.5–0.7 | 0.2–0.3 | 0.4–0.5 |
| Horse | 0.5–1.3 | 0.2–0.5 | 0.4–0.9 |
| Sheep | 0.7–1.0 | 0.2–0.5 | 0.5–0.9 |
| Chicken | 1.0–2.5 | 0.5–1.6 | 0.5–1.5 |
| Duck | 0.6–1.1 | 0.4–0.6 | 0.5–1.0 |

## The Horse Manure Case Study

Solomon provides a vivid illustration of manure variability using two neighbors' horse manure:

**Neighbor "Ken"**: Grows what seems like an excellent garden using horse manure exclusively. His horses are fed like athletes — premium hay, oilseed meals, broad vitamin supplements. The resulting manure is rich and balanced enough to produce visible results.

**Another neighbor**: Sells horse manure from animals kept in a back pasture. The pasture vegetation is pathetic despite all the horse urine deposited there — the horses eat poor-quality local grass hay. The manure barely supports rose bushes and low-demand plants.

Same animal species, radically different manure quality, driven entirely by diet.

## Why Solomon Recommends Limited Reliance on Manure

Given this unpredictability, Solomon's recommendation is clear:

1. **Use manure primarily for organic matter**: Its primary value is feeding the soil ecology, improving soil structure, and increasing water-holding capacity — not as a precision nutrient source.

2. **Use COF for nutrients**: The Complete [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-solomon]] provides known, consistent amounts of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and trace minerals.

3. **Apply minimum effective amounts of manure**: Use only enough to supply the soil ecology's requirement for organic matter. Excessive manure can unbalance soil minerals.

4. **Never rely solely on manure for fertility**: Even the best manure may not provide complete nutrition, particularly trace minerals.

## Chicken Manure — The Best Readily Available Option

Among commonly available manures, chicken manure in sacks (partially composted but not labeled "compost") is the most potent:
- Typical NPK of 4-3-2 — strong stuff
- Available at reasonable prices from [[solomon-garden-centers-seedling-traps]]
- Must be used carefully to avoid over-application
- Take care not to poison plants with excessive chicken manure — while not as dangerous as [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]], overuse is possible

## Steer Manure — Common but Weak

Sacked steer manure is commonly heaped in front of supermarkets in springtime at low prices. However:
- May contain semi-decomposed sawdust (dilutes value)
- Usually has little fertilizing value
- Does feed soil microbes and [[query-how-do-fungi-improve-soil-structure]]
- Helps roots breathe
- Has been at least partially composted — not raw manure
- Useful when not hugely overapplied

## Chemical Fertilizer Comparison

Solomon contrasts manure with inexpensive [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]] to illustrate a key point:
- All inexpensive chemical fertilizers lack sufficient calcium, magnesium, and trace minerals
