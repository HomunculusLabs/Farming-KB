# Shamanism in Tryptamine Context

## Overview

[[terence-mckenna]]'s analysis of shamanism in relation to [[mckenna-tryptamine-hallucinogens-and-consciousness]]
centers on the observation that modern [[mycophobia-in-western-culture]] has lost its shamanic
tradition entirely, leaving individuals without established frameworks for
navigating the extraordinary [[mckenna-food-gods-designer-states-of-consciousness]] that tryptamine compounds
can produce. McKenna's firsthand experience with indigenous shamanic practices,
particularly in the Amazon, informed his understanding of shamanism as a
practical technology for exploring non-ordinary states of consciousness.

## Shamanism as Technique

McKenna emphasized that "shamanism is primarily techniques, not ritual." While
shamanic practices are often embedded in elaborate ceremonial contexts, the core
of shamanism is a set of empirically refined techniques "that have been worked
out over millennia that make it possible, though perhaps not for everyone, to
explore these areas." This distinguishes shamanism from religion in the Western
sense — shamanism is fundamentally experiential and pragmatic rather than
faith-based.

## Identification of Shamanic Potential

In archaic societies where shamanism is a thriving institution, individuals
with shamanic potential are identified through specific signs:

- **Oddness or uniqueness**: A marked departure from ordinary personality or
  behavior patterns that suggests access to non-ordinary states.
- **Epilepsy**: Often considered a signature of shamanic potential in
  preliterate societies, possibly because epileptic seizures provide direct
  experience of altered neurological states.
- **Survival of extraordinary ordeals**: People struck by lightning who live,
  or those who nearly die of disease and fight their way back to health after
  weeks in an "indeterminate zone," are thought to possess "strength of soul."
- **Hypersensitivity to trance states**: A natural facility for entering and
  navigating [[weil-the-natural-mind-altered-states-normalcy]] of consciousness.

## Characteristics of the Shaman

McKenna's fieldwork among shamans [[fungi-around-the-world-species-distribution]] led him to identify several
distinguishing characteristics:

- **Extraordinary centeredness**: "In traveling [[mushrooms-fungi-from-around-the-world-guide]] and dealing
  with shamans, I find the distinguishing characteristic is an extraordinary
  centeredness." This quality enables the shaman to navigate extreme states
  of consciousness without being overwhelmed.
- **Intellectual sophistication**: Shamans are typically intellectuals who are
  alienated from society. The anthropological literature presents shamans as
  embedded in tradition, but "once one gets to know them they are always very
  sophisticated about what they are doing."
- **Phenomenological expertise**: Shamans are "the true phenomenologists of
  this world" who possess deep knowledge of plant chemistry, yet describe
  these phenomena using the language of "spirits."
- **[[mckenna-shaman-centeredness-and-peripheral-social-role]] position**: Shamans occupy the edges of society. They
  may sit at the headman's side in council meetings, but they return to their
  huts at the village's edge afterward. They are sacred and therefore excluded
  from ordinary political power.

## Spirit as Technical Language

McKenna argued that Westerners fundamentally misunderstand shamanic language
about "spirits." The word spirit, as used by shamans, "is a technical gloss
for a very complicated concept," comparable to the way a quantum physicist
might use the term "charm" to describe a property of quarks. "We hear the
word 'spirits' through a series of narrowing declensions of meaning that are
worse almost than not understanding."

This misreading extends to the Western concept of "hallucination," which
implies that shamanic experiences are illusory. McKenna noted that the
original Latin meaning of *alucinare* is "to wander in one's mind," and that
traveling or journeying in inner space is "actually quite appropriate" as a
description of these experiences.
