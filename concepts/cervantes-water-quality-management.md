# Water Quality Management

Water quality directly affects nutrient availability, plant health, and final yield. Cannabis plants are composed of more than 80 percent water, making water quality one of the most critical yet often overlooked aspects of cultivation.



## pH Management

Cannabis grows best in soil with a pH from 6.5 to 7.0. Within this range, marijuana can properly absorb and process available nutrients most efficiently. If the pH is too low (acidic), acid salts chemically bind nutrients, and roots are unable to absorb them. An alkaline soil with a high pH causes nutrients to become unavailable. Hydroponic solutions perform best in a pH range from 5.8 to 6.8.

Check the pH of irrigation water at least once a week. In dry climates such as the desert Southwest, irrigation water is often alkaline with a pH above 6.0. In rainy climates such as the Pacific Northwest, water is often acidic with a pH below 6.0. The pH and EC of water supplies can change throughout the year. Raw water pH above 6.0 helps keep fertilizer mixes from becoming too acidic.



## pH Adjustment

The easiest and most convenient way to raise and stabilize soil pH is to add fine dolomite lime. Mix one cup per cubic foot of potting soil. Dolomite has a neutral pH of 7 and can never raise the pH beyond 7.0. Hydrated lime acts faster but can toxify soil if overapplied. To lower pH, use phosphoric acid or citric acid products designed for horticultural use.



## pH Measurement

Electronic pH testers are economical and convenient. Less expensive meters are accurate enough for casual use. Clean the probes after each test and wipe away corrosion. Pack the soil around probes and water with distilled or neutral pH water before testing. Perpetual pH-metering devices are available and most often used to monitor hydroponic nutrient solutions.



## Electro-Conductivity (EC) and TDS

EC and TDS meters measure the concentration of dissolved salts (nutrients) in water. EC is measured in millisiemens per centimeter (mS/cm); TDS is measured in parts per million (ppm). Water that contains 300 ppm or less dissolved solids is generally safe for cannabis. If raw water contains more than 300 ppm of dissolved solids, use a reverse-osmosis device to purify the water. Add nutrients to pure water to avoid nutrient problems.

Dissolved salts caused by saline water and fertilizer quickly build up to toxic levels in container gardens. Excessive salts inhibit seed germination, burn root hairs and tips or edges of leaves, and stunt the plant. Flush excess salt buildup by applying two gallons of water per gallon of medium and repeat using a mild pH-corrected fertilizer solution.



## Reverse Osmosis (RO)

Reverse osmosis removes 95-99 percent of dissolved solids from water, providing a clean slate for nutrient mixing. RO water is essential when source water has high mineral content or when precise nutrient control is required. RO systems waste 2-4 gallons of water for every gallon of purified water produced. Combine RO water with cal-mag supplements since RO removes beneficial calcium and magnesium along with contaminants.



## Water Temperature

Water temperature affects root health and nutrient uptake. Ideal water temperature is 65-75 degrees F (18-24 degrees C). Cold water below 55 degrees F (13 degrees C) slows root function and nutrient absorption. Warm water above 80 degrees F (27 degrees C) reduces dissolved oxygen levels and promotes root pathogens. In hydroponic reservoirs, use water chillers to maintain optimal temperature. For hand-watering, allow tap water to reach room temperature before applying.



## Water Sources

In general, water that tastes good to people also tastes good to cannabis. Municipal water supplies are usually treated and tested. Well water quality varies widely and should be tested. Rainwater can be excellent but may be acidic and should be tested. Distilled water is pure but lacks beneficial minerals. Spring water varies in mineral content. Always test new water sources before relying on them.



## Irrigation Practices

Water frequently as soon as the soil surface dries out. Irrigate soil and soilless mixes when they are dry one-half inch below the surface. As long as drainage is good, it is difficult to overwater fast-growing cannabis. Flowering marijuana uses high levels of water to carry on rapid floral formation. Withholding water stunts flower formation.

Use plenty of water and allow up to 10 percent runoff during each watering. Runoff prevents fertilizer buildup in soil. Water early in the day so excess water evaporates from the soil surface and leaves. Add a few drops of biodegradable liquid dish soap concentrate to irrigation water to help it penetrate soil more thoroughly.



## Flushing

Flush plants with plain water to wash out built-up toxic salts. Flush again with a diluted nutrient solution. Leach growing medium every two to four weeks if using soft or saline water. Stop fertilization seven to ten days prior to harvest. Latent nutrient accumulation in foliage imparts a fertilizer-like taste. Products like Final Flush expedite the removal of built-up chemicals from cannabis plants.



## Chlorine and Chloramine

Municipal water supplies often contain chlorine or chloramine for disinfection. Chlorine can dissipate by leaving water in an open container for 24 hours, but chloramine is more stable and requires treatment. Chlorine and chloramine can harm beneficial soil microorganisms, particularly in organic growing systems. Use a carbon filter or dechlorination product to remove these chemicals. Some growers use vitamin C (ascorbic acid) to neutralize chloramine.



## Watering Best Practices

Irrigate soil and soilless mixes when dry one-half inch below the surface. Add a few drops of biodegradable liquid dish soap to irrigation water as a wetting agent to help it penetrate soil more thoroughly. Apply about one-quarter to one-half as much water as the plant needs, wait 10-15 minutes for it to soak in, then apply more until soil is evenly moist. Use a moisture meter to eliminate guesswork. Water early in the day so excess water evaporates before nightfall.



## Reverse Osmosis Water

Reverse osmosis (RO) water provides a clean baseline for nutrient mixing by removing 95-99 percent of dissolved solids, chlorine, chloramine, and other contaminants. RO systems force water through a semi-permeable membrane under pressure, producing nearly pure water. The drawback is that RO systems waste 2-4 gallons of water for every gallon of purified water produced. RO water must be calibrated with mineral supplements or nutrient solutions since it lacks the beneficial calcium and magnesium found in most water sources. For growers with consistently poor water quality, RO systems are a worthwhile investment.
