# Fungal Symbiosis with Plants

Fungi form the oldest and most widespread symbiotic relationships with plants on Earth. These associations range from mutualistic (both partners benefit) to parasitic (one partner harmed). The mycorrhizal symbiosis — established 400-460 million years ago — was critical in enabling plants to colonize land (Remy et al. 1994). This page covers the spectrum of fungal-plant symbioses beyond the mycorrhizal types detailed in [[mycorrhizal-fungi]].

## The Symbiosis Spectrum

Plant-fungal interactions exist on a continuum from mutualism to pathogenicity (Dighton, 2003):

| Type | Fungus benefits | Plant benefits | Examples |
|------|----------------|----------------|----------|
| **Mutualism** | Carbon, habitat | Nutrients, water, defense | Mycorrhizae, endophytes |
| **Commensalism** | Carbon, habitat | Minimal or none | Some root endophytes |
| **Parasitism** | Carbon, resources | Harmed, diseased | Pathogenic fungi |

The balance can shift along this continuum depending on environmental conditions, plant health, and nutrient availability. A mutualistic mycorrhiza under low-phosphorus conditions may become commensal or even weakly parasitic when phosphorus is abundant.

## Types of Fungal-Plant Symbiosis

### Ectomycorrhizae (ECM)
Detailed in [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]. Briefly: dense fungal sheath around root surfaces of woody plants (pines, oaks, beeches). Produce visible mushrooms (porcini, chanterelles, truffles). Critical for forest ecosystems.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae (AM/AMF)
Detailed in [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]. Briefly: penetrate root cells forming arbuscules. Associated with most crops, vegetables, grasses. Produce glomalin (27-30% of soil carbon in colonized soils). No above-ground fruiting body.

### Ericoid Mycorrhizae
- Associated with plants in the Ericaceae family (blueberries, cranberries, heath, heather, rhododendrons)
- Fungal hyphae penetrate epidermal cells of fine hair roots
- Enable plants to access organic nitrogen and phosphorus from acidic, low-nutrient soils
- The endophyte *Hymenoscyphus ericae* produces proteinases and phosphatases to break down organic matter
- Critical for plants in bogs, heathlands, and acidic forest understories
- Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi show significant heavy metal tolerance (Bradley et al. 1981)

### Orchid Mycorrhizae
- Every orchid requires a fungal partner for seed germination — orchid seeds have no endosperm
- The fungus provides carbon and nutrients to the seedling (reverse of typical mycorrhiza)
- Common fungal partners: *Rhizoctonia*, *Ceratobasidium* species
- Mature orchids may retain mycorrhizal associations or shift to partial independence
- Some orchids are fully mycoheterotrophic their entire lives (no photosynthesis)
- Orchid mycorrhizal fungi produce the siderophore basidiochrome for iron chelation (Haselwandter et al. 2006)

### Endophytic Fungi

Endophytes are fungi (and bacteria) that live within plant tissues without causing visible disease symptoms. They represent a largely unexplored frontier in plant biology.

**Leaf Endophytes:**
- Found in photosynthetic tissues of virtually all plant species examined
- In grasses: clavicipitaceous endophytes (e.g., *Neotyphodium/Epichloe*) produce alkaloids toxic to herbivores and grazing animals
- Infected grasses show increased drought tolerance (loline alkaloids act as osmoregulators — Belesky and Malinowski 2000)
- Endophytes alter apoplastic carbohydrate concentrations in host leaves (higher glucose and fructose in infected plants)
- Some endophytes defend against leaf-mining insects (Faeth and Hammon 1997)
- May influence plant community composition by altering competitive fitness of infected species (Clay 1997)

**Root Endophytes (non-mycorrhizal):**
- Dark septate endophytes (DSE) like *Phialocephala fortinii* colonize root cortices
- Produce the siderophore ferricrocin for iron acquisition (Bartholdy et al. 2001)
- Found in stressed environments (arctic, alpine, contaminated soils)
- Some DSEs may facilitate nutrient uptake similar to mycorrhizae, but the mechanisms are less understood
- Co-occur with mycorrhizal fungi; interactions between endophyte and mycorrhiza communities are complex

**Woody Plant Endophytes:**
- Found in stems, branches, and leaves of trees
- Arnold et al. (2001) found diverse endophyte communities in neotropical trees
- May provide defense against pathogens and influence decomposition rates
- Some transition from endophytic to saprotrophic lifestyle as leaves senesce

## Plant-Fungal Signaling

The molecular dialogue between plants and symbiotic fungi is a rapidly advancing field.

### How Plants Signal to Fungi
- **Strigolactones**: Plant root exudates that trigger hyphal branching in AM fungi (Akiyama et al. 2005). Also act as endogenous plant hormones regulating shoot branching
- **Flavonoids**: Released by legume roots; signal to both rhizobia (for nitrogen fixation) and mycorrhizal fungi
- **Root exudates**: General class of compounds (sugars, amino acids, organic acids) that attract and feed mycorrhizal fungi in the rhizosphere

### How Fungi Signal to Plants
- **Mycorrhizal factors (Myc factors)**: Lipochitooligosaccharides (LCOs) secreted by both rhizobia and AM fungi that activate plant symbiosis signaling pathways
- **Common Symbiosis Signaling Pathway (CSSP)**: Shared genetic pathway for both rhizobial and mycorrhizal symbioses. Involves calcium spiking in the nucleus, decoded by calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (CCaMK)
- **Effector proteins**: Some mycorrhizal fungi secrete effectors that suppress plant defense responses, allowing colonization

### Defense Suppression
- Plants recognize beneficial fungi differently from pathogens
- AM fungi temporarily activate plant defense pathways during early colonization, then suppress them
- This suppression may also provide systemic protection against other pathogens (induced systemic resistance)
- The same signaling pathways that enable symbiosis are derived from ancient pathogen defense mechanisms (Parniske 2008)

## Common Mycorrhizal Networks

Mycorrhizal mycelia can interconnect multiple plants of the same or different species, creating a "wood wide web" (Simard et al. 1997):

- **Carbon transfer**: Photosynthate moves from shaded to sun-exposed seedlings through shared ECM networks (up to 10% of carbon fixed)
- **Nutrient sharing**: Nitrogen and phosphorus transfer from nitrogen-fixing plants to neighbors
- **Seedling establishment**: Established trees may supply carbon and nutrients to seedlings via mycorrhizal networks
- **Allelopathy**: Some plants may use the network to transmit toxic compounds to competing species
- **Warning signals**: Plants under insect attack may send chemical signals through the network to neighbors, triggering defense responses

## Relevance to Homesteading

Understanding fungal symbiosis is foundational to living soil management:
- **No-till practices** preserve hyphal networks — see [[no-till-farming]]
- [[cover-cropping]] maintains living roots that feed mycorrhizal fungi year-round
- [[compost-tea-aact]] with fungal foods (kelp, humic acids) encourages mycorrhizal colonization
- Excessive phosphorus fertilizer (even organic) disrupts the plant-fungi carbon exchange
- See [[soil-food-web]] for the broader ecosystem context
