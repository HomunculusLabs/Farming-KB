## Bird Problems with Berry Fruit

Production of blueberries, strawberries, and raspberries attracts
fruit-eating species such as blackbirds, thrushes, starlings, and
parrots. There are several practical aids to reduction of crop
losses due to birds:

- Cage culture, which completely excludes birds
- Hawk kites flown above crops at critical ripening periods
- Self-pick systems that make bird losses economically tolerable

## Cage Culture System

Cage culture involves completely enclosing the berry crop area
with wire mesh. Growers of sweet cherries, blueberry, and table
quality raspberry all report economic advantage in completely
caging over the area of crop. Such an advantage gives yields of
up to 30 times that of open field conditions.

### Cage Design Specifications

- Typical cage dimensions are about 20 m by 10 m by 2.4 m high
- 10 mm mesh is used for the wire
- Piping plus mesh forms the structural frame

### Internal Polyculture

Within the cages, a polyculture of smallfruit plus espalier fruits
(nectarine, peach) is safe from birds and even large moth species.
One or two such cages will support a grower aiming for high quality
markets. Ground preparation for cage culture may involve:

- Ridging for species such as blueberry (ridges of 1.5 m wide,
  0.6 m high, with a drainpipe at base is ideal)
- Fitting of drip irrigation
- Deep mulch for pH adjustment and soil moisture conservation
- Careful schedule of liquid manures selected for crop

### Biological Pest Control Inside Cages

In cage culture, it is beneficial to use species such as tree
frogs, small lizards, and insectivorous birds (such as quail) in
the cages for pest control, and to select plants of high fruit
yield. Quail connect to [[mollison-designers-swale-spacing-and-contour-interval]], ensuring water retention on slopes.

## Cool Humid Berry Species

The cool humid climates support a great variety of berry crops:
gooseberry; red, black, and white currants; blueberry; cranberry;
cherry, salal, and service berry; and a variety of bramble berries.
Six to fifteen plants of 78 species yields a great deal of fresh
or frozen food of high vitamin C value. Many such rows can act as
[[mollison-designers-windbreak-configurations-for-crops-and-orchards]] in the garden.

Berries demand little but a humus-rich and somewhat acid soil,
high ammonia nitrogen (provided by birds and rodents in the wild
as urea), and a thick mulch to discourage grasses. Berries pioneer
for and protect seedling trees, so that advancing forest edges
often develop bramble and cane thickets. In the wild, Vaccinium
spp. (blueberries, cranberries) thrive in oxygen-poor soil with
specific heath-family root associates to provide nitrogen.

## Economic Considerations

Cage culture requires higher initial investment in materials and
construction but yields dramatically higher returns per unit area.
Self-pick systems require less capital but need good road access
and proximity to population centres. Both systems benefit from
mulch-based soil management, which reduces weed competition and
maintains the acidic soil conditions preferred by most berry
species. The choice between systems depends on scale, market
access, labour availability, and the specific berry species being
grown.
