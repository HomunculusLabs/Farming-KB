# Sugar and the Dominator Culture: Nature Mute and Cultural Psychosis

Terence McKenna argued that sugar was not merely an addictive substance
but a catalyst for a deeper cultural transformation — the entrenchment
of what he called the "ego-dominator" style of social organization.
Sugar's role, in McKenna's analysis, extended far beyond nutrition or
even economics; it became a symbol and accelerant of humanity's
progressive disconnection from the natural world.

## The Dominator Style

McKenna's theory of cultural evolution posits that human societies can
be characterized by their relationship to the natural world. Partnership
societies, which he associates with Archaic and indigenous cultures,
operate in balance with the vegetable matrix of planetary life. Dominator
societies, by contrast, are organized around ego, hierarchy, and the
subjugation of both nature and other human beings.

When temporal distance from the original "partnership paradise" increases,
McKenna argues, the hold of cultural neurosis deepens. The connection
with the vegetable/feminine matrix of planetary life slips into the
past, and manifestations of unchecked ego proliferate. Slavery, nearly
unknown during the medieval period when the notion of private property
restricted ownership to a privileged few, returned with a vengeance to
fill the need for manpower in the labor-intensive colonial cultivation
of sugar.

## The Philosophical Foundations of Domination

McKenna traces the intellectual lineage of dominator culture through
several key figures. Thomas Hobbes envisioned human society as the
inevitable subjugation of the weak by the strong — a social contract
born of fear and competition rather than cooperation. Jeremy Bentham's
notion of the ultimate economic basis of all social worth reduced human
value to a calculation of utility and pleasure.

These philosophical frameworks, in McKenna's view, signal that values
which seek to nurture the earth and participate with it in natural
emotive balance had been forsaken for the rapacious self-centeredness
of what he calls "Faustian science." The soul of the planet, already
diminished by Christian monotheism to the dimensions of a human being,
was finally denied any existence at all by the heirs of Cartesian
rationalism.

## Nature Is Not Mute

McKenna reserves particular scorn for Jean-Paul Sartre's assertion that
"nature is mute." This statement, in McKenna's reading, represents the
culmination of a process of deepening cultural psychosis — an obsession
with ego, money, and the sugar/alcohol drug complex that reaches its
apex in mid-twentieth century existentialism.

His response is unequivocal: nature is not mute, but modern humanity is
deaf — made deaf because it is unwilling to hear the message of caring,
balance, and cooperation that nature communicates. In a state of denial,
McKenna argues, we must proclaim nature mute — how else to avoid facing
the crimes committed for centuries against nature and each other?

He draws an explicit parallel between the Nazi assertion that Jews were
not true human beings and the industrial argument that environmental
destruction is inconsequential. Both rely on what he calls a
"dis-ensouling" strategy — denying the intrinsic value of the target
to make its exploitation psychologically tolerable.

## Sugar as Cultural Accelerant

In McKenna's framework, sugar reinforces dominator culture on multiple
levels simultaneously. Physiologically, it is addictive, creating cycles
of craving and consumption that mirror the cycles of acquisition and
exploitation that characterize dominator economies. Culturally, it is
defined as a food rather than a drug, which allows its addictive
properties to remain unexamined and its destructive consequences to be
externalized.

The synergy between sugar and caffeine is particularly significant.
Caffeine is bitter, and the discovery that it could be made palatable
with honey or sugar created a powerful positive feedback loop: sugar's
tendency toward addiction was reinforced by its use to mask the
bitterness of stimulating alkaloids. Together, these substances
supported the industrial civilization's emphasis on efficiency at the
expense of what McKenna calls "Archaic human values."

## The Ego-Drug Complex

McKenna identifies what he calls a "sugar/alcohol drug complex" that
underpins the psychological infrastructure of modern civilization. This
complex is not merely about chemical dependence but about a whole mode
of relating to the world — one based on extraction, stimulation, and
domination rather than reciprocity, balance, and participation.

Only a terminal addiction to the ego and styles of brutal domination
could give rise, in McKenna's view, to a mass mental environment in
which the assertion that nature is mute could appear plausible. Sugar
stands at the watershed of this transformation, reinforcing and
supporting industrial civilization's unreflecting emphasis on
productivity over presence.

## Sugar as Unnecessary

McKenna is careful to emphasize that sugar is entirely unnecessary to
the human diet. Before the arrival of industrial cane and beet sugar,
humanity managed well enough without refined sucrose. Sugar contributes
nothing nutritionally that cannot be obtained from other easily
available sources. It is, in McKenna's stark assessment, a "kick" and
nothing more.

Yet for this kick, the dominator culture of Europe was willing to
betray the ideals of the Enlightenment through its collusion with slave
traders. In 1800, virtually every ton of sugar imported into England had
been produced with slave labor. The ability of the ego-dominator culture
to suppress these realities McKenna finds nothing short of astonishing.

## A Distillation of Wrongheaded Attitudes

McKenna's argument about sugar is ultimately that it represents a
distillation of all the wrongheaded attitudes that attend human thinking
about drugs. We define sugar as food while demonizing other substances.
We externalize its costs while criminalizing their users. We celebrate
the cultures it built while ignoring the suffering it required. Sugar's
real significance, for McKenna, lies not in its chemistry but in what
it reveals about the civilization that cannot live without it.
