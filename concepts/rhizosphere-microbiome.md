# Rhizosphere Microbiome

The rhizosphere microbiome refers to the diverse community of microorganisms
including bacteria, fungi, archaea, and viruses that inhabit the narrow zone
of soil directly influenced by plant root secretions. First conceptualized by
Lorenz Hiltner in 1904, the rhizosphere is one of the most complex and
biologically active ecosystems on Earth, supporting microbial densities up to
1,000 times greater than bulk soil. This [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] plays fundamental
roles in plant nutrition, disease suppression, stress tolerance, and nutrient
cycling.

## Defining the Rhizosphere

The rhizosphere is not a uniform zone but rather a gradient of microbial
activity radiating outward from the root surface, divided into three
compartments with distinct communities and [[fungal-melanin-biosynthesis-ecological-functions]].

The **endorhiza** (endosphere) consists of microbes that have colonized the
internal root tissue itself, living within the apoplast or between plant cells.
The **rhizoplane** is the root surface where microbes adhere directly to the
epidermis and root hairs. The **rhizosphere proper** is the thin layer of soil
(typically 1-2 mm) immediately surrounding the root that is chemically
modified by root exudates.

[[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] become progressively more selective moving inward. Bulk
soil contains the most diverse communities, the rhizosphere represents a first
level of selection driven by root exudates, and the endorhiza represents a
second level of selection driven by host plant genotype.

## Root Exudates and the Rhizosphere Effect

Plants actively shape their rhizosphere microbiome through root exudates --
sugars (glucose, fructose, sucrose), amino acids, organic acids (citrate,
malate, oxalate), phenolics, flavonoids, enzymes, mucilage, and secondary
metabolites. Root exudation can account for 10-40% of total photosynthetically
fixed carbon, representing a significant carbon investment by the plant.

This massive carbon input creates the "rhizosphere effect" -- the enrichment
of microbial populations near roots compared to bulk soil. The specific
composition of exudates varies between species, cultivars, developmental
stages, and environmental stresses, meaning each plant cultivates its own
unique microbial community.

## Key Microbial Functions

**Nitrogen fixation** is performed by diazotrophic bacteria (Rhizobium,
Azotobacter, Azospirillum) that convert atmospheric N2 into available forms.
Free-living diazotrophs contribute up to 20-50 kg N/ha/year in some systems.

**Phosphate solubilization** is carried out by bacteria and fungi releasing
organic acids or phosphatase enzymes to dissolve insoluble mineral phosphates.
Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and mycorrhizal fungi are particularly important.

**Phytohormone production** includes synthesis of auxins (indole-3-acetic acid),
cytokinins, gibberellins, and ethylene regulators. Approximately 80% of
[[lowenfels-rhizosphere-bacteria-plant-interaction]] can produce IAA, the most common growth-promoting trait.

**Biocontrol** involves pathogen suppression through antibiotic production
(2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol by Pseudomonas), competition for nutrients,
lytic enzyme production (chitinases, glucanases), and induction of systemic
resistance in the host plant.

**[[singh-fungal-siderophore-production-iron-acquisition]]** uses high-affinity iron-chelating compounds that
sequester environmental iron, making it available to the plant while denying
it to pathogenic microorganisms.

## The Two-Tier Selection Model

Evidence supports a two-tier selection model for rhizosphere assembly. In the
first tier, [[cannabinoid-microbiome-correlation-confounded-edaphic-factors]] (soil type, pH, texture, organic matter, moisture,
salinity, [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]]) determine bulk soil microbial composition.
This serves as the source pool from which rhizosphere microbes are drawn.

In the second tier, plant genotype exerts selective pressure shaping which
soil-derived microbes colonize the rhizosphere and root interior. Cannabis
studies showed soil type as the strongest community determinant, while
cultivar identity primarily determined relative abundance patterns within the
endorhiza. Diversity decreases from bulk soil to rhizosphere to endorhiza as
each transition represents an additional selective filter.
