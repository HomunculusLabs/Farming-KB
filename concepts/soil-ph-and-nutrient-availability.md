# Soil pH and Nutrient Availability

## Overview

[[soil-ph]] — the measure of hydrogen ion concentration — is one of the most fundamental properties of soil, directly controlling the solubility and availability of essential plant nutrients. Understanding pH dynamics is critical for managing productive gardens, pastures, and broadacre agricultural systems.

## The pH Scale

The pH scale ranges from **0 (strongly acidic)** to **14 (strongly alkaline)**, with 7.0 as neutral. In natural systems, readings rarely fall below 1.9 (citrus juice) or above 11.0 (alkali flats). Most cultivated soils fall between pH 4.5 and 10.0, with the sweet spot for most vegetables being **pH 6.0–7.5**. Outside the range of pH 4.0–10.0, only specialized bacteria and highly adapted organisms can survive.

## Acids and Alkalis in Soil

### Metallic Oxides (Bases)
Metallic oxides form **alkaline solutions**. The common soluble bases in soil include:
- Magnesium
- Calcium
- Potassium
- Barium
- Sodium
- Selenium

These give up hydroxide ions (OH⁻), turning litmus blue and raising soil pH.

### Non-Metallic Oxides (Acids)
Non-metallic oxides form **acidic solutions**. The common soluble acid compounds include:
- Silica (forms silicic acid)
- Sulphur (forms sulphuric acid)
- Carbon dioxide (forms carbonic acid)
- Phosphorus (forms phosphoric acid)
- Nitrogen (forms nitric acid)
- Chlorine (forms hydrochloric acid)

These release excess hydrogen ions (H⁺), turning litmus red and lowering soil pH.

## How Acids Form in Natural Systems

Rainfall is a primary driver of soil acidification. As rain falls, atmospheric carbon dioxide dissolves to form **weak carbonic acid** — the same compound found in soft drinks. This carbonic acid helps dissolve metallic oxides in soil, bringing rock and soil minerals into solution where they become available to plants.

Industrial sulphur emissions, pyrite minerals in rocks, and organic decomposition all contribute additional acids. In moderation, all of these acids are beneficial for nutrient supply to plants. Notably, weak nitric acid acts as a **plant growth stimulant**.

## pH is Not Static

A critical insight from soil science is that pH is **never uniform or constant**. It varies across multiple dimensions:

- **Temporal variation**: Diurnal (daily) and seasonal changes driven by rainfall, plant growth, and temperature fluctuations.
- **Spatial mosaics**: pH varies dramatically across soil [[crumb-structure]], on colloid surfaces, and at individual microsites — sometimes within millimetres.
- **Vertical gradients**: Surface mulches tend to be more acidic, while deeper layers can be more alkaline where evaporation, worm casts, and capillary action draw bases upward.
- **Landscape patterns**: Slope position, rock type, and vegetation type all impose larger-scale pH mosaics.

Root hairs are remarkable in their ability to both **create** and **seek out** ideal pH environments, provided there are no gross imbalances. This means a healthy garden soil naturally contains a diverse range of pH levels at different locations.

## Measuring pH

Standard pH measurement involves grinding mixed soil samples to damp pastes or measuring electrical conductivity. These methods produce an **average** pH value, but as with all measurements, the act of sampling alters the very thing being measured. The averaged figure masks the rich mosaic that actually exists in the soil.

## pH and Plant Nutrient Availability

The relationship between pH and nutrient availability is not linear. Each nutrient has an optimal pH range for maximum availability, illustrated in nutrient availability charts (originally from Nelson, 1968, for mineral soils, and Lucas & Davis, 1961, for organic soils).

- At extreme pH values (below 4.0 or above 10.0), most nutrients become locked up or unavailable.
- In the pH 6.0–7.5 range, the widest spectrum of plant nutrients remains available.
- Specific micronutrients like iron, manganese, and zinc become more available at lower pH, while molybdenum becomes more available at higher pH.

## Buffers: Nature's pH Stabilizers

Two key substances buffer soil pH against rapid changes:

### Humus
Soil humus is itself a powerful buffer. It can maintain satisfactory plant growth even when pH fluctuates, because humic compounds moderate the chemical environment around root zones.

### Calcium (Lime)
Calcium acts as a long-term pH buffer. Limed and mulched gardens rarely show plant deficiency symptoms precisely because these buffering systems keep nutrients available across a wider pH range.

## Practical Implications for Soil Management

1. **Don't obsess over a single pH number** — healthy soil is a mosaic, not a monolith.
2. **Mulch and compost are your best tools** — they buffer pH while adding nutrients and feeding soil life.
3. **Keep pH within the 4.5–10.0 range** as a broad target; aim for 6.0–7.5 for intensive vegetable production.
4. **Calcium is essential** — even outside the optimal pH range, calcium presence enables a much wider range of plants to grow.
5. **Trust the plants** — foliage deficiency symptoms are more reliable indicators of nutrient problems than pH tests alone.
