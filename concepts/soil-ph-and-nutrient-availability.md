## Soil pH and Nutrient Availability

- **Landscape patterns**: Slope position, rock type, and vegetation type all impose larger-scale pH mosaics.

Root hairs are remarkable in their ability to both **create** and **seek out** ideal pH environments, provided there are no gross imbalances. This means a healthy garden soil naturally contains a diverse range of pH levels at different locations.

## Measuring pH

Standard pH measurement involves grinding mixed soil samples to damp pastes or measuring electrical conductivity. These methods produce an **average** pH value, but as with all measurements, the act of sampling alters the very thing being measured. The averaged figure masks the rich mosaic that actually exists in the soil.

## pH and Plant Nutrient Availability

The relationship between pH and nutrient availability is not linear. Each nutrient has an optimal pH range for maximum availability, illustrated in nutrient availability charts (originally from Nelson, 1968, for mineral soils, and Lucas & Davis, 1961, for organic soils).

- At extreme pH values (below 4.0 or above 10.0), most nutrients become locked up or unavailable.
- In the pH 6.0–7.5 range, the widest spectrum of plant nutrients remains available.
- Specific micronutrients like iron, manganese, and zinc become more available at lower pH, while molybdenum becomes more available at higher pH.

## Buffers: Nature's pH Stabilizers

Two key substances buffer soil pH against rapid changes:

### Humus
Soil humus is itself a powerful buffer. It can maintain satisfactory plant growth even when pH fluctuates, because humic compounds moderate the chemical environment around root zones.

### Calcium (Lime)
Calcium acts as a long-term pH buffer. Limed and mulched gardens rarely show plant deficiency symptoms precisely because these buffering systems keep nutrients available across a wider pH range.

## Practical Implications for Soil Management

1. **Don't obsess over a single pH number** — healthy soil is a mosaic, not a monolith.
2. **Mulch and compost are your best tools** — they buffer pH while adding nutrients and feeding soil life.
3. **Keep pH within the 4.5–10.0 range** as a broad target; aim for 6.0–7.5 for intensive vegetable production.
4. **Calcium is essential** — even outside the optimal pH range, calcium presence enables a much wider range of plants to grow.
5. **Trust the plants** — foliage deficiency symptoms are more reliable indicators of nutrient problems than pH tests alone.
