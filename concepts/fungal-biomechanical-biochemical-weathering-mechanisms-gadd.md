# Fungal Biomechanical and Biochemical Weathering Mechanisms

Fungi are among the most effective biological agents of rock and mineral
weathering, employing a dual strategy of biomechanical force and biochemical
attack that operates synergistically to [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]] geological substrates.
This combination gives fungi advantages over bacteria and abiotic processes,
enabling them to weather minerals at higher pH values and across a wider
redox range than prokaryotes. The study of these mechanisms — geomycology
— reveals how microscopic organisms shape the Earth's surface over
geological timescales and influence the availability of essential nutrients
for all terrestrial life.

## Biomechanical weathering

Fungi can physically disrupt mineral substrates through several mechanisms.
Hyphal penetration into rock pores, cracks, and fissures exerts turgor
pressure that can widen existing fractures and create new ones. As hyphae
grow, they expand within confined spaces, generating forces sufficient to
separate mineral grains along crystal boundaries. This process is
particularly effective in porous rocks like sandstone and limestone, where
pre-existing pore networks provide entry points for fungal colonization.

Biomechanical weathering can be both direct and indirect. Direct effects
include the physical disruption of mineral grains by penetrating hyphae and
the expansion of hyphal biomass within rock cavities. Indirect effects arise
from the production of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and
mucilaginous sheaths that surround hyphae. These hydrated polymers undergo
wetting and drying cycles that generate shrink-swell stresses at the
rock-fungus interface, gradually prying mineral grains apart.

The filamentous growth habit of fungi is central to their biomechanical
effectiveness. Unlike bacteria, which grow as discrete cells, [[fungal-hyphae]]
form continuous tubular structures that can exert sustained directional
force. The ability to form linear organs of aggregated hyphae — cords and
rhizomorphs — further concentrates mechanical force, allowing some fungi
to bore through relatively coherent rock substrates.

## Biochemical weathering

Biochemical weathering operates through the excretion of metabolites that
chemically attack mineral structures. The two primary biochemical
mechanisms are proton-promoted dissolution and ligand-promoted dissolution.

Proton-promoted dissolution occurs when fungi excrete protons (H+),
lowering the pH at the hyphal-mineral interface. Acidification weakens
metal-oxygen bonds in silicate and oxide minerals, accelerating their
dissolution. Fungi generate protons through several metabolic pathways,
including the respiration-linked extrusion of protons to maintain charge
balance during ammonium uptake, and the oxidation of [[dighton-fungal-dissolved-organic-carbon-production]]
compounds.

Ligand-promoted dissolution involves the excretion of [[fungal-organic-acid-metal-solubilization]] anions
and other chelating agents that form soluble complexes with metal cations
in mineral lattices. When a chelator binds to a structural [[fungal-metal-ion-resistance-mechanisms]], it
reduces the saturation state of the mineral at the surface, driving further
dissolution. Oxalic acid is particularly important because it both
acidifies the environment and forms strong complexes with calcium, iron,
and aluminum. Citric, malic, succinic, and gluconic acids contribute
additional chelating capacity.

## Synergistic interaction of mechanisms

Biomechanical and biochemical weathering do not operate independently. The
physical penetration of hyphae into rock creates fresh [[fungal-community-profiling-rock-mineral-surfaces-gadd]]
exposed to biochemical attack, while biochemical dissolution weakens mineral
structures, making them more susceptible to mechanical disruption. This
synergy means that the combined weathering effect is greater than the sum of
either mechanism alone.

Fungal hyphae penetrate along grain boundaries and crystal planes that have
already been weakened by chemical dissolution. Conversely, the physical
separation of mineral grains by [[bloomfield-fungal-biology-and-hyphal-growth]] increases the surface area
available for acid attack. This feedback loop accelerates weathering
progressively as fungal colonization advances deeper into the substrate.

## Rock types susceptible to fungal weathering

Fungi have been documented weathering an extraordinarily wide range of rock
and mineral types. These include limestone, soapstone, marble, granite,
sandstone, andesite, basalt, gneiss, dolerite, amphibolite, and quartz.
Alkaline (basic) rocks appear more susceptible to fungal attack than acidic
rocks. Specific minerals known to be degraded by fungi include silicate
minerals (mica, orthoclase, feldspar), iron and manganese-bearing minerals
(biotite, olivine, pyroxene), and phosphates (apatite).
