# Fungal Metal Redox Transformations — Detailed

## Overview

Fungi mediate a remarkable range of metal transformations in the environment, including oxidation, reduction, methylation, complexation, precipitation, and solubilization. These processes fundamentally influence metal mobility, toxicity, and bioavailability in soils and aquatic systems. This page draws primarily from Gadd's comprehensive treatment of fungal roles in metal biogeochemistry. See [[fungal-roles-in-metal-biogeochemistry]] for the broader context.

## Solubilization Mechanisms

### Organic Acid Production
Fungi produce a broad spectrum of organic acids that solubilize metals from minerals and solid matrices:

- **Citric acid**: Derived from the TCA cycle; strong chelator of Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, and other trivalent metals
- **Oxalic acid**: Common metabolite; forms insoluble metal oxalates with Ca²⁺, Cu²⁺, and other metals
- **Gluconic acid**: Produced from glucose by glucose oxidase; effective at mobilizing metals from fly ash
- **Malic, fumaric, succinic, tartaric, and itaconic acids**: Additional TCA cycle-derived leaching agents

In soil environments, organic acids reach millimolar concentrations near fungal hyphae and plant roots. Fungal organic acids can create 3–10 km pores in feldspars and hornblendes (Jongmans et al., 1997).

### Ligand-Induced Solubilization
Metal mobilization occurs through three principal mechanisms:

1. **Proton-induced solubilization**: H⁺ from organic acids displaces metals from mineral surfaces
2. **Ligand-induced solubilization**: Organic acids form soluble metal-ligand complexes (e.g., metal-citrate, metal-gluconate)
3. **Redoxolysis**: Redox reactions change metal oxidation states, affecting solubility

Metal-citrate complexes are particularly mobile in soil, which raises concerns about unintended metal dispersion during bioremediation.

### Risk Considerations
Solubilization processes can release metals from stabilized forms. For example:
- Rock phosphate amendments can release cadmium through organic acid attack
- Pyromorphite (Pb₅(PO₄)₃Cl), a stable lead form, is solubilized by A. niger — potentially undermining phosphate-based lead remediation strategies

## Oxidation and Reduction

### Metal Oxidation
Fungi can oxidize reduced metal species:

- **Manganese(II) to Mn(III/IV)**: Catalyzed by manganese peroxidase (MnP) and laccase enzymes in white-rot fungi. Mn(III)-organic acid chelates act as diffusible oxidants.
- **Iron(II) to Fe(III)**: Mediated by fungal siderophores and phenol oxidase activity
- **Arsenic(III) to As(V)**: Some fungi oxidize more toxic As(III) to less toxic As(V)

### Metal Reduction
Fungi reduce oxidized metal species through enzymatic and non-enzymatic pathways:

- **Mercury(II) to Hg(0)**: Volatilization via mercuric reductase enzymes; some fungi produce dimethylmercury
- **Silver(I) to Ag(0)**: Reduction to elemental silver nanoparticles
- **Gold(III) to Au(0)**: Fungi like Verticillium and Fusarium can precipitate gold nanoparticles
- **Uranium(VI) to U(IV)**: Reduction to less soluble U(IV) by enzymatic processes
- **Chromium(VI) to Cr(III)**: Reduction of toxic Cr(VI) to less toxic Cr(III) by cell-associated reductases; also occurs non-enzymatically at low pH in presence of biosorbent material
- **Selenium oxyanions**: Reduction to elemental selenium (Se⁰) by various fungi

## Methylation and Volatilization

Fungi can convert metals and metalloids into volatile methylated species:

- **Mercury**: Biomethylation to methylmercury (CH₃Hg⁺) and dimethylmercury ((CH₃)₂Hg), which are highly toxic and bioaccumulate in food chains
- **Arsenic**: Methylation to mono-, di-, and trimethylarsine; fungi produce volatile arsenicals from both inorganic and organic arsenic compounds
- **Selenium**: Methylation to dimethyl selenide ((CH₃)₂Se), which is volatile and less toxic
- **Tin**: Biomethylation of inorganic tin and organotin compounds

The methylation capacity of fungi has significant implications for metal/metalloid cycling and entry into food chains.

## Complexation and Binding

### Extracellular Metal-Binding Molecules
Fungi secrete a range of extracellular compounds that bind metals:

- **Siderophores**: High-affinity Fe(III)-chelating compounds (ferrichromes, fusigen, coprogens, rhodotorulic acid) that also bind other metals including Al³⁺, Ga³⁺, In³⁺, actinides, and lanthanides
- **Organic acids**: Citrate, oxalate, and other acids form soluble complexes with metals
- **Exopolysaccharides**: Slime matrices that bind and concentrate metals at cell surfaces
- **Melanin**: Cell wall-associated pigment with high metal-binding capacity, particularly for radionuclides
- **Metallothioneins**: Low-molecular-weight, cysteine-rich intracellular proteins that bind Cu²⁺, Cd²⁺, Zn²⁺, and other metals
- **Phytochelatins**: Metal-induced peptides (structure: (γ-Glu-Cys)n-Gly) that complex intracellular metals; also explored as metal detection tools

## Immobilization Mechanisms

### Precipitation
Fungi promote metal immobilization through precipitation:

- **Sulfide precipitation**: Sulfate-reducing conditions produce H₂S, which precipitates metals as insoluble sulfides
- **Phosphate precipitation**: Soluble phosphatase activity releases phosphate that precipitates metals (e.g., lead phosphate)
- **Polyphosphate granules**: Intracellular polyphosphate bodies sequester metals
- **Oxalate precipitation**: Formation of insoluble metal oxalate crystals (e.g., copper oxalate, zinc oxalate) at cell surfaces and in surrounding medium

### Biosorption
Non-metabolism-dependent binding to cell walls concentrates metals from solution. See [[fungal-chromogens-and-color-change]]
