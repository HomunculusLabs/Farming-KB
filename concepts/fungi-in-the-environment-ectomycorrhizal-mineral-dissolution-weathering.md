# Ectomycorrhizal Mineral Dissolution and Rock Weathering

Nearly all land plants depend on symbiotic mycorrhizal fungi for access to
mineral nutrients. Among the various mycorrhizal types, ectomycorrhizal fungi
are particularly active agents of mineral weathering, capable of dissolving
soil minerals and mobilizing nutrients through proton and organic acid
excretion, metal complexation, and accumulation within their mycelia. Their
distinctive morphology, with an extraradical mycelial network extending far
beyond the root into the soil matrix, positions them at the front line of
plant-mineral interactions in forest ecosystems worldwide.

## Ectomycorrhizal Morphology and Mineral Access

Ectomycorrhizas are characterized by a fungal sheath that entirely encloses
root tips and a Hartig net of hyphae that penetrates between root cortical
cells without entering the cells themselves. Beyond this intimate root
interface, the extraradical mycelium extends into the surrounding soil as
individual hyphae and aggregated rhizomorphs. This extensive mycelial network
vastly increases the absorptive surface area available for mineral exploration
compared to roots alone. The hyphal tips, often operating within soil
micropores three to ten micrometres in diameter, create chemically distinct
microenvironments where mineral dissolution can proceed at rates far exceeding
bulk soil processes.

## Proton-Promoted and Ligand-Promoted Dissolution

Ectomycorrhizal fungi mediate mineral weathering through two primary
biochemical mechanisms operating simultaneously. Proton-promoted dissolution
occurs as fungi excrete protons into their rhizosphere, acidifying the
microenvironment around mineral surfaces. This acidification derives from
excess cation uptake, organic acid excretion, and respiratory carbon dioxide
that forms carbonic acid in soil solution. Ligand-promoted dissolution
involves the excretion of metal-complexing organic ligands that attack mineral
surfaces directly, weakening metal-oxygen bonds and solubilizing structural
cations.

During growth, mycorrhizal fungi excrete a suite of low-molecular-mass
carboxylic acids including malic, succinic, gluconic, and oxalic acid. This
process is termed heterotrophic leaching and represents a major pathway for
nutrient acquisition from insoluble mineral sources. In podzol E horizons
beneath European coniferous forests, the weathering of hornblendes, feldspars,
and granitic bedrock has been attributed specifically to these organic acids
excreted by ectomycorrhizal hyphae. Hyphal tips produce concentrations ranging
from micromolar to millimolar, sufficient to dissolve primary silicate minerals
within the restricted volume of soil micropores.

## Responses to Different Mineral Substrates

Ectomycorrhizal mycelia regulate their growth and activity in response to the
mineral substrates they encounter. Carbon allocation within the mycelium of
Hebeloma crustuliniforme in symbiosis with Scots pine was significantly greater
when colonizing potassium feldspar patches compared to quartz patches, reflecting
the greater nutritional reward offered by the weatherable silicate. The
ectomycorrhizal fungus Piloderma extracted potassium and magnesium from biotite,
microcline, and chlorite to satisfy its nutritional requirements and
precipitated mycogenic calcium oxalate crystals on the hyphae. Suillus
granulatus and Paxillus involutus released potassium, calcium, titanium,
manganese, and lead from apatite and wood ash, accumulating these elements
especially in their rhizomorphs.

## Zinc Phosphate Dissolution and Phosphorus Acquisition

Mobilization of phosphorus is regarded as one of the most important functions
of ectomycorrhizal fungi. A detailed experimental study of zinc phosphate
dissolution by the Paxillus involutus and Scots pine association demonstrated
that phosphate mineral dissolution, phosphorus acquisition, and zinc
accumulation by the plant depended on three interacting factors: the
mycorrhizal status of the pines, the zinc tolerance of the fungal strain, and
the phosphorus status of the environment.

Under phosphorus-replete conditions, ectomycorrhizal roots accumulated
significantly higher amounts of phosphorus than non-mycorrhizal roots. Total
zinc mobilization was least for the ectomycorrhizal association with a
zinc-tolerant strain, which appeared to employ a toxic metal avoidance
strategy restricting metal entry into cells, thereby protecting plant tissues
from excessive zinc accumulation. Under phosphorus deficiency, however, the
zinc-tolerant ectomycorrhiza mobilized and accumulated the highest amount of
zinc, demonstrating that the balance between nutrient acquisition and metal
toxicity protection is strongly modulated by phosphorus availability.

## Metal Toxicity Amelioration

Ectomycorrhizal fungi protect host plants from toxic metals through multiple
mechanisms operating at the mycorrhizal interface. The hyphal sheath and Hartig
net can filter toxic metals through biosorption to cell walls and extracellular
polysaccharides. The hydrophobicity of the fungal sheath restricts metal
mobility into root tissues. Organic acids and other substances released by the
fungus can complex metals in the rhizosphere, reducing their bioavailability.
Most studies demonstrating amelioration of metal toxicity to hosts have been
most efficient when the mycobiont was itself metal-resistant.

## Applications in Revegetation and Remediation

The capacity of ectomycorrhizal fungi to enhance plant growth on contaminated
soils and to immobilize or accumulate pollutants has generated interest in
their application for re-vegetation, re-forestation, and phyto- and
rhizo-remediation strategies. Metal-resistant ectomycorrhizal strains can
simultaneously improve plant nutrition while reducing metal uptake, enabling
tree establishment on degraded or contaminated sites. However, mycorrhizal
interactions are variable and context-dependent, and their outcomes may shift
in response to both biotic and abiotic factors, including the specific
mycobiont and host genotype, soil chemistry, and the nature and concentration
of the contaminant.
