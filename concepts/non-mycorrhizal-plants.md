# Non-Mycorrhizal Plants

Plants that do not form mycorrhizal associations — the exceptions to the rule that 80-95% of terrestrial plants partner with fungi. Understanding these plants is important for crop rotation, companion planting, and managing mycorrhizal populations. See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for the mycorrhizal overview and [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]] for agricultural management.

## Why Some Plants Don't Need Mycorrhizae

These plants have evolved alternative strategies for nutrient acquisition:
- **Extensive, fast-growing root systems** with dense root hairs that explore soil pores efficiently
- **Cluster roots** (proteas, lupines) — closely spaced lateral roots covered in root hairs that pump out organic acid exudates
- **Carnivorous capabilities** — obtain nutrients from aboveground organisms (insects)
- **Epiphytic nutrition** — grab nutrients from air and rain (some orchids)
- **Parasitic strategies** — tap into other plants' resources
- **Specialized root structures** — dauciform roots (sedges), capillaroid roots (rushes), sand-binding roots

## Families That Do Not Form Mycorrhizae

| Family | Examples | Notes |
|--------|----------|-------|
| Brassicaceae | Mustard, kale, cabbage, broccoli, radish, rapeseed | Some release allelopathic chemicals that prevent mycorrhizal spore germination |
| Caryophyllaceae | Pinks, carnations | Extensive root hair systems |
| Chenopodiaceae (Amaranthaceae) | Amaranth, goosefoot, spinach | Specialized root structures |
| Polygonaceae | Knotweed, buckwheat | Allelopathic when decomposing |
| Portulacaceae | Purslane | Dense root hair coverage |
| Proteaceae | Proteas, banksias, macadamia | Cluster roots with phosphate-solubilizing bacteria |
| Cyperaceae | Sedges, papyrus | Dauciform roots (carrot-shaped, dense root hairs) |
| Restionaceae | Restios (South African rushes) | Capillaroid root mats at soil surface |

## Allelopathic Risks

Several non-mycorrhizal plants release chemicals that actively harm mycorrhizal fungi:

- **Garlic mustard** (*Alliaria petiolata*) — an invasive species in North America. Its root exudates include allelopathic chemicals that kill the mycorrhizal symbionts of native trees (maples, etc.). In Minnesota, introduced Asian earthworms reduce forest duff too quickly, creating bare soil where garlic mustard thrives, amplifying the damage to mycorrhizal networks.

- **Brassicas** (mustards, rapeseed, radishes) — release allelopathic chemicals during decomposition that may prevent mycorrhizal spore germination. These chemicals persist in soil after plants are removed.

- **Buckwheat** (*Fagopyrum esculentum*) — does not form mycorrhizae and its decomposition may inhibit mycorrhizal establishment.

## Agricultural Implications

### Crop Rotation

When planning rotations, avoid consecutive non-host crops to prevent mycorrhizal population decline. After a brassica or beet crop, follow with a strongly mycorrhizal crop (corn, legumes) and consider inoculation if the soil has been fallow.

### Companion Planting

Non-mycorrhizal plants won't benefit from mycorrhizal inoculation. They can be planted in areas where mycorrhizal populations are low without disadvantage. See [[companion-planting-reference]] and [[companion-planting-reference]] for compatibility data.

### Weed Management

Some troublesome weeds (knotweed, purslane) are non-mycorrhizal — they don't need the fungal network and can thrive even in degraded soils where mycorrhizal populations are suppressed.

## Alternative Nutrient Strategies

### Cluster Roots (Proteaceae, Lupinus)

Proteas and lupines form closely spaced lateral roots near the soil surface covered in dense root hairs. These support phosphate-solubilizing bacteria in their rhizospheres and release organic acid exudates that dissolve organic matter to access phosphorus directly.

### Dauciform Roots (Cyperaceae)

Some sedges form swollen, carrot-shaped lateral roots densely covered in root hairs that exude organic acids for nutrient acquisition.

### Sand-Binding Roots

Some sedges and rushes in Western Australia have roots that literally bind sand particles, which scientists hypothesize enhances nutrient uptake through the attached mineral surfaces.
