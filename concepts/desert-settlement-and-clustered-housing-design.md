# Desert Settlement and Clustered Housing Design

The arrangement of buildings within desert settlements profoundly affects thermal comfort, energy consumption, and liveability. The characteristic "colony of swallows' nests" appearance of traditional arid-area settlements — from Asia and India to the Middle East, the Mediterranean, and the Americas — is not coincidental. This clustered morphology has independently arisen across desert cultures because it represents an optimal response to extreme heat, intense sun, and drying winds. Modern [[wadi]] (dry watercourse) site with adequate run-off
- Flat sites in both hot and cold deserts can be very cold at night
- A **[[thermal-belt]]** exists approximately 10–20 m above the peneplain, with a sharply defined frost line

### Ideal Site Characteristics

- **Wadi sites**: Excellent in very hot deserts; narrow east-west wadis are particularly well-shaded
- **Fold mountain foothills**: Need a run-off area or spring nearby
- **Main considerations**: Potential for water harvest and storage on or near the house site (excluding deep groundwaters), equable climate, and variety of aspects and soils
- **Cliff and scarp bases**: Rock shelters "built in" with mud brick provide cool, secure dwellings safe from flooding, rain, and sun

## Modern Linear Settlements

Where cultures prefer more space and privacy, linear arrangements can achieve similar thermal performance:

- A line of bungalows or cabins on an **east-west axis**
- **Dense strips of vegetation** on the shade side provide wind protection and cooling
- The site itself must be correctly landscaped for the strategy to work
- **Village Homes in Davis, California** is cited as an ideal model for this approach

## Street and Road Design

### Narrow Streets

- Roads and car parks must be **shaded and therefore narrow**
- Broad-paved (bituminous) boulevards create uncontrollable local heat islands
- Narrow streets with 2–3 storey buildings provide continuous shade
- Trellis and lattice shading over lanes creates pleasant, practical cool refuges

### Legal Standards

- In Davis, California, at least 60% of paved areas must be shaded by law
- White canvas-covered markets of Istanbul are models of good commercial area design
- Painting roof areas or top surfaces of lattice white reflects heat back above the settlement

## Vegetation Around Desert Houses

Strategic vegetation placement is critical for thermal performance:

### West Side (Heat Protection)

- The low westerly afternoon sun adds the most heat to buildings
- **No windows** in west walls to avoid direct heat gain
- West wall should be **painted white** and fully shaded
- Permanent screens of **evergreen trees**, thick-leaved vines, or turf banks shade and shelter the west wall

### East Side (Morning Sun)

- Can be part-shaded by **deciduous trees** or vines
- Small windows are acceptable
- Morning sun is welcome for warming after cold desert nights

### Sun Side (Winter Heating)

- Glazing is most useful on the sun (equator-facing) side
- 100% glazing in cold desert areas, down to 25% in warm areas
- **Deciduous vines** on the sun side prevent excessive heating in summer while allowing winter sun penetration
- External (not internal) bamboo, wooden, or aluminium blinds prevent heat entry on hot winter days

### Shade Side

- The shade side is where the **enclosed trellis** (shadehouse) is built
- Dense vegetation strips provide cooling air for the house

## Building Orientation and Form

- Houses should be **elongated east-west** so that winter sun can reach all room floors
- **Eave width** on the sun side permits winter sun to enter rooms while excluding summer sun
- Houses can be very compact when shaded outdoor trellis areas are extensive
- An excellent design feature places the **winter kitchen indoors** but opening to a screened-in **summer kitchen** under thickly trellised areas
- Multiple storeys are appropriate on restricted sites (wadi banks, cliff bases, small valleys)
- Single storey housing suits more open sites but needs dense vegetation or trellis on the shade side

## Integration of House, Water, and Crops

The desert house functions most effectively as an integrated system rather than a standalone structure:

- Flat parapet roof areas serve for grain drying, laundry, pigeon lofts, and recreation
- Trellis systems provide shade, cooling, and food production
- Water cisterns on the shade side provide cool drinking water
- Vine crops integrate with roof and wall trellis systems
- Shadehouses serve as summer living areas and cool-air sources simultaneously

## The Pitfalls of Modern Design

Modern desert housing frequently ignores these time-tested principles, resulting in:

- Deforestation from excessive firewood consumption for heating
- Reliance on fossil fuels and continuing pollution
- Financial strain on poor and low-income families (energy can be 30–40% of household expenditure)
- Health effects from smoky indoor fires
- Illness from cold and tiredness after cold nights in poorly insulated dwellings
- Uncontrollable local heat from broad bituminous boulevards and parking areas

## Summary

Desert settlement design is fundamentally about creating shade, controlling airflow, and integrating vegetation with built form. The clustered, multi-storey settlement pattern with narrow east-west streets represents the thermally optimal arrangement for dense living, while linear layouts with dense vegetation buffers serve cultures that prefer more space. In all cases, the desert house works best as a compact night-time retreat integrated with extensive shaded outdoor living areas, where vegetation, orientation, and building mass combine to maintain comfort without mechanical energy input.
