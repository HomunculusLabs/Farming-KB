# Medicinal Mushrooms

From Chapter 4 of *Mycelium Running* by [[paul-stamets]]. Mushrooms contain polysaccharides, glycoproteins, proteoglycans, ergosterols, triterpenoids, enzymes, acids, and antibiotics. Each species has a "signature architecture and defense against microbes."

## Antiviral Activity

Mushrooms with directly demonstrated antiviral activity include:

- **Pleurotus ostreatus**: Ubiquitin-like glycoprotein inhibits HIV (Wang and Ng 2000)
- **Lentinula edodes**: Water-soluble lignin (Suzuki et al. 1990); antiviral substance (Sarkar et al. 1993)
- **Ganoderma lucidum**: Methanol-soluble fractions selectively inhibit herpes simplex 1 and 2, vesicular stomatitus virus (Eo et al. 1999, 2000)
- **Trametes versicolor**: Polysaccharopeptide inhibits HIV type 1 (Collins and Ng 1997)
- **Cortinarius caperatus**: Inhibits varicella zoster (shingles), influenza A, RSV (Piraino and Brandt 1999)
- **Fomes fomentarius**: Inhibits tobacco mosaic virus (Aoki et al. 1993)

## Three Ways Mushrooms Protect Against Disease

1. **Direct antimicrobial action**: Antibiotics secreted by mycelium (cold-water extracts from 10 species reduced E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus by >99.99%)
2. **Host-mediated immune response**: Mushrooms activate natural immune defenses in mammalian cells (Stamets 2003b)
3. **Mycofiltration**: Custom mycelial mats reduce environmental pathogens from sources like sewage and feedlots

## Antibacterial Potency

Stamets' lab tested cold-water extracts from mycelial exudates of 10 mushroom species. The most antibacterially active were:
- Oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)
- Birch polypore (Piptoporus betulinus)
- Agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis)

These reduced colony-forming units of both E. coli O157:H7 and Staphylococcus aureus from >100 million to the 1,000-10,000 range in 48-72 hours — more than 99.99% inhibition.

## Mushrooms and HIV/AIDS

- NIH funded a clinical trial using oyster mushrooms with protease inhibitors at UCSF (approved 2003)
- Oyster mushrooms contain natural lovastatin isomer — reduces LDL cholesterol by up to 30% at 15g/day for 30 days (Gunde-Cimerman and Plemenitas 2002)
- Protease inhibitors cause LDL accumulation; oyster mushrooms may dually mitigate side effects while fighting AIDS
- Mushroom cultivation enterprises in developing countries can simultaneously combat hunger, recycle waste, build soil, and fight HIV

## Using Mushrooms Against Forest Blights

### Fighting Armillaria with Other Mushrooms

The introduction of select saprophytic or [[endophytic-fungi]] can forestall parasitic blights:

- **Sparassis crispa** (cauliflower mushroom): Secretes sparassol; outcompetes and parasitizes Armillaria in vitro
- **Hypholoma fasciculare/capnoides**: Reduced Armillaria root rot by up to 67% in British Columbia field trials (Chapman et al. 2004)
- **Trametes versicolor** (turkey tail): Fast-growing mycelium overgrows Armillaria
- **Pearce and Malajczuk (1990)**: Hypholoma competitively excluded Armillaria from karri stumps in Western Australia

### Mycobarrier Strategy

By prefilling susceptible forest niches with beneficial species, landowners can forestall blight invasion:
1. Inoculate stumps at the perimeter of an advancing Armillaria blight
2. Use combinations: turkey tail (above-ground) + Hypholoma (below-ground) + cauliflower
3. Erect "mycelial perimeters" as species barriers

Recommended saprophytic sentinels for forest health: Hypholoma capnoides, Pleurotus ostreatus, Stropharia rugoso-annulata, Trametes versicolor, and Sparassis crispa.

## The Precautionary Principle

Saprophytic mushrooms do not "infect" — they degrade already dead tissue. The saprophytic genome is no longer geographically restricted since spores travel on hikers, birds, automobiles, and winds. Promoting saprophytes conforms to the precautionary principle: doing nothing in the face of impending ecological disaster may be riskier than action.

## Enokitake (Flammulina velutipes) — Antitumor Pioneer

First mushroom with antitumor agents brought to Western scientific attention. Koinatsu (1963) and Watanabe et al. (1964) first reported antitumor properties. Key compounds:
- **FVP** (Flammulina velutipes polysaccharide) -- protein-bound polysaccharide
- **Flammulin** -- water-soluble polysaccharide
- **Proflamin** (Ikekawa 2001) -- low molecular weight immunomodulator

Epidemiological evidence: Ikekawa found unusually low cancer rates among [[medicinal-mushroom-guide-enoki|enoki]]take growers near Nagano, Japan, attributed to frequent consumption. Also inhibits Staphylococcus aureus. Candidate for cold mycoremediation (mycelium runs at temperatures inhibiting most other fungi).

## Maitake (Grifola frondosa) — Beta-glucan Powerhouse

One of the most studied medicinal mushrooms, with antitumor, antidiabetic, and antiviral properties. Beta-glucans constitute 10-20% of dry weight (up to 41% total complex carbohydrates). Key compound: **D-fraction** (1,6-branched 1,3-beta-D-glucan, MW ~1 million).

Clinical evidence (Nanba 1997): In 165 advanced-stage cancer patients, maitake D-fraction showed tumor regression or significant improvement in 11/15 breast cancer, 12/18 lung cancer, and 7/15 liver cancer patients. Combined with chemotherapy, response rates improved 12-28%.

Mechanisms:
- Stimulates tumor necrosis factors (TNFs) in prostate cancer (Fullerton et al. 2000)
- Increases immune-competent cells 58-68% when combined with chemotherapy (Kodama et al. 2002)
- Stimulates nitric oxide production from macrophages (Sanzen et al. 2001)
- Antidiabetic: single dose lowered circulating glucose 25% in insulin-resistant mice (Manohar et al. 2002)

Heat treatment note: 212-250F enhances bioavailability of beta-glucans; >302F degrades them into inactive subfractions.

## Reishi (Ganodenna lucidum) -- Biological Response Modifier

The most revered medicinal mushroom in Asian history. Does NOT act directly as a tumoricidal agent; instead functions as a **biological response modifier**:
- Stimulates macrophages via interleukins-1, -2, -6, -10
- Activates natural killer cells, T cells, and tumor-necrosis factors
- More than 100 distinct polysaccharides and 119 triterpenoids identified

Key medicinal actions:
- **Anti-inflammatory**: Comparable to prednisone but without side effects (Stavinoha et al. 1990). Treats arthritis, brain inflammation, prostate inflammation
- **Cardiovascular**: Increases coronary flow via vasodilatation, decreases diastolic blood pressure. Inhibits platelet aggregation, treats atherosclerosis
- **Antioxidant**: Scavenges superoxide anions, interrupts free radical chain reactions
- **Anti-aging**: Reduces oxidative stress damage
- **Antiviral**: Prevented death of HIV-infected lymphocytes, inhibited viral replication (Kim et al. 1994)
- **Liver protection**: Ganoderenic acid A inhibits beta-glucuronidase (liver dysfunction enzyme)
- **Nerve regeneration**: Patent filed for aqueous extract nerve-regenerating properties (Olmogi et al. 2004)

Polysaccharides caused 5-10x increase in TNFs and substantial T-lymphocyte augmentation (Wang et al. 1997). Bidirectional immunomodulation: immune-enhancing AND anti-inflammatory, dose-dependent.

Preparation: Hot water extraction for teas/tinctures. Daily dose: 3-5g per person. Concern about excessive bleeding prior to surgery due to adenosine fractions.

## Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus) -- Nerve Regenerator

TCM prescribes for stomach ailments and GI cancer prevention. Dr. Mizuno (Shizuoka University) isolated 5 distinct antitumor polysaccharides. Key unique compounds:

- **Erinacines** and **Hericenones** -- nerve growth stimulant (NGS) factors (Kawagishi et al. 1991)
- **Erinacine O** -- isolated from liquid culture, precursor to erinacine family (Kenmoku et al. 2002)

These compounds stimulate neurons to regrow and rebuild myelin. Potential applications:
- Treating senility and Alzheimer's disease
- Repairing neurological trauma
- Increasing cognitive abilities
- Improving muscle/motor response pathways (muscular dystrophy)

Low molecular weight compounds pass through the blood-brain barrier intact (Kawagishi 2002). Kolotushkina et al. (2003) confirmed myelin-generating influence on nerve and cerebellar glia cells in vitro. Also has antimicrobial properties against Aspergillus and Candida species. A "smart food" candidate for families with histories of nerve disease, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, or muscular dystrophy.

## Tinder Conk (Fomes fomentarius) -- Ancient Antimicrobial

Used since Stone Age (11,600 B.C.) for cauterizing wounds, treating inflammation, and starting fires. Found with Otzi the ice man. Okanagan-Colville Indians used for arthritis. Medicinal properties:
- Bitter diuretic tea from boiling conks
- Antimicrobial, immune-boosting water extract
- Novel ergosterol peroxides (Rosecke and Konig 2000)
- Strong antiviral properties (Aoki et al. 1993; Piraino and Brandt 1999)
- Inhibits Bacillus subtilis reproduction (Suay et al. 2000)
- Highest enzyme activity with T. versicolor for D-glucose to dicarbonyl sugar conversion (Vole et al. 1985)
- Mycelium secretes crystalline structures that attract and consume E. coli

## Artist Conk (Ganoderma applanatum) -- Forest Sentinel

Widespread perennial polypore (up to 50 years). Strong antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus from methanol extracts (Smania et al. 1999). Antitumor polysaccharides (Sasaki 1971; Ikekawa 1969). Bitter diuretic tea. Dried conk smoke repels insects. Potential purgative for intestinal parasites.

## Elm Oyster (Hypsizygus ulmarius) -- Garden-Friendly Medicinal

Though medicinal properties remain under-explored, unpublished Japanese research suggests high anticarcinogenic activity. TCM practitioners recommend for stomach and intestinal diseases. The fact that it resists spoilage longer than Pleurotus species suggests inherent antibacterial compounds. May have cholesterol-lowering properties similar to P. ostreatus.

## Hypholoma sublateritium (Cinnamon Cap) -- Unexplored Potential

Medicinal properties unknown but worth investigation given its aggressive Armillaria-fighting capabilities. Produces cord-like rhizomorphs indicating powerful enzymatic systems. Fruited for 8 consecutive years on alder logs, suggesting sustained bioactive compound production.
