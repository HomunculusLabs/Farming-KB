# Bioregionalism in Permaculture

Bioregionalism in permaculture is the practice of organizing human settlement, economy, and culture around natural ecological boundaries rather than political borders. david holmgren identifies bioregionalism as one of the key characteristics of future sustainable cultures that will emerge during energy descent.

A bioregion is a geographic area defined by natural characteristics such as watershed boundaries, soil types, climate zones, and native ecosystems. The bioregional approach recognizes that human communities are most sustainable when they align their activities with the carrying capacity and natural patterns of their specific place.

## The Bioregional Foundation

Permaculture ethics is informed by a range of sources including indigenous and traditional cultures, and more recently through the concept of bioregionalism.

This understanding that cultures need to be adapted to place is central to permaculture design. It means that specific techniques and species choices must be adapted to local conditions rather than applied as universal formulas.

The [[permaculture-climate-zones]] framework provides one dimension of bioregional awareness, but true bioregionalism goes further to include geology, soils, native ecology, and cultural heritage.

david holmgren worked in the temperate landscapes of south-eastern Australia with a strong bioregional focus, developing his principles and patterns from direct observation of his home region.

## Bioregional Political and Economic Structures

As the rate of change during energy descent slows, more enduring and diverse bioregional cultures will emerge. Holmgren expects these cultures to have bioregional political and economic structures giving a renewed geographic diversity.

This represents a reversal of the trend toward globalization and centralization that has characterized the fossil fuel era. As energy becomes more expensive and less available, local and regional systems become more viable than global ones.

The [[permaculture-household-economics]] approach provides the household-scale foundation for bioregional economic networks, while [[permaculture-community-design]] provides models for community-level organization.

Bioregional patterns of mineral imbalance are important considerations. Understanding the specific nutrient profile of a region helps guide soil amendment strategies and dietary choices.

## Culture of Place

Contributing to the evolution of a culture of place by supporting and celebrating the local in knowledge, food, art, and culture is a key value for bioregional permaculture.

This means preferring local food systems over globalized supply chains, local building materials over imported ones, and local knowledge systems over universalized expert advice.

Acknowledging the validity and value in all pre-existing cultures of place, and freely adopting bits that seem useful in a local context, is part of the bioregional approach.

This does not mean rejecting all outside knowledge, but rather applying the permaculture principle of valuing diversity while filtering innovations through local conditions and needs.

The [[bioregional-organization]] page provides more detail on practical structures for bioregional governance and coordination.

## Learning from Indigenous Cultures

The permaculture focus on learning from indigenous tribal cultures is based on evidence that these cultures existed in relative balance with their environments and survived longer than any recent experiments in civilization.

This is not romanticization of indigenous life but recognition that cultures adapted to specific bioregions over long periods developed knowledge systems finely tuned to local carrying capacity.

Traditional ecological knowledge includes detailed understanding of local species, seasonal patterns, water management, soil care, and resource harvesting that took generations to develop.

[[permaculture-design]] draws on these knowledge systems while combining them with modern scientific understanding to create hybrid approaches suited to contemporary conditions.

## Bioregional Diversity

Bioregionalism values diversity at the regional scale. Just as a healthy ecosystem has diverse species, a healthy civilization has diverse cultures adapted to diverse places.

The fossil fuel era has homogenized both ecology and culture, replacing diverse local systems with standardized global ones. Energy descent will likely reverse this trend as global supply chains become less reliable.

Future bioregional cultures will be biogenetically, racially, culturally, and intellectually cross-fertilized, giving natural hybrid vigor while remaining rooted in place.

The [[permaculture-ethics-foundation]] of caring for the earth naturally leads to bioregional awareness, because caring for a specific place requires understanding its unique characteristics and limitations.

## Practical Bioregional Design

Practical bioregional permaculture design includes: mapping local watersheds and understanding water flow patterns, cataloging native species and their ecological roles, identifying local building materials and their properties, and developing food systems based on locally adapted species.

It also includes building local seed networks through [[permaculture-seed-saving]], creating local exchange systems to reduce dependence on national currency, and developing regional energy systems based on locally available sources.

The [[permaculture-climate-adaptation]] approach is inherently bioregional because climate impacts and adaptation strategies vary significantly across regions.

[[permaculture-earthworks]] must be designed for local soil types, rainfall patterns, and topography, all of which are bioregional characteristics.

The [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] framework provides the timeline context for bioregional transition, recognizing that different regions will experience energy descent at different rates and with different characteristics depending on their resources and preparedness.
