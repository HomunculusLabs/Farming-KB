# Mycological Dark Taxa

## Defining Dark Taxa

"Dark taxa" refers to fungal lineages that are known only from DNA sequences extracted from environmental samples, with no described morphology, culture, or formally assigned taxonomic name. These organisms are detected through metabarcoding or metagenomic surveys but cannot be linked to any known species. They exist in a taxonomic limbo, biologically real and ecologically active, but invisible to traditional taxonomy and nomenclature systems.

The concept was formalized in the early 2010s as high-throughput sequencing studies began revealing vast numbers of fungal sequences that had no match in reference databases. Researchers realized that the unknown portion of [[fungal-taxonomy]] today and has profound implications for our understanding of [[fungal-biodiversity-estimates]] that circulate in the literature (typically 2.2 to 3.8 million species) likely underestimate the true extent of fungal diversity because most calculation methods cannot account for lineages that have never been observed by traditional means.

## Why Fungi Go Dark

### Non-Fruiting Life Histories

Many fungi either do not produce fruiting bodies or produce them only under rare or unpredictable conditions. Some are obligate biotrophs that exist entirely within host tissues and may never emerge. Others have been separated from their natural environment and can no longer be induced to sporulate in the laboratory. The yeast stage of many fungi is far easier to detect than any sexual reproductive structure they might produce under specific environmental triggers.

### Unculturability

The vast majority of fungi detected by environmental DNA cannot be grown in pure culture. This may be because they depend on complex interactions with other organisms (hosts, bacterial partners, other fungi) that cannot be replicated on artificial media. Obligate [[fungal-discovery-methods]] ineffective for these ecologically critical groups.

### Primer Bias

Even DNA-based detection is imperfect. The universal ITS primers used in most metabarcoding studies were designed based on known fungal groups and may not amplify all fungal lineages equally. Some early-diverging fungal groups have ITS regions with unusual structures or nucleotide compositions that make them poor targets for standard PCR primers. These "primer-blind" taxa may be abundant but invisible to standard surveys.

### Cryptic Diversity

DNA sequencing has revealed that many morphologically defined fungal "species" actually consist of multiple genetically distinct lineages that are reproductively isolated and may occupy different ecological niches. These [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]], decomposition, and plant-fungal interactions. Some may be important symbionts or pathogens whose impacts on agriculture and natural ecosystems remain entirely unrecognized. The [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]], deep ocean sediments, and extreme environments such as hot springs and Antarctic soils tend to have higher proportions of dark taxa than temperate terrestrial environments that have been more thoroughly sampled by traditional mycological methods. Plant rhizospheres also harbor numerous dark taxa that may represent important but undescribed symbiotic relationships.
