# Mycological Dark Taxa

## Defining Dark Taxa

"Dark taxa" refers to fungal lineages that are known only from DNA
sequences extracted from environmental samples, with no described
morphology, culture, or formally assigned taxonomic name. These organisms
are detected through metabarcoding or metagenomic surveys but cannot be
linked to any known species. They exist in a taxonomic limbo —
biologically real, ecologically active, but invisible to traditional
taxonomy.

The concept was formalized in the early 2010s as high-throughput
sequencing studies began revealing vast numbers of fungal sequences that
had no match in reference databases. Researchers realized that the
unknown portion of fungal diversity was not merely a matter of
undersampling; it represented entire clades of fungi that had never been
seen, cultured, or described by any traditional means.

## The Scale of the Problem

Dark taxa are estimated to constitute the majority of fungal diversity.
In most environmental DNA surveys, 60-90% of detected fungal OTUs cannot
be assigned to described species, and many cannot even be placed at the
genus or family level. The UNITE database, which curates fungal ITS
sequences, contains hundreds of thousands of sequences that remain
unidentified at the species level.

A landmark 2017 analysis by Hibbett and colleagues estimated that dark
fungal taxa might represent 50% or more of all fungal species. More
conservative estimates still place the figure at 30-40%. Regardless of
the exact proportion, dark taxa represent one of the largest gaps in
biological knowledge.

## Why Fungi Go Dark

Several factors contribute to the existence of dark taxa:

### Non-Fruiting Life Histories

Many fungi either do not produce fruiting bodies or produce them only
under rare or unpredictable conditions. Some are obligate biotrophs that
exist entirely within host tissues and may never emerge. Others have
been separated from their natural environment and can no longer be
induced to sporulate in the laboratory.

### Unculturability

The vast majority of fungi detected by environmental DNA cannot be grown
in pure culture. This may be because they depend on complex interactions
with other organisms (hosts, bacterial partners, other fungi) that cannot
be replicated on artificial media. Obligate mycorrhizal fungi, for
example, require a living plant root to complete their life cycle.

### Primer Bias

Even DNA-based detection is imperfect. The universal ITS primers used
in most metabarcoding studies were designed based on known fungal groups
and may not amplify all fungal lineages equally. Some early-diverging
fungal groups have ITS regions with unusual structures or nucleotide
compositions that make them poor targets for standard PCR primers.
These "primer-blind" taxa may be abundant in the environment but
invisible to standard surveys.

### Cryptic Diversity

DNA sequencing has revealed that many morphologically defined fungal
"species" actually consist of multiple genetically distinct lineages
that are reproductively isolated and may occupy different ecological
niches. These cryptic species contribute to the dark taxa problem
because they are genetically distinct but morphologically indistinguishable
from known species.

## Proposals for Naming Dark Taxa

The existence of dark taxa has created a crisis in fungal taxonomy and
nomenclature. Traditional taxonomic practice requires a physical type
specimen and formal Latin diagnosis to name a species. But if a fungus
has never been cultured or collected as a fruiting body, there is no
specimen to deposit in a herbarium.

Several solutions have been proposed:

### Candidatus Nomenclature

Borrowed from bacteriology, the *Candidatus* prefix has been proposed as
a provisional designation for fungi known only from DNA. This would
allow researchers to assign names to dark taxa without formal
description, facilitating communication and reference in the literature.

### Sequence-Based Nomenclature

A more radical proposal would allow DNA sequences to serve as type
specimens. Under this framework, a fungal species could be validly
described based solely on a DNA sequence from an environmental sample,
with the sequence deposited in a curated database serving as the type.
This approach has been debated extensively within the mycological
community and remains controversial.

### Phylogenetic Nomenclature

Some researchers advocate for phylogenetic definitions of taxa based on
clade membership rather than possession of diagnostic morphological
features. This approach, used in some other biological disciplines, would
allow dark taxa to be formally recognized based on their phylogenetic
position even in the absence of morphological data.

## Ecological Importance of Dark Taxa

Despite being taxonomically invisible, dark taxa are ecologically
ubiquitous and likely play critical roles in ecosystem functioning.
Environmental DNA studies show that dark fungal taxa are abundant in
soils worldwide, suggesting they participate in nutrient cycling,
decomposition, and plant-fungal interactions. Some may be important
symbionts or pathogens whose impacts on agriculture and natural
ecosystems remain entirely unrecognized.
