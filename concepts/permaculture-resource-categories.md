# Resource Categories in Permaculture

In permaculture, resources are defined as useful reserves created when living components and technological units translate incoming natural energies (sun, wind, rain) into storable forms. Some resources must remain within the system for its own purposes — fish stocks must be maintained to produce more fish. An ideal technology should, at minimum, fuel itself. The surplus beyond system needs constitutes yield.

Understanding what resources are and how they respond to use is fundamental to permaculture design, because not all resources can be used in the same way or to the same ends.

## The Ethics of Resource Use

Ethics around resource use evolve from understanding the consequences of exploitation. Forests, soils, air, water, sunlight, and seeds are regarded as part of a common heritage — resources belonging to everyone. A second category includes those fabricated or developed resources created by individual or group work, whose presence or absence does not apparently affect the common resource. However, since everything created is made from the common resource, no clean boundary exists between the two categories.

## Five Categories of Resources

A use-and-results approach reveals five distinct resource categories based on what happens when a resource is used:

### Category 1: Resources That Increase by Modest Use

These resources flourish when used moderately and deteriorate when neglected. Green browse exemplifies this: if deer do not browse shrubs, the plants become woody and unpalatable. A browsed biennial, unable to flower, may tiller out and become perennial. Seedling trees can be maintained at browse height, but ungrazed specimens "escape" to unbrowsable height and shade out other palatable plants.

**Information** is perhaps the most important resource in this category. It withers or becomes outdated if not used. Too little impoverishes a system, but when freely used and exchanged, it flourishes and increases.

Overgrazing can cause extinction of palatable browse and browsers through damage, but underbrowsing produces similar effects through woody dominance. Balance is essential.

### Category 2: Resources Unaffected by Use

These resources remain intact regardless of how they are used. In impalpable terms, a view or a good climate is unaffected by use. In palpable terms, diverting part of a river to hydroelectric generation or irrigation (with water returned after use) leaves the resource unchanged. A stone pile serving as mulch, heat store, or water runoff collector is similarly unaffected. A well-managed ecosystem is the paradigmatic example.

### Category 3: Resources That Disappear or Degrade If Not Used

These are perishable resources that must be harvested or captured to be of value. Examples include:

- Unharvested annual crops
- Grass that could be stored for winter
- Irruptions of oceanic fish
- Swarms of bees or grasshoppers
- Ripe fruit
- Water runoff during rains

Failing to use these resources results in their complete loss — they cannot be recovered once the moment passes.

### Category 4: Resources Reduced by Use

These resources diminish with consumption and must be managed carefully to avoid depletion. Examples include fish or game stocks unwisely harvested, clay deposits, mature forests, and fossil fuels like coal and oil. The key distinction from Category 3 is that these resources persist if left alone, whereas Category 3 resources are lost through inaction.

### Category 5: Resources That Pollute or Destroy Other Resources If Used

These are the most dangerous category — resources whose use actively damages other resources. Examples include:

- Residual poisons in ecosystems
- Radioactive materials
- Super-highways and large concrete areas
- Large buildings
- Sewers running pollutants to the sea

These resources are permanent pollutants in terms of human lifetimes and represent the antithesis of sustainable design.

## Sustainability Implications

Categories 1 through 3 are the resources most commonly produced in natural systems and rural living situations, and they constitute the only sustainable basis of society. Categories 4 and 5 result primarily from urban and industrial development. If Category 4 and 5 resources are not used to produce permanent beneficial changes to the ecosystem, they become pollutants.

A sane society manages Categories 1 through 4 wisely, bans the use of Category 5, and regulates all uses to produce sustainable yield. This is called **resource management** and has been successfully applied to some fish and animal populations, but seldom to human lives and economies.

## The Principle of Enough

The key principle underlying wise resource use is the **principle of "enough."** This is basic to understanding societies in chaos or systems in disorder. When systems accept more resources than they can productively use, the result is not abundance but pathology.

Both over- and undersupply of resources produce similar systemic disorder, except that oversupply produces more grotesque results in living systems. Undersupply can be partially managed through reduced growth and wider spacing of organisms, but oversupply causes inflated growth, crowding, and sociopathy in social organisms. In human populations, both gross over-nutrition and under-nutrition are common pathologies requiring ethical resource management to balance.

## Policy of Resource Management

A responsible human society must ban the use of resources that permanently reduce yields of sustainable resources — pollutants, persistent poisons, radioactives, large areas of concrete and highways, sewers from city to sea. Failure to do so will cause the society itself to fail.

Programs of highway building, city expansion, release of persistent biocides, and loss of soil bring societies down more surely and permanently than war. Governments that tolerate desertification, land salting, concreted highways, and city sprawl take more good land permanently out of life production than the loss of territory to a conqueror. This immorality is often termed "progress" and "growth" to confuse the ignorant and supplant local self-reliance for the temporary ends of centralized power.

The economic dimension is stark: superhighways and overpasses in Massachusetts alone need approximately $400 billion to repair, and the collapsing sewer systems of London and New York some $80 billion. Neither jurisdiction can raise this money, demonstrating that unthinking historical development strategy can cripple a future society. Today's luxuries are tomorrow's disasters.

Investment priorities at both national and household levels should be decided using the same resource categorization criteria, directing energy and capital toward Categories 1 through 3 and strictly regulating Category 4 while eliminating Category 5.
