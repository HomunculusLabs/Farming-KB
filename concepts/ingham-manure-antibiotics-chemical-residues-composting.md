# Manure Antibiotics and Chemical Residues in Composting

## Overview

Dr. Elaine Ingham addresses the practical challenges of [[hamilton-worm-composting-manure]] and organic materials that contain antibiotic residues, deworming medications, herbicides, insecticides, fungicides, and [[fungal-biosorption-of-heavy-metals-by-filamentous-fungi]]. These contaminants can inhibit the [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] essential for proper composting. Understanding how to detect and remediate contaminated compost is critical for producing safe, biologically active compost [[ingham-actinomycetes-soil-biology-and-compost-tea]].

## Sources of Contamination

Manure from livestock operations frequently contains residues that interfere with composting:

- **Dewormers (anthelmintics)** — the most problematic class of contaminants
- **Herbicide residues** — particularly persistent compounds like clopyralid and aminopyralid
- **Insecticides** — broad-spectrum neurotoxins that kill beneficial soil organisms
- **Fungicides** — directly target the fungi essential for [[humus-formation-and-soil-organic-matter]]
- **Antibiotics** — subtherapeutic and therapeutic doses from veterinary use
- **Heavy metals** — copper sulfate and sulfur from hoof treatments and supplements

Grass clippings are another common contamination vector, as lawn herbicides can persist through the composting process and damage plants when the finished compost is applied.

## Hierarchy of Problem Severity

Ingham ranks common contaminants by the difficulty they pose for composting:

1. **Strongylid medicine (de-wormer)** — most difficult to overcome
2. **Herbicide residues** — particularly persistent synthetic auxins
3. **Insecticides** — broad-spectrum toxicity to soil foodweb organisms
4. **Fungicides** — directly suppress the [[air-pollution-fungal-community-responses]] needed for decomposition
5. **Copper and sulfur** — heavy metals from hoof treatments; accumulate over time
6. **Antibiotics** — generally easier to deal with than the above, as long as concentrations are not overwhelming

## Detecting Contamination: The Temperature Test

The primary diagnostic tool is monitoring compost temperature during the initial decomposition phase:

- **Normal pattern**: Temperature rises within 24-48 hours of pile construction as bacterial activity increases, reaching thermophilic range (55-65°C / 131-149°F)
- **Abnormal pattern**: Temperature stays low for an extended period after starting the windrow or pile

If temperature does not respond normally — staying low too long — it signals that something is inhibiting the [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]]. This is the first indicator that antibiotic or chemical residues may be present.

## Remediation Strategies

### Initial Response: Add Energy

When contamination is suspected:

- Add **[[aact-fish-hydrolysate-marine-brewing-ingredients-ingham]]** as a nitrogen source (particularly effective for de-wormer problems)
- Add **molasses** as an energy source for the [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]]
- These provide the organisms with resources needed to decompose the inhibitory compounds

The rationale: organisms need energy to produce the enzymes required to break down complex pharmaceutical molecules. Without adequate food resources, they cannot mount an effective metabolic response to the contaminants.

### Second Response: Inoculate with Specialized Microbes

If temperature remains low after adding energy sources:

- Use commercial compost starters that contain bacteria capable of degrading the specific contaminants
- Examples Ingham mentions: EM inoculum ([[comparison-bokashi-vs-effective-microorganisms]]), Biostart, Organica, Agri-Energy compost starters
- These products contain specific bacterial strains that "chew up these nasty materials"

### Persistent Problems

If remediation efforts fail:
- The contamination level may exceed the capacity of the microbial community
- Additional compost starting materials may be needed to dilute the contaminant concentration
- Some compounds (notably clopyralid) may not have known microbial decomposers that occur routinely on grass clippings
- Chlordane can be decomposed by some bacteria and fungi, but they are not always present in compost starting materials

## Specific Contaminants

### Clopyralid

- No known bacteria or fungi that routinely decompose clopyralid are found on grass clippings
- Contaminated compost can damage broadleaf plants (tomatoes, peas, beans) when applied as mulch
- Prevention (keeping contaminated grass clippings out of compost) is the only reliable strategy

### Chlordane

- Some bacteria and fungi can decompose chlordane
