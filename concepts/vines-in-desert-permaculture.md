# Vines in Desert Permaculture

## Overview

Vines have a key role to play in desert gardens and [[mikania]]* — mile-a-minute), as great heat can build up on the western aspect late in the afternoon. The eastern side of the trellis needs to be left quite open, while the western side can be of wide-spaced pole crop.

### Planning Considerations

- 50% or more shade helps gardens in hot clear deserts
- Side trellis is spaced to shut out most of the westerly sun
- Palms can serve as shade providers
- Mulch is of great benefit in all desert situations
- Allow for light penetration to crops below

## Vines for Human Comfort

Considerable comfort can be brought to over-hot houses by attaching trellis systems. The best results are achieved through several strategies:

### 1. Western Sun Screening
Stand a dense evergreen vine crop out from the west-facing wall:
- *Mikania*, *[[dolichos]]*, or *Pelargonium* species work well
- Also grow a vine trained above and out from any existing west-facing roof
- These are well placed at the edge of a verandah if one exists

### 2. Eastern Sun Management
Grow vines (winter deciduous or summer deciduous) in a screen fixed out from the eastern sun. This allows warming light in while filtering excess intensity.

### 3. Verandah Cooling
Washing down the verandah and spraying vines with water rapidly cools the shaded verandah areas.

### 4. Rear Door Trellis
Build extensive closed trellis over the rear door on the shade side to provide a cool air source. Preferably include:
- A thick bark mulch below the vine
- Fine sprays to damp it down
- Roof vents via the indoor ceiling or a small sun-side glasshouse to draw in the cool air of the trellis

### 5. Roof Cover
Run a perennial non-invasive vine (*Mikania* or *Pyrostegia*) completely across the roof.

### 6. Water Tank Placement
Place a water tank under the vine crop to:
- Keep the garden air cool through evaporation
- Cool the stored water

## Vines as Mulch and Forage Sources

Vines can be vigorous producers of foliage for:

- **[[cotter-outdoor-mushroom-garden-mulch-beds]]** — regular pruning provides a steady supply
- **Feeding small domestic livestock** — many vine species are palatable fodder
- **Shade vines** can also provide some stick fuel wood for efficient cook-stoves

## Vines in the Desert Landscape

Several vine crops are desert-adapted. Including gourds and yam legumes (ground vines), some very basic reserve foods can be set out in:

- Shaded situations
- Moist wastewater areas
- Soakage sites

Some vines survive dry, harsh, stony, or dune conditions. All, however, thrive and yield better if a few cubic feet of humus is pitted below the plant when it is set out. Valuable vines grown in large containers can await rains, or the soakage from water harvesting, before being placed out for permanent field growth.

## Fencing for Desert Gardens

### The Critical Need

Over much of the world's drylands, the great impediment to home garden food production is the presence of wild, feral, and domestic browsing animals. It is much cheaper to:

- Fence out these devastating and ever-hungry animals from settlements and gardens
- Than to airlift emergency food aid to starving people
- Or to withstand the social costs of poverty and famine

### Fencing Requirements

Fencing is a primary requisite for intensive food production in deserts. Fenced corridors or "stock routes" can permit milk and draft animals to enter some restricted areas of settlements, but sound fences (preferably electrified) are needed to exclude:

- Goats
- Camels
- Cattle
- Donkeys
- Sheep

### Natural Regeneration

Within fenced areas, sometimes surprising natural regeneration of trees can occur. Hardy food plants can be set out in unirrigated areas if fencing is provided. These form a:

- Firewood resource
- Mulch resource
- Medicinal resource for [[gardening-in-hard-times]]

### Harmless Livestock

Poultry, domestic rabbits, bees, guinea pigs, and pigeons are all relatively harmless livestock for dryland homes and can be kept within fenced garden areas.

### Low-Cost Fencing Alternatives

Where no money is available for post-and-wire fencing, more labourious alternatives include:

- **Ditch and bank systems** — rock-faced or thorn-crowned
- **Woven fences** — of plant materials (reeds in the Caspian area)
- **[[comparison-living-fences-vs-hedgerows]]** — cactus in Mexico, *[[euphorbia-tirucalli]]* and *[[lycium-ferocissimum]]* in Africa and parts of Australia
- **Combinations** — stone walls, rock, thorny shrubs, and steel pickets or wires

In affluent societies, burnt brick walls are common. In Afghanistan and Iran, unburnt mud brick walls surround large gardens. Even within [[permaculture-for-urban-areas]] of the third world, [[permaculture-domestic-animals]] range and destroy vegetation.

Where hunting will support dogs, large domestic dogs will defend a house area (at the cost of feeding the dog). It is a matter of adapting to local materials, labour costs, and customs.

## Soil Management

### Humus Decomposition in Drylands

In drylands, any soil humus can rapidly decompose to nitrates with heat and water, giving a sometimes lethal flush of nitrate to new seedlings. Dry cultivated soils exacerbate this effect.

### The Role of Mulch

Mulches or litter on top of the soil prevent both:

- Soil cracking
- The lethal effect of rapid temperature gains that cook feeder roots at the surface

When subsequent rains come, plants with mulched roots have more living roots to absorb water.
