# Mushroom Casing Soil Recipes and Preparation

A casing layer is a non-nutritive layer of material applied on top of
fully colonized substrate to induce and support [[bacteria-and-microorganisms-in-mushroom-fruiting]]. The casing
provides a humid microclimate at the substrate surface, protects the mycelium
from drying, and serves as a water reservoir that mushrooms draw from during
development. Unlike the substrate itself, the casing contains minimal
nutrients — its role is environmental, not nutritional.

## Why Casing is Necessary

Many mushroom species, including *[[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]]*, *P. mexicana*, and
*[[gartz-panaeolus-subbalteatus-taxonomy-and-intoxication-history]]*, require a casing layer to initiate fruiting. The
casing layer triggers the shift from vegetative mycelial growth to
reproductive [[fungal-fruiting-body-formation-environmental-triggers]] through several mechanisms:

- Creates a high-humidity zone at the substrate surface
- Provides a physical barrier that modifies CO₂ concentration gradients
- Introduces microflora that may stimulate fruiting (in non-sterile casing)
- Buffers temperature fluctuations at the colonized substrate surface

## Standard Peat-Based Casing Recipe

The most widely used casing recipe consists of three ingredients mixed in
specific ratios:

- **10 parts peat moss** — The primary component, providing water retention
  and structure. Peat is naturally acidic (pH 3.5–4.5), which must be
  corrected with lime.
- **5 parts vermiculite** — A mineral that improves aeration and water
  retention. Prevents the casing from compacting and improves drainage.
- **2 parts limestone (marl)** — Raises the pH of the peat to a range
  suitable for mushroom growth (pH 7.0–7.5) and provides calcium.

## Preparation Procedure

### Dry Mixing
All three ingredients are combined in their dry form and mixed thoroughly.
Even distribution of the lime throughout the peat is essential — pockets of
unlimed acidic peat can harbor contaminants or inhibit mycelial growth.

### Moisture Adjustment
Water is gradually added while stirring the mixture. The goal is maximum
water retention without turning the mixture into mud. The proper consistency
is reached when the casing holds together when squeezed but no water drips
out. If the casing becomes too wet, add more dry ingredients to correct it.
The amount of water needed depends on the initial moisture content of the
peat, which varies by source and season.

### Packaging for Sterilization
The moistened casing soil is loaded into oven bags (nylon), autoclave bags
(polypropylene), or jars for sterilization. Bags should not be sealed tightly
— they need to allow steam penetration while containing the material.

### Sterilization
The casing soil is sterilized in a pressure cooker at 15 PSI for one hour.
While some authors recommend only pasteurizing casing soil to preserve
[[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]], sterilized casing has been found to produce more
reliable results, particularly for indoor cultivation where contamination
pressure is high.

### Cooling and Application
After sterilization, the casing must cool completely to room temperature
before application. Applying hot casing will damage or kill the mycelium
beneath. Cooled casing is spread over the colonized grain in a layer
1.5–2.0 cm thick. The surface should be kept rough (with small valleys and
hills) rather than perfectly smooth — this irregular texture provides more
surface area for pin formation.

## Application to Cased Trays

The standard procedure for casing trays:

1. Shake fully colonized grain loose from the jar and pour into a 1-litre
   disposable plastic tray
2. Break up any large lumps using the clean rim of the jar
3. Level the grain surface evenly
4. Apply casing soil using a large spoon and fork to spread a 1.5–2.0 cm
   layer
5. Maintain a rough surface texture with small valleys and hills
6. Cover the tray with aluminum foil and place at 20–25°C
