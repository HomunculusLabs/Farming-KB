# Microclimate Management Techniques

A microclimate is a small area where the climate differs from the surrounding
region. Through deliberate design using windbreaks, water features, thermal
mass, and strategic plant placement, you can create localized conditions that
allow plants to thrive outside their normal range. The fundamental principle is
that weather is not a problem but an asset to be used and managed.

## The Principle of Climate as Asset

Most gardeners complain about too much sun, too little sun, too much wind, or
too much rain. Conditions are never perfect. Yet with planning, the real
extremes of weather and climate can be used and defended. You must set up
microclimates using trellises and water diversion, either to retain moisture
and coolness in the desert or to reflect heat and store water in a colder
climate.

You have a great deal of control over the climates of small areas through the
use of windbreaks, irrigation, and terraces. You could grow just about
anything, anywhere, if you set up a microclimate for it. Many different
microclimates are already in existence on most land, most of them related to
slopes or water.

## Managing Frost

Frost happens when the land loses heat rapidly, usually just before the sun
comes up. Understanding your region's yearly frost dates and the lowest and
highest temperatures your plants and animals will tolerate is essential.

The best way to prevent frost is to stop water vapor from condensing by keeping
the air moving. Try not to create any blockage that would cause [[hemenway-cold-air-drainage-and-frost-management]] to
pool in one spot. In the northern hemisphere, the north side of a slope is
usually colder, so situate your house and gardens on the south side.

### The Thermal Belt

Cold air settles on the tops of hills and also flows downward, pooling in
valleys. This leaves the thermal belt, located partway up a slope, a little
warmer. If you are on a slope or hill, locate your most frost-sensitive
elements in this thermal belt zone. Some plants require frost to produce fruit,
and frost also adds water to the soil, so you would not want to eliminate it
entirely.

### Frost Protection Methods

A wall built with dark stones absorbs heat and can prevent frost by radiating
heat at night. Plants will grow faster next to a wall like this. A light-
colored wall will assist some sun-loving plants to ripen, so use whichever
color works best for the plants growing there.

A large body of water will warm and cool more slowly than the surrounding
environment, modifying the temperature of its immediate vicinity. This is why
there is less frost near the ocean.

Trees insulate the earth and trap heat in, preventing frost. One of the
easiest ways to protect your gardens from frost is to create a canopy of trees
surrounding a clearing no wider than half the height of the trees. Build this
up over time by first planting a quick-growing [[old-fruit-variety-selection]] which can shelter
a frost-hardy legume species. When the frost-hardy canopy has grown, trim back
the other trees. In very cold climates use evergreen varieties to provide
biomass that holds in heat.

## Managing Wind

Too much wind can harm wind-sensitive plants, blow away seeds, lower soil
temperature, dry up moisture, kill young animals, and make work intolerable.
Winds of 15 mph are strong enough to reduce production. Winds of 20 mph cause
physical damage to plants. A windbreak can either block winds or channel them
in the direction you want them to go.

### Windbreak Design

When planning sectors, note the direction of the prevailing wind by tying a
streamer or flag on a stake. Successful natural windbreaks use pioneer species
that are easy to grow, with fibrous stems and fleshy, fuzzy, or needle leaves.
Use an earth mound or tall solid fence to protect the plants as they grow.

The quickest way to shelter the Zone 1 garden from wind is to build a trellis
extending from the corners of the house and plant climbing vines. These create
a nice living space, insulate structures, control wind flow, trap the sun, and
provide food. They grow fast enough to provide wind shelter while trees mature.

Because cool air always goes downhill, a slope creates a wind that sweeps
through a valley. The side of a hill facing the prevailing wind experiences
faster wind speeds going uphill, while the leeward side is disrupted and
slower. A similar effect happens near large bodies of water.

## Managing Temperature

Every 330 feet of altitude away from sea level equals a temperature change of
1 degree of latitude from the equator. A body of water modifies surrounding air
temperature through evaporation. In hot areas, even a small fountain can cool
the surrounding area.

### Temperature Modification Methods

The most obvious way to create a warm microclimate is with a greenhouse,
particularly valuable in winter. An earth mound built on the west side of the
house protects from the hot evening sun and provides insulation, because earth
slows down temperature changes.

In spring, remove mulch from intensive growing areas so the dirt can warm up,
since mulch has no heat-conducting properties. At every other time, use mulch
to help soil retain moisture, reduce erosion, maintain stable temperature, and
stop weeds.

Plants release water vapor, cooling the air and causing humidity. Filling a
porous earthenware pot with water and covering the top with a heavy, wet fabric
creates a similar cooling effect. Forests absorb [[fukuoka-textdoc-three-dimensional-solar-energy-methodless-method]] during the day,
shading the forest floor, while cool air is drawn in. At night, warm air flows
out.

Blocking the sun is a quick way to cool something down. A thick hedge on the
west side blocks the hottest sun and wind. A deciduous tree on the sunny side
shades the wall and roof in summer but allows sun in winter. Shiny trees like
poplar planted in an arc facing the sun can reflect light and warmth back onto
a garden.

## Managing Light

Plants need light, and redirecting sunlight can mean the difference between
green tomatoes and red ones. Plants with a high need for light and warmth can
be placed on the sunny side of banks or on a small hill behind a pond. Trees
with light-colored leaves reflect heat and light. A sun-facing wall reflects
the sun in winter, helping plants ripen faster and more completely.
