# AACT Aerobic vs Anaerobic Brew Comparison — Ingham Framework

Dr. [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] is fundamentally different from and superior to anaerobic fermentative teas for soil and plant health.

## The Five Criteria for Beneficial Amendments

Ingham defines five functions that a beneficial soil amendment should perform:

1. **Plant protection** — prevent or reduce disease and pest pressure
2. **Promote [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]]** — facilitate conversion of nutrients to plant-available forms
3. **Improve soil nutrient levels** — add or retain nutrients in the root zone
4. **Build soil structure** — improve aggregation, porosity, and water-holding capacity
5. **Remove toxins** — break down or neutralize harmful compounds

Each brewing method is evaluated against all five criteria. The differences are not matters of degree but of fundamentally different mechanisms — and in some cases, opposite outcomes.

## Criterion 1: Plant Protection

### Aerobic Mechanism (Competition, Not Toxicity)

AACT protects plants through four non-toxic, mechanical mechanisms:

1. **Competition for food resources**: Beneficial organisms consume the same food sources that pathogens would use, starving potential disease organisms.
2. **Competition for space**: Beneficial organisms physically occupy infection sites on leaf surfaces, roots, and in soil.
3. **Consumption of pathogens**: Predatory organisms — protozoa, nematodes, and certain beneficial fungi — actively consume disease organisms.
4. **Competition for infection sites**: All plant surfaces are occupied by beneficial organisms before pathogens can gain a foothold.

These are the same mechanisms by which cats control mice — mechanical, biological population control. The EPA classifies such interactions as "mechanical control methods," providing a regulatory pathway distinct from pesticide registration. AACT should never be considered a pesticide.

### Anaerobic Mechanism (Toxicity)

Fermentative teas protect plants through the production of toxic compounds. These toxins inhibit pathogen growth on agar plates in laboratory settings. However, they are non-specific (harming beneficial organisms alongside pathogens), dangerous to human health at uncontrolled concentrations, unpredictable in field conditions, and associated with nutrient loss, pH problems, and foul odors.

Anaerobic brews could legitimately be classified as pesticides and should be registered with the EPA.

**Key distinction**: Aerobic teas prevent disease by creating an environment where pathogens cannot survive. Anaerobic teas attempt to kill pathogens with toxins. The former builds long-term resistance; the latter provides short-term suppression at a cost.

## Criterion 2: Nutrient Cycling

### Aerobic Nutrient Cycling

Aerobic compost tea contains the complete nutrient cycling food chain:

1. **Bacteria and fungi** immobilize nutrients in their biomass, preventing leaching.
2. **Protozoa** consume bacteria and release nitrogen and other nutrients in plant-available forms (primarily ammonium).
3. **[[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]]** consume both bacteria and fungi, releasing larger pulses of plant-available nutrients.
4. When plants actively take up nutrients, microbial growth slows and the community shifts to seek new nutrient sources.

This creates a self-regulating nutrient delivery system that responds to plant demand — a natural slow-release fertility system.

### Anaerobic Nutrient Failure

Anaerobic brews cannot perform nutrient cycling:

1. **No beneficial fungi**: Strictly aerobic; cannot survive anaerobic conditions.
2. **No flagellates or amoebae**: The primary nutrient cyclers cannot survive without oxygen.
3. **No nematodes**: Killed by anaerobic conditions.
4. **Nutrient volatilization**: Nitrogen is lost as ammonia and [[fungal-hyphae]] produce glues** binding microaggregates into macroaggregates (>0.25mm), with hyphae physically enmeshing soil particles.
3. **Protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods** create pores as they move through soil, forming channels for air and water.
4. **Earthworms** create macropores that function as water reservoirs against gravity.

The result is improved water-holding capacity, infiltration, aeration, and root penetration.

### Anaerobic Structure Destruction

Anaerobic conditions actively destroy soil structure. Aerobic bacteria are killed (eliminating microaggregate glues), aerobic fungi are attacked by anaerobic bacteria (eliminating macroaggregate networks), and protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods are killed. Existing aggregates collapse as biological glues are degraded.
