## What Is Kimchi

Kimchi is a traditional Korean fermented vegetable dish, most commonly made with napa cabbage and Korean chili flakes (gochugaru). It is Korea's national dish and a cornerstone of Korean cuisine, with hundreds of regional and seasonal varieties. The fermentation process relies on [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] (LAB) naturally present on the vegetables, which convert sugars into lactic acid, preserving the vegetables and developing complex sour, umami, and spicy flavors.

Kimchi is a living food — it continues to ferment and evolve in flavor over weeks and months. Fresh kimchi is crisp and mildly tangy, while well-aged kimchi (mugeun-ji) develops deep sourness and is prized for stews and fried rice.

## Core Ingredients

### Napa Cabbage

The foundation of the most common kimchi variety (baechu-kimchi).

- Select firm, heavy heads with tightly packed leaves
- Remove the tough outer leaves (save one or two for wrapping)
- Quarter lengthwise and cut into sections, or halve and keep whole for traditional presentation
- Brine the cabbage before seasoning

### Salt

- Coarse sea salt (Korean sea salt / cheongju-gyeom preferred) — fine salt dissolves too quickly and makes cabbage overly soft
- Do not use iodized table salt
- Salt concentration in brine: roughly 8-10% (about 1/2 cup salt per gallon of water)

### Gochugaru (Korean Chili Flakes)

- Provides the signature red color and moderate heat
- Gochugaru is different from regular chili powder — it is sun-dried, mild-to-medium heat, slightly sweet
- Do not substitute with cayenne, red pepper flakes, or chili powder — the flavor profile is entirely different
- Adjust quantity based on heat preference: 1/4 to 1/2 cup per large head of cabbage

### Fish Sauce and Jeotgal (Salted Seafood)

- Fish sauce (aekjeot) — anchovy-based, adds umami depth
- Saeujeot (salted tiny shrimp) — traditional, adds complexity
- Myeolchi-aekjeot (anchovy fish sauce) — most common
- Vegan alternatives: soy sauce, miso paste, or seaweed-based substitutes

### Aromatics and Seasonings

- Garlic — 6-12 cloves, minced
- Ginger — 1-2 tablespoons, minced or grated
- Korean radish (mu) — julienned or matchstick-cut, roughly 1 cup
- Scallions (green onions) — 4-6, cut into 1-inch pieces
- Sugar or Asian pear — 1 tablespoon sugar or 1/4 grated Asian pear for sweetness and enzyme activity
- Water — a small amount to thin the seasoning paste

## The Brining Process

Brining draws water out of the cabbage, creating the right texture and salt concentration for fermentation.

1. Prepare cabbage: quarter, rinse, and sprinkle salt between the leaves (for whole-leaf method) or submerge in brine (for cut method)
2. Brine for 2-6 hours, turning occasionally
3. Cabbage is ready when the leaves are pliable and bend easily without snapping
4. Rinse the cabbage 2-3 times in cold water to remove excess salt
5. Drain thoroughly for 30-60 minutes — excess water dilutes the seasoning and slows fermentation

## Making the Seasoning Paste (Yangnyeom)

1. In a large bowl, combine gochugaru with 2-3 tablespoons water to form a paste
2. Add minced garlic, minced ginger, fish sauce (3-4 tablespoons), and sugar
3. Add julienned Korean radish and toss to coat
4. Add chopped scallions and mix well
5. Taste and adjust: more fish sauce for umami, more gochugaru for heat, more sugar for sweetness
6. For a smoother paste, blend some or all ingredients in a food processor

## Assembling and Packing

1. In a large bowl, combine the drained cabbage with the seasoning paste
2. Massage the paste thoroughly into every leaf, working from the outside in
3. Every surface should be coated — bare spots invite mold
4. Pack the seasoned cabbage tightly into the fermentation vessel
5. Press down firmly to eliminate air pockets
6. Traditional: wrap in the reserved outer cabbage leaf before packing
7. Pour any remaining liquid from the mixing bowl over the packed cabbage

## Fermentation Vessel

### Traditional

- Onggi (Korean earthenware pot) — breathable, ideal for fermentation
- Burying onggi partially underground maintains stable temperature

### Modern Options

- Glass jars with loose lids (allow CO2 to escape)
- Glass jars with airlock lids (fermentation crocks with airlocks)
- Harsch/Gartopf crocks — German-style fermentation crocks with water-seal lids
- Food-grade plastic buckets — not ideal long-term but functional

### Key Requirements

- Wide mouth for easy packing and access
- Non-reactive material (glass, ceramic, food-grade plastic)
- CO2 must be able to escape — never seal airtight during active fermentation
- Keep out of direct sunlight

## Fermentation Timeline

- Room temperature: 68-72F (20-22C) is ideal
- Day 1-2: active bubbling, CO2 production visible
- Day 3-5: tangy, lightly fermented — "fresh" kimchi stage
- Day 5-7: good balance of sour and crunchy — standard fermentation
- Day 7-14: distinctly sour, flavors deepening
- Day 14-21: well-fermented, good for cooking
- 1-3 months: strongly sour, ideal for stews (jjigae) and fried rice

Refrigeration at any point slows fermentation dramatically. Kimchi keeps in the refrigerator for 3-6 months and continues to slowly age.

## Regional and Seasonal Varieties

- Baechu-kimchi — the standard napa cabbage kimchi
- Kkakdugi — cubed Korean radish kimchi
- Oi-sobagi — cucumber kimchi (stuffed)
- Chonggak-kimchi — ponytail radish kimchi (with greens attached)
- Yeolmu-kimchi — young summer radish kimchi (a cold-weather favorite)
- Dongchimi — watery white radish kimchi (no chili)
- Baek-kimchi — white kimchi (no chili, for those avoiding spice)
- Gat-kimchi — mustard leaf kimchi
- Nabak-kimchi — water kimchi with sliced radish and cabbage

## Storage

- Active fermentation: room temperature, loosely covered
- Slowing fermentation: transfer to refrigerator
- Long-term: keep refrigerated in the original vessel or transfer to airtight jars
- Freeze: not recommended — texture degrades significantly
- Kimchi juice (the liquid) is prized as a seasoning, broth base, or drink

## Health Benefits

- Rich source of lactic acid bacteria ([[lactobacillus]], Leuconostoc, Weissella species)
- High in vitamins A, B, C, and K
- Contains dietary fiber from the vegetables
- Capsaicin from gochugaru has anti-inflammatory properties
- Fermentation increases bioavailability of nutrients
- Supports gut microbiome health

## Troubleshooting

- Too salty — rinse cabbage more thoroughly during brining, or reduce brining time
- Too watery — cabbage was not drained long enough, or not packed tightly enough
- White film on surface (kahm yeast) — harmless; skim it off, ensure vegetables are submerged
- Mold (fuzzy, colored growth) — if localized, remove affected portion and surrounding area; if widespread, discard
- Too sour — expected with long fermentation; use for cooking instead of eating fresh
- Not fermenting — room temperature too cold, salt concentration too high, or chlorinated water
