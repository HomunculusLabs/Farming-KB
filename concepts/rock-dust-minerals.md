# Rock Dust and Mineral Amendments

Rock dust and mineral amendments are ground geological materials used to replenish trace minerals and improve soil fertility. Over time, agricultural soils lose minerals through leaching, [[cole-crop-harvest-and-storage]], and erosion. Rock dusts provide a slow-release source of essential and trace elements, improve soil structure, and support microbial activity. They are foundational to remineralization strategies in regenerative agriculture.

## Why Mineral Amendments Matter

- **Trace mineral depletion**: Modern agricultural soils are often deficient in trace minerals due to decades of NPK-focused fertilization. See [[nutrient-dense-gardening]] for the connection between soil minerals and food quality.
- **Slow-release nutrition**: Unlike soluble fertilizers, rock dusts break down gradually through microbial and chemical weathering, providing nutrients over years rather than weeks.
- **Soil biology support**: Minerals feed microbial communities and support enzymatic processes essential for [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] function.
- **CEC improvement**: Some rock dusts (especially those with high clay mineral content) increase [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]] capacity.
- **Paramagnetic properties**: Certain rock dusts generate weak magnetic fields that some researchers associate with improved plant growth and microbial activity, though this remains controversial.

## Types of Rock Dust and Mineral Amendments

### Basalt Rock Dust

Ground volcanic basalt. One of the most widely recommended rock dusts for general soil remineralization. Rich in silica (SiO2), calcium, magnesium, iron, and trace elements. Moderately fast weathering compared to granite. Improves soil structure and provides a broad spectrum of micronutrients.

- **Application rate**: 10-50 lbs per 1,000 sq ft (1-5 tons/acre)
- **NPK**: Typically <0.1-0.1-0.5 (low, but not the point)
- **Key minerals**: Si, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn, Cu, Zn, Co, Mo
- **Best for**: General remineralization, sandy soils, food gardens

### Granite Rock Dust

Ground granite. Very slow-releasing due to the hardness of granite minerals (quartz, feldspar, mica). Provides potassium, silica, and trace minerals over many years. Best used as a long-term investment rather than for immediate correction.

- **Application rate**: 20-100 lbs per 1,000 sq ft (2-10 tons/acre)
- **Key minerals**: K, Si, Al, Fe, Na
- **Best for**: Long-term potassium supply, clay soil structure improvement

### Azomite (Hydrated Sodium Calcium Aluminosilicate)

A trademarked product mined from volcanic ash deposits in Utah. Contains over 70 trace minerals. One of the most complete mineral spectrum amendments available. Very fine particle size increases surface area and weathering rate.

- **Application rate**: 5-10 lbs per 1,000 sq ft
- **Key minerals**: Si, Ca, K, Mg, Fe, plus 67 trace elements
- **Best for**: Broad-spectrum trace mineral supplementation, container gardens

### Glacial Rock Dust

Rock powder from glacial moraine deposits. Naturally ground by glaciers over millennia, producing a fine, diverse mineral mix. Contains a wide spectrum of minerals from diverse parent rocks. The gold standard of rock dusts for many organic gardeners.

- **Application rate**: 10-30 lbs per 1,000 sq ft
- **Key minerals**: Broad spectrum, varies by source
- **Best for**: General remineralization, building mineral reserves

### Paramagnetic Rock Dust

Rock dusts with high paramagnetic susceptibility (measured in CGS units). These include certain volcanic rocks, basaltic sands, and specific mineral deposits. Proponents (particularly Philip Callahan) argue that paramagnetic soils enhance plant growth through electromagnetic effects on root systems and soil biology.

- **Application rate**: Varies by product; typically 10-30 lbs per 1,000 sq ft
- **CGS range**: Effective range reported at 100-3,000 CGS
- **Best for**: Experimental use, orchards, long-term soil building

### Greensand (Glauconite)

An iron-potassium silicate mineral from ancient marine deposits. Slow-releasing source of potassium (approximately 3-7% K2O) and iron. Also contains magnesium and many micronutrients. Excellent for sandy soils with low CEC.

- **Application rate**: 10-30 lbs per 1,000 sq ft
- **NPK**: 0-0-3 to 0-0-7
- **Key minerals**: K, Fe, Mg, Si, plus 30+ trace elements
- **Best for**: Slow-release potassium, iron deficiency, moisture retention in sandy soils

### Gypsum (Calcium Sulfate, CaSO4·2H2O)

Not technically a rock dust but a mineral amendment worth including. Provides calcium and sulfur without affecting soil pH (unlike lime). Improves soil structure in clay soils by promoting flocculation of clay particles. Essential where sodium levels are high, as it displaces Na+ from exchange sites.

- **Application rate**: 20-50 lbs per 1,000 sq ft
- **Best for**: Clay soil structure, calcium without pH change, sodium remediation
- **See also**: [[soil-ph-management]] for calcium amendments comparison

### Langbeinite (K-Mag, K2Mg2(SO4)3)

A naturally occurring mineral source of potassium, magnesium, and sulfur. Neutral pH (does not affect soil acidity). Chloride-free, making it safe for chloride-sensitive crops. Excellent for gardens needing both K and Mg.

- **Application rate**: 5-10 lbs per 1,000 sq ft
- **NPK**: 0-0-22 (approximately)
- **Key minerals**: K (22%), Mg (11%), S (22%)
- **Best for**: Potassium-magnesium deficiency, tobacco and potato soils (chloride-free)

### Soft Rock Phosphate (Colloidal Phosphate)

Finely ground phosphate rock with high availability compared to hard rock phosphate. Contains approximately 18-20% P2O5 plus 18-20% calcium. Best applied in conjunction with [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and acidic soil conditions, which increase phosphorus solubility.

- **Application rate**: 10-30 lbs per 1,000 sq ft
- **Best for**: Long-term phosphorus supply, calcium addition, mycorrhizal integration

### Limestone (Agricultural Lime)

While primarily a pH amendment, limestone also provides calcium and magnesium. See [[soil-ph-management]] for detailed liming information.

## Application Strategies

### Initial Soil Building

For new gardens or severely depleted soils, apply rock dusts heavily the first year:
1. Test soil for baseline mineral levels via [[soil-testing]]
2. Apply basalt or glacial rock dust at 20-50 lbs/1,000 sq ft
3. Add targeted amendments based on test results (gypsum for clay, soft rock phosphate for low P)
4. Incorporate into top 6 inches of soil
5. Apply compost or [[composting-systems]] to accelerate microbial weathering

### Maintenance Applications

Established gardens benefit from lighter annual applications:
- 5-10 lbs per 1,000 sq ft of broad-spectrum rock dust (basalt, azomite, or glacial)
- Target specific deficiencies identified by [[interpreting-soil-test-results]]
- Top-dress with compost to maintain biological activity

### Combination with Biological Inputs

Rock dusts work synergistically with biological amendments:
- [[compost-tea-aact]] applications increase the microbial population that weathers rock dust
- [[effective-microorganisms-em]] can accelerate mineral release
- [[teaming-with-fungi-mycorrhizal-inoculation-techniques]] dramatically increases [[query-how-does-phosphorus-availability-change-across-soil-ph-levels]] from rock phosphate
- Fermented preparations like [[fermented-mixed-compost-fmc]] can incorporate rock dust into their mix

### Container and Raised Bed Gardens

Container soils have no access to the mineral reservoir of native earth. Rock dusts are essential:
- Mix 1-2 cups of azomite or basalt dust per cubic foot of [[solomon-potting-mix-and-seedling-soil-recipe]]
- Add soft rock phosphate at 1 cup per cubic foot for phosphorus
- Reapply lightly each season when refreshing container soil

## Remineralization Theory

The remineralization movement, popularized by John Hamaker and later by the Remineralize the Earth organization, argues that:

1. Ice age glaciation ground fresh rock into mineral-rich dust, creating the fertile soils that supported early agriculture
2. These minerals have been leached and harvested away over millennia of farming
3. Returning rock dust to soils restores [[holmgren-mineral-balance-soil-fertility]], improves crop nutrition, and can help sequester atmospheric carbon through enhanced plant growth
4. Rock dust application to forests and degraded lands can accelerate ecological restoration

While the science supporting broad claims about remineralization is mixed, controlled studies consistently show benefits from targeted mineral amendment, especially for trace element deficient soils.
