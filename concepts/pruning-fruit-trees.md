# Pruning Fruit Trees

Pruning is the selective removal of branches to shape the tree, improve fruit quality, increase light penetration, remove diseased or dead wood, and maintain tree health. Proper pruning is one of the most important skills for fruit tree care and directly affects yield, fruit size, and tree longevity.

## Why Prune

### Benefits of Proper Pruning

- **Improved light penetration**: Sun-exposed fruit ripens better, develops more sugar, and has fewer disease problems
- **Better air circulation**: Reduces humidity within the canopy, decreasing fungal disease pressure
- **Easier harvest**: Well-shaped trees are more accessible for picking
- **Disease management**: Removal of infected wood slows the spread of canker, fire blight, and other diseases
- **Tree vigor control**: Pruning balances vegetative growth with fruit production
- **Structural strength**: Removing weak crotches and crowded branches prevents breakage under fruit load
- **Renewal**: Removing old wood stimulates new productive growth

### When Not to Prune

- Do not prune in late fall or early winter (dormancy) in regions with severe cold — fresh cuts are more susceptible to winter injury
- Do not remove more than 25-30% of the tree's canopy in a single year
- Do not paint pruning wounds with tar or wound dressing — research shows these slow healing and can trap moisture and pathogens

## Central Leader Training

The central leader system is used for apples, pears, sweet cherries, and some plums. It produces a conical tree shape with a dominant central trunk.

### Structure

- One dominant central trunk (the leader) extending upward
- Scaffold branches arranged in whorls around the leader, spaced 6-12 inches vertically apart
- Lower branches are longer than upper branches, creating a cone shape
- First scaffold branches begin 24-36 inches above the ground for standard trees, 18-24 inches for dwarf

### Training Steps

**Year 1 (Planting)**
- If the tree is an unbranched whip (single stem), head it back at 30 inches above ground to encourage branching
- If the tree already has branches, select 3-4 well-spaced scaffold branches and remove the rest
- Scaffold branches should be distributed around the trunk, not all on one side
- Branches should emerge at wide angles (45-60° from vertical)

**Years 2-5 (Framework Development)**
- Select new scaffold branches each year, spaced 6-12 inches above the previous set
- Remove any branches that compete with the central leader
- Spread narrow-angled branches using clothespins, toothpicks, or spreaders (wide angles = stronger, more productive branches)
- Remove branches that grow downward or cross through the center
- Limit the tree to 4-5 sets of scaffold branches total

**Years 5+ (Maintenance Pruning)**
- Remove dead, diseased, or broken wood
- Thin crowded branches to maintain good light distribution
- Remove water sprouts (vigorous vertical shoots) from the trunk and scaffold bases
- Remove suckers from the rootstock below the graft union (see [[grafting-fruit-trees]])
- Thin fruiting spurs if they become too dense (especially on apples)

## Open Center (Vase) Training

The open center system is used for peaches, nectarines, plums, apricots, and some cherries. It creates a vase-shaped tree with no central leader and an open, sun-filled center.

### Structure

- No central trunk above the first scaffold branches
- 3-5 main scaffold branches radiating outward from the trunk like spokes on a wheel
- Open center allows maximum light penetration and air flow
- Height maintained at 8-12 feet for easy harvest

### Training Steps

**Year 1 (Planting)**
- Cut the tree back to knee height (18-24 inches) to force low branching
- Select 3-5 scaffold branches that are evenly spaced around the trunk (like compass points)
- Ideal branch angles: 45-60° from vertical
- Remove the central leader just above the highest scaffold branch
- Remove any branches below 18 inches

**Years 2-3**
- Scaffold branches develop secondary branches (sub-scaffolds)
- Select 2-3 sub-scaffolds per main scaffold, spaced along the branch (not clustered at the tip)
- Remove shoots growing into the center of the tree
- Maintain the open center — any shoot growing upright through the middle is removed
- Tip scaffold branches if they grow too long (cut back to an outward-facing bud)

**Years 3+ (Maintenance Pruning)**
- Peaches and nectarines fruit on 1-year-old wood: remove 50% of last year's growth each year to stimulate new fruiting wood
- Plums and apricots fruit on 2-3 year old spurs: lighter renewal pruning, removing older unproductive wood
- Remove dead, diseased, or broken branches annually
- Maintain the vase shape — do not allow the center to fill in

## Espalier

Espalier is the practice of training trees to grow flat against a wall, fence, or wire framework in a formal, two-dimensional pattern. It is both decorative and productive, ideal for small spaces.

### Common Espalier Patterns

- **Horizontal cordon**: Branches trained to horizontal wires at specific heights. Most common and easiest pattern.
- **Palmette verrier**: Branches trained upward at 45° angles in a fan shape.
- **Candelabra**: Vertical branches at the same height, like candlesticks.
- **Belgian fence**: Multiple trees trained into a diagonal lattice pattern.
- **Fan**: Branches radiating from a short trunk in a fan shape, trained against a wall.

### Training Steps

1. **Install support**: Wires at 12-18 inch intervals on a wall, fence, or between posts. Use wire anchors and turnbuckles for tension.
2. **Plant the tree**: Position the tree 6-12 inches from the support structure. Cut back to the lowest wire.
3. **Select framework branches**: Choose 2-3 branches per tier to train along the wires. Remove all others.
4. **Tie and train**: Use soft ties (raffia, tree tape, or flexible rubber ties) to secure branches to the wires. Check ties monthly and loosen as branches thicken.
5. **Summer pruning is critical**: Pinch back unwanted growth in June-July. In espalier, summer pruning does most of the shaping; winter pruning is for cleanup.
6. **Maintain**: Prune back horizontal branches to 2-3 buds beyond the last fruiting spur each winter to maintain the pattern.

### Best Trees for Espalier

- Apples and pears are the classic espalier subjects (spur-bearing, flexible, long-lived)
- Fig trees are excellent for warm-climate espalier
- Stone fruits (peach, plum) are less common but possible with fan training
- Citrus can be espaliered in mild climates

## Pruning Cuts

### Types of Cuts

**Thinning Cut**: Removes a branch entirely back to its point of origin (another branch or the trunk). Does not stimulate vigorous regrowth. This is the preferred cut for most pruning situations.

**Heading Cut**: Removes only the end portion of a branch, cutting to a bud or side branch. Stimulates vigorous regrowth behind the cut. Used for heading back whips, renewing old wood, and shaping young trees. Avoid heading mature branches — it creates dense, tangled growth.

### Making the Cut

1. **Identify the branch collar**: The swollen area where the branch meets the trunk. This contains specialized cells that heal the wound.
2. **Cut just outside the collar**: Do not cut into the collar (damages healing cells) and do not leave a stub (dies back and becomes an entry point for rot).
3. **Three-cut method for large branches** (over 1 inch diameter):
   - First cut: Undercut the branch 6-12 inches from the trunk (prevents bark stripping)
   - Second cut: Cut from the top, just outside the first cut, removing the branch
   - Third cut: Remove the remaining stub, cutting just outside the branch collar

## Timing by Tree Type

### Apples and Pears

- **Winter pruning (February-March)**: Primary structural pruning. Tree is dormant; branch structure is visible.
- **Summer pruning (June-August)**: Remove water sprouts, thin crowded areas, improve light. Less vigorous regrowth from summer cuts.
