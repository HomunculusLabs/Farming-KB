## Greenhouse End-Wall Ventilation: High-Low Systems for Mobile Houses

## Overview

End-wall ventilation is the primary method for managing temperature and airflow in Coleman's [[blesching-cannabis-fever-temperature-regulation]]
- This is one of the primary reasons Coleman's newer greenhouses are 22 feet wide by 48 feet long (a ratio of 2.18:1, well within the safe range)
- The previous generation of houses was 30 by 100 feet (3.33:1), which exceeded the end-wall ventilation threshold

### Design Implications
The 3:1 ratio means that end-wall ventilation determines the maximum practical length for any tunnel-style greenhouse. Growers planning end-wall-only ventilation must calculate their maximum length based on structure width, or supplement with sidewall ventilation for longer structures.

## Evolution of End-Wall Design

### First Generation: Sliding Houses

#### High Ventilation
Each end wall has two 4-foot-wide by 5-foot-tall access doors positioned side by side in the center of the wall. The base of the doors is 16 inches above ground level — an important height that prevents cold air from blowing directly across the crops when doors are opened for ventilation.

#### Low Ventilation
A strip of plastic covers the space between a permanent crossbar (installed beneath the doors, extending from one side of the greenhouse to the other) and the soil surface. This strip can be rolled up like a roll-up sidewall during summer, providing low-level airflow through the end walls.

#### The Crossbar Trade-off
- The crossbar stiffens the end bows to prevent splaying in or out when moving the house
- Workers must step over the crossbar when entering or exiting — described as awkward but a fair trade for structural integrity
- The crossbar also provides a framework for the low-ventilation roll-up system

### Second Generation: Improved Design for Rolling Houses

Moving to wheels on rails eliminated the need for the structural crossbar, since directional stability of the leading edge is assured by the rail system. This freed up the entire end wall for [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] presents a transitional challenge:

- Once snow is no longer a problem, the door can be rolled up from the bottom during the day and down at night, functioning like a roll-up sidewall
- When cold air drafting directly across crops might be detrimental, a 2-foot-wide piece of plastic is stretched horizontally across the bottom of the door opening as an **air barrier**
- This air barrier allows ventilation above crop level while preventing ground-level cold drafts — the same principle as the elevated door base in the sliding house design

### Summer Ventilation (Primary Concern: Maximum Airflow)
- Roll up the doors at both ends entirely and tie them in place
- This ensures maximum ventilation and allows easy access for harvesting or soil preparation for succession crops
- With a 12-foot-wide opening at both ends of a 22-foot-wide house, summer airflow is very effective
- The entire end wall essentially becomes an open passage

## Structural Stability Considerations

### Corner Stakes
Stakes are driven into the ground and bolted to the corners of the end wall door opening to stabilize the frame. These are removed before the greenhouse is moved.

### The Crossbar Function (Sliding Houses)
The crossbar beneath the doors serves dual purposes:
1. Prevents end walls from splaying during moves
2. Provides structural rigidity for the entire end frame

When the crossbar was eliminated in the wheeled-house design, corner stakes became the primary end-wall stabilization method.
