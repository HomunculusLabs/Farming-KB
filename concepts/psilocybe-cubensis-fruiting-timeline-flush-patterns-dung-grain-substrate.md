# Psilocybe Cubensis Fruiting Timeline and Flush Patterns on Dung-Grain Substrate

## Overview

*Psilocybe cubensis* fruiting on horse dung and rice grain substrate follows
a predictable timeline from inoculation through multiple flushes of mushroom
production. The Gartz et al. (1990) study documented detailed fruiting
parameters for this species, including the timing of first fruit body
appearance, the number of mushrooms per flush, total dry mass yield, and
the number of productive flushes. These data provide baseline benchmarks
for cultivators working with this substrate combination.

## Substrate Composition

The dung-grain substrate used in the Gartz study consisted of a horse dung
and rice grain mixture prepared in water. Additionally, the substrate
contained 5 grams of KH2PO4 (monopotassium phosphate) per liter. The
addition of KH2PO4 serves as a buffer and phosphorus source, helping to
maintain substrate pH and provide essential nutrients for fungal metabolism.
This substrate was prepared in jars and sterilized before inoculation.

## Fruiting Timeline: Control vs. Brassinosteroid-Treated

### Control Cultures

In control cultures without brassinosteroid supplementation, the first
sporocarps (fruit bodies) appeared within 4 to 5 weeks after inoculation.
This timeline represents the standard fruiting initiation period for *P.
cubensis* on dung-grain substrate when no growth-promoting compounds are
added. The 4-5 week window encompasses the period required for complete
substrate colonization followed by the physiological transition from
[[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] to reproductive development.

### Brassinosteroid-Treated Cultures

When 22S,23S-homobrassinolide was added at 10^-2 ppm (0.01 ppm), the first
fruit bodies appeared in only 3 to 3.5 weeks after inoculation. This
represents an acceleration of approximately 1-2 weeks, or roughly a 25-40%
reduction in the time to first harvest. The acceleration is attributable to
faster substrate colonization (mycelium grew 2-3 times faster on agar in
the same study) combined with a possible direct effect of the brassinosteroid
on the fruiting initiation process.

## First Flush Characteristics

The first flush is typically the largest and most commercially significant
flush in *P. cubensis* cultivation. The Gartz study documented significant
differences between control and treated cultures in first flush production.

### Control First Flush

Control cultures produced one to three mushrooms in the first flush. This
relatively low first-flush count is typical for dung-grain substrates, which
tend to produce fewer but larger individual mushrooms compared to grain-only
substrates.

### Brassinosteroid First Flush

Brassinosteroid-treated cultures produced four to seven mushrooms in the
first flush, representing a 2-3 fold increase in first-flush mushroom count
compared to controls. This increase in fruit body number directly translates
to higher total yield per flush. The larger first flush suggests that the
brassinosteroid promotes more prolific pinning ([[chen-maitake-primordia-initiation-fruiting]]) in
addition to accelerating the overall fruiting timeline.

## Multi-Flush Production

Both control and brassinosteroid-treated cultures produced five flushes
during the fruiting period. However, the variation in mushroom number
between flushes was notable, with the brassinosteroid-treated cultures
showing more consistent production across flushes. The ability to produce
multiple flushes from a single [[cotter-compost-substrate-preparation-manure-mushroom-cultivation]] is economically
important in cultivation, as it maximizes the yield obtained from the labor
and materials invested in substrate preparation and sterilization.

## Dry Mass Yield

Total dry mass is the most important yield metric for comparing cultivation
methods, as it accounts for variation in individual mushroom size and water
content. The Gartz study reported dry mass per 10 grams of substrate.

### Control Yield

Control cultivations yielded 2.3 to 2.9 grams dry mass per 10 grams of
substrate, representing a [[stamets-biological-efficiency-yield]] (dry mass yield divided by
dry substrate mass) of approximately 23-29%.

### Brassinosteroid Yield

Brassinosteroid-treated cultures yielded 3.4 to 3.9 grams dry mass per 10
grams of substrate, representing a biological efficiency of approximately
34-39%. This represents a yield increase of approximately 40-50% over
control cultures, which is a substantial improvement by any cultivation
standard.

## Incomplete Fruit Body Suppression

A common problem in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] is the formation of incomplete
fruit bodies that fail to fully develop. These aborts consume substrate
resources without producing harvestable material and can serve as sites for
mold contamination. The Gartz study noted that the formation of incomplete
fruit bodies, normally present in mushroom cultivation, was almost completely
suppressed by brassinosteroid treatment. This reduction in aborts contributes
to the higher overall yield and more efficient substrate utilization in
treated cultures.

## Morphological Effects on Fruiting Bodies

Interestingly, the brassinosteroid treatment also influenced the morphology
of the fruit bodies that were produced. Treated cultures yielded mushrooms
that lacked the typical membranous annulus (ring) on the stem. The annulus
is a remnant of the partial veil that covers the gills during development
and is a characteristic taxonomic feature of *P. cubensis*. Its absence
in brassinosteroid-treated cultures suggests that the steroidal compound
interferes with normal veil development or that the accelerated growth rate
prevents proper [[brassinosteroid-suppressed-annulus-formation-psilocybe-cubensis]] before the veil tears.

## Cultivation Optimization Implications

The data from the Gartz study suggest several approaches to optimizing *P.
cubensis* cultivation on dung-grain substrate:

- **Substrate buffering**: The addition of KH2PO4 at 5 g/L provides pH
  buffering and phosphorus supplementation that supports vigorous growth
- **Growth promotion**: Ultra-low doses of brassinosteroids (10 ppb) can
  significantly accelerate colonization and increase yield
- **Flush management**: Expecting 5 flushes allows planning for extended
  harvest periods and efficient substrate utilization
- **Abort management**: Any treatment that reduces incomplete fruit body
  formation improves both yield and hygiene
