# Permaculture Community Enterprises and Self-Reliance

The final section of [[bill-mollison]]'s [[permaculture-design]] Course focuses on the practical enterprises, organizational systems, and community structures that make [[permaculture]] viable as a complete way of life. This goes far beyond agriculture into publishing, seed supply, manufacturing, communications, and political activism.

## The Enterprise Philosophy

Mollison's core enterprise principle is straightforward: "We do not handle anything that doesn't promote self-reliance." The Tagari community operates businesses that serve the permaculture network while generating income for community members. The emphasis is always on enablement over charity—giving people the means to produce, not giving them products or money directly.

The model distributes enterprises across many individuals rather than concentrating them. Each person maintains a primary activity (often gardening or farming) while operating a specialized business part-time. A travel agent spends most of their time in the garden and occasionally books flights. A real estate agent drives between properties to check on orchards and tenants between garden tasks.

## The Travel Agency

A community travel agency is one of the first enterprises Mollison recommends. Permaculture consultants must fly extensively—to China, America, and throughout their regions. Normal agency fees run approximately 15%, meaning 20 consultants flying at $2,000 each generates $6,000 in fees. Rather than sending this to a conventional agency, a community member earns it by booking flights part-time.

In Australia, the Tagari community had to study to become qualified travel agents since they knew none when starting. Mollison advises finding someone already qualified in the consultancy network who can serve as an agent immediately.

## The Real Estate Business

Real estate serves permaculture communities in several ways. Licensed agents within the network can manage properties, earning $1,000 to $2,000 annually per property for oversight, leasing, and ensuring tenants maintain orchards and avoid bulldozing trees. The agents take no ownership risk—they simply manage.

Beyond management, real estate agents provide control over what happens across large areas of land. The goal is not ownership but influence—ensuring that properties are maintained productively rather than destructively. In Australia, six qualified real estate agents were found who had left conventional practice to live simply in the bush but retained their qualifications.

## The Self-Reliance Seed Company

The Self-Reliance Seed Company (later replaced by Phoenix Seed Co.) represents perhaps the most carefully designed permaculture enterprise. Its principles include:

- **Open-pollinated seeds only**: All varieties allow gardeners to save their own seed
- **Distributed production**: Seed is never grown on large centralized farms (which could be sprayed). Instead, individual growers collect seed from forests, gardens, and wild populations
- **Grower margins**: The person who collects seed receives both the grower's margin and the packager's margin, eliminating middlemen
- **Self-reducing sales**: The explicit goal is that seed exchanges expand while commercial sales shrink, until every wanted plant is grown locally
- **Functional cataloging**: Plants listed not alphabetically but by function—Bee Forages, Chicken Forages, Cattle Forages, Deer Forages, Fireproof Plants—and by climate zone

The company provides stamped envelopes to growers who request them. Growers collect seed, fill envelopes, and sell at near-retail prices. A cataloger (essentially a publisher) earns from printing the catalog. The system also facilitates free seed exchanges between members.

Every packet includes instructions on saving seed, reinforcing the self-reliance mission. If seed proves unsatisfactory, the full packet price is refunded.

## Manufacturing: The Yeoman's Plow

Mollison outlines a distributed manufacturing model using [[geoff-wallace]]'s plow as an example. Components are produced by three separate small manufacturers—one makes the shoe, another the shank, and an unemployed group makes frames (ten at a time). A primary manufacturer bolts parts together and appoints distributors.

Permaculture designers become the distribution network, carrying only small brochures. Clients who want a plow are connected through this network. Pre-orders finance production runs. The entire system can be started with no capital or very little.

The broader manufacturing philosophy extends to any permaculture-specific hardware: oil presses, grain cleaners, seed cleaners, husker-grinders, field stills, and small processing equipment. The critical gap Mollison identifies is processing hardware—while chippers, plows, and seeders are available, pressers, oil seed extractors, juicers, stills, and dryers are the "real bases of self-sufficiency on a small site" and remain difficult to find.

## The Hardware Assessment and Selection Group

Mollison envisions a dedicated group that evaluates and acquires permaculture hardware: solar equipment, ducting, squirrel cage fans, automatic vents, sealants, insulation, and retrofit materials. This group negotiates with manufacturers for distribution rights, preferably manufacture rights, starting with retail as a fallback.

The vehicle strategy demonstrates this approach: by registering 51 consultants' 17 Toyotas as a fleet, they secured 15% off list price. Combined with the Institute's sales tax exemption, the savings were substantial. The community's engineering group also develops fuel efficiency modifications and alcohol-gas conversion kits.

## Tanks and Small-Scale Infrastructure

Shallow tanks (15 to 18 inches deep, 4 to 6 feet diameter) are identified as critically lacking in America. Made of precast concrete without drain holes, they serve multiple functions: bulb growing with an inch of water over saturated soil, pond creation with 6 to 12 inches of soil, stock watering, poultry watering, and aquaculture nurseries. A single aquaculture nursery might use 40 to 50 of these tanks. They weigh approximately 1,000 pounds when full and are easily produced from molds.

## Communications Network

The Australian permaculture network developed a sophisticated communications system operating at multiple levels:

### Ham Radio and Computer Network
A $300 package coupled with ham radio licenses allows Apple computers to communicate globally. Users store information (species lists, design data) that can be queried by any other node. When someone requests a bee plant list for an alkaline hillside in Timbuktu, the local node routes to the central database, which returns the list within two seconds and automatically charges the requester's account. No paperwork, no mailing.

### Radio Broadcasting
Weekly half-hour permaculture radio programs reach 6 to 12 million listeners across Australia. These programs raise public awareness and mobilize political action.

### Emergency Action Network
Six key telephone points across the nation link weekly. A single message can reach the entire country through a chain of local dissemination groups. This system was used to jam the Tasmanian Parliament switchboard for a week, forcing midnight legislation to stop aerial spraying of farmland.

### The Down to Earth Association
This fast tape-copying service duplicates important messages and distributes them to media contacts on both government and private sides, achieving broadcast through public access programs.

## Political Activism Tactics

Mollison describes several effective tactics for political change:

- **State Emergency Services**: When dangerous spraying was planned, Mollison called the State Emergency Service to warn of danger to pregnant women and farm animals. They were legally required to broadcast the warning, creating public embarrassment for the sprayers.
- **Act of defiance**: Rather than merely providing information (which is "just news"), the Self-Reliance Seed Company was established as a direct act of defiance against seed patenting legislation. The combination of publicity, voice, and action proved too embarrassing for legislators to maintain their position.
- **Unified voice**: "If you do cooperate and stop chopping up the whole system into little bunches of people... if you do speak with one voice, brother, you are a loud voice."

## Permaculture Associations

Australian permaculture associations evolved into powerful local organizations that:

- **Share species**: Members exchange plants, seeds, and genetic material
- **Visit each other's places**: Cross-pollination of design ideas and techniques
- **Organize housework cooperatives**: Groups of parents with small children rotate cleaning each other's homes and gardens on Saturdays
- **Welcome new residents**: Groups of up to 100 people arrive with trailers and tractors to clean up properties for newcomers, then throw a party
- **Identify urban resources**: The Perth association cataloged every large useful tree in the city, including dragon's blood trees and South African wild plums unknown to most residents
- **Produce publications**: Some associations publish quarterlies and newsletters
- **Take on degraded land**: One association moved onto deteriorated sheep country with thousands of seeds for restoration
- **Train new designers**: Many association members become permaculture designers themselves

## The Watts Self-Help Group

Mollison's account of Barney Mull and the Watts Self-Help Group illustrates [[permaculture-principles]] applied in the most challenging urban environment. Barney, a former debt collector who became an alcoholic and was told by a doctor he would die if he didn't stop drinking, went to Watts expecting to die. Local children fed him for eight years. When he recovered, he devoted his life to them.

His strategy for taking over gang territory involved color-coded baseball caps for identification, a green van for rapid insertion, scouting teams, and a system where gang leaders were surrounded and told "You're dead—we've killed you" before negotiating cooperation. Through this method, Barney eventually united 70 gangs that ran employment, gardens, and the entire district.

Firms in the area gave Barney thousands of dollars, most of which he returned. He progressed from 15% to 85% self-financing and employed accountants. The firms trusted him with their money because they couldn't operate without community support.

Barney was shot not long after Mollison's visit, but as Mollison notes: "He had counted himself dead years before, and we must all count ourselves dead before we tackle real risks."

## Enablement Over Charity

The distinction between enablement and charity is repeatedly emphasized. The community funds a college to teach aboriginal gardening but does not give aborigines money. They give orange trees, not money to buy orange trees. They give fishhooks, not fish. The principle is always to provide self-reliance.

## The Larger Vision

Mollison closes with a call for a "university of minds, a companionship" with no stratification. The system is designed to be superseded if it succeeds. The goal is to get people competent on their own ground, in their own home country, with robust support systems. He frames this as life or death: "I think it's life or death, and I'm hitting hard on the life side."
