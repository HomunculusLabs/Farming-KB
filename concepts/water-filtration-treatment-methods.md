## Coagulation and Flocculation

- A sand filter bed should be approximately **1.2 metres deep** to provide adequate contact time and filtration depth.
- The grading runs from **fine sand at the top** to **coarser material at the bottom**, creating a gradient that traps progressively smaller particles through the depth of the bed.
- Periodic maintenance involves **sweeping** the surface layer to remove accumulated biofilm and debris, and occasionally **roasting** or replacing the upper sand layer to restore filtration capacity. Roasting burns off organic clogging material and rejuvenates the bed.
- Sand filters are effective at removing particulates, protozoan cysts, and many bacteria, and they support beneficial microbial communities that contribute to biological treatment.

## Activated Charcoal Filtration

Activated charcoal (carbon) provides powerful adsorption of dissolved organic compounds, odours, some heavy metals, and chemical contaminants:

- Charcoal can be **activated from bones, willow, or coconut husks**, each source producing carbon with slightly different adsorption properties. The activation process creates a highly porous internal structure with enormous surface area.
- Bone char is particularly effective at removing fluoride; coconut husk carbon excels at organic compound adsorption. Willow charcoal has historically been used in simple household filters.
- Charcoal filters are typically arranged as **vertical columns** through which water passes by gravity. Multiple columns in series increase contact time and removal efficiency.
- As a final polishing stage, **carbon and [[silicon]] dioxide columns** provide the last line of chemical and particulate removal, producing clear, clean water suitable for a wide range of uses.

## Biological Treatment Systems

### Bacterial and Microbial Removal

Bacteria are the workhorses of biological water treatment, breaking down organic matter and converting dissolved nutrients:

- **Trickle filters** pass water over media surfaces that support resident bacterial biofilms. As water trickles through, bacteria consume organic waste and transform nitrogenous compounds from ammonia to nitrite to nitrate.
- **Carbon addition** in the form of simple carbohydrates ([[methanol]], [[ethanol]], or acetic acid) encourages bacterial growth, ensuring a robust microbial population capable of handling nutrient loads. This is particularly important in systems receiving high-nitrogen waste streams.
- Bacterial action removes nitrogen, reduces BOD (biological oxygen demand), and decomposes dissolved organic contaminants. Maintaining aerobic conditions in the filter bed is critical for these processes.

### Phytoplankton and Higher Plants

- **Phytoplankton** absorb dissolved nutrients and produce oxygen through photosynthesis, supporting aerobic bacterial activity. They form the base of aquatic food webs in treatment ponds.
- **Aquatic plants** (water hyacinth, water lilies, Azolla) provide surface coverage that limits light penetration, reducing algal overgrowth, while their root systems harbour biofilms that filter water passing through them.
- **Watercress** is particularly effective at removing dyes and nitrates from contaminated water, making it valuable for treating agricultural runoff or greywater containing dissolved chemicals.
- **Water lilies, Azolla, and water hyacinth** serve double duty: they stabilise the water surface against wind disturbance and exclude excess light, suppressing unwanted algal blooms while providing habitat for filtration organisms.

## Pond Bank Stabilisation

Erosion of pond banks introduces sediment and turbidity into water bodies. Stabilising plantings prevent this:

- **Juncus** (rushes) and **Scirpus** (bulrushes) have dense, fibrous root mats that hold soil along water margins and withstand periodic inundation.
- **Grasses** provide quick-establishing ground cover on embankments and spillways, protecting against rainfall splash and surface runoff erosion.
- **Phyla nodiflora** (frogfruit) is a low-growing, spreading ground cover suited to wet margins, forming dense mats that resist scouring.
- **Bamboo** provides deep structural root reinforcement on larger earthworks and dam walls, with the added benefit of producing useful pole material.

These plants create a biological buffer zone that filters runoff before it enters open water, trapping sediments and absorbing excess nutrients.

## Mussel Filtration

Freshwater mussels offer a remarkable natural filtration capacity:

- A single mussel can filter **up to 100 litres per day**, removing suspended algae, bacteria, and fine particulates from the water column.
- Mussels serve as living **pH monitors** — their behaviour, feeding rate, and shell condition reflect water chemistry changes over time.
- They are highly **sensitive to biocides** and pollutants, making them both effective filters and early-warning indicators of water quality degradation in the system.
- Mussel beds should be placed in protected, slow-flow zones where they can feed continuously without being disturbed by strong currents or predation.

## Soil and Aquatic Plant Filtration

Soils act as complex living filters when water is directed through them:

- Constructed wetlands and reed beds channel water through graded soil media planted with aquatic species, combining physical, chemical, and biological treatment in a single passive system.
- Root-zone filtration combines physical straining with biological breakdown as water passes through the rhizosphere — the zone of intense microbial activity around plant roots.
- These systems require no machinery and operate entirely on gravity flow, making them ideal for rural and off-grid applications.

## No-Machinery Gravity-Flow Systems

A core permaculture principle in water treatment is the elimination of mechanical pumps and energy inputs wherever possible:

- Treatment trains are arranged on **graded land** so water moves from one stage to the next by gravity alone, from the highest elevation intake to the lowest outlet.
- Sequential stages typically follow: aeration → settling → skimming → sand filtration → biological treatment (plants and bacteria) → charcoal polishing.
- This approach dramatically reduces maintenance, energy costs, and system complexity while producing water of high quality. It also increases system resilience, as there are no mechanical components to fail.

## Design Considerations

When planning a permaculture water treatment system, several factors influence the choice and sequencing of methods:

- **Source water quality** determines which contaminants must be addressed and how many treatment stages are required. Clear spring water needs less treatment than turbid agricultural runoff.
- **Available fall** (elevation change) across the site governs how many gravity-fed stages can be accommodated. Steeper terrain allows more stages; flat sites may require fewer, larger treatment cells.
- **Climate and temperature** affect biological treatment rates. Warm climates support faster bacterial activity and year-round plant growth, while cold climates may slow treatment significantly in winter.
- **Output quality requirements** dictate the final polishing needed. Drinking water demands charcoal or SiO₂ columns; irrigation water may need only settling and biological treatment.
- **Maintenance capacity** should match the system's complexity. Simple systems with fewer stages are more forgiving of intermittent attention.

## Summary of Treatment Stages

| Stage | Method | Purpose |
|-------|--------|---------|
| Pre-treatment | Aeration, skimming, sieving | Remove debris and gases |
| Primary | Settling, coagulation/flocculation | Reduce turbidity and suspended solids |
| Secondary | Sand filtration, soil filtration | Remove fine particles and pathogens |
| Biological | Bacteria, phytoplankton, aquatic plants | Decompose organics, remove nutrients |
| Polishing | Activated charcoal, SiO₂ columns | Adsorb dissolved chemicals and odours |
| Monitoring | Mussels, plant health indicators | Detect contamination events |
