# Practical Farm Establishment, Site Selection, and Soil Preparation

In *The Natural Way of Farming*, Masanobu Fukuoka provides detailed
guidance on establishing a natural farm from scratch. This covers the
critical decisions of site selection, land evaluation, soil management,
and the transition from conventional or degraded land to a
self-sustaining natural farming system. Unlike his philosophical
chapters, this material is grounded in decades of practical experience.

## The Natural Farming Philosophy of Establishment

Fukuoka's approach to farm establishment is guided by a single principle:
trust nature. Rather than imposing an elaborate design on the land, the
farmer should observe what the land wants to become and facilitate that
natural tendency. This means selecting sites that already possess the
fundamental conditions for agriculture, rather than attempting to force
agriculture onto unsuitable land through massive earthworks, drainage
projects, or chemical interventions.

He repeatedly warns against the mentality of conquering nature. The road
to a truly natural farm lies not in defeating or controlling natural
forces, but in surrendering to them, working within the conditions they
provide.

## Selecting the Right Site

### Terrain and Climate

The ideal site for a natural farm is one where nature has already
demonstrated agricultural potential. Fukuoka looks for land with gentle
slopes, good natural drainage, access to water, and a history of
vegetation growth. Flat alluvial plains near rivers are excellent
because they have rich, deep soils deposited by centuries of natural
flooding. However, these same lands are often the most sought-after by
conventional agriculture.

Gentle hillside land can be excellent if it receives adequate rainfall
and has deep soil. Steep slopes present challenges for access and
erosion, though Fukuoka notes that terracing, when done with natural
materials and following the contour of the land, can convert difficult
terrain into productive growing space. He cautions that large-scale
terracing projects requiring heavy machinery almost always do more harm
than good by destroying the natural soil structure.

### Water Access

Water is the most critical factor. Fukuoka favors sites with natural
springs, streams, or reliable rainfall. In areas dependent on
irrigation, he recommends using water from ponds, reservoirs, or
streams that flow through the land rather than pumped groundwater.
Natural water sources bring with them nutrients and microorganisms that
support soil health.

The presence of water also determines what can be grown. Rice, his
primary crop, requires standing water during part of its growth cycle.
Orchards need less water but benefit from consistent moisture. A good
farm site offers a mix of wet and dry areas to support diverse cropping.

### Evaluating Soil Quality

Fukuoka takes a different approach to soil evaluation than conventional
agriculture. Instead of sending samples to a laboratory for chemical
analysis, he observes the native vegetation, soil color, texture,
drainage, and the presence of earthworms and other soil organisms.

Dark, crumbly soil with abundant earthworms indicates healthy, active
soil life. Hard, compacted, pale soil with no visible organic matter
suggests poor conditions. He notes that soil analysis can be misleading
because it measures only chemical constituents present at a moment in
time, not the dynamic biological processes that make nutrients available
to plants.

## Land Reclamation and Preparation

### Clearing Vegetation

When establishing a farm on overgrown or abandoned land, Fukuoka
recommends a gradual approach rather than wholesale clearing. Cutting
all vegetation at once exposes the soil to erosion and disrupts the
ecosystem. Instead, selectively remove the largest trees and unwanted
plants while preserving ground cover.

For heavily forested land, he suggests a two-stage process. First,
thin the canopy to allow light to reach the ground. Then, as ground
vegetation establishes itself, gradually remove remaining trees that
compete with crops. This mimics the natural succession process and
maintains soil health throughout the transition.

### Soil Building Without Fertilizer

The cornerstone of Fukuoka's soil preparation is the rejection of all
artificial fertilizers. Instead, he builds soil through natural
processes:

- **Green manure crops**: Planting legumes and other nitrogen-fixing
  plants that add organic matter when they die and decompose. Clover,
  vetch, and various bean crops serve this purpose.

- **Mulching**: Covering the soil surface with straw, leaves, and other
  organic material. This suppresses weeds, retains moisture, moderates
  soil temperature, and provides a continuous source of organic matter
  as the mulch decomposes from below.

- **Natural composting**: Allowing crop residues and weeds to decompose
  in place rather than removing them. Fukuoka scatters straw and chaff
  from harvested rice directly back onto the fields.

- **Earthworm cultivation**: Earthworms are the farmer's most important
  allies. They aerate the soil, break down organic matter, and create
  channels for water infiltration.

Fukuoka emphasizes that soil preparation is not a one-time event but an
ongoing process. The soil improves year after year under natural farming
as organic matter accumulates and biological activity increases.
