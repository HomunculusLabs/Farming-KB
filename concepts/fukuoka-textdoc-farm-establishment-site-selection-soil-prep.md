## The Natural Farming Philosophy of Establishment

Fukuoka's approach to farm establishment is guided by a single principle:
trust nature. Rather than imposing an elaborate design on the land, the
farmer should observe what the land wants to become and facilitate that
natural tendency. This means selecting sites that already possess the
fundamental conditions for agriculture, rather than attempting to force
agriculture onto unsuitable land through massive earthworks, drainage
projects, or chemical interventions.

He repeatedly warns against the mentality of conquering nature. The road
to a truly natural farm lies not in defeating or controlling natural
forces, but in surrendering to them, working within the conditions they
provide.

## Selecting the Right Site

### Terrain and Climate

The ideal site for a natural farm is one where nature has already
demonstrated agricultural potential. Fukuoka looks for land with gentle
slopes, good natural drainage, access to water, and a history of
vegetation growth. Flat alluvial plains near rivers are excellent
because they have rich, deep soils deposited by centuries of natural
flooding. However, these same lands are often the most sought-after by
conventional agriculture.

Gentle hillside land can be excellent if it receives adequate rainfall
and has deep soil. Steep slopes present challenges for access and
erosion, though Fukuoka notes that terracing, when done with natural
materials and following the contour of the land, can convert difficult
terrain into productive [[jadam-soil-compaction-heavy-machinery]] almost always do more harm
than good by destroying the natural soil structure.

### Water Access

Water is the most critical factor. Fukuoka favors sites with natural
springs, streams, or reliable rainfall. In areas dependent on
irrigation, he recommends using water from ponds, reservoirs, or
streams that flow through the land rather than pumped groundwater.
Natural water sources bring with them nutrients and microorganisms that
support soil health.
