## Phyllotaxy — Nature's Order

Fukuoka turned to natural forests for his evidence. There, "The trunks of these trees
never branch or twist as long as they are not cut or harmed." He observed that "No
part of the plant is in disarray or confusion." Leaves "always grow either alternately
or oppositely. The direction and even the angle at which a leaf grows is always the
same." This regularity is not coincidental: "The arrangement of the leaves on a plant
always and unerringly obeys a fixed law called phyllotaxy."

On peach, persimmon, mandarin, orange, and cherry trees, he documented that the sixth
leaf always grows directly above the first, and the eleventh above the sixth. The
distance between overlapping leaves is consistently five inches, meaning "Two leaves
will not overlap, or two branches emerge, within any five-inch length along the
branch." Branches maintain their spacing without interference: "Never does one branch
cross over another; lower and upper branches maintain the same distance over their
entire length." His conclusion was unequivocal: "Not a single wasted leaf, not a
single branch lacking—that is the true form of a plant."

## The Bonsai Analogy

To illustrate how human interference corrupts [[aact-natural-growth-regulators-bacteria-fungi-humus-cycle-ingham]], Fukuoka drew on the
familiar example of bonsai cultivation. "Even lightly pinching new buds on a pine
seedling totally alters the shape of the emerging branches; the young tree can be
trained into a garden pine or even a bonsai." And "Once a bonsai, the pine can never
be restored to a full-size tree."

The process of deformation follows a predictable pattern. "The gardener prunes the
young shoots of a pine... the second year several suckers grow out from each of these
wounds... by about the third year, the branches of the pine become entangled and
crooked." The critical threshold is reached early: "Once the pruning shears have been
taken to the tree and branches emerge in complicated shapes, the tree can no longer be
left alone." Even the smallest intervention has outsized consequences: "Just by
nipping one bud at the tip of a sapling, what should have grown into a straight pine
with one trunk develops instead into a complex tree with several leaders."

## Pruning Creates Need for More Pruning

Fukuoka's central insight about pruning can be stated plainly: "Pruning only creates a
need for more pruning." He described how casually growers approach this irreversible
act: "People will stand in front of a citrus tree and, saying that these branches here
are growing so closely that they are shutting off sunlight, casually make a few quick
cuts with the shears. But they never stop to consider the enormous impact this has on
the tree."

The consequence of that single intervention extends across the entire life of the
tree: "Because of this single pruning, the grower will have to continue pruning the
tree for the rest of its lifetime." The alternative is to begin differently:
"Training the branches at the start into a shape close to the natural form of the tree
will make the pruning shears unnecessary."

## Discovery of the Natural Form — The Central Leader System

Fukuoka did not arrive at his understanding through theory alone. He applied natural
[[cho-poultry-natural-farming-methods]] to an established Satsuma orange grove of a couple hundred trees per
acre. The trees had been trained into a conventional wineglass shape, standing six to
seven feet tall. When he simply discontinued pruning, the results were chaotic:
"Large numbers of scaffold branches and laterals grew out at once... crisscrossing,
doubling back, growing in strange, twisted shapes." He conceded that "After this
experience, even I had to admit that abandoning the trees to their own devices was a
sure path to ruin."

He then swung to the opposite extreme with heavy pruning, but this too failed—inner
branches withered and [[berry-fruit-production-cool-humid-climates]] dropped. The breakthrough came when he planted
citrus from seed and carefully observed their natural growth, allowing many types to
grow entirely unpruned. Through this patience, "I was able to divine with considerable
certainty the natural form of a citrus tree."

When he reported his findings at the Ehime Prefectural Fruit Growers Association,
stating that "the natural form of the citrus tree is not what it had been thought to
be, but a central leader type form," it "created a stir... but was laughed off as just
so much nonsense by the farmers."

## Natural Form Examples

Through observation, Fukuoka identified the natural forms of numerous fruit species:

- **Early-ripening Satsuma**: low, pyramidal form
- **Late-ripening Satsuma**: tallish, cypress-like conical form
- **Summer orange, shaddock, persimmon, chestnut, pear, apple, loquat**: tall,
  cedar-like conical form

"All are basically central leader type trees. Their differences in form arise primarily
from the differing number, angle, and directions of the scaffold branches." The
diversity of shapes seen in orchards, he argued, is not natural variation at all:
"People have merely been misled by the various forms that these fruit trees have taken
after being disturbed by their environment and human intervention."

## Five Advantages of the Natural Form

Fukuoka identified five key advantages of allowing fruit trees to grow in their
natural central leader form:

1. **Maximum growth and sunlight exposure yield maximum production.** An unnatural
   form "reduces the tree's natural powers and commits the grower to unending labors."

2. **An erect central trunk produces little entanglement.** As a result, "pruning
   gradually decreases" and only "minimum care needed" remains.

3. **The conical form allows oblique sunlight to penetrate the interior** of the
   canopy. By contrast, the open-center inverse triangle shape widely used in
   commercial orchards "reduces light to base" of the tree.

4. **Nutrients distribute optimally.** The tree maintains a "balanced external shape"
   and produces a "full harvest each year" without the boom-bust cycle of alternate
   bearing.

5. **The root system mirrors the aboveground portion.** A naturally shaped tree
   develops "deep roots," producing a "healthy, resistant tree."

## Conclusion on Fruit Growing

Fukuoka situated his argument against pruning within his broader philosophy of natural
farming. "The principles of no weeding, no fertilization, and no pruning cannot be
achieved independently; they are closely and inextricably tied to each other." When a
tree grows in its natural form without chemical inputs, "In principle, disease and pest
damage do not exist."
