# Mushroom Symbolism in Ancient Cultures

[[terence-mckenna]]'s Food of the Gods traces the diffusion of mushroom symbolism across ancient cultures, arguing that the presence of mushroom imagery in diverse civilizations is evidence of a once-universal psychedelic mushroom religion that was progressively suppressed, symbolized, and eventually forgotten.

## The Four Stages of Abandonment

McKenna proposes a universal pattern for how mushroom-using cultures lose their entheogenic practice:

1. **Substitution**: Other psychoactive plants replace the original mushroom sacrament
2. **Symbolic replacement**: Inactive materials stand in for the original substance
3. **Purely symbolic religion**: Dogma and ritual replace direct experience
4. **Active repression**: The original practice is criminalized and its memory suppressed

Mushroom symbolism in ancient cultures represents the archaeological residue of stages 2 and 3: the mushroom has been replaced by symbols, but the symbols preserve a memory of the original practice.

## The Wassons' Discovery of Mushroom Diffusion

McKenna draws heavily on the Wassons' research documenting the cultural diffusion of mushroom lore:

- **Siberian Amanita traditions**: Koryak legends of Big Raven receiving "spirits" from the god Vahiyinin that grew from his spittle--these spirits being fly agaric mushrooms granting supernatural strength and wisdom
- **Scandinavian Bronze Age**: Mushroom motifs on bronze razors (c. 1000 BCE) depict stylized cross-sectional views suggesting worship
- **European folktales**: Yugoslav peasants trace fly agaric origins to Votan, chief of gods; Hungarians call it "boland gamba" or "mad mushroom"; Germans speak of the "fool's mushroom"
- **Mycophobia**: The Wassons traced the widespread European fear of mushrooms to cultural taboos originally designed to protect shamanic mushroom use from profanation

## Wasson's Summary

Wasson: "Death will come if the layman presumes to eat this forbidden fruit, the Fruit of Knowledge, the Divine Mushroom of Immortality that the poets of the Rig Veda celebrated. The fear of this 'death' has lived on as an emotional residue long after the shaman and his religion have faded from memory, and here is the explanation for the mycophobia that has prevailed throughout Northern Europe, in the Germanic and Celtic worlds."

## Mesoamerican Mushroom Stones

The mushroom stones of highland Guatemala provide the most extensive archaeological evidence of mushroom symbolism. Dating from approximately 1000 BCE to 900 CE, these stone carvings depict anthropomorphic and zoomorphic figures surmounted by or emerging from mushroom caps. The early forms (1000-500 BCE) are simple "monolithic" designs; middle forms introduce anthropomorphic faces; late forms show elaborate human figures with hands in various positions, clearly associated with specific rituals.

## Mushroom Imagery in Greek and Christian Art

McKenna and Wasson argued that mushroom symbolism persists in classical and Christian art:

- A 2,500-year-old vessel from Anatolia with "two grinning anthropomorphic mushrooms embossed on its surface"
- Small mushroom-shaped green stone objects found in Yugoslavia
- The Wasson-Hofmann-Ruck theory that the purple associated with Demeter's robe in the [[eleusinian-mysteries]] matches the purple of ergot sclerotia
- Wasson's analysis of mushroom motifs in Christian frescoes and illuminated manuscripts (developed in his later work on The Tree of Knowledge)

## Universal Patterns in Mushroom Symbolism

Several symbolic patterns recur across mushroom-using cultures:

- **Underworld/journey imagery**: The mushroom facilitates a descent into the underworld of the psyche
- **Death and rebirth**: The mushroom experience involves ego death followed by renewal (the Greek phrase "thantos thanatos" associated with the Eleusinian Mysteries)
- **Divine communication**: The mushroom speaks; it gives messages, predictions, and visions
- **Fruit of Knowledge**: Wasson explicitly identified the mushroom with the forbidden fruit of Genesis
- **Bull associations**: The Wassons connected mushroom imagery to bull-cult symbols across multiple cultures

## The Persistence of Symbol

Even when the original mushroom use has been completely forgotten, the symbolic vocabulary persists. McKenna argues that this persistence is evidence of the mushroom's extraordinary psychological power: an experience profound enough to generate enduring cultural symbols even after the practice that created them has been lost for thousands of years.

# Wasson's Rediscovery of Mushroom Cults

R. Gordon Wasson (1898-1986), a vice president of J.P. Morgan and amateur mycologist, was responsible for the single most important event in the modern history of ethnomycology: the rediscovery of the living mushroom cult of Mesoamerica. His work, spanning three decades, brought indigenous psilocybin mushroom use to worldwide attention and catalyzed the entire field of psychedelic ethnobotany.

## The Mushroom in Pre-Columbian Mesoamerica

Wasson documented the continuity of a mushroom-based religious practice in the Mazatec region of Oaxaca, Mexico, where "flesh of the gods" had been used since at least the pre-Columbian era. This practice, driven underground by Spanish colonial suppression, survived as a closely guarded tradition among a small number of curanderos (healers) and their communities.

The Mesoamerican mushroom tradition is attested in:

- **The "mushroom stones"** of highland Guatemala: Small carved stone figures dating from approximately 1000 BCE to 900 CE, found at archaeological sites throughout the Guatemalan highlands. These stones typically depict a human figure (or sometimes a jaguar or bird) beneath or emerging from an umbrella-shaped mushroom cap. Wasson and his colleagues argued these represent ritual mushroom use.

- **Codex and manuscript evidence**: Post-conquest documents, including the Florentine Codex compiled by Bernardino de Sahagun, contain descriptions and illustrations of psychoactive mushroom use.

- **Continuing practice**: The Mazatec mushroom ceremonies Wasson participated in, led by curanderas such as Maria Sabina, represent the living continuation of a tradition that had survived nearly 500 years of colonial suppression.

## Maria Sabina and the 1955 Velada

Wasson's first mushroom ceremony took place on June 29-30, 1955, in Huautla de Jimenez, Oaxaca, guided by the Mazatec curandera Maria Sabina. This event, later published in Wasson's essay "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" in Life magazine (1957), introduced the existence of a living psilocybin mushroom religion to an international audience.

The Life article inadvertently triggered a flood of countercultural visitors to Huautla, disrupting the traditional practice and making Maria Sabina a figure of global celebrity. She later expressed regret about the publicity, noting that the mushrooms had lost their power because "the foreigners took the mushroom from us."

## Wasson and Soma

Wasson's most ambitious scholarly project was his identification of the Vedic Soma as Amanita muscaria (fly agaric), published in Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality (1968). Drawing on extensive linguistic, botanical, and textual analysis of the Rig Veda, Wasson argued that Soma was not a metaphorical but a literal botanical substance: the fly agaric mushroom consumed by Indo-European priests in ancient rituals.

### Wasson's Doubts About Amanita

However, McKenna documents that Wasson himself was not fully confident in the Amanita identification. In a 1977 letter to McKenna, Wasson wrote:

"Your question about Str[opharia] cubensis has also bothered me. When Roger Heim and I went to India in 1967, in the Simlipal Hills of Orissa, I was given an account of a mushroom growing in cow's dung that tallied perfectly with Str. cubensis even to its psychoactive powers... It will be necessary to pursue Str. cubensis further not only in India but elsewhere in the world. Of course Str. cubensis must flourish in India. Did it play a part in the abandonment of Soma?"

Wasson further noted: "Inebriation from Str. cubensis and the other psilocybin species is clearly, in my opinion, superior to Amanita muscaria." Eventually, however, Wasson publicly contradicted this private view and maintained his published position.

## Wasson and Eleusis

Wasson's final major project was his contribution to The Road to Eleusis (1978), co-authored with [[albert-hofmann]] and Carl Ruck. In this work, Wasson proposed that the Eleusinian Mysteries of ancient Greece involved the consumption of a kykeon (sacred beverage) containing ergot alkaloids derived from [[claviceps-purpurea]] infecting barley. This ergotized beer theory was the first academically credible proposal that a Western classical institution was based on psychedelic intoxication.

## Legacy

Wasson's contributions fundamentally altered several fields simultaneously:

- **Ethnomycology**: Created as a discipline largely through Wasson's work
- **Psychedelic research**: His identification of Psilocybe mexicana led directly to Albert Hofmann's isolation and synthesis of psilocybin in 1958
- **Comparative religion**: Demonstrated that psychoactive plant use was a universal feature of prehistoric and indigenous religion
- **Archaeology**: His identification of the mushroom stones as evidence of a pre-Columbian mushroom cult was initially controversial but has gained substantial acceptance

Robert Graves, who had anticipated Wasson's insights about mushrooms in antiquity, suggested to Wasson that he visit Mazatecan Mexico to gather evidence supporting their shared theories. The resulting collaboration between Wasson (banker-mycologist), Hofmann (chemist), and Ruck (classicist) represents one of the most productive interdisciplinary teams in the study of psychoactive plants.

# Teonanacatl

Teonanacatl (Nahuatl: teotl "god" + nanacatl "mushroom" = "flesh of the gods" or "god's flesh") is the Aztec/Mesoamerican term for psychoactive mushrooms, documented by Spanish chroniclers during the colonial period. The continued use of teonanacatl by indigenous peoples of Oaxaca, Mexico, was the living tradition that R. Gordon Wasson brought to worldwide attention in the 1950s.

## Colonial Documentation

The Spanish conquistadors and missionaries documented the use of psychoactive mushrooms extensively but suppressed the practice as "diabolical." The most important colonial sources include:

- **Bernardino de Sahagun's Florentine Codex** (c. 1560): Contains detailed descriptions of the mushrooms, their effects, and the rituals surrounding their use. Sahagun's native informants described the mushrooms as producing visions, causing those who ate them to see visions, sometimes laugh, sometimes weep, and sometimes become intoxicated as if by wine.

- **The Inquisition records**: Legal proceedings against indigenous mushroom users provide circumstantial evidence of continuing practice despite official suppression.

The Spanish attempted to completely eradicate the mushroom cult but succeeded only in driving it underground. The practice survived in remote highland communities, particularly among the Mazatec, Chinantec, and Mixe peoples of Oaxaca.
