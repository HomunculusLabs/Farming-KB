# Fungi and Climate Change

How fungi influence and respond to climate change: carbon storage, greenhouse gas emissions, feedback loops, methane cycling, and climate-driven shifts in fungal communities. Based on Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, *Fungi in the Environment* (2006). See also [[fungal-nutrient-cycling-mineral-transformations]], [[fungi-soil-food-webs]], [[fungal-biodiversity-biogeography]], and [[mycorrhizal-types]].

## Overview

Fungi are critical but underappreciated players in the global climate system. They mediate major carbon fluxes between the atmosphere and terrestrial ecosystems, influence greenhouse gas emissions, and modify the capacity of soils to store carbon. As climate changes alter temperature, precipitation, and atmospheric CO2 concentrations, fungal communities and their ecosystem functions will shift -- with potentially significant feedback effects on climate itself.

## Carbon Storage by Fungi

### Soil Organic Carbon

Fungi are among the most important agents of soil carbon storage:

- **Global significance**: Soil contains approximately 2,500 Gt of organic carbon -- more than three times the carbon in the atmosphere (~870 Gt) and four times the carbon in all living organisms. Fungal processes are central to determining how much of this carbon remains stored vs released to the atmosphere.
- **Fungal-derived carbon**: Fungal necromass (dead hyphae, spores, fruiting bodies) contributes disproportionately to stable soil organic matter. Fungal cell wall components (chitin, melanin, glomalin) are highly resistant to decomposition and persist in soil for decades to centuries.
- **Carbon use efficiency**: Fungi incorporate 40-60% of consumed carbon into biomass (vs 20-40% for bacteria), meaning a larger proportion of decomposed carbon is retained in soil rather than respired as CO2.
- **Aggregate stabilization**: Fungal hyphae and glomalin bind soil particles into aggregates that physically protect organic matter from decomposition, further enhancing carbon storage. See [[glomalin]].

### Glomalin and Stable Carbon

- **Glomalin**: Produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, glomalin is a glycoprotein that coats hyphae and soil particles. It persists in soil for 7-42+ years and contributes 1-5% of total soil organic carbon.
- **Total glomalin stocks**: Global estimates of glomalin-related soil protein (GRSP) suggest it represents a significant pool of stable carbon, potentially hundreds of gigatonnes worldwide.
- **Climate significance**: Glomalin is highly resistant to microbial degradation and contributes to long-term carbon sequestration. Management practices that promote AM fungi (no-till, perennial crops, reduced phosphorus fertilization) increase glomalin stocks.

## Fungal Respiration and CO2 Release

### Decomposition-Driven CO2 Flux

- Fungal respiration accounts for a substantial fraction of total soil CO2 efflux (soil respiration). In forest soils, fungal respiration may represent 30-50% of total soil respiration.
- **Temperature sensitivity**: Fungal decomposition rates increase with temperature, following Q10 values (rate increase per 10C) of approximately 2-3. This means that warming accelerates fungal decomposition and CO2 release.
- **Climate warming feedback**: If warming accelerates fungal decomposition of soil organic matter, the released CO2 could amplify warming -- a positive feedback loop. However, the magnitude of this feedback depends on substrate quality, moisture, and fungal community composition.

### The Warming-Decomposition Paradox

There is active debate about whether warming will cause a net release of stored soil carbon:

- **Positive feedback hypothesis**: Warming accelerates decomposition of existing soil organic matter, releasing CO2 and amplifying warming. This is particularly concerning for permafrost soils and peatlands, which store enormous carbon stocks.
- **Acclimation hypothesis**: Fungal communities may acclimate to warming, reducing their temperature sensitivity over time. Long-term warming experiments often show initial increases in respiration followed by acclimation.
- **Substrate depletion**: Warming may preferentially decompose labile carbon pools, leaving behind increasingly recalcitrant material that decomposes slowly regardless of temperature.
- **Moisture constraints**: Warming without increased precipitation can reduce moisture, limiting fungal activity. In dry ecosystems, moisture may be a stronger constraint than temperature.

## Greenhouse Gas Emissions

### Nitrous Oxide (N2O)

Fungi contribute to N2O emissions, though bacteria (denitrifiers) are the dominant source:

- *Fusarium oxysporum* and related species can reduce nitrate to N2O under anaerobic conditions
- Fungal denitrification occurs in waterlogged soils, compost, and the rhizosphere
- Fungal N2O production is estimated to contribute approximately 0.1-1% of total global N2O emissions -- small but potentially significant in specific environments
- The fungal N2O pathway uses different intermediates than bacterial denitrification (nitrite is reduced directly to N2O without NO as an intermediate)

### Methane (CH4)

Fungi play complex roles in methane cycling:

- **Methane oxidation**: Some fungi can oxidize methane, though methanotrophic bacteria are the dominant biological methane oxidizers. Certain wood-decay fungi (e.g., some *Penicillium* species) show methanotrophic activity.
- **Indirect effects on methane**: Fungal activity in wetlands influences oxygen availability, which in turn affects methane production by methanogenic archaea. Fungi that aerate wetland soils through hyphal growth can reduce methane production by creating aerobic microsites.
- **Methane emissions from fungi**: Some studies suggest that certain fungi produce small amounts of methane, particularly under aerobic conditions. The "aerobic methane mystery" -- methane production in well-oxygenated environments -- may partly involve fungal activity.

## Climate Change Effects on Fungal Communities

### Temperature Effects

- **Warming and species composition**: Warming favors thermotolerant species and may shift community composition toward species with higher temperature optima. This could alter decomposition rates and nutrient cycling patterns.
- **Range shifts**: Fungal distributions are shifting toward higher latitudes and elevations in response to warming. Ectomycorrhizal fungi with narrow temperature tolerances may be particularly vulnerable.
- **Fruiting patterns**: Long-term monitoring in Europe shows that fungal fruiting seasons have extended by several days per decade, with autumn fruiting starting later and spring fruiting starting earlier. Some species now fruit in winter that previously did not.

### Precipitation Changes

- **Drought stress**: Reduced precipitation limits fungal activity directly and indirectly by reducing plant photosynthate supply to mycorrhizal fungi. Drought can cause rapid loss of extraradical mycelium.
- **Increased rainfall**: In some regions, increased precipitation enhances fungal activity and decomposition. However, waterlogged conditions can shift communities from aerobic to anaerobic species.
- **Extreme events**: Drought followed by intense rainfall can cause pulses of decomposition as rehydrated fungal communities rapidly metabolize accumulated substrates.

### Elevated CO2

- **Plant-mediated effects**: Elevated CO2 generally increases plant photosynthesis, which increases belowground carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi. This can enhance fungal biomass and activity in the rhizosphere.
- **Nutrient feedback**: Increased mycorrhizal activity under elevated CO2 may enhance phosphorus and nitrogen acquisition, partially alleviating nutrient limitation and sustaining the CO2 fertilization effect.
- **Quality vs quantity**: While elevated CO2 increases the quantity of plant-derived carbon entering soil, it may reduce litter quality (higher C:N ratio), potentially slowing decomposition despite greater carbon inputs.

## Ecosystem-Scale Feedbacks

### Forest Ecosystems

The shift between ECM- and AM-dominated forests has climate implications:

- **ECM forests**: Slower decomposition, more carbon storage, cooler soil temperatures, greater nitrogen retention
- **AM forests**: Faster decomposition, less carbon storage, warmer soil temperatures, more rapid nitrogen cycling
- **Climate-driven shifts**: Warming and increased nitrogen deposition favor AM trees over ECM trees in many temperate regions, potentially accelerating carbon loss from forest soils
- **The " Gadgil effect "**: ECM fungi suppress bacterial decomposition in forests, creating a positive feedback for carbon storage. If ECM dominance declines, this suppression weakens.

### Permafrost and Polar Regions

- **Permafrost thaw**: As permafrost thaws, previously frozen organic matter becomes available for decomposition. Fungal communities in thawing permafrost are initially dominated by opportunistic species that rapidly metabolize labile carbon.
- **Polar warming**: Warming in polar regions is occurring 2-3x faster than the global average. This is causing rapid shifts in fungal community composition, with potential consequences for decomposition rates and nutrient cycling in these carbon-sensitive ecosystems.

### Peatlands

- **Sphagnum-fungal relationships**: Peatlands store approximately 30% of global soil carbon. Fungi associated with Sphagnum moss (including ericoid mycorrhizal fungi) play key roles in peat formation and decomposition.
- **Drainage and warming**: When peatlands are drained or warmed, fungal communities shift from slow-growing, acid-tolerant species to faster-growing decomposers that accelerate peat decomposition and CO2 release.

## Fungi as Climate Mitigation Tools

### Soil Carbon Sequestration

Management practices that enhance fungal activity can increase soil carbon storage:

- **No-till agriculture**: Preserves fungal hyphal networks and glomalin, increasing stable carbon pools. See [[no-till-farming]].
- **Perennial crops and cover crops**: Maintain continuous fungal communities and carbon inputs. See [[cover-cropping]].
- **Reduced nitrogen fertilization**: Excess nitrogen favors bacterial communities over fungal. Moderate nitrogen use promotes AM fungi and fungal-dominated decomposition.
- **Organic amendments**: Compost and organic matter additions feed fungal communities and promote humus formation.

### Mycoremediation of Greenhouse Gases

- **Biochar-fungal interactions**: Biochar ([[biochar]]) can be colonized by fungi, enhancing its stability and carbon storage potential. Mycorrhizal fungi may improve plant growth on biochar-amended soils.
- **Methanotrophy**: Enhancing fungal methane oxidation in landfills, rice paddies, and wetlands could reduce methane emissions.

## Knowledge Gaps

Several critical questions remain incompletely answered:

1. How will specific fungal functional groups respond to interacting climate drivers (warming, altered precipitation, elevated CO2, nitrogen deposition)?
2. What is the contribution of fungal necromass to long-term soil carbon pools at the global scale?
3. How does the mycorrhizal type (AM vs ECM) of dominant vegetation influence ecosystem carbon balance under climate change?
4. Can fungal methane oxidation be enhanced for climate mitigation?
5. How rapidly can fungal communities adapt to changing climate conditions?
