The stoichiometry of fungal biomass (C:N:P) varies among species and functional groups, with typical ratios of approximately 100:10:1 for carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus respectively. Saprotrophic basidiomycetes tend to have wider C:N ratios than ascomycetes, reflecting their adaptation to decomposing nitrogen-poor substrates like wood. Mycorrhizal fungi, which receive carbon directly from host plants, often have even lower C:N ratios in their biomass. Understanding these stoichiometric relationships is important for predicting how fungal communities will respond to changes in substrate quality and nutrient availability.

## Phosphorus Use Efficiency

Fungal phosphorus use efficiency is particularly important in phosphorus-limited ecosystems such as tropical soils and many forest types. Fungi can access phosphorus from organic sources through the production of phosphatase enzymes, and from mineral sources through organic acid exudation and solubilization. Mycorrhizal fungi are especially important for plant phosphorus nutrition, as their extensive mycelial networks increase the effective volume of soil explored for phosphorus by orders of magnitude compared to roots alone.

The concept of phosphorus use efficiency connects to broader discussions of [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] health in agricultural systems. In natural ecosystems, efficient phosphorus cycling by fungi helps maintain productivity in low-phosphorus environments. In agricultural systems, practices that support fungal communities—such as reduced tillage, organic amendments, and [[fungal-metagenomics]] is an active area of research. Communities dominated by species with high CUE may be more efficient at converting organic matter into biomass, but may also be more sensitive to environmental fluctuations. Understanding these trade-offs is essential for predicting how fungal-mediated decomposition will respond to global change.

## Fungal Efficiency in Different Ecological Niches

Fungal resource use efficiency varies significantly across ecological niches, reflecting adaptation to different substrate types and environmental conditions. Wood-decay fungi growing on nitrogen-poor substrates like heartwood have evolved metabolic strategies to minimize nitrogen losses, including efficient nitrogen recycling through autophagy and the production of nitrogen-fixing bacterial endosymbionts in some tropical species. Leaf litter decomposers face substrates with higher nitrogen content but greater variability in quality, requiring flexible metabolic strategies. Ectomycorrhizal fungi, which receive carbon from host plants, allocate resources differently than free-living saprotrophs, investing more heavily in foraging structures (extramatrical mycelium) and less in extracellular enzyme production. Endophytic fungi, living within plant tissues, operate under unique constraints where resource efficiency must be balanced with the need to avoid triggering host defenses.

## Efficiency Trade-offs and the Growth Rate-Yield Hypothesis

The growth rate-yield hypothesis predicts a fundamental trade-off in microbial physiology: organisms that grow rapidly tend to have lower biomass yield per unit of substrate consumed, while organisms that grow slowly tend to be more efficient. This trade-off has been observed in fungal communities across environmental gradients. Fast-growing fungal species that colonize fresh organic matter tend to have lower CUE, investing more carbon in growth rate and enzyme production at the expense of biomass accumulation. Slower-growing species that dominate later in decomposition have higher CUE, converting a larger proportion of available substrate into persistent fungal biomass. This trade-off has implications for carbon storage: ecosystems dominated by slow-growing, high-efficiency fungi tend to store more carbon in soil, while systems dominated by fast-growing, low-efficiency fungi release more carbon as CO2.

## Climate Change Implications

Climate change is expected to alter fungal resource use efficiency through multiple pathways. Rising temperatures directly reduce CUE by increasing respiration rates relative to growth rates, a relationship that has been demonstrated experimentally for a wide range of fungal species. Altered precipitation patterns affect moisture availability, which influences both substrate access and enzyme diffusion. Increased nitrogen deposition may shift fungal community composition toward species with lower CUE, as nitrogen-loving opportunistic fungi replace the slow-growing, resource-conserving species that dominate in nitrogen-limited ecosystems. The net effect of these changes on ecosystem carbon balance remains uncertain but is likely to accelerate carbon loss from soils in many ecosystems, particularly boreal and temperate forests where fungal decomposition plays a dominant role in carbon cycling.

## Measuring Carbon Use Efficiency

Quantifying fungal carbon use efficiency requires measuring both carbon uptake and carbon allocation to different metabolic fates. Respirometry — measuring CO2 production by fungal cultures or soil samples — provides the carbon lost through respiration. Biomass measurements (through extraction and weighing of fungal tissue, or through proxy methods like ergosterol quantification or DNA-based biomass estimates) provide the carbon retained in fungal tissue. CUE is calculated as biomass carbon produced divided by total carbon consumed (biomass + respiration). Each of these measurements introduces potential errors: respirometry captures CO2 from both fungal and non-fungal sources in mixed communities, biomass extraction is incomplete and may damage fragile structures, and proxy methods assume constant conversion factors that may not hold across species or environmental conditions. Despite these challenges, methodological improvements in high-throughput respirometry and molecular biomass estimation are steadily improving the accuracy and precision of CUE measurements.

## Fungal Strategies for Phosphorus Acquisition

Fungi have evolved diverse strategies for acquiring phosphorus, a nutrient that is often limiting in terrestrial ecosystems. Ectomycorrhizal fungi produce extracellular phosphatase enzymes that cleave phosphate groups from organic molecules, making phosphorus available to both the fungus and its host plant. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi access inorganic phosphate from soil solution through extensive hyphal networks that explore soil volumes far beyond the root zone. Saprotrophic fungi solubilize mineral phosphate through the production of organic acids (particularly oxalic and citric acid) that chelate calcium, iron, and aluminum cations that bind phosphate in soil minerals. Some fungi produce phosphatases that remain active after secretion and can accumulate in soil, creating "enzyme pools" that continue to release phosphate even after fungal biomass has declined. The efficiency of these phosphorus acquisition strategies varies with soil chemistry, plant community composition, and fungal species identity.

## Nitrogen Use Efficiency and Stoichiometric Regulation

Fungal nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) determines how effectively fungi convert acquired nitrogen into biomass versus excreting excess nitrogen as ammonium or other waste products. Fungi maintain relatively constant tissue carbon-to-nitrogen ratios (typically 10:1 to 15:1) through stoichiometric regulation, meaning they must balance carbon and nitrogen acquisition. When decomposing nitrogen-poor substrates like wood (C:N ratios of 400:1 or higher), fungi invest in nitrogen-scavenging enzymes and may immobilize nitrogen from the surrounding environment, competing with plants for available nitrogen. When decomposing nitrogen-rich substrates, fungi mineralize excess nitrogen, releasing ammonium that becomes available for plant uptake. This nitrogen mineralization-immobilization switch makes fungi critical regulators of plant-available nitrogen in most terrestrial ecosystems. The metabolic cost of maintaining nitrogen homeostasis — including amino acid synthesis machinery, transaminases, and nitrogen transport proteins — is a significant component of fungal maintenance metabolism that reduces overall carbon use efficiency.

## Wood Decay Strategies and Resource Partitioning

The three major wood decay strategies employed by fungi — white rot, brown rot, and soft rot — differ fundamentally in their resource use efficiency. White rot fungi (e.g., *Phanerochaete chrysosporium*) produce lignin-degrading peroxidases and can fully decompose both lignin and cellulose, achieving the most complete substrate utilization but at high enzymatic cost. Brown rot fungi (e.g., *Serpula lacrymans*) use a non-enzymatic Fenton chemistry system to depolymerize cellulose while largely leaving lignin behind, achieving faster mass loss with lower enzymatic investment but leaving a chemically altered residue. Soft rot fungi (e.g., *Chaetomium globosum*) tunnel through wood cell walls using cellulases and hemicellulases, processing material slowly but maintaining activity under conditions of high moisture and low oxygen that inhibit other decay types. These different strategies represent distinct trade-offs between decomposition completeness, enzymatic investment, and environmental tolerance that determine which fungal taxa dominate in different decomposition niches.

## Fungal Resource Economics and Community Assembly

The concept of resource economics provides a powerful framework for understanding fungal community assembly and succession during decomposition. R-strategist fungi (copiotrophic species) invest heavily in rapid growth and enzyme production, dominating early decomposition when labile resources are abundant. K-strategist fungi (oligotrophic species) grow slowly but maintain metabolic activity under resource limitation, dominating later stages of decomposition when only recalcitrant materials remain. Intermediate strategists occupy the transition between these extremes. This succession pattern has been documented across diverse substrates and ecosystems, from leaf litter decomposition in forests to composting systems. The resource economic traits of fungal species — their growth rates, enzyme production patterns, and stoichiometric flexibility — are increasingly used to predict how fungal communities will respond to environmental changes such as warming, nitrogen deposition, and altered precipitation regimes, providing a mechanistic basis for forecasting ecosystem-level consequences of fungal community shifts.

## Temperature-Resource Interaction Effects

The interaction between temperature and resource quality creates complex effects on fungal resource use efficiency that are critical for understanding ecosystem responses to climate change. At moderate temperatures, fungi exhibit the highest carbon use efficiency because enzyme systems function optimally and maintenance energy costs are lowest. At low temperatures, reduced enzyme kinetics slow both growth and respiration, but respiration typically declines more than growth, leading to increased carbon use efficiency — a pattern that helps explain the accumulation of organic matter in cold ecosystems such as tundra and boreal forests. At high temperatures, maintenance energy costs increase exponentially while enzyme denaturation reduces catalytic efficiency, leading to decreased carbon use efficiency. The quality of the substrate interacts with temperature: nutrient-rich substrates support higher CUE at all temperatures, while nutrient-poor substrates compound the negative effects of high temperature on efficiency. These temperature-resource interactions determine how decomposition rates and carbon storage respond to warming, with important implications for climate feedbacks.

## Ecological Significance

Fungal resource use efficiency is a master variable that controls the fate of carbon and nutrients in decomposing organic matter. The efficiency with which fungi convert assimilated substrates into biomass versus respiring it as CO2 determines whether decomposition pathways lead to carbon sequestration or carbon release. In ecosystems where fungal decomposition dominates, such as forest soils and peatlands, even modest shifts in fungal resource use efficiency can translate into significant changes in ecosystem carbon balance over time.

The stoichiometric flexibility of fungi—their ability to maintain relatively constant tissue nutrient ratios despite variable substrate quality—creates predictable patterns of nutrient immobilization and mineralization during decomposition. When fungi decompose nutrient-poor substrates, they immobilize nutrients from the surrounding environment, temporarily reducing nutrient availability for plants. This immobilization capacity makes fungi important regulators of plant-available nutrients and links fungal resource use efficiency directly to plant productivity and community composition.

## Research Methods

Resource use efficiency is quantified through a combination of respiration measurements, biomass accumulation assays, and stable isotope tracing. The 13C labeling approach allows direct partitioning of assimilated carbon between biomass and respiration. Calorespirometry simultaneously measures metabolic heat production and CO2 evolution, providing an integrated measure of metabolic efficiency. Metabolic flux analysis using 13C-labeled substrates and metabolomic profiling reveals the intracellular pathways through which carbon is allocated to growth versus maintenance processes.
