# World as Tessellation of Events

## Overview

The permaculture pattern model culminates in the recognition that the world we inhabit is not a collection of separate objects and phenomena but a **tessellation of interrelated events**—overlapping, interpenetrating patterns that form a single, self-regulating totality. This understanding, drawn from both indigenous wisdom and modern physics, provides the philosophical foundation for permaculture's ethic of earth care and its design methodology based on working with natural patterns rather than imposing artificial ones.

## The Unity of Natural Patterns

### Observing the Patterned World

A single landscape contains a vast array of patterned phenomena operating simultaneously:

- **Geological patterns**: Ancient volcanic craters, calderas eroded by the sea, hills stepped by successive sea-level changes recording long-term cycles
- **Biological patterns**: Trees rising from soils, birds nesting, new life forms arising from seeds and eggs
- **Atmospheric patterns**: Wind spirals sweeping in from the west with weekly regularity, bearing fractal forms of turbulent clouds, producing lightning and thunder in seasonal pulses
- **Temporal patterns**: Day follows night, life follows death follows life in endless cycles

All of these phenomena represent a **unity of patterns long repeated and based on one master pattern**, each preparing for new evolutions and dissolutions. It is the number and complexity of such cycles that create life opportunities, and life itself is the only integrative force in this part of the universe.

## Pattern as Design Framework

### The Role of Pattern in Permaculture Design

Understanding the underlying patterns that link all phenomena creates a powerful abstract tool for designers. At any point in the design process, appropriate patterning can:

- **Assist the achievement of sustainable yield** from flows, growth forms, or information flux
- **Result in energy savings** when imposed on domestic or village assemblies
- **Produce satisfactory aesthetics and function** while sustaining organisms inhabiting the designed habitat
- **Permit elements to flow and function** in beneficial relationships

Patterning is the way designers frame their designs—the template into which information, entities, and objects are fitted from observation, map overlays, analytic divination of connections, and the selection of specific materials and technologies. As Mollison states: *"The pattern is design, and design is the subject of permaculture."*

## Philosophical Foundations

### David Bohm and Implicate Order

The physicist David Bohm (1980) urged going beyond regarding ourselves as merely interactive with each other and the environment, toward seeing all things as **"projections of a single totality."** The word "implicate" in Bohm's work comes from the Latin *enfolded*—when we separate individuals, effects, or disciplines from this enfolded order, we must recognize that we have only part of the unknowable totality, not the truth itself.

Key implications:
- There are **no true opposites**, only phases of the same phenomenon
- As we experience this totality, incorporate new information, and develop consciousness, we are **fundamentally changed**
- *"To fail to take this into account must inevitably lead one to a serious and sustained confusion in all that one does."*

### Genealogy as Pattern

In the matter of genealogy, pattern study enables consciousness of ourselves in the time and pattern stream. As origins, we "determine"—or rather define and are defined by—our ancestry as much as we define and are defined by our descent. The sense of "all are present here" is revealed by pattern: being encapsulated in, and a pervading part of, a personal genealogical pattern which is itself a result of innumerable variables.

### The Kalahari Perspective

A Kalahari bushman would express this understanding as: *"We are the dreams of a dreamer."* This captures the insight that substance itself may only be process—that we can never fully define substance, only know a few local patterns and thus have some weak predictive capacity. It is the patterns our local patterns cannot know that will surprise us: the strike of cosmic lightning from an unguessed source or stress.

## Convergence of Science and Indigenous Wisdom

### Lovelock's Gaia Hypothesis

James Lovelock (1979) expressed the combination of scientific insights and older tribal beliefs asserting the **interdependence of animate and inanimate events**. The universe and the earth behave as self-regulating and self-generated constructs, very much akin to a single organism or a thought process. The conditions that make life possible are balanced about such fine tolerances that it seems close to certain that many processes exist specifically to preserve this equilibrium in its dynamic stability.

### Birch and Cobb's Process Philosophy

Biologists Birch and Cobb, in *The Liberation of Life* (1984), deny the validity of the existence of individual organisms or separate events. All exist in a field of events or as an expression of one life force. Organisms exist only as inseparable parts of their event environments, in continual process of exchange with the animate and inanimate entities that surround them.

As reviewed by Warwick Fox in *The Ecologist*: we must view the cosmos as "an infinite complex of interrelated events"; all things "are in actuality enduring societies of events." We are acted upon and acting, created and creating, shaped and shaping.

### Capra and the Physics of Connection

Theoretical physicist Fritjof Capra (1976) contributed to these unifying worldviews, all of which stand in conflict with the current ethics governing political, educational, and economic systems. Yet all contribute to an increasing effort to unify and cooperate in a **common ethic of earth-care**, without which human existence has no meaning in the universe.

## Implications for Permaculture Design

### From Separation to Integration

The recognition of the world as a tessellation of events demands a fundamental shift in design approach:

1. **Stop designing isolated components**: Every element exists in a field of relationships; designing one element without considering its connections leads to systemic dysfunction
2. **Work with existing patterns**: Rather than imposing rectilinear grids or artificial geometries, identify the natural patterns already present and design to enhance them
3. **Think in terms of process, not objects**: Soil is not a static substance but an ongoing process of decomposition, mineral cycling, and biological activity; water is not a resource to be piped but a flow to be guided
4. **Design for reciprocal exchange**: Every element should both give and receive; one-way flows deplete systems
5. **Respect the unknowable**: Local patterns cannot predict global disruptions; build resilience through diversity and redundancy

### The Ethical Imperative

Pattern understanding contributes to the continuing evolution of new worldviews based on the essential oneness of all phenomena. This is not merely philosophical abstraction but has direct practical consequences:

- **Earth-care** becomes not a sentimental preference but a rational recognition of our inseparability from the systems that sustain us
- **People-care** follows from understanding that all people are expressions of the same totality
- **Fair share** (return of surplus) is the recognition that nothing is truly separate and all surplus belongs to the system as a whole

The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.

Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
