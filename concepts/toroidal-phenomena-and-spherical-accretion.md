## Toroidal Phenomena and Spherical Accretion

Toroidal (doughnut-shaped) phenomena and spherical accretion-expulsion models represent two of the most fundamental [[permaculture]] designers with a deep understanding of how energy and matter organize themselves in three-dimensional space.

## Toroidal Phenomena

### Definition and Ubiquity

A **torus** (plural: tori) is a doughnut-shaped surface of revolution generated by rotating a circle about an axis coplanar with but not intersecting the circle. In nature, tori arise as closed three-dimensional vortices and are implicit in the core model of permaculture pattern understanding.

Tori are widespread natural phenomena visible in:
- **Atomic explosions**: Photographic stills reveal violently rotating tori around and crowning the ascending column of smoke and debris
- **Atmospheric thermals**: Violent up-draughts caused by local heat create tori much appreciated by soaring birds and glider pilots, who ride the inner (ascending) circle of doughnuts of hot air generated over deserts on hot afternoons
- **Biological systems**: A slow-cycling torus of nutrients surrounds the stem of any tree — crown-drip carries nutrients to the ground, and roots return them via the stem
- **Smoke rings**: Deliberately blown smoke rings are simple, visible examples of toroidal flow

### The Torus as a Closed Vortex

A torus is essentially a **closed three-dimensional vortex**. It can be generated by:
- Discontinuous or explosive flow
- Pulses in flow
- Continuous rotational processes around an axis

### Special Forms

Several special toroidal forms are significant:

**Robinson Congruence**: A complex toroidal form portraying the space-time form of a mass-less particle such as a proton, representing annidated (nested) tori. This connects toroidal geometry to fundamental physics.

**Möbius Strip**: A one-sided twisted toroid (often portrayed by M.C. Escher in his art) that enables crossing an edge without lifting a pencil — a topological curiosity with profound implications for understanding continuous surfaces in nature.

**O-rings**: We rely on a torus of rubber to inflate tyres and seal circular hatches, demonstrating the practical engineering applications of toroidal geometry.

**Biological tori**: Many life forms produce tori — some sea snail egg masses are toroidal in structure.

### The Torus as a Special Case

A torus can be understood as a special or truncated case of the [[mycorrhiza]]**: Tree-like forms where kinetic forces do not act strongly; the generator is **life or growth itself**
- **Crystals and ice**: Slow growth into cavities; the generator is **molecular forces**
- **Lobulations and lattices**: Freeze-thaw or swell-shrink patterns in flat environments; the generator is **physical/chemical cycling**

### Lobulation

Lobulation — the production of rounded, bulging shapes — differs in origin from kinetic-energy flow systems:
- Occurs when kinetic forces do not act strongly
- Seen in flat, sheltered environments as freeze-thaw or swell-shrink patterns
- Examples include ice floes on quiet ponds, hexagonal stone patterns on tundra, and the slow growth of crystals
- These forms are fractals generated at the molecular level
- Can be thought of as a response of nature or life to a world that threatens "no difference"

### Kinetic vs. Chemical Processes

If hills wear down, the antepenultimate (third from last) surfaces produce lateral, two-dimensional life patterns, as lichen on a rock. Kinetic erosion processes are exchanged for physical and chemical processes at the molecular level, but even this creates sufficient difference in media for life forms to express themselves and for differences to arise in surface patterns.

## Design Implications

### Understanding Cycles

The toroidal nutrient cycle around a tree (crown-drip to roots) provides a model for designing closed-loop nutrient systems in agriculture and settlement design. Every element in a permaculture system should participate in such cycles.

### Scale Awareness

The same patterns recur from molecular to planetary scales. Designers who understand this can apply insights from one scale to another — the way a tree cycles nutrients mirrors how a planet manages its atmosphere and hydrosphere.

### Working with Pulsers

Weak pulsing forces (like the sun's 11-year cycle or seasonal variations) can be harnessed rather than resisted. Understanding the rhythm of natural pulsers allows designers to time interventions for maximum effect.

### Fractal Applications

When kinetic forces do not dominate, molecular and chemical processes create fractal patterns. Designers can work with these tendencies rather than against them, allowing self-organization to create efficient structures.
