# Fukuoka's Argument Against Pruning — Natural Tree Form

**Updated:** 2026-04-18

One of Masanobu Fukuoka's most detailed and technical arguments concerns the pruning of fruit trees. Based on 30+ years of observation in his citrus orchard, Fukuoka concluded that pruning is not only unnecessary but actively harmful -- that it destroys the natural form of trees and creates a self-perpetuating cycle of intervention. His argument is grounded in careful observation of phyllotaxy (the arrangement of leaves and branches on plants) and the growth patterns of natural versus cultivated trees.

## The Problem: Pruning Creates the Need for More Pruning

Fukuoka's central insight: "Once the pruning shears have been taken to the tree and branches emerge in complicated shapes, the tree can no longer be left alone. Unless it is carefully tended each year and each branch meticulously trained and pruned, the branches entangle, causing some to weaken and die."

### The Chain Reaction

1. The first pruning cut (even nipping one bud on a sapling) alters the tree's natural growth pattern
2. Wounds from pruning cause adventitious buds to emerge in abnormal positions
3. These superfluous branches crowd, bend, twist, and spread confusion as they grow
4. The resulting tangled form requires further corrective pruning
5. Each new cut creates more wounds and more abnormal growth
6. The cycle continues for the life of the tree

"Because even lightly pinching new buds on a pine seedling totally alters the shape of the emerging branches, the young tree can be trained into a garden pine or even a bonsai. But although the first pruning can make a bonsai of a pine, once a bonsai, the pine can never be restored to a full-size tree."

## Evidence from Natural Forests

Fukuoka points to trees in natural forests as proof that pruning is unnecessary:

- **Mountain pines:** The central trunk rises straight and true, putting out branches at equal vertical spacings in a radial arrangement. One can clearly trace the chronology of branch emergence. The spacing and angle of branches are regular and orderly. Never does one branch grow too long or cross another.
- **Cedars:** Trunks never branch or twist as long as they are not cut or harmed. Branches on right and left sides do not run up against each other or cross.
- **Bamboo, cryptomeria, Japanese cypress, camphor, camellia, Japanese maple:** All observe the phyllotaxy and divergence specific to that species.

"Not a single wasted leaf, not a single branch lacking -- that is the true form of a plant."

## Phyllotaxy: The Mathematical Order of Nature

Fukuoka provides detailed observations on phyllotaxy -- the fixed mathematical laws governing leaf and branch arrangement:

- In a given plant, leaves always grow either alternately or oppositely
- The direction and angle at which a leaf grows is always the same; "never is there even the slightest deviation"
- If the angle between one leaf on a fruit tree branch and the next is 72 degrees, then all subsequent leaves will emerge at 72 degrees
- The sixth leaf on peach, persimmon, mandarin orange, orange, and cherry tree branches is always located directly above the first leaf
- The eleventh leaf is always directly above the sixth
- When the distance between consecutive buds is one inch, the distance from one leaf to the next leaf directly above it is always five inches
- Two leaves will not overlap, or two branches emerge, within any five-inch length along the branch

### The Consequence

Because of phyllotaxy, "the branches and leaves of natural plants all receive equal ventilation and sunlight" without any human intervention. Every leaf and branch grows in an orderly and regular arrangement. No part of the plant is in disarray or confusion.

## Why Conventional Trees Appear to Need Pruning

Fukuoka identifies two critical reasons why conventional fruit trees seem to need pruning:

### 1. The Transplant Wound

When a fruit tree sapling is planted conventionally:
- The roots are trimmed during digging
- The stem is cut back to 1-2 feet
- This first pruning alone robs the tree of its natural form
- The sapling begins putting out buds and suckers in a complex, confused manner

"Like the transplanted mountain pine to the top of which the gardener takes his shears, once pruned, a tree cannot be left untended."

### 2. The Convenience of Operations

Pruning is done not for the tree's benefit but for the convenience of human operations:

- Tillage and weeding require clear space beneath trees (but if you eliminate tillage and weeding, this reason disappears)
- Pesticide spraying requires accessible tree surfaces (but if you eliminate pesticides, this reason disappears)
- Fertilizer application requires clear trunk access (but if you eliminate fertilizer, this reason disappears)
- Harvesting is the only remaining reason for tree shape considerations

"The ideal form of a tree is the form best suited to these various human operations and to harvesting. Thus it is not the natural form that gardeners and growers seek, but a shape artificially pruned and trained to the convenience and benefit of the grower."

## Specific Tree Form Observations

### Citrus Trees

Fukuoka's most detailed observations concern citrus:

- Scientists claim the natural form of a citrus tree is hemispherical with primary scaffold branches extending at 40-70 degrees
- "In truth no one [[natural-farming]]he true form of a citrus tree is that of a large, upright tree or a low bush"
- It is not known whether it grows like a cryptomeria with one tall central trunk, in the manner of a camellia, or round like paperbush

Fukuoka initially tried natural farming on an established Satsuma orange grove:
- Trees had been trimmed in wineglass shape, 6-7 feet tall
- When he discontinued pruning, large numbers of scaffold branches grew out at once
- Branches crisscrossed, doubled back, and grew in twisted shapes
- Disease sites developed where branches tangled
- One dying branch caused others to wither and die
- Fruit formation became irregular
- The tree produced a full crop only every other year

He then tried heavy pruning and thinning, leaving only several rising suckers:
- Four or five primary scaffold branches were still too many
- Growth at the center was poor
- Inner branches gradually withered
- Sharp drop in fruit production in the interior portion

### The Phyllotaxy of Citrus

Fukuoka's key observation about citrus phyllotaxy:
- If one draws a mental picture of the natural form and protects the tree from the local environment, it will thrive, producing good fruit year after year
- The natural form must be determined by observing the specific phyllotaxy of each species
- No published work had ever discussed pruning based on basic factors such as the phyllotaxy of citrus trees

## The Pruning Paradox

Fukuoka describes a profound paradox:

- The problems that pruning supposedly solves (branch crossing, poor ventilation, dense foliage, poor light penetration, dead lower branches, fruit only at surface) are in fact caused by pruning itself
- A tree that has never been pruned does not develop these problems
- The "natural form" that growers think they see in abandoned orchards is actually the confused form of trees that were initially pruned and then neglected -- this is NOT the true natural form
- "Just as no one is so foolish as to strike his left hand with his right, no persimmon or chestnut tree has branches on the right that compete with those on the left"

## Practical Recommendations

Based on his experience, Fukuoka offers these guidelines:

1. **Start from the beginning:** Trees must be allowed to grow in their natural form from the start (or as close as possible)
2. **Minimal corrective pruning:** If a tree must be managed, limit pruning to minimal corrections aimed at bringing it closer to its natural form
3. **Never nip the leader:** The central leader should never be cut; allow it to grow straight up
4. **Eliminate operations that require pruning:** By eliminating tillage, weeding, fertilization, and pesticide spraying, the reasons for pruning disappear
5. **Be patient:** Converting a conventionally pruned tree to natural form takes years and may involve temporary production losses

## The Deeper Principle

Fukuoka's argument against pruning embodies his broader philosophy:

- Human intervention creates problems that require more human intervention
- Nature already has perfect solutions that humans cannot improve upon
- The first step toward natural farming is to stop doing things that create the need for further action
- "Pruning only creates a need for more pruning, but if the grower realizes that trees not in need of pruning also exist in this world and is determined to grow such trees, they will bear fruit without pruning"
