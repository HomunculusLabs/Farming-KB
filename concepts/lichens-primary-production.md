## Lichens in Primary Production

Lichens are symbiotic associations between a fungus (mycobiont) and a photobiont
(algal or cyanobacterial partner) that play a surprisingly significant role in
primary production across many terrestrial ecosystems. Though often overlooked
compared to vascular plants, lichens contribute meaningfully to carbon fixation,
nitrogen input, and soil development — particularly in extreme or stressed
environments where higher plants are scarce.

## From Soil Formation to Primary Production

As discussed in the context of [soil formation](lichens-soil-formation), lichens
are pioneer colonizers of bare rock and mineral surfaces. Through physical and
chemical weathering, they break down substrates and contribute organic matter,
initiating soil development. This foundational role transitions naturally into
primary production: once established, lichens fix atmospheric carbon through
their photobionts, becoming net contributors to ecosystem energy budgets.

## Carbon Fixation by Lichens

Lichens are poikilohydric organisms — their water content equilibrates with the
environment, meaning they are metabolically active only when hydrated (typically
during dew, rain, or fog events). Despite this intermittent activity, they fix
significant quantities of carbon:

- In **pinyon-juniper woodlands** of the American Southwest, lichens fix
  approximately **28 kg C per hectare per year** (Beymer & Klopatek 1991).
- Of this fixed carbon, **34–36%** is incorporated directly into soil organic
  matter, representing a substantial input to belowground carbon pools.
- **Light use efficiency** ranges from **0.5–2% per unit dry weight**, strongly
  correlated with intercepted irradiance during hydrated periods.

## Nitrogen Dynamics and Limitation

### Nitrogen Limitation in Mat-Forming Lichens

Mat-forming lichens — extensive ground-covering species common in boreal and
arctic tundra — are frequently **limited by [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]]**. Crittenden
et al. (1994) demonstrated that these lichens exhibit **near 100% N interception
efficiency**, meaning they capture virtually all inorganic nitrogen deposited
onto their surfaces from atmospheric sources. This extraordinary efficiency
highlights both the strength of N limitation and the competitive advantage
lichens gain in oligotrophic habitats.

### Biological Nitrogen Fixation by Soil Crusts

Cyanobacterial lichens within [[biological-soil-indicators]] (cryptogamic covers) are
critical nitrogen inputs in arid and semi-arid ecosystems. Belnap (2002)
reported N fixation rates of **1–13 kg N/ha/yr** in Utah desert soil crusts — a
range that reflects variability in crust composition, moisture availability, and
temperature. These inputs are ecologically vital in nitrogen-poor desert soils,
supporting vascular plant productivity and microbial activity.

## Growth Rates Across Biomes

Lichen growth rates are inherently slow, varying dramatically by growth form,
substrate, and climate:

| Growth Form / Habitat | Rate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Terricolous (ground), Sweden | 0.2–0.4 g/g dry weight; 30–70 g/m² | Palmqvist & Sundberg 2000 |
| Epiphytic (bark), temperate | 0.01–0.02 g/g dry weight | Palmqvist & Sundberg 2000 |
| Arctic fruticose lichens | 2.4–10.6 mg/g per week; ~10% biomass increase per season | Cooper & Wookey 2001 |

The contrast between terricolous and epiphytic forms is striking: ground-dwelling
lichens grow roughly **10–20× faster** than their epiphytic counterparts,
reflecting differences in water availability, nutrient access, and microclimate
stability. Arctic fruticose lichens (e.g., *Cladonia*, *Cetraria* species)
achieve their ~10% seasonal biomass increase through a compressed growing season
of continuous summer light and periodic hydration.

## Epiphytic Lichen Ecology

Epiphytic lichens colonizing tree bark exhibit strong successional patterns tied
to forest structure. Sillett et al. (2000) studied [[douglas-fir]] forests and
found:

- **Highest lichen biomass and diversity** in **clear-cut areas** (due to
  increased light) and **old-growth stands** (due to structural complexity).
- **Lowest colonization** in **young, dense forests** where canopy closure
  severely limits light penetration.
- *[[lobaria-oregana]]*, a large cyanolichen, preferentially colonizes **rough bark
  in old-growth** stands, where moisture retention and stability favor its
  establishment.

These patterns underscore how forest management practices directly affect
non-vascular primary production and associated biodiversity.

## Environmental Limitations

### Tropical Environments

Lichens are surprisingly scarce in tropical lowland forests, and Lange et al.
(2000) illuminated one key reason: in the cyanolichen *Leptogium*, **47–88% of
photosynthetically fixed carbon is lost as respiratory expenditure**. High
temperatures and sustained metabolic costs in warm, humid environments make the
[[lichen-symbiosis]] energetically marginal, limiting tropical lichen diversity and
productivity.

### Light Stress and Photoprotection

The fungal partner provides critical protection for the photosynthetic
apparatus. In *[[xanthoria-parietina]]*, the [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]] **parietin**
functions as a **blue-light filter**, shielding the algal photobiont from excess
visible radiation (Solhaug & Gauslaa 1996). This photoprotective role is
essential for lichen survival in high-light environments such as exposed rocks,
tree canopies, and Arctic surfaces where UV and photosynthetically active
radiation can be intense.

## Fungal Ecosystem Services: An Overview

Table 3.1 (Dighton) summarizes the broad suite of ecosystem services provided
by fungi, many of which are exemplified by lichens:

| Service Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Soil formation** | Rock weathering, organic matter accumulation |
| **Soil fertility** | Nutrient cycling, N fixation by cyanolichens |
| **Soil stability** | Crust formation, aggregate binding |
| **Primary production** | Carbon fixation via photobiont symbioses |
| **Plant defense** | Endophytic protection, [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] |
| **Community structure** | Host-specific symbioses, succession drivers |
| **Secondary production** | Food for invertebrates, wildlife forage |

Lichens contribute directly to at least five of these seven categories,
underscoring their ecological importance far beyond their modest biomass.

## Transition: Mycorrhizal Fungi in Primary Production

While lichens are autonomous primary producers (their photobionts are embedded
within the thallus), **mycorrhizal fungi** influence primary production through
an entirely different mechanism: by forming mutualistic associations with plant
roots, they dramatically enhance host plant nutrient and water uptake. This
symbiosis indirectly amplifies whole-ecosystem primary production far beyond
what plants could achieve alone. [[mycorrhizal-contributions-terrestrial-carbon-cycle]] to carbon dynamics,
nutrient cycling, and plant community structure form the subject of the
following sections.
