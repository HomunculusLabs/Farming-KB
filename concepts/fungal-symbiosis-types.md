## Fungal Symbiosis Types

## Overview

Fungi form a remarkable diversity of symbiotic relationships with other organisms. These range from mutually beneficial partnerships to parasitism and predation. Symbiotic fungi are foundational to most terrestrial ecosystems, facilitating nutrient exchange, plant health, soil structure, and ecosystem stability. This page covers the major types of fungal symbiosis.

## Mycorrhizal Associations

Mycorrhizae are symbiotic associations between fungi and plant roots. The term literally means "fungus root." Over 80% of all land plants form mycorrhizal associations. See [[glomeromycota-arbuscular-mycorrhizae]] for detailed coverage of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae (AM)

- Partners: Glomeromycota fungi with roots of most herbaceous plants, grasses, and many trees
- Structure: Fungal hyphae penetrate root cells and form highly branched arbuscules
- Function: Fungi provide phosphorus and other minerals; plants provide carbohydrates (lipids)
- Host range: Very broad — single fungal species can colonize many plant species
- See [[mycorrhizal-inoculation]] for application methods

### Ectomycorrhizae (ECM)

- Partners: Basidiomycota and Ascomycota with roots of trees (pine, oak, birch, beech, eucalyptus, dipterocarp)
- Structure: Hyphae form a dense sheath (mantle) around root tips and a Hartig net between root cells (no cell penetration)
- Function: Enhanced nitrogen and phosphorus uptake, protection from pathogens, water access
- Host range: Narrower than AM — specific fungal-tree pairings are common
- Ecological importance: Dominant in boreal and temperate forests
- Examples: Amanita, Boletus, Laccaria, Pisolithus, Suillus species

### Ericoid Mycorrhizae

- Partners: Ascomycota with roots of plants in the order Ericales (heaths, heathers, blueberries, cranberries, rhododendrons)
- Structure: Hyphae form coils within root cells
- Function: Critical for nutrient uptake in acidic, low-nutrient soils; can decompose organic matter directly
- Ecological significance: Enables ericaceous plants to dominate nutrient-poor habitats like bogs and heathlands

### Orchid Mycorrhizae

- Partners: Basidiomycota (and some Ascomycota) with orchid roots
- Structure: Hyphae form coils (pelotons) within root cells
- Function: Orchid seeds are microscopic and contain no endosperm — they absolutely require fungal symbionts for germination. The fungus provides both carbon and nutrients to the developing orchid.
- Specificity: Often highly specific — individual orchid species may require particular fungal species
- Lifecycle: Some orchids remain dependent on fungi throughout their lives (mycoheterotrophic orchids)

## Endophytes

Endophytic fungi live inside plant tissues without causing apparent disease. They are found in virtually all plants studied.

### Clavicipitaceous Endophytes

- Found in grasses (Festuca, Lolium)
- Systemic — colonize entire plant through seeds (vertical transmission)
- Produce alkaloids that deter herbivores (insects, grazing mammals)
- Can improve drought tolerance and competitive ability
- Trade-off: alkaloids can be toxic to livestock (e.g., fescue toxicosis in cattle)

### Non-Clavicipitaceous Endophytes

- Found in leaves, stems, and roots of many plant species
- Diverse fungal groups (Ascomycota dominant)
- Horizontal transmission via spores
- Functions: pathogen resistance, stress tolerance, growth promotion
- Potential for use in [[mycorrhizal-inoculation|bioactive inoculants]]

## Lichens

Lichens are composite organisms formed by a symbiotic relationship between a fungus (mycobiont) and a photosynthetic partner (photobiont — either green algae or cyanobacteria). The fungal partner is usually an Ascomycete; the photobiont provides carbohydrates through photosynthesis, while the fungus provides structure, moisture retention, and mineral access.

### Lichen Types

- **Crustose**: Crust-like, tightly attached to substrate
- **Foliose**: Leaf-like, loosely attached with a distinct upper and lower surface
- **Fruticose**: Shrubby or hair-like, branching structures

### Ecological Roles

- Pioneer organisms on bare rock (initiate soil formation)
- Bioindicators of air quality (sensitive to sulfur dioxide)
- Nitrogen fixation (cyanobacterial photobionts fix atmospheric nitrogen)
- Food and habitat for wildlife (reindeer lichen, nesting material)

## Ant Gardens (Attine Ant-Fungus Mutualism)

Leafcutter ants (tribe Attini) cultivate fungi for food in specialized underground gardens. This is one of the most sophisticated mutualisms in nature.

### The System

1. Worker ants cut fresh leaf fragments and carry them to the nest
2. Leaves are chewed into a pulp and used as substrate for fungal growth
3. The cultivated fungus (Leucoagaricus gongylophorus) is the ants' primary food source
4. Ants actively tend the garden, removing competing molds and pathogens
5. Ants carry fungal hyphae to new nests when colonies bud

### Coevolution

This mutualism has been maintained for over 50 million years. The ants have specialized structures (mycangia) for carrying fungal inoculum. The fungus has lost the ability to reproduce sexually in some lineages, depending entirely on the ants for dispersal. See [[fungal-parasites-and-predators]] for parasites that attack these gardens.

## Other Symbioses

### Fungal-Endosymbiont Relationships

- Some fungi harbor endosymbiotic bacteria that produce toxins or enzymes beneficial to the fungal host
- Rhizoxin-producing Burkholderia bacteria live within the rice pathogen Rhizopus microsporus

### Dark Septate Endophytes

- Melanized fungi found in plant roots, especially in arctic, alpine, and stressed environments
- Function poorly understood but may aid in nutrient uptake and stress tolerance
