# Trans-Pacific Species Pairs in Psilocybe: Southern Hemisphere Biogeographic Connections

## Overview

Guzmán, Allen & Gartz (1998) highlighted remarkable trans-Pacific
biogeographic connections in the genus Psilocybe, where closely related
species occur on opposite sides of the Pacific Ocean despite vast oceanic
barriers. These disjunct distributions suggest ancient Gondwanan
connections, long-distance dispersal events, or convergent evolution in
similar humid forest habitats. The most striking examples involve species
from Mexico and [[psilocybe-zapotecorum-section-zapotecorum-americas-pacific-distribution-guzman-1998]] provides another striking example. Psilocybe
zapotecorum, widely distributed in Mexico and South America, is closely
related to P. aucklandii from New Zealand. P. zapotecorum is one of the
most important sacred mushrooms used by indigenous peoples of southern
Mexico, where it is known by names such as "derrumbes" (landslides)
referring to its habit of fruiting abundantly after rains on disturbed
ground.

P. aucklandii, its New Zealand counterpart, occupies similar habitats
in the Auckland region. The morphological similarity between these
geographically separated species is so close that they were long
considered to potentially represent a single widespread species before
detailed study revealed consistent morphological differences supporting
their status as distinct but closely allied taxa.

## Other Trans-Pacific Connections

Additional trans-Pacific relationships exist between American and Asian
Psilocybe species. Psilocybe argentipes and P. subcaerulipes from Japan
are very closely related to P. graveolens, P. muliercula, P. pintonii
and P. zapotecorum from the Americas. These relationships suggest
historical connections between the mycobiota of eastern Asia and the
Americas, a pattern also observed in other groups of agarics and
boletes by Horak (1983).

Horak reported that South American and Mesoamerican species of agarics
and boletes are closely related to those of southeastern Asia (New
Guinea, Indonesia, and Australia), including species of Cystoagaricus,
Galerina, Mycena, and Paxillus. The Psilocybe trans-Pacific
connections align with this broader pattern of fungal biogeography.
