# Permaculture Fire Control Strategies

Fire is one of the most common and dangerous hazards in many landscapes. In [[permaculture-design]], fire control is not an afterthought but a fundamental consideration that shapes placement of every element — houses, roads, water systems, and plantings. The approach combines landscape-level fuel reduction, strategic element placement, physical protection structures, and client education.

## Understanding Fire Behavior

### Fire Periodicity

Fire has a periodicity specific to each site, determined by two factors:

1. **Rate of fuel accumulation** on site — the critical factor.
2. **Amount of moisture contained** on site.

Ridge tops are far more fire-prone than valley systems. Sun-facing (equatorial) slopes are more fire-prone than shaded slopes. Ridge-top vegetation may even be fire-dependent, with species that germinate only after fire.

In areas with 30–40 inches of annual rainfall, a catastrophic fire occurs roughly every 25–30 years. Fire periodicity can be determined by examining cross-sections of old trees or from historical records.

### The Fire Cycle Feedback

The fire cycle exhibits strong positive feedback:

- **Delaying fire** increases [[humus]] and moisture, making the site less likely to burn again. Even delaying fire by one period confers advantage.
- **Frequent burning** removes moisture-retaining humus, kills more than it consumes, and creates fire-prone litter buildup. Areas that naturally burn every 30th year may burn every 8th to 10th year once shorter burn intervals are established.

### How Fire Actually Kills

The real danger of fire is **radiation**, not direct flame contact:

- **400 feet** before a fire: your hair catches alight.
- **200 feet**: your body splits and fat catches alight.
- **100 feet**: you become a torch.

Radiation kills birds hundreds of feet from flames — they fall from the sky. Pigs die quickly. Goats survive by leaning into the radiant heat. Humans survive by dodging and hiding behind shadows (radiation barriers).

### Fire Movement

Fire travels at approximately 400 miles per hour. It spirals upward, increasing in breadth at the base. Burning debris is carried aloft in massive spirals, then dropped to start new spirals. A single fire can cover a thousand square miles in an hour. You cannot outrun or drive away from it — you must hold your position and shelter.

## Defensive Sequences

### Primary Strategy: Reduce Fuel

The fundamental defense is to reduce available fuel through:

- **Non-fuel surfaces**: Roads, ponds, swales, pit mulching areas.
- **Browsing and grazing**: Chickens break up ground litter and mix it with oxygen for [[decomposition]]. Short grazers (sheep, [[wallaby]]) on the fire side reduce standing litter to one inch.
- **Fire-resistant plantings**: Species with high ash content, high water content, low total bulk, and dense growth. Ice plants and thick-leaf evergreens whose litter decomposes quickly are excellent choices.

### Species to Avoid

**Never** use high volatile oil content plants on the downhill (fire-danger) side:

- **Eucalypts** are a positive no-no — fire weeds that carry cones opening only after fire.
- **Pines** are equally problematic — litter accumulators forming hard, volatile litter that carries large ground fires.
- Both species are fire-adapted "weeds" that depend on fire to extend their range.

### The 100-Foot Zone

You only need 100 feet of non-fuel systems between the house and the forest. This is achievable with strategic planting of fire-resistant species, swales, and ponds. Work from valleys upward with low-fuel vegetation plantings, re-establishing the valley-type rain forest species that would naturally occur if fire were absent.

### Mulch Safety

Contrary to intuition, mulches are not a significant fire risk. Experimental ignition around mulches shows they may smolder with a half-inch surface fire but will not spread. Sawdust is similarly safe.

## Protecting the House

### Sprinkler Systems

**Downhill sprinklers** are the primary active defense. If activated before fire arrival (when the ground is still wet), fire will not cross the wetted area. If the fire is already present when sprinklers activate, water converts to steam before reaching the ground and is ineffective.

**Roof sprinklers** (monsoon systems) are critical for house survival. Most houses ignite when:
1. Ash falls on the roof and slides down.
2. Ash accumulates against chimneys and fills gutters.
3. Wind blows heat under the roof.
4. Tar paper and insulation ignite from below.

A gravity-fed monsoon sprinkler on the roof ridge, operated for the essential half-hour during ash fall, washes the roof continuously and keeps gutters flowing. The system must be privately owned — public water systems fail when everyone draws simultaneously.

### House Construction

**Wooden houses survive fire better than stone or brick**, with 13–15% higher survival rates. This is counterintuitive but well-documented:

- Stone transmits heat rapidly to inner surfaces.
- Brick is equally fast at heat transmission.
- Wood does not transmit heat through its fabric and has better drafting systems.
- White-painted or any reflective surface provides additional radiation protection.

### Roof Design

In areas with snow, fire, or heavy leaf drop:
- Install a **rolled-under section** on the bottom edge of the roof.
- Place gutters **below** this rolled section.
- Leaves slide off, snow slides off, melt enters the gutter, and fire ash falls clear.

### Physical Fire Shelters

For high-risk sites, construct **T-shaped or L-shaped pits** with earth berms. These can also be road conduit sections, earthed over. Clients can shelter underground during fire passage, emerging when radiation has passed.

A simple backhoe-dug shelter into an earth bank, normally away from the fire direction on the slope, about six feet deep, accessible from a back door, can be the critical difference between life and death. A root cellar serves double duty as a fire shelter.

## Client Education

### Critical Instructions

1. **Never jump into water during fire** — dissolved oxygen is depleted, and people will faint and drown.
2. **Never run from fire** — you have a better chance running straight at it.
3. **Don't try to drive** — petrol evaporates, bearings melt, tires ignite.
4. **Keep your mouth shut** — don't breathe; lungs will burn out.
5. **Duck behind things** — get out of direct radiation.

### Evacuation Procedure

Instruct clients with specific procedures:
1. Stand on the side away from approaching fire with woolen clothing and blankets soaked in water.
2. Move to the prepared shelter.
3. Have drinking water available in the shelter.
4. After the fire front passes, walk around to the opposite side of the house (the fire will have gone past).

### Vulnerable Populations

Fire typically builds to maximum intensity between 2:00 and 3:00 p.m. — when people at home are often families with young children who may not have vehicles. These vulnerable groups must be specifically instructed and provided with accessible shelter.

## Landscape-Level Fire Planning

### Valley and Ridge Patterns

Never place clients at the head of a converging valley — these act as fire funnels. Don't place housing where ridges converge. The interaction between fire behavior and terrain is well-documented locally; traces and effects of previous fires show exactly where not to build.

### Integrated Design

Good fire control and good [[permaculture]] element placement are entirely compatible:
- Construct ponds in front of the house with the road beside them.
- The pond bank should rise toward the fire side, providing a radiation barrier.
- Fire banks up the driveway combined with hedges and swales create multiple defensive layers.
- Plant Coprosma and similar dense, high-water-content species as near-house radiation shields.

### The Mediterranean Warning

Greece was once covered in wet rain forests with enormous oak and columnar beeches. Repeated burning has reduced it to a skeleton of its former self. The entire Mediterranean and much of North Africa has reached a state where soil, plants, and hills are burned — you can't burn rocks, and there's nothing left to burn. This represents the terminal state of the fire cycle feedback: once an area burns frequently enough, it enters irreversible degradation.

## Hazardous Materials

Store high explosives, fuel drums, and similar materials in separate sheds, dispersed from living areas. When one ignites, the others must not be affected.
