# Mycoremediation of Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are carcinogenic compounds formed during incomplete combustion of organic materials. They consist of two or more fused benzene rings, with two- and three-ring structures (naphthalene, anthracene, phenanthrene) being far more biodegradable than four-, five-, and six-ring structures (benzo[a]pyrene, coronene).

## Occurrence and Environmental Significance

PAHs originate from multiple anthropogenic and natural sources including incomplete combustion of fossil fuels, petroleum spills and refining operations, coal tar and creosote production, municipal waste incineration, and vehicular emissions. The US EPA lists 16 PAHs as priority pollutants based on their frequency of occurrence, toxicity, and potential for human exposure. Among these, benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by IARC. PAHs persist in soils and sediments due to their hydrophobicity and chemical stability, with half-lives ranging from weeks for low-molecular-weight PAHs to years for high-molecular-weight compounds.

## Why Fungi Excel at PAH Degradation

Bacteria use intracellular dioxygenases that require substrate uptake through cell walls. PAHs with more than 2-3 rings are extremely water-insoluble and cannot cross microbial cell walls. White-rot basidiomycetes overcome this limitation through extracellular oxidative enzyme systems (LiP, MnP, laccase) that generate diffusible oxidizing agents capable of attacking insoluble polymeric substrates (Harvey & Thurston; Bumpus et al., 1985). The same non-specific radical chemistry used for lignin depolymerization applies to PAH oxidation. See [[white-rot-fungi-bioremediation]] for organism details.

## Fungal Metabolism of Specific PAHs

- **Naphthalene (2 rings):** Readily degraded by multiple fungal species through initial epoxidation at the 1,2-position by cytochrome P450, followed by cis-dihydrodiol formation, dehydrogenation to 1,2-dihydroxynaphthalene, and subsequent ring cleavage to salicylaldehyde and salicylic acid.
- **Phenanthrene (3 rings):** Angularly arranged, moderately biodegradable. Metabolized via cytochrome P450 and ligninolytic enzymes. Initial oxidation occurs at both the angular ring (C-9,10 positions) and the K-region (C-1,2 positions), producing phenanthrene trans-dihydrodiols that undergo further oxidation and ring cleavage.
- **Anthracene (3 rings):** Linear arrangement, more biodegradable than angular isomers. Oxidized to anthraquinone via initial one-electron oxidation, followed by further metabolism through the anthraquinone pathway to phthalic acid derivatives.
- **Pyrene (4 rings):** Degraded by MnP and laccase systems. Initial oxidation produces 1-hydroxypyrene and other hydroxylated intermediates. Further oxidation yields pyrene quinones and ring-cleavage products.
- **Benzo[a]pyrene (5 rings):** One of the most carcinogenic PAHs. Degraded by P. chrysosporium through LiP-mediated oxidation. LiP generates BaP cation radicals that react with water to form benzo[a]pyrene-7,8-diol, further oxidized to benzo[a]pyrene-7,8-dione. Partially mineralized to CO2.
- **Chrysene (4 rings):** Degraded by white-rot fungi though at slower rates than lower-ring PAHs. MnP-mediated oxidation produces chrysene quinones.
- **Fluoranthene:** A non-alternant PAH degraded by laccase-mediator systems and MnP. Oxidation occurs preferentially at the 2,3-bond.
- **Fluorene and acenaphthene:** Lower-molecular-weight PAHs more readily attacked by both cytochrome P450 systems and ligninolytic enzymes. Fluorene is oxidized to 9-fluorenone; acenaphthene to acenaphthenone.
- **Benz[a]anthracene:** Degraded by white-rot fungi through initial oxidation at the bay-region, producing diol epoxides that are further metabolized.

## Enzymatic PAH Degradation

**Peroxidase-catalyzed:** LiP oxidizes PAHs via veratryl alcohol-mediated one-electron oxidation, generating PAH cation radicals that undergo subsequent non-enzymatic reactions. Miscible solvents (acetone, DMSO) enhance PAH solubility and degradation rates. Cyclodextrins form inclusion complexes with PAHs, increasing bioavailability. See [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] and [[mycoremediation-enzymology]] for details.

**Laccase-catalyzed:** Laccase oxidizes PAHs with lower ionization potentials. Mediator compounds (1-hydroxybenzotriazole, ABTS) extend laccase substrate range to higher-redox-potential PAHs. Laccase from Trametes versicolor degrades anthracene and phenanthrene effectively with mediator addition.

## PAH Degradation in Soils

Factors affecting soil PAH biodegradation are complex and interrelated:

- **Aging and bioavailability:** PAHs that have resided in soil for extended periods become sequestered in organic matter and micropores, reducing their bioavailability to fungal enzymes. Aging is one of the primary limitations on remediation efficiency.
- **Organic matter content:** Moderate organic matter enhances fungal colonization and enzyme production, but excessive organic matter can sorb PAHs and limit access.
- **Cosubstrates:** Wood chips, straw, and corn cobs enhance fungal colonization and sustained enzyme production in soil by providing lignocellulosic growth substrates. These amendments are critical for maintaining fungal viability in nutrient-poor soils.
- **Surfactant effects:** While surfactants can improve PAH solubility, some (Triton X-100, Tergitol NP-10) inhibit fungal growth and enzyme production at effective concentrations. Non-ionic surfactants are generally less toxic than ionic ones.
- **Cyclodextrin inclusion complexes:** Cyclodextrins enhance PAH bioavailability by forming water-soluble inclusion complexes, improving mass transfer to fungal hyphae without the toxicity issues associated with synthetic surfactants.

## Bound Residue Formation

PAH disappearance in soil does not always equate to mineralization. A significant fraction of PAHs is transformed into bound residues — non-extractable material incorporated into soil organic matter or humic substances through covalent bonding. This distinction is critical: true mineralization produces CO2 and represents complete pollutant removal, while bound residue formation merely changes the chemical form. Bound residues may be relatively stable but can potentially be released if soil conditions change.

## Compost Bioreactors for PAH Treatment

Composting approaches using lignocellulosic substrates (wood chips, straw, leaves) provide favorable conditions for fungal PAH degradation:

- **Substrate selection:** Hardwood chips and wheat straw support robust white-rot fungal colonization and sustained ligninolytic enzyme production.
- **Aeration:** Forced or passive aeration maintains aerobic conditions essential for oxidative enzyme function and prevents anaerobic pocket formation.
- **Moisture and nutrient supply:** Moisture content of 50-70% water-holding capacity and balanced C:N ratios (25:1 to 40:1) optimize fungal activity.
- **Temperature management:** Thermophilic composting phases (50-60°C) may inhibit some fungal species; mesophilic operation or inoculation after cooling may be necessary.

## Fungal-Bacterial Co-cultures

Combined systems exploit complementary metabolism: fungi initially attack high-molecular-weight PAHs through extracellular enzymes, producing oxidized intermediates that bacteria then mineralize more completely. This consortium approach achieves broader PAH removal and higher mineralization rates than either organism alone. Fungi generate quinones, diols, and carboxylated intermediates that serve as carbon sources for bacterial communities. See [[combined-biological-remediation-approaches]] for detailed discussion.

## Bioreactor Types for PAH Treatment

Several bioreactor configurations have been applied:

- **Immobilized reactors:** Fungal biomass immobilized on carriers (porous glass, polyurethane foam, wood chips) provides high cell density and operational stability for continuous PAH treatment. Polyurethane foam is particularly effective due to its high surface area and oxygen transfer properties.
- **Closed-batch feed reactors:** Periodic feeding of PAH-contaminated material to fungal cultures maintains enzyme induction without substrate inhibition. Fed-batch strategies optimize the balance between nutrient supply and enzyme production.
- **Compost reactors:** Large-scale composting of PAH-contaminated soil mixed with organic amendments, leveraging natural fungal succession and thermophilic/mesophilic phase transitions. Windrow and static pile configurations are commonly used.

## Performance and Time Frames

PAH bioremediation timelines vary significantly based on compound molecular weight and environmental conditions:

- Low-MW PAHs (naphthalene, phenanthrene): Substantial degradation within 2-4 weeks under optimal conditions.
- Medium-MW PAHs (anthracene, pyrene, fluoranthene): Measurable removal in 4-8 weeks, with mineralization typically requiring 30-60 days.
- High-MW PAHs (benzo[a]pyrene, benzo[a]anthracene): Degradation is slower, often requiring 60-120 days for significant transformation. Complete mineralization of 5-6 ring PAHs is rarely achieved by fungi alone.

Mineralization percentages generally range from 5-20% for high-MW PAHs to 40-80% for low-MW PAHs. However, total disappearance (including bound residue formation) can exceed 90% for lower-ring compounds.

## Fungal Species Performance

Among white-rot fungi used for PAH degradation, Phanerochaete chrysosporium remains the most extensively studied, but other species show comparable or superior performance for specific PAHs:

- Trametes versicolor demonstrates strong laccase-mediated degradation of phenanthrene and anthracene.
- Pleurotus ostreatus shows robust activity in soil systems with lignocellulosic amendments.
- Bjerkandera adusta produces MnP with exceptionally high specific activity for PAH oxidation.
- Irpex lacteus degrades a broad spectrum of PAHs in contaminated soil with minimal nutrient supplementation.

# White Rot Fungi Bioremediation of Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Updated: 2026-04-18

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are widespread hazardous environmental pollutants released by burning of fossil fuels and wood, coal mining, and oil drilling. Several PAHs are mutagenic and carcinogenic. White rot fungi can degrade at least 22 PAHs with 70-100% breakdown in 27 days in nitrogen-limited cultures of Phanerochaete chrysosporium, the model organism for [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]].

## Enzymatic Degradation Mechanisms

PAHs are degraded through oxidation by [[fungal-ligninolytic-enzyme-systems-overview]] including lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese-dependent peroxidase (MnP), and laccase:

- **Lignin peroxidase** directly oxidizes PAHs to corresponding quinones. Benzo[a]pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, anthracene, pyrene, and perylene are all directly oxidized by purified LiP from P. chrysosporium. LiP catalyzes H2O2-dependent one-electron oxidation to aryl cation radicals.

- **Manganese peroxidase** produces small amounts of quinones as intermediates in the degradation of anthracene and benzo[a]pyrene. MnP was the predominant ligninolytic enzyme during PAH degradation by P. laevis, and its levels were stimulated by Mn(II) in the culture medium.

- **Laccase** from Coriolopsis gallica oxidizes benzo[a]pyrene, 9-methylanthracene, 2-methylanthracene, anthracene, biphenylene, acenaphthene, and phenanthrene. Laccases of Trametes versicolor carry out oxidation of acenaphthene, acenaphthylene, anthracene, and fluorene, mediated by small molecular weight compounds such as phenol, aniline, and 4-hydroxybenzyl alcohol.

## Mediator Systems

Mediators enhance the range of compounds laccases can attack. ABTS (2,2'-azinobis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)) and 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HBT) mediate the transformation of anthracene by T. versicolor laccase. Laccases degrade phenanthrene to phenanthrene-9,10-quinone and 2,2'-diphenic acid as major products.

## Surfactant Enhancement

Two- to fivefold augmentation of degradation of anthracene, pyrene, and benzo[a]pyrene occurs in the presence of non-ionic surfactants such as Tween 80 by Bjerkandera sp. (Kotterman, Rietberg & Field, 1998). Surfactants increase PAH bioavailability, which is often the rate-limiting factor in bioremediation of hydrophobic pollutants.

## Non-Ligninolytic Pathways

Both ligninolytic and non-ligninolytic cultures of P. chrysosporium degraded radiolabelled phenanthrene to CO2, suggesting that enzymes beyond the ligninolytic system are also involved in the PAH degradation pathway.

## Field Applications

P. chrysosporium has been applied to treatment of coal- and creosote-contaminated soils supplemented with wood chips, corn cobs, or sawdust. PAH constituents were reduced to 10-20% of original levels in laboratory trials. Bjerkandera sp. strain BOS55 removed 38.5% of benzo[a]pyrene from soil after 56 days of incubation.

## Challenges

Field-scale experiments in which PAH-contaminated soils were mixed with corn cobs, sawdust, or bark chips and heavily inoculated with P. chrysosporium (10-30% w/w) did not always show significant changes in PAH concentration. Practical difficulties arise from competition by indigenous soil organisms and difficulty growing sufficient fungal biomass. Indigenous soil bacteria can antagonize P. chrysosporium growth depending on pH and available nitrogen/carbon sources. PCR-based detection methods may help assess fungal survival in treated soils.
