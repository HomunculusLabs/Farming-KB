# Seed Saving and Variety Selection

## Overview

Steve Solomon's experience as founder of Territorial Seed Company gave
him deep insight into the commercial seed industry and the critical
importance of starting with high-quality seeds from reliable sources. In
*Gardening When It Counts*, he argues that successful [[home-vegetable-garden-planning]]
begins with choosing the right varieties from honest suppliers, and that
seed saving is an essential skill for resilient, self-reliant food
production.

## Problems with the Commercial Seed Industry

Solomon is blunt about problems in the seed trade. Mass-market seed
companies and [[solomon-garden-center-seedling-traps-transplant-buyer-awareness]] producers are not always ethical in
their product offerings. Picture-packet seeds sold in big-box stores may
be old stock with declining germination rates. Seedling trays may
contain varieties chosen for shipping durability rather than garden
performance.

An honest seed business tests varieties in actual garden conditions
before offering them for sale. Many mass-market suppliers simply
resell whatever is cheapest from wholesale growers, without regard for
whether the variety performs well in [[cool-climate-home-garden-mollison]] conditions. The result
is that many beginning gardeners fail not because of their own mistakes
but because they started with poor genetic material.

## Selecting Reliable Seed Suppliers

Solomon recommends purchasing from specialized mail-order [[spanish-cannabis-seed-companies]]
that conduct their own [[coleman-seed-selection-and-variety-trials]]. These companies offer detailed
variety descriptions based on actual performance data, not marketing
copy. They typically offer fewer varieties but each one has been tested
and selected for specific qualities.

Key indicators of a reliable seed supplier include germination rate
guarantees, detailed growing instructions, honest descriptions of both
strengths and weaknesses of each variety, and a track record of serving
serious gardeners rather than casual consumers.

Gardeners should be skeptical of varieties that claim to do everything
well. The best varieties are typically excellent at a few specific
things — disease resistance, flavor, storage quality, or regional
adaptation — rather than being merely adequate at everything.

## Open-Pollinated vs Hybrid Varieties

For gardeners interested in seed saving, open-pollinated (OP) varieties
are essential. OP varieties breed true from saved seed, meaning the
[[next-generation-sequencing-hidden-fungal-diversity]] will closely resemble the parent. This allows the
gardener to develop locally adapted strains over successive seasons.

Hybrid varieties (F1) do not breed true. Seed saved from hybrid plants
produces offspring that segregate into a range of types, most of which
are inferior to the original hybrid. While hybrids can offer excellent
garden performance, they create dependency on the seed company because
the gardener must purchase fresh seed each year.

Solomon strongly advocates for open-pollinated varieties, especially
for gardeners preparing for hard times when commercial seed sources may
become unavailable or unaffordable. The ability to produce your own seed
is a cornerstone of food self-reliance.

## Basics of Seed Saving

Seed saving is not technically difficult for most common vegetable
crops, but it does require knowledge of each species' reproductive
biology. Some vegetables are self-pollinating (beans, peas, tomatoes,
lettuce) and are easy to save seed from without cross-pollination
concerns. Others are outcrossing (brassicas, squash, corn, beets) and
require isolation distances to prevent unwanted cross-pollination.

The basic process involves allowing selected plants to mature fully
beyond the eating stage, harvesting the seed when it is fully dry and
ripe, cleaning it of chaff and debris, and storing it in cool, dry
conditions. Properly saved seed of many vegetable species maintains
good germination for three to five years or more.
