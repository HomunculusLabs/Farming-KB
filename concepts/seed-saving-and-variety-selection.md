Hybrid varieties (F1) do not breed true. Seed saved from hybrid plants
produces offspring that segregate into a range of types, most of which
are inferior to the original hybrid. While hybrids can offer excellent
garden performance, they create dependency on the seed company because
the gardener must purchase fresh seed each year.

Solomon strongly advocates for open-pollinated varieties, especially
for gardeners preparing for hard times when commercial seed sources may
become unavailable or unaffordable. The ability to produce your own seed
is a cornerstone of food self-reliance.

## Basics of Seed Saving

Seed saving is not technically difficult for most common vegetable
crops, but it does require knowledge of each species' reproductive
biology. Some vegetables are self-pollinating (beans, peas, tomatoes,
lettuce) and are easy to save seed from without cross-pollination
concerns. Others are outcrossing (brassicas, squash, corn, beets) and
require isolation distances to prevent unwanted cross-pollination.

The basic process involves allowing selected plants to mature fully
beyond the eating stage, harvesting the seed when it is fully dry and
ripe, cleaning it of chaff and debris, and storing it in cool, dry
conditions. Properly saved seed of many vegetable species maintains
good germination for three to five years or more.
