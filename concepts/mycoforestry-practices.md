## The Cortes Island Mycoforestry Research Project

In 2003, a mycoforestry research project was established on Cortes Island, British Columbia, on land that had been clear-cut. The site became an experimental theater for testing mycoforestry strategies as an alternative to traditional forestry methods, which often involve burning the debris on clear-cut land. The long-term experiment compared the effects of introducing mycorrhizae and wood chip topdressing as a source of delayed-release nutrients and moisture retention.

For this experiment, 35,000 seedlings of Douglas fir and cedar were planted. Half of all the tree seedlings were root-dipped in a mycorrhizal spore-mass slurry containing spores from two species of puffballs. Half of the cedars were exposed to approximately half a million spores of the endomycorrhizal species Glomus intraradices, while half of the Douglas firs were exposed to a similar quantity of the ectomycorrhizal species Rhizopogon parksii.

Around the base of half of the trees in each treated and untreated group, a topdressing of wood chips was applied — approximately one gallon per tree. After 10 months, the inoculated trees showed a net 5 percent increase in overall height and 7 percent increase in girth respectively, significant given the short time frame.

## Forest Fire Prevention Through Mycoforestry

Mushroom mycelium is the grand demolecularizer of plant fibers (lignin and cellulose), creating soil as an end consequence. Foot-deep beds of wood chips have been observed to decompose into 1 to 2 inches of rich soil in 2 to 3 years when inoculated with mycelium, or in 4 to 5 years from natural mycoflora alone.

Wood chips left on the forest floor provide multiple benefits: delayed-release nutrients to build soils, supporting mycofiltration membranes that reduce erosion and siltation, providing cavity habitats for diverse populations of bacteria, fungi, plants, insects, and animals, moisture retention, and protection against forest fires.

Where wood chips touch the ground, fungi easily grow into them and transport moisture with their threadlike mycelium. The myceliated wood chips then become like a sponge, retaining water and lessening fire danger. The argument that wood debris fuels forest fires does not hold true if debris is brought in contact with decomposing mycelium already residing in the forest floor. Moist wood does not easily burn.

## The Economic Case

Rotating tree crops every 40 years thins the soil faster than it can be built, with ever-diminishing returns. Such practices accelerate premature decline as root wads cannot support the trees above them. Logging companies could chip wood debris, leave it in the cut forestland, and disperse these nutrient fragments over the ground to help refuel the carbon cycle while reducing the danger of fire.

The added expense does not thrill most investment loggers, who wish to maximize returns and minimize costs. The incentive is for those who take a long-term view of the forest and its ecological health. Timberland that has lost topsoils is slower to recover with each successive crop of trees. Unless the depletion of the nutritional topsoil bank is reversed, the future economic return on timber will be bleak.

The current practice of burning or hauling wood debris robs biomass from the carbon cycle. Decomposition by fungi buffers carbon dioxide emission and cycles much of the gas back into the flourishing ecosystem, since carbon dioxide is heavier than air and permeates soils where plant cells absorb it as raw material for creating cellulose, lignin, carbohydrates, proteins, and sterols.
