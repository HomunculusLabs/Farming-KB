## The Input-Output Framework

A broader classification reduces all characteristics to two categories:

- **Outputs, yields, or products** are **resources** if used productively, or become **pollutants** if not used constructively by another part of the system.
- **Inputs, needs, or demands** must be supplied; if not supplied by other parts of the system, then **external energy** or **extra work** must be found.

This leads to two critical definitions:

### A Pollutant

> A pollutant is an output of any system component that is not being used productively by any other component of the system.

Manure that is not composted or spread is a pollutant. CO₂ that is not absorbed by plants is a pollutant. Sound that serves no purpose is a pollutant. The definition is not moral — it is systemic. Pollution is a design failure.

### Extra Work

> Extra work is the result of an input not automatically provided by another component of the system.

If chickens must be carried food from a distant source rather than finding it on-site through foraging, that carrying is extra work. If water must be pumped rather than delivered by gravity, the pumping is extra work. Extra work represents energy that the designer failed to capture from within the system.

Since both pollution and extra work are unnecessary results of an incompletely designed system, the design task is clear: connect every output to an input, and supply every need from within the system.

## Making Connections Between Components

To enable a design component to function, it must be placed in the right position and connected to other components. The process involves three stages:

### 1. Placement

Putting components where they can serve each other. Ducks placed in a swamp may take care of themselves, producing eggs and meat while recycling seeds and frogs. For other components, placement alone is insufficient — active connections must be arranged.

### 2. Connection

Arranging physical or biological links between components. A solar collector must be linked by pipes to hot water storage. Chickens must have access to the orchard. Compost must be positioned between kitchen and garden.

### 3. Regulation

Observing and regulating what has been done. Regulation may involve:
- Confining or insulating a component
- Guiding it by fencing, hedging, or one-way valves
- Adjusting timing through seasonal management

Once all three stages are achieved, the designer can relax and let the system self-regulate.

## The Four Connection Questions

For any component, the designer should ask:

1. **Of what use are the products of this component to the needs of other components?**
2. **What needs of this component are supplied by other components?**
3. **Where is this component incompatible with other components?**
4. **Where does this component benefit other parts of the system?**

The answers provide a plan of relative placement and assist the access of one component to others.

## The Traditional Farm as a Design Problem

Mollison uses the example of a typical small farm to illustrate the consequences of failed connections. A traditional small farm contains all the components for self-governance — house, barn, glasshouse, chicken-house, pond, hedgerow, orchard, pasture, cropland, woodlot — but nothing is connected to anything else. No component supplies the needs of others. All inputs must come from outside, and all outputs become waste.

This is why most farms are rightly regarded as places of hard work and are energy-inefficient.

### Redesign Through Connection

By analyzing the interactions between the farm's components, beneficial connections emerge:

- **House ↔ Chicken**: The house produces food wastes that chickens consume; chickens produce eggs, feathers, and methane that serve the house.
- **Glasshouse ↔ Chicken**: The glasshouse needs CO₂, manure, heat, and water; chickens produce all of these. Chickens can supply night heat via body heat.
- **Orchard ↔ Chicken**: The orchard needs weeding, pest control, and manure; it provides insects and fallen fruit for chicken forage. The orchard and chickens need each other — they require only to be placed together.
- **Woodlot ↔ Chicken**: The woodlot needs management and pest control; it yields insects, seeds, shelter, and warmth.
- **Cropland ↔ Chicken**: The cropland needs manuring and cultivation; chickens provide both by scratching and depositing manure.
- **Pasture ↔ Chicken**: The pasture needs cropping and manuring; it provides food for chickens and other animals.
- **Pond ↔ Chicken**: The pond needs manure to fuel its ecosystem; it yields fish and water plants.

Just by moving the same components into a beneficial design assembly — without inventing anything new — the chicken, glasshouse, or orchard works for the system rather than against it. The redesign converts waste streams into resource flows and external inputs into internal cycles.
