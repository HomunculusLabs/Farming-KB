# Agrocybe Cultivation (Black Poplar / Pioppino)

## Overview

*[[agrocybe-aegerita-detailed]]*, commonly known as the black poplar mushroom or pioppino, is a relatively easy-to-grow species on hardwood substrates. Though it often grows on poplar trees in the wild, it has a broad host spectrum that extends well beyond poplar. It is sold widely in Europe and increasingly in the United States under the Italian name "pioppino." Its distinctly nutty flavor makes it a valued addition to any cultivation portfolio.

**Important identification note:** *Agrocybe* can be more difficult to identify in the field than species like shiitake or [[oyster-mushrooms]]. When cultivating outdoors on logs where uninvited species may fruit alongside your planted crop, take care to distinguish your harvest. Thoroughly study and become confident in identification before consuming.

## Difficulty Level

- **Outdoor cultivation:** 1 (beginner)
- **Indoor cultivation:** 2 (intermediate)

## Mycelium and Spawn

- **Appearance:** White and linear
- **Metabolites:** Exudes light yellow on overcolonized spawn and substrates
- **Fragrance:** Sweet to slightly anise-like
- **Commercial spawn:** Available as supplemented sawdusts and wooden dowels for outdoor use; [[grain-spawn]] is more difficult to find, making lab culture important for commercial indoor production

## Fruitbody Development

- Cap centered on the stem with slightly descending gills
- Gills are smoky gray at first, darkening at maturity
- Gills are **never yellow or greenish** (unlike the poisonous sulfur tuft, *[[hypholoma-fasciculare]]*, which has yellow to orange caps and greenish gills)
- Cap color: cream-colored to light brown
- **[[spore-print]]:** Dark gray to black (same as *Hypholoma*, so identification must rely on cap and gill color)

## Fruiting Conditions

- **Temperature range:** 50–65°F (10–19°C) — on the cool side
- **Seasonal timing:** Typically fruits in spring in [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]]
- **Cold shock:** Indoor cultures may benefit from a mild cold shock after colonization to initiate fruiting
- **Between flushes:** Allow containers to rest at warmer temperatures for two weeks after harvest, then rehydrate with cold water and return to cooler environment to stimulate next flush
- **Outdoor:** Soaking logs in ice-cold water can trigger fruiting in temperate climates

## Wild Spawn Expansion

Since *Agrocybe* fruits from stumps and logs but does not reproduce easily from stem base samples alone:

1. Pull back the bark from fruiting logs
2. Find elongated stem bases or colonized wood near the attachment point
3. Harvest viable mycelium from these areas
4. Experiment with natural **cardboard expansion methods** for preliminary isolations

## Laboratory Culture

Spores of this genus germinate rapidly and outcompete most mold spores on agar, making spore prints valuable both for identification and for year-to-year strain storage. Adding screened sawdust or fine particulates of the [[pf-tek-maximum-fruiting-substrate-formula]]/wood type helps spore germinations diverge into high-quality, locally-adapted strains.

**Preferred agar:** [[malt-extract-agar]] (over potato extract agar).

## Preferred Substrates

*Agrocybe* is a **hardwood omnivore** — it can be grown on any type of non-coniferous wood but is not well-suited to agricultural waste (wheat straw, cotton hulls, dried vegetable matter). Growing on agricultural waste would require heavy grain spawn inoculation, which is not economically viable.

**Indoor:** Sterilized, supplemented sawdust is ideal, closely matching the mushroom's native substrate in nutrition and density.

## Outdoor Cultivation

- **Substrate:** Fresh logs, stumps, or wood chips (cut no more than one month before inoculation)
- **Placement:** Benefits from ground contact — use submerged log technique covered with wood chips
- **Alternative:** Wood chips alone with supplemented sawdust spawn, between vegetable rows or along woodland pathways
- **Cardboard:** Can also be cultivated on cardboard outdoors
- **Spawn type:** Sawdust, plug, or cardboard spawn
- **Time to fruiting:** 8–10 months
- **Expected yields:** Good — clusters harvested in tight bundles, 2 flushes a few weeks apart, patch fruits for 2–3 years

## Indoor Cultivation

- **Substrate:** Sterilized, supplemented sawdust
- **Colonization:** 3–4 weeks after inoculation
- **[[cold-shock-fruiting-trigger-species-specificity-psilocybe-panaeolus]]:** Poking supplemental holes in the bag's overhead space helps trigger fruiting; once primordia form, cut bags and roll down to just above them
- **Yield strategy:** Tight clusters (rather than rangy groupings or single specimens) have longer shelf life — encourage this with narrow bag openings rather than large exposed surfaces
- **Spawn type:** Grain or supplemented sawdust
- **Time to fruiting:** 4–6 weeks
- **Expected yields:** Good — 2 flushes a few weeks apart

## Harvesting

- Mature 5–8 days after emerging, depending on [[greg-green-temperature-and-humidity-control]]
- Cut singletons at the base, or pull entire clusters together
- Trim wood debris or attached substrate before storing

## Storage

Fresh mushrooms keep up to two weeks at 38–42°F (3–6°C). When dried, caps rehydrate well and stems can be ground into powders.

## Marketing

This is a crunchy, nutty mushroom excellent in stir-fries. Sold under various names — "black poplar" at general markets and "pioppino" at upscale restaurants. Since loose mushrooms damage easily, sell in bundles wrapped in paper with just the caps exposed.

## Nutritional Value and Medicinal Uses

- **Protein:** ~27% dry weight
- **Antiviral properties:** Active against both human and plant viruses (including tobacco mosaic)
- **Antibacterial properties:** Effective against *E. coli*, *Streptococcus aureus*, *Bacillus cereus* and *B. subtilis*, and *Salmonella typhimurium*
- **Anti-inflammatory:** High levels of anti-inflammatory compounds
- **Anti-cancer:** Extracts shown to suppress many cancer cell lines, including some forms of leukemia
- **Traditional use:** In Chinese medicine, used to treat spleen disorders

## Mycoremediation Applications

*Agrocybe* species produce **laccase**, an oxide reductase enzyme that gives them a wide spectrum of biodegradative activity. They are known for their ability to break down **endocrine disruptors** like BPA, making them useful candidates for mycofiltration projects targeting hormone-disrupting pollutants in water.
