# Mycorrhizal Symbiosis Mechanisms

Mycorrhizal symbiosis is the mutually beneficial association between fungal
hyphae and plant roots, found in over 90% of terrestrial plant species. This
ancient partnership, dating back over 400 million years, was likely critical
to the colonization of land by plants. The fungus provides soil-derived
minerals and water to the plant, while receiving photosynthetically fixed
carbon in return. Beyond this simple exchange, the symbiosis involves complex
molecular signaling, nutritional trading, and defense coordination.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF)

The most widespread form, involving Glomeromycotina fungi that form
arbuscules (tree-like branched structures) inside root cortical cells.
AMF are obligate biotrophs, entirely dependent on their plant host for
carbon. They associate with the majority of herbaceous plants, many crops,
and some woody species. Key genera include *Rhizophagus*, *Funneliformis*,
and *Gigaspora*. AMF produce vesicles for lipid storage and extensive
extraradical hyphal networks extending far beyond the root zone.

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (EMF)

Predominantly associating with woody plants (especially Pinaceae, Fagaceae,
and Betulaceae), EMF form a dense mantle (sheath) around root tips and a
Hartig net of intercellular hyphae between root cells. They do not penetrate
individual cells. EMF include many basidiomycetes (*Amanita*, *Laccaria*,
*Pisolithus*) and ascomycetes (*Tuber*, *Cenococcum*). EMF are critical for
forest ecosystem functioning and can produce ectomycorrhizal fruiting bodies
(mushrooms and truffles).

### Other Types

- **[[ericoid-mycorrhiza]]**: Association with Ericaceae in acidic, nutrient-poor
  soils; fungi penetrate root cells.
- **[[orchid-mycorrhiza]]**: Obligate symbiosis in which orchid seeds require
  fungal colonization for germination and early nutrition.
- **Ectendomycorrhiza**: Intermediate form with both mantle and intracellular
  penetration, found in some Pinaceae genera.

## Molecular Recognition and Signaling

Plant roots exude strigolactones into the rhizosphere, which stimulate AMF
hyphal branching and metabolism. The fungus responds by producing
Mycorrhizal Lipochitooligosaccharides (Myc-LCOs) and, in some species,
short-chain chitin oligomers (COs). These signals are perceived by plant
LysM receptor-like kinases, triggering a symbiotic signaling cascade involving
calcium spiking, activation of the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase
CCaMK, and downstream transcription factors (CYCLOPS, RAM1).

The common symbiosis signaling pathway (CSSP) is shared between mycorrhizal
and rhizobial symbioses, reflecting their evolutionary origin in a common
ancestor with the early land plants.

## Nutrient Exchange Mechanisms

### Phosphorus Uptake

AMF dramatically enhance plant phosphorus acquisition through thin hyphae
(2-5 μm diameter) that access soil pores inaccessible to roots. Inorganic
phosphate is taken up by fungal phosphate transporters, incorporated into
polyphosphate chains, and transported through the hyphae to the arbuscule,
where it is released to the plant. Plant phosphate transporters (PT4 family)
at the periarbuscular membrane mediate uptake into the plant cell.

### Nitrogen Transfer

[[fungal-hyphae]] absorb ammonium and amino acids from soil organic matter and
transfer nitrogen to the plant, often as arginine or glutamine. In EMF,
proteins and other organic nitrogen compounds are mobilized through
extracellular enzyme secretion (proteases, peptidases) and the resulting
amino acids are translocated to the Hartig net.

### Carbon Flow

Plants allocate 10-20% of their photosynthetically fixed carbon to
mycorrhizal fungi, primarily as hexoses (glucose, fructose) at the
arbuscule interface. The fungus rapidly converts these to lipids
(triacylglycerols) for storage and transport. This carbon cost is offset
by the improved nutrient acquisition and stress tolerance conferred by the
symbiosis.

## Common Mycorrhizal Networks

Individual mycorrhizal fungi can connect multiple plants simultaneously,
forming common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) that facilitate interplant
resource transfer and signaling. Through CMNs, carbon, nitrogen, and defense
signals can move from established plants to seedlings, and between different
species. The ecological significance of these networks in forest dynamics,
plant community composition, and invasive species success remains an active
area of research.

## Agricultural Applications

Mycorrhizal inoculants are increasingly used in sustainable agriculture to
reduce fertilizer dependency, improve drought tolerance, and enhance soil
structure. However, effectiveness varies with soil conditions, crop species,
and inoculant formulation. High-phosphorus soils can suppress mycorrhizal
colonization, creating a feedback loop that reduces the long-term benefits
of these associations.
