## Foliar Fungi (Leaf-Colonizing Fungi)

Foliar fungi encompass the diverse assemblage of fungal organisms that colonize the surfaces and tissues of living leaves. This group includes saprobes, obligate biotrophic parasites, and opportunistic pathogens spanning multiple taxonomic kingdoms (Fungi and Straminipila). They are among the most conspicuous and ecologically significant components of phyllosphere microbiology, particularly in tropical and subtropical ecosystems where moisture and warmth promote prolific fungal growth on foliage.

## Sooty Molds (Dothideales)

Sooty molds are saprobic ascomycetes within the order Dothideales that form dense, dark, velvety to crusty mats on the surfaces of living leaves and stems. They are especially abundant [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] but occur worldwide wherever honeydew-producing insects are present.

### Ecology and Nutrition

- Sooty molds are non-parasitic and do not directly penetrate host tissue; they grow on the excrement (honeydew) produced by sap-feeding insects, particularly aphids and scale insects.
- They display no host-plant preference — their distribution is governed entirely by the presence of honeydew rather than plant species identity.
- Individual colonies frequently comprise mixed assemblages of 8 or more species co-occurring on the same leaf surface, reflecting the low specificity of these fungi.

### Growth Forms

Two principal growth forms are recognized:

- **Deciduous sooty molds**: Thin, easily detached crusts that peel away from leaf surfaces during dry months. These are the more ephemeral form, closely tied to seasonal insect activity and rainfall patterns.
- **Permanent sooty molds**: Thick, cushion-like, densely adherent crusts that persist on substrates year-round and can accumulate to substantial thickness over successive growing seasons.

### Key Families and Morphology

- **Metacapnodiaceae**: Characterized by distinctive tapering moniliform (bead-like) hyphae that are readily recognizable under the microscope. These hyphae form the structural framework of the colony.
- **Seuratiaceae**: Form dark, gelatinous, lobed thalli that differ markedly in texture and appearance from the Metacapnodiaceae.

### Collection and Study

The standard collection technique for sooty molds is the **colloidal leaf peel method**, which involves applying a solution of collodion or parlodion directly to the colonized leaf surface. As the solution dries, it entraps the superficial fungal mycelium, allowing it to be peeled away as a coherent film for microscopic examination.

### Notable Occurrences

In the Nothofagus (southern beech) forests of New Zealand, [[sooty-mold]] growth can be extraordinarily profuse, with tree trunks covered in dark fungal mats extending up to 9 meters in height. These dramatic accumulations illustrate the sheer biomass that sooty molds can achieve under favorable conditions.

## Black Mildews (Meliolales)

Black mildews are obligate plant parasites belonging to the order Meliolales within the Ascomycota. They form dark, superficial, circular colonies on leaf surfaces and are among the most species-rich groups of foliar fungi, with more than 1,580 described species — approximately 1,400 of which belong to the single genus *Meliola*.

### Morphology and Anatomy

- The mycelium is superficial, dark, and readily visible to the naked eye as discrete circular patches on leaves.
- Hyphae are **hyphopodiate**, bearing specialized attachment structures called **capitate hyphopodia** (head-like, swollen cells that anchor the fungus to the leaf surface).
- *Meliola* species produce distinctive **mycelial setae** — stiff, dark, erect hair-like structures arising from the colony surface.
- Ascospores are dark brown and typically 4–5 celled (septate).

### Host Range and Distribution

- More than 90% of recorded hosts are dicotyledonous angiosperms, but black mildews also colonize monocots, gymnosperms, and pteridophytes.
- The group reaches its greatest diversity in the tropics and is commonly encountered in scrub and open parkland habitats.
- They are notably rare in the dense understory of closed-canopy rain forests, likely due to reduced light and air circulation.
- Black mildews are absent from arid subtropical regions where humidity is insufficient for their development.

### Reproduction

Black mildews lack known asexual (anamorphic) states entirely, reproducing exclusively through sexual ascospore production. This reliance on sexual reproduction may contribute to their high species diversity through sustained genetic recombination.

## Fly-Speck Fungi (Microthyriaceae)

Fly-speck fungi belong to the family Microthyriaceae within the order Microthyriales. They are named for their appearance — small, dark, flattened, shield-shaped ascomata scattered across leaf surfaces resembling fly droppings.

- The family comprises 447 species distributed across 45 genera.
- *Micropeltis* is the largest genus, containing more than 100 species.
- These fungi are found primarily on living leaves in tropical and subtropical regions.
- Ascospores range from uniseptate to multiseptate; the superficial mycelium produces haustoria — specialized feeding structures that penetrate host epidermal cells to extract nutrients.

## Downy Mildews (Oomycota)

Downy mildews are not true fungi but rather plant-parasitic Straminipiles (Oomycota) within the orders Peronosporales and Sclerosporales. Despite their common name, they are among the most economically destructive foliar pathogens worldwide, with more than 300 described species.

- The characteristic sign is white to gray "down" on the lower leaf surface, produced by erect sporangiophores emerging through stomata and bearing sporangia at their branch tips.
- Downy mildews parasitize 54 dicotyledonous families and 2 monocotyledonous families; *Peronospora* is the largest genus (~260 species).
- They are obligate biotrophic parasites with intercellular hyphae and intracellular haustoria for nutrient absorption.
- Free moisture on leaf surfaces is required for sporulation, making them strongly dependent on environmental conditions.
- With the single exception of *Verrucalvus*, downy mildews cannot be grown in pure axenic culture.

## Taphrinales

The order Taphrinales contains a single genus, *Taphrina*, comprising approximately 95 species of biotrophic microfungi. These organisms cause dramatic symptoms including galls, witches' brooms, leaf curl, and fruit distortion.

- *[[taphrina-deformans]]* causes [[peach-leaf-curl]] — severe puckering, reddening, and thickening of peach leaves in spring.
- Asci are produced in a palisade layer arising directly from the host epidermis — *Taphrina* forms no specialized fruiting bodies (ascomata), making it unique among ascomycetes.
- A yeast-like (saprobic) phase allows the fungus to survive on plant surfaces or in soil between infection periods.
- Species are highly host-specific, restricted to a single plant family at most, and almost exclusively temperate in distribution.

## Protomycetales

The Protomycetales are represented primarily by the genus *Protomyces*, with approximately 10 described species. These fungi cause gall formation on plants in the families Apiaceae (umbellifers) and Asteraceae (composites). Overwintering occurs via thick-walled **chlamydospores**, durable resting structures capable of surviving adverse conditions. Unlike many obligate foliar parasites, *Protomyces* species are readily cultured on artificial media, making them comparatively accessible for laboratory study.

## Other Foliar Fungi

### Epiphytic Yeasts and Common Saprobes

The phyllosphere supports a rich community of yeast species, including *Sporobolomyces*, *Rhodotorula*, and *Cryptococcus*. The ubiquitous ascomycete *[[aureobasidium-pullulans]]* is also a common leaf-surface colonist, often appearing as a slimy black coating on senescing foliage.

### Leaf-Spotting Fungi

A number of genera are routinely associated with leaf spots on a wide range of hosts:

- *Alternaria*, *Ascochyta*, *Cercospora*, *Cladosporium*, *Corynespora*, *Phyllosticta*, and *Pestalotia* are among the most common leaf-spotting fungi encountered globally.
- These fungi range from opportunistic saprobes colonizing senescent tissue to aggressive necrotrophic pathogens causing significant foliar disease.

### Temperate Tree Foliage Parasites

On the foliage of temperate forest trees, several genera are particularly important:

- *Coccomyces*, *Discula*, *Gnomonia*, *Mycosphaerella*, *Rhytisma*, and *Venturia* are dominant parasites of deciduous tree leaves.
- *Rhytisma* species cause tar spot on maple leaves, forming conspicuous black stromatic lesions.
- *Venturia inaequalis* causes apple scab, one of the most economically important foliar diseases of temperate fruit crops.

### Tropical Pathogens

*Pyricularia grisea* (rice blast) is among the most devastating foliar diseases [[termite-and-ant-ecology-in-tropical-agriculture]], causing extensive yield losses in rice production worldwide.

### Needle-Cast Fungi on Conifers

Conifer needles are colonized by specialized ascomycetes that cause needle-cast diseases, resulting in premature needle abscission:

- Key genera include *Lophodermium*, *Lophodermella*, *Elytroderma*, *Hypodermella*, and *Lirula*.
- These fungi are of considerable importance in forestry, as severe infections can reduce growth and increase tree mortality in managed and natural conifer stands.

## Leaf Surface Microhabitat

The two surfaces of a leaf provide markedly different environments for fungal colonization:

- The **abaxial (lower) leaf surface** typically supports greater fungal growth and diversity than the adaxial (upper) surface. This asymmetry is attributable to two factors: the abaxial surface is more sheltered from direct sunlight and desiccation, and it bears a higher density of stomata, which provide entry points for pathogens and create favorable microenvironments.
- The **adaxial surface**, with its thicker cuticle, greater exposure to ultraviolet radiation, and more rapid drying, supports a comparatively depauperate fungal community.
