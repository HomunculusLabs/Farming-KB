# Hard Times Gardening Philosophy and Self-Sufficiency

## Overview

Steve Solomon's *Gardening When It Counts* is written explicitly
for gardeners facing economic hardship, resource scarcity, or
societal disruption. Drawing from decades of personal experience —
including years of near-total food self-sufficiency in the 1980s
and retirement to remote Tasmania — Solomon argues that the intensive
raised-bed methods promoted by most gardening media are unsuitable
for hard-times conditions and advocates a return to wider-spacing,
lower-input approaches.

## The Coming Hard Times

Solomon identifies several converging forces creating a "global
river of change":

**Energy scarcity:** Civilization will soon base itself on something
other than oil, or won't have much civilization left. Everything made
with oil will cost more: gasoline, food, clothing, transportation,
heating. Chemical and many [[coleman-british-adoption-of-french-intensive-gardening]] (close spacing,
deep digging, heavy fertilization, raised beds) became fashionable.
Solomon himself was an advocate, founding Territorial Seed Company
in 1979 and writing books recommending [[coleman-seed-selection-and-variety-trials]]: when plants are spaced
far enough apart for each to develop full potential, the results
were surprising:
- Well-separated plants needed far less irrigation than intensive
  beds
- Individual vegetables grew much larger
- Taste was better than crowded vegetables
- Many species yielded more per unit area, not less as intensive
  gurus claimed

The key insight: our vegetable-growing ancestors knew how to grow
food with little or no irrigation. In most [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]],
vegetables can be grown with minimal water if properly spaced.

## Land Requirements

Producing a significant portion of your food requires a fair bit of
land — not acres, but more than a few postage-stamp beds in a tiny
backyard. Solomon references the British allotment system: during
World War II, every council was required to provide an allotment to
any resident who requested one. By law, each plot had to be at least
300 square yards (2,700 square feet or about 250 square meters).
Some people took two plots — one for vegetables, one for small fruit.

This established a historical benchmark: 250+ square meters is
considered the minimum area needed for meaningful household food
production. Complete family food self-sufficiency requires
considerably more land.

blackberries picked in summer and frozen, and frozen bananas bought
