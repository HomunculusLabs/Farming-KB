# Hard Times Gardening Philosophy and Self-Sufficiency

## Overview

Steve Solomon's *Gardening When It Counts* is written explicitly
for gardeners facing economic hardship, resource scarcity, or
societal disruption. Drawing from decades of personal experience —
including years of near-total food self-sufficiency in the 1980s
and retirement to remote Tasmania — Solomon argues that the intensive
raised-bed methods promoted by most gardening media are unsuitable
for hard-times conditions and advocates a return to wider-spacing,
lower-input approaches.

## The Coming Hard Times

Solomon identifies several converging forces creating a "global
river of change":

**Energy scarcity:** Civilization will soon base itself on something
other than oil, or won't have much civilization left. Everything made
with oil will cost more: gasoline, food, clothing, transportation,
heating. Chemical and many organic fertilizers are petroleum or
natural gas products, making them increasingly expensive.

**Economic pressure:** Globalization will continue reducing real
purchasing power of working people in industrialized nations.
Attempts to protect domestic labor from Chinese or Indian competition
will prove futile. People will have less to spend while costs rise.

**Water scarcity:** Rural well flow declining as more households pump
from the same aquifer. Watersheds degrading, reducing groundwater
recharge. Electricity costs rising, making pumping more expensive.
Municipal water treatment and pumping are energy-dependent.

**Soil fertility limits:** Intensive raised-bed systems require large
quantities of manure, compost, and fertilizer that may become
unavailable or unaffordable. The intensive approach demands more time
and effort than the slightly increased yield justifies.

## The Intensive vs. Wide-Spacing Debate

During the 1970s fat years, intensive gardening (close spacing,
deep digging, heavy fertilization, raised beds) became fashionable.
Solomon himself was an advocate, founding Territorial Seed Company
in 1979 and writing books recommending intensive methods.

His conversion came through variety trials: when plants are spaced
far enough apart for each to develop full potential, the results
were surprising:
- Well-separated plants needed far less irrigation than intensive
  beds
- Individual vegetables grew much larger
- Taste was better than crowded vegetables
- Many species yielded more per unit area, not less as intensive
  gurus claimed

The key insight: our vegetable-growing ancestors knew how to grow
food with little or no irrigation. In most temperate climates,
vegetables can be grown with minimal water if properly spaced.

## Land Requirements

Producing a significant portion of your food requires a fair bit of
land — not acres, but more than a few postage-stamp beds in a tiny
backyard. Solomon references the British allotment system: during
World War II, every council was required to provide an allotment to
any resident who requested one. By law, each plot had to be at least
300 square yards (2,700 square feet or about 250 square meters).
Some people took two plots — one for vegetables, one for small fruit.

This established a historical benchmark: 250+ square meters is
considered the minimum area needed for meaningful household food
production. Complete family food self-sufficiency requires
considerably more land.

## Solomon's Personal Experience

From mid-1980 through mid-1983, most of Solomon's household food was
homegrown vegetables, supplemented by apples from an old orchard,
blackberries picked in summer and frozen, and frozen bananas bought
as overripes at bargain prices. Purchased food was minimal: occasional
brown rice or millet, sometimes a chunk of ordinary cheese or milk,
olive oil, and a few items he couldn't grow.

When seed germination dropped below ethical selling levels, he cooked
the seeds. The nutritional quality of this diet was exceptional —
superior to what money could buy at the store.

## The Seed Company Perspective

As a seedsman who founded Territorial Seed Company, Solomon provides
unique insight into the seed trade. An honest seed business does not
sell just any variety recommended by Somebody Else — it decides what
to sell after testing numerous varieties in trials. Trials require
wide spacing so each plant develops fully, which is how Solomon
discovered the flaws of intensive spacing.

He warns that seedling raisers and mass-market picture-packet seed
purveyors are not always ethical. The successful home gardener must
start with strong seeds and truly healthy transplants of dependable,
productive varieties from reputable suppliers.

## The Gardening Grandfather

Solomon positions himself as the gardening grandfather most readers
never had — someone who grew up on a farm, who sharpened shovels
and hoes, who knew the earth. Garden magazines and centers promote
appealing merchandise as essential, but successful vegetable gardening
requires only a few hand tools, properly maintained and properly used.

## Krasil'nikov and Soil Biology

Solomon references Russian soil researcher Krasil'nikov's work on
"biologically active substances" — organic chemicals produced by
the soil ecology that plants absorb and benefit from. Plants grown
in biologically active, mineral-balanced soil contain more
vitamins, minerals, amino acids, and other nutritional compounds.
This understanding underpins the COF approach: the goal is not
merely maximizing yield but maximizing the nutritional quality of
food produced per unit of land and labor.

## Practical Philosophy

The book's philosophy is fundamentally practical: don't follow
trends, don't buy unnecessary equipment, do start with good seeds,
do understand your soil, do space plants widely enough, and do
accept that gardening when it counts means gardening for results
rather than for aesthetic satisfaction. The methods are designed
for people who need a good result and can't afford costly mistakes.
