# Levelling Tools and Techniques

Levelling is a foundational skill in permaculture earthworks, essential for designing and constructing spillways, drains, swales, dam walls, house pads, and gravity-fed water systems. Accurate levelling ensures that water flows where intended, structures remain stable, and earthworks function efficiently over time. Bill Mollison emphasises that many levelling tasks on a permaculture site can be accomplished with simple, low-cost tools — though professional surveyors remain valuable for complex or large-scale projects.

## Mass Movement and Slope Stability

Before undertaking any earthworks, designers must understand the types of **mass movement** that can destabilise slopes. Mollison catalogues these as part of the broader context of working safely with earth:

- **Avalanche** — rapid mass movement of snow, ice, rock, or soil down a slope
- **Landslide** — general term for the downslope movement of rock and soil under gravity
- **Slump** — rotational movement along a curved failure surface, common on saturated clay slopes
- **Rock fall** — free fall of detached rock from cliffs or steep faces
- **Rock slide** — rapid sliding of bedrock along a planar surface
- **Talus shift** — movement of accumulated debris at the base of cliffs
- **Rock creep** — very slow, imperceptible downslope movement of rock material
- **Soil creep** — gradual downslope movement of soil, driven by freeze-thaw cycles, wetting-drying, and gravity
- **Frost heave** — upward displacement of soil caused by expansion of ice lenses within the ground
- **Debris flow** — fast-moving mixture of water, rock, soil, and organic matter
- **Earth flow** — slow to moderate flow of water-saturated soil on gentle slopes
- **Mud flow** — rapid flow of very wet soil, typically after heavy rainfall
- **Soil flow** — viscous flow of saturated fine-grained soil
- **Quick clays / quick sands** — sensitive soils that liquefy catastrophically when disturbed, particularly during earthquakes

Understanding these failure modes informs safe placement of structures, appropriate drainage to prevent saturation, and correct grading of slopes before earthworks begin.

## Water as the Ancient Levelling Reference

Water has served as the fundamental levelling reference for millennia. It always finds its own level regardless of the shape of its container, making it an ideal reference surface. All of the simple levelling tools described below exploit this principle in one form or another.

## The Hose ("Bunyip") Level

The **bunyip level** (also called a water level) is perhaps the most versatile and widely used low-cost levelling tool in permaculture. It works on the principle that water in a connected hose will seek the same level at both ends regardless of the terrain between them.

### Materials Required

- 20 metres of clear plastic hose (allowing work across wide features such as dams and swales)
- Two corks with small breather holes drilled through them
- Two cork balls (to plug the hose ends and prevent spillage during transport)
- Two graduated or marked stakes
- Clamps (to secure the hose to the stakes)

### Preparation

1. Fill the hose completely with water, ensuring no air bubbles remain trapped inside — bubbles introduce inaccuracies
2. Insert a cork with breather hole into each end of the hose
3. Seal the ends with cork balls to prevent water loss while moving the level around the site

### Use

1. Remove the cork balls from both ends
2. Place one end of the hose against a marked stake at the starting point of the survey
3. Extend the hose to the second point and hold the other end against the second stake
4. Allow the water to settle — it will equalise at the same height on both stakes
5. Read the water level on each stake; if both readings are identical, the two points are at the same elevation
6. For drains, the desired fall can be set — e.g., a 1:500 fall means a 1-metre drop over 500 metres of distance

The bunyip is especially useful for surveying drains, checking contour lines across swales, and setting outlet levels for dams and tanks.

## The Plane Table

The **plane table** is a slightly more sophisticated levelling device that provides both a level reference and a sighting mechanism. It consists of a tripod-mounted surface fitted with a U-shaped tube (or a flat glass plate) and a bowl of water with a floating sight piece.

### Setup and Use

1. Set the tripod on firm ground and adjust legs until the U-tube (or water bowl) indicates level
2. The floating sight piece in the water bowl provides a true horizontal sight line
3. Sight across to a target stake to read elevation differences

### Checking Accuracy

To verify the plane table is reading correctly:

1. Place two stakes a known distance apart on level ground
2. Take a reading from the first stake to the second
3. Reverse the instrument and sight back from the second stake to the first
4. The readings should be identical; any discrepancy indicates the instrument needs recalibration

### Use Cases

- Setting siphon levels for transferring water between dams
- Positioning dam outlet pipes at the correct elevation
- Determining tank placement for gravity-fed water distribution systems

## The Long Inclinometer

The **Long Inclinometer** is a tractor-mounted bubble level used for larger-scale earthworks. It consists of a long spirit level attached to a tractor or machine, allowing the operator to read slope angles directly while moving across the landscape. This tool is particularly useful during the construction phase of earthworks, where the operator needs to maintain a consistent grade over long distances while operating heavy machinery.

## The "A" Frame Level

The **A-frame level** is the simplest of all levelling tools and is easily constructed from scrap materials. It is widely used for **contour pegging** — marking lines of equal elevation across a slope.

### Construction

1. Take two straight legs of equal length and nail them together at the apex to form an "A" shape
2. Attach a horizontal cross-piece between the two legs at a convenient working height
3. Suspend a plumb bob from the apex so that it hangs freely, crossing the cross-piece

### Finding the Level Mark

1. Place the A-frame on a roughly level surface with both feet on the ground
2. Allow the plumb bob to settle and mark where the string crosses the cross-piece
3. Reverse the A-frame (swap the positions of the two legs) on the same spot
4. Allow the plumb bob to settle again and mark the second crossing point
5. The true level mark is the point exactly halfway between the two marks

### Contour Pegging

Once calibrated, the A-frame is used to walk across a slope, placing pegs at regular intervals wherever the plumb bob string aligns with the level mark. The resulting line of pegs marks a contour — a line of constant elevation — which serves as the guide for swale construction, terracing, and planting.

## Hiring Surveyors vs. Using Simple Tools

Mollison offers practical guidance on when to engage a professional surveyor versus relying on simple tools:

- **Simple tools** (bunyip, A-frame, plane table) are sufficient for most on-farm earthworks including swales, small dams, drains, and house pads
- **Professional surveyors** are warranted for large-scale developments, legal boundary surveys, complex engineering structures, and situations requiring precise documentation
- Simple tools have the advantage of being inexpensive, immediately available, and — critically — they help the designer develop an intimate, hands-on understanding of the land's topography
- The process of pegging contours with an A-frame forces the designer to walk the land repeatedly, noticing subtleties of slope, soil, and vegetation that a surveyor's report might obscure
