# Holmgren Ecological Footprint Analysis

Updated: 2026-04-19

David Holmgren discusses ecological footprint analysis in *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002) as a practical tool for measuring total environmental impact, alongside the more rigorous but less accessible EMERGY analysis methodology.

## Two Methodologies Compared

Holmgren references two primary tools for quantifying sustainable development:

1. **Ecological Footprint** — developed by Mathis Wackernagel, this model measures total environmental impact in terms of the land area required to sustain consumption patterns. A household calculation spreadsheet is available from Redefining Progress.

2. **EMERGY Analysis** — developed by Howard T. Odum, this measures all available energy used in making a product, expressed in units of one type of energy. Written in capitals to avoid confusion with ordinary energy, EMERGY is a contraction of "embodied energy."

Holmgren notes that the Ecological Footprint model "is not as rigorous as EMERGY analysis but is much more easily applied and is gaining popular currency."

## How Ecological Footprint Works

The ecological footprint method converts all consumed resources to a figure representing the area of land required to generate those resources and dispose of the wastes. Like all environmental accounting methods, it depends on calculations using regional or national data and relies on assumptions that simplify complex relationships. It has gone through several cycles of refinement and is being widely applied to measuring the total environmental impact of nations and households.

Comparative figures for all countries show a global average of 2.9 hectares of productive land being used to support each person, although only 2.2 hectares are available. In other words, humanity is eating into natural capital. Close to the top in consumption are the United States, at 12.2 hectares per person, and Australia, at 8.5 hectares per person.

## The Melliodora Example

Using a spreadsheet for calculating household footprint, at Melliodora the Holmgren household uses 3.1 hectares per person. Besides confirming that it is possible to live in an affluent country and not feel deprived while consuming little more than one-third the Australian average, the interactive nature of the spreadsheet allowed them to identify the sensitivity of the index to various aspects of lifestyle and consumption patterns.

## Costa Rica Modeling

Holmgren references modeling of Costa Rica using ecological footprint analysis, which suggested that approximately 80% of the population could be sustainably supported through permaculture design strategies. However, EMERGY analysis of the same country suggested only 53% could be supported.

This demonstrates the power of footprint analysis to show what is possible when we redesign systems for lower impact, while also showing that EMERGY accounting generally provides a deeper level of challenge to current conceptions of sustainability.

## Application to Ethics

The ecological footprint connects to the permaculture ethic of [[holmgren-permaculture-ethics-foundations]]. It provides a concrete way to "Set Limits to Consumption and Reproduction" by translating abstract environmental impact into understandable land-area terms. Holmgren uses it as a tool for setting limits and monitoring performance in the ethical framework.

The issue of population growth is a vexed one with many different perspectives. The world is probably already overpopulated for the long-term good of humanity and other species. The ecological footprint is one relatively simple method to audit and reorganise our personal demand on natural resources.

## Connection to Individual Responsibility

Holmgren uses these tools to support his argument that permaculture designers use careful observation and thoughtful interaction to reduce the need for both repetitive manual labour and for non-renewable energy and high technology. Traditional agriculture was labour intensive, industrial agriculture is energy intensive, and permaculture-designed systems are information and design intensive.

From an ethical perspective, Holmgren emphasizes the need to focus on what is appropriate for us to do, rather than on what others should do. This is especially important in a world divided between rich and poor nations and people.

## EMERGY vs. Footprint: Complementary Tools

Although EMERGY accounting may provide a more severe message about environmental cost than the Ecological Footprint, it also has a positive aspect in that it reorganises our understanding of benefits, redefining what we mean by wealth and work. By redesigning both sides of the ledger, it allows us to better distinguish between productive use of natural resources and wasteful ones.

Holmgren's interpretation of EMERGY accounting is that it reinforces the proactive developmental perspective of permaculture rather than the impact minimisation that lies behind ecological footprint accounting and the environmental mainstream.

## Proactive vs. Reactive Approaches

This distinction between proactive development and reactive impact minimisation is crucial. The ecological footprint tends to focus on reducing harm, while permaculture's EMERGY-informed approach focuses on building productive systems. Both perspectives are valuable, but the proactive orientation aligns better with [[holmgren-catch-and-store-energy-detailed]] and the principle of creating rather than merely conserving.

## Critique of Current Approaches

The tools reveal that in modern affluent societies, energy flow has become so reliably available that energy capture — and even more so, energy storage — has ceased to be a major concern. People rely on money to buy, and purchasing power depends on credit dependent on permanent employment. Economic rationalism has led to the decline of large-scale stores of food, fuels, materials, essential spare parts, increasing vulnerability to disruption and disaster.

## Household Application

The ecological footprint concept reinforces the need to rebuild [[holmgren-household-energy-storages]] and local self-reliance. In an emerging low-energy world, people will rediscover opportunities to harvest and store immediately available renewable energies and wasted resources across rural and urban landscapes, households, and local economies.

## EMERGY Yield Ratio

One application of EMERGY accounting is the calculation of EMERGY yield ratio. This compares the EMERGY (inherent value) of a resource with the feedback of EMERGY from the economy required to produce that resource. A value greater than 1 indicates a net gain to the economy in EMERGY. A value over 4 is a high-value source comparable to many current resources fuelling the economy.

Studies of biomass fuels show that annual crops have yield ratios little better than 1, while wood plantations yield 1.5 to 4, and 300-year-old rainforest yields 12. The more the work is left to nature, the greater the net yield but the longer the time required.
