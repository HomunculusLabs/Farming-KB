# Psilocybe Diversity in Mexico

Mexico stands as the global epicenter of neurotropic fungal diversity,
harboring more species of psychoactive mushrooms than any other country on
Earth. With 76 total species of neurotropic fungi and 44 species and varieties
of Psilocybe (representing 39 percent of the world total), Mexico occupies a
uniquely important position in the study of these remarkable organisms. This
extraordinary diversity is closely linked to Mexico's complex topography, varied
climate zones, and its position as a biological crossroads between the
northern and southern hemispheres.

## Species Richness Overview

Mexico's 76 neurotropic fungal species represent approximately 35 percent of
the 216 species recognized worldwide by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998). Of
these, 44 belong to the genus Psilocybe, making Mexico the undisputed center
of Psilocybe diversity globally. For comparison, the entire United States and
Canada together have only 21 neurotropic taxa of Psilocybe, and Europe has
just 14. This disparity is all the more remarkable considering that North
America and Europe have been far more intensively studied mycologically than
Mexico.

Beyond Psilocybe, Mexico's neurotropic mycota includes species of Amanita
(A. muscaria, A. pantherina), Claviceps (C. purpurea), Conocybe (C.
siligineoides, C. smithii), Copelandia (C. cyanescens, C. tropicalis),
Gymnopilus (several species), Hypholoma (H. naematoliformis), Inocybe, Panaeolus,
and Panaeolina, as well as Cordyceps species and various gasteromycetes used
in traditional ceremonies.

## Vegetation Zones and Fungal Distribution

The distribution of neurotropic Psilocybe species in Mexico is strongly
correlated with vegetation type. Of the 42 neurotropic Psilocybe species
reported in Guzmán's 1983 monograph, 34 (81 percent) are found in mesophytic
(humid montane) forests, 4 in tropical forests, and 4 in coniferous forests.
This pattern holds even though coniferous forests have been more intensively
collected and studied than other vegetation types in Mexico.

Mesophytic forests, characterized by high humidity, moderate temperatures,
and rich understory vegetation, provide ideal conditions for Psilocybe species.
These forests are found in mountainous regions throughout central and southern
Mexico, particularly in the states of Oaxaca, Puebla, Veracruz, Hidalgo, and
Chiapas. The cloud forests of these regions harbor the greatest concentration
of neurotropic Psilocybe species in the world.

Tropical lowland forests, though less species-rich for Psilocybe, harbor
important species such as Psilocybe cubensis and Copelandia cyanescens in
pasture and disturbed areas. The coniferous forests of the high mountains,
particularly those dominated by Pinus species, support species such as
Psilocybe aztecorum at elevations up to 4,000 meters.

## Mexico as a Biogeographical Crossroads

Mexico occupies a unique biogeographical position where the mycofloras of
northern and southern hemispheres converge. This conjunction of northern and
southern mycobiotas is particularly evident in the genus Psilocybe. Northern
species such as P. caerulipes, common in the deciduous forests of eastern
North America, reach northeastern Mexico (Zacualtipan, Hidalgo) through
continuous mesophytic forest corridors. Meanwhile, southern species such as
P. yungensis and P. subyungensis, common in South America, extend northward
into Mexico through mesophytic forests associated with Alnus (alder).

This pattern of north-south convergence makes Mexico a zone of extraordinary
fungal biodiversity. Similar distribution patterns have been observed in other
genera. For example, Pleurotus hirtus, a South American species, reaches
Mexico in tropical forests, while P. levis, a North American species, occurs
in Mexican mesophytic forests. The result is a country where species from both
hemispheres coexist and interact.

## Key Regions of Neurotropic Fungal Diversity

### Oaxaca

The state of Oaxaca is the single most important region for neurotropic fungi
in Mexico, and arguably in the world. It is home to numerous indigenous groups
(Mazatec, Mixtec, Chinantec, Zapotec, Trique, and others) with well-documented
traditions of sacred mushroom use. Oaxaca harbors species such as Psilocybe
caerulescens, P. mexicana, P. zapotecorum, P. muliercula, P. hoogshagenii, and
many others. The Mazatec region of the Sierra Mazateca is where Wasson's
famous 1955 velada ceremony with curandera Maria Sabina took place, sparking
worldwide scientific interest in these fungi.

### Puebla and Hidalgo

The states of Puebla and Hidalgo contain important mesophytic forest regions
with diverse Psilocybe populations. Psilocybe caerulipes reaches its
southernmost known distribution in Hidalgo. The Nevado de Toluca region
(straddling the borders of the State of Mexico and Michoacan) is notable for
the traditional use of Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides together
with Psilocybe muliercula.

### Chiapas

Chiapas, in southern Mexico, supports tropical and subtropical neurotropic
species including several endemics. Psilocybe chiapanensis is known only from
this state. The tropical lowlands of Chiapas provide habitat for Psilocybe
cubensis and related species.

### Veracruz

Veracruz, with its diverse topography from tropical lowlands to cloud forests,
supports numerous neurotropic species. Its Gulf of Mexico slope position
provides abundant rainfall ideal for Psilocybe fruiting.

## Endemic Species

Mexico has a remarkably high number of endemic neurotropic fungal species,
many known from only a single locality or a small geographic area. Endemic
Psilocybe species include P. muliercula, P. chiapanensis, P. laurae, P.
banderiliensis, P. barrerae, P. herrerae, P. jaliscana, P. isabelae, P.
jacobsii, P. rzedowskii, P. uxpanapensis, P. veraecrucis, P. villarrealii,
P. wassoniorum, P. weldenii, P. wrightii, and P. xalapensis. Conocybe
siligineoides is also considered endemic, known from only a single collection
by Wasson in Oaxaca in 1955; despite extensive subsequent fieldwork, this
species has never been re-collected.

Hypholoma naematoliformis is another Mexican endemic. The concentration of
endemic species underscores the importance of Mexican forests for fungal
conservation.
