# Allegro's End of a Road and Personal Motivation

The question of what motivated [[john-allegro]] to write
[The [[sacred-mushroom-and-the-cross]] (1970)
is arguably more interesting than the arguments contained in the book itself.
Allegro's companion volume, The End of a Road (1970), published almost
unnoticed in the same year, provides crucial evidence for understanding the
personal crisis that drove him to produce his most controversial work.

## Allegro's Early Career

John Marco Allegro (1923-1988) was a man of genuine scholarly talent. He
was appointed as a Lecturer in [[allegro-old-testament-figures-as-fertility-deities]] Studies at Manchester University
and, most significantly, was selected as the only British scholar on the
international team assembled to examine the newly discovered Dead [[dead-sea-scrolls-entheogenic-reading]]
in 1947.

This appointment was a mark of considerable distinction. The Dead Sea Scrolls
were the most important biblical manuscript discovery of the twentieth
century, and being included in the editorial team placed Allegro at the
centre of one of the most exciting developments in biblical scholarship.

## Conflict with the Church

While working on the Scrolls, Allegro came into increasing conflict with the
Church establishment. He grew impatient with the slow pace of work and urged
his colleagues to make their findings available to the public. The scholarly
team, dominated by Catholic priests, preferred a more cautious approach.

The Church had particular reasons for caution. The Scrolls were believed at the
time to be a "missing link" between Judaism and Christianity. If Christianity
had merely evolved out of Judaism, this would undermine the whole
philosophical foundation of Christian claims to uniqueness, namely that Christ
was the incarnation of God, bringing with him the New Covenant that represented
a sudden and decisive rupture with the Old.

These worries eventually proved unfounded: the Scrolls did not form a missing
link as such. But the Church's demands for caution [frustrated Allegro, who responded by
publishing his own popular book, The Dead Sea Scrolls (1956), against the
wishes of his colleagues.

The book sold some 250,000 copies and became the one book most people read on
the subject. It triggered a flurry of outraged letters in The Times.
Increasingly seen as a "loose cannon," Allegro was marginalised within the
academy until eventually he was removed from the Scrolls team altogether.

## The Crisis of Faith

Alongside his professional conflicts, Allegro suffered what appears to have
been a profound and catastrophic loss of faith. He found himself unable to
sustain the belief in the inviolate truth of the Bible that his scholarship
required. The newly emerging textual and archaeological findings created
tensions he could not resolve.
