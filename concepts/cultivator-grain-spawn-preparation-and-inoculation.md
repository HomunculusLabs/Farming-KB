# Grain Spawn Preparation and Inoculation Methods

## Overview

Mushroom spawn is used to inoculate prepared substrates and consists of a carrier
material fully colonized by mushroom mycelium. The type of carrier varies by species,
although rye grain is the choice of most spawn makers. In 1932, Dr. James Sinden
patented a new spawn-making process using cereal grain as the mycelial carrier,
setting the standard for most modern spawn production.

## Historical Development of Spawn

During the 1800s, growers gathered mycelium from natural habitat supplemented
with horse manure. This "virgin spawn" contained many contaminants. Pure culture
techniques then allowed strain selection for the first time, with sterilized
compost as the standard medium until Sinden's grain-based innovation.

## The Advantage of Grain Spawn

The distinct advantage of grain spawn is the increased number of inoculation sites.
Each individual kernel becomes a point from which mycelium can spread. A liter of
rye grain spawn containing approximately 25,000 kernels represents a vast improvement
over inocula transmitted by coarser materials.

## Cereal Grain Options

### Rye (Secale cereale)
Availability, low cost, and ability to separate into individual kernels make it
the recommended choice. Commercial rye has approximately 30-55 kernels per gram
depending on the variety.

### Wheat
Works equally well as rye for spawn making and fruitbody production. Organic wheat
has 34 kernels per gram.

### Millet
Has a higher number of inoculation points (166 kernels/gram) but is more difficult
to formulate. The company Amycel successfully developed a millet-based process.

### Sorghum (Milo)
Has spherical kernels and works relatively well but can be difficult to obtain.
The Stoller Spawn Company has used milo for years.

### Grass Seed (Rye and Wheat)
Have 415-450 kernels per gram, far more than grain. Disadvantages include tendency
to lose moisture and inability to separate into individual kernels. Widely used to
promote sclerotia formation in Psilocybe tampanensis and Psilocybe mexicana.

### Rice
Utilized by few cultivators. Kernels tend to clump together due to the sticky
outer coat, even when balanced to recommended moisture levels.

## The Contamination Challenge

A single gram of commercial rye contains an estimated 50,000-100,000 bacteria,
more than 200,000 actinomycetes, 12,000 fungi, and numerous yeasts. To sterilize
one gram requires destruction of more than 300,000 contaminants. In a spawn jar
with over 100 grams of grain plus water, the cell population soars to astronomical
figures. Bacteria divide every 20 minutes at room temperature, multiplying into
millions within hours.

## Sterilization Requirements

For liquids, standard steam sterilization is 25 minutes at 15 psi (250 F). For
solids like rye, the time must increase to ensure steam penetrates small air
pockets and structural cavities where thermoresistant organisms survive. A full
hour at 15 psi is the minimum recommended for jars of rye grain.

## Spawn Container Selection

The standard spawn container for home cultivators is the quart mason jar while
commercial makers prefer gallon jars. Narrow-mouthed containers are recommended
over wide-mouth because they have less chance of contaminating from airborne
spores due to their smaller openings. The best combination uses synthetic filter
discs with one-piece screw-top lids having a 3/8-1/2 inch hole drilled in center.

## Moisture Management

The optimum moisture content for grain spawn is 49-54%. Commercial rye is
approximately 11% water by mass. Excessive water favors bacterial growth, causes
kernels to explode during sterilization, and makes colonized grain difficult to
break into individual kernels. Balanced moisture spawn easily breaks apart,
ensuring maximum inoculation points.

### Spawn Formulas

**Quart Jars:** 1 cup rye grain + 2/3 to 3/4 cup water (240ml grain, 170-200ml
water)

**Half Gallon Jars:** 3 cups rye grain + 1.75 cups water (600ml grain, 400-460ml
water)

Chalk (CaCO3) and gypsum (CaSO4) can be added at 1-3 parts per 100 parts grain
(dry weight) in a 1:4 ratio. These are optional for most species but necessary
for Agaricus brunnescens. Add 10% more water when using calcium buffers.

## Agar-to-Grain Inoculation

1. Select vigorously growing culture covering no more than 3/4 of agar surface
2. Avoid cultures that have entirely overrun the dish (contaminants often enter
   along the margin)
3. Flame sterilize scalpel, cut triangular wedge of mycelium-covered agar
4. Transfer quickly to jar, exposing grain for minimal time
5. A petri dish 2/3 covered should inoculate 6-8 quart jars (maximum 10-12)
6. The "double wedge" technique (cutting one wedge in half) speeds colonization
7. Replace lids and shake jars thoroughly to distribute mycelial fragments
8. Incubate at appropriate temperature; grain should be fully colonized in 7-10
   days. A second shake may be necessary on Day 4 or 5

## Grain-to-Grain Transfer

Fully colonized grain masters are used for further spawn production. Masters must
be transferred within a few days of full colonization, otherwise kernels do not
break apart easily. Each quart master can inoculate 6-10 quart jars or 4-6 half
gallon jars, providing enormous expansion of mycelial mass.

## Post-Sterilization Contamination Prevention

Hot jars cooling suck in air with airborne contaminants. An average room has
10,000 particulates per cubic foot versus fewer than 100 in a sterile lab.

1. Clean the pressure cooker exterior before entering the sterile room
2. Inoculate jars immediately after cooling to room temperature -- never leave
