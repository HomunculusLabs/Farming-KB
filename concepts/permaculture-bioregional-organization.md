# Permaculture Bioregional Organization

Updated: 2026-04-18

Bioregional organization is a core principle in permaculture design
that structures human settlements and governance according to natural
ecological boundaries rather than political or administrative ones.
David Holmgren places bioregionalism at the center of his vision for
sustainable human civilization, arguing that the mismatch between
political boundaries and ecological realities is a primary driver of
environmental destruction.

## Defining the Bioregion

A bioregion is a geographical area defined by natural characteristics
rather than political boundaries. These characteristics include:

- **Watersheds**: The area drained by a river system or catchment
- **Climate zones**: Areas sharing temperature, rainfall, and
  seasonal patterns
- **Geology and soils**: Areas with similar rock types and soil
  formation processes
- **Vegetation communities**: Areas dominated by characteristic
  plant assemblages
- **Landforms**: Topographic features that influence water flow,
  microclimate, and human settlement patterns

The Australian Land Systems approach, developed by the CSIRO,
provides a model for bioregional classification that identifies
repeating patterns of landform, soils, and vegetation across the
landscape. Each land system has characteristic capabilities and
limitations that should inform human use.

## Why Bioregionalism Matters

Holmgren argues that bioregional organization is essential because:

1. **Ecological feedback**: When political boundaries match ecological
   boundaries, the consequences of environmental management decisions
   (or mismanagement) are felt by the same people who made them. This
   creates accountability and incentive for sustainable management

2. **Appropriate scale**: Bioregional boundaries define a scale at
   which people can understand and manage their resource base. The
   watershed scale is particularly appropriate because it defines a
   complete water cycle

3. **Local adaptation**: Each bioregion has unique characteristics
   that require locally adapted solutions. Generic, centralized
   approaches fail because they ignore bioregional specificity

4. **Resilience**: Bioregional self-reliance creates resilience
   against disruptions to global supply chains and energy systems

## Governance and Decision-Making

Bioregional organization implies a fundamental restructuring of
governance. Rather than top-down administration from distant capitals,
decisions about land use, water management, and economic development
should be made at the bioregional scale by people who live within
the bioregion and directly experience its ecological conditions.

Holmgren envisions bioregional governance as:

- **Participatory**: All residents of a bioregion have a voice in
  decisions that affect their shared ecological home
- **Adaptive**: Management strategies evolve based on ecological
  feedback and monitoring
- **Precautionary**: Decisions err on the side of protecting
  ecological function when outcomes are uncertain
- **Incremental**: Change happens through small, observable steps
  rather than grand plans imposed from above

## Australian Land Systems as Model

The CSIRO's Australian Land Systems classification provides a
practical model for bioregional organization. Each land system is
defined by its characteristic pattern of landform, soil, and
vegetation, and each has documented capabilities and limitations for
various land uses. This systematic approach allows for rational
planning that respects ecological realities.

Holmgren advocates extending this approach to all aspects of
bioregional planning: not just land use capability but also water
management, settlement patterns, economic activity, and cultural
expression.

## Bioregional Economy

A bioregional economy is one that operates primarily within the
ecological limits and capacities of its bioregion. Key principles:

- **Local production for local consumption**: Food, building
  materials, and energy are produced within the bioregion wherever
  possible
- **Bioregional trade surplus**: Specialized products are exported
  only after local needs are met, creating genuine surplus rather
  than export-dependent monoculture
- **Internal energy capture**: Energy needs are met from renewable
  sources within the bioregion (solar, wind, biomass, hydro)
- **Closed-loop material cycles**: Waste products become inputs for
  other processes within the bioregion

## Cultural Identity

Holmgren links bioregionalism to cultural identity and sense of
place. When people identify with their bioregion -- its landscapes,
its seasons, its native plants and animals -- they develop a
stewardship ethic that transcends short-term economic calculations.
The bioregion becomes not just a resource base but a source of
cultural meaning and belonging.

## Transition Strategies

Moving toward bioregional organization requires:

1. **Bioregional mapping**: Documenting the ecological
   characteristics and boundaries of local bioregions
2. **Bioregional education**: Teaching people about their local
   ecology and its management implications
3. **Local institution building**: Creating organizations that
   operate at the bioregional scale
4. **Economic localization**: Developing local production capacity
   for essential goods and services
5. **Networking**: Connecting bioregional organizations into larger
   cooperative networks
