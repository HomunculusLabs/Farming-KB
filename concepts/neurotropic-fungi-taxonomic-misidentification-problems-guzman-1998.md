# Neurotropic Fungi: Taxonomic Confusion and Misidentification

## Overview

The taxonomic study of neurotropic fungi has been plagued by persistent
problems of misidentification, taxonomic inconsistency, and unreliable
chemical analyses. Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998) identified these issues
as among the most significant obstacles to accurate knowledge of the
diversity, distribution, and chemistry [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] from Venezuelan specimens identified as this
species. Guzmán concluded that the Venezuelan material almost certainly
belongs to an undescribed neurotropic species that resembles *P. pseudobullacea*
morphologically. The true *P. pseudobullacea* was excluded from the list of
neurotropic fungi.

Similarly, Høiland (1978) reported psilocybin in *P. atrobrunnea*, but
Guzmán determined that Høiland's fungus was probably a different species with
similar appearance, such as *P. coprinifacies* or *P. mairei*, since *P.
atrobrunnea* is not a neurotropic fungus.

## The Panaeolus Antillarum Confusion

*Panaeolus antillarum* is frequently misidentified as *Copelandia* spp. by
recreational users. Both fungi present white fruit bodies and grow together
on cow dung [[conocybe-cyanopus]]* and *C. smithii*, related species, were shown to contain
psilocybin by Benedict et al. (1967). Mantle and Waight (1969) erroneously
reported that this study found psilocybin in *C. siligineoides* rather than
the correct species, perpetuating confusion in the literature.

## Questionable Chemical Reports

Several chemical studies have been identified as doubtful or erroneous:

- **Stropharia cyanea / Psilocybe caerulea** — Bresinsky and Besl (1990)
  considered reports of hallucinogenic principles in these species to be
  doubtful
- **Mycena pura** — Samorini (1989) pointed out that reports of
  psychoactive properties are unreliable
- **Psilocybe bohemica / P. coprinifacies / P. mairei / P. serbica** —
  Krieglsteiner (1984, 1986) considered all of these to be synonyms of
  *P. cyanescens*, but Guzmán and other authors maintain them as distinct
- **Gymnopilus, Rickenella, Hygrocybe** — Stijve and Kuyper (1988)
  did not find psilocybin in several species reported as neurotropic

## The Age Factor in Chemical Analysis

The age of studied specimens is a critical but often overlooked variable.
Repke et al. (1977a) showed variations in baeocystin content according to
specimen age: *P. baeocystis* and *P. cyanescens* showed no trace of indoles
in specimens analyzed 20–60 days after collection.

Mexican Indian knowledge corroborates this: old dried specimens kept
for more than one year are considered "not good" and discarded. Guzmán
observed that normal doses were entirely inactive because specimens had
been dried for almost a year. Ohenoja et al. (1987) detected decreased
psilocybin in herbarium *P. semilanceata* by collection age: 0.014%
(1869), 0.67% (1954), and 0.84% (1976) dry weight.
