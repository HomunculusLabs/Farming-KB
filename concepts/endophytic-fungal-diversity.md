# Endophytic Fungal Diversity

## Overview

Endophytic fungi (endophytes) are fungi that inhabit healthy plant tissues without causing apparent disease symptoms. They represent one of the most diverse yet poorly documented components of [[fungal-biodiversity]]. Found in virtually every plant species examined, endophytes contribute enormously to fungal species richness and may constitute as many as 1 million undescribed species globally. Their ecological roles span the full symbiotic spectrum from protective mutualism to latent pathogenicity.

Higher plants furnish complex, multilayered, spatially and temporally diverse habitats that support species-rich assemblages of microorganisms. Microfungi are dominant components of those assemblages, colonizing foliar and twig surfaces (epiphytes), internal tissues of foliage (foliar endophytes), young and old bark (bark endophytes), and wood (xylem endophytes and wood decomposers). The increasing interest in cryptic occupation of internal tissues of healthy plants by endophytic microfungi has led to a growing awareness that higher plants likely harbor a reservoir of undiscovered fungi.

## Definition and Circumscription

Endophytic fungi colonize living plant tissue without causing any immediate, overt negative effects. This definition encompasses virtually the entire spectrum of symbiotic interactions: parasitism, commensalism, and mutualism. The term originated in the 19th century but its contemporary meaning differs substantially (Large 1940; Carroll 1986). Applications are not always consistent among investigators (Petrini 1991; Wilson 1995b; Saikkonen et al. 1998).

The behavioral differences between many fungi considered "endophytic" and those considered "latent pathogens" are slight and may simply reflect differences in the duration of the latent or quiescent phase and the degree of injury sustained by the host during active fungal growth. Pathogenic fungi capable of symptomless occupation of their hosts during a portion of the infection cycle — "quiescent infections" (Williamson 1994) — and strains with impaired virulence can be considered endophytes (Schardl et al. 1991, 1994; Fisher et al. 1992; Fisher and Petrini 1992; Freeman and Rodriguez 1993), as can a variety of commensal saprobic and mutualistic fungi that have cryptic, nonapparent patterns of host colonization.

A defining feature is episodic growth: fungi exhibit a prolonged, inconspicuous period in which growth and colonization cease temporarily, resuming after a physical or maturational change in the host. Whether they ultimately are considered commensal saprobes, latent pathogens, or protective mutualists, this episodic growth pattern is characteristic of endophytes.

## Taxonomic Breadth

Endophytes span multiple fungal phyla. For grass hosts (Poaceae), endophytes are primarily Neotyphodium anamorphs of Balansiae (Clavicipitaceae) — few species, but extensively colonized. For nongrass hosts, endophytes are taxonomically diverse, many species with limited host ranges, and nonsystemically transmitted via spores.

Key taxonomic groups include Xylariaceae (common in tropical foliage), Hypocreales, Pleosporales, and numerous sterile mycelia that remain unidentified. Molecular approaches (rDNA sequencing) have revealed that conventional culture-based methods dramatically under-sample endophyte diversity, with many taxa failing to sporulate in culture. The proportion of Basidiomycetes reported from tree bark and wood endophyte surveys is generally low, though their true abundance may be underestimated by culture-based methods.

## Global Distribution

Endophytic colonization of land plants by fungi is ubiquitous. Fungal surveys during the past 20 years have demonstrated that endophytic colonization of land plants by fungi is universal. Endophytes are known from:
- Tropical, temperate, and boreal forests
- Arctic and alpine environments (Petrini 1987; Fisher et al. 1995)
- Xeric environments (Mushin and Booth 1987; Mushin et al. 1989)
- Mosses, hepatics, ferns and fern allies (Fisher et al. 1992; Schmid and Oberwinkler 1993)
- Estuarine plants (Salicornia, Spartina, Suada)
- Tropical palms (Rodrigues and Samuels 1992; Fröhlich and Hyde 2000)
- Broad-leaved trees (Arrhenius and Langenheim 1986; Lodge et al. 1995)
- Epiphytes and mistletoes (Dreyfuss and Petrini 1984; Petrini et al. 1990)
- Diverse herbaceous annuals and deciduous/evergreen perennials

Detailed investigations of the internal mycobiota of plants frequently uncover novel taxa and reveal new distributions of known species. Because endophytic infections are inconspicuous, the species diversity is relatively high both within and among individual host species, and a relatively small proportion of potential hosts have been examined.

## Host Specificity and Diversity Patterns

Endophyte diversity varies enormously by host and tissue type. A single conifer needle may harbor several dozen species. Representative species counts from the literature illustrate the scale:

|------|-------------|---------------|----------|-----------|
| Abies alba | Needles | 120 | Switzerland | Sieber-Canavesi and Sieber 1993 |
| Heisteria concinna | Leaves | 242 | Panama | Arnold et al. 2000 |
| Ouratea lucens | Leaves | 259 | Panama | Arnold et al. 2000 |
| Quercus ilex | Twigs, leaves | 149 | Spain | Collado et al. 2000 |
| Juniperus communis | Leaves | 114 | Switzerland | Petrini and Müller 1979 |

| Picea mariana | Roots | 97 | Ontario | Summerbell 1989 |

Tissue specificity is common: different endophyte communities colonize foliage, bark, wood, and roots of the same host. Endophytes may be highly host-specific, and detailed investigations frequently uncover novel taxa. Roughly 80% of species in species-rich fungal groups (endophytes, soil fungi) are widely distributed, leaving only 20% with more limited distributions.

## Ecological Roles

Endophytes produce a remarkable array of biologically active secondary metabolites:
- **Antibacterial/antifungal activity**: More than 30% of endophytic isolates from ericaceous plants showed activity (Fisher et al. 1984b); Dreyfuss (1986) reported antibiotic activity from Pleurophomopsis, Cryptosporiopsis, and a sterile endophyte from Abies alba
- **Insecticidal compounds**: Johnson and Whitney (1994); Hensens et al. (1999)
- **Cytochalasins and indole diterpenes**: Common in Xylariaceae (Dreyfuss 1986; Hensens et al. 1999)
- **Ergot alkaloids**: In systemic grass endophytes, providing herbivore protection
- **Bioactive ensembles**: Strains of Pezicula species (anamorph Cryptosporiopsis) from deciduous and coniferous hosts produce diverse secondary metabolites (Fisher et al. 1984a; Noble et al. 1991; Schulz et al. 1995)
- **Antifungal substances**: Nongrass endophytes produce antifungal (Peláez et al. 2000) or antibacterial substances

The critical unanswered question: are these metabolites produced in planta during quiescent occupation at concentrations sufficient to benefit the host? In vitro production does not guarantee in planta function. In vitro, many of these compounds are intracellular and so, although they may have survival value for the endophyte through interference competition, their general role in protection of living hosts has not yet been determined (Saikkonen et al. 1998).

## Grass Endophytes vs. Nongrass Endophytes

Systemic clavicipitaceous grass endophytes and nonsystemic nongrass endophytes differ fundamentally:

| Characteristic | Grass Endophytes | Nongrass Endophytes |
|---------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Taxonomic group | Clavicipitaceae (Neotyphodium) | Diverse ascomycetes and basidiomycetes |
| Species richness | Few species | Many diverse species |
| Colonization | Systemic, extensive | Restricted, localized |
| Transmission | Seed-transmitted | Spore-transmitted |
| Species per host | One species per host | Multiple species concurrently |
| Sporulation on host | Suppressed | Often seasonal, ephemeral |
| Alkaloid production | Well-documented | Less understood |
| Host range | Cool-season grasses | Broad range of plants |

These should not be regarded as biologically or ecologically homologous (Stone et al. 2000). Much has been published on the highly specific nature of grass-endophyte symbiosis, the effects of fungal alkaloids in infected hosts, and the fitness benefits conferred to host grasses (Clay 1988, 1990, 1994; Saikkonen et al. 1998). Symptomless endophytes of Lolium, Festuca, and other genera of pooid grasses are interspecific hybrid strains derived from Epichloë species that cause partial or complete host sterility (choke disease).

## Transition from Endophytism to Saprotrophy

A large proportion of endophytic fungal species become saprobic on the death of their host — an advantageous strategy because their intimate association with the plant allows immediate utilization of the dead tissue. Furthermore, many endophytic species enter an intermediate necrotrophic stage as the host tissue becomes moribund, allowing even earlier colonization and utilization of energy resources. Nutritional metamorphoses have not been studied adequately, though observations integrating information from endophytes and saprobes are beginning to reveal what are frequently very complex ecological systems.

Many common endophytic fungi represent the earliest developmental stages of necrotrophic or latent pathogens. The frequent occurrence of species typical of plant surfaces among endophytic colonists suggests that many endophytes may originate from the epiphytic mycobiota and subsequently colonize internal tissue. This continuum between commensalism, mutualism, and parasitism presents particular problems for estimating endophytic diversity, as the same fungal genotype may be classified differently depending on when and where it is sampled.

## Research Challenges

The absence or deficiency of basic taxonomic information remains a major obstacle. Fungi that neither grow nor sporulate in culture must be detected by molecular methods. Even with molecular tools, linking environmental sequences to described species remains problematic. Endophytic microfungi typically are present as internal, unseen, microscopic hyphae; their presence is revealed externally only when they sporulate, usually a seasonal and ephemeral event. Detection and quantification generally require selective isolation procedures.

The lack of an easily available source of information on distributions and ecological preferences of endophytic fungal species is yet another major barrier to inventory work. Such a publication would allow surveyors of ecosystems to reduce dramatically the numbers of species with which their taxa should be compared when attempting identifications.

## Endophytes and Global Species Diversity

Investigation of even well-characterized, economically important plants for endophytic fungi frequently yields novel taxa. Studies of endophytic fungi are needed to provide information fundamental for evaluating global fungal diversity and distribution. Given that only a small proportion of potential hosts have been examined, endophytes may represent a substantial number of undiscovered fungi (Stone et al. 1996; Arnold et al. 2000).
