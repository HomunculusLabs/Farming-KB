# Timber Frame Basics
Timber framing is a traditional building method where large timbers are joined together with wooden connections (joinery) rather than nails or metal fasteners. The resulting structural frame is strong, durable, and beautiful, with timbers often left exposed as an architectural feature. Timber frames pair naturally with natural infill materials like straw bales, light straw-clay, and hempcrete.
## History
Timber framing has been practiced for thousands of years across Europe, Asia, and the Americas. Notable traditions include:
- **European half-timber (Fachwerk):** Exposed timber frame with wattle-and-daub or brick infill
- **Japanese post-and-beam:** Complex joinery with minimal metal, often using reclaimed timber
- **New England timber frame:** Brought by English colonists; evolved into the classic American barn
- **British cruck frame:** Large curved timbers (crucks) forming the full height of the building
The craft declined with the introduction of stick framing (dimensional lumber with nails) in the mid-1800s but has seen a strong revival since the 1970s.
## Wood Selection
### Species
Choose locally available species when possible:
- **Oak (white, red):** Traditional choice, extremely strong, rot resistant, takes joinery well. Heavy and hard to work with hand tools.
- **Douglas fir:** Strong, straight-grained, widely available in western North America. Excellent for beams and posts.
- **Eastern white pine:** Light, easy to work, adequate for smaller structures. Less rot resistant — keep out of ground contact.
- **Hemlock:** Strong, rot resistant, widely available in eastern North America. Can be brittle in tension.
- **Cedar (western red, eastern):** Naturally rot resistant, lighter weight. Good for exposed exterior elements.
- **Recycled/reclaimed timber:** Old-growth timbers from demolished buildings, often superior quality to new lumber
### Grading and Sizing
- **Timbers are specified by rough-sawn dimensions:** A "6x8" is actually 6 inches by 8 inches before planing
- **Posts:** Typically 6x6 to 8x8 depending on span and load
- **Beams:** 6x8 to 8x12 or larger for long spans
- **Braces:** 4x4 to 5x5, cut at 45-degree angles
- **Quality criteria:** Straight grain, minimal knots, no rot or insect damage, checks (surface cracks) are acceptable if not structural
### Green vs. Kiln-Dried
- **Green timber:** Fresh cut, higher moisture content. Easier to cut joinery but will shrink as it dries, potentially loosening joints. Use draw-bored pegs to maintain tight joints.
- **Air-dried timber:** Seasoned for 1+ year, reduced shrinkage. Ideal for precision joinery.
- **Kiln-dried timber:** Expensive, may introduce internal stresses. Generally unnecessary for timber framing.
## Essential Joinery
Timber frame joinery uses interlocking wood-to-wood connections secured with wooden pegs (trunnels). Metal fasteners are minimized or eliminated.
### Mortise and Tenon
The fundamental timber frame joint. A rectangular hole (mortise) is cut into one timber, and a corresponding projection (tenon) is cut on the mating timber.
- **Through tenon:** Tenon passes completely through the mortise, visible on the far side. Strong and classic.
- **Blind tenon:** Tenon stops inside the mortise, not visible. Cleaner appearance.
- **Shouldered tenon:** The tenon has a shoulder that seats against the face of the mortised timber, providing bearing surface and alignment.
- **Uses:** Post-to-beam, post-to-plate, brace-to-post, brace-to-beam connections
### Dovetail Joint
A wedge-shaped tenon that locks into a corresponding mortise. The angled shape prevents the joint from being pulled apart under tension.
- **Through dovetail:** Both halves visible — classic and strongest version
- **Half-dovetail (lap dovetail):** One timber is notched, the other is dovetailed into it. Common for connecting joists to beams.
- **Uses:** Floor joists to girders, purlins to plates, tie beams to posts in tension connections
### Other Common Joints
- **Scarf joint:** Joins two timbers end-to-end to create a longer member. Many variations (bladed, keyed, spline, splayed).
- **Bridle joint:** Open mortise and tenon used for temporary bracing during raising, sometimes left as permanent.
- **Tongue and fork:** Used at rafter peaks; each rafter is split (forked) and the opposing rafter's tongue fits into the fork.
- **Birdsmouth:** A notch cut into the underside of a rafter where it sits on the top plate.
### Pegging
- Pegs are typically made from hardwood (oak, locust, black walnut) — 1/2 to 1 inch diameter
- **Draw-boring:** The peg hole in the tenon is drilled slightly offset from the hole in the mortise. Driving the peg through pulls the joint tight, compensating for shrinkage.
- Pegs should be driven in, not hammered brutally — they split if forced.
## Frame Design
### Structural Bents
A bent is a cross-sectional assembly of posts, beams, and braces that forms one structural unit of the frame. Bents are erected one at a time along the length of the building and connected by longitudinal members (plates, girders).
- Bents are typically spaced 12-16 feet apart
- Each bent is assembled on the ground (on sawhorses or a leveling platform)
- Braces are critical — they prevent racking (parallelogram deformation) under lateral loads
### Roof Systems
- **Common rafter:** Rafters bear on plates, ridge, and sometimes purlins
- **Principal rafter and purlin:** Large principal rafters support purlins, which in turn carry common rafters
- **Hammerbeam:** A sophisticated medieval design where short horizontal beams (hammerbeams) project from the wall and support the roof structure
- **King post truss:** A central vertical post with angled struts — simple and efficient for spans up to 30 feet
- **Queen post truss:** Two vertical posts with a connecting tie beam — suitable for longer spans
## Raising the Frame
Raising a timber frame is a community event in traditional building.
### Preparation
- Pre-assemble bents on level ground
- Pre-fit all joints (test assembly)
- Organize rigging: ropes, blocks, come-alongs, or a crane
- Prepare raising points (staking for gin poles or anchor points for guy lines)
- Safety briefing for all participants
### Raising Process
1. **Bents first:** Raise each bent sequentially, plumb it, and brace it temporarily
2. **Connect bents:** Install longitudinal plates, girders, and joists to connect the bents
3. **Roof frame:** Install rafters, ridge, purlins
4. **Final pegging:** Drive pegs after the frame is fully assembled and plumbed
5. **Enclosure:** The frame is now ready for infill or enclosure
### Equipment
- **Hand raising:** Gin poles, ropes, blocks, lots of people. Traditional and satisfying for smaller frames.
- **Crane raising:** Modern approach, faster and safer for large frames. Cost varies by region and crane size.
## Enclosure Systems
A timber frame provides the structure; the enclosure system provides the walls. Popular options for natural builders:
- **Straw bale infill:** Excellent insulation, pairs well with timber frame (see [[straw-bale-construction]])
- **Light straw-clay:** Insulative infill within temporary forms between timbers
- **Hempcrete:** Pack or spray hemp-lime mix around the frame
- **Cob:** Build cob walls between the timbers or use cob as an exterior skin
- **Enclosed timber frame (stick-framed wrap):** Conventional 2x walls built around the outside of the timber frame, insulated with fiberglass, cellulose, or mineral wool
- **Structural insulated panels (SIPs):** Pre-fabricated foam-core panels attached to the outside of the frame
## Tools
### Hand Tools (Traditional)
- **Broad axe and hatchet:** Hewing timbers to dimension
- **Hand saws (rip and crosscut):** Cutting timbers to length
- **Chisels (1/4 inch to 2 inch):** Cutting mortises and tenons
- **Mallet:** Driving chisels — use a wooden mallet to avoid damaging chisel handles
- **Boring machine or brace and bit:** Drilling peg holes and mortises
- **Hand plane (jack and smoothing):** Flattening and finishing surfaces
- **Drawknife and spokeshave:** Shaping curved elements
- **Slick:** Large chisel pushed by hand for paring surfaces flat
### Power Tools (Modern)
- **Chain mortiser:** Efficiently cuts mortises of consistent depth
- **Circular saw with beam-cutting blade:** Rough cutting of timbers
- **Reciprocating saw with long blade:** Trimming tenons
- **Drill press or boring machine:** Clean peg holes
- **Planer (portable or stationary):** Milling rough-sawn timber to consistent dimensions
- **Angle grinder with flap disc:** Cleaning up joints and shaping
