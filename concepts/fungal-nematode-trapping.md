# Fungal Nematode-Trapping Interactions

Nematophagous fungi are a diverse group of fungi that have evolved specialized mechanisms to capture, kill, and digest nematodes. These fungi occupy a unique ecological niche at the interface of fungal and animal kingdoms, functioning as both predators of free-living nematodes and parasites of plant-parasitic nematodes. Their ecological significance spans soil food web regulation, biological control of agricultural pests, and the regulation of animal parasite populations in pastoral ecosystems.

## Trapping Mechanisms

Nematophagous fungi have evolved a remarkable array of mechanisms to capture nematodes. These can be broadly categorized into three groups based on their mode of action: adhesive traps, constricting structures, and toxic secretions.

Adhesive trapping mechanisms include sticky secretions from ordinary hyphae, as seen in some species of Nematoctonus, and specialized adhesive knobs produced by modified hyphae. Species of Arthrobotrys and Dactylella produce constricting rings or nets of hyphae that close around nematodes by an almost instantaneous tactile-induced change in turgor pressure within the hyphal cells creating the noose. The speed of this response is remarkable, with closure occurring in milliseconds when a nematode triggers the trap.

A third mechanism involves the production of toxic secretions. Barron and Thorn (1987) discovered that the basidiomycete fungi Pleurotus ostreatus, P. strigosus, P. subareolatus, and P. cornucopiae produce minute spathulate secretory cells that secrete droplets of toxins capable of killing nematodes on contact within 30 seconds. Subsequent to nematode death, fungal hyphae penetrate the nematode body through natural openings and destroy the internal tissues.

## Endoparasitic Nematophagous Fungi

In addition to trapping fungi, some nematophagous fungi function as endoparasites, entering the nematode body and digesting it from within. These include species that produce adhesive spores that germinate on the nematode cuticle and produce germ tubes that penetrate the body wall, as well as species that produce motile zoospores that actively seek out nematodes in moist soil environments. The endoparasitic strategy is particularly effective against sedentary nematodes such as root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) and cyst nematodes (Globodera and Heterodera spp.).

## Regulation of Ruminant Parasites

One of the most ecologically significant applications of nematophagous fungi involves their role in regulating intestinal nematode parasites of grazing livestock. Many free-living nematodes in soil have a pathogenic stage as gut intestinal parasites of ruminant animals, such as Trichostrongylus colubriformis and other strongyles. Conidia and chlamydospores of the nematophagous fungi Duddingtonia and Arthrobotrys are capable of surviving passage through the ruminant gut (Faedo et al., 1997).

After passage, these spores germinate in the dung, where the free-living stage of the nematode develops at high population densities and from which it becomes a potential source of infection for other grazing animals. The nematophagous fungi offer effective control of the free-living nematode population by trapping and killing them, significantly reducing the infective potential of the nematode population. Duddingtonia flagrans spores consistently survived better than spores of Arthrobotrys spp. through gut passage, making D. flagrans the most promising candidate for biological control applications.

## Biological Control of Plant-Parasitic Nematodes

Kerry and Jaffee (1997) identified two key factors influencing the success of nematophagous fungi as biocontrol agents for plant-pathogenic nematodes. First is the survival of conidia and trapping structures in soil, which is affected by environmental conditions and the availability of organic matter. Second is density-dependent parasitism, where the efficiency of nematode trapping is closely correlated to the density of nematode-trapping fungal structures in soil, which relates to conidial density. Providing adequate inoculum density to achieve significant nematode kill rates may be a limiting factor for the success of specific fungal species as biocontrol agents.

Viaene and Abawi (1998) showed that both nematophagous fungi of the genera Arthrobotrys and Monacrosporium and parasitic fungi of the genera Fusarium, Verticillium, and Alternaria were important in reducing populations of juvenile Meloidogyne hapla and parasitizing nematode egg masses. Kerry (1988) demonstrated that populations of the cereal cyst nematode could be controlled by Nematophthora gynophial and Verticillium chlamydosporium, which parasitize female nematodes and eggs. Approximately 150 fungal species have been identified with these properties.

## Interactions with Mycorrhizal Fungi

In the presence of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, Elsen et al. (2001) found that the population of the plant-burrowing nematode Radopholus similis was significantly reduced. The major effect of the mycorrhizae was on nematode females, suggesting potential long-term population regulation through the reduction in fecundity rather than direct killing. This interaction suggests that the combined effects of mycorrhizal fungi and nematophagous fungi could provide synergistic protection of plants from nematode damage.

## Ecological Significance in Soil Food Webs

Nematophagous fungi occupy a critical position in soil food webs, connecting the fungal decomposer community with the nematode-dominated microfaunal community. By regulating nematode populations, these fungi indirectly influence nutrient cycling rates, as nematodes are important regulators of bacterial activity and nutrient mineralization in soil. The predatory activity of nematophagous fungi also redirects nitrogen from nematode biomass into fungal biomass, affecting nitrogen availability for plant uptake.

The density-dependent nature of nematode trapping by fungi suggests a self-regulating predator-prey dynamic that contributes to the stability of soil food webs. When nematode populations are high, trapping structures are more likely to encounter prey, increasing fungal growth and reproduction. When nematode populations decline, the fungi may revert to saprotrophic growth or produce survival structures such as chlamydospores until prey populations recover.

## Challenges and Future Directions

The reduced ability to recover fungi from the feces of animals in tropical environments (Manueli et al., 1999) suggests that the use of nematophagous fungi as biocontrol agents may be restricted by climate. The survival of conidia and trapping structures in different soil types and under different management regimes requires further investigation. Additionally, the role of nematophagous fungi as food for other soil organisms, and the effects of this predation on their biocontrol efficacy, are poorly understood. Integration of nematophagous fungi into integrated pest management strategies, combined with other biological and cultural control methods, offers the most promising approach for sustainable nematode management.

## Ecological Significance

Nematophagous fungi play a crucial role in regulating nematode populations across diverse ecosystems, from agricultural soils to pastoral grasslands. By converting nematode biomass into fungal biomass, these fungi redirect nitrogen and other nutrients from animal-dominated to fungal-dominated food web pathways, influencing the overall flow of energy and matter through soil ecosystems. Their predatory activity is particularly significant in agricultural contexts where plant-parasitic nematodes cause substantial crop losses estimated at over $100 billion annually worldwide.

The density-dependent nature of nematode trapping creates self-regulating population dynamics that contribute to the stability of soil food webs. When nematode populations are high, trapping structures encounter prey more frequently, stimulating fungal growth and reproduction. When nematode populations decline, the fungi revert to saprotrophic growth or produce survival structures, maintaining a baseline population capable of rapid response to future prey availability.

## Research Methods

Investigation of nematophagous fungi employs molecular identification techniques, culture-based enumeration, and in situ trapping assays. Quantitative PCR allows monitoring of specific nematophagous fungal species in soil, while metagenomic approaches reveal the diversity and distribution of nematode-trapping genes across environments. Microcosm experiments with defined fungal and nematode populations provide controlled systems for studying trapping efficiency, while field trials evaluate the efficacy of nematophagous fungi as biological control agents under realistic agricultural conditions.

## Trapping Mechanisms

Nematophagous fungi—fungi that capture and consume nematodes—have evolved a remarkable array of trapping structures that represent some of the most sophisticated predatory adaptations in the fungal kingdom. These structures include adhesive networks (three-dimensional webs of adhesive hyphae), adhesive knobs (single adhesive cells on short stalks), adhesive columns, constricting rings (three-celled rings that inflate rapidly to trap passing nematodes), and non-constricting traps (funnel-shaped structures that nematodes enter but cannot escape). The production of trapping structures is induced by the presence of nematodes and their chemical signals, including specific peptides and pheromones.

Constricting rings are among the most dramatic fungal trapping devices. When a nematode enters the ring, the three cells of the ring inflate within approximately 0.1 seconds, gripping the nematode tightly. The rapid inflation is powered by water influx driven by osmotic pressure changes, making it one of the fastest movements in the fungal kingdom. After capture, the fungus penetrates the nematode cuticle using specialized infection pegs and digests the internal tissues, absorbing the nutrients to support fungal growth and reproduction.

## Toxin-Producing Nematophagous Fungi

In addition to mechanical trapping, some nematophagous fungi use chemical strategies to kill nematodes. Endoparasitic fungi such as [[nematode-trapping-fungi]] in the genera Nematoctonus and Drechmeria produce adhesive spores that stick to nematode cuticles, germinate, and penetrate the nematode body. These fungi complete their entire life cycle within the nematode, eventually killing the host and producing spores that are released to infect new hosts. Other fungi produce nematotoxic compounds, including volatile organic compounds that repel or kill nematodes, and water-soluble toxins that paralyze nematode prey.

The chemical ecology of nematophagous fungi has attracted interest for biological control applications. Species of Purpureocillium (formerly Paecilomyces lilacinus) and Pochonia chlamydosporia are commercially available as biological nematicides for use against plant-parasitic nematodes in agriculture. These fungi parasitize nematode eggs and females, reducing nematode populations without the environmental and health risks associated with synthetic nematicides.

## Ecological Significance

Nematophagous fungi are important regulators of nematode populations in soil ecosystems. In natural soils, nematode grazing on fungi can be intense, and predatory fungi provide a top-down control on nematode communities that helps maintain soil food web stability. The abundance and activity of nematophagous fungi are influenced by soil management practices: reduced tillage, organic amendments, and diverse cropping systems tend to support higher populations of nematophagous fungi, contributing to natural nematode suppression.
