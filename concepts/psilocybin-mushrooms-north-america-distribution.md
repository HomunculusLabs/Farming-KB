# Distribution of Psilocybin Mushrooms in North America

North America has a rich psilocybin mycoflora, particularly in the Pacific Northwest and the southeastern United States. In the Pacific Northwest alone, more than 4,000 mushroom species have been identified, with more than a dozen containing psilocybin. Stamets notes that he spent hundreds of hours hunting before finding his first Psilocybe.

## Pacific Northwest (Washington, Oregon, British Columbia)

The Pacific Northwest is the epicenter of North American psilocybin mushroom diversity, particularly for lignicolous (wood-decomposing) species. Many of the most potent species are rare in the wild but prolific in urban and suburban settings.

### Psilocybe azurescens
The most potent species in the world (up to 1.78% psilocybin). Naturally grows along the northern Oregon coast near Astoria, from the Columbia River to Tillamook, favoring dune grasses (Ammophila maritima). Fruits from late September through December, often after first frost. Outdoor beds have been established in California, Ohio, and elsewhere. Cap caramel-colored when young, fading to straw; stem thick and cartilaginous. One of the most distinctive and sought-after species in the genus.

### Psilocybe cyanescens
"Wavy-capped Psilocybe." Common in wood chips, sawdust, and mulched gardens. Found from San Francisco to southern Alaska. Widely spread in the UK and across temperate Europe -- from Italy, Germany, Spain, and Sweden. Loves wood-chip trails bordered by rhododendrons and shade-providing shrubs. Andrew Weil (1975, 1977) brought this mushroom to the forefront of awareness after collecting it in Oregon. Potency analyses are variable: Beug and Bigwood (1982b) reported maxima of 1.68% psilocybin and 0.28% psilocin, while Gartz (1994) reported 0.30% psilocybin, 0.51% psilocin, and 0.02% baeocystin.

### Psilocybe baeocystis
"Knobby tops." Common throughout the Pacific Northwest on conifer mulch, wood chips, and Douglas fir cones. Abundant where wood chips and bark have been used for landscaping. Contains both psilocybin and baeocystin.

### Psilocybe stuntzii
"Blue veil Psilocybe." Frequents schoolyards, athletic fields, and landscaped areas. Thousands of students disperse spores from these sites daily. A wood-chip species that has spread widely through human activity.

### Psilocybe cyanofibrillosa
"Rhododendron Psilocybe." Found along coastal regions from Northern California to British Columbia, especially in rhododendron gardens and nurseries. One of several Psilocybes that thrive in the landscaping habitats humans create.

### Psilocybe pelliculosa
The primary woodland Psilocybe of the region. Thrives on disturbed grounds such as trails and abandoned forest roads in conifer forests. A smaller, less conspicuous species than P. cyanescens.

### Psilocybe silvatica
Gregarious but not cespitose on wood debris or wood chips in well-decayed conifer substratum in the fall. Known from Ontario, the Pacific Northwest, Michigan, New York, and northern Europe. Contains psilocybin and/or psilocin, estimated not to be potent.

### Panaeolus subbalteatus
Common in grassy areas and manured soils throughout the Pacific Northwest.

### Habitat Notes
The majority of wood-decomposing Psilocybes thrive not in wild forests but in the landscaping mulch beds of densely populated areas. The use of decorative wood chips created new habitats for these species. Rhododendron gardens are particularly productive and can support perennial patches. Stamets notes knowing several rhododendron gardens "faithfully maintained by dedicated caretakers who have unwittingly succeeded in promoting perennial patches of P. cyanescens."

## Southeastern United States

The subtropical southeast supports the principal dung-dwelling species.

### Psilocybe cubensis
The most commonly cultivated species worldwide. Found on dung of cattle, horses, and buffalo. Fruits spring through fall, with May and June being best months. Common from Texas through Florida. Thought to have arrived with Spanish missionaries via Brahman cattle from West African islands. Today it can be collected from Brahman cattle dung in subtropical pastures circumnavigating the globe.

### Panaeolus cyanescens
Found in Hawaii, Louisiana, and Florida. A strong producer of psilocybin and/or psilocin, though highly variable. Widespread in tropical and subtropical zones.

### Gymnopilus spectabilis
"Big laughing mushroom." Widely distributed from Texas to California to Washington. An extremely bitter mushroom that varies substantially in its psilocybin content from one geographical region to the next. Moderately active to inactive depending on collection. Prefers hardwoods in eastern North America and conifers in the western regions. A large mushroom, caps reaching 5-40 cm broad.

## Northeastern and North Central United States

### Psilocybe caerulipes
"Blue foot." Found in deciduous forests with American beech (Fagus grandifolia), from Ohio to the northeastern states. Rare in these environments unless human activity has introduced populations. Fruiting in summer and fall. Contains psilocybin and baeocystin.

### Gymnopilus validipes
Common on hardwood logs and stumps. First reports of activity came from people who confused it with the honey mushroom (Armillaria mellea). Potent in Ohio, Michigan, and Massachusetts.

### Psilocybe quebecensis
Cold-weather species from Quebec, fruiting in sphagnum bogs.

## Western Mountain States

### Psilocybe semilanceata
Found west of the Cascades from Northern California to British Columbia. Especially abundant around clumps of sedge grass in damp pastures. A temperate grassland species found throughout much of the world -- England, Ireland, France, Germany, Holland, Belgium, Sweden, Norway, Switzerland, Italy, Chile, and South Africa. Moderately active to highly active; one French specimen measured at 1.71% psilocybin.

### Alpine Discovery
A lone, strongly bluing Psilocybe resembling P. pelliculosa was discovered in 1993 above Telluride, Colorado at 10,000-11,000 feet. After more than fifty years of concerted efforts by competent mycologists who concluded psilocybin mushrooms simply did not exist there, this find demonstrated that psilocybin mushrooms can exist even in high-alpine habitats previously thought barren. The species was determined to be new to science.

### Conocybe cyanopus
Reported from Washington, Colorado, and temperate regions.

## Notable Historical Notes

- Psilocybe caerulescens was first reported near Montgomery, Alabama in 1923 on sugarcane mulch, but has not been re-collected from that locality since.
- Many Pacific Northwest species likely arrived from Europe in soil around imported ornamental plants (rhododendrons, roses, azaleas).
- In the mid-1970s, hunting for Psilocybes in the Pacific Northwest and southeastern US "approached a national sport," with estimated thousands of hunters daily in western Washington fields.
- Psilocybe weilii, discovered in Georgia, grows in red clay soils -- an unusual habitat for the genus.
