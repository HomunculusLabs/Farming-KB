### Necrotrophy

Necrotrophic fungi have traditionally been regarded as strongly host-specific, resulting in numerous species names defined more by host identity than by fungal features. DNA analysis is revealing that such genera are systematically diverse, and species recognized by molecular evidence do not always correlate with host-based classifications. This challenges traditional species concepts in necrotrophic genera such as Colletotrichum and Phomopsis.

## Life Strategies and Survival

### Accessory Vegetative Structures

Most microfungi are relatively long-lived but sporulate (and become directly identifiable) only for short periods. They produce various structures for survival during adverse conditions:

- **Melanized fruiting bodies**: Protection for enclosed spores during drought, low temperature, and UV exposure
- **Melanized hyphae**: Dark-pigmented hyphae that withstand environmental extremes
- **Chlamydospores**: Thick-walled resting spores
- **Sclerotia**: Compact masses of hardened mycelium
- **Bulbils**: Small vegetative propagules
- **Appressoria and setae**: Specialized structures for attachment, infection, or defense

Unless these structures are morphologically distinctive, species are difficult to identify without culturing to induce sporulation.

### Teleomorph-Anamorph Connections

Many microfungi have more than one morphologically distinct spore-bearing stage in their life cycles, producing both a teleomorph (sexual stage) and one or more anamorphs (asexual stages). This creates significant challenges:

- The timing of sexual and asexual reproduction is often unknown
- Multiple anamorphs (synanamorphs) may be produced by a single species
- The anamorph may precede, follow, or occur simultaneously with the teleomorph
- Anamorphs may form at different locations on the mycelium than teleomorphs

For Diplocarpon rosae and Rhytisma acerinum, the anamorph (Marssonina and Melasmia respectively) is formed as a necrotroph on leaves. After leaves fall, the anamorph ceases sporulation, and a teleomorph stroma is formed for overwintering. Ascospores reinfect new leaves in spring, producing the anamorph again — a complete seasonal cycle.

## Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling

### Temporal Scale

It may be years before plant debris fully decays and residues are incorporated into soil. In humid tropical ecosystems, the process is much more rapid. This temporal gradient creates a physical interface between plant debris and soil that must be accounted for in community analyses.

### Enzymatic Capabilities

Microfungi produce a remarkable array of extracellular enzymes for degrading plant cell wall polymers:
- Cellulases for cellulose breakdown
- Lignin peroxidases and manganese peroxidases for lignin degradation
- Xylanases for hemicellulose decomposition
- Pectinases for pectin degradation
- Cutinases for cuticle breakdown

The enzymatic competence of stress-tolerant microfungi allows them to persist on recalcitrant substrates even when growth rates are minimal.

## Biogeography and Distribution Patterns

Knowledge of fungal biogeography is very limited for most microfungi. Distribution patterns are linked to modes of nutrition and dispersal:

### Cosmopolitan Species
Some taxa, such as members of the Caliciales, are presumed cosmopolitan due to ancient evolutionary history. Ruderal species are often widely distributed due to efficient dispersal and rapid colonization ability.

### Host-Associated Species
Fungi confined to particular host groups have distributions coextensive with those of their plant associates, though other factors may further limit their range.

### Human-Mediated Dispersal
Many dung fungi, soil fungi, and litter fungi are distributed widely partly because of extensive human introduction through agriculture, horticulture, and trade.

### Sampling Bias
The most commonly encountered microfungi in ecosystem analyses are those that sporulate profusely, are easily isolated into pure culture, and have short generation times. Such species are inherently more likely to spread widely and be recorded reliably. Research in genuinely undisturbed habitats is badly needed to assess natural distribution patterns.

## Taxonomic Challenges

Species of microfungi associated with dead plant tissue continue to be identified based on morphological characters from host material rather than cultures or molecular characters. Key challenges include:

- Morphological features subject to environmental modification over time
- Structures used for identification (fruiting bodies, conidiophores) may be absent or poorly developed in culture
- Lack of information on morphology in culture for many species
- Molecular data showing that host-based species concepts are often artificial
- Anamorph-teleomorph connections remaining unknown for many species
- DNA from herbarium specimens often too degraded for full analysis

The need for species concepts that conform to modern evolutionary theory, supported by both morphological and molecular evidence, represents one of the most significant challenges in microfungal systematics.
