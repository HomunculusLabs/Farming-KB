The year that the Kochi post-season cultivation program began to
eradicate the yellow rice borer, Fukuoka was assigned to an eastern
district of the prefecture. His job was to make certain that not a
single stalk of rice remained standing as food for the season's
first generation of rice borers until the end of June. He combed
the entire district, making his rounds from the hilly back country
and mountains to the coast.

## The Discovery at Kotogahama

Once, as he was passing through a pine wood along the shore at
Kotogahama, he spotted a large number of young rice seedlings that
had sprouted from unhulled seed spilled where farmers had threshed
rice the year before. This volunteer rice later led to one of the
most important discoveries in his farming career: nature's ability
to "plant without sowing seed."

If rice seed scattered on the ground by accident could germinate
and grow without any human assistance, then perhaps rice could be
grown by simply scattering seed on unplowed, untilled fields. This
simple observation challenged the fundamental assumption that rice
required a carefully prepared nursery bed, transplantation into
puddled soil, and intensive management to produce a crop.

## Early Direct Seeding Experiments

The first experiments followed the same procedure as for preparation
of a rice nursery bed, first plowing the field, then flooding and
tilling it, followed by direct seeding. The experiment consisted
of drilling, seeding in straight rows, and broadcasting. The main
object was to examine the effects of different sowing techniques
and the sowing rate and density. Planting rates of approximately
20, 30, 60, 100, 230, and 1000 seeds individually per square yard
were tested.

The results were both expected and surprising. Aside from the
extremely dense planting, the number of heads per square yard was
about 400 to 500 in all cases, and the number of grains per head
from 60 to 120. Yields were therefore about the same regardless of
seeding density. This demonstrated that rice plants naturally
self-regulate their tillering and panicle production according to
available space and resources.

## Problems Encountered

Several problems did arise during these early experiments. Where
the soil was rich in organic matter and bad water collected, the
seed sank into the ground and germination was poor. Deep flooding
of the field resulted in plants that tended to lodge easily. And
the amount of weeding required was so great that the method had
little practical value at the time.

These difficulties did not discourage further experimentation.
The poor germination in waterlogged soil pointed toward the need
for better drainage, the lodging problem toward lower seeding
densities and shorter varieties, and the weed problem toward the
eventual solution of cover cropping with clover.

## Direct Seeding Between Barley Rows

The next phase involved direct seeding of rice between rows of
barley, a "lazy man's" method adapted from techniques attempted
by farmers long ago. This led to a later technique of direct-
seeding rice between barley for several years, but germination
and weed control problems eventually forced its abandonment.

Specific problems encountered included poor rice germination, the
inability to fight off mole crickets, sparrows, and mice, arduous
intertilling work with a hoe, poor water retention, and complicated
patterns of weed emergence along the water's edge. However, this
period produced several important clues.

## The Clover Revolution Breakthrough

The critical breakthrough came from the idea of controlling weeds
with weeds. Clover and Chinese milk vetch were sown over the
ridges of maturing barley one month before barley harvest, producing
a rich growth of these herbs among the barley. Although not
immediately successful, this gave the clue that led to the method
of rice and barley cropping in a ground cover of clover.

Vegetable seeds such as mustards, beans, and squash were also tried,
and although none grew well enough for home consumption, they taught
valuable lessons about the relationships between specific crops in a
rotation. The opposite was tried as well: seeding and growing rice in
fields of tomato, eggplant, and cucumber. Rice yields were better in
these vegetable fields than vegetables in rice paddies.

## From Ridges to Level Fields

As the barley method progressed from high-ridge to low-ridge to
level-field cultivation, the rice method followed suit. From seeding
in single rows at wide 18-inch intervals, the progression moved to
narrowly spaced rows 6 to 8 inches apart, then to individual seeds
at intervals of 6 by 8 inches, and finally to naked barley sown
over the entire surface of the field without plowing or tilling.
This was the start of the no-tillage direct-seeding of naked barley.

## The Leap to No-Tillage

Because the method resulted in high-yield cultivation of barley and
dense individual planting, it became increasingly difficult to sow
rice among the barley. The key insight was that since the exact same
method was being used for growing both rice and barley in succession,
both crops could be grown as a single cropping system. This system
was named "direct-seeding, no-tillage rice/barley succession."

The ultimate test was whether rice yields would decline after
several years of continuous no-tillage farming. After more than
twenty years of no-tillage cultivation, with all rice and barley
straw and hulls returned to the land, there was never any sign of
a decline in yields due to reduced soil fertility. This sealed the
conviction that no-tillage farming is sound in practice and became
a basic principle of natural farming.
