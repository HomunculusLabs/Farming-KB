# Fungal Phosphorus Solubilization

Fungal phosphorus solubilization is the process by which fungi convert
insoluble forms of phosphorus in soil into soluble forms that are
available for uptake by plants and other organisms. This process is
ecologically critical because phosphorus is often the most limiting
nutrient for plant growth in terrestrial ecosystems, and a large
proportion of soil phosphorus exists in forms that are not directly
available to plants.

Fungi contribute to phosphorus solubilization through several
mechanisms including the production of organic acids, the secretion of
phosphatase enzymes, and the release of protons.

## The Phosphorus Availability Problem

Phosphorus in soils exists in both organic and inorganic forms, the
majority of which are unavailable to plants. Inorganic phosphorus is
predominantly bound as insoluble calcium, iron, or aluminum phosphates,
depending on soil pH. In acidic soils, phosphorus is primarily bound as
iron and aluminum phosphates, while in alkaline and calcareous soils,
it occurs mainly as calcium phosphates including apatite.

Organic phosphorus, which can constitute 30 to 80 percent of total
soil phosphorus, occurs mainly as phosphate esters, including inositol
phosphates, phospholipids, and nucleic acids. The discrepancy between
total soil phosphorus and plant-available phosphorus is a major
constraint on primary productivity in many ecosystems.

Fungi play a central role in bridging this gap by solubilizing and
mineralizing phosphorus from both inorganic and organic sources. The
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] association can supply up to 80
percent of a plant's phosphorus requirements, particularly in
low-phosphorus soils. This symbiosis represents one of the oldest and
most widespread mutualisms on Earth, dating back over 400 million
years.

## Organic Acid-Mediated Solubilization

The production of organic acids is one of the primary mechanisms by
which fungi solubilize inorganic phosphorus compounds. Organic acids
solubilize phosphate minerals through several mechanisms: lowering the
pH of the microenvironment, directly chelating the cations (calcium,
iron, aluminum) bound to phosphate, and competing with phosphate for
adsorption sites on soil mineral surfaces.

Oxalic acid is one of the most important organic acids in fungal
phosphorus solubilization. It is produced by many saprotrophic and
[[mollison-designers-mycorrhizal-fungi-and-soil-microbiology]] is particularly effective at solubilizing calcium
phosphates. Citric acid, another common fungal metabolite, is effective
against both calcium and iron phosphates. Other organic acids involved
in phosphorus solubilization include succinic, malic, formic, malonic,
tartaric, and fumaric acids.

Jongmans and colleagues demonstrated that micromolar concentrations of
organic acids (succinic, citric, oxalic, formate, and malate) secreted
by saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal fungi were responsible for the
dissolution of minerals in pine forest ecosystems. The production of
these acids at the hyphal-soil interface creates localized zones of
enhanced phosphorus solubility.

## Phosphatase Enzymes

Fungi produce a range of phosphatase enzymes that release phosphate
from organic phosphorus compounds. Acid phosphatases are the most
widely studied and are produced by both saprotrophic and mycorrhizal
fungi. These enzymes hydrolyze phosphate ester bonds, releasing
inorganic phosphate that can be absorbed by the fungus and, in the case
of mycorrhizal fungi, transferred to the host plant.

The production of phosphatase enzymes by fungi is regulated by the
availability of inorganic phosphate. Under conditions of phosphorus
deficiency, fungi increase the production and secretion of phosphatases,
enhancing their capacity to access organic phosphorus sources. This
regulatory response ensures that energy is invested in phosphatase
production only when the demand for phosphorus exceeds the available
inorganic supply.

Ectomycorrhizal fungi are particularly important producers of
phosphatases in forest soils. The extraradical mycelium of
ectomycorrhizal fungi produces phosphatases that can access organic
phosphorus sources at some distance from the root surface. The
[[mycorrhizal-phosphatase-activity-elf97-detection]] of mycorrhizal
mycelium can be 10-100 times higher per unit length than that of
non-mycorrhizal roots.

Phosphatase production is regulated by plant phosphorus demand through
a signaling pathway involving strigolactones — when a plant is
phosphorus-deficient, it releases more strigolactones, stimulating
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] and phosphatase
production.

## Mycorrhizal Phosphorus Solubilization

Mycorrhizal fungi, particularly ectomycorrhizal and [[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]]
species, are major contributors to phosphorus solubilization in many
ecosystems. These fungi extend the effective phosphorus-absorbing
surface of the root system by producing extraradical hyphae that
explore soil volumes beyond the depletion zone around the root.

The ectomycorrhizal fungus *[[pisolithus-tinctorius]]* demonstrated
significant capacity to access phosphorus from insoluble sources.
Studies have shown that ectomycorrhizal fungi can access phosphorus in
deeper soil layers than those being colonized by surface feeder roots,
effectively keeping [[fungal-role-in-phosphorus-cycling]] in the biotic components of the
forest ecosystem.

[[singh-ericoid-mycorrhizal-fungi-remediation]], associated with plants in the Ericaceae, are
particularly effective at accessing organic phosphorus sources. These
fungi produce phosphatases and can take up phosphate released from
[[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]], providing a significant competitive advantage in the
phosphorus-poor, acidic soils where ericaceous plants typically grow.

## Synergism with Bacteria

Phosphorus solubilization in soil often involves synergistic
interactions between fungi and bacteria. Azcon and colleagues (1976)
showed synergistic interactions between bacteria and arbuscular
mycorrhizae of lavender in the acquisition of phosphorus from rock
phosphate. The combination of mycorrhizal fungi and
phosphate-solubilizing bacteria was more effective than either organism
alone in providing phosphorus to the host plant.

Singh and Kapoor (1998) demonstrated that a consortium of
phosphate-solubilizing organisms, including the arbuscular mycorrhizal
fungus *Glomus fasciculatum*, the saprotrophic fungus *Cladosporium
herbarum*, and the bacterium *Bacillus circulans*, enabled mung bean
plants to better obtain phosphorus from rock phosphate than each
organism alone.

These synergistic consortia are important for understanding the full
potential of biological phosphorus solubilization in agriculture and
ecosystem management.

## Influence on Soil Phosphorus Pools

Fungal phosphorus solubilization activities influence the distribution
of phosphorus among different soil pools. By converting insoluble
phosphorus into soluble forms that are taken up by plants and
microorganisms, fungi contribute to the cycling of phosphorus from
relatively stable pools into biologically active pools.
