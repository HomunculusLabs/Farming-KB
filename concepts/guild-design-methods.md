# Guild Design Methods

Two guild design methods, super-guilds, case studies, food forest integration, and troubleshooting for plant guilds. From Toby Hemenway's Gaia's Garden. See [[gaia-garden-plant-guilds]] for guild types (apple guild, walnut guild, insectary guilds, etc.).

## Designing Custom Guilds: Two Methods

### Method 1: Field Observation (Intimate Method)
1. Walk through native plant communities in your region with a field guide
2. Observe which species regularly occur together
3. Note relationships: who grows under whom, what insects visit, what leaf litter accumulates
4. Substitute domesticated relatives of useful native species
5. Identify functional roles (N-fixer, accumulator, insectary, mulcher) and fill gaps

### Method 2: Armchair Method (Research-Based)
1. Search for lists of major plant communities native to your area (ecology books, university databases)
2. Find a community matching your site conditions (soil, climate, exposure)
3. List all component species from the reference
4. Evaluate each species for:
   - Is it useful for humans (food, medicine, craft)?
   - Does it have domesticated relatives?
   - What functional role does it play (N-fixing, insectary, mulch)?
   - Is it common across multiple communities (good buffer/transitional plant)?
   - Does it show pest damage (avoid these)?
5. Select species or substitutes to create your guild
6. Fill any missing functional roles with species from guild references

### Questions to Ask for Guild Design
- What is the dominant tree species of the community?
- Which plants offer food for wildlife? Are they desirable in your yard?
- Which plants have domesticated relatives that provide food for people?
- Which species are common to more than one community (buffer plants)?
- What species generates most of the leaf litter (good mulch plant)?
- Does the community contain nitrogen fixers or nutrient accumulators?
- Do any plants show exceptional insect damage (avoid as guild members)?

## Super-Guilds (Interconnected Guilds)

Multiple guilds can be combined into a "super-guild" — a larger, more deeply connected community. Guilds based on different tree types act as subunits, linked by buffer plants.

### Example: Apple-Walnut Super-Guild
- Alternate apple guilds and walnut guilds in an orchard layout
- Separate them with rows of mulberries and nitrogen-fixing trees (black locust, tagasaste, alder)
- Fill alleys between trees with berry bushes, flowering shrubs, vegetables, and flowers

### Benefits of Super-Guilds
- Nitrogen fixers boost growth of all nearby trees
- Mulberries buffer sensitive species from walnut toxins
- Diverse food production prevents dietary boredom
- Blossoms and shelter attract many wildlife species
- More robust than isolated guilds

### Guild Limitations
- **Space-hungry**: One or two guilds will fill most of a small city lot
- **Slow to establish**: Fruit trees take several years to bear
- **Temperate-zone complexity**: Allelopathic interactions are more problematic in deep temperate soils than in shallow [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]]
- **Site-specific**: What works in New York may not work in California
- **Debugging difficulty**: Tracing problems in interconnected communities is challenging
- **Harvesting style**: More like foraging than row-crop harvesting — you pluck a few of many things

## Case Study: Oregon White Oak Guild

Hemenway's armchair method applied to his Oregon bioregion, based on Franklin and Dyrness's "Vegetation of Oregon and Washington." The white oak/hazelnut community contains 15+ species:

**Trees**: Oregon white oak (Quercus garryana), Pacific madrone (Arbutus menziesii), mazzard cherry (Prunus avium), black hawthorn (Crataegus douglasii)
**Shrubs**: California hazelnut (Corylus cornuta), serviceberry (Amelanchier alnifolia), oceanspray (Holodiscus discolor), snowberry (Symphoricarpos albus), thimbleberry, trailing blackberry, sweetbriar rose (Rosa rugosa)
**Ground plants**: Broad-petaled strawberry (Fragaria virginiana), poison oak (substitute lemonade berry, Rhus integrifolia), yerba buena (Satureja douglasii), sweet cicely (Osmorhiza chilensis), American vetch (Vicia americana)

**Substitutions made**: Domestic filberts for wild hazel, grafted cherries for mazzard, lemonade berry for poison oak, thornless blackberries, Rosa rugosa for native rose. Added comfrey and artichoke as initial mulch plants; extra insectary plants (dill, fennel) and N-fixers (beans, clover, ceanothus) to fill gaps.

## Integrating Guilds into Food Forests

- Place guilds around each canopy tree in the food forest
- Connect guilds with shared [[permaculture-plants-food-forest-ground-covers-bamboos]] and insectary corridors
- Use nitrogen-fixing shrubs as guild edges and windbreaks
- Design so beneficials can move between guilds easily
- Layer guilds vertically: canopy guild, understory guild, ground-level guild

See [[food-forest-design]] and [[food-forest-layers-and-species]] for integration.

## Troubleshooting Guild Failures

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Central tree declining | Competition from guild plants | Thin aggressive plants, add mulch ring |
| Pest outbreaks | Missing insectary plants | Add more flowering species with staggered bloom |
| Poor growth, yellow leaves | Nitrogen deficiency | Add more leguminous fixers |
| Weeds overwhelming | Insufficient ground cover | Add denser living mulch, sheet mulch |
| Waterlogging | Poor drainage in guild | Add drainage, mound plantings, select tolerant species |
| Too much shade | Canopy too dense | Thin canopy, select shade-tolerant understory species |
