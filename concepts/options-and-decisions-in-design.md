## Options and Decisions in Design

Design as a selection of options and pathways based on decisions is a [[maps]], overlays on those maps, and overlays on the overlays. While more expensive, potentially time-consuming, and possibly the most confusing of all design approaches, it has value when used appropriately.

### Strengths of Map Overlays

A good site map makes landscape design much easier and far more visual. Maps indicate sensible options and hypotheses — dam sites, soil-crop suitability, water storage locations — that can later be verified against actual site conditions including available clay for dams, existing useful vegetation, and threatened habitat.

### Limitations of Map Overlays

The methodology has significant weaknesses:

- **Omission of minutiae**: Map overlays cannot capture the fine-grained site details revealed by direct observation
- **Static nature**: Maps cannot reveal evolutionary processes or dynamic interactions over time
- **Unmappable factors**: Ethical, financial, and cultural constraints resist cartographic representation
- **Remote rigidity**: Systems designed primarily from maps tend toward inflexibility, making adaptation to fresh conditions difficult and occasioning unforeseen work and expense

### Balancing Methods

The more empirical and flexible observation and option methods both allow flexible responses to changing conditions, whereas purely analytical and overlay approaches tend toward remote rigidity. The most effective designs combine the broad spatial understanding of maps with the dynamic responsiveness of observation-based methods.

## The Decision Flow

The complete options-based design process follows this general flow:

1. **Ethical foundation**: Establish core ethics (care of earth, care of people, return of surplus) as the highest-level decision filter
2. **Goal setting**: Define distant goals informed by ethics and client aspirations
3. **Option generation**: Enumerate possible pathways for each design domain (crops, structures, water, energy, income)
4. **Constraint filtering**: Eliminate options that conflict with site conditions, resources, regulations, or ethics
5. **Prioritization**: Rank remaining options by urgency, finance, skills, resources, and energy available
6. **Limited trial**: Implement a manageable subset of options for testing
7. **Observation and feedback**: Monitor outcomes and adjust based on real-world results
8. **Iteration**: Refine and expand the design over time as experience accumulates

This iterative approach ensures that design remains responsive to reality rather than locked into a predetermined plan that may prove unsuitable once implemented.
