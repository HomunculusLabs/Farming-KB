# Fungi in Mangrove and Coastal Ecosystems

## Overview

Mangrove swamps and coastal ecosystems support distinct fungal
communities that play essential roles in the decomposition of plant
litter, nutrient mineralization, and the coupling of terrestrial and
marine [[mangrove-ecosystems-and-coastal-restoration]] harbor unique assemblages of
ascomycetes, [[fungal-decomposition-processes]] and influence
nutrient export to near-shore marine environments.

## Mangrove Fungal Communities

### Community Composition

Mangrove swamps occur in sheltered coastal areas away from direct wave
action. The litter from these ecosystems is more diverse than that of
salt marsh systems, and high rates of [[air-pollution-fungal-community-responses]] is almost exclusively saprotrophic,
consisting of some 23 species of ascomycete, 17 mitosporic species, and
two basidiomycetes.

In addition to true fungi, the chromistan group (oomycetes) including
chytrids are important decomposers of mangrove plant material. The
composition of mangrove fungal communities is distinct from those
associated with decomposition in salt marsh ecosystems (Newell, 1996).

### Fungal Biomass Dynamics

Fungal biomass in decaying mangrove leaves is much lower than in
decaying salt marsh vegetation (less than 1 mg per gram compared to 60
to 85 mg per gram) (Newell and Fell, 1992; Newell, 1996). Bacteria
represent a very small percentage of mangrove decomposers (0.7 mg per
gram). However, the actual fungal biomass may be greater than
ergosterol measurements suggest, because many marine oomycetes (e.g.,
Halophytophthora) do not contain ergosterol.

Newell and Fell (1992) documented significant changes in fungal biomass
during decomposition of red mangrove leaves. Lulworthia grandispora,
an ascomycete, may comprise 50% of the [[fungal-decomposition-resource-quality-determinants]] changes.

## Salt Marsh Fungal Communities

### Biomass and Trophic Importance

Fungal biomass in decomposing salt marsh material is substantial,
reaching 60 to 85 mg per gram of organic mass. This high fungal biomass
is important for sustaining invertebrate herbivore populations. The
amphipod Ulorchestia spartinophila has complex dietary requirements and
grows best when fed on decaying leaves containing high fungal biomass,
demonstrating the trophic significance of fungal decomposer activity.
