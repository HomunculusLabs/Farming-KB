## Mycelial Colonization and Contamination Resistance

After inoculation, the mycelium was allowed to colonize the brown rice
substrate under appropriate incubation conditions. Badham made an important
practical observation: once the hyphae had fully covered the substrate,
aseptic conditions were no longer necessary for subsequent handling. This
reflects the competitive advantage that fully colonized mushroom mycelium
enjoys over potential contaminants. The established fungal network
consumes available nutrients and occupies the substrate volume, creating an
environment where airborne contaminants struggle to establish. This
property significantly simplified the experimental workflow by removing the
need for sterile technique during the transfer and fruiting phases.

## Stage Two: Vermiculite Transfer System

Once the brown rice was fully colonized, Badham transferred the hyphal
mass to 13 centimeter diameter pie tins for the fruiting stage. Each pie
tin received the colonized rice along with vermiculite and 25 milliliters
of water. The vermiculite served multiple functions in this transfer
system. As an inert, expanded mineral material, vermiculite provides
excellent water retention through its sponge-like structure while
maintaining air porosity around the mycelial mass. This combination of
moisture availability and gas exchange is critical for supporting the
metabolic demands of developing fruiting bodies, which have high
respiratory rates and require both water and oxygen.
