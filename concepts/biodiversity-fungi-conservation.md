## Threats to Fungal Diversity

### Habitat Destruction

The primary threat to fungal diversity is habitat destruction, particularly:

- **Tropical deforestation**: Where the majority of undescribed fungal species are thought to occur
- **Forest fragmentation**: Breaking continuous habitat into smaller patches disrupts fungal populations and their dispersal networks
- **Logging and wood removal**: Reducing the diversity of woody substrata and decay stages essential for wood-inhabiting fungi
- **Soil disturbance**: Cultivation, erosion, and contamination affect soil habitats and their diverse fungal communities

### Pollution and Environmental Change

- **Air pollution**: Has been shown to affect basidiomycete communities in European forests
- **Global warming**: Alters the temperature and moisture regimes that control fungal fruiting and community composition
- **Water and soil pollution**: Contamination affects fungal communities in aquatic and terrestrial environments
- **Nitrogen deposition**: Changes in soil chemistry favor some fungal groups over others

### Overharvesting

- **Commercial mushroom harvesting**: Anthropogenic harvesting of sporocarps may obscure or eliminate aspects of fungal biology that occur in situ
- **Collection pressure**: Popular edible fungi face local depletion from intensive collecting

### Invasive Species

- Introduced species (e.g., Sirex noctilio and Amylostereum areolatum in the Southern Hemisphere) can become serious pathogens in new environments
- Invasive plants may alter mycorrhizal communities and soil fungal assemblages

## Documented Declines

During the past 20–30 years, species compositions and abundances of macrofungi at European sites have changed markedly:

- Several previously common species are no longer encountered
- A number of species have been designated as rare or endangered (Arnolds 1988, 1991; Pegler et al. 1993)
- Without baseline data from prior surveys, these changes could not have been detected

## Monitoring and Baseline Data

The most basic requirement for fungal conservation is baseline data:

- Fungal surveys provide the data against which changes can be measured in response to natural or anthropogenic perturbations
- Standardized methods for each operational group of fungi are necessary for quantitative sampling and cross-site comparisons
- Monitoring programs should be designed to detect changes in species composition, abundance, and community structure
- Fungi are used as indicators of ecosystem health and effects of management practices in programs such as the Forest Ecosystem Management Assessment Team (FEMAT) monitoring of old-growth forests in the Pacific Northwest

## The Role of Inventories

Comprehensive fungal inventories contribute to conservation through:

- **Discovery of new species**: Providing data essential for conservation assessment
- **Herbarium specimens**: Creating voucher collections that serve as the foundation for taxonomic, ecological, and molecular studies
- **Distribution data**: Documenting geographic ranges needed for Red List assessments
- **Ecological information**: Understanding habitat requirements and host specificity

## Knowledge Dissemination

Conservation depends on effective communication:

- Survey data should be reported not only in scientific publications but in formats accessible to broad audiences
- Field guides and websites disseminate information to naturalists, ecotourists, and citizen scientists
- Electronic venues (CD-ROMs, websites) are imperative for rapid data sharing
- Information must reach citizens, land managers, and politicians to inform decisions about zoning, parklands, farming, and logging practices

## Incorporating Fungi into Conservation Policy

Fungi remain largely absent from conservation legislation and policy:

- Most conservation frameworks focus on plants and animals
- Fungal Red Lists exist for some European countries but are far less comprehensive than those for fauna and flora
- The pivotal roles of fungi in all ecosystems (decomposition, nutrient cycling, mycorrhizae, food webs) justify their inclusion in conservation planning
- Standardized, vetted survey methods must be developed and adopted before fungi can be effectively incorporated into monitoring programs

## Challenges Unique to Fungal Conservation

- **Cryptic diversity**: Many fungi are microscopic or produce ephemeral fruiting structures, making population assessment difficult
- **Inability to culture**: Most fungi cannot be cultured, limiting our ability to study and propagate them
- **Lack of public awareness**: Fungi are generally less visible and less appreciated than plants and animals
- **Taxonomic impediment**: Shortage of trained mycologists limits the rate of species discovery and description
