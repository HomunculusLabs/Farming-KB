## Zone One Intensive Garden Design

Zone one in [[permaculture]] is the area of most intensive human interaction — the garden
immediately surrounding the home. It receives the highest frequency of visits, the most
careful management, and the greatest investment of mulch, [[compost]], and labor. Bill
Mollison's design course presents zone one as a fully integrated system of intensive
annual gardens, protective barriers, small-scale water features, and carefully selected
plant placements that maximize yield while minimizing effort.

## The Fully Mulched Garden

Zone one gardens are distinguished by complete mulch coverage. Unlike outer zones where
spot-mulching may suffice, zone one demands total soil protection to maintain the high
biological activity and moisture retention needed for [[mollison-designers-market-garden-intensive-production-design]]. The mulch
suppresses weeds, moderates temperature, retains moisture, and feeds the soil as it
decomposes.

Triple digging — a deep soil preparation technique loosening earth to roughly 60 cm — is
often employed. This involves digging trenches, loosening subsoil with a fork, and
moving topsoil forward across the bed. The result is a deep, well-aerated root zone
supporting exceptionally dense plantings. However, Mollison stresses that technique
should match the gardener's age, physical inclination, and conviction. An elderly
gardener might choose no-dig [[sheet-mulching]] instead. The principle remains: create deep,
friable, well-drained soil through whatever means suit the individual.

## Two Classes of Annual Garden Plants

Mollison divides annual plants into two functional categories that determine placement:

**Frequently picked plants** require daily attention: soft herbs (basil, cilantro),
broccoli (harvested continuously for side shoots), parsley, salad greens, cherry
tomatoes, and pole beans. Because the gardener visits them constantly, they border
pathways for effortless access. Reaching across a bed to pluck herbs compacts soil;
border placement eliminates this entirely.

**Harvest-once plants** are lifted in a single operation: tubers, root crops (carrots,
beets, parsnips), and head vegetables (cabbage, cauliflower). Since they need no
regular attention, they sit behind the frequently picked plants, deeper in the bed,
growing undisturbed until harvest.

The garden's physical layout thus mirrors human interaction frequency. The gardener
walks the path, plucks border plants, and rarely enters the bed — reducing compaction
and keeping harvest-once crops undisturbed.

## Garden Borders and Weed Exclusion

The zone one perimeter serves a critical defensive function. Effective barrier plants
share several characteristics: fireproof or fire-resistant foliage, dense shade beneath
the canopy dark enough to suppress weed germination, low-light adaptation so plants
thrive in their own shade, and dense fibrous roots that block rhizomatous weeds. This
living wall requires only occasional pruning rather than active weeding.

## Fencing and Animal Protection

Zone one fencing uses a layered defense: a buried mesh apron extending 30-60 cm outward
prevents chipmunks, rabbits, and gophers from tunneling; wire mesh fencing 1.5-2 m
high excludes medium-sized mammals; and electric wire at the top deters deer. This
investment preserves the gardener's labor and crop value.

## Wind Barriers and Hedgerows

Wind protection improves microclimate stability, reduces evapotranspiration, and extends
[[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]. Suitable species include sunflowers (annual windbreak with edible
seed), [[jerusalem-artichoke]] (tall perennial screen with edible tubers), Siberian pea
tree (nitrogen-fixing with edible seeds and chicken forage), and comfrey (deep-rooted
mineral accumulator producing abundant mulch when cut). Every element performs multiple
functions — a core [[permaculture-principle-1-observe-interact]].

## Ponds in Zone One

Small ponds (1-2 m²) serve production and ecological roles. Watercress provides
nutrient-dense greens; duckweed feeds chickens; the pond moderates temperatures and
provides emergency irrigation.

Ecologically, ponds support frog populations critical for pest control. Mollison
describes stratification by tadpole buoyancy: tree frog tadpoles are buoyant and stay
near the surface, while ground frog tadpoles are heavier and remain near the bottom.
This allows multiple frog species to coexist in a small water body, each in a distinct
niche, collectively consuming enormous quantities of slugs and insects.

## Transition to Zone Two

Zone two steps down in management intensity. Gardens use spot-mulching rather than full
coverage. This domain includes main crop gardens (potatoes, corn, beans, squash), orchard
trees, and [[permaculture-domestic-animals]] — chickens, ducks, milk goats, and a milk cow. The reduced
mulching reflects both larger scale and lower visit frequency.

## Animal Integration and Manure Collection

Animal housing at the zone one/two boundary serves dual purposes: convenient daily
management and easy manure transport into zone one. Mollison recommends expanded mesh
flooring, which lets manure fall through to a collection area below — simplifying
collection, keeping animals clean, reducing ammonia, and supplying continuous compost
material.

Pigs demonstrate a useful behavioral duality: clean on open grass, messy when confined.
Released into spent beds after harvest, they root out crop residues, consume pests and
grubs, and fertilize the soil — acting as biological tillers that prepare beds for the
next season with minimal human labor.

## The Lawn as Status Symbol

Mollison critiques the conventional lawn as a product of the British landscape ethic —
a status symbol demonstrating the owner could afford unproductive land. Exported
worldwide through colonialism, it demands enormous inputs (water, fertilizer, mowing,
chemicals) while producing nothing edible. He frames this as "the nuclear family trap":
culturally enforced maintenance of sterile greenspace locking families into consumption
patterns that could feed them instead. Replacing [[query-how-do-i-replace-my-lawn-with-productive-or-ecological-plantings]] gardens represents
both [[mycorrhizal-fungi-ecological-restoration]] and economic liberation.

## Productive Front-Yard Design

Examples illustrate replacing ornamentation with food production: pumpkins on council
strips (grassy verges between sidewalk and street) show public spaces yielding food; the
Davis village project models community-integrated productive landscaping with front
yards designed for food. Front-yard gardening makes agriculture visible, normalizes
productive landscaping, and inspires neighbors.

## Economic Self-Sufficiency

Mollison cites individuals earning significant income from small-scale intensive
gardening: a parsley farmer near Melbourne sustained a full living from a tiny intensively
managed area; a city clerk grew flowers commercially in residential garden space,
eventually changing careers. These demonstrate that intensive mulching, strategic plant
placement, and high-frequency management generate genuine economic return from modest
holdings. Intensity of management, not extent of land, determines productive output per
unit area.

## Design Principles Summary

Zone one design rests on interconnected principles: complete soil coverage through
mulching, spatial arrangement based on harvest frequency, defensive perimeter design
against weeds and wildlife, integration of water and wind elements for microclimate and
pest control, and strategic animal placement for manure cycling. The zone functions as
the permaculture system's engine — its small area producing disproportionate yields
through careful design and daily attention. These principles are universally applicable
regardless of climate or scale, making zone one the most accessible entry point into
permaculture practice.
