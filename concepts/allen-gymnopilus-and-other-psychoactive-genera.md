# Gymnopilus and Other Psychoactive Genera in Australia

Beyond the well-known *Psilocybe* and *Copelandia* species, several other
genera of mushrooms found in Australia and New Zealand contain
psilocybin and/or other psychoactive alkaloids. These lesser-known genera are
important for understanding the full diversity of [[cattle-dispersal-psychoactive-fungi-australia]] in the
Australasian region.

## *Gymnopilus* Species

*Gymnopilus* is a genus of wood-inhabiting mushrooms, many of which are large
and conspicuous. Some species are known to contain psilocybin and psilocine,
though they are generally less potent than *Psilocybe* or *Copelandia*
species. The genus is characterized by its rusty-orange to rusty-brown [[muscarine]]. However, some *Inocybe*
species are known to contain psilocybin, making the genus potentially
dangerous for amateur collectors who cannot distinguish between
psilocybin-containing and muscarine-containing species.

### Australian Records

*Inocybe* species are common in Australian forests, often associated with
mycorrhizal tree roots, particularly in Eucalyptus and *Nothofagus*
forests. The presence of psilocybin in Australian *Inocybe* species has
not been conclusively documented, though the genus is widespread and
diverse in the region. The risk of confusing psilocybin-containing
Inocybes with deadly muscarine-containing species makes this a genus
that should be approached with extreme caution.

### Identification Challenges

*Inocybe* species are notoriously difficult to identify, often requiring
[[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] of spores and cystidia. They are typically small to
medium-sized brown mushrooms with fibrous or scaly caps and a distinctive
"mousy" or spermatic odor. These characteristics make them unappealing to
most collectors, which fortunately reduces the risk of accidental
ingestion of toxic species.

## *Panaeolus* Species (Non-Copelandia)

### *Panaeolus foenisecii* (Pers. ex Fr.) Kuhn.

Commonly known as the "mower's mushroom" or "haymaker's mushroom," this
species is one of the most ubiquitous mushrooms in Australian lawns and
pastures. Historically there was some controversy over whether it contained
psilocybin, but modern analyses have generally found it to be non-
psychoactive or only very weakly active. It is common on grass and dung in
lawns, pastures, and gardens throughout Australia and New Zealand.

### *Panaeolus antillarum* (Fr.) Dennis

Known as the "hysteria fungus," *P. antillarum* has been implicated in
historical accounts of animal poisoning and hallucinogenic effects. Despite
its reputation, modern analyses have not confirmed the presence of
psilocybin in this species. It is a large species found on cow dung in
tropical and subtropical regions of Australia. Its common name derives from
early reports of cattle exhibiting unusual behavior after grazing in
pastures where it grew.

### *Panaeolus acuminatus* (Schulz. ex Fr.) Quell.

A small, delicate species found on dung in Australia. It is not considered
psychoactive but is included here for completeness given its frequent
occurrence alongside psychoactive *Panaeolus* and *Copelandia* species in
the same dung habitats.

## *Panaeolina foenisecii*

Sometimes placed in its own genus *Panaeolina* rather than *Panaeolus*,
this species is the most common mushroom in Australian suburban lawns. It
is small, brown, and grows in grass from autumn through spring. Despite
its abundance, it is not considered significantly psychoactive. The
taxonomic distinction between *Panaeolina* and *Panaeolus* hinges on the
presence of a germ pore on the spores: *Panaeolina* spores lack a germ
pore, while *Panaeolus* spores possess one.
