## Fodder Pond Sequences

Fodder pond sequences are cascading interconnected ponds where
organisms flow progressively downstream through trophic levels,
from manure producers at the top to predator fish at the outlet.

This mimics natural stream ecology while giving designers control
over species composition and harvest points at every stage.

## The Cascading Design Principle

As water falls through a pipe from one pond to the next, upstream
migration is prevented while downstream migration is possible or
even aided.

Arranged along spoon or V-drains, multiple origins and
destinations can be achieved.

A perched pond above the predator system maintains a
predator-free forage-fish polyculture until organisms overflow
downstream.

## Stage A — Origin Pond

The highest pond receives clean water and hosts foundation
species: manurial animals (ducks, freshwater mussels, amphipods,
mud shrimp, crabs, snails, shrimp, frog larvae); non-invasive
plants (taro, [[azolla]]); plus inevitable insect colonizers.

Structures include rotted logs, reed beds, and brush piles.
Edges feature [[comfrey]], vining legumes, and fruit plants.

## Stage B — Secondary Pond

Stage A organisms flow downstream, joining more predatory
invertebrates and plankton-feeding fish (minnows, Paragalaxids,
Saxilaga).

Useful edibles like kangkong grow on mounds; reed beds and logs
provide refuge; edges feature mulberry, berries, legumes, and
comfrey.

## Stages C and D — Predator Ponds

These follow the same enrichment pattern.

Products can be harvested at any level: shrimp, snails, ducks,
frogs, taro, [[tilapia]], perch, or trout.

Even pH can be altered through the chain for different species,
and niches arranged for both high-oxygen and low-oxygen-demand
organisms.

Systems can accept phosphate- and nitrate-polluted water if
appropriate processing organisms are present.

## Increasing Diversity

Three key strategies:

1. **Headwater locations** create small ecological islands
2. **Salting selected ponds** develops semi-estuarine communities
3. **Manuring with trace elements and fertilizers** produces
   large quantities of forage fish and crustaceans for
   downstream predators

## The Swingle Principle

Swingle (1966) proposed that up to 50% of a catfish pond should
be shallows — primarily to provide food (shrimp, invertebrates)
for the main fish, not at the cost of reducing fish numbers but
by reducing the energy needed to feed them.

## Yield Expectations

- **Extensive unfertilized**: 10–90 kg/ha/year
- **Fertilized extensive**: 200–500 kg/ha/year
- **Intensive polycultures**: 2,000–5,000 kg/ha/year
- **Cage culture with accessory foods**: 5,000–20,000+ kg/ha/year

In intensive systems, fertilizer and food represent 60–96% of
costs, making on-site food production increasingly critical.

## The Garden-Pond Symbiosis

Tilapia and carp eat starchy crops (pumpkin, banana, sweet
potato, taro, beans, grains) plus fallen insects, seeds, and
fruit from fringing vegetation.

Tilapia especially consumes many fruits and leaves from garden
weeds and vines — which is why market gardens and fish ponds
belong together.

The relationship is doubly beneficial: nutrient-rich pond water
doubles garden crop production compared to reservoir irrigation.

## Grass Carp and Prawn Synergies

Grass carp trim fringing vegetation like [[dolichos]], preventing
weed encroachment into taro.

They not only coexist with Macrobrachium prawns but increase
prawn yields through fecal detritus, matching growth from
commercial chicken pellets in monoculture ponds.

## Design Principle

It is always preferable to create conditions for yield at the
pond edge than to import food from elsewhere at great energy
cost.

Planting comfrey, clover, Dolichos, [[tradescantia]], and lucerne
along margins provides complete fodder systems that eliminate
external feed dependencies.
