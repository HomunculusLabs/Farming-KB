# Opium, Tobacco, and Paradoxical Attitudes

## The Opium Wars

McKenna devoted considerable attention to the Opium Wars of the 19th century as a paradigmatic example of the paradoxical attitudes that dominator cultures hold toward drugs. The British Empire, having established a lucrative trade in Indian opium, forced China to accept the drug through military coercion when the Chinese government attempted to ban it. The resulting wars (1839-1842 and 1856-1860) established a pattern that McKenna saw recurring throughout modern history: the powerful dictate which drugs are acceptable and which are prohibited, based not on pharmacological merit but on economic and political interests.

The Opium Wars reveal the core hypocrisy of dominator drug policy: the same British establishment that used military force to open Chinese markets to opium simultaneously enacted draconian domestic laws against opium use at home. The drug was profitable when sold to foreigners but dangerous when consumed by British citizens. This double standard, McKenna argued, is not an anomaly but the defining feature of dominator drug policy.

## Opium as Medicine and Commodity

Opium has a long and complex history as both medicine and recreational substance. The opium poppy (Papaver somniferum) was cultivated in the ancient Mediterranean and Near East, and opium preparations were used as painkillers, sedatives, and treatments for diarrhea for thousands of years. Paracelsus's laudanum (tincture of opium in alcohol) became a standard medical preparation in Renaissance Europe, and opium derivatives like morphine and codeine remain essential medicines today.

McKenna noted that opium's medical utility has always coexisted uneasily with its recreational and addictive potential. The same compound that eases suffering also enslaves. This duality, McKenna suggested, reflects the broader ambivalence of dominator culture toward all psychoactive substances: they are simultaneously valued as medicines and feared as threats to social order.

## Tobacco: The Dominator Drug

Tobacco, McKenna argued, is the quintessential dominator drug. Unlike psychedelics, which expand consciousness and promote egalitarianism, tobacco is a drug of compulsion and habit that serves the interests of power. Nicotine is highly addictive, produces a mild stimulant effect that enhances focus and alertness (useful for repetitive labor), and generates enormous profits for the corporations that sell it.

The history of tobacco in the New World is inseparable from the history of colonialism and slavery. Tobacco plantations in Virginia and the Caribbean were among the first large-scale agricultural enterprises in the Americas, and they depended heavily on enslaved labor. The profits from tobacco helped fund the expansion of British imperial power in the same way that [[mckenna-sugar-slavery-and-dominance]] did.

## The Paradox of Prohibition

McKenna identified a fundamental paradox at the heart of drug prohibition: the drugs that are most aggressively prohibited — psychedelics, cannabis, coca — are generally the least harmful to health and social order, while the drugs that are most aggressively promoted and subsidized — sugar, alcohol, tobacco, caffeine — are among the most harmful. This paradox, he argued, cannot be explained by pharmacology or public health; it can only be explained by politics and economics.

The [[mckenna-drug-war-and-human-freedom]] is, in McKenna's view, not a public health initiative but an instrument of social control. By criminalizing certain drugs while promoting others, the dominator culture reinforces its preferred modes of consciousness — alert, competitive, aggressive — while suppressing alternative modes — introspective, empathetic, boundary-dissolving — that might challenge the existing power structure.

## The Role of Racism

McKenna traced the paradoxical attitudes toward drugs to racism and cultural imperialism. The drugs associated with non-white cultures — cannabis (associated with Mexicans and African Americans), coca (associated with Andean peoples), opium (associated with Chinese immigrants) — were systematically demonized through campaigns of disinformation and racial fear-mongering. The drugs associated with European culture — alcohol, tobacco, caffeine — were normalized and promoted.

Harry Anslinger's campaign against marijuana in the 1930s explicitly played on racial fears, with Anslinger claiming that marijuana caused "reefer madness" among Mexican immigrants and African Americans. The subsequent criminalization of marijuana was, McKenna argued, as much about controlling racial minorities as about controlling drugs. See [[mckenna-cannabis-culture-history]].

## Coca vs. Cocaine

The distinction between coca leaf and cocaine illustrates the paradox particularly clearly. Coca leaf, chewed by Andean peoples for thousands of years, is a mild stimulant with low addiction potential and significant nutritional value. Cocaine, the refined alkaloid extracted from coca, is a powerful and addictive stimulant with significant health risks. McKenna noted that the United States has spent billions of dollars eradicating coca plants in Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia — destroying a traditional and relatively benign practice — while simultaneously tolerating (and in some contexts promoting) the use of far more dangerous substances like alcohol and prescription opioids.

## Toward a Rational Drug Policy

McKenna argued that a rational approach to drug policy would begin by distinguishing between drugs that expand consciousness and promote well-being (psychedelics, cannabis in moderation) and drugs that contract consciousness and promote dependence (heroin, methamphetamine, alcohol in excess). Rather than criminalizing all psychoactive substances, society should, in his view, create a legal framework that promotes safe, responsible use of consciousness-expanding substances while providing treatment and support for those struggling with addiction.

This approach, which anticipates the [[psilocybin-and-mystical-experience]] and [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]] movements of the 21st century, remains controversial but has gained significant mainstream support in recent years. The decriminalization of psilocybin in Oregon (2020) and Colorado (2022), and the rescheduling of cannabis across much of the world, represent partial vindications of McKenna's position.

## Alcohol as the Dominator Drug

McKenna reserved some of his most pointed analysis for alcohol, which he considered the quintessential drug of dominator culture. In his framework, alcohol and psilocybin represent opposite poles of psychoactive experience — and the social systems that prefer each drug reflect fundamentally different relationships to consciousness, power, and nature.
### Alcohol vs. Mushroom Consciousness
Where psilocybin dissolves ego boundaries, promotes empathy, and reveals the interconnectedness of all life, alcohol reinforces ego, aggression, and social hierarchy. Alcohol is a depressant that disinhibits without expanding awareness — it removes social restraints without providing insight. McKenna argued that this makes alcohol the ideal drug for a culture built on domination: it facilitates competition, violence, sexual aggression, and submission to authority, all while dulling the critical faculties that might question the dominator order. The mushroom, by contrast, promotes egalitarianism, ecological awareness, and the direct experience of a reality that transcends social hierarchy.
### The Indo-European Alcohol Complex
McKenna traced the association between alcohol and dominator culture to the Indo-European peoples of the Eurasian steppe. These were the Kurgan pastoralists described by Marija Gimbutas — the horse-riding warriors whose invasions overthrew the goddess-worshipping cultures of Old Europe. Their rituals centered on fermented beverages: mead, beer, and later wine. The Vedic Soma ritual, the Greek symposium, the Roman convivium — all were organized around communal drinking that reinforced social bonds within a hierarchy. The intoxication of alcohol promotes bonhomie among in-group members while heightening hostility toward out-groups, a combination perfectly suited to a warrior culture.
### Christianity and the Sacrament of Wine
The Christian Eucharist, in McKenna's reading, represents the final institutionalization of alcohol as the dominator sacrament — replacing the visionary plant sacraments of earlier religions with a symbolic consumption of wine that provides no visionary experience. The Eleusinian Mysteries, which had sustained a tradition of genuine plant-based ecstatic revelation for nearly two thousand years, were suppressed by Christian authorities in A.D. 268. McKenna saw this as the decisive moment when Western civilization severed its last remaining connection to the "vegetable Logos" and committed fully to the dominator trajectory. See [[mckenna-food-gods-partnership-dominator]] for the broader framework.
### The Social Costs of Alcohol
McKenna noted the bitter irony that the drug responsible for the most devastating social costs — domestic violence, liver disease, accidental death, addiction — is the one that every dominator society has actively promoted, subsidized, and celebrated, while criminalizing far less harmful substances. The global burden of disease attributable to alcohol vastly exceeds that of all illegal drugs combined, a fact that, in McKenna's view, exposes the [[mckenna-drug-war-and-human-freedom]] as fundamentally dishonest.

## Modern Parallels

McKenna's analysis of the Opium Wars and 19th-century drug policy finds disturbing parallels in the 21st century, where the patterns of dominator drug policy have intensified rather than diminished.
### The Opioid Epidemic
The American opioid crisis (roughly 1995-present) recapitulates the Opium Wars pattern with grim precision. Pharmaceutical corporations — principally Purdue Pharma, manufacturer of OxyContin — aggressively marketed prescription opioids to doctors and patients, generating billions in profits while creating a nationwide epidemic of addiction. When prescription opioids became restricted, users migrated to heroin and then to fentanyl, killing over 500,000 Americans between 1999 and 2020. The crisis was driven not by pharmacological necessity but by corporate profit — precisely the pattern McKenna identified in the British opium trade. The racial dimension also persists: the opioid epidemic was initially treated as a public health crisis when it affected predominantly white communities, even as the crack cocaine epidemic a generation earlier had been treated as a criminal justice problem affecting Black communities. See [[mckenna-drug-war-and-human-freedom]] for McKenna's analysis of how race shapes drug policy.
### Ibogaine and Psychedelic Addiction Treatment
The failure of dominator drug policy to address addiction has paradoxically strengthened the case for the very substances it prohibits. Ibogaine, a naturally occurring psychedelic from the West African shrub Tabernanthe iboga, has shown remarkable ability to interrupt opioid dependence, often eliminating withdrawal symptoms and reducing craving after a single administration. See [[iboga-and-ibogaine-profile]] for details on ibogaine's pharmacology and anti-addictive mechanisms. The fact that this plant-based treatment remains illegal in many countries while pharmaceutical opioids were actively promoted illustrates McKenna's central paradox: the drugs that might genuinely help people are suppressed, while the drugs that enslave them are subsidized.
### The Shifting Landscape
The partial decriminalization of psilocybin in Oregon (2020) and Colorado (2022), the rescheduling of cannabis across much of the world, and the renewal of clinical research into psychedelic therapy at institutions like Johns Hopkins and Imperial College London represent a potential crack in the dominator edifice. McKenna would likely have seen these developments as early signs of the [[mckenna-archaic-revival]] — a hesitant, partial recovery of the plant-based knowledge that dominator culture spent millennia suppressing. Whether this trend continues or is rolled back remains an open question, but the fundamental tension McKenna identified — between drugs of domination and drugs of liberation — continues to define global drug policy.
### Caffeine: The Acceptable Stimulant
McKenna's analysis of dominator drugs also extends to caffeine, the world's most widely consumed psychoactive substance. Like alcohol, tobacco, and sugar, caffeine is universally available, socially encouraged, and rarely recognized as a drug despite its powerful pharmacological effects. Caffeine promotes alertness, focus, and productivity — qualities that serve industrial and corporate economies. McKenna noted that the global spread of coffee and tea paralleled the spread of industrial capitalism: coffee houses were centers of commercial and intellectual activity in 17th-century Europe, and the British tea trade — enforced by the same imperial apparatus that fought the Opium Wars — was among the most profitable enterprises of the colonial era. The fact that caffeine, alcohol, sugar, and tobacco together constitute the pharmacological foundation of modern industrial society — while the plants that expand consciousness and promote ecological awareness are criminalized — is, for McKenna, the defining absurdity of dominator drug policy.

## Key Points

- McKenna's analysis connects psychoactive plant use to fundamental patterns in human cultural evolution
- The [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]], while controversial, raises important questions about the role of psychoactive substances in human cognitive development
- McKenna's critique of dominator culture draws on both anthropological evidence and contemporary political analysis
- The [[psychedelic-mushroom-history]] provides a broad context for understanding McKenna's specific claims
- The pattern of criminalizing consciousness-expanding plants while promoting consciousness-contracting substances is, for McKenna, the defining hypocrisy of dominator culture
- McKenna's analysis anticipates the modern harm reduction movement and rational drug policy reform

## Significance in Psychedelic Thought

McKenna's work represents one of the most ambitious attempts to construct a comprehensive theory of the role of psychoactive plants in human history and culture. While academic anthropologists have been skeptical of many specific claims, his ideas have been enormously influential in psychedelic culture and continue to inspire research into the relationship between psychoactive substances and human consciousness. The concept of an Archaic Revival — the recovery of ancient plant-based wisdom — has resonated with the growing interest in ethnobotany, ecological consciousness, and the therapeutic potential of psychedelic substances documented in [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]].

## Related McKenna Topics

McKenna's analysis fits within his broader framework of the [[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-thesis]], which traces the relationship between humans and psychoactive plants from prehistory to the present. The themes explored here connect directly to his discussions of [[mckenna-dissolution-of-the-dominator-culture]] and the [[mckenna-archaic-revival]] as counter-movements to the suppression of plant-based ecstatic religion.

## The Sugar Parallel

McKenna extended his analysis of dominator drugs to include sugar, which he regarded as the most insidious of all dominator substances precisely because it is not recognized as a drug. Sugar is addictive, widely available, heavily subsidized, and devastating to health — contributing to obesity, diabetes, heart disease, and systemic inflammation — yet it is marketed as food and given to children from infancy. The [[mckenna-sugar-slavery-and-dominance]] parallels the opium and tobacco stories: sugar plantations in the Caribbean and Brazil were among the most brutal slave economies in history, and the profits from sugar helped finance the industrial revolution and the expansion of European imperial power. McKenna argued that sugar, like alcohol and tobacco, serves dominator culture by pacifying the population while generating enormous profits for those who control its production and distribution. The fact that sugar is consumed universally and unquestioningly — unlike alcohol or tobacco, which are at least recognized as drugs — makes it the most successful instrument of dominator pharmacology.

## The Pharmaceutical Continuum

The pattern McKenna identified in the Opium Wars — powerful entities profiting from addictive substances while criminalizing alternatives — did not end in the 19th century. The modern pharmaceutical industry's relationship with psychoactive drugs represents a continuation of the same dynamic, reframed through the language of medicine and patent law. Opioid manufacturers like Purdue Pharma generated billions by marketing highly addictive prescription painkillers while funding anti-marijuana campaigns that positioned cannabis as dangerous — a substance that, in McKenna's framework, belongs to the partnership category of consciousness-expanding plants. The pharmaceutical industry's hostility to psychedelic research, which persisted from the late 1960s until the early 2000s, can be understood as a structural defense of the dominator pharmacological order: patented, profit-generating, dependence-producing drugs are promoted, while non-patentable, consciousness-expanding plants are suppressed. The scheduling of 2C-B in 1995 — despite its relatively benign safety profile compared to alcohol or prescription opioids — exemplifies this pattern. See [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]] for the contemporary overturning of this suppression.
