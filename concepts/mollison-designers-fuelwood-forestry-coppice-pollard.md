# Fuelwood Forestry: Coppice, Pollard, and Sustainable Yield

Mollison addresses fuelwood production as a critical component of permaculture
land use in Permaculture: A Designer's Manual. In a world increasingly dependent
on fossil fuels, the sustainable production of wood fuel from designed
woodlots represents both an energy strategy and an ecological rehabilitation
tool. Coppicing and pollarding are ancient management practices that convert
broadleaf trees into renewable, high-yield fuel sources while maintaining
continuous canopy cover and soil protection.

## The Case for Fuelwood

Wood remains the primary cooking and heating fuel for a significant portion of
the world's population. Even in industrialized nations, wood fuel offers a
renewable, carbon-neutral energy source when managed sustainably -- the carbon
released during combustion equals the carbon sequestered during tree growth.
Mollison argues that every permaculture site should dedicate a portion of its
area to fuelwood production, integrated with [[food-forest-design-guide]]
and [[permaculture-earthworks]].

## Coppicing

Coppicing is the practice of cutting a tree at or near ground level to
stimulate regrowth from the stump (stool). Most broadleaf species resprout
vigorously after cutting, producing multiple straight stems suitable for fuel,
fencing, and craft use.

**How coppicing works:** When a tree is cut, dormant buds at the base of the
stump and in the root collar are released from apical dominance. These buds
develop into new shoots that grow rapidly, supported by the existing root
system. The result is a multi-stemmed stool that can be re-harvested on a
regular rotation.

**Coppice rotation:** The interval between harvests depends on the intended
product and species growth rate:

- Short rotation (2-5 years): Small-diameter wood for kindling, bean poles,
  pea sticks, craft material. Species: willow, poplar, hazel, alder.
- Medium rotation (5-15 years): Medium-diameter wood for domestic fuel, fence
  rails, tool handles. Species: oak, ash, sweet chestnut, birch, hazel.
- Long rotation (15-30 years): Larger-diameter wood for construction timber
  and industrial fuel. Species: oak, ash, beech.

For domestic fuelwood, a 7-15 year rotation produces wood of convenient size
for stoves and fireplaces. Mollison recommends a mixed rotation system with
different areas cut in different years to ensure continuous annual production.

**Coppice management:** Cut stools cleanly at an angle to shed water. Avoid
splitting the stool or damaging the bark. The best time to cut is during
dormancy (late autumn to early spring). Cut in sequence: if managing a block
of coppice, cut one section per year on rotation rather than clear-cutting the
entire area.

## Pollarding

Pollarding is similar to coppicing but cuts are made higher on the trunk
(typically 2-3 m above ground). This keeps the new growth above the reach of
grazing animals and produces a high canopy that shades the ground while
allowing pasture or crops beneath.

Pollarding is particularly valuable in silvopastoral systems where livestock
and trees share the same land. The elevated canopy reduces browsing damage and
provides shade for animals. Pollarded trees also have a distinctive, gnarled
appearance that creates valuable habitat features.

**Pollard cycle:** Similar to coppice rotations. Cut every 5-15 years
depending on species and desired product size. The "lollipop" form created by
repeated pollarding is characteristic of traditional European and Asian
landscapes.

**Species suitable for pollarding:** Willow, poplar, ash, oak, elm, linden,
mulberry, horse chestnut, and many Eucalyptus species. Avoid pollarding
conifers -- most do not resprout reliably.

## Fuelwood Species Selection

Mollison provides guidance on selecting species for fuelwood production based
on growth rate, calorific value, coppicing ability, and site adaptation:

**Fast-growing, lower calorific value:**
- Willow (Salix spp.): Very fast growth, excellent coppicer, grows in wet
  soils. Calorific value: ~16-17 MJ/kg (air-dried). Best in short rotation.
- Poplar (Populus spp.): Fast growth on moist sites. Calorific value:
  ~17-18 MJ/kg. Good for short to medium rotation.

**Moderate growth, good calorific value:**
- Hazel (Corylus avellana): Excellent coppicer, produces straight stems,
  valuable for craft use as well as fuel. Calorific value: ~18-19 MJ/kg.
- Alder (Alnus spp.): Nitrogen-fixing, tolerates wet soils. Burns well
  when dry. Calorific value: ~18 MJ/kg.
- Birch (Betula spp.): Good calorific value (~19-20 MJ/kg), burns well even
  when slightly green. Useful for kindling.

**Slower growth, high calorific value:**
- Oak: Excellent fuel, dense, long-burning. ~19-20 MJ/kg. Good long-rotation coppicer.
- Ash: Premier fuelwood -- burns green or dry, ~20-21 MJ/kg, excellent coppicer.
  Mollison's top recommendation for temperate systems.
- Beech: Dense, hot-burning. ~20 MJ/kg. Good coppicer.
- Sweet chestnut: Fast for a hardwood, coppices well, ~19-20 MJ/kg. Dual-purpose:
  fuel and nut production.
- Eucalyptus: Fast-growing, ~19-20 MJ/kg, coppices vigorously. Best in
  subtropical and Mediterranean climates.

## Sustainable Yield Calculation

Sustainable yield is the maximum annual harvest without depleting the resource:
Annual yield = (Total area x Mean Annual Increment) / Rotation length. For
example, 1 hectare of ash at 8 cubic meters/hectare/year on a 10-year rotation
yields 8 cubic meters per year sustainably. Well-managed temperate coppice
produces 5-15 tonnes of air-dried wood per hectare per year. One tonne of
air-dried hardwood roughly equals the energy of 400-500 liters of heating oil.

## Woodlot Design

Design fuelwood woodlots using [[pdc-zone-analysis-guide]] principles.
In temperate climates, woodlots typically occupy Zone 3-4. Design
considerations:

**Species mix:** Plant multiple species for resilience against pests, diseases,
and climate variation. Include nitrogen-fixing species (alder, black locust)
to improve soil fertility. Maintain a proportion of long-rotation species
for larger fuelwood and habitat.

**Access:** Design rides (access paths) at regular intervals for extraction
and firebreaks. Rides also provide edge habitat and sunny glades.

**Understory:** Use the shadier, protected environment for shade-tolerant crops
(mushrooms, ginseng, medicinal herbs) or livestock shelter.

**Integration with water:** Position woodlots on upper slopes to protect
[[mollison-designers-pond-construction-liner-sealing]] and riparian areas from
sediment and nutrient runoff. Maximize edge between woodlot and open land using
irregular boundaries for greatest diversity and productivity.
