Sweet Fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) is a versatile perennial herb grown for its edible bulbs, leaves, and seeds. It has many medicinal uses but can be invasive in North America. It serves multiple functions in [[permaculture-wildlife-corridors]] as a culinary herb, medicinal plant, and insectary.

## Botanical Description

Fennel is an aromatic perennial herb growing 3-6 feet (90-180 cm) tall with feathery, finely divided, anise-scented foliage. It produces flat clusters of small yellow flowers in mid to late summer. The plant has a thick, bulbous base (in Florence fennel varieties) and a deep taproot. It can live 5-10 years and self-seeds prolifically.

## Uses

- Edible (bulb, leaves, seeds)

- Pest control (companion planting)

- Medicinal herb

- [[permaculture-livestock-forage-systems]] — attracts pollinators

- [[mulch-producing-plants]] from spent stalks

## Growing Conditions

- Easy to grow

- Full sun

- Well-drained soil

- Prefers soil pH 6.0-8.0

- Drought tolerant once established

- USDA zones 5-10

- Warning: Listed as invasive in parts of North America — manage spread carefully

## Propagation

Fennel is most commonly grown from seed, direct sown in spring after frost danger has passed. Seeds germinate in 7-14 days. Thin seedlings to 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) apart. Division of established clumps in early spring also works well. Florence fennel (bulb type) is typically grown as an annual.

## Harvesting and Use

- Dried seeds: used as a spice in cooking and baking

- Root bulb and leaves: used raw or cooked in salads, soups, and roasted dishes

- Many traditional medicinal applications

- Harvest seeds when they turn brown and dry on the plant

- Cut leaves as needed throughout the growing season

## Companion Planting

Fennel is a valuable companion plant that attracts beneficial insects including parasitic wasps, ladybugs, and hoverflies. However, it is a poor companion for most garden vegetables — it can inhibit the growth of beans, tomatoes, kohlrabi, and cilantro. Plant fennel in a dedicated herb area rather than intermixed with vegetables.

## Medicinal Uses

Fennel has a long history of medicinal use. The seeds are a traditional remedy for digestive issues including bloating, gas, and colic. Fennel tea is a common after-dinner digestive in many cultures. The essential oil has antimicrobial and antispasmodic properties. Fennel is also used traditionally to support respiratory health.

## Critical Safety Warning

Poison Hemlock (Conium maculatum) looks very similar to fennel and grows near water or wet soil. To distinguish them:

- Crush the leaves and smell

- Fennel has the distinctive smell of licorice

- Hemlock smells musty

Always verify identification before consuming any fennel-like wild plant. Hemlock is deadly poisonous — even small amounts can be fatal.

## Culinary Uses

Fennel bulbs are delicious roasted, grilled, or shaved raw into salads. The feathery fronds make a flavorful garnish. Seeds are used in Italian sausage, bread, pickles, and herbal teas. Florence fennel (Foeniculum vulgare var. dulce) is the preferred bulb type for cooking.

## Pests and Diseases

Fennel is generally pest-free due to its aromatic oils. Aphids may occasionally affect new growth but rarely cause significant damage. The swallowtail butterfly caterpillar feeds on fennel foliage — these can be left alone or moved to other plants, as the butterflies are beneficial pollinators.
