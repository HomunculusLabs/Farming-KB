## Fungi in Population and Community Regulation

Population and community regulation by fungi occurs through both promotion and reduction of growth, fitness, and reproductive potential of organisms. When the effect of a fungus is asymmetric across species — benefiting some more than others, or harming some while sparing others — the competitive balance within the community shifts over successive generations, leading to changes in species dominance and community composition.

## Mechanisms of Fungal-Mediated Regulation

Fungi influence populations and communities through diverse mechanisms. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] enhance plant nutrient acquisition and water uptake, endophytes reduce herbivore damage, and [[saprotrophic-fungi]] drive decomposition and [[nutrient-mineralization]]. Conversely, fungal pathogens suppress plant production (both biomass and fecundity), reduce animal populations, and can drive species toward local extinction. The net effect on any community depends on the balance between these positive and negative interactions, and critically, on whether the effects are distributed symmetrically or asymmetrically across community members.

## Mycorrhizae and Plant Succession

### The Role of Mycorrhizal Inoculum in Primary Succession

One of the major determinants of success for early colonizing plants during seral succession is nutrient availability. Mycorrhizal associations enhance a plant's ability to sequester nutrients from limited resources, providing a competitive advantage in nutrient-poor successional environments. The dispersal of hypogeous fungal spores by rodents is an important mechanism for delivering mycorrhizal inoculum to plants establishing on bare ground.

Spore deposition by small mammals is not random — animals defecate in middens and deposit more spores at active feeding sites than elsewhere. This creates patchy distributions of mycorrhizal inoculum potential across the landscape, which in turn influences which plant species can successfully establish in each microhabitat.

### Evidence from Volcanic Succession Sites

Allen (1991) demonstrated that vegetation colonizing Mount Saint Helens after the 1980 eruption consisted entirely of mycorrhizal species (both arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal). In contrast, studies in the Galápagos Islands found mixtures of arbuscular mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal plants, with the distribution related to soil nutrient content — nonmycorrhizal plants in more fertile lowland soils and mycorrhizal plants in poorer rocky soils.

At Mt. Koma, Japan, Titus and Tsuyuzaki (2002) found that arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization of *Agrostis scabra* showed no effect of microsite, while *Campanula lasiocarpa* had higher colonization rates near rock than on flat sites. Willow (*Salix reinii*) was heavily ectomycorrhizal across all sites, suggesting that models of mycorrhizal dependence in succession are both environmentally and species-dependent.

### Glacier Forefront Succession

The forefront of Lyman Glacier provides a model system for studying mycorrhizal roles in primary succession. Trappe (1988) identified spores of various fungal genera in the feces of pika, voles, chipmunks, marmots, mountain goats, and mule deer on the glacier forefront. Jumpponen et al. (1999) identified "safe sites" — concave surfaces of coarse rocky particles that trap tree seeds and protect them from desiccation. These sites also concentrate foraging mammals, leading to deposition of mycorrhizal spore-laden feces that enhance seedling survival.

The dark-septate mycorrhizal fungus *[[phialocephala-fortinii]]* significantly enhanced growth of lodgepole pine (*[[pinus-contorta]]*), an early colonizer of the glacier forefront, but only in the presence of added nitrogen. Total plant phosphorus, however, was significantly enhanced by mycorrhizal colonization even without added nitrogen. During succession on glacial till, microbial communities shift [[query-how-does-soil-naturally-transition-from-bacterial-to-fungal-dominance]], with carbon-use efficiency changing from high respiration rates to biomass accumulation — indicating that fungi stabilize the developing ecosystem and facilitate net carbon fixation.

### Succession of Ectomycorrhizal Communities

Ectomycorrhizal species undergo successional changes during primary succession. Jumpponen et al. (1999, 2002) identified 68 ectomycorrhizal species from 25 genera on the Lyman Glacier forefront, with no single species occurring on all three successional sites. Ectomycorrhizal diversity peaked where tree canopies began to overlap, consistent with other studies (Dighton et al., 1986; Last et al., 1987; Visser, 1995). This diversity peak likely relates to the paucity of available phosphorus and increasing proportions of nutrients locked in organic forms.

Conn and Dighton (2000) showed that ectomycorrhizal community composition on different tree litters reflects appropriate enzyme functions relative to nutrient availability. Where phosphorus was immobilized during early litter decomposition, the ectomycorrhizal community contained a greater proportion of acid phosphatase-producing types.

### The Importance of Mycelial Networks

Maintaining a continuous mycelial network is critical for rapid mycorrhizal development during colonization. Amaranthus and Perry (1989) showed that Douglas fir planted into partially cleared sites (retaining mycorrhizal roots on remaining trees) had approximately 90% survival, compared to only 50% in totally cleared areas. The existing mycelial network provides ecosystem stability through carbon [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]] between connected plants, allowing new recruits to access larger resource pools than they could independently.

### Driver vs. Passenger Hypotheses

For arbuscular mycorrhizal communities, Hart et al. (2001) proposed two competing hypotheses: the **driver hypothesis**, in which mycorrhizal fungi actively determine plant species composition through interspecific differences in colonization and persistence potential; and the **passenger hypothesis**, in which mycorrhizal communities are passively shaped by the plant community and environmental conditions. Distinguishing between these mechanisms remains a central challenge in mycorrhizal ecology.

## Ecosystem Services Provided by Fungi

Fungi provide a comprehensive suite of ecosystem services that underpin population and community regulation:

- **Soil formation**: Rock dissolution by lichens, saprotrophs, and mycorrhizae; particle binding
- **Soil fertility**: Decomposition of organic residues, nutrient mineralization, soil aggregate stability
- **Primary production**: Direct lichen production, enhanced nutrient accessibility through mycorrhizae, plant defense
- **Plant community structure**: Mediation of plant-plant interactions through mycorrhizae and pathogens
- **Secondary production**: Fungi as food sources for fauna
- **Population regulation**: Pathogens controlling biomass of plants and animals
- **Pollutant modification**: Degradation by saprotrophs and mycorrhizae
- **Carbon sequestration**: Carbon storage through mycorrhizal networks

## Synthesis

Fungi regulate populations through both positive and negative pathways. The critical insight is that when fungal effects are asymmetric — benefiting some species more than others, or harming some disproportionately — they act as selective forces that reshape community composition over ecological time scales. Understanding which fungi act as drivers versus passengers, and under what environmental conditions each mechanism predominates, is essential for predicting ecosystem responses to disturbance, succession, and global change.
