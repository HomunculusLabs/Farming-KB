# Ingham — Cover Crops and Soil Biology

Elaine Ingham's approach to cover crops emphasizes their role as living biological management tools that feed specific soil organism groups, maintain soil structure, and prevent the loss of soil fertility during fallow periods.

## Cover Crops as Biological Food Sources

Green cover crops through winter supply a source of food for soil microbes, as do the residues of harvested plants. The key principle is that different cover crop types feed different organism groups:
- **Bacterial-dominated plants** (annual grasses, brassicas, legumes) support bacterial growth in soil
- **Fungal-dominated plants** (perennials, broadleaf plants, mature grasses) support fungal growth

The particular set of microbes that a specific plant needs will be benefited by growing that plant as a cover crop. The residues must remain on the soil surface as foods to sustain the microbes through winter and into the next growing season.

## The Tillage-Cover Crop Interaction

When cover crop residues are plowed or tilled into soil:
- Bacteria bloom rapidly (disturbance stimulates bacterial growth)
- Organic residues are burned off much more rapidly
- Soils become bacterial-dominated
- Organic matter is lost
- Soil fertility declines over time

This has been documented repeatedly in agriculture. The alternative is to maintain residues on the surface (mulch) or minimally incorporate them, preserving fungal networks and organic matter.

## Returning Fertility Through Biology

To return fertility to soil after conventional management, three elements are needed:
1. **The right organisms** — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes in appropriate ratios for the desired crop
2. **The right foods** — organic matter and specific food resources for each organism group
3. **The right habitat** — adequate oxygen, moisture, temperature, and soil structure for the target organisms

Cover crops contribute all three: they host specific organism communities on their roots, provide food through root exudates and surface residues, and their root systems maintain soil structure.

## Non-Host Cover Crops for Nematode Management

Cover crops can be selected as non-host plants for specific pest nematodes:
- The cover crop must be a non-host for the target nematode species
- Root systems should be colonized with mycorrhizal fungi
- The general diversity of fungi in aerobic compost includes nematode-trapping and parasitic fungi
- Beneficial nematodes (bacterial-feeders, fungal-feeders, predators) interfere with root-feeding species

With all these mechanisms working together, Ingham reports reducing root-feeding nematodes from nearly 100 per gram to below detectable levels within 6 months.

## Seasonal Considerations

### Fall Applications
Fall is a critical time for soil biology management:
- Apply AACT to establish beneficials on bark and branch surfaces
- Focus on soil drenches to establish organisms in soil
- Add fungal foods to promote fungal growth and soil structure building
- Cover crops maintain living root networks that feed soil organisms
- Decomposition processes speed up, improving soil tilth

### Winter
- If soil temperatures are above freezing, organisms can still establish
- Apply AACT before soil freezes so organisms get into soil
- Speed decomposition processes and hold nutrients in soil
- Reduce fertilizer needs for next season

### Spring
- Cool-loving bacteria and fungi activate as soil thaws
- Most rapid decomposition rates occur under snow
- Cover crop residues break down as temperatures rise
- AACT applications jumpstart biological activity

## Selecting Cover Crops by Soil Biology Goals

| Goal | Cover Crop Type | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Build bacterial biomass | Annual grasses, legumes | Increases bacterial dominance |
| Build fungal biomass | Perennials, mature grasses, brassicas | Increases fungal dominance |
| Nematode management | Non-host species for target pest | Reduces root-feeding nematodes |
| Mycorrhizal support | Mycorrhizal-dependent species | Builds VAM/ecto networks |
| Organic matter | High-biomass species | Feeds diverse organism groups |
| Weed suppression | Dense canopy species | Reduces weed seed germination |

## Tillage Recommendations

Ingham distinguishes between types of tillage disturbance:
- **Raking or light surface disturbance**: Minimal impact on organisms
- **Disk, chisel, deep rip**: Significant impact, but time of year matters
- **Rototilling**: Severe — "fungal voices screaming out in agony as they are shredded"

The recommendation: till to the minimum disturbance possible to achieve results needed. As soil health improves, less tillage is needed. However, no-till means fungal biomass will build, which may be too great for early successional crops — in that case, some tillage is necessary.
