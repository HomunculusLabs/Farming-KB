# Nitrogen Deposition Effects on Ectomycorrhizal Diversity and Function

Nitrogen deposition from atmospheric sources has emerged as one of the most significant drivers of change in ectomycorrhizal fungal communities worldwide. While nitrogen is an essential plant nutrient, chronic elevated deposition disrupts the mutualistic balance between trees and their mycorrhizal partners, with cascading consequences for forest ecosystem function.

## The Nitrogen Paradox in Mycorrhizal Ecology

Mycorrhizal fungi evolved to help plants acquire nutrients—particularly nitrogen and phosphorus—from soil. The irony of nitrogen pollution is that it disrupts the very mutualism that evolved to facilitate nitrogen acquisition. When nitrogen becomes readily available from atmospheric deposition, the plant's dependency on mycorrhizae for N decreases, fundamentally altering the economics of the symbiosis.

Research from multiple continents has shown that nitrogen addition has a greater effect on the ability of basidiomycete fungi to produce fruiting structures than it has on actual changes in root colonization. This suggests the fruiting response may be more sensitive than the symbiotic function itself, though the functional consequences of community composition changes remain poorly understood.

## Below-Ground vs. Above-Ground Responses

A striking finding across multiple studies is the disconnect between below-ground and above-ground fungal responses to nitrogen:

### Root Colonization Studies
- **Kårén and Nylund (1997)**: Ammonium sulfate addition to Norway spruce forests decreased fine root biomass but did not significantly alter the degree of ectomycorrhizal colonization
- **Arnebrant and Söderström (1992)**: Total mycorrhizal colonization of Scots pine roots decreased from 70% to 55% over 13 years of fertilization (1700 and 950 kg N ha⁻¹)
- **Johansson (2000)**: No significant effects of ammonium nitrate (0–75 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹) on root production or mycorrhizal colonization of heather (*Calluna vulgaris*), suggesting heathland decline under N deposition is not caused by direct impacts on ericoid mycorrhizae

### Fruit Body Responses
- **Termorshuizen (1990)**: Nitrogen addition significantly reduced basidiomycete ectomycorrhizal fruit body numbers without corresponding changes in root colonization
- **Rühling and Tyler (1991)**: 3× ambient N caused near-complete cessation of mycorrhizal fruiting in beech woodlands

### Molecular Evidence
Jonsson (1998) provided the most definitive evidence of this disconnect using molecular identification of root tips in the NITREX experiment, showing only 1–4% correspondence between fruit body species and mycorrhizal species on roots. Yet fruit body communities showed more dramatic compositional shifts than root communities, suggesting that fruiting investment is sacrificed before the symbiotic partnership itself is abandoned.

## Species-Specific Responses to Nitrogen

Not all ectomycorrhizal fungi respond equally to nitrogen addition. The NITREX experiments in Sweden revealed a spectrum of responses:

### Decreased by Additional Nitrogen
- *Cortinarius* spp. (dominant genus strongly suppressed)
- *Russula* spp. (dominant genus strongly suppressed)

### Unchanged
- *Lactarius* spp. (showed little change across treatments)

### Increased with Nitrogen
- *Paxillus involutus* (notably increased)
- *Lactarius rufus* (notably increased)

This differential response suggests that nitrogen deposition acts as a selective filter, promoting nitrophilic species while suppressing those adapted to nitrogen-limited conditions. The functional implications are significant: *Cortinarius* species are known to produce protein-degrading enzymes for organic N acquisition, while nitrogen-tolerant species like *Paxillus involutus* may rely more on inorganic N uptake.

## Mycelial Growth and Physiological Responses

The effects of nitrogen extend beyond community composition to the physiology of individual fungal species:

### Growth Inhibition
Arnebrant (1994) showed that ammonium sulfate and ammonium nitrate at 1–4 mg g⁻¹ peat significantly reduced mycelial growth of *Paxillus involutus* and *Suillus bovinus* in mycorrhizae synthesized on lodgepole pine seedlings.

### Growth Stimulation at Low Doses
Kieliszewska-Rokicka (1992) found that small amounts of NH₄-N (0.17–19 mM) increased both growth and acid phosphatase activity of *Paxillus involutus* in agar culture. This suggests a complex, non-linear response where low N additions may stimulate mycorrhizal function while chronic high inputs suppress it.

### Nitrogen Uptake Affinity and Stress Avoidance
Wallander et al. (1999) demonstrated that fungal isolates with inherently high NH₄ uptake affinity had their N uptake rates inhibited to a greater extent under elevated N than low-affinity isolates. High-affinity isolates translocate a greater proportion of assimilated N to shoots. They concluded that low uptake rates may enable ectomycorrhizal fungi to avoid the stress of elevated nitrogen loading—an elegant example of how evolutionary adaptations to N limitation become maladaptive under N saturation.

## Phosphorus-Nitrogen Interactions

Chronic nitrogen addition disrupts the balance between N and P availability, with important consequences for mycorrhizal function:

### Liebig's Law of the Minimum
Harrison et al. (1995) showed that potassium and phosphorus became increasingly limiting for Sitka spruce growth with increased N addition, supporting the hypothesis that N saturation induces secondary nutrient limitations.

### Phosphorus Cycling Disruption
Carreira et al. (2000) demonstrated that acidifying pollutants alter the inorganic P subcycle by:
- Increasing P sorption capacity in soil
- Decreasing labile P concentration
- Decreasing acid phosphatase activity (from 382 to 263 mg pNP h⁻¹ g⁻¹)
- Increasing the organic P component of soil

They proposed that nitrogen saturation leads to reduced P availability through two mechanisms: (1) changes in soil acidity affecting inorganic and organic P cycling, and (2) increased P demand induced by N saturation.

### Community Composition Shifts
The relative availabilities of inorganic and organic P, combined with N saturation, may strongly influence mycorrhizal community structure by promoting fungal species with high phosphatase-producing potential while suppressing species capable of acquiring nitrogen from organic sources. This has particular implications for ericaceous plants, whose ericoid mycorrhizae are specifically adapted to extract nitrogen from organic sources.

## Saprotrophic Responses to Nitrogen

While ectomycorrhizal fungi generally decline under nitrogen addition, saprotrophic fungal communities show contrasting responses:

- Rühling and Tyler (1991): Leaf litter saprotrophs including *Mycena*, *Clitocybe*, *Lepista*, *Agaricus*, and *Lycoperdon* increased fruiting under N fertilization in beech woodlands
- Kuyper (1989): Nitrogen addition stimulated saprotrophic fungi and leaf litter decomposition where leaf litter N was low, but suppressed saprotrophic activity where litter N was already high

The stimulation of saprotrophic activity under elevated N likely results from the increased availability of nitrogen helping decompose recalcitrant, high C:N ratio litter. This shift from mycorrhizal to saprotrophic dominance has profound implications for carbon and nutrient cycling in forest soils.

## The Diversity-Function Debate in Mycorrhizal Ecology

Van der Heijden et al. (1998) suggested that plant productivity is highest when arbuscular mycorrhizal diversity is greatest. Baxter and Dighton (2001) extended this to ectomycorrhizae using laboratory diversity manipulation experiments with communities of one, two, or four species assemblages constructed on birch seedlings. They found that:

- Ectomycorrhizal diversity per se was a better determinant of improved seedling nutrient content than actual species composition or colonization rates
- The diversity effect was stronger for P uptake than for N uptake into plant tissues

This finding has critical implications for understanding the consequences of nitrogen deposition: if diversity loss per se reduces ecosystem function, then even if some nitrogen-tolerant species persist, the overall functional capacity of the mycorrhizal community may decline.

## Knowledge Gaps

Despite extensive research, significant gaps remain:
- The functional consequences of community change (enzyme production, nutrient uptake rates, pathogen protection) have not been fully investigated
- The combined effects of multiple pollutants on community function are poorly understood
- The role of mycorrhizal diversity in maintaining ecosystem-level processes under stress requires much more experimental investigation
- Translation from controlled laboratory diversity experiments to field conditions remains a major challenge
