## Informal Economy and Community Currencies

In [[permaculture-design]], the informal economy represents a vital layer of [[permaculture-community-resilience]] — a web of exchange relationships that operates alongside (and often beneath) the formal monetary system. [[bill-mollison]] emphasized that strong communities develop their own systems of value exchange, reducing dependence on external financial institutions and creating local self-reliance. These systems are not merely economic conveniences; they are expressions of the [[permaculture-ethics]] of earth care, people care, and fair share.

## The Informal Economy

The informal economy encompasses all transactions and exchanges that occur outside the conventional monetary framework. These systems arise naturally when people recognize that their skills, time, and goods have intrinsic value that can be traded without the mediation of national currency. In permaculture communities, the informal economy often forms the backbone of mutual aid and collective action, enabling people to meet their needs through relationships rather than purchasing power alone.

Informal economies are especially critical in rural areas, among low-income populations, and in regions where national currency is scarce or unstable. They provide a buffer against economic downturns, inflation, and unemployment — precisely the conditions that permaculture design seeks to make communities resilient against.

## Barter Systems

Barter — the direct exchange of goods and services without money — is the oldest form of economic interaction. In a permaculture context, barter allows people to exchange surplus produce, skills, labor, and materials. A gardener may trade vegetables for carpentry work; a builder may exchange construction labor for preserved foods.

While simple barter works well for one-to-one exchanges, it becomes limited by the "double coincidence of wants" — both parties must want what the other offers at the same time and in comparable value. This limitation leads to more sophisticated community exchange systems that use internal accounting to decouple the act of giving from the act of receiving.

## Labor Exchange Groups

Labor exchange groups formalize the barter of human effort. Members pool their labor for collective projects, with each person's contribution recorded and reciprocated over time. These groups are particularly effective for large-scale tasks that would be impossible or unaffordable for individuals acting alone — building homes, establishing [[gaias-garden-creating-community-gardens]], or constructing shared infrastructure.

### The Bendigo Home Builder's Club

Mollison cites the Bendigo Home Builder's Club in Australia as a model example. In this system:

- **35 people** joined together to build homes for one another
- An annual membership fee of **$5/year** covered administrative costs
- Labor was measured in **units of hours**, with all labor considered **equal in value** — a doctor's hour of work was worth the same as a laborer's
- Members used **legally binding exchange forms** to record labor given and received
- Each member accumulated credit by working on others' homes, which was then "spent" when their own home was built

The egalitarian valuation of labor is perhaps the most radical aspect of this model. By treating all hours as equal, the system acknowledges that skilled and unskilled work are both essential to any project. This principle directly challenges the hierarchical wage structures of the formal economy and affirms the permaculture commitment to social equity.

## Community Barter Clubs

Community Barter Clubs extend the labor exchange concept to include goods and services beyond construction. Members operate a system of **debits and credits** — when you provide a good or service to another member, your account is credited; when you receive, your account is debited. This creates a closed-loop economy where the community's internal trade circulates value without requiring external currency.

The accounting framework of barter clubs effectively creates a [[mollison-local-currency-systems-and-community-banking]], even though no physical tokens change hands. The record-keeping system itself becomes the medium of exchange, tracking who owes what to whom across the entire network of members.

## Exchange Newsletters and Brokerage Houses

To facilitate wider exchange, communities can publish **exchange newsletters** listing goods and services available from members, along with their prices in the community's unit of account. These **brokerage houses** use **Trade Units** as a standardized measure of value, enabling multi-party trades that overcome the limitations of direct barter.

A member selling firewood can accumulate Trade Units and spend them with a different member offering plumbing repairs, even though those two members have no direct trading relationship. The brokerage function — whether performed by a person, a publication, or a digital platform — is what transforms simple barter into a genuine marketplace.

## The LET System (Local Employment Trading System)

The **[[let-system]]**, developed by Michael Linton in the early 1980s, is one of the most influential community currency models in the permaculture toolkit. It represents a mature, scalable approach to local economic exchange.

### How LET Works

- Members use local **"green dollars"** as a complementary currency for goods and services within the community
- Green dollars **carry no interest** — there is no cost to holding a positive or negative balance
- All member balances are **visible to all participants**, creating transparency and trust
- The currency is **not issued externally** by any central authority; it is created through transactions
- Green dollars **cannot be cashed in** for national currency — they exist solely for local exchange

The interest-free nature of green dollars is particularly significant. In the conventional economy, interest creates a cycle of debt accumulation that concentrates wealth and compels endless growth. By eliminating interest, the LET System removes the artificial scarcity that plagues national currencies and allows trade to serve human needs rather than financial returns.

### Employment and Economic Inclusion

The LET System enables what Mollison describes as **full employment** at the community level. Because anyone can offer services and receive payment in green dollars, people who are excluded from the formal economy (unemployed, underemployed, retired, disabled) can participate meaningfully in economic life. A person with no formal qualifications can offer gardening, child-minding, or home repair and earn green dollars to purchase what they need from other community members.

This inclusion is not charity — it is genuine economic participation. The LET System recognizes that every person has capacities that others value, and provides the accounting infrastructure to make those capacities tradable.

## The Formal Economy: Cooperatives

While informal systems provide grassroots resilience, permaculture also recognizes the value of formal economic structures organized on cooperative principles. True cooperatives adhere to a well-established set of guiding principles:

- **Open membership** — anyone who can use the cooperative's services may join
- **Democratic organization** — each member has one vote regardless of capital contributed
- **Limited interest on share capital** — returns to investors are capped to prevent exploitation
- **Surplus belongs to members** — profits are distributed according to use, not investment
- **Education** — cooperatives educate their members and the public about cooperative principles
- **Cooperation between cooperatives** — cooperatives strengthen the movement by working together

These principles distinguish genuine cooperatives from conventional businesses. Where corporations prioritize shareholder returns, cooperatives prioritize member welfare. Where corporations concentrate ownership, cooperatives distribute it. This structural difference aligns cooperatives closely with permaculture ethics.

## The Mondragon Worker Cooperatives

Mollison highlights the **[[mondragon-cooperatives]]** in the Basque region of Spain as the world's most successful example of worker-owned enterprise. At the time of writing, the Mondragon network comprised:

- **96 cooperatives** operating across diverse sectors including manufacturing, retail, finance, and education
- **17,000 workers** employed collectively across the federation
- A required **$5,000 investment** from each worker-member to join, ensuring personal commitment
- A capital structure split **20% collective / 80% individual** capital account, balancing shared and personal ownership
- Democratic governance where every worker has a voice in strategic decisions

Mondragon demonstrates that large-scale, competitive enterprises can operate on cooperative principles, providing both economic efficiency and social equity. The federation's own internal bank, Caja Laboral, provided finance and technical assistance to new and existing cooperatives — an example of how cooperative finance can fuel [[cooperative-enterprise-and-formal-economy]].

## Community Financial Institutions

Beyond cooperatives in production, permaculture communities benefit from cooperative financial structures:

- **Community savings and loans** — member-run credit facilities that keep capital circulating locally rather than being extracted by distant banks
- **Investment funds** — pooled resources directed toward community-benefiting projects such as renewable energy, affordable housing, or local food systems
- **Leasing companies** — shared ownership of expensive [[coleman-small-farm-equipment-and-tools]], reducing individual costs and enabling access to productive assets that no single member could afford alone

## Ethical Foundations

Underlying both the informal and cooperative economies is a shared ethical foundation that Mollison draws from permaculture's core principles. When money itself is designed to serve people rather than exploit them — through interest-free currencies, equitable labor valuation, and democratic governance — economic activity becomes an expression of community values rather than a force that undermines them.

The distinction between these community-based systems and conventional capitalism is not merely technical but philosophical. Conventional economics treats labor as a commodity to be purchased at the lowest possible price, and money as a store of value to be accumulated. [[pdc-right-livelihood-and-permaculture-economics]] treats labor as a gift exchanged between neighbors, and money as a medium of circulation that must keep moving to sustain the community.

## Practical Implementation

For communities seeking to establish these systems, Mollison suggests starting small and building trust gradually. A labor exchange can begin with just a handful of committed members. A LET System can be launched with a simple ledger — paper or spreadsheet — before investing in digital tools. A cooperative can start as an informal association before incorporating legally.

The key ingredient is not capital or technology but **trust**: the confidence that contributions will be honored, that accounts will be kept honestly, and that all members will participate in good faith. This trust is built through transparent record-keeping, democratic governance, and the shared experience of working together on projects that benefit everyone.

## Integrating Formal and Informal Economies

The permaculture approach envisions a layered economy where formal cooperatives provide stable employment and essential services, while informal exchange systems — barter, LETS, labor exchanges — fill gaps, include marginalized people, and build social cohesion. Together, these systems create communities that are economically resilient, socially equitable, and far less vulnerable to external financial shocks.

> "The informal economy is not a substitute for the formal one; it is its essential complement." By weaving these systems together, communities can achieve a degree of economic sovereignty that neither system could provide alone.
