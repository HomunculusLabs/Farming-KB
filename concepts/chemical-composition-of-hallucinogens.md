## Chemical Composition of Hallucinogens

Hallucinogenic plants owe their psychoactive effects to a relatively small number of chemical compound classes. All are organic compounds — they contain carbon as an essential structural element and were formed in the metabolic processes of plants. No inorganic plant constituents (minerals, metals) are known to produce hallucinogenic effects. These compounds can be divided into two broad categories: nitrogen-containing and non-nitrogen-containing.

## Non-Nitrogenous Hallucinogens: The Cannabinols

The most important hallucinogens lacking nitrogen are the active principles of **Cannabis**: terpenophenolic compounds classified as dibenzopyrans, collectively called **cannabinoids**. The principal psychoactive constituent is **Δ¹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)**.

Cannabinoids are not alkaloids. They are oily, non-nitrogenous organic compounds derived from terpene biosynthesis pathways. A fresh Cannabis plant yields mainly cannabidiolic acids, which are biosynthetic precursors. Over time, these convert:

- Cannabidiolic acid → tetrahydrocannabinols (active) → cannabinol (inactive)

This degradation occurs more rapidly in tropical climates than in cooler environments, contributing to the enormous variability in potency among plant material from different sources.

## Nitrogen-Containing Hallucinogens: Alkaloids

The vast majority of plant hallucinogens are **alkaloids** or related nitrogenous bases.

### What Are Alkaloids?

Alkaloids are a diverse group of approximately 5,000 known compounds characterized by:

- Complex molecular structures
- Presence of nitrogen (in addition to carbon, oxygen, and hydrogen)
- Slightly alkaline (basic) pH in solution — hence the name
- Plant origin (though some protoalkaloids occur in animals)

Many medicinal and toxic plants, as well as hallucinogenic species, owe their biological activity to alkaloids. Familiar examples include morphine, quinine, nicotine, strychnine, and caffeine.

### The Indole Alkaloids

The **indole nucleus** is the single most important structural motif among hallucinogenic alkaloids. The indole ring system consists of a benzene ring fused to a pyrrole ring. In hallucinogenic compounds, the indole nucleus most frequently appears as a **tryptamine derivative**.

#### Tryptamine Structure

Tryptamines may be "simple" (unsubstituted) or carry various side-chain groups on the phenyl ring:

- **Hydroxy** (—OH)
- **Methoxy** (—OCH₃)
- **Phosphogloxy** (—OPO₃H)

| Simple tryptamines | DMT, 5-MeO-DMT, bufotenine | Virola, Anadenanthera, Phalaris |

| Lysergamides | LSD (semisynthetic) | Derived from Claviceps (ergot fungus) |

#### Serotonin Connection

The structural similarity between indolic hallucinogens and **serotonin** (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) is of fundamental importance. Serotonin is a neurotransmitter present in the nervous tissue of warm-blooded animals and plays a major role in the biochemistry of the central nervous system. The indole ring appears in both the hallucinogenic tryptamines and serotonin itself. This molecular mimicry is the basis of the leading theory for how hallucinogenic tryptamines exert their effects — by binding to serotonin receptors (particularly 5-HT2A) and disrupting normal serotonergic signaling.

A parallel relationship exists between **mescaline** (a phenylethylamine alkaloid from peyote) and the neurohormone **norepinephrine** (noradrenaline), which share structural similarities.

### Other Alkaloid Classes

Several other alkaloid families have hallucinogenic or psychoactive properties, though they may operate through different mechanisms:

- **Isoquinolines**: Include some aporphine alkaloids from Annonaceae and other families
- **Tropanes**: Atropine, scopolamine, hyoscyamine — found in Solanaceae (belladonna, henbane, mandrake, datura); more deliriant than truly hallucinogenic
- **Quinolizidines**: Found in some Leguminosae species
- **Isoxazoles**: Include ibotenic acid and muscimol from Amanita muscaria

## Pseudohallucinogens

These are poisonous plant compounds that cause what might be called secondary hallucinations or pseudohallucinations. They are not true hallucinogenic agents but so severely disrupt normal body function that they induce a delirium accompanied by apparent hallucinations.

### Essential Oil Components

Some components of plant **essential oils** — the aromatic compounds responsible for characteristic odors — act as pseudohallucinogens. The most well-known example is **myristicin** from nutmeg ([[ipomoea-tricolor]])
- **Plant extracts/resins**: Hashish

### Smoking

Originally a New World practice (tobacco), now widely applied to cannabis. Other hallucinogens are occasionally smoked, including **Virola** resin and **tupa**.
