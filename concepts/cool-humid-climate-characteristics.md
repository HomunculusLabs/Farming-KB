# Cool Humid Climate Characteristics

Cool humid climates, classified as mesothermal regions, encompass some of the most productive agricultural and ecological zones on Earth. These regions span the mid-latitudes of both hemispheres, including much of Europe, northern and eastern North America, southern South America, southeastern Australia, New Zealand, and the Andean slopes. They are characterized by seasonal temperature variation with cold winters and moderate summers, abundant year-round precipitation, and diverse biological productivity.

## Climate Parameters

The defining thermal characteristic of cool humid climates is a coldest month averaging below 0°C and a warmest month above 10°C. Precipitation occurs in all months, though a winter-wet period is typical, with mid-to-late summer drought common. Plant growth is concentrated in spring and summer, with a less pronounced autumn growth period before the winter resting phase.

These climates fall within the westerly wind belt, subject to the "roaring forties" (40–45° latitude) and the influence of polar low-pressure cells. A typical 7–10 day sequence of frontal rains prevails, punctuated by occasional easterly gales from stable high-pressure cells. Winds blowing off coastal seas in summer are particularly damaging, as salt-burn affects trees and gardens.

## Climatic Subsets

Several distinct climatic subsets exist within the broad cool humid category:

- **Foggy coastal climates**: Northern California to Vancouver, western Europe, southern Chile, and Tasmania
- **Winter-wet mid-latitude climates**: Most of Europe, China, Japan, and the Pacific Northwest of North America
- **Continental interiors**: Canada, the northern United States, and inland Eurasia with more extreme temperature swings
- **Mountain climates**: High foothill areas throughout the tropics and subtropics, including the outer slopes of the Himalayas
- **Cool tropical highlands**: Lack frost entirely, allowing some year-round growth

## Original Vegetation

The native vegetation of cool humid regions is remarkably diverse. Mixed broadleaf and pine forests (both evergreen and deciduous) dominate most areas. Wet sclerophyll forests appear in regions with moderate rainfall, while species-rich meadows, cool swamps, marshes, fens, and bogs occupy wetter sites. Periglacial lakes and moraine deposits cover vast areas of the northern continents, and cool prairies or steppes develop in treeless areas recently covered by ice.

Key forest genera include oak (*Quercus*), maple (*Acer*), beech (*Fagus*), alder (*Alnus*), fir (*Abies*), and various pines. These forests produce significant "mast" — fallen foods such as acorns and beech nuts — along with truffles and diverse fungi, making natural forests rich food resources.

## Agricultural Productivity

### Berry Crops

No region is so suited to berry production as cool humid climates. Berry species occupy ecological niches from high montane to seashore sites. Key genera include:

- **Vaccinium spp.**: Blueberries and cranberries thrive in coastal or upland bogs with oxygen-poor acidic soils
- **Ribes spp.**: Gooseberries and currants (red, black, white) are widely adapted
- **Rubus spp.**: Bramble berries including blackberries and raspberries
- Other notable berries: salal, serviceberry, cherry, and strawberry

### Wild Food Resources

The forests contain many species of wild-gathered edible fungi, some long since cultivated on logs of oak, poplar, birch, and sassafras (notably shiitake and *Pleurotus* species). The waters are rich in salmonid fish, suckers, carp, pike, and sturgeon.
