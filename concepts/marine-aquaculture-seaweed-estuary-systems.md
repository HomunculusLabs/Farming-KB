## Marine Aquaculture and Seaweed Estuary Systems

Marine aquaculture represents one of the most productive and underutilized edges [[ecological-succession-in-permaculture-design]]. The intertidal zone—the boundary between land and sea—offers extraordinary opportunities for food production, material harvesting, and ecosystem management. Bill Mollison observed that traditional coastal communities worldwide had developed sophisticated systems for working with these edges, from Irish kelp harvesting to Hawaiian reef gardening.

## Eelgrass and Sea Grasses

Eelgrass (*Zostera* and *Posidonia* species) are among the most important marine plants for permaculture practitioners. They function as powerful nutrient absorbers—studies cited by Mollison suggest that a bag of super-phosphate placed in an eelgrass bed will be completely absorbed within three days. When eelgrass washes ashore, it can be composted for approximately 10 days to produce what Mollison considers the best insulation material available.

The composting process burns off organic matter that would otherwise create foul odors while leaving behind a chocolate-colored, fibrous frame material. Unlike mineral fiber insulation, eelgrass insulation carries none of the associated health risks and lasts essentially indefinitely. It can also be used as garden mulch, though caution is needed with livestock since cattle will consume it.

### Harvesting and Legal Access

In many jurisdictions, ancient grant titles extend to the low tide mark, while more modern titles may be set back 100 feet from the beach. However, intertidal zones can often be accessed through lease arrangements. In Australia, Mollison noted that regardless of who leases intertidal areas, any individual retains the right to harvest a single eelgrass load. The material is free and abundant—if not collected, it either blows inland (where it builds beach plants) or breaks down into silica skeletons and returns as mud to the channels, ultimately fertilizing offshore waters.

Eelgrass typically blows ashore at predictable locations, making it straightforward to install simple catch fences. There is enough eelgrass washing up globally to insulate the entire world safely, converting a transient marine resource into permanent use with significant energy savings.

## Intertidal Aquaculture Design

### Octopus and Shellfish Systems

Intertidal zones that alternate between ponds at low tide and flooded areas at high tide represent ideal habitat for octopus, which feed on mollusks but require sheltered breeding locations. By placing ordinary clay pots in these intertidal ponds, octopus will naturally colonize them—emerging at high tide to feed and returning to their pots at low tide. These same systems support sponge cultivation.

### Fish Traps and Scour Holes

Complex aquaculture systems can be designed by combining natural drift events with constructed elements: barrier fences, drift fences, and pond scour holes. These self-maintaining systems concentrate fish traps, scour holes, and growing areas into productive complexes. Mollison recommends starting in miniature—taking small logs to a shoreline, observing effects, building small fences, and scaling up only when the design proves sound.

### Sub-Surface Dams and Intertidal Fields

Sub-surface dams—leaky rubble walls rather than true dams—can create shallow still-water systems in intertidal zones. These replicate natural conditions where stone dikes or barriers in tidal rivers create productive habitat. When mussels attach to the walls and shrimp move in, a cascade of [[king-stropharia-ecological-interactions-permaculture]] follows. The salt marsh to low tide zone is described as "a fascinating area to play with."

## Irish Stone Fields and Kelp Harvesting

The Irish developed remarkable intertidal farming systems that remain visible from the air. Their "fields" consist of rows of stone placed across hard ocean bottoms. These stone walls support enormous growth of desirable seaweeds. Interestingly, smaller fields were found to be more productive than larger ones because the stone walls generated radiant heat, creating warmer microenvironments that boosted growth.

### Kelp Harvesting Techniques

The Irish harvested kelp by cutting it and tying ropes around large bundles—10 to 12 tons at a time. Using the tide, they floated these massive loads to shore, depositing them in bog channels as the tide receded. The kelp was then processed:

- **Stems** were stacked and dried like firewood, then fired with peat to extract potash for field fertilization
- **Fronds** were laid down as mulch

This system represents one of the most efficient examples of using tidal energy for large-scale material transport.

## Estuary Systems and Species Diversity

Estuaries offer unique opportunities for polyculture aquaculture because they provide access to cold freshwater, warm saltwater, and various salinity gradients. By designing adjoining ponds with different salinity levels, it is possible to grow species ranging from trout to grey mullet to eels, since estuaries receive both fresh water inflows and twice-daily warm saltwater tides.

## Raft Culture

Raft culture extends aquaculture beyond the intertidal zone. The Irish developed large-scale raft systems for salmon production after wild salmon runs collapsed due to river "improvement" (bulldozing riverbeds, which destroyed oxygenating salmon weirs) and offshore overfishing by gill nets. Their solution was to bring salmon inshore and raise them in giant floating nets moored in quiet tidal areas behind islands.

Rafts are applicable across all aquatic systems:
- **Tiny rafts** on small ponds serve as insect attractants
- **Larger rafts** support plants at fixed root levels, including pot plants and daffodils
- **Ring nets** in the sea can grow fish, mussels, oysters, and algae

## Phosphate Recovery from Sea Birds

One of the most innovative concepts Mollison presents is the creation of artificial phosphate islands through sea bird management. Sea birds concentrate phosphate from marine environments and deposit it at roosting sites. By constructing roosting platforms—such as concrete pylons supporting platforms the size of football fields—it is possible to capture this nutrient flow.

A single such installation on a desert coast of West Africa reportedly generates enough phosphate to make its builder an annual millionaire. The phosphate is collected both as liquid (rain runoff piped ashore and evaporated) and as solid (shoveled into bags). Mollison estimates that 200 terns consistently roosting in the right location could entirely supply an island or village with phosphate—a critically lacking mineral in many developing regions.

The Dutch have applied similar principles with bat roosts built throughout agricultural fields, which serve dual purposes: controlling mosquitoes and providing critical manure that is carefully collected and distributed.

## Spiralina (Spirulina) Production

Spirulina, an algae Mollison refers to as "the hippie food additive," can be produced in tanks under continuous production systems. Key properties include:

- **Water desalination**: A modest plant can desalinate 10,000 gallons per day
- **Fuel value**: Higher BTU than coal when used as fuel
- **Nutrition**: 86% total protein, of which 68% is complete protein, with essentially no cellulose
- **Productivity**: Three crops per day in summer
- **Waste treatment**: Cleans up sewage and grey water
- **Animal feed**: Can be fed to ducks and pigs or processed into powder

Mollison noted that spirulina was greatly overpriced at $30/pound when it should cost approximately 1.5 cents, suggesting enormous potential for localized production.

## Grey Water Recycling and Sewage Systems

[[natural-farming-guide-permaculture-design-for-small-holdings]] water extends into urban wastewater management. Grey water from showers and sinks can be shunted directly to mulch basins using simple double-mouth fittings. Shower water is immediately taken up by plants, and grey water can be recycled through greenhouses where it releases heat. A particularly effective design places the shower inside the greenhouse.

For sewage treatment, Mollison advocated a half-pipe open channel system leading from toilets directly to tree crops. Unlike round pipes, which become invaded by roots, these open-bottom channels cannot be blocked. Small dams molded into the pipe create continual ponds behind them, allowing water to be absorbed by nearby trees. The system works indefinitely, even with invasive tree species planted alongside.

## Large Dams: A Critical Assessment

Mollison was sharply critical of large dam projects like the Aswan High Dam, noting that all studies show they:

- Reduce river fertility below them by trapping silt
- Sharply increase disease, particularly [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]]
- Change or destroy fisheries downstream
- Release cold water from their base that wipes out temperature-sensitive species
- Have very low biological utility
- Give rise to centralized power systems and polluting industries

He advocated instead for "reasonably small impoundments," noting that beavers provide an excellent model—building multiple small dams for different purposes, along with canals and bog places for comprehensive water control.

## Riparian Zone Management

Flood plains offer significant opportunities for productive design. When trees like willows and poplars line riverbanks, floods typically only tilt about four trees before the next one remains upright. By planting at right angles to the bank with a nice taper toward the river, designers can create scour-hole lagoons that provide fish habitat, collect firewood and mulch, and deposit silt suitable for crops like asparagus.

Large trees can be established through direct planting: poplars grown to 90 feet can be sawn off at the base, placed in eight-foot holes, and will continue growing. This technique enables rapid establishment of substantial windbreaks and riparian buffers.
