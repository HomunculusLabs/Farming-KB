# Mollison: Seed Resources and Sovereignty

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 12, Section 12.6)

## The Seed Control Crisis

Mollison presents evidence that seed, especially the 20-30 basic food crop seeds of grains and brain legumes, is subjected to a concerted effort at total control by a few agro-chemical multinationals.

This is part of a global power play to control nations and mineral resources by controlling food through seed patents, seed trade contracts, and seed retail outlets.

The goal is de facto government by controlling food via aid allocation and market control, which Mollison characterises as a sick and destructive use of power.

## The Response: Seed Exchange Networks

In response, people and organisations everywhere have set up seed exchanges, seed libraries, open-pollinated and non-patented seed companies.

Hundreds of thousands of growers have studied home seed saving systems.

Despite the enormous loss of locally adapted seed (estimated at 85% of European varieties) resulting from monopoly control of market seed by patenting, the encouraging result of people power is that most gardeners or farmers today can assemble more species and varieties of seed than ever before in history.

They achieve this by using seed exchanges and local collections instead of patented hybrid seeds from controlled markets.

## The Role of Individual Gardeners

Every gardener who is opposed to external control preserves a few valuable varieties.

Many of these are heirloom or locally reliable traditional food plants specific to site, climate, or regional preference.

In total, these species and varieties give any new gardener a vast range of potential product.

Perennials and trees providing staple food have largely usurped seed patenting, offering a pathway to food independence outside the patent system.

## Regional and National Networks

A region in any climate can escape external food control via a local, regional, and national network of seed exchanges and open-pollinated, non-hybrid, non-patented seed.

It is particularly important for small farmers to grow and share seed, as seed outlets for farmers are bought up by the agro-chemical industry.

Seeds are altered to suit corporate purposes rather than the purposes of local food production.

## Seed Sources

Seed sources are numerous and listed in most organic farming or permaculture publications.

The key principle is to maintain open-pollinated varieties that breed true from saved seed, avoiding F1 hybrids that do not breed true and must be repurchased each season.

## Connection to Permaculture Design

In permaculture design, seed saving is integrated into the overall system through seed-saving gardens, dedicated seed-crop areas in zone 1-2, and the selection of plants specifically for their seed-producing characteristics.

The seed garden becomes a zone 1 element, visited frequently for selection and harvesting.

Seed sovereignty is directly connected to the permaculture ethic of fair share and the principle of producing no waste.

When every household saves seed, the community builds resilience against corporate control of food systems.

## Importance for Food Security

Mollison emphasises that seed control equals food control equals political control.

By maintaining diverse seed collections at the household and community level, permaculture practitioners create a distributed insurance system against crop failure, corporate monopoly, and political manipulation of food supply.

The diversity maintained in home gardens and community seed banks exceeds anything available from commercial sources, providing both nutritional breadth and genetic resilience.
