# Dryland Settlements and Salting Control

## Overview

Dryland environments present some of the most challenging conditions for human settlement and agriculture. Bill Mollison's permaculture approach to drylands emphasizes earth-sheltered building, water harvesting, native plant utilization, and the critical management of dryland salting — a widespread problem caused by deforestation and inappropriate agricultural practices. The strategies documented here span arid Australia, the American Southwest, the Canary Islands, and Central Australia.

## Earth-Sheltered and Underground Structures

Dryland settlements benefit enormously from earth-sheltered architecture, which provides natural insulation against extreme temperature fluctuations. Several documented examples illustrate the diversity of approaches:

**Underground Orchards and Caves:** In Fresno, California, hand-dug underground caves open out to shaded pits containing fig, grape, citrus, and pomegranate plantings. The design covers 2.8 hectares and was excavated by M. Forestieri. Roots grow in cool, easily mulched pits while the ground surface is funnel-shaped to catch and direct water to plant containers. Looking up from underground, the sky is completely covered by the canopy of fruit trees.

**Earth-Bermed Structures:** Traditional earthed-up barns in high cold deserts near Aspen, Colorado, provide winter hay storage and livestock shelter insulated by snow. In South Australia, buried dairy sheds keep milk and food cool, assisted by vine, earth, or tree shade. Earth-sheltered houses with solar hot water, turf roofs, and slab floors at Village Homes in Davis, California, demonstrate good comfort control in dryland climates.

**Domes and Greenhouses:** Early versions of the Windstar Foundation's "Biodome" at Old Snowmass, Colorado (7,500 feet ASL) use solar electricity and heating ponds to grow tender crops year-round in an area where the growing season averages only 60 days. Frogs and finches provide biological pest control, while tilapia and hydroponic vegetables are grown in integrated pond systems.

**Living Shade Screens:** At St. Theresa Mission in Central Australia, house verandahs are screened with Bryonia (a cucurbit), Lab-lab bean, and tomato vines. These living screens cool west-facing and sunward walls, sustained by waste wash-water and greywater.

## Opuntia and Multi-Use Desert Plants

The prickly pear cactus (*Opuntia*) exemplifies the multi-functional approach central to permaculture design. At Las Palmas in the Canary Islands, Opuntia provides fruit (known locally as "tuna"), vegetable food, cattle forage, and hedging material. It also serves as a host plant for the cochineal scale insect, which has been used locally as a food coloring agent for centuries.

Many desert *Acacia* species provide abundant poultry seed, as do *Lycium* and *Solanum* species. *Solanum* species in particular provide abundant fruit and seed suitable for chicken forage, while many woolly *Solanum* species serve as dryland food or pot herbs. This demonstrates the principle that even in the harshest environments, multi-functional plant species can support integrated food systems.

## Desert Nebkas and Soil Building

The Gidgee (*Acacia cambagei*) creates desert mounds or nebkas — approximately 1.5 meters high and 12 meters across — through natural dust accretion around sessile or weeping desert trees. Many desert trees perform this function; *Acacia* species in the Kalahari, for example, provide deep, absorbent soils and mulch for vines, other trees, and burrowing animals.

This natural soil-building process is significant: in environments where soil is scarce and fragile, nebkas create fertile microsites that support additional plant life. The dust-trapping capacity of these trees gradually builds soil horizons that would otherwise not exist, creating conditions suitable for cultivated crops.

## Desert Fencing and Protective Structures

The Ocotillo (*Fouquieria splendens*) in Tucson, Arizona, serves as a spiny living fence that guards Papago (O'odham) gardens. This species can be cultivated specifically for fencing, can be live-set to grow as a gated barrier, and is widely used as reinforcement or base for mud wall construction. Its utility as a construction material for desert structures makes it an important multi-purpose crop.

## Dryland Salting: Causes and Solutions

One of the most serious consequences of deforestation, overgrazing, and wheat cultivation in dryland areas is widespread dryland salting. In Western Australia's Boddlington area, the effects are clearly visible as collapsed soil at seepage lines. The presence of sea barley grass (*Hordeum maritimum*) serves as a biological indicator of soil collapse.

### Interceptor Banks

Interceptor banks represent the primary engineering solution to dryland salting. These structures are designed to:

1. **Divert surface water and throughflow** off agricultural fields to natural valleys
2. **Isolate soil blocks** for rain leaching, preventing a "cascade" effect of salty overland flow downhill
3. **Prevent rising saline groundwater** from reaching the soil surface

### Design Specifications

A classic interceptor bank as documented at Quairading, Western Australia:
- **Depth:** 1.5 meters
- **Width:** 4 meters
- **Spacing:** No more than 100 meters apart, or 1 meter of vertical separation on slopes
- **Construction:** A bulldozer rams subsoil on the downhill wall of the bank
- **Alignment:** Ends on the streamline as marked by distant trees

Soil pits below properly constructed interceptor banks show no throughflow and no rising salted groundwater, confirming their effectiveness.

### Biological Indicators

Several plant species serve as indicators of impending soil collapse from salting:
- **Sea barley grass** (*Hordeum maritimum*) — indicates soil collapse
- **Rushes** (*Juncus* species) — indicate imminent soil collapse, surface flooding, and anaerobic soils
- **Spike rush** — another indicator of imminent soil collapse

When interceptor banks are properly installed, upslope areas may show flooding (indicated by rush growth), but downslope crops are protected from saline water damage.

### Systematic Application

At Beermullah, Western Australia, series of interceptor banks in wheat fields systematically isolate soil blocks from overland flow and salting effects. This approach treats the landscape as a whole system, recognizing that water movement across slopes is the primary vector for salt transport in dryland agricultural areas.

## Canary Islands Dryland Agriculture

The volcanic Canary Islands demonstrate innovative dryland agriculture techniques:

**Deep Mulch Cultivation:** At Las Palmas, apple and pear crops are sustained by a deep (0.3 meter) mulch of *Euphorbia* (deep cinder) using volcanic cinder to produce fruit, potato, and other crops in an area receiving mainly night fog and condensation rather than rainfall.

**Fog Harvesting:** The technique of using volcanic cinder as a deep mulch capitalizes on the islands' unique climate, where moisture arrives primarily as nocturnal fog and condensation rather than rain. The porous volcanic material captures and retains this atmospheric moisture, making it available to plant roots.

## Key Principles

1. **Earth integration** — Buildings should work with, not against, the earth for thermal regulation
2. **Multi-functional plants** — Every species in a dryland system should serve multiple purposes
3. **Soil building through vegetation** — Desert trees like Acacia create their own fertile microsites
4. **Water as the limiting factor** — All design decisions should prioritize water capture and conservation
5. **Biological indicators** — Plant communities reveal soil health and salinity conditions
6. **Engineering integrates with ecology** — Interceptor banks work with natural water flow patterns to prevent salting
7. **Traditional knowledge** — Indigenous building and agricultural techniques are often the most appropriate for dryland conditions
