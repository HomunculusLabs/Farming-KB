# Stream Orders and Branching Magnitudes

Mollison presents a detailed analysis of stream ordering and branching
patterns in the Designers' Manual, demonstrating that the hierarchy of
stream orders reveals critical information about landscape processes,
geological substrates, and the life forms that inhabit each level of
the drainage network.

## Stream Patterns Reveal Landscape History

Streams take up many ground patterns depending on the processes that
have formed the underlying landscape (block faulting, folding,
volcanism) and the erosion and permeability characteristics of the
underlying rock itself (limestone, mudstone, sandstone, clay). The
ultimate pattern of a stream network in landscape depends on process
and substrate.

We can easily see that stream patterns are the sum of preceding events
that gave rise to the geological processes and rock types, so that
streams have a lot to tell us about such processes, a skill learnt in
photo interpretation. Stream patterns alone reveal whether the
underlying rock is folded, faulted, or jointed.

## Reading Dendritic Stream Orders

If we abstract a fairly normal dendritic (treelike) stream branching
pattern, we can find out several things from the pattern alone:

- The ORDER of channels: the volume or SIZE of branches.
- The NUMBER OF BRANCHES in each order.
- The TOTAL CHANNEL LENGTH in each order.
- The MEANDER FREQUENCIES in each order, or the behaviour of flow
  in the orders of branches.

Streams usually have from one to seven orders, depending on their age,
size, or gradient (fall over distance). An easy gradient develops as
streams cut back their headwaters and fill in (aggrade) their lower
reaches; meanders increase, and the velocity of flow decreases. These
older streams, like a mature tree, have developed all their branches.

## The Bifurcation Ratio

As the branches join up to make ever larger orders of channels, about
3 times as many smaller branches join up to make each larger group,
and so on. However, the individual lengths of any one branch in each
order increase by 2 times as the order increases from 1 to 6. This is
a very general rule of stream branching, even in non-dendritic
patterns, and holds true for many streams.

The typical progression from order 1 to order 6:
- Order 1 (runnels): many small channels, short length, high gradient
- Order 2 (creeks): fewer channels, double the length
- Order 3-4 (streams): progressively fewer, longer channels
- Order 5-6 (rivers and estuaries): very few, longest channels

The bifurcation ratio of approximately 3:1 and the length ratio of
approximately 2:1 are remarkably consistent across diverse landscapes.

## Meander Frequencies and Von Karman Trails

Meanders occur in a predictable way depending on the volume and
gradient (flow). Regular meanders depend on certain velocities and
stream width, as do stability of Von Karman vortex trails. The ratio
for meanders or trails is about 1:3.6.
