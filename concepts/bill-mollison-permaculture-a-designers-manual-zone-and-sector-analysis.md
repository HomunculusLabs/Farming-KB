# Zone and Sector Analysis

Zone and sector analysis is one of the most powerful and practical tools in
the permaculture design toolkit. Developed by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren,
this spatial planning method organizes elements on a site based on the
frequency of human attention they require and the flow of natural energies
across the landscape. It directly implements the [[permaculture-design]]
of relative location and energy-efficient planning. Zone analysis addresses
human management intensity while sector analysis maps natural forces such as
sun, wind, fire, and water.

## Zone 0: The Home Center

Zone 0 is the house or dwelling itself, the center of activity and the point
from which all zones radiate outward. Mollison considered the home the most
intensively managed element of any permaculture system. In Zone 0, energy
conservation, passive solar design, water harvesting from rooftops, indoor
food production such as sprouts and herbs, and composting toilets are all
integrated. The house functions as a microclimate generator, producing heat
that benefits adjacent zones. Zone 0 connects to [[energy-efficient-house-design]]
principles including thermal mass, insulation, and orientation for solar gain.

## Zone 1: The Intensive Garden

Zone 1 is the area immediately surrounding the home that receives daily
attention. Elements here include kitchen gardens, salad greens, herbs,
propagation areas, seedling nurseries, small livestock like rabbits or quail,
and compost bins. Mollison recommended placing the most frequently harvested
and maintained crops closest to the kitchen door. This zone features raised
beds, intensive polycultures, and succession planting to maximize production
per square meter. Worm farms, small greenhouses, and tool sheds also belong in
Zone 1 where they are accessible in all weather conditions. The [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]]
technique is often applied most intensively in this zone.

## Zone 2: The Orchard and Food Forest

Zone 2 is visited less frequently, perhaps every few days. It typically
contains fruit trees, berry bushes, vegetable beds for less demanding crops,
beehives, larger composting systems, and small poultry houses. This zone
receives regular but not intensive management. Mollison envisioned Zone 2 as
a food forest system with [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]] containing
multiple canopy layers, ground covers, and climbing plants. Swales and
greywater systems from the house often flow through this zone, connecting it
to [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-water-harvesting-and-earthworks]] infrastructure. Chickens, ducks, or
geese may forage here in movable pens to control pests and fertilize the soil.

## Zone 3: The Main Crop Area

Zone 3 contains broadacre crops, pastures, large orchards, and grazing areas
that require weekly or seasonal attention. This zone features [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-broadacre-and-rural-design]]
strategies including alley cropping, silvopasture, and large-scale [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]]
systems. Animals such as cattle, sheep, or pigs may be managed through
rotational grazing systems here. Zone 3 typically contains hedgerows as wind
breaks and wildlife corridors, demonstrating [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-edge-effect-and-ecotone-design]]
to increase biodiversity and productivity at field boundaries. Firewood
plantations and timber trees are also common in this zone.

## Zone 4: The Forage and Gathering Zone

Zone 4 is a semi-wild area managed occasionally for foraging, timber, and
wildlife habitat. It might include managed woodlots, nut tree groves, wild
food gathering areas, and native forest with selective harvesting. Mollison
described Zone 4 as the zone where the designer steps back and allows natural
processes to dominate while still extracting useful yields. Hunting, mushroom
foraging, bamboo harvesting, and seed collection for [[cannabis-seed-banks-and-genetics]]
all occur in this zone. It serves as a buffer between the intensively managed
zones and the wilderness beyond.

## Zone 5: The Wilderness

Zone 5 is an unmanaged natural area left entirely to its own ecological
processes. Mollison insisted that every permaculture design include a Zone 5
because it provides a baseline for ecological health, a refuge for wildlife,
a source of genetic diversity, and a place for contemplation and learning. The
designer observes Zone 5 to understand natural patterns that inform design in
managed zones. Zone 5 also provides ecosystem services including water
purification, pollinator habitat, and carbon sequestration that benefit the
entire property.

## Sector Analysis: Mapping Natural Energies

While zones radiate from the center based on human management intensity,
sectors map the directional flows of external energies across a site. Mollison
identified several key sectors that the designer must assess and plan for.

### Sun Sector

The sun sector maps solar angles through the seasons, determining where to
place [[energy-efficient-house-design]] elements, solar panels, greenhouses,
and deciduous shade trees. In the Southern Hemisphere, the sun arcs to the
north, while in the Northern Hemisphere it arcs to the south. Summer and
winter sun paths differ significantly and must both be considered for passive
solar design and crop planning.

### Wind Sector

Wind analysis identifies prevailing wind directions, cold winter winds, and
cooling summer breezes. Windbreak plantings of multi-purpose trees and shrubs
deflect cold winds while funneling cooling breezes toward living spaces. Wind
turbines may be placed in exposed positions while sensitive crops and animal
housing are sheltered from damaging winds.

### Fire Sector

In fire-prone landscapes, the fire sector maps the direction from which
wildfire is most likely to approach. Mollison recommended creating firebreaks,
removing ladder fuels, and planting fire-resistant species in the fire sector.
Water storage features such as dams and tanks should be positioned uphill in
the fire sector to provide emergency water supplies.

### Water Sector

The water sector maps the flow of surface water, groundwater, and runoff
across the site. [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-water-harvesting-and-earthworks]] infrastructure including
swales, contour banks, and diversion drains intercept and direct water to
storage. The water sector analysis determines where to place dams, ponds,
and [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems]] for maximum passive water distribution.

### Wildlife and View Sectors

Wildlife corridors and scenic views are also mapped as sectors. Hedgerows
connect habitat patches, and decks or windows are oriented to capture views
while maintaining privacy. These considerations connect to [[community-economics-in-permaculture]]
principles of creating beautiful, functional living environments.

## Integration of Zones and Sectors

The power of this analysis lies in overlaying zone and sector maps to create
an integrated design. A windbreak in the fire sector might also produce fruit
or timber. A swale on contour in the water sector might also serve as a
roadbed and firebreak. The permaculture designer layers these analyses to
place elements where they simultaneously serve multiple functions, fulfilling
the core [[permaculture-design]] of efficiency and multifunctionality.
