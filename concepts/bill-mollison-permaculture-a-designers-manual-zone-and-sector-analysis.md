# Zone and Sector Analysis Zone and sector analysis is one of the most powerful and practical tools in the permaculture design toolkit. Developed by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, this spatial planning method organizes elements on a site based on the frequency of human attention they require and the flow of natural energies across the landscape. It directly implements the [[energy-efficient-house-design]]
principles including thermal mass, insulation, and orientation for solar gain.

## Zone 1: The Intensive Garden

Zone 1 is the area immediately surrounding the home that receives daily
attention. Elements here include kitchen gardens, salad greens, herbs,
propagation areas, seedling nurseries, small livestock like rabbits or quail,
and compost bins. Mollison recommended placing the most frequently harvested
and maintained crops closest to the kitchen door. This zone features raised
beds, intensive polycultures, and [[query-what-is-succession-planting-and-how-do-i-plan-a-succession-planting-schedule]] to maximize production
per square meter. Worm farms, small greenhouses, and tool sheds also belong in
Zone 1 where they are accessible in all weather conditions. The  containing
multiple canopy layers, ground covers, and climbing plants. Swales and
greywater systems from the house often flow through this zone, connecting it
to [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-broadacre-and-rural-design]]
strategies including [[comparison-alley-cropping-vs-silvopasture]], silvopasture, and large-scale [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-edge-effect-and-ecotone-design]]
to increase biodiversity and productivity at field boundaries. Firewood
plantations and timber trees are also common in this zone.

## Zone 4: The Forage and Gathering Zone

Zone 4 is a semi-wild area managed occasionally for foraging, timber, and
wildlife habitat. It might include managed woodlots, nut tree groves, wild
food gathering areas, and native forest with selective harvesting. Mollison
described Zone 4 as the zone where the designer steps back and allows natural
processes to dominate while still extracting useful yields. Hunting, mushroom
foraging, bamboo harvesting, and seed collection for [[energy-efficient-house-design]] elements, solar panels, greenhouses,
and deciduous shade trees. In the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]], the sun arcs to the
north, while in the Northern Hemisphere it arcs to the south. Summer and
winter sun paths differ significantly and must both be considered for passive
solar design and crop planning.

### Wind Sector

Wind analysis identifies prevailing wind directions, cold winter winds, and
cooling summer breezes. Windbreak plantings of multi-purpose trees and shrubs
deflect cold winds while funneling cooling breezes toward living spaces. Wind
turbines may be placed in exposed positions while sensitive crops and animal
housing are sheltered from damaging winds.

### Fire Sector

In fire-prone landscapes, the fire sector maps the direction from which
wildfire is most likely to approach. Mollison recommended creating firebreaks,
removing ladder fuels, and planting fire-resistant species in the fire sector.
Water storage features such as dams and tanks should be positioned uphill in
the fire sector to provide emergency water supplies.

### Water Sector

The water sector maps the flow of surface water, groundwater, and runoff
across the site. [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems]] for maximum passive water distribution.

### Wildlife and View Sectors

Wildlife corridors and scenic views are also mapped as sectors. Hedgerows
connect habitat patches, and decks or windows are oriented to capture views
while maintaining privacy. These considerations connect to [[permaculture-design]] of efficiency and multifunctionality.

## Integrating Zones and Sectors

The power of zone and sector analysis lies in their integration. A well-designed
permaculture site overlays the zone-based attention gradient onto the sector-
based energy flow map, placing elements where they receive optimal attention
and exposure. For example, a greenhouse might be positioned in Zone 1 (high
attention) within the solar sector (maximum sun exposure) while being sheltered
from the cold wind sector by a windbreak. Similarly, animal housing is placed
in Zone 2 for convenient daily care, located to capture winter sun and deflect
cold winds. Mollison emphasized that zones are not concentric circles but
rather irregular shapes modulated by topography, access paths, and sector
influences. See [[permaculture-zone-and-sector-planning]] and
[[williams-pdc-zone-analysis]] for extended treatment.
