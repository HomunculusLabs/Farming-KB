## A New Synthesis for System Design

[[kenneth-watt]] and others. Rather than a return to primitive agriculture, permaculture is a forward-looking design science that devises systems for local, regional, and personal needs using the best available understanding of ecological function.

Had this approach been taught from the beginning, humanity would inhabit a stable and functional landscape. Instead, previous generations—lacking time or information—established the continuing mis-designed households, towns, and cities that characterize the modern world. The concept of "free" energy from fossil fuels delivered what Mollison calls the final blow to commonsense community, enabling materialistic societies to exploit distant peoples while remaining oblivious to the inevitable accounting to come.

## Concentration on Settled and Agricultural Lands

Permaculture as a design system concentrates primarily on already-settled areas and agricultural lands. Almost all of these require drastic rehabilitation and fundamental rethinking. The strategy is to integrate food supply and settlement design so efficiently—catching water from roof areas, placing nearby fuel forests that receive wastes and supply energy—that most of the globe can be freed for the rehabilitation of natural systems. These rehabilitated areas need never be regarded as "of use to people" except in the broadest sense of global ecological health.

## Anthropocentric vs. Natural Ecosystems

The fundamental difference between a cultivated (designed) ecosystem and a natural system lies in intent. In a natural ecosystem, humans are only a small part of the total species assembly, and only a small portion of its yields are directly available to people. In a permaculture garden, almost every plant is selected to provide or support some direct yield for people. Household design is thus inherently human-centered (anthropocentric).

This anthropocentric aim is valid for settlement design, but a complementary nature-centered ethic is needed for wilderness conservation. The two are inseparable in practice: it is impossible to do much for nature without governing human greed, and impossible to govern greed without supplying human needs from existing settlements. If settlements can achieve self-sufficiency, agricultural land can be withdrawn from production and natural systems allowed to flourish.

## Nutrient and Energy Recycling

In natural ecosystems, recycling of nutrients and energy is a function performed by many species—fungi, bacteria, insects, and animals each play their role. In permaculture gardens, this recycling becomes a direct human responsibility. Wastes must be returned to the soil through compost or mulch. Soil is actively created in gardens, whereas in nature many species carry out that function.

Around homes, water can be caught for garden use, but humans rely on natural forested landscapes for larger-scale ecosystem services: condenser leaves and clouds that keep rivers running with clean water, maintenance of the global atmosphere, and the sequestration of gaseous pollutants. Even anthropocentric people are therefore well-advised to assist in the conservation of existing forests and the rehabilitation of degraded lands. Human survival demands the preservation of all existing species and a place for them to live.

## Four Ethical Statements on Natural Systems

Mollison articulates four foundational ethical statements regarding humanity's relationship with natural systems:

1. **Implacable and uncompromising opposition** to further disturbance of any remaining natural forests, where most species are still in balance
2. **Vigorous rehabilitation** of degraded and damaged natural systems to stable states
3. **Establishment of plant systems for human use** on the least amount of land possible for existence
4. **Establishment of plant and animal refuges** for rare or threatened species

Permaculture as a design system deals primarily with the third statement, but responsible practitioners subscribe to all four. Mollison further argues that all species needed or useful in settlement designs should be employed, provided they are not locally rampant and invasive.

## Three Parallel Responses to Environmental Crisis

In a world losing forests, species, and whole ecosystems, Mollison identifies three concurrent and parallel responses:

1. **Care for surviving natural assemblies:** Leave wilderness to heal itself without human intervention
2. **Rehabilitate degraded or eroded land:** Use complex pioneer species and long-term plant assemblies including trees, shrubs, and ground covers
3. **Create complex living environments:** Incorporate as many species as possible—saved from wherever on earth they originate—into human-designed systems

## The Global Species Exchange

The need for species refuges extends beyond the local. Humanity is fast approaching the point where refuges for all global life forms are needed, alongside regional and national parks for indigenous plants and animals. While local flora and fauna are valued as "native," Mollison proposes that all life can logically be viewed as "native to earth." While efforts should focus on preserving systems that are still local and diverse, new or recombinant ecologies should also be built from global resources, especially to stabilize degraded lands.

The species composition of a typical garden already reflects this global exchange: plants, animals, and soil organisms from every major landmass and many islands. Jet travel has merely accelerated processes long established by continental drift, bird migration, wind transport, and waterborne rafting of debris. Everything will, in time, either become extinct, spread more widely, or evolve into new forms—and all three processes are happening simultaneously, with the rates of extinction and exchange accelerating.

New adapted hybrids are already arising: palms, sea grasses, snails, and microorganisms from many continents meet, mix, and produce new accommodations to their environments. The very chemistry of air, soil, and water is in flux—metals, chemicals, isotopes, gases, and plastics are present on Earth that have never before existed, or never in such form and quantity.

## Two Fundamental Responsibilities

Mollison distills these considerations into two primary responsibilities:

- **Primarily:** Get one's house, garden, and place of living in order so that it supports the inhabitants
- **Secondarily:** Limit human population on earth, lest humanity itself become the final plague

These duties are intimately connected because stable regions create stable populations. If cities, homes, and gardens are not organized to feed and shelter their occupants, all other natural systems must be laid waste to compensate. Truly responsible conservationists have gardens that support their food needs and are working to reduce energy consumption to a modest level—what can be supplied by local wind, water, forest, or solar power.

## The Hypocrisy of Armchair Environmentalism

Mollison directly challenges the inconsistency he observes in mainstream environmentalism. It is hypocrisy to pretend to save forests while buying daily newspapers and packaged food; to preserve native plants while relying on chemical agricultural production for food; and to adopt a diet that demands broadscale food production. The philosopher-gardener or farmer-poet is distinguished by a genuine sense of wonder and real feeling for the environment—a practical, lived engagement rather than an abstract position.

Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
