In this chapter from "The Road Back to Nature," Fukuoka connects natural [[fukuoka-soil-building-approach]], where the goal is not to add nutrients to the soil but to create conditions in which the soil's own fertility can express itself.

## Malady Arises from the Unnatural

- Mental disharmony with nature hardens the mind

- Unnatural physical care stiffens the body

- Continuing to endure such conditions and failing to exercise moderation leads to serious illness

- To recover: lighten the heart and live easily — without strain

Fukuoka's disease theory is essentially a theory of accumulation. Small deviations from natural living — poor food, insufficient rest, excessive mental activity — accumulate over time until they manifest as illness. This is analogous to his understanding of soil degradation: each application of chemical fertilizer, each pass of the plow, moves the soil further from its natural state until it can no longer support healthy plant life.

The prescription — "lighten the heart and live easily" — sounds simple but is radical in its implications. It means rejecting the entire modern project of self-improvement through effort and discipline. The path to health is not more exercise, better diet, or more rigorous self-care, but less — a return to the ease and spontaneity of natural living.

## Return to the Infant State

- "If you do nothing and simply return to the natural body of an infant, then your health will improve"

- If you have become neurotic from thinking too much, return to the state of an infant who thinks of nothing, and the mind will be cured

- When told to "become an infant," the mind does not return; when told to "become a fool," one is not bright enough to do so

- The alternative: allow that person to amuse himself with total abandon, or cast him into the mountains and leave him there to himself

The infant metaphor connects this chapter to [[fukuoka-nature-as-teacher]]: nature heals not through specific interventions but through the sheer fact of being present in a living, self-organizing system. The visitor does not need to do anything; they simply need to be there long enough for nature's influence to take effect.

## The Unity of Health and Farming

For Fukuoka, there was ultimately no distinction between farming the land and caring for the body. Both are expressions of the same principle: trust the [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-and-spiritual-life]]
