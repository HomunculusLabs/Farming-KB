# Charcoal and [[biochar]] in Compost Tea Systems

## Charcoal in Brewing

People have started adding charcoal to tea, thinking it would be a
good thing to do. The only issue is that when you put charcoal in
tea, it adsorbs all the foods onto its surface, starving the
organisms. After about two hours, the organisms begin to go
anaerobic because there is no food left in solution. If you put
charcoal in tea, you need to add a huge amount of food to overcome
this adsorption effect.

## The Adsorption Problem

Charcoal works by adsorption, which means materials are physically
bound to the surface of the charcoal. This is different from
absorption, where materials are taken into the interior of a
substance. The extremely high surface area of charcoal means that
even small amounts can remove significant quantities of dissolved
nutrients and organic compounds from the tea solution, effectively
starving the very organisms you are trying to grow.

## When Charcoal Can Be Useful

Charcoal is useful for removing toxins, which is exactly what it
does in your tea if you add it. It removes the food resources and
many of the other soluble materials from the tea. If you are trying
to remediate a contaminated tea or a situation where undesirable
compounds are present, charcoal can help. But for normal compost
tea brewing intended to grow beneficial organisms, charcoal
presents a significant challenge.

## Biochar vs. Charcoal

Biochar is essentially charcoal that has been produced with
intentional parameters, typically through pyrolysis at specific
temperatures. The distinction is important because biochar may have
different surface properties depending on production method. Both
forms present the same fundamental challenge when added to actively
aerated compost tea: they remove food resources from solution.

## Pre-Inoculating Charcoal

If you want to use charcoal or biochar as a soil amendment, the
better approach is to pre-inoculate it before application. Mix the
charcoal with good compost and keep it moist for several weeks to
months, allowing the organisms to colonize the surfaces. The
charcoal then becomes a habitat for beneficial microorganisms
rather than a sink that depletes your tea of resources.

## Charcoal in Compost Piles

Adding charcoal to compost piles is different from adding it to tea.
In a compost pile, the charcoal has time to interact with the full
community of organisms and the complex nutrient cycling that occurs
over weeks to months. The composting process can help pre-inoculate
the charcoal. However, the same adsorption effects occur and may
slow down the composting process if too much is added relative to
the available food resources.

## Historical Context

Terra preta soils in the Amazon demonstrate that charcoal-amended
soils can maintain fertility for centuries. However, these soils
were not created by adding raw charcoal to liquid tea extracts.
They developed through long-term processes of charcoal incorporation
into soil along with organic matter, where microbial communities had
time to establish complex relationships with the charcoal surfaces.

## Practical Recommendations

For compost tea brewers, the recommendation is clear: do not add
charcoal or biochar directly to your tea brew unless you are
prepared to compensate with significantly increased food resources.
If you want the benefits of biochar in your soil program, apply it
separately from your tea applications and focus on building biology
on the biochar through compost or direct soil application over time.

## Surface Area Considerations

The effectiveness of charcoal at adsorbing materials is directly
related to its surface area. Finer charcoal has more surface area
per unit volume and therefore more adsorption capacity. This means
that finely ground biochar will remove food from tea even faster
than coarser material. Particle size matters when considering any
use of charcoal in biological systems.

## Charcoal as a Soil Amendment

Despite the challenges of using charcoal in tea brewing, charcoal
and biochar have value as long-term soil amendments. When added to
soil, charcoal provides a stable carbon structure that can persist
for hundreds to thousands of years. Over time, the surfaces become
colonized by soil microorganisms, creating a habitat that protects
beneficial organisms and helps retain moisture and nutrients in the
root zone. The key is to separate the charcoal application from the
tea brewing process.

## Biochar Particle Size for Soil

For soil applications, larger particle sizes of biochar are
generally preferred over finely ground material. Larger particles
provide habitat without excessive adsorption of nutrients. The
macroporous structure of coarser biochar creates spaces where
organisms can live, water can be retained, and roots can grow.
Fine biochar, with its enormous surface area, is more likely to
tie up nutrients temporarily until it becomes fully colonized by
microorganisms.

## Compost Tea as a Biochar Inoculant

Rather than adding biochar to tea, a better approach is to add
compost tea to biochar. Apply biochar to your soil and then
irrigate with compost tea, or mix biochar with compost before
soil application. This way the tea organisms can colonize the
biochar surfaces in situ, building the biological community that
makes biochar beneficial. This approach avoids the adsorption
problem that occurs when biochar is placed directly in tea.
