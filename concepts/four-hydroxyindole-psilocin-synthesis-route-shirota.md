# 4-Hydroxyindole as Starting Material for Psilocin Synthesis

## Overview

Shirota, Hakamata, and Goda's 2003 paper in the *Journal of Natural
Products* describes a concise, large-scale synthesis of psilocin and
[[psilocybin]] that achieves gram-scale production without chromatographic
purification. The synthesis begins from commercially available
4-hydroxyindole and proceeds through a four-step sequence for psilocin
followed by a phosphorylation step for psilocybin. The choice of
4-hydroxyindole as starting material and acetylation as the protection
strategy distinguish this route from earlier approaches.

## Why 4-Hydroxyindole

4-Hydroxyindole (3) is the ideal starting material for psilocin
synthesis because psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine)
contains the indole ring system with a hydroxyl group at the
4-position. Beginning with 4-hydroxyindole means the core ring
structure and the critical 4-OH substituent are already in place,
eliminating the need for late-stage functionalization of the
aromatic ring.

Commercially, 4-hydroxyindole is available in bottles of greater
than 25 grams (>185 mmol), making it practical for large-scale
synthesis. This availability is an important practical consideration,
as many published psilocin syntheses start from materials that are
either expensive or not readily available in bulk quantities.

## Protection Strategy: Acetylation

The first synthetic step protects the 4-hydroxy group by
acetylation. 4-Hydroxyindole is dissolved in anhydrous
dichloromethane with pyridine and acetic anhydride at 0°C,
then stirred for 2 hours at room temperature. The product,
4-acetylindole (4), is obtained as ivory white crystals after
workup and filtration in constant yield (34 g scale).

The [[shirota-4-hydroxyindole-acetyl-protection-experimental]] route was chosen over the benzyl ether
protection used by Nichols and Frescas. While [[shirota-acetyl-versus-benzyl-protection-psilocin-synthesis-stability-forensic-standards]]
was also explored, "a separate step was needed for its
deprotection." The acetyl group, by contrast, is removed
during the subsequent LiAlH4 reduction step, eliminating the
need for a dedicated deprotection. This telescoping of steps
contributes to the overall conciseness of the route.

### Spectroscopic Characterization of 4-Acetylindole

The 1H NMR spectrum (CDCl3, 400 MHz) shows: δ 8.27 (1H, br s,
H-1), 7.22 (1H, d, J = 8.0 Hz, H-7), 7.15 (1H, t, J = 8.0 Hz,
H-6), 7.11 (1H, t, J = 2.8 Hz, H-2), 6.85 (1H, dd, J = 0.5, 8.0
Hz, H-5), 6.41 (1H, m, H-3), 2.39 (3H, s, OCOCH3). The 13C NMR
shows the characteristic acetyl carbonyl at δ 169.6 and the
C-4 signal at δ 143.6, confirming successful protection.

## Oxalylation: Formation of the Glyoxalylamide

The second step converts 4-acetylindole to the glyoxalyl chloride
intermediate (5) by treatment with oxalyl chloride in diethyl ether
at 0°C, followed by addition of n-hexane and storage overnight at
-20°C. The resulting yellow crystals are filtered and dissolved in
tetrahydrofuran.

The glyoxalyl chloride intermediate is then treated with
dimethylamine in THF with pyridine to yield the glyoxalylamide (6)
as ivory white crystalline powder in 80% yield. The two-step
acylation-amidation conversion proceeds in over 80% overall yield,
providing the key intermediate for the final reduction step.

### Spectroscopic Characterization of Glyoxalylamide

The 1H NMR of compound 6 shows characteristic signals: δ 10.40
(1H, br s, H-1), 7.52 (1H, d, J = 3.2 Hz, H-2), 3.02 (3H, s,
NCH3), 2.92 (3H, s, NCH3), 2.50 (3H, s, OCOCH3). The two N-methyl
singlets at slightly different chemical shifts confirm the N,N-
dimethyl substitution pattern. The 13C NMR shows the glyoxalyl
carbonyls at δ 185.4 and 170.9.
