# Manure Water and Fertilizer Application for Mushrooms

Falconer conducted extensive experiments with manure water and
fertilizer solutions for mushroom beds over two growing seasons
(1887-88 and 1888-89), testing various formulations systematically on
part of every bed. His findings on saltpeter, salt, and different
manure water preparations represent some of the most detailed
Victorian-era research on mushroom fertilization.

## The Principle of Mulching

Endeavoring by all fair means to lessen the necessity for watering the
beds was Falconer's first principle. Mulching the beds and maintaining
a moist atmosphere were the best preventives against dryness. After the
beds are spawned and molded, covering them with a light coating of
strawy litter or hay prevents drying, but this mulching should be
removed when it is near time for the young mushrooms to appear.

A light sprinkling of water over the mulching every few days, but never
enough to reach the soil, assists in preserving enough moisture in the
bed under the mulch and also in the atmosphere of the house.

## Clean Water for Mushroom Beds

Clean, soft water at a temperature of 80 to 90 degrees is ideal for
mushroom beds. Water a little warmer or colder will not hurt, but water
higher than 110 degrees might injure the pinheads, while water lower
than the average house temperature would chill the bed. A small or
medium-sized watering pot with a long spout and a fine rose sprinkler
should be used.

Water is applied in a gentle shower over the bed, mushrooms and all,
but never enough to allow it to settle in pools or run off in streams.
Clean water sprinkled over the mushrooms does not appear to hurt them,
but they should never be touched with manure water as it stains them.

Just as soon as the surface of the bed shows signs of dryness it should
receive water, the quantity depending upon the condition of the bed.
The key rule is never to let a bed get very dry before watering it. A
very dry bed requires a heavy watering, and such a sudden change might
injuriously affect the young mushrooms and spawn.

## Manure Water for Bearing Beds

In beds that have recently come into bearing, manure water may be
employed with advantage, though this is not at all imperative since
excellent crops are continually produced without it. For beds in full
bearing, however, manure water is beneficial.

It is applied from a small watering pot with a long narrow spout but
no rose, pouring the liquid on gently over the surface of the bed,
running it freely between the clumps but never touching any of the
mushrooms. For this reason a rose should not be used with manure water.

The manure water that gave Falconer most satisfaction was prepared by
dumping two bushels of fresh horse droppings into a forty-five gallon
barrel, filling with water, stirring well, and leaving to settle
overnight. For use, one pailful of this liquid is added to a pailful
of warm water at about 80 to 90 degrees.

## Comparative Quality of Manure Waters

Falconer tested many types of manure water and ranked them clearly:

- Fresh horse droppings in water: the best of all.
- Dark colored liquid, the drainings from manure piles: the poorest,
  having a deadening rather than quickening effect upon the beds.
- Cow manure and sheep manure: make good liquid manure, but horse
  manure is still preferred.
- Hen and pigeon manure and guano: given fair tests but not
  satisfactory, with always a risk in their use.
- Barnyard tank contents: not much good.
- Fresh urine from horse and cow stables diluted twelve to fifteen
  times its bulk: gave favorable results.

Mushrooms not only bear with impunity but appear to enjoy a stronger
liquid manure more than any other cultivated plants. Falconer was
satisfied that the weak liquids usually recommended for pot and garden
plants would be barely more efficacious than plain water for mushrooms.

## Saltpeter as Mushroom Fertilizer

Saltpeter (potassium nitrate) is an excellent fertilizer for mushrooms.
Falconer used it in two ways. First, powdered and mixed in the soil for
casing the beds at the rate of two ounces of saltpeter to the bushel
of earth. Second, dissolved in water at the rate of two ounces of
saltpeter to eight gallons of water, and sprinkled over the beds.

The saltpeter enriched the casing soil and provided nitrogen in a form
readily available to the developing mushrooms. Both methods could be
used in combination, with the saltpeter-amended casing soil providing
baseline nutrition and the liquid application supplementing during the
bearing period.

## Common Salt as Insecticide and Fertilizer

Falconer used common salt both as an insecticide and as a fertilizer,
and was satisfied that it proved beneficial in both capacities. As an
insecticide, he sprinkled it broadcast on the surface of the beds,
always on the bare places and never touching the mushrooms, leaving it
for a day or two, then washing it into the soil with a fine, gentle
sprinkling of water. This was directed at destroying the anguillula
(eel worms) that caused black spot disease.

As a fertilizer, salt was dissolved at the rate of four ounces in ten
gallons of water, and with this solution the beds were sprinkled. The
salt provided sodium and chlorine, both of which are micronutrients
that can benefit fungal growth in small quantities.

A too-dry atmosphere is remedied by sprinkling the floors, walls, or
litter coverings gently and only enough to wet the surfaces. Better to
moisten frequently than drench at any one time. The air should always
feel moist but not raw or chilly, and the earth on the beds should
retain its dark, moist appearance. In houses heated by flues or stoves,
floors and walls may need dampening several times a day; with hot water
pipes in tight houses, frequent sprinkling is not necessary.

The late James Barnes of England, a grand old gardener, described his
method of maintaining moist heat in winter by placing hot stable manure
inside and often stirring it over. Mr. John G. Gardner of Jobstown,
New Jersey, one of Barnes's old pupils and a most successful mushroom
grower, practiced this same method of moistening the atmosphere by hot
manure steam. This was a natural, gentle way to add both warmth and
humidity to the mushroom house without the risks of cold-water
sprinkling.
