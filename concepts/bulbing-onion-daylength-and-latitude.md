# Bulbing Onion Daylength and Latitude

To fully comprehend growing bulbing onions, you need to understand how daylengths work at different latitudes and how they interact with onion varieties. This is the single most important concept for onion growing success.

## The Daylength Basics

- On the two equinoxes (March 21 and September 21), every place on Earth has exactly 12 hours of day and 12 hours of night
- The longest and shortest days occur on June 21 and December 21
- The farther from the equator you go, the longer the longest day is
- At 45° latitude, the longest day is about 15 hours, 35 minutes; at 35° latitude, it is only about 14 hours, 15 minutes

## How Bulbing Works

A variety bred to start bulbing when the daylength reaches 15 hours and 34 minutes will behave very differently depending on latitude:

| Latitude | Effect |
|----------|--------|
| 40° | Will begin bulbing about one day after the solstice |
| 45° | Same variety begins bulbing much later — advantage of summer to grow big tops |
| 25° (tropical) | That same variety begins bulbing in February — far too early |

**Key insight**: Bulbing is triggered by daylength. The variety determines the critical daylength. The latitude determines when that daylength occurs. The time between sowing and bulbing determines how big the tops get, which determines bulb size.

## The Three Types

### Short-Day Varieties

- Intended for growing below about 32° latitude
- Grown where winters are only a bit frosty at worst
- Sweetest, mildest sort — favorite for eating raw
- **The trickiest to grow**: if the plant grows too big during an unusually mild winter, it will fail to make bulbs and instead produce seedstalks ("bolters")
- The key to success: divine the correct sowing date
- Do not make soil super-fertile — instead, side-dress heavily after growth has securely resumed in spring
- Some have been bred for extraordinary cold-hardiness and can be grown over winter in maritime climates

### Intermediate-Day Varieties

- Intended for growing between 32° and 38° latitude
- Sown in spring, tend to be on the sweet side
- Some are hardy enough to overwinter in a maritime climate

### Long-Day Varieties

- Intended for growing from 38° latitude toward the pole
- Sown in the spring, tend to be hard, pungent, and long-keeping
- Exactly what is needed in a climate with a severe winter

## The Main Tricks for Large Bulbs

1. **Use a variety that matches your latitude** — short-day in the south, long-day in the north
2. **Direct-seed as early as possible** in spring to give maximum vegetative growth before bulbing

## Transplanting for Lunkers

Gardeners seeking to produce extra-large onions often grow their seedlings in flats or trays, putting them out in the garden as soon as spring weather has settled. This gives them a head start. When directly seeded as early as possible, long-day varieties won't achieve "lunker" class, but still make a respectable harvest.

## Water Management

Bulbing onions require constantly moist soil. The usual procedure:
- Thin progressively until seedlings stand 3-4 inches (7-10 cm) apart
- Irrigate until bulbing is about half completed
- Then let the soil get rather dry at the time the shriveling tops fall over
- No [[foliar-feeding]] after (or even a few weeks before) bulbing begins

## Harvest

- When about half the tops have fallen over, push down the rest
- Wait a few days and pull them from the earth
- Loosen the soil first with a shovel if needed
- Ideally, let them lie on dry soil in the sun to cure for a few days
- If the weather isn't settled, bring them indoors, spread on a tarp, and allow them to dry until tops have completely shriveled
- Store in mesh bags (onion sacks) for lots of air circulation in cool, dry conditions

## Saving Seed

### The Right Method

In mild-winter climates, do not produce onion seed by sowing seed in late summer ("seed-to-seed"). This shortcut does not permit you to select perfect bulbs and reject off-types. Always grow onion seed by planting mature, hand-selected bulbs.

### Process

1. Choose well-wrapped, middle-sized bulbs with correct shape and narrow necks (which usually result in longer storage)
2. Include at least 50 bulbs in the gene pool
3. Where winter is severe, plant bulbs out in spring after wintering them
4. Set about 1 foot (30 cm) apart in rows 2 feet (60 cm) apart
5. Plan on staking up or propping up the seedstalks
6. Where winter is mild, bulbs may be set out in autumn

Onions are bee-pollinated and require isolation from other onion varieties.
