## Overview

The brassica family (Brassicaceae) includes some of the most nutritious and productive cool-season crops: broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, kale, collards, kohlrabi, and turnips. These crops thrive in cool weather, are cold-hardy, and provide harvests from spring through winter. However, they face significant pest pressure and require careful management.

## Crop Profiles

### Broccoli
- Main head matures in 60-90 days from transplant
- After harvesting the central head, side shoots continue producing for 4-6 weeks
- Varieties: Belstar, Green Magic, Di Cicco (heirloom, many side shoots), Waltham 29
- Heat-tolerant: Green Magic, Premium Crop
- Requires rich soil and consistent moisture for tight heads
- Space plants 18-24 inches apart

### Cabbage
- Heads mature in 60-110 days depending on type
- Early types: Golden Acre, Copenhagen Market (60-70 days, smaller heads)
- Mid-season: Premium Late Flat Dutch (80-90 days)
- Late/storage: January King, Storage No. 4 (100-110 days, large dense heads)
- Red cabbage: Ruby Perfection, Mammoth Red Rock
- Space plants 12-18 inches for early types, 18-24 inches for large types
- Excellent for sauerkraut and fermentation

### Cauliflower
- Requires the most precise growing conditions of the brassicas
- Heads mature in 55-85 days from transplant
- Varieties: Snowball, Graffiti (purple), Cheddar (orange), Veronica (green romanesco)
- Self-blanching varieties eliminate the need to tie leaves over the head
- Sensitive to heat and temperature fluctuations — best as a fall crop in most zones
- Space 18-24 inches apart

### Brussels Sprouts
- Longest season brassica — 90-120 days from transplant to harvest
- Sprouts form along the tall stem; harvest from bottom up
- Flavor improves after frost (cold converts starches to sugars)
- Varieties: Jade Cross, Long Island Improved, Diablo, Gustus
- Space 18-24 inches apart
- May need staking in windy locations (plants grow 2-3 feet tall)
- Remove lower leaves as sprouts develop to direct energy upward

### Kale
- Most cold-hardy brassica — survives to 10F unprotected
- Harvest outer leaves continuously; plant produces for months
- Varieties: Lacinato (Dinosaur), Winterbor, Red Russian, Siberian, Vates
- See [[lettuce-and-leafy-greens]] for kale in salad mixes
- Excellent for fall and winter harvests
- Space 12-18 inches apart

## Transplant Timing

Brassicas are typically started indoors and transplanted, rather than direct-sown.

### Starting Seeds Indoors
- Start seeds 6-8 weeks before transplant date
- Use sterile seed-starting mix; sow 1/4 inch deep
- Ideal germination temperature: 70-75F
- Transplant when seedlings have 2-4 true leaves (4-6 weeks old)
- Harden off for 7-10 days before transplanting (gradual exposure to outdoor conditions)

### Spring Planting
- Transplant broccoli and cabbage 2-4 weeks before last frost
- Cauliflower: 2-3 weeks before last frost (hurry before heat)
- Brussels sprouts: Start early — they need a long cool season
- Kale: Very cold-tolerant; can transplant 4-6 weeks before last frost

### Fall Planting (Often Better)
- Start seeds indoors in June-July for fall transplant
- Transplant into garden in July-August (6-8 weeks before first fall frost)
- Fall brassicas face less pest pressure and produce better quality
- Cauliflower and Brussels sprouts are especially reliable as fall crops
- Cabbage for storage: plant mid-summer for fall maturation

### Soil Preparation
- Rich, fertile soil with plenty of organic matter
- pH 6.0-7.0
- Brassicas are heavy feeders — work in 2-3 inches of compost plus balanced organic fertilizer
- Ensure adequate calcium to prevent clubroot
- Avoid following other brassicas in the same bed (3-4 year rotation minimum)

## Pest Pressure

Brassicas are notorious for attracting pests. Prevention and early intervention are essential.

### Cabbage Worms (Imported Cabbageworm)
- White butterflies lay eggs on undersides of leaves
- Green caterpillars chew holes in leaves and bore into heads
- Management:
  - Floating row covers prevent egg-laying (most effective)
  - [[bacillus-thuringiensis]] (Bt) applied to leaves kills caterpillars
  - Handpick caterpillars in small plantings
  - Companion plant with thyme, dill, or mint (may deter egg-laying)
  - Neem oil or spinosad sprays as secondary options

### Cabbage Loopers
- Similar to cabbage worms but move in a looping motion
- Same management strategies as cabbage worms

### Diamondback Moth
- Small moths; larvae feed on undersides of leaves
- Can develop resistance to Bt — rotate control methods
  - Row covers remain effective
  - Spinosad is effective against resistant populations

### Aphids (Cabbage Aphids)
- Gray-green aphids cluster in heads and leaf crevices
- Difficult to wash out of tight heads like cauliflower
- Management:
  - Spray with insecticidal soap or strong water jets
  - Encourage ladybugs, lacewings, and parasitic wasps
  - Remove and destroy heavily infested plants

### Flea Beetles
- Tiny jumping beetles that shotgun small holes in leaves
- Worst on young seedlings
- Floating row covers on seedlings are the best prevention
  - kaolin clay (Surround WP) sprayed on leaves as a deterrent
  - Plants outgrow damage once established

### Slugs and Snails
- Feed on leaves at night; leave slime trails
- Beer traps, [[diatomaceous-earth]], copper tape barriers
  - Handpick at night with a flashlight

### Integrated Pest Management Summary
1. Use row covers from transplant until plants are established
2. Monitor regularly by checking undersides of leaves
3. Handpick large caterpillars
4. Apply Bt at first sign of caterpillar damage
5. Encourage beneficial insects with flowering companion plants
6. Rotate crops to break pest cycles
7. Clean up crop residue at end of season to reduce overwintering pests

## Cold Tolerance

Brassicas are among the most cold-tolerant vegetable families.
