# Living Plant Systems and Insectary Gardens

Toby Hemenway's guide to creating gardens that support beneficial insects, pollinators, and wildlife through intentional plant selection. From Gaia's Garden, Chapter 6.

## Why Attract Beneficial Insects

A healthy garden depends on a complex web of relationships between plants, insects, birds, and other creatures. Without pollinators, many crops fail. Without predators, pest populations explode. The ecological garden intentionally creates habitat for these essential partners.

## The Insectary Garden

An insectary is a garden specifically designed to attract and sustain beneficial insects. It can be a dedicated bed, a border planting, or integrated throughout the garden.

### Plant Categories for Insectaries

1. **Nectar plants** — provide food for adult beneficial insects
2. **Pollen plants** — essential protein source for many predators
3. **Host plants** — plants where insects lay eggs and larvae develop
4. **Shelter plants** — provide overwintering habitat and protection from weather

### Key Insectary Plants

Many of the best insectary plants are common herbs and flowers:

- **Umbellifers** (dill, fennel, cilantro/coriander, parsley, Queen Anne's lace, yarrow): Small, shallow flowers perfect for tiny parasitic wasps and hover flies
- **Composite flowers** (cosmos, sunflowers, asters, zinnias, marigolds): Provide landing platforms and accessible nectar
- **Mint family** (lavender, rosemary, thyme, oregano, bee balm, catnip): Long-blooming, attractive to bees and other pollinators
- **Legumes** (clover, vetch, peas, beans): Attract predatory wasps and provide nitrogen fixation

### Designing for Continuous Bloom

Beneficial insects need food throughout the growing season. Plan for:

- **Early spring:** Crocus, snowdrops, early-blooming bulbs, winter-blooming heathers
- **Spring:** Clover, phacelia, alyssum, candytuft, early-blooming herbs
- **Summer:** Dill, fennel, yarrow, cosmos, zinnias, buckwheat, sunflowers
- **Late summer/fall:** Asters, goldenrod, sedum, late-blooming herbs, chrysanthemums

## Butterfly Gardens

Butterfly gardens require specific host plants for caterpillars:

- **Monarch:** Milkweed (Asclepias species)
- **Swallowtail:** Dill, fennel, parsley, carrot family plants
- **Painted lady:** Thistle, hollyhock, mallow
- **Sulfur:** Clover, alfalfa

Adult butterflies need nectar from flat-topped or tubular flowers in bright colors (purple, red, orange, pink).

## Food Hedges and Hedgerows

Hedgerows are linear plantings of shrubs, trees, and herbs that serve multiple functions:

- **Wind protection** for gardens and livestock
- **Wildlife corridors** connecting habitat patches
- **Berry and nut production** for humans and wildlife
- **Insectary function** when including flowering shrubs
- **Privacy screening** and boundary definition
- **Microclimate creation** through wind reduction and shade

### Hedgerow Plant Selection

Choose a mix of species for year-round interest and function:

- **Canopy trees:** Oak, hawthorn, crabapple, serviceberry
- **Shrubs:** Elderberry, blackberry, raspberry, currant, gooseberry, rugosa rose, butterfly bush
- **Herbaceous layer:** Comfrey, yarrow, clover, native wildflowers
- **Climbers:** Honeysuckle, clematis, grape, hop

## Bird Habitat

Birds are major insect predators. To attract them:

- Provide water sources (birdbaths, small ponds)
- Plant fruit-bearing shrubs and trees for food
- Create dense shrubby areas for nesting and cover
- Leave some seed-bearing plants standing through winter
- Avoid pesticides that kill their insect food supply

## The Meadow Garden

Meadow gardens offer an alternative to lawns:

- Support enormous biodiversity compared to turf
- Require minimal maintenance once established
- Provide nectar and pollen for pollinators
- Create seasonal beauty with native wildflowers
- Can include mown paths for access

### Establishing a Meadow

1. Remove existing vegetation
2. Prepare seed bed (light raking, no deep tilling)
3. Sow native wildflower and grass seed mix
4. Keep moist until established
5. Mow once or twice per year (spring and late fall)
6. Allow some areas to go to seed for self-seeding

## Net-and-Pan Pattern for Drylands

In dryland gardens, plant trees in small depressions connected by shallow trenches:

- Rain and runoff collected by trench network and delivered to tree bases
- Mulch collects in basins, building soil
- Expands the effective water capture area enormously
- Trees survive and thrive on rainfall alone
