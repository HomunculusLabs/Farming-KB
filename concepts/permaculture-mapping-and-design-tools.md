# Permaculture Mapping and Design Tools

updated: 2026-04-18

Mapping and design tools in permaculture range from simple field observation methods to systematic data overlay techniques. Mollison's Designers' Manual presents mapping as essential to understanding site potentials and communicating design intent, while cautioning that maps alone cannot replace direct field observation.

## Base Maps

A base map is the foundation document for any permaculture design. It should include: property boundaries (accurate from title or survey), contours (from topographic maps or surveyed levels), existing structures and roads, water features (streams, springs, dams), vegetation cover, fence lines, and north arrow with scale.

Sources for base map data include: topographic survey maps, aerial photographs (historical photos show change over time), property surveys, county soil surveys, geological maps, and local government planning maps. For large properties, walk the boundaries and key features with GPS.

## Map Overlay Method

The data overlay method (Method 5 in Mollison's design methodology) uses multiple transparent maps layered to reveal optimal placement patterns. Each map shows a single factor:

- Water map: streams, springs, run-off paths, dam sites, irrigation potential
- Slope map: contour intervals, gradient classes (flat 0-3%, gentle 3-8%, moderate 8-15%, steep 15%+)
- Aspect map: orientation of slopes to sun and prevailing wind
- Soil map: soil types, depth, drainage, erosion risk
- Vegetation map: existing plant communities, forest, pasture, weeds
- Access map: existing roads, tracks, pedestrian paths, potential routes
- Microclimate map: frost pockets, heat zones, wind tunnels, shade areas
- Wildfire risk map: fire approach sectors, fuel loads, defendable space
- View and privacy map: desirable and undesirable outlooks

When overlaid, areas where multiple favorable factors converge indicate optimal placement for specific functions. Where factors conflict, the designer must prioritize or modify conditions.

## Sector Diagrams

A sector diagram maps incoming energies arriving from outside the system. Drawn as wedges or arrows from the center (house/settlement) outward toward the compass direction from which each energy originates. Key sectors include:

- Sun sectors: summer and winter sun arcs
- Wind sectors: prevailing winds, cold winds, hot winds
- Fire sectors: wildfire approach directions
- Water flow sectors: flood and run-off directions
- Wildlife sectors: migration routes, foraging patterns
- View sectors: desirable outlooks, unpleasant views to screen
- Noise sectors: roads, industrial areas to buffer

## Zone Maps

Zones are mapped as modified concentric rings from the house or village center, distorted by site shape, access routes, and topographic features. Wildlife corridors (wedges of Zone 5) can extend to the front door. Frequently-used paths can extend Zone 1 along their length.

## Field Observation Tools

Mollison emphasizes that no complex of map overlays will ever supplant field observation. Essential field tools:

- Camera and notebook for recording observations
- Video recorder for reviewing processes (run-off, wind patterns)
- Soil auger or probe for subsurface investigation
- Clinometer for slope measurement
- Compass for aspect determination
- Temperature data loggers for microclimate mapping
- Simple water level (A-frame or hose level) for contour finding

## Observation Approaches

1. Child-like and non-selective: "I wonder why..." as preface to observation
2. Thematic: focus on one theme (water, energy sources, regeneration potential)
3. Instrumental: measure specific factors (temperature gradients, wind speed, light reflection)
4. Experiential: use all senses, be conscious of details, sensations, and total ambience

## Design Sketches and Plans

After analysis, design proceeds through: concept sketch (broad placement), master plan (detailed placement of all major elements), implementation plan (phased development sequence), and detail drawings (specific structures, earthworks, plantings).

## The A-Frame Level

A simple A-frame level made from two legs, a crossbar, and a plumb bob can accurately find contour lines on slopes. Essential for swale placement, terracing, and dam siting. More sophisticated water-tube levels (hose filled with water) can measure over longer distances.
