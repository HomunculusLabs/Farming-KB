# Trichoderma in Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT)

## Overview

Trichoderma is a genus of beneficial fungi widely used as a biocontrol agent in agriculture. In the context of Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT), Trichoderma spores can be added directly to the brew, where they germinate and grow into active hyphae. Dr. Elaine Ingham's field guide provides detailed guidance on incorporating Trichoderma into compost tea systems, emphasizing proper fungal food requirements and correct application methods.

## Key Species

### Trichoderma

Trichoderma species are filamentous fungi that function as parasites on other fungi. They produce enzymes that break down the cell walls of certain pathogenic fungi. Trichoderma operates as a mycoparasite rather than a predator — it attaches to and digests the hyphae of target fungi through enzymatic action, not through physical predation.

### Gliocladium

Often discussed alongside Trichoderma, Gliocladium spores share similar behavior in compost tea. Both genera germinate and grow when adequate fungal food resources are present in the brew. They are typically added as commercial inoculant products containing dormant spores.

### T-22

T-22 is a specific Trichoderma harzianum strain widely available as a biocontrol product. It can be added to tea at the beginning of the brew cycle. The spores germinate and begin growing hyphae during the 24-hour brewing period, making them active immediately upon application to soil. T-22 is particularly useful for protecting seeds and seedlings from disease fungi.

## How Trichoderma Works in Compost Tea

### Germination Process

When Trichoderma spores are added to AACT with adequate fungal food, they germinate and grow out as hyphae during the brewing cycle. The fungus increases in biomass but does not go through sexual reproduction or sporulation in the tea. Instead, the spores (a few micrometers in size) grow into active hyphae (hundreds of micrometers) over 24 hours. This biomass increase means the fungal coverage area expands dramatically compared to the original spore application.

### Fungal Food Requirements

Trichoderma and Gliocladium perform best in tea when a minimum level of fungal biomass is already present. The Soil Foodweb Inc. (SFI) compost tea report specifies desired fungal biomass ranges that should be met for optimal Trichoderma performance. Fungal foods that support Trichoderma growth in tea include:

- **Humic acids** — complex carbon compounds that support beneficial fungi
- **Fish hydrolysate** — provides protein and complex nutrients
- **Oatmeal** — a carbohydrate source that feeds fungi
- **Molasses** (at higher concentrations) — supports fungal growth

A typical application rate is approximately one teaspoon per 5 gallons of tea, or one cup per 50 gallons.

### What Trichoderma Does NOT Do

An important clarification from Ingham's work: Trichoderma does **not** produce toxins. Claims about toxin production have never been substantiated in the microbiological literature. Trichoderma's biocontrol action is purely enzymatic — it secretes enzymes that digest the cell walls of target pathogenic fungi.

## Application Methods

### In-Tea Addition

Trichoderma spores can be added at the beginning of the brew cycle. The spores germinate and grow during brewing, resulting in active hyphae ready to colonize soil upon application. This method provides the fastest onset of biocontrol activity.

### Pre-Spray Addition

Spores can also be added just before spraying the tea onto plants or soil. The tea serves as a carrier medium that distributes the spores evenly across the application area. This approach is particularly useful when you want the spores to germinate in situ rather than in the brewer.

### Soil Drench

For Trichoderma, soil drenching is often more effective than foliar spraying. Because Trichoderma does not follow along roots the way mycorrhizal fungi do, it needs to be distributed through the soil matrix. A soil drench ensures the fungus can grow through the soil profile and establish in the root zone.

### Compost Incorporation

Adding Trichoderma to compost before applying the compost to soil is another effective approach. The fungus colonizes the compost material, creating a widespread inoculum when the compost is spread.

## Critical Distinctions

### Trichoderma vs. Mycorrhizal Fungi

A critical distinction: **Trichoderma spores can be added to compost tea, but mycorrhizal spores should not.** Mycorrhizal spores are too delicate just after germination and are damaged by the agitation of the tea brewing process. Mycorrhizal spores should be added to the tea just before application, or applied separately to soil.

### Trichoderma Does Not Follow Roots

Unlike arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), Trichoderma does not form a symbiotic relationship with plant roots and does not follow root growth. Placement around seeds can help protect seedlings, but roots rapidly grow away from the application zone. For ongoing protection, the fungus needs to be established throughout the soil, making soil drench applications more effective than point-placement.

## Biomass Increase in Tea

The distinction between biomass increase and reproduction is important. In compost tea, Trichoderma does not reproduce sexually or produce new spores. Instead, existing spores germinate and extend hyphae, increasing the total fungal biomass. This biomass increase is valuable because:

- A single spore covering a few micrometers grows into a hyphal network covering hundreds of micrometers
- The expanded hyphal network has much greater surface area for enzyme production and interaction with soil
- Active hyphae are immediately functional upon soil application, unlike dormant spores which need time to germinate

## Practical Considerations

### Label Compliance

Ingham notes that using commercial Trichoderma products in compost tea may not be listed on product labels, which can create legal compliance issues. The guidance is to never suggest using a product in a way not listed on its label.

### Tea Quality Prerequisites

For Trichoderma to perform well in AACT:
- The tea must contain adequate fungal food (humic acids, fish hydrolysate, etc.)
- Minimum fungal biomass thresholds should be met
- Dissolved oxygen must remain above 5.5 ppm throughout the brew
- The compost used must contain beneficial fungi that can be extracted into the tea

### Straw Hyphae vs. Dust

When harvesting fungal compost for tea brewing, look for strands of hyphae running through the compost material. Avoid compost that produces dust clouds when handled — dust indicates dead, desiccated fungal material rather than living hyphae.

## Environmental Conditions for Trichoderma Activity

### Temperature

Trichoderma species are mesophilic organisms that grow best between 25–30°C (77–86°F). However, they remain active at lower temperatures typical of early spring and late fall applications. Their activity slows significantly below 15°C (59°F), which means timing applications to coincide with soil warming is important for effective colonization.

### Soil pH

Trichoderma tolerates a wide pH range (pH 4.0–8.0) but performs optimally in slightly acidic to neutral soils (pH 5.5–7.0). This broad tolerance makes Trichoderma compatible with most agricultural and horticultural soils without requiring pH adjustment.

### Moisture

Like all soil fungi, Trichoderma requires adequate soil moisture for hyphal growth. Extremely dry conditions cause hyphal desiccation, while waterlogged conditions favor anaerobic bacteria that compete with or inhibit fungal growth. Maintaining soil moisture at field capacity supports optimal Trichoderma colonization.

### Competition with Other Microorganisms

Trichoderma must compete with the existing soil microbiome for resources. In soils with healthy, diverse microbial communities, Trichoderma integrates into the food web as one component of the fungal community. In degraded soils with low biological activity, Trichoderma can establish more rapidly but may not persist as long without the support of a diverse soil food web.

## Limitations and Caveats

### Not a Silver Bullet

Trichoderma is one tool in an integrated disease management approach. It works best as part of a comprehensive soil health program that includes adequate organic matter, diverse microbial communities, and appropriate cultural practices. Relying solely on Trichoderma without addressing underlying soil health issues will produce disappointing results.

### Specificity of Action

Trichoderma does not attack all pathogenic fungi equally. Its enzymatic action targets certain fungal cell wall compositions more effectively than others. Understanding which pathogens are present in your system helps determine whether Trichoderma is the appropriate biocontrol agent.

### Compost Tea Carrier Limitations

The effectiveness of Trichoderma delivered via compost tea depends entirely on tea quality. A poorly made tea — one that goes anaerobic, lacks adequate fungal food, or contains insufficient dissolved oxygen — will not support Trichoderma germination and growth. The tea must meet basic quality parameters for the inoculant to be viable.

### Persistence in Soil

Trichoderma does not permanently establish in all soil types. Without ongoing organic matter inputs and favorable soil conditions, Trichoderma populations may decline over time. Repeated applications through compost tea or compost amendments may be necessary to maintain protective populations, especially in intensively managed agricultural soils.
