# Trichoderma in Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT)

## Overview

Trichoderma is a genus of beneficial fungi widely used as a biocontrol agent in agriculture. In the context of Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT), Trichoderma spores can be added directly to the brew, where they germinate and grow into active hyphae. Dr. Elaine Ingham's field guide provides detailed guidance on incorporating Trichoderma into compost tea systems, emphasizing proper fungal food requirements and correct [[environmental-sensing-filamentous-fungi-read]] that function as parasites on other fungi. They produce enzymes that break down the cell walls of certain pathogenic fungi. Trichoderma operates as a mycoparasite rather than a predator — it attaches to and digests the hyphae of target fungi through enzymatic action, not through physical predation.

### Gliocladium

Often discussed alongside Trichoderma, Gliocladium spores share similar behavior in compost tea. Both genera germinate and grow when adequate fungal food resources are present in the brew. They are typically added as commercial inoculant products containing dormant spores.

### T-22

T-22 is a specific [[basidiomycete-mating-systems-sexual-reproduction]] or sporulation in the tea. Instead, the spores (a few micrometers in size) grow into active hyphae (hundreds of micrometers) over 24 hours. This biomass increase means the fungal coverage area expands dramatically compared to the original spore application.

### Fungal Food Requirements

Trichoderma and Gliocladium perform best in tea when a minimum level of fungal biomass is already present. The Soil Foodweb Inc. (SFI) compost tea report specifies desired fungal biomass ranges that should be met for optimal Trichoderma performance. Fungal foods that support Trichoderma growth in tea include:

- **Humic acids** — complex carbon compounds that support beneficial fungi
- **Fish hydrolysate** — provides protein and complex nutrients
- **Oatmeal** — a carbohydrate source that feeds fungi
- **Molasses** (at higher concentrations) — supports fungal growth

A typical application rate is approximately one teaspoon per 5 gallons of tea, or one cup per 50 gallons.

### What Trichoderma Does NOT Do

An important clarification from Ingham's work: Trichoderma does **not** produce toxins. Claims about toxin production have never been substantiated in the microbiological literature. Trichoderma's biocontrol action is purely enzymatic — it secretes enzymes that digest the cell walls of target pathogenic fungi.

## Application Methods

### In-Tea Addition

Trichoderma spores can be added at the beginning of the brew cycle. The spores germinate and grow during brewing, resulting in active hyphae ready to colonize soil upon application. This method provides the fastest onset of biocontrol activity.

### Pre-Spray Addition

Spores can also be added just before spraying the tea onto plants or soil. The tea serves as a carrier medium that distributes the spores evenly across the application area. This approach is particularly useful when you want the spores to germinate in situ rather than in the brewer.

### Soil Drench

For Trichoderma, soil drenching is often more effective than foliar spraying. Because Trichoderma does not follow along roots the way [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-cannabis-cultivation]] (AMF), Trichoderma does not form a symbiotic relationship with plant roots and does not follow root growth. Placement around seeds can help protect seedlings, but roots rapidly grow away from the application zone. For ongoing protection, the fungus needs to be established throughout the soil, making soil drench applications more effective than point-placement.

## Biomass Increase in Tea

The distinction between biomass increase and reproduction is important. In compost tea, Trichoderma does not reproduce sexually or produce new spores. Instead, existing spores germinate and extend hyphae, increasing the total fungal biomass. This biomass increase is valuable because:

- A single spore covering a few micrometers grows into a hyphal network covering hundreds of micrometers
- The expanded hyphal network has much greater surface area for enzyme production and interaction with soil
- Active hyphae are immediately functional upon soil application, unlike dormant spores which need time to germinate
