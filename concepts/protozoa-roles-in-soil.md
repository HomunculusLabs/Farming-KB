# Protozoa Roles in Soil

Updated: 2026-04-18

Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic organisms -- animal-like, non-algal,
non-fungal unicellular organisms across several kingdoms. Over 60,000 kinds
are known, and a majority live in soil. All require moisture to lead an active
life. They are almost always heterotrophs, obtaining nutrients by ingesting
bacteria primarily, but also occasional fungi and other protozoa.

A teaspoon of good garden soil contains several thousand protozoa, compared to
a billion bacteria. While protozoa are much larger than bacteria (5 to 500
micrometers versus 1 to 4 micrometers), they are still microscopic.

## Three Types of Soil Protozoa

### Amoebae

Amoebae are pseudopods -- single-celled organisms with amorphous forms. They
move by pouring their cytoplasm into false appendages called pseudopodia ("false
feet"). They lack a mouth and ingest bacteria by surrounding and engulfing them
in gas bubbles, transmitting digestive enzymes into the vesicle. Amoebae are
the largest of the soil protozoa and come in two forms: those with shell-like
exoskeletons with predefined holes, and those without shells that are
relatively large but transparent.

### Ciliates

Ciliates are smaller than amoebae but still much larger than their bacterial
prey. They are covered with rows of hairs (cilia) that beat like oars,
propelling the organism toward food and away from enemies. The cilia also
create currents that bring bacteria into the ciliate's mouth region. The
familiar paramecium is a ciliate protozoan.

### Flagellates

Flagellates are the smallest and most numerous type of soil protozoa. They
have one or two long, whip-like hairs (flagella) that allow them to move about
in search of food. Most are heterotrophic, but a few (like Euglena) produce
their own food via photosynthesis.

## Population Control and Succession

Protozoa arrive in areas of bacterial abundance in a consistent progression.
First come flagellates (smallest, able to access tiny soil pores), then
ciliates, and finally amoebae. On average, a single protozoan can consume
10,000 bacteria per day. The combined grazing pressure eventually reduces
bacterial populations, at which point larger protozoa begin eating smaller
ones, allowing bacterial populations to stabilize.
