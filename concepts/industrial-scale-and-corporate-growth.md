## Industrial Scale, Speed and Corporate Growth

> Industrial society has reorganised human systems around concentrated energy sources, creating scale mismatches that lie at the heart of unsustainability.

## Overview

[[david-holmgren]] identifies the shift from human-scale, solar-powered economies to fossil-fuel-driven industrial systems as one of the most destructive transformations in human history. This transformation affected not only the scale of production and consumption but also the speed of life, the structure of landscapes, and the lifespan and power of institutions. Understanding these shifts is essential for any [[permaculture-design]] that aims to move beyond industrial models of thinking. The permaculture principle of "Use [[small-and-slow-solutions]]" directly challenges the industrial assumptions of scale and speed.

## Pre-Industrial Solar Economies

Before the Industrial Revolution, human land-use systems were fundamentally decentralised and solar-powered. Daily life was structured around the sun: people worked within walking distance of their homes, agricultural rhythms followed seasonal cycles, and transport depended on bullocks and other draft animals. Time was measured by natural rhythms rather than mechanical clocks — town clocks did not appear until the 14th century, and even then their influence was limited compared to the sun-driven patterns of rural life.

In these economies, durability was measured by quality rather than speed. Tools, buildings, and clothing were built to last generations. The pace of life reflected the limits of muscle power and biomass energy, creating patterns of work and rest that were sustainable over centuries. International trade was restricted to high-value, low-weight durable commodities such as spices, silk, and precious metals — goods that could justify the enormous energy cost of long-distance overland or sea transport.

Even before fossil fuels, the shift from bullocks to horses represented a significant speed increase in land transport. This seemingly modest change foreshadowed the much larger acceleration that industrial energy would bring, demonstrating that even incremental increases in speed and power begin to reorganise economic geography and social patterns.

## Industrial Scale and the Concentration of Energy

The defining characteristic of industrial society is the reorganisation of human systems around concentrated energy — primarily fossil fuels. This concentration of energy enabled production, transport, and governance to operate at scales previously unimaginable, but it came at a profound cost: the disempowerment of people and communities. When energy is abundant and cheap, it becomes economically rational to centralise, mechanise, and scale up — but this rationality ignores the social and ecological costs.

### Landscape Transformation

Fossil-fuel scale has systematically overridden fine-grained landscape character that evolved over centuries of human settlement. Hedgerows were destroyed to accommodate larger machinery, fields were amalgamated into ever-larger units, and diverse small-scale land-use patterns were replaced by monocultures. The landscape lost its human scale and ecological diversity. Architects and planners working within industrial frameworks have increasingly sought to recreate human-scale environments, recognising that people function best at scales matching their sensory and cognitive capacities — but these efforts remain constrained by the larger industrial systems they inhabit.

### Industrial Speed

Bulk transport, powered by cheap fossil energy, reconfigured economic geography entirely. Industries no longer needed to be near their markets or raw materials; supply chains stretched across continents. Commuting became normal, car dependence grew, and "fast food" replaced local cuisine. The pace of consumption accelerated, and goods designed for obsolescence replaced those built for durability.

Even seemingly progressive transport solutions like European high-speed rail commuting (covering 100–200 km daily) remain fundamentally unsustainable. They depend on enormous infrastructure and energy inputs and merely relocate the scale problem rather than solving it. True sustainability requires redesigning settlement patterns so that most daily needs are met within walking or cycling distance.

## Failures of Techno-Optimism

History is littered with grand industrial visions that never materialised or failed spectacularly:

- **Supersonic passenger planes** (Concorde) — commercially unviable and environmentally destructive
- **Space colonies** — a techno-fantasy that ignored ecological realities and energy constraints
- **Hobart freeways** — planned but never built as public resistance and costs mounted
- **Victorian-era power stations** planned at 2000 MW each — far beyond what was needed or feasible
- **"Too cheap to meter" nuclear electricity** — a promise that proved both economically and environmentally false

These failures reveal a consistent pattern: techno-optimism systematically overestimates the benefits of scale and speed while underestimating costs, complexity, ecological limits, and unintended consequences. Each failed mega-project consumed resources that could have supported countless small-scale, decentralised solutions.

## Small is Beautiful: Schumacher"s Challenge

E.F. Schumacher's landmark work *Small is Beautiful* and his concept of **Intermediate Technology** offer a direct counterpoint to industrial gigantism. Intermediate Technology is characterised by:

- **Small scale** — matching human capacities and local needs
- **Simplicity** — understandable, maintainable, and repairable by the people who use it
- **Labour intensity** — providing meaningful employment rather than displacing workers with machines
- **Local resources** — using materials and energy available nearby
- **Local markets** — reducing dependence on long supply chains and distant economic forces

Schumacher argued that the industrial model of "bigger is better" was not only ecologically destructive but also economically inefficient when the full costs — including social and environmental externalities — were properly accounted for. His work remains foundational for permaculture thinking about appropriate scale.

## Ecological Housing: The Small House Principle

Holmgren extends the small-scale principle directly to housing: **a small house is an ecological house**. Larger houses require more materials to build, incur higher mortgage debt that chains residents to industrial employment, demand more consumer goods to fill their space, encourage an indoor sedentary lifestyle disconnected from land and community, and cost more to maintain, heat, and cool. The trend toward ever-larger homes is driven by industrial economics — cheap materials, cheap energy, and status signalling — rather than genuine human need.

Permaculture design advocates for modest, efficient dwellings integrated with outdoor living spaces, productive gardens, and community infrastructure. The energy saved by building smaller can be invested in landscape regeneration and community resilience.

## Corporate Growth and Institutional Lifespan

### The Acceleration of Bigness

Banks and corporations are getting bigger faster, driven by growth imperatives that are independent of human need or ecological capacity. Canadian politician **Paul Hellyer** offered a sharp critique: *capitalism without regulation is an engine without a governor*. Unconstrained corporate growth accelerates resource extraction, consolidates political power, and shortens institutional accountability. The pursuit of growth for its own sake has become the dominant organising principle of industrial economies, overriding all other considerations of equity, ecology, or community wellbeing.

### Corporate Alternatives

Not all business models follow the gigantism pattern. Progressive manufacturers have demonstrated that small working groups can be more productive, innovative, and satisfying than large hierarchical organisations. These models align more closely with [[permaculture-principles]] of small-scale, decentralised systems where people maintain direct connection to the consequences of their work.

### Gigantism Creates Open Niches

The very scale of industrial corporations creates ecological and economic niches that smaller, more agile enterprises can fill:

- **Boutique breweries** competing with industrial beer conglomerates
- **Artisan bakeries** reviving traditional grain varieties and fermentation methods
- **Non-GMO seed companies** serving farmers outside the industrial seed pipeline
- **DIY industry** empowering household and community self-reliance
- **Desktop publishing** undermining corporate media monopolies
- **Personal computers** defeating corporate mainframe plans

These examples demonstrate that industrial-scale systems are not inevitable or invincible — they create the very conditions for their own alternatives. As corporations grow larger and more rigid, the spaces between them become more hospitable to small-scale, responsive enterprises.

### Corporate Dematerialisation

A significant trend in late industrial capitalism is corporate dematerialisation: large corporations shed physical assets — factories, land, equipment — while maintaining control over capital flows and information systems. This shift from material production to financial and informational control further disconnects economic power from ecological accountability. A corporation that owns no land or factory feels no direct responsibility for the environmental degradation its supply chain causes.

### The Lifespan Mismatch

Perhaps the most critical insight is the **mismatch of scale and lifespan** at the heart of industrial unsustainability. Most corporations are shorter-lived than individual humans, yet they dominate global affairs, shape landscapes, and consume resources on timescales far beyond their own existence. Forests, soils, and ecosystems operate on timescales of centuries to millennia; corporations operate on timescales of years to decades. This temporal mismatch means that corporate decision-making systematically discounts long-term ecological costs in favour of short-term financial returns.

A system dominated by institutions with shorter lifespans than the people they affect, operating at scales larger than any individual can comprehend or influence, is structurally incapable of sustainability.

## The Speed-Culture Feedback Loop

Industrial speed does not merely accelerate existing activities — it creates new needs and dependencies. Fast transport enables long-distance commuting, which necessitates more fast transport. Fast food requires industrial agriculture, which depends on fast fertiliser and fast distribution. Each increase in speed generates demand for further speed increases, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that moves further and further from the sustainable tempo of solar-powered systems.

Breaking this feedback loop requires conscious design choices: choosing local over distant, slow over fast, durable over disposable. In permaculture, the principle of "Use Small and Slow Solutions" is not merely a preference for aesthetics or tradition — it is a recognition that speed and scale, beyond a certain threshold, become sources of instability rather than efficiency.

## Scale in Agriculture

The industrialisation of agriculture provides a particularly clear example of the scale problem. Small diversified farms have been replaced by large monocultures dependent on synthetic inputs, heavy machinery, and global commodity markets. This shift has degraded soils, polluted waterways, destroyed rural communities, and made food systems vulnerable to supply chain disruption.

Permaculture responds with polyculture systems designed for human-scale management — farms that a family or small community can manage with hand tools and modest animal power, producing diverse yields while [[building-soil]] health and ecological resilience. The productivity of such systems, measured per hectare rather than per labour hour, often exceeds that of industrial monocultures.

## From Consumer to Producer

Industrial scale depends on keeping people in the role of passive consumers. The gigantism of corporations creates the illusion that ordinary people cannot produce for themselves — that food must come from supermarkets, energy from utilities, information from media conglomerates. Permaculture design systematically reverses this relationship, restoring people's capacity to meet their own needs through gardening, building, repair, and community exchange. Every act of self-provisioning is also an act of resistance to industrial scale.

## Implications for Permaculture Design

- **Design for human scale** — systems should match the sensory, cognitive, and energetic capacities of people
- **Prioritise durability over speed** — build and grow for longevity, not rapid turnover
- **Value local resources and markets** — reduce dependence on long-distance transport and global supply chains
- **Resist gigantism** — in land use, housing, business, and governance alike
- **Recognise institutional lifespan** — design systems that outlast any single organisation or institution
- **Embrace niche opportunities** — industrial scale creates space for small-scale, diversified alternatives
- **Apply temporal thinking** — design for centuries, not financial quarters
- **Beware dematerialised power** — institutions that control information and capital without owning physical assets may be the hardest to hold accountable
