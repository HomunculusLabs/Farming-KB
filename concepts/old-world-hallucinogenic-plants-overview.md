# Old World Hallucinogenic Plants: Overview and Cultural Significance

Existing evidence indicates that man in the Old World — Europe, Asia, Africa, and Australia — has made less use of native plants and shrubs for their hallucinogenic properties than has man in the New World. This disparity is one of the most puzzling questions in ethnopharmacology, and its causes remain debated among scholars of [[cultural-evolution-and-psychoactive-substances]] across cultures.

There is little reason to believe that the vegetation of one half of the globe is poorer or richer in species with hallucinogenic properties than the other half. Why, then, should there be such a striking difference in utilization patterns? Several hypotheses have been advanced, but none has achieved consensus.

## The Old World-New World Disparity

Compared with only 15 to 20 species used hallucinogenically in the Eastern Hemisphere, the species used hallucinogenically in the Western Hemisphere number more than 100. This difference is remarkable given the comparable botanical diversity of both hemispheres and the universal human tendency to explore psychoactive plants.

One hypothesis suggests that man in the Old World simply has not discovered many of the native hallucinogenic plants that may exist. Another possibility is that some Old World hallucinogenic species are too toxic in other ways to be safely utilized for their psychoactive properties. A third explanation is that Old World cultures were culturally less interested in narcotics, perhaps due to the earlier development of alcohol-based intoxicants that filled a similar social role.

Despite these uncertainties, it is clear that the New World cultures — particularly in Mexico and South America — were practically enslaved by the religious use of hallucinogens, which acquired a deep and controlling significance in almost every aspect of life. In the Old World, by contrast, psychoactive plants typically played a more limited role alongside other intoxicants.

## Major Old World Hallucinogenic Plants

Despite the smaller number of species, some Old World hallucinogens hold places of primacy throughout the world. Cannabis is undoubtedly the most widespread of all hallucinogens globally, with a documented history stretching back thousands of years. The [[practitioner-cannabis-ritual-use-and-sacrament]] spans cultures from India to Scythia to ancient Egypt.

The several solanaceous ingredients of medieval witches' brews — [[henbane-hyoscyamus-niger-ethnobotany]], [[belladonna-atropa-belladonna-ethnobotany]], and [[mandrake-mandragora-officinarum-in-european-folklore]] — greatly influenced European philosophy, medicine, and even history for many years. These [[solanaceous-hallucinogens-of-medieval-european-witchcraft]] contain tropane alkaloids and were central to the [[european-witchcraft-hallucinogenic-plants]].

[[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] (the fly agaric mushroom) may be one of man's oldest hallucinogens. Its strange effects may have contributed to early ideas of deity, and recent studies suggest it was the mysterious God-narcotic soma of ancient India. The mushroom grows in the north temperate regions of both hemispheres, though the Eurasian type possesses stronger hallucinogenic effects than its North American counterpart.

## Regional Traditions of Old World Hallucinogen Use

### Siberia and Northern Asia

In Siberia, Amanita muscaria served as an orgiastic and shamanistic inebriant. The use was discovered in 1730 and subsequently documented among several isolated groups including the Ostyak and Vogul (Finno-Ugrian peoples) in western Siberia, and the Chuckchee, Koryak, and Kamchadal tribes in northeastern Siberia. These tribes had no other intoxicant until they learned of alcohol relatively recently.

The Siberian practice included eating the mushrooms sun-dried or toasted slowly over a fire, or taking them in reindeer milk or with the juice of wild plants such as species of Vaccinium and Epilobium. A curious practice involved the ritualistic drinking of urine from men who had become intoxicated, as the active principles pass through the body and are excreted unchanged or as still-active derivatives.

### Central Asia and the Near East

The [[scythian-cannabis-steam-bath-ritual]] used cannabis in elaborate steam bath rituals along the Volga River some 3,000 years ago. They threw cannabis seeds and leaves on hot stones to produce intoxicating smoke, a custom described by the Greek historian Herodotus around 500 B.C. Objects connected with cannabis use have been found in frozen tombs of the ancient Scythians in the Altai Mountains.

In Turkestan, [[turkestan-mint-lagochilus-inebrians]] (Lagochilus inebrians) served as an intoxicant among the Tajik, Tartar, Turkoman, and Uzbek tribesmen for centuries. The leaves, gathered in October, were toasted and sometimes mixed with honey and sugar to reduce their intense bitterness.

### Africa

In South Africa, the Hottentots reportedly used [[kanna-sceletium-south-african-narcotic]] (Mesembryanthemum expansum and M. tortuosum, also classified as Sceletium) as a vision-inducing narcotic. Early reports from more than two centuries ago described how those who chewed the root experienced awakened "animal spirits," sparkling eyes, laughter, and "thousands of delightsome ideas."

In Botswana, the Bushmen of the Dobe region consider Kwashi (Pancratium trianthum) to be psychoactive. The bulb of this perennial is rubbed over incisions in the head to induce visual hallucinations. Several other Pancratium species contain psychoactive principles, mostly alkaloids.

### India and the Middle East

[[datura-metel-old-world-ethnobotany]] has been known in India since prehistory, with a long history extending to other countries as well. Some writers have credited it with being responsible for the intoxicating smoke associated with the Oracle of Delphi. Early Chinese writings report a hallucinogen identified with this species, and Avicenna mentioned it under the name jouzmathel in the 11th century.

[[syrian-rue-peganum-harmala-ethnobotany]] (Peganum harmala) grows from the Mediterranean to northern India, Mongolia, and Manchuria. Its seeds possess known hallucinogenic alkaloids, especially harmine and harmaline. The extraordinary esteem in which the peoples of Asia hold the plant may indicate a former religious use as a hallucinogen, though purposeful visionary use has not been conclusively established through field work.

## Ancient Near Eastern and Egyptian Psychoactive Plant Use

[[ancient-cannabis-use-near-east-and-egypt]] in the Near East dates to remarkable antiquity. Hemp fabrics from the late 8th century B.C. have been found in Turkey. Specimens have turned up at an Egyptian site nearly 4,000 years of age. In ancient Thebes, cannabis was made into a drink with opium-like effects.

Indian medical writing compiled before 1000 B.C. reports therapeutic uses of cannabis. That the early Hindus appreciated its intoxicating properties is attested by such names as "heavenly guide" and "soother of grief." The Chinese referred to cannabis as "liberator of sin" and "delight giver." The Greek physician Galen wrote, about A.D. 160, that general use of hemp in cakes produced narcotic effects.

The [[eleusinian-initiation-ritual-sequence-and-comparative-mysteries]] represents perhaps the most famous mystery in Old World ethnopharmacology. The Aryan conquerors who swept across India thousands of years ago worshiped soma, drinking it in religious ceremonies. Many hymns in the Rig-Veda are devoted to soma and describe the plant and its effects. The use of soma eventually died out, and its identity was an enigma for 2,000 years.

## The Role of Hallucinogens in Old World Cultural Development

The role of hallucinogens in the cultural and social development of many areas of the Old World is only now being investigated in depth. At every turn, its extent and depth are becoming more evident. Some Old World hallucinogens played an extraordinarily vital religious role in the early Aryan cultures of northern India, while others shaped the medical and magical traditions of medieval Europe.

Much more research is needed in the study of hallucinogens and their uses in the Eastern Hemisphere. The discovery of new psychoactive plant uses continues, as does the reassessment of ancient texts and archaeological evidence for clues about forgotten plant-based practices.

## Methods of Preparation and Administration

Old World cultures employed diverse methods for preparing and administering their hallucinogenic plants. The Scythians pioneered the inhalation method by throwing cannabis parts on hot stones in enclosed steam baths. Siberian peoples ate Amanita muscaria sun-dried or toasted, and developed the remarkable practice of recycling intoxication through urine consumption. Indian and Middle Eastern cultures prepared Datura in fermented drinks or steeped leaves in water.

In Europe, the solanaceous hallucinogens were incorporated into complex brews and ointments. Medieval witches' preparations were particularly elaborate, combining multiple ingredients including [[henbane-hyoscyamus-niger-ethnobotany]], [[belladonna-atropa-belladonna-ethnobotany]], [[mandrake-mandragora-officinarum-in-european-folklore]], and sometimes toad secretions. The addition of plant additives or admixtures to major hallucinogenic species — a practice also well-documented in the New World — appears in several Old World traditions.

The [[kykeon-composition-and-ergot-alkaloid-hypothesis]] of the Eleusinian Mysteries in ancient Greece may represent another example of sophisticated plant admixture, potentially involving ergot alkaloids. Similarly, the preparation methods for soma in Vedic India remain unknown but likely involved complex processing to modify or activate the psychoactive constituents.

## Comparison with New World Traditions

While the New World boasts a greater diversity of hallucinogenic species in active use, the Old World tradition is arguably deeper in terms of its influence on subsequent civilization. The solanaceous plants of Europe contributed directly to the development of modern pharmacology — atropine, scopolamine, and hyoscyamine are all derived from Old World hallucinogenic plants and remain essential in modern medicine.

The [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] tradition of Siberia has been tentatively linked to the origins of some of the world's oldest religious practices. The Rig-Vedic soma cult, if indeed based on this mushroom, would represent one of the longest continuous psychoactive plant traditions in human history, spanning potentially from 2000 B.C. to the present day in various forms.

The cannabis tradition, which originated in Central Asia and spread to virtually every continent, represents perhaps the most successful psychoactive plant diaspora in human history. From its ancient origins in Chinese medicine and Scythian ritual to its current global presence, cannabis has maintained cultural significance across millennia and across radically different societies.

## Modern Research and Rediscovery

Modern ethnopharmacological research continues to uncover new aspects of Old World hallucinogenic plant use. The [[ergot-and-lysergic-acid-history]] (Claviceps purpurea), known since the Middle Ages as the cause of St. Anthony's Fire, was found to contain lysergic acid derivatives — the same class of compounds that includes LSD. This connection between a medieval European plague and one of the most potent synthetic hallucinogens illustrates how much remains to be learned about Old World psychoactive plants.

The [[beta-carboline-chemistry]] from Syrian rue, once known primarily as a dye plant and folk medicine, are now recognized as essential components of the Amazonian [[beta-carboline-chemistry]] brew, serving as monoamine oxidase inhibitors that allow orally inactive DMT to become psychoactive. This pharmacological connection between an Old World plant and a New World preparation underscores the global nature of human experimentation with psychoactive substances.
