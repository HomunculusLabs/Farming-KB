# Presporulating Mycelium as Mycopesticides

## Overview

Presporulating mycelium of entomopathogenic fungi represents a breakthrough
discovery in biological pest control. Unlike conventional approaches that use
fungal spores, this technique employs the vegetative mycelial stage of
insect-parasitizing fungi before they produce spores. The key insight, discovered
by [[beauveria-bassiana]] actually attracts target
insects rather than repelling them, creating a self-dispersing biopesticide
system.

## The Problem with Spore-Based Mycopesticides

Traditional mycopesticides rely on spores of entomopathogenic fungi. When
insects come into contact with spores, the spores attach, germinate, and bore
through the insect exoskeleton using chitin-dissolving enzymes. Once inside, the
mycelium forks through internal organs, causing malaise, necrosis, and death
within days. The dead insect then becomes a platform for further sporulation.

However, this approach has a fundamental weakness: insects have evolved to
detect and avoid the spores of pathogenic fungi. Social insects such as ants
and termites maintain sophisticated sentry systems. If an infected individual is
recognized, it is killed and placed in a graveyard away from the nest. The
insects effectively "know" a mold plague when they sense or smell one, making
spore-based delivery systems impractical for colony-level control.

## Discovery of the Presporulating Technique

The discovery emerged from Stamets' work combating carpenter ants (Camponotus
modoc). While culturing Metarhizium anisopliae, he noticed white V-shaped
sectors of growth showing delayed or absent spore formation. By successively
transferring these presporulating sectors, he isolated a nonsporulating mycelial
phenotype. When placed on rice and offered to carpenter ants, the ants were
attracted, carried kernels back to their nest, and the colony was destroyed
within weeks. The mycelium acted as a Trojan horse, attractive before
sporulation and lethal after distribution.

## How Presporulating Mycelium Works

The presporulating mycelium emits attractants and feeding stimulants that entice
insects. Once consumed or contacted, the mycelium retains its full pathogenic
capabilities. Key mechanisms include:

- **Attraction**: Presporulating mycelium produces volatile compounds that
  attract target insects. Termites in choice tests preferred mycelium to wood.
- **Arrestant behavior**: Termites encountering the mycelium stop and do not
  continue foraging, remaining in contact with the pathogen.
- **Recruitment**: Infected insects recruit other colony members to the
  mycelium source, creating a compounding effect. Unlike contact poisons like
  pyrethrins that kill quickly before insects return to the colony, the
  presporulating mycelium allows time for dispersal throughout the nest.
- **Internal destruction**: In termites, the mycelium kills the large protozoa
  in the digestive tract, preventing cellulose metabolism and starving the
  insects. The insects then sicken and become susceptible to both internal and
  external fungal infection.

## Species and Strains

Several genera of entomopathogenic fungi have demonstrated presporulating
attractant properties:

- **Metarhizium anisopliae**: Green mold prevalent in soils, effective against
  termites, ants, locusts, mosquitoes, and mites. Does not infect plants,
  mammals, fish, bees, or beneficial insects.
- **Beauveria bassiana**: White mold producing attractants prior to sporulation.
  Historically significant for the muscardine disease in silk trade.
- **Hirsutella, Paecilomyces, Mucor, Aspergillus, Nomuraea**: Other genera
  likely to produce similar attractants and feeding stimulants.

Strain specificity is notable: Metarhizium strains isolated from one insect
species' colony may not attract other insect species. Some strains produce
species-specific attractants, potentially enabling precision-targeted products.

## Extract-Based Applications

Water and ethanol extracts of presporulating mycelium on rice also function as
powerful attractants and feeding stimulants. Serial dilution showed baseline
extracts worked best after dilution with water, reducing production costs. These
extracts could enhance conventional pesticide delivery by attracting insects to
treatment stations.

## Research Validation

Controlled trials at Texas A&M University's Entomology Department under Dr.
Roger Gold confirmed the approach against:

- **Formosan termites** (Coptotermes formosanus)
- **Eastern subterranean termites** (Reticulitermes flavipes)
- **Fire ants** (Solenopsis invicta)

All species were attracted to the mycelium, fed upon it, carried fragments back
to nests, and died from fungal infection within two to three weeks.

## Advantages Over Conventional Pesticides

The presporulating mycelium approach offers multiple benefits:

- Replaces toxic pesticides with an effective natural method
- Protects groundwater and habitats from contamination
- Insects become their own agents of dispersal and destruction
- The fungal genome serves as an indigenous source of new strains, limiting
  tolerance buildup and minimizing harm to nontargeted organisms
- Provides long-term protection of treated sites

## The Cordyceps Connection

Some entomopathogenic fungi exhibit dimorphism, expressing as both a mold and a
mushroom. The genus Cordyceps represents the mushroom fruiting stage. One
species, Cordyceps lloydii, infects carpenter ants and compels them to climb to
the forest canopy before dying, ensuring maximum spore dispersal.

## Limitations and Considerations

While promising, several factors require consideration:

- Insects may eventually learn to recognize presporulating mycelium as
  pathogenic, though the natural rarity of this state makes recognition
  difficult
- Constant switching between available strains can further prevent adaptation
- Active constituents have not been molecularly characterized and may involve
  suites of synergistic compounds rather than single molecules
- EPA approval is required for commercial products

## Economic Context

The global pesticide industry is valued at approximately $9 billion in the
United States, with biopesticides comprising about 5% ($450 million) and
growing at 15% annually. Termite damage to buildings exceeds $10 billion per
year in the United States alone. The presporulating mycelium approach represents
a significant opportunity within the expanding biopesticide market.
