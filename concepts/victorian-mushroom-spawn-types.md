# Victorian [[mushroom-spawn]] Types

In the late 19th century, mushroom spawn was obtained through several distinct channels, each with unique characteristics regarding preparation, appearance, vigor, and availability. [[william-falconer]] documented these in detail in *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* (1891), providing the most comprehensive English-language account of the spawn trade during the Victorian era.

## Overview of Spawn Categories

Victorian mushroom growers relied on four principal types of spawn:

- **Brick spawn** (English spawn)
- **Flake spawn** (French spawn)
- **Mill-track spawn** (natural spawn from horse paths)
- **Virgin spawn** (wild spawn from fields and manure piles)

Each type had distinct trade networks, manufacturing processes, and reputations among growers. The choice of spawn was considered the single most important factor in mushroom-growing success.

## Brick Spawn (English Spawn)

Brick spawn was the most commonly imported spawn in America during the 1890s. It was manufactured primarily around London, England, by specialized firms who kept their methods closely guarded. Market gardeners in the United States imported approximately 4,000 bushels (64,000 pounds) of brick spawn annually, with about half handled through New York seedsmen alone.

### Physical Characteristics

Brick spawn was sold in the form of dry, hard, solid manure bricks completely permeated with mushroom mycelium. Bricks from different manufacturers varied in size and weight:

- Some varieties packed ten bricks to the bushel
- Others packed fourteen bricks per bushel
- The most common size measured approximately 8.5 inches long, 5.25 inches wide, and 1.25 inches thick
- The standard "9x6x2 inch" London brick weighed exactly one pound but shrank during drying
- In America, brick spawn was retailed by weight rather than by measure

### Manufacturing and Quality

The manufacturing process was a closely held trade secret. Spawn makers impregnated prepared manure bricks with mycelium, and the resulting product varied considerably in quality. Falconer noted that appearance alone was an unreliable indicator of spawn vitality. Some bricks showed little visible mycelium yet were perfectly good, while others heavily threaded with bluish-white mold could be either excellent or worthless.

A decisive mushroom smell was considered a positive sign, though even this was not infallible. The bricks were dried hard for preservation and shipped across the Atlantic, surviving the journey when properly stored.

## Flake Spawn (French Spawn)

Flake spawn, universally known as "French spawn," was imported from France and sold in two retail forms:

- **Boxed**: Neatly packed in thin wooden boxes, each containing two or three pounds of spawn
- **Bulk**: Sold loose by weight or measure in larger quantities

### Physical Description

Flake spawn consisted of flakes of half-rotted strawy manure thoroughly interpenetrated with mushroom mycelium. Approximately 4,500 pounds of French flake spawn were imported into the United States annually during the 1890s.

The flake form was generally considered more vigorous than brick spawn by many experienced growers, though opinions varied. Some American growers preferred the brick form for its convenience in handling and planting, while others insisted that French flake produced larger mushrooms and longer-bearing beds.

### Cultural Significance

The rivalry between English brick spawn and French flake spawn was a persistent topic of debate among Victorian mushroom cultivators. Falconer reported that some growers used both types, leaning toward one or the other based on their personal experience, but noted that no definitive controlled comparison had been conducted.

## Mill-Track Spawn (Natural Spawn)

Mill-track spawn occupied a legendary status among Victorian growers. It was the natural spawn that spread through thoroughly amalgamated horse droppings in the mill-tracks, or the cleanings from mill-tracks, where horses had trodden and compressed manure over years.

### Characteristics

- Sold in large, irregular, somewhat soft lumps
- Considered the finest quality spawn by experienced English growers
- Highly esteemed by commercial spawn makers for impregnating their manufactured bricks
- Far more vigorous than artificially prepared spawn

### Decline and Disappearance

The supply of genuine mill-track spawn was already declining by the 1890s. As horses gave way to steam as a motive power in mills, the traditional mill-tracks were no longer maintained, and the accumulated manure beds that had produced this natural spawn disappeared. American seedsmen continued to advertise "mill-track spawn," but Falconer noted that what they sold under this name was merely ordinary English brick spawn. The real mill-track spawn was no longer available commercially.

### Why It Was Superior

The superiority of mill-track spawn was attributed to the fact that it arose naturally from mushroom spores under ideal conditions: the constant trampling of horses created a perfectly compressed, aerated, and moisture-retentive manure bed in which the mycelium could develop over many years without disturbance. This slow, natural colonization produced mycelium of exceptional vigor.

## Virgin Spawn (Wild Spawn)

Virgin spawn was the term for natural or wild spawn that occurred without any artificial aid, found in:

- Open fields where mushrooms grew naturally
- Manure piles on farms
- Spontaneous appearances in gardens and pastures

### Origin and Vigor

Virgin spawn was believed to be produced directly from mushroom spores that had germinated naturally, rather than from surviving fragments of old spawn. It was considered far more vigorous than any manufactured spawn, and commercial spawn makers actively sought it out to use as the starter culture for their brick and flake production.

### Practical Limitations

Despite its superior vigor, virgin spawn was seldom used directly for spawning mushroom beds because it was difficult to obtain in useful quantities. When discovered, Falconer advised securing all that could be found, bringing it indoors to a loft or shed, spreading it out to dry thoroughly, and then storing it for use as one would French flake spawn. He reported obtaining larger and heavier individual mushrooms from virgin spawn, and beds that bore longer, though total crop weight was comparable to artificial spawn.

### Identification in the Field

Virgin spawn appeared as a netted mass of white strings traversing manure. It was sometimes found in manure piles that had been undisturbed for some time, and occasionally in rich garden soil where mushrooms had appeared spontaneously in previous seasons.

## Spawn Storage and Handling

Proper storage was critical for maintaining spawn viability. Falconer provided detailed guidance:

### Optimal Conditions

- Store in a dry, airy place, somewhat dark if convenient
- Maintain temperature between 35 and 65 degrees Fahrenheit
- Keep in small quantities, well-covered
- Never store in cellars, cupboards against walls, or close, damp buildings

### Enemies of Spawn

- **Dampness combined with heat**: Caused the mycelium to begin growing prematurely in storage, which was ruinous
- **Hard frost**: Destroyed the vitality of both brick and flake spawn, despite the fact that natural spawn survived winter in the ground
- **Age**: Spawn could remain viable for several years if properly kept, but fresh spawn was always strongly preferred

### Fresh Versus Old Spawn

Falconer was emphatic about the importance of using fresh spawn. Given that it took three months from gathering manure for beds until mushroom harvest, the risk and expense of using old spawn was considered unjustifiable. He had used old spawn often enough to be convinced of its general worthlessness unless preserved with the greatest care.

## The Spawn Trade

The Victorian mushroom spawn trade was a significant commercial enterprise. Leading importers in major American cities handled thousands of pounds annually. The demand was described as "unprecedented," with some leading importers selling out their supply before November. Market growers, rather than private gardeners, drove the demand, as they had discovered there was substantial profit in [[mushroom-cultivation]].

The spawn trade was characterized by secrecy. Market growers zealously guarded their cultivation methods, and spawn makers kept their manufacturing processes confidential. This secrecy contributed to the mystique surrounding mushroom growing and made reliable information difficult to obtain for beginners.

## Historical Significance

The Victorian spawn types represent an important transitional period in mushroom cultivation, between the entirely empirical methods of earlier centuries and the scientific pure-culture spawn that would emerge in the early 20th century. The reliance on natural spawn and empirically manufactured bricks and flakes would soon be superseded by laboratory-produced pure culture spawn, but the basic principles of spawn selection, storage, and handling established during this era remained relevant to mushroom cultivation.
