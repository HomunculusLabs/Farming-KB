# Phytochrome and Plant Light Sensing

## Overview

Phytochrome is a photoreceptor pigment protein that plays a central role in
how plants perceive and respond to light. Discovered through the work of
multiple research groups in the mid-twentieth century, phytochrome mediates
a wide range of developmental responses including [[cannabis-seed-germination]], shade
avoidance, de-etiolation, and — most significantly — the photoperiodic
control of flowering first described by Garner and Allard. Phytochrome
exists in two interconvertible forms that respond to different wavelengths of
light, enabling plants to sense not only the presence of light but also its
quality, duration, and the transitions between light and darkness that
define the daily photoperiod.

## Molecular Structure and Two Forms

Phytochrome proteins consist of an apoprotein covalently bonded to a
chromophore called phytochromobilin, a linear tetrapyrrole molecule related
to the pigments found in chlorophyll. The chromophore is responsible for
light absorption, while the apoprotein mediates [[fungal-environmental-sensing-signal-transduction]] to
downstream cellular responses. Phytochrome exists in two stable isomeric
forms: Pr (P660), which absorbs [[blue-light-phototropism-red-light-photoperiodism-dual-plant-vision-system]] at approximately 660 nanometers,
and Pfr (P730), which absorbs far-red light at approximately 730
nanometers. These two forms are photointerconvertible: when Pr absorbs red
light, it isomerizes to Pfr, and when Pfr absorbs far-red light, it
reverts to Pr. This reversible photoconversion is the fundamental molecular
switch that allows phytochrome to act as a light sensor.

## Pr-Pfr Equilibrium and Biological Activity

The Pfr form of phytochrome is generally considered the biologically active
form that triggers downstream developmental responses. When a plant is
exposed to sunlight, which contains both red and far-red wavelengths, an
equilibrium is established between Pr and Pfr, with Pfr typically comprising
the majority of the phytochrome pool under full sunlight. During the dark
period, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr through a thermal process known as dark
reversion, and some Pfr is also degraded by the proteasome. The rate of
this dark reversion and degradation means that the duration of the dark
period directly determines how much Pfr remains when dawn arrives. It is
this residual Pfr level — or its absence — that the plant uses to measure
[[photoperiodism-plants-measure-night-length]] and determine whether the photoperiodic conditions are
appropriate for flowering.

## Night-Break Experiments and Photoperiodic Measurement

The role of phytochrome in photoperiodism was decisively demonstrated
through night-break experiments. If a short-day plant (which requires long
nights to flower) is given a brief flash of red light during its dark
period, flowering is inhibited. However, if the red light flash is
immediately followed by a far-red light flash, flowering is restored. This
red/far-red reversibility is the hallmark of phytochrome action and
provided the key evidence that phytochrome is the photoreceptor mediating
photoperiodic responses. The red flash converts Pr to Pfr, resetting the
plant's night-length counter. The subsequent far-red flash converts the Pfr
back to Pr, effectively undoing the [[night-break-experiments-and-agricultural-light-manipulation]] and allowing the plant to
continue "counting" the dark period as if the interruption had never
occurred.

## Role in Seed Germination

One of the earliest known phytochrome-mediated responses is the control of
seed germination in light-sensitive species. Many small-seeded species,
including lettuce ([[lactuca-sativa]]) and certain Arabidopsis ecotypes, require
light exposure for successful germination. When such seeds are buried too
deeply in soil, they remain dormant because the filter effect of the soil
depletes red light and enriches the far-red component of any light that
reaches them. At the soil surface, however, the red-rich sunlight converts
phytochrome to the Pfr form, which activates the germination program. This
ecologically important mechanism prevents germination when seeds are too
deep to reach the surface as seedlings, thereby serving as a depth-sensing
system that optimizes seedling establishment.

## Shade Avoidance Syndrome

Phytochrome also mediates the shade avoidance syndrome, a suite of
responses that plants exhibit when growing in the shade of other plants. The
canopy of neighboring vegetation absorbs most red light for photosynthesis
while transmitting or reflecting far-red light. This shifts the red-to-far-
[[phytochrome-red-far-red-ratio-shade-detection-plants]] (R:FR) of the light reaching shaded plants. Phytochrome detects
this shift: the enriched far-red light converts Pfr back to Pr, signaling
to the plant that it is being shaded. In response, the plant elongates its
stems and petioles, accelerates leaf growth, reduces branching, and may
flower earlier — all strategies designed to escape the shade and capture
more light. This response has significant implications for agriculture and
forestry, where dense planting can trigger shade avoidance and reduce crop
yield by redirecting resources from fruit or [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]] to stem
elongation.
