# Fungal Species Estimates and the Discovery Gap

The fungal kingdom represents one of the most significant yet poorly documented
domains of biodiversity on Earth. Current estimates suggest that between 74,000
and 120,000 fungal species have been formally described, yet the total number of
extant species is conservatively estimated at approximately 1.5 million (Hawksworth
1991). This means that at most only about 5% of all fungal species have been
discovered and named. Some authorities consider even the 1.5 million figure to be
too low, with evidence from tropical plant studies and molecular investigations
suggesting the actual number could be substantially higher.

## The Hawksworth Estimate and Its Basis

The 1.5 million species estimate, first proposed by David Hawksworth in 1991,
is the most widely cited and generally accepted working hypothesis for total
fungal diversity. The estimate is derived from a ratio-based approach: in
[[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]], approximately six times as many fungal species occur at a site
as native plant species. Given the approximately 270,000 described species of
vascular plants, this ratio yields an estimated 1.5 million fungal species. The
figure has been supported by subsequent studies at sites in southern England
where 2,500 to 2,700 fungal species have been recorded from areas of 211 to 400
hectares over more than 25 years of intensive survey by expert mycologists. The
correspondence between fungal and plant diversity at these well-studied sites
reinforces the validity of the 6:1 ratio.

## The Scale of Undiscovered Diversity

No single site on Earth has been comprehensively surveyed for fungi. Even at the
most intensively studied localities in Europe, new species continue to be found.
Fungi cannot be trapped or fogged and collected en masse in the same way as
insects, which imposes severe constraints on [[biodiversity-macrofungi-inventory-methods]]. Many fungal
species produce fruiting bodies only at intervals of several years, and those
structures may then persist for only a few hours or days, making them easy to
miss during sampling. In contrast, other fungi such as lichen-forming species
and many polypores are perennial and can be found at any time of year.

## Cryptic and Hidden Fungi

A substantial proportion of fungal diversity is effectively invisible without
specialized techniques. Many fungi live inside plants (endophytes) or arthropods
without producing externally visible structures. The same host plant may support
different [[biodiversity-fungi-soil-fungal-communities]] during different stages of its life cycle, from
flowering through senescence to decay. Some fungi are found only by examining
leaves and woody surfaces microscopically, or they can be detected only by
isolating and culturing the fungi until they sporulate. Fruiting bodies of many
microfungi are extremely small, frequently as small as 50 micrometers in
diameter, with conidiophores only about 10 micrometers long.

## Pleomorphism and the Naming Problem

Many fungi have more than one morphologically distinct spore-bearing stage in
their life cycles, a phenomenon known as pleomorphism. In so-called pleomorphic
fungi, only one stage may be found at any given time. In some instances, the
different stages have been given separate scientific names, creating significant
nomenclatural confusion. The teleomorph (sexual stage) and anamorph (asexual
stage) may have been described independently by different researchers at
different times. Additional conidial states (synanamorphs) may further complicate
matters. This historical practice of dual nomenclature means that the number of
described fungal species may be inflated, and some species may be counted more
than once.

## Challenges of Fungal Inventory

The challenges of documenting [[biodiversity-fungal-biodiversity-estimation-methods]] are multifaceted. Ephemeral
fruiting patterns mean that single visits to a site will capture only a fraction
of the species present. Microscopic taxa require specialized equipment and
expertise for detection. Many fungi cannot be cultured on standard media, and
standard media, and those that can may not sporulate under laboratory conditions,
preventing accurate identification. The lack of trained fungal taxonomists
worldwide further constrains inventory efforts. No region of the world as yet
has a complete mycota equivalent to a vascular-plant flora, a condition likely
to persist for some time. [[biodiversity-of-fungi-pcr-molecular-methods-fungal-diversity]] are beginning to address some of
these challenges by allowing detection of fungal DNA directly from environmental
samples, but these techniques introduce their own biases and complications.

## Implications for Conservation

Fungi play pivotal roles in all ecosystems, driving [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]], forming
essential symbioses with plants, and serving as food sources for countless
organisms. Changes in fungal species composition and abundance can serve as
indicators of ecosystem health and the effects of environmental perturbations.
During the past 20 to 30 years, species compositions and abundances of
macrofungi at sites in Europe have changed markedly, with several previously
common species no longer encountered. Without baseline inventory data, such
changes could not have been detected. The current dearth of knowledge about
fungal diversity makes it impossible to assess [[fungal-biodiversity-conservation-status]] for the vast
majority of species.
