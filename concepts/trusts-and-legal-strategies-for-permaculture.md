# Trusts and Legal Strategies for Permaculture

## Overview

Trusts in the public interest form the legal backbone upon which churches, universities, schools, research establishments, hospitals, public services, aid programs, and charities operate. Within [[permaculture]], trusts provide the essential legal and financial infrastructure for land conservation, community development, [[ethical-investment-and-earth-banking]], and the creation of self-funding organizations that can operate independently of government grants or corporate sponsorship. Understanding [[permaculture-village-development-and-trust-structures]] is fundamental to implementing permaculture at the community and bioregional scale.

## The Scale of Trust-Based Organizations

The prevalence of trusts in modern society is far greater than most people realize. About 18 to 20 percent of businesses may be non-profit trusts owned or operated by the charitable trusts that benefit from them. France alone is estimated to have 100,000 public interest groups, each with its own areas of interest and subscribers, with about 10,000 forming up annually (and presumably a similar number fading out as their work becomes redundant or completed).

It is quite possible — even sensible — to completely replace the bureaucracy of public services with a series of locally administered trusts. Holland (in particular) largely supplants expensive paid public services (burdened with heavy salary and capital costs, and liable to inaction, self-interest, and executive inefficiency) with publicly formed trusts called *stichtings*. In any small country, such trusts can run all public operations, with "government" becoming simply a way of conveying tax capital back to the regions via local trusts.

Trusts can also self-fund via non-profit businesses to become foundations, fully equipped with their own income sources, making them independent of gifts, grants, or government funding.

## Trust Structure and Legal Framework

### Basic Components

Legally, a trust body consists of two fundamental elements:

1. **A Trustee**: The legal entity that holds and administers the trust's assets
2. **A Trust Deed**: The registered document that defines the purposes, powers, and limitations of the trust

The trust deed is registered with the public company registrar and must clearly define:
- The purposes of the trust (what it exists to achieve)
- The group to whom it applies (e.g., "all citizens of Australia" or "those suffering from spina bifida")
- The duration of the trust (if intended to be "forever," legally stated as something like "until 21 years after the death of the last descendant of the Ming emperors")
- The "will" of the trust, specifying what happens to assets if the trust closes, fails, or completes its mission

### The Trustee Company

It is highly advisable to make the trustee a private company rather than an individual. A corporate trustee offers several advantages:

- **Directors are few in number**: Three or four are enough, and they can appoint replacements if one dies or resigns
- **Anonymity**: Directors can remain anonymous if desired
- **Perpetuity**: A company does not die, unlike its directors, ensuring continuity
- **Efficiency**: A small group of active trustees can act quickly and decisively without reference to the cumbersome and often uninvolved "board of directors" that some trusts appoint

It is wise to restrict directorships of a trustee company to those who are very active in trust affairs and who preferably live close to each other in one region.

### Naming Conventions

Trusts often include words such as: church, foundation, institute, communion, school, congregation, charity, bureau, trust, or company. When trusts are formed to trade, they can take or own any business name that suits their work.

## The Dual-Trust System: Charitable Trust Plus Trading Trust

The most powerful and recommended structure for permaculture organizations is the dual-trust system, consisting of two linked trusts:

### Trust A: The Charitable Trust
- Holds all real property that is not at risk: land, buildings, copyrights, leases, and important equipment that is clear of debt
- Accepts volunteer or contract labor and can pay employees
- Takes no commercial risks — it does not trade
- Can receive tax-deductible donations
- Its main beneficiary is the charitable purpose defined in its deed

### Trust B: The Non-Profit Trading Trust
- Conducts normal business operations, taking commercial risks
- Can rent or lease real property from Trust A
- Pays wages and reimburses costs to staff or volunteers
- Gifts all profits to Trust A (the charitable trust) or to any other charity
- Can involve itself in any area of business that supports the charitable mission

### How the System Works Together

The trading trust generates income through business activities. After paying wages, costs, and operating expenses, it gifts its remaining profits to the charitable trust. These profits are not taxable as income to the charitable trust and may be tax-deductible to the giver. The charitable trust, protected from commercial risk, holds the core assets (land, buildings, equipment) and uses the gifted income to fund its charitable activities. If the trading trust fails commercially, the charitable trust's assets remain protected.

This structure means the overall organization can be fully self-funding without depending on grants or donations, while maintaining the tax advantages and asset protection of charitable status.

## Types of Trusts

### Charitable Trusts
Formed for public benefit purposes such as education, relief of poverty, environmental conservation, or advancement of religion. Gifts to charitable trusts are not taxable and can be tax-deductible to donors.

### Trading (or Unit Discretionary) Trusts
Also known as non-profit corporations, these trusts conduct businesses and trade, giving away their profits annually to named beneficiaries. If the beneficiaries are individuals, such gifts are taxed as private income; if the beneficiaries are charitable trusts, the gift is not taxable.

### Developmental and Property Trusts
These purchase real estate for improvement, lease, or rental on behalf of many small investors. By improving properties, their value increases, and under present rulings, taxation is not incurred on that increase if the property is held for 10 or more years. The trust itself is also not taxed on its income from investors.

## Associations and Volunteer Networks

Any trust can have an unregistered, no-cost association of volunteers, aides, or clients who can publish a newsletter and generally assist the trust in its affairs. This association provides a democratic participatory layer without the legal complexity of formal membership structures.

## Practical Considerations for Trust Formation

### Finding the Right Lawyer

Unless trust formation is a common practice in a particular country, very few lawyers can set up (or even know about) trusts properly. They often give bad advice to groups, setting up litigious or cumbersome systems that give endless trouble and necessitate agreement among many people — an end that is, in practice, impossible to honestly attain. Corporate lawyers are often the most knowledgeable about trust structures.

### Reducing Legal Costs

It is usually possible to buy a copy of the trust deeds of other ethical organizations and use these as a model for a local trust, significantly reducing legal costs. Some law societies service ethical trusts at no charge for their time.

### Professional Support

A good accountant is necessary to lay out the bookkeeping and give advice, and an efficient office manager is needed to communicate with the trust's target population. Other [[permaculture-designers-manual-legal-structures]] such as companies, cooperatives, credit unions, and public investment trusts are routine arrangements well-outlined in company law.

## Corporate Involvement

Many large companies set up and fund non-profit organizations or charitable trusts as a means to reduce taxable income, carry out educational services, or obtain public goodwill. Some businesses tithe to worthy trusts that they believe in — a tithe is usually a tenth of income, but in practice ranges from 5 to 15 percent. Permaculture organizations can benefit from these corporate relationships.

## Decentralization Over Centralization

As so few dedicated people can operate a trust effectively, it is far better to set up many local trusts than to risk the power-centered inefficiencies of a monstrous hierarchical system, such as some religious sects and foundations have become. These are essentially fossilized and no longer of relevance to ordinary people. Every dissenter or group of dissenters should therefore set up trusts to promulgate their own views, or form an independent trust in a cooperative network of like trusts.

## The Permaculture Trust Model

Several small, independent but cooperative Permaculture Institutes and allied groups already exist with associated non-profit trusts operating businesses to fund them. In this way, many trusts are independent of gifts or grants and become self-reliant for funds. This model — a cooperative network of small, independent trusts rather than one large centralized organization — is the recommended approach for permaculture implementation at scale.
