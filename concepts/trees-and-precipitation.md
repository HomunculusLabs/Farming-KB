# Trees and Precipitation

Trees have helped create both our soils and our atmosphere — soils through mechanical (root pressure) and chemical (humic acid) breakdown of rock, combined with life processes as humus and myriad decomposers; atmosphere through gaseous exchange, establishing and maintaining an oxygenated atmosphere and an active water-vapour cycle essential to life. Forests may be doing approximately **80%** of the work of atmospheric maintenance, with the rest due to oceanic or aquatic exchange.

## The Five Basic Effects of Trees on Water Vapour and Windstreams

1. **Compression of streamlines** and induced turbulence in air flows
2. **Condensation phenomena**, especially at night
3. **Rehumidification** by the cycling of water to air
4. **Snow and meltwater effects**
5. **Provision of nuclei for rain**

## Compression and Turbulence Effects

### Air Interaction with the Forest Edge

Windstreams flowing across a forest are partly deflected over the forest canopy (approximately **60%** of the air) and partly absorbed into the trees (approximately **40%**). Within **1,000 metres (3,300 feet)**, the air entering the forest — with its tonnages of water and dust — is brought to a standstill. The forest has swallowed these great energies, resulting in:

- An almost imperceptible warming of air within the forest
- Generally increased humidity averaging **15–18% higher** than ambient air
- Air in which no dust is detectable

Under the forest canopy, negative ions produced by life processes cause dust particles to clump and adhere to each other, producing a fallout of dispersed dust. At the forest edge, thick-stemmed and specially wind-adapted trees buffer the frontline attack of wind.

### The Critical Importance of Forest Edges

If a windward forest edge is cut, removing these defences, **windburn by salt, dust abrasion, or plain wind force may kill or throw down the inner forest** of weaker stems and less resistant species. This commonly observed phenomenon is called **"edge break."** Conversely, a forest can be established by planting tough, resistant trees as windbreak to protect subsequent downwind plantings.

**Design principle: Forest edges are essential and permanent protection and should never be cut or removed.**

### Ekman Spirals and Rainfall Enhancement

Winds deflected over the forest cause compression in the streamlines, extending to **twenty times the tree height**. A 12-metre (40-foot) line of trees compresses air to 244 metres (800 feet) above, creating more water vapour per unit volume and cooling the ascending air stream — both conditions conducive to rain.

A single hedgerow of **40% permeability** will cause similar compression. In flat country, especially in the path of onshore winds, fine grid placements of rain gauges in countries like Holland and Sweden reveal that **40% of rainfall measured downwind of trees and mounds** 12 metres or more in height is caused by this compression phenomenon.

Wind streaming over a hedgerow or forest edge describes a **spiral section, repeated 5–8 times downwind**, creating a series of compression fronts parallel to the windbreak. This phenomenon, first described by Ekman for compression fronts over waves at sea, may result in a ranked series of clouds, often very regular in their rows. They are deflected by drag and the Coriolis force, so wind after the hedgerow may blow 5–15 degrees off its previous course.

## Condensation Phenomena

### Coastal Condensation

On sea-facing coasts, the relatively warmer land surface creates quiet inshore airflows toward evening, and cooler water-laden air flows inland. Where humid air flows over rapidly cooling surfaces — glass, metal, rocks, or the thin laminae of leaves — condensation occurs. On leaves, this is greatly aided by colonies of bacteria (*Pseudomonas*) which also serve as nuclei for frost crystals.

Condensation drip can be as high as **80–86% of total precipitation** on the upland slopes of islands or sea coasts. This phenomenon produced the dense rainforests of Tasmania, Chile, Hawaii, Washington/Oregon, and Scandinavia, the redwood forests of California, and the giant laurel forests of the pre-conquest Canary Islands (now arid due to almost complete deforestation by the Spanish).

### Tree Surface Area for Condensation

A single tree such as a giant Til (*Ocotea foetens*) may present **16 hectares of laminate leaf surface** to sea air, and there can be 100 or so such trees per surface hectare. The taller the trees — giant redwoods and white pines — the larger the volume of moist air intercepted, and the greater the precipitation that follows.

All types of trees act as condensers: Canary Island pines, laurels, holm oaks, redwoods, eucalypts, and Oregon pines. Evergreens work all year, but even deciduous trees catch moisture in winter. On Table Mountain (South Africa) and Lanai (Hawaii), fog drip has been measured at **130–330 cm**, and in both cases condensation exceeds rainfall.

**The effects of condensation can be quickly destroyed. Felling forests causes rivers to dry up, swamps to evaporate, shallow water to dry out, and drought to grip the land — all within the lifetime of a person.**

## Rehumidification of Airstreams

### Forests as Cloud-Makers

Forests are cloud-makers from both water vapour evaporated from leaves by day and water transpired as part of life processes. On high islands, standing clouds cap forested peaks but disappear if forests are cut. The great bridging cloud that once reached from the forests of Maui to Kahoolawe disappeared as cutting and cattle destroyed the upper forests on Maui, lifting the cloud cap from Kahoolawe and leaving it a desert island.

A large evergreen tree such as *Eucalyptus globulus* may pump out **3,600–4,500 litres of water per day**. With sixty or so trees per hectare, many tens of thousands of litres of water are returned to the air to become clouds.

### Critical Research Findings

- A forest can return **75% of its water to air** in large enough amounts to form new rain clouds
- Forested areas return **ten times as much moisture** as bare ground and **twice as much as grasslands**
- The release of water from trees and other plants accounts for **half, or even more, of all moisture returned to air**
- Over forests, **twice as much rain falls** than is available from incoming air — the forest produces **50% of its own rain**
- Of the 75% of water returned by trees to air: 25% is evaporated from leaf surfaces and 50% is transpired; the remaining 25% infiltrates the soil and reaches streams

**Drought in one area may relate directly to deforestation in an upwind direction.**

## Snow and Meltwater Effects

Although trees intercept some snow, the effect of shrubs and trees is to **entrap snow at the edges of clumps** and hold **75–95% of snowfall in shade**. Melting is delayed for **2–10 days** compared with bare ground, resulting in a more gradual release of meltwater.

On open ground, snow may sublime directly to air without productive effect. Even a thin belt of trees entraps large quantities of driven snow in drifts, resulting in protracted release of meltwater to river sources and sustained stream-flow at lower altitudes. When forests were cleared at Pyramid Lake, Nevada in 1846, streams ceased to flow and lake levels fell.

## Provision of Nuclei for Rain

The upward spirals of humid air from forests carry insects, pollen, and bacteria aloft. These organic aerial particles create the nuclei for rain formation. The materials given up by vegetation may be a critical factor in rainfall inland from forests — Kenyan organic tea leaf litter caused water to freeze in a test chamber at only -5°C, making it a much better seeding agent than silver iodide (which requires -8°C to -10°C).

## Design Strategies for Increasing Local Precipitation

1. **Cool the air** by shade or by providing cold surfaces using trees, shrubs, metals, or glass
2. **Cool air by forcing it to higher altitudes** via windbreaks, updraughts from heated surfaces, or mechanical means
3. **Provide condensation nuclei** for raindrops — pollen, bacteria, and organic particles from vegetation
4. **Compress air** to increase water per unit volume by forcing streamlines to converge over trees (Ekman spirals)

**Trees, especially crosswind belts of tall trees, meet all of these criteria in one integrated system.** They also store water for local climatic modification. Total precipitation can be significantly changed (in either direction) by design actions on site, particularly within 30–100 km of coasts.
