# [[fruit-tree-pruning]] and Training

Pruning and training fruit trees is essential for
maintaining healthy, productive trees. The aim is to
restrict growth, encourage fruiting buds, and shape the
tree for maximum light exposure and ease of harvest.

## Feeding and Watering Fruit

Apply two handfuls of blood, fish, and bone meal per
square yard/meter in early spring. Feeding roots are at
the tips of the root system, so apply fertilizer in a
wide band from the branch tips to halfway back to the
trunk. Mulch with well-rotted manure or compost around
the stem for trace elements. Water when fruit is swelling;
stop when fruits color up to prevent fungus disease.
Apply water through a sprinkler for at least an hour at
a time in dry weather.

## Thinning Fruit

Trees drop excess fruit naturally in midsummer. Delay
thinning until after this natural drop. Remove the central
"crown" fruit from each cluster. Thinning does not reduce
total harvest weight; it produces fewer but larger fruits.
How much to thin depends on the variety.

## Training a Dwarf Pyramid

A free-standing tree shaped like a Christmas tree,
ideal for smaller gardens. Plant 5ft (1.5m) apart.

### Initial Pruning
Immediately after planting, cut the stem back to a bud
within 2ft (60cm) of ground level. This establishes the
conical shape. All subsequent pruning is done in summer
to restrict growth.

### Second Winter
Select five or six evenly spaced lower branches with a
wide angle to the stem. Cut them back to a downward- or
outward-facing bud, leaving 10in (25cm) long. Remove
any other branches at this level completely. Cut
branches above this level back to 6in (15cm).

### Ongoing Maintenance
Every winter, prune the leading shoot and each branch
to leave 6in (15cm) of the current year's growth. Once
the tree reaches desired height, prune twice as hard:
new growth to 3in (7cm), sideshoots to 2in (5cm), and
secondary shoots to 1in (2.5cm).

## Training a Cordon

Cordons take very little room and become decorative.
Grow against post-and-wire supports or on existing fences
with canes tied to wires at 2ft (60cm) intervals. Plant
trees 2ft 6in (75cm) apart.

### Initial Pruning
Cut the leading shoot back by one-third after planting.
Cut sideshoots to a downward-facing bud, leaving 3in
(7cm) each. Prune secondary shoots to 1in (2.5cm).

### Summer Pruning
Every summer, prune new sideshoots to 3in (7cm) and
secondary shoots to 1in (2.5cm). Lower the cane to make
room. When the tree reaches the desired length, prune the
main shoot in summer.

## Training an Espalier

Espaliers are trained flat against a wall or fence with
horizontal tiers of branches. Plant trees 12 to 15ft
(3.5 to 4.5m) apart. In the first winter, select two
buds and train them horizontally. Repeat each summer for
additional tiers. After that, prune each arm as for cordon
training.

## Stepovers

A modern variation of the espalier, stepovers are simply
single-tier espaliers grown on wires 2ft (30cm) above the
ground. Ideal for edging vegetable beds or borders. Train
two shoots horizontally after the first year, then prune
as for cordons.

## Encouraging Growth

If a tree fails to produce shoots along one part of the
stem, divert the flow of growth-retarding hormone by
taking a notch out of the bark above the bud you want to
encourage. This simple technique can fill in bare areas.

## Weed Control Under Fruit Trees

The best method of weed control around fruit trees is
mulching with manure, compost, or black plastic. Keep the
area beneath trees clear of weeds, which compete for water
and nutrients. A well-mulched tree requires less water
and produces better fruit.

## Winter Pruning

Most winter pruning should be completed before new growth
starts. Prune fan-trained cherry and plum trees in winter
when they are dormant. Remove dead, diseased, or crossing
branches first. Always cut to an outward-facing bud to
encourage an open, well-shaped tree. Paint large pruning
wounds to prevent disease entry.

## Summer Pruning

Summer pruning restricts growth and encourages fruiting
buds. This is particularly important for trained forms
like cordons, espaliers, and fans. Pinch back sideshoots
to five or six leaves. Summer pruning also improves air
circulation through the tree canopy, reducing the risk of
fungal diseases.
