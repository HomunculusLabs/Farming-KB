# Watershed Management

## Overview

A watershed is the entire land area that drains to a common outlet—a stream, river, lake, or ocean. Watershed management is the integrated planning and management of land and water resources within a watershed to protect water quality, manage flood risk, and maintain ecological health. Even small homesteads exist within watersheds, and individual land management decisions cumulatively determine watershed health. This concept connects directly to [[riparian-zone-restoration]], [[soil-erosion-control]], and [[water-management]] practices.

## Understanding Watersheds

### Watershed Hierarchy
Watersheds are nested within larger watersheds:

- **Subwatershed**: Small drainage areas (tens to hundreds of acres); manageable at the individual or community level
- **Watershed (HUC-10)**: 10,000-100,000+ acres; typically managed by local agencies and watershed groups
- **Basin (HUC-6)**: Major river basins covering thousands of square miles
- **Regional basin (HUC-4)**: Large drainage areas spanning multiple states

The US Geological Survey (USGS) Hydrologic Unit Code (HUC) system provides a standardized framework for delineating and referencing watersheds at multiple scales.

### Watershed Processes
Water moves through watersheds via several pathways:

- **Precipitation**: Rain, snow, sleet, and hail deliver water to the watershed surface
- **Interception**: Vegetation canopies capture 10-40% of precipitation before it reaches the ground
- **Infiltration**: Water soaks into the soil surface; rate depends on soil type, cover, and antecedent moisture
- **Percolation**: Water moves downward through the soil profile to the water table
- **Overland flow**: Water that cannot infiltrate moves across the surface toward streams
- **Subsurface flow**: Water moves laterally through the soil profile toward stream channels
- **Groundwater flow**: Deep water moves slowly through aquifers to discharge points (springs, stream baseflow)
- **Evapotranspiration**: Water returns to the atmosphere through plant transpiration and soil evaporation

## Watershed Assessment

### Physical Assessment
Evaluate the physical characteristics of the watershed:

- **Topography**: Slope steepness and length determine runoff potential and erosion risk
- **Soils**: Soil type, depth, infiltration rate, and erodibility affect water movement and quality
- **Geology**: Bedrock type influences groundwater availability, mineral content, and stream chemistry
- **Land use**: Forest, agriculture, urban, and wetland areas each affect water quantity and quality differently
- **Drainage network**: Stream density, channel condition, and connectivity affect flood response

### Biological Assessment
Living organisms serve as indicators of watershed health:

- **Macroinvertebrate sampling**: Aquatic insect communities are sensitive to pollution and habitat degradation; the EPA uses macroinvertebrate indices as standard bioassessment tools
- **Fish surveys**: Fish species diversity and abundance indicate water quality and habitat condition
- **Riparian vegetation surveys**: Assess the composition, width, and condition of riparian buffers
- **Bird and wildlife surveys**: Indicate overall habitat quality and connectivity
- biodiversity assessment methods provide systematic frameworks for biological monitoring

### Chemical Assessment
Water quality parameters to monitor:

- **Temperature**: Affects dissolved oxygen, metabolic rates, and species survival
- **Dissolved oxygen**: Critical for aquatic life; below 5 mg/L causes stress, below 2 mg/L is lethal for most fish
- **pH**: Influences chemical reactions and species tolerance; natural range 6.5-8.5
- **Nutrients**: Nitrogen and phosphorus from agriculture and wastewater cause eutrophication
- **Turbidity**: Measures suspended sediment; high turbidity reduces light penetration and damages gills
- **Bacteria**: E. coli and fecal coliform indicate contamination from human or animal waste
- **Conductivity**: Measures dissolved ions; elevated conductivity indicates pollution

## Best Management Practices (BMPs)

### Agricultural BMPs
Practices that reduce agricultural impacts on watershed health:

- **Cover crops**: Maintain living root systems and soil cover year-round; reduce erosion by 50-90% and nutrient runoff by 40-60%
- **Nutrient management**: Apply fertilizer based on soil tests and crop needs; timing applications to avoid runoff periods
- **Integrated pest management**: Minimize pesticide use through biological controls, crop rotation, and targeted applications
- **Buffer strips**: Vegetated strips between cropland and waterways filter runoff; see [[riparian-zone-restoration]]
- **Grazing management**: Rotational grazing, riparian exclusion, and proper stocking rates protect streams and pastures
- **No-till farming**: Eliminates tillage erosion, increases infiltration, builds soil organic matter; see [[no-till-gardening-method|no till]]

### Forestry BMPs
- **Riparian buffer zones**: Leave undisturbed buffers along streams during timber harvest (minimum 35-100 feet)
- **Road management**: Minimize road density; maintain drainage on existing roads; close unused roads
- **Stream crossings**: Use bridges or culverts rather than fords; design for 25-50 year flood events
- **Harvest planning**: Avoid clearcutting on steep slopes near streams; use variable retention harvesting

### Urban and Homestead BMPs
- **Rain gardens**: Bioretention areas that capture and filter stormwater runoff from roofs, driveways, and lawns
- **Rain barrels and cisterns**: Capture roof runoff for later use; reduce peak stormwater flows
- **Permeable surfaces**: Gravel driveways, permeable pavers, and pervious concrete allow infiltration
- **Disconnect downspouts**: Direct roof runoff to lawns, gardens, or rain gardens rather than to storm drains
- [[graywater-systems|greywater]] systems: Treat and reuse household wastewater for landscape irrigation
- **Lawn management**: Reduce lawn area; raise mowing height; reduce fertilizer and pesticide use
- [[permaculture-earthworks|earthworks]]: Swales, berms, and basins manage water flow on the homestead scale

### Construction BMPs
- **Silt fencing**: Geotextile barrier that traps sediment on construction sites; requires proper installation and maintenance
- **Sediment basins**: Temporary ponds that capture and settle sediment from construction runoff
- **Stabilized construction entrances**: Stone pads at site access points reduce mud tracking onto roads
- **Phased grading**: Disturb only the area needed for current construction phase; stabilize completed areas immediately
- **Immediate seeding and mulching**: Never leave bare soil exposed; seed and mulch within 48 hours of final grading

## Watershed Restoration

### In-Stream Restoration
- **Stream daylighting**: Buried or piped streams are uncovered and restored to surface flow
- **Channel re-meandering**: Straightened channels are reconfigured with natural meander patterns
- **Habitat structures**: Log jams, root wads, and boulder clusters create pool-riffle sequences and cover habitat
- **Fish passage**: Remove barriers (dams, poorly designed culverts) to restore upstream-downstream connectivity
- **Floodplain reconnection**: Remove or set back levees to allow rivers to access their natural floodplains

### Upland Restoration
- **Reforestation**: Plant trees on degraded land to reduce runoff, stabilize soil, and restore habitat
- **Wetland restoration**: Restore drained wetlands to recover water storage, filtration, and habitat functions; see [[wetland-ecology]]
- **Prairie restoration**: Establish native grasslands on marginal cropland; see [[prairie-ecology]]
- **Invasive species removal**: Remove and replace invasive plants that degrade habitat and water quality; see [[invasive-species-management]]

### Watershed-Scale Planning
- **Priority area identification**: Use GIS analysis to target restoration in subwatersheds with the highest pollutant loads or most degraded habitat
- **Impervious surface limits**: Maintain impervious surface below 10% of watershed area to protect stream health
- **Riparian corridor continuity**: Maintain continuous riparian buffers along all streams and rivers
- **Land conservation**: Protect critical areas through conservation easements, land acquisition, and voluntary stewardship agreements

## Monitoring and Adaptive Management

### Monitoring Program Design
Effective monitoring programs include:

- **Baseline assessment**: Document pre-restoration conditions
- **Reference sites**: Monitor undisturbed sites for comparison
- **Regular sampling**: Seasonal or quarterly monitoring of key parameters
- **Standardized methods**: Use consistent protocols to ensure data comparability over time
- **Data management**: Store data in organized databases with quality control procedures

### Adaptive Management Cycle
1. **Assess**: Evaluate current conditions and identify problems
2. **Plan**: Develop management strategies with measurable objectives
3. **Implement**: Carry out planned actions
4. **Monitor**: Track changes in response to management
5. **Evaluate**: Compare results to objectives
6. **Adjust**: Modify management based on monitoring results
7. Repeat the cycle

## Community Watershed Management

Watershed health depends on collective action:

- **Watershed groups**: Local organizations that coordinate conservation efforts across property boundaries
- **Cost-share programs**: Government programs (NRCS, state agencies) that help landowners implement BMPs
- **Education and outreach**: Workshops, field days, and demonstration projects that promote watershed stewardship
- **Water quality monitoring networks**: Volunteer monitoring programs that engage citizens in data collection
- **Landowner cooperatives**: Groups of landowners working together on landscape-scale conservation projects
