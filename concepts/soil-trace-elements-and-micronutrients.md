# [[soil-ph]], biological activity, and parent rock composition. Deficiency or excess of any single trace element can cascade through entire ecosystems, affecting crop yields, animal health, and human nutrition.

## Sulphur (S)

Many anaerobic bacteria (**thiobacilli**) fix sulphur, which is why anaerobic ferment of plant and animal materials is rich in sulphur-based amino acids and of high nutritional value. Sulphur oxidised by anaerobic bacilli also removes most heavy metals from flow-through systems such as sewage digesters, precipitating them as insoluble sulphates.

### Soil Applications

Sulphur is used in **drylands to reduce pH** and make iron, zinc, and trace elements available to plants. With ammonium sulphate, soils tend to become acidic, reducing plant yields — so sulphur application must be managed carefully. Clover, in particular, may show sulphur deficiency in the subtropics during wet summers.

### Biological Role

Sulphur is part of all proteins and present in the body as the two amino acids **methionine** and **cysteine**. The vitamins thiamin and biotin contain sulphur. Deficiencies do not occur if meat protein is sufficient, or if anaerobic ferment of leaf materials is part of food preparation. Many vegetarians obtain their amino acids from yeasts or bacteria rather than from fresh plant material.

Some thiobacilli can operate down to pH 1.0, demonstrating remarkable acid tolerance and their role in sulphur cycling under extreme conditions.

## Chlorine (Cl)

Chlorine is used by plants and is normally available as salt. It is a trace element used only in minute amounts, but it concentrates significantly in crops — 350 ppm in soil can give 1000 ppm in harvested crop tissue.

### Health Warnings

Chlorine was used as a war gas and today is used to "sterilise" water supplies. It is a **dangerous gas to inhale**. In water and in contact with organic materials, chlorine releases **chloroform**, a carcinogenic gas. **Avoid chlorinated water if possible.**

## Potassium (K)

Potassium is used in large quantities by plants and is usually plentiful in arid areas. It is deficient on sandy, free-draining coastal soils.

### Removal Factors

Not much potassium is removed by livestock, but intensive cropping of potatoes, beans, flax, and the export of hay may remove soil reserves below plant needs. Potassium is readily absorbed on [[gypsum]] is applied at 30 tonnes/ha and excess sodium removed by flushing out as sodium sulphate.

### Ideal Ion Ratios

The proportion of the four major ions in ideal agricultural soils should be approximately:

**Calcium : Magnesium : Potassium : Sodium = 50 : 35 : 6 : 5**

Key interactions:
- Adding potassium can increase sodium
- Sodium is antagonistic to (displaces) calcium
- Magnesium ions should always be less than calcium ions
- Do not add too much [[dolomite]] if clay soils already contain adequate magnesium

### Plant Functions

Calcium is an essential part of cell walls, enzymes, and chromosome structure. Plants need large amounts. It is lost (excreted) in stress and needs replacement after prolonged stress periods.

### Sex Ratio and Health Effects

Low calcium areas produce predominantly **male** farm animals and humans (as a primary sex ratio). Lack of calcium produces skeletal and metabolic malfunction. [[seed-pelleting]], or foliar sprays. Even periodic flooding mobilises manganese. A typical deficiency situation occurs on poor sands heavily dressed with lime.

### Biological Cycling

Bacteria fix insoluble manganese and can create problems in pipes and concrete water raceways even at 2–3 ppm. **Aluminium sulphate** (from acid rain on soil) may mobilise manganese, mercury, and cadmium to lethal levels. Manganese leaches out of acid soils and deposits in alkaline horizons as manganese-iron concretions, and on sea floors as larger manganese nodules.

## Iron (Fe)

Iron is normally plentiful in soils and is a critical element for both plants and animals. It plays essential roles in chlorophyll synthesis, energy transfer, and oxygen transport.

### Deficiency Symptoms

Plants show **interveinal leaf yellowing** in alkaline drylands or on heavily limed garden soils. In people, iron deficiency causes **anaemia** with characteristic lassitude and ill-health.

### Remedies

- **Gardens**: Sulphur application or iron chelates
- **Direct addition**: Small amounts of ferric sulphate
- **Soil indicators**: Ferrous iron spicules in soil assist ethylene formation; ferric iron (oxides) are "unavailable" (Fe₂O₃)
- **Geological indicators**: Iron concretions and iron staining in sands are often associated with microbial action along plant root traces

### Human Health

Blood iron is as important in energy transfers as copper is in invertebrates. Deficiencies must be watched in women of reproductive age, infants, and children. Iron-deficiency anaemia is common in deserts or after blood losses from menses or dysentery.

**Dietary sources**: Veal, liver, fish eaten with soya beans, lettuce, parsley, maize. Meat and ascorbic acid (vitamin C) increase iron absorption. Iron in bone marrow is needed for new blood cells. Clinically, ferrous salts can treat excessive blood loss.

### Excess Iron

People brewing beer or wine, or cooking cereals in iron pots, may ingest excess iron. Alcoholism and diabetes exacerbate iron excess, though this is an unusual condition overall.

## Cobalt (Co)

Cobalt can be deficient on poor coastal sands. It is necessary to many legumes and especially needed by livestock, who become "unthrifty" if deficient — particularly in highly alkaline areas.

### Application

Only an ounce or two per acre is needed, supplied as a cobalt "bullet" in the rumen of sheep. Cobalt is necessary for synthesis of **vitamin B₁₂** in ruminants; if deficient, further metabolic problems cascade. Often diagnosed via B₁₂ tests on blood levels. Deficiency can cause anaemia; toxicity can occur with excess. Radioactive cobalt is a serious poison.

## Nickel (Ni)

Nickel is not well-noted as a plant nutrient or poison, but with copper it can intensify poisoning in people — for example, in office rooms using urns, or in mining areas. Many people demonstrate skin reactions (rash) from nickel jewellery such as bracelets.

## Copper (Cu)

Copper is a necessary micronutrient that becomes a poison at higher concentrations. In plants, copper is an enzyme activator concentrated in the chloroplasts of leaves.

### Deficiency Conditions

Copper is deficient on:
- Shell-grit dunes
- Acid peaty soils
- Coastal heaths over poor quartzite sands
- Deeply weathered basalts

**Black sheep** are good biological indicators — if copper-deficient, they develop whitish or brown wool, or white-coloured "steely" wool.
