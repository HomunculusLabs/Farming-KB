## Agrocybe — Nutritional and Medicinal Profile

### Nutritional Value

Agrocybe species are notably protein-rich, containing approximately **27% protein by dry weight**, placing them among the more nutritious cultivated mushrooms. They have been used for centuries in **Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)**, particularly for treating spleen disorders and supporting digestive health.

### Medicinal Properties

Agrocybe exhibits a broad spectrum of bioactive compounds:

- **Antiviral activity** — Effective against both human and plant viruses, including the tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), making it of interest in agricultural phytosanitary applications.
- **Antibacterial activity** — Demonstrated efficacy against a range of pathogenic bacteria:
  - *Escherichia coli*
  - *[[staphylococcus-aureus]]*
  - *Bacillus cereus*
  - *[[bacillus-subtilis]]*
  - *Salmonella typhimurium*
- **Anti-inflammatory** properties support their traditional use in TCM formulations.
- **Anti-cancer potential** — Research has shown activity against leukemia cell lines.

### Mycoremediation Potential

Agrocybe produces the **laccase enzyme**, which enables it to break down a range of environmental contaminants. Notably, it is effective at degrading **endocrine-disrupting chemicals** such as **bisphenol A (BPA)**, making it a candidate for bioremediation of contaminated water and soil.

## The Genus Auricularia (Wood Ear / Tree Ear)

### Species

The genus includes several commercially and ecologically important species:

- **_A. auricula_** — The classic wood ear, most widely cultivated
- **_A. fuscosuccinea_** — Brownish-red fruiting bodies, less common in cultivation
- **_A. polytricha_** — Hairy wood ear, closely related and often confused with *A. auricula*

### Difficulty Rating

| Environment | Difficulty |
|-------------|-----------|
| Outdoor     | 1 (Easy)  |

### Ecology and Natural Habitat

Auricularia species are **saprophytic jelly fungi** found worldwide on dead or dying hardwood. They are among the most resilient fungi in nature, capable of surviving extreme desiccation and rehydrating to resume growth and fruiting. This durability is central to both their ecological role and their commercial value.

### Mycelium and Spawn

- Mycelium is white and relatively fast-growing on [[grain-spawn]].
- **Wild spawn expansion** via the **cardboard method** is highly effective — mycelium readily colonizes damp cardboard, making it accessible for low-tech cultivation.
- **Lab isolation** presents challenges due to the jelly-like consistency of fresh tissue, which complicates sterile culture work. Dried specimens can be rehydrated for isolation but contamination rates are higher than with firmer-fleshed mushrooms.

### Fruitbody Development

Auricularia produce distinctive **cup-shaped, ear-like fruiting bodies** with a gelatinous, rubbery texture. They are true jelly fungi (Tremellales), with fruitbodies that are translucent when fresh and thin, becoming darker and more brittle when dried.

### Cultivation Methods

**Outdoor cultivation** on hardwood logs is straightforward but slow. Logs may take 10–14 months to begin fruiting, and yields are generally modest. However, outdoor beds require minimal maintenance once established.

**Indoor cultivation** on supplemented hardwood [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] is far more productive. First harvest occurs within 50–60 days of inoculation, and growers can expect three or more flushes per block. Maintaining high humidity (85–95%) and adequate fresh air exchange is critical for proper ear formation.

### Harvesting and Storage

- **Fresh storage:** 3–4 weeks refrigerated — among the longest shelf lives of any cultivated mushroom.
- **Dried storage:** Excellent; wood ear **dries exceptionally well** and reconstitutes fully in water, regaining much of its original texture.

### Marketing

Wood ear is described as **"virtually indestructible"** from a supply chain perspective. It ships well, stores indefinitely when dried, and reconstitutes reliably. These traits make it highly attractive for [[magic-mushroom-retail-markets-and-commercial-distribution-channels]], especially in Asian markets where it is a staple ingredient in soups and stir-fries.

### Nutritional and Medicinal Value

- **Protein:** 8–10% by dry weight
- **Anticoagulant properties** — Contains compounds that inhibit blood platelet aggregation, traditionally valued for cardiovascular health
- **Anti-inflammatory** activity supports immune function

### Mycoremediation

Auricularia plays a role in **habitat renewal**, particularly in arid and degraded environments. Its ability to colonize dead wood in harsh conditions and contribute to [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] makes it useful for [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]] projects.

## Cultivation Notes and Yield

- Wood ear (Auricularia) can produce 3-4 flushes over 6-8 weeks on supplemented sawdust blocks
- Blewits (Lepista nuda) are **difficult to cultivate commercially** — most foraging is wild-harvest
- Wood ear substrate: hardwood sawdust + 10-15% bran, sterilized at 15 PSI for 90 minutes
- Blewits prefer **composted leaf litter** and can be established in outdoor mushroom beds
- Dried wood ear reconstitutes well — market demand is primarily for dried product in Asian cuisine

## Culinary Uses

- Wood ear has a mild, earthy flavor valued primarily for its **crunchy gelatinous texture**
- Essential ingredient in hot and sour soup, stir-fries, and Buddhist vegetarian dishes
- Blewits have a distinctive **violet color** when young, fading to tan with age; flavor is earthy and slightly sweet
- Wood ear must be cooked thoroughly — raw consumption can cause allergic reactions in some individuals
