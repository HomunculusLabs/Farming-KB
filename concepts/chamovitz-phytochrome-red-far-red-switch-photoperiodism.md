# Phytochrome and the Red Far-Red Light Switch

The discovery of phytochrome in the 1950s revealed that plants possess a
sophisticated molecular switch for measuring the duration of darkness, one of the
most elegant light-sensing mechanisms in all of biology. Daniel Chamovitz, in
"What a Plant Knows," traces the story of this discovery from Darwin's
phototropism experiments through the Maryland Mammoth tobacco mystery to the
identification of the phytochrome pigment itself.

## From Phototropism to Photoperiodism

Charles Darwin and his son Francis established in 1880 that plants could "see"
light, demonstrating that phototropism, the bending of plants toward light, was
directed by light perception at the tip of the shoot. Their classic experiment
involved five seedlings: an untreated control, one with its tip removed, one with
its tip covered by a lightproof cap, one with its tip covered by a clear glass cap,
and one with its middle section covered by a lightproof tube. Only the seedlings
that could receive light at their tips bent toward the light source.

Julius von Sachs subsequently discovered in 1864 that blue light was the primary
color inducing phototropism in plants, while plants were generally insensitive to
other colors in this regard. But the question of how plants measure the length of
day to regulate flowering remained unresolved for decades following these
foundational discoveries.

## The Maryland Mammoth Mystery

The breakthrough came with a remarkable tobacco strain discovered in 1906 in the
valleys of southern Maryland. These valleys had been home to America's greatest
tobacco farms since the first European settlers arrived at the end of the
seventeenth century, and farmers had always planted their crop in spring and
harvested in late summer.

The new strain, aptly named Maryland Mammoth, grew to four and a half meters in
height and produced almost a hundred leaves, yet rarely flowered. It would only
stop growing when the frosts set in. While its robust growth seemed like a boon,
its failure to flower meant farmers could not harvest seed for the next year's
crop.

Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of Agriculture
demonstrated in 1918 that by limiting the light the plants received, moving them
into a dark shed each afternoon, they could force the Maryland Mammoth to flower.
Simply limiting the amount of light the plants saw was enough to cause them to
stop growing leaves and start making flowers and seeds. This phenomenon, called
photoperiodism, provided the first strong evidence that plants measure how much
light they take in.

## Short-Day and Long-Day Plants

Photoperiodism research revealed two major categories of flowering behavior. Short-
day plants, such as chrysanthemums and soybeans, flower only when the days become
sufficiently short. Long-day plants, such as irises and barley, require long days
to initiate flowering. This classification transformed agriculture by enabling
farmers to manipulate flowering schedules through controlled lighting.

Florida farmers learned to grow Maryland Mammoth through the mild months, and the
plants would eventually flower in midwinter when the days were shortest. This
practical application demonstrated that the plant's internal calendar could be
overridden by controlling the light environment.

## Plants Measure Night, Not Day

A series of experiments around the time of World War II revealed a
counterintuitive fact: plants do not measure the length of the day but rather the
length of the continuous period of darkness. Scientists found that they could
prevent a short-day plant like the soybean from flowering during naturally short
days simply by turning the lights on for a few minutes in the middle of the night.
Conversely, they could cause a long-day plant like the iris to flower in the
middle of winter by interrupting the long night with a brief flash of light.

This discovery had immediate commercial application. Chrysanthemum farmers could
keep their plants from flowering until just before Mother's Day by turning on
lights for a few minutes each night throughout autumn and winter. Then, two weeks
before the target date, they would stop the night-lighting, and all the plants
would flower simultaneously, ready for harvest and shipping.

## The Color Specificity: Only Red Matters

Scientists next investigated what color of light the plants were using to measure
the night. The answer was surprising: only red light could interrupt the dark
period and influence flowering. Blue or green flashes during the night had no
effect on flowering, but even a few seconds of red light were sufficient to reset
the plant's internal clock.

This was a remarkable finding because it established that plants use different
photoreceptor systems for different purposes. Blue light directs phototropism,
telling the plant which direction to bend. Red light governs photoperiodism,
telling the plant how long the night has been. The plant effectively has two
different kinds of eyes, each sensitive to different colors and serving different
functions.

## The Far-Red Reversal

The most elegant discovery came in the early 1950s when Harry Borthwick and
colleagues at the same USDA laboratory where Maryland Mammoth had been studied
found that far-red light, light with wavelengths slightly longer than bright red
and most often barely visible at dusk, could cancel the effect of red light on
plants.

The experimental results were striking. If irises, which normally do not flower in
long nights, received a pulse of red light in the middle of the night, they would
produce flowers. But if far-red light was shone on them immediately after the red
pulse, it was as if they had never seen the red light at all. They would not
flower. If red light was then applied after the far-red, they would flower.
Far-red again, and they would not. This on-off switching could be repeated
multiple times, with only a few seconds of each color being sufficient.

## The Phytochrome Switch

By 1960, Warren L. Butler and colleagues demonstrated that a single photoreceptor
was responsible for both the red and far-red effects. They named it phytochrome,
meaning "plant color." In its simplest model, phytochrome functions as a
light-activated molecular switch with two stable states:

- Red light activates phytochrome, converting it to a form primed to receive
  far-red light (the active Pfr form)
- Far-red light inactivates phytochrome, converting it to a form primed to
  receive red light (the inactive Pr form)

On a philosophical level, the plant can be said to "remember" the last color it
saw. This molecular memory allows the plant to track the duration of continuous
darkness with remarkable precision.

## Ecological Logic of the Red Far-Red System

The ecological logic of this system is elegant. In nature, the last light any
plant sees at the end of the day is far-red light, which signals the plant to
"turn off" its photoperiodic response. In the morning, the plant sees red light
and "wakes up." By measuring how long ago it last saw red light, the plant can
determine the length of the dark period and adjust its growth and flowering
accordingly.

This mechanism allows plants to track seasonal changes with great precision. As
days lengthen in spring, the daily period of uninterrupted far-red signaling at
dusk shortens relative to the red signaling at dawn, triggering flowering in
long-day plants. As days shorten in autumn, the reverse occurs, triggering
flowering in short-day plants.

## The Leaf as the Photoperiodic Eye

A surprising finding was that the photoperiodic "eye" is not located in the plant
tip, as Darwin had shown for phototropism. Instead, phytochrome for photoperiodism
is located in the leaves. Experiments demonstrated that illuminating any single
leaf during the night is sufficient to regulate flowering in the entire plant. If
all leaves are removed, leaving only the stem and apex, the plant becomes
completely blind to light flashes for photoperiodic purposes, even if the entire
above-ground plant is illuminated.

Phytochrome in the leaves receives the light cues and initiates a mobile signal
that propagates throughout the plant and induces the flowering response at the
growing tips. This elegant division of labor allows the plant's light-sensing
organs to be distributed across its structure, maximizing light capture while the
response occurs at the growing points.

## The Two-Photoreceptor System

The discovery of phytochrome alongside the earlier identification of blue-light
photoreceptors for phototropism revealed that plants possess at least two distinct
photoreceptor systems working in parallel. Blue-light receptors guide spatial
orientation and directional growth through the shoot tip. Phytochrome, responsive
to red and far-red light, governs temporal perception and the measurement of day
length through the leaves. Together, these systems give plants a remarkably
sophisticated capacity to sense and respond to their light environment, one that
Chamovitz argues legitimately deserves to be called a form of vision.
