# Compost Tea Insect Pest Suppression

[[elaine-ingham]]'s field guide documents numerous cases where [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) suppresses insect pests without chemical pesticides, working through indirect biological mechanisms rather than direct toxicity.

## How Compost Tea Repels Insects

Compost tea does not typically kill insects directly. Instead, it works through several indirect mechanisms that make plants less attractive or less vulnerable to pest attack.

When beneficial microbes colonize leaf surfaces, they occupy the ecological niches that pest organisms need to establish themselves. A leaf coated with diverse bacteria and fungi provides no foothold for pest organisms or their eggs.

Healthy plants produced with adequate biological support have higher brix levels (dissolved solids in plant sap), which correlates with greater pest resistance. Insects often preferentially attack stressed or nutrient-imbalanced plants.

The full soil food web supports plant immune responses, enabling stronger chemical defenses against herbivory. Plants under biological management produce more [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] that deter feeding.

## Root Grubs and Wireworms

Root-feeding pests like white grubs (scarab beetle larvae) and wireworms (click beetle larvae) are particularly challenging because they attack below ground, where foliar sprays cannot reach them.

Compost tea applied as a soil drench can suppress root-feeding pests by establishing predatory nematodes and fungi that attack pest larvae in the root zone.

[[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]] in the compost tea inoculum actively hunt root-feeding larvae, entering through body openings and releasing bacteria that kill the host within 24-48 hours.

Fungal pathogens including [[beauveria-bassiana]] and [[metarhizium-anisopliae]], which may be present in well-made compost tea, can infect and kill soil-dwelling insect larvae.

The key is building the complete soil food web so that multiple lines of defense — predation, competition, and parasitism — work together against root pests.

## Foliar Insect Deterrence

Ingham reports that compost tea applications to foliage can reduce populations of leaf-feeding insects including aphids, thrips, and whiteflies.

The mechanism appears to be primarily through improving plant health and brix levels. Insects detect plant quality through chemoreceptors and preferentially feed on plants with lower brix readings.

Compost tea foliar sprays also establish beneficial microbes on leaf surfaces that may produce compounds insects find distasteful or that interfere with insect feeding behavior.

Application timing matters — early-season applications that establish beneficial populations before pest arrival are more effective than reactive applications after pest outbreaks begin.

## Tent Caterpillars

Tent caterpillars (Malacosoma species) defoliate trees by constructing silk tents in branch crotches and feeding on surrounding leaves.

Compost tea suppression of tent caterpillars relies on improving tree health and activating plant defenses rather than direct contact killing.

Trees treated with compost tea and supported by a healthy soil food web produce leaves with higher [[fungal-tannin-and-phenolic-decomposition]] concentrations, which deter caterpillar feeding.

Additionally, compost tea applications may support populations of natural caterpillar predators including parasitic wasps, predatory stink bugs, and birds that are attracted to more diverse and insect-rich canopy ecosystems.

Foliar coverage must be thorough, reaching into the interior canopy where caterpillars feed, to establish microbial communities on the leaves they consume.

## Mites and Bulb Mites

Mite infestations, including spider mites on foliage and bulb mites in stored or in-ground bulbs, represent different challenges requiring different approaches.

Spider mites thrive in hot, dry conditions on stressed plants. Compost tea improves plant hydration and stress tolerance, making the environment less favorable for mite population explosions.

Bulb mites (Rhizoglyphus species) attack stored bulbs and in-ground bulb crops. Compost tea as a soil drench around bulb plantings can establish [[query-what-are-predatory-mites-and-how-can-i-use-them-for-pest-control]] fungi that keep pest mite populations in check.

The competitive exclusion principle applies: when beneficial microbes and mites occupy the bulb surface and surrounding soil, pest mites have difficulty establishing.

## Scale on Rhododendrons

Scale insects on rhododendrons and other ornamentals are protected by their waxy shell covers, making them resistant to most contact insecticides and even many biological controls.

Compost tea addresses scale through a multi-pronged approach: improving overall plant vigor so the plant can tolerate some scale presence, supporting natural enemies like parasitic wasps, and potentially softening scale covers through microbial activity.

Repeated applications of compost tea throughout the growing season are typically necessary, as the microbial populations need time to establish and scale has multiple overlapping generations.

## Beauveria — Fruit Fly Larvae Suppression

Ingham specifically highlights [[query-how-do-i-use-beauveria-bassiana-for-pest-control]] as a beneficial fungus that repels and kills fruit fly larvae in soil.

When compost tea contains Beauveria spores, soil drench applications around susceptible crops can infect fruit fly larvae as they pupate in the soil, breaking the pest life cycle.

The combination of Beauveria with humic acids in compost tea appears to enhance spore germination and infection rates, making the biological control more effective.

This approach is particularly valuable in orchard and berry crops where fruit flies cause direct economic damage and where chemical controls may leave residues on harvestable fruit.

## Eastern Rots and Banana Pests

Ingham documents compost tea success against various rots and pest complexes in tropical crops including bananas.

Eastern rots and similar fungal disease complexes on bananas are suppressed by the competitive microbial communities established through compost tea applications.

The tropical climate that favors these pathogens also supports rapid microbial colonization from compost tea, making biological approaches especially effective in warm, humid growing conditions.

## Moss and Algae Suppression

While moss and algae are not insects, Ingham includes them in the pest suppression section because they compete with crop plants and indicate soil conditions that favor them over desired species.

Moss and algae proliferation typically indicates compaction, poor drainage, low nutrient availability, or excess moisture — conditions that also stress crop plants.

Compost tea addresses the underlying biology: by improving soil structure through microbial activity, increasing nutrient cycling, and supporting competitive plant growth, the conditions that favor moss and algae are gradually eliminated.

## Integration with Other Management Practices

Compost tea pest suppression works best as part of an integrated biological management system, not as a standalone treatment.

Regular compost tea applications (monthly or biweekly during the growing season) maintain microbial populations that provide continuous pest suppression.

Combining compost tea with other biological practices — cover cropping, [[coleman-bed-preparation-and-minimal-tillage]], mulching, and diverse rotations — creates an ecosystem that naturally suppresses pest outbreaks.

Soil drench applications should target the root zone, while foliar sprays need thorough coverage of both upper and lower leaf surfaces for maximum effectiveness.

## Key Takeaways

- Compost tea suppresses insects primarily through indirect mechanisms: improving plant health, establishing competitive leaf and soil microbiomes, and supporting natural predators.
- Root-feeding pests like grubs and wireworms are controlled through soil drench applications that introduce predatory nematodes and entomopathogenic fungi.
- Foliar pests are deterred by higher brix levels in biologically-supported plants and by beneficial microbial communities on leaf surfaces.
- Beauveria bassiana in compost tea is specifically effective against fruit fly larvae in soil.
- Consistent, season-long applications are more effective than reactive treatments after pest outbreaks appear.
