# Pulsers and Biological Timing

## Overview

All flows in nature and in organisms pulse. Whether blood in veins, wind across landscapes, water in streams, lava from volcanoes, or traffic on roads, the movement of materials and energy follows rhythmic, pulsing patterns. These pulses are organized by **pulsers**—mechanisms that start, run for a preset time, and stop. Understanding pulsers is essential for understanding how living systems grow, maintain their forms, and eventually cease to function.

## The Nature of Pulsers

Pulsers are the rhythmic organizers of flow. They take the form of:

- **Chemical spirals** in sheets of cells that swirl in sequence to create pulsing movements in organs and viscera
- **Traffic lights** that organize the flow of vehicles
- **Biological clocks** that regulate metabolism, breeding, and behavior
- **Chemical oscillators** like the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction, where organic acids seeded with ionic catalysts (iron, cerium) produce regular spiral waves

These pulsers can start, run for a predetermined duration, and stop. This is how organisms grow, how organs function, and why organisms eventually die.

### The Heartbeat Quota

All mammals have an allotted number of heartbeats in relation to their body size. Small animals with rapid heart rates live shorter lives; large animals with slow heart rates live longer. When the allotted beats are exhausted, the organism dies. This principle extends broadly: every pulsing system carries an inherent energy budget that, once consumed, terminates the process.
