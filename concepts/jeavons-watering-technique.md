# Watering Technique in Biointensive Gardens
Watering in the [[jeavons-grow-biointensive-method-overview]] method approximates natural rainfall
as much as possible. The fine rain of water absorbs beneficial
airborne nutrients as well as air, helping the growth process. This
page covers the specific techniques, timing, and [[fungal-biodiversity-assessment-methods]]
for watering beds and flats in the biointensive garden.
## Tools for Gentle Watering
### For Flats and Seedlings
An English Haws sprinkling can with fine holes in the sprinkler's
"rose" is the preferred tool. The rose points upward so that water
pressure builds up and goes into the air first, where much of the
pressure is dissipated. The water then softly falls on the plants
from above like rain, with only the force of gravity pulling it
down.
### For Planting Beds
The same rainfall-simulation method can be used with a water gun or a
valve unit with a fan spray nozzle. A heavy-duty hose may be needed
to contain the water pressure. The gentle method packs down the soil
less and plants are not hit and damaged by a hard spray.
### Watering Wand
A watering wand allows more precise direction of water under a
plant's leaves. Water falls in a circular pattern, landing about 3
feet from the waterer. This is especially useful for plants that
should not have wet leaves.
## Leaf Wetness Considerations
Different plants have different tolerance for wet foliage:
### Plants That Like Wet Leaves
Members of the cabbage family benefit from overhead watering. It is
all right and in fact beneficial to water these plants from overhead.
### Plants That Suffer from Wet Leaves
Tomatoes, peas, and members of the squash and melon families can
suffer from wilt and mildew, and their fruit may rot when leaves
are wet — especially in foggy or humid climates. To avoid spraying
their leaves, hold the fan just above the soil and point it sideways,
or use a watering wand to direct water beneath the leaves.
In drier climates, overhead watering of these sensitive plants
probably will not matter. Adjust your technique to your local
conditions.
## Timing of Watering
### Mature Plants in Beds
Water when the heat of the day first subsides — about 2 hours before
sunset during summer, and earlier during winter. However, weather
conditions, especially cloud cover, may necessitate earlier watering.
### Why Late Afternoon Is Best
- The soil, warmed during the day, warms the cool water from the
  hose so it is more temperate when it reaches plant roots
- Roots suffer less shock from temperature change
- Soil and plants have more time to absorb water during the cooler,
  less windy night
- Plants do significant growing at night and need adequate moisture
- Water can percolate into the soil for 12+ hours before sun and wind
  cause significant evaporation
### Why Other Times Are Less Ideal
- **Early morning**: Much water lost to evaporation from sun and wind
- **Midday**: Maximum evaporation loss; least effective watering
- **Evening**: Plants more susceptible to mildew and rust from
  unevaporated water on leaves
### Seeds, Seedlings, and Immature Plants
May require watering in the morning, at noon, and late afternoon.
