# Fungal-Faunal Interactions in Ecosystems

## Overview

Fungi interact with animals in virtually every ecosystem on Earth, forming relationships that range from mutualistic to pathogenic. These interactions are critical to ecosystem functioning, influencing decomposition rates, nutrient cycling, plant community structure, and population regulation of both fungi and animals. The breadth of fungal-faunal interactions spans terrestrial, freshwater, and marine environments, and includes both vertebrate and invertebrate partners.

## Marine and Aquatic Fungal-Faunal Interactions

### Wood Decomposition in Marine Ecosystems

In marine ecosystems, the low resource quality of wood encourages tight linkages between fungi and fauna for its decomposition. Wood-boring marine mollusks preferentially settle and feed on wood that has been previously colonized by fungi and partially decomposed, rather than invading fresh wood. These associations have become so specialized that the wood-boring crustacean the gribble (*[[limnoria-tripunctata]]*) shows increased longevity when feeding on wood colonized by fungi. More significantly, the gribble is incapable of reproduction on any substrate unless marine fungi are included as part of its diet. This dependency likely arises from the enhanced availability of proteins, essential amino acids, and vitamins that fungi provide — nutrients otherwise unavailable in the wood substrate.

### Fungal Pathogens of Marine Animals

Fungi serve as major pathogens of marine animals. Mitosporic fungi cause disease in crustaceans and damage corals and juvenile clams. Dematiacious [[fungal-hyphae]] (*Exserohilum rostratum* and *Curvularia* spp.) have been isolated from the mantle and shell of juvenile *Tridacna crocea* clams, where they are associated with declining health. Many oomycetes are highly destructive pathogens in finfish, mollusks, and shellfish. While relatively few fungi have been reported as pathogens of marine plants, many marine algae are susceptible to fungal pathogens, primarily chytrids and oomycetes. The overall role of fungi in marine ecosystem health remains significantly under-researched.

### Gut Symbionts in Aquatic Animals

Like terrestrial and aquatic arthropods, marine isopods contain fungi in their gut to assist in the breakdown of plant food. Several genera and species of Patagonian intertidal isopods contain trichomycetes of the genus *Palavascia*. Similarly, trichomycetes are found as endosymbionts in black fly larval guts, with significant differences in the abundance of different fungal species among seasons, within sites, and among sites. New recruits to the black fly larval population rapidly become colonized by *Harpella melusinae*, suggesting a strong dependence on this fungus as a gut symbiont.

## Fungi as Food Resources

### Vertebrate Mycophagy

Fungi serve as important food resources for numerous vertebrate and invertebrate animals. For vertebrates, fungal consumption is often seasonal — particularly important when other foods are scarce. In Arctic regions, when the ground is snow-covered and vascular plant food is limited, reindeer forage for lichens as their main food source. Small mammals worldwide make use of fungi at times when other food sources are depleted or when fungi are particularly abundant. Many small mammals also forage below ground for hypogeous fungi, whose [[spore-dispersal]] is entirely dependent on these creatures. The [[mushroom-cultivation]] industry for human consumption is a multimillion dollar enterprise in the United States alone, testament to the food, medicinal, and cultural value of fungi.

### Invertebrate Mycophagy

For invertebrates, both the fruiting body and the mycelium form a food base. The nutritional value of fungal tissue is high, especially for certain groups such as nematodes. Selective feeding on specific fungal species can influence the proportion of females in nematode populations, their fecundity, and hence the size of subsequent populations. Soil arthropods, including collembola and mites, are also somewhat dependent on fungal mycelia for their growth and development.

## Grazing Effects on Fungal Communities and Ecosystem Processes

### Comminution and Inoculum Transport

Animals grazing on fungi can significantly affect the rate of fungal-mediated ecosystem processes. The rate of decomposition of leaf litter depends on the rate of colonization by fungi, which can be facilitated by animals through the physical breakdown of leaf litter into smaller pieces (comminution) and the active transport of fungal inocula (spores or hyphal fragments) into leaf litter.

### Compensatory Growth

The actual effect of grazing on fungal mycelia can significantly alter fungal growth patterns. Intense grazing reduces hyphal growth, whereas moderate rates of grazing can stimulate compensatory growth and actually increase overall fungal growth. This compensatory effect can help fungi grow and colonize new resources. Animals are selective in their feeding — selection of preferred fungal species provides an asymmetric selection pressure on the fungal community. Under intense grazing pressure, the growth of the preferred fungus declines relative to less preferred species.

### Classic Example: Collembola and Forest Floor Fungi

A classic example involves the preferred grazing of *Marasmius androcaceus* over *Mycena galopus* by the collembolan *Onychiurus armatus*, which resulted in both fungal species being displaced from their optimal habitat in the forest floor of a Sitka spruce stand. Changes in the distribution of decomposer fungi can affect the rate of leaf litter decomposition and nutrient mineralization.

## Spore Dispersal by Animals

Animals carry fungal propagules either on their bodies or in their gut, a function critical for spreading inocula through ecosystems. Some fungal groups have evolved to rely entirely on faunal dispersal of their spores:

- **Hypogeous fungi** (e.g., truffles) rely entirely on small mammals to consume the fruit body and disseminate spores in their feces.
- **Phallales** (Gasteromycetes) produce sticky, malodorous spore masses that attract flies for dispersal.

Fungal spores can survive passage through the guts of earthworms and soil arthropods, thereby being spread to new resources such as organic substrates for saprotrophic fungi or roots for mycorrhizal fungi. Earthworms have potential as delivery systems of pathogenic fungi to sites where other pests reside in soil, aiding in biological control.

## Fungal Cultivation by Insects

### Ant and Termite Fungiculture

Some species of ants and termites rely so heavily on fungi as a food source that they actively cultivate preferred fungal species within their colony nests. Through selective grazing, the most nutritious fungal material is fed to developing young. Fungal colonies are cleaned of contaminating fungi that are either less palatable or that might compete against the preferred species. To decompose high C:N ratio plant material or material containing high levels of lignin, fungal-derived enzymes are essential. Fungi also translocate nutrients (particularly nitrogen) into recalcitrant plant materials.

### Bark Beetle-Mycangium Associations

Bark beetles carry fungi in a specialized structure called a mycangium on their legs. The fungi decompose wood in the phloem of the tree, translocate nitrogen from surrounding N-rich plant material into the wood, and make this resource more palatable and nutritious for developing beetle larvae. Complex multispecies interactions involving bark beetles, phoretic mites, and competing fungi create intricate ecological webs.

## Fungi as Pathogens and Population Regulators

### Entomopathogenic Fungi

Almost all animals are subject to attack by pathogenic fungi. Entomopathogenic fungi that attack insects can be important regulators of populations in natural ecosystems. The effect of fungi follows density-dependent interaction patterns, where the fungal impact on the population is maximal when the animal population is at its peak. When environmental conditions favor fungal growth, these pathogens can cause devastating effects on host animals. Many of these fungi are being investigated as biocontrol agents of crop pests.

### Nematode-Trapping Fungi as Biocontrol

Nematode-trapping fungi can be introduced into pastures through cattle feces to densities that significantly reduce populations of juvenile gut parasitic nematodes residing in soil. This approach significantly reduces the incidence of gut parasitic nematodes in cattle and improves cattle yield and product quality.

### Amphibian and Fish Pathogens

Fungal pathogens of frogs and fish can cause major population declines. Fungal diseases of economically important mollusks, particularly bivalves, have serious economic impacts. The decline in anuran populations due to fungal pathogens is of particular concern for conserving biodiversity in the tropics. Given climate change scenarios producing conditions conducive for fungal growth, fungal diseases of animals may become increasingly important.

## Ecosystem-Level Consequences

The interactions between fungi and fauna have profound ecosystem-level consequences:

1. **Decomposition regulation**: Animal grazing alters fungal community composition, which in turn affects decomposition rates and nutrient mineralization.
2. **Mycorrhizal function**: Selective grazing of mycorrhizal fungi by soil fauna can affect plant growth and fecundity, potentially altering plant community structure.
3. **Succession dynamics**: Animal dispersal of mycorrhizal spores is critical for plant establishment during primary and secondary succession.
4. **Population control**: Fungal pathogens serve as density-dependent regulators of animal populations.
5. **Carbon and nutrient cycling**: The balance between bacterial and fungal-dominated microbial communities, influenced by faunal interactions, affects carbon use efficiency and net ecosystem carbon fixation.

## Research Challenges

A significant challenge in fungal-faunal interaction research is extrapolating from controlled studies to natural ecosystems. Many findings have been obtained from greenhouse or laboratory studies, or from field experiments at unrealistic animal densities. The effects observed in contrived studies may not resemble those actually occurring in nature. The degree of dependence of many animals on fungi as a food source remains incompletely understood, and the question of what would happen to faunal populations and communities if fungi were removed from ecosystems remains largely unanswered.
