## Ectomycorrhizal Biocontrol and Fungal Balance in Primary Production

## Overview

Ectomycorrhizal (EC) fungi provide dual protection mechanisms against root pathogens that surpass those of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizae]]. The fungal sheath (mantle) that envelops colonized short roots creates a physical barrier, while chemical interference through antibiotic production directly inhibits pathogen growth. Combined with the complex role fungi play in ecosystem primary production, these interactions represent a critical but incompletely understood component of terrestrial ecosystem function.

## Ectomycorrhizal Biocontrol Mechanisms

### Physical Defense: The Fungal Sheath
Unlike arbuscular mycorrhizae, [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] create a substantial fungal mantle that physically surrounds short roots. This sheath acts as a mechanical barrier that impedes direct access of pathogenic fungi to the root surface (Marx, 1973). The [[hartig-net]], which penetrates between root cortical cells, further modifies the root's defensive physiology.

### Chemical Defense: Antibiotic Production
Ectomycorrhizal fungi produce antimicrobial compounds that directly inhibit pathogen growth. Key evidence includes:

- **Fravel (1988)** documented the role of antibiotics in the biocontrol of plant diseases by [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]
- **Chakravarty and Hwang (1991)** demonstrated chemical interference of pathogen growth by ectomycorrhizal fungi
- **Duchesne (1994)** provided evidence for the role of ectomycorrhizal fungi in biocontrol through antibiotic mechanisms

### Combined Defense Effects
The simultaneous presence of both physical and chemical defense mechanisms makes ectomycorrhizal protection more robust than that provided by arbuscular mycorrhizae, which rely primarily on improved host plant nutrition.

## Evidence from Nursery Studies

### Pine Tree Protection
The protective effect of EC fungi against pine root pathogens has been demonstrated primarily in nursery conditions:

- **Marx (1969, 1980)** showed that ectomycorrhizal fungi protect pine roots from pathogenic infections through both antagonism to pathogenic fungi and soil bacteria
- **Marx and Davey (1969a,b)** demonstrated resistance of both aseptically formed and naturally occurring mycorrhizae to infection by *Phytophthora cinnamomi*

### Douglas Fir Protection
- **Sylvia and Sinclair (1983)** showed that *Laccaria laccata* suppressed *Fusarium oxysporum* on [[douglas-fir]] seedlings

### Jack Pine Protection
- **Chakravarty and Hwang (1991)** found that *Laccaria laccata* also protected *Pinus banksiana* seedlings from Fusarium damping-off

### Chestnut Seedling Protection
Branzanti et al. (1999) demonstrated the protective effect of four ectomycorrhizal fungi on chestnut seedlings:

| Mycorrhizal Fungus | Pathogen Challenge | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| *Laccaria laccata* | *Phytophthora cambivora* | No infection signs |
| *Hebeloma crustuliniforme* | *P. cinnamomi* | No infection signs |
| *H. sinapizans* | *P. cambivora* | No infection signs |
| *Paxillus involutus* | *P. cinnamomi* | No infection signs |

Five months after challenging half the seedlings with *Phytophthora* spores, mycorrhizal plants showed no sign of infection, and EC fungi increased seedling growth and biomass in the presence of the pathogen.

## Knowledge Gaps in Natural Ecosystems

Despite strong evidence from nursery conditions, several critical questions remain unanswered:

- The role of EC fungi as biocontrol agents has only been observed on seedling trees in the artificial confines of nurseries
- The potential role of these interactions in maintaining plant communities in natural ecosystems has only been speculated (Rayner, 1993)
- Most studies involve monospecific crops, making extrapolation to diverse natural communities uncertain

## Synopsis: Fungi and Primary Production

Fungi are important in multiple ways in moderating the rate of primary production by plants:

### Lichen Symbiosis
As an essential partner in the lichen symbiosis, fungi regulate water and nutrient supply for photosynthesis. Lichens are sensitive to land use change and pollution, making them important bioindicators. Key unknowns include:
- How much does total net primary production drop if the lichen community is reduced?
- What are the consequences of lichen removal on organisms higher up the food web?

### Mycorrhizal Diversity and Function
Not all mycorrhizal types function identically:
- Different species within the same functional group vary in their ability to access inorganic and organic nutrient sources
- Their responses to environmental stimuli differ, leading to dominance shifts depending on environmental conditions
- Some tree species change their dominant mycorrhizal associates from arbuscular to ectomycorrhizae as they age
- Some ericaceous plants can support ericoid, ecto-, and possibly arbuscular mycorrhizae simultaneously (Dighton and Coleman, 1992; Smith et al., 1995)

### Interplant Resource Sharing
The existence of linkages among plants by mycorrhizal fungal hyphae has been conclusively demonstrated (Simard et al., 1997a,b). This resource sharing:
- Occurs among plants of the same species and among plants of different species
- Has altered concepts of how plant species interact in communities
- Raises questions about ecosystem stability and resilience
- Raises questions about functional redundancy when linkage occurrence is high

### Pathogens in Natural Ecosystems
Much evidence of negative effects of fungal pathogens comes from agricultural literature or studies of exotic interactions. Critical questions include:
- How important are fungal pathogens in natural ecosystems?
- Have current ecosystems evolved with a balance of resistance so that only damaged or weak individuals are significantly impacted?
- Has coevolution produced a fine balance among ecosystem components that could be damaged if the fungal community alone is significantly altered?

## Emerging Research Directions

The chapter identifies several priority areas for future research:
- Understanding relative importance of lichen primary production across ecosystem types
- Determining how different mycorrhizal types respond to climate change and pollutants
- Quantifying actual resource sharing among plant species in natural ecosystems
- Understanding functional significance of mycorrhizal type transitions during plant development
- Evaluating long-term effects of manipulating mycorrhizae on natural ecosystem functioning

The central thesis is that fungi play a profound but subtle role in plant production, and the global consequences of plant–fungus interactions are still incompletely understood. The long-term effects of manipulating fungal communities—whether to control pests or enhance plant growth—must be carefully considered against the backdrop of coevolved ecosystem balance.
