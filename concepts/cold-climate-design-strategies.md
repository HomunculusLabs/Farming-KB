## Cold Climate Design Strategies

[[avalanche-phenomenon]] present unique design opportunities as well as challenges. Many cold
phenomena — snow, ice, frost — can be harnessed as productive resources rather than
merely endured as hazards. [[permaculture-design]] for cold regions takes advantage of
insulation, albedo, freeze concentration, and the predictable physics of water in its
frozen states.

## Phenomena of Cold Climates

Considerable energy is needed to create snow, hail, and ice, and a special set of
techniques exploits the characteristics of cold phenomena:

- **Albedo (reflection) of snow** — used as a heating device, bouncing low-angle
  winter sun onto buildings and growing areas
- **Insulation values of snow** — snow deeper than 15 cm insulates soils, buildings,
  and [[permafrost]] effectively
- **Preservation of ice** — for [[root-cellars-and-cold-storage]] and summer cooling of food
- **Ice cover on ponds** — provides winter access to deep water areas for fishing
- **Ice effect in trees** — debris trapping along meltwater rivers
- **Stratification of seed** — cold-area crop seeds stratified by refrigeration

## Design Precautions for Cold

Essential [[mycoremediation-bioreactor-design-considerations]] for cold climates include:
- **Pipe insulation** — bury pipes below frozen soil levels (minimum 1 metre depth)
- **Well placement** — within insulated earth, preferably within building fabric
- **Roof construction** — steep roof slope in heavy snowload areas
- **House design** — optimized for heat storage and insulation in extreme cold
- **Frost heave management** — bare soil experiences frost heave; garden techniques
  and attached [[glasshouse-growing-cool-temperate-climates]] must account for this
- **Water storage** — earth dams for summer gardens
- **[[avalanche-phenomenon]] site avoidance** — prevent settlement in high-risk zones

## Freezing as a Concentration Method

Freezing concentrates sugars, alcohol, and salt solutions as efficiently as heating
distils water or alcohol. Open pans of maple sugar solution can have surface ice
removed regularly until a sugar concentrate remains. Salts in water and alcohol in
ferment liquors are concentrated in the same way. This principle — analogous to
freeze distillation for applejack — works because ice crystals exclude dissolved
solids as they form.

## Frost Heave as Natural Seedbed Preparation

Frost heave on bare, part-eroded, or exposed soils creates ideal seedbed conditions
for clover and other cover seed during the thaw period. Only bare soils show this
phenomenon, and these are precisely the soils most in need of stabilisation. Frosted
and late-snow areas present an ideal surface to spread manures in late winter, so that
meltdown deposits seed and manure on the ground surface for spring growth. Sawdust
mixed in gives a clear visual picture of seed or manure spread on snow.

## Wildlife Cooperation for Food Gathering

In autumn, acorns, filberts, and hickory nuts are gathered by wildlife as winter
stores. Field and pack rats bring in smaller seed such as wild rice from marshes. If
artificial storages are provided — hollow pipes, logs, engine manifolds, nest boxes,
or wall cavities — foragers will fill them. Seed so-collected is sound, clean, and
neatly stored. Providing **15% is left** for the workers' winter food, **85% can be
collected for human use**.

As snow melts from the forest floor, winter acorns begin to sprout. At this time,
pigs and poultry released from winter quarters can forage sprouted acorns, as can
sheep and cattle. The food value of sprouted seeds is higher than unsprouted seeds by
factors of **10 to 100 times** (for vitamin and sugar content).

## Snow Melt Patterns and Early Planting

The pattern of snow melt indicates insulated, absorbent, reflective, and
heat-generating environments. Black objects, soot, and leaves sink into snow. Snow
melts rapidly in front of bare-leaf, white-stemmed birch stands for about **4 metres**
from their stems. Rock walls backed by birch give early warm sites for planting
vegetables.

The Chinese use slanted **bamboo and straw lean-tos** to achieve early vegetable
growth and [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] season. The shade side of such shelters accumulates snow
for insulation. Stone walls, when embayed, form very warm early sites, as do
semicircles of tyres facing into the low sun. These can be plastic or glass-covered
to assist heat retention. Piles of tyres topped with glass create miniature
grow-holes, especially effective when earth-filled or half-filled with water to
retain daytime heat.

## Ice Storage and Practical Applications

Ice frozen inside insulated boxes in winter, and stored insulated from the ground,
will last through a summer in very shaded places. Thick straw or sawdust lids
preserve underground ice for summer use. Before 1830 and the advent of mechanical
refrigeration, uninsulated ice pits — into which winter snow was packed and then
earthed over — were the basis of chilled drinks.

### Pond Ice Considerations
Depth is the critical factor in cold-water ponds. If ponds freeze to 15–90 cm
(6–36 inches), that depth of water is unavailable in winter. Storages need to be
left unfilled, slope-sided, or open-topped to cope with expanding ice. Pipes must
be buried to 1 metre or allowed to trickle continuously at 4°C/day.

The most effective approach is placing water storage within the house cellar
structure as a well or cistern below floor level. This water doubles as a thermal
mass heat store for both glasshouses and houses.

### Ice Lenses for Solar Concentration
Ice, like glass and water, is a transparent refracting medium suitable for lens
construction. Very cold areas can construct cheap ice lenses for solar energy
concentrators. Poured in moulds, these lenses can be focused to direct heat to
storages or machines during daylight hours. Complex lens components can be glued
together with water. Ice can also be moulded around axles and towed to summer
storage locations as an ice buggy.

## Snow as Reflector and Insulator

Snow insulates and reflects simultaneously. Earth sculpturing around dwellings or
drifts can produce reflected heat of great intensity. Houses built in earth-formed
reflectors can use snow for heat and insulation — **60–70% more heat** reaches walls
through snow reflection at low sun angles. Reflected and concentrated light gives
intense heat storable as hot water, molten metal, steam-heated earth, or heated salt
solutions.

Snow is nearly a **black body** for radiation, with values from **0.986** (clean) to
**0.962** (dirty). Thin snow cover chills soil to **35°C below air temperature**, but
snow thicker than 15 cm insulates effectively. Albedo ranges from **20%** for muddy
slush to near **100%** for fresh dry snow, most effective at the low sun angles of
dawn, evening, and winter.

Light penetrates snow 10–15 cm (most absorbed by water in the snow mass), but in
hoarfrost and dry snow, light reaches 30–60 cm. About 30% of outgoing radiation is
as heat (long wavelength). Snow is completely non-transparent to incoming long-wave
radiation, so melting more commonly occurs from below due to earth heat. This basal
melting causes poor snow-mass cohesion and may trigger snow creep or avalanche.
