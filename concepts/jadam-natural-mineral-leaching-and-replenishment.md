# JADAM Natural Mineral Leaching and Replenishment

JADAM identifies two critical pathways through which soil loses its mineral
content: "natural leaching" and "forced leaching." Understanding both
mechanisms is essential for maintaining long-term soil fertility and achieving
high-quality, mineral-rich produce.

## Natural Leaching of Minerals

Natural leaching refers to the inevitable loss of inorganic nutrients from
soil that occurs simply through the act of harvesting crops. When fruits and
vegetables are removed from the field, the minerals they contain are
permanently taken from the soil. Unlike organic nutrients (carbon, hydrogen,
oxygen), which can be replenished through photosynthesis using sunlight, air,
and water, inorganic minerals are finite resources. What is taken out is taken
out permanently; minerals are not reproduced endlessly through the operation
of sun, earth, and water.

JADAM coined the term "natural leaching of minerals" to describe this
process. The surface-layer soil where most crop roots reside is called the
"plow layer," typically about one meter (3.3 ft) deep. About 96% of a
plant's dried mass comes from organic nutrients (C, H, O), while only 4%
comes from inorganic nutrients. However, that 4% is critically important for
crop health and produce quality.

The consequences of mineral depletion are measurable. In the 1940s in the
US, the saying was that "an apple a day keeps the doctor away" because a
single apple contained all the minerals a person needed in a day. Today, a
person would need to eat thirty-two apples to obtain the same mineral
content. All vegetables and fruits are now significantly deficient in
minerals compared to the past.

## Forced Leaching of Minerals

Forced leaching is a more damaging process caused by human agricultural
practices. It occurs when farmers remove and destroy crop residues (stems,
leaves, branches, roots) after harvest. Crop roots select and absorb specific
nutrients from the soil pool, and these nutrients are reflected in the
composition of stems and leaves. When residues are removed rather than
returned to soil, those carefully selected nutrients are permanently lost.

Many agricultural experts advise farmers to completely eliminate crop
residues to reduce disease and pest pressure the following season, a
practice called "field sanitation." However, JADAM argues this is misguided.
If removing crop residues truly reduced pest problems, pesticide use should
have decreased over the years. Instead, each year farmers become more deeply
entangled in the trap of pesticides. Promoting crop residue removal
effectively encourages farmers to remove nutrients from soil, creating a
vicious cycle that benefits fertilizer and pesticide companies.

JADAM addresses mineral depletion through several complementary approaches.

contributing to their fertility.

Phyllite solution is the second major mineral replenishment tool. JADAM
encourages the use of phyllites, a type of rock, to replenish minerals in
depleted soil. Fine-powdered phyllite rock is immersed in water to create
"phyllite solution." This is especially important for soil with serious
mineral deficiency. The phyllite solution can be added directly to JMS
culturing containers by placing rock powder in a mesh bag.
