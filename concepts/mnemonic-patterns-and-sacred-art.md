# Mnemonic Patterns and Sacred Art

## Overview

Tribal art in its myriad forms — song, dance, sculptural or painted objects, designs — serves primarily as a public and ever-renewed mnemonic, or memory-aid. Apparently simple spiral or linear designs can combine thousands of bits of information in a single, deceptively simple pattern. The decorative function is incidental to the educational and therefore sacred information function. "Decoration" is the trivial aspect of such art.

## The Buddhist Mantra: Om Mani Padme Hum

One of the most elegant examples of mnemonic pattern is the Buddhist chant **"Om mani padme hum"** (pronounced "Aum ma-ni pay-may hung" by Tibetans and Nepalese), meaning "the jewel in the lotus: hum." Each syllable encodes a layer of meaning about the nature of reality:

### Om (Aum) — Awakening
The beginning harmonic, the sound of all stillness and the sounds of all time. It is the fundamental harmonic that recalls the universe itself — a signing on to the totality of existence.

### Ma-ni — The Unchanging Essence
The diamantine core of all phenomena; the truth, represented as a diamond, jewel, or thunderbolt. Sometimes represented in paintings as a blue orb or a radiant jewel, and sometimes as a source of lightning or fire. It is the unchanging reality beneath all change.

### Pay-may — The Unfolding Process
"Enfolded in the heart of the lotus" (mani enfolded). The visible and everyday unfolding of events, petals, or patterns, thus revealing the essential unchanged core (mani) to our understanding. The lotus represents the **implicate order** of tessellated and annidated events, and the process of unfolding the passage of time to successive revelations. At the core is unchanging understanding — nirvana, the ideal state of Buddhism.

### Hung — The Present Declaration
"It is here, now." A declamation of belief by the chanter in the words. It also prefaces the "Om" or beginning of the new chant cycle. In a long sequence of such short chants, all words follow their predecessors. This is the reminder mnemonic of implicate time: all events are present now, and forever repeated in their form.

## Dorje: The Primordial Thunderbolt

**Dorje**, or Dorje-chang, is the Tibetan Buddha-figure who holds the dorje (thunderbolt), represented as a radiant stone symbolising cosmic energy. Dorje is "the primordial Buddha of Tibet" who began the great succession of current and past reincarnations. His colour is blue, for eternity, and he may carry a bell to signify the voiceless wisdom of the inanimate, or the sound of the void.

This figure connects across cultures:
- **Thor** of the Norsemen — Mjollnir (his hammer), from which derives Mjollnirstaun and eventually Mollison (by way of invasions into Scotland and migrations)
- **Durga** of the Hindu tradition
- Thunderbolts and "thunderers" of other tribal peoples worldwide

Even personal names may thus remind us of the essential oneness of events and beliefs around us.

## Universal World Concepts in Tribal Art

We can choose from tribal chants, arts, and folk decoration many such mnemonic patterns, which in their evolution over the ages express very much the same world concept as modern physics and biology. Such thoughtful and vivid beliefs come close to realising the actual nature of observed events, indicating a way of life and a philosophy rather than a dogma or set of measures.

Beliefs so evolved:
- **Precede** the emphasis on the individual
- **Transcend** the division of life into disciplines and categories
- Are derived from contemplation of natural events

When searching for the roots of belief — or more specifically, meaning — we come again and again to the one-ness underlying science, word, song, art, and pattern: **"The jewel in the heart of the lotus."**

## The Drift from Nature-Based Systems

Many world beliefs share an essential core, but there is also a discernible drift from nature-based, essentially universal systems toward:

- Personalised or humanoid gods
- Dogma and fanaticism
- Symbols without meaning or practical use in our lives
- Replacement of public information by exotic, secretive, irrelevant, and basically uninformed centralised belief systems

Many other world-concepts based on analogies of rainbows, serpents, and song cycles relate to aspects of the integrated world view found in Amerindian and Australian tribal cultures.

## Modern Art vs. Sacred Art

### The Degradation of Meaningful Art

Much of modern art is individualistic and decorative; some "motif" art is plagiarised from ancient origins but no longer serves an educational or sacred function. While entertainment and decoration are valid, they represent a minor or incidental function compared to the original purpose of tribal art.

Social comment is a common art form in theatre and song, and spirited dances and songs are cheering or uplifting. But in modern monoculture, meaningful songs and patterns are largely absent — replaced by the jingles of advertisements and purely decorative, trivial patterns, alongside education divorced from relevant long-term observations of the natural world.

### Cultural Genocide Through Artifact Removal

Sacred calendars melted down to bullion or objets d'art represent a degradation of generations of human effort and knowledge. The sacred art of tribal peoples hidden in museum storerooms constitutes a form of **cultural genocide** — removing knowledge from its context and trivialising objects to decorations or loot.

Human information, as a tribal art form, is most frequently debased and destroyed not for monetary gain but for the replacement of public information by centralised belief systems. The fanatic cares not what is destroyed if it empowers the repressive hierarchy then imposed. Most tribal art has been burnt, looted, destroyed, and broken by invading belief systems — by those seeking secret power rather than open knowledge, or by those who are merely destructive.

Book-burning and image-breaking is the reaction of the alienated or intellectually-deprived to the accumulated wisdom of their revelationary ancestors. We most damage ourselves when we destroy information and aids to understanding.

## The Challenge for Artists and Educators

It is a challenge to artists to:
- Study and portray knowledge in a compact, memorable, and transmissible form
- Research and recreate for common use surviving art forms that still retain their meaning
- Re-integrate art with science and with society's functions and needs

It is a challenge to educators to revive the meaningful geometries, songs, and dances that gave us — and our work — meaning.

## Applied Art: The Flowform Example

Some modern sculptural forms serve both aesthetic and functional purposes. The **[[flowform]]** systems of the Virbella Institute, Emerson College, Kent, UK, are modelled on older Roman water cascades and serve both an aesthetic and a water-oxygenation function, assisting water purification. This represents a small step toward applied art as patterning in everyday use.
