# Mushroom Lighting and Environmental Requirements

Successful mushroom fruiting depends on precise environmental control. Unlike plants, mushrooms do not photosynthesize, but they respond to light, temperature, humidity, CO2, and air movement as triggers for development. This page synthesizes the research and practical knowledge on environmental parameters.

## Light

### Role of Light in Fruiting

Light is required for basidiocarp (mushroom) initiation in Psilocybe cubensis. The research by Badham (1980) established the definitive action spectrum:

**Key findings**:
- Basidiocarp initials form ONLY when cultures are illuminated
- Extremely short light exposures are sufficient: as little as 0.0025 seconds of xenon-arc flash induces initiation
- Light-induced initiation is saturated at a dose of 0.345 x 10^4 ergs/cm2 at 460nm
- **Most effective wavelengths**: UV and blue (370nm, 440nm, 460nm)
- **Ineffective wavelengths**: Green and red above 510nm

This is consistent with research on other mushroom species:
- Coprinus domesticus: Blue light above 1.5 x 10^4 ergs/cm2/sec induces mature basidiocarp formation; green, red, and far-red fail
- Schizophyllum commune: Spectral sensitivity peaks in blue and UV; no wavelengths above 525nm are photoinductive
- Favolus arcularis: Photoinduction observed between 350-560nm with six peaks

### Practical Lighting Recommendations
- **Type**: "Cool white" fluorescent lighting (used in research at 2685 lux, 12 hours/day)
- **Intensity**: Low-intensity is sufficient; ambient room light or indirect sunlight works
- **Duration**: 12 hours on / 12 hours off cycle is standard; continuous light is acceptable
- **Phototropism**: Mushrooms grow toward light. A small fluorescent plant light encourages upright growth.
- **Color**: Blue spectrum (450-470nm) is most effective for pinning; standard cool white fluorescents contain adequate blue wavelengths

### Geotropism

Mushrooms exhibit negative geotropism -- the stipe (stem) grows upward against gravity. This response is linked to spore formation: when spores begin to form, a negative geotropic curvature occurs. In sporeless mutants, this curvature does not happen, suggesting that hormones from the lamellae (gills) regulate geotropic response.

## Tropisms (Badham, 1982)

Badham's wind tunnel studies on P. cubensis revealed several tropic responses:

- **Anemotropism (wind response)**: The basidiocarp stipe grows INTO the wind up to the time of spore formation. When rotated with the stipe perpendicular to wind, fruitbodies grow upright.
- **Phototropism**: Stipes grow toward light sources
- **Geotropism**: Stipes grow upward (negative geotropism)
- **Hormonal regulation**: Agar diffusates from the gills can partially replace the influence of the pileus (cap), suggesting mushroom hormones regulate tropic responses

## Temperature

### By Species

| Species | Incubation | Fruiting |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| P. cubensis | 70-80F (21-27C) | 65-85F (18-30C); grows even at 60F |
| Panaeolus cyanescens | ~25C | ~25C or slightly higher |
| P. azurescens | ~20C | 7-10C (45-50F) -- autumn fruiting |
| P. cyanescens | ~20C | Cool autumn temperatures |

### Temperature Effects
- Too hot during incubation: mycelium sweats, produces metabolites, culture sickens
- Too hot during fruiting: spreading mycelium but no pinning
- Too cold: slow growth but generally not harmful
- Temperature fluctuations cause condensation, which is undesirable
- Keep temperature as constant as possible

## Humidity

### Requirements
- Near 100% relative humidity is ideal for fruiting
- The mushroom needs to "bathe in a shroud of floating water molecules"
- **Molecularized water** (evaporated) is what the fungus absorbs, not water droplets
- Direct spraying of fruitbodies can drown delicate mycelial structures

### Humidity Management
- PF dual chambered terrarium achieves near-100% humidity through spray misting
- Perlite humidification provides excellent passive humidity
- Fogging systems for larger setups
- Casing layers provide a humid microclimate at the substrate surface

### Symptoms of Low Humidity
- Fuzzy white mycelium growing on caps (not contamination, but suboptimal)
- Deformed, convoluted, withering mushrooms and primordia
- Abortive primordia that stop developing

## Fresh Air Exchange (FAE) and CO2

### Why FAE Matters
- Mushrooms produce CO2 during metabolism
- Elevated CO2 causes elongated stems, small caps, and poor development
- Panaeolus species are especially CO2-sensitive: insufficient FAE causes tall spindly mushrooms with small caps and many aborts
- Fresh air supplies oxygen and removes CO2

### Air Exchange Rates (Stamets)
- P. cubensis: 2-3 air changes per hour maximum
- Agaricus brunnescens: 4-6 air changes per hour maximum
- Air should be filtered (HEPA) to prevent contamination introduction

### CO2 Levels
- High CO2 during spawn run: beneficial (promotes mycelial growth)
- Reduced CO2 for pinning: essential trigger for fruiting initiation
- Low CO2 during cropping: maintains proper mushroom development

## The Pinning Strategy

Stamets outlines a basic pinning strategy involving:

1. **Full colonization**: Substrate must be completely colonized by mycelium
2. **Casing application** (if applicable): Non-nutritive layer applied to provide humid microclimate
3. **Environmental shift**: Reduce temperature, increase humidity, introduce light and fresh air
4. **Primordia formation**: Small pin-like structures appear within days to weeks
5. **Development**: Primordia develop into mature fruitbodies

The relationship between primordia formation and yield is direct -- more healthy primordia lead to higher total yields.

## Watering

- Substrate should not be allowed to dry out during cropping
- Spray casing layers with a spray bottle to maintain moisture
- Do not directly spray mushrooms
- Siphon pooled water from fruiting chambers
- Outdoor beds need regular watering from September onward (once or twice weekly)

## Environmental Monitoring

Stamets recommends monitoring equipment including:
- Thermometers and thermostats for temperature control
- Humidistats for humidity management
- CO2 meters for air exchange management
- Smoke test for air circulation assessment (smoke should dissipate within 20 seconds on the cropping surface)
