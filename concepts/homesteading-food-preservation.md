# Homesteading Food Preservation

Preserving the harvest extends seasonal abundance into year-round food security. The core methods are drying, fermentation, freezing, canning, and root cellaring — each suited to different foods.

## Root Cellaring

The simplest and most energy-efficient preservation method:
- Temperature: 32-40°F (0-4°C)
- Humidity: 85-95%
- Crops: root vegetables (potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips), cabbages, apples, onions, winter squash
- No electricity required — use [[earth-cellar-construction]] or [[stone-cellar-building]]
- Store crops in layers separated by straw or sawdust
- Check weekly; remove any rotting specimens immediately
- Potatoes and onions must be stored separately (onions release ethylene gas)

## Drying

### Sun Drying
- Best for herbs, fruits (tomatoes, apples, figs, berries), and hot peppers
- Requires 85-100°F with low humidity and good airflow
- 3-5 days depending on thickness
- Protect from insects with netting or screens

### Dehydrator
- More reliable than sun drying; adjustable temperature
- Vegetables: 125-135°F
- Fruits: 135-145°F
- Herbs: 95-100°F (preserve volatile oils)
- Leathery texture = done; crispy = too dry (will crumble)
- Condition dried fruit: store in jars for 1 week, shake daily, check for moisture

### Storage
- Dried foods store 6-12 months at room temperature
- Store in airtight jars, vacuum-sealed bags, or mylar bags with oxygen absorbers
- Keep cool, dark, and dry
- Rehydrate by soaking in water 2-4x the food volume

## Fermentation

The oldest and most nutritious preservation method:
- **Sauerkraut**: Shredded cabbage + 2% salt by weight, pack tightly, ferment 3-6 weeks
- **Kimchi**: Cabbage, radish, chili, garlic, ginger + 3% salt, ferment 1-3 weeks
- **Pickles**: Vegetables in brine (5% salt solution), ferment 1-4 weeks
- **Sourdough**: Flour + water + wild yeast, daily feeding, use for bread
- Fermented foods are alive — store in cool conditions to slow fermentation

## Freezing

- Most vegetables require blanching first (boil 2-3 min, ice bath, drain, dry)
- Blanching deactivates enzymes that cause off-flavors during frozen storage
- Freeze on trays first, then transfer to bags (prevents clumping)
- Vacuum-seal for longest storage (12-18 months)
- Most fruits freeze well without blanching (spread on trays)

## Canning

### Water Bath Canning (high-acid foods only)
- pH below 4.6: pickles, jams, jellies, tomatoes (with lemon juice), fruit
- Boil jars submerged for 10-20 minutes
- Creates a vacuum seal as jars cool

### Pressure Canning (low-acid foods)
- Required for: vegetables, meats, soups, beans
- Temperature must reach 240°F (115°C) to kill Clostridium botulinum spores
- 10-15 PSI depending on altitude
- Botulism risk is real — follow tested recipes exactly
