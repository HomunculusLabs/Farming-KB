# Fukuoka Straw Mulch Philosophy

Updated: 2026-04-19

Masanobu Fukuoka places straw mulch at the foundation of his natural farming system in *The Natural Way of Farming*, calling it the starting point of natural rice farming and "a bold and revolutionary step in rice farming."

## The Foundation of Natural Rice Farming

"Natural rice farming began with straw." Straw mulch serves multiple critical functions simultaneously:

- Promotes seed germination by maintaining soil moisture

- Suppresses winter weeds by blocking light to the soil surface

- Enriches the soil as it decomposes

- Provides habitat and food for soil organisms

- Protects soil from erosion and weather damage

## The Timing Principle

The timing of straw application is critical. Rice straw must be scattered over the field immediately after threshing. If rice straw is scattered uncut, it takes just two or three hours. The simplicity of the operation is essential — if rain falls on a plowed field before seeding, the field turns to mud making direct seeding impossible. Quick, careless scattering is actually better than meticulous placement because the whole system must work within narrow time windows.

No matter how conscientious a farmer is in his work, each operation is part of a carefully ordered system. A sudden change in weather or even a small disruption in the work schedule can upset the timing of an operation enough to lead to a major failure. See [[fukuoka-scattering-straw-technique]].

## Rice vs. Barley Straw

Rice straw is scattered after the rice harvest to protect germinating barley. Barley straw is scattered after the barley harvest to protect germinating rice. Barley straw must be returned to the field as soon as possible after threshing because once dried barley straw is wet by rain, it becomes more than five times as heavy and very difficult to transport. Potassium also leaches out of wet barley straw.

Often, attempting to do a careful job can be self-defeating, for with all the trouble it takes to get out the cutters and other motorized equipment, one is often tempted to just leave the straw lying about.

## The Burning Tragedy

Fukuoka mourns the widespread practice of burning straw: "Straw produced on Japanese rice fields is of great importance as a source of organic fertilizer and for protecting the fields and enriching the soil. Yet today this practice of burning such invaluable material is spreading throughout Japan."

At harvest time in early summer, no one stops to wonder about the smoke hanging over the plain from the burning barley straw in the fields. Fukuoka predicts that the failure to return straw to the land will decide the fate of Japanese national lands.

## Replacing Compost

Straw mulch replaces both compost and plowing. The conventional practice was to apply straw only when fully decomposed as prepared compost, based on the belief that raw straw spreads rice blast disease. Fukuoka demonstrated that fresh straw works better within a no-tillage system.

The agricultural technician has always regarded rice straw as nothing but a source of rice diseases and pests, so the common and accepted practice has been to apply the straw only when fully decomposed as prepared compost. That rice straw must be burned as a primary source of rice blast disease is virtually gospel in some circles, as illustrated by the burning of rice straw on an immense scale in Hokkaido under the urging of plant pathologists.

## The Philosophical Dimension

The straw mulch exemplifies Fukuoka's principle of working with nature rather than against it. In nature, leaves fall and decompose where they land, feeding the soil beneath. Straw mulch mimics this natural pattern. The human error is removing straw from the field, processing it through composting (or worse, burning it), and then attempting to return it in a different form.

By killing the soil with plow and chemical fertilizer, and rotting the roots through prolonged summer flooding, farmers create weak, diseased rice plants that require the nutritive boost of chemical fertilizers and the protection of pesticides. Healthy rice plants have no need for the plow or chemicals. And compost does not have to be prepared if rice straw is applied to the fields half a year before the rice is sown.

## Connection to Soil Self-Improvement

Fukuoka's broader argument is that soil naturally improves itself: "It is in the nature of soil to swell and grow more porous with each passing year." Straw mulch supports this natural process by feeding the soil ecosystem without disruption, unlike plowing which interferes with these processes.

See [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] and [[fukuoka-soil-building-approach]].

## Integration with the Whole System

Straw mulch does not work in isolation. It is part of a complete system that includes clover ground cover, direct seeding in clay pellets, intermittent irrigation, and the return of all organic matter to the field. The system works only when all elements are present together.

The method of rice cultivation is extremely simple, but because it is a highly advanced technique, quite unlike extensive farming, each operation must be performed with great precision. Straw application is the connective tissue that holds the seasonal cycle together.

## The Microbial Ecosystem of Straw

A single piece of rice straw dropped onto the earth undergoes a remarkable succession of organisms. The straw draws flies that lay eggs. Pathogenic fungi spread rapidly but are soon consumed by trichoderma fungi and other beneficial organisms. Nematodes feed on fungi, bacteria on nematodes, predaceous mites on other mites. As fibrin-decomposing fungi exhaust their food, they are supplanted by lipoid- and lignin-decomposing bacteria. Lactic acid bacteria round off the process with lactic acid fermentation, at which point all trace of the straw disappears.

This is just the briefest look at the total decomposition of a single piece of straw over several days. What man knows is only the most minute, infinitesimal part of this natural order.

## From Small Matters, Great Consequences

"It is from just such a small matter that shall emerge the future of Japanese agriculture." Fukuoka elevates the simple act of scattering straw to a matter of civilizational significance. The choice between burning and returning straw represents the choice between destruction and regeneration of the land itself.
