# Ecological Gardening Principles

Three core ecological principles underpin ecological gardening: the
niche, succession, and biodiversity. These concepts, drawn from
observing natural ecosystems, provide the foundation for designing
gardens that work with nature rather than against it.

## The Niche

A niche is the role played by each organism in an ecosystem. Think of
a niche as a profession, and habitat as the workspace for performing
that job. As habitat becomes more varied, more niches appear. Often,
providing habitat triggers a cascade of niches, which is precisely
what ecological gardeners try to do.

The Bullock brothers' farm on Orcas Island illustrates this principle.
By creating fertile habitat, they opened up opportunities for life to
exploit. Cattails colonized a renewed bog, muskrats arrived to eat the
cattails, and otters moved in to prey on the muskrats. Where none of
these three species could survive before, all three now thrive because
the brothers provided habitat and soil nutrients. They supplied the
beginnings, and nature did the rest.

In gardening terms, this means designing landscapes that offer many
different roles for organisms to fill. Each plant, insect, and animal
in the garden should have multiple niches it can occupy, and each
niche should be filled by multiple species for redundancy.

## Succession

When plants first colonize bare earth, a progression begins. Certain
annual grasses, herbs, and flowers arrive first. Called pioneer plants,
they are well adapted to invading naked or disturbed soil. We know most
of them as weeds: crab grass, dandelion, sheep sorrel, pigweed,
chicory, and many more. Their job is to shelter bare soil from rain,
and ferry nutrients from deep in the soil to the surface.

If left alone, short annuals are soon replaced by taller perennials.
In five to fifteen years, the weedy field becomes clothed with
perennial shrubs. With enough rain, in two or more decades the shrubs
give way to a young forest. This progression is called succession.

Conventional gardens mimic [[cover-crops]] to accelerate succession and create mature, low-
maintenance landscapes quickly.

## Biodiversity

Biodiversity is the variety of organisms present, considered from many
levels: cultivar, species, genus, family, and on up to include all
five kingdoms, as well as the diversity of habitats and ecosystems.

Diverse habitat shrinks pest problems. A [[comparison-mandala-garden-design-vs-keyhole-garden-design]]:

- **[[ozier-pdc-composting-and-nutrient-cycles]]**: Open (many imports) vs. closed (recycling)
- **Plant diversity**: Usually low vs. high
- **Food chains**: Short and linear vs. complex and weblike
- **Stability**: Low vs. high resistance to disturbance
- **Decomposers**: Unimportant vs. critically important
- **Microclimates**: Few and harsh vs. many and mild
