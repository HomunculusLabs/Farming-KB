# Soap and Candle Making

From Permaculture: A Designers' Manual by Bill Mollison.

## Overview

Soap and candle making are referenced in the Designers' Manual as essential domestic crafts within self-reliant permaculture systems. Both products can be produced from on-farm materials, reducing dependence on external supplies and ensuring that the chemicals released into wastewater and soil systems are known and manageable.

## Soap Making

### Potash Soaps
The manual references potash soaps as the older, more environmentally benign type of soap. Potash (potassium carbonate) is produced by leaching water through wood ashes and evaporating the resulting lye. When combined with fats or oils, potash produces soft soap or, with further processing, hard soap.

The older potash soaps are noted as preferable to modern detergents in garden and agricultural contexts because they break down more readily in soil systems and do not contain the synthetic additives, phosphates, and borates found in commercial detergents.

### Soap Chemistry and Soil Health
The manual provides detailed guidance on the interaction of soaps and detergents with soils:
- Plain soaps are recommended for crop use where greywater is irrigated to gardens
- Soaps and detergents are themselves polluting if they contain borates, residual biocides, or synthetic additives
- Soap lathers easily in soft (acid) waters
- In hard water, calcium and magnesium ions interfere with soap and release sodium ions, allowing soaps to lather
- Sodium ions from soaps can deflocculate soil clays and damage leaves at concentrations exceeding 1,000 ppm

### Ingredients for Soap Production
The manual references several on-farm sources for soap-making materials:
- Animal fats (tallow from cattle, sheep, pigs) as the primary fat source
- Plant oils from coconuts, olives, and oil palm
- Potash from wood ash leaching
- Sodium compounds from wood ash (soda ash)

### Environmental Considerations for Soap Use
A typical analysis of a powdered detergent or soap could include:
- Sodium or potassium salts or polysulphates
- Silicates, sulphates, or bicarbonates
- Borates (problematic for garden use)
- Residual biocides concentrated in animal fats (DDT, Dieldrin, Hexachlor from dairy cattle)
- Additives such as resins (hardeners), scents, dyes, and brighteners
- Faecal bacteria and viral or worm pathogens from washing

Common soaps are best used where waste water goes to garden beds because they break down more readily than detergents. However, water must be checked for excessive nitrates and fluorine, both common dryland pollutants.

### Boron and Detergent Pollution
Boron, commonly found in detergents, can cause plant poisoning. The manual specifically recommends using soap, especially in drylands, where citrus and grains in particular can be boron-poisoned in dry seasons. Borax is poisonous to seed and insects, and surplus boron can cause anaemia in people. In soils, 0.75 ppm is ideal, but 1.0 ppm can be toxic to plants.

## Candle Making

### Tallow Candles
Tallow (rendered animal fat) is the primary material for candle production in traditional permaculture systems. Candles represent one of the many useful products derived from animal husbandry, alongside meat, leather, bone charcoal, and manures.

### Candlenut (Aleurites moluccana)
The candlenut tree is specifically referenced in the manual as a source of lighting fuel in Hawaii. The candlenut is one of the large nut trees noted for multiple uses including oil production for light and fuel. In traditional Hawaiian systems, the oily kernels of the candlenut were strung on coconut leaf midribs and burned as torches, with each nut providing approximately 15 minutes of light.

### Chinese Tallow Tree
The Chinese tallow tree (Sapium sebiferum) is referenced for its waxy seed coating, which has been used for candle and soap production. This tree represents a dual-purpose crop providing both industrial wax and oil.

### Wax Production
Beeswax from [[permaculture-designers-manual-rabbit-housing]] operations provides a high-quality wax for candles. In integrated permaculture systems, beeswax represents a value-added product from the pollination services that bees provide to crops and orchards.

## Integration with Permaculture Systems

### Kitchen and Domestic Economy
Soap and candle making are part of the broader domestic economy that the manual advocates. In the tropical garden design, the kitchen area is a central hub where food processing, preservation, and domestic crafts take place. The outdoor stove area is positioned near herb gardens and water sources for efficiency.

### Animal Systems Integration
Both soap and candle production integrate with animal management:
- Tallow from cattle, sheep, and pigs provides raw material for both candles and soap
- Bone from slaughtered animals provides charcoal for filtration
- Manure feeds [[permaculture-designers-manual-biogas-digester]] or compost systems
- Hides provide leather for household goods

### Forest Garden Products
Tree crops provide oils and waxes for domestic use:
- Coconut oil for soap and lighting
- Olive oil for soap production
- Oil palm for soap and lamp oil
- Candlenut for lighting fuel
- Beeswax from forest-edge beekeeping

### Waste Stream Management
The manual emphasises knowing the composition of soaps and detergents used in the household, as these chemicals end up in greywater systems that irrigate gardens. Making soap from known ingredients allows the designer to control what enters the soil system. This is particularly important in drylands where evaporation concentrates any chemical pollutants.

## Self-Reliance and Community Production

The manual argues that community self-reliance extends to domestic necessities. The real cause of a lack of basic supplies in any country is not finance, but restrictive practices. Even cement and mortar can be made if needed using kilns fired by wood, as can pottery, bricks, and roof tiles. Similarly, soap and candles can be produced locally from farm and forest products without requiring external inputs or money.

The older potash soaps are highlighted as an appropriate technology because they rely on wood ash (a byproduct of heating and cooking) and animal or plant fats (byproducts of food production), creating useful products from waste streams while avoiding the environmental problems of commercial detergents.
