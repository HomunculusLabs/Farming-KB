## Understanding Coastal Dynamics

Coastal zones are shaped by the interaction of waves, tides, currents, wind,
and geological substrate. These forces create a range of landforms including
barrier islands, estuaries, deltas, cliffs, and beaches. The permaculture
designer must first read the landscape to understand which processes are
constructive (building land) and which are destructive (eroding land).

Sea levels have fluctuated dramatically throughout geological history. During
the last glacial maximum, sea levels were approximately 120 metres below
current levels. The Holocene transgression that followed created the coastal
configurations we recognize today. Understanding that coastlines are
inherently unstable is the first step toward sensible design.

Mollison describes mangroves as a multi-species zonation system. Red mangroves
(Rhizophora) colonize the seaward edge, suspended on stilt roots above the
tidal range. Black mangroves (Avicennia) occupy the mid-intertidal zone with
pneumatophores rising from the mud. White mangroves (Laguncularia) and button
mangroves (Conocarpus) occupy the higher, less frequently flooded positions.

This zonation is not random. Each species has evolved specific tolerances for
salinity, inundation frequency, and substrate type. When designing mangrove
restoration or aquaculture integration, the designer must respect these natural
patterns and plant species in their correct elevational position.

## Salt-Tolerant Species Selection

a broad palette of halophytic and salt-tolerant species. These include:

- **Casuarina** (she-oak): Excellent for windbreaks and sand stabilization.
- **Tamarix** (salt cedar): Extremely salt-tolerant; useful for inland saline
  areas as well.
- **Nypa palm** (Nypa fruticans): A mangrove palm producing sugar-rich sap.
- **Salicornia**: Edible halophyte suitable for salt-marsh rehabilitation.
