## Humus Sources and Mulch Systems in the Tropics

## Overview

Mulch provision is the cornerstone of tropical home gardens, and green mulch combined with tree legumes forms the essential accompaniment of main crops and tree crops. In the humid tropics, where 80–90% of available nutrients are contained in plant and animal biomass rather than in the soil itself, maintaining a continuous cycle of organic matter return is the single most important practice for sustainable food production.

## Sources of Humus for Tropical Soils

### Logs and Branches

Large logs and tree branches serve multiple purposes in tropical systems. The rapid breakdown of wood under the combined influence of rain, heat, termites, and fungi means that whole logs can be levered together or placed inline cross-slope to act as planting sites. This technique is particularly useful on bare clay soils, eroded areas, and isolated atolls using old palm tree trunks.

**Traditional Hawaiian practice:** Logs of the kukui tree (*Aleurites moluccensis*) are placed in the shallow water of taro terraces to rot. As decomposition proceeds, edible fungi appear and are harvested. The remaining log is then crushed and spread in the terrace. Leaves and branches of kukui and other forest trees are gathered for the same purpose.

**Marjorie Spears' method (Queensland, Australia):** Temporary roughwood terraces are built across a deforested slope using rejected logs, creating complex and rich gardens based on this strategy combined with green legume mulch. Logs are available from palms, and fast-growing acacia species can be close-planted specifically for this purpose.

### Detritus from Stands

Several plant species provide silica-rich mulch:
- Palms (fronds and spathes)
- Bamboo thickets (stems and spathes)
- *Casuarina* spp.
- *Acacia* spp.
- Grain and nut husks
- Copra operation residues

Shredded or chopped, this material serves as mulch and releases silica for plant growth. A secondary effect in alkaline island soils is pH reduction — trials on coral islands showed reduction from pH 8.5 to 6.5 using silica-rich mulches.

### Aquatic Weeds

Floating aquatics provide excellent crop mulch:
- **Water-fern** (*Azolla* spp.) — has largely replaced kukui as taro mulch in Hawaii; provides nitrogen through symbiotic *Anabaena* algae
- **Water-lettuce** (*Pistia*) — successfully used in Africa
- **Water hyacinth** (*Eichornia*)
- **Algal mats**
- **Reeds and rushes** from ditches

In the wet-dry tropics, shallow flood-water bunds collect or produce these plants, which can be gathered as rolls of dry material when water dries out in the winter period, providing garden mulch in abundance from temporarily impounded water. *Azolla* and *Anabaena* provide nitrogen fixation, making them doubly valuable.

### Crop Wastes and Manures

- Corn husks, kitchen wastes (including bones)
- Human and animal manures
- Cardboard and newspaper as grass-suppressing weed mulch
- Nut husks for complete weed suppression

In the Seychelles, cinnamon leaves and branches from pollarded stumps are considered excellent mulch for vegetable crops, with the bark serving as a valuable spice.

### Hedgerow and Mulch Plants

Hedgerow species provide near-continuous mulch sources:
- **Windbreak/barrier plants:** Hibiscus, *Casuarina*, banana grass, palms, leguminous trees (*Gliricidia*, *Acacia*, *Prosopis*)
- **Garden barrier species:** Lemongrass (*[[cymbopogon-citratus]]*) and comfrey (*[[symphytum-officinale]]*) — both prevent kikuyu grass intrusion and provide repetitive cutting for mulch

**Soft herbaceous mulch plants:** Nasturtium, comfrey, marigolds, tobacco plants, mature taro tops, and other *Araceae* suppress grass and provide constant "slash" mulch.

**Soft legumes for mulch and nitrogen:** *Sesbania*, vetch, Haifa clover, cowpea, lablab bean, soya bean, *Desmodium*, *Siratro*, and *Centrosema*. These can be slashed or interplanted with grains to provide nitrogenous ground cover. Lablab conveniently dies down just before grains ripen in the winter dry season.

## Application Guidelines

- Apply mulch thickly at 20–25 cm (8–10 inches) deep when first establishing home gardens
- Later mulch can be derived from green herbage, borders, and windbreaks
- Seed-free mulch (second-cut grass) is best for placing around valuable crops
- Seed-head mulch should not be placed in gardens where it might become a nuisance
- All weed and grass species produce weed-free mulch when not in seed
- Raking under bamboo and clump canes, routing leaf mulch through animal bedding, helps with fire control
- Special mulches can adjust pH: pine and legume mulch benefit bromeliads (pH 4–5); buckwheat and nut husks raise soil pH
