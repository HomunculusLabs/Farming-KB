# JADAM Monoculture Critique

## Overview

JADAM challenges one of the most widely accepted principles in agriculture: that repeated monoculture necessarily degrades soil and must be addressed through crop rotation. By observing how nature handles the continuous growth of single species, JADAM argues that the real problem is not monoculture itself but the removal of crop residues and the disruption of mineral cycling.

## The Conventional View

One of the toughest problems farmers face is soil degradation due to repeated monoculture. The conventional explanation holds that when one type of plant is grown on the same land repeatedly, year after year, that plant absorbs from the soil the types of nutrients it needs or prefers. After repeated cultivations, those nutrients are depleted from the soil. Ultimately, monoculture leads to decreased yield and unhealthy crops. The standard solution offered by modern agriculture is to rotate crops.

## JADAM's Challenge

JADAM has a deep-rooted suspicion about this widely-accepted idea. The challenge begins with a simple question posed to nature: "Do you grow plants in rotation?" The answer, JADAM argues, is unequivocal: "No. I do not farm in rotations. I plant the same species in the same place again and again."

There is no concept of rotational growing in the wild. A seed can fall in a place and the same species can grow 10 or 100 or even 1,000 years. Its offspring will cover the area after being born, and then be reborn many times over. Additionally, there is no such problem as repeated monoculture growth disorders. In fact, the soil becomes richer and richer; an untouched land will fill itself with life and establish a beautiful forest.

## The Mineral Cycling Explanation

How does nature achieve successful monoculture while human monoculture fails? The answer lies in mineral recycling:

In nature, leaves, branches, trees, roots, and fruits -- the whole body of the plant -- fall down to be disintegrated by microbes and are returned to the soil. The roots from a tree reach deep into the earth, grabbing and pulling up the minerals needed to build the body and sustain metabolism. When these fall back on the soil, the minerals previously held are released. The wind might blow leaves away, animals might consume fruits, but eventually the lost minerals end up somewhere at the bottom of another tree. They have moved but have not been removed. The deeper the roots reach, the more supply of minerals is brought up into the daylight. That is how the forest becomes greener and healthier over the years.

## The Human Error

What is happening with human monoculture is the exact opposite. After taking away the fruits, humans eliminate all crop residues. Crop residues contain valuable nutrients that should be released back to the soil so plants can grow again. Rotational cultivation is offered as a panacea but completely misses the point.

JADAM does not perceive crop residues (branches, leaves, and any remaining parts of the plant) as a source of disease; it sees them as a source of nutrients. These must be put back into the soil. ## The Biodiversity Argument Against Rotation

Proponents of crop rotation often cite biodiversity as a reason to alternate crops. However, JADAM points out that biodiversity in nature does not come from farmers planting different species in sequence. It arises naturally from the complex web of ecological relationships -- soil organisms, weather patterns, wild plants, insects, and animals. True biodiversity is a property of the ecosystem, not a property of the planting schedule.

In a healthy, living soil, even monoculture can coexist with rich microbial and invertebrate diversity. The problem is not the crop species above ground but the life (or lack thereof) in the soil below. When crop residues are returned and chemical inputs minimized, soil biology supports the same crop year after year.

## Real-World Validation

JADAM has documented numerous case studies on its website where farmers cured "monoculture disorder" by simply returning crop residues to the soil instead of removing them. These cases span multiple crop types including peppers, strawberries, and fruit trees.
