# Permaculture Designers Checklist for Drylands

The drylands checklist from [[permaculture-design]] rests on a sequence of interventions that build resilience over time. Designers must think in decades, establishing conditions for natural succession to accelerate.

- **Commence freshwater infiltration at the top of catchments**, working progressively downstream. Catchment repair is the single most important intervention in drylands — restoring the ability of landscapes to absorb and retain rainfall before it becomes destructive runoff. Water that infiltrates at the highest points has the longest path through the soil profile, maximising groundwater recharge and supporting vegetation at all elevations below.

- **Give upwind areas first attention** for soil pitting and windbreak tree establishment. Wind is a primary agent of degradation in drylands; slowing it at the upwind edge protects everything downwind. Soil pitting creates micro-catchments that trap wind-blown seeds and organic matter, while windbreaks reduce evaporative stress across the entire site.

- **Establish plant nuclei or oases at favoured sites**, then follow with corridor planting between them. Nuclei act as seed sources and microclimate refugia from which broader restoration can spread. These islands of productivity serve as bases for gradual expansion into more challenging terrain.

- **Spread pelleted seed over larger areas** to await favourable seasonal conditions. Pelleted seeds protect germplasm from desiccation and predation until rains trigger germination, allowing passive revegetation at landscape scale without the labour-intensive requirements of direct planting.

These broad strategies are interdependent: windbreaks enable garden establishment, which in turn supports settlement health and reduces pressure on imported food systems. The sequence of implementation matters — infrastructure and shelter must precede intensive food production.

## Garden and Food Supply

Food production in drylands requires careful site selection and intensive local management. Every square metre of productive garden must be optimised for water efficiency and microclimate benefit.

- **Establish shaded and mulched gardens within settlement** boundaries. [[tree-temperature-effects-and-microclimate-modification]] through shade structures and heavy organic mulch dramatically reduces evapotranspiration and extends the productive growing window. Mulch also builds soil organic matter and supports beneficial microbial activity.

- **Establish settlement windbreak and shade trees** as a priority before food gardens. Shelterbelts reduce wind speed, lower temperatures, and protect vulnerable crops from desiccating winds. Shade trees around dwellings also reduce cooling energy requirements.

- **Extend successful species along corridors** of sandy river beds, wadis, water runnels, and foothills where subsurface moisture and favourable soils provide natural pathways for [[drip-irrigation]] systems that deliver water directly to root zones with minimal evaporative loss. Drip irrigation is far more efficient than spray or flood methods in dryland conditions.

- **Broadscale crop needs** demand windbreak protection and water harvesting at a ratio of approximately 20 hectares of runoff catchment to 1 hectare of sown crop. This ratio ensures adequate moisture concentration for reliable crop production.

- **Seek safe dam sites near settlement** in shaded valleys or off streams diverted to drop silt before reaching storage. Dam sites must be carefully assessed for catchment stability and evaporation losses. Shaded valley sites experience significantly lower evaporative losses than exposed locations. Sites should also be evaluated for sediment yield — excessive siltation can halve a dam's effective lifespan within a decade.

- **Store water in sand-filled dams** or gabion-stabilised terraces that reduce evaporation and protect stored water from contamination. Subsurface storage in sand is one of the most effective strategies for minimising evaporative losses in arid climates. Water stored in sand remains cool, clean, and accessible through shallow wells or infiltration galleries.

- **Use water wisely** and route greywater to toilet systems for secondary use, closing the nutrient and water loop within settlements. [[greywater-recycling-drylands]] reduces total freshwater demand by a significant margin.

- **Never use deep bores or pumped well water** beyond the recharge capacity of the aquifer. Over-extraction leads to salinisation and permanent aquifer damage that may take centuries to reverse.

- **Test all bore water rigorously** for salts, radioactives, nitrates, fluorine, and biological contamination before any domestic use. Groundwater quality in drylands is highly variable and often unsuitable without treatment.

- **Wherever water is infiltrated into the ground**, plant trees to maintain transpiration flows and keep salt levels down. Tree roots act as biological pumps that prevent rising water tables and surface salinisation, making them essential companions to any water infiltration strategy.

These [[water-management]] principles form a hierarchy: prioritise surface capture (roof tanks, swales) over subsurface extraction (bores, wells), and always match extraction rates to demonstrated recharge capacity.

## Health

Settlement health in drylands depends on managing environmental hazards and ensuring nutritional adequacy through local production. The arid environment creates specific health risks that must be addressed through design rather than medical intervention.

- **Reduce dust in settlements** via trees and soil pitting to avoid chronic sinus and respiratory problems associated with airborne particulates. Dust is a persistent health hazard in arid environments and contributes to a range of respiratory conditions.

- **Check for water-borne disease** wherever children swim, particularly in standing water bodies that may harbour parasites and pathogens endemic to warm, shallow water.

- **Supply ample vitamins** via home garden vegetables and fruits. Fresh produce is often scarce in remote [[dryland-settlements-and-interceptor-banks]], making the home garden a critical public health intervention.

- **Check plants, soils, and blood levels** for essential minerals, especially zinc and iron, which are commonly deficient in dryland diets and soils. Mineral deficiencies are widespread in populations dependent on dryland agriculture and can have serious developmental consequences for children.

- **Reduce imported carbohydrates** (sugars and starches), relying instead on locally-grown crops that provide more balanced nutrition with lower ecological and financial cost. This shift also reduces dependence on supply chains that may be unreliable in remote dryland regions.

Together, these health guidelines emphasise self-reliance and prevention. A well-designed permaculture settlement addresses health through environmental design, diversified local food production, and regular monitoring of nutritional and environmental indicators.

## Humid Cool to Cold Climates: Overview

The manual transitions in Section 12 to cool humid climates where precipitation exceeds evaporation and winter frosts are common. These climates span from Mediterranean areas poleward to boreal forests (Taiga) that rim the tundra. They are predominantly winter-wet climates experiencing fogs and frosts.

Contemporary broadscale crop agriculture was developed in these [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] but has been inappropriately exported to tropics and deserts where neither soil, water, nor financial resources can support it. The original pre-agricultural landscape was a mosaic of mixed forests, extensive hedgerows, small fields, permanent meadows, and relatively small vegetable plots — a diverse and stable productive system that supported human communities for millennia.

Trade pressures from subsidised agriculture destroyed this stable land use pattern, forcing large-scale grain crop and feed-lot systems on farmers. Fields were coalesced, hedgerows destroyed, and few significant forests remain within farmed areas. Acid rain from heavy industrial activity has damaged foothill forests and thin soils, while ozone and soil losses have created what amounts to the "desertification" of areas like southern England, where wind effects on exposed broadscale grain fields are pronounced.

The 40-year thrust for maximum production succeeded in creating surpluses in almost every farm product at enormous cost to the public and the land. The central problem for mesothermal climates is not subsistence (as in deserts and tropics) but rather how to transition to a sustainable future without huge public subsidy of modern agriculture.

Since approximately 1970, a widespread home garden movement has emerged, with increasing development of urban farms under community control. Home garden production in dollar terms now equals or exceeds farm production, and gardening continues to grow in popularity. A pronounced consumer swing toward lighter diets — less fats, starches, and sugars, with greater consumption of naturally produced vegetables, fruits, lean meats, fish, and poultry — aligns well with permaculture principles.

The societies occupying these climates have achieved zero or negative population growth, lessening pressure on land while farmland has been forced into uneconomic overproduction. Near cities, 60–80% of farm income may be derived from social facilities rather than farm product, suggesting that farms and forests are increasingly valued as recreational and ecological reserves. This demographic stability, combined with growing consumer awareness of food quality and environmental costs, creates favourable conditions for the transition to permaculture-based land management.

## Key References

The drylands and cool climate sections draw on several foundational works in land restoration and dryland management:

- **Bradshaw & Chadwick**, *The Restoration of Land* (University of California Press, 1980) — comprehensive treatment of ecological restoration principles and techniques.
- **Cribb**, *Wild Food in Australia* (Collins, Sydney, 1974) — a basic reference on Aboriginal food plants and their ecological context.
- **Corbett**, *A Better Place to Live* (Rodale Publications, 1981) — practical guidance on sustainable settlement design.
- **Hall et al.**, *The Use of Trees and Shrubs in the Dry Country of Australia* (Dept. of National Development, [[mollison-designers-conifer-forestry-and-timber]] Bureau, Canberra, 1972) — the standard reference on Australian dryland tree species and their applications.
- **Harrington**, *Western Edible Wild Plants* (University of New Mexico Press, 1972) — covering edible plant species of the American Southwest.
- **Hagedorn et al.**, *Dune Stabilization: A Survey of Literature* (Germany Agency for Technical Cooperation, 1977) — an excellent summary of dune formation and stabilisation techniques.
- **Newbigin**, *Plant and Animal Geography* (Methuen & Co., London, 1972) — biogeographic context for understanding species distribution in arid and temperate zones.
- **Sharma**, *Ecodevelopment in Arid and Semi-Arid Areas of Asia* (Second World Congress on Land Policy, 1983) — addressing the Thar Desert and strategies for food, fodder, fibre, and shelter production.
- **Southwest Bulletin** (April 1980) — containing valuable material on desert plants and agriculture by Gary Nabhan and others.
- **Weber**, *Reforestation in Arid Lands* (VITA Publications, 1977) — practical guidance on tree establishment under arid conditions.

These references collectively span several continents and ecological zones, reflecting the universal applicability of the [[permaculture-design-principles]] outlined in the checklist.
