# Wicking Bed Construction

[[query-how-do-i-build-a-wicking-bed-for-my-garden]] is a self-watering garden bed that uses capillary action to draw water upward from a built-in reservoir below the soil. Plants access water from below, reducing surface evaporation and dramatically cutting water use. Wicking beds can reduce water consumption by 50-80% compared to conventional garden beds while providing more consistent soil moisture.

## How Wicking Beds Work

The wicking bed concept mimics a natural water table:

1. **Reservoir zone:** A waterproof container holds a volume of water in the bottom of the bed
2. **Wicking medium:** A layer of coarse material (gravel, scoria, sand) sits in and above the reservoir, wicking water upward through capillary action
3. **Soil zone:** A layer of growing soil sits above the wicking medium. Water continues to wick up into the root zone by capillary action through the soil particles
4. **Plants:** Roots grow down into the moist soil zone, accessing water as needed

The key insight is that water moves upward through soil by capillary action (the same force that lets a paper towel absorb water). The finer the soil particles, the higher water can wick — but the slower the movement. A well-designed wicking bed balances wicking height with adequate drainage.

## Components

### Container

The outer container must be waterproof to hold the reservoir:

- **Raised timber beds lined with pond liner:** Most common DIY approach. Build a box from treated or naturally rot-resistant timber (cedar, redwood, black locust), then line with EPDM pond liner (45 mil minimum).
- **IBC totes (intermediate bulk containers):** Cut the top off a food-grade IBC tote. The plastic is already waterproof. Very cost-effective.
- **Old bathtubs, stock tanks, or troughs:** Pre-made waterproof containers. Ensure they're clean and free of chemicals.
- **Concrete blocks or masonry:** Build a masonry box and seal with waterproofing compound or liner.
- **Half wine barrels:** Good for small wicking beds (1-2 plants)

### Reservoir

The reservoir holds water in the bottom of the bed:

- **Volume:** Typically 8-12 inches deep, occupying the bottom third of the bed
- **Fill pipe:** A vertical PVC pipe (3-4 inch diameter) that extends from the reservoir to above the soil surface. Use this to fill the reservoir and monitor water level.
- **Overflow outlet:** A critical safety feature. A hole drilled near the top of the reservoir (just below the soil layer) with a pipe leading outside the bed. If the reservoir is overfilled or heavy rain occurs, excess water drains out rather than flooding the soil zone.

### Wicking Medium

The layer that bridges the reservoir and soil:

- **Scoria (volcanic rock):** Lightweight, excellent wicking properties, widely available. Best all-around choice.
- **Coarse river sand:** Good wicking, heavy, inexpensive. Must be coarse enough to allow water flow.
- **Gravel:** Works but has less wicking ability than scoria or sand. Use smaller gravel (3/4 inch or less).
- **Wood chips or mulch:** Can work but decompose over time, reducing wicking capacity. Not recommended as primary wicking medium.
- **Geotextile fabric:** Place a layer between the wicking medium and the soil to prevent soil particles from washing down into the reservoir.

### Soil Layer

The growing medium sits on top of the wicking medium:

- **Depth:** Minimum 8-10 inches above the wicking medium. 12 inches is ideal for most vegetables.
- **Soil mix:** Use a well-draining, nutrient-rich mix. A blend of garden soil, compost, and aeration material (perlite, pumice, coarse sand) works well.
- **Do not use pure potting mix:** It's too light and wicks poorly. Include some mineral soil for better capillary action.
- **Do not use garden soil straight:** It can become compacted and inhibit wicking. Mix with compost and aeration material.

## Sizing

### Bed Dimensions

- **Width:** 3-4 feet maximum. Any wider and it becomes difficult to reach the center from the sides.
- **Length:** As long as you want. 4-12 feet is common.
- **Total depth:** 20-24 inches (8-12 inch reservoir + 10-12 inch soil)
- **For IBC totes:** Approximately 40x48 inches, 30 inches deep. Cut to 20-24 inches for easier access.

### Reservoir Capacity

- A 4x8 foot bed with a 10-inch reservoir holds approximately 200 liters (53 gallons)
- A full reservoir can last 1-3 weeks depending on weather, plant size, and bed configuration
- Fill frequency: Check weekly during peak summer; every 2-3 weeks in spring/fall

## Construction Steps (Timber Box with Liner)

### 1. Build the Box

- Cut timber to size: two long sides, two short ends
- Assemble with exterior-grade screws or galvanized nails
- Add vertical cleats at the corners for extra strength
- Level the site thoroughly — the bed must be level for even water distribution
- Place the box on a level surface (pavers, compacted gravel, or level ground)

### 2. Install the Liner

- Line the entire interior of the box with EPDM pond liner (45 mil)
- Fold corners neatly; overlap by at least 6 inches at seams
- Staple or tack the liner to the top edge of the box
- Check for holes or tears — patch with pond liner patch kit if needed

### 3. Install Overflow

- Drill a hole through the side of the box and liner at the top of the reservoir zone (10-12 inches from the bottom)
- Insert a PVC pipe fitting (1 inch) and seal with silicone on both sides
- Attach a short length of PVC leading outside the bed

### 4. Install Fill Pipe

- Place a 3-4 inch PVC pipe vertically in one corner of the bed
- The bottom of the pipe should sit at the bottom of the reservoir
- The top extends 2-3 inches above the soil surface
- Drill several holes in the bottom portion of the pipe to allow water to flow out into the reservoir
- Cap the top to prevent debris and mosquitoes

### 5. Add Wicking Medium

- Fill the reservoir zone (bottom 8-12 inches) with scoria, coarse sand, or gravel
- Level the surface of the wicking medium
- Lay geotextile fabric over the wicking medium to separate it from the soil layer

### 6. Add Soil

- Fill the remaining depth with your soil mix (10-12 inches)
- Mound slightly in the center for drainage away from edges
- Do not compress — soil should be loose for good wicking and root growth

### 7. Fill the Reservoir

- Fill the reservoir through the fill pipe until water flows from the overflow
- Wait 24 hours for the wicking medium and soil to fully saturate from below
- The soil surface should feel moist but not waterlogged

## Planting

### Best Plants for Wicking Beds

- **Vegetables:** Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, lettuce, kale, chard, beans, peas, cucumbers, zucchini, carrots, beets, radishes
- **Herbs:** Basil, parsley, cilantro, thyme, oregano
- **Avoid:** Potatoes (too much moisture causes rot), Mediterranean herbs that prefer dry conditions (rosemary, lavender, sage) — these do better in the upper zones or in separate beds

### Planting Pattern

- Space plants as you would in a conventional raised bed
- Tall plants on the north side (to avoid shading), short plants on the south
