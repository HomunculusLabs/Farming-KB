# Nitrogen Fixers in Guild Design

In Gaia's Garden, Toby Hemenway treats nitrogen-fixing plants as a
critical component of ecological garden design and
[[hemenway-guild-design-roles-and-assembly]]. Since
atmospheric nitrogen is freely available and nitrogen is the most
commonly limiting nutrient in gardens, including nitrogen fixers reduces
or eliminates the need for purchased fertilizers while keeping nutrients
cycling within the plant community.

## How Nitrogen Fixation Works

Nitrogen-fixing plants harbor bacteria or fungi among their roots that
extract nitrogen from the air and convert it to plant-available form.
This symbiotic relationship is one of nature's most important fertility
mechanisms. In legumes, Rhizobium bacteria form nodules on the roots;
in non-leguminous species such as alder and Elaeagnus, Frankia fungi
perform the same function.

## The Live-Nitrogen-Transfer Debate

A long-standing debate questions whether nitrogen-fixing plants must die
to release their fixed nitrogen to surrounding plants. Hemenway cites
both research and personal experience to settle the argument: live
N-fixers are at least as growth-boosting as dead ones. The mechanism
is the constant growth and dieback of fine root tips. During the natural
wet/dry soil cycle, the fixer's roots slough off, including the
nitrogen-bearing nodules. Surrounding plants and microbes then absorb
these nutrients as the roots decay.

William King's research, published in the Journal of Agronomy, used
radioactive tracer compounds to show that when ryegrass was interplanted
with clover, 80 percent of the nitrogen in the ryegrass had come from
the clover. The clover was pulling nitrogen from the air and feeding it
directly to the neighboring grass through root turnover.

## Nitrogen-Fixing Species for Gardens

Hemenway lists nitrogen fixers in all sizes, from ground covers to trees:

### Ground Cover Nitrogen Fixers

- **White Dutch clover** (Trifolium repens) -- the most versatile garden
  nitrogen fixer; tolerates foot traffic, mowing, and interplanting;
  permanent [[gaias-garden-living-mulches-ground-covers]];
  bee-attracting flowers
- **Crimson clover** (Trifolium incarnatum) -- annual, showy red flowers,
  good bee forage
- **Subterranean clover** (Trifolium subterraneum) -- self-seeding annual
- **Alsike clover** (Trifolium hybridum) -- tolerates wet soils
- **Sweet clover** (Melilotus spp.) -- biennial, grows 5+ feet tall,
  deep taproot, excellent [[gaias-garden-mulch-plants-ecological-garden]]
- **Vetch** (Vicia spp.) -- climbing annual, good for interplanting
  with grains

### Shrub Nitrogen Fixers

- **Siberian pea shrub** (Caragana arborascens) -- hardy, nitrogen-
  fixing, edible seeds; used in [[gaias-garden-deer-deflecting-food-hedges]]
- **Buffaloberry** (Shepherdia argentea) -- native, drought tolerant,
  edible berries
- **Elaeagnus species** (autumn olive, goumi, Russian olive) -- fast
  growing, edible fruit, drought tolerant
- **Ceanothus** (California lilac) -- native to western North America,
  beautiful blue flowers
- **Scotch broom** (Cytisus scoparius) -- very aggressive; useful on
  degraded sites but can become invasive
- **Laburnum** (Laburnum spp.) -- ornamental, bee-beloved

### Tree Nitrogen Fixers

- **Black locust** (Robinia pseudoacacia) -- fast growing, rot-resistant
  timber, bee forage, fixes large amounts of nitrogen
- **Alder** (Alnus spp.) -- excellent for wet sites, fast growing
- **Acacia** (Acacia spp.) -- many species, warm-climate trees
- **Mesquite** (Prosopis spp.) -- desert-adapted, deep rooted

### Edible Annual Nitrogen Fixers

- **Fava beans** (Vicia faba) -- edible beans, large seeds, cold hardy;
  Hemenway pokes one or two dozen fava seeds under each fruit tree in
  late summer for spring harvest and mulch material
- **Cowpeas** (Vigna unguiculata) -- edible, heat tolerant, stacks food
  production into the guild
- **Garden peas** (Pisum sativum) -- cool season edible
- **Common beans** (Phaseolus vulgaris) -- warm season edible
- **Peanuts** (Arachis hypogaea) -- edible, warm season

## Nitrogen Fixers in Cover Crop Mixes

Hemenway emphasizes that nitrogen fixers should be blended with
non-legumes in [[gaias-garden-cover-crops-soil-building]]
mixes. Soil organisms consume ten to fifty times more carbon than
nitrogen, so a cover crop rich only in nitrogen can actually deplete
more organic matter than it adds. The rule: blend legumes with grasses
or grains at roughly 10-40 percent non-legume content. Commercial cover
crop mixes typically contain oats, annual ryegrass, or other grasses to
balance the nitrogen from legumes.

## Nitrogen Fixers in Guild Design

When building a [[hemenway-guild-design-roles-and-assembly]]
around a central fruit tree, nitrogen fixers serve a specific role:

- Perennial clovers or alfalfa planted as understory ground cover
- Siberian pea shrub or Elaeagnus species as companion shrubs
- Fava beans or cowpeas interplanted as annuals
- The Bullock brothers on Orcas Island routinely plant a nitrogen-fixing
  shrub such as autumn olive in the same hole as a fruit tree,
  reporting that "trees planted with a nitrogen fixer grow faster"

## Design Principle: Go Heavy on Nitrogen Fixers

Hemenway's key observation, drawn from both personal experience and
research at the Land Institute: when building soil or feeding hungry
plants, go heavy on the nitrogen fixers. Nitrogen-fixing species abound
in early successional ecosystems -- young fields, pioneer dune
communities, freshly burned forests -- precisely because disturbed soils
need nitrogen most. The ecological gardener applies this lesson by
including nitrogen fixers generously during the establishment phase of
any garden, orchard, or food forest.
