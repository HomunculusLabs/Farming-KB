## Soil Biota Overview

On semi-arid pasture, surface observations suggest modest animal life — perhaps 3-6 sheep per hectare. Yet the same hectare supports 2-5 tonnes of pasture grubs or up to 65 tonnes of earthworms. The overwhelming majority of animal biomass is underground, not visible from the surface.

Even continuously wheat-cropped land — subjected to 140 cropping cycles at Rothamsted in the United Kingdom — still supports 0.5 tonnes of living microbial biomass per plough layer, with 1.2 tonnes per hectare of organic carbon returned annually.

Soil biomass growth is slow and sporadic, governed by humus and food turnover rates.

At Rothamsted, the mean age of humus was measured at 1,400 years, derived from the forests that preceded wheat cultivation on the site. This deep time signature underscores how slowly soil organic matter accumulates relative to how quickly it can be degraded.

During growth, approximately 30% of plant energy is lost as sugars and other compounds via root exudates. This massive below-ground energy subsidy fuels the microbial and faunal communities that sustain soil fertility.

The dynamics of this exudation are central to [[bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]] balance is a key indicator of soil succession and health.

Every square metre of forest topsoil contains an estimated 1,000 species of animals and 1-2 kilometres of fungal hyphae. The diversity of [[bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]].

Fungi are more effective than bacteria at breaking down wood and large plant material. They can transport materials over considerable distances — for example, moving nitrogen into decaying wood — and can shuttle nutrients into higher plants via direct root cell contact.

These transport functions are central to [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], is one of the most important symbioses in terrestrial ecosystems.

Fungi can also send hyphae many metres to invade trees, acting as either pathogens or decomposers. The capacity for long-range resource transport makes fungi uniquely powerful agents in soil ecosystems.

The cycle of life and death among soil organisms releases nutrients at multiple microsites, with timing dependent on temperature, moisture, and season. This staggered release pattern provides a steady nutrient supply to plants rather than the pulse-release typical of synthetic fertilisers.

Termites store calcium drawn from subsoils in their mounds, concentrating minerals at the surface. Bacteria similarly store soil minerals in their biomass.

## Size Classification of Soil Organisms

Soil biota are classified by body size into four groups, each occupying distinct ecological niches and performing different functions within the soil food web.

**Microflora and Microfauna (1-100 micrometres):**

Bacteria, fungi, nematodes, protozoa, and rotifers belong to this category. They are the primary decomposers and nutrient cyclers in soil, responsible for the bulk of organic matter breakdown and mineralisation.

See [[fungal-earthworm-interactions]] cover many of these groups.

**Macrofauna (2-20mm):**

Woodlice, harvestmen, amphipods, centipedes, millipedes, earthworms, beetles, spiders, slugs, snails, and ants. Macrofauna are the ecosystem engineers — earthworms in particular reshape soil structure.

See [[fungal-contributions-soil-structure]] together maintain the physical architecture that supports root growth and water infiltration.

Earthworms act as an "innumerable army of pistons pumping air in and out of soils on a 24-hour cycle," with activity increasing at night. This continuous aeration is essential for maintaining aerobic conditions that support beneficial microbial communities.

The dry-weight protein content of earthworms ranges from 55-71%, derived entirely from inedible plant wastes. This makes them an extraordinarily efficient converter of low-grade organic material into high-quality protein, relevant to [[fungal-earthworm-interactions]].

The turnover of nitrogen by earthworms exceeds the litter fall of plants, meaning earthworms process and cycle more nitrogen than all above-ground plant inputs combined. This underscores their central role in the nitrogen economy of healthy soils.

Mollison suggests that the most sustainable yields from land may be grubs and caterpillars rather than sheep — and that these invertebrates can be converted into poultry or fish food. This reframing of "pests" as protein harvests aligns with permaculture's principle of turning problems into solutions.
