# Soil Biota and Earthworm Ecology

The living component of soils — collectively termed soil biota — constitutes the vast majority of animal biomass in most terrestrial ecosystems. Drawing from Bill Mollison's synthesis in Section 8.12 of the Permaculture Designers Manual, this concept page explores the hidden world beneath our feet and its critical role in nutrient cycling, soil structure, and ecosystem health.

See [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] for a broader treatment of these interconnected relationships.

## Soil Biota Overview

On semi-arid pasture, surface observations suggest modest animal life — perhaps 3-6 sheep per hectare. Yet the same hectare supports 2-5 tonnes of pasture grubs or up to 65 tonnes of earthworms. The overwhelming majority of animal biomass is underground, not visible from the surface.

Even continuously wheat-cropped land — subjected to 140 cropping cycles at Rothamsted in the United Kingdom — still supports 0.5 tonnes of living microbial biomass per plough layer, with 1.2 tonnes per hectare of organic carbon returned annually.

Soil biomass growth is slow and sporadic, governed by humus and food turnover rates.

At Rothamsted, the mean age of humus was measured at 1,400 years, derived from the forests that preceded wheat cultivation on the site. This deep time signature underscores how slowly soil organic matter accumulates relative to how quickly it can be degraded.

During growth, approximately 30% of plant energy is lost as sugars and other compounds via root exudates. This massive below-ground energy subsidy fuels the microbial and faunal communities that sustain soil fertility.

The dynamics of this exudation are central to [[composting-and-soil-biology]].

At Rothamsted, the biomass proportions were measured as follows:

- 50% fungi

- 20% bacteria

- 20% yeasts, algae, and protozoans

- 10% macrofauna (earthworms, nematodes, arthropods, molluscs)

The [[bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]] balance is a key indicator of soil succession and health.

Every square metre of forest topsoil contains an estimated 1,000 species of animals and 1-2 kilometres of fungal hyphae. The diversity of [[fungal-decomposition]] across soil profiles is staggering and largely unappreciated.

The destructive potential of tillage is made clear by Canadian data showing that plough cropping reduced humus to just 1% of original levels. This catastrophic loss underscores why permaculture design favours minimal soil disturbance.

Climatic variation strongly influences soil biota populations.

Acid coniferous and oak forest soils yield few earthworms, and humic peats yield even less. Worm-recycled soil mass varies enormously across climates, ranging from 2 to 150 tonnes per hectare.

## Fungal vs Bacterial Balance

The balance between fungal and bacterial communities in soil is shaped by disturbance regimes.

Ecological disturbance, predation, aeration, disturbed soils, low oxygen, and compaction all favour bacteria over fungi. Conversely, stable, undisturbed environments with adequate oxygen tend to favour fungal dominance. This dynamic is explored in depth in [[bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]].

Fungi are more effective than bacteria at breaking down wood and large plant material. They can transport materials over considerable distances — for example, moving nitrogen into decaying wood — and can shuttle nutrients into higher plants via direct root cell contact.

These transport functions are central to [[fungal-nutrient-cycling-mineral-transformations]].

Higher plants supply energy to their fungal root associates in the form of sugars, receiving minerals and nutrients in return. This mutualistic exchange, mediated by [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], is one of the most important symbioses in terrestrial ecosystems.

Fungi can also send hyphae many metres to invade trees, acting as either pathogens or decomposers. The capacity for long-range resource transport makes fungi uniquely powerful agents in soil ecosystems.

See [[fungal-mineral-nutrient-immobilization]] for how fungi temporarily lock up nutrients during decomposition.

## Soil Biota as Nutrient Reserve

Soil biota function as a living reserve of otherwise easily leached nutrients, particularly nitrogen and sulphur. These organisms gather, store, and concentrate nutrients that would otherwise be lost from the system.

The role of biota in [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] cannot be overstated.

The cycle of life and death among soil organisms releases nutrients at multiple microsites, with timing dependent on temperature, moisture, and season. This staggered release pattern provides a steady nutrient supply to plants rather than the pulse-release typical of synthetic fertilisers.

Termites store calcium drawn from subsoils in their mounds, concentrating minerals at the surface. Bacteria similarly store soil minerals in their biomass.

The [[fungal-role-in-nitrogen-cycling]] demonstrates how specific organisms sequester and release key nutrients.

Plants are not passive recipients in this system — they are direct predators of soil fauna. Algae eat bacteria, fungi consume nematodes, and plants scavenge the dead bodies of soil organisms.

The relationships in [[fungal-grazing-soil-fauna]] illustrate this predatory web.

## Size Classification of Soil Organisms

Soil biota are classified by body size into four groups, each occupying distinct ecological niches and performing different functions within the soil food web.

**Microflora and Microfauna (1-100 micrometres):**

Bacteria, fungi, nematodes, protozoa, and rotifers belong to this category. They are the primary decomposers and nutrient cyclers in soil, responsible for the bulk of organic matter breakdown and mineralisation.

See [[nematodes-in-soil-ecology]] for detail on this critical group.

**Mesofauna (100 micrometres - 2mm):**

Mites, springtails, small myriapods, enchytraeid worms, false scorpions, and termites. These organisms fragment organic matter, stimulating microbial decomposition.

Details on soil arthropods and [[fungal-earthworm-interactions]] cover many of these groups.

**Macrofauna (2-20mm):**

Woodlice, harvestmen, amphipods, centipedes, millipedes, earthworms, beetles, spiders, slugs, snails, and ants. Macrofauna are the ecosystem engineers — earthworms in particular reshape soil structure.

See [[earthworm-breeding]] for propagation methods.

**Megafauna (20mm+):**

Crickets, moles, rodents, wombats, and rabbits. These larger animals aerate soil through burrowing and contribute organic matter through waste and decomposition.

Numbers per square metre in temperate grassland illustrate the incredible density of soil life:

- Nematodes: 120 million

- Mites: 100,000

- Springtails: 45,000

- Enchytraeid worms: 20,000

- Molluscs: 10,000

## Earthworms

Charles Darwin wrote in 1881: "Worms have played a more important part in the history of the world than most persons would at first suppose." More than 10 tons of dry earth annually passes through earthworm bodies per acre, a process that aerates, mixes, and enriches the soil.

Copious worm life is a reliable sign of healthy soil (Satchell, 1984). Worms recycle manure and leaves, keeping soil structure open and porous. Their burrowing activity and [[fungal-contributions-soil-structure]] together maintain the physical architecture that supports root growth and water infiltration.

Earthworms act as an "innumerable army of pistons pumping air in and out of soils on a 24-hour cycle," with activity increasing at night. This continuous aeration is essential for maintaining aerobic conditions that support beneficial microbial communities.

The dry-weight protein content of earthworms ranges from 55-71%, derived entirely from inedible plant wastes. This makes them an extraordinarily efficient converter of low-grade organic material into high-quality protein, relevant to [[earthworm-breeding]] systems.

Earthworms accumulate pollutants to concentrations 14-20 times higher than surrounding soil levels, including DDT, lead, cadmium, and dioxins. While this is a concern for organisms that eat worms, it also means earthworms can function as biological concentrators — a double-edged ecological role.

The presence of moles, blackbirds, and small hawks in abundance is an indication of soil health, as these predators depend on rich soil fauna. Where these animals are absent, it serves as a warning to check for biocides and other chemical contaminants.

The interactions between earthworms and other soil organisms are detailed in [[fungal-earthworm-interactions]].

The turnover of nitrogen by earthworms exceeds the litter fall of plants, meaning earthworms process and cycle more nitrogen than all above-ground plant inputs combined. This underscores their central role in the nitrogen economy of healthy soils.

Mollison suggests that the most sustainable yields from land may be grubs and caterpillars rather than sheep — and that these invertebrates can be converted into poultry or fish food. This reframing of "pests" as protein harvests aligns with permaculture's principle of turning problems into solutions.

The role of [[biofertilizers-and-soil-inoculants]] complements this approach by building the soil biology that supports these productive invertebrate populations.
