Sepp Holzer's fruit tree planting method stands in direct contrast to conventional arboriculture. Developed through decades of experimentation at the Krameterhof (1,100-1,500m altitude in Austria), his approach produces resilient, self-sufficient trees that do not require regular pruning, staking, or chemical inputs.

## The Conventional Method -- Why It Fails

Holzer learned conventional fruit tree cultivation in agricultural school and applied it rigorously for one year, with disastrous results:

### Conventional Steps (and their problems)
1. **Dig a 1m-wide, 40-50cm-deep hole** -- unnecessary disturbance of soil structure
2. **Install galvanised wire mesh** -- voles dig down from above and bypass it; mesh eventually rusts
3. **Mix in chemical fertiliser** -- causes rapid, weak growth; branches cannot lignify properly and break under snow or fruit load
4. **Hammer in a stake and tie tree** -- tree becomes dependent on artificial support; never develops its own stability
5. **Prune branches below 45 degrees (apple) or 60 degrees (pear)** -- removes natural resilience; creates wounds that invite disease
6. **Remove inner branches** -- supposed to let in light but actually weakens the tree's structure
7. **Remove grass within 1m of tree** -- exposes soil to erosion, temperature extremes, and moisture loss
8. **Spray pesticides and poison voles** -- contaminates soil, kills beneficial organisms

The result: trees become "addicted" to regular fertilising and watering, susceptible to scab, fungus, and frost, easily damaged by wind or snow, and vulnerable to every kind of pest. At high altitudes, conventionally treated trees died while neglected ones thrived.

## Holzer's Method

### Step 1: Leave All Branches Below the Graft
Do NOT cut off branches between the graft union and the ground. These side shoots serve multiple purposes:
- Natural browsing protection (hares eat lowest, roe deer middle, red deer upper)
- Provide photosynthesis and energy to the young tree
- Create a bushy base that protects the trunk

### Step 2: Dig In Simply
No metre-deep holes. Just dig the tree in well. The root ball is dug in a square plate shape, which:
- Is easy to place on the ground or replant
- Is well-rooted and strengthened by the plant community
- Does not require netting or mesh
- Accompanying plants protect the root ball from drying out during transport and establishment

### Step 3: Cover with Mulch or Stones
**Mulch**: holds moisture, rots down to become fertiliser
**Stones**: stabilise the tree by weighing down roots; "sweat" (condensation collects underneath, helpful for newly planted trees); balance temperature; create habitat for worms, lizards, slow worms, and ground beetles

### Step 4: Sow Supporting Plants
Sow soil-improving plants around the tree immediately. Deep-rooted pioneer plants are particularly suitable:
- Lupins ([[permaculture-tree-lupin]])
- Sweet clover
- Lucerne
- Broom

Their deep roots aerate the soil and prevent water buildup in the topsoil. Fruit trees are particularly sensitive to water accumulation -- it causes stunting, reduced yield, and increased disease/pest susceptibility.

### Step 5: Create Microclimates
If the location is not ideal, create microclimates:
- Suntraps using stones or water bodies that reflect heat
- Windbreaks using trees or shrubs
- [[holzer-raised-bed-design-principles]] for improved drainage
- Stone walls that absorb and radiate heat

## Why No Pruning

When trees are NOT pruned:
- Branches retain their natural resilience
- Under the weight of fruit, branches sink down, naturally allowing sunlight to reach the crown
- Branches can support themselves on the ground when weighed down by fruit or snow
- Trees stabilise themselves and are less likely to grow at an angle
- Trees can adapt to terrain

When trees ARE pruned (conventionally):
- Trees lose resilience
- Branches stick up rigidly and break under snow or heavy fruit loads
- Pruning creates wounds that increase disease risk (fungal diseases, fire blight)
- Trees respond by producing water sprouts, creating a vicious cycle of more pruning
- Causes unnecessary stress

## Why No Chemical Fertiliser

Fertilised trees grow faster but do not lignify properly. At high altitudes, poorly lignified branches cannot withstand extreme temperatures. Chemical fertiliser puts the tree out of balance and reduces winter survival.

## Why No Staking

Staked trees never develop their own root anchorage and trunk strength. When the stake is eventually removed (or rots), the tree is vulnerable to windthrow. Self-supporting trees develop stronger, more extensive root systems.

## Replanting

When Holzer replants a tree, he digs up the other plants along with it in the square plate root ball. This:
- Saves the work of sowing a new area
- The plant community strengthens the root ball during transport
- No net or mesh is needed
- The tree re-establishes faster with its companion plants

## Grafting

Holzer sows tree seeds and then grafts desired varieties onto the resulting rootstock. This approach:

- Costs no more than planting any other kind of tree
- Requires patience, as trees take longer to give their first yield
- Produces hardy, independent trees adapted to local conditions

## Cultivated and Wild Fruit Trees Together

Holzer mixes cultivated and wild fruit trees throughout the farm:

### Wild Species Planted

- Crab apple (Malus sylvestris)
- Wild pear (Pyrus pyraster)
- Wild cherry (Prunus avium)
- Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa)
- Rowan (Sorbus aucuparia)
- Wild service (Sorbus torminalis)
- Service tree (Sorbus domestica)
- Cornelian cherry (Cornus mas)
- Snowy mespilus (Amelanchier ovalis)

### Benefits of Mixed Culture

- Wild fruit trees pollinate many cultivated varieties
- Different flowering times prevent complete crop failure from late frost
- Continuous pollen availability ensures good fruit set
- Wild fruit is valued for schnapps, vinegar, jams, juices, and medicinal purposes
- Fruit trees in paddocks provide windfall feed for [[holzer-livestock-integration-pigs-cattle]]
- Blossoms feed bees and other pollinating insects
- Wood from fruit trees (especially pear and cherry) is valued as quality timber

## High-Altitude Fruit Growing

Holzer cultivates fruit trees up to 1,500m above sea level, where conventional wisdom says fruit growing ends at 1,000m. Advantages of high-altitude fruit:
- Cherries ripen in September when the harvest is long over at lower altitudes
- Plums, pears, and apples develop very intense flavour due to harsh nights
- Higher prices from distillery and vinegar specialists
- Health-conscious consumers value the intense flavour

Supposedly "demanding" varieties like Golden Delicious can adjust to high altitudes if given shelter from wind and [[climate-and-microclimate-management]].
