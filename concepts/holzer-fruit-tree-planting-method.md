    11|Sepp Holzer's fruit tree planting method stands in direct contrast to conventional arboriculture. Developed through decades of experimentation at the Krameterhof (1,100-1,500m altitude in Austria), his approach produces resilient, self-sufficient trees that do not require regular pruning, staking, or chemical inputs.
    12|
    13|## The Conventional Method -- Why It Fails
    14|
    15|Holzer learned conventional fruit tree cultivation in agricultural school and applied it rigorously for one year, with disastrous results:
    16|
    17|### Conventional Steps (and their problems)
    18|1. **Dig a 1m-wide, 40-50cm-deep hole** -- unnecessary disturbance of soil structure
    19|2. **Install galvanised wire mesh** -- voles dig down from above and bypass it; mesh eventually rusts
    20|3. **Mix in chemical fertiliser** -- causes rapid, weak growth; branches cannot lignify properly and break under snow or fruit load
    21|4. **Hammer in a stake and tie tree** -- tree becomes dependent on artificial support; never develops its own stability
    22|5. **Prune branches below 45 degrees (apple) or 60 degrees (pear)** -- removes natural resilience; creates wounds that invite disease
    23|6. **Remove inner branches** -- supposed to let in light but actually weakens the tree's structure
    24|7. **Remove grass within 1m of tree** -- exposes soil to erosion, temperature extremes, and moisture loss
    25|8. **Spray pesticides and poison voles** -- contaminates soil, kills beneficial organisms
    26|
    27|The result: trees become "addicted" to regular fertilising and watering, susceptible to scab, fungus, and frost, easily damaged by wind or snow, and vulnerable to every kind of pest. At high altitudes, conventionally treated trees died while neglected ones thrived.
    28|
    29|## Holzer's Method
    30|
    31|### Step 1: Leave All Branches Below the Graft
    32|Do NOT cut off branches between the graft union and the ground. These side shoots serve multiple purposes:
    33|- Natural browsing protection (hares eat lowest, roe deer middle, red deer upper)
    34|- Provide photosynthesis and energy to the young tree
    35|- Create a bushy base that protects the trunk
    36|
    37|### Step 2: Dig In Simply
    38|No metre-deep holes. Just dig the tree in well. The root ball is dug in a square plate shape, which:
    39|- Is easy to place on the ground or replant
    40|- Is well-rooted and strengthened by the plant community
    41|- Does not require netting or mesh
    42|- Accompanying plants protect the root ball from drying out during transport and establishment
    43|
    44|### Step 3: Cover with Mulch or Stones
    45|**Mulch**: holds moisture, rots down to become fertiliser
    46|**Stones**: stabilise the tree by weighing down roots; "sweat" (condensation collects underneath, helpful for newly planted trees); balance temperature; create habitat for worms, lizards, slow worms, and ground beetles
    47|
    48|### Step 4: Sow Supporting Plants
    49|Sow soil-improving plants around the tree immediately. Deep-rooted pioneer plants are particularly suitable:
    50|- Lupins ([[permaculture-tree-lupin]])
    51|- Sweet clover
    52|- Lucerne
    53|- Broom
    54|
    55|Their deep roots aerate the soil and prevent water buildup in the topsoil. Fruit trees are particularly sensitive to water accumulation -- it causes stunting, reduced yield, and increased disease/pest susceptibility.
    56|
    57|### Step 5: Create Microclimates
    58|If the location is not ideal, create microclimates:
    59|- Suntraps using stones or water bodies that reflect heat
    60|- Windbreaks using trees or shrubs
    61|- [[holzer-raised-bed-design-principles]] for improved drainage
    62|- Stone walls that absorb and radiate heat
    63|
    64|## Why No Pruning
    65|
    66|When trees are NOT pruned:
    67|- Branches retain their natural resilience
    68|- Under the weight of fruit, branches sink down, naturally allowing sunlight to reach the crown
    69|- Branches can support themselves on the ground when weighed down by fruit or snow
    70|- Trees stabilise themselves and are less likely to grow at an angle
    71|- Trees can adapt to terrain
    72|
    73|When trees ARE pruned (conventionally):
    74|- Trees lose resilience
    75|- Branches stick up rigidly and break under snow or heavy fruit loads
    76|- Pruning creates wounds that increase disease risk (fungal diseases, fire blight)
    77|- Trees respond by producing water sprouts, creating a vicious cycle of more pruning
    78|- Causes unnecessary stress
    79|
    80|## Why No Chemical Fertiliser
    81|
    82|Fertilised trees grow faster but do not lignify properly. At high altitudes, poorly lignified branches cannot withstand extreme temperatures. Chemical fertiliser puts the tree out of balance and reduces winter survival.
    83|
    84|## Why No Staking
    85|
    86|Staked trees never develop their own root anchorage and trunk strength. When the stake is eventually removed (or rots), the tree is vulnerable to windthrow. Self-supporting trees develop stronger, more extensive root systems.
    87|
    88|## Replanting
    89|
    90|When Holzer replants a tree, he digs up the other plants along with it in the square plate root ball. This:
    91|- Saves the work of sowing a new area
    92|- The plant community strengthens the root ball during transport
    93|- No net or mesh is needed
    94|- The tree re-establishes faster with its companion plants
    95|
    96|## Grafting
    97|
    98|Holzer sows tree seeds and then grafts desired varieties onto the resulting rootstock. This approach:
    99|
   100|- Costs no more than planting any other kind of tree
   101|- Requires patience, as trees take longer to give their first yield
   102|- Produces hardy, independent trees adapted to local conditions
   103|
   104|## Cultivated and Wild Fruit Trees Together
   105|
   106|Holzer mixes cultivated and wild fruit trees throughout the farm:
   107|
   108|### Wild Species Planted
   109|
   110|- Crab apple (Malus sylvestris)
   111|- Wild pear (Pyrus pyraster)
   112|- Wild cherry (Prunus avium)
   113|- Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa)
   114|- Rowan (Sorbus aucuparia)
   115|- Wild service (Sorbus torminalis)
   116|- Service tree (Sorbus domestica)
   117|- Cornelian cherry (Cornus mas)
   118|- Snowy mespilus (Amelanchier ovalis)
   119|
   120|### Benefits of Mixed Culture
   121|
   122|- Wild fruit trees pollinate many cultivated varieties
   123|- Different flowering times prevent complete crop failure from late frost
   124|- Continuous pollen availability ensures good fruit set
   125|- Wild fruit is valued for schnapps, vinegar, jams, juices, and medicinal purposes
   126|- Fruit trees in paddocks provide windfall feed for [[holzer-livestock-integration-pigs-cattle]]
   127|- Blossoms feed bees and other pollinating insects
   128|- Wood from fruit trees (especially pear and cherry) is valued as quality timber
   129|
   130|## High-Altitude Fruit Growing
   131|
   132|Holzer cultivates fruit trees up to 1,500m above sea level, where conventional wisdom says fruit growing ends at 1,000m. Advantages of high-altitude fruit:
   133|- Cherries ripen in September when the harvest is long over at lower altitudes
   134|- Plums, pears, and apples develop very intense flavour due to harsh nights
   135|- Higher prices from distillery and vinegar specialists
   136|- Health-conscious consumers value the intense flavour
   137|
   138|Supposedly "demanding" varieties like Golden Delicious can adjust to high altitudes if given shelter from wind and [[climate-and-microclimate-management]].
   139|
   140|## Key Diagnostic Principle
   141|
   142|When a tree does not grow properly, the reason is usually:
   143|- Wrong location (too windy, warm, cold, wet, or dry)
   144|- Unfavourable soil conditions (especially [[soil-compaction-solutions-ingham]])
   145|- Water accumulation around roots
   146|
   147|Observe trees closely and learn to recognise by the colour of leaves and bark whether a tree is happy in its habitat.
