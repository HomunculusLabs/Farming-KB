# Fungal Biodiversity Crisis

## Overview

The fungal kingdom represents one of the most poorly documented branches of the
tree of life. Current estimates suggest there are between 2.2 and 3.8 million
fungal species on Earth, yet only about 148,000 have been formally described.
This means roughly 93 to 96 percent of fungal diversity remains unknown to
science, a gap far larger than for plants or animals. The scale of this
undocumented diversity constitutes what many mycologists consider a biodiversity
crisis of the first order.

## Extinction Rates

Fungal extinction is occurring, but at rates that we cannot accurately measure
because we do not know how many species exist. The IUCN Red List includes only
a tiny fraction of known fungal species, and cannot assess species that have
not been formally described. This means fungal extinction may be occurring
unnoticed and at rates far exceeding the rate of species description.

Co-extinction of fungi with their host plants or animal partners represents a
particularly poorly documented pathway of biodiversity loss. When a host species
goes extinct, its specialized fungal symbionts, pathogens, and endophytes may
disappear as well. Because many fungi depend on specific host plants, the loss
of a single habitat type can result in the disappearance of dozens of undescribed
fungal species.

## Threats to Fungal Diversity

Habitat destruction is the single greatest threat to fungal biodiversity.
Deforestation, agricultural expansion, and urbanization eliminate the substrates
and microhabitats that fungi depend on. Tropical rainforests, which harbor the
highest fungal diversity, are being cleared at alarming rates. Each hectare of
forest lost may represent the extinction of numerous undescribed fungal species.

## Knowledge Gaps

Several factors contribute to the description deficit. Fungi are often
microscopic or cryptic, making field identification difficult. Many species
produce visible fruiting bodies only under narrow environmental conditions. The
historical bias in biodiversity research toward animals and plants has meant
fewer resources devoted to fungal systematics. Training programs in fungal
taxonomy have declined even as the magnitude of the unknown has become clearer.

Environmental DNA sequencing has revealed that the unseen fungal world is far
more diverse than morphology alone suggested. High-throughput sequencing regularly
recovers fungal DNA sequences that cannot be assigned to any known species.
However, molecular data alone cannot replace formal taxonomic description. The
gap between sequence-based discovery and formal taxonomy continues to widen.
