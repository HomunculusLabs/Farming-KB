# Direct Seeding Non-Cultivation

Direct seeding non-cultivation is Masanobu Fukuoka's method of growing rice and
winter grain in succession without ploughing, transplanting, or applying
chemical inputs. Detailed in *The One-Straw Revolution* (1978), this technique
produces approximately 22 bushels (1,300 pounds) per quarter acre for both rice
and winter grain, matching or exceeding top Ehime Prefecture yields. In some
areas, harvests of 27.5 bushels (1,650 pounds) per quarter acre have been
realized. It takes only an hour or two for one farmer to sow seeds and spread
straw across a quarter acre, and the entire year's planting finishes by New
Year's Day.

## Annual Seeding Schedule

The yearly cycle follows a precise sequence timed to natural conditions.

**Early October**: White clover is broadcast among the ripening rice at
approximately one pound per quarter acre. Fast-growing varieties of winter
grain (rye and barley) are then sown at 6.5 to 13 pounds per quarter acre.
For inexperienced farmers or fields with hard or poor soil, it is safer to sow
more seed. As the soil improves and the farmer becomes more familiar with the
method, the amount of seed can be reduced. The clover and grain sprout and
grow an inch or two by harvest time. During the rice harvest, the sprouted
seeds are trampled by harvesters' feet but recover immediately. After
threshing, all rice straw is spread over the field.

**Mid-November to Mid-December**: Clay-enclosed rice seeds are broadcast among
the young barley or rye plants at 4.5 to 9 pounds per quarter acre. Toward
the end of April, Fukuoka checks the germination of the fall-sown seed and
broadcasts more pellets as needed. A thin layer of chicken manure is spread
over the field to help decompose the straw, completing the year's planting.

**May**: Winter grain is harvested. After threshing, all straw is scattered
over the field. Water is allowed to stand in the field for a week or ten
days, weakening weeds and clover and allowing rice to sprout through the
straw.

**June to July**: Rainwater alone sustains the plants. No irrigation is needed
during these months. Even in years with very little rain, the soil stays moist
below the layer of straw and green manure.

**August**: Fresh water is run through the field about once a week without
being allowed to stand. **Autumn**: The rice harvest arrives.

## Clay Pellet Seed Protection

Rice seeds sown in autumn and left uncovered are vulnerable to mice, birds,
and rotting. Fukuoka solved this by enclosing seeds in clay pellets. The
process involves spreading unhulled rice seed on a flat pan or basket, shaking
it in a circular motion while dusting fine powdered clay over it and adding a
thin mist of water. This forms tiny pellets about half an inch in diameter,
ideally with one seed in each pellet.

An alternative method involves soaking unhulled rice seed for several hours in
water, mixing it with moist clay by kneading with hands or feet, then pushing
the clay through a screen of chicken wire to separate it into small clods. The
clods are dried for a day or two until they can be easily rolled into pellets.
In one day, enough pellets can be made to seed several acres. The pellets
protect seeds from predation while allowing germination on the soil surface
where oxygen exposure is optimal. Seeds enclosed in pellets and covered with
straw germinate well and do not rot even in years of heavy rainfall.

## Rice Plant Form and Dry-Field Growth

Fukuoka did not try to raise tall, fast-growing plants with big leaves.
Instead, he kept plants as compact as possible, with small heads, avoiding
over-nourishment, and letting them grow true to the natural form of the rice
plant. While tall plants yielding 2,000 pounds of straw might produce only
1,000 to 1,200 pounds of rice, his small plants producing 2,000 pounds of
straw yielded 2,000 pounds of rice. In good harvests, the rice yield reached
about 2,400 pounds, which is 20 percent heavier than the straw.

Rice plants grown in a dry field do not grow as tall. Sunlight reaches the
base and lower leaves uniformly. One square inch of leaf produces about six
grains of rice. Three or four small leaves are more than enough to produce a
hundred grains per head. Sowing thickly and not trying to grow large plants
yields 250 to 300 grain-bearing stalks per square yard.

If you show a rice plant from Fukuoka's field to a farmer, he will know
immediately that it looks as a rice plant should and that it has the ideal
shape, that the seeds were sprouted naturally and not transplanted, that the
plant could not have been grown in a lot of water, and that chemical fertilizer
was not applied.

## Temporary Flooding Strategy

Rice plants grow best when soil water content is between 60 and 80 percent of
water-holding capacity. When not flooded, plants develop stronger roots and
become extremely resistant to disease and insect attacks. In June, during the
monsoon season, Fukuoka held water in the field for about one week. Few
dry-field weeds can survive even so short a period without oxygen, and the
clover also withers and turns yellow. The idea was not to kill the clover but
only to weaken it to allow rice seedlings to become established.

When the water was drained, the clover recovered and spread to cover the
field's surface again beneath the growing rice plants. After that, he hardly
managed water at all. For the first half of the season, he did not irrigate at
all. In August, he let in water a little at a time but never allowed it to
stand.

## Duck Integration for Fertility

For many years, Fukuoka let ducks loose in the fields to supply manure. If
introduced as ducklings while the seedlings were still young, the ducks grew
up together with the rice. Ten ducks supplied all the manure necessary for a
quarter acre and also helped to control the weeds. This practice ended when
national highway construction made it impossible for ducks to cross the road
and return to the coop.

## Long-Term Results and Soil Improvement

The yields continued to increase over twenty years of continuous use, and the
soil became richer every year. At first, the red clay soil in Fukuoka's fields
was weak and unsuited for growing rice. Brown spot disease frequently occurred.
But as the field gradually grew in fertility, the incidence of disease
decreased, and lately there had been no outbreaks at all. By spreading straw,
growing clover, and returning all organic residues, the earth comes to possess
all the nutrients needed to grow rice and winter grain year after year.

## Adaptability and Scientific Validation

Fukuoka felt that natural farming could be applied in other areas and to the
raising of other indigenous crops. In areas where water is less available,
upland rice, buckwheat, sorghum, or millet might replace flooded rice. Instead
of white clover, other varieties of clover, alfalfa, vetch, or lupine might
serve as field cover. Testing centres across Japan produced no evidence to
contradict the method's universal applicability. The speed with which direct
seeding was spreading in Okayama Prefecture was described as eye-opening.

"There is probably no easier, simpler method for growing grain," but it took
Fukuoka over thirty years to reach this simplicity.
