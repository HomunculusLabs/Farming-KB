## Village Development and Legal Structures

## Overview

The development of permaculture villages requires careful planning across multiple domains: site selection, subdivision approval, financial structuring, legal organization, and staged construction. [[bill-mollison]] outlines a systematic approach to village development that integrates [[williams-pdc-ecological-design-principles]] with sound legal and financial frameworks, drawing on successful models like the [[mondragon-cooperatives]] of Spain.

## The Development Process

### Site Planning and Approval

The village development process follows a defined sequence:

1. **Select a suitable site** with adequate water, soil, and climate resources
2. **Obtain a survey and subdivision plan** that respects ecological zones and waterways
3. **Negotiate staged development** with the shire (local government) if building must occur in phases
4. **Establish accurate cost estimates** based on roading, water supply, and sewerage infrastructure
5. **Prepare a detailed site plan and proposal** for the village, including zones for housing, food production, forestry, recreation, and commerce
6. **Convene prospective purchasers** through advertisement and obtain firm commitments; issue the proposal and site plan
7. **Obtain sealed subdivision permission** from the local authority
8. **Sell to prospective buyers**, using a trust fund for road, water, and site preparation
9. **Reinvest profits and skills** into future projects once initial costs are cleared

### Financing Models

Several approaches to financing village development are viable:

- **Developer-funded**: The developer holds all capital for survey and planning
- **Investor-funded**: The developer raises funds from investors for the proposal stage
- **Buyer trust account**: Potential buyers form a trust account to purchase and develop the land collectively
- **Mixed model**: A combination of developer and investor funds, with trust funds established for specific stages

Trust funds can be released when development is fully approved by the shire council. Roading and services are installed at stages demanded by the shire or region, spreading capital requirements over time.

## Legal Structures: The Two-Trust Model

### Trust A — The Land Bank

The developer establishes **Trust A** as a land bank to hold commons (village land) for the common good and for future development. Key features include:

- The developer acts as the initial foundation director (settlor)
- The developer should retire as soon as the site has 10–12 residents, who then assume directorship of the trust lands and cash assets
- **3–4 directors are sufficient** to manage the trust effectively
- Trust A holds assets for the public good, does not take risks, and leases or rents to Trust B

Trust A administers common lands, recreation reserves, and sites for future structures such as schools, restaurants, machine shops, or [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]] areas.

### Trust B — The Trading Trust

Residents should, as early as possible, set up a **separate unit discretionary trust (Trust B)** for trading operations. Advantages include:

- Currently immune from company tax in many jurisdictions
- Reduces family tax burden
- Enables a wide variety of enterprises to be initiated under one organizational umbrella
- Can duplicate or triplicate itself to accommodate new enterprises and to insulate successful operations from risk
- Can handle financial systems such as leasing and lending units

### Relationship Between Trusts

The two trusts form a complementary pair:

| Feature | Trust A (Land Bank) | Trust B (Trading) |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Hold commons for public good | Conduct trade and enterprise |
| Risk | Conservative, no risk-taking | Accepts business risk |
| Income | Leases and rents from Trust B | Trading profits, services |
| Beneficiaries | The community as a whole | Trust A is one of its beneficiaries |

### Title Systems

**Cluster and strata titles** provide privacy, negotiability, and autonomy to individuals and families. Titles and houses can be resold, traded, or transferred to Trust A. However, a land trust remains necessary to administer common lands regardless of the title system used.

## Optimal Village Size

### Population and Social Function

Human settlements vary in their ability to provide resources, develop self-reliance, and maintain healthy social dynamics according to population size:

- **~100 income-producing people**: A significant financial institution can be village-based
- **~500 people**: All people can know each other; social affairs strengthen community bonds
- **~2,000 people**: Theft and competitiveness become more common; sectarian divisions emerge; "ecumenical alliances" are lost

### The Recommended Growth Strategy

Mollison recommends starting small (about **30 adults**), building to **200–300 people**, and then proceeding slowly and by choice to **500**, at which point the village should "calve" into new neighbourhoods or daughter villages.

### Confederation of Villages

Alliances of 200–500 household-size hamlets can form very viable manufacturing or trading alliances while maintaining safe [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]]. Many tribes of ~200 have historically confederated to alliances of 5,000–7,000, sharing special products by trade and arranging out-marriages.

### The Mondragon Model

The **Mondragon Cooperatives of Spain** initially grew large (3,000–5,000 members) but later reduced to cooperatives of 300–500 to preserve individual identity. A group of such small cooperatives can collectively manufacture any vehicle or machine if each produces a component part.

### The Neighbourhood Factor

Knowledge of each other"s names — the neighbourhood factor — is identified as a **primary survival factor**. This was demonstrated in the 1967 Hobart fires, where casualties in "anonymous" commuter suburbs were many times higher than in neighbourhoods where people knew and cared for others.

## The Development Group

The developmental group works best as a **small core of 2–4 people**, each with special skills such as:

- Real estate and property law
- Permaculture and site design
- Planning and regulatory compliance
- Legal structuring

Surveyors, road-builders, builders, and landscapers are usually locally available. Some contractors may work for equity in the project, either as village occupants or for resale at a later date.

### Handover Protocol

The development group should hand over a **site design and user's manual** to the directors of Trust A, who then:

- Display and circulate the initial design
- Record changes and modifications
- Keep essential land areas clear for productive use

All initial designs are made to be changed — a design gives a starting point, not an end point. The challenge is to change the design for the better.

## Organizational Structure

A well-organized community uses independent decision-making groups, each separately accounted, with elected representatives coordinating between [[effective-working-groups]] and management. Each group maintains its own accounting and pays fees for shared vehicles and equipment. Community land, forests, and orchards are managed collectively.
