# Icaros in Ayahuasca Ceremony

Icaros are sacred songs sung by ayahuasqueros ([[shipibo-ayahuasca-shamans-practice]]) during
ceremonial sessions. They are a central element of Amazonian shamanic practice,
particularly among the Shipibo people of Peru's Ucayali region. Icaros serve
multiple functions within the ceremony: they structure the ritual experience,
guide the visionary content of participants, invoke protective spirits, and are
considered by practitioners to have active healing and energetic properties.

## Definition and Cultural Context

The word "icaro" (also spelled "ikaro") derives from the Quechua language and
refers to the melodic, often improvisational songs performed by shamans during
[[metzner-amazonian-ayahuasca-ceremonies-icaros-and-healing-methods]] and other healing rituals. Among the Shipibo, icaros are
considered to be received through visionary contact with plant spirits during
the shaman's own dietas (periods of retreat and plant apprenticeship). Each
shaman develops a personal repertoire of icaros over years of training, and
specific songs may be associated with particular plants, spirits, or healing
purposes.

Icaros are not merely musical performances — they are understood by
practitioners as active technologies of consciousness. The sung melodies are
believed to directly influence the visionary experience of ceremony
participants, shaping the content, intensity, and emotional quality of the
ayahuasca journey. Some icaros are said to call protective spirits, others to
remove negative energies or illnesses, and still others to guide participants
through challenging psychological material.

## Musical Characteristics

Icaros vary widely in style but share some common characteristics:

- **Melodic structure:** Most icaros feature narrow melodic ranges with
  repetitive motifs that evolve gradually over the course of the song. The
  melodies often follow pentatonic or modal scales.
- **Rhythm and tempo:** Tempos range from slow, meditative drones to rapid,
  rhythmic chanting. The rhythm typically accelerates as the ceremony
  progresses and the effects of the ayahuasca intensify.
- **Vocal technique:** Singing styles include full-voice projection, whispering,
  falsetto, and harmonized overtones. Some shamans produce unusual vocal
  sounds including whistling, clicking, and guttural tones.
- **Instrumental accompaniment:** Many icaros are sung a cappella, but some
  shamans accompany themselves with simple instruments such as the shacapa
  (a bundle of dried leaves used as a rattle), small drums, or the
  chacapa (a leaf-bundle percussion instrument).

## Functional Roles in Ceremony

### Opening and Setting Intention

The ceremony typically begins with the shaman singing an opening icaro that
establishes the sacred space, calls protective spirits, and sets the
energetic tone for the session. This opening song creates a ceremonial
container and marks the transition from ordinary reality into the ritual
space.

### Guiding the Experience

As the ayahuasca takes effect, the shaman sings icaros that are believed to
influence the direction and quality of participants' visions. Different songs
may be used to navigate different phases of the experience — for example,
calming songs during periods of anxiety, energizing songs during periods of
fatigue, or clarifying songs when visions become confused.

### Healing and Cleansing

Specific icaros are directed at individual participants for healing purposes.
The shaman may sing an icaro while blowing tobacco smoke (mapacho) over a
participant or performing other physical interventions. These healing icaros
are believed to extract illness, negative energy, or spiritual blockages from
the participant's body and energy field.

### Protection

Protective icaros are sung to shield participants from malevolent spirits or
difficult energies that may arise during the ceremony. The shaman may also
sing protective icaros to create a boundary around the ceremonial space.

### Closing

The ceremony concludes with closing icaros that ground participants, express
gratitude to the plant spirits and protective entities, and mark the return
to ordinary consciousness.

## Observations from Tourist Ceremonies

In the context of [[stuart-r-ayahuasca-tourism-cautionary-ethnography]], the role and experience of icaros
changes in several ways. Tourists who do not speak Spanish or the indigenous
languages cannot understand the lyrics of icaros, which means they experience
the songs primarily as pure sound and melody. This can still be powerfully
affective — the music itself, regardless of linguistic comprehension, is a
major structuring element of the ceremony.

One observer noted hearing icaros being sung in the darkness from the home of
a practitioner known as "E" in [[shipibo-san-francisco-ceremonial-center-ucayali]] during private family ceremonies,
suggesting that the most authentic and deeply felt icaro performances may
occur in non-commercial contexts where the shaman is serving his own
community rather than foreign visitors.

In commercial tourist ceremonies, icaros may be performed in a more
standardized manner, optimized for the general expectations of a group of
strangers rather than tailored to individuals. However, the musical and
energetic impact of the icaros remains one of the most consistently praised
aspects of the ayahuasca ceremony experience, even among tourists who cannot
understand the words.

## The Dietas and Icaro Acquisition

Shamans typically acquire their icaros during dietas — extended periods of
isolation in the jungle during which they consume specific teacher plants
under the guidance of a master shaman. During these dietas, the apprentice
shaman receives songs directly from the plant spirits in visions. The number
and quality of a shaman's icaros is often considered a measure of their
power and expertise. A well-trained shaman may know hundreds of icaros, each
with specific applications.
