## Mushroom Fruiting Body Morphology

Mushroom fruiting bodies are the reproductive structures produced by
basidiomycete and some ascomycete fungi. Their primary function is to
produce and disperse spores, ensuring survival and spread across
landscapes. Morphology is enormously diverse, ranging from familiar
umbrella-shaped agarics to bracket fungi, puffballs, coral fungi, bird
[[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]] [[palmer-about-mushrooms-habitat-and-seasonal-guide]] patterns.

## Gills, Pores, and Spore-Bearing Surfaces

The spore-bearing surface (hymenophore) is located on the underside of
the cap and is one of the most important taxonomic features. In agarics,
it consists of gills (lamellae), thin blade-like structures radiating
from the stalk to the cap margin. Gill attachment to the stalk can be
free, adnexed, adnate, notched, or decurrent, each describing a
different connection mode that helps distinguish genera and species.

In boletes and polypores, the hymenophore consists of pores, where
spores are produced within tubes opening downward. Other hymenophore
types include teeth (hedgehog mushrooms), ridges (chanterelles), and
smooth surfaces (cup fungi). The evolution of different hymenophore
types represents remarkable convergent evolution in the fungal kingdom,
with similar structures evolving independently in distantly related
lineages facing similar dispersal challenges. See
[[bloomfield-buller-drop-surface-tension-spore-catapult-basidiospore-discharge]]
for the physics of spore release from these surfaces.

## The Stalk (Stipe)

The stalk, or stipe, supports the cap and elevates the spore-bearing
surface above the substrate for improved [[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]] by air
currents. Stalks may be central, eccentric, or lateral, and in some
species absent entirely, as in bracket fungi and some puffballs. The
surface texture can be smooth, fibrous, scaly, or powdery, and may bear
a ring (annulus) from the partial veil covering gills during
development. A volva at the base indicates a universal veil that
enclosed the entire immature mushroom, characteristic of Amanita and
Volvariella.

Internal stalk structure can be diagnostic: solid, stuffed with
cottony tissue, or hollow. Some species exude latex or other fluids when
the stalk is cut, providing additional identification characters.
Stalk thickness and length vary enormously depending on species and
growing conditions, with CO2 levels strongly influencing elongation.

## Development and Veil Structures

Many mushroom species develop through a button or egg stage where the
immature fruiting body is enclosed by protective tissue layers. As the
mushroom expands rapidly during fruiting, this protective tissue may be
left behind as a volva at the base, a ring on the stalk, or patches on
the cap surface. These veil remnants are critical for identifying many
species, particularly in Amanitaceae where confusion between edible and
deadly poisonous species can be fatal.
