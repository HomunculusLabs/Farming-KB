## Syncretic Entheogenic Churches

[[metzner-syncretic-folk-religious-hallucinogenic-ceremonies]] ceremonies as a third paradigm
for entheogenic use, distinct from both Western psychotherapy and classical
[[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]] the NAC enjoyed legal access to
entheogens in most Western states. NAC ceremonies follow a consistent format with
a "roadman" presiding, assisted by a drummer, firekeeper, and cedar-man for
purification.

Participants sit on the ground in a circle around a blazing central fire in a
tipi. The ceremony lasts all night, with almost constant singing of traditional
peyote songs involving a rapid rhythmic beat. There is virtually no discussion of
healing processes or visions during or after the ceremony, although individual
participants may have healing or visionary experiences. NAC ceremonies are legally
limited to persons with 25% or more Indian ancestry, though some ceremonial
leaders have opened their ceremonies to non-Indians.

The Huichol Indians of Northern Mexico follow a more traditional shamanic model,
involving careful and lengthy apprenticeships for curanderos and a group
pilgrimage to the sacred land of Wirikuta to find the rare peyote cactus. Their
ceremonies involve much singing and telling of creation myths and sacred stories.

## Brazilian Ayahuasca Churches

In Brazil, no less than three organized churches use ayahuasca as their main
sacrament: Santo Daime, Uniao de Vegetal (UDV), and Barquinha. Each was founded
by rubber tappers working in the Amazon region in the 1950s who came into contact
with the hallucinogenic vine through mestizo or Indian ayahuasceros.

The founder of each church reported a significant vision that instructed them to
organize a church using the brew as the principal sacrament. Members come from
all walks of life and both urban and rural environments in Brazil. Each church
has by now several thousand members in Brazil, and two have significant satellite
centers in [[metzner-peyote-ceremonies-native-american-church-and-huichol-traditions]] the hoasca churches tends to
reduce the incidence of alcoholism and drug addiction among members.

As noted by anthropologists, a further societal function is to provide a
protective shield of traditional lore against the encroachments of Christian
missionaries and the seductions of Western consumer culture. Metzner cited the
work of Michael Taussig and James Fernandez in documenting this protective
function, which operates not only at the individual level of addiction prevention
but at the cultural level of preserving indigenous identity and knowledge systems.

## Contrast with Shamanic Healing

Metzner drew a distinction between entheogen-based shamanic rituals and folk
[[entheogenic-folk-religious-ceremonies]], though he acknowledged this is "in some ways arbitrary"
and that there exists "a continuum of ritual forms and practices." The emphasis
in shamanic practices is [[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]], usually in small groups of around
a dozen, or sometimes with just one or two afflicted individuals. The folk
religious ceremonies involve larger groups, and the aspect of healing and
divination tends to recede in favor of group worship, singing, and prayer.

Instead of a shaman or healer, [[ayahuasca-churches-syncretic-ceremonies]] feature priests and
officiants. There is very little discussion or sharing of visions or insights,
in contrast to the integrative processing that follows shamanic healing sessions.
The community bonding function, while present in shamanic work, becomes the
primary purpose of the syncretic ceremony.
