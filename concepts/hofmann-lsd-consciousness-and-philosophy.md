## LSD, Consciousness, and Hofmann's Philosophical Views

## Childhood Visionary Experiences

Hofmann's philosophical orientation was shaped by childhood experiences of
visionary states. On a May morning during a walk on Martinsberg above Baden,
Switzerland, the young Hofmann experienced the forest transformed: "All at once
everything appeared in an uncommonly clear light. It shone with the most
beautiful radiance, speaking to the heart, as though it wanted to encompass me
in its majesty. I was filled with an indescribable sensation of joy, oneness,
and blissful security."
These experiences, which he could not communicate to adults, shaped the main
outlines of his worldview and convinced him of the existence of a miraculous,
powerful, unfathomable reality hidden from everyday sight. Unexpectedly, much
later in middle age, a link was established between his profession as a
research chemist and these visionary experiences from childhood, through the
discovery of LSD.

## The Problem of Reality

Of greatest significance to Hofmann was the insight attained as a fundamental
understanding from all his LSD experiments: what one commonly takes as "the
reality," including the reality of one's own individual person, is not
something fixed but rather something ambiguous. There is not only one, but many
realities, each comprising also a different consciousness of the ego.
This is not merely a philosophical proposition but an existential insight. The
first planned LSD experiment was deeply moving and alarming precisely because
everyday reality and the ego experiencing it, which Hofmann had considered the
only reality, dissolved, and an unfamiliar ego experienced another, unfamiliar
reality. The problem concerning the innermost self also appeared -- itself
unmoved, it was able to record these external and internal transformations.

## The Receiver Model of Consciousness

Hofmann developed a model of consciousness as a receiver. Reality is
inconceivable without an experiencing subject, without an ego. It is the
product of the exterior world (the sender) and a receiver (an ego in whose
deepest self the emanations of the exterior world, registered by the sense
organs, become conscious). If one of the two is lacking, no reality happens.
The entry of another reality under the influence of LSD may be explained by the
fact that the brain, the seat of the receiver, becomes biochemically altered.
The receiver is thereby tuned into another wavelength than that corresponding to
normal, everyday reality. Since the endless variety and diversity of the
universe correspond to infinitely many different wavelengths, many different
realities can become conscious. These different realities are not mutually
exclusive but are complementary, forming together a portion of the all-
encompassing, timeless, transcendental reality.
The true importance of LSD and related hallucinogens lies in their capacity to
shift the wavelength setting of the receiving self, and thereby to evoke
alterations in reality consciousness. This cosmogonic power makes the cultish
worship of hallucinogenic plants as sacred drugs understandable.

## Self and World: The Fundamental Distinction

The essential characteristic difference between everyday reality and the world
picture experienced in LSD inebriation concerns the relationship between ego
and the outer world. In normal consciousness, ego and the outer world are
separated; one stands face-to-face with the outer world, which has become an
object. In the LSD state, the boundaries between the experiencing self and the
outer world more or less disappear. Feedback between receiver and sender takes
place.
A portion of the self overflows into the outer world, into objects, which
begin to live, to have another, deeper meaning. In an auspicious case, the new
ego feels blissfully united with the objects of the outer world and
consequently with fellow beings. This experience of deep oneness can intensify
to a feeling of the self being one with the universe -- a condition of cosmic
consciousness analogous to spontaneous religious enlightenment, with the unio
mystica. In both conditions, a reality is experienced that exposes a gleam of
the transcendental reality, in which universe and self, sender and receiver,
are one.

## The Western Entelechy Neurosis

Hofmann drew on Gottfried Benn's essay "Provoziertes Leben" to characterize
the Western concept of reality that separates self and world as "the schizoid
catastrophe, the Western entelechy neurosis." The Hellenistic-European
principle of victory through effort, cunning, force, and later European
Darwinism produced an ego that analyzed matter, tested, sorted, and reduced it
to formulas. Matter became a concept that "hung like a disaster over the West."
