# Psilocybe cyanescens — Wavy-Capped Psilocybin Mushroom

*[[psilocybe-cyanescens]]* is a potent psilocybin-containing mushroom distinguished by its intense blue-staining reaction, preference for woody substrates, and characteristic wavy, undulating cap margins. Originally described from Kew Gardens, England in 1946, it has since been found across much of Europe, North America, and parts of North Africa, establishing itself as one of the most widely distributed psychoactive *Psilocybe* species outside of the tropics.

## Taxonomic Complex

*P. cyanescens* sits at the center of what Krieglsteiner called the **"Psilocybe [[guzman-allen-gartz-cyanescens-complex-coprinifacies-bohemica-european-taxonomy]]"** — a group of strongly bluing mushrooms that grow on raw compost and plant debris. Many names in the mycological literature are now considered synonyms:

- *Hypholoma cyanescens* R. Maire
- *Hypholoma coprinifacies* (Rolland ss. Herink) Pouzar
- *Geophila cyanescens* (R. Maire) Kühner & Romagnesi
- *[[psilocybe-serbica]]* Moser & Horak
- *[[psilocybe-mairei]]* Singer
- *[[psilocybe-bohemica]]* Sebek

Guzmán's geographic division of this complex was found to be inaccurate — specimens from North Africa (treated as *P. mairei*), England and Holland (*P. cyanescens*), and Serbia/Bohemia (*P. serbica*) likely all belong to a single widely distributed species. The species appears to cover a vast area with morphological variation along climate and terrain gradients, as expected for a "young" species that is still expanding into new locations.

The [[growing-gourmet-pleurotus-sajor-caju-taxonomic-confusion]] persists because original descriptions were based on isolated collections compared only with dried [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]], without DNA analysis or cross-breeding experiments with single-spore mycelia.

## Morphology

### Macroscopic Features

**Cap:** 5–40 mm broad. Conic at first with cortinate fibrils ascending steeply to the stem, fading quickly. Bell-shaped later, partially with an acute umbo. Expanding to plane with **undulating or wavy margins** — the namesake characteristic. No remnants of veil. Also broadly convex to umbonate in older specimens.

- **Fresh and moist:** Deep chestnut brown
- **Drying:** Fading to whitish, with stains of bluish to blue-green coloration

**Gills:** Attachment adnate to broadly subdecurrent. Light to dirty beige when young, changing to cinnamon-purple brownish as spores mature. Blue staining reaction is slight in response to pressure.

**Stem:** 30–85 mm long, uniformly thick at 1.5–3 mm. Stems and mycelial fibers **turn blue in response to touch**, even at very cool temperatures, if blue stains are not already present.

**Odor:** Somewhat like flour or potatoes.

**Spores:** 6–8 × 9–14 µm.

### Distinguishing Features

- **Intense blue staining:** Far more pronounced than *P. semilanceata*; blue staining develops rapidly on handling, even in cold conditions
- **Wavy cap margins:** A distinctive macroscopic character, especially in mature specimens
- **Woody substrate:** Grows on decaying wood, plant debris, and mulch — not on grassland or dung like *P. semilanceata*

## Discovery and Early History

### Kew Gardens, 1946

Wakefield described *P. cyanescens* from bluing, dark-spored mushrooms collected at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, England. The mushrooms were observed growing on small pieces of wood in forested areas during the fall season for several years. It was suggested that the species occurred **adventitiously** — that its spores had been imported from overseas together with other plant materials brought into the botanical gardens. This pattern of appearance in botanical gardens has been noted for other introduced mushroom species.

### European Expansion

Following the initial Kew discovery:

- **1972:** Fruiting bodies found in the Jura Mountains of Switzerland, growing gregariously on grass and decaying reeds
- **1972, 1984:** Collected on the Mediterranean island of Corsica
- **1975:** Discovered in Holland
- **1976:** Found in the Saarland region of Germany
- **Southern Black Forest:** Collected in 1959 and 1963
- **1979:** Vogtland area
- **1982:** Rheinland area
- **1982, 1983:** Near Hamburg and Bremen, Germany — particularly interesting because greenhouse operations in these areas may have facilitated introduction

## Global Distribution

*P. cyanescens* has been confirmed from:

- **Europe:** England, Holland, Switzerland, Austria, Germany, Corsica, and through the "cyanescens complex" likely Serbia, Bohemia, and North Africa
- **North America:** British Columbia, Northern California, Oregon, Washington — Guzmán considers [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] specimens identical to those from Kew Gardens
- **North Africa:** Previously treated as *P. mairei*

The species appears to be actively expanding its range, consistent with its status as a "young" species that has not yet fully established itself across all suitable habitats.

## Habitat and Ecology

Unlike *P. semilanceata*, which is a grassland/pasture species, *P. cyanescens* is a **lignicolous** (wood-inhabiting) mushroom:

- **Substrate:** Decaying wood chips, plant debris, raw compost, mulch, leaves, and twigs
- **Common locations:** Botanical gardens, landscaped areas with wood chip mulch, forest edges, gardens
- **Season:** Fall, typically October–November
- **Growth habit:** Gregarious, often in dense clusters on woody debris

The association with wood chips and garden mulch explains much of the species' recent expansion — the modern practice of using bark and wood chip mulch in landscaping has created extensive new habitat for this species in urban and suburban areas throughout Europe and North America.

## Possible Introduction

The theory that *P. cyanescens* was introduced to Europe from overseas (likely North America) via imported plant material is supported by:

1. Its sudden appearance at multiple botanical gardens (which regularly receive plant material from international sources)
2. Its absence from pre-20th-century European mycological records
3. The strong similarity between European and Pacific Northwest specimens
4. Its continued association with horticultural and landscaping activities

The species may have been a natural component of Pacific Northwest mycoflora that was inadvertently transported to European botanical gardens in soil or on plant roots during the 19th or early 20th century, subsequently establishing itself in the wild.

## Relationship to Other Species in the Complex

The "cyanescens complex" represents a taxonomically challenging group of closely related mushrooms. Key issues:

- **Microscopic overlap:** Spore sizes and microscopic characters are poorly delineated and often overlap between described species
- **Morphological variation:** Specimens from different locations show variation correlated with climate and terrain
- **DNA analysis needed:** Definitive resolution requires DNA sequencing and cross-breeding experiments with single-spore mycelia
- **Guzmán's geographic scheme rejected:** The attempt to divide the complex into separate species by geography (*P. mairei* in North Africa, *P. cyanescens* in England, *P. serbica* in Serbia) has not held up to scrutiny

The current consensus, as presented by Gartz and Krieglsteiner, is that these represent a single widespread species with ecotypic variation rather than distinct species.
