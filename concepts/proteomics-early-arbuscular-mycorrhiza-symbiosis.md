# Proteomics of Early Arbuscular Mycorrhiza Symbiosis

## Overview

Proteomic analysis of the early stages of arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM)
symbiosis reveals the protein-level changes that accompany fungal
recognition, root colonization, and the establishment of the symbiotic
interface. Using two-dimensional gel electrophoresis and mass
spectrometry, researchers have identified differentially expressed
proteins involved in signal transduction, defense responses, primary
metabolism, and symbiosis-related functions in Medicago truncatula roots
during early colonization by [[glomus-mosseae]]. These findings complement
transcriptomic data and reveal important discrepancies between mRNA
levels and actual protein accumulation.

## Methodological Approach

The study of AM symbiosis proteomics involves simultaneous extraction
of mRNA and proteins from mycorrhizal root tissues. Proteins are
separated by two-dimensional electrophoresis using immobilized pH
gradient strips for the first dimension and SDS-PAGE for the second.
Protein spots of interest are excised from Coomassie Blue-stained gels,
subjected to trypsin digestion, and identified by peptide mass
fingerprinting using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. Both blind
proteomic analysis (examining all differentially displayed spots) and
targeted analysis (examining spots predicted from transcriptomic data)
have been employed to characterize the proteomic response to AMF
colonization.

## Defense-Related Proteins

Several defense-related proteins are differentially expressed during
early AM colonization. Peroxidase and peroxidase precursor proteins
increase in response to G. mosseae inoculation. Peroxidase activities
have been reported in response to both ectomycorrhizal and endomycorrhizal
symbioses. Chalcone reductase, an enzyme in the phenylpropanoid
pathway, is up-regulated and responds to early events of elicitor-mediated
signaling. Two glutathione-S-transferase (GST) proteins are identified,
one accumulating and one decreasing. GST accumulation at very early
stages, prior to fungal colonization, suggests a priming effect. GST
expression may be part of plant defense or involved in arbuscule
degradation at later stages.

## Signal Transduction Proteins

Multiple signal transduction proteins are modulated during the early
stages of AM symbiosis. Serine/threonine kinase and guanine nucleotide
binding proteins (GTP-binding proteins) increase in abundance. These
proteins are anchored on the cytoplasmic cell membrane and act as
mediators in cellular processes including signal transduction, protein
transport, and growth regulation. MAP kinases have been reported as
up-regulated during early root colonization, consistent with the
activation of signaling cascades that precede fungal entry into root
tissues.

## Primary Metabolism Proteins

Changes in primary metabolism are reflected in the accumulation of
alanine aminotransferase, an enzyme involved in nitrogen metabolism.
This finding indicates that metabolic reprogramming accompanies the
initial stages of symbiotic interaction, potentially redirecting carbon
and nitrogen flow to support the developing fungal partner. The
modulation of primary metabolic enzymes at the protein level during
early colonization suggests that the plant begins preparing its
metabolic infrastructure before substantial fungal biomass has
accumulated within root tissues.

## Discrepancy Between Transcriptomics and Proteomics

A notable finding from combined transcriptomic and proteomic studies
is the limited correspondence between mRNA and protein levels during
early AM symbiosis. While in silico prediction of transcripts identified
13 proteins potentially detectable on 2-D gels, only a few results were
common at both levels. This discrepancy can arise from several factors.
Spot overlapping on 2-D gels may mask the protein of interest. Proteomic
approaches without sub-cellular purification detect only major proteins.
Incorrect assignment of start sites, post-translational modifications,
and protein breakdown can all affect the relationship between
transcript abundance and detectable protein product.

## The Transient Defense Response

The defense response during AM symbiosis is characteristically weak and
transient compared to pathogenic interactions. Events including signal
perception, signal transduction, and defense gene activation occur, but
the response does not reach the intensity seen during pathogen attack.
This attenuated defense response may reflect the low capacity of AMF to
trigger strong defense reactions, or the activation of a plant mechanism
that actively suppresses defense responses once the symbiotic nature of
the interaction is recognized. The transient character allows initial
defense activation that may serve a surveillance function, followed by
suppression that permits fungal accommodation within root tissues.

## Auxin-Inducible GST and Developmental Regulation

Auxin-inducible GSTs show time-dependent increases in transcript levels
in M. truncatula roots upon inoculation with G. mosseae. This finding
links hormonal signaling to the defense response during mycorrhizal
colonization. Auxin-inducible GSTs may be developmentally regulated
when roots are colonized by AM fungi, suggesting cross-talk between the
hormonal pathways that regulate root development and the defense
pathways that respond to fungal presence. The Tau class GST that was
down-regulated during early AM colonization has also been reported as
down-regulated in response to G. intraradices in both wild-type and
hyper-mycorrhizal mutant genotypes.

## Future Directions in Sub-Cellular Proteomics

To obtain mycorrhiza-related protein identification at higher throughput,
more precise definition of both the mycorrhizal stage under study and
the particular cell compartment is needed. Comparative sub-cellular
proteomics directed towards microsomes and plasmalemma fractions have
proven useful for identifying proteins not previously reported in AM
symbiosis. Further exploration of cell wall, mitochondrial, and
vacuolar compartments promises to reveal additional proteins involved in
recognition events, nutrient exchange, and signaling during the
establishment of the arbuscular [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]].
