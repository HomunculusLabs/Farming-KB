# Brassinosteroid-Suppressed Annulus Formation in [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]] of *P. cubensis* and related species.

## What Is the Annulus?

The annulus is a ring-like structure on the stipe (stem) of many agaric mushrooms. It forms when the partial veil — a sheet of tissue extending from the cap margin to the stem — tears as the cap expands during fruit body maturation. The torn veil remains attached to the stem as a ring. In *Psilocybe cubensis*, the annulus is typically:

- **Membranous:** Relatively thick and substantial, not merely a fragile thread or cobweb-like structure.
- **Persistent:** It remains attached to the stem after the mushroom has fully matured and been picked, rather than disappearing quickly.
- **Positioned near the apex:** Located in the upper portion of the stem, typically 1–3 cm below the cap.
- **Dual-layered in some strains:** Often with an outer (exterior) surface and an inner (superior) surface of different texture, the inner surface sometimes bearing fine radial striations.
- **Colour:** Usually whitish to slightly yellowish, sometimes darkening with age or with spore deposition. The annulus often has a darker-coloured zone where spores have fallen onto the superior (inner) surface during cap expansion.

In dried herbarium specimens, the annulus is typically preserved as a thin, papery ring on the stem, though it may become brittle and fragment with handling. Its presence or absence is routinely noted in taxonomic descriptions and is one of the first features checked during macroscopic identification.

The annulus is listed as a diagnostic macroscopic feature of *Psilocybe cubensis* in virtually all taxonomic keys and field guides for the species. Its absence in normally-developed fruit bodies would be unusual and might lead to misidentification or confusion with other *Psilocybe* species that naturally lack an annulus (such as *P. semilanceata*, which has no partial veil). Some field guides even include the phrase "with a persistent membranous annulus" as part of the formal species description for *P. cubensis*.

## The Brassinosteroid Effect

In the Gartz study, brassinosteroid-treated cultures produced fruit bodies in which the annulus was **absent or severely reduced**. This suggests that the brassinosteroid (22S,23S-homobrassinolide at 10⁻² ppm) interfered with the normal development of the partial veil tissue. There are several possible mechanisms:

### 1. Altered Veil Development

The partial veil may develop incompletely or with altered structural integrity. If the veil tissue is thinner or more fragile due to the brassinosteroid's influence on hyphal differentiation, it may tear earlier in development or may not form a substantial enough structure to leave a visible remnant on the stem.

### 2. Accelerated Cap Expansion

The brassinosteroid-treated mushrooms grew faster overall (first fruit appeared 2–3 weeks earlier than controls). If cap expansion is accelerated relative to veil development, the veil may be torn and shed before it has fully differentiated into the thick, membranous tissue that normally persists as the annulus. The timing of cap expansion relative to veil maturation may be critical.

### 3. Direct Interference with Veil Differentiation

Brassinosteroids [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] involves a complex hormonal and genetic programme. Exogenous brassinosteroids may interact with endogenous fungal hormones (such as the "mor phogen" or "fruiting hormone" proposed by various researchers) in ways that alter the developmental programme, with the annulus being a particularly sensitive readout of this interaction. In *Coprinopsis cinerea* and *Schizophyllum commune*, genetic studies have identified multiple genes involved in fruiting body development, including those specifically regulating cap expansion, stipe elongation, and veil formation. Brassinosteroids may modulate the expression or activity of analogous genes in *P. cubensis*.

### 5. Tissue-Specific Sensitivity

It is possible that the cells of the partial veil are particularly sensitive to brassinosteroid signalling, while the cells of the cap, stipe, and gills are less affected. This tissue-specific sensitivity would explain why the annulus is suppressed while other morphological features appear normal. Differential sensitivity to hormones across tissues is a well-documented phenomenon in both plants and animals and could extend to fungi as well.

## Could the Annulus Effect Be Exploited?

From a cultivation perspective, the annulus is not functionally important — it is the remnant of a developmental structure that serves no purpose in the mature mushroom. Its absence does not affect spore dispersal, nutritional value, or alkaloid content. However, the observation that brassinosteroids can selectively alter one morphological feature while leaving others intact suggests that these compounds (or similar plant hormones) could potentially be used to modify other, more commercially relevant mushroom characteristics, such as:

- **Stem thickness and cap size ratio** (affecting yield and appearance)
- **Flesh density and water content** (affecting shelf life and drying characteristics)
- **Spore production** (reducing spore load for cleaner indoor environments)
- **Resistance to veiling or aborting** (increasing harvest reliability)

The absence of the annulus has significant implications for the identification of the cultivated species. If a cultivator were to encounter brassinosteroid-treated *P. cubensis* without knowledge of the treatment, the lack of the annulus — a primary diagnostic character — could lead to:

- **Misidentification** as a species that naturally lacks an annulus (e.g., *P. semilanceata*, *P. cyanescens* var. *cyanescens*).
- **Confusion with other genera** that have annulus-less species. For example, some *Gymnopilus* or *Inocybe* species lack an annulus and could potentially be confused with an exannulate *P. cubensis* by inexperienced identifiers, though microscopic features would readily distinguish them.
- **Taxonomic misplacement** in photographic or herbarium documentation. Museum specimens or photographs labelled as one species based on macroscopic features may be incorrect if environmental or chemical factors have altered key characters.

This underscores the general principle that cultivated mushrooms may exhibit morphological features that differ from their wild counterparts due to [[fukuoka-textdoc-sawdust-wood-chips-orchard-soil-acidification-experiment]] nterparts. These differences arise from several factors:

- **Substrate effects:** Different substrates can produce markedly different fruit body morphology. Mushrooms grown on straw, wood chips, compost, or grain all have characteristic appearances even within the same species.
- **Environmental conditions:** Temperature, humidity, light intensity, and CO₂ levels all influence morphology. High CO₂ produces elongated stems and small caps; low CO₂ produces the opposite pattern.
- **Strain variation:** Selective breeding and strain isolation have produced cultivated strains with distinct morphological features that may differ significantly from the "wild type" description.
- **Chemical treatments:** As demonstrated by the brassinosteroid study, exogenous chemicals can modify developmental outcomes in ways that may affect taxonomically relevant characters.

Cultivators, taxonomists, and forensic analysts should be aware of these sources of morphological variation and avoid relying solely on macroscopic features for species identification, particularly with cultivated material.

## Natural Annulus Variation Across Psilocybe Species

Annulus morphology varies naturally across *Psilocybe* species and even between strains within a single species:

- ***P. cubensis*:** Well-developed membranous annulus (in most strains). One of the most reliable macroscopic identification features for this species.
- ***P. semilanceata*:** No annulus — the partial veil is absent or extremely rudimentary. The stem is bare and smooth throughout its length, which is a key diagnostic feature distinguishing it from other *Psilocybe* species.
- ***P. cyanescens*:** Annulus present but often evanescent (disappearing quickly) and fragile. It may be reduced to a thin fibrillose zone or may disappear entirely by maturity.
- ***P. azurescens*:** Annulus persistent and substantial, similar to *P. cubensis*. The annulus is often thick and double-layered.
- ***P. tampanensis*:** Annulus present but sometimes fragile and easily dislodged. May disappear during handling or drying.
- ***P. bohemica* (syn. *P. serbica*):** Annulus present but variable in prominence. Some collections show a well-developed ring while others show only a faint zone.
- ***P. subaeruginosa*:** Annulus usually present, membranous to submembranous, and persistent.

The brassinosteroid-induced annulus absence in *P. cubensis* is distinct from this natural variation because it represents an environmentally induced modification of a normally consistent morphological feature, rather than a stable species-level or strain-level character. In the brassinosteroid case, the same strain of *P. cubensis* produced both annulate (normal) and exannulate (treated) fruit bodies depending on whether brassinosteroid was present in the substrate, demonstrating the plasticity of this character.
