# Hurricane and Cyclone Resilient Design

Bill Mollison addresses hurricanes and cyclones as major landscape forces
that demand specific design responses. In cyclonic or hurricane areas,
catastrophic winds may become the overriding design modification around
which all other factors must be arrayed. This page covers the behavior
of tropical cyclones and the permaculture strategies for designing
resilient settlements and landscapes.

## Understanding Hurricanes and Cyclones

Hurricanes are large, slow phenomena covering hundreds of square miles,
mainly confined to coasts facing large stretches of tropical seas with
very large heat cells. They develop as great rising spirals driven by
oceanic warmth. Although hurricanes cannot persist far inland, they can
push storm surges and flooding well into coastal interiors.

Modern satellite photographs are used to track hurricanes, and there is
usually a few days' warning for coastal areas. Evacuation is sometimes
ordered. Well-built towns (such as Darwin, Australia, after its cyclonic
devastation in 1972) can withstand cyclones with minimal damage, but
such stoutness is usually only built in after an initial and sometimes
total destruction.

## Design Strategies for Cyclonic Regions

### Building and Shelter Design

It is possible to strictly regulate and supervise buildings to be safe
in hurricanes. In areas where flimsy constructions are normal, designers
should plan refuge trenches and caves for emergency shelter. All such
shelter must be in well-drained hillside sites, never in flood-prone
lowlands.

For hurricane areas, a strong central core or refuge structure should be
desetermined as the first priority. Ground-level flooding due to storm
surge in hurricanes must be accounted for -- structures should be either
elevated or sited well above potential surge lines.

### Landscape and Vegetation Design

In cyclonic regions, the chill factor from wind removal of heat from
surfaces and evaporation of fluids retards plant growth and lowers the
efficiency of solar devices and insulation. Therefore, shelter strategies
become paramount:

- Dense, wind-resistant tree belts should surround settlements and
  gardens, as described in [[mollison-windbreak-design]].
- Species with fibrous stems (palms), fleshy leaves, hard needles, or
  waxy coatings resist wind damage best.
- Trees on the windward side will be heavily wind-flagged; taller
  species should be placed in their lee.
- On coasts subject to cyclones, it may not be until after the fourth
  or even fifth tree row that wind-prone fruit or nut trees will yield.

### Site Selection

Sites should be sheltered from hurricanes, tsunami, and storm surge. Key
considerations include:

- Elevation above storm surge lines.
- Natural wind barriers such as ridges or existing forest.
- Well-drained soils to prevent flooding during intense rainfall.
- Proximity to sturdy refuge structures.

## Tornadoes

Tornadoes, while distinct from hurricanes, are another severe wind
phenomenon addressed by Mollison. Unlike hurricanes, tornadoes occur
over land and sea from shear effects at the junction of hot and cold
air masses, creating intense low-pressure vortices. They may occur in
quite cold inland areas, last only seconds or minutes, and affect only
a few square kilometres.

Wind speeds may reach 120 km/h, at worst 280 km/h, exceeding hurricane
winds. The conditions for tornadoes include:

- Thunderstorms with fast-growing cumulonimbus clouds
- A persistent source of warm moist air feeding the updraught side
- Cold dry air entering from another direction
- Vortex formation from wind-shear effects at the border of the
  conflicting system

## Key Principles

1. In cyclonic areas, wind protection overrides all other design factors.
2. Design refuge structures as the first priority in hurricane zones.
3. Site settlements above storm surge lines on well-drained hillside
  locations.
4. Use multiple rows of wind-resistant species for gradual wind
  reduction.
5. Plan for evacuation routes and emergency shelter access.
6. Build stoutly from the outset rather than waiting for destruction to
  teach the lesson.
