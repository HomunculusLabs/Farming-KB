# pH and Nutrient Availability in Garden Soils

Updated: 2026-04-18

Soil pH -- the measure of hydrogen ion concentration on a logarithmic scale from 0 (most acidic) to 14 (most alkaline) -- is arguably the single most important chemical property of soil. It determines whether nutrients are available for plant uptake and which organisms dominate the [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]]. The pH scale is logarithmic: a soil at pH 6 has ten times more hydrogen ions than one at pH 7.

## The pH-Phosphorus Connection

Phosphorus availability is dramatically affected by pH:

- **Below pH 6**: Phosphorus reacts with iron and aluminum, forming insoluble compounds that plants cannot absorb. This is the dominant P-lockup mechanism in acidic soils.
- **Above pH 7**: Phosphorus reacts with calcium, forming calcium phosphate compounds that are similarly unavailable. This is the dominant P-lockup mechanism in alkaline soils.
- **Optimal range**: pH 6.0-7.0 provides the best phosphorus availability for most garden plants. At this range, P remains soluble and plant-accessible.

This is why liming acid soils can dramatically improve phosphorus availability -- it reduces the iron-aluminum bonds holding phosphorus. Conversely, in alkaline soils, sulfur can be added to lower pH and release phosphorus.

## pH and Nitrogen Form

Soil pH strongly influences which form of nitrogen dominates, and this in turn affects the microbial community:

- **Ammonium (NH4+)** dominates in **acidic soils** (pH below 6). This favors [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]] -- mycorrhizal fungi prefer ammonium nitrogen. Coniferous forests naturally create acidic, fungal soils.
- **Nitrate (NO3-)** dominates in **alkaline soils** (pH above 7). This favors [[ingham-cover-crops-soil-biology]] -- nitrifying bacteria prefer higher pH conditions. Agricultural soils are often managed toward bacterial dominance.
- **Nitrifying bacteria** (Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter) are most active at pH 7-8. Below pH 6, their activity drops sharply, causing ammonium to accumulate rather than converting to nitrate.

At pH 8+, ammonium (NH4+) can volatilize as ammonia gas (NH3), representing a significant nitrogen loss in alkaline soils.

## pH and Microbial Communities

- **Fungi dominate in acidic conditions** (pH 4-6): Acidic soils favor mycorrhizal associations and fungal decomposition pathways. This is why forest soils tend to be fungal.
- **Bacteria dominate in neutral to alkaline conditions** (pH 7-8): Most beneficial bacteria prefer neutral to slightly alkaline conditions.
- **Earthworms** prefer neutral to slightly alkaline soils (pH 6.5-7.5).

## pH and Micronutrient Availability

Most micronutrients become increasingly available as pH decreases, but there is a danger zone:

- **Iron (Fe), Manganese (Mn), Zinc (Zn), Copper (Cu), Boron (B)**: All more available at lower pH. Deficiencies of these elements are very common in alkaline soils (pH above 7) where they become locked up.
- **Chelation**: In acidic soils, organic molecules (chelators) bind to iron and other metals, keeping them soluble and plant-available despite the acidic conditions. This natural chelation is critical for micronutrient uptake.
- **Toxicity risk**: Below pH 5, aluminum and manganese can reach toxic levels that damage plant roots.

## pH and Macronutrient Availability

| Nutrient | Most Available At | Notes |
|----------|------------------|-------|
| Nitrogen | 6.5-7.5 | As nitrate; ammonium preferred below 6 |
| Phosphorus | 6.0-7.0 | Locked up below 6 (Fe/Al) and above 7 (Ca) |
| Potassium | 6.0-7.5 | Widely available across pH range |
| Calcium | 7.0-8.5 | Deficient in acid soils |
| Magnesium | 7.0-8.5 | Deficient in acid soils |
| Sulfur | 6.0-7.0 | Available as sulfate across range |

## Managing pH: Liming Materials

### Calcitic Limestone (CaCO3)
- Primary calcium source; raises pH
- Apply in autumn for best results (winter freezing/thawing helps incorporation)
- Maximum effect: raise pH about 1 point per season
- Use when soil needs calcium but not extra magnesium

### Dolomitic Limestone (CaMg(CO3)2)
- Contains both calcium (~20% Ca) and magnesium (~10% Mg)
- Raises pH while adding magnesium
- **Caution**: Too much magnesium binds soil particles and reduces permeability; impacts uptake of other nutrients
- Use only when both calcium AND magnesium are needed

### Other Liming Materials
- **Wood ashes**: Fast-acting liming agent; contains K (0-1-3); raises pH quickly; use sparingly
- **Oyster shell flour**: Slow-release calcium source; gentle pH adjustment

## Managing pH: Acidifying Materials

- **Elemental sulfur**: Converted to sulfuric acid by soil bacteria (Thiobacillus); slow but effective
- **Aluminum sulfate**: Fast-acting but can cause aluminum toxicity; avoid in organic systems
- **Sulfate of potash-magnesia (Sul-Po-Mag)**: Provides K, Mg, and S with slight acidifying effect
- **Pine needles and peat moss**: Mild, slow acidification as they decompose

## Optimal pH Ranges for Common Garden Plants

| Plant Type | Optimal pH Range |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Most vegetables | 6.0-7.0 |
| Potatoes | 4.8-5.5 |
| Blueberries | 4.5-5.5 |
| Brassicas | 6.5-7.5 |
| Legumes (with rhizobia) | 6.0-7.5 |
| Asparagus | 7.0-8.0 |
| Tomatoes | 6.0-6.8 |
| Lawn grasses | 6.0-7.0 |

## Key Principle: Test Before Adjusting

Always [[solomon-soil-testing-and-interpretation-low-input]] before applying amendments. Over-liming is as damaging as having acidic soil. pH adjustments should be gradual -- never try to change pH by more than 1 point in a single season.
