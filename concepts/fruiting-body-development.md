# Fruiting Body Development

The transition from vegetative mushroom-life-cycle-stamets. Called pinning, pin setting,
pinhead initiation, or fructification, this process involves the formation of
primordia, which are knots of mycelium that precede development into small
mushrooms. Understanding the factors that regulate this change enables
cultivators to reliably trigger and control fruiting.

## Environmental Triggers for Fruiting

In nature, [[growing-gourmet-primordia-formation-pinning]] is primarily influenced by seasonal changes in
environmental conditions. In temperate climates, most mushrooms fruit during
the cool, wet autumn, whereas in tropical and subtropical climates, fruiting
occurs during the rainy season. The fruiting period ends when conditions
become too hot, too cold, or too dry. The mycelium then lies dormant or grows
slowly, reactivated by warming temperatures, during which it expands its
network, absorbs nutrients, and rebuilds energy reserves.

Mushrooms fruit indoors in response to much the same conditions that trigger
fruiting in the wild. Several [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]], working in combination,
provide an ideal environment. Most [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]] fruit at lower
temperatures than the optimum for [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]]. A drop in temperature is
usually accompanied by increased humidity and fresh air exchange.

## Key Fruiting Parameters

Given sufficient substrate nutrients, the interaction of water, humidity,
temperature, fresh air, carbon dioxide, and light all play determining roles
in the fructification process. In some cases, specific microorganisms must
also be present before fruiting can occur. The modification of any one of
these factors beyond the fruiting requirements can inhibit or stop the
process.

### Temperature

The first step in pinhead initiation is to lower substrate and air
temperature from the mycelial growth optimum to the fruiting range. This
temperature shock is accomplished by ventilation with cool fresh air, thereby
slowing [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] and signaling to the mycelium that it is time to
fruit. Within 48 hours, substrate temperature should fall to fruiting
temperatures. Research by Lambert (1938) showed that a pinhead of Agaricus
brunnescens with a diameter of 2 millimeters fully develops into a mature
mushroom in twenty-two days at 50 degrees F, in ten days at 60 degrees F, and
in six days at 70 degrees F, though overall yields diminish at higher
temperatures.

### Humidity

Water is essential for nutrient absorption by the mycelium, and vaporous
water creates the humid microclimate critical for developing primordia. During
casing colonization, the relative humidity of the growing room should be
maintained at 95%. After pinheads reach pea size (3 to 5 mm), humidity is
lowered to 85 to 92%, increasing the evaporation rate, which is an essential
requirement for pinhead maturation.

### Carbon Dioxide

Primordia have a low tolerance to carbon dioxide and need ample fresh air.
For Agaricus brunnescens, pinning does not proceed properly at CO2
concentrations above 2000 ppm. Lowering CO2 to below 2000 ppm is critical.
Once pinning has begun, CO2 levels can be raised slightly to 1500 to 2000
ppm for pinhead development. If CO2 remains too high, the mycelium totally
covers the casing surface, a condition called overlay, which makes the casing
impervious to water and produces few pinheads.

### Light

While mycelium has no requirement for light, many species need light to
initiate pinheads and to mature into healthy mushrooms. A 12-hour on/off
light cycle is standard for most species. Blue spectrum light (natural
daylight or fluorescent) is particularly effective for promoting primordia
formation in many cultivated species.
