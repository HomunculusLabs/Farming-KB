Coleman uses flat-topped wire wickets placed every 4 feet
(120 cm) along the length of each 30-inch bed.

The wickets hold the floating row cover about 12 inches
(30 cm) above the soil surface.

This spacing is close enough to prevent the row cover from
sagging onto the plants during rain or snow loads.

The wire wickets are simple, inexpensive, and reusable year after year, made from 9-gauge wire cut to length and bent into arches. The row cover is laid over the wickets and the edges are buried in the soil on both sides to seal the protected microclimate.

## Space Utilization Strategy

Coleman aims to never leave a greenhouse bed unplanted. As soon as one crop is harvested, residues are removed, the bed is re-prepared, and the next crop is planted. This continuous occupation maximizes return on the fixed greenhouse investment. The succession schedule requires careful planning — at any given time during winter, a single greenhouse may contain crops at three or four different stages of growth simultaneously.

## Practical Considerations

Path surfaces should be covered with wood chips or gravel to
prevent mud and allow access even during wet conditions.

Tools and supplies should be stored in an organized manner at
the end of each house to minimize time spent searching for
equipment during cold weather.

Harvest containers should be staged at the end of beds before
beginning work, so that cut crops can be moved quickly to the
washing area without exposure to cold air.

A small bench or stool at each work station reduces fatigue
during planting, weeding, and harvesting tasks that require
extended periods of bending or kneeling.

## End-Wall and Door Design

The end walls of Coleman's hoop houses serve multiple
functions beyond simply enclosing the structure.

They provide access points for workers, equipment, and
ventilation. A standard design includes a large sliding or
hinged door on one or both ends, plus smaller personnel doors.

In winter, the end walls also serve as the primary location
for temperature monitoring equipment.

Thermometers placed at crop height inside the house provide
the data needed to assess whether interior conditions are
within acceptable ranges for the crops being grown.
