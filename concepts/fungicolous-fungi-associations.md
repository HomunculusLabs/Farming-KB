# Fungicolous Fungi Associations

[[fungicolous-fungi]] are fungi that grow on other fungi, encompassing a diverse
spectrum of interspecific interactions. The term includes [[lichenicolous-fungi]]
as well as fungi inhabiting sporocarps, sclerotia, spores, mycelia, and
oospores of non-lichenized fungi. This guild plays important roles in fungal
community structure, nutrient cycling, and natural biocontrol.

## Diversity Estimates

The true diversity of fungicolous fungi is difficult to quantify. Rudakov (1978)
catalogued ~1,700 nonlichenicolous species. Hawksworth (1979, 1981) identified
~1,100 conidial species on ~2,500 host fungi including lichens. Separately,
~1,500 lichenicolous species have been described, many highly host-specific.
These figures are likely significant underestimates.

## Types of Fungicolous Associations

Relationships range across the full symbiotic spectrum:
- **Neutralistic:** No visible effect on host; the fungus merely uses the host
  as a physical substrate.
- **Mutualistic:** Both partners benefit; relatively rare among fungicolous fungi.
- **Antagonistic/competitive:** The fungicolous fungus harms or outcompetes the
  host through parasitism, antibiosis, or resource competition.

## Biotrophic vs. Necrotrophic Mycoparasitism

**Biotrophic mycoparasitism:** The parasite derives nutrition from living host
cells without causing rapid cell death. The host cytoplasm remains viable and
the interaction is often highly specific. Biotrophs have not been demonstrated
to produce extracellular toxins or lytic enzymes; nutrient acquisition relies
on direct cytoplasmic connections.

**Necrotrophic mycoparasitism:** The parasite kills host cells before or during
colonization, absorbing nutrients from dead tissue. Necrotrophs release toxins
and lytic enzymes — particularly chitinases — into the environment. Examples:
*Trichoderma* spp., *Talaromyces flavus*, *Schizophyllum commune*.

**Intermediate forms:** The binary distinction is an oversimplification. A
semi-biotrophic mode (biotrophic organisms later becoming necrotrophic or
saprotrophic) may be more primitive than strict biotrophy or saprotrophy
(Cooke and Whipps 1980). Trophic relationships can be host-dependent: the same
species may act as a biotroph on one host and a necrotroph on another.

## Rudakov's Six Groups

Rudakov (1978) classified fungicolous fungi into six trophic groups:
1. **Biotrophs:** Obligate parasites growing only on living host tissue.
2. **Facultative biotrophs:** Primarily biotrophic but capable of limited growth
   on dead or moribund host tissue.
3. **Necrotrophs:** Kill host cells before or during colonization.
4. **Facultative necrotrophs:** Primarily necrotrophic but can establish
   biotrophic-like relationships under certain conditions.
5. **Semisaprophytic mycophiles:** Colonize senescing or partially dead fungal
   structures, positioned between parasites and free-living saprobes.
6. **Saprophytic associates:** Found consistently on fungal substrata but
   primarily exploiting dead or dying tissue.

## Hyphal Interference

Hyphal interference is a phenomenon where one fungus causes degenerative changes
in another upon close approach, without penetration. Affected host hyphae
exhibit **vacuolation** (excessive vacuole formation), **lipid droplet
accumulation** (metabolic disruption), and **plasmalemma invagination** (cell
membrane folding signaling stress). These diffuse changes represent non-contact
antagonism mediated by diffusible metabolites.

## Commensalism and Hyperparasitism

**Commensalism:** The fungicolous fungus benefits (shelter or substrate) while
the host is unaffected; many saprophytic associates fall in this category.
**Hyperparasitism:** A fungicolous fungus parasitizes another fungicolous
fungi — creating multilayered interaction networks.

## Mycoparasite-Host Interface Types (Jeffries and Young 1994)

### Necrotrophic Interfaces (host cytoplasm degenerates)
1. **Contact necrotrophic:** No penetration; damage via hyphal interference.
   Examples: *Arthrobotrys superba*, *Tilletiopsis* spp. on powdery mildew.
2. **Invasive necrotrophic:** Hyphae penetrate the host cell wall and enter the
   cell with considerable intracellular growth. Examples: *Trichoderma* spp.,
   *Talaromyces flavus*, *Schizophyllum commune*.

### Biotrophic Interfaces (host cytoplasm remains healthy)
3. **Haustorial:** A short haustorial branch penetrates the host hypha. Common
   among Zygomycota and Tremellales; *Sporidesmium sclerotivorum* on
   *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*.
4. **Fusion:** Micropores develop in walls of host and parasite hyphae at
   contact points, allowing cytoplasmic exchange. Examples: *Gonatobotrys
   simplex*, *Hansfordia parasitica*, *Melanospora zamiae*.
5. **Intracellular:** The complete mycoparasite thallus enters a host hypha.
   Common among chytrids and some oomycetes.

## Host Defense Reactions

Host fungi exhibit defenses including **callosities** (localized wall thickenings
at penetration points), general **wall thickenings**, and **colacosomes/
lenticular bodies** (specialized surface structures, Swart 1975). Lipid
droplets and plasmalemma invaginations are also stress responses documented in
*Coprinus heptemerus*/*Ascobolus crenulatus* and *Phlebiopsis gigantea*/*
Heterobasidion annosum* interactions (Ikediugwu 1976a, b).

## Determining the Mode of Interaction

Assessing whether an association is biotrophic, necrotrophic, or commensalistic
requires multiple microscopy approaches:
- **Water mounts** with cotton blue or lactic acid for basic observation.
- **Phloxine staining** differentiates living (unstained) from dead (stained) cells.
- **Fluorescence microscopy** with immunolabeled dyes visualizes specific
  components at the interface.
- **Transmission electron microscopy** reveals ultrastructural details of
  penetration, haustorial structure, and organelle integrity.
- **Infrared photomicrography** detects enzymatic activity sites.
- **Radiolabeled compounds** (e.g., ¹⁴C-glucose) trace nutrient flow.

## Physiological Mechanisms of Mycoparasitism

- **Toxins:** Necrotrophs release low-molecular-weight metabolites that damage
  host membranes.
- **Lytic enzymes:** Chitinases, glucanases, and proteases degrade host cell
  walls; chitinase is particularly critical.
- **Antibiotic metabolites:** Production is medium-dependent. Efficient
  mycoparasites (*Acremonium strictum*, *Clonostachys rosea*, *Trichothecium
  roseum*, *Sistotrema brinkmannii*) overgrow other fungi without inhibition.
- **Volatile metabolites:** *Trichoderma* spp. mediate interactions via both
  volatile and nonvolatile secondary metabolites.

## Gene Transfer Between Parasite and Host

Horizontal gene transfer from host to parasite has been demonstrated in the
mucoralean association between *Absidia glauca* and *Parasitella parasitica*
(Kellner et al. 1993; Wöstemeyer et al. 1995). The prototrophic parasite
compensates for artificially induced nutritional deficiencies in the host,
suggesting mycoparasitic interactions can involve genetic exchange with
significant evolutionary consequences.

## Host Range and Evolution

Most mycoparasites are unspecialized, infecting diverse hosts; some are also
entomogenous or nematophagous. Lichenicolous fungi are generally highly
specialized. The fungicolous habit is common in Chytridiomycota, Oomycota,
Hyphochytridiomycota, and Tremellales. Obligate parasitism may be a primitive
trait from which saprotrophic subgroups arose repeatedly; specific
parasite-host associations are products of long coevolution.
