## Sequestrate Fungi — Survey Methods, Sampling Protocols, and Community Analysis

## Abundance and Diversity Measures (Continued)

### Relative Importance and Abundance

**Sporocarp biomass as a direct measure.** Sporocarp biomass provides a direct indication of fungal productivity and serves as an important food source for forest-dwelling animals. Biomass varies significantly with mycorrhizal presence across stands and within species across moisture gradients. Luoma (1988) found that sequestrate community structure, as assessed by sporocarp biomass, changed in accordance with a vegetation/moisture gradient, and variation in production can serve as an indicator of community response to environmental conditions.

However, biomass alone cannot reliably index the ecological or physiological functioning of the fungal mycelial body. The influence of sporocarp size on nutrient uptake efficiency, translocation, and competitive ability for root space remains unknown. Community structure from sporocarp biomass may not reflect structure from ectomycorrhizal examination in the same stand (Gardes and Bruns 1996); using biomass as the sole indicator of dominance is clearly tenuous.

**Numbers of sporocarps** are a less desirable importance measure because across all species, sporocarp count for an individual species is poorly correlated with total dry weight in a given plot (Luoma et al. 1991). Species frequency—presence in plots or stands—is therefore preferred.

**Species frequency** provides a measure of "commonness" and offers a mechanism for interpreting biomass values. A species with high frequency but relatively low biomass presents a different importance perspective than high biomass with limited occurrence (Luoma et al. 1991). Frequency may convey importance by presence/absence at the stand level or by plot within stands. The concept derives from vascular plant ecology, where it evaluates regularity of distribution (homogeneity), but must be interpreted cautiously because variations in plot size, number, and vegetation structure strongly affect results (Cain and de Castro Oliveira 1959; Grieg-Smith 1983).

**Combining biomass and frequency data** yields the most appropriate comparisons between species and communities. Luoma et al. (1997) found a positive correlation between biomass and mycorrhiza frequency for several species, including *Lactarius rubrilacteus* and the sequestrate *Hysterangium coriaceum*, *Leucogaster rubescens*, and *Truncocolumella citrina*. Bruns (personal communication), however, found little correlation for several other species, indicating the relationship is not universal.

### Quantitative Assessment

Fogel (1976) conducted the first quantitative assessment of sequestrate fungal communities: 50 1-m² quadrats sampled monthly for 3 years, later reviewing quantification techniques and correlating production with temperature and moisture (Fogel 1981). Vogt et al. (1981) assessed hypogeous biomass in two *Abies amabilis* stands using 12 random 4-m² quadrats monthly for 6 months (288 m² total). Vogt et al. (1992) reviewed sporocarp production studies including sequestrate species.

Luoma et al. (1991) compared biomass from sample areas of 500–5900 m² and found small areas increase the likelihood of overestimating stand-level biomass. They concluded that dispersed 4-m² plots require 800–1400 m² total for adequacy. Adequate replication and sampling intensity are keys for statistically sound comparisons (Stafford 1985).

**Clustering and overestimation.** Fungal sporocarps are relatively clustered (Fogel 1976, 1981; States 1985). Random quadrats falling within localized concentrations may substantially overestimate stand-level biomass, and few random plots exacerbate this via clustering effects. Systematic placement of fewer plots reduces coincidence with concentrations and overestimation tendency (Luoma et al. 1991), while providing better coverage of stand variation since random plots tend to cluster (Mueller-Dombois and Ellenberg 1974). Nonrandom distribution at the plot level reflects gregarious to caespitose sporocarp formation (Fogel 1976; Arora 1987). The mycelial colony occupies a discrete area, and with adequate biomass a single colony can produce several to many sporocarps (Dahlberg and Stenlid 1994). Older stands may show greater clustered production due to longer colony establishment and greater host species richness, increasing host-specific sequestrate species (Molina et al. 1994).

## Species Richness

Richness is the total species count in the assessment unit (subsample, plot, stand, or landscape segment). The species-area curve (Cain and de Castro Oliveira 1959) examines sampling adequacy, but is problematic with fungi because presence is not obvious. Hunt and Trappe (1987) showed documenting all sequestrate ectomycorrhizal species requires collecting over several years; their species-area curve had not stabilized after 32 months across 1536 m². Even with multi-year sporocarp sampling, mycorrhizal richness may be greatly underestimated. Luoma et al. (1996a) found 43 sequestrate species and ~100 mushroom species from 27,000 m² of sporocarp plots, while 192 ectomycorrhizal morphological types came from only 0.45 m² of soil cores in the same area (Eberhart et al. 1996; Luoma et al. 1997).

Ectomycorrhizal morphological typing is subjective and labor-intensive, prohibiting rapid assessment. Until molecular tools routinely confirm identities, morphological typing remains inconclusive and incomparable between communities; sporocarps remain the primary index of sequestrate species richness.

## Inventory, Survey, and Monitoring

### Definitions

- **Inventory:** Cataloging extant specimens (e.g., herbarium listings).
- **Survey:** Delineating species extent at a location.
- **Monitoring:** Observing populations through time.
- **Adaptive monitoring/surveying:** Flexible response following target species discovery.
- **Intensive sampling:** Delineating population extent via strip plots or concentric-circle plots centered on a known location.

### Sampling Protocols

Protocols should be supervised by personnel trained in their use and in fungal identification. Before sampling, personnel should familiarize themselves with target species biology, ecology, habitat associations, and morphology to improve field accuracy and search efficiency. For **alpha diversity**, restrict sample sites to relatively homogeneous habitat. Sample vegetation, stand age, stem density, elevation, aspect, and topographic position at multiple sites to determine homogeneity.

### Periodicity

sequestrate fungi can fruit any time depending on weather and substratum. Some fruit mid-dry-season in buried wood or near water; most should be sampled during rainy season(s). Fruiting periods are restricted for some species (Fogel 1976; Hunt and Trappe 1987; Luoma et al. 1991): *Rhizopogon parksii* fruits only late summer/autumn in western Oregon, while *R. vinicolor* in the same stands fruits mostly spring/early summer. Some Ascomycetes form sporocarps in autumn but mature the following winter/spring. Out-of-season finds may have been frozen (*R. parksii*) or dried in situ (*R. vinicolor*) rather than decaying.

Freezing truncates or delays maturation; high temperatures accelerate substrate drying and curtail fruiting. Because timing is uncertain and strongly seasonal (Luoma 1988), each site should be sampled repeatedly during **2 consecutive months** of each fruiting season at **biweekly intervals** (sporocarps persist ~2 weeks). Across elevational gradients: visit low-elevation south-facing slopes first in spring, last in autumn; high-elevation north-facing slopes last in spring, first in autumn (Luoma 1988). Some sporocarps appear year-round, so **year-round sampling** is important for community structure surveys. **3+ years** are needed to assess community structure or rarity (Richardson 1970). The cryptic nature of sequestrate sporocarps (fruiting below litter, some within mineral soil) makes detection difficult; **2–4 years** minimum before concluding a species is absent.

## Surveying Community Structure

Three preferred methods are **plotless transects**, **line transects**, and **randomized plots** (permanent or temporary). Selection depends on clear objectives and available resources.

### Line Transects

Sample plots are located along a line (straight or not) through the area of interest, well dispersed to intercept wider microsite variety than a single circular plot of equivalent area (Ruhling et al. 1984; Mehus 1986; Ohenoja and Metsänheimo 1982; Luoma et al. 1996b). Repeated transects must be ≥20 m from previous ones. Plots every 6 m along a 50-m transect yield usually 25 4-m² plots (Luoma et al. 1996b). On slopes: 8, 9, 8 plots on upper/mid/lower strata. A "collection" = sporocarps of the same species from one 4-m² plot. **100 m² per 5–15 hectare stand** gives a reasonable pooled diversity estimate. Plots are flagged to avoid resampling. Alternative: plots 25 m apart horizontally (along contours), transects 75–150 m apart vertically. Consult a statistician on sample design.

### Randomized Plots

Statistically sound but logistically cumbersome: every potential location must have equal selection probability via fine-grid enumeration and random numbers table. This placement difficulty should not be confused with the separate need to assign treatments to experimental units randomly. O'Dell et al. (1999) used stratified random for permanent strip plots but needed only 3 per unit. For many small plots over extended periods, **systematic placement** is preferred: easier to implement, better coverage of stand variation, avoids clustering inherent in random placement (Mueller-Dombois and Ellenberg 1974).

### Plotless Transects (Time-Constrained Search)

Personnel search freely for a predetermined time, including diverse microhabitats
(near/far from trees, thick/thin litter, under shrubs, in open). Claridge et al.
(2000) showed this is **more effective than fixed-size plots** for species
diversity. Duration is predetermined by preliminary trials (e.g., 5 min × 2 people
= 10 person-minutes). The procedure repeats until the species curve levels off,
indicating person-minutes needed to find all taxa. Replication provides probability
levels for complete detection.

Claridge et al. found **100 person-minutes** (4 persons × 25 min) gave very high
probability of finding all sequestrate taxa in 1000-m² plots. Sites must be
sampled repeatedly over the fruiting season for **2–4+ years**. A complete assay
may require far more sampling (Egli et al. 1997), but intensive raking at that
scale would disturb the entire study area.

## Surveying Rare Species

Surveys of rare species may focus on assessment of existing populations or
detection of new ones. Two techniques, both tied to habitat assessment, are used
in searching for new populations. Many sequestrate taxa have specific habitat
requirements (e.g., soil type or associated host species). Rare sequestrate fungi
should first be sought in habitats similar to those where extant specimens have
been found. The most likely habitats are identified on the landscape and searched
during the most likely fruiting period, using plotless transects guided by
ecological indicators or systematic placement of standard plots.

Plotless transects are useful because large numbers of target microsites can be
searched by each collector. Indicators of fruiting include fresh digs by small
animals and raised compacted humus material. Productive microhabitats generally
include soils adjacent to well-decayed logs and protected areas within small areas
heavily disturbed by animals such as wild pigs. Small animals (squirrels, mice,
voles) commonly unearth sporocarps one at a time as they mature, leaving pits
2–8 cm deep and 5–8 cm in diameter. Many sequestrate fungi fruit in clusters, so
further exploration within 30–60 cm of a suspected fruiting spot often reveals
additional specimens.

Existing populations may be revisited to (1) assess population viability, (2)
further delimit the population, or (3) detect nearby populations. A previously
sampled circular plot can be resampled if enough time has elapsed for recovery, or
a concentric zone extending 0.47 m beyond the original plot can be sampled. To
explore for nearby populations, additional plots extending away from the original
thallus can be installed in a starburst pattern.

Because sampling for sequestrate fungi is disruptive—particularly for rare
species—it should be used cautiously. Disturbance of microhabitat may have an
adverse impact, rendering it uninhabitable. This is particularly evident with
coarse woody debris that is dismantled during sampling; woody debris does not
return to its former structure rapidly. Soil substrata and their concomitant herbs
and forbs typically return to pre-disturbance levels 1–2 years after sampling.
