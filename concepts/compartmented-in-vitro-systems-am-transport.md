# Compartmented In Vitro Systems for AM Transport Studies

## Overview

Compartmented culture systems are essential experimental tools for studying
nutrient and element transport in [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF). These
systems physically separate root compartments from hyphal compartments,
allowing researchers to investigate the specific contributions of
extraradical mycelium (ERM) to [[mycorrhizal-nutrient-transport]] without
confounding factors from soil matrices or competing microorganisms.

## Historical Development

The essential role of AMF in phosphorus uptake and transfer to host plants
was first convincingly demonstrated in the late 1960s and early 1970s using
bi-compartmented pot culture systems with isotopic tracers. The broader
context of AM biology is covered in
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]]. Studies by
Hattingh et al. (1973), Pearson and Tinker (1975), Rhodes and Gerdemann
(1975), and Cooper and Tinker (1978, 1981) established that AM fungi actively
transport phosphorus from soil to plants through their hyphal networks. These
pioneering systems allowed roots and AMF to develop in a root compartment (RC)
while the hyphal compartment (HC) was restricted to exclusive development of
the ERM.

## Limitations of Pot-Based Systems

Traditional compartmented pot systems, while groundbreaking, presented
several drawbacks:

  1. Presence of undesirable microorganisms influencing element
     bioavailability and transport processes
  2. Difficulty visualising the development of both symbiotic partners
  3. Possible interaction between the element under study and the soil
     matrix
  4. Potential direct root uptake of tracers due to diffusion or mass-flow
     leakage toward the root compartment
  5. Complexity of performing physiological and molecular studies on
     extraradical fungal mycelium and mycorrhizal roots

These limitations motivated the development of compartmented in vitro
(monoxenic) culture systems that have become widely used in contemporary
mycorrhizal research.

## In Vitro Bi-Compartmented Systems

### Principle

In vitro systems use bi-compartmented Petri plates (typically 90 mm diameter)
to physically separate the root organ or whole plant from the hyphal
compartment using barriers that allow fungal hyphae but not roots to pass.
These systems are maintained under sterile conditions, eliminating
confounding microbial influences.

### Culture Media

Two media formulations are most widely used for in vitro AMF culture:

**Modified Strullu-Romand (MSR) medium** contains a full complement of
macroelements (N as nitrate and ammonium, P, K, Ca, Mg, S), microelements
(Fe, Mn, Zn, B, Mo, Cu), and vitamins (Ca pantothenate, biotin,
pyridoxine, thiamine, cyanocobalamin, nicotinic acid).

**Minimal (M) medium** (Becard and Fortin 1988) provides a simpler
formulation with nitrogen supplied only as nitrate, supplemented with
glycine and myo-inositol.

### Key Equipment

  - Horizontal or vertical laminar flow hood for sterile manipulations
  - Stereo-microscope (10x to 40x magnification)
  - Cooled/heated incubator and climatic chamber
  - Rotating agitator for liquid cultures
  - Thermo-fusible glue applicator for sealing compartments
  - Bi-compartmented Petri plates, scalpels, forceps, cork-borers
  - Cellophane wrap, Parafilm, silicon grease

### Root-Organ vs. Whole Plant Systems

Both root-organ cultures (transformed roots) and whole plants can serve as
hosts. Root-organ systems offer the advantage of simplified growth conditions
and easier handling, while whole plant systems better represent the full
physiological context of the [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]].

## Applications

Compartmented in vitro systems enable research on:

  - **Phosphorus uptake kinetics**: Quantifying P uptake by ERM and transfer
    rates to host plants using isotopic tracers — see
    [[fungal-role-in-phosphorus-cycling]] and
    [[isotopic-tracers-mycorrhizal-research]] for methodology
  - **Metal transport**: Studying uptake and translocation of heavy metals
    and micronutrients by AMF hyphae
  - **Carbon allocation**: Tracking photosynthate flow from host to fungus
    using 13C or 14C labelling
  - **Molecular studies**: Performing gene expression analyses on isolated
    extraradical mycelium without soil contamination
  - **Nutrient competition**: Investigating interactions between different
    nutrient sources and their effects on fungal transport

## Advantages Over Soil Systems

The sterile, defined environment of in vitro systems provides:

  - Complete control over nutrient composition and availability
  - Elimination of microbial interference with transport processes
  - Direct visualisation of fungal and root development
  - Precise recovery of both roots and extraradical mycelium for analysis
  - Reproducibility essential for physiological and molecular studies
  - Compatibility with
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity]] surveys

These systems have been instrumental in advancing our understanding of the
[[mycorrhizal-compartmented-culture-systems]] and continue to be essential
tools in mycorrhizal research.
