## The Fungal Biodiversity Estimation Gap: How Many Species Remain Undiscovered

## Overview

Fungi constitute one of the largest and least-known groups of organisms on Earth despite their ubiquity and ecological importance. The gap between described fungal species and estimated total species represents one of the most significant challenges in biodiversity science. As habitats are degraded and destroyed each year, hundreds or perhaps thousands of fungal species are being lost even before they are known to science.

## Current State of Fungal Taxonomy

### Described Species

At least **74,000**, but possibly as many as **120,000**, fungal species have been named and described to date. This number encompasses filamentous fungi, lichen fungi, molds, mushrooms, slime molds, some chromists (oomycetes), and yeasts, although some groups are now classified in different kingdoms.

### Estimated Total Species

In 1990, the number of fungi actually present on Earth was estimated conservatively at **1,500,000 species**. That figure has been widely cited and generally accepted as a working hypothesis. Evidence accumulated since that time, especially from studies on plants in the tropics but also from critical molecular investigations, suggests that the figure may be too low.

### The Biodiversity Gap

If the 1.5 million estimate is accepted, then at most only about **5% of the fungal species on Earth** are known. Some mycologists argue that even this estimate is conservative and that the true number of fungal species may exceed 5 million, which would put the described proportion below 2%.

## Why Are So Many Fungi Undiscovered?

### Detection Challenges

Unlike insects or plants, fungi cannot be trapped, fogged, or collected en masse. Several factors make fungal discovery exceptionally difficult:

**Ephemeral fruiting:** Many fungi produce fruit bodies only at intervals of many years, and those structures may persist for only a few hours. A survey conducted at the wrong time will miss entire communities.

**Microscopic size:** Many fungi are found only by examining leaves and woody surfaces microscopically, or they can be detected only by isolating and culturing the fungi until they sporulate. These cryptic species are invisible to standard survey methods.

**Pleomorphy:** Some fungi have more than one sporing stage in their life cycles.
