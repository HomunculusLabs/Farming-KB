# Constructed Wetland Design for Contaminant Treatment

Constructed wetlands are engineered systems designed to utilize the natural processes involving wetland vegetation, soils, and their associated microbial assemblages to treat contaminated water.

These systems have evolved from simple wastewater treatment applications to complex, multi-functional ecosystems capable of treating a wide range of contaminants including organic pollutants, metals, nutrients, and pathogens.

This page focuses on the design principles and engineering considerations for constructed wetland treatment systems, complementing the broader discussion in [[constructed-wetlands-wastewater-treatment]].

## Types of Constructed Wetlands

Constructed wetlands are classified into two main types based on their hydrology: surface flow (SF) wetlands and subsurface flow (SSF) wetlands.

Surface flow wetlands consist of shallow basins with emergent vegetation growing in standing water, where water flows above the substrate surface.

SF wetlands provide wildlife habitat and aesthetic value but may have limited treatment efficiency during cold weather because of reduced biological activity and potential for short-circuiting.

Subsurface flow wetlands are further divided into horizontal flow (HF) and vertical flow (VF) configurations.

Horizontal flow wetlands have water flowing horizontally through a gravel or sand substrate, maintaining subsaturated conditions that promote anaerobic processes including denitrification and sulfate reduction.

Vertical flow wetlands receive intermittent loading of wastewater that percolates vertically through the substrate, providing excellent oxygen transfer and promoting aerobic degradation processes.

Hybrid systems that combine multiple wetland types in series, such as VF followed by HF, can achieve both aerobic and anaerobic treatment in a single facility.

The selection of wetland type depends on the target contaminants, climate, available land area, and treatment performance requirements.

## Design Parameters and Sizing

The hydraulic loading rate, expressed as the volume of water treated per unit wetland surface area per day, is the primary design parameter for constructed wetlands.

Organic loading rate, based on biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) or chemical oxygen demand (COD), determines the size required for organic matter removal.

Nitrogen loading rate determines the wetland area needed for nitrification and denitrification, which require different redox conditions that may be provided by different wetland cells in series.

Metal loading rate determines the treatment area needed for metal precipitation and sorption, and also affects the frequency of substrate replacement or clogging.

The hydraulic residence time, the average time water spends in the wetland, must be sufficient for the target treatment processes to occur.

Typical hydraulic residence times range from 2 to 10 days for SSF wetlands and 5 to 15 days for SF wetlands, depending on the contaminants being treated and the required treatment efficiency.

Substrate characteristics including grain size distribution, porosity, hydraulic conductivity, and sorption capacity affect both hydraulic performance and treatment efficiency.

The aspect ratio (length-to-width ratio) influences the degree of plug flow versus mixed flow conditions, with higher aspect ratios promoting more uniform treatment.

## Vegetation Selection and Role

Wetland plants play multiple roles in contaminant treatment including direct uptake, root zone oxygen release, provision of attachment surfaces for microorganisms, and enhancement of substrate filtration.

Commonly used wetland plant species include Phragmites australis (common reed), Typha latifolia (cattail), Scirpus species (bulrushes), and Juncus species (rushes).

Phragmites australis is the most widely used species because of its vigorous growth, deep root system, high oxygen transfer capacity, and tolerance of a wide range of water quality conditions.

Plant roots release oxygen into the rhizosphere, creating aerobic microsites within the otherwise anaerobic substrate that support nitrification and aerobic degradation of organic compounds.

The root zone also provides a large surface area for biofilm development, where microbial communities responsible for most contaminant transformation are concentrated.

Plant uptake of metals and nutrients provides direct contaminant removal, though this pathway typically accounts for a small fraction of total removal compared to microbial and sediment processes.

The selection of plant species should consider local climate adaptability, treatment performance, invasive species potential, and the ecological compatibility of the wetland with surrounding habitats.

## Microbial Processes in Wetland Treatment

Microbial processes are responsible for the majority of contaminant transformation and removal in constructed wetlands.

Aerobic degradation of organic matter occurs in oxygenated zones near plant roots and at the water surface in SF wetlands.

Anaerobic processes including denitrification, sulfate reduction, methanogenesis, and fermentation occur in the anoxic and anaerobic zones of the substrate.

The alternating aerobic and anaerobic conditions created by plant root oxygen release and water level management enable sequential treatment processes that would require separate reactors in conventional treatment systems.

Nitrogen removal occurs through sequential nitrification in aerobic zones followed by denitrification in anaerobic zones, with the plant root zone providing the interface between these conditions.

[[fungal-bacterial-coculture-bioremediation]] describes the synergistic relationships between bacteria and fungi that contribute to organic pollutant degradation in wetland environments.

[[fungal-biofilm-bioremediation]] describes the biofilm communities that develop on plant root surfaces and substrate particles in wetland systems.

Phosphorus removal occurs primarily through adsorption to substrate materials and precipitation with iron, calcium, or aluminum, with biological uptake accounting for a smaller fraction.

Metal removal occurs through precipitation as sulfides or hydroxides, adsorption to substrate and organic matter, and plant uptake.

Pathogen removal occurs through predation, UV inactivation, adsorption, and natural die-off in the wetland environment.

## Substrate Selection for Enhanced Treatment

The substrate material in subsurface flow wetlands serves as both the growth medium for plants and microorganisms and as a reactive medium for contaminant removal.

Gravel is the most commonly used substrate because of its availability, low cost, and adequate hydraulic conductivity.

Sand provides higher surface area for microbial attachment but has lower hydraulic conductivity that can lead to clogging.

Sorptive substrates including zeolite, blast furnace slag, and activated carbon can be incorporated to enhance removal of specific contaminants.

Zeolite has high cation exchange capacity that enhances ammonium removal, while slag provides alkalinity and metal adsorption capacity.

Biochar-amended substrates have shown enhanced removal of both organic and inorganic contaminants in laboratory and pilot-scale studies.

Substrate clogging, caused by accumulation of organic matter, biofilm growth, and chemical precipitates, is the primary operational problem in SSF wetlands and limits system lifetime.

Design features that mitigate clogging include adequate pre-treatment to remove suspended solids, use of coarser substrate in the inlet zone, and periodic resting of wetland cells to allow decomposition of accumulated organic matter.

## Applications for Specific Contaminant Types

Constructed wetlands for petroleum hydrocarbon treatment combine aerobic degradation of aliphatic and low molecular weight aromatic compounds with anaerobic degradation of higher molecular weight PAHs.

As described in [[fungal-pah-degradation-mechanisms]], fungal communities in wetlands contribute to PAH degradation through ligninolytic enzyme production.

Wetlands for textile dye treatment, as discussed in [[textile-dye-bioremediation]], use both plant-based dye uptake and microbial dye degradation for color removal.

Mine water treatment wetlands, described in [[acid-mine-drainage-treatment]], use limestone and organic substrates to neutralize acidity and precipitate metals from AMD.

Pharmaceutical-contaminated wastewater, as discussed in [[pharmaceutical-bioremediation]], can be treated in constructed wetlands where diverse microbial communities transform a wide range of drug compounds.

[[fungal-cytochrome-p450-biodegradation]] and [[fungal-treatment-pharmaceutical-wastewater]] describe the specific contributions of fungi to pharmaceutical removal in wetland systems.

Stormwater treatment wetlands remove sediments, nutrients, metals, and organic contaminants from urban runoff, providing water quality protection for downstream receiving waters.

Integration of [[bioremediation-monitoring-and-assessment]] protocols with wetland operation ensures that treatment performance is tracked over time and that maintenance or design modifications are implemented as needed.
