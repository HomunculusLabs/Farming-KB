# Fungal Spore Discharge and Dispersal

## Overview

Spore production and dispersal is central to fungal biology. Fungi have evolved remarkable mechanisms to launch their reproductive cells into the air, water, or onto passing animals. From the surface-tension catapult of basidiomycetes to the explosive asci of ascomycetes, and the swimming zoospores of water molds, these mechanisms represent some of the most sophisticated biomechanical systems in nature.

## Basidiomycete Spore Discharge (Surface-Tension Catapult)

Mushrooms and their relatives (basidiomycetes) discharge spores using a surface-tension catapult mechanism:

1. A fluid drop (Buller's drop) forms at the base of each basidiospore on the basidium
2. The drop expands over several seconds, contacting both the spore and the adjacent spore/adaxial surface of the gill
3. As the drop expands, surface tension pulls the spore toward the drop
4. At a critical moment, the drop fuses with the spore surface, redistributing mass
5. The sudden shift in the center of mass combined with the collapse of the drop's meniscus launches the spore horizontally from the gill
6. The spore then falls clear of the gill and is carried away by air currents

### Buller's Discoveries
A.H.R. Buller (1874-1944), working at the University of Manitoba, was the first to systematically study mushroom spore discharge:
- Measured spore fall velocities using a tilted microscope with calibrated silk threads in the eyepiece, connected to an electric tapper inscribing marks on a rotating drum
- Found spores fell slightly faster than Stokes' law predicted — later explained by Buller's drop increasing effective diameter
- Published six volumes of "Researches on Fungi" (1909-1934), funding early volumes himself
- Discovered the drop in 1910 and hypothesized surface tension was the driving force, though the exact mechanism remained unexplained for 70+ years

### Spore Velocity and Dispersal
- Spores are microscopic (typically 5-15 micrometers)
- Fall velocity is governed by Stokes' law: related to spore size, density, and air viscosity
- The horizontal launch velocity from the catapult is sufficient to clear the gills
- Wind carries spores from centimeters to many kilometers
- A single mushroom can produce millions to billions of spores
- The "invisible rain" of spores can be visualized with a strong beam of light

## Ascomycete Spore Discharge (Explosive Asci)

Ascomycetes use a fundamentally different mechanism:
- Spores (ascospores) form inside fluid-filled sacs called asci
- As the ascus matures, water enters by osmosis, pressurizing it like a hypha
- When the ascus tip opens (often explosively), pressurized fluid shoots the spores into the air
- This is a "cannon" mechanism, bearing no relationship to the basidiomycete surface-tension catapult
- In Cordyceps, each ascus elongates through a pimple opening, fires its eight spores one at a time, then retracts — the next ascus then protrudes to fire

## Zoospore Discharge (Water Molds and Chytrids)

Oomycetes (water molds) and chytrids discharge swimming spores into water:

### Sporangial Development
1. When a colony is starving, hyphal tips stop extending and darken as cytoplasm is transferred into them
2. The tip is isolated by a septum, forming a sporangium
3. Cytoplasm is cleaved into individual spore packets
4. A nozzle forms at the sporangium tip
5. After a "marble" phase of stillness, the membrane lining the sporangium breaks
6. Residual pressure (about 0.01 atmospheres after collapse from 4-5 atmospheres) is sufficient to push spores through the nozzle

### Zoospore Types
- **Primary zoospore**: teardrop-shaped, with anterior and posterior flagella; swims counterclockwise
- **Secondary zoospore**: kidney-shaped, reniform, with two flagella from a lateral groove; swims clockwise at twice the speed of primary spores
- The secondary zoospore appears to be the more recent evolutionary innovation

### Swimming Mechanism
- Posterior flagellum: smooth, oscillates like a tadpole tail, creates forward thrust
- Anterior flagellum: clothed with mastigonemes (lateral hairs) that reverse thrust direction, pulling the spore like a swimmer's arms in breaststroke
- Combined push from behind and pull from front propels zoospores up to 1 meter per hour
- At 10 micrometers body length swimming 27 body lengths per second — equivalent to a human swimming 160 km/h

### Chemotaxis and Galvanotaxis
Zoospores are equipped with targeting hardware:
- **Chemotaxis**: sense chemical gradients (sugars, amino acids) from wounded plant roots or animal tissues
- **Galvanotaxis**: respond to weak electrical currents from ion pump activity in living cells; healthy roots have a different electrical signature than wounded ones
- Can navigate around obstacles, crawl over surfaces, and spend hours exploring before settling

## Conidium Dispersal (Ingoldian Fungi)

Ingoldian fungi (named after C.T. Ingold) produce conidia (asexual spores) for dispersal in streams:
- Spores are shaped like anchors, crescents, or other configurations that promote attachment to submerged surfaces
- They are passive — no motility, no targeting — relying on water flow for transport
- When they contact a submerged leaf, they stick and germinate
- This parallels the role of chytrids and water molds in aquatic decomposition

## Fungal Luminescence

Some fungi are bioluminescent:
- The Jack-o'-lantern (Omphalotus olearius) emits a green glow from its gills
- Panellus stypticus was studied extensively by Buller, who rose at 4 AM and wore horse blinders during his commute to preserve dark-adapted eyes
- Luminescence may serve to attract insects for spore dispersal (insects are drawn to greenish light)
- Some luminescent fruiting bodies emit enough light for reading; soldiers in WWI trenches allegedly attached mushrooms to helmets for nighttime navigation

## Wind Dispersal

For most terrestrial fungi, wind is the primary dispersal vector:
- Spores launched above the gills or pore surfaces enter turbulent air
- Even gentle air movements can carry spores significant distances
- Gills are precisely spaced and oriented to maximize efficient spore release
- The mushroom cap acts as a windshield, creating updrafts that lift spores away
