## Fukuoka's Clay Pellet Seed Broadcasting

Clay pellet seed broadcasting is one of Masanobu Fukuoka's most innovative
practical techniques and a cornerstone of his [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]] projects worldwide, most
ambitiously for desert revegetation.

## How Clay Pellets Are Made

The basic process for creating clay pellets (sometimes called "seed balls" or
"tsuchi dango") is straightforward and requires no specialized equipment. Seeds
are first mixed with a small quantity of soil rich in organic matter and
microorganisms. This seed-soil mixture is then coated in a layer of clay, which
is shaped into small balls or pellets, typically ranging from marble to golf ball
size depending on the seeds they contain.

The clay coating serves several critical functions. It protects seeds from being
eaten by birds, rodents, and insects during the period between broadcasting and
germination. It buffers the seeds from extreme temperature fluctuations and
desiccation. And it delays germination until sufficient rainfall has penetrated
the clay and saturated the seeds, ensuring that germination occurs only when
conditions are favorable for seedling survival.

The soil layer within the pellet contains [[food-forest-establishment]]. The
technique has been used in large-scale ecological restoration projects in India,
Africa, and the Middle East. Organizations such as the Fukuoka-inspired "Green
Belt Movement" have employed variations of the technique for community-based
reforestation.

In urban settings, seed balls offer a low-tech, low-cost method for
establishing plants in degraded or neglected areas without requiring soil
preparation, irrigation, or ongoing maintenance. The technique has become
particularly popular for establishing wildflower meadows, [[fungal-biodiversity-in-extreme-environments]], requiring repeated applications over multiple years.
Species selection must be carefully matched to local conditions for the best
results. Despite these challenges, the technique remains one of the few
low-input approaches to large-scale ecological restoration that works with
rather than against natural processes.
