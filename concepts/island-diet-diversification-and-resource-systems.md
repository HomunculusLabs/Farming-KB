# Island Diet Diversification and Resource Systems

## Overview

Small island systems present unique challenges and opportunities for food production and resource management. In [[food-forest-establishment]].

- **Citrus species**: Oranges, lemons, limes, grapefruits, and mandarins are well-suited to tropical and subtropical islands. Citrus trees provide year-round fruiting when multiple varieties are planted, creating a continuous harvest calendar. They also serve as windbreaks when planted on island perimeters.

- **Vine fruits**: Passionfruit, granadilla, and other climbing fruits use vertical space efficiently — a critical strategy on land-limited islands. These vines can be trained over existing structures, shade trees, and trellis systems, producing abundant yields without occupying ground-level growing space.

### Polycultures of Minor Fruits and Nuts

Beyond the major fruit species, Mollison advocates for establishing polycultures of minor fruits and nuts that collectively provide a diverse and resilient food supply. These include:

- **Pandanus** (screw pine) for its nutrient-rich fruit segments
- **Sea almond** (*[[terminalia-catappa]]*) for edible nuts and shade
- **Canistel** and **sapodilla** for sweet, calorie-dense fruits
- **Cashew** for both nuts and the cashew apple fruit
- **Breadfruit varieties** — expanding beyond a single cultivar to multiple types that fruit at different seasons
- **Macadamia and candlenut** as high-fat, high-calorie nut crops

The polyculture approach ensures that if any single species fails due to pest, disease, or weather events, the overall food system remains productive. Diversity in fruiting times spreads labor and harvest throughout the year rather than concentrating it in seasonal peaks.

## Addressing Mineral Deficiencies in Island Soils

Island soils, particularly those derived from coral limestone or volcanic ash, frequently suffer from micronutrient deficiencies that directly affect human health. The most critical deficiencies are **zinc** and **iron**, both of which are essential for immune function, cognitive development, and energy metabolism.

### The Mulch Strategy

The primary permaculture solution to mineral deficiency is the systematic application of deep mulch layers. Mulched soils on islands serve multiple functions simultaneously:

1. **Nutrient cycling**: Decomposing mulch releases trace minerals bound in organic matter, making zinc, iron, magnesium, and calcium available to plant roots
2. **Moisture retention**: Critical on islands where freshwater is limited and evaporation rates are high
3. **Soil building**: Over time, mulch converts thin island soils into deep, humus-rich growing media
4. **Salt management**: Thick mulch layers prevent salt spray from reaching root zones of sensitive crops

### Species for Mineral Accumulation

Certain plants are particularly effective at accumulating trace minerals from soil and making them biologically available. Deep-rooted trees such as **Leucaena**, **Gliricidia**, and **Moringa** draw minerals from subsoil layers and deposit them in leaf litter. When these leaves are used as mulch, the minerals enter the surface [[soil-food-web]] and become available to food crops. Moringa leaves themselves are exceptionally rich in iron and zinc and can be consumed directly as a green vegetable.

## Unique Island Species and Their Roles in Polycultures

Islands around the world host unique endemic species that can play important roles in permaculture systems when understood and managed appropriately.

### Land Crabs

Land crabs are prolific on tropical islands and serve as both a protein source and a soil engineer. Their burrowing activity aerates soils, and their consumption of fallen organic matter accelerates nutrient cycling. In polyculture designs, land crabs can be managed through designated foraging zones that allow population control while benefiting from their ecological services.

### Giant Tortoises

Historically present on many islands, giant tortoises are efficient browsers of low vegetation. In permaculture systems they serve as living lawnmowers and fertilizer distributors. Their manure enriches soils, and their grazing patterns can be directed to maintain firebreaks and clear understory vegetation around food trees.

### Marine Iguanas and Komodo Dragons

While not typically integrated into food production systems, these species illustrate the unique ecological niches that islands create. Marine iguanas on the Galápagos demonstrate the potential for marine-terrestrial nutrient transfer — a principle that can be applied through deliberate design. Understanding apex predator ecology (as exemplified by Komodo dragons) is essential for designing livestock protection systems on islands where such predators exist.

### Flightless Birds

Flightless birds such as rails, megapodes, and historically moa and dodo species evolved in island ecosystems free from mammalian predators. In permaculture design, ground-dwelling birds can serve as pest controllers (eating insects and small mollusks), soil aerators, and egg/meat sources. Protection from introduced predators (rats, cats, mongooses) is essential for maintaining their populations.

## Tidal Flats and Marine Cultivation Systems

Tidal flats represent one of the most productive and underutilized resources available to island communities. Permaculture design treats the intertidal zone as an extension of the food production system.

### Fish and Shellfish Impoundments

Rock walls, bamboo fences, and earthworks can be used to create impoundments in tidal zones that trap fish and shellfish as the tide recedes. These systems require minimal maintenance and provide a reliable protein harvest. Key species for impoundment include:

- **Milkfish** and **mullet** for tidal fish ponds
- **Oysters**, **clams**, and **mussels** for shellfish beds
- **Mangrove crabs** for controlled harvest

### Mangrove Integration

Mangrove forests serve as the structural foundation of marine cultivation systems. They provide:

- Nursery habitat for juvenile fish and crustaceans
- Shoreline stabilization and erosion control
- Wood for construction and fuel
- Tannins for traditional medicine and preservation
- Filter capacity that maintains water quality in adjacent aquaculture systems

Designing mangrove impoundments that allow controlled tidal exchange creates productive polyculture zones where multiple species coexist and support one another.

## Island Energy Resources

Energy self-sufficiency is critical on islands where imported fuels are expensive and supply chains are unreliable. Mollison identifies several locally available energy sources:

### Tide-Flow Turbines

Channels between islands or between an island and a reef can generate reliable electricity through underwater turbines driven by tidal currents. Unlike wind or solar, tidal energy is predictable and constant, making it ideal for base-load power generation.

### Biogas Systems

Organic waste — including food scraps, animal manure, and aquatic plant material — can be converted to methane through anaerobic digestion. Biogas provides cooking fuel and can power generators. The effluent (digestate) serves as a high-quality fertilizer, closing the nutrient loop.

### Wind Energy

Trade winds provide consistent energy on many tropical and subtropical islands. Small-scale wind turbines can supplement other energy sources, particularly during seasonal wind patterns.

### Solid Fuels from Coconut

Coconut husks and shells are abundant on tropical islands and serve as excellent solid fuel sources. Coconut charcoal burns hot and clean, making it suitable for cooking and small-scale industrial processes. The production of coconut charcoal also produces coconut vinegar as a byproduct.

## Rising Sea Level Threats

Climate change and rising sea levels pose existential threats to low-lying islands. Permaculture responses include:

- **Mangrove restoration** as a living shoreline defense
- **Elevated food production systems** including raised beds and container gardens
- **Salt-tolerant crop selection** to maintain productivity as saltwater intrusion increases
- **Freshwater storage and catchment** systems designed for longer dry periods
- **Seed saving and genetic preservation** of island-adapted varieties

## Design Principles Summary

1. Maximize species diversity within limited land area
2. Use vertical space through vine crops and multi-story food forests
3. Close nutrient loops through mulching, composting, and biogas systems
4. Integrate marine and terrestrial food production
5. Design for energy self-sufficiency using local resources
6. Plan for climate resilience and sea level rise
7. Protect and utilize endemic species as functional elements
