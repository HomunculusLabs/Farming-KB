# Mycorrhizal Network Diversity

Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic associations with the roots of over 80%
of terrestrial plant species, creating extensive belowground networks that
are fundamental to ecosystem functioning. The diversity of mycorrhizal
fungi is enormous and profoundly understudied, with the majority of species
remaining undescribed.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

There are several distinct types of mycorrhizal symbioses, each involving
different fungal groups:

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF)**: Formed by Glomeromycota, these are
  the most widespread mycorrhizal type, associating with the majority of
  herbaceous plants and many tropical trees. AMF form intracellular
  arbuscules within root cells for nutrient exchange. Roughly 300 species
  have been described, but molecular data suggest the true diversity may
  be an order of magnitude higher.

- **Ectomycorrhizal fungi (EMF)**: Formed primarily by Basidiomycota and
  Ascomycota, these fungi sheath root tips and form a Hartig net between
  root cells. EMF associate mainly with woody plants in temperate and
  boreal forests (e.g., pines, oaks, dipterocarps). Over 20,000 species
  are estimated to exist, with perhaps 7,000-10,000 described.

- **Ericoid mycorrhizae**: Formed by Ascomycota with plants in the
  Ericaceae family (heaths, heathers, blueberries). These fungi are
  critical in acidic, nutrient-poor soils where they enable plants to
  access organic nitrogen.

- **Orchid mycorrhizae**: Formed by Basidiomycota with orchid seedlings,
  which are obligately dependent on their fungal partners for carbon
  during early development. Some orchids remain mycoheterotrophic
  throughout their lives.

## The Common Mycorrhizal Network

Individual mycorrhizal fungi simultaneously connect to multiple plants,
forming a "wood wide web" through which carbon, nutrients, water, and
chemical signals can be transferred. This common mycorrhizal network (CMN)
has been shown to facilitate seedling establishment, buffer stressed
plants against drought, and mediate competitive interactions between
plants. The diversity of fungal partners influences the structure and
function of these networks — more diverse fungal communities create more
redundant and resilient networks.

## Diversity Patterns and Drivers

Mycorrhizal fungal diversity varies dramatically across environmental
gradients. Soil pH, moisture, temperature, and host plant diversity all
exert strong influences. In forests, EMF diversity typically increases
with tree species richness and stand age. Land use change — particularly
conversion of forest to agriculture — causes severe declines in
mycorrhizal diversity, with cascading effects on plant community
composition and ecosystem productivity.

## Knowledge Gaps

Despite their ecological importance, most mycorrhizal fungal species
have never been cultured, and many are known only from environmental DNA
sequences. Their taxonomy remains in flux, with frequent revisions as
phylogenetic methods improve. The functional significance of
mycorrhizal diversity — how different species contribute to ecosystem
processes — is an active area of research with major implications for
forest management and restoration.
