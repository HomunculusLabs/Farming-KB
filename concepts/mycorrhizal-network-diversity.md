## Mycorrhizal Network Diversity

Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic associations with the roots of over 80% of terrestrial plant species, creating extensive belowground networks that are fundamental to ecosystem functioning. The diversity of mycorrhizal fungi is enormous and profoundly understudied, with the majority of species remaining undescribed. These symbiotic relationships are among the oldest and most widespread mutualisms on Earth, with fossil evidence dating back over 400 million years to the earliest land plants.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

There are several distinct types of mycorrhizal symbioses, each involving different fungal groups with unique ecological roles:

- **[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF):** Formed by Glomeromycota, these are the most widespread mycorrhizal type, associating with the majority of herbaceous plants and many tropical trees. AMF form intracellular arbuscules within root cells for nutrient exchange. Roughly 300 species have been described, but molecular data suggest the true diversity may be an order of magnitude higher. AMF are obligate symbionts — they cannot complete their life cycle without a host plant.
- **Ectomycorrhizal fungi (EMF):** Formed primarily by Basidiomycota and Ascomycota, these fungi sheath root tips and form a Hartig net between root cells. EMF associate mainly with woody plants in temperate and boreal forests (e.g., pines, oaks, dipterocarps). Over 20,000 species are estimated to exist, with perhaps 7,000-10,000 described. EMF include many prominent mushroom-forming genera such as *Amanita*, *Boletus*, *Lactarius*, and *Russula*.
- **Ericoid mycorrhizae:** Formed by Ascomycota with plants in the Ericaceae family (heaths, heathers, blueberries). These fungi are critical in acidic, nutrient-poor soils where they enable plants to access organic nitrogen. Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi produce powerful extracellular enzymes that break down complex organic matter, effectively mining nitrogen from recalcitrant soil organic matter.
- **Orchid mycorrhizae:** Formed by Basidiomycota with orchid seedlings, which are obligately dependent on their fungal partners for carbon during early development. Some orchids remain mycoheterotrophic throughout their lives, never developing photosynthetic capacity and relying entirely on their fungal symbionts for nutrition. The [[mycelial-network-structure]] that underlies these [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]] is remarkably complex, with individual fungal genets spanning tens of meters or more in some forest ecosystems. In some cases, a single fungal individual may connect hundreds of trees across a forest stand.

## Factors Driving Mycorrhizal Diversity

### Environmental Gradients

Mycorrhizal fungal diversity responds strongly to environmental conditions:

- **Soil pH:** AMF diversity tends to peak in neutral to slightly alkaline soils, while EMF diversity is often highest in acidic soils. Soil pH influences the availability of phosphorus and other nutrients that mediate the cost-benefit balance of the mycorrhizal symbiosis.
- **Climate:** Temperature and precipitation gradients shape mycorrhizal communities, with distinct assemblages in tropical, temperate, and boreal regions. Arbuscular mycorrhizae dominate in tropical grasslands and savannas, while ectomycorrhizae dominate in boreal and temperate forests.
- **Successional stage:** Early-successional plant communities are typically dominated by AMF-associated species, while late-successional forests shift toward EMF dominance. This successional shift has major implications for nutrient cycling and carbon storage.
- **Land use intensity:** Agricultural intensification, deforestation, and urbanization dramatically reduce mycorrhizal diversity. Tilled agricultural soils typically host simplified AMF communities dominated by a few generalist species, compared to the diverse assemblages found in undisturbed ecosystems.

### Host Plant Identity

Plant community composition is a primary driver of mycorrhizal fungal diversity:

- **Host specificity:** While most mycorrhizal fungi have broad host ranges, some degree of specificity exists. Certain EMF species associate preferentially with particular plant families or genera, creating non-random patterns of association.
- **Plant diversity:** Higher plant diversity generally supports higher mycorrhizal diversity, as each plant species provides a distinct niche and resource environment. This relationship has important implications for [[environmental-dna-fungal-survey]] methodology are now standard in mycorrhizal research.
- **Root tip sorting:** For EMF, morphotyping of colonized root tips followed by molecular identification remains a gold standard, but is labor-intensive and requires taxonomic expertise.
- **Metagenomics:** Shotgun metagenomic approaches are increasingly used to capture total mycorrhizal community diversity without PCR bias, though they require deeper sequencing and more complex bioinformatic analysis.

## Global Patterns and Undescribed Diversity

Global estimates suggest that the majority of mycorrhizal fungal species remain undescribed. The [[undescribed-fungal-taxa]] problem is particularly acute for tropical regions, where mycorrhizal surveys have been sparse and species richness is expected to be highest. Current estimates suggest:

- AMF: Perhaps 1,000-3,000 species globally, with only ~300 formally described
- EMF: Perhaps 20,000-25,000 species globally, with ~7,000-10,000 described
- Ericoid and orchid mycorrhizal fungi: Even more poorly known, with perhaps thousands of species remaining undescribed

## Ecological and Applied Importance

Mycorrhizal network diversity has critical implications for ecosystem function and human land management:

- **Ecosystem productivity:** Diverse mycorrhizal communities enhance plant productivity through complementary resource acquisition strategies and more complete exploitation of soil nutrient pools
- **Ecosystem resilience:** Diverse mycorrhizal networks buffer ecosystems against disturbance, disease, and climate change by providing functional redundancy
- **Carbon sequestration:** Mycorrhizal fungi are major pathways for carbon flow from atmosphere to soil, and their diversity influences the rate and stability of this carbon flux. Glomalin, a glycoprotein produced by AMF, contributes significantly to stable soil organic matter pools
- **Agricultural sustainability:** Mycorrhizal diversity in agricultural systems reduces the need for phosphorus fertilizers and improves crop resilience to drought and disease. Reduced tillage practices help preserve existing mycorrhizal networks
- **Restoration ecology:** Understanding mycorrhizal diversity is essential for restoring degraded ecosystems, as many plant species depend on specific mycorrhizal partners for establishment and growth
- **Forest management:** Timber harvesting practices that remove coarse woody debris disrupt EMF networks that depend on this substrate. Retaining woody debris and reducing soil compaction helps maintain mycorrhizal diversity in managed forests

## Threats to Mycorrhizal Diversity

Human activities pose significant threats to mycorrhizal fungal diversity worldwide:

- **Nitrogen deposition:** Atmospheric nitrogen pollution from industrial agriculture and fossil fuel combustion shifts competitive dynamics among mycorrhizal types, often favoring AMF over EMF with cascading effects on forest composition
- **Soil compaction:** Heavy machinery, overgrazing, and urban development physically disrupt mycorrhizal networks and reduce root-fungal contact
- **Climate change:** Altered precipitation patterns and temperature regimes are reshaping mycorrhizal communities, with uncertain consequences for ecosystem function
- **Invasive species:** Non-native plants and fungi can disrupt existing mycorrhizal networks, sometimes through incompatible mycorrhizal associations that fail to support native plant communities
