# Toadstool Eating and Mushroom Identification History

The history of [[mushroom-identification]] and the cultural fear of "toadstools" in
19th-century America represents a fascinating intersection of folk belief,
scientific ignorance, and gradual enlightenment. Julius A. Palmer Jr., a leading
mycological popularizer of the era, documented this history extensively, drawing
on [[palmer-mushroom-cooking-and-personal-experience]], medical correspondence, and botanical scholarship to
trace how public understanding of fungi evolved from blanket superstition toward
informed discrimination.

## Childhood Impressions and Folk Superstition

Palmer recalled that in his childhood, all wild fungi were indiscriminately
called "toadstools." The universal injunction was simple and absolute: "Throw it
away; it's poison." No distinction was made between harmless edible species and
genuinely toxic ones. This reflexive fear pervaded rural and urban communities
alike, and Palmer's early curiosity about mushrooms was met with uniform
discouragement from adults who could offer no reasoned basis for their alarm.

## The State of Published Knowledge

General-interest magazines provided little reliable information, offering only
scattered references to historical poisonings — Claudius allegedly poisoned by
a boletus, Czar Nicholas by *[[amanita]] muscarius* — alongside romanticized
accounts of Russian intoxicant fungi. These anecdotes reinforced a vague sense
of universal danger without practical guidance.

Encyclopedias were scarcely better, offering Latin binomials but appending
disclaimers that even the field mushroom (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*) "might be
noxious under certain unknown conditions." Palmer denounced this as "utter
falsehood" — a formulation that discouraged inquiry without evidence.

## Chemistry and the Deliquescent Fungi

Even professional chemists were largely unfamiliar with [[bloomfield-fungal-biology-and-hyphal-growth]]. S. Dana
Hayes, a government assayer, initially insisted that nothing in nature would
liquefy in the manner characteristic of deliquescent fungi (those in the genus
*Coprinus*, commonly known as inky caps). He maintained this position until
Palmer personally showed him a specimen dissolving before his eyes — a vivid
illustration of how empirical observation could overturn entrenched skepticism
among trained scientists.

## Medical Ignorance in the 1870s

The medical profession's grasp of fungal toxicology was remarkably deficient. In
1877, a prominent physician begged Palmer for any reference on mushrooms,
confessing he could locate no authoritative information. Dr. Brinton of
Philadelphia copied Palmer's article, appending that "the subject is so
important, the information so vague."

### Dr. Shadle's Letter (1885)

Dr. Shadle's 1885 letter addressed the clinical challenge of distinguishing
*Amanita* poisoning from milder fungal intoxication. The interval between eating
and symptom onset was diagnostically critical: *amanitine* poisoning typically
manifested after many hours, whereas milder poisons produced symptoms rapidly.
This mattered because atropine was indicated for certain milder poisonings but
was ineffective against phalloidine and amanitine toxins of deadly *Amanita*
species.

## The Attitudes of Professional Botanists

Palmer found that even trained botanists were ill-informed about the edibility
of larger fungi. Dr. M. A. Curtis, a respected American mycologist, used
edible fungi in his own household but was astonished to learn that a
deliquescent mushroom could be safely eaten. Dr. Sprague, another botanical
authority, consumed only canned mushrooms — never fresh wild specimens.
Professor W. G. Farlow of Harvard, though a distinguished student of fungi,
devoted his attention chiefly to microscopic forms and plant pathology rather
than to the macrofungi relevant to foragers. Professor Charles H. Peck of the
New York State Museum named and described numerous varieties but offered no
guidance on edibility. Edward A. Frost, a New England mycologist, followed the
same taxonomic pattern — meticulous description, complete silence on the
practical question of whether a given species was safe to eat.

## Foreign Textbooks and Indiscriminate Labeling

European textbooks compounded the confusion by labeling any species with an
unpleasant taste, odor, or appearance as "poisonous." Entire genera —
*Boletus*, *Lactarius*, *Russula*, and *Amanita* — were routinely marked
poisonous with no gradation. In reality, most *Boleti*, *Lactarii*, and
*Russule* are harmless; only *Amanita* contains genuinely deadly species.

## Palmer's Contributions

Palmer's 1877 *Popular Science Monthly* article was his first major attempt to
bring order to this chaos, conveying accurate mycological knowledge to a general
readership. His 1879 article in the *Moniteur Scientifique* (Paris) was, by his
account, the first attempt anywhere to segregate *Amanita* from other
non-esculent fungi and define its poisons' specific action. By isolating
*Amanita* as uniquely dangerous and distinguishing its toxicology from that of
merely unpleasant-tasting fungi, Palmer laid groundwork for the practical
mushroom-safety approach later authorities would refine.

His September 1893 article "Toadstool Eating" in the *Boston Journal*
continued this campaign, arguing that informed discrimination rather than
blanket avoidance was the correct approach to wild fungi.

## Folk Wisdom: Peeling as the "Aristocratic Test"

Among the popular beliefs Palmer examined was the folk notion that peeling a
mushroom was an "aristocratic test" of its safety — that if the skin could be
readily removed, the mushroom was wholesome, and if not, it was dangerous.
Palmer dismissed this as having no scientific basis, noting that peelability
was a morphological characteristic related to genus and species, not toxicity.

## Palmer's Philosophy: Mushroom and Toadstool as One

Palmer's central philosophical position was summarized in his maxim: "Every
mushroom is a toadstool and every toadstool is a mushroom." The words, he
insisted, were synonymous — a botanical fact obscured by centuries of
superstition. Only one variety, *[[agaricus-campestris-biology-and-life-cycle]]* (the common field
mushroom), was cultivated commercially and sold under the name "mushroom." All
other species — edible or not — were relegated to the pejorative category
"toadstool." This linguistic convention, Palmer argued, was a principal obstacle
to rational public understanding of fungi.

## Legacy

Palmer's work represents a pivotal moment in the popularization of mycology in
the United States. By documenting the ignorance of physicians, chemists, and
botanists alongside the superstitions of the general public, he demonstrated
that the problem of mushroom identification was not merely one of folk
credulity but of a systemic failure across all levels of educated society. His
insistence on distinguishing *Amanita* as the sole genus of deadly concern, and
his rejection of indiscriminate labeling, pointed the way toward the
evidence-based approach to [[fungal-foraging-strategies-heterogeneous-environments]] that characterizes modern mycology
and field guides.
