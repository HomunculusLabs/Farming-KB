# Farm Yield Strategies

[[permaculture]] applies its design principles at the farm scale through a comprehensive set of practical strategies that increase yields far beyond what conventional monoculture can achieve. These strategies operate across water, land, forestry, tree crops, markets, social structures, and tillage practices — and their interactions multiply the benefits further.

## Water Storage (12–20% of Landscape)

Devoting 12–20% of the landscape to water storage provides multiple yield increases:

1. **Direct product increase**: Animal protein production from fish, which are more efficient at food conversion than cattle. Water is more productive per unit area than land.
2. **Land product increase**: Irrigation from stored water and water nutrient quality from fish manure.
3. **Interaction effects**: Ducks on water increase yields in and around ponds through pest control, weed control, and manuring.
4. **Microclimatic buffering**: Water bodies moderate temperature extremes for surrounding land.

## Land Forming

Reshaping land contours for [[water-management]] provides:

1. **Even irrigation** — no dry areas or waterlogging, increasing uniform crop production.
2. **Land stability** — reduction of soil loss from water run-off or salting.
3. **Gravity flow** — replaces pumped water, saving energy (site-dependent).
4. **Water recycling** — captured and redirected for multiple uses.

## Soil Development

Active soil building strategies yield:

1. **Deeper root penetration** — increasing plant access to nutrients and water.
2. **Zero run-off** — maximum water infiltration through absorption.
3. **Soil microclimate buffering** — moderating temperature and moisture at the root zone.
4. **Essential nutrient supply** — [[building-soil]] organic matter and mineral availability.

## Forestry and Tree Systems (20–30% of Landscape)

Allocating 20–30% of landscape to integrated forestry and tree systems provides:

1. **Shelter effects**: Increased plant yields, animal protein, and microclimate buffering both above and below ground.
2. **Increased carrying capacity** due to shrub and tree fodder.
3. **Nutrient recycling** via legumes and deep-rooted trees.
4. **Intrinsic forest products**: Nectar for honey, seeds, firewood from fallen timber.
5. **Insect and bird habitat**: Escapement refuges and pest predator habitat.
6. **Wildlife corridors**: Connecting habitats across the landscape.

## Tree Crops and Windbreaks (Non-Industrial Forestry)

Dedicated tree crop systems provide:

1. **Precipitation increase** due to night condensation and improved water penetration.
2. **Product increase** due to superiority of perennials over annuals in bulk production, energy savings, and length of yield season.
3. **Rainfall increase** due to trees planted cross-wind.
4. **Reduced feeding costs** through selected self-forage browse, including drought-proof stockfeed and medicinal qualities of perennial plants.
5. **On-farm durable timber** — fence posts, construction material, reducing purchased inputs.
6. **Reduced carcass loss** due to shivering, sweating, and exposure in livestock.
7. **Increased crop production** in sheltered areas.
8. **Increased carcass weight** because cattle graze all day on shaded pasture instead of sheltering from the sun.
9. **Reduced evaporation** from ponds due to reduced wind over water surfaces.

## Market and Process Strategies

Beyond field production, market and processing strategies add value:

1. **Selected crops for specialty markets** — e.g., fresh herbs near a concentration of restaurants for premium price per hectare.
2. **Direct marketing** — self-pick, mail order, direct dispatch, wayside sale.
3. **Processing to higher-order products** — e.g., seed to oil, crude eucalyptus oil to refined fractions.
4. **On-farm fuel processing** — money saved by processing fuels on farm, plus sale of surplus fuel.

## Social and Financial Strategies

Community and financial strategies multiply farm viability:

1. **Farm-link strategy** — urban groups contract to buy specific produce from the farmer, providing market stability.
2. **Education income** — field days and educational courses.
3. **Agri-tourism** — guest houses, holiday farms, rental income from urban visitors.
4. **Direct investment** — city people investing in particular farms.
5. **Local financial infrastructure** — formation of local credit unions and banks, recycling money locally.
6. **Equipment pooling** — vehicle and implement pools with neighbors; coordinated sowing and reaping schedules (capital saved up to 90%).
7. **Labor exchange** with neighbors.
8. **Produce and marketing cooperatives**.

## No-Tillage or Low-Tillage Farming

Reducing tillage saves multiple resources simultaneously:

- Energy in reduced tillage operations
- Soil structure and organic matter
- Water through reduced evaporation
- Time between crops (faster turnaround)

## Real-World Evidence

Mollison cited the experience of farmer Norm Sims (Weekly Times, 5 Jan 1983), who applied several site strategies simultaneously:

- **Laser levelling** of fields for even irrigation
- **Water reticulation** and storage/recycling
- **Grazing rotation** — 26 paddocks in a 21-day rotation (average field of 1.6 ha each with trough water-points)
- **Central access road**
- **Crop for concentrated rations** grown on-farm
- **Pasture area reduced** to give best watering regime

Results included: expected doubling of production using half the irrigation water (4× benefit), reduced salinity, pasture production that "never looked better" even in severe drought, and irrigation time reduced from six days to two.

## The Real Source of Yield Increases

Mollison emphasized that primary increases in crop yield do **not** come primarily from varietal selections — a fiction promoted by agricultural companies, seed patent holders, agricultural researchers, and extension officers. Rather, the greatest yield increases come from:

1. **Attention to site design and development**
2. **Wise enterprise selection** suited to the modified site
3. **Concurrent marketing and processing strategy**

These are often permanent or durable strategies whose continuous benefit accrues to the farmer alone, reducing the role of middlemen and traders — which is not in the commercial interest of those parties to encourage.
