# Soil [[fungal-bioremediation]] Strategies

Soil contamination by organic pollutants (petroleum hydrocarbons, PAHs,
pesticides, chlorinated solvents) and heavy metals represents one of the
most widespread environmental challenges globally. Fungal-based
bioremediation strategies offer unique advantages for soil treatment due
to the filamentous growth habit of fungi, which enables them to bridge
soil aggregates, penetrate pollutant microsites, and extend their
metabolic influence beyond the immediate colony radius through
extracellular enzyme secretion and hyphal transport networks.

## In-Situ Approaches

In-situ soil bioremediation treats contamination without excavating or
transporting soil, offering significant cost advantages and minimizing
disturbance to contaminated sites. Fungal in-situ strategies include:

### Bioaugmentation

Bioaugmentation involves the deliberate introduction of specific
pollutant-degrading fungal strains into contaminated soil to enhance
the indigenous biodegradation capacity. Approaches include:

- **Inoculation with white-rot fungi**: Species such as [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]], [[staycare-trametes-versicolor-bioremediation-applications]], and [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]
  are introduced as spores, mycelial fragments, or colonized substrate
  (wood chips, straw, grain spawn). Pre-colonized substrates provide
  both inoculum and a nutrient base for fungal establishment.
- **Enzyme supplementation**: Direct application of purified or crude
  [[singh-fungal-laccase-enzymes-bioremediation]] and peroxidase
  preparations to contaminated soil.
- **Consortium bioaugmentation**: Combined inoculation of multiple
  fungal species or fungal-bacterial consortia to achieve broader
  pollutant degradation spectra through complementary metabolic
  capabilities.

Challenges for bioaugmentation include competition with indigenous
microorganisms, predation by soil fauna, moisture and temperature
fluctuations, and difficulty achieving uniform distribution of inoculum
in heterogeneous soil environments.

### Biostimulation

Biostimulation enhances the activity of indigenous soil fungi (and other
microorganisms) by modifying environmental conditions to favor
degradation activity:

- **Nutrient amendment**: Addition of nitrogen, phosphorus, and trace
  elements to overcome nutrient limitations in aged contaminated soils.
  C:N:P ratios of approximately 100:10:1 are typically targeted.
- **Oxygen enhancement**: Tilling, venting, or peroxide injection to
  maintain aerobic conditions essential for ligninolytic enzyme
  activity.
- **Moisture optimization**: Maintaining soil water content at 50-80%
  of water-holding capacity to support fungal growth without creating
  anaerobic conditions.
- **pH adjustment**: Liming acidic soils or adding sulfur to alkaline
  soils to bring pH into the optimal range (5.0-7.0) for fungal
  activity.
- **Carbon source addition**: Supplementation with low-cost organic
  substrates (straw, sawdust, compost) that simultaneously provide
  carbon for fungal growth and induce ligninolytic enzyme production.

### Combined Bioaugmentation-Biostimulation

The most effective field applications typically combine both approaches:
introducing competent fungal strains while simultaneously optimizing
soil conditions for their survival and activity. This integrated
strategy addresses both the capability gap (through bioaugmentation)
and the environmental constraint gap (through biostimulation).

## Mycorrhizal Involvement in Soil Bioremediation

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] form symbiotic associations with plant roots and play
important roles in soil bioremediation:

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (ECM)
- Form sheaths around fine roots of trees (Pinus, Quercus, Betula)
- Produce extracellular enzymes (laccases, MnP) that degrade organic
  pollutants in the rhizosphere
- Species such as [[pisolithus-tinctorius]], [[laccaria-laccata]], and
  [[suillus-luteus]] have demonstrated PAH and petroleum hydrocarbon
  degradation capability
- Extend the effective remediation zone beyond the root zone through
  extensive extraradical mycelial networks

### [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF)
- Form intracellular arbuscular structures in roots of most herbaceous
  plants
- Enhance phytoremediation by improving plant nutrient acquisition and
  stress tolerance in contaminated soils
- Reduce metal toxicity to host plants through sequestration in
  fungal structures (vesicles and extraradical hyphae)
- Glomus, Rhizophagus, and Funneliformis species are
  commonly applied in [[mycorrhiza]]-assisted phytoremediation

### Mycorrhizal Mechanisms
- Direct enzymatic degradation of organic pollutants by fungal enzymes
- Enhanced plant growth in contaminated soils, increasing
  phytoremediation potential
- Altered rhizosphere microbial communities favoring degradation
- Metal sequestration reducing plant metal uptake and toxicity
- Stabilization of soil structure through hyphal aggregation

## Land Treatment and Biopile Systems

### Landfarming
Contaminated soil is spread in thin layers (15-30 cm) over treatment
areas and periodically tilled to maintain aeration. Fungal inoculation
combined with nutrient and moisture management achieves degradation of
petroleum hydrocarbons and PAHs over treatment periods of weeks to
months. Landfarming is cost-effective for large volumes of moderately
contaminated soil.

### Biopiles
Engineered piles of contaminated soil (1-3 m height) with forced aeration
and controlled moisture. Biopiles offer better process control than
landfarming, with temperature monitoring, nutrient delivery systems,
and leachate collection. Fungal inoculation into biopiles as colonized
substrate (bulking agents such as wood chips or compost) enhances
ligninolytic activity.
