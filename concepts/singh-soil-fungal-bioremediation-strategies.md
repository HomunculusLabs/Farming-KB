    17|# Soil [[fungal-bioremediation]] Strategies
    18|
    19|Soil contamination by organic pollutants (petroleum hydrocarbons, PAHs,
    20|pesticides, chlorinated solvents) and heavy metals represents one of the
    21|most widespread environmental challenges globally. Fungal-based
    22|bioremediation strategies offer unique advantages for soil treatment due
    23|to the filamentous growth habit of fungi, which enables them to bridge
    24|soil aggregates, penetrate pollutant microsites, and extend their
    25|metabolic influence beyond the immediate colony radius through
    26|extracellular enzyme secretion and hyphal transport networks.
    27|
    28|## In-Situ Approaches
    29|
    30|In-situ soil bioremediation treats contamination without excavating or
    31|transporting soil, offering significant cost advantages and minimizing
    32|disturbance to contaminated sites. Fungal in-situ strategies include:
    33|
    34|### Bioaugmentation
    35|
    36|Bioaugmentation involves the deliberate introduction of specific
    37|pollutant-degrading fungal strains into contaminated soil to enhance
    38|the indigenous biodegradation capacity. Approaches include:
    39|
    40|- **Inoculation with white-rot fungi**: Species such as [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]], [[staycare-trametes-versicolor-bioremediation-applications]], and [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]
    41|  are introduced as spores, mycelial fragments, or colonized substrate
    42|  (wood chips, straw, grain spawn). Pre-colonized substrates provide
    43|  both inoculum and a nutrient base for fungal establishment.
    44|- **Enzyme supplementation**: Direct application of purified or crude
    45|  [[singh-fungal-laccase-enzymes-bioremediation]] and peroxidase
    46|  preparations to contaminated soil.
    47|- **Consortium bioaugmentation**: Combined inoculation of multiple
    48|  fungal species or fungal-bacterial consortia to achieve broader
    49|  pollutant degradation spectra through complementary metabolic
    50|  capabilities.
    51|
    52|Challenges for bioaugmentation include competition with indigenous
    53|microorganisms, predation by soil fauna, moisture and temperature
    54|fluctuations, and difficulty achieving uniform distribution of inoculum
    55|in heterogeneous soil environments.
    56|
    57|### Biostimulation
    58|
    59|Biostimulation enhances the activity of indigenous soil fungi (and other
    60|microorganisms) by modifying environmental conditions to favor
    61|degradation activity:
    62|
    63|- **Nutrient amendment**: Addition of nitrogen, phosphorus, and trace
    64|  elements to overcome nutrient limitations in aged contaminated soils.
    65|  C:N:P ratios of approximately 100:10:1 are typically targeted.
    66|- **Oxygen enhancement**: Tilling, venting, or peroxide injection to
    67|  maintain aerobic conditions essential for ligninolytic enzyme
    68|  activity.
    69|- **Moisture optimization**: Maintaining soil water content at 50-80%
    70|  of water-holding capacity to support fungal growth without creating
    71|  anaerobic conditions.
    72|- **pH adjustment**: Liming acidic soils or adding sulfur to alkaline
    73|  soils to bring pH into the optimal range (5.0-7.0) for fungal
    74|  activity.
    75|- **Carbon source addition**: Supplementation with low-cost organic
    76|  substrates (straw, sawdust, compost) that simultaneously provide
    77|  carbon for fungal growth and induce ligninolytic enzyme production.
    78|
    79|### Combined Bioaugmentation-Biostimulation
    80|
    81|The most effective field applications typically combine both approaches:
    82|introducing competent fungal strains while simultaneously optimizing
    83|soil conditions for their survival and activity. This integrated
    84|strategy addresses both the capability gap (through bioaugmentation)
    85|and the environmental constraint gap (through biostimulation).
    86|
    87|## Mycorrhizal Involvement in Soil Bioremediation
    88|
    89|[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] form symbiotic associations with plant roots and play
    90|important roles in soil bioremediation:
    91|
    92|### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (ECM)
    93|- Form sheaths around fine roots of trees (Pinus, Quercus, Betula)
    94|- Produce extracellular enzymes (laccases, MnP) that degrade organic
    95|  pollutants in the rhizosphere
    96|- Species such as [[pisolithus-tinctorius]], [[laccaria-laccata]], and
    97|  [[suillus-luteus]] have demonstrated PAH and petroleum hydrocarbon
    98|  degradation capability
    99|- Extend the effective remediation zone beyond the root zone through
   100|  extensive extraradical mycelial networks
   101|
   102|### [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF)
   103|- Form intracellular arbuscular structures in roots of most herbaceous
   104|  plants
   105|- Enhance phytoremediation by improving plant nutrient acquisition and
   106|  stress tolerance in contaminated soils
   107|- Reduce metal toxicity to host plants through sequestration in
   108|  fungal structures (vesicles and extraradical hyphae)
   109|- Glomus, Rhizophagus, and Funneliformis species are
   110|  commonly applied in [[mycorrhiza]]-assisted phytoremediation
   111|
   112|### Mycorrhizal Mechanisms
   113|- Direct enzymatic degradation of organic pollutants by fungal enzymes
   114|- Enhanced plant growth in contaminated soils, increasing
   115|  phytoremediation potential
   116|- Altered rhizosphere microbial communities favoring degradation
   117|- Metal sequestration reducing plant metal uptake and toxicity
   118|- Stabilization of soil structure through hyphal aggregation
   119|
   120|## Land Treatment and Biopile Systems
   121|
   122|### Landfarming
   123|Contaminated soil is spread in thin layers (15-30 cm) over treatment
   124|areas and periodically tilled to maintain aeration. Fungal inoculation
   125|combined with nutrient and moisture management achieves degradation of
   126|petroleum hydrocarbons and PAHs over treatment periods of weeks to
   127|months. Landfarming is cost-effective for large volumes of moderately
   128|contaminated soil.
   129|
   130|### Biopiles
   131|Engineered piles of contaminated soil (1-3 m height) with forced aeration
   132|and controlled moisture. Biopiles offer better process control than
   133|landfarming, with temperature monitoring, nutrient delivery systems,
   134|and leachate collection. Fungal inoculation into biopiles as colonized
   135|substrate (bulking agents such as wood chips or compost) enhances
   136|ligninolytic activity.
   137|
   138|### Composting
   139|Co-treatment of contaminated soil with organic amendments in aerated
   140|piles. Thermophilic composting (50-65 degrees C) followed by
   141|mesophilic curing allows sequential degradation by thermophilic bacteria
   142|and then ligninolytic fungi. White-rot fungi become dominant during the
   143|curing phase, degrading residual recalcitrant compounds.
   144|
   145|## Field Implementation Considerations
   146|
   147|- **Site characterization**: Detailed analysis of pollutant type,
   148|  concentration, distribution, soil type, and existing microbiota.
   149|- **Inoculum preparation**: Large-scale production of fungal inoculum
