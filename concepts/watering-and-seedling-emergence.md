# Watering and Seedling Emergence

Proper watering during the germination and seedling emergence phase is one of the most misunderstood aspects of gardening. The instinct to water frequently is often exactly wrong — overwatering kills more seeds and seedlings than underwatering. Understanding the relationship between moisture, temperature, soil structure, and seedling health is essential for reliable germination.

## The Overwatering Problem

### Why We Overwater

The natural fear is that if seeds dry out while sprouting, they will die. This fear leads many gardeners to water daily or even multiple times per day after sowing. But every time you water:

1. **[[soil-temperature]] drops**: The water is almost always cooler than the soil, and evaporation further cools the surface. This slows or stops the germination process.
2. **Soil structure is damaged**: The impact of water droplets can cause crust formation (see [[soil-crust-formation-and-tilth]]), creating an impenetrable barrier for emerging shoots.
3. **Fungal diseases thrive**: Wet, cool conditions are ideal for damping-off and other seedling diseases.

### Damping-Off

Damping-off is a fungus disease that invades and kills seedlings before and after emergence. The most visible symptom is when the stems of tiny seedlings pinch off at the soil line and the seedling falls over. This disease thrives in wet, cool soil and is one of the most common causes of seedling failure.

**Prevention**: Avoid watering sprouting seeds unless they actually need it. Damping-off does not thrive in dryish soil.

### Powdery Mildew on Cucurbits

Powdery mildew attacks members of the cucurbit family (squash, zucchini, melons, cucumber) in particular. It thrives in damp, cool conditions and can attack cucurbit seedlings before emergence. Most gardeners have seen powdery mildew cover the leaves of cucurbits at the end of summer when weather gets cool and humid.

**Prevention**: Minimize watering during the emergence period. Ensure good air circulation.

## The Ready-to-Till Moisture Test

### How Capillary Action Works

In undisturbed soil, moisture moves upward through capillary channels — tiny continuous spaces between soil particles. This is how deep soil moisture reaches the surface. However, when soil is loosened by a shovel, tiller, or fork, these capillary channels are broken. Moisture cannot rise from below to replace what evaporates from the surface.

This means moisture evaporates quickly from loose, recently worked soil — much faster than from undisturbed soil.

### The Footprint Test

The simplest way to assess soil moisture before sowing:

1. Walk across a newly worked bed
2. Look at your footprints
3. **If footprints stay dark (moist)**: The soil has adequate moisture for sowing
4. **If footprints dry out quickly (light-colored)**: The soil is too dry and needs watering

### Timing Your Watering

When the footprint test shows the soil is too dry:

1. **Water the bed** thoroughly
2. **Wait a day or two** before sowing — this allows the soil temperature to recover and excess moisture to drain
3. **Check again** with the footprint test
4. **Sow when footprints stay moist**

Never water immediately before sowing if you can avoid it — the cold water will lower soil temperature right when the seeds need warmth most.

## The Seed's Water Strategy

### Root First, Shoot Second

When a seed germinates, the embryo's first action is to send down a root. This is a survival strategy — once the root system is working, there is less chance the seedling will die from lack of water before the shoot emerges.

### After Root Establishment

Once the root is extending downward into moist soil:

- The seedling can draw its own water
- Additional surface watering is not needed
- The seedling is relatively safe from desiccation
- Surface watering at this point mainly risks cooling the soil and promoting disease

## Watering Established Seedlings

### The Transition Period

After seedlings have emerged and developed their first true leaves, their water needs change:

- They now have leaves that transpire (lose water through stomata)
- Their root systems are still small and near the surface
- They need regular moisture but not saturation
- The goal is to encourage roots to grow deep by watering less frequently but more thoroughly

### The Finger Test

For established seedlings, check soil moisture by pushing your finger into the soil beside the plant:

- **Moist at 1-2 inches**: No watering needed
- **Dry at 1 inch, moist below**: Water soon
- **Dry at 2+ inches**: Water now

### Deep Watering Principle

When you do water established plants, water deeply rather than frequently:

- Deep watering encourages deep root growth
- Shallow, frequent watering keeps roots near the surface where they are vulnerable to drying out
- A thorough soaking every few days is better than a light sprinkle every day

## Fertigation: Watering with Fertilizer

"Fertigation" is the practice of applying liquid fertilizer through irrigation water. This can be especially valuable for:

- Transplants during the establishment period
- Heavy-feeding crops during rapid growth
- Plants showing nutrient deficiency symptoms

### Methods

1. **Compost tea**: Steep finished compost in water for 24-48 hours, strain, and apply
2. **Manure tea**: Similar to compost tea but using well-rotted manure
3. **Commercial liquid fertilizer**: Diluted to appropriate strength per label directions

### The Muddy Slurry Transplant Technique

For newly planted transplants, fill the planting hole with liquid fertilizer or compost tea, then immediately push soil back in around the root ball, creating a muddy slurry. This creates a seamless connection between the root ball and surrounding soil, eliminating transplant shock.

## Watering and Soil Type

### Sandy Soils

- Drain rapidly and dry out quickly
- May need more frequent watering
- Benefit from organic matter additions to improve water retention
- Water can be applied more frequently because drainage is good

### Clay Soils

- Hold moisture well but drain slowly
- Easy to overwater — waterlogging kills roots by depriving them of oxygen
- Water less frequently but thoroughly when you do
- Allow drying between waterings

### Loamy Soils

- The ideal balance of drainage and moisture retention
- Most forgiving of watering mistakes
- Still benefit from the "less frequent, more thorough" approach
