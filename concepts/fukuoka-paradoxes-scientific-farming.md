# Fukuoka on the Paradoxes of Scientific Farming

where each intervention necessitates further intervention, trapping farmers

## The Self-Creating Problem Cycle

According to Fukuoka, scientific truth appears to be truth, plain and
simple, but it is only scientific truth, not absolute truth. Things appear
to have value, but invariably man has set up the conditions that give them
their apparent value. For example, the reason a field must be plowed is that
man has created the conditions that require it. The farmer feels as a result
that doing so is worthwhile.

## The Unnecessary Nature of Scientific Intervention

What would have happened had the earth been left alone to begin with?
Leaving a cultivated field untended is abandonment, but that is not what
Fukuoka is talking about. If the land is not abandoned, but instead left
in a natural state; if, as in the mountain forests, the earth is not
plowed or tilled, but left to itself, in a few thousand years, a rich
soil develops.

Man kills and destroys the soil. Then he packs it into pots, takes it to
the laboratory and runs tests on it. Based on the results, he concludes
that by increasing the plowing depth from one inch to two inches, one can
harvest an extra five bushels of grain, and that plowing to a depth of
three inches will give even higher yields. Of course, all that makes
perfect sense when you take dead soil and subject it to tests under
unnatural conditions.
