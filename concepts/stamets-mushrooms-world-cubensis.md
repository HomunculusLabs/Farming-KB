# [[psilocybe-cubensis]] — Taxonomy and Cultivation

Psilocybe cubensis (Earle) Singer is the most commonly cultivated psilocybin
mushroom in the world and arguably the most widely known psychoactive fungus.
Originally described as Stropharia cubensis Earle, it has several taxonomic
synonyms including Psilocybe cubensis var. caerulescens, Stropharia
cyanescens Murr., and Stropharia caerulescens.

## Taxonomy and Synonyms

The species has a complex nomenclatural history. [[psilocybe-subcubensis]] Guzman
is virtually identical, differing only in its smaller spores (9.9-13 by 6.1-7.1
microns) and slightly smaller pleurocystidia. P. subcubensis has been collected
throughout much of subtropical Mexico, Colombia, Bolivia, Ecuador, Honduras,
El Salvador, Venezuela, and Australia, and is probably more widely distributed
than currently reported.

## Macroscopic Description

Cap 1.5-8 cm broad. Conic-campanulate when young, often with an acute umbo,
becoming convex to broadly convex and finally plane with age. Reddish cinnamon
brown in young fruiting bodies, becoming lighter with age to more golden brown,
fading to pale yellow or white near the margin while the umbo remains darker
cinnamon brown. Surface viscid when moist but soon dry; universal veil leaving
spotted remnants on cap. Flesh whitish, soon bruising bluish.

Gills adnate to adnexed, soon seceding, close, narrow to slightly enlarged in
the center. Pallid to grayish in youth, becoming deep purplish gray to nearly
black in maturity, often mottled.

Stem 40-150 mm long by 5-15 mm thick, thickening towards the base. Whitish
overall but may discolor to yellowish; bruising bluish where injured. Partial
veil membranous, leaving a well-developed, white, persistent membranous
annulus that often bruises bluish and becomes dusted with purplish brown spores.

## Microscopic Features

Spores dark purplish brown to violaceous brown in deposit, subellipsoid, 11.5-17
by 8-11 microns. Basidia 2- or 3-spored, but usually 4-spored. Pleurocystidia
nearly pear-shaped, sometimes mucronate, 18-30 by 6-13 microns. Cheilocystidia
fusoid-ventricose with an obtuse or subcapitate apex, sometimes
sublageniform, 17-32 by 6-10 microns.

## Habitat and Distribution

Scattered to gregarious, primarily on dung of bovines (cattle, buffalo), horse,
or elephant dung in well-manured grounds in the spring, summer, and fall.
Found throughout the southeastern United States, Mexico, Cuba, Central America,
northern South America, the subtropical Far East (India, Thailand, Vietnam, and
Cambodia), and regions of Australia (Queensland). The largest fruitings typically
occur in May and June.

## Potency

On the psilometric scale, P. cubensis is rated "moderately potent." Reported
maxima include .50% psilocybin and .25% psilocin (Heim and Hofmann 1958), .63%
psilocybin and .11% psilocin (Gartz 1994). Stijve and de Meijer (1993) found
.15% psilocybin and .50% psilocin in a Mexican strain, and .15% psilocybin and
.33% psilocin in an Amazonian strain.

Analyses vary substantially due to complex variables. Bigwood and Beug (1982a)
found a fourfold variation in cultivated specimens and up to tenfold in wild
specimens. In one extraordinary collection, 1.3% psilocybin and .45% psilocin
were detected. Mushrooms grown indoors seem consistently more potent than
field-collected specimens, probably due to nutritional factors and protection
from ultraviolet radiation.

## Substrate and Potency Enhancement

Gartz (1989) determined that psilocin levels of flushes from sterilized cow
dung and rice (2:1) were naturally low (.19%), but could be raised to 3.3%
with the addition of only 25 millimolars of tryptamine into 10 grams of
substrate. His study showed that at least 22% of psilocybin was derived from
the introduced radioactively tagged tryptamine, reinforcing that substrate
composition directly affects potency.

## Cultivation History

P. cubensis has been widely cultivated in the United States and Europe since
the publication of Oss and Oeric (1976) and Stamets and Chilton (1983). Most
spores were brought back by travelers to Mexico, Guatemala, Ecuador, Colombia,
and the Amazon in the mid-1970s. Strains carried the names of their origins:
Amazonian, Palenque, Matias Romero, and Ecuadorian. The Oaxacan shamans prefer
[[psilocybe-caerulescens]], P. aztecorum, P. zapotecorum, or P. mexicana over
P. cubensis for ceremonial use.

## Historical Spread

P. cubensis is thought to have come with the Spaniards during the Cortes
expeditions, possibly carried via trade routes from subtropical African islands
to the New World. "San isidro" is an epithet used by indigenous peoples that
carries a reference to Spanish Catholicism. Today, P. cubensis can be collected
from Brahman cattle dung in subtropical pastures around the globe.
