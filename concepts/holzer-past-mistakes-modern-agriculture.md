# Past Mistakes in Modern Agriculture

From "Sepp Holzer's Permaculture" by Sepp Holzer.

## The Damage of Modern Land Management

In the last few decades many mistakes have been made with the management of land. In the name of agriculture we try to correct perceived imperfections in the terrain and drain unwanted water.

Rocks and cliffs are blasted to make the fields and meadows suitable for mechanised farming. Wetlands in which the most beautiful orchids grow are drained and dense spruce monocultures are planted there instead.

The result of these monocultures and this irresponsible attitude to nature is already well known: the catastrophes are becoming greater and greater. Floods, landslides and damage caused by storms and snow are becoming more prevalent. Valuable biomass and fertile humus are being lost. Holzer's alternative -- detailed in [[hugelkultur-bed-construction-detailed]] techniques that turn woody debris into productive growing mounds.

Holzer's catalogue of damage reads like a litany of the sins of industrial agriculture. Each intervention -- drainage, blasting, clearing, planting monocultures -- was undertaken for a narrow, short-term purpose: to increase the area available for mechanized crop production.

Each intervention created secondary problems that required further intervention, in a spiral of escalating damage that continues to this day.

The draining of wetlands is perhaps the most consequential of these mistakes. Wetlands are among the most productive and ecologically valuable landscapes on earth.

They filter water, store carbon, moderate floods, recharge aquifers, and provide habitat for an extraordinary diversity of species. Destroying them for agriculture gains a few hectares of marginal cropland and loses irreplaceable ecosystem services.

## Loss of Biodiversity

This narrow-minded attitude is causing the soil to lose its capacity to store water -- entire areas of land are acidifying and turning to desert.

Eventually, the widespread use of pesticides and fertilisers will poison the ground water. Biodiversity is being seriously threatened: instead of well-structured habitats there is suddenly only a monocultural landscape.

Humans disturb the balance of nature and then begin to fight against 'pests' and 'weeds' for which they have only themselves to blame. A new industry, agrochemicals, has devoted itself to the destruction of these enemies by chemical means.

If conditions are conducive to only a few species then they are the ones that will become the most pervasive. Their natural predators and rivals, which help to keep the system in balance, are gone.

The pest outbreaks that plague conventional agriculture are not natural disasters but self-inflicted wounds.

When a monoculture of wheat replaces a diverse meadow, the insects and fungi that specialize on wheat find an unlimited food supply. Their natural predators -- the birds, spiders, parasitic wasps, and predatory insects that kept them in check in the diverse landscape -- have nowhere to live.

The result is predictable and inevitable: pest explosion, pesticide application, further predator decline, worse pest explosion.

Holzer's analysis of this cycle connects directly to the [[natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] principle of letting nature guide cultivation rather than imposing human designs.

I started by using machines to improve and widen the terraces I made as a child. Then I wanted to make proper use of the springs on my land. I made ponds and lakes across the farm to breed fish. This is how the permaculture landscape of the Krameterhof began to emerge.

Holzer's personal story is significant because it demonstrates that the transition from conventional to permaculture farming is not merely theoretical. He walked the path himself, starting from the same agricultural education and institutional pressures that shape most farmers today.

His rejection of that education in favor of his own experiential knowledge is an act of intellectual courage that many farmers find inspiring but few have been willing to replicate.

## The Role of Subsidies and Institutions

The agricultural institutions that pressured Holzer to adopt modern techniques -- agricultural colleges, Chambers of Agriculture, subsidy programs -- are not neutral conduits of knowledge.

They are shaped by political and economic interests that favor large-scale, input-intensive farming. The subsidy systems they administer often make it financially impossible for small farmers to adopt alternative methods, even when those methods would be more productive and sustainable.

Holzer's experience illustrates a pattern that is now well-documented around the world: agricultural institutions tend to promote methods that increase farmer dependence on purchased inputs (seeds, fertilizers, pesticides, machinery) while discouraging methods that reduce this dependence.

This is not a conspiracy but a structural bias: the institutions are funded by and accountable to the industries that profit from input sales.

The critique of institutional agriculture connects Holzer's work to the broader [[sepp-holzer-method]].

Together, they demonstrate that the mistakes of modern agriculture are not inevitable but chosen, and that they can be unchosen.
