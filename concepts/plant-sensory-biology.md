# Plant Sensory Biology

## Overview

**Plant sensory biology** studies how plants perceive and respond to
environmental stimuli. Daniel Chamovitz's *What a Plant Knows* (2012) explores
this through the framework of human senses — sight, smell, touch, hearing,
[[plant-gravitropism-and-spatial-awareness]], and memory — arguing plants possess analogous (though
mechanistically different) sensory capabilities.

This does not mean plants are conscious. Rather, they have evolved
sophisticated information-processing systems that detect, integrate, and
respond to environmental cues with specificity rivaling animal senses.

## Sight

Plants monitor light continuously. Through phytochromes, cryptochromes, and
phototropins, they perceive color, direction, intensity, duration, and
spectral quality. See [[plant-photoreception]] and
[[phototropism-light-sensing]].

## Smell

Plants detect [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]] compounds (VOCs) through mechanisms analogous
to olfaction:

- **Ripening detection**: Ethylene from ripening fruits triggers cascading
  ripening in neighbors — "one rotten apple spoils the bunch."
- **Herbivore eavesdropping**: Attack triggers volatile release (jasmonic acid
  derivatives) that primes defenses in neighboring plants before attack reaches
  them.
- **Microbe signaling**: Root volatiles attract beneficial mycorrhizae and
  repel pathogens.
- **Cross-species detection**: Plants detect VOCs from unrelated species to
  assess competition or incoming threats.

Plant VOC detection involves receptor proteins binding specific volatiles and
initiating signaling cascades — functionally analogous to olfactory receptors
despite independent psychoactive tryptamines in nature distribution ecology and evolutionary origins.

## Touch

Plants respond to mechanical stimulation through **mechanosensation**:

- **Thigmomorphogenesis**: Chronic stimulation (wind, touch) produces shorter,
  stockier growth — adaptive for reducing breakage risk.
- **Thigmonasty**: Rapid movement in response to touch (*Mimosa pudica*
  leaflets fold within seconds; *Venus flytrap* snaps shut when trigger hairs
  fire twice within ~20 seconds).
- **Gravitropism**: Statolith cells in root caps detect gravity through
  sedimentation of dense starch granules, orienting roots downward.
- **Vine tendril coiling**: Tendrils coil around supports via differential
  cell growth on opposite sides.

Mechanosensory channels (MSLs, MCA proteins) translate physical forces into
electrical and [[query-how-do-plants-use-chemical-signals-to-defend-against-insects]].

## Hearing

The most controversial aspect of plant sensory biology. Plants lack auditory
structures but show some vibration responses:

- Some studies show roots grow toward sound of running water
- Plants produce defensive chemicals in response to caterpillar chewing
  vibrations (but not to other vibrations)
- Flowers can amplify specific frequencies, making nectar guides more
  detectable to bees
- Stressed plants produce cavitation clicks in xylem vessels, though whether
  others perceive these sounds is debated

Evidence for acoustic perception is less robust than for other senses.

## Spatial Awareness

Plants maintain [[plant-gravitropism-spatial-orientation]] through multiple systems:

- **Gravitropism**: Statolith-based gravity sensing provides vertical reference
- **Phototropism**: Light direction provides horizontal and vertical cues
- **Proprioception**: Plants sense their own mechanical state (tension,
  compression, bending), maintaining posture and correcting growth errors
- **Polar auxin transport**: Asymmetric auxin distribution creates and
  maintains organ polarity (apical-basal axis)

Vines finding and climbing supports, or roots navigating obstacles, demonstrate
sophisticated spatial processing without a nervous system.

## Memory

Plants exhibit several forms of "memory" without neurons:

- **Vernalization memory**: Prolonged cold periods trigger epigenetic changes
  (histone modifications at FLC) that persist through cell divisions and time
  flowering for spring.
- **Stress priming**: Prior stress exposure (drought, pathogen) creates faster,
  stronger responses to subsequent stresses via epigenetic marks and signaling
  protein accumulation.
- **Circadian memory**: The ~24-hour circadian clock persists for days in
  constant conditions, "remembering" the light/dark cycle.
- **Phototropism memory**: Auxin gradients persist after light removal, as
  redistribution takes time.

## Evolutionary Context

Plant sensory systems evolved independently of animal senses — **convergent
evolution** toward similar functional capabilities. Plants and animals last
shared a common ancestor over 1.5 billion years ago. Molecular mechanisms are
entirely different (no homologous receptors, no neurons, no brains), but the
information-processing problems are similar.

## Misconceptions

Chamovitz distinguishes plant sensory biology from popular misconceptions:

- Plants do **not** have consciousness or [[dosage-unpredictability-psilocybin-subjective-experience]]
- Plant "pain" responses are mechanical/chemical, not experiential
- Talking to plants has no demonstrated benefit beyond CO₂ from exhalation
- Backster's polygraph experiments have been thoroughly debunked
