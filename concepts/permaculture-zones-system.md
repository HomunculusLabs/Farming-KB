# Permaculture Zones System

The zone system is one of the most widely recognized and applied tools in [[permaculture-design]]. Developed by Mollison and refined through decades of practice, zones organize the landscape by the frequency of human attention and intervention required. Elements that need daily care are placed close to the center of activity; those needing less attention are placed farther away.

## Zone 0: The Home

The house itself -- the center of the system. Zone 0 design includes [[passive-solar-design]], energy efficiency, water conservation, indoor food production (sprouts, mushrooms, fermentation), and the creation of a comfortable, low-energy living environment. Mollison emphasizes that the house should be designed as a living system, not a box that consumes energy.

## Zone 1: The Intensive Garden

The area immediately surrounding the home, visited daily. This zone contains:
- Vegetable gardens and salad beds
- Culinary herbs
- Small fruit trees (dwarf varieties, espalier)
- Greenhouse or cold frames
- Compost bins and worm farms (linked to [[composting-systems]])
- Small animal systems (chickens, rabbits) for daily management
- Propagation nursery

Mollison describes zone 1 as the most intensively managed area, where the design maximizes yield per square meter through succession planting, vertical stacking, and careful timing.

## Zone 2: The Orchard and Food Forest

Visited several times per week. This zone contains:
- Main fruit and nut trees
- Berry bushes and brambles
- [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]] understory plantings
- Larger animal systems (poultry tractors, bee hives)
- Pond or water feature
- Main composting area

The orchard zone demonstrates the principle of stacking -- trees, shrubs, groundcovers, and root crops occupying the same vertical and horizontal space.

## Zone 3: The Farm

Visited weekly to monthly. This zone contains:
- Broadacre crops
- Pasture for grazing animals
- Large-scale tree crops (olives, chestnuts, pecans)
- [[permaculture-earthworks-guide]] for water distribution
- Windbreaks and shelterbelts
- Seed saving areas

Mollison treats zone 3 as the commercial production zone -- where surplus is generated for trade and storage.

## Zone 4: The Forage and Timber Zone

Visited seasonally. This zone contains:
- Timber and pole woodlots
- Foraging areas (wild foods, mushrooms, nuts)
- Large-scale water storage
- Wildlife habitat corridors
- Minimal management systems that produce with little human input

## Zone 5: The Wilderness

The unmanaged zone -- left entirely to natural processes. Zone 5 serves multiple purposes:
- A reference library of natural ecosystem patterns
- Wildlife habitat and corridor
- Genetic reservoir of native species
- A place for observation and learning
- Fire buffer and watershed protection

Mollison insists that every design should include a zone 5, even if small. "If we don't have zone 5, we don't have a reference to design from."

## Zone Dynamics

Zones are not rigid boundaries but gradients of management intensity. They can be modified by topography (a steep slope might push zone 1 farther from the house), access paths (a good road extends zone 3), and sector influences (a cold wind sector might shift elements to more sheltered positions).

Holmgren notes that the zone concept becomes more complex when applied at community and bioregional scales. In [[permaculture-community-scale-design]], the zones expand to include neighborhood food systems, local markets, and regional resource flows.

## Common Errors

Mollison identifies frequent zone design mistakes:
- Placing zone 1 elements too far from the kitchen
- Failing to connect zones with efficient paths
- Ignoring the zone implications of slope and aspect
- Designing zones on paper without considering the actual daily movement patterns of residents
