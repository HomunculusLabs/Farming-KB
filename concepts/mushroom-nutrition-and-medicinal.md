# Mushroom Nutrition and Medicinal Properties

Mushrooms are nutrient-dense functional foods with a long history of medicinal use in traditional Chinese medicine. Modern research confirms immune-modulating, anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory, and adaptogenic properties in many species.

## Nutritional Profile of Common Edible Mushrooms

Mushrooms are low in calories, fat, and sodium while providing substantial protein, fiber, and micronutrients:

- **Protein**: 20-35% of dry weight; contain all essential amino acids. Oyster and shiitake are particularly protein-rich
- **Dietary fiber**: 8-12% dry weight; includes chitin (structural polysaccharide) and beta-glucans. Promotes gut health and satiety
- **B vitamins**: Rich in riboflavin (B2), niacin (B3), pantothenic acid (B5), folate. Some species provide B12 (particularly shiitake)
- **Minerals**: Excellent source of selenium, potassium, phosphorus, copper, and zinc. Ergothioneine (a unique antioxidant) is concentrated in mushrooms
- **Vitamin D**: When exposed to UV light, ergosterol in mushrooms converts to vitamin D2 -- the only significant non-animal source

The FDA officially designates mushrooms as "healthy foods." Although most fresh mushrooms are 90% water, examining dry weight reveals their true nutritional density.

Key points from Stamets' analyses:
- Protein ranges from 3% (agarikon) to 35% (shiitake, portobello, nameko)

- Dietary fiber ranges from ~20% (Agaricus spp.) to ~50% (Pleurotus spp.) of dry weight
- Fat is very low (0.3-4%), with polyunsaturated fats comprising 10-30% of total
- All mushrooms contain zero cholesterol

### Vitamin D from Ergosterol (Sunlight Activation)

Mushrooms are the only significant non-animal food source of vitamin D. UVB light converts ergosterol to provitamin D2 (ergocalciferol). Key findings from Stamets' experiments:

| Species | Condition | Vitamin D (IU/100g) |
|---------|-----------|---------------------|
| Shiitake | Grown dark, dried indoor | 134 |
| Shiitake | Grown filtered light, sun-dried | 21,400 |
| Shiitake | Sun-dried, gills UP | **46,000** |
| Shiitake | Sun-dried, gills down | 10,900 |
| Shiitake | Grown outdoors, dried dark | 1,620 |
| Reishi | Dried indoor, no sun | 66 |
| Reishi | 6-8 hours sun exposure | 2,760 |
| Maitake | Dried indoor, no sun | 460 |
| Maitake | 6-8 hours sun exposure | **31,900** |

The FDA recommends 400 IU/day; some physicians recommend up to 800 IU. Just 1g of sun-dried maitake (~10g fresh) provides close to the FDA recommendation. Vitamin D production is concentrated in gills (highest), caps (half as much), and stems (a third as much). Optimal conversion occurs at ~70% moisture. Overexposure to UV degrades vitamin D (2+ hours continuous exposure causes 12% loss).

Vitamin D deficiency is a cofactor in breast, prostate, and colon cancers and some immune disorders. A 2005 Harvard study (Christiani) found lung cancer surgery patients with higher vitamin D had 3-fold better survival rates.

### Enzyme Inhibitors and Medicinal Properties (Ch. 13)

Mushrooms secrete suites of extracellular enzymes (laccases, cellulases, lignin peroxidases, manganese superoxide dismutases) and also produce enzyme inhibitors with medical significance:

**Aromatase inhibitors** (interrupt androgen-to-estrogen conversion, significant for postmenopausal breast cancer risk): Strongest in Agaricus brasiliensis, Fomitopsis officinalis, Ganoderma resinaceum, Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus ostreatus.

**5-alpha-reductase inhibitors** (limit testosterone-to-dihydrotestosterone conversion, associated with prostate cancer): Strongest in Cordyceps sinensis, Fomitopsis officinalis, Agaricus brasiliensis, Ganoderma resinaceum, Grifola frondosa, Trametes versicolor.

Ergosterols in mushrooms are thought to inhibit angiogenesis (proliferation of blood vessels supporting tumors).

### Habitat Influence on Nutrition

Nutrient levels vary greatly by substrate and growing region:
- Oyster mushrooms on sawdust: 32g protein/100g dry weight, 89mg niacin
- Same oyster on straw: 27g protein/100g, 54mg niacin
- Selenium in button mushrooms varies significantly by region (higher in Texas/Oklahoma, lower in Florida/Pennsylvania)
- Mushrooms bioaccumulate heavy metals -- organic cultivation does not guarantee toxin-free if the environment is contaminated

### Cooking and Digestibility

Mushrooms should always be cooked before consumption. Their tough chitinous cell walls are largely indigestible raw -- uncooked mushrooms pass through the digestive tract intact, imparting little nutritional benefit. Heat softens tissues and allows digestive enzymes to penetrate the cell walls.

### Why Grow Mushrooms Organically

Mushrooms can benefit the immune system, but ingesting those carrying industrial toxins sabotages their health-enhancing effects. Mushrooms bioaccumulate heavy metals and pesticides from their environment. A. brasiliensis grown in China was found to contain cadmium above European and American health agency standards, even when organically cultivated, due to air pollution (Stijve et al. 2003).

## Medicinal Compounds by Species

### Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)
- **Lentinan**: beta-1,3-glucan polysaccharide; approved as adjuvant cancer therapy in Japan. Enhances immune response via macrophage and NK cell activation
- **Eritadenine**: lowers cholesterol by reducing lipid absorption
- **AHCC**: active hexose correlated compound from shiitake mycelium; immune enhancer

### Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus)
- **Erinacines**: cyathane-type compounds from mycelium; stimulate nerve growth factor (NGF) synthesis in the brain. Investigated for cognitive support and neurodegenerative conditions
- **Hericenones**: compounds from fruiting body; also promote NGF production
- Showed promise in mild cognitive impairment studies

### Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum)
- **Ganoderic acids**: triterpenoids with anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and anti-tumor properties
- **Ganoderma polysaccharides**: beta-glucans with immunomodulatory activity
- Adaptogenic: helps the body resist stress and restore homeostasis
- Used in TCM for over 2,000 years as a "supreme tonic"

### Turkey Tail (Trametes versicolor / Coriolus versicolor)
- **PSK (Polysaccharide-K)**: protein-bound polysaccharide from mycelial culture; approved cancer drug in Japan since 1977. Potent T-cell and macrophage activator
- **PSP**: similar polysaccharide-protein complex; specific activity for antigen-presenting cells
- Among the most studied [[medicinal-mushroom-guide|medicinal mushrooms]] in clinical oncology

### Maitake (Grifola frondosa)
- **Grifron-D (D-fraction)**: beta-glucan from fruiting body; activates macrophages, NK cells, and T-cells
- **MD-fraction**: purified extract with enhanced immunomodulatory activity
- X fraction and other bioactive compounds under investigation

### Oyster Mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)
- **Pleurotin**: antibiotic compound
- **Lovastatin**: naturally occurring statin; lowers cholesterol
- **Beta-glucans**: immune support
- Rich in ergothioneine antioxidant

## Beta-Glucans and Immune Modulation

Beta-glucans are the primary bioactive polysaccharides in medicinal mushrooms:
- Mostly beta-1,3 and beta-1,6 linked glucans with beta-1,3 backbone
- Bind to specific receptors (Dectin-1, CR3) on immune cells (macrophages, NK cells, neutrophils)
- Activate innate (non-specific) and acquired (specific) immune responses
- Not cytotoxic to normal cells; work by potentiating the host's own immune defense
- Mechanisms include: cytokine production (IL-2, IFN-gamma), complement activation, and enhanced phagocytosis
- Mushroom glucans are mostly non-toxic with well-documented dose-response relationships

## Adaptogenic Properties

Several mushroom species are classified as adaptogens -- substances that help the body maintain homeostasis under stress:
- Reishi: modulates immune response bidirectionally (stimulates when underactive, calms when overactive)
- Cordyceps: enhances oxygen utilization, athletic endurance, and stress resilience
- Maitake: supports blood sugar regulation and cardiovascular health
- Adaptogens work gently over time; not acute-acting pharmaceuticals

## Preparation Methods

- **Tinctures**: alcohol extraction (ethanol) dissolves triterpenoids and some non-polar compounds. Standard ratio 1:5 (herb:menstruum). Effective for reishi triterpenes
- **Hot water teas/decoctions**: extracts water-soluble polysaccharides (beta-glucans). Simmer 30-60 minutes for medicinal mushrooms with tough fruiting bodies
- **Dual extraction**: combines alcohol and water extraction to capture both polysaccharides and triterpenoids. Most complete method for reishi, turkey tail, chaga
  1. Decoct in water, strain (captures beta-glucans)
  2. Extract marc in ethanol for 2-4 weeks, strain (captures triterpenes)
  3. Combine both extracts
- **Powdered supplements**: dried and ground fruiting body or mycelium. Easy but less bioavailable than extracts
- **Mycelium fermentation**: liquid-cultured mycelium provides uniform, controlled products

## Dosage Guidelines (General)

These are traditional/clinical ranges; consult a practitioner:
- Dried mushroom powder: 1-3g/day for maintenance; 3-9g/day for therapeutic use
- Tinctures: 1-3 mL (20-60 drops) 2-3x daily
- Dual extracts: follow manufacturer instructions; typically 0.5-2g/day of concentrated extract
- Start low and increase gradually; medicinal mushrooms are generally well-tolerated

## Contraindications

- Autoimmune conditions: immune-stimulating mushrooms (turkey tail, shiitake) may exacerbate overactive immune responses. Consult a healthcare provider
- Blood-thinning medications: reishi may increase bleeding risk due to antiplatelet activity
- Pre-surgery: discontinue mushroom supplements 2 weeks before surgery
- Mushroom allergies: rare but possible; those allergic to molds may react
- Pregnancy/breastfeeding: insufficient safety data for most species; exercise caution
- Always inform healthcare providers about mushroom supplement use
