## Soil Organic Carbon Fractions and Turnover Dynamics

## Overview

Soil organic carbon (SOC) is the largest terrestrial carbon pool, containing approximately 2,400 gigatons of carbon in the top three meters of soil — roughly three times the atmospheric carbon pool and four times the biotic carbon pool. Understanding SOC as a heterogeneous mixture of fractions with different chemical compositions, turnover times, and [[knf-leaf-mold-humus]] is operationally divided into fractions based on particle size, density, chemical extractability, and biological stability:

### Particulate Organic Matter (POM)

- Defined as organic particles larger than 53 micrometers (sometimes 20 μm cutoff used).
- Consists of partially decomposed plant and animal residues that are visually identifiable.
- Light fraction: separated by density flotation (typically < 1.6-1.85 g/cm³).
- Characterized by relatively rapid turnover times (months to decades).
- High C:N ratio (typically 15-50), reflecting recent plant inputs.
- POM is a labile carbon source for soil microorganisms and is sensitive to management practices.
- Tillage accelerates POM decomposition by disrupting soil aggregates and exposing organic matter to microbial attack.
- Conservation tillage, cover crops, and residue retention increase POM stocks.

### Mineral-Associated Organic Matter (MAOM)

- Organic carbon adsorbed to or occluded within [[gadd-fungal-carbon-sequestration]] potential.
- Biochar application directly adds to this pool.

## Conceptual Models of SOC Turnover

### The Three-Pool Model
Most soil carbon simulation models divide SOC into three pools:
- **Active pool** (labile): DOC, microbial biomass, and readily decomposable POM. Turnover: months to years.
- **Slow pool** (intermediate): Stabilized POM and some MAOM. Turnover: decades.
- **Passive pool** (resistant): Highly processed MAOM, pyrogenic carbon. Turnover: centuries to millennia.

### The Microbial Efficiency-Matrix Stabilization (MEMS) Framework
A more recent conceptual model emphasizing that:
- Carbon stabilization depends on microbial processing efficiency.
- Microbes with high carbon use efficiency produce more necromass (dead microbial residues) relative to CO₂.
- Microbial necromass is rich in amino sugars and is readily stabilized on mineral surfaces.
- The chemical nature of plant inputs matters less than the environmental conditions that determine microbial efficiency.
- This framework helps explain why clay-rich soils accumulate more carbon: clays promote microbial stabilization rather than direct plant residue adsorption.

## Factors Controlling SOC Turnover

### Climatic Factors
- **Temperature**: [[dighton-litter-quality-fungal-decomposition-rates]] approximately double for every 10°C increase (Q₁₀ ≈ 2). Higher temperatures accelerate both decomposition and root inputs, with complex net effects.
- **Moisture**: Decomposition is optimal at 60% water-filled pore space. Both waterlogging (anaerobic conditions) and drought limit decomposition.
