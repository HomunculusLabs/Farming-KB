# Mushroom Cultivation on Wood Logs

Growing mushrooms on logs is one of the most accessible and rewarding forms of [[holzer-mushroom-cultivation]] and food production in permaculture systems. It transforms low-value wood into gourmet and medicinal food with minimal inputs, embodying [[permaculture-principles]] of producing no waste and obtaining a yield from otherwise underutilized resources.

## Why Grow Mushrooms on Wood

Mushroom cultivation on wood is, along with keeping livestock and growing plants, an important branch of production on the Krameterhof. It began as a significant income source in the 1980s, with button, oyster, shiitake, and king stropharia mushrooms selling with great success. This demonstrates the [[sepp-holzer-method]] of diversifying farm income through multiple productive streams.

The majority of cultivated mushrooms that grow on wood are cultivated in almost exactly the same way, which makes growing them much easier. Any differences are usually limited to slight preferences in log size, type of wood, temperature, and humidity. Most of the particularly tasty and healthy mushrooms belong to this group.

Mushroom cultivation does not require a large amount of space. It is possible to grow enough mushrooms for personal consumption on just 2 square meters of balcony space. For farmers, mushroom cultivation can make a lucrative source of income with minimal costs and work.

## Understanding Fungal Biology

Fungi are not plants; they belong to a separate kingdom. They are one of the most important decomposers (saprotrophs) in the soil, converting accumulated biomass into nutrients that plants can absorb. Without them the cycle of nature could not function.

The visible mushroom is nothing other than the fruiting body of a fungus, comparable to an apple on an apple tree. The fungus consists of the much larger and mostly hidden mycelium, which is composed of thread-like cells (hyphae). If the fungus is compared to an apple tree, the mycelium corresponds to the trunk, branches, and roots.

Fungi, unlike plants, cannot produce organic material from inorganic material (mineral nutritive salts) because they do not contain chlorophyll and therefore cannot photosynthesise. They need nutrients from organic material (the substrate), which are absorbed by the mycelium. This connects to [[permaculture-soil-strategies]] and soil food web understanding.

## Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

Many fungi develop mycorrhizae and form symbiotic associations with plants. The fungus hyphae colonise the ends of the plant's roots, take over part of the plant's nutrition and help the plant absorb water and mineral nutrients. Nitrogen and phosphates are also made more easily accessible to the plant by the decomposing activity of the fungus.

The fungus also profits from the symbiosis because the plant supplies it with the products of photosynthesis (principally carbohydrates). Many plants form these associations because of inherent advantages. If a plant does not have access to its specific symbiotic partner, it will grow poorly.

Frequently poor soil or unfavourable climatic conditions are blamed for poor growth, whereas it is usually enough to incorporate a little soil from the plant's natural environment. The previously stunted plant will then thrive because it has access to its symbiotic partner.

## Mushroom Species for Wood Cultivation

All of the mushrooms listed below grow on hardwood on the Krameterhof. The oyster mushroom varieties can also be cultivated on straw. Fruiting body production occurs in most varieties from a temperature of at least 10 degrees Celsius. Only the enoki mushroom fruits from temperatures of 2 degrees Celsius.

### Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)

One of the most interesting culinary and medicinal mushrooms, with excellent flavour and many proven healing properties. It can lower cholesterol, is effective against colds, and strengthens the immune system. It has been scientifically confirmed to have a positive effect in the treatment of cancer. Shiitake can be cultivated on narrow logs or branches, and can be eaten raw or used to make tea.

### Oyster Mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)

Excellent culinary mushrooms that are very easy to cultivate. Although not particular about the kind of wood, they grow especially well on beech, maple, and elm.

### King Oyster Mushroom (Pleurotus eryngii)

Very popular because of their excellent flavour. Their thick fleshy stems are very versatile. They can be grown like other oyster mushrooms.

### Golden Oyster Mushroom (Pleurotus citrinopileatus)

Recognizable by the way they grow in large yellow clusters. They are excellent culinary mushrooms.

### Sheathed Woodtuft (Kuehneromyces mutabilis)

Small mushrooms that grow in clusters with very intense flavour, used predominantly in sauces and soups. Particularly undemanding in terms of cultivation.

### Enoki (Flammulina velutipes)

Also known as the winter mushroom because it bears fruit in autumn and winter. Used as a culinary mushroom and for flavouring sauces and soups.

### Judas' Ear (Auricularia auricula-judae)

Popularly used in Asian cuisine and as a medicinal fungus for treating nausea and lowering blood pressure. Prefers elder wood but grows well on other kinds.

### Black Poplar Mushroom (Agrocybe aegerita)

Very aromatic and used mostly for adding flavour to dishes. Requires higher temperatures and prefers softwood (poplar and willow).

## Substrate Selection

Whole logs are the best substrate for beginners. It is also possible to use a substrate mixture of sawdust and other plant material, although this involves more work and is riskier. Logs take up less space than other substrates and improve the look of any garden.

Only hardwood should be used for most species (beech, oak, etc.), though the black poplar mushroom prefers softwood (poplar, willow, alder, birch). The duration and yield of the crop differs greatly depending on whether hardwood or softwood is used. Mushrooms colonise softwood much more quickly, leading to an earlier yield, but softwood logs decompose more quickly so the yield is shorter-lived.

Cultures on softwood can grow through and give a yield in just six to twelve months. Hardwood usually requires twice that time, but the yield lasts substantially longer. Under good conditions, expect a total yield of 20 to 30 percent of the weight of the wood. In the long run, hardwood generally gives higher yields than softwood.

## Using Fresh and Healthy Wood

One of the most important factors for success is using fresh and healthy wood. It must not have been previously colonised by other fungi, which will displace cultivated mushrooms and cause the crop to fail. Freshly cut wood should be used whenever possible.

A slice of wood should be cut from each end of the log before inoculation to reduce the danger of colonisation by other fungi. Wood stored for more than half a year is probably no longer suitable. Tree stumps are an exception: they should be inoculated once they have stopped sprouting.

Healthy wood is recognized by having no dark or rotten areas. The cut surface must be light in colour and firm. The wood must contain enough moisture for the mycelium to grow through properly. Logs with a length from half a meter to a meter and a diameter of at least 20 cm are easiest to manage.

## Contamination Concerns

Only uncontaminated raw materials should be used for culinary and medicinal mushrooms. Mushrooms can absorb harmful substances and retain them. Trees near busy roads, motorways, or industrial areas usually contain large amounts of heavy metals that accumulate in the bark, making the logs unusable.

Even straw or compost produced conventionally may be unsuitable for organic mushroom cultivation. This principle of clean sourcing connects to broader [[permaculture-ethics]] of care for people and the earth.

## Medicinal Benefits

Mushrooms have long been recognized as both healthy food and medicine. Indigenous mushrooms like the honey fungus (Armillaria mellea) have been used as a laxative for centuries. The giant puffball (Calvatia gigantea) and agarikon (Laricifomes officinalis) were used to staunch bleeding.

Shiitake is one of the most interesting East Asian medicinal mushrooms. Judas' ear fungus is used to treat nausea or lower blood pressure. The reishi mushroom (Ganoderma lucidum) treats sleeping disorders and strengthens the immune system. Mushrooms are low in calories and rich in vitamins and minerals, making them valuable additions to [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]] systems.
