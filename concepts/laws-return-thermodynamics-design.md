## Laws of Return and Thermodynamics in Design

## The Law of Return

The single law offered to us by the education we derive from nature is the Law of Return, stated in several forms:

- "Whatever we take, we must return"
- "Nature demands a return for every gift received"
- "The user must pay"

In practical terms, this law means every object must responsibly provide for its replacement, and society must, as a condition of use, replace an equal or greater resource than that used. Inherent in this law are the concepts of replanting, recycling, durability, and the correct or beneficial disposal of wastes. Nature has extreme penalties for those who break such laws, and for their descendants and neighbours.

Mollison extends this principle to consumption choices: we must never buy books or newspapers that do not tax (i.e., fund replanting), nor goods where the manufacturer does not recycle or replant the materials of manufacture. We must carefully study how to use our wastes, including our body wastes.

## Immanence and Aesthetic Responsibility

The Law of Return extends beyond material accounting to aesthetic and spiritual responsibility. If a landscape delights us, we should not insult it with castles on peaks, roadways, and clear-cuts. We should return the pleasure we get from natural prospects and maintain their integrity. There is no reason not to bury necessary constructs in earth or clothe them with vegetation. If we want pleasure in life, then we should preserve the life around us.

## Understanding Entropy

Entropy is bound or dissipated energy—it becomes unavailable for work or not useful to the system. Mollison provides vivid examples:

- The waters of a mountain stream that have reached the sea
- The heat, noise, and exhaust smoke an automobile emits while travelling
- The energy of food used to keep an animal warm, alive, and mobile

Ambient and useful energy storages are degraded into less useful forms until they are no longer of any use to our system. This understanding transforms the designer's question from "How do I generate energy?" to "How can I best use energy before it passes from my site or system?"

## The Source-to-Sink Strategy

The permaculture design strategy is to set up an interception net from "source to sink." This net is a compound web of life and technologies, designed to catch and store as much energy as possible on its way to increasing entropy.

In practice, this means designing to catch and store as much water as possible from the hills before it ends up at its "sink" in the quiet valley lake. If we made no attempt to store or use water as it passes through our system, we would waste a freely available resource that could sustain crops, livestock, and human needs.

## Energy Accounting in Permaculture

The energy dimension of permaculture design involves careful accounting:

- **Energy entering the system**: Solar radiation, wind, water flow, biological inputs
- **Energy stored**: In biomass, soil [[permaculture-principles]] can produce dramatic improvements. The worse the initial condition, the greater the potential improvement through good design.

This connects to the broader social dimension: just join with one or two friends to make your way in the confusion. Others will follow and learn. The design revolution is not a mass movement that must be organised from the top down, but a distributed process of individuals and small groups applying sensible principles to their own situations.

## Integration of Science and Design

Mollison does not reject science outright but seeks to integrate it within a broader framework that includes observation, contemplative understanding, practical trial, and ethical consideration. Scientific method is one way to know about the real world, but not the only way. The most productive approaches combine:

- Rigorous observation of natural systems
- Pattern recognition across diverse phenomena
- Empirical testing of design ideas
- Commonsense management and continuous adjustment
- Ethical evaluation of outcomes

The designer's task is to apply these approaches simultaneously, creating systems that are both scientifically sound and practically effective, while remaining responsive to the unique conditions of each site and the needs of its occupants.
