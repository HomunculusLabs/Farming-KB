# Five Historical Periods of Neurotropic Fungi Discovery and Use

The history of human interaction with neurotropic (hallucinogenic) fungi spans millennia, from ancient indigenous use to modern scientific investigation and recreational drug culture. Drawing on the framework originally proposed by Stamets (1978, 1996) and expanded by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998), the history can be divided into five distinct periods, each characterized by different patterns of use, understanding, and cultural significance.

## Period 1: Ancient Indigenous Use (Pre-Columbian to 16th Century)

The earliest period encompasses the traditional use of neurotropic fungi by indigenous peoples across multiple continents. This period predates written scientific records and is known primarily through archaeological evidence, colonial-era chronicles, and surviving oral traditions.

### Mesoamerica
The most extensively documented ancient use of neurotropic fungi occurred in Mesoamerica, particularly in what is now Mexico. The Aztec (Nahua) people used fungi they called "teonanácatl" — meaning "divine mushroom" or "flesh of the gods" — in religious ceremonies. The Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún documented this practice in his Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva España (1569-1582), providing the earliest written European account of psychoactive mushroom use.

The Nahua used teonanácatl in ceremonies presided over by priests, who would consume the mushrooms to communicate with the gods, divine the future, and heal the sick. The mushrooms were considered sacred and their use was tightly controlled by religious authorities.

Beyond the Nahua, numerous other indigenous groups in Mexico — including the Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Chinantec, and Trique — maintained their own mushroom traditions. Many of these traditions survive to the present day, particularly among the Mazatec of Oaxaca.

### Siberia
In northern Asia, the Koryak and Chukchee peoples of Siberia used Amanita muscaria (fly agaric) as a sacred intoxicant. Wasson and Wasson (1957) brought attention to this practice, noting that Amanita muscaria was an important sacred fungus in the Siberian region. The Koryak would consume the mushroom, sometimes in the dried form or after processing, to achieve visions and communicate with spirits. The practice of drinking the urine of individuals who had consumed Amanita muscaria — which concentrates the active compound muscimol while filtering out the toxic ibotenic acid — is among the most distinctive features of Siberian mushroom use.

### New Guinea
Singer (1958, 1960a), Heim and Wasson (1965), and Heim (1965a, 1978) reported the use of Psilocybe, Russula, and Boletaceous fungi as sacred mushrooms among several aboriginal groups in New Guinea. These traditions, while less extensively documented than the Mexican practices, demonstrate that indigenous use of neurotropic fungi was not confined to the Americas. The New Guinea findings were particularly significant because they showed independent cultural adoption of psychoactive fungi in a region with no historical connection to Mesoamerica, suggesting multiple independent discoveries of fungal psychoactivity across human cultures.

### Africa
Although less well documented, there is evidence that psychoactive fungi were used in parts of Africa. The use of Amanita muscaria has been suggested in some Central and East African traditions, though the evidence remains circumstantial. Some researchers have proposed that certain African fungi traditions may represent the oldest known use of psychoactive mushrooms, potentially predating Mesoamerican practices, but this hypothesis remains debated.

### North America
Various North American indigenous peoples also used fungi with psychoactive properties, though the extent of this use and the specific species involved remain less well documented. Some evidence suggests that certain puffballs (gasteromycetes) were used in religious ceremonies by North American Indian peoples, referred to as "fallen stars" because they typically grew in circles (fairy rings) on the prairies.

## Period 2: Erroneous and Uncertain Early Studies (Late 19th to Mid-20th Century)

The second period is characterized by early but often inaccurate scientific attempts to identify and classify neurotropic fungi. During this time, Western scientists began to take notice of indigenous mushroom practices but frequently misidentified the species involved.

### Schultes and the Panaeolus Error
Schultes (1939) initially stated that the neurotropic fungi in Mexico belonged only to Panaeolus campanulatus var. sphinctrinus. This identification was later shown to be incorrect, as the mushrooms used in Mexican ceremonies were primarily species of Psilocybe. Singer (1949) followed Schultes' position and also initially misidentified some materials, including Psilocybe cubensis based on some unidentified collections from Mexico by Schultes.

### Early Confusion with Polypores and Other Fungi
During this period, various fungi were erroneously attributed with neurotropic properties. For example, some polypores containing hordenine, N-methyltyramine, and tyramine — such as [[laetiporus-sulphureus]] and [[meripilus-giganteus]] — were considered psychotropic. These produce certain chemical reactions in the central nervous system resulting in dizziness and disorientation, but the first species is a common edible fungus in Mexico and elsewhere. Thoen (1982) commented on the use of polypores in religious ceremonies in several regions, but the neurotropic properties attributed to them were often questionable.

### Cordyceps and Elaphomyces in Mexican Traditions
Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides were found in popular markets in Mexico as sacred fungi. These species, used by indigenous peoples of the Nevado de Toluca region together with Psilocybe muliercula, are closely related taxonomically to Claviceps purpurea (ergot). However, chemical analyses by Hofmann failed to find active indole compounds, likely because the specimens had been preserved for too long before analysis.

### Mycena and Other Genera
Several species of Mycena were reported as hallucinogenic during this period. Mycena cyanorrhiza from New Zealand was reported by Reid and Eicker (1991) to contain psilocybin. Mycena pura was reported to have narcotic properties, but other studies failed to confirm these findings. The uncertainty surrounding Mycena species illustrates the broader pattern of this period: enthusiastic but unreliable reports that required subsequent verification.

### The Role of Mistaken Identity
A recurring theme in this period was the confusion between different genera of fungi. Many early reports attributed hallucinogenic properties to species that were later shown to be inactive, or misidentified active species as belonging to the wrong genus. This was partly due to the limitations of available taxonomic keys and the difficulty of preserving specimens properly for later analysis. The advent of DNA barcoding and modern chemotaxonomy has largely resolved these ambiguities.

## Period 3: Scientific Investigation and Rediscovery (1950s-1960s)

The third period represents the true scientific awakening regarding neurotropic fungi, catalyzed by the work of R. Gordon Wasson and his collaborators.

### The Wasson Expedition
Wasson and Wasson (1957) published their famous article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" in Life magazine, bringing global attention to the use of psychoactive mushrooms in Mexico. This article introduced the English-speaking world to the concept of "magic mushrooms" and sparked widespread scientific and popular interest. Wasson, a vice president at J.P. Morgan, had become fascinated by the question of whether ancient cultures had used mushrooms in religious ceremonies — what he called "ourancestry's most startling cultural achievement." His initial visits to Mexico in the 1950s, accompanied by his wife [[valentina-pavlovna-wasson]] Wasson, led to the rediscovery of active mushroom ceremonies that had been kept secret from outsiders for centuries. The Life article, with its dramatic photographs and evocative prose, reached millions of readers and triggered a wave of scientific and popular interest in psychoactive fungi.

### Heim's Mycological Research
Roger Heim, a French mycologist at the Muséum National d'Histoire Naturelle in Paris, conducted extensive fieldwork in Mexico and was the first to scientifically identify and cultivate many of the Psilocybe species used by indigenous peoples. Heim (1956a, b, 1957a, c, 1958a, b) described new species and established the taxonomic framework for the genus Psilocybe as it relates to neurotropic fungi.

### Discovery of Psilocybin
Albert Hofmann, working at Sandoz Laboratories in Switzerland and collaborating with Heim, isolated and identified the active compounds [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] from Psilocybe mexicana and other species in 1958-1959. This chemical characterization transformed the field from ethnographic curiosity to rigorous pharmacological science.

### Singer's Taxonomic Contributions
Rolf Singer, working independently and sometimes collaboratively with these researchers, contributed important taxonomic work including Singer (1949, 1958, 1959, 1960a) and Singer and Smith (1958). Singer's classification of the Strophariaceae, including the placement of many Psilocybe species, remains influential.

## Period 4: Recreational Use and Cultural Diffusion (1960s-1980s)

The fourth period saw the transformation of neurotropic fungi from objects of indigenous ritual and scientific study into recreational drugs, driven by the counterculture movement of the 1960s.

### The Counterculture Movement
The discovery of psilocybin coincided with the rise of the 1960s counterculture. Psilocybin and LSD became symbols of the psychedelic movement, associated with expanded consciousness, spiritual exploration, and rebellion against mainstream values. Timothy Leary and Richard Alpert's experiments at Harvard with psilocybin (before their dismissal in 1963) brought further public attention. Leary famously urged people to "turn on, tune in, drop out," and while his primary substance was LSD, psilocybin mushrooms became a natural adjunct to the psychedelic counterculture. The Merry Pranksters' cross-country bus ride, documented in Tom Wolfe's "The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test," further embedded psychedelic culture in the American consciousness.

### Underground Cultivation Manuals
During this period, the first informal cultivation guides began to circulate. The publication of Psilocybin: Magic Mushroom Grower's Guide by Oss and Oeric (a pseudonym for Terrance and Dennis McKenna) in 1976 represented a watershed moment, providing accessible instructions for home cultivation using rice-based substrates. This guide, along with subsequent publications, laid the groundwork for the later development of the PF TEK and other popular cultivation methods.

### Degeneration of Traditional Use
Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz noted that this period saw a "degeneration of the traditional use of these fungi, mainly in Mexico." The influx of Western seekers, hippies, and tourists disrupted traditional mushroom ceremonies, which had been carefully regulated by indigenous shamans within specific cultural contexts. The commercialization of sacred practices led many indigenous communities to restrict or abandon their mushroom traditions.

### Legal Prohibition
The recreational use of hallucinogenic mushrooms prompted governments of many countries to prohibit their use and commerce. In the United States, psilocybin and psilocin were placed under Schedule I of the [[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]] Act. Similar restrictions were enacted across Europe, Australia, and elsewhere. However, underground markets and illegal trade continued to develop.

### Spread of Cultivation
During this period, the techniques for cultivating psilocybin mushrooms spread through underground publications and word of mouth. The basic principles of mushroom cultivation — substrate preparation, sterilization, inoculation, and fruiting — became accessible to amateur mycologists despite legal restrictions.

## Period 5: Recapitulation and New Discoveries (1990s-Present)

The current (fifth) period involves a recapitulation of knowledge, the description of new species, and more sophisticated chemical analyses.

### Taxonomic Advances
New species of neurotropic fungi continue to be described from around the world. Guzmán (1998a, 1999b) and colleagues described new species of Psilocybe from the United States, Mexico, Colombia, Puerto Rico, Spain, Thailand, and New Zealand. Gartz et al. (1995) and Stamets and Gartz (1995) discovered new species from South Africa and the United States respectively.

### Chemical Re-evaluation
Modern analytical techniques have allowed the re-evaluation of earlier chemical studies. Many earlier reports of psilocybin in various species have been found to be erroneous due to misidentification of specimens, analytical errors, or contamination. Stijve and Kuyper (1988) failed to find psilocybin in several species previously reported to contain it, and the controversy over [[guzman-panaeolina-foenisecii-psilocybin-controversy-conflicting-chemical-analyses]] illustrates the difficulty of chemical analysis in this field.

## Practical Applications

The principles and techniques discussed here have wide-ranging applications
across multiple disciplines and contexts. Practitioners and researchers
continue to explore new ways to integrate these concepts into modern practice,
adapting traditional knowledge to contemporary challenges and opportunities.

## Key Considerations

in this topic. Understanding these considerations helps practitioners make

## Historical Context

The historical development of this subject reflects centuries of accumulated
knowledge and practical experience. From traditional methods passed down
through generations to modern scientific approaches, the evolution continues
to inform current best practices and research directions.

Practitioners frequently encounter several challenges when working with
these concepts. Climate variability, resource limitations, and knowledge
