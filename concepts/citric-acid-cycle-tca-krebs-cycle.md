## Overview

The citric acid cycle, also known as the tricarboxylic acid
(TCA) cycle or Krebs cycle, is a series of chemical
reactions that oxidize acetyl-CoA derived from
carbohydrates, fats, and proteins into [[carbon-dioxide-retention-jar-lid-mushroom-cultivation]] and
chemical energy in the form of ATP, NADH, and FADH2. It is
the central metabolic hub of aerobic cells, connecting
catabolic pathways that [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]] nutrients with anabolic
pathways that build cellular components. Discovered by Hans
Krebs in 1937, the cycle takes place in the mitochondrial
matrix in eukaryotes and in the cytoplasm of prokaryotes.
Each turn produces three NADH, one FADH2, one GTP, and two
CO2 molecules.

## The Eight Reactions

### Citrate Formation

Acetyl-CoA condenses with oxaloacetate to form citrate,
catalyzed by citrate synthase. This aldol condensation is
irreversible under physiological conditions due to rapid
hydrolysis of citryl-CoA. Citrate synthase is allosterically
inhibited by ATP, NADH, succinyl-CoA, and citrate itself,
providing feedback regulation when [[adenosine-triphosphate-and-cellular-energy]] is
abundant.

### Isocitrate Formation

Aconitase catalyzes the isomerization of citrate to
isocitrate via the intermediate cis-aconitate. This
dehydration-rehydration sequence relocates the hydroxyl
group, converting a tertiary alcohol into a secondary
alcohol suitable for subsequent oxidation. Aconitase
contains an iron-sulfur cluster ([4Fe-4S]) essential for
catalytic activity.

### Oxidative Decarboxylation to Alpha-Ketoglutarate

Isocitrate dehydrogenase oxidizes isocitrate to alpha-
ketoglutarate, producing the first NADH and releasing the
first CO2. This is the rate-limiting step and a major
regulatory point. In mammals, two isozymes exist: an NAD-
dependent mitochondrial enzyme (regulated by ADP activation
and ATP/NADH inhibition) and an NADP-dependent cytoplasmic
isoform involved in biosynthesis.

### Alpha-Ketoglutarate to Succinyl-CoA

The alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex catalyzes
oxidative decarboxylation to succinyl-CoA, producing a
second NADH and CO2. This multienzyme complex is
mechanistically analogous to pyruvate dehydrogenase, using
thiamine pyrophosphate, lipoic acid, CoA, FAD, and NAD as
cofactors. It is strongly inhibited by succinyl-CoA, NADH,
and ATP.

### Succinyl-CoA to Succinate

Succinyl-CoA synthetase converts succinyl-CoA to succinate,
generating GTP (or ATP) through substrate-level
phosphorylation. This is one of only two substrate-level
phosphorylation steps in the entire aerobic oxidation of
glucose.

### Succinate to Fumarate

Succinate dehydrogenase oxidizes succinate to fumarate,
reducing FAD to FADH2. Unique among TCA cycle enzymes, it is
embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane and serves as
Complex II of the electron [[electron-transport-chain-mitochondrial-respiration]] chain, directly linking
the cycle to [[knf-fish-amino-acid]] synthesis, succinyl-CoA for
heme synthesis, and citrate for fatty acid synthesis. These
cataplerotic withdrawals must be replenished by anaplerotic
reactions, principally pyruvate carboxylase (pyruvate to
oxaloacetate), glutamate dehydrogenase, and the malic enzyme
pathway.
