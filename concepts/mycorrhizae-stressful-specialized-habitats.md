## Mycorrhizal Fungi in Stressful and Specialized Habitats

## Overview

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] enable plants to colonize and persist in environments that would otherwise be inhospitable. From salt marshes and coastal dunes to heavy-metal-contaminated soils and waterlogged forests, mycorrhizal associations extend the ecological amplitude of their host plants. This concept page examines how mycorrhizal fungi function in extreme and specialized habitats, drawing on research from diverse ecosystem types.

## Mycorrhizae in Salt Marshes and Estuarine Environments

### Occurrence in Halophytes

Mycorrhizal colonization of salt marsh plants was once considered unlikely due to the anaerobic, saline conditions. However, research has confirmed widespread AM associations in these habitats:

- **Hildebrandt et al. (2001)**: Documented AM colonization of halophytes in central European salt marshes, finding colonization levels comparable to terrestrial habitats despite the stressful conditions.
- **Hoefnagels et al. (1993)**: Found VA mycorrhizae in salt marshes in North Carolina, demonstrating that AM fungi persist and function in intertidal environments.
- **Brown and Bledsoe (1996)**: Studied spatial and temporal dynamics of mycorrhizas in *[[jaumea-carnosa]]*, a tidal saltmarsh halophyte, finding that colonization patterns respond to tidal inundation regime.
- **Johnson-Green et al. (2001)**: Investigated soil salinity and AM colonization of *Puccinella nuttallana*, documenting the relationship between salinity gradients and mycorrhizal function.
- **Miller (2000)**: Examined AM colonization of semi-aquatic grasses along a wide hydrological gradient, finding that mycorrhizal colonization persists even under periodically flooded conditions.

### Functional Significance in Salt Marshes

- **Cooke and Lefor (1990, 1998)**: Compared VA mycorrhizae in plants from disturbed and undisturbed coastal salt marshes, and surveyed mycorrhizal status of wetland plants, finding that mycorrhizae are more common in transition zones than in continuously inundated areas.
- **Cooke et al. (1993)**: Observed vertical distribution of VA mycorrhizae in roots of salt marsh grasses growing in saturated soils, noting that colonization decreases with depth as oxygen becomes limiting.
- **McHugh (2001)**: Studied effects of [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]], [[phosphorus-availability]], salinity, and inundation period on seedling growth of two saltmarsh grasses (*[[spartina-alterniflora]]* and *S. cynosuroides*) in nursery conditions.

### Anaerobic Fungi in Salt Marsh Soils

- **Padgett and Celio (1990)**: Discovered a newly recognized role for aerobic fungi in anaerobic salt marsh soils, suggesting that fungal fermentation may contribute to decomposition in waterlogged sediments.
- **Mansfield and Bärlocher (1993)**: Documented seasonal variation of fungal biomass in salt marsh sediments, finding peaks that correspond to periods of plant litter input.

### Mangroves and Aquatic Trees

- **Khan (1993)**: Documented occurrence and importance of mycorrhizae in aquatic trees of New South Wales, Australia.
- **Khan and Belik (1995)**: Reviewed occurrence and ecological significance of mycorrhizal symbiosis in aquatic plants, concluding that mycorrhizae are more widespread in wetland and aquatic habitats than previously assumed.

## Mycorrhizae in Coastal Dunes

- **Gemma and Koske (1997)**: Studied AM fungi in sand dune plants of the north Atlantic coast, finding that dune plants are heavily colonized despite the nutrient-poor, drought-prone conditions. AM fungi are critical for dune plant establishment, as they enhance P uptake from the sandy substrate.
- **Sylvia (1989)** (via Jasper, 1994): Noted that dune systems often have low indigenous mycorrhizal inoculum due to sand movement and vegetation disturbance, making inoculation important for dune restoration.

## Mycorrhizae in Restored and Degraded Ecosystems

### Surface Mine Reclamation

- **Marx (1980)**: Documented the role of mycorrhizae in forestation of surface mines, demonstrating that inoculation with ECM fungi dramatically improves seedling survival and growth on mine spoils.

### Desertified Mediterranean Ecosystems

- **Herrera et al. (1993)**: Showed successful inoculation of woody legumes with selected AM fungi and rhizobia to recover desertified Mediterranean ecosystems, establishing the value of dual inoculation for restoration.

### Ethiopian Highlands

- **Michelsen (1993)**: Demonstrated growth improvement of Ethiopian acacias by addition of VA mycorrhizal fungi or roots of native plants to non-sterile soil, providing a practical protocol for tropical restoration.

## Waterlogging and Mycorrhizal Function

- **Gadgil (1972)**: Studied the effect of waterlogging on mycorrhizas of radiata pine and Douglas fir, finding that prolonged flooding reduces ECM colonization and alters fungal community composition.
- **Cooke and Lefor (1990)**: Noted that mycorrhizal frequency is higher in transitional (less frequently inundated) zones of salt marshes compared to continuously flooded areas.

## Mycorrhizae and Soil Weathering

### Rock Weathering by Mycorrhizal Fungi

- **Chen et al. (2000)**: Reviewed weathering of rocks induced by lichen colonization, documenting how lichen fungi physically disrupt rock surfaces and chemically dissolve minerals through organic acid production.
- **Berthelin and Leyval (1982)**: Demonstrated the ability of symbiotic and non-symbiotic rhizospheric microflora of maize to weather micas and promote plant growth and nutrition.
- **Cromack et al. (1979)**: Documented calcium oxalate accumulation and soil weathering in mats of the hypogeous fungus *Hysterangium crassum*, showing that ECM fungal mats can significantly accelerate mineral weathering rates.

### Phosphate Solubilization

- **Lapeyrie et al. (1991)**: Demonstrated phosphate-solubilizing activity of ECM fungi in vitro, showing that some ECM species can dissolve insoluble calcium phosphates through organic acid exudation.
- **Azcón et al. (1976)**: Showed utilization of rock phosphate in alkaline soils by plants inoculated with mycorrhizal fungi and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria, establishing the potential for combined microbial inoculation to improve P availability.

## Mycorrhizae in Montane and Alpine Ecosystems

- **Barni and Siniscalco (2000)**: Studied vegetation dynamics and AM associations in old-field successions of the western Italian Alps, finding that mycorrhizal colonization changes predictably during succession and may influence the rate and trajectory of community development.
- **Largent et al. (1980)**: Documented occurrence of mycorrhizae on ericaceous and pyrolaceous plants in northern California, expanding the known distribution of multiple mycorrhizal types on single host species.

## Ericoid Mycorrhizae in Acidic Habitats

### Heavy Metal Tolerance

- **Bradley et al. (1982)**: Demonstrated the role of mycorrhizal infection in heavy metal resistance in ericaceous plants, finding that ERM fungi can sequester toxic metals in their tissues, reducing exposure to the host plant.
- **Dighton and Coleman (1992)**: Studied phosphorus relations of roots and mycorrhizas of *Rhododendron maximum* in the southern Appalachians, documenting ERM function in naturally acidic forest soils.

### Calcicole-Calcifuge Problem

- **Lee (1999)**: Reviewed the calcicole-calcifuge problem, discussing how mycorrhizal associations influence plant distribution across soil pH gradients. ERM plants are typically calcifuge (acid-loving), partly because their mycorrhizal fungi function optimally under low-pH conditions.

## Wetland Mycorrhizae: A Reappraisal

The traditional view that mycorrhizae are unimportant in wetlands has been challenged by accumulating evidence:

- **Khan and Belik (1995)**: Argued that mycorrhizae are ecologically significant in many aquatic and semi-aquatic habitats, not just terrestrial ones.
- **Miller (2000)**: Showed that AM colonization persists across a wide hydrological gradient, suggesting that mycorrhizal function is not limited to well-drained soils.
- **Cooke and Lefor (1998)**: Surveyed mycorrhizal status of Connecticut wetland plants and transition zones, finding that many wetland species form functional mycorrhizal associations.

The emerging picture is that mycorrhizae are important in a broader range of habitats than previously appreciated, including many that were considered too wet, too saline, or too contaminated for fungal symbiosis to function effectively. The ability of mycorrhizal fungi to buffer environmental stress extends the fundamental niche of their host plants and contributes to ecosystem resilience in the face of environmental change.
