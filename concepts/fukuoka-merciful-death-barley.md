# The Merciful Death for Barley — Japanese Agricultural Policy

## The Wheat Experiment

Forty years before Fukuoka wrote, increasing political hostility

Even after the farmer had taken great pains to grow the crop, it
would often rot during harvest. These varieties proved unreliable
and highly susceptible to disease.

Traditional Japanese varieties of rye and barley, by contrast, could
be harvested in May before the rainy season, making them comparatively
safe crops. Farmers had wheat cultivation forced upon them regardless.
Everyone laughed and said there was nothing worse than growing wheat,
but they patiently went along with government policy. The taste of the
ground and toasted American wheat was so terrible that people almost
choked on it.

## The Abandonment of Winter Grain

After the war, American wheat was again imported in large quantities,
causing the price of domestically grown wheat to fall. The government
simultaneously encouraged farmers to stop growing the traditional
[[fukuoka-breakdown-japanese-agriculture]] for
centuries -- was deliberately dismantled in favor of dependence on
imported grain.

## Fukuoka's Stand

About ten years before writing, Fukuoka was chosen to represent Ehime
Prefecture in NHK television's "Outstanding Farmer of the Year"
competition. A screening committee member asked him, "Mr. Fukuoka,
why don't you give up growing rye and barley?" He answered that rye
and barley are easy crops to raise, and by growing them in succession
with rice, the greatest number of calories can be produced from
Japan's fields.

It was made clear that no one who stubbornly goes against the will
of the Ministry of Agriculture could be named Outstanding Farmer.
Fukuoka responded that if that is what keeps someone from getting the
award, he was better off without it. One committee member later
confided that if he were to leave the university and take up farming,
he would farm as Fukuoka does -- rice in summer, barley and rye over
the winter.

## The "Merciful Death" Slogan

Around the same time, the government slogan for ending winter grain
cultivation called for "A merciful death" -- the practice of growing
winter grain and rice in succession should pass away quietly. But
"merciful death" was too gentle a term; Fukuoka argued that the
Ministry of Agriculture really wanted winter grain to "die in the
ditch." When he realized the program's main purpose was to promote
a rapid end to growing winter grain, he exploded in indignation.

Forty years earlier, the call had been to grow foreign wheat -- a
useless and impossible crop. Then Japanese varieties of rye and
barley were declared inferior to American grain. As the standard of
living rose, the word went out to eat meat, drink milk, and change
from rice to bread. Corn, soybeans, and wheat were imported in
ever-increasing quantities. Japanese agriculture adopted measures
that forced farmers into part-time jobs so they could buy the crops
they had been told not to grow.
