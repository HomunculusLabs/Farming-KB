# Mycorrhizal Inoculation

## Overview

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the roots of approximately 95% of all plant species. The fungi extend the effective root surface area by 700 to 1000 times, providing the plant with water, phosphorus, nitrogen, copper, zinc, molybdenum, and other nutrients in exchange for carbohydrates from root exudates. Inoculation introduces these fungi where they are absent or depleted.

## Types of Mycorrhizae

### Endomycorrhizae (VAM - Vesicular-Arbuscular Mycorrhizae)

The most common type. Fungal hyphae actually penetrate and grow inside the plant root cells, forming specialized structures called arbuscules (tree-like exchange organs) and vesicles (storage organs). Used by most shrubs, softwood trees, perennials, vegetables, and annuals.

### Ectomycorrhizae

Fungal hyphae grow around and between root cells but do not penetrate them. They form a visible sheath (mantle) around the root. Associated primarily with conifers and hardwood trees (birch, oak, beech, hickory).

### Ericaceous Mycorrhizae

Associated with plants in the heath family (rhododendrons, azaleas, blueberries). Not yet commercially available.

## Which Type to Use

| Plant Type | Mycorrhizal Type |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Conifers (pine, spruce, fir) | Ectomycorrhizal |
| Hardwood trees (birch, oak, beech, hickory) | Ectomycorrhizal |
| Most shrubs | Endomycorrhizal |
| Softwood trees | Endomycorrhizal |
| Perennials | Endomycorrhizal |
| Vegetables and annuals | Endomycorrhizal |
| Heath family (rhododendrons, azaleas, blueberries) | Ericaceous |

## Benefits

- Dramatically increased [[phosphorus-availability|phosphorus uptake]] from chemically locked-up sources
- Extended root reach into soil pores too small for roots
- Improved water uptake and drought tolerance
- Access to nitrogen, copper, zinc, and molybdenum
- Production of glomalin (sequesters metals, improves soil aggregation)
- Protection against root pathogens through physical barriers and competition
- Interconnected fungal networks can transport phosphorus between different plants

## Inoculation Methods

### For New Plantings

Commercial mycorrhizal preparations come as dry powders or grains. Mycorrhizal spores must come into direct contact with roots within 24 hours of being exposed to moisture in order to grow. Apply by:

1. Sprinkling spores directly onto roots before planting
2. Dipping roots into spore mixture before planting
3. Rolling seeds in spore mixture
4. Watering in after planting

### For Existing Trees and Shrubs

More difficult to colonize established plants. Options include:

1. Soil drench with [[compost-tea-recipe-guide|compost tea]] containing fungal inoculants
2. Collecting local soil from root zones of thriving plants of the same species (contains spores)
3. Looking for mushrooms under the drip line as evidence of existing mycorrhizal associations

### Collecting Local Inoculum

You can collect local mycorrhizal fungi by taking soil from root areas of thriving plants. While endomycorrhizal fungi are invisible without a microscope, ectomycorrhizal fungi may produce visible mushrooms that are great spore sources. Roll seeds or roots in collected soil to inoculate.

## When Inoculation Is Needed

Mycorrhizal fungi are ubiquitous in established natural systems and usually only need to be introduced when:

- Starting new planting areas (especially from seed indoors in sterile media)
- Growing in compost-heavy mixes that lack mycorrhizal spores
- Soils have been degraded by compaction, repeated tilling, or chemical applications
- No mushrooms are observed around existing trees and shrubs
- Trees and shrubs are not performing well

## Key Points

- Get the right type of mycorrhizae for the plant (endo vs ecto)
- Spores need direct root contact and moisture to germinate
- Add fungal foods (humic acids, kelp, fish hydrolysate) to support establishment
- Avoid tillage which destroys existing mycorrhizal networks
- Mycorrhizal fungi are fragile -- compaction and anaerobic conditions kill them
