# Transition from Organic to Natural Farming

## Overview

Fukuoka observed that organic farming advocates had begun sensing the limits of their method on large farms and were casting about for other methods to supersede organic farming. He argued that the changeover to natural farming involves a **sweeping, Copernican transformation** — not a mere adjustment of techniques, but a fundamental shift in worldview. It is not something that can be accomplished overnight.

## The Problem with Organic Farming at Scale

During his 1979 tour of America, Fukuoka visited farms that had turned from the blind alley [[critique-of-scientific-agriculture]] and were attempting to make organic farming work. He found them baffled by a specific practical problem: **how to handle compost and manure on large acreages**.

With the help of machinery, farmers could feed hay and fodder to cattle and collect animal wastes, but they generally had trouble when it came to transporting that compost and manure out to the fields and spreading it over large areas. Even at the Rodale farm — the flagship of American organic agriculture — he saw large piles of compost and manure left exposed everywhere on the fields.

Fukuoka's response was characteristically direct: *"It's more important to create a grassy cover of green manure than to spend so much effort on composting."* People showed a great deal of interest in this idea. He felt that once the [[microdosing-theory-and-practice]] underlying his belief that composting is unnecessary take hold, it may be possible to convert even large farms from organic farming to natural farming.

## Why Composting Is Unnecessary

In Fukuoka's natural farming system, the living soil cover of clovers, weeds, and straw mulch replaces compost entirely. The green manure plants growing in place:

- Fix nitrogen from the atmosphere through their root systems
- Create humus through root exudation and natural leaf fall
- Protect soil structure from erosion and compaction
- Maintain soil moisture and biological activity
- Eliminate the need for collecting, transporting, and spreading organic matter

The energy and labour required to make, move, and apply compost represents a fundamental inefficiency that natural farming eliminates. Rather than managing decomposition in a pile and then distributing the result, natural farming lets decomposition happen in situ. The soil biology — fungi, bacteria, earthworms, and other organisms — does the work of decomposition [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]] without human intervention.

### The Practical Advantages

- **No compost piles to manage** — eliminating turning, watering, and monitoring
- **No transportation costs** — nutrients cycle where plants need them
- **No timing constraints** — green manures grow in synchrony with crop cycles
- **No risk of compost failures** — anaerobic conditions, [[green-cannabis-soil-flushing-nutrient-leaching]], or incomplete decomposition
- **Reduced labour** — the system maintains itself rather than requiring periodic intensive work sessions

### Why Organic Farmers Resist

The resistance to abandoning compost comes partly from the investment organic farmers have made in composting infrastructure and knowledge. It also comes from a deep-seated belief that soil fertility requires human management through organic matter addition. Fukuoka challenged this by demonstrating that undisturbed soil with living vegetation cover maintains and builds fertility without any external organic matter inputs.

## Large-Scale Natural Rice Farming

### Harlan Lundberg's California Conversion

The most dramatic evidence that natural farming can work at scale came from Fukuoka's encounter with Harlan Lundberg, a farmer with a 7,500-acre operation in the Chico area of California. After speaking with Fukuoka for half a day, Lundberg grew excited and decided right then and there to switch some of his fields over to natural farming.

Fukuoka was surprised. He had not anticipated that converting wet-rice cultivation in California to natural farming would be so feasible. California's climate proved suited to the cultivation of rice by natural methods. The conversion could start immediately by:

1. **Abandoning the tractor** — eliminating mechanical soil disturbance
2. **Changing the variety of rice and barley planted** — selecting varieties adapted to natural conditions
3. **Ceasing synthetic fertiliser and pesticide applications**

Moreover, because yields actually went up, Fukuoka believed that if the will existed, natural farming could take hold and spread throughout America's great farming belt. With the American temperament — pragmatic, results-oriented, and willing to try new approaches — he thought it quite possible that natural farming would succeed earlier in America than in Japan.

## The Hollow Mimicry Problem

Despite the encouraging signs, Fukuoka issued a strong caution: *"Until the spirit of [[korean-natural-farming]] [[fukuoka-western-eastern-philosophy-agriculture]] that rejects [[fukuoka-descartes-western-philosophy-nature]] is fully understood, any effort made is just hollow mimicry."*

Natural farming is more than just a means for growing healthy foods or enriching the earth. Without the philosophical and [[fukuoka-textdoc-do-nothing-movement-spiritual-foundation]], techniques borrowed from natural farming become just another set of inputs and methods — essentially the same mindset as conventional agriculture with different materials.

### Signs of Hollow Mimicry

- Adopting natural [[holzer-alpine-farming-techniques]] while maintaining the goal of maximising production
- Treating natural farming as a product to be marketed rather than a way of life
- Applying techniques selectively without understanding the whole system
- Seeking to "improve" natural farming through additions of inputs

## Natural Farming Beginnings in America

Fukuoka found natural farming in America in its earliest stages. Several entry points had emerged:

### Natural Diet as Gateway

Most often, the incentive [[query-how-do-clay-seed-balls-work-for-natural-farming]] came from an interest in natural diet. People who had changed their eating habits wanted food grown without chemicals. This dietary motivation provided a practical entry point even before philosophical understanding developed.

### Zen Centres and Back-to-Nature Groups

More than 250 Zen centres reportedly operated in the U.S., most with farms. Rather than viewing these as outposts for Eastern thought, Fukuoka saw them as Zen farms that had totally merged with the American scene. On weekends, people from cities participated freely in farm work and Zen training irrespective of religion or sect. They enjoyed farm work and Zen training as a means of achieving serenity — places where Zen and agriculture were united.
