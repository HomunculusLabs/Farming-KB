# Cold Air Drainage and Frost Pocket Management

Toby Hemenway provides detailed guidance on understanding and
managing cold air drainage, one of the most important yet often
overlooked factors in [[mollison-designers-forest-condensation-and-micro-climate-rainfall]] design. Cold air is
heavier than warm air and flows downhill like water, pooling in
low spots and creating frost pockets that can devastate gardens
and orchards. Understanding this phenomenon allows gardeners to
avoid planting mistakes and even use cold air patterns to their
advantage.

## The Physics of Cold Air

Just as capturing warm air is important for microclimate
management, eliminating unwanted cold air is equally critical.
Cold air drains downhill, so it is important not to block its
escape routes. Hemenway tells of gardeners who planted a fine
stand of bamboo just downslope of their garden, only to find
that it stopped cold air drainage, turning their garden into a
chilly frost pocket. They moved the bamboo, and the garden
warmed considerably.

The mixing of air is a key element in creating microclimates.
When wind sweeps across a bare plain, air at head height or
above mixes well, and temperatures are fairly even from head
height to a few hundred feet above. But at the ground, friction
slows the air and prevents good mixing. On a sunny day, the
temperature an inch from the ground can be 20 degrees hotter
than at eye level. This steep vertical temperature gradient
creates uneven conditions.

When trees, walls, and houses are added to a bare landscape,
friction of wind against obstacles causes turbulence. Eddies
of stillness form, and pockets of hot or cold air gather.
Microclimates are born from both vertical and horizontal air
mixing irregularities.

## Slope and Elevation Effects

Since cold air sinks, orchardists know that fruit trees on high
ground can survive frosts that blacken the blossoms of trees
just a few feet lower. This knowledge can be used strategically.
In regions with late frosts, gardeners often set fruit trees in
the coldest part of their yard, such as a low spot or on the
northeast side of the house. The trees stay chilly and delay
budding until late spring, after the final frosts have passed.

Conversely, cold-sensitive plants can be placed on higher
ground or on slopes where cold air drains away rather than
pooling. The ideal frost-free site is on a gentle mid-slope,
above the valley floor where cold air collects but below the
ridge top where exposure to wind is greatest.

## Observing Microclimates

Hemenway recommends practical methods for finding warm and cool
spots in the garden:

- After a light frost, observe which places lose their frost
  coating first. These are ideal sites for starting early
  flowers or cool-weather vegetables.
- Watch plants themselves for clues. If you have multiple
  specimens of the same species, note which ones hold their
  leaves longer and leaf out earlier. These plants are in
  favorable microclimates.
- Pets can be unexpected microclimate indicators. Dogs and
  cats instinctively seek out the warmest spots for napping.
- Insects are savvy microclimate users. Bees often sleep inside
  daffodil blossoms because the flower interior can be 15
  degrees Fahrenheit warmer than outside air, due mainly to
  reduced air mixing.

## Color and Soil Effects

Color creates microclimates too. A dark wall absorbs heat while
a light one reflects light. Researchers found that peaches
grown against a dark wall grew faster but did not ripen fruit
any earlier than in the open. However, when peaches and grapes
were grown against a light-colored wall, fruit set was heavier
and earlier. The reflected light had more effect on fruiting
than the heat.

Soil color matters as well. Light-colored soils heat up slower
than dark ones. Maori farmers darken their soil with charcoal
to help it warm in spring. Tibetans toss dark rocks on snow-
covered fields to speed the thaw. Soil density also affects
temperature: dense clay warms far slower than fluffy, sandy
soil. Mulch, with its insulating air pockets, can keep soil
from freezing but greatly retards spring warming. Pull mulch
aside when temperatures rise to speed soil heating.

## Designing for Cold Air Flow

When designing a garden, consider these cold air management
principles:

- Never plant dense barriers on the downhill side of sensitive
  plantings; they will trap cold air and create frost pockets.
- Use fences or hedges with gaps at the base to allow cold air
  to drain through.
- Place cold-hardy plants in low spots and frost-tender plants
  on slopes or high ground.
- Use [[gaias-garden-suntraps-and-microclimate-protection]] and
  thermal mass walls to counteract cold air pooling.
- Deciduous trees on the south side cool a house in summer
  while letting in winter light. Evergreens on the windward
  side block winter winds.

By understanding how cold air moves through the landscape,
gardeners can extend the growing season by weeks, reduce frost
damage, and place plants in locations where they will thrive
with minimal intervention.
