# Magic Mushroom Smartshop Retail in Europe

The commercial retail of hallucinogenic mushrooms became a
significant phenomenon in Europe, centered primarily in the
Netherlands. The [[psilocybin-mushrooms]]
through licensed "smartshops" for over a decade. Key features of
the Dutch system:

- **Legal basis**: Fresh psilocybin mushrooms occupied a legal
  grey area. While psilocybin itself was scheduled under the
  Opium Act, fresh mushrooms were not explicitly listed. Dried
  mushrooms were treated as a preparation and were illegal.
- **Licensing**: Smartshops operated as regular retail businesses
  with local government permits. They were subject to standard
  business regulations but not pharmaceutical or drug-specific
  controls.
- **Product range**: Shops typically stocked 10-20 mushroom
  varieties, sold by weight (fresh, in sealed containers). Prices
  ranged from approximately €7-15 per 10-15g portion.
- **Staff guidance**: Shop employees provided dosage advice and
  information about effects. Some shops distributed printed guides.
- **Customer base**: Primarily tourists, with domestic users
  constituting a smaller segment. Young adults (18-25) were the
  largest demographic.

## Prevalence of Use

The EMCDDA report documents lifetime prevalence of magic mushroom
use across EU countries based on general population surveys:

| Country | Lifetime Prevalence (Adults) |
|---|---|
| United Kingdom | 6.8-8.6% |
| Netherlands | 4.3-7.4% |
| Czech Republic | 4.3% |
| Ireland | 4.2% |
| Germany | 1.9-3.2% |
| France | 1.0-1.7% |
| Belgium | 0.6-1.5% |
| Spain | 1.1% |

Prevalence was highest among young adults (15-24), with the UK
consistently showing the highest rates in Europe.

## Online Market Expansion

As internet commerce grew, the mushroom market expanded beyond
physical smartshops:

- **Online retailers**: Websites based in the Netherlands and
  other countries began shipping mushroom grow kits, spore
  syringes, and dried material. Jurisdictional ambiguity made
  enforcement difficult.
- **Grow kits**: Pre-sterilized substrate jars and grow kits
  became a major product category. These contained legal materials
  (substrate, spores) but enabled [[salvia-divinorum]], kratom) as their main revenue stream

## Regulatory Changes and Closure

The Dutch tolerance policy ended in 2007-2008 following high-
profile incidents:

- A 2007 incident involving a French tourist's death in Amsterdam
  (though the causal link to mushrooms was disputed) catalyzed
  political pressure
- The Dutch government reclassified fresh psilocybin mushrooms
  as a Schedule I substance in December 2008
- All smartshops ceased selling mushrooms, though many remained
  open selling other products
- The ban was estimated to reduce but not eliminate mushroom use,
  with online markets and home cultivation filling some of the gap
- "Truffle" products (sclerotia of *Psilocybe tampanensis* and
  related species) remained legal for several more years before
  also being restricted

## Patterns of Use

Survey data from the EMCDDA revealed consumption patterns:

- Most users consumed mushrooms infrequently (1-5 times per year)
- Group settings were the most common context (parties, nature,
  festivals)
- The UK clubbing survey found 5.2% of clubbers used mushrooms
  in the past month
- Mushrooms were perceived as "natural" and therefore safer than
  synthetic drugs by many users
- Users frequently combined mushrooms with cannabis to modulate
  effects or extend the experience

## Impact on Public Health

The EMCDDA assessed the public health burden of mushroom retail:

- Hospital presentations related to mushroom use were relatively
  rare compared to other substances
- Acute toxicity was low; most emergency presentations involved
  psychological distress rather than physical harm
- The retail environment provided some harm reduction benefits
  (dosage information, product consistency) compared to foraged
  or home-grown material
- Tourist use in unfamiliar settings increased risk of adverse
  psychological reactions
