# Mushroom Habitats and Ecological Niches

Each mushroom species occupies a specific ecological niche defined by substrate, temperature, moisture, associated organisms, and geography. Understanding these niches is essential for both identification and cultivation. From *Mycelium Running* by [[stamets-mycoremediation-principles]].

## Overview

Mushrooms occupy virtually every terrestrial habitat on Earth. Their ecological niches are defined by their nutritional strategy (saprophytic, mycorrhizal, parasitic, endophytic), the substrate they colonize, the environmental conditions they require, and their relationships with other organisms. The key to successful cultivation is matching the right species to the right habitat.

## Major Habitat Categories

### Forest Habitats

#### Old-Growth Forests
The richest mushroom habitat. More than 527 mushroom species grow in old-growth forests; at least 109 are native to the Pacific Northwest. More than 2,000 species of mycorrhizal mushrooms associate with Douglas firs alone.

Key species: agarikon (*Fomitopsis officinalis*), noble polypore (*Bridgeoporus nobilissimus*), matsutake (*Tricholoma magnivelare*), chanterelles, boletes, hedgehogs, cauliflower mushroom (*Sparassis crispa*)

Old-growth forests are endangered: less than 5% of Pacific Northwest old-growth survives today. These forests are irreplaceable reservoirs of fungal genetic diversity.

#### Second-Growth and Managed Forests
Younger forests with different fungal communities. Saprophytic species are more common as woody debris accumulates. Mycorrhizal diversity is reduced compared to old-growth.

Key species: oyster mushrooms (*Pleurotus*), chicken-of-the-woods (*Laetiporus*), clustered woodlover (*Hypholoma*), shaggy mane (*Coprinus comatus*)

#### Clear-Cut and Disturbed Forests
Morels (*Morchella*) are classic disturbance-adapted fungi, appearing after fires, logging, or floods. These pioneer saprophytes use simple sugars and modify substrates for secondary colonizers.

### Wood-Based Habitats

#### Living Trees (Parasitic/Endophytic)
Some fungi grow on living trees as parasites or endophytes. *Armillaria* species cause devastating root rot. *Fomes fomentarius* (amadou) can behave endophytically, maintaining a benign presence within the tree. *Inonotus obliquus* (chaga) forms sclerotia on living birches.

#### Dead and Dying Wood (Saprophytic)
The primary habitat for most cultivated mushrooms. Wood type matters enormously:
- **Hardwoods** (oak, alder, beech, maple, poplar): Support the widest range of species; preferred by shiitake, oyster, maitake, lion's mane
- **Conifers** (fir, hemlock, pine, spruce): Fewer species; some like *Hypholoma capnoides* can decompose even cedar and redwood (20+ year aged)
- **Fruitwoods**: Notoriously poor for shiitake cultivation

Tree density affects timing: denser hardwoods like oak produce for up to 6 years; rapidly decomposing hardwoods like alder produce in 6-12 months but last ~3 years.

### Grassland and Meadow Habitats

Key species: meadow mushroom (*Agaricus campestris*), shaggy mane (*Coprinus comatus*), agaricus (*Agaricus bernardii*), blewit (*Lepista nuda*), parasol (*Macrolepiota procera*)

### Garden and Compost Habitats

Key species: button/portobello (*Agaricus bisporus*), garden giant (*Stropharia rugoso-annulata*), wine cap, almond portobello (*Agaricus brasiliensis*)

### Urban and Disturbed Habitats

Mushrooms appear in surprising urban locations: along roadsides, in mulch beds, on construction wood, in landscaping. Utility poles, railroad ties, and treated wood can support species with powerful enzymes (*Laetiporus sulphureus* grows on CCA-treated poles).

## Environmental Parameters

### Temperature
Temperature is a critical factor determining which species fruit and when:

| Category | Temperature Range | Key Species |
|---|---|---|
| Cold-loving | 30-50°F | Enokitake, winter mushroom |
| Cool-season | 50-65°F | Shiitake, oyster, blewit |
| Warm-season | 60-80°F | Agaricus, chicken-of-the-woods |
| Hot-season | 70-90°F | Some tropical *Pleurotus* |

### Moisture
- Mycelium requires consistent moisture for growth but can survive extended dry periods
- Mushrooms typically fruit after rain events or seasonal moisture changes
- Myceliated wood chips retain moisture far better than bare soil
- Humidity fluctuations can damage incubating logs — shade cloth or tarps help

### Light
- Most mushrooms don't need light for mycelial growth
- Light triggers fruiting body formation (mushroom development) in most species
- UV light converts ergosterols to vitamin D2 in the fruiting body
- Some mycelia are phototropic (orient toward light)

### pH
- Most cultivated mushrooms prefer slightly acidic to neutral conditions (pH 5.5-7.0)
- Some species like *Agaricus* prefer more alkaline conditions (pH 6.5-7.5)
- Mycelium can modify substrate pH through metabolic secretions

## Substrate Preferences by Species

| Species | Preferred Wood | Secondary Wood | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shiitake | Oak, beech | Alder, poplar, eucalyptus | Avoid fruitwoods |
| Oyster | Alder, cottonwood | Many hardwoods, straw, paper | Very adaptable |
| Maitake | Oak | Beech, maple | Brown rot specialist |
| Lion's mane | Oak, beech | Maple, birch | White rot |
| Reishi | Oak, plum | Many hardwoods | Also grows on hemlock |
| Chicken-of-the-woods | Oak | Beech, hemlock (L. conifericola) | Avoids conifers (mostly) |

## Geographic Distribution

Mushrooms have evolved within specific biogeographic ranges:
- **Shiitake**: Native only to Japan, Korea, China — not found wild in North America
- **Matsutake**: Circumpolar northern hemisphere; prized in Japan, Korea, Pacific Northwest
- **Chanterelles**: Widespread in northern temperate forests
- **Porcini**: Europe, North America, Asia; associated with pines and oaks
- **Agarikon**: Restricted to old-growth Pacific Northwest forests — endangered habitat

## Phenology (Seasonal Timing)

Mushrooms fruit in response to seasonal cues:
- **Spring**: Morels, oysters, shiitake (early flushes), pheasant back
- **Summer**: Chicken-of-the-woods, chanterelles, garden giant, boletes
- **Fall**: Matsutake, hedgehog, lion's mane, blewits, shaggy parasol, enoki
- **Winter**: Enokitake, velvet foot, some oyster species

The exact timing varies by species, latitude, elevation, and seasonal weather patterns. El Nino years can dramatically shift fruiting times.
