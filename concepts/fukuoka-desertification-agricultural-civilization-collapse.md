# Desertification and the Collapse of Agricultural Civilizations

## Overview

One of [[masanobu-fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy]]'s most striking arguments in "The Road Back to Nature" is his historical analysis of desertification. He observes that the three great birthplaces of grain — Mesopotamia, China, and Egypt — all became deserts after the onset of cultivation and civilization. Rather than attributing these transformations to climate change, Fukuoka places the blame squarely on human [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]]: deforestation, soil depletion, and the destruction of vegetative cover. He extends this analysis to contemporary California, arguing that modern mechanized agriculture is following the same destructive pattern.

This framework challenges the conventional narrative that deserts are primarily natural phenomena or the result of shifts in climate. For Fukuoka, desertification is overwhelmingly a human creation — the inevitable consequence of agriculture that works against nature rather than with it.

## The Three Great Grain Birthplaces

Wild, primitive wheat arose on the Mesopotamian plains in the Middle East. Wild rice originated in southern China, Burma, and remote parts of Assam. Rice also existed since antiquity in the Saharan region of Africa. Ancient humans who settled in these three great birthplaces of grain began cultivating the wild rice and wheat that grew there, coming by an abundance of food.

It was here that the Mesopotamian, Chinese, and Egyptian civilizations were built up. But today, each of these regions has been totally transformed to desert. All that remains are vast, desolate ruins.

> "I strongly doubt that this was the result of changes in climate and desertification that created food shortages, scattered people and destroyed civilizations."

Fukuoka challenges the conventional explanation head-on. The standard view holds that climate shifts caused rainfall to decrease, which led to crop failures, famine, and the collapse of civilizations. But Fukuoka argues the causal arrow points in the opposite direction: human activity caused the vegetation to die, which altered weather patterns and reduced rainfall.

## The Mechanism of Destruction

What actually happened, according to Fukuoka, followed a clear and predictable sequence:

First, trees were cut down in the name of civilization. Forests were cleared for farmland, timber, and fuel.

Second, advances were made in [[cho-poultry-natural-farming-methods]] and slash-and-burn agriculture was adopted. These practices stripped the land of its vegetative cover and depleted the soil of its organic matter.

Third, the depleted soil caused a decline in vegetation. As plant diversity diminished, the ground became exposed to sun and wind.

Fourth, the loss of vegetative cover altered the local microclimate. With fewer plants to transpire water vapor into the atmosphere, cloud formation decreased and rainfall diminished.

Finally, the process of desertification was set into motion — a self-reinforcing cycle in which less vegetation led to less rain, which led to even less vegetation.

> "What actually happened was that trees were cut down in the name of civilization. Advances were made in farming methods and slash-and-burn agriculture adopted. All this depleted the soil, causing a decline in vegetation and setting the process of desertification into motion."

## California: A Desert in the Making

Fukuoka's travels through California in 1979 convinced him that the same process was repeating itself in modern America. He observed that as soon as one leaves San Francisco, the color of the land turns straw-yellow — a clear transition to desert climate.

His investigation led him to conclude that California's desertification was triggered by the introduction of foxtail grass by the Spaniards. The foxtail seeds set and mature around June, and the plant grows to such density that other species cannot establish themselves. The fields and hills turned yellow, and the seeds were spread by birds and beasts across the entire region.

The result was dramatic. At thirty degrees Celsius, the heat reflected from the yellow ground surface raised temperatures to forty degrees — turning the region into a scorching desert.

> "Perhaps rain falls from above meteorologically, but philosophically it falls from below. If there is vegetation on the ground, then water vapor rises here, condenses into clouds, and falls back down to earth as rain."

## Deficit Farming

Fukuoka coined the term "deficit farming" to describe modern agriculture's systematic depletion of soil fertility. In California, he found that the soil near the surface was seriously depleted from being irrigated and worked with twenty- or thirty-ton machines four or five times a year until it became hard as clay. The sun baked and dried this, creating fist-sized cracks in the surface.

Yet the ground at the corners of the fields, where the caterpillar tractors didn't pass, was soft, rich soil — proof that the land itself was not inherently poor, but had been impoverished by agricultural practices.

Scientists claimed that [[fukuoka-natural-livestock-farming]] enriches the land, but Fukuoka found the opposite to be true wherever he looked. The Spaniards were the first to raise cattle in America, and the land grew poorer instead of richer. Cattle ranching reduces vegetation variety, and the application of modern farming methods creates a negative cycle.

## Agriculture Run Amok

Fukuoka was shocked by what he found in the American heartland. Farmers relied almost entirely on monoculture — nothing but corn in the midwest corn belt, soybeans state after state to the east. The crops were not raised by natural forces but processed with petroleum energy. Even the water was often piped in from hundreds of miles away and sprayed over fields with sprinklers.

When this water evaporates, it draws salt up from within the soil. This continuous deposition of salt in the topsoil eventually turns irrigated land into a salt field — the same process that destroyed ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt.

> "When the soil gives out and the fields are abandoned by ranchers, fruit growers come in. They set up sprinklers on the weary land, spread [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]], and grow plums, apricots, and oranges. This no longer is crop production under natural conditions; it is farming based on petroleum energy."

## The Larger Pattern

Fukuoka saw the same pattern repeating worldwide. The land near cultural centers is invariably poor, and the very proximity of a cultural flowering attests to the part played by man in that desertification. In Africa, Iran, and Iraq, vast areas that once flourished have become arid and unproductive through injury inflicted by human activity.

When man cuts down trees and his livestock feeds on grasses, the varieties of plant life invariably dwindle. The simplified vegetative cover is easily destroyed. If the land is taken over by yellow grasses such as foxtail, the increased reflection of sunlight raises ground temperature, upsetting weather patterns and causing the rapid evaporation of ground moisture, transforming the land into desert.

> "The land does not become a desert because the water disappears. Rather, there are fundamental conditions preceding and underlying the appearance of that water; namely, the perishing of trees and other plants."

## Reversing Desertification

Fukuoka proposed a radical approach to combating desertification: rather
than trying to bring in water through dams and irrigation (which often
fails due to salt buildup), the first priority should be to cover the
ground with a mantle of vegetation by broadcasting the seeds of many
different kinds of plants over a broad area.

Even if 99 percent of the seeds fail, some will take hold somewhere.
This germination provides precious clues about what can grow in that
environment. The process should continue with full expectation of
failure, seeding again the second and third year. Any species showing
even slight survival should be reseeded.

This approach requires no dams, no irrigation systems, no soil tests —
only seeds and patience. It is, in essence, do-nothing farming applied
at landscape scale. In places where the soil still contains clay, there
is hope. Fukuoka told the young people of Somalia: "This earth is not
yet dead. The land must be awakened from its slumber with natural
farming."

## The Seed Ball Solution for Arid Lands

Fukuoka's seed ball technique proved particularly suited to
desertification reversal. Seeds encased in clay pellets can survive
the harsh conditions of arid environments — the clay retains enough
moisture for germination while protecting seeds from being eaten by
birds or blown away by wind.

In practice, Fukuoka would mix seeds of many different species — grains,
legumes, vegetables, and even tree seeds — encase them in clay, and
scatter them broadly over degraded land. The variety ensured that at
least some species would find conditions suitable for growth.

> "If cactuses grow on arid land, then other succulents will grow
> there as well. Stonecrop, amaranthus, and wormwood will take hold
> at an early stage. Or perhaps plants of the lily family such as
> wild rocambole, leek, and garlic will be found to grow."

The key insight is that nature itself, not human engineering, knows
best which plants will thrive in a given environment. The farmer's
role is simply to offer the seeds and let nature make the selection.

## A Warning for Modern Civilization

Fukuoka's analysis carries a profound warning. Up until about twenty
years before writing, he had been optimistic. Even ten years before, he
thought that if the supply of energy ran out, mankind would be able to
get by practicing [[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]]. But he came to realize these hopes
were wishful.

> "Human intelligence has overdeveloped and become sidetracked. Today,
> man has metamorphosed into a creature that can remain unconcerned
> even if it loses sight of and destroys nature and God."

The destruction of the world's great agricultural civilizations was
not an accident of climate but the predictable outcome of farming
methods that treated soil as an inert medium rather than a living
system. The same pattern is repeating today across the globe, from
California's Central Valley to the Sahel.
