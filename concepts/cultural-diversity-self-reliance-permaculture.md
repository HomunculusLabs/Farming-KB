# Cultural Diversity and Self-Reliance in Permaculture Design

Cultural diversity — the variety of human languages, traditions, food systems, and ways of understanding the world — is both nested within and intimately connected to biological diversity. In [[permaculture]], cultural diversity is not merely a value to preserve but a practical resource for designing sustainable systems. As global industrial culture erases both biological and cultural diversity, permaculture offers a framework for constructing new cultures of place from the remnants of many traditions.

## Language Diversity and Loss

Human cultural diversity is nested within the bioregional diversity that gave rise to it. Even in Europe, where centuries of empires and nation-states have tended to homogenize culture and punish diversity, regional variety redevelops as soon as central pressure relaxes. The diversity of dialects and cultures from one region to another reflects the constantly renewed power of the land as a formative force in human culture.

The scale of language loss is staggering and accelerating:

- Europe is home to less than 4% of the world's languages — likely reflecting past losses as much as inherently lower diversity
- About **90% of Australia's 250 Aboriginal languages** are near extinction
- Only seven Aboriginal languages have more than 1,000 speakers
- Only two or three are likely to survive the next 50 years
- The predicted loss of most of the world's languages in the next century **rivals biodiversity loss** as a devastating consequence of industrial culture

This represents a direct loss of **indigenous knowledge and local sustainable design**, most of which has never been documented or passed on. Every language encodes unique ways of understanding local environments, seasonal patterns, ecological relationships, and management practices.

## Cultural Globalization and Its Impacts

### The Modernity Paradox

Most people today find themselves with one foot in a tradition of place and one in modernity — which they frequently perceive as the culture of some other (dominant) people. For Aboriginal Australians it is white culture; for many people worldwide it is American culture, increasingly delivered by media and the internet.

Elements of local cultural domination persist, but they often disguise the fact that "modernity" delivered by media and corporations represents the **greatest cultural imperialism the world has ever seen**. Predominantly Anglo-American in origin, with English as its lingua franca, modern global capitalism driven by fossil-fuel wealth is consuming human cultural, agricultural, and natural diversity and replacing it with a global monoculture at all levels.

### The Normalization of Modernity

Global culture is accepted as normality, while one's own tradition becomes an anachronistic leftover. Whether people view modernity positively or negatively, they generally understand it in relation to their own tradition. Few people recognize:

- The **commonalities between all traditions of place** — shared patterns of local adaptation
- The **unique nature of modernity** as a global culture of no-place, disconnected from specific landscapes and ecological conditions

This asymmetry makes the usual comparison between "local" and "global" misleading, because it assumes the global culture of no-place is the normal baseline against which local traditions are measured.

### Cultural Survival Requires Connection to Nature

Indigenous and traditional people widely recognize that removing culture from nature, land, food, and practical expression causes the most valued aspects — language, kinship, spiritual beliefs — to become disconnected and eventually dead tradition. A culture that exists only in books, museums, and ceremonies, severed from its ecological foundation, is already extinct in functional terms.

## New Cultures of Place

### Permaculture as Cultural Reconstruction

Permaculture uses patterns common to traditional cultures for design principles and models. The diversity of design solutions, strategies, techniques, and species constitutes a toolkit for constructing **new cultures of place**. The imperative is clear: wherever we live, we must become new indigenes.

Some may perceive this as "cultural appropriation" to be resisted. Holmgren views it instead as contributing to recognition of the value of traditional culture, knowledge, and biological diversity — a prerequisite to "fair cultural trade." The key conditions are:

- **Sources must be clearly acknowledged and respected**
- Use of knowledge should not be critical to generating substantial private or corporate wealth
- Indigenous knowledge should be recognized as an important foundation, not a resource to be extracted

### Hybrid Vigor in Cultural Reconstruction

The sharing of experiences between traditional peoples provides understanding of **diversity within basic patterns** that are widespread or even universal. These commonalities between traditions of place offer a powerful alternative to the usual local-vs-global comparison.

Holmgren assumes globalization will run its course to the extent allowed by non-renewable energy and resources. After that, new cultural and natural biodiversity will need to be constructed from **remnants of many different systems**, both natural and cultural. The image is one of collecting pieces for a new jigsaw puzzle — a search to understand and value what nature and other people contribute, combined with determination to create new indigenous cultures of place from the best of all relevant diversity.

This is not nostalgia for a lost past but **active design for a sustainable future** using the best available elements from all traditions.

## Self-Reliance and Diversity of Yield

### The Diversity Imperative in Home Food Production

The application of self-reliance to food supply has enormous implications for diversity within production systems. In current high-energy systems, marketplace diversity provides for people's needs even though production systems feeding those markets are almost all monocultures. Growing our own food immediately confronts us with the reality that people have diverse needs, so systems supplying those needs must be diverse in their products.

### Affluent Countries

Holmgren argues that gardening should be regarded as serious agriculture, requiring a major increase in skilled food-growers who can feed families and neighbors from food gardens. For garden agriculture to begin replacing central market systems, the **diversity of yields** possible from those systems will be a key success factor.

A sobering reality: not even the Garden of Eden could provide the ingredient diversity that many people have grown accustomed to from supermarkets. Therefore, gardening strategies that maximize yield diversity will be most successful in attracting consumers away from high-energy dependencies. This may explain the popularity of "permaculture gardens" over traditional food gardens, which tend to produce small versions of market gardens — great abundance of a few varieties with little variation or surprise.

The rapidly increasing popularity of **heritage vegetable varieties** can be attributed to politics, novelty-seeking, and practical need, but it is having the effect of maintaining diversity abandoned by agribusiness.

### Poor Countries

For the urban and landless poor, gardens provide the only nutritional balance to a monocultural staple diet. A moderate diversity of fresh food — including animal protein from poultry and small livestock — can mean the difference between bare survival and well-being.

Cuba provides one of the best models: after the collapse of the Soviet Union and under US economic blockade, the remarkable growth of urban and organic agriculture demonstrates redevelopment of diverse fresh food production to meet local needs. Cuba is relatively poor yet urbanized, with a long history of modern market-based agriculture.

### Diverse Sources of Supply

In any incremental development of less energy-dependent systems, **diversity of supply sources** is important. A practical approach might combine:

- A home garden for fresh produce
- A local market or subscription farm for items not easily grown at home
- Barter with friends and neighbors
- Some purchases of organic produce from the central market system

This layered approach provides both fresh food security and a gradual transition away from total dependence on centralized systems.

## Strategies for Diversity at Melliodora

The strategies applied at [[melliodora]] (Holmgren's home and demonstration site) to maintain food production diversity include:

- **Physical design** — raised beds, keyhole layouts suited to sequential sowings of small batches
- **Microclimatic exploitation** — using variation created by built environment to grow early or climatically marginal crops
- **Temporal diversity** — varieties suited to early and late yields extending the harvest season
- **Storage and preservation** — varieties suited to storage and preservation providing year-round food security
- **Semi-wild species** — self-sown varieties and species that provide yield when more favored crops fail, serving as a biological safety net

## The Dynamics of Diversity: Proliferation and Culling

### The Diversity Debate in Permaculture

A common misunderstanding is that permaculture's emphasis on diversity means a random mix of species creates stability. [[bill-mollison]] corrected this: it is the **number of functional connections between species**, not the number of species, that creates stability. Zoos and botanical gardens are very diverse but not stable because they lack the functional connections contributing to self-regulation.

### The Natural Progression: Proliferation Then Culling

Experienced food gardeners balance fascination with diversity against the need to concentrate on what produces the greatest weight and nutritional value for the least input. After years of experimentation, natural culling occurs — removing plants that don't perform well and concentrating on proven producers.

This is not a failure of the diversity principle but its **successful operation**. The process follows a clear pattern:

1. **Proliferation** — plant many species and varieties to discover what works in the specific local conditions
2. **Observation** — learn which species thrive, which struggle, which produce useful yields
3. **Culling** — remove underperformers to concentrate resources on proven successes
4. **Refinement** — the resulting system is more functional precisely because diversity provided the raw material for selection

### Why Initial Diversity Is Necessary

Designing self-reliant systems always proceeds without complete knowledge of what will work, for several reasons:

- **Lack of local sustainable traditions** as models — industrial agriculture has destroyed much traditional knowledge
- **Inherent complexity and individuality** of integrated systems designed for energy descent
- **Novel factors** — new species, knowledge, or technology (trace elements, [[drip-irrigation]])
- **Co-evolutionary forces** that operate once systems are established, producing unexpected outcomes

This inherent uncertainty provides strong incentive to experiment broadly. Much of what is tried will fail, but novel solutions emerge that could never have been predicted. The culling process produces the final functional system, but **without the initial diversity, there is literally nothing to cull**.

### Forestry Models

In forestry, direct seeding generally produces far healthier and better timber than planted trees because hundreds of thousands of seedlings provide much greater diversity to thin out, leaving only the very best. Thinning regrowth forests reduces tree diversity but may increase overall forest diversity by allowing understorey species to thrive.

The key insight: it is fine to focus on culling for productivity as long as natural processes maintain diversity. The gains from culling are made possible by nature's diversity, and this must never be forgotten.

### Natural Proliferation as Model

Nature appears profligate in producing seed, insects, young plants, and animals — vast numbers that are culled to provide sustenance for organisms higher up the food chain. This wasteful-seeming abundance is actually an elegant strategy for maintaining diversity, enabling selection, and ensuring system resilience. [[permaculture-design]] should emulate this pattern: plant abundantly, observe carefully, and cull ruthlessly based on performance.
