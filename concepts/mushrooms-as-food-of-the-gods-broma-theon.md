# Mushrooms as Food of the Gods (Broma Theon)

**Source:** The Road to Eleusis, Chapter I — R. Gordon Wasson

## Overview

The Greek phrase broma theon — literally "food of the gods" — was applied by ancient authors to mushrooms, providing linguistic evidence for the sacred status of fungi in classical Greek religion. Wasson argued that this terminology, combined with other philological and ethnographic evidence, pointed to a now-forgotten [[gordon-wasson-mushroom-discovery]] tradition underlying the [[lesser-and-greater-eleusinian-mysteries]].

## Classical References

Wasson cited several ancient Greek sources that referred to mushrooms as divine food:

- The word "mushroom" appears in contexts where its consumption is associated with the divine or the miraculous

- Mushrooms were categorized separately from ordinary foods in Greek taxonomy

- The association of mushrooms with the gods was so strong that certain species were considered taboo for common consumption

## Porphyry's Testimony

The Neoplatonic philosopher Porphyry (3rd century CE) explicitly discussed mushrooms as divine food. Wasson cited Porphyry as evidence that a philosophical tradition in late antiquity still preserved the memory of mushrooms' sacred role — a memory that had been suppressed or forgotten in mainstream religion.

Porphyry's discussion of [[porphyry-mushrooms-as-divine-food]] was part of a broader philosophical inquiry into vegetarianism and the proper relationship between humans and the natural world. His references to mushrooms as theon broma placed them in a category of foods that mediated between the human and divine realms.

## Comparative Ethnomycology

The concept of mushrooms as "food of the gods" has direct parallels in contemporary entheogenic traditions:

- In [[mazatec-mushroom-culture-and-velada]] culture, psilocybin mushrooms are called "the holy children" or "little saints" — sentient beings, not mere food

- In Siberian shamanism, Amanita muscaria is consumed as the flesh of a divine being

- The [[psychedelic-secrecy-comparative-eleusis-mesoamerica]] in Mesoamerica mirrors the concealment of the Eleusinian potion

Wasson's comparative method demonstrated that societies with [[gordon-wasson-mushroom-discovery]] traditions consistently elevate the fungi above ordinary food, treating them as divine gifts or even as divine beings themselves.

## Linguistic Analysis

The Greek mycological vocabulary revealed a cultural pattern of avoidance and reverence:

- Words for mushrooms were often euphemistic or periphrastic

- The ordinary Greek word for mushroom was used in specific ritual contexts

- The classification of mushrooms as broma theon placed them in a category that included ambrosia and nectar — the foods of the Olympian gods

This linguistic pattern of reverence and avoidance is characteristic of what Wasson called "mycophobia" — the cultural fear of mushrooms that he argued was a secondary development from an earlier, positive "mycophilic" phase in which mushrooms were sacred.

## The Eleusinian Connection

If the [[kykeon-composition-and-ergot-alkaloid-hypothesis]] contained an ergot-derived psychedelic rather than mushrooms, the broma theon terminology may reflect an older, mushroom-based tradition that was later replaced by or combined with the barley-ergot preparation. The transition from mushroom to ergot would represent a shift from gathering wild fungi to cultivating grain — paralleling the broader Greek transition from nomadic to agricultural society.

## Greek Mushroom Terminology and Taboo

The Greek language preserves several intriguing traces of a once-sacred relationship with mushrooms. The primary Greek word for mushroom, mykēs, was used in contexts suggesting both reverence and fear. Compound words and specialized vocabulary suggest that the Greeks possessed a more detailed mycological classification than casual familiarity would produce.

Wasson argued that the pattern of mycophobia in Greek culture — the avoidance, suspicion, and sometimes revulsion directed at mushrooms — was itself evidence of a suppressed mycophilic tradition. Cultures that had once held mushrooms sacred sometimes developed the most intense aversion to them, as if the memory of their power made them dangerous. This dynamic parallels the [[psychedelic-secrecy-comparative-eleusis-mesoamerica]] found in other entheogenic traditions.

## Mushrooms in the Classical Greek Diet

Despite the cultural ambivalence, mushrooms were consumed in ancient Greece. Athenaeus and other culinary writers mention mushrooms as food, but always with an edge of caution. Certain species were known to be poisonous, and the line between edible and toxic varieties — notoriously difficult to determine even with modern knowledge — contributed to the general anxiety surrounding fungal foods.

The [[mushroom-foraging-safety]] would have reinforced the cultural pattern of reverence and avoidance. A food that could be either divine or deadly depending on identification was naturally surrounded by ritual precautions and expert knowledge — precisely the conditions under which specialized priestly knowledge of psychoactive species could develop and be preserved.

## Wasson's Mycophobia/Mycophilia Hypothesis

Wasson's broader theoretical framework divided world cultures into "mycophilic" (mushroom-loving) and "mycophobic" (mushroom-fearing) traditions. He argued that this division reflected a deep cultural memory: societies that had once used psychoactive mushrooms in religious contexts either preserved the positive association (becoming mycophilic) or suppressed it so thoroughly that only fear remained (becoming mycophobic).

Greek culture, in Wasson's analysis, was mycophobic — but the broma theon terminology was a fossil of the earlier mycophilic phase. This hypothesis has influenced the broader ethnomycology-as-discipline|field of ethnomycology, though it has also been criticized as overly simplistic.
