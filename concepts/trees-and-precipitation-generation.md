# Trees and Precipitation Generation

## Trees as Creators of Soils and Atmosphere

Trees have helped create both our soils and atmosphere. The first through mechanical (root pressure) and chemical (humic acid) breakdown of rock, adding life processes as humus and myriad decomposers. The second through gaseous exchange, establishing and maintaining an **oxygenated atmosphere** and an active water-vapour cycle essential to life.

Forests may be doing about **80% of the work** of maintaining atmospheric composition, with the rest due to oceanic or aquatic exchange. Many cities and most deforested areas (such as Greece) no longer produce the oxygen they consume.

## The Five Basic Effects of Trees on Water Vapour and Windstreams

1. **Compression of streamlines** and induced turbulence in air flows
2. **Condensation phenomena**, especially at night
3. **Rehumidification** by the cycling of water to air
4. **Snow and meltwater effects**
5. **Provision of nuclei** for rain

### Compression and Turbulence Effects

Windstreams flowing across a forest are partly deflected over the forest (approximately **60% of the air**) and partly absorbed into the trees (approximately **40% of the air**). Within 1,000 metres (3,300 feet), the air entering the forest — with its tonnages of water and dust — is brought to a **standstill**.

The forest swallows these great energies, resulting in:
- An almost imperceptible warming of the air within the forest
- Generally increased humidity in the trees (averaging **15-18% higher** than ambient air)
- Air in which no dust is detectable

Under the forest canopy, **negative ions** produced by life processes cause dust particles to clump and adhere to each other, creating a fallout of dispersed dust.

#### Edge Protection — The "Edge Break" Phenomenon

At the forest edge, thick-stemmed and specially wind-adapted trees buffer the frontline attack of the wind. If a windward forest edge is cut and these defences removed, windburn by salt, dust abrasion, or plain windforce may kill or throw down the inner forest of weaker stems and less resistant species. Mollison termed this commonly observed phenomenon **"edge break."**

Conversely, a forest can be established by planting tough, resistant trees as windbreak to protect subsequent downwind plantings. Forest edges are therefore **essential and permanent protection** and should never be cut or removed.

#### Streamline Compression and Rainfall

Winds deflected over the forest cause compression in the streamlines, an effect extending to **twenty times the tree height**. A 12-metre (40-foot) line of trees compresses the air to 244 metres (800 feet) above, creating more water vapour per unit volume and cooling the ascending air stream — both conditions conducive to rain.

In flat country, fine grid placements of rain gauges in countries such as Holland and Sweden reveal that **40% of the rainfall** measured downwind of trees and mounds 12 metres or more in height is caused by this compression phenomenon.

### Ekman Spirals

Wind streaming over a hedgerow or forest edge describes a **spiral section**, repeated 5-8 times downwind, creating a series of compression fronts parallel to the windbreak. This phenomenon was first described by Ekman for compression fronts created over waves at sea.

The Ekman spirals over trees or bluffs may result in a ranked series of clouds, often very regular in their rows. They are deflected by drag and the Coriolis force, so the wind after the hedgerow may blow **5-15 degrees off** its previous course.

In total, hedgerows across wind systems have a profound effect on the airstreams passing over them, and a subsequent effect on local climate and rainfall.

## Condensation Phenomena

### Coastal and Island Condensation

On sea-facing coasts of islands and continents, the relatively warmer land surface creates quiet inshore airflows towards evening. Where humid air flows over the rapidly cooling surfaces of leaves, **condensation occurs** and droplets of water form. This is greatly aided by colonies of bacteria (*Pseudomonas*) which also serve as nuclei for frost crystals.

### Condensation Drip Exceeds Rainfall

Condensation drip can be as high as **80-86% of total precipitation** on the upland slopes of islands or sea coasts. This condensation produced:
- The dense rainforests of Tasmania, Chile, Hawaii, Washington/Oregon, and Scandinavia
- The redwood forests of California
- The giant laurel forests of pre-conquest Canary Islands (now arid due to almost complete deforestation by the Spanish)

### Condensation Surface Area

A single tree such as a giant Til (*Ocotea foetens*) may present **16 hectares of laminate leaf surface** to the sea air. With approximately 100 such trees per surface hectare, trees enormously magnify the available condensation surface. The taller the trees (giant redwoods, white pines), the larger the volume of moist air intercepted and the greater the resulting precipitation.

All types of trees act as condensers — Canary Island pines, laurels, holm oaks, redwoods, eucalypts, and Oregon pines. Evergreens work all year, but even deciduous trees catch moisture in winter.

### Fog Drip Measurements

- Table Mountain (South Africa) and Lanai (Hawaii): fog drip measured at **130-330 cm**, exceeding rainfall in both cases
- Newfoundland: **158 days of fog per year**
- Wherever mountains or foothills face onshore night winds, fog condensation will probably exceed rainfall

### Destruction of Condensation Effects

The effects of condensation can be quickly destroyed. Felling of forests causes:
- Rivers to dry up
- Swamps to evaporate
- Shallow water to dry out
- Drought to grip the land

All of this can occur within the lifetime of a single person.

## Design Implications for Precipitation Enhancement

Trees, especially crosswind belts of tall trees, provide an integrated system for increasing local precipitation by:
- Cooling air through shade and altitude forcing
- Providing cold condensation surfaces (leaves)
- Compressing air streamlines to concentrate water vapour
- Generating turbulence (Ekman spirals)
- Supplying organic condensation nuclei (pollen, bacteria, leaf dust)

Total precipitation can be changed in either direction by site design. Within 30-100 km of coasts (where much of the world's population lives), local precipitation is particularly responsive to forest management.
