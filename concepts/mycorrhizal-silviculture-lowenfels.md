## Density of Mycorrhizal Colonization

The scale of mycorrhizal colonization in forests is staggering:
- In a Swedish forest study, 95% of all root tips had mycorrhizal associations
- As many as **1.2 million ectomycorrhizae** were found in a single square meter of soil
- A mature tree can be associated with **ten or more** different fungal species simultaneously
- Individual mycorrhizal fungi can simultaneously associate with more than one plant species

## Ecological Functions in Forests

### Nutrient Cycling

Mycorrhizal fungi are essential for forest nutrient cycling:
- They decompose organic matter and release nutrients
- They access minerals by growing into rock and dissolving them
- They transfer nutrients between trees through shared mycelial networks
- Decaying leaves near colonized root systems contain mycorrhizal structures, evidence of nutrient recycling

### Mother Tree Networks

In managing a forest, silviculturists focus on the largest trees. These "mother trees" serve critical ecological functions:
- They protect the health of the understory through shade and organic debris
- They share their mycelial network with seedlings and young trees in the undercanopy
- They share the benefits of sunlight-gathering with progeny via the underground extracellular network
- When they die, much of their nutrients are returned to the fungal network for reuse

### Fire Recovery

Unlike arbuscular mycorrhizal spores, ectomycorrhizal spores can survive forest fires by forming spore banks that reside in soil until needed:
- Shrub species rapidly sprout after fires, feeding mycorrhizal populations until regenerating trees can connect
- This enables mycorrhizal regeneration immediately after fire rather than waiting for spore dispersal
- Monterey pine (Pinus radiata) seedlings germinate after fires and serve as host plants for mycorrhizal recovery

## Heavy Metal and Radiation Remediation

Mycorrhizal fungi protect plants from damage by heavy metals and can even filter radioactive materials:

**Heavy metals sequestered:** arsenic, copper, zinc, iron, lead, cadmium, nickel, mercury, chromium, and aluminum

**Radiation example — Chernobyl:**
- A robot sent inside the destroyed nuclear reactor in 2002 found fungi growing on walls that used radioactivity as an energy source
- Native trees can be inoculated with mycorrhizal fungi (Gomphidius glutinosus, Craterellus tubaeformis, Laccaria amethystina) that absorb radioactive cesium
- Some fungal tissues can filter radioactive cesium, immobilizing it within fungal tissue for years

## Practical Silviculture

### Nursery Inoculation

Commercial nurseries sometimes use sterile growing media and fumigation, which can destroy mycorrhizae:
- Always add mycorrhizal propagules at planting time
- Use Rhizophagus intraradices for red cedar, redwood, and giant sequoia seedlings
- Good general-purpose ectomycorrhizal nursery fungi: [[laccaria-laccata]], Inocybe species
- For oak inoculation: [[ectomycorrhizal-associations]]. This dramatic example illustrates the importance of matching the right fungi to the right trees.

### Mine Site Reclamation

Mycorrhizae can assist in reclaiming mine sites and chemically damaged land:
- They take up and isolate toxins including heavy metals
- They improve survival of seedlings in disturbed soils
- They enhance growth and survival during drought periods
- The goal is to return disturbed sites to natural conditions through fungal-aided plant establishment
