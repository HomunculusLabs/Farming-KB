## AACT Foliar Application and Sticker-Spreaders

Applying [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) to plant foliage introduces [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]] directly to leaf and stem surfaces, where they can occupy ecological niches, compete with pathogens, stimulate plant immune responses, and improve nutrient cycling on the phyllosphere (leaf surface ecosystem). Proper application technique and the use of sticker-spreaders determine whether organisms survive and colonize after spraying.

## Why Apply AACT to Foliage?

Foliar application of compost tea provides several distinct benefits beyond soil drenching:

1. **Disease suppression:** Beneficial bacteria and fungi physically occupy space on leaf surfaces, competing with pathogens for resources and excreting antimicrobial compounds.
2. **Plant immune priming:** Contact with beneficial microorganisms triggers systemic acquired resistance (SAR) and [[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]] (ISR) in plants.
3. **Nutrient foliar feeding:** Some nutrients in the tea are absorbed directly through leaf stomata and cuticle.
4. **Decomposition of leaf litter:** Fungi on leaf surfaces begin breaking down organic debris, preventing pathogen colonization of dead tissue.
5. **Extended protection:** A well-established microbial community on foliage provides ongoing protection between applications.

## Soil vs. Foliar Application

| Factor | Soil Application | Foliar Application |
|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Primary target | Root zone biology | Leaf/stem surface biology |
| Organism focus | Bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes | Primarily bacteria and fungi |
| Coverage needed | Root zone saturation | 70–80% of leaf surface |
| Frequency | 2–4 times per season | Every 7–14 days during growth |
| Timing | Pre-planting, early season | Throughout growing season |

## Sticker-Spreaders

A sticker-spreader (also called an adjuvant) is a substance added to spray solutions that helps the liquid adhere to and spread across plant surfaces. Without a sticker-spreader, AACT can bead up and roll off waxy or hairy leaf surfaces, leaving most of the biology on the ground rather than on the plant.

### Aloe Vera

Aloe vera gel is one of the most recommended natural sticker-spreaders for AACT. It contains polysaccharides and saponins that help the tea spread evenly and adhere to leaf surfaces. Pure, food-grade aloe vera (without preservatives) is ideal. Typical rates are 1–2 ounces of aloe gel per gallon of tea. Aloe also provides additional nutrients and mild [[medicinal-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties]] that do not harm beneficial organisms.

### Bacterial and Fungal Adhesion

If the compost tea contains actively growing, healthy microorganisms with good biomass, many species naturally produce extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) that help them adhere to surfaces. Well-made tea with robust organism growth may adhere adequately without added sticker-spreaders, especially on less waxy leaf surfaces. However, for consistent results, a sticker-spreader is recommended.

### Saponin-Based Spreaders

Saponins are natural surfactants found in many plants. Commercial saponin-based products like Nu-Film are effective sticker-spreaders that are compatible with AACT biology. Saponins reduce surface tension, allowing the tea to spread in a thin, even film across leaf surfaces rather than beading up.

### Products to Avoid

- **Dormant oils:** These coat the leaf in a thick film that can smother beneficial organisms along with pests. Not compatible with AACT application.
- **Synthetic surfactants:** Many conventional agricultural adjuvants contain chemicals toxic to beneficial microorganisms.
- **Fish hydrolysates as spreaders:** While fish hydrolysate is an excellent AACT food source, using it at high rates as a foliar spray can attract pests and cause odor issues. Use it carefully and at appropriate dilution.

### Unsulphured Molasses

Unsulphured molasses serves a dual purpose — it acts as a mild sticker-spreader due to its sugar content increasing solution viscosity, and it provides food for bacteria in the tea. However, molasses should not be relied upon as the sole sticker-spreader, as its effectiveness varies with concentration and other factors.

### Rain and Sticker-Spreaders

After application, rainfall can wash AACT off leaf surfaces. Sticker-spreaders improve rain resistance but cannot prevent wash-off in heavy or prolonged rain events. If significant rain is expected within 4–6 hours of application, it may be preferable to delay spraying. Light dew or brief showers generally do not remove well-adhered organisms.

## Leaf Coverage

### Minimum Coverage Target

At least **70% of leaf surface area** should be covered with tea for effective biological protection. Incomplete coverage leaves gaps where pathogens can establish. Coverage should be assessed on both upper and lower leaf surfaces, though lower surfaces are more challenging to reach with conventional spray equipment.

### Achieving Adequate Coverage

- **Spray pressure:** Moderate pressure (40–80 PSI) creates fine droplets that coat surfaces without blasting organisms off.
- **Nozzle selection:** Flat fan or cone nozzles provide better coverage than flood nozzles.
- **Spray volume:** Sufficient volume to wet the leaf surface without excessive runoff. Typically 20–50 gallons per acre, depending on crop density.
- **Application speed:** Slow enough to allow thorough coverage; walking speed for handheld applications.
- **Multiple angles:** Spray from multiple directions to reach undersides of leaves.

### Attaching to Leaf Surfaces

Beneficial organisms need to physically attach to the leaf surface to colonize and provide protection. Factors affecting attachment:

- **Leaf surface characteristics:** Waxy cuticles, hairy surfaces (trichomes), and leaf orientation all affect spray retention.
- **Organism motility:** Some bacteria are motile and actively seek favorable microhabitats on the leaf surface.
- **Moisture:** A thin film of moisture on the leaf surface helps organisms establish initially.
- **Time of day:** Early morning or late evening applications avoid rapid drying from sun and heat.

## Application Temperature

- **Optimal range:** 60–80°F (15–27°C) for both organism survival and plant receptivity.
- **Avoid hot conditions:** Spraying in temperatures above 85°F can stress both plants and organisms; rapid evaporation prevents adequate leaf contact time.
- **Fungal sensitivity:** Fungi are particularly sensitive to UV radiation and high temperatures. Evening applications favor fungal establishment.
- **Cold weather:** Organism reproduction rates slow significantly below 55°F. Applications in cool weather may require more frequent reapplication.

## Reapplication

### Why Reapply?

AACT organisms on leaf surfaces face ongoing challenges:

- **UV radiation:** Direct sunlight damages microbial cells.
- **Desiccation:** Dry conditions kill unprotected organisms.
- **Rain wash-off:** Heavy rains remove organisms.
- **Nutrient depletion:** Without food, organisms cannot maintain populations.
- **Plant growth:** New leaf tissue has no established microbial community.

### Reapplication Frequency

- **Standard:** Every 7–14 days during active growth periods.
- **High disease pressure:** Every 5–7 days.
- **Cool weather:** Every 10–14 days (slower organism reproduction).
- **Warm, humid conditions:** Every 7–10 days (faster turnover).

## Additional Nutrients

Foliar AACT applications can be enhanced with additional nutrients, though care must be taken not to overwhelm the biology:

- **Nitrogen:** Apply in protein or amino acid forms rather than nitrate. Nitrates at high foliar concentrations can damage leaf tissue and select for undesirable organisms.
- **Trace minerals:** Iron citrate and other chelated minerals can be added to address specific deficiencies.
- **Compost tea food:** Small amounts of molasses or kelp can extend organism survival on leaf surfaces.
