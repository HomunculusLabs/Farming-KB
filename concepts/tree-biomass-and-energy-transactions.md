    44|## The Biospheric Value of Trees
    45|
    46|The total value of a single large tree to its ecosystem is immense and
    47|difficult to overstate. A mature forest giant weighing 50 to 100 tonnes
    48|represents centuries of accumulated energy capture, soil building, microclimate
    49|creation, and habitat provision. Trees generate their own soils through leaf
    50|fall and root turnover, regulate local water cycles through transpiration and
    51|interception, and provide the structural framework upon which thousands of
    52|other species depend.
    53|
    54|Beyond their direct biomass, trees create conditions that enable entire
    55|biological communities. A single oak may support hundreds of species of
    56|insects, dozens of bird species, mosses, lichens, fungi, and epiphytic plants.
    57|The energy stored in its wood feeds decomposer food webs for decades after the
    58|tree dies. The shade it casts creates microclimates where understory plants
    59|can establish. No human-made structure can replicate this density of function.
    60|
    61|The tree's value extends far beyond its timber or fruit yield; it is a
    62|keystone of the energy and nutrient systems upon which all surrounding life
    63|depends.

    65|## Consequences of Deforestation
    66|
    67|When forests are removed, the energy transactions they mediated collapse. The
    68|consequences are cascading and severe:
    69|
    70|- **Drought and water loss**: Without tree cover, rainfall runs off rather
    71|  than infiltrating. Transpiration — which returns moisture to the atmosphere
    72|  and drives regional rainfall patterns — ceases entirely. Landscapes dry out
    73|  and local precipitation declines. Regions that were once reliably moist can
    74|  become arid within years of forest removal.
    75|
    76|- **Nutrient loss**: The nutrient cycling systems maintained by tree roots,
    77|  leaf litter, and associated soil organisms are disrupted. Topsoil erodes
    78|  rapidly without the binding and sheltering effect of roots and canopy.
    79|  Nutrients held in living biomass are released and washed away.
    80|
    81|- **Soil salinisation**: Rising water tables in cleared landscapes bring
    82|  dissolved salts to the surface, poisoning the soil for future plant growth.
    83|  Vast areas of previously productive farmland in Australia and elsewhere have
    84|  been rendered barren by this process.
    85|
    86|- **Acid rain and atmospheric degradation**: Forests buffer atmospheric
    87|  chemistry. Their removal contributes to increased carbon dioxide, altered
    88|  rainfall chemistry, and broader atmospheric instability.
