by foliage and converted through photosynthesis into chemical energy stored in

physical structure of trunks and canopies, reducing erosion and microclimatic
extremes. Precipitation is intercepted, slowed, filtered, and redistributed
through canopy drip, stem flow, and root-zone infiltration.

Trees thus act as intermediaries between atmospheric forces and the soil,
transforming chaotic environmental inputs into stable, productive systems. The
efficiency of these energy translations increases with the maturity and
complexity of the tree. A seedling intercepts minimal light and precipitation,
but a mature canopy tree with a deep root system, extensive mycorrhizal
connections, and a well-developed crown processes enormous quantities of energy
and material daily.

This is why old-growth forests are far more productive and stable than young
plantations: the accumulated energy-processing infrastructure of centuries
cannot be replicated by planting new seedlings. The mature tree has invested
decades or centuries into building the root networks, soil relationships,
canopy architecture, and microclimate conditions that maximise its
energy-processing capacity.

## The Biospheric Value of Trees

The total value of a single large tree to its ecosystem is immense and
difficult to overstate. A mature forest giant weighing 50 to 100 tonnes
represents centuries of accumulated energy capture, soil building, microclimate
creation, and habitat provision. Trees generate their own soils through leaf
fall and root turnover, regulate local water cycles through transpiration and
interception, and provide the structural framework upon which thousands of
other species depend.

Beyond their direct biomass, trees create conditions that enable entire
biological communities. A single oak may support hundreds of species of
insects, dozens of bird species, mosses, lichens, fungi, and epiphytic plants.
The energy stored in its wood feeds decomposer food webs for decades after the
tree dies. The shade it casts creates microclimates where understory plants
can establish. No human-made structure can replicate this density of function.

The tree's value extends far beyond its timber or fruit yield; it is a
keystone of the energy and nutrient systems upon which all surrounding life
depends.

## Consequences of Deforestation

When forests are removed, the energy transactions they mediated collapse. The
consequences are cascading and severe:

- **Drought and water loss**: Without tree cover, rainfall runs off rather
  than infiltrating. Transpiration — which returns moisture to the atmosphere
  and drives regional rainfall patterns — ceases entirely. Landscapes dry out
  and local precipitation declines. Regions that were once reliably moist can
  become arid within years of forest removal.

- **Nutrient loss**: The [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] systems maintained by tree roots,
  leaf litter, and associated soil organisms are disrupted. Topsoil erodes
  rapidly without the binding and sheltering effect of roots and canopy.
  Nutrients held in living biomass are released and washed away.

- **Soil salinisation**: Rising water tables in cleared landscapes bring
  dissolved salts to the surface, poisoning the soil for future plant growth.
  Vast areas of previously productive farmland in Australia and elsewhere have
  been rendered barren by this process.

- **Acid rain and atmospheric degradation**: Forests buffer atmospheric
  chemistry. Their removal contributes to increased carbon dioxide, altered
  rainfall chemistry, and broader atmospheric instability.
