# Ultra-Low-Cost Farming Overview

## Core Philosophy

Ultra-low-cost (ULC) farming, as developed by JADAM (Jayonul Damun Saramdul, meaning "people who are like nature"), is a revolutionary approach to agriculture that seeks to reduce farming costs by 10 to 100 times compared to conventional methods while maintaining or improving yield and quality. Founded by Youngsang Cho in 1991 and building on the natural farming legacy of his father Hankyu Cho, JADAM has grown to nearly 60,000 member farmers across South Korea and is being adopted worldwide.

The fundamental insight of ULC farming is that traditional agriculture was inherently low-cost and self-sufficient. For thousands of years, farmers used what was freely available around them — organic matter, local materials, and nature's processes. Modern agriculture has inverted this principle: farmers now buy seeds, seedlings, fertilizers, pesticides, and microorganisms from the marketplace, creating dependency on input manufacturers and their agents. JADAM's mission is to bring farming back to the hands of farmers by restoring their knowledge, methods, and self-sufficiency.

## SESE Principles

JADAM promotes four guiding principles that must be satisfied for any farming technology to qualify as truly organic and practical:

- **Simple**: principles are straightforward and easy to understand
- **Easy**: inputs and methods are easy to make and apply
- **Scientific**: methodology is grounded in observable natural processes
- **Effective**: processes are highly effective and produce low-cost results

These SESE criteria ensure that organic farming can actually replace conventional farming at scale. If organic farming remains difficult and expensive, it will forever be practiced by a minority.

## The Nature-Emulation Framework

ULC farming is built on three philosophical pillars derived from observing nature:

1. **Do as nature does**: Nature has farmed successfully for millions of years without any external inputs. Forests become richer every year without compost bins, C/N ratio calculations, or imported fertilizers. Leaves fall on the soil surface in autumn, break down over winter, and fertilize the soil by spring. ULC farming emulates this "fresh, surface, autumn" approach rather than the modern "fermented, deep, spring" method.

2. **I and others are one**: Understanding crops requires understanding them as living beings with needs similar to our own. Just as humans need comfortable temperatures, proper nutrition, and clean water, so do plants. This principle encourages farmers to empathize with their crops — for example, recognizing that plastic mulching can raise soil temperatures to lethal levels (45°C) that no human could endure.

3. **The good and the bad are one**: This principle rejects the dualistic thinking that pervades modern agriculture — that "good" microbes must be promoted and "bad" ones eliminated, or that fermentation is good while putrefaction is bad. In reality, all microorganisms serve functions in nature. The key is maintaining diversity and balance, not waging war against specific organisms.

## Self-Sufficiency and Independence

The core economic argument of ULC farming is that farmers should not need money to farm. Historically, a person with no means of living could at least survive off the land. Today, one must already have money in order to farm. JADAM reverses this by teaching farmers to make all necessary inputs themselves:

- **Fertilizers**: Liquid fertilizers from crop residues, wild grass, food waste, and even human manure
- **Pesticides**: [[jadam-pest-control-with-natural-pesticides-jhs]] from locally available herbs, oils, and minerals
- **Microorganisms**: [[knf-indigenous-microorganism-culture]] solutions collected from local leaf mold
- **Wetting agents**: Environment-friendly surfactants from canola oil and caustic potash

JADAM has demonstrated that it is possible to prepare pesticide and fertilizer at approximately $100 per acre (0.4 ha) per year, compared to the $6,000 average for conventional organic farming.

## The Problem with High-Cost Agriculture

JADAM identifies several structural problems with both conventional and modern organic farming:

- **Corporate control**: Input manufacturers develop technologies that serve their profit, not farmers' needs. When farming becomes easy and self-sufficient, manufacturers cannot sell their products.
- **Expert dependency**: Farmers have been conditioned to rely on experts for diagnosis and treatment, surrendering their own judgment and knowledge.
- **Energy dependence**: Modern agriculture relies heavily on oil, electricity, and heavy machinery — systems vulnerable to price shocks and supply disruptions.
- **Soil degradation**: Heavy machinery, chemical inputs, and incorrect fertilization methods have created compacted soil layers that prevent root penetration, water infiltration, and nutrient cycling.

## Applicability

ULC farming methods can be applied across all types of agriculture: conventional farms, hydroponics, commercial operations, large-scale plantations, and home or urban gardens. The system is designed to be universally accessible, requiring only locally available materials and simple techniques.

## Indigenous Microorganisms: The Foundation

A cornerstone of ULC farming is the use of indigenous microorganisms collected from local leaf mold. In nature, all microorganisms are beneficial because they all serve functions in decomposition and nutrient cycling. Leaf mold — the dark, crumbly soil found under piles of decomposing leaves in forests and fields — is described as "black gold" teeming with locally adapted microorganisms. These microbes are the best workers for local farms because they have already adapted to the regional environment. JADAM teaches farmers to collect this leaf mold and use it as a starter for microorganism culture, creating JADAM Indigenous Microorganism Solution (JMS) that can be applied every time crops are watered.

This approach directly challenges the commercial microorganism industry, which sells specific strains of bacteria at high prices. JADAM argues that microorganism products should not be commercialized because the most effective microbes for any region can only come from that region. The leaf mold method is not only free but also provides far greater microbial diversity than any commercial product.

## Key Innovations

Two of JADAM's most significant inventions that enable ultra-low-cost farming:

- **JADAM Wetting Agent (JWA)**: An environment-friendly surfactant made from canola oil and caustic potash. Wetting agents are essential for pesticides and foliar applications to properly coat plant surfaces.
- **JADAM Sulfur (JS)**: A powerful germicide made simply from sulfur and other permitted ingredients.

Both are unpatented and openly shared so all farmers can benefit.

## Transitioning to Ultra-Low-Cost Farming

JADAM recommends a gradual transition rather than an abrupt shift from conventional to organic farming. Begin by applying ULC methods to one-tenth of your total cropland. Master soil management and [[cervantes-fertilizer-application-strain-feeding]] basics first, then learn more advanced pest control methods. Expand the proportion of organic land as confidence grows.

Before transitioning, farmers should honestly assess their readiness:

- Can you manage soil microbial changes without chemical crutches?
- Have you solved soil compaction problems?
- Do you trust wild grass as [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-solomon]]?
- Can you formulate appropriate fertilizer programs for each crop?
- Can you control aphids, mites, and powdery mildew with natural methods?
- Are you confident that yield and quality will not decrease?

Many organic farming failures stem from inability to control aphids, mites, and powdery mildew. Learning to make and apply JADAM natural pesticides is essential for success.

## The Role of Water

Water is considered the starting point of all health in the JADAM system. Over 70% of the human body is water; for plants, the figure approaches 95%. The quality of water and fertilizer directly impacts plant cell formation and overall health. JADAM recommends using mineral water (spring water) over purified water, and emphasizes that adequate water intake is critical for both human and plant health. JADAM Mineral Water (JMW) is made by immersing leaf mold in water with rocks, creating a mineral-rich solution for crops.

## Global Relevance

The ULC approach is particularly relevant in an era of global free trade in agricultural products, rising energy costs, climate change, and financial instability. Countries with high-cost farming systems cannot compete with cheap imports. JADAM argues that farming should be free from dependence on money — ultra-low-cost and self-sufficient — providing resilience against economic shocks. The rising value of arable land in the face of sea level rise and desertification makes soil stewardship increasingly critical.

## Historical Context

JADAM builds on a long Korean tradition of organic farming spanning over 4,000 years. Before the introduction of chemical agriculture in the 1960s, all farming households kept cows, pigs, or chickens with completely self-sufficient feed systems. Animal manure was valued as fertilizer. Farming was done without money — everything needed was available nearby. The introduction of "modern" chemical methods devastated this traditional system, contaminating land and waters with synthetic inputs while creating dependency on purchased products.

Hankyu Cho, who pioneered Natural Farming in Korea starting from his demonstration farm in 1965, developed inputs including indigenous microorganisms, [[bionutrients-lab-fermented-plant-juice-fpj]], lactic acid bacteria, natural calcium, and natural phosphorus. His son Youngsang Cho founded JADAM in 1991 and advanced the system further, particularly with the inventions of JADAM [[jadam-nutrient-management-wetting-agent-and-calcium]] JADAM Sulfur, making it possible to produce all farming inputs at home from locally available, inexpensive materials.
