# Agrocybe, Auricularia, and Clitocybe Mushroom Cultivation

Three genera of cultivated mushrooms—*Agrocybe* (black poplar/pioppino), *Auricularia* (wood ear), and *Clitocybe* (blewit)—offer diverse cultivation opportunities spanning beginner to intermediate difficulty levels. Each occupies a distinct [[ecological-niche]] and presents unique characteristics relevant to both home growers and commercial cultivators.

## Black Poplar / Pioppino (*Agrocybe aegerita*)

### Description and Ecology

The black poplar mushroom, sold in Europe and increasingly in the United States under the name pioppino, grows on hardwood substrates. Though commonly associated with poplar trees in the wild, it has a broad host spectrum making its range much wider than its common name suggests. The primary identification caveat is that *Agrocybe* can be more difficult to distinguish from uninvited species when cultivated outdoors on logs or buried rounds. It should never be confused with *[[hypholoma-fasciculare]]* (sulfur tufts), a poisonous species with yellow to orange caps and yellow-green gills—*Agrocybe* has cream-colored to light brown caps with smoky gray to dark gills.

### Mycelium and Fruiting

Mycelium is white and linear, exuding a light yellow metabolite when overcolonized. The smell is sweet to slightly anise-like. Fruiting temperatures are cool: 50–65°F (10–19°C). Indoor fruitings are less abundant than oyster or shiitake, but the distinctly nutty flavor makes it a valued addition to any cultivation program.

The mushroom typically fruits in spring in [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]] and benefits from a mild cold shock to initiate fruiting. A productive cycle involves: colonization at warmer temperatures, a move to cooler environments for fruiting, a two-week rest period after harvest, then rehydration with cold water and a return to cool temperatures to stimulate subsequent flushes.

### Cultivation Methods

**Outdoor:** Fresh logs, stumps, or wood chips work well. Logs should be cut no more than one month before inoculation. The mushroom benefits from ground contact—submerged log techniques with wood chip covering are effective. [[cardboard-cultivation-low-tech-spawn]] is also possible. Inoculation to fruiting takes 8–10 months, with two flushes a few weeks apart. Patches continue fruiting for 2–3 years.

**Indoor:** Sterilized, [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] is ideal. Colonization takes 3–4 weeks. Tight clusters of mushrooms have longer shelf life than rangy groupings, so narrow bag openings are preferred over large exposed surfaces for market growers. Inoculation to fruiting takes 4–6 weeks.

### Nutritional and Medicinal Value

Protein content averages 27 percent (dry weight). *Agrocybe* species contain antiviral properties (active against both human and plant viruses including tobacco mosaic), antibacterial properties (against *E. coli*, *Streptococcus aureus*, *Bacillus cereus* and *B. subtilis*, and *Salmonella typhimurium*), high levels of anti-inflammatory compounds, and anti-cancer activity including against some forms of leukemia. In Chinese medicine, it treats spleen disorders.

### Mycoremediation Applications

*Agrocybe* is notable for its laccase enzyme activity—an oxide reductase capable of breaking down endocrine disruptors like BPA, making it useful for mycofiltration projects.

## Wood Ear (*Auricularia auricula*, *A. polytricha*)

### Description and Ecology

Wood ears are thin, rubbery, crunchy jelly fungi that are virtually flavorless but prized in Asian and tropical cuisines for their texture. They are extremely resilient, capable of drying completely, rehydrating, and continuing to grow and produce spores over several months. Their gelatinous fruitbodies form as firm, roundish nodules that appear hazy when young, then develop the characteristic cup shape and become more transparent with maturity.

### Mycelium and Fruiting

Mycelium is white and linear, browning with age, exuding a light yellow metabolite. The smell can be slightly unpleasant and musty. Fruiting temperatures range from 60–80°F (16–27°C) depending on strain. Rotating among strains with different temperature windows maximizes harvest periods in temperate climates without excessive energy investment for heating or cooling.

A key advantage is extremely low insect pressure—wood ears are not highly sought after by insects, slugs, or other fungus-loving organisms. This allows transferring colonized indoor bags directly to outdoor fruiting structures without significant losses.

### Cultivation Methods

**Outdoor:** Fresh logs, stumps, or wood chips. The mushroom likes to form on the open face of stumps, squeezing out from under the bark surface, making half-buried rounds an excellent choice. Inoculation to fruiting takes 10–14 months. Yields are very low outdoors and slow to mature.

**Indoor:** Sterilized or super-pasteurized supplemented sawdust with 5 percent wheat or rice bran (dry weight). Colonization takes 3 weeks. To initiate fruiting, make 3–4 inch slices in the bag on opposite sides or as an X where mushrooms are desired. Inoculation to fruiting takes 50–60 days, with three or more flushes 2–3 weeks apart. Yields reach approximately 1 pound per 5 pounds of substrate.

### Laboratory Isolation

As jelly fungi, wood ears are more difficult to isolate from fruitbodies than fleshy gilled mushrooms. The rubbery bodies are thin and reluctant to yield mycelium without contaminants. The preferred approach is spore printing from mature specimens, or a primitive method of inserting wet cardboard under bark near fruiting sites, allowing mycelium to colonize the cardboard, then transferring hyphal tips to antibiotic agar for cleanup.

### Nutritional and Medicinal Value

Moisture content is approximately 90 percent, with 8–10 percent protein (dry weight). Wood ears are well known for anticoagulant properties (commonly used as blood thinners) and anti-inflammatory actions. In homeopathy they treat pancreatic imbalances.

### Mycoremediation Applications

*Auricularia* may be best used for habitat renewal in devastated ecosystems rich in lignicolous debris, such as logged forests. Their tolerance for greater sun exposure than other wood-decomposing fungi makes them useful for soil building in arid, sunny environments.

## Blewit (*Clitocybe nuda*)

### Description and Ecology

The blewit is a fall and early winter mushroom triggered by heavy frost or freeze. It forms complex alliances—or battles—with [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] in the soil. In the field, blewits are often detected by spotting a sliver of purple or blue lifting fallen leaves, with many more hidden nearby as bumps and mounds on the forest floor. The large cap seems to prefer the CO₂-rich microclimate under its leaf covering. Blewits resemble some blue *Cortinarius* species (some potentially toxic), but are distinguished by their creamy pink spore print versus the rusty orange of *Cortinarius*.

### Mycelium and Fruiting

Blewit mycelium is bluish and linear, with few metabolites except during contamination or on pasteurized substrates. Fruiting requires a significant cold shock—most strains do not produce until temperatures have dropped below freezing for at least a few days, followed by a brief warming period. Local strains are always preferred for cultivation.

### Wild Expansion: The "Blewit Bomb"

An inventive expansion technique involves placing stem bases, soil and all, in a blender with water, pulverizing the mixture, freezing it in a ziplock bag for several days, then placing the frozen chunk atop a compost pile. As it slowly melts, it coats the compost with both blewit mycelium and potentially the microbial triggers needed for fruiting. This approach has produced successful naturalized patches.

### Cultivation Methods

**Outdoor:** Composted hardwood leaves, spent oyster substrate, composted sawdusts, or manure-rich composts. Blewits prefer complex growing media. Shallow beds 8–10 inches deep are dressed with microbial casing soil after colonization. Time to colonization: 6–12 months; fruiting depends on cold weather onset. Expected yields: 4–5 pounds per 16 square feet of beds, though sporadic. Beds may need replacement every 2 years.

**Indoor:** More challenging and unpredictable. A sequenced flow of colonized trays, cased with microbe-rich solution from previous fruitings, then briefly frozen before fruiting, is the recommended strategy. Adding [[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]] as a food source may trigger feeding or fruiting cycles. Minimum cycle: approximately 6 weeks. Expected yields: 2–3 pounds per square foot.

### Nutritional and Medicinal Value

Approximately 90 percent water with 24–26 percent protein (dry weight). Blewits contain compounds inhibiting both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria with high activity against tuberculosis, plus antifungal and antibacterial compounds active against *[[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]]* and *Serratia aureus*.

### Mycoremediation Applications

Extracts from blewit fruitbodies can serve as foliar bactericides and insecticides. The genus is also well known for hyperaccumulating metals in its mycelium, making it suitable for mycofiltration of contaminated soil and water.
