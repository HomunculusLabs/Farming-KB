# Soil Microscope Analysis

Direct microscopic examination of soil and compost tea is the primary tool for assessing soil biology health. [[elaine-ingham]]'s [[soil-food-web]] approach uses 400x magnification to identify and count bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes, providing actionable data for soil management.

## Equipment Needed

### Minimum Setup
- Compound microscope with 400x magnification (4x objective + 10x eyepiece minimum)
- 100x oil immersion lens (optional but recommended for identifying bacteria vs clay particles)
- Glass slides and coverslips
- Clean pipettes or droppers
- Distilled water
- Light source (built-in or external)

### Recommended
- 1000x oil immersion capability
- Phase contrast (improves visibility of transparent organisms)
- Digital camera adapter for documentation
- Sedimentation chamber (for nematode counting)

## Direct Count Method (Bacteria and Fungi)

### Slide Preparation
1. Collect soil sample from root zone (0-6 inch depth), composite from 5+ spots
2. Mix 1 gram of soil with 99 mL of distilled water (1:100 dilution)
3. Shake vigorously for 1 minute
4. Place one drop on a clean glass slide
5. Cover with coverslip (avoid bubbles)
6. Examine at 400x

### Counting Protocol
1. Count organisms in 10 random fields of view
2. Calculate average organisms per field
3. Use the field diameter to estimate total biomass
4. Convert to micrograms per gram of soil

### What You See
- **Bacteria**: Tiny round, rod, or spiral shapes (1-5 micrometers). Difficult to distinguish from clay at 400x — use 1000x oil immersion. Active bacteria appear individually dispersed; inactive bacteria clump in biofilms
- **[[fungal-hyphae]]**: Long, branching threads (2-10 micrometers wide). Look for: diameter (thin = saprophytic, thick = possibly pathogenic), coloration, septation (cross-walls). Active hyphae are clear/transparent; inactive are darker
- **Spores**: Round structures (10-100 micrometers). [[fungal-spores]] indicate disturbance or stress — healthy soil has mostly active hyphae, not spores

## Protozoa Assessment

### Preparation
1. Use same dilution as above, but examine immediately (protozoa move fast)
2. Slightly higher magnification helps (400-1000x)

### Identification
- **Flagellates**: Tiny, darting movement with whip-like flagella
- **Amoebae**: Slow, flowing movement, shape constantly changing
- **Ciliates**: Larger, hair-like cilia, fast coordinated movement
- Ciliates in high numbers indicate anaerobic conditions (they tolerate low oxygen)

### Interpretation
- Protozoa graze on bacteria, releasing plant-available nitrogen
- 10,000-100,000 protozoa per gram of soil = good nutrient cycling
- Low protozoa = nutrients are locked up in bacterial biomass

## Nematode Assessment

### Extraction
1. Place 50g soil on a coarse mesh over a dish of water
2. After 48 hours, nematodes migrate through the mesh into the water
3. Collect water and examine under microscope

### Identification (400x)
- **Bacterial-feeders**: Small, slender, fast-moving. Most common in disturbed soils
- **Fungal-feeders**: Spear-like mouthparts, slower. Important in forest/grassland soils
- **Predatory**: Large, visible mouthparts. Eat other nematodes — beneficial
- **Root-feeders**: Spear mouthparts, often found with swollen body. Plant parasites — harmful
- **Beneficial ratio**: Predators + fungal-feeders should be 10-20% of total nematodes

## Compost Tea Assessment

### Method
1. Take 1 mL of actively aerated compost tea
2. Place on slide, cover, examine at 400x
3. Look for: active bacterial movement, fungal hyphae length, protozoa types

### Good Tea Indicators
- Bacteria: active, individual (not clumped), 10,000-100,000 per mL
- Fungi: visible hyphae with active growing tips, 10-100+ micrometers long
- Protozoa: present (flagellates minimum)
- No anaerobic smell (rotten egg, sewage)

### Bad Tea Indicators
- Dominated by ciliates (anaerobic conditions)
- Bacterial clumps (poor aeration during brewing)
- No visible fungal hyphae (inadequate fungal food sources)
- Foul smell (anaerobic)
