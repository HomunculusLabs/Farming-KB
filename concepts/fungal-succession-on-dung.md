# Fungal Succession on Dung

The fungal colonization of herbivore dung represents one of the most predictable and well-studied successional sequences in mycology. Coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungi have evolved specialized life histories that are intimately linked to the dung substrate and the animals that produce it. The succession of fungal species on dung follows a characteristic temporal pattern that reflects changes in the chemical composition of the substrate, competitive interactions among fungal species, and the interactions between fungi and the invertebrate animals that visit dung.

## The Dung Substrate

Fresh herbivore dung is a nutritionally rich substrate containing partially digested plant material, digestive enzymes, microbial biomass, and a diverse community of microorganisms. The chemical composition of dung varies with the diet and digestive physiology of the herbivore, with differences between ruminant and non-ruminant dung being particularly important for fungal colonization.

Dung from ruminant animals (cattle, sheep, deer) has undergone extensive microbial fermentation in the rumen and is typically richer in microbial biomass and lower in undigested plant fiber than dung from non-ruminants (horses, rabbits). These differences influence the species composition of the coprophilous fungal community and the rate and pattern of succession.

## Spore Dispersal and Survival

Coprophilous fungi have evolved specialized dispersal mechanisms to ensure that their spores are deposited on fresh dung. Many species produce spores that are adapted to passage through the herbivore digestive tract. The spores are deposited on vegetation in the herbivore's feeding area, ingested along with the vegetation, and excreted in the dung. The digestive process can stimulate spore germination or break dormancy, ensuring that spores are activated upon deposition in the fresh dung.

Some coprophilous fungi produce sticky spore masses that adhere to the bodies of dung-visiting insects, particularly flies. These insects carry the spores to fresh dung deposits, where they become established. The interaction between coprophilous fungi and dung-visiting insects represents a mutualistic dispersal relationship.

Coprophilous fungal spores can remain viable in the environment for extended periods, forming a persistent spore bank in soil and on vegetation. This spore bank ensures that spores are available for colonization when fresh dung is deposited, even if suitable dung has been absent from the area for some time.

## Successional Stages

The succession of coprophilous fungi on dung typically proceeds through several well-defined stages. The sequence was first described in detail by Harper and Webster (1964) and has been confirmed by numerous subsequent studies.

The first fungi to appear on fresh dung are typically zygomycetes, including species of Pilobolus, Mucor, and Rhizopus. These fast-growing fungi colonize the dung surface within the first few days after deposition. Pilobolus species are particularly notable for their phototropic sporangia, which are discharged toward light sources to maximize dispersal distance.

After the initial zygomycete phase, ascomycete fungi begin to fruit. The "primary" succession of ascomycetes includes species of Saccobolus, Ascobolus, Podospora, and Sporormiella, which typically fruit within the first one to two weeks. These species are followed by a "secondary" succession including species of Sordaria, Coprinus, and other genera that fruit over a period of weeks to months.

## Resource Partitioning Among Coprophilous Fungi

The temporal succession of coprophilous fungi on dung reflects resource partitioning among species with different enzymatic capabilities and growth strategies. Early colonizers are adapted to exploit the readily available soluble nutrients and simple carbohydrates in fresh dung. As these labile resources are depleted, later colonizers with the ability to degrade more complex substrates, including cellulose and hemicellulose, become dominant.

The competitive interactions among coprophilous fungi are influenced by the physical structure of the dung pat. Fungi that colonize the dung surface have access to oxygen and can discharge spores freely but are exposed to desiccation and UV radiation. Fungi that colonize the interior of the dung pat are protected from these stresses but face greater competition from bacteria and other microorganisms.

## Interactions with Dung Fauna

Invertebrate animals, particularly flies, beetles, and earthworms, play important roles in the coprophilous fungal succession. Dung flies (Scatophaga species) and other insects visit fresh dung to feed and lay eggs, and in doing so transport fungal spores between dung pats. The larvae of dung flies and beetles consume fungal mycelium and spores, creating grazing pressure that can reduce fungal biomass and alter community composition.

Earthworms are major consumers of dung in many ecosystems. The passage of dung through earthworm guts can modify the fungal community by selectively digesting some species and promoting the dispersal of others. The burrowing activity of earthworms also mixes dung with soil, creating conditions that favor different fungal species than those found on the undisturbed dung surface.

## Nutrient Cycling Significance

The decomposition of dung by coprophilous fungi returns nutrients to the ecosystem in plant-available forms. Dung represents a significant nutrient input in grazed ecosystems, and the rate at which dung nutrients are mineralized and released influences pasture productivity. Coprophilous fungi contribute to this mineralization by decomposing the organic matter in dung and releasing nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients.

The immobilization of nutrients in fungal biomass during the early stages of dung decomposition is followed by net mineralization as the fungal biomass turns over. The timing and magnitude of this mineralization pattern influence the synchrony between nutrient availability and plant demand in grazed ecosystems.

## Factors Affecting Species Composition

The species composition of the coprophilous fungal community is influenced by the type of herbivore that produced the dung, the season of deposition, the geographic location, and the management practices in the ecosystem. Dung from different herbivores supports different fungal communities, reflecting differences in dung chemistry and the specific spore-dispersal relationships between fungi and herbivores.

Seasonal factors influence the timing and composition of fungal fruiting on dung. Temperature and moisture conditions affect the rate of dung decomposition and the phenology of fungal sporulation. In temperate regions, the composition of the coprophilous fungal community varies between summer and winter dung deposits.

## Ecological Importance

Dung fungal succession plays a vital role in nutrient cycling in grassland ecosystems. By decomposing herbivore dung, these fungi return nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon to the soil, making nutrients available for plant growth. The rapid colonization of fresh dung also prevents competing bacteria from dominating the resource, ensuring efficient decomposition.

## Classic Succession Patterns

The succession of fungi on herbivore dung follows remarkably predictable patterns that were first systematically documented by mycologists in the mid-20th century. The sequence can be divided into primary, secondary, and tertiary colonizers based on the timing of fruiting body appearance and the ecological strategies employed.

Primary colonizers (appearing within days) are typically fast-growing species with broad substrate tolerance. These include members of the genus Pilobolus, whose sporangia are forcibly discharged toward light sources — an adaptation that increases the likelihood of spores landing on nearby vegetation where they will be consumed by herbivores. Coprinus species are also early colonizers, rapidly colonizing fresh dung and fruiting within a week.

Secondary colonizers (appearing after 1-3 weeks) tend to be more specialized and include species of Sphaerobolus, which forms a spherical fruiting body that opens like a miniature cannon to discharge its spore mass. Tertiary colonizers (appearing after several weeks to months) are often slower-growing species that decompose more recalcitrant components of the dung, including partially digested plant cell wall material.

## Detailed Species Succession Timeline

The temporal sequence of fungal fruiting on herbivore dung has been documented with considerable precision. Understanding this timeline reveals how competitive interactions and substrate changes drive community assembly. The following sequence is typical for temperate ruminant dung:

### Days 1 to 4: The Zygomycete Phase
Within hours of deposition, zygomycete fungi germinate from spores that survived passage through the herbivore gut. Pilobolus species dominate this earliest phase, recognizable by their phototropic sporangia borne on tall stalks. These sporangia are explosively discharged toward light, traveling up to 2 meters to land on nearby grass blades where they will be consumed by the next grazing animal. Mucor and Rhizopus species also appear during this window, rapidly colonizing the nutrient-rich dung surface. Their mycelium is typically colorless and cottony, spreading across the dung pat before competitors can establish.

### Days 5 to 14: Primary Ascomycete Phase
As the zygomycetes deplete the most readily available nutrients, a wave of ascomycete fungi begins to fruit. Saccobolus species are among the earliest, producing tiny orange or yellow cup-shaped apothecia directly on the dung surface. Ascobolus species follow with slightly larger fruiting bodies. These primary ascomycetes utilize the remaining simple sugars and amino acids. Podospora species and Sporormiella species also fruit during this period, producing perithecia that release spores through narrow ostioles adapted to protect spores from desiccation. Sporormiella is particularly notable because its spores are preserved in lake sediments and have been used by paleoecologists as a proxy for herbivore population density over thousands of years.

### Weeks 2 to 6: Secondary Decomposer Phase
The genus Coprinus (inkcaps) dominates this phase. Coprinus sterquilinus, C. niveus, and C. radiatus are classic dung Coprinus species that fruit abundantly during this window. Their fruiting bodies are deliquescent, meaning the gills liquefy from the bottom up to release spores in a self-liquefying mass. This adaptation ensures continuous spore release even in the absence of wind. Sordaria species also appear during this period, producing dark perithecia that contain elongated spores with gelatinous sheaths adapted for insect dispersal.

### Weeks 6 to 16: Tertiary and Late Successional Phase
The final phase involves fungi capable of decomposing the most recalcitrant components of dung, including lignified plant fibers that survived partial digestion. Species of Podospora with more robust perithecia, Thecotheus species, and various sterile mycelia dominate this late stage. By this time, the dung pat has typically lost much of its original mass and is fragmenting, merging with the underlying soil. Basidiomycete fungi including Bolbitius vitellinus and Conocybe species may also appear during this late phase, often persisting until the dung pat has fully decomposed.

## Spore Dispersal Mechanisms in Detail

Coprophilous fungi employ an impressive diversity of spore dispersal strategies. Forcible discharge is the most dramatic mechanism: Pilobolus uses a turgor-powered cannon to launch entire sporangia, while ascomycetes such as Ascobolus and Saccobolus discharge individual ascospores from cup-shaped fruiting bodies. The spores of many coprophilous ascomycetes are specifically adapted for insect dispersal, possessing sticky coatings or gelatinous appendages that adhere to the bodies of dung-visiting flies and beetles. Some species produce spores with elaborate appendages that increase their surface area and promote entrapment on vegetation. Still others, like the basidiomycete Coprinus, rely on deliquescence and rain splash to distribute spores from the dissolving gills. The multiple dispersal strategies reflect the central challenge of coprophily: spores must reach the digestive tract of a herbivore, which means they must be positioned on vegetation that will be consumed. This requirement has driven the evolution of some of the most sophisticated ballistic and adhesive dispersal mechanisms in the fungal kingdom.

## Herbivore-Dung Fungus Specificity

The relationship between herbivore species and their dung fungal communities is surprisingly specific. Dung from different herbivores supports distinct fungal assemblages, driven by differences in diet, digestive physiology, and dung chemistry. Ruminant dung (cattle, sheep, deer) tends to support a more diverse fungal community because the extensive rumen fermentation pre-digests plant material and creates a substrate rich in microbial biomass. Non-ruminant hindgut fermenters like horses produce dung with coarser plant fragments and different nutrient profiles, favoring different fungal species. Rabbit cecal dung, which is re-ingested (coprophagy), has its own specialized fungal community. The geographic distribution of herbivores has also shaped coprophilous fungal biogeography: fungal species associated with kangaroo dung in Australia, bison dung in North America, and water buffalo dung in Southeast Asia show distinct geographic patterns that reflect co-evolutionary histories between fungi and their herbivore hosts.

## Ecological Role in Nutrient Cycling

The decomposition of herbivore dung by fungal communities is a critical link in the nutrient cycling of grazed ecosystems. In a typical pasture, dung deposition returns approximately 80 percent of the nitrogen, 60 percent of the phosphorus, and 90 percent of the potassium consumed by grazing animals to the soil surface. Without coprophilous fungi and other dung decomposers, this nutrient return would be extremely slow, and nutrients would remain locked in undecomposed dung pats that smother underlying pasture grass. Fungal decomposition breaks down the complex organic matrix of dung, releasing plant-available ammonium, phosphate, and potassium. The mycelial networks of coprophilous fungi also physically connect the dung pat to the surrounding soil, creating channels through which nutrients and water can move. In ecosystems with large herbivore populations such as the African savanna or the North American Great Plains, the cumulative effect of dung fungal decomposition on landscape-scale nutrient cycling is immense.

## Paleontological and Paleoenvironmental Significance

Coprophilous fungal spores, particularly those of Sporormiella, have become invaluable tools in paleoecology for reconstructing past herbivore populations and environmental change. Sporormiella spores are resistant to degradation and preserve well in lake sediments and peat deposits. By analyzing the abundance of Sporormiella spores in sediment cores, researchers can estimate the historical biomass of herbivore populations in a region. The dramatic decline of Sporormiella spores at the end of the Pleistocene in North America, approximately 12,900 years ago, provides some of the strongest evidence for the extinction of megafaunal herbivores such as mammoths, ground sloths, and giant bison. Similarly, the decline of Sporormiella spores following human colonization of Madagascar and New Zealand correlates with the extinction of large flightless birds and other megaherbivores.
