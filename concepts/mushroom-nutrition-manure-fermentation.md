# Mushroom Nutrition and Manure Fermentation

Benjamin Minge Duggar's research on mushroom nutrition, published in 1905, revealed fundamental insights about why fermented manure is essential for cultivation — not because fermentation chemically improves the substrate, but because it creates the biological conditions that allow mushroom mycelium to compete with other microorganisms.

## Fermentation as a Biological Requirement

General experiments had long shown that mushrooms grow best in fermented stable manure, but Duggar's careful analysis revealed a paradox: chemically, fermentation is probably more injurious than beneficial. When grown in sterilized fresh manure under pure culture conditions, mushroom mycelium grew as rapidly and more profusely than in fermented manure similarly treated. This demonstrated conclusively that fermentation is not chemically essential.

Fermentation is essential for biological reasons. Fresh manure contains very readily soluble and relatively unstable organic products that become the seat of pronounced and abundant bacterial activity. In the presence of this rapid bacterial activity during the first stage of fermentation, mushroom mycelium will not grow — and this failure is not due merely to the usually accompanying increased temperature. Even when the manure is used in quantities small enough that noticeable temperature increase does not occur, the mycelium still fails to establish.

Fermentation acts by effecting a complete change in the bacterial activity. It serves to remove or transform rapidly fermentable products and induces certain changes in the insoluble material and straw, whereby the mushroom mycelium is then able to grow slowly and to compete with the microorganisms next involved. The nutrients used by the mushroom are both the gradually soluble organic products and the necessary inorganic salts.

The practical duration of fermentation was generally considered to be from two to three weeks, with turning the manure at intervals of several days. The well-prepared compost was dark brown or blackish in color, relatively uniform, with straw in a more or less soft and pliable condition but not destroyed. Its moisture content was high — it could be squeezed firmly in the hand without more than a slight expression of water. The degree of preparation was judged by the color and the softness of the straw, the disappearance of the original strawy character and of the original odor.

Horse manure from stables where the animals are fed on mixed grain and hay was regarded as the most suitable. The urine and the excrement, together with the litter, constituted the mass. Fresh manure from grain-fed horses contained the most active forms of nitrogen and readily fermentable organic matter, making it superior to manure from animals on less concentrated diets.

## Bacterial Species Involved in Fermentation

The composting process is driven by successive waves of microorganisms, each adapted to the changing chemical and thermal conditions of the decomposing manure:

### Phase 1: Mesophilic Stage (Ambient to 45°C / 113°F)
During the initial days of composting, mesophilic bacteria dominate. Key genera include:
- **Thermus species:** Thermophilic bacteria that initiate the breakdown of simple sugars and proteins in fresh manure
- **Bacillus subtilis and B. licheniformis:** Common soil bacteria that rapidly multiply on the abundant nutrients in fresh manure, producing antibiotics that inhibit some competing organisms
- **Pseudomonas species:** Aerobic bacteria that degrade organic acids and contribute to ammonia production
- **Proteus and Enterobacter species:** Facultative anaerobes from the animal gut that continue their activity in fresh manure, contributing to the initial rapid metabolism of soluble organics

### Phase 2: Thermophilic Stage (45-80°C / 113-176°F)
As bacterial metabolism generates heat, thermophilic organisms replace mesophilic ones:
- **Thermoactinomyces vulgaris:** A thermophilic actinomycete that degrades cellulose and hemicellulose in the straw, producing the characteristic "sweet" earthy smell of well-made compost
- **Thermobifida fusca:** An important thermophilic cellulose degrader that breaks down straw structural polysaccharides
- **Clostridium thermocellum:** An anaerobic thermophile that produces cellulase enzymes capable of degrading crystalline cellulose
- **Saccharomonospora viridis:** A thermophilic actinomycete contributing to humus formation

### Phase 3: Cooling and Curing Stage (40-25°C / 104-77°F)
As the compost cools, a second wave of mesophilic organisms recolonizes:
- **Streptomyces species:** Actinomycetes that complete the decomposition of complex organics and contribute earthy-smelling geosmin compounds
- **Trichoderma species:** Thermotolerant fungi that degrade remaining cellulose and help suppress competing organisms in the finished compost
- **Various fluorescent Pseudomonads:** Beneficial bacteria that colonize the compost and may provide biological protection against mushroom pathogens

## Modern Composting Methods

Contemporary commercial mushroom compost production has evolved considerably from Duggar's era while maintaining the same biological principles:

### Phase 1 Composting (Outdoor)
Raw materials (typically wheat straw, horse or poultry manure, gypsum, and water) are mixed in long rows called windrows. The windrows are turned by specialized machinery every 2-3 days for 7-14 days. Turning incorporates oxygen, redistributes moisture, and ensures uniform decomposition. Gypsum (calcium sulfate) is added at 50-100 kg per ton of dry ingredients to improve structure, buffer pH, and provide calcium and sulfur. Internal temperatures reach 70-80°C, killing pathogens and weed seeds.

### Phase 2 Composting (Indoor Pasteurization and Conditioning)
After Phase 1, the compost is moved into specially designed rooms or tunnels for controlled Phase 2 processing:
1. **Pasteurization:** Compost is filled into bulk containers or trays and brought to 58-60°C for 8-10 hours using steam injection. This kills pests, nematodes, and competing fungi.
2. **Conditioning:** Temperature is gradually reduced to 48-52°C and held for 5-7 days. During this critical period, thermophilic microbes convert remaining ammonia to microbial protein that the mushroom can utilize. The compost must be fully "cleared" of free ammonia before spawning, as residual ammonia is toxic to mushroom mycelium.

### Tunnel Composting
Modern Dutch and British systems use forced-air tunnels for both Phase 1 and Phase 2 composting. Compost is filled into enclosed concrete tunnels with perforated floors through which conditioned air is blown. This provides precise control of temperature, oxygen, and moisture throughout the process, producing more consistent compost with higher biological efficiency than traditional outdoor methods.

## Alternative Substrates

While horse manure remains the traditional substrate, modern cultivators have developed successful alternatives:

- **Poultry manure and straw:** Poultry manure is nitrogen-rich and, when mixed with wheat or rice straw, produces compost equal or superior to horse manure. The higher nitrogen content allows shorter composting times. Typical formulation: 1 ton wheat straw, 500 kg poultry manure, 50 kg gypsum.
- **Synthetic compost:** Formulated from agricultural by-products without animal manure. Typical ingredients include wheat straw, brewers' grain, cottonseed meal, molasses, gypsum, and urea or ammonium sulfate as nitrogen supplements. Produces reliable results but requires careful formulation.
- **Spent mushroom substrate (SMS):** Compost that has already been through one mushroom crop can be re-composted with supplements and used for a second crop, though yields decline. SMS is also used as a soil amendment or mulching-guide|mulch.
- **Sawdust-based substrates:** For [[mushroom-training-shiitake-cultivation]], [[cotter-oyster-mushroom-cultivation]], and [[falconer-paris-cave-mushroom-cultivation]] mushrooms, hardwood sawdust supplemented with wheat bran or rice bran is the standard substrate. These species are primary decomposers that do not require the fermented manure compost needed by Agaricus.

## The Ammonia Question

The once-prevalent belief that the "ammonia" of the manure might constitute the only important fertilizer in mushroom culture was identified as erroneous. However, manure treated to effect the loss of much free ammonia was found to be injured for manurial purposes.

The relationship between nitrogen availability and mushroom nutrition proved more complex than early growers assumed. While nitrogen was essential, it could not act alone — the interplay between nitrogen compounds, carbon sources, and microbial ecology determined whether a substrate would support productive fruiting.

## Growth on Synthetic Media

Duggar demonstrated that the mycelium of *Agaricus campestris* grows quite well upon synthesized media — nutrient media prepared from known chemical substances such as ordinary nutrient fertilizer salts with the addition of casein, peptone, or similar organic nitrogen sources. Laboratory experiments indicated that all of the nitrogen could be supplied as inorganic salts, provided a suitable organic compound as a source of carbon was furnished.

Experiments with adding mineral nutrients or fertilizers to manure produced contradictory results, though the addition of phosphorus (as phosphate) and lime were often advantageous. The variable quality of manure and spawn, and the impracticability of cooperating with extensive growers, prevented adequate study of nutrition on a practical basis. Some growers reported success with adding superphosphate or ground bone to the compost, while others found such additions made little difference.

The practical experience of generations of growers had established that fresh horse manure, properly fermented, was the most reliable substrate. Attempts to substitute other materials — including loam, leaf mold, and various composted organic wastes — were only partially successful. The unique combination of nitrogen compounds, carbon sources, and mineral content in fermented horse manure proved difficult to replicate with other materials, though this did not prevent extensive experimentation.

## Temperature Requirements

Under standard mushroom growing methods, a more or less uniform air temperature of 54 to 56 degrees F was judged optimal. A good practical growing range extended from 50 to 65 degrees F. Growing continuously above 60 degrees F or below 50 degrees F was not commercially successful, though much depended upon other factors.

High temperature proved more injurious than cold. A temperature of 72 degrees F sustained for 24 hours was fatal to pin heads and injurious to spawn. However, pure cultures of *Agaricus campestris* grew well up to 85 degrees F when sufficient moisture was present — demonstrating that high temperature itself does not directly kill the mycelium. Rather, elevated temperatures stimulate competing organisms (insects, fungi, and bacteria) that either crowd out or directly attack the mushroom mycelium and young pin heads.

At Cornell University in 1912, Duggar demonstrated that complete fumigation of compost permits continuous cultivation at a temperature somewhat above normal. This confirmed that the injurious effects of high temperature operate indirectly through biological competition rather than direct thermal damage.

Heavy mushrooms were best produced by not pushing the beds to the highest permissible temperature. Holding temperature below 50 degrees F for a week or two before buttons began to form produced unusually large specimens. Beds spawned in November, frozen several times over winter, and warmed in March bore abundantly with mushrooms of unusual size.

## Moisture Requirements

The atmosphere should contain a fairly high relative humidity of 60 to 75 percent. A gradual but slow evaporation from the surface of the beds was altogether desirable, regulatable by controlling ventilation. The undesirable extremes were caves with no air circulation on one hand, and exposure to drying winds on the other. Neither saturated conditions with water dripping from walls nor excessively dry environments were suitable for commercial production.

## Light and Mushroom Growth

Duggar addressed the common misconception that light is injurious to mushroom cultivation. Since mushrooms are ordinarily grown in cellars or light-proof houses, many assumed light was harmful. In reality, young buttons in pastures and fields are exposed to direct light as soon as they burst through the sod. Light has no directly injurious effect upon the quality or productiveness of cultivated mushrooms.

Sunlight is accompanied by heat, so direct sun through cellar windows could raise temperature undesirably. If temperature can be regulated, light causes no harm. The apparent injurious effects of light under glass were wholly a matter of temperature. Mushrooms can be and are grown in greenhouses, both under the benches and on the benches, under proper conditions of temperature and moisture.

This finding has practical significance for modern growers who may wish to use transparent or translucent growing structures, provided adequate temperature control is maintained. The traditional cellar or cave was chosen not because mushrooms require darkness but because these spaces provided stable temperatures and high humidity — the factors that actually determine success.

## Commercial Mushroom Compost Production

Modern commercial operations produce compost on an industrial scale. A typical medium-sized facility processes 50-100 tons of raw materials per week. The compost is produced in bulk, spawned, and delivered to growing rooms in automated systems. Yield targets for commercial white button mushrooms (*Agaricus bisporus*) are typically 25-35 kg per square meter over a 35-40 day cropping period, representing approximately 1 kg of mushrooms per kg of dry compost ingredients. The industry's ongoing challenge is improving biological efficiency — the ratio of fresh mushroom yield to dry substrate weight — through optimized compost formulations, selective breeding, and improved environmental control.

## Implications for Modern Cultivation

Duggar's findings remain relevant to modern mushroom cultivation. The recognition that fermentation serves primarily biological rather than chemical functions informs current composting practices. His observation that mycelium grows at higher temperatures in pure culture than in beds anticipates modern sterile cultivation techniques. The principle that competing organisms, not temperature itself, limit warm-season production underlies contemporary approaches to [[contamination-identification-prevention]] and [[mushroom-sterile-technique-detailed]].

His work on synthetic media demonstrated that mushrooms are nutritionally flexible, capable of utilizing a range of nitrogen and carbon sources. This finding prefigured the development of modern supplemented substrates and formulated composts that go well beyond traditional horse manure. The key insight — that the substrate must be biologically stable before inoculation — remains the foundation of all commercial [[mushroom-cultivation-bulk-substrates]] preparation today.
