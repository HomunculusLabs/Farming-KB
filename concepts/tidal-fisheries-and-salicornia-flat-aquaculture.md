## Tidal Fisheries and Salicornia Flat Aquaculture

The intergrade between estuary and land—often over [[salicornia]] (salt marsh) flats—represents a vast, underutilized resource for food production. Mollison provides detailed designs for converting barren mud-flats and intertidal sands into productive polycultural systems.

## The Salicornia Flat Problem

Salicornia flats are weakly inundated by tides twice daily. Swan graze these at high tide; domestic geese appreciate the forage of succulents and seagrasses. However, few fish occupy these salt flats, and mosquitoes find multitudinous breeding grounds there. "It is more productive to vary the system by pond and bank designs than to try to manage the unmodified system."

## Designed Estuarine Ponds

### Design Parameters (Colin Sumner, Tas. Fisheries)
- Ponds about 1.8 m deep
- Very gradual bank slopes (~1:3)
- A foot of tide as flushing
- Browsers: periwinkles, Salinator shellfish, shrimp, mullet to keep algae at tolerable levels
- Excellent oyster culture conditions
- Broodstock at mid-bank
- Spat caught on broken shell in trays for transfer to nearby estuaries (40% air time, 60% submerged)
- Geese on range; ti-tree or coastal shrubs on embankments

### Viable Species
- Mullet (with food available or cultured)
- Crab
- Octopus
- Seaweeds
- Shrimp

## The Policy Case for Inshore Fisheries

### Energy Reality
"Sedentary, shore-based, estuarine, inland, and intertidal or tidewater fisheries, now much depleted and neglected, will not only yield higher than deep-sea fisheries, but can reduce or eliminate the main costs of transport, packaging, and storage."

Offshore intensive process fisheries (Japan, Russia, the West) "are doomed in energy terms."

### Species Susceptible to Rearing
Very few shellfish and inshore species are NOT susceptible to rearing or management:
- Oyster
- Crayfish
- Eel
- Octopus
- Seagrasses
- Algae
- Shrimp
- Sand bivalves
- Scalefish

### The Misallocation
"Most research, bureaucratic facilitation, capital input and human effort goes into the offshore multinational, non-sustainable long-range fisheries; fisheries which employ very few men, allow little local industry, and waste enormous tonnages of fish and by-products."

## Essential Structures for Intertidal Reform

1. **Reef walls** of tyres, pipes, stone
2. **Drift fences** to catch seagrass and direct fish
3. **Rafts** for rope suspension of mollusc spat and algae; ring-rafts to rear fish in tideways (as in Ireland where salmon are reared to adulthood in seaways)
4. **Flow-governed tide pools** for correct oyster exposure
5. **Fry traps** for stocking inshore and Salicornia pools
6. **Paddle-powered electric or mechanical systems** at restricted tide-flow points
7. **Evaporative pans** for salt, chemical, and brine-shrimp production (latter as fry food)
8. **Islands** for marine wildfowl refuges and phosphate collection
9. **Deeps** for fish refuges with cover nets or cormorant refuges
10. **Manured seagrass fields** wave-protected by low bunds
11. **Sub-surface (permeable) walls** to retard tide flow in scoured estuaries
12. **[[growing-gourmet-substrate-materials-wood-types]]** to catch new fry or algal forms

## Tidal Stone Traps

Where tides fall 1.2 m or more on rocky coasts:
- 90 cm stone wall encloses rocky clefts or tidal flats
- At high tide, small school fish (garfish, squid, mullet, perch) enter over wall
- Fish feed on algae or are baited by crushed mussels
- Trapped as tide falls
- Refuge pool (or roofed, slotted tank) holds catch at low water
- Late night tides give best catch
- Old gate or door in wall allows system to remain open when not in use
- Provide small fish for stocking manured ponds
- Substrate for oyster and mussel

## Stillwater Funnel Traps

When tide-fall is too slight, or in lakes and lagoons:
- Lead fence plus series of funnels guide fish (eels, trout, perch) to enclosures
- On dams, end enclosure can be under the house verandah
- Widely used in marine shallows (Scotland, Malaysia) and deeper water (Italy, for tuna)
- Made of brush, stone, wire, or fabric netting
- Final catching pens roofed or completely enclosed to foil predators and jumping fish

## Seagrasses: Zostera, Heterozostera, and Posidonia

### Multiple Values
- **Public resource**: Excellent insulators; very resistant to burning
- **Safe insulation**: Safe from [[blesching-cannabis-lung-cancer]] caused by mineral fibres
- **Building insulation**: Under slabs, fumed with creosote or encased in thin plastic; insulates from ground cold, prevents heat escape to earth
- **Forage**: Spring growth grazed by waterfowl; heavy summer [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]] (forage seeds or flour)
- **Soil conditioner**: Sea-grass mulch

### Status
- On sale for roof and ceiling insulation in Victoria
- Considered a "nuisance" on beaches in Adelaide—"careful usage converts a nuisance to a very conservative resource, long-lasting and safe"

## The Sea-Land Nutrient Cycle

### Guano
- Guano from seafowl caught as liquid run-off from solid rafts or stony islands
- Provides essential local phosphate and nitrogenous fertilizer for adjacent land crops
- Even large artificial platforms commercially viable (southwest Africa)
- Pelican and cormorant roost on "islands," deposit tons of guano
- In [[berry-fruit-production-cool-humid-climates]], rain dissolves guano: need [[water-storage-tanks]] or covered solar evaporative pans
- "Sea-grass mulch and guano close the sea-land cycle of nutrients"

### Seabird Management
- Sea fowl (especially seagull rookeries near towns) seldom structured as guano reserves
- "In the waste society their riches are lost to the sea, while guano is mined elsewhere"
- Burrowing and surface-nesting seabirds managed for eggs, meat, fine down, manurial output
- Muttonbird industry of Tasmania yields millions of birds annually; rookeries fast increasing under good management
- By-products of down: best insulation for doonas, beds, rooms
- Adults survive in captivity; progeny return to new rookeries
- Any seaside property can have a rookery if located in general range of selected species

### Seal Rookeries
Can be developed (as on Pribiloff Islands) to yield skins, guano, and protein foods. Once removed, seals often fail to return or are shot by gill-net fishermen as "pests," lowering total sea yield.

### Tropical Equivalents
Dugong, manatee, turtle, and crocodile have similar niches and management potential.

## The Rookery Effect on Fisheries

"It is known to all seine fishermen that shallows off such rookeries are very productive of seagrasses, hence fish such as pike, garfish, and whiting." The guano of seals and seabirds, culled from squid, rough fish, and krill, is the basis of high manurial turnover in inshore sea pastures.

## Key Design Advantages

- Slope, sun reflection from still-ponds, and mixed ecology apply to seawater as to freshwater
- Tide range of 1–9 m enables "free" flushing and governable draining
- Filling higher-level impoundments for later release to lower ponds
- Flow of open sea species, fry, and algal forms as food
- Swan, geese, some duck species prefer saltwater locations or Salicornia and Zostera fields
- Mullet, eels, and bream: easily managed with different food requirements
- Mussels and oysters attach to stone or Zostera, hold sand banks with byssal "roots," provide bream and human food

## Pollution Control Implications

"The implications for control of polluting industry are obvious." Due to metal processing and wood pulp, Tasmania has offshore levels of zinc, cadmium, mercury as high as anywhere in the world. Coasts of all industrial nations are overfished or polluted, negating natural fish protein.

"Water-based ecosystems are as complex as those on land. The ecosystem approach must be applied there just as in terrestrial food production." — Sciences Council of Canada, 1979.
