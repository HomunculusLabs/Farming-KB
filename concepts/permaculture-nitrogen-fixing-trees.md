# Permaculture Nitrogen-Fixing Trees

Nitrogen-fixing trees are among the most valuable elements in permaculture design. They convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms available to other plants through symbiotic relationships with bacteria in their root nodules, building soil fertility while providing numerous other products and services.

## Why Nitrogen-Fixing Trees Matter

Nitrogen is the most commonly limiting nutrient in terrestrial ecosystems. Rather than importing nitrogen through synthetic fertilizers, permaculture design relies on biological nitrogen fixation -- a free, perpetual, and ecologically integrated source of fertility. Nitrogen-fixing trees are particularly valuable because they:

- Provide permanent, long-term nitrogen contribution (unlike annual legumes)
- Produce mulch, timber, fodder, and other yields simultaneously
- Create shelter and habitat for beneficial organisms
- Can serve as nurse crops for establishing other trees
- Improve soil structure through root penetration and organic matter addition

## Key Species

### Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus palmensis)

**Family**: Fabaceae/Papilionaceae
**Origin**: Canary Islands

One of the most useful pioneer species in permaculture. A fast-growing nitrogen-fixing legume growing to 7m, capable of productive growth in areas of low rainfall (300mm).

- **Stock feed**: Crude protein 17-31% (dry matter basis), comparable to lucerne. Stock carrying capacity of 35 sheep/ha by year 6, compared to 1 sheep/ha on pasture alone.
- **Bee forage**: Flowers in winter when little else is in bloom. Staggered cutting extends flowering period.
- **Poultry forage**: Seeds are valuable free-range chicken forage (25% crude protein).
- **Nurse crop**: In New Zealand trials, fruit and nut trees growing within 1m of tagasaste consistently outgrew controls.
- **Timber and fuel**: Good firewood; potential for tool handles and craft work.
- **Management**: Trim early to encourage bushy form; arrange in lock-up fodder systems for rotational grazing.

### Alders (Alnus spp.)

**Family**: Betulaceae

A genus of 30 species notable for being non-leguminous nitrogen fixers (through Schinzia alni bacteria), capable of fixing up to 300 kg/ha/year.

- **Alnus glutinosa** (Common/Black Alder): To 27m, grows along watercourses and wetlands. Wood hardens in water, used for underwater structures, pumps, sluices. Bark used medicinally and for dyeing. Leaves eaten by horses, goats, cattle, sheep.
- **Alnus cordata** (Italian Alder): To 27m, grows rapidly on most sites including chalk and dry soils.
- **Alnus rubra** (Red Alder): To 40m, one of North America's most productive trees (10-11 cu m/ha/yr). Vigorous, cope with grasses, ideal nurse crop. Popular for furniture and panelling.
- **Soil building**: Wide-spaced planting of alders has been known to increase fodder production several-fold through nitrogen fixation and shelter.
- **Inoculation**: Necessary on new soils to ensure nitrogen fixation.

### Black Locust (Robinia pseudoacacia)

**Family**: Fabaceae/Papilionaceae

A fast-growing nitrogen-fixing legume to 25m, living up to 200 years. Native to the Appalachian and Ozark mountains of the United States.

- **Timber**: Hard, heavy, durable, strong, very rot resistant. Used for mine timbers, fence posts, poles, railway sleepers.
- **Stock feed**: Foliage readily eaten by livestock; seed suitable as poultry feed.
- **Honey**: Source of nectar producing honey regarded as one of the finest in the world.
- **Firewood**: Burns like coal, slow to ignite, excellent for charcoal production.
- **Conservation**: Commonly planted for erosion control, shelter belts, and wildlife habitat.
- **Caveat**: Can be invasive due to suckering habit and thorns. Plan for confinement by livestock or use non-suckering cultivars.

### Carob (Ceratonia siliqua)

**Family**: Caesalpinaceae

The largest leguminous tree in Europe (to 15m), native to the Mediterranean. Life span of 500+ years.

- **Food**: Pods are fleshy, flavorful, and nutritious (37-57% total sugars, 2-21% crude protein). Commercially sold as chocolate substitute. Potential for alcohol fuel production.
- **Drought tolerance**: Grows in minimum rainfall of 250mm. Taproots traced to 20m depth.
- **Frost tolerance**: Sensitive below -4C when young; mature trees survive to -8C.
- **Yields**: Up to 1 tonne of pods from a single tree in good season; approximately 8 tonnes/ha commercially.
- **Fire resistance**: Does not burn easily; generally recovers when scorched.
- **Companions**: Grows well with tagasaste, Albizia lophantha, and Acacia decurrens as nurse plants.

### Honey Locust (Gleditsia triacanthos)

**Family**: Caesalpinaceae

A moderately fast-growing, long-lived legume native to North America, reaching 24-30m. Rainfall requirement: 425-1500mm.

- **Fodder**: Pods are high-quality stock feed (especially the pulp)
- **Shade**: Deciduous, allowing light through in winter to pasture below
- **Drought tolerance**: Deep root system withstands drought
- **Soil adaptability**: Tolerant of alkaline, acid, and saline soils

### Wattles (Acacia spp.)

**Family**: Mimosaceae

A large genus of approximately 850 species native to Australia, many with valuable uses:

- **Acacia aneura** (Mulga): Outstanding fodder tree, surviving 200-500mm rainfall. Provides sufficient protein, calcium, and vitamin A from 1.4kg daily ration of leaf.
- **Acacia ampliceps**: Tolerates alkaline, saline, and waterlogged conditions. Good fuel and posts.
- **Timber**: Many species yield useful wood for fuel, crafts, fence posts, and furniture. Some are prime furniture timbers.
- **Seed**: Edible in some species; generally good poultry fodder.
- **Bee forage**: Usually good source of pollen.

## Design Placement

- Use as nurse crops when establishing food forests or orchards
- Plant in shelterbelts and windbreaks for multi-function barriers
- Integrate into grazing systems as protein-rich fodder
- Place in [[permaculture-guild-design-method|guilds]] around food-producing trees
- Use in [[permaculture-earthworks-guide|earthworks stabilization]] on slopes and waterways
