# Glyoxalylamide Reduction Pathway for [[glyoxalylamide-intermediate-in-tryptamine-synthesis]] esis from 4-Acetylindole
## Overview of the Synthetic Strategy
The glyoxalylamide reduction pathway represents a highly efficient and practical route for the synthesis of
[[psilocin]] (4-hydroxy-N,N-[[dimethyltryptamine]]) from protected indole precursors, as demonstrated by Shirota,
Hakamata, and Goda in their 2003 Journal of Natural Products publication on the concise large-scale synthesis
of psilocin [[cia-mkultra-and-psilocybin]] in]].
This three-step core sequence—acylation with oxalyl chloride, amidation with dimethylamine, and reduction with
lithium aluminum hydride—converts the protected 4-acetoxyindole starting material into psilocin in overall
high yield with minimal purification requirements at each intermediate stage.
The pathway exploits the electrophilic reactivity of oxalyl chloride to install a glyoxylyl (oxalyl) acyl group
at the 3-position of the indole ring, followed by nucleophilic displacement of the chloride by dimethylamine
to form a glyoxalylamide intermediate, and finally hydride reduction of both the amide carbonyl and the
ketone carbonyl to yield the fully saturated tryptamine side chain with the N,N-dimethylamino terminus.
This approach offers significant advantages over alternative tryptamine synthesis routes that require benzyl
protection/deprotection sequences, chromatographic purification of intermediates, or harsher reaction
conditions that can compromise the sensitive indole ring system or the 4-hydroxy/4-acetoxy substituent.
## Starting Material: 4-Acetoxyindole from [[4-hydroxyindole]]
The synthesis begins with commercially available [[acetylation-protection-psilocin-synthesis-4-hydroxyindole]], which is first protected by acetylation of
the phenolic hydroxyl group to afford 4-acetoxyindole, the direct substrate for the glyoxalylamide
reduction sequence.
Acetylation of the phenolic hydroxyl is essential because the free 4-hydroxyl group would interfere with
subsequent Friedel-Crafts-type acylation at the indole 3-position, potentially undergoing side reactions
including O-acylation or [[decomposition]] under the acidic and electrophilic conditions employed in the oxalyl
chloride step.
The acetyl protecting group is stable throughout the acylation, amidation, and reduction sequence and can be
removed under mild basic hydrolysis conditions after psilocin has been formed, or it may be carried forward
into the phosphorylation step for [[psilocybin-synthesis]] depending on the overall synthetic plan.
Shirota and colleagues chose acetylation protection because it is operationally simple, high-yielding, and
introduces minimal additional mass or steric bulk that could complicate subsequent synthetic transformations
or purification of the final products.
## Step 1: Oxalyl Chloride Acylation at the Indole 3-Position
The first key transformation in the glyoxalylamide pathway is the Friedel-Crafts-type acylation of 4-acetoxyindole
with oxalyl chloride to install the glyoxylyl (alpha-ketoacyl) functionality at the indole 3-position,
yielding intermediate 5 in the Shirota scheme.
This acylation is performed by dissolving 4-acetoxyindole in anhydrous diethyl ether under an inert atmosphere,
cooling the solution in an ice bath, and adding oxalyl chloride dropwise with careful temperature control to
manage the exothermic reaction and minimize side product formation.
After the addition is complete, the reaction mixture is transferred to a freezer and allowed to stand overnight
at subambient temperature, during which the desired 3-glyoxylyl-4-acetoxyindole intermediate crystallizes as
yellow crystals from the reaction mixture.
The yellow crystalline product (intermediate 5) can be isolated by simple filtration and washing with
n-hexane, eliminating the need for chromatographic purification and providing material of sufficient purity
for direct use in the subsequent amidation step.
The use of n-hexane as the wash solvent is advantageous because it effectively removes residual ether, unreacted
oxalyl chloride, and other soluble impurities from the crystalline product while the glyoxylyl intermediate
remains insoluble and can be collected in high purity by vacuum filtration.
The overnight crystallization at freezer temperature is a critical practical detail that ensures high recovery
of the intermediate and demonstrates the scalability of the protocol, as crystallization-based isolation is
far more practical at large scale than chromatographic purification would be.
## Step 2: Dimethylamine Amidation to Form the Glyoxalylamide
The second step converts the glyoxylyl chloride intermediate into the corresponding glyoxalylamide by
displacing the acyl chloride with dimethylamine, introducing the N,N-dimethylamino moiety that will ultimately
become the terminal amine of the psilocin ethylamine side chain.
In the Shirota protocol, this amidation is accomplished by treating the isolated 3-glyoxylyl-4-acetoxyindole
with dimethylamine in a solvent system comprising tetrahydrofuran (THF) and pyridine, where pyridine serves
both as a cosolvent and as a base to scavenge the hydrochloric acid generated during the substitution reaction.
The reaction proceeds at ambient temperature or with mild heating, and progress is monitored by thin-layer
chromatography (TLC) using Merck silica gel 60 F254 plates with visualization by both UV absorption at 254 nm
and Ehrlich's reagent staining, which produces the characteristic purple color indicative of indole-containing
compounds.
The glyoxalylamide intermediate precipitates from the reaction mixture upon completion, allowing isolation by
filtration without the need for chromatographic purification, consistent with the overall synthetic philosophy
of avoiding chromatography at every stage to maximize practicality and scalability.
The dimethylamine amidation step is highly regioselective, as the chloride is displaced exclusively at the
terminal carbonyl of the oxalyl group rather than at the indole-attached carbonyl, reflecting the greater
electrophilic reactivity of the terminal acyl chloride functionality in the glyoxylyl diacid chloride system.
## Step 3: Lithium Aluminum Hydride Reduction to Psilocin
The third and final step in the glyoxalylamide reduction pathway is the reduction of the glyoxalylamide
intermediate with lithium aluminum hydride (LiAlH4) in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF) under an inert argon
atmosphere to afford 4-acetoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, which upon deprotection yields psilocin.
LiAlH4 is a powerful reducing agent capable of reducing both the amide carbonyl and the ketone carbonyl of
the glyoxalylamide in a single operation, converting the C=O bonds to CH2 groups and thereby constructing the
fully saturated two-carbon ethylamine bridge between the indole 3-position and the N,N-dimethylamino terminus.
The reduction is performed under carefully controlled conditions with the LiAlH4 added portionwise to a
stirred suspension of the glyoxalylamide in dry THF at 0 degrees Celsius under argon, followed by gradual
warming to room temperature and stirring until TLC analysis indicates complete consumption of the starting
material.
After completion, the reaction is carefully quenched by sequential addition of water, aqueous sodium hydroxide,
and additional water (the standard Fieser workup protocol for LiAlH4 reductions) to destroy excess
hydride and convert aluminum salts to filterable aluminum hydroxide precipitates.
The organic layer containing the product is separated, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), filtered,
and concentrated under reduced pressure to afford 4-acetoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine as the protected psilocin
precursor, which can be deprotected by mild basic hydrolysis to yield psilocin as the free base.
The overall yield of the glyoxalylamide reduction pathway from 4-acetoxyindole to psilocin exceeds 85% across
the three steps, representing a significant improvement over alternative routes that typically require
additional protection/deprotection sequences and provide lower overall material throughput.
## Workup and Purification: Sodium Sulfate Drying
The workup procedure following the LiAlH4 reduction step is a critical practical element of the Shirota
protocol, as it determines both the purity and the practical yield of the psilocin product obtained from the
glyoxalylamide reduction pathway.
After quenching the excess LiAlH4 and hydrolyzing the aluminum complexes, the reaction mixture is extracted
with an organic solvent (typically ethyl acetate or dichloromethane) and the combined organic extracts are
washed with brine to remove residual water-soluble impurities before drying over anhydrous sodium sulfate.
Sodium sulfate is preferred over magnesium sulfate for drying in this context because it is a milder drying
agent that is less likely to cause decomposition or rearrangement of the sensitive indole alkaloid products,
particularly when the reaction mixture contains trace acidic components from the workup procedure.
The dried organic solution is filtered through a pad of Celite diatomaceous earth to remove the drying agent
and any residual aluminum hydroxide solids, providing a clear filtrate that can be concentrated directly
without the need for further filtration or chromatographic purification.
This straightforward workup protocol exemplifies the overall synthetic design philosophy of the Shirota route,
which prioritizes operational simplicity and avoids chromatographic purification at every intermediate stage
to maximize scalability and minimize material losses during isolation.
## Comparison with Alternative Synthesis Routes
The glyoxalylamide reduction pathway offers several distinct advantages over alternative routes to psilocin
that have been reported in the literature, particularly those requiring benzyl or other protecting groups for
the indole nitrogen or the 4-hydroxy position during synthesis.
Benzyl-protected syntheses, such as those employing N-benzyltryptamine intermediates, require additional steps
for both the introduction and subsequent removal of the benzyl group, typically via catalytic hydrogenolysis
with palladium on carbon, adding operational complexity and reducing overall yield.
Other reported routes to psilocin include the Leimgruber-Batcho indole synthesis from o-nitrotoluene
derivatives, the Speeter-Anthony tryptamine synthesis from gramine derivatives, and reductive amination of
4-hydroxyindole-3-acetaldehyde with dimethylamine, each of which has specific limitations in terms of yield,
scalability, or the need for chromatographic purification of intermediates.
The Shirota glyoxalylamide route is distinguished by its use of inexpensive and readily available reagents
(oxalyl chloride, dimethylamine, LiAlH4), its avoidance of chromatographic purification at all stages, and
its high overall yield (>85%) that makes it suitable for the gram-scale production of psilocin required for
forensic reference standards and pharmacological research.
The crystallization-based isolation of both the oxalyl chloride acylation product (yellow crystals from
ether/hexane) and the glyoxalylamide intermediate further enhances the practical appeal of this route, as
crystallization is inherently more scalable and reproducible than chromatographic methods for intermediate
isolation in multistep organic synthesis.
## Application to Psilocybin Synthesis via Phosphorylation
The psilocin produced by the glyoxalylamide reduction pathway serves as the direct precursor for psilocybin
synthesis through phosphorylation of the 4-hydroxyl group, as described in the second phase of the Shirota
synthetic sequence.
Following deprotection of the 4-acetoxy group, psilocin is phosphorylated using tetrabenzylpyrophosphate to
install the phosphate ester, yielding a zwitterionic N,O-dibenzyl phosphate intermediate that can be isolated
by filtration in 85% yield.
Catalytic hydrogenolysis of the benzyl groups from this intermediate then affords psilocybin in 72%+ yield,
completing the two-phase synthetic sequence from 4-hydroxyindole to both major bioactive constituents of
psilocybin-containing fungi.
The overall synthetic strategy, combining the glyoxalylamide reduction pathway to psilocin with the
tetrabenzylpyrophosphate phosphorylation to psilocybin, represents one of the most concise and practical
routes reported in the literature for the preparation of these important indole alkaloids at scale.
