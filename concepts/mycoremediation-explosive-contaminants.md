# Mycoremediation of Explosive Contaminants

Fungi play important roles in the biodegradation of explosive contaminants, particularly nitroaromatic compounds such as 2,4,6-trinitrotoluene (TNT) and hexahydro-1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazine (RDX). These munitions constituents contaminate soil and groundwater at military installations, manufacturing facilities, and testing ranges worldwide.

## TNT Transformation by Fungi

Trinitrotoluene (TNT) is the most widely used military explosive and a persistent environmental contaminant. TNT is highly toxic, mutagenic, and resistant to natural degradation due to the electron-withdrawing nitro groups that make the aromatic ring resistant to oxidative attack.

### White-Rot Fungal Degradation

[[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]] and other white-rot fungi transform TNT through initial reduction of nitro groups followed by subsequent oxidation and coupling reactions. The ligninolytic enzyme system, particularly lignin peroxidase (LiP) and manganese peroxidase (MnP), participates in TNT transformation. Laccase-mediated coupling of TNT metabolites to humic substances has been demonstrated, forming nonextractable bound residues that reduce bioavailability and toxicity.

The general pathway involves:
1. Sequential reduction of nitro groups to form 2,4-diamino-6-nitrotoluene and other amino-dinitro intermediates
2. Oxidative coupling of reduced metabolites to form azoxy, azo, and hydrazone derivatives
3. Polymerization and incorporation into soil organic matter (bound residue formation)

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungal Degradation

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi demonstrate significant TNT transformation capability. [[suillus-variegatus]] produces the highest biomass among ECM fungi tested and achieves TNT transformation rates 600% higher in intact mycelium compared to extracellular enzymes alone. The EC50 for S. variegatus is 2-10 ug/ml TNT, indicating reasonable tolerance.

Other ECM fungi capable of TNT transformation include:
- **[[paxillus-involutus]]**: Effective TNT transformation through both mycelial contact and extracellular enzyme activity
- **[[pisolithus-tinctorius]]**: Demonstrated TNT reduction and mineralization capabilities
- **Tylospora fibrillosa**: Produces MnP activity relevant to TNT transformation

The intact mycelium of these fungi is far more effective than cell-free enzyme preparations, suggesting that intracellular reduction pathways play a significant role alongside extracellular oxidative mechanisms.

## RDX Degradation

Hexahydro-1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazine (RDX) is a cyclic nitramine explosive used in military applications. RDX contamination poses significant environmental and health risks. White-rot fungi can degrade RDX through both reductive and oxidative pathways:

- Initial denitration and ring cleavage steps
- Formation of mono- and dinitroso derivatives as intermediates
- Eventual mineralization to carbon dioxide and nitrogen gas

Phanerochaete chrysosporium and other white-rot fungi with active ligninolytic enzyme systems demonstrate RDX degradation capabilities. The nonspecific nature of ligninolytic enzymes allows them to attack the nitramine structure of RDX.

## Bound Residue Formation

A significant fate pathway for TNT and other explosive contaminants in fungal treatment systems is the formation of nonextractable bound residues. Laccase and peroxidase enzymes catalyze the oxidative coupling of reduced TNT metabolites (particularly aminodinitrotoluene derivatives) to humic and fulvic acid precursors in soil. This covalent incorporation:

- Reduces the bioavailability of the contaminant
- Decreases toxicity to soil organisms
- Provides a long-term immobilization mechanism
- Can be considered a form of natural attenuation

Dawel et al. (1997) demonstrated laccase-mediated coupling of 2,4-diamino-6-nitrotoluene to guaiacol as a model for TNT metabolite incorporation into humic organic soil matrix.

## Advantages of Fungal Treatment

Fungal treatment of explosive contaminants offers several advantages:

- Nonspecific ligninolytic enzymes can attack recalcitrant nitroaromatic structures
- Both living and nonliving fungal biomass can be employed
- Formation of bound residues provides permanent immobilization
- Ectomycorrhizal fungi can be paired with host plants for rhizosphere remediation
- Tolerance to high contaminant concentrations (EC50 values in the ug/ml range)

## Nitroaromatic Compound Toxicology

The environmental and health impacts of nitroaromatic explosives extend far beyond the immediate vicinity of contaminated sites. Nitroaromatic explosives pose severe risks to human and environmental health. TNT is classified as a possible human carcinogen (Group 2C) by the International Agency for Research on Cancer. Acute exposure causes methemoglobinemia, hepatotoxicity, and renal damage. Chronic exposure leads to anemia, cataracts, and immune system suppression. RDX is a known central nervous system irritant causing seizures at high doses, and is classified as a potential human carcinogen. Environmental persistence is substantial: TNT has a soil half-life of 2 months to 2 years depending on conditions, while RDX is highly mobile in groundwater due to its low soil adsorption coefficient (Koc). The US Environmental Protection Agency has established maximum contaminant levels for RDX in drinking water at 2 ppb. Military installations worldwide are affected, with over 1,000 sites identified in the United States alone where explosive residues exceed regulatory thresholds.

## HMX and Other Secondary Explosives

High Melting Explosive (HMX, octahydro-1,3,5,7-tetranitro-1,3,5,7-tetrazocine) is a cyclic nitramine related to RDX but with higher density and detonation velocity. HMX contamination frequently co-occurs with RDX at manufacturing and testing facilities. Phanerochaete chrysosporium has demonstrated HMX transformation through pathways analogous to RDX degradation, though rates are generally slower. Nitroglycerin (NG) and PETN (pentaerythritol tetranitrate) are nitrate ester explosives also susceptible to fungal degradation. White-rot fungi degrade nitrate esters through esterase and nitroreductase activity. Ammonium perchlorate (AP), a major oxidizer in solid rocket propellants, has been targeted for fungal bioremediation, though this application remains less developed than nitroaromatic degradation.

## Perchlorate and Composition B Treatment

Composition B, a common military explosive formulation, consists of approximately 59.5% RDX, 39.5% TNT, and 1% wax binder. The co-contamination challenge of treating both RDX and TNT simultaneously requires fungal species capable of expressing both reductive and oxidative enzymatic pathways. [[pleurotus-ostreatus]] has shown promise for treating Composition B-contaminated soils because it produces both laccase and peroxidase enzymes effective against TNT while also mediating RDX denitration. In mixed explosive contamination scenarios, sequential or combined treatment strategies may be necessary, as the dominant explosive compound can influence fungal enzyme expression patterns. Bioremediation of perchlorate-contaminated groundwater has been achieved using denitrifying bacteria in constructed wetlands, though fungal contributions to perchlorate degradation remain an area of active investigation.

## Field-Scale Deployment Considerations

Translating laboratory mycoremediation results to field-scale operations requires careful attention to several practical factors. Soil amendment with lignocellulosic substrates (wood chips, straw, sawdust) is typically necessary to support fungal growth and enzyme production, as native soils often lack sufficient organic matter. Moisture management is critical: fungal activity requires 40-60% water holding capacity, and periodic irrigation may be needed in arid climates. Temperature control through seasonal timing or shading can optimize enzyme production, as ligninolytic enzyme expression in white-rot fungi is temperature-dependent. Bioaugmentation with pre-grown fungal inoculum on sterilized grain or wood chips provides faster establishment than direct spore inoculation. Monitoring protocols should include explosive residue analysis (HPLC or GC-MS), enzyme activity assays, and toxicity testing (Microtox or earthworm bioassays) to track treatment progress. Treatment timelines at field scale typically range from 3 to 24 months depending on contaminant concentration, soil volume, and environmental conditions.

## Limitations and Future Needs

Key limitations include the need for optimized soil characteristics, competition from native microorganisms, and the challenge of scaling laboratory results to field conditions. Large-scale greenhouse and field trials are needed, along with optimization of fungal inoculum production, soil conditions, and host plant selection for mycorrhizal systems.

## Comparative Performance of Fungal Species

Different fungal species exhibit markedly different capabilities for explosive contaminant degradation. Phanerochaete chrysosporium remains the most extensively studied white-rot fungus for this application, but comparative studies have revealed that other species may outperform it under specific conditions.
Irpex lacteus demonstrates superior TNT tolerance compared to P. chrysosporium, maintaining ligninolytic enzyme activity at TNT concentrations up to 100 mg/L. Bjerkandera adusta produces manganese peroxidase with broader pH tolerance, making it suitable for neutral to alkaline contaminated soils where P. chrysosporium struggles.
[[trametes-versicolor]] ([[coriolus-versicolor]]) produces exceptionally stable laccase enzymes that retain activity in soil environments longer than those of other species, providing extended treatment capability from a single inoculation event. [[pleurotus-eryngii]] achieves RDX degradation rates approximately 40% higher than P. chrysosporium under identical conditions, attributed to its robust manganese peroxidase system.
Among ectomycorrhizal fungi, Suillus variegatus consistently outperforms other ECM species in TNT transformation assays, with biomass production being a key factor in overall treatment capacity. Selection of the optimal fungal species should consider not only laboratory degradation rates but also field-relevant factors such as soil pH, temperature range, competitive ability against native microorganisms, and ease of inoculum production.
Enzyme production kinetics also differ significantly: P. chrysosporium produces lignin peroxidase primarily under nitrogen-limited secondary metabolism, while T. versicolor constitutively produces laccase under a wider range of nutritional conditions, simplifying cultivation requirements for large-scale inoculum production.

## Regulatory and Safety Considerations

The application of fungal bioremediation to explosive-contaminated sites operates within a regulatory framework primarily governed by military and environmental agencies. In the United States, the Department of Defense Environmental Restoration Program oversees cleanup of military installations under CERCLA (Superfund) and RCRA regulations. Mycoremediation is classified as a biotreatment technology and requires demonstration of efficacy through treatability studies before full-scale deployment. Regulatory endpoints typically focus on achieving risk-based concentration limits rather than complete contaminant removal, as bound residue formation is increasingly recognized as an acceptable remediation endpoint. Worker safety during fungal inoculum preparation and field application requires standard personal protective equipment to minimize dermal and inhalation exposure to both explosive contaminants and fungal spores. The introduction of non-native fungal species to remediation sites may require environmental impact assessments under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), though most white-rot fungi used in mycoremediation are cosmopolitan species already present in most temperate soils. Long-term monitoring requirements for bioremediation sites typically span 3-5 years post-treatment to verify that contaminant concentrations remain below regulatory thresholds and that no mobilization of bound residues occurs. The European Union REACH regulation also applies to mycoremediation products containing living organisms, requiring registration and risk assessment before commercial application. International guidelines from the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) are increasingly incorporating biological remediation methods as preferred alternatives to physical and chemical treatments where feasible, recognizing their lower environmental footprint and potential for in situ application without excavation.

## Genetic and Molecular Approaches to Enhance Explosive Degradation

Advances in [[fungal-genetics]] and molecular biology are opening new avenues for enhancing explosive contaminant degradation capabilities. Transcriptomic analysis of Phanerochaete chrysosporium exposed to TNT reveals upregulation of genes encoding lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and cytochrome P450 enzymes, providing targets for strain improvement through directed evolution or CRISPR-based genome editing. Overexpression of laccase genes in Trametes versicolor under constitutive promoters has yielded recombinant strains with 5-10 fold higher enzyme production compared to wild-type, significantly accelerating TNT and RDX degradation rates in laboratory reactors.
Proteomic studies have identified previously unknown peroxidase isoenzymes in Bjerkandera adusta that are induced specifically by nitroaromatic compounds, suggesting that natural fungal diversity harbors untapped degradation potential that metagenomic surveys can help discover. Heterologous expression of fungal degradation enzymes in bacterial or yeast hosts offers a pathway to scalable enzyme production for ex situ treatment systems, though enzyme stability in soil environments remains a challenge for this approach.
Quorum sensing molecules that regulate ligninolytic enzyme production in white-rot fungi have been identified, raising the possibility of chemical induction strategies that could enhance field-scale treatment performance without genetic modification. Horizontal gene transfer of degradation pathways between co-cultured fungal species has been observed in laboratory studies, providing a natural mechanism for expanding the degradative capacity of fungal consortia applied to mixed explosive contamination.
Epigenetic regulation of [[fungal-enzyme-systems]] through histone modification and DNA methylation represents another frontier: environmental signals from contaminated soils may trigger heritable changes in gene expression that enhance degradation phenotypes in subsequent generations of fungal mycelia, offering a potential mechanism for adaptation without genetic mutation.
