# Mycoremediation of Explosive Contaminants

Fungi play important roles in the biodegradation of explosive contaminants, particularly nitroaromatic compounds such as 2,4,6-trinitrotoluene (TNT) and hexahydro-1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazine (RDX). These munitions constituents contaminate soil and groundwater at military installations, manufacturing facilities, and testing ranges worldwide.

## TNT Transformation by Fungi

Trinitrotoluene (TNT) is the most widely used military explosive and a persistent environmental contaminant. TNT is highly toxic, mutagenic, and resistant to natural degradation due to the electron-withdrawing nitro groups that make the aromatic ring resistant to oxidative attack.

### White-Rot Fungal Degradation

[[pisolithus-tinctorius]]**: Demonstrated TNT reduction and mineralization capabilities
- **Tylospora fibrillosa**: Produces MnP activity relevant to TNT transformation

The intact mycelium of these fungi is far more effective than cell-free enzyme preparations, suggesting that intracellular reduction pathways play a significant role alongside extracellular oxidative mechanisms.

## RDX Degradation

Hexahydro-1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazine (RDX) is a cyclic nitramine explosive used in military applications. RDX contamination poses significant environmental and health risks. White-rot fungi can degrade RDX through both reductive and oxidative pathways:

- Initial denitration and ring cleavage steps
- Formation of mono- and dinitroso derivatives as intermediates
- Eventual mineralization to carbon dioxide and nitrogen gas

[[singh-ligninolytic-enzymes-fungal-bioremediation]] allows them to attack the nitramine structure of RDX.

## Bound Residue Formation

A significant fate pathway for TNT and other explosive contaminants in fungal treatment systems is the formation of nonextractable bound residues. Laccase and peroxidase enzymes catalyze the oxidative coupling of reduced TNT metabolites (particularly aminodinitrotoluene derivatives) to humic and fulvic acid precursors in soil. This covalent incorporation:

- Reduces the bioavailability of the contaminant
- Decreases toxicity to soil organisms
- Provides a long-term immobilization mechanism
- Can be considered a form of natural attenuation

Dawel et al. (1997) demonstrated laccase-mediated coupling of 2,4-diamino-6-nitrotoluene to guaiacol as a model for TNT metabolite incorporation into humic organic soil matrix.

## Field-Scale Deployment Considerations

Translating laboratory mycoremediation results to field-scale operations requires careful attention to several practical factors. Soil amendment with lignocellulosic substrates (wood chips, straw, sawdust) is typically necessary to support fungal growth and enzyme production, as native soils often lack sufficient organic matter. Moisture management is critical: fungal activity requires 40-60% water holding capacity, and periodic irrigation may be needed in arid climates. Temperature control through seasonal timing or shading can optimize enzyme production, as ligninolytic enzyme expression in white-rot fungi is temperature-dependent. Bioaugmentation with pre-grown fungal inoculum on sterilized grain or wood chips provides faster establishment than direct spore inoculation. Monitoring protocols should include explosive residue analysis (HPLC or GC-MS), enzyme activity assays, and toxicity testing (Microtox or earthworm bioassays) to track treatment progress. Treatment timelines at field scale typically range from 3 to 24 months depending on contaminant concentration, soil volume, and environmental conditions.

Key limitations include the need for optimized soil characteristics, competition from native microorganisms, and the challenge of scaling laboratory results to field conditions.
