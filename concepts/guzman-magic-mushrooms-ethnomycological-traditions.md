# Ethnomycological Traditions of Neurotropic Mushrooms

## Overview

Neurotropic fungi have been used by indigenous peoples across the world
for religious, divinatory, and healing purposes. The ethnomycological
study of these traditions, pioneered by R. Gordon Wasson and Roger Heim,
reveals deep cultural relationships between humans and psychoactive
mushrooms spanning thousands of years.

## Mesoamerican Mushroom Cultures

### Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico

The Mazatec people of the Sierra Mazateca in Oaxaca are the most
well-documented mushroom-using culture, largely through the work of
Wasson and Heim in the 1950s. Key Mazatec species include:
- **Psilocybe mexicana**: Known as "nti-xi-tho" (sacred mushroom that
  springs forth); the first species chemically studied by Hofmann;
  used by curanderos (healers) for divination and healing
- **P. caerulescens**: Known as "derrumbes" (landslides) because it
  grows on disturbed soil; used in Mazatec ceremonies
- **P. hoogshagenii**: Used by Mazatec healers
- **Conocybe siliginoides**: Known as "nti-si-tho" by the Mazatec;
  used as a sacred mushroom in ceremonies
- **Dictyophora indusiata**: Three forms used for divination by the
  Chinantec Indians of Oaxaca (Heim and Wasson, 1958; Wasson, 1959a;
  Guzman, 1997)

The Mazatec use these mushrooms in nighttime veladas (ceremonies)
conducted by curanderos for diagnosing illness, finding lost objects,
and communicating with spirits. The mushrooms are typically consumed
fresh in pairs during the ritual.

### Nahua Indians

The Nahua (Aztec descendants) have a well-documented tradition of
mushroom use. The Aztec name for sacred mushrooms was "teonanacatl"
("flesh of the gods"), documented by the Spanish chronicler Bernardino
de Sahagun in the 16th century. The Aztecs celebrated the mushroom
during the feast of Teonanacatl, and used P. cubensis (known as
"san isidros") and other species in religious ceremonies.

### Zapotec Indians

The Zapotec use **P. zapotecorum** (known as "di nize taaya" meaning
"fungus of the genius"), one of the most widely distributed subtropical
neurotropic species, ranging from Mexico through Central America to
South America and New Zealand.

### Mixe, Chinantec, and Mixtec Indians

The Mixe of Oaxaca use neurotropic mushrooms in healing ceremonies
(Lowy, 1977; Lipp, 1990, 1991). The Chinantec use Dictyophora
indusiata for divination (Heim and Wasson, 1958). The Mixtec used
"sacred puffballs" (Ott et al., 1975) including Vascellum pratense, V.
intermedium, and Lycoperdon oblongiosporum, though Ott concluded these
lacked genuine neurotropic properties.

### Church of "Nuestro Senor del Honguito"

Guzman, Wasson, and Herrera (1975) documented a church in
Chignahuapan, Puebla, Mexico, dedicated to the worship of "Our Lord of
the Mushroom" (Nuestro Senor del Honguito), representing a unique case
of syncretism between Catholicism and pre-Columbian mushroom worship.

## Ritual Preparation and Use

In Mexico, neurotropic mushrooms are typically consumed fresh during
nighttime veladas (ceremonies) conducted by curanderos. Mushrooms are
traditionally eaten in pairs, with the curandero consuming one pair and
the patient another. P. muliercula ("hombrecitos" or little men) was
eaten either alone or together with P. zapotecorum. Some groups prepared
semifermented mixtures of Psilocybe species (Guzman, 1983).

Traditional doses were carefully calibrated by experienced curanderos.
Modern analysis showed that normal doses of P. mexicana contain
approximately 10 mg of psilocybin, with effects lasting 4-6 hours.

## New Guinea Aboriginal Use

The "mushroom madness" of the Kuma people of New Guinea was documented
by Heim and Wasson (1965). They used several species of Boletus,
Heimiella, and Russula in ritual contexts. However, Ott (1993) noted
that scientific evidence for the neurotropic properties of many New
Guinea species is lacking.

## Asian Traditions

### Soma Hypothesis

Wasson (1968) proposed that [[amanita-muscaria-guide]] was the identity of the
Vedic Soma, the sacred drink of ancient India. This hypothesis has been
debated extensively, with supporters (Doniger, 1990; Riedlinger, 1990;
Ruck, 1990) and critics.

### Japanese and Southeast Asian Traditions

P. venenata was known in Japan as a poisonous toadstool since Imai
(1932). Matsuda (1960) documented hallucinations caused by P. venenata.
Traditional Japanese use of Dictyophora indusiata and Cordyceps species
has been documented by Imazeki and Hongo (1983, 1987). In Chinese
medicine, Dictyophora indusiata and Panaeolus papilionaceus (Li, 1977)
have been used for centuries.

Allen and Merlin (1992a, 1992b) documented psychoactive fungi use in
Thailand on Koh Samui and Koh Pha-Ngan. In the Philippines, Cox (1981)
documented use of Copelandia cyanescens in Samoa.

## Siberian Traditions

The use of Amanita muscaria by Siberian peoples (Chukchee, Koryak,
Kamchadal) is one of the best-documented traditions outside the
Americas (Wasson and Wasson, 1957; Singer, 1958, 1959). They consumed
the mushroom directly or drank the urine of those who had eaten it, as
the active compounds pass through largely unmetabolized.

## European and Archaeological Contexts

In Europe, there is no confirmed pre-modern tradition of deliberate
psilocybin mushroom use, though P. semilanceata has been known since
Sowerby (1797-1809). Lowy (1972) documented mushroom symbolism in Maya
codices. Pre-Columbian mushroom stones from Guatemala (dating to
approximately 1000 BCE) provide the earliest archaeological evidence of
mushroom cults in Mesoamerica.

## Contemporary and Modern Use

Psilocybin mushrooms are used worldwide in modern contexts including
recreational use, therapeutic applications, and neo-shamanic ceremonies.
The diversity of available species has facilitated global distribution
of these practices.
