# Mollison: Dam Types and Landscape Placement

Source: Bill Mollison, [[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 7, Section 9)

## Overview of Dam Types

Mollison describes a comprehensive typology of small dams and their optimal placement in the landscape.

Earth dams or weirs with retaining walls 6 metres high or less, having a large oversize stable spillway, are no threat to life or property if well-made.

They need not displace populations, stop flow in streams, create health problems, fill with silt, or block fish migrations.

Dams made anywhere but as barriers on streams effectively add to stream flow in the [[mushroom-long-term-storage]].

## Saddle Dams

Saddle dams are usually the highest available storages, on saddles or hollows in the skyline profile of hills.

They can be fully excavated below grade or walled on either side or both sides of the saddle.

Shapes include circular, oblong, or shark-egg shaped with horns or extensions at either end.

Primary uses are for wildlife, stock watering, and high-elevation [[water-storage-techniques]].

Saddle dams can also serve as storages for pumped water used for energy generation.

## Ridgepoint Dams

Ridgepoint dams (also called horseshoe dams) are built on the sub-plateaus of flattened ridges, usually on a descending ridgeline, and below saddle dams.

Their shape is typically that of a horse's hoof.

They can be made below grade or walled by earth banks.

Uses are similar to saddle dams: wildlife, stock, and limited irrigation.

Ridgepoint dams are of limited irrigation use but very useful for runoff and pumped storages.

## Keypoint Dams

Keypoint dams are located in the valleys of secondary streams in humid landscapes, at the highest practical construction point in the hill profile.

This is where the stream profile changes from convex to concave, which can be judged by eye.

A descending contour then picks up all other keypoints on the main valley.

Primary use is to store irrigation water for lower slopes.

A second or third series can be run below the primary series, with the spillway of the last dam returned upstream to meet the main valley.

## Contour Dams

Contour dam walls are built on contour wherever the slope is 8% or less.

Contours and dam walls can be concave or convex to the fall line across the slope.

Uses include irrigation, aquaculture, and flood-flow basins in semi-arid areas.

Contour dams are practical on slopes of 7-9 degrees as part of a general series on farms.

## Barrier Dams

Barrier dams are always constructed across a flowing or intermittent stream bed.

They need ample spillways, careful construction, and fish ladders on biologically important streams.

Made most frequently as energy systems, they are also used for irrigation if constructed well above the main valley floors where crops are grown.

Mollison notes that almost all modern assessments would condemn large-scale barrier dams on their record of past fiascos, but a sober assessment of small barrier dams shows multiple benefits.

## Turkey's Nest Dams

Turkey's nest dams are above-grade tanks where water has to be pumped in, often by windmill or solar pump.

They are common in flatlands as stock water tanks or for low-head irrigation.

These require energy input for filling but provide gravity-fed distribution from the elevated position.

## Check Dams

Check dams are barrier dams not intended to create water storages but to slow water flow.

In drylands, permeable gabion barriers of rock-filled mesh baskets create silt fields and water-spreading across eroding valleys.

These are a primary tool for rehydration of arid landscapes.

## Low Barrier Dams

Low barrier dams of 1-4 metres high can assist stream oxygenation, provide permanent pools, be stepped to allow fish ladders or bypasses, and provide sites for modest power generation.

## Dam Efficiency

The efficiency of dams depends on the flatness of the area behind the wall.

A V valley, U valley, plateau, or field should be as level as possible for greatest efficiency.

The key to efficiency is the length of the dam wall compared with the length of water dammed.

Short dams at constricted sites where the valley behind is flattish are very cost-effective.

## Construction Principles

Earth must be free of boulders, logs, grass clumps, and topsoil, which is critical to earth stability.

Shrinkage of well-compacted dams is less than 1%.

Earth should be neither so dry as to crumble nor so wet as to slump or squash out under the roller.

Slopes are safe at a ratio of 3:1 (inner) and 2 or 2.5:1 (outer), with freeboard at 0.9 metres.

A key should be cut to prevent shear and cut off base seepage, needed on all walls 1.8 metres or more high.

## Landscape Integration

Small dams are jewels in the landscape when fenced and planted to 30-60 metres of forest and fruit surround.

Crests can be gravelled and safely used as roads to cross valleys or bogs.

Special deep areas, islands, peninsulas, and shelves can be made inside the dam for birds, plants, and wildfire-immune houses.
