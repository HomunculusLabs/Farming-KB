# Cebil — Anadenanthera colubrina Snuff

Cebil (also known as Villca or Vilca) is a psychoactive snuff prepared from the seeds of Anadenanthera colubrina, a tree native to the southern Andean region of South America. The ritual use of Cebil seeds represents the longest continuous shamanic practice in the Americas, with archaeological evidence dating back approximately 4,500 years in the Puna region of northwest Argentina.

## Botanical Description

Anadenanthera colubrina (syn. Piptadenia colubrina) is a tree in the legume family (Fabaceae) that grows 9 to 50 feet (3-18 meters) tall. It has an almost black bark, often adorned with conical thorns, and finely pinnate leaves reaching up to 1 foot (30 cm) in length.

The yellowish-white flowers are round and grow in clusters. The leathery, dark brown fruit pods grow to about 1 foot (35 cm) long and contain very flat, red-brown seeds measuring one-half to 1 inch (1-2 cm) wide with rounded to right angles.

The tree is native to northwest Argentina, southern Bolivia, and parts of Peru and Brazil. The Puna region of northwest Argentina represents the area with the longest uninterrupted ritualistic or shamanic use of psychoactive substances in the world.

## Alkaloid Chemistry

The seeds of Anadenanthera colubrina contain tryptamine alkaloids as their principal psychoactive constituents. Some varieties of Cebil seed contain exclusively bufotenine (C12H16ON2) as the active ingredient. Other seed samples have yielded 5-MeO-MMT, DMT, DMT-N-oxide, bufotenine, and 5-OH-DMT-N-oxide.

Chemical analysis of dried seeds from trees in northeast Argentina (Salta province) found mostly bufotenine at concentrations exceeding 4%, along with a related substance (possibly serotonin), but no other tryptamines or alkaloids. Seeds taken from the garden of a Mataco shaman contained an extraordinary 12% bufotenine content. The ripe pods of the fruit also contain some bufotenine.

This chemistry distinguishes Cebil from its close relative [[anadenanthera-peregrina]] (Yopo/Cohoba), which contains primarily DMT and 5-MeO-DMT rather than bufotenine.

## Archaeological Evidence

In the Atacama Desert of northern Chile, the art historian and archaeologist C. Manuel Torres excavated and studied over six hundred prehistoric graves at the oasis of San Pedro de Atacama. Nearly every interred person was accompanied by numerous tools dedicated to the ritual sniffing of Cebil, demonstrating the central importance of this practice in the region's pre-Columbian cultures.

In the Puna region of northwest Argentina, the oldest archaeological proof of ritual or shamanic Cebil use has been found. Numerous ceramic pipes have been discovered in certain caves, and the bowls of some pipes still contain Cebil seed residue. The psychoactive use seems in particular to have influenced the culture of Tiahuanaco — literally, "City of the Gods" — which is considered the mother of Andean civilizations.

## The Villca Question

A psychoactive substance cited in colonial-era sources under the name Villca (also rendered Huilca, Huillca, Vilca, Vilcas, Wil'ka, Willca, or Willka) is possibly identical to Cebil. Villca was of great ritual and religious significance in Peru before the arrival of the Spaniards.

Villca was known to the Incan high priests and soothsayers (umu) as Villca or Villca camayo. A holy Indian relic (huaca) was known as Villca or Vilcacona, and an especially holy mountain is known as Villca Coto. Villca seeds had ceremonial significance for the Incas as a psychoactive additive for chicha (fermented corn beverage). The "juice" of Villca was added to the fermented drink, and the soothsayer who consumed it would then be able to look into the future.

Villca was also the name for enemas used for medicinal or shamanic purposes. The many spellings of the name across colonial sources reflect the difficulty Spanish chroniclers had in transcribing Quechua and Aymara terms.

## Contemporary Use

The shamans of the Wichi (Mataco Indians) of northwest Argentina still use a snuff made of Cebil today. The shamans smoke the dried or roasted seeds, preferably in a pipe or rolled in a cigarette. The Cebil seeds are for them a means to enter and influence another reality — as shaman Fortunato Ruiz expressed it, Cebil is "a gateway to a visionary world."

As some Matacos have converted to Christianity in recent years, they have come to identify Cebil with the biblical Tree of Knowledge. But they do not see Cebil as a "forbidden fruit"; rather, they see it as the fruit of a holy tree used by shamans for healing.

## Visionary Effects

The hallucinations triggered by Cebil snuff last for roughly twenty minutes and include strong hallucinations, which are often only black and white and seldom in color. They are not typically geometric in nature but are strongly flowing and "decentralized," very reminiscent of the imagery produced by the pre-Columbian Tiahuanaco culture.

Many examples of pre-Columbian snuff paraphernalia (snuff tablets, snuff pipes) displaying the iconography of the Tiahuanaco culture have been found in Puna and the Atacama Desert. The intertwined snakes coming out of the head of the oracle god at Chavin de Huantar are clearly inspired by Cebil hallucinations.

Cebil seeds also have psychoactive effects when smoked. The effects are very strong for about thirty minutes and then fade. They begin with a feeling of heaviness in the body. After five to ten minutes, visual hallucinations begin with the eyes closed, often featuring worm- and snakelike images flowing into one another. Sometimes geometric, symmetrical, or crystallographic hallucinations occur, but strong visions of a realistic nature (flying, traveling in another world, transforming into an animal) are rare.
