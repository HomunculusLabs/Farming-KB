# Mushroom Hormones and Gill-Directed Tropism

## Overview

The hypothesis that mushroom fruiting bodies produce endogenous hormones—particularly
from the gill (lamellae) tissue—has been a recurring theme in fungal developmental
biology since the 1960s. Decapitation experiments, agar diffusate studies, and
comparative work on fertile vs. sterile basidiocarps have all pointed to the gills
as a source of regulatory compounds that influence tropic behavior, stipe elongation,
and the transition from primary growth to spore-directed orientation.

## Historical Development of the Hormone Hypothesis

The concept of mushroom hormones emerged independently in several laboratories:

- **Gruen (1963)**: Demonstrated endogenous growth regulation in *[[agaricus-bisporus]]*
  carpophores, showing that some factor from the pileus influenced stipe elongation.
  Removal of the pileus altered growth patterns, suggesting a top-down regulatory
  signal.

- **Hagimoto (1963)**: Proposed the existence of a specific "[[mushroom-growth-hormone-decapitation-gill-diffusate-evidence-basidiomycetes]]"
  linked to the [[spore-formation-geotropic-response-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]] of fruiting bodies in *Agaricus bisporus*. This
  hormone appeared to control the upward curvature of the stipe during maturation.

- **Hagimoto and Konishi (1960)**: Showed that agar diffusates from the gills could
  partially replace the influence of the pileus on stipe growth, narrowing the
  source of the active compound to the lamellae themselves.

These early studies established the paradigm that gill tissue produces one or more
diffusible substances that modulate the growth direction and elongation rate of the
stipe.

## Decapitation Experiments

Decapitation (removal of the pileus/cap) has been the primary experimental approach
for demonstrating gill-derived hormonal regulation:

1. In intact fruiting bodies, the stipe elongates and then curves upward
  ([[coprophilous-habitat-negative-geotropism-spore-dispersal-adaptation]]) as the pileus expands and spores mature.

2. When the pileus is removed before this transition, the stipe continues to
  elongate but does not exhibit the characteristic upward curvature.

3. Application of agar that has absorbed diffusates from gills to decapitated
  stipes partially restores the geotropic response, confirming that a diffusible
  factor from the gills is involved.

This experimental paradigm has been replicated across multiple species including
*Agaricus bisporus*, *Coprinus* spp., and *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]*, suggesting a
conserved mechanism among agarics.

## Connection to Spore Production in Psilocybe Cubensis

Badham (1982) provided particularly compelling evidence linking gill-derived
hormonal signals to spore production in *P. cubensis*:

- **Fertile vs. sterile basidiocarps**: When fertile (spore-producing)
  basidiocarps reached Stage 2 (nearly spherical pileus), they exhibited a
  negative geotropic response—the pileus turned upward with lamellae facing
  downward. This response was absent in sterile (sporeless) mutants.

- **Light dependence**: The negative geotropism required some light, but this
  requirement appeared secondary—light was needed for spore production, and
  the geotropic response was coupled to sporulation rather than to light
  perception directly.

- **Second tropism model**: Badham proposed that a substance produced in the
  gills during spore formation modifies the primary anemotropic response,
  causing the mushroom to respond primarily to gravity. In the absence of
  spore production (sterile mutants), this second tropism never activates,
  and the mushroom continues to respond primarily to wind.

This model elegantly explains why sporeless mutants fail to exhibit the
negative geotropic response: without active spore-producing gill tissue,
the hormonal signal that triggers the transition from wind-directed to
gravity-directed growth is never generated.

## Agar Diffusate Technique

The agar diffusate method is a simple but powerful bioassay for detecting
gill-derived growth factors:

1. A thin layer of agar is placed in contact with the gills of a mature
   mushroom for a defined period (typically several hours).
2. The agar absorbs any diffusible compounds released by the gill tissue.
3. Small blocks of this "loaded" agar are then applied to decapitated stipes
   or to intact stipes whose pileus has been removed.
4. Restoration of the tropic response indicates the presence of an active
   factor in the diffusate.

Hagimoto and Konishi used this technique to demonstrate that the active
compound could diffuse through agar, placing constraints on its molecular
size (small enough for agar gel diffusion) and stability (retained activity
after absorption and re-release from the agar matrix).

## Identity of the Mushroom Growth Hormone

Despite decades of investigation, the precise chemical identity of the mushroom
growth hormone remains elusive. Several candidates have been considered:

- **Auxin-like compounds**: Early researchers detected indole-3-acetic acid (IAA)
  and related compounds in mushroom tissue (Konishi and Hagimoto, 1961), but
  these appear to be present at levels too low to account for the tropic effects.
  Furthermore, no evidence suggests that higher plant hormones regulate mushroom
  growth directly.

- **Gibberellin-like substances**: Pegg (1973) detected gibberellin-like
  substances in basidiomycete sporophores, but their functional significance
  in tropism was not established.

- **Novel fungal compounds**: The most likely scenario is that the gill-derived
  growth factor is a compound unique to basidiomycetes, not homologous to any
  known plant hormone. Its identification likely requires modern metabolomic
  approaches applied to gill tissue at different developmental stages.

## Functional Significance

The gill-directed tropism serves a clear adaptive purpose: ensuring that
spore-producing surfaces are oriented optimally for spore dispersal. By
switching from wind-directed growth (which elevates the pileus) to
gravity-directed growth (which orients the gills horizontally for
spore release), the mushroom maximizes the distance spores travel before
deposition and increases the area of ground covered by the spore print.

This two-phase growth strategy—first reaching upward into the air column,
then leveling off for spore release—is particularly important for
coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) species like *P. cubensis*, whose
spores must reach fresh herbivore dung to complete their life cycle.
