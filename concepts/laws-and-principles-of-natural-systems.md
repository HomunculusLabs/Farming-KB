## Laws and Principles of Natural Systems

## The Limits of Scientific Method in Biology

Before examining specific laws and principles, it is essential to understand the limitations of applying scientific method to living systems. Living systems do not lend themselves to strict scientific definition for two fundamental reasons: life is always in a process of change, and life systems react to investigation or experiments. There will never be "laws" in biology in the way there are laws in physics.

Rigorous scientific method requires rigorous control of variables, which presumes two things that are impossible in life systems:

1. That you know all variables before you start — in order to control some and measure others
2. That you can in fact control all or any variables without creating disorder in the life system

Every experiment is carried out by people and results are imparted to people. Thus living things conduct and impart knowledge. To ignore life in the system studied, one has to ignore oneself. Life exists in conditions of flux, not imposed control, and responds to any form of control in a new fashion. Living things respond to strict control — either by removal of stimuli or by constant input of stimuli — by becoming uncontrolled, or by dysfunction, or by going mad.

## The Law of Return

The single law offered by natural systems is the Law of Return: "Whatever we take, we must return." This can be stated in many forms:

- "Nature demands a return for every gift received"
- "The user must pay"
- "Every object must responsibly provide for its replacement; society must, as a condition of use, replace an equal or greater resource than that used"

Inherent in this law are the concepts of replanting, recycling, durability, and the correct or beneficial disposal of wastes. Nature has extreme penalties for those who break such laws, and for their descendants and neighbors. The law operates at every scale — from the molecular to the planetary.

### Practical Applications of the Law of Return

- **Replanting**: Every tree harvested must be replaced, ideally with a greater number of trees
- **Recycling**: All materials must be cycled back into productive use
- **Durability**: Products must be designed to last, reducing the rate at which replacement resources are needed
- **Waste as resource**: Every waste product must become an input to another process — animal manure becomes fertilizer, kitchen scraps become compost, greywater becomes irrigation

### The Tree Tax Example

Mollison applied this principle directly: his book carried a tree tax so that readers could continue using books (which consume trees in production). The principle extends to all consumption: never buy books or newspapers that do not tax (fund replanting), nor goods where the manufacturer does not recycle or replant the materials of manufacture.

## The Laws of Thermodynamics

### First Law: Conservation of Energy

As stated by Watt: "All energy entering an organism, population or ecosystem can be accounted for as energy which is stored or leaves. Energy can be transferred from one form to another, but it cannot disappear, or be destroyed, or created. No energy conversion system is ever completely efficient."

As stated by Asimov: "The total energy of the universe is constant and the total entropy is increasing."

### Second Law: Entropy

Energy conversions are never 100% efficient. Some energy is always lost as heat, noise, or other forms that are not useful to the system. This dissipated energy is called entropy. Entropy is the waters of a mountain stream that have reached the sea; the heat, noise, and exhaust smoke of an automobile; the energy of food used to keep an animal warm and alive.

### Design Implications

The designer's task is to use energy as efficiently as possible before it degrades to entropy. This means:

- **Cascade energy use**: Each energy flow should perform multiple functions before reaching its sink
- **Store energy**: Capture energy when available for use when needed (water tanks, thermal mass, food preservation)
- **Minimize waste**: Every output should become an input to another process
- **Use biological systems**: Living systems capture and store energy far more efficiently than mechanical systems

## Self-Regulation and the Role of Authority

A critical principle derived from observing natural systems is that self-regulation is more effective than external control. Natural ecosystems maintain balance not through top-down authority but through millions of feedback relationships between organisms and their environment.

When self-regulating function is removed, systems degrade. [[water-management]] to landscape absorption and storage rather than relying on irrigation infrastructure
- **Community**: Return decision-making to local participants rather than relying on centralized governance

## Principles vs. Dogmas

Principles differ from dogmas in that there are no penalties for error, only learning from error, which leads to new evolution. Dogmas are rules intended to force centralized control, often through guilt, and every such rule or law represents a failure of the social system.

It is too late to fail, but never too late to adopt sensible principles for guidance and to throw away the rule book. In design, we must accept that immutable rules will not apply and instead be prepared to be guided by flexible principles and directives, taking each case as unique but gaining confidence from findings and solutions elsewhere.

## Principles from Observation of Natural Systems

Several key principles emerge from the study of natural systems:

### Cooperative Associations

Cooperative species and associations of self-supporting species make healthy communities. [[permaculture-design]], this accounting includes:

- **Energy inputs**: Sunlight, water, wind, biological nitrogen fixation, human labor
- **Energy storages**: Biomass (trees, soil organic matter), water in tanks and dams, food stored, seed saved
- **Energy outputs**: Harvested products, exports from the system, entropy losses
- **Energy cycling**: How many times energy is used within the system before leaving

A well-designed permaculture system maximizes internal energy cycling while minimizing external inputs and entropy losses. The ideal is a system that produces more energy than it consumes — a net energy gain rather than the net energy loss characteristic of conventional agriculture.
