# Grain Spawn Cultivation for Mushrooms

Growing fungal [[mycelium]] on sterilized grain — commonly called "[[grain-spawn]]" — is the critical bridge between laboratory-scale agar culture and production-scale [[mushroom-cultivation]]. The technique was originally developed by San Antonio (1971) for producing the common button mushroom (*[[agaricus-bisporus]]*) from cased grain spawn under laboratory conditions, and was adapted by Oss & Oeric (1976) for indoor cultivation of *[[stropharia-cubensis]]*. Today, grain spawn remains the standard method used by both commercial and hobbyist cultivators worldwide.

## Why Grain?

Grain provides an ideal substrate for mycelial expansion for several reasons:

- **Nutrient density:** Grains are rich in complex carbohydrates (starch), proteins, minerals, and vitamins that support rapid mycelial growth
- **Physical structure:** Individual grains provide enormous surface area — millions of discrete inoculation points once the mycelium has fully colonized the mass
- **Uniformity:** Grain of a consistent size packs predictably in jars or bags, allowing reproducible results
- **Shakeability:** Grain jars can be shaken to redistribute mycelium, dramatically accelerating colonization

The goal at this stage is to produce a mass of 50–100 grams of thoroughly colonized grain per jar, which then serves as the inoculum for the fruiting stage.

## Suitable Grains

While many grains support fungal growth, the most commonly used options include:

| Grain | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Rye** | Preferred by Oss & Oeric; excellent nutrient profile, inexpensive, consistent results |
| **Wheat** | Good alternative; widely available |
| **Barley** | Suitable but slightly less consistent than rye |
| **Oats** | Works but can be sticky |
| **Brown rice** | Effective but more expensive |
| **Sorghum** | Good in warm climates |
| **Millet** | Small grain size allows many inoculation points; popular in modern practice |
| **Triticale** | Wheat-rye hybrid; performs well |
| **Buckwheat** | Usable but not optimal |

**Critical warning:** Grain marketed as animal feed is often treated with fungicides. Always use grain packaged for human consumption.

## Rye Grain Spawn Recipe (per quart jar)

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Whole rye grains | 112 g |
| Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) | 2.0 g |
| Potassium phosphate (K₂HPO₄) | 0.2 g (optional) |
| Tap or distilled water | 180 ml |

### Ingredient Notes

- **Calcium carbonate:** Does not need to be high-purity laboratory grade. Powdered oyster shell, powdered limestone, or powdered chalk are all suitable. Oyster shell powder is sold as a feed supplement by many agricultural suppliers.
- **Potassium phosphate:** Optional; can be omitted if unavailable. It serves as a pH buffer and phosphorus source.

## Jar Preparation

1. Use clean, wide-mouth quart mason jars with dome and ring lids
2. Add dry ingredients to each jar
3. Add water
4. Screw lids on **loosely**, with the rubber seal of the inner lids inverted so jars will not seal during sterilization
5. The inverted seal allows pressure equalization during sterilization while still providing coverage

## Sterilization

### Pressure Cooker Setup

1. Barely cover the bottom of the pressure cooker with tap water
2. Place jars in the cooker with lids loose; jars can be stacked in two tiers if the cooker is large enough
3. Seal the cooker lid but **leave the stopcock open**
4. Heat on high until vigorous steam vents from the stopcock
5. Close the stopcock and bring pressure to 15–20 psi
6. Reduce heat to maintain pressure without exceeding it
7. Sterilize at pressure for **1 hour** (grain requires longer sterilization than agar due to its density)
8. Remove from heat and **allow pressure to return to zero** before opening the stopcock
9. Open the stopcock, remove the lid, and carefully remove the jars

### Post-Sterilization

1. Inspect each jar for cracks; discard any flawed jars immediately
2. Firmly tighten the lid on each jar
3. Shake jars vigorously — the rye will have absorbed water and swelled to several times its original volume
4. Inscribe the date of inoculation or a code number on each jar for tracking
5. Leave lids tightened until jars have cooled completely (at least 2 hours, or to room temperature)

## Inoculation

### Scalpel Method

1. Use a disposable pre-sterilized #11 scalpel (available from medical supply houses)
2. Flame the blade over an alcohol lamp before each transfer
3. Insert the scalpel into an agar culture grown in a petri dish or baby food jar
4. Cut a grid into the mycelial surface, creating small squares of approximately 1–1.5 cm²
5. A single 4-inch petri dish yields 9–20 such squares
6. For each transfer, re-sterilize the scalpel, spear a mycelium-covered agar square, and rapidly transfer it to a mason jar
7. Lift the jar lid just enough to insert the inoculum
8. Firmly tighten the lid and shake the jar vigorously to distribute inoculation points
9. **Immediately re-loosen the lid** — proper aeration is essential for fungal growth

### Syringe Method (Liquid Inoculation)

An alternative method that may improve sterility:

1. Sterilize water in a flask sealed with aluminum foil (in the pressure cooker alongside the grain jars)
2. Using a sterile disposable syringe, take up approximately 10 ml of sterile water
3. Inject onto the surface of an agar culture (slant tubes are ideal for this)
4. Swirl vigorously until mycelial fragments are visible suspended in the water
5. Take up the mycelial suspension in the syringe
6. Inject 1–2 ml into each grain jar through the loosened lid
7. Flame the needle between each jar

This method is standard in professional mycological work and reduces the time each jar is open, potentially lowering contamination rates.

## Incubation

### Environmental Requirements

- **Temperature:** 70–80°F (21–27°C), maintained as constant as possible
- **Humidity:** 95% relative humidity (maintained naturally by the closed jars)
- **Light:** Not required at this stage; in fact, darkness is preferable

### Incubation Setup

A styrofoam box with a clear plastic window in the lid (available from pet stores and tropical fish dealers) provides an excellent incubation chamber. The mycelium generates metabolic heat as it grows, and the styrofoam insulation helps maintain a stable temperature.

### Colonization Timeline

| Day | Expected Development |
|---|---|
| 1–3 | Mycelium grows off the agar inoculum and onto the rye |
| 4 | Mycelial mat approximately the size of a fifty-cent piece; **first shake** |
| 5–8 | Mycelium spreading from multiple points after shaking |
| 6–8 | **Second shake** if rye is not yet fully permeated |
| 8–14 | Complete permeation — jar should be fully white |

**Variability:** Colonization time can range from under 6 days to 2–3 weeks depending on environmental conditions. Factors that influence speed include:

- **Temperature:** Warmer, more humid coastal climates (e.g., California) promote faster growth
- **Altitude:** High-altitude, low-humidity environments (e.g., Colorado) may slow growth significantly
- **Strain vigor:** More vigorous dikaryotic strains colonize faster
- **Media history:** Mycelium grown on enriched MEA with yeast extract tends to be more vigorous than PDA-grown cultures
- **Aeration:** Properly loose lids are critical — the mycelium is aerobic and requires oxygen

### The First Shake

The first shake is critical timing. When mycelial growth has formed a mat roughly the size of a fifty-cent piece (around day 4), firmly tighten the lid and shake vigorously. This breaks up the mycelial mat and redistributes it throughout the grain mass, creating dozens of new growth points that accelerate colonization.

After shaking, check for contaminated jars (they become more obvious after shaking) and set them aside. Re-loosen lids immediately after shaking.

### Complete Permeation

A fully colonized jar shows **snow-white mycelium** throughout, occasionally with a light blue tinge. Any other color — green, yellow, black, grey, pink — indicates contamination and the jar should be discarded.
