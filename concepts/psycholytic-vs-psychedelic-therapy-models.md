## Psycholytic vs. Psychedelic Therapy Models

## Overview

[[hallucinogenic-drugs]] in [[hallucinogenic-drugs-in-psychotherapy-and-shamanism]] distinguishes between two primary therapeutic models: the psycholytic and the psychedelic.

These models represent fundamentally different approaches to the use of hallucinogenic compounds in clinical settings, differing in dosage, frequency, therapeutic goals, and theoretical framework.

The psycholytic model, developed primarily in Europe, uses low to moderate doses of LSD administered repeatedly over many sessions to facilitate psychoanalytic exploration.

The psychedelic model, developed primarily in North America, uses high doses administered in fewer sessions to produce a transformative mystical or peak experience.

Both models emerged in the 1950s and 1960s and produced significant clinical results before being shut down by the political reaction against psychedelics in the late 1960s and early 1970s.

Understanding the distinction between these models is essential for contemporary researchers seeking to revive psychedelic therapy, as each model has different strengths, limitations, and appropriate applications.

## The Psycholytic Model

The psycholytic model was developed in the early 1950s by European psychiatrists, most notably Ronald Sandison in England and Hanscarl Leuner in Germany.

The term "psycholytic" literally means "mind-loosening" and refers to the use of low to moderate doses of hallucinogens to reduce psychological defenses and facilitate psychoanalytic exploration.

In this model, LSD is typically administered in doses ranging from 25 to 200 micrograms, repeated at weekly or biweekly intervals over a course of ten to thirty sessions.

The low dose is intended to produce a state of heightened emotional responsiveness and reduced defensiveness without overwhelming the patient's ego.

The therapeutic process resembles conventional psychoanalysis: the patient explores memories, emotions, and fantasies with the guidance of a therapist, but the hallucinogen facilitates access to material that would otherwise be repressed.

The psycholytic approach is grounded in the psychoanalytic tradition and views the therapeutic value of hallucinogens primarily in terms of their ability to loosen defenses and accelerate the psychoanalytic process.

## The Psychedelic Model

The psychedelic model was developed in the late 1950s and early 1960s by North American researchers, most notably [[stanislav-grof]] and [[mystical-experience]] itself, which is believed to produce lasting personality change.

The psychedelic approach draws on the tradition of [[powell-psilocybin-mystical-experience]] and [[metzner-william-james-radical-empiricism-multiple-consciousness]]'s concept of the "twice-born" self, viewing the therapeutic value of hallucinogens in terms of their capacity to produce spiritual transformation.

## Dosage and Frequency Differences

The distinction between the two models is most clearly expressed in their differing approaches to dosage and frequency of administration.

The psycholytic model favors low doses administered frequently, producing a gradual deepening of the therapeutic process over many sessions.

The psychedelic model favors a single high dose, producing a dramatic and potentially life-altering experience in a compressed timeframe.

The dosage difference reflects a fundamental disagreement about the mechanism of therapeutic action: whether it is gradual psychological insight or sudden mystical transformation.

In practice, some clinicians combined elements of both models, using low doses for initial exploration and high doses for subsequent breakthrough experiences.

The optimal approach likely depends on the individual patient, the nature of the condition being treated, and the skill and orientation of the therapist.

## Therapeutic Goals

The psycholytic and psychedelic models have different but overlapping therapeutic goals.

The psycholytic model aims primarily at the resolution of neurotic symptoms, the working through of repressed material, and the integration of split-off aspects of the personality.

It is particularly suited to the treatment of [[cannabis-for-anxiety-disorders]], personality disorders, and psychosomatic conditions, where the loosening of defenses and access to unconscious material are therapeutically valuable.

The psychedelic model aims at a more radical transformation: the dissolution of the ego, the transcendence of ordinary self-concern, and the establishment of a new, more expansive identity.

It is particularly suited to the treatment of alcoholism, terminal illness anxiety, and existential crisis, where a profound shift in perspective can produce rapid and lasting improvement.

Both models have been applied to a wide range of conditions, and the choice between them depends more on the clinician's theoretical orientation than on any clear evidence of superiority for specific conditions.

## Set and Setting in Each Model

The concept of "[[set-and-setting]]"—the psychological state of the individual and the physical and [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-polydrug-use-social-context]] of the experience—is central to both models but is operationalized differently.

In the psycholytic model, set and setting are managed to create a safe, controlled environment in which the patient can explore difficult material without becoming overwhelmed.

The therapist maintains an active, interpretive role, guiding the patient's exploration and helping to contain and process the emotions that emerge.

In the psychedelic model, set and setting are managed to maximize the likelihood of a mystical or transcendental experience, often incorporating music, art, and natural settings.

The therapist takes a more passive, supportive role, trusting the experience to unfold according to its own logic and intervening only to provide reassurance or guidance.
