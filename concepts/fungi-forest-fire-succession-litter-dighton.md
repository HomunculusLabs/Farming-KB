# Fungi in Forest Ecosystems — Fire, Succession, and Litter Dynamics (Dighton)

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: John Dighton, *Fungi in Ecosystem Processes*, Chapters 2-4.

## Fire and Mycorrhizal Community Dynamics

Fire is a natural disturbance in many forest ecosystems that maintains both plant and fungal diversity. The effects of fire on fungal communities depend on fire severity, soil damage, and the speed of forest re-establishment.

### Fire Effects on Ectomycorrhizal Communities
Visser (1995) and Jonsson et al. (1999a) documented changes in ectomycorrhizal species composition resulting from forest fire. These changes suggest a succession of mycorrhizal fungi during re-establishment of mature forest (Frankland, 1992, 1998; Boerner et al., 1996).

### Inoculum Continuity
Where fire damage to soil-surface organic matter is minimal, residual ectomycorrhizal communities survive on the dying roots of former forest trees. If forest re-establishment is rapid, these dying roots act as a source of mycorrhizal inoculum, maintaining species diversity similar to the pre-fire forest (Baar et al., 1999; Jonsson et al., 1999b). This continuity allows the forest to maintain stability across fire-disturbance cycles.

### Fire-Altered Soil Conditions
Nutrient conditions after fire are influenced by:
- Degree of nutrient mineralization from the fire
- Loss of organic matter
- Changes in physical characteristics of soil (increased heating from solar radiation absorption by darkened soil surface)

These altered conditions affect relative survival of mycorrhizal species and their physiological function, potentially shifting the community composition even when inoculum survives.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae and Fire
In dry sclerophyllous shrub communities in Australia, Torpy et al. (1999) showed that fire effects on arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization of roots were more closely related to the density of host plants than to direct fire effects on the mycorrhizae themselves.

## Forest Succession and Fungal Community Change

### Litter Quality and Decomposition
The quality of litter resources changes predictably during forest succession:
- **Early succession** — fast-decomposing litter from herbaceous plants and pioneer tree species (low lignin, high nitrogen)
- **Mid succession** — mixed litter quality as forest composition diversifies
- **Late succession** — slow-decomposing litter from dominant canopy species (high lignin, low nitrogen)

These changes in resource quality drive corresponding shifts in the decomposer fungal community.

### Leaf Litter Harvesting Effects
Repeated harvesting of forest floor leaf litter in Swedish spruce forests reduced the abundance of ectomycorrhizae on roots but not the number of species (Mahmood et al., 1999). This suggests that mycorrhizal diversity is resilient to moderate disturbance, though function may be reduced.

### Leaf Litter Manipulation Experiments
Baar and de Vries (1995) showed on Scots pine forest floors in The Netherlands that:
- Complete litter removal increased ECM fungal species diversity
- Doubling litter reduced diversity below control levels
- Intermediate litter levels supported the richest communities

This suggests a humpbacked diversity-productivity relationship for ECM fungi with respect to litter resources.

## Leaf Litter Patch Dynamics

### New Jersey Pine Barrens Study
Dighton et al. (2000) demonstrated that in mixed forest ecosystems, localized leaf litter patches create distinct microenvironments that select for different ectomycorrhizal communities:

**Patch formation**: Large litter patches accumulated behind ericaceous understory vegetation (huckleberry and blueberry), which acted as leaf litter dams.

**Chemical differentiation**: Large litter patches had different leaf species composition (higher proportion of oak leaves) than small patches, altering both resource quality and soil chemistry through differential leachates.

**Community response**: Different ECM fungal communities developed on pine and oak roots invading patches of different sizes and compositions.

### Oak-Pine Litter Mixing
Conn and Dighton (2000) showed that pitch pine seedling roots favored mixed oak-pine litter communities over either species alone. During decomposition:
- Oak leaves immobilized phosphorus
- Pine needles immobilized nitrogen

The ECM communities developing on roots in oak-containing litters had higher proportions of phosphatase-producing mycorrhizae, an adaptation to phosphorus limitation during initial oak decomposition stages.

### Allelopathic Effects of Leaf Litter
Baar et al. (1994) showed that extracts of pine leaf litter:
- Reduced growth of *Laccaria proxima* and *Rhizopogon luteolus*
- Only affected *Paxillus involutus* and *Xerocomus badius* at high concentrations

Extracts of the grass *Deschampsia flexuosa*:
- Inhibited growth of *L. proxima*, *P. involutus*, and *R. luteolus*
- Enhanced growth of *Laccaria bicolor*

Koide et al. (1998) showed differential effects of polyphenols (catechin, epicatechin gallate) and volatile compounds (alpha- and beta-pinene) on ECM fungi, with pine phenolics stimulating *Suillus intermedius* but reducing *Amanita rubescens*.

## Rhododendron Effects on Forest Regeneration

Walker et al. (1999) documented that *Rhododendron maximum* (an ericaceous shrub) severely reduces hardwood and coniferous seedling regeneration in the southern Appalachians:
- Hemlock seedlings in rhododendron thickets had 19% ECM colonization vs 62% outside thickets
- *Cenococcum geophilum* colonization was higher inside thickets (10.4%) than outside (4.6%) in year one, but this difference disappeared after year two
- Seedling shoot biomass was reduced 50% in the second year within rhododendron thickets

## Defoliation and Herbivory Effects

### Canopy Defoliation
Cullings et al. (2001) showed that 50% defoliation of lodgepole pine in a mixed pine-spruce forest in Yellowstone National Park:
- Altered ECM species composition on both defoliated pine and untreated spruce
- Reduced the ecosystem-dominant *Inocybe* species in defoliated plots
- Maintained similar total colonization and species richness

This demonstrates that changing the photosynthetic capacity of one species can affect mycorrhizal associations of neighboring trees of different species.

### Elk Browsing of Willow
Peinetti et al. (2001) showed winter browsing of willow by elk:
- Induces higher shoot biomass production but similar leaf biomass
- Produces fewer but larger shoots and leaves
- Inhibits flowering
- Likely increases soil nitrogen uptake while reducing belowground carbon allocation

These changes would alter the demands placed on mycorrhizal communities — reduced carbohydrate supply from the host but increased nitrogen demand.

## Significance for Forest Management

- Prescribed fire management must consider effects on mycorrhizal inoculum reservoirs
- Litter management (removal or retention) directly influences ECM community diversity
- Mixed-species forests create more heterogeneous mycorrhizal communities than monocultures
- Invasive understory species like rhododendron can impede forest regeneration through mycorrhizal suppression
- Selective herbivory management affects both aboveground production and belowground fungal partnerships
