# Vegetable Garden Spacing Systems

Steve Solomon identifies four fundamental spacing systems for vegetable
gardening, each representing a different philosophy about the relationship
between plants, soil, water, and labor. The choice of spacing system is one
of the most consequential decisions a gardener makes — it determines water
requirements, soil fertility demands, pest pressure, labor input, and
ultimately the [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] and total caloric yield of the garden.
Solomon's key insight, gained through decades of variety trialing at
Territorial Seed Company, is that wider spacing often produces more food
per unit of area, not less — a direct contradiction of [[coleman-british-adoption-of-french-intensive-gardening]]
dogma.

## The Four Spacing Systems

### 1. Intensive / Raised Bed Spacing
Popularized by John Jeavons (Grow Biointensive) and Mel Bartholomew (Square
Foot Gardening), intensive spacing places vegetables close together in deeply
dug, heavily amended raised beds. Plants are spaced so their leaves barely
overlap at maturity, supposedly shading the soil to conserve moisture and
suppress weeds.

**Advantages:** Maximizes output from small spaces; efficient for urban
gardens with limited land; visually appealing raised bed aesthetics.

**Disadvantages (Solomon's critique):** Requires enormous inputs of water,
compost, and fertilizer. Plants compete intensely for water and nutrients,
stressing them and making them more susceptible to [[mushroom-pests-and-diseases]]. The
labor required to maintain intensive beds is disproportionately high relative
to the slightly increased yield. Intensive beds produce smaller individual
vegetables that must be harvested promptly or they lose quality rapidly.

Solomon practiced intensive gardening for years and ran a seed company that
promoted it, but his variety trials — where plants must be spaced far enough
apart to develop fully — revealed that well-spaced plants produced larger,
better-tasting vegetables with less water and less fertilizer per unit of
food produced.

### 2. Traditional Row Spacing
The historical standard: plants in straight rows far enough apart to walk
between, with enough space between plants within the row for full
development. This is the system used by commercial farmers and was the
universal home garden method before the intensive gardening movement.

**Advantages:** Easy to cultivate with hoe or rototiller between rows;
good air circulation reducing disease; each plant has room to develop fully;
lower water requirements than intensive systems; simpler [[coleman-compost-and-organic-fertility-management]].

**Disadvantages:** Uses more total land area; paths between rows consume
space not used for production; can look "wasteful" to gardeners accustomed
to intensive beds.

### 3. Reduced Irrigation Spacing
A middle ground where plants are spaced wider than intensive but closer
than traditional row spacing, with modest irrigation. This system
acknowledges that some supplemental water may be needed but seeks to
minimize it through thoughtful spacing.

### 4. Dry Gardening (Zero Irrigation) Spacing
The most widely spaced system, developed for regions with dry summers or
for gardeners who lack reliable irrigation. Plants are placed far enough
apart that each can survive on natural rainfall alone, accessing soil
moisture from a large root zone that doesn't overlap with neighbors.

Solomon mastered this system after selling Territorial Seed in 1986 and
dedicated years to researching pre-irrigation [[container-gardening-techniques]]. He
documented his findings in *Waterwise Vegetables* (now out of print). The
key principle is that when plants don't compete for water, their roots
explore a much larger soil volume, accessing nutrients and moisture that
crowded plants never reach.

**Advantages:** No irrigation infrastructure needed; extremely
drought-resilient; minimal water cost; plants develop deep, extensive root
systems; lower pest and disease pressure due to reduced plant stress.

**Disadvantages:** Requires significantly more land; not suitable for very
small gardens; yields per square foot are lower (but yield per plant is
often higher); requires careful variety selection for [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]].

## Solomon's Recommended Approach

Solomon advocates what he calls "practical spacing" — wider than intensive
but adapted to the gardener's specific situation. His guidelines:

- **With reliable irrigation:** Space plants at least as far apart as seed
  packets recommend, and often farther. The "thinning to final spacing"
  instructions on seed packets are usually minimum distances, not optimal.
- **With limited irrigation:** Increase spacing by 50% over standard
  recommendations. Use mulch to conserve soil moisture.
- **Without irrigation:** Double or triple standard spacing. Choose
  drought-adapted varieties. Time planting to coincide with seasonal
  rainfall patterns.

## The Yield Paradox

Solomon's variety trials consistently showed that properly spaced plants
produced more total food per unit of area than crowded plants, once the
entire system was accounted for. A crowded tomato plant might produce 15
pounds of small, watery fruit requiring daily watering and weekly
fertilizer, while two well-spaced plants in the same area would produce
25 pounds of large, flavorful fruit with half the water and no
supplemental fertilizer. The key is that widely spaced plants access soil
resources from a much larger volume, effectively mining nutrients and
moisture that intensive plants never reach.

## Land Requirements

Solomon estimates that 2,000-4,500 square feet (185-420 square meters)
of actual growing area can supply most of a family's vegetable needs,
depending on climate, soil quality, and whether irrigation is available.
The British wartime allotment standard was 2,700 square feet. In mild
climates without freezing winters, double the garden area and rotate half
into grass ley every 4-5 years to break disease cycles and rebuild soil.
