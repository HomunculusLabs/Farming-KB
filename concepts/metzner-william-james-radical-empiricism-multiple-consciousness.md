## James's Nitrous Oxide Research

William James (1842–1910), the father of American psychology, conducted
personal experiments with nitrous oxide (laughing gas) in the 1870s and 1880s.
These experiences led him to conclusions that anticipated many of the findings
of twentieth-century psychedelic research. James found that the gas produced
states of consciousness in which the boundaries of the self dissolved, in
which metaphysical insights appeared with overwhelming certainty, and in which
the relationship between mind and matter appeared fundamentally different from
the assumptions of ordinary consciousness.

James published his observations in *The Varieties of Religious Experience*
(1902), where he argued that mystical states, whether produced by drugs,
meditation, or spontaneous religious experience, share four characteristics:
ineffability (they cannot be adequately described in words), noetic quality
(they convey a sense of insight or revelation), transiency (they are brief),
and passivity (the subject feels grasped by a superior power).

## The Plurality of Consciousness Forms

The passage Metzner quoted from James is among the most frequently cited in
consciousness studies: "Our normal waking consciousness, rational consciousness
as we call it, is but one special type of consciousness, whilst all about it,
parted from it by the filmiest of screens, there lie potential forms of
consciousness entirely different."

James's radical empiricism held that all forms of conscious experience deserve
equal epistemic respect—that no form of consciousness should be privileged a
priori as more "real" or "valid" than another. This was a radical departure
from both the materialist assumption that ordinary waking consciousness is the
only legitimate mode of awareness and the religious assumption that only
certain approved forms of religious experience are valid.

## Connection to Shamanic Worldview

Metzner used James's framework to bridge the gap between Western psychotherapy
and indigenous shamanism. The shamanic worldview, which recognizes "multiple
worlds" accessible in expanded states of consciousness and affirms the reality
of "spirit beings" encountered in dreams and visions, has been considered
"completely beyond the pale of both reason and science."

## The Two Radical Challenges to Western Worldview

Metzner identified two elements in shamanic traditions that pose "the most
direct and radical challenge to the accepted Western worldview": (1) the
belief that there are multiple realities or "worlds" that can be explored in
expanded states of consciousness; and (2) the belief that "spirits," the
beings one encounters in dreams and visions, are just as real as the physical
organism.

James's radical empiricism provides a philosophical framework for taking both
of these claims seriously. If all forms of consciousness are epistemically
legitimate, then the worlds revealed in shamanic states are not illusions or
projections but genuine modes of access to aspects of reality that ordinary
waking consciousness cannot perceive. Similarly, if the beings encountered in
expanded states present themselves with consistency and independence, the
radical empiricist must at least entertain the possibility that they are real
in some sense.

## From Hybrid Rituals to Expanded Worldview

Metzner observed that participants in hybrid shamanic-therapeutic circle
rituals "seem to have expanded their belief systems beyond the boundaries of
the conventional materialistic paradigm of Western science and psychology."
While accepting the validity of Western psychological insights—including those
of Freud, Jung, and Reich—they have come "to accept the reality of nonmaterial
spirit beings and to recognize that we live in multiple worlds of consciousness."

This expansion of worldview is, in Metzner's account, a natural consequence
of sustained engagement with entheogenic plant medicines combined with
meditative or therapeutic insight processes. The plant medicines "function to
amplify awareness and sensitize perception, particularly amplifying somatic,
emotional and instinctual awareness." This amplified awareness, in turn,
makes the limitations of the materialist paradigm increasingly apparent.

## Contemporary Relevance

James's radical empiricism has experienced a revival in recent decades, driven
in part by the resurgence of psychedelic research and the growing acceptance
of contemplative practices in mainstream psychology. The concept of
"consciousness pluralism"—the idea that consciousness exists in multiple
legitimate forms—has become a serious topic in philosophy of mind, cognitive
science, and interdisciplinary consciousness studies.

Metzner's use of James to frame the convergence between Western psychotherapy
and indigenous shamanism anticipates this broader intellectual trend. The
question is no longer whether non-ordinary states of consciousness are "real"
in some meaningful sense, but how to integrate the insights derived from these
states into a comprehensive understanding of human nature and the nature of
reality.
