# Chrysanthemum Greenhouse Photoperiod Manipulation

The commercial chrysanthemum industry provides one of the most dramatic examples
of photoperiod manipulation in horticulture. By controlling the duration of
uninterrupted darkness, growers can delay flowering for months and then trigger
synchronized blooming across thousands of plants, enabling year-round production
of a species that naturally flowers only in autumn.

## The Natural Photoperiodic Behavior

Chrysanthemums are short-day plants that naturally flower in autumn as days
shorten. Their critical [[photoperiodism-plants-measure-night-length]] is typically 9-12 hours depending on the
cultivar, meaning they initiate flowering when continuous darkness exceeds this
threshold. Under natural conditions, this produces a reliable autumn bloom, but
it also means that chrysanthemums are not available as cut flowers during spring
and summer — precisely when demand is highest for occasions like Mother's Day.

## The Night-Interruption Technique

The solution developed by greenhouse growers exploits the discovery that plants
measure the length of continuous darkness. Throughout autumn and winter, when days
are naturally short, growers prevent flowering by turning on greenhouse lights for
a few minutes in the middle of each night. This brief light interruption breaks
the long dark period into two shorter periods, neither of which exceeds the
critical night length. The plants remain vegetative, continuing to produce leaves
and stems.

When the desired flowering date approaches, the night-interruption lighting is
stopped. Approximately two weeks after the last light interruption, all plants
begin flowering simultaneously. This synchronized bloom is ideal for commercial
harvest and shipping, ensuring a uniform product reaches market.

## The Mother's Day Production Cycle

The classic application targets Mother's Day, which falls in May in the United
States — a spring holiday that occurs months after chrysanthemums would naturally
flower. Growers begin the cycle in late autumn or early winter, starting plants
from cuttings under long-day conditions (natural short days supplemented with
night-interruption lighting). The plants grow vegetatively through winter and
[[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]], accumulating biomass. Approximately two weeks before Mother's Day,
the night lights are turned off. The plants perceive the now-uninterrupted long
nights of spring and initiate flowering, producing blooms ready for harvest and
shipment in time for the holiday.

## Technical Requirements

Successful photoperiod manipulation requires precise control over both the timing
and quality of light. The light interruption must occur during the middle of the
dark period for maximum effectiveness. Red light is most efficient, as it
specifically activates [[phytochrome]], the photoreceptor responsible for photoperiod
perception. Modern operations typically use LED fixtures emitting in the red
spectrum (approximately 660 nm) for [[aquaculture-design-principles-and-energy-efficiency]].

Light intensity during the interruption need not be high — just a few seconds of
sufficient red light is enough to activate phytochrome. Typical commercial
systems use 2-5 foot-candles of red light for 2-4 minutes per night. However, the interruption
must be reliably delivered every night throughout the [[cervantes-vegetative-phase]]. Any missed
night can allow the dark period to exceed the critical threshold and initiate
premature flowering, which would disrupt the synchronized production schedule.

## Energy and Economic Considerations

Night-interruption lighting represents a significant energy cost for greenhouse
operations, particularly during winter when heating and supplementary lighting
are already major expenses. However, the economic returns from producing a
high-value crop out of season typically justify the energy investment. Advances
in LED technology have substantially reduced the energy cost of photoperiod
manipulation compared to older incandescent or high-pressure sodium systems.

## Broader Relevance

The chrysanthemum example illustrates principles that apply across many
photoperiod-sensitive crops. Cannabis cultivators use the same fundamental
approach to control the vegetative-to-flowering transition. Strawberry growers
manipulate day length to control runner production versus flowering. Poinsettia
producers use night-interruption to delay the development of the characteristic
red bracts until the holiday season. In each case, understanding and controlling
photoperiod enables growers to produce crops on demand rather than being
constrained by natural seasonal cycles.

## Cultivar-Specific Responses

Not all chrysanthemum cultivars respond identically to photoperiod manipulation.
Early-flowering cultivars have shorter critical night lengths and may require more
aggressive night-interruption to remain vegetative. Late-flowering cultivars have
longer critical nights and are easier to hold in the vegetative state. Spray-type
chrysanthemums (multiple small flowers per stem) and standard-type (single large
flower per stem) may also differ in their photoperiodic sensitivity. Commercial
growers select cultivars based on their intended production schedule and the
specific photoperiodic characteristics of each variety.

## Blackout Curtains and Light Pollution

In addition to night-interruption lighting, some chrysanthemum production uses
blackout curtains to artificially shorten the day. During summer, when natural
days are long, blackout curtains are pulled over the plants in late afternoon to
create an early sunset, ensuring the critical night length is met. This approach
is used when producing autumn-flowering crops during summer, or when forcing
early-flowering varieties for specific market dates. Light pollution from street
lights or neighboring operations can inadvertently disrupt photoperiodic control,
making [[coleman-greenhouse-orientation-site-selection-wind-protection-coleman]] and light-exclusion measures important considerations for
greenhouse operations.

## Future Directions

Research continues to optimize photoperiod management through understanding of
the underlying molecular mechanisms. The identification of genes such as CONSTANS
and FLOWERING LOCUS T that mediate the photoperiodic [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] may
eventually allow genetic approaches to photoperiod manipulation, reducing or
eliminating the need for energy-intensive lighting systems. Until such approaches
are commercially viable, photoperiodic lighting remains an essential tool in the
chrysanthemum grower's toolkit.
