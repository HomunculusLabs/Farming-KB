## Overview

In *What a Plant Knows*, [[plant-photoreception-visible-light]] as a special or isolated phenomenon, Chamovitz places it squarely within the continuum of all electromagnetic radiation — from the longest radio waves to the shortest gamma rays. This framing is essential for appreciating what plants can sense that humans cannot, and it lays the groundwork for understanding plant vision as a genuinely perceptual system.

## Light as Electromagnetic Waves

Light is electromagnetic radiation that falls within the visible portion of the spectrum. Crucially, light shares fundamental physical properties with all other types of electromagnetic signals, including microwaves, radio waves, X-rays, and infrared radiation. The only difference between these forms of radiation is the wavelength — the distance between successive peaks of the wave.

Chamovitz emphasises that the electromagnetic spectrum is a single continuous phenomenon. The boundaries we draw between "radio waves," "light," and "X-rays" are arbitrary human categories imposed on a seamless physical reality. This continuity matters because it helps explain why both humans and plants have evolved to detect different slices of the same spectrum. A radio wave and a beam of blue light are made of the same stuff; they simply oscillate at vastly different scales.

## Scale Across the Spectrum

Chamovitz illustrates the enormous range of electromagnetic wavelengths with vivid examples that make the abstract scale tangible:

- **Radio waves** used for AM radio are extraordinarily long — "almost half a mile in length." This is why AM radio antennas are many storeys tall; they must be physically proportional to the waves they are designed to receive. A structure that is too short simply cannot interact efficiently with such long wavelengths.
- **X-ray waves** sit at the opposite extreme — "very, very short, one trillion times shorter than radio waves." This extreme shortness is precisely why X-rays can pass through solid bodies, revealing bones and internal structures that visible light cannot penetrate. Their tiny wavelength allows them to slip between atoms.

Visible light occupies a narrow band between these extremes, with wavelengths ranging from approximately **0.0000004 metres** (400 nanometres) at the short end to **0.0000007 metres** (700 nanometres) at the long end. The entire visible spectrum spans less than a factor of two in wavelength — a razor-thin slice of the full electromagnetic range.

## Wavelength and Colour

Within the visible spectrum, different wavelengths correspond to different colours that human eyes and brains interpret:

- **Blue light** has the shortest wavelength in the visible range, around 400–500 nanometres
- **Red light** has the longest wavelength in the visible range, around 620–700 nanometres
- **Green, yellow, and orange** fall between blue and red in order of increasing wavelength

The rainbow"s colour pattern is not arbitrary — it is always oriented in the same direction, from short waves (blue/violet) through to long waves (red). This ordering reflects the underlying physics of wavelength and is consistent every time light is dispersed into its component colours, whether by a prism, a raindrop, or any other refractive medium.

## Human Photoreception

Human eyes detect light through specialised proteins called **photoreceptors**. These molecules absorb electromagnetic energy from the visible spectrum and convert it into biochemical signals that the brain interprets as vision. Chamovitz draws a direct and illuminating analogy: photoreceptors in the eye function essentially like radio antennas absorbing radio waves. Both are biological or physical structures tuned to receive specific wavelengths from the electromagnetic spectrum.

Human photoreception is limited to a narrow band of wavelengths — roughly 400 to 700 nanometres. We are blind to the vast majority of the electromagnetic spectrum that surrounds us at all times. Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays all wash over us continuously, invisible and unfelt except for occasional indirect effects like warmth or sunburn.

## Plant Photoreception: Beyond Human Vision

Plants detect a significantly broader range of the electromagnetic spectrum than humans do. Two critical regions that plants can perceive but humans cannot are:

### Ultraviolet (UV) Light

UV light has wavelengths shorter than those of visible violet light, typically below 400 nanometres. In humans, UV exposure causes sunburn and skin damage — a direct physical effect we feel but cannot see. We instinctively avoid intense sunlight, but we cannot perceive UV radiation itself.

Plants, however, possess photoreceptors (such as UVR8) that actively sense UV radiation. This ability allows them to detect and respond to UV intensity, triggering protective responses such as the production of UV-absorbing pigments like flavonoids and anthocyanins. These compounds act as a sort of molecular sunscreen, shielding delicate cellular machinery from UV-induced damage. Plants effectively "see" a colour that humans are completely blind to, and they use that information to protect themselves in ways humans must achieve through clothing or artificial sunscreen.

### Infrared (IR) Light

Infrared radiation has wavelengths longer than those of visible red light, extending from about 700 nanometres to 1 millimetre. In humans, infrared is experienced as heat — it warms the skin but is invisible to the eye. We sense its effects indirectly, but we cannot see infrared radiation itself.

Plants detect infrared light (particularly far-red light around 730 nanometres) and use information from this part of the spectrum to sense their thermal environment and to guide growth and developmental decisions. The ratio of red to far-red light is especially important as a signal for [[phototropin-blue-light-receptor-plant-directional-sensing]] drives phototropism — the ability of a plant to grow toward a light source.
- **Light obstruction**: whether another plant has grown above them and is blocking their access to light. This awareness enables shade-avoidance responses, where plants redirect growth to reach unobstructed light.
- **Light duration**: how long lights have been on, which allows plants to track day length and coordinate seasonal behaviours such as flowering (photoperiodism).

Each of these capabilities relies on specialised photoreceptor proteins that absorb specific wavelengths and trigger distinct biochemical pathways. Together, they give plants a detailed and multi-dimensional picture of their light environment.

## The Photoreceptor Antenna Analogy

Chamovitz"s comparison between photoreceptors and radio antennas is a central pedagogical device that runs throughout the book. Just as a radio antenna is physically tuned to the wavelength of the signal it is designed to receive, photoreceptor proteins are molecularly tuned to absorb specific wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation. A blue-light photoreceptor absorbs short wavelengths; a red-light photoreceptor absorbs long wavelengths. Plants possess multiple types of photoreceptors — cryptochromes for blue/UV light, phototropins for directional blue-light sensing, phytochromes for red and far-red light, and others — each tuned to a different part of the spectrum.

This diversity of photoreceptors gives plants a multi-spectral view of their environment that far exceeds human visual capacity. While humans rely on just three types of cone photoreceptor (plus rod cells for low light), plants deploy an entire family of light-sensing proteins, each feeding information into different signalling cascades. The result is not "vision" in any anthropomorphic sense, but a genuinely perceptual system that extracts detailed, multi-dimensional information from the electromagnetic environment.

## From Detection to Knowledge

Chamovitz draws an important distinction between merely detecting light and actually *knowing* something about it. A thermometer detects infrared radiation, but it does not know what the radiation means. Plants, by contrast, integrate photoreceptor signals with internal circadian clocks, hormonal pathways, and developmental programs to generate adaptive responses. When a plant detects that the red-to-far-red ratio has shifted, it "knows" that a neighbour is growing overhead and alters its stem elongation accordingly. This integration of wavelength information with internal state is what transforms detection into genuine perception.

## Comparing Human and Plant Light Perception

| Feature | Humans | Plants |
|---|---|---|

| UV detection | No (skin damage only) | Yes (via UVR8) |

| Intensity sensing | Yes (pupil response) | Yes |
| Duration sensing | Limited | Yes (photoperiodism) |
| Shade detection | No | Yes (red:far-red ratio) |

## Implications

Understanding the electromagnetic spectrum in this way reframes plant biology entirely. Plants are not passive organisms responding only to visible light. They are active, multi-spectral sensors continuously monitoring UV, visible, and infrared radiation to make sophisticated decisions about growth, defence, and reproduction.

Chamovitz"s treatment of the spectrum underscores a broader theme of the book: the sensory world of plants is richer and more complex than human perception alone would suggest, and much of what plants "know" about their environment is mediated through their extraordinary ability to read the electromagnetic spectrum. Recognising this capacity challenges the common assumption that plants are unfeeling, unperceiving organisms and opens the door to a more nuanced appreciation of plant intelligence and sensory ecology.
