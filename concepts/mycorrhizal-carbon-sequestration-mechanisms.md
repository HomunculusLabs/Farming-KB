# Mycorrhizal Carbon Sequestration Mechanisms

## The Mycorrhizal-Carbon Nexus

The mycorrhizal symbiosis, characterized by a reciprocal exchange of photosynthetically
fixed plant carbon in return for nitrogen or phosphorus, represents one of the most
significant but historically overlooked components of the terrestrial carbon cycle.
Over 80% of plant species invest substantial amounts of their below-ground carbon
into mycorrhizal fungal symbionts. At the global scale, the annual carbon flux
through soil respiration is ten times greater than fossil fuel combustion, recycling
approximately 10% of atmospheric CO2. Roots and their associated mycorrhizas
constitute the single most important component of this flux, and mycorrhizal
contributions are among the most sensitive to anthropogenic disturbance of both
carbon and nitrogen biogeochemical cycles.

## The Three Major Mycorrhizal Types

Central to understanding mycorrhizal contributions to the carbon cycle is
appreciation of the three dominant types and the ecosystems they define:

- **Ericoid mycorrhizas (ERM)** dominate in heathlands and northern tundra,
  where ericaceous plants (*Calluna*, *Vaccinium*, *Erica*, *Rhododendron*) are
  frequently dominant. These plants produce extremely recalcitrant litter
  enriched in polyphenolic compounds and low-molecular-mass phenolics.

- **Ectomycorrhizas (EM)** dominate in the boreal coniferous forest — the
  world's largest vegetation system — stretching as a continuous circumpolar
  belt 1000–2000 km wide around the Northern Hemisphere, and in temperate
  deciduous forests. Over 90% of root tips in these forests are normally
  symbiotic at any time, so virtually all labile carbon released from living
  roots passes through the fungi.

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizas (AM)** dominate throughout temperate grasslands,
  savannas, subtropical regions, and tropical rainforests. AM plants typically
  produce litter of lower recalcitrance and higher nitrogen content that
  decomposes relatively quickly.

## Direct Carbon Inputs from Mycorrhizal Mycelium

Mycorrhizal mycelia are major components of soil microbial biomass, receiving a
quality and quantity of carbohydrate directly from plants that is unparalleled
among soil microbial populations. By adding new carbon to soil, mycorrhizal
mycelia are functionally distinct from saprotrophs, which only decrease and
alter existing detrital carbon stocks. Mycorrhizal carbon inputs are chemically
distinct from those of their host plants: chitin rather than cellulose cell walls,
and melanin rather than lignin. These chemical differences are reflected in
distinct δ13C signatures between mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi.

For ectomycorrhizal fungi, carbon inputs typically range from 10% to 30% of net
photosynthate in many forest ecosystems. A conservative estimate of the EM
contribution to soil microbial biomass in boreal forest is 32%, based on field
observations following large-scale girdling experiments. External EM production
has been estimated at 420–590 kg ha⁻¹ year⁻¹, with approximately 80% of total EM
biomass existing as extra-radical mycelium. EM mycelium accounts for over 70%
of total soil microbial biomass in some Swedish coniferous forests.

For arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in temperate grassland, external mycelium
receives more than 9% of net carbon fixation by grassland in summer, with most
of this carbon transferred within the first 3 days after photosynthesis. AM
mycelia make up approximately 50% of fungal length in soil under herbaceous
plants and account for 20%–30% of total soil microbial biomass in temperate
grassland.

## Carbon Persistence and Recalcitrance

The quality and persistence of mycorrhizal carbon inputs determine the extent
of sequestration. Several factors contribute to unusual persistence:

- **High C:N ratios**: EM mycelium has C:N ratios of 18.5–21.9, unusually high
  for soil microbial biomass, reflecting efficient internal nitrogen recycling.
  High C:N ratios slow decomposition by nitrogen-limited decomposer organisms.

- **Recalcitrant compounds**: Many EM fungi produce robust hydrophobic multicellular
  hyphal cords persistent for months, and heavily melanized hyphae that decay
  very slowly. *Cenococcum geophilum* produces abundant melanized hyphae and
  sclerotia under dry soil conditions that are directly implicated in soil
  carbon sequestration. Mycorrhizal colonization of *Pinus edulis* roots
  resulted in a 30% lower rate of carbon decomposition compared to non-
  mycorrhizal roots.

- **Glomalin**: This hydrophobic protein secreted by AM hyphae has a residence
  time of 6–42 years and contributes up to 15% of the total stable soil organic
  carbon pool in grasslands and 4%–5% in tropical rainforest soils. Glomalin
  also plays a major role in soil aggregation.

- **Chemical defence**: EM mycelia are better defended against fungal-feeding
  animals than many saprotrophs, owing to melanin, polyphenolic compounds, and
  specific toxins. Some EM fungi kill fungivorous microarthropods and predate
  them for nitrogen, reducing decomposition rates.

## Indirect Effects on Decomposition

The indirect effects of mycorrhizas on carbon sequestration may exceed their
direct inputs. ERM and EM fungi exert positive feedback mechanisms that suppress
saprotrophic decomposition: by competing for limiting labile nutrient sources,
by producing nutrient-poor partially decomposed residues with elevated C:N and
C:P ratios, and through direct antagonism toward wood-decay saprotrophs. EM
mycelia can territorially exclude wood-decay fungi, the organisms best adapted
to decompose lignocellulose. In heathland and boreal forest, the near-complete
monopoly of labile organic nutrients by ERM and EM suppresses species with AM
that are less well adapted to compete for these nutrients.

AM fungi contribute to carbon sequestration through soil stabilization. Their
hyphae and glomalin secretions stabilize macroaggregates (greater than 2 mm
diameter), and organic matter entrapped inside these aggregates is biologically
and chemically stabilized to a greater degree than bulk soil organic matter.

## Mycorrhizal Carbon Fluxes and Plant Communities

The magnitude of carbon flux through mycorrhizal networks has driven the
evolution of mycoheterotrophic plants — over 400 achlorophyllous plant species
in approximately 90 genera that depend exclusively on fungi for carbon by
exploiting mycorrhizal relationships. About 10% of all plant species depend
on exploiting fungal carbon, at least transiently. These include the enormous
orchid family (approximately 17,500 species), many ferns and clubmosses with
subterranean gametophytes, and fully mycoheterotrophic plants like *Monotropa*
and *Corallorhiza* that tap into EM networks connecting trees.

Carbon costs and benefits of mycorrhizal associations are fungus-specific and
unequally shared among plants. Grasses with extensive fibrous root systems
contribute disproportionately to the carbon cost of supporting mycorrhizal
networks that most benefit coarser-rooted forbs. This asymmetry influences
plant community composition and has far-reaching implications for theories
of plant competition and ecosystem functioning.

## Biome-Scale Patterns

The most important biomes for soil carbon sequestration — heathland and boreal
forest — together cover approximately 70% of the vegetated land mass of the
Northern Hemisphere and contain the majority of global soil carbon stocks,
mainly in peat and raw humus. Variations in dominant mycorrhizal types are
important drivers of ecosystem carbon cycling and storage patterns. These
mycorrhizal-mediated processes demand radical re-appraisal of ecosystem
carbon flux models and conventional concepts of plant competition.
