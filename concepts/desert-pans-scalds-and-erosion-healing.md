# Desert Pans, Scalds, and Erosion Healing

The flat or depressed areas of desert landscapes — pans, scalds, claypans, and erosion gullies — present some of the most challenging rehabilitation problems in arid zone permaculture. These features result from complex interactions of geology, hydrology, and often human-induced degradation through overgrazing. Understanding their formation and applying targeted healing strategies is essential for restoring productive function to degraded desert lands.

## Desert Pans and Basins

### Classification

Flat areas and near-circular depressions in deserts are classified by their origin and formation process:

- **Tectonic basins**: Large-scale depressions formed by geological processes. These are the settings where salt lakes and large dune seas (draas) form.
- **Deflection (wind) hollows**: Fairly large depressions formed by wind action over long periods.
- **Pans**: Depressions with flat floors that gather run-off. Two main types exist:
  - **Claypans**: Clay-based evaporation areas where suspended silts carried by water settle out when water evaporates.
  - **Salt pans**: Salt-based depressions where evaporated water leaves mineral deposits.

### Claypans

Claypans rarely overflow. They receive silty waters from clay soils, and after pooling for a period, clay settles out and water evaporates. Claypans exhibit a dynamic wet-dry cycle:

- **Wet phase**: Tall canegrass (*Eragrostis australasica*) can grow, ducks and waterfowl nest, and the swamp areas serve as wildlife habitat.
- **Drying phase**: As swamps dry out, marsupials and rodents take refuge in the remaining moisture.
- **Dry phase**: Traditionally, Aboriginal peoples would burn the dried canegrass in autumn, knowing it would regenerate in wet periods.

### Clay Treatment

Claypans can be modified through soil amendments:

- **Gypsum**: Decreases clay capacity to infiltrate water (useful where run-off collection is desired).
- **Sodium carbonate or bentonite**: Seals clays where they are to be used to store water.
- **Natural montmorillonite or smectite clays**: Swell and form impermeable surfaces after a short period of rain, impeding infiltration.

### Gilgais

Small depressions called gilgais form from the swelling and shrinking of plastic clays. These are circular in outline and serve as useful water-capture systems where erosion has left them as hollows. Sand-filled, they cease to function — so their maintenance depends on preventing sand accumulation.

### Desert Soil Profiles

Soils in older deserts usually show yellow or reddish colour from iron oxide complexes. Composition varies widely:

- **Free sands**: Found in dune systems and eroded areas.
- **Heavy, compacted, or cracking clays**: Notoriously sticky when wet, preventing all vehicle movement. Most typical of flats and evaporation pans.
- **Clay-loams**: Softer soils typical of dune swales and foothills, more amenable to cultivation.

## Scalds

### Formation and Characteristics

A scald is a bare clay-pan surface where a duplex soil (originally light sandy loams over a sharply-defined transition to deep clay below) has lost its upper soil layer. The remaining clays are often solonetic — with a high content of sodium ions. When first wetted in rain, these clays "melt," effectively sealing the surface and preventing infiltration. The wet crust formed is almost impermeable — the same principle used deliberately to seal clays for leading run-off water to tanks.

Scalds are not hollows but have flow into and out of the area (distinguishing them from claypans). Most scalds are produced by overgrazing.

### Scald Revegetation Strategy

Revegetation of scalds uses a systematic bank-building approach:

1. Grade up a bank 10-15 cm high, with the cut on the lower side.
2. Water backs up a considerable distance behind this bank — aim for one-third of the total area to be treated.
3. Allow two-thirds of bare soil as run-off area feeding the treated section.
4. Pre-sow seed on the treated surface; germination occurs as water backs up.
5. The low bank eventually "melts" flat, and the next one-third can be treated successively.

Over 2-3 years, complete vegetative cover can be re-established. If banks are made too high or too solid, plants will drown. Alternative approaches include spiral earth-casting and ridges sown at their apex, which are also successfully used to seed claypans.

### Ecological Importance of Scald Healing

Revegetation of scalds has significance beyond the immediate area. In Australia, plague locusts lay their eggs and hatch most successfully in the narrow shaded edges of scalds. Since most scalds are produced by overgrazing, the desert grazier effectively inflicts plague on whole regions by allowing scald formation. Healing scalds therefore has landscape-scale pest management benefits.

## Gully Erosion Healing

### Small Gullies

Small gullies can be addressed with straightforward techniques:

- **Filling**: Physical filling with rock, soil, and organic material.
- **Fencing**: Exclusion of livestock to allow natural vegetation recovery.
- **Planting**: Direct revegetation of the gully floor and sides.

### Large Gullies

Large gullies require multiple coordinated strategies:

- **Diversion of headwaters**: Preventing further water concentration at the gully head.
- **Swales on side walls**: Holding water on gully sides to promote vegetation establishment and reduce lateral erosion.
- **Gabions on gully floor**: Wire cages filled with rock placed along the gully floor to slow water flow and trap sediment.
- **Fencing**: Complete livestock exclusion.
- **Tree planting**: Establishing deep-rooted vegetation to stabilise banks and floor.
- **Spreader banks**: Structures that spread headwaters gently downstream rather than allowing concentrated flow.

### Principles of Erosion Healing

The fundamental principle in all erosion healing is to slow water down, spread it out, and allow it to infiltrate. Fast, concentrated water is the primary agent of erosion in arid lands. Every structure and planting strategy aims to convert destructive kinetic energy into gentle infiltration and sediment deposition.

## Broader Context

These rehabilitation techniques are part of a larger permaculture strategy for desert lands that recognises the interconnected nature of desert degradation. Overgrazing causes scalds, which promote locust plagues, which further degrade vegetation. Vehicle damage destroys delicate biological soil crusts, accelerating wind erosion. The healing approach works systemically — addressing surface stability, water infiltration, vegetation establishment, and browsing pressure simultaneously to create self-reinforcing recovery processes.

The permaculture designer's role in these landscapes is not merely to plant trees or build structures, but to read the landscape processes, identify the points of intervention where small efforts produce cascading benefits, and design systems that work with natural recovery mechanisms rather than against them.
