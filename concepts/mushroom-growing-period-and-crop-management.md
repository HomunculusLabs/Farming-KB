## Watering During Production

### The Critical Importance of Adequate Water

Beds yielding heavily require considerable water. The now almost discarded custom of adding no water to beds so long as mushrooms are evident has caused extensive failures. The sole cause of inadequate returns in several extensive cultures was identified as a dearth of water at the right time.

### Watering Principles

- **Apply water of approximately bed temperature** to avoid thermal shock
- **Water frequently rather than heavily**: Apply sufficient water each time to slightly more than counterbalance evaporation
- **Keep casing soil moist**: If the casing is kept moist, there will be little danger of the bed drying out
- **Variable frequency**: A dry room may require sprinkling twice a day; a favorable cellar no oftener than once a week
- **Surface indications**: Generally satisfactory criteria if followed closely
- **Beware of overwatering in poorly ventilated conditions**: When ventilation is poor and evaporation practically nil, nothing is more disastrous than too much water

### Heating and Moisture Management

When hot water or steam heat are employed, controlling conditions is comparatively simple. With dry heat, moisture conditions will be irregular at best. Forcing stoves or heating plants in cold weather necessitates more water, and the changes from a moist to a dry atmosphere induce cracking of the upper surface of the mushrooms—much to their market injury.

## Covering Methods

### Newspaper Covers

When unfavorable conditions are temporary, the simplest cover method is to spread **newspapers over the bed**. This provides temporary protection from drying without permanent infrastructure.

### Framework Covers

When unfavorable conditions are likely to be permanent:

1. **Construct a framework** extending 6 to 12 inches above the bed
2. **Cover with cheesecloth or unbleached cotton** arranged to be readily removable for harvesting

### Critical Warning

Such coverings must never be employed where they retain so much moisture that the effect is to induce a **"stemmy" development** of the mushrooms at the expense of caps. The balance between moisture retention and adequate ventilation must be maintained.

### Open-Air Covers

In open-air culture, matting is often employed over the frames, performing the double duty of retaining moisture and preserving a more uniform temperature.

## Between Crops: Sanitation

### Immediate Cleanup

Directly after beds cease to be productive or remunerative, they should be removed and the house thoroughly cleaned and ultimately fumigated or sprayed. Beds coming into bearing in mid-winter usually cease production with the warm weather of May or early June; those bearing earlier may not last until warm weather.

### Danger of Reusing Old Materials

**None of the old compost, soil, or other materials** used in spent beds should be employed in any subsequent mushroom crop. Reuse would be a source of great danger, probably increasing many times the amount of disease and insect injury. The spent material is, however, excellent compost for flowers, lawns, and gardens.

### Cleaning Protocol

1. **Dump beds** and remove all old compost
2. **Clean all wooden supports and boards**, as well as walls and floors
3. **Remove boards** from the house or cellar and, when dry, whitewash with lime-wash made from good stone lime, or thoroughly spray with strong copper sulphate solution
4. **Spray cellar walls** with one of these washes, or fumigate with hydrocyanic acid or sulphur fumes

This thorough sanitation between crops is essential for preventing the buildup of pests, diseases, and competitive organisms that can devastate subsequent crops.
