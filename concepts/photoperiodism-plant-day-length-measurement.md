# Photoperiodism: How Plants Measure Day Length

## Overview

Photoperiodism is the physiological response of plants to the length of day and night,
and it is one of the most important timing mechanisms in the plant kingdom. Through
photoperiodism, plants can determine the time of year and coordinate critical life
events — especially flowering — with the appropriate season. The discovery of
photoperiodism in the early [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]] revealed that plants possess a far more
sophisticated ability to measure and respond to light than anyone had previously
imagined, and it opened the door to the discovery of [[phytochrome]], the remarkable
red/far-red [[phototropin-blue-light-receptor-plant-directional-sensing]] that underlies much of plant visual biology.

## The Maryland Mammoth Tobacco

The story of photoperiodism begins in the tobacco fields of southern Maryland, where
farmers had grown tobacco since the first European settlers arrived at the end of the
seventeenth century. The standard practice was to plant in spring and harvest in late
summer. Some plants were left to flower and produce seed for the next year.

In 1906, farmers noticed a new strain that never seemed to stop growing. It could
reach four and a half metres in height, produce almost a hundred leaves, and would
only stop when frosts set in. This strain, named Maryland Mammoth, was both a blessing
and a curse. Its vigorous leaf production seemed like a boon, but it rarely flowered,
meaning farmers could not reliably harvest seed.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard, two scientists at the US Department
of Agriculture, set out to determine why Maryland Mammoth did not know when to stop
making leaves and start making flowers. They planted the tobacco in pots and divided
them into two groups. One group was left outside with natural daylight. The other was
put in the field during the day but moved to a dark shed every afternoon, artificially
shortening the hours of light.

The results were dramatic. Simply limiting the hours of light was enough to cause
Maryland Mammoth to stop growing leaves and start flowering. If exposed to the long
days of summer, it kept growing. With artificially shorter days, it flowered. Garner
and Allard had discovered photoperiodism — the first strong evidence that plants
actively measure how much light they receive and use that information to control their
development.

## Short-Day and Long-Day Plants

Garner and Allard's discovery prompted research that classified plants by their
flowering responses. Short-day plants flower only when the day is shorter than a
critical threshold. These include chrysanthemums and soybeans, which naturally flower
in autumn as days shorten. Long-day plants flower only when the day exceeds a critical
length. Irises and barley are long-day plants, flowering in late spring or early
summer when days are long.

This classification was enormously useful for agriculture. It meant farmers could
manipulate flowering by controlling the light a plant sees, growing flowers and crops
out of their natural season. Florida farmers realised they could grow Maryland Mammoth
for many months without frost and that the plants would eventually flower in midwinter
when days were shortest — solving the seed-production problem that had plagued Maryland
growers.

## The Critical Discovery: Plants Measure Darkness

The concept of photoperiodism raised an obvious question: do plants measure the length
of the day, or the length of the night? The answer was one of the most surprising
findings in plant biology.

Around World War II, scientists discovered they could manipulate flowering by turning
lights on and off in the middle of the night. They could prevent a short-day plant
like the soybean from flowering even during short autumn days — all by turning on the
lights for just a few minutes at midnight. Conversely, they could cause a long-day
plant like the iris to flower in midwinter by turning on the lights briefly in the
middle of the night. These experiments proved conclusively that plants measure not the
length of the day but the length of the continuous period of darkness. A single brief
flash of light in the middle of the night is enough to reset the plant's internal
clock and convince it that the night is over.
