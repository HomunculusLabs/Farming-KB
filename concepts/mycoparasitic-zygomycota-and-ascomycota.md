# Mycoparasitic Zygomycota and Ascomycota

Mycoparasitism — the phenomenon of one fungus parasitizing another — is a widespread and ecologically significant strategy among fungi. Mycoparasitic fungi attack other fungi through a range of mechanisms including direct hyphal penetration, secretion of lytic enzymes, antibiosis via secondary metabolites, and coil formation around host hyphae. These interactions shape fungal community structure in soil, decaying wood, leaf litter, and clinical settings. Two major phyla harbor important mycoparasites: **Zygomycota** and **[[ascomycota]]**, each with distinct lineages, host specificities, and modes of action.

## Mycoparasitic Zygomycota

### Dimargaritales

The order **Dimargaritales** contains obligate mycoparasites classified into two families: **Dimargaritaceae** and **Piptocephalidaceae**. These zygomycetous fungi are typically found growing on other Mucorales in nature and are characterized by their relatively simple thalli and haustorial feeding structures that penetrate host hyphae to absorb nutrients.

- **Dimargaritaceae** — species in this family produce distinctive dimargaritate (two-spored) sporangiola and form coiled haustoria within host hyphae. They are commonly encountered on dung and decaying plant material where mucoralean hosts are abundant. Their obligate biotrophic nature makes them difficult to culture without a living host, contributing to their underrepresentation in fungal collections.
- **Piptocephalidaceae** — members such as **Piptocephalis** and **Syncephalis** are frequently isolated from soil and dung substrates. They produce merosporangia bearing a linear series of spores and are fast-growing, quickly overrunning their mucoralean hosts. The merosporangium is a distinctive spore-bearing structure that aids in their identification in mixed culture.

Both families are largely understudied in environmental surveys, suggesting their true diversity is considerably greater than currently documented. Molecular environmental sampling from soil and dung habitats is likely to uncover numerous new species within these lineages.

### Ecological Significance of Zygomycetous Mycoparasites

Mycoparasitic Zygomycota play important but frequently overlooked roles in regulating fungal populations within their microhabitats. By parasitizing fast-growing mucoralean decomposers, dimargaritalean mycoparasites can slow decomposition rates and alter succession patterns on dung and decaying plant matter. Their obligate nature means they are highly sensitive to environmental disturbances, making them potential bioindicators of habitat quality and microhabitat stability.

The genus **Spinellus** and **Syzygites**, while not obligate, similarly influence the reproductive success of their agaric hosts by destroying or deforming fruit bodies before spore release can occur. This can have cascading effects on fungal population dynamics, particularly for host species that rely on a narrow fruiting window for effective spore dispersal.

### Nonspecific Mycoparasites on Mucorales

Several genera act as opportunistic, nonspecific mycoparasites targeting members of the **Mucorales**. These fungi are frequently observed in field collections when conditions favor both the host and the parasite simultaneously.

- **Spinellus** — recognized by its tall, slender sporangiophores bearing small spinulose sporangia, commonly found parasitizing mushrooms of the genera **Mycena** and **Collybia**. The parasite emerges dramatically from the host pileus, creating a striking visual effect in the field.
- **Syzygites** — especially **Syzygites megalocarpus**, a conspicuous parasite of agaric fruit bodies (particularly in the family **Coprinaceae**), producing a web-like mycelial covering over the host and erect sporangiophores. It is one of the most frequently encountered zygomycetous mycoparasites in temperate forests.
- **Dicranophora** — a rare genus with species that parasitize fungal fruit bodies, noted for its distinctive branching patterns and sporangial morphology. Specimens are infrequently collected, suggesting either genuine rarity or simply a lack of targeted observation.
- **Parasitella** — **Parasitella simplex** is a well-studied mycoparasite of Mucorales that forms specialized haustoria and can undergo a sexual (zygospore-forming) interaction with its host, a remarkable example of parasexual compatibility between parasite and host genomes.
- **Mortierella** — while primarily saprotrophic, some **Mortierella** species exhibit mycoparasitic tendencies toward other zygomycetes, particularly under nutrient-limited conditions where direct exploitation of a living fungal substrate provides a competitive advantage.

## Mycoparasitic Ascomycota

Ascomycota contains the majority of described mycoparasitic fungi, distributed across several orders with varying degrees of host specificity and ecological roles. The diversity of mycoparasitic strategies in this phylum reflects its overall taxonomic breadth and ecological versatility.

### Laboulbeniales

The **Laboulbeniales** are predominantly ectoparasites of arthropods, but the genus **Pyxidiophora** bridges two worlds: its teleomorph grows on arthropod-associated substrates (often beetle elytra), while its anamorph stages can function as mycoparasites or saprotrophs on fungal hyphae and spores. This dual lifestyle makes **Pyxidiophora** a particularly interesting genus for understanding evolutionary transitions between parasitic strategies. Spores are dispersed by arthropod vectors, linking the arthropod and fungal communities in complex ecological networks.

### Leotiales

The order **Leotiales** includes several genera with mycoparasitic members, many of which are inconspicuous and easily overlooked in field surveys:

- **Orbilia** — the teleomorph genus of the well-known nematode-trapping anamorphs **Arthrobotrys** and **Dactylella**. While famed for their predatory devices (adhesive networks, constricting rings, and adhesive knobs) that capture nematodes, **Orbilia** species also exhibit facultative mycoparasitic behavior, colonizing and degrading other fungal hyphae in soil.
- **Arthrobotrys** and **Dactylella** — anamorphic genera producing trapping structures for nematodes but also capable of mycoparasitism, particularly against soil-borne fungal pathogens. Their dual predatory and mycoparasitic capabilities make them key regulators of soil micro-food webs.
- **Helicogonium** — a genus of minute, inconspicuous ascomycetes that grow as obligate mycoparasites on other discomycete fruit bodies in the field. Species are often overlooked due to their cryptic nature and require careful microscopic examination for detection.
- **Unguiculariopsis** — another genus of tiny, host-specific mycoparasites found on fungal fruit bodies, particularly on **Helotiales** and related taxa. Their small apothecia may be mistaken for host reproductive structures.
- **Llimoniella** and **Rhymbocarpus** — genera of mycoparasitic discomycetes that colonize the apothecia of other ascomycetes, forming their own fruiting structures directly on host tissue. They effectively hijack the host's reproductive effort.
- **Skyttea** — a genus of inoperculate discomycetes that parasitize lichens and non-lichenized fungi, adding to the diversity of mycoparasites in the **Leotiales** and blurring the line between lichenicolous and mycoparasitic lifestyles.

### Chaetothyriales

- **Capronia** — species of **Capronia** have been reported as mycoparasites in various contexts. This genus is of additional interest because some members are known opportunistic human pathogens (causing phaeohyphomycosis), highlighting the clinical relevance of understanding mycoparasitic ecology and evolution. The evolutionary link between mycoparasitism and pathogenicity in this genus warrants further investigation.

The **Chaetothyriales** as a whole are a fascinating group for studying the evolution of fungal lifestyles. Many members are rock-inhabiting or associated with extreme environments, and the transition from environmental mycoparasitism to opportunistic human pathogenicity in **Capronia** may reflect pre-adaptations to stress tolerance and enzymatic capabilities originally evolved for degrading fungal cell walls. Understanding these transitions has implications for predicting emerging fungal pathogens.

### Sordariales / Ceratostomataceae

The family **Ceratostomataceae** within the **Sordariales** houses several important mycoparasitic genera, many of which are commonly encountered as secondary colonizers of fungal cultures:

- **Melanospora** — a genus of perithecial ascomycetes commonly found growing on other fungi. Species are frequently encountered as contaminants or parasites on fungal cultures and in nature on decaying substrates. Their dark perithecia are conspicuous against lighter-colored host mycelia.
- **Persiciospora** — related to **Melanospora**, with species that function as generalist mycoparasites, producing conspicuous perithecia on host fungal tissue. The genus name refers to the distinctive persicicolored (peach-colored) perithecial walls of some species.
- **Sphaerodes** — species of **Sphaerodes** are mycoparasites or hyperparasites, sometimes found on other ascomycete perithecia or on mucoralean hosts. Their small, globose perithecia can be abundant on heavily colonized substrates.
- **Hypomyces armeniacus** — a distinctive species within this complex that parasitizes fungal fruit bodies, producing bright-colored perithecia. Despite sharing the genus name with the Hypocreales mycoparasites, its placement within Ceratostomataceae reflects the complex taxonomic history of these groups.
- **Syspastospora** — an understudied genus with mycoparasitic tendencies, often associated with wood-inhabiting fungi. Molecular data suggest it may harbor significant cryptic diversity.
- **Sphaeronaemella** — a genus producing small, membranous perithecia on the fruit bodies or mycelia of other fungi, contributing to the hidden diversity of fungal-fungal interactions in terrestrial ecosystems.

### Hypocreales Overview

The **Hypocreales** represents the most diverse and economically significant order of mycoparasitic fungi, encompassing species used in biological control, species that cause devastating crop diseases, and species of pharmaceutical importance. Members of this order produce perithecia that are often brightly colored (orange, red, yellow), making them conspicuous on host substrates.

#### Hypocreaceae: Hypocrea and Trichoderma

**Hypocrea** is the teleomorph genus corresponding to the ubiquitous anamorph **Trichoderma**. Species of **Trichoderma** are among the most intensively studied mycoparasites due to their commercial importance as biocontrol agents against plant pathogenic fungi.

**Antagonism mechanisms of Trichoderma include:**

- **Mycoparasitic coiling and penetration** — **Trichoderma** hyphae sense chemical signals from host fungi, grow toward them, coil around host hyphae, and penetrate the cell wall using appressorium-like structures combined with lytic enzymes (chitinases, β-1,3-glucanases, proteases).
- **Peptaibols** — non-ribosomally synthesized, linear peptide antibiotics rich in α-aminoisobutyric acid (Aib). They form voltage-dependent ion channels in fungal membranes, disrupting membrane integrity and leading to cell death of competing fungi. **Trichoderma** species produce a remarkable diversity of peptaibols (over 300 described).
- **Volatile inhibitors** — **Trichoderma** emits a suite of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) with antifungal activity, including 6-pentyl-α-pyrone (6-PAP) and various sesquiterpenes, which can inhibit spore germination and hyphal growth of competing fungi at a distance.
- **Gliotoxin** — an epipolythiodioxopiperazine (ETP) toxin produced by some **Trichoderma** strains (notably **T. virens**), which interferes with host cellular processes through redox cycling and thiol reactivity, inhibiting host enzyme function.
- **Nutrient competition** — efficient substrate colonization and rapid nutrient uptake allow **Trichoderma** to outcompete other fungi for resources, a mechanism sometimes termed "nutrient exhaustion."
- **Induced systemic resistance** — beyond direct mycoparasitism, **Trichoderma** can prime plant defense responses through root colonization, providing indirect protection against a broad spectrum of fungal pathogens.

**Trichoderma harzianum** vs **Lentinula** — a particularly notable interaction involves **T. harzianum** as a pathogen of cultivated shiitake mushrooms (**Lentinula edodes**), where aggressive **Trichoderma** strains cause "green mold" disease on mushroom compost, resulting in significant economic losses in commercial mushroom production. This interaction underscores the dual nature of **Trichoderma** as both a beneficial biocontrol agent and a problematic pathogen depending on context.

#### Hypocreopsis Species

**Hypocreopsis** species are resupinate (crust-forming) fungi in the **Hypocreales** that grow on wood and may exhibit mycoparasitic tendencies, overgrowing and colonizing other wood-inhabiting fungal fruit bodies. Their inconspicuous growth form means they are likely under-recorded in biodiversity surveys. Some species are of conservation concern due to their rarity and specific old-growth forest habitat requirements.

**Hypocreopsis** species produce strikingly large, orange to reddish, effused-reflexed fruiting bodies with a rubbery to gelatinous texture. They are typically found on the bark of ancient trees, often in association with other wood-inhabiting fungi that may serve as their hosts. Their dependence on old-growth forest conditions makes them particularly vulnerable to habitat loss and forest management practices.

#### Hypomyces: Mycoparasites of Macromycetes

The genus **Hypomyces** is one of the most recognizable groups of mycoparasitic fungi, specializing as hyperparasites on the fruit bodies of larger fungi (macromycetes). **Hypomyces** species often dramatically alter the appearance of their hosts, transforming gilled mushrooms, boletes, polypores, and other basidiomycetes into brightly colored, sterile masses covered with the parasite's perithecia.

**Key features of Hypomyces:**

- **Host specificity** — different **Hypomyces** species target different host groups. **Hypomyces lactifluorum** parasitizes lactarii and russuloids, turning them orange and rendering them commercially valuable as "lobster mushrooms." **Hypomyces chrysospermus** attacks boletes, progressing through white, golden, and brown stages. **Hypomyces completus** targets species of **Lactarius** and **Russula**, while **Hypomyces hyalinus** attacks **Amanita** species.
- **Anamorph connections** — the anamorphs of many **Hypomyces** species belong to the genus **Cladobotryum**, which produce conidia on branched conidiophores. **Cladobotryum** species are also significant as pathogens of cultivated mushrooms, causing cobweb disease on **Agaricus bisporus** and other commercial species, leading to substantial crop losses in mushroom farms worldwide.
- **Ecological impact** — by destroying fruit bodies of ectomycorrhizal and wood-decay fungi, **Hypomyces** species can influence forest ecosystem dynamics and fungal community composition, potentially affecting nutrient cycling and plant-fungal symbioses.

### Bionectriaceae: Bionectria and Clonostachys

The family **Bionectriaceae** includes the teleomorph genus **Bionectria** and its corresponding anamorph **Clonostachys**. These fungi are widespread in soil, plant debris, and rhizosphere environments, where they function as mycoparasites, saprotrophs, and occasionally plant endophytes.

- **Bionectria** species produce bright-colored (often red or orange) perithecia and have been documented parasitizing a range of fungal hosts, including other ascomycetes and basidiomycetes. Their presence on decaying wood often indicates an active mycoparasitic interaction with co-occurring fungi.
- **Clonostachys** — the anamorphic stage produces penicillate conidiophores bearing slimy conidial heads. **Clonostachys rosea** (= **Gliocladium roseum**) is a well-known mycoparasite and biocontrol agent effective against **Botrytis cinerea** and other plant pathogenic fungi, utilizing a combination of mycoparasitism, antibiosis, and competition.
- Some **Clonostachys** species produce volatile antibiotics and chitinolytic enzymes that contribute to their antagonistic activity against fungal pathogens, making them promising candidates for development as commercial biocontrol products.

### Comparative Overview of Mycoparasitic Strategies

Across the Zygomycota and Ascomycota, mycoparasitic fungi employ several broad strategies that can be summarized as follows:

- **Necrotrophic mycoparasitism** — the parasite kills the host cell rapidly, then absorbs nutrients from the dead tissue. **Trichoderma** species often operate in this mode, combining toxin production with enzymatic degradation.
- **Biotrophic mycoparasitism** — the parasite maintains a living host interface, typically through haustoria, and derives nutrients without immediately killing the host. Dimargaritalean Zygomycota and **Helicogonium** exemplify this strategy.
- **Fusion-dependent mycoparasitism** — hyphal fusion (anastomosis) with the host precedes parasitic takeover, as observed in some **Parasitella** and **Trichoderma** interactions where cytoplasmic exchange occurs before host degradation.

Understanding which strategy a given mycoparasite employs is critical for predicting its ecological impact and potential applications in biological control.

## Summary

Mycoparasitic fungi span a remarkable taxonomic breadth across **Zygomycota** and **Ascomycota**, employing diverse strategies from direct hyphal penetration and enzymatic degradation to antibiosis via peptaibols, gliotoxin, and volatile compounds. The **Hypocreales** — particularly **Trichoderma** and **Hypomyces** — represent the best-studied lineages, yet even within these groups, vast unexplored biodiversity remains. The cryptic nature of many mycoparasites (e.g., **Helicogonium**, **Unguiculariopsis**, dimargaritalean zygomycetes) suggests that environmental sequencing and focused microscopic surveys will continue to reveal new species and interactions, underscoring the "unknown biodiversity" of mycoparasitic fungi and their critical but underappreciated roles in ecosystem function.
