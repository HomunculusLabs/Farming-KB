# Oyster Mushroom Green Mold Disease

## Overview

Green mold disease is one of the most significant and widespread problems in [[oyster-mushroom-cultivation]] worldwide. Any disease caused by green-colored fungi on mushroom bags or beds is classified as green mold disease. The green coloration comes from the spores (conidiospores) produced by the pathogen, not from the hyphae themselves — the hyphal stage is typically white, making early detection extremely difficult. More than 30 fungal species have been reported as causal agents of green mold disease on cultivated mushrooms, making this one of the most complex disease categories in mushroom pathology.

## Why Green Mold Is So Prevalent

Mushroom cultivation conditions inherently favor the pathogens that cause green mold. The combination of high humidity and warm temperatures that oyster mushrooms require for optimal growth are precisely the conditions under which Trichoderma and related fungi thrive. Several compounding factors increase disease pressure:

- **Chemical limitations** — there is a strict limit on chemical use for disease control [[contamination-prevention-in-mushroom-cultivation]] because mushrooms are marketed as natural, healthy foods, leaving growers with few direct treatment options
- **Continuous cultivation** — growing houses involved in continuous cropping cycles accumulate pathogen loads over time as spores build up in the environment
- **Environmental control gaps** — many growing houses, especially in [[low-cost-mushroom-growing-house-construction-developing-countries]], are not well equipped for the precise environmental control needed to suppress competitor organisms
- **Multiple entry vectors** — pathogens can invade at every stage: through contaminated spawn, insufficient sterilization, dirty tools, shoes, clothes, hands during inoculation, and through open doors and windows during incubation

## Pathogens: Trichoderma and Hypocrea

### Trichoderma Species

The major pathogens of green mold disease on oyster mushroom include:

- **Trichoderma virens** (syn. Gliocladium virens) — aggressive competitor that rapidly colonizes substrate
- **Trichoderma viride** — common contaminant producing dense green spore masses
- **[[trichoderma-harzianum]]** — significant pathogen; different biotypes affect different regions (T. harzianum biotype 2 is major in Europe, while T. harzianum biotype 4 predominates on button mushrooms in the USA)
- **Trichoderma koningii** — less aggressive but frequently encountered

These species reproduce primarily through asexual spores (green conidiospores) that are easily dispersed by air currents, water splash, and physical contact.

### Hypocrea Species

Some Trichoderma species have both an asexual cycle (producing green spores) and a sexual stage classified as Hypocrea. Hypocrea spp. form white or brown stromata — compact, pillow-like structures in which sexual spores (ascospores) are produced. Recently, Hypocrea strains that do not produce an asexual (green-spore) stage have been identified as causing severe problems in oyster [[mushroom-cultivation-in-kenya]] Korea. These are particularly dangerous because they cannot be detected by the characteristic green coloration that growers typically watch for.

## Symptoms and Detection

### Early Stage (White Mycelial Phase)

During the early hyphal growth stage, green mold pathogens are nearly impossible to distinguish from oyster mushroom mycelium by visual inspection alone. Both appear white. However, careful observation reveals differences:

- Green mold fungi form **denser mycelia** with more aerial (upright) hyphae compared to the thinner, more appressed growth of oyster mushroom mycelium
- Affected areas may appear slightly more cottony or fluffy than surrounding healthy mycelium
- Growth rate may differ from the expected colonization speed of the oyster mushroom strain

### Mid Stage (Sporulation)

Green color appears when the pathogen begins producing conidiospores from aerial hyphae. If the pathogen was introduced during the spawning stage, green patches typically become visible 10–15 days later on the cultivation bed or within substrate bags.

### Late Stage (Advanced Colonization)

Mushroom hyphae stop growing at the edges of green patches and are gradually covered and replaced by the advancing green mold front. The substrate in affected areas becomes unusable and may serve as a source of spores that contaminate adjacent bags or beds.

### Hypocrea-Specific Symptoms

Hypocrea infections are particularly insidious because they do not produce the characteristic green coloration. A white stroma formed by Hypocrea closely resembles the primordia (pins) of oyster mushroom, leading growers to mistake the pathogen for early fruiting. By the time the stroma is recognized as abnormal, the pathogen has already occupied the substrate deeply and extensively. Brown stromata are somewhat easier to identify but still require careful inspection.

## Control Measures

### Prevention (Primary Strategy)

Sanitation and strict hygiene are the most important control methods for green mold disease. The Mushroom Growers' Handbook recommends these basic practices:

- Keep doors closed at all times; avoid exposing substrates to open air during spawning
- Install screens on all windows and doors to exclude mushroom flies (which carry spores)
- Inspect mushroom bags and beds carefully and frequently for early detection
- Remove mushroom debris and stumps shortly after each harvest
- Keep floors clean; never dump waste near mushroom houses
- Disinfect or pasteurize spent substrate before removing it from growing houses
- Clean and disinfect growing houses thoroughly between crops
- Clean and disinfect all equipment frequently
- Wear clean clothes and shoes; wash hands before entering mushroom houses

### Active Treatment

When green mold is detected:

- **Spray or drench** affected spots with a 500 ppm solution of Sporgon (prochloraz-manganese complex, 50% active ingredient) — this is reported as the most effective chemical treatment
- **Preventive application** of 250–500 ppm Sporgon before pasteurization can reduce green mold and Hypocrea disease incidence
- Benomyl and thiabendazole have historically been used as control agents, but resistant strains of the pathogens have become common in Korea
- Recent experimental results show that prochloraz-manganese complex is by far the most effective treatment among available options
- Severely contaminated bags or substrate should be removed entirely and destroyed to prevent spore dispersal

### Cultural Practices

- **Thorough pasteurization** is essential — severe infestations are most commonly found in poorly pasteurized substrates with uneven moisture content. Substrate temperature should reach 60–65°C throughout the entire mass for the full recommended duration
