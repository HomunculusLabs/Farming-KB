## Flow Over Landscapes and Objects

## Overview

The movement of fluids and gases over landscapes and objects creates characteristic patterns that are fundamental to understanding how natural systems function and how to design effective [[overbeck-jet]] to Kármán vortex streets, these flow phenomena reveal universal principles about energy dissipation, resource distribution, and the creation of productive edge conditions in designed landscapes.

## The Overbeck Jet

### Description

The Overbeck jet, called the "apple core" model, is a simple involuted mushroom-shaped form that arises when one fluid or gas is jetted into another medium. It can be produced by jetting smoke, fluid, gases, or oils into other media, but it also occurs as part of the natural streaming of fluids and gases past fixed objects.

### Natural Occurrences

The Overbeck jet appears commonly in nature:
- **Mushrooms**: The classic involuted cap form
- **River mouths**: Rivers flooding into the sea create this characteristic shape
- **Jellyfish**: The bell and trailing tentacles follow this pattern
- **Smoke plumes**: Rising smoke entering still air

The pattern represents the simplest half-form of the basic flow model, demonstrating how a jet of one medium creates a distinctive flow pattern as it enters and interacts with a surrounding medium.

## Vortices and Vortex Shedding

### Formation from Bluff Bodies

When a fixed object (a "bluff body") is located in a flow — whether a post in a stream, an island in tidal waters, or a tree in wind — whirlpools or **vortices** are shed alternately from each side of the object. Each side generates its own vortex with a different rotation, creating beautiful and complex forms.

### The Kármán Vortex Street

This alternating pattern of vortices is known as a Kármán vortex street. The sets of vortices shed from bluff bodies form the basis of the work at the [[flowform]] work demonstrates how sculpted vessels can replicate natural vortex patterns to:
- **Oxygenate water**: The folding and mixing action of vortices dramatically increases dissolved oxygen
- **Pattern water**: Some biodynamic and water-quality practitioners believe vortex-treated water has enhanced biological properties
- **Mix solutions**: Vortex flow provides thorough mixing without mechanical agitation

### Wind Design

Understanding flow over objects allows designers to:
- **Create shelter**: Position windbreaks and shelterbelts to protect sensitive areas
- **Direct airflow**: Channel cooling breezes through buildings or growing areas
- **Generate turbulence**: Deliberately create turbulent zones for pollination or pest disruption
- **Prevent damage**: Avoid placing vulnerable structures in zones of accelerated flow

### Stream Enhancement

In salmon streams, weirs can be deliberately installed to induce turbulence, which:
- Creates varied flow velocities needed by different fish species
- Increases dissolved oxygen through surface agitation
- Sorts and deposits gravel for spawning beds
- Creates pool habitats for resting fish

Conversely, smooth and even conduits are used for efficient energy generation in wind or hydraulic systems, where turbulence represents energy loss rather than benefit.

## The Role of Obstacles in Flow

### Bluff Bodies as Design Elements

Any fixed object in a flow creates characteristic patterns. In design, this means:
- **Trees** act as bluff bodies that modify wind and water flow, creating microclimates and accumulation zones
- **Rocks and boulders** in streams create pool-riffle sequences and eddies that provide habitat
- **Buildings and walls** redirect wind and create sheltered courtyards or accelerated wind tunnels
- **Islands and peninsulas** deflect tidal and current flows, creating depositional and erosional zones

### Shape Matters

The shape of the obstacle determines the flow pattern it creates:
- **Streamlined forms**: Minimize turbulence and energy loss (efficient flow)
- **Blunt forms**: Maximize vortex shedding and mixing (turbulent flow)
- **Lobular forms**: Create complex multi-eddy patterns with varied micro-habitats
- **Spiral forms**: Guide flow in rotating patterns for mixing or compact form

## Flow and the Core Model

The flow phenomena discussed — Overbeck jets, vortices, and streaming patterns — are all expressions of the fundamental core model of permaculture pattern understanding. They represent how energy moves through media and how that movement creates form, structure, and productive edge conditions.

### The Universal Nature of Flow Patterns

Flow patterns observed in one medium are analogous to those in others:
- **Muddy water entering the sea** resembles **smoke entering still air**
- **Jet streams** resemble **river currents**
- **Vortices behind a post** resemble **galactic spiral arms**

This universality means that understanding flow in one context provides insight into flow in all contexts, making it a powerful tool for the permaculture designer working across diverse media and scales.

## Practical Guidelines

1. **Observe existing flow patterns** before modifying a landscape — they reveal where energy concentrates and dissipates
2. **Use obstacles strategically** to create desired flow effects — shelter, mixing, acceleration, or deposition
3. **Match form to function**: Streamlined shapes for efficient conveyance, blunt shapes for mixing and turbulence
4. **Scale appropriately**: Flow patterns repeat at every scale, so principles learned from small models apply to large landscapes
5. **Work with flow, not against it**: Designs that accommodate natural flow patterns require less energy to maintain than those that resist them
