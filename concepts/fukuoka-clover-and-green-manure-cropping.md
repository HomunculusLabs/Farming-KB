# Fukuoka Clover and Green Manure Cropping

Clover and other [[mollison-designers-cover-crops-and-green-manure-species]] form the living foundation of Masanobu Fukuoka's grain production system. By maintaining a permanent ground cover of leguminous plants, Fukuoka replaced chemical fertilizers, compost preparation, herbicides, and much of the labor associated with conventional farming. He called his system the "clover revolution" in rice and barley cropping, framing it as a direct alternative to modern scientific farming with its dependence on chemicals and large machinery.

## The Role of Clover in the System

In Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-no-till-grain-production-method]], clover (primarily ladino clover) serves multiple simultaneous functions:

- **Nitrogen fixation**: As a legume, clover fixes atmospheric nitrogen through its root nodules, supplying the nitrogen needs of the rice and barley crops without chemical fertilizers.
- **Weed suppression**: A dense clover cover prevents winter and summer weeds from establishing, eliminating the need for herbicides or hand weeding.
- **Soil protection**: The living ground cover prevents erosion from rainfall and maintains soil structure.
- **Soil improvement**: Clover roots penetrate and loosen the soil, adding organic matter as they die and decompose.
- **Moisture retention**: The clover canopy reduces evaporation from the soil surface.
- **Habitat creation**: Clover provides habitat and forage for beneficial insects that help control pests.

## The Annual Clover Cycle

The clover cycle is interwoven with the rice-barley succession cropping:

### Autumn Seeding

Clover seeds (approximately 1 lb per quarter-acre) are broadcast in September-October over the standing heads of ripening rice. The high soil moisture at this time promotes germination, and winter weeds have not yet appeared, giving the clover a competitive advantage. A second seeding may be done in March-April to fill any gaps.

### Winter Growth

Through the winter, clover establishes as a dense ground cover beneath the maturing barley. The barley grows tall above the clover, and the two crops coexist without competition -- the barley draws nutrients from deeper soil layers while the clover fixes nitrogen near the surface.

### Spring Transition

In late May, when the barley is harvested, the clover is cut along with the grain. This does not interfere with the harvest. After the barley straw is scattered over the field, the clover regrows from the cut stumps. By early June, the clover produces a luxuriant growth that threatens to overwhelm the young rice seedlings emerging from their [[fukuoka-seed-balls-and-direct-seeding]].

### Managed Suppression

To prevent the clover from choking the rice, Fukuoka floods the field for four to seven days. This weakens but does not kill the clover, giving the rice seedlings a chance to break through the cover. After draining, the clover gradually recovers during the rice growing season, maintaining its ground cover function without competing with the now-taller rice.

## Beyond Clover: The Green Manure Portfolio

While clover was Fukuoka's primary ground cover for grain fields, his broader system employed a diverse range of green manure plants for different contexts:

### In Grain Fields
- Ladino clover for the primary living mulch in rice-barley succession
- Chinese milk vetch as an alternative or complement to clover, also usable as early livestock feed
- Bur clover for single-cropped rice on dry fields

### In Orchards
- Ladino clover and alfalfa as primary ground covers
- Acacia as a green manure tree providing shade, wind protection, and nitrogen fixation
- Hairy vetch for soil improvement
- Various vegetables intercropped with the green manure cover

### For Land Restoration
- Black wattle for depleted mountain soils, where it prepares the ground for cedar and cypress succession
- Kudzu, common vetch, and bur clover in rotation systems
- Japanese alder as a year-round green manure tree

## Fukuoka's Philosophy of Green Manure

Fukuoka's use of green manure was grounded in his [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy]], but it also reflected deep ecological understanding. He articulated several principles:

**Weeds enrich the soil**: Far from being enemies of crops, all plants contribute to soil development. "The deep penetration of grass roots into the earth loosens the soil. When the roots die, this adds to the humus, allowing soil microbes to proliferate and enrich the soil." His [[fukuoka-no-weeding-no-pesticides]] principle held that weeding is not merely unnecessary but positively harmful in orchards.

**Remove weeds with weeds**: When wild weeds are problematic, "it is wiser to remove weeds with weeds than to pull weeds by hand." By establishing desired green manure species that outcompete undesirable weeds, the farmer achieves a "weedless" method that allows weeds to grow -- but the right weeds.

**Grasses plow the soil**: "The roots of grasses and green manure crops work the soil down to twelve inches, fifteen inches, or more." This connects to his [[fukuoka-why-tillage-destroys-soil]] argument that mechanical tillage is unnecessary because plant roots perform the same function more effectively.

**Succession and coexistence**: In a natural meadow, plants "appear to grow and die in total confusion, but upon closer examination, there are laws and there is order here." Grasses sprout, flourish, weaken, and die in ongoing succession. "By studying and making use of the properties of weeds, one weed can be used to drive out a large number of other weeds."

## The Clover Revolution

Fukuoka explicitly framed his clover-based system as a "revolution" opposing modern scientific agriculture:

> "I called this the basic pattern of natural rice and barley farming because I was certain that this technique enabled the farmer for the first time to farm without using any pesticides or chemical fertilizers."

He noted that when the method was studied at agricultural testing stations throughout Japan, researchers found "no basic problem with the no-tillage, succession cropping of rice and barley using straw mulch." The remaining challenge was weed control, which Fukuoka solved by adding the green manure ground cover and biennial cultivation patterns.

## Crop Rotation with Green Manure

Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-crop-rotation-natural-farming]] system integrated green manure into crop rotations for all types of crops:

- Leguminous green manure suppresses summer weeds and enriches the soil
- Minor grains that deplete soil when single-cropped can be rotated with green manure and root vegetables to improve and enrich the land
- Cruciferous winter vegetables send down deep roots that improve soil structure
- Under fruit trees, green manure resolves problems of continuous cropping and extends tree life

He observed that his method of rice-barley cropping under a green manure cover was "in a sense, just a mimicry of nature" -- but a mimicry that worked far better than the artificial inputs it replaced. He noted that about seventy percent of the nitrogenous components absorbed by rice and barley are supplied directly by the soil. If all straw and chaff were returned to the fields, farmers would need to apply only about fifteen percent of the nitrogenous components required -- and a green manure cover can supply this.

## The Soil Beneath the Clover

Fukuoka's observations of soil under permanent clover cover revealed principles that connect to his broader [[fukuoka-living-soil]] understanding. Under a continuous ground cover, he found that the soil becomes darker and richer each year without any fertilizer application. The clover roots die and regrow, continuously adding organic matter. Earthworms appear where there is humus, and moles follow the earthworms, further aerating the soil.

This contrasts sharply with what happens under conventional management, where weeding and plowing "depletes the fertility of the soil, creates a deficiency of trace components, diminishes the soil's vitality, hardens the topsoil, kills off microbes, and turns rich, living, organic material into a dead, inanimate, yellowish-white mineral matter the only function of which is to physically support the crops."

The green manure approach thus serves not only as a replacement for chemical nitrogen but as a comprehensive soil restoration strategy. It addresses the physical structure (roots loosen and aggregate soil), biological activity (microbes proliferate), chemical balance (nutrients cycle naturally), and water management (mulch retains moisture) of the soil simultaneously.
