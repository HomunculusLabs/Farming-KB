# Acidifying Pollutants and Mycorrhizal Community Structure

The response of mycorrhizal fungal communities to acidifying pollutants represents one of the best-documented cases of anthropogenic disruption to below-ground ecosystems. Research across Europe and North America has revealed consistent patterns of community shift, though the mechanisms and specific responses vary considerably among fungal species, host trees, soil types, and pollutant combinations.

## Macrofungal Fruit Bodies as Bioindicators

In European forest monitoring programs, the ratio of saprotrophic to ectomycorrhizal macrofungal fruit bodies has been adopted as a sensitive biological indicator of ecosystem health. Fellner (1988) and Colpaert and Van Tichelen (1996) established this approach, which relies on the consistent observation that [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] are more sensitive to acidifying pollutants than saprotrophic species.

### The Fellner and Pesková Forest Decline Staging System

Fellner and Pesková (1995) formalized a staging system for forest decline based on macrofungal community composition:

| Stage | ECM Fungi | Lignicolous Species |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Latent** | Decreasing to 40% | Increasing to >30% |
| **Acute** | Decreasing to <40% | Increasing to >40% |
| **Lethal** | Decreasing to <20% | Increasing to >55% |

This staging system provides a practical field assessment tool, though researchers acknowledge the limitation that fruit body production may not reflect actual root colonization levels (Termorshuizen and Schaffers, 1989; Egli et al., 1993).

## Specific Mycorrhizal Community Changes

Research has documented consistent shifts in ectomycorrhizal community structure under acidifying pollution:

### Finnish Studies
Markkola and Ohtonen (1988) found that in polluted Finnish forests:
- **Decreased**: *Piloderma*, *Dermocybe*, *Hebeloma*, and "type 03" ectomycorrhizae
- **Increased**: *Cenococcum* (notably tolerant)

### Lysimeter Experiments
Dighton and Skeffington (1987) applied simulated acid rain to *Pinus sylvestris* in lysimeters and found:
- Reduction in multibranched mycorrhizal morphotypes
- Reduction in mycorrhizae with large amounts of extraradical hyphae
- The greatest effect was on fungal hyphal growth rather than the mycorrhizal structure itself

### Nitrogen Addition Experiments (NITREX)
Brandrud (1995) compared macrofungal flora across nitrogen treatments in Swedish Norway spruce forests:
- **Dominant genera reduced**: *Cortinarius*, *Russula*
- **Little change**: *Lactarius*
- **Increased with added N**: *[[paxillus-involutus]]*, *Lactarius rufus*
- Overall species diversity, number of fruiting fungi, and total fruit bodies did not differ significantly

Jonsson (1998) showed poor correspondence between fruit bodies and root tips in the same NITREX experiment—only 1–4% correspondence using molecular identification—yet fruit body communities showed more dramatic compositional shifts than root communities.

### Beech Woodland Nitrogen Experiments
Rühling and Tyler (1991) applied 3× and 9× ambient nitrogen deposition to beech woodlands:
- Near-complete cessation of mycorrhizal fungal fruiting
- Increased fruiting by saprotrophic leaf litter fungi: *Mycena*, *Clitocybe*, *Lepista*, *Agaricus*, *Lycoperdon*
- The increased N likely stimulated decomposition of recalcitrant leaf litter

## Aluminum Toxicity Mechanisms

The solubility of aluminum in acidified soils is a primary mechanism of mycorrhizal damage. Key findings include:

- Schier (1985): Tree roots damaged by increased Al availability in soil solution
- Thompson and Medve (1984): 146 mM Al suppressed hyphal growth of *Cenococcum*, *Pisolithus*, and *Thelephora*, but *Suillus* showed no reduction below 1000 mM
- Kottke and Oberwinkler (1990): Al:Ca, Al:Mg, and Al:PO₄ ratios are important determinants; Al can be immobilized into polyphosphate droplets in hyphae
- Jansen et al. (1990): Improved growth at low Al:PO₄ ratios even at high Al concentrations

## Phosphorus Availability and Acidification

Changes [[fungal-role-in-phosphorus-cycling]] represent an important secondary effect of acidification:

### Forest Decline in the Vosges
Estivalet et al. (1990) linked forest decline in the Vosges region of France to reduced phosphorus availability. They attributed this to changes in the rhizospheric microbial community, where *Penicillium*, *Trichoderma*, *Acremonium*, and *Cylindrocarpon* were more regularly isolated from declining soils and may have antagonistic (but not pathogenic) effects on Norway spruce ectomycorrhizae.

### Phosphatase Activity
Antibus and Linkins (1992) showed that liming reduced acid phosphatase activity of the mycorrhizal community in the litter layer, likely through synergistic effects of liming on both N and P availability. Carreira et al. (2000) demonstrated that acidifying pollutants alter the inorganic P subcycle by increasing P sorption capacity and decreasing labile P concentration. Acid phosphatase activity decreased from 382 to 263 mg pNP h⁻¹ g⁻¹ in acid-misted versus control plots.

## Nitrogen Saturation and Nutrient Imbalance

Chronic nitrogen addition can induce secondary nutrient limitations following Liebig's "Law of the Minimum":

- Harrison et al. (1995): K and P became increasingly limiting for Sitka spruce growth with increased N addition
- Arnebrant (1994): Ammonium sulfate and [[plant-available-nitrogen-ammonium-nitrate]] at 1–4 mg g⁻¹ peat significantly reduced mycelial growth of *Paxillus involutus* and *[[suillus-bovinus]]*
- Kieliszewska-Rokicka (1992): Small amounts of NH₄-N (0.17–19 mM) increased growth and acid phosphatase activity of *Paxillus involutus*—a possible synergistic effect of N causing temporary P deficiency

Wallander et al. (1999) showed that fungal isolates with inherently high NH₄ uptake affinity were inhibited more by elevated N than low-affinity isolates, suggesting that low uptake rates may enable ectomycorrhizal fungi to avoid stress from elevated nitrogen loading.

## Fungal Species Changes in Dutch Pastures

Arnolds (1989b,c) documented dramatic changes in fungal communities in a nutrient-poor acidic Dutch pasture receiving long-term inorganic and organic fertilizer:

### Species Lost
- *Hygrocybe ceracea*
- *Entoloma conferendum*
- *Mycena cinerella*
- *Geoglossum glutinosum*

### Species Increased (6–400 fold)
- *[[marasmius-oreades]]*
- *Panaeolina foenescii*
- *Clitocybe amarescens*
- *Panaeolus acuminatus*
- Increased coprophytic fungal species

The plant community shifted toward nitrogen-tolerant species, and while total fungal species number remained similar, the community composition changed dramatically. These observations parallel trends expected from atmospheric nitrogen deposition.

## Conservation Implications

Arnolds' observations of species loss under nitrogen pollution led to the development of "red data" lists for fungal species conservation (Arnolds, 1989b, 1997). The United Kingdom subsequently adopted inclusion of fungi and nonvascular plants in conservation lists (Watling, 1999). This represents growing recognition that fungal biodiversity is threatened by anthropogenic influences and requires active conservation measures.

## Methodological Considerations

A critical finding across multiple studies is that fruit body surveys may not accurately reflect below-ground mycorrhizal community composition. Jonsson's (1998) molecular analysis showing only 1–4% correspondence between fruit bodies and root tips has major implications for monitoring programs that rely solely on fruit body surveys. Effective assessment requires combining multiple methods: fruit body surveys, root tip morphology, and molecular identification techniques.
