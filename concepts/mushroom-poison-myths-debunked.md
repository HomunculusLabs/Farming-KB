Julius A. Palmer's 1894 work *About Mushrooms* provides one of the most thorough

authorities, personal poisoning experiences, and years of systematic experimentation.
Palmer devoted seven years to studying the edible fungi of New England, testing
varieties on his own stomach with sometimes dangerous results. His central conclusion—that no universal test exists
for mushroom safety—was radical for its time and remains foundational today.

## The Silver Spoon Test

Perhaps the most persistent myth is that cooking mushrooms with a silver spoon will
reveal poison: if the silver turns black, the mushroom is dangerous; if bright, safe.
This belief was so entrenched that Boston physicians recommended it to patients in
the 1870s.

Palmer traced the error chemically. Mushrooms in decomposition generate hydrogen
sulfide during cooking, which discolors silver regardless of toxicity. The test
merely detects spoilage, not fungal alkaloids. Conversely, perfectly fresh Amanita
specimens leave silver bright while containing toxins "surer than arsenic."

## The Solid vs. Hollow Stem Rule

Italian foragers held that mushrooms with solid stems were edible, hollow ones
poisonous. A boy employed by the Boston cook Gori told Palmer exactly this. While
true for the common meadow mushroom, many edible fungi have hollow stems and some
poisonous species are solid. The rule is not general and cannot be extended safely.

- **Viscous surfaces indicate poison** — No correlation exists.
- **Fungi not attacked by insects are poisonous** — Some deadly species are heavily

- **Milky secretions indicate danger** — Excellent edible Lactarius species exude
  copious milk; dry-stemmed fungi can be deadly.
- **Color change when cut indicates poison** — Many edibles change color; some

Cordier named fifty varieties as counterexamples, concluding: "No one of these
characteristics is exclusively the property either of esculent mushrooms or of
poisonous mushrooms."

## The Animal Test Myth

People assume fungi eaten by animals are safe for humans. Palmer showed otherwise:
pigs ate [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]];
rabbits swallow fungi that would sicken humans. Conversely, a dog died in twelve
hours from Agaricus melleus (Honey Mushroom), and a cat died in convulsions from
[[ramsbottom-poisonous-fungi-identification-guide]] in contact for twelve hours. After eating only the
washed edibles, he experienced violent poisoning within thirty minutes—too quickly
for stomach digestion. He proposed mushrooms "inoculate each other, either with or
without contact," their virus permeating harmless specimens. Even holding an
Amanita in a closed, perspiring hand produced full poisoning symptoms. Merely
smelling specimens in a closed box caused vomiting three hours later.

## The Fundamental Truth

Palmer's conclusion, echoed by every serious mycologist since his time: "no such touchstone is
infallible." The only safe approach is learning the specific characteristics of
each individual species. No shortcut exists, no universal rule, and no folk test
can substitute for actual knowledge. As Cordier stated: "The true, perhaps the sole means of
avoiding mistakes, when we wish to use mushrooms as food, is to learn to know the
characteristics which are peculiar to each species by itself."
