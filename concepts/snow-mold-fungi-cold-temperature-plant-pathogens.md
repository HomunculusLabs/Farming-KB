# Snow Mold Fungi

Snow molds are low-temperature-tolerant soil-borne fungi that
damage and kill grasses, cereals, and other plants during cold
seasons (Lebeau and Logsdon 1958; Nelson and Sturges 1982). The
name derives from their ability to grow at the bases of
snow-covered plants, producing extensive mycelial mats that
become visible soon after snow melts. Snow molds represent a
distinct ecological category within
[[psychrotolerant-psychrophilic-fungi-ecology]].

## Definition and Habitat

Snow molds occupy a unique ecological niche at the interface
of plant pathology and cold-adapted mycology. They are active
under snow cover where temperatures hover near freezing, an
environment that inhibits most fungal competitors while
providing sufficient moisture for growth. The insulating effect
of snow creates a microclimate with relatively stable
temperatures, often slightly above 0 degrees C, that permits
sustained fungal activity throughout winter months.

Beyond their field occurrence, snow molds can also damage
fruits and crops stored at low temperatures. This dual capacity
to attack both field-grown and stored plant materials
underscores their economic significance in agriculture and
post-harvest food preservation.

## Major Species

Several fungal species are recognized as the primary agents of
snow mold disease:

- Coprinus psychromorbidus (often called the low-temperature
  basidiomycete; Traquair et al. 1987): A basidiomycete that
  produces conspicuous fruiting structures on affected plants

- Microdochium nivale (formerly Fusarium nivale): An ascomycete
  anamorph that causes pink snow mold, one of the most
  economically important snow mold diseases worldwide

- Myriosclerotinia borealis (Saito 1998): A discomycete that
  forms sclerotial survival structures, enabling persistence
  between growing seasons

- Typhula ishikariensis and other Typhula species (Hsiang et al.
  1999): Basidiomycetes that produce sclerotia and cause
  gray snow mold, particularly damaging to turfgrasses and
  winter cereals in northern regions

## Pathogenic Mechanisms

Snow mold fungi infect plants during the cold season through
several mechanisms. Under snow cover, prolonged leaf wetness
and high humidity favor infection. The fungi produce
extracellular enzymes and toxins that break down plant cell
walls and kill host tissue. Some species, like Typhula
ishikariensis, form melanized sclerotia that serve as
long-term survival structures, allowing the fungus to persist
in soil and plant debris during unfavorable conditions.

The insulating snow layer both protects the fungus from
extreme cold and creates the humid conditions necessary for
infection. In the absence of snow cover, snow mold damage is
generally reduced, illustrating the critical role of the snow
microclimate in disease development.

## Geographic Distribution

Snow mold fungi are most problematic in regions with prolonged
snow cover, including northern temperate zones, boreal
forests, and subarctic regions. They are significant pathogens
of winter wheat, rye, and turfgrasses in Scandinavia, northern
Japan, Canada, the northern United States, and mountainous
regions worldwide. Their distribution correlates strongly with
snow cover duration and winter severity.

## Relationship to Psychrophilic Fungi

Snow molds are closely related to the broader category of
[[psychrotolerant-psychrophilic-fungi-ecology]]. While true
psychrophiles have optima below 16 degrees C and maxima at 20
degrees C, snow molds are psychrotolerant, capable of growth
at low temperatures but with higher temperature optima. This
distinction is important because psychrotolerant fungi
generally have wider geographic distributions than strict
psychrophiles.

The study of snow mold fungi contributes to understanding of
[[fungal-responses-extreme-environments]] and provides models
for how fungi adapt to and exploit cold environments. Their
ability to cause disease at low temperatures distinguishes
them from most other cold-adapted fungi, which are typically
saprobic.

## Ecological Context

Snow molds interact with other components of cold-ecosystem
fungal communities. In forest soils, psychrophilic fungi
(predominantly Mortierella and Mucor species) differ
completely from communities obtained at standard incubation
temperatures (Carreiro and Koske 1992). Snow molds represent
a specialized component of these cold-adapted communities,
occupying the niche of low-temperature plant pathogen within
a broader matrix of saprobic and [[mycorrhizal-diversity-assessment]]
interactions.
