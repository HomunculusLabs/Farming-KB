## The Three Sisters

The classic companion planting example used by native peoples of the Americas:
- **Corn** provides a support structure for beans
- **Beans** fix nitrogen in the soil
- **Squash** shades the ground, preventing weeds

Together they produce much more food per square foot than grown separately.

## Apple Tree Guild Example

Multiple rings of plants under the apple tree canopy:
1. **Base:** Fava beans (legume, nitrogen fixer)
2. **Groundcover:** Clover with dandelions and chicory
3. **Middle ring:** Comfrey, artichoke, yarrow, nasturtiums, dill
4. **Outer ring:** Daffodils (stop grass encroachment)

Each plant serves a purpose: attracting bees, providing mulch, fixing nitrogen, hindering grass.

## Strategic Plant Placement

Plants can be placed to:
- **Attract predators:** Provide food/shelter to friendly insects that eat pests
- **Sacrifice themselves:** Attract pests away from valuable crops
- **Trap pests:** Attract and kill or trap pests (avoid creating winter pest habitat)
- **Provide nutrients:** Fix nitrogen, create friendly bacteria, or be cut as mulch
- **Create shelter:** Prevent frost, stop wind, make mulch, create microclimates

## Animal Integration

- **Pigs/birds foraging:** Clean up fallen fruit, simultaneously fertilizing
- **Insect control birds:** Attracted with flowers/herbs that attract insects, which attract birds that eat larvae/eggs from tree bark
- **Duck slug control:** Allow ducks in garden fall-spring for total slug/snail control; they stay in the marsh in summer
- **Predator control:** Dogs raised with chickens will protect poultry from foxes, deer, rabbits

## Coaction Study

A systematic method to determine which species work well together:

1. When Species A and B are placed together, they affect each other positively (+), negatively (-), or not at all (0)
2. If A benefits and B is harmed, A is a parasite — avoid this pairing
3. Observe each species in a community relative to others
4. Track results over time (numbers of healthy plants, not just +/-)
5. Put species together so each one benefits from something
6. Remove any species that negatively affects others

## Companion Planting Table

Key groups:
- **Nightshade:** tomato, potato, pepper, eggplant
- **Brassica:** cabbage, broccoli, kohlrabi, brussels sprouts, cauliflower
- **Fruit tree:** apple, peach, pear, etc.

Common beneficial companions: calendula, chives, dill, nasturtium, marigold, comfrey, yarrow, borage, basil.

Common antagonistic pairs: tomatoes + potatoes, beans + onions, cabbage + strawberries, fennel + most vegetables.

## Succession Timing

Some companions must be grown in succession. Corn must be planted before beans and squash, or beans grow too quickly and knock corn over. Pay attention to growing times and use observation — keep track of what grows well together and what doesn't in your specific conditions.

## Variety Selection Criteria

1. **Adaptation to Region:** Search local nurseries, gardens, town plantings, and older homesteads to list species yielding in region.

2. **Resistance to Disease:** Select older hardy varieties showing little effect of fungal or insect attack, enabling sound fruit yields without dangerous chemical biocides.

3. **Site Selection:** Well-drained, sunfacing slopes in cool areas; shaded slopes where late frosts are expected (reduces freeze/thaw damage).

4. **Plant Guilds:** See below.

## Plant Guilds for Pome and Stone Fruit Orchards

The enemy of deciduous orchards is **grass** — non-grass crops below tree canopies are ideal.

### Guild Components

- **Spring Bulbs** (Narcissus, hyacinth, Allium trigetrum): Flower and die back by early summer, creating grass-free zones plus bulb, flower, and honey crops. Iris and tuberous-rooted flowers also assist grass control.

- **Spike Roots** (comfrey, dandelion, globe artichoke): Cover ground, encourage worms, yield mulch and crop. Soil below their foliage is soft, free-draining, open to surface roots, and cool.

- **Insectary Plants** (Umbelliferae, small-flowered plants): Fennel, dill, Queen Anne's lace, tansy, carrot, and parsnip flowers attract tachynid/predatory wasps, robber flies, ladybirds, jewel beetles, and pollinator bees. Windbreak species: Quillaja, Photinia, Tamarix, Acacia.

- **Herb Layer:** Catnip, fennel, dill (any Umbelliferae), small daisy varieties (Compositae), Phyla nodiflora, and flowering ground covers attract wasps, pollinators, and insectivorous birds.

- **Nitrogen and Nutrient Crops:** Clovers, tagasaste, Acacia provide root-level nitrogen. Crotalaria controls root nematodes; marigolds fumigate soil.

- **Frog/Lizard Habitat:** Small ponds or troughs throughout orchards breed frogs for leaf insect control. Nasturtium prevents soil drying and provides mulch.

## Pest Reduction Strategy

Pest species in a well-designed orchard can be reduced to **4-7% of monocrop orchard levels** through:

1. Selection of disease-resistant stock
2. Minimal or no pruning
3. Predation by birds, frogs, lizards, wasps, and predatory insects
4. Interplant of leguminous trees and non-main-crop species
5. Reduced crop stress with windbreak and mulch
6. Ground foraging by chickens, pigs, geese to clean up windfalls

## Animals in the Orchard

- **Chickens** at 120-240/ha (70-100/acre) scavenge windfalls, control codlin moth and fruit fly to insignificant proportions
- **Pigs** introduced when trees are 3-7 years old to handle windfall fruits that breed pests
- **Sheep** and later **controlled cattle grazing** permitted in standard-pruned orchards of 7-20 years
- **Trees 10+ years** are far less susceptible to grass competition
