# Strain Senescence and Degeneration in Mushroom Cultivation

Strain senescence is the progressive loss of vigor and fruiting
ability in mushroom mycelium as cell divisions accumulate over
successive transfers. Understanding this phenomenon is critical
for every cultivator who depends on reliable, productive strains.
As Paul Stamets notes, every sexually reproducing organism on
this planet is limited in the number of its cell replications.
Without further recombination of genes, cell lines decline in
vigor and eventually die.

## The Nature of Senescence

Senescence in mushrooms operates through a mechanism that
remains poorly understood despite being frequently encountered
by cultivators. The onset of senescence is affected by DNA
rearrangements of a discontinuous mitochondrial gene. Mushrooms,
like every sexually reproducing organism, can generate only a
limited number of cell divisions before vitality falters. Signs
of a strain dying include the inability to colonize substrate,
produce [[growing-gourmet-mycelial-morphology-classification-agar-media]]
changes from desirable rhizomorphic forms to cottony (tomentose)
formations. The disappearance of rhizomorphs is an indication
of loss of vigor. When a rhizomorphic mycelium degenerates
with age, tomentose formations typically take over.

## Species Susceptibility

Different species show varying susceptibility to senescence.
Morels seem particularly susceptible to degeneration. Morchella
angusticeps loses its ability to form micro-sclerotia in as few
as 6 or 7 plate transfers from the original tissue culture.
Many strains of Morels, Shiitake, and King Stropharia express
mutations when transferred on media for more than 10 petri
dishes.

## Cryogenic Preservation

For long-term preservation, cryogenic storage under liquid
nitrogen is the best method. Liquid nitrogen storage vessels
are commonly held at -302 F (-150 C). Test tube slants filled
with specially designed cryoprotectant media (10% glycerol
and dextrose) help the mycelium survive the shock of sudden
temperature change. A slow, controlled cooling rate of -1
degree C per minute results in higher survival rates than
sudden immersion.
