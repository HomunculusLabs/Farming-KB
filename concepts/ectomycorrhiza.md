# Ectomycorrhiza Ectomycorrhiza (ECM) is a form of symbiotic association between certain soil fungi and the roots of woody plants, primarily trees and shrubs in temperate, boreal, and some tropical forests. Unlike arbuscular mycorrhizae, ECM fungi do not penetrate root cells but instead form a sheath around the root tip and a network between root cells, creating an interface for nutrient exchange that is critical for forest ecosystem function. [[ectomycorrhizal-associations]] have evolved independently in multiple fungal
lineages, primarily within the Basidiomycota and Ascomycota. Approximately
7,000 to 10,000 fungal species are estimated to form ECM associations, though
the majority do not produce visible fruiting bodies and can only be detected
through molecular methods such as DNA metabarcoding of soil samples.

Major ECM fungal groups include:

- **Basidiomycota**: Amanita, Boletus, Russula, Lactarius, Cortinarius,
  Suillus, Tricholoma, and Hebeloma. These genera produce the conspicuous
  mushrooms commonly seen in forests and are among the most collected wild
  mushrooms for food.
- **Ascomycota**: Tuber (truffles), Cenococcum, and various other genera,
  many of which produce underground fruiting bodies (hypogeous fungi) that
  rely on animals for spore dispersal.

The diversity of ECM fungi in a forest is often remarkable, with a single tree
hosting dozens to hundreds of different ECM fungal species simultaneously,
each potentially providing different services to the host plant.

## Host Plants

ECM associations are formed primarily with woody plants from several major
families:

- **Pinaceae**: Pines, spruces, firs, larches, and hemlocks. Conifers are
  among the most ECM-dependent plant families, with most species unable to
  survive without their fungal partners.
- **Fagaceae**: Oaks, beeches, and chestnuts. These ecologically dominant
  trees depend on ECM fungi for nutrient acquisition in the acidic soils
  where they typically grow.
- **Betulaceae**: Birches, alders, and hazels. Birch trees are among the
  most promiscuous ECM hosts, forming associations with a wide range of
  fungal partners.
- **Salicaceae**: Willows and poplars. These fast-growing trees form ECM
  associations that contribute to their ability to colonize disturbed sites.
- **Myrtaceae**: Eucalyptus and relatives in Australia and the tropics.
  Eucalyptus ECM associations have been widely studied for their role in
  forestry plantations.
- **Dipterocarpaceae**: Dominant trees in Southeast Asian tropical forests.
  The ECM associations of dipterocarps are among the least studied but most
  ecologically important in tropical ecosystems.

## Nutrient Dynamics

ECTomycorrhizal fungi are particularly effective at accessing nitrogen and
phosphorus from organic matter. Many ECM fungi produce extracellular enzymes
including proteases, chitinases, and phosphatases that break down organic
nitrogen and phosphorus compounds in soil organic matter. This capability
gives ECM trees a competitive advantage on nutrient-poor or acidic soils
where mineral nutrients are scarce.

The [[query-how-does-the-nitrogen-cycle-work-in-a-garden]] in boreal and temperate forests is dominated by ECM
fungi, which serve as the primary pathway for [[symbiotic-fungi-mycorrhizal-nitrogen-transfer-networks]] from soil
organic matter to trees. Without ECM associations, coniferous forests on
nutrient-poor soils would be severely nitrogen-limited and unable to sustain
the productivity levels observed.

## Forest Succession

ECM associations play a key role in forest succession. Pioneer species that
colonize disturbed sites are often [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], while later
successional species in temperate and [[fungal-diversity-boreal-forests-taiga]] tend to be
ectomycorrhizal. This shift influences soil chemistry, [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]], and
the competitive dynamics among tree species. ECM fungi can also inhibit the
establishment of non-mycorrhizal or AM plant species through competitive
exclusion, helping to maintain forest [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]].

## Climate Change Implications

Climate change is expected to significantly impact ECM associations. Warming
temperatures may shift the ranges of both ECM fungi and their host trees,
potentially disrupting established symbiotic partnerships. Drought stress
affects ECM function, while increased atmospheric CO2 may alter carbon
allocation to fungal partners. The response of ECM networks to these changes
is a critical uncertainty in climate-ecosystem models.
