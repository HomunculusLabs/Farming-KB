## Dryland Permaculture Strategies

Dryland permaculture encompasses a diverse toolkit of earthworks, water harvesting, biological management, and settlement design techniques developed for arid and semi-arid environments. These strategies prioritize water conservation, soil building, and the creation of microclimates that enable productive systems in regions with limited and unpredictable rainfall.

Bill Mollison documented numerous field-tested examples from Australia, the Americas, Nepal, and the Canary Islands demonstrating that even the harshest drylands can support abundant life when appropriate design principles are applied. The key insight is that water scarcity is not an absolute barrier to productivity — it is a design constraint that can be addressed through systematic observation and intervention.

## Swales in Dryland Systems

Swales are level trenches excavated along contour lines to capture, slow, and infiltrate surface runoff. In drylands, they serve as primary water-harvesting infrastructure, transforming ephemeral rainfall into deep-soil moisture reserves. Unlike diversion drains, swales hold water on-site, allowing it to percolate vertically into the soil profile where it is protected from evaporation.

### Documented Installations

- **Koonibba, South Australia**: Swales constructed on Aboriginal land demonstrated rapid establishment of productive vegetation. Within six months, watermelon, sorghum, corn, and cucurbits were thriving on the swale banks, while taro grew successfully in the water-collecting base — a remarkable result in an arid environment where conventional agriculture would fail entirely.

- **Pumpenbil, New South Wales**: Swale construction validated the technique's effectiveness across varied Australian dryland contexts, showing consistent improvement in soil moisture profiles and vegetation response even on relatively poor soils.

- **Village Homes, Davis, California**: The most cited residential-scale example. Swales installed throughout this planned community created a progressively deepening soil-stored water reservoir that converted surrounding desert into productive landscape. Over years of operation, cumulative swale-based infiltration raised the local water table and enabled lush gardens in formerly arid ground.

### Design Principles

Swale spacing must account for slope, soil type, and expected rainfall intensity. The goal is to ensure even moderate rainfall events are fully captured and infiltrated rather than lost to runoff. On gentle slopes, swales may be spaced 10–30 meters apart; steeper terrain requires closer spacing to prevent concentrated overland flow from exceeding swale capacity. The excavated soil is placed on the downslope side to form a planted berm.

## Gabions and Floodway Stabilization

Gabions are wire cages or baskets filled with rocks, used to stabilize watercourses, [[query-how-do-i-garden-on-a-slope-and-prevent-erosion]], and shape floodwater flow. They are particularly valuable in drylands where infrequent but intense rainfall produces destructive flash floods capable of reshaping entire landscapes in hours.

### Applications

- **Alice Springs, Australia**: Gabions installed along floodways successfully stabilized channels that would otherwise erode rapidly during storm events, protecting infrastructure and allowing vegetation establishment.

- **Charikot, Nepal**: Gabions used to arrest gully erosion on hillsides, preventing loss of valuable topsoil and enabling re-vegetation of degraded slopes in a monsoon-driven erosion environment.

### Hard Banks and Scour Holes

Hard banks — compacted earth or rock structures — can be placed to constrict flood water flow deliberately. This constriction accelerates water velocity through a narrow section, creating scour holes downstream that serve as deep-water infiltration points. The technique converts destructive flood energy into a beneficial water-storage mechanism, turning erosion from a problem into a design tool that recharges groundwater.

## Greywater Gardens

In areas with brick-hard, impermeable soils, conventional infiltration of greywater is impossible. The Koonibba Aboriginal gardeners developed an ingenious solution: absorption trenches lined with plastic sheeting and backfilled with organic matter and manure. This created a contained, fertile medium that could absorb and process household greywater, supporting productive gardens in ground that would otherwise be completely unworkable. The technique demonstrates how local knowledge combined with permaculture design overcomes severe site limitations through creative adaptation of available materials.

## Range Management and Animal Integration

### Goat Management

Goats present both challenges and opportunities in dryland systems. Free-ranging goats severely reduce ground cover through selective and intensive browsing, accelerating desertification by removing the vegetation that retains soil moisture. However, tethered goats can be strategically employed as a land-preparation tool. By tethering goats over specific sites — particularly in granite outcrop areas — their concentrated manure and urine create nutrient-rich niches that become ideal planting positions for orchard trees, converting barren rock into productive agricultural land over time.

### Native Grass Restoration

Indian rice grass (*Oryzopsis hymenoides*) is a native dryland species that served as a staple grain for Shoshone peoples. Re-seeding programs focused on this species can restore both ecological function and food production capacity to degraded rangelands. The grass is drought-tolerant, highly nutritious, and well-adapted to the soil and climate of interior drylands, making it ideal for permaculture-based range restoration.

### Wildlife Capacity and Shelter

Wallaroo cave shelters play a significant role in determining wildlife capacity in arid landscapes. Shelter availability often limits population size more than food availability. Understanding these refuge relationships is critical for sustainable wildlife management in drylands where thermal stress is a constant threat.

## Guano Harvesting

Guano — accumulated bird or bat excrement — is an extraordinarily rich source of phosphorus, nitrogen, and trace minerals. In dryland systems where soil fertility is often the primary limiting factor after water, guano harvested from tree roosts provides a concentrated, locally-sourced fertilizer. Trees serving as communal roosting sites can be managed as ongoing fertilizer production systems, with periodic harvesting for application to gardens and orchards. This approach integrates animal habitat management directly with agricultural fertility cycles.

## Arroyo Floodwater Harvesting

Arroyos — dry desert washes carrying water only during and after rain events — represent significant water resources that can be captured despite their ephemeral nature. Effective arroyo harvesting systems incorporate:

- **Concrete barriers** placed across the channel to slow water flow and encourage infiltration into streambed sediments
- **Trash screens** installed upstream to prevent debris from clogging storage infrastructure during flood events
- **Galeria tunnels** — horizontal underground collection tunnels — that intercept infiltrated water and store it in the subsurface profile, protected from evaporation by overlying earth

These systems transform brief, violent flood pulses into long-term groundwater reserves accessible through wells or galeria tunnels, providing reliable water supplies far beyond individual storm events.

## Cave Dwellings in Volcanic Tuff

The Canary Islands provide an outstanding example of vernacular dryland architecture: cave houses carved into volcanic tuff (welded ash fall deposits). Tuff is soft enough to excavate by hand but structurally stable, thermally massive, and naturally insulating. Cave dwellings maintain remarkably constant internal temperatures regardless of external conditions — cool in extreme heat, warm during cold nights. This approach eliminates energy-intensive heating and cooling and demonstrates how geological materials can directly serve shelter needs in dryland climates with minimal manufacturing or transport costs.

## Windstorm and Dust Management

[[dryland-settlements-and-interceptor-banks]] frequently suffer from windstorm dust problems. Wind erosion of exposed soil generates airborne particulates that degrade air quality, damage infrastructure, and create serious respiratory health hazards. Mitigation strategies include:

- **Street paving** to eliminate exposed dirt surfaces serving as dust sources within settlements
- **Street re-alignment** perpendicular to prevailing wind directions to reduce channeling effects
- **Vegetation belts** and windbreaks at settlement perimeters to intercept and slow wind

Settlement planning in drylands must account for prevailing wind patterns from the outset, as retrofitting wind management after construction is far more costly than designing for it initially.

## Soil and Plant Nutrition: The Periodic Table

Mollison emphasizes that effective dryland permaculture requires understanding the full range of elements relevant to soil, plant, and animal nutrition. The periodic table provides the organizing framework:

- **Macronutrients**: Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sulfur (S)
- **Micronutrients**: Iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), boron (B), molybdenum (Mo), chlorine (Cl)
- **Trace elements**: Cobalt (Co), selenium (Se), iodine (I), and others essential for animal and human health

In dryland soils, many elements are present but unavailable due to high pH, low organic matter, or mineral imbalances. Permaculture addresses this through biological cycling — deep-rooted plants, microbial associations, and animal integration solubilize and redistribute minerals from subsoil to root zone. Understanding which elements are limiting allows targeted interventions rather than generic, wasteful approaches.

## Integration and Design Synthesis

The power of dryland permaculture lies not in any single technique but in their integrated application. Swales capture water that gabions protect from erosion. Greywater gardens supplement rainfall harvesting. Goats prepare planting sites that guano fertilizes. Cave dwellings reduce energy demand while wind management protects the food-producing landscape.

Together, these strategies form coherent systems that increase in resilience and productivity over time, turning the fundamental challenges of dryland environments — scarcity of water, fertility, and shelter — into the organizing principles of sustainable design. The periodic table reminds us that no element is wasted in a well-designed system; every material flow can be captured and redirected toward productive ends.
