# Dryland Permaculture Strategies

**Updated:** 2026-04-18

Dryland permaculture encompasses strategies for designing productive systems in arid and semi-arid regions where water is the primary limiting factor. These strategies focus on water harvesting, soil protection, drought-adapted species, and energy-efficient design to create resilient landscapes despite harsh conditions.

## The Dryland Challenge

In drylands, the primary design problem is water scarcity. Rainfall is low (typically under 500mm annually) and often arrives in brief, intense storms. Evaporation rates are high, soils are often poor, and vegetation is sparse. Yet drylands support billions of people and have been home to sophisticated agricultural civilizations for millennia.

The key insight is that in drylands, water is the master variable -- every other design decision flows from water management.

## Core Strategies

### Water Harvesting
The first priority in drylands is to capture every drop of water that falls on the site:

- **Swales on contour:** Shallow ditches and berms on contour slow, spread, and sink runoff water into the soil. Even small swales significantly increase infiltration in arid landscapes
- **Micro-catchments:** Small earthworks (half-moons, V-shaped bunds, zai holes) around individual plants concentrate rainfall
- **Rooftop catchment:** Collect rainwater from buildings into tanks or cisterns for supplemental irrigation
- **Rock mulch catchment:** Rock piles concentrate dew and funnel water to plant roots
- **Floodwater harvesting:** Divert seasonal flood flows from wadis into infiltration basins or agricultural fields
- **Sand dams:** In seasonal riverbeds, sand dams store water within sand, protecting it from evaporation

### Soil Protection
Exposed soil in drylands is quickly lost to wind and water erosion and loses organic matter rapidly:

- **Rock mulch:** Stones protect soil from sun and wind, reduce evaporation, and radiate heat at night (frost protection). Desert plants often grow best under or beside rocks
- **Organic mulch:** Where available, wood chips, straw, or compost reduce evaporation and build soil
- **Living ground covers:** Hardy low-growing plants protect soil surface
- **Minimize bare soil:** Every square foot of bare soil is losing water and organic matter

### Wind Control
Hot drying winds dramatically increase evaporation:

- **Windbreaks:** Trees and shrubs on the windward side of gardens and homes reduce wind speed and create sheltered microclimates
- **Hedgerows:** Dense plantings that filter wind rather than blocking it entirely
- **Fences and walls:** Temporary or permanent structures for immediate wind protection

### Shade and Microclimate Creation
Direct sun accelerates evaporation and stresses plants:

- **Nurse trees:** Establish fast-growing trees first to create shade for slower-growing species
- **Partial shade structures:** Pergolas, shade cloth, or lattice provide relief during hottest periods
- **East-west orientation:** Plant rows and structures to maximize morning/afternoon shade
- **Thermal mass walls:** Stone or earth walls absorb heat during day and release at night, moderating temperature extremes

## Plant Strategies

### Drought-Adapted Species
- **Deep-rooted perennials:** Access water far below the soil surface
- **Succulents:** Store water in leaves, stems, or roots (agave, aloe, prickly pear)
- **Phreatophytes:** Plants that grow near permanent water tables
- **Native species:** Always include locally adapted native plants

### Key Dryland Genera
- **Legumes for nitrogen fixation:** Acacia, Prosopis (mesquite), Cassia, Carob, Cercidium
- **Fruit trees:** Date palm, fig, pomegranate, olive, jojoba, citrus (in milder drylands)
- **Multipurpose trees:** Mesquite (pods for food/fodder, wood, nitrogen fixation), Neem
- **Forage plants:** Atriplex (saltbush), spineless cactus, Leucaena

### Planting Techniques
- **Waffle gardens:** Depressed square beds with raised edges to concentrate water
- **Zai holes:** Planting pits filled with organic matter that capture and hold water
- **Pot planting:** Plant in buried porous pots that slowly release water to roots
- **Deep planting:** Plant seeds and seedlings deeper than normal to access moisture

## Animal Integration
- **Goats:** Excellent browsers of drought-adapted shrubs; provide milk, meat, and fiber
- **Chickens:** Can be integrated with careful water management
- **Bees:** Many dryland plants are excellent nectar sources; bees require relatively little water
- **Rabbits:** Efficient converters of forage to protein; manure for gardens

## Design Layout
In drylands, the classic permaculture zone system is modified:
- Zone 1 (intensive garden): Small, close to house, protected, water-intensive
- Zone 2 (orchard): Moderate water needs, wind-protected, mulched
- Zone 3 (field crops): Minimal irrigation, drought-adapted species
- Zone 4 (grazing/forage): Extensive, minimal intervention
- Zone 5 (wilderness): Leave undisturbed to protect watershed and biodiversity

## Seasonal Strategies
- **Wet season:** Maximize water capture; sow seeds of annuals; establish new plantings
- **Dry season:** Conserve water; protect soil; harvest from established perennials
- **Hot season:** Provide shade; minimize evaporation; avoid disturbing soil

## Traditional Dryland Agriculture

Many indigenous dryland agriculture systems offer valuable models:
- **Nabatean water harvesting:** Ancient desert farmers in the Negev captured runoff from large areas to irrigate terraced fields
- **Mesoamerican milpas:** Corn, beans, and squash adapted to seasonal drylands
- **Australian Aboriginal fire management:** Fire-stick farming to promote new growth and manage water
