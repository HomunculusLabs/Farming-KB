# Approaches to Sampling Macrofungi

Sampling macrofungi presents unique methodological challenges compared to sampling plants or animals. This concept page covers the major approaches, their strengths and limitations, and key considerations for designing robust fungal surveys.

## Inventorying Versus Monitoring

### Inventory Surveys

Inventory surveys aim to increase knowledge of fungal diversity, habitat preferences, and geographic distributions. They serve conservation prioritization and assessment of land management impacts. Inventories document what species are present in an area, providing baseline data for future comparisons.

### Monitoring Programs

Monitoring surveys assess the effects of commercial harvest, long-term population trends, and pollutant impacts on fungal populations. Monitoring requires baseline inventory data before trends can be meaningfully interpreted.

Monitoring differs from inventory in several key ways:

- Designed to track population trends rather than overall species richness
- Conducted over longer time periods
- Typically focuses on fewer taxa, often selected as indicator species
- Requires more sites for statistical power
- Demands archiving and careful documentation to enable repetition over 20+ years

### Indicator Species Selection

Pearson (1994) established eight criteria for selecting indicator species:

1. Taxonomically stable and well-known
2. Well-known natural history
3. Readily surveyed and manipulated
4. Sensitive to disturbances
5. Component of a widely distributed higher taxon
6. Habitat specificity
7. Economic value
8. Distributions correlated with other groups

**Macrofungi as indicator organisms:** Macrofungi are of intermediate value as indicators, meeting approximately 5 of the 8 criteria. Their limitations include poor taxonomic knowledge, difficulty of survey, and poorly understood natural histories. However, they are sensitive to human disturbances (air pollution, logging, hurricanes, volcanic eruptions, landslides), include globally distributed genera as well as locally endemic species, encompass economically valuable taxa, and influence many other organisms.

### Challenges of Monitoring Macrofungi

Key drawbacks include limited taxonomic expertise, pronounced annual fluctuations in fruiting, and a lack of well-tested protocols. Annual variation is particularly problematic: species may not fruit for years but remain present as dormant propagules. Notably, there is low correlation between sporocarp-based and PCR-based estimates of ectomycorrhizal diversity (Gardes and Bruns 1996; Peter et al. 2001), indicating that above-ground fruiting surveys may poorly reflect actual fungal community composition.

## Inventorying Macrofungi

### Quantification Difficulties

Quantifying macrofungal populations is inherently difficult. Individual mycelia may produce anywhere from one to many sporocarps, and sporocarp sizes, numbers, and dry weights vary considerably. Sporocarps represent an unknown fraction of total fungal biomass, making direct population estimates unreliable.

### Traditional Approach

The traditional approach involves walking through an area and collecting conspicuous specimens in an opportunistic manner. While this is simple and accessible, it does not allow rigorous comparisons between sites, times, or studies.

### Plot and Transect Sampling

Plot/transect sampling ensures all taxa are scrutinized, reducing the likelihood of overlooking [[cryptic-species]]. In the first year, specimens may be identified only to genus, but repeated sampling over years enables species-level identification.

The best abundance measure for macrofungi is **plot frequency** — the number of subplots in which a taxon occurs. This reflects the minimum area occupied by the species. However, plot frequency provides only a rough estimate because multiple sporocarps may arise from one or several mycelia (Jacobson et al. 1993; Dahlberg and Stenlid 1994). The size, shape, and spacing of sampling units are all important design aspects that influence results.

## Types of Sampling Units

### Natural Sampling Units

Natural sampling units are discrete, observable substrata such as individual leaves, logs, or cones. These are particularly useful for fungi with narrow substrate specificity.

### Arbitrary Sampling Units (Plots)

Plots are arbitrary sampling units used for decomposers of litter/humus and for [[mycorrhizal-fungi]], which lack discrete substrata. Plots range from 1 m² to 1000 m² and may be square, rectangular, or circular.

**Moving plots** are relocated each sampling period. They avoid disturbance effects from repeated visits but conflate annual variation with spatial variation due to the patchy distributions of many fungi.

**Permanent plots** remain in fixed locations. They provide good diversity estimates, annual phenology data, and are easy to relocate, but may underestimate diversity across a larger area.

## Sampling Vegetative Mycelia

Beyond sporocarps, researchers can sample sclerotia, mycorrhizae, mycelia, mycelial strands, cords, and rhizomorphs. Molecular analyses of ectomycorrhizae have revealed that many species rarely or never produce above-ground sporocarps (Gardes and Bruns 1996). Standard methods for sampling ectomycorrhizae are described in Johnson et al. (2000). Additionally, humid chambers can encourage fruiting of some ascomycetes and small agarics, providing a bridge between vegetative and sporocarp-based detection.

## Convenience Versus Plot Sampling

### Convenience Sampling

In convenience sampling, experienced individuals visit a location and document all taxa they encounter. This approach is traditional among taxonomists and is efficient for preliminary estimates and rapid biodiversity assessments. For example, at Barlow Pass, Washington, an amateur mycological society made weekly visits over 2 years (Ammirati et al. 1994), accumulating substantial species lists. Convenience sampling may detect more large and showy taxa than plot sampling, though this remains an untested hypothesis.

**Drawbacks:** Precludes rigorous comparisons between sites or over time, lacks standardization of effort, and collector bias may cause certain habitats or taxa to be overlooked.

### Plot Sampling

Plot sampling provides quantitative data through standardized area or time. It ensures inconspicuous taxa are collected and the imposed scrutiny typically detects more species than convenience approaches.

## Dispersed Subsamples

Using contiguous subplots is problematic because of **spatial autocorrelation** (Murakami 1987): adjacent subplots are more likely to contain the same species, leading to underestimates of total richness. This can be addressed by dispersing plots across the study area (Luoma et al. 1991; Lodge and Cantrell 1995a; O'Dell et al. 1999; Mueller and Mata 2000).

More spatially explicit studies are needed, as saprobic and ectomycorrhizal species may have fundamentally different distribution patterns. Walker and Ammirati (unpublished) found that saprobic species-area curves leveled off sooner than ectomycorrhizal curves in Sitka spruce stands. However, Schmit et al. (1999) found no difference in species-effort curve shape among ecological guilds in an oak forest, suggesting that guild-specific patterns may depend on the ecosystem studied.

## Adaptive Sampling

In adaptive sampling, effort is modified in response to observations during the survey. When a threshold value for target organisms is reached, additional sampling is triggered in neighboring areas. This approach is particularly useful for rare and patchily distributed organisms (Thompson 1992). Statistical methods exist to correct for bias introduced by unequal sampling effort (Thompson 1992; Thompson and Seber 1996).

## Determining Adequate Sampling

Deciding on total area, plot size, number and distribution of plots, and sampling frequency is not trivial. A common rule states that sampling is adequate when every taxon occurs in at least two sampling units — but this is extremely difficult, if not impossible, to achieve for macrofungi given their sporadic fruiting patterns.

### Species-Effort Curves

Species-effort (or species-area) curves plot cumulative species against sampling effort. Species counts increase steeply at first and then level off. Macrofungi generally require larger areas than plants to approach an asymptote. Arnolds (1992) recommended 1000 m² plots in forests and 500 m² in grasslands, with a minimum of 5 plots per community type.

### Cumulative Species Over Years

Long-term studies reveal that species lists continue growing well beyond initial sampling years:

- Ammirati and O'Dell (unpublished): continuous increase over 5 years for mycorrhizal fungi
- Schmit et al. (1999): increase over 3 years for both saprobic and mycorrhizal fungi
- Straatsma et al. (2001): new species detected each year for 21 consecutive years

A minimum inventory duration of **10 years** is desirable. Sampling design should be iterative, with early data informing modifications to later efforts.

### Complementarity

Complementarity measures the proportion of unique species between two samples. The index is defined as C_jk = U_jk / S_jk (Colwell and Coddington 1994), where U_jk is the number of species unique to one sample and S_jk is the total number of species across both samples. Efficient sampling is achieved when complementarity is approximately 0.5. Lodge and Cantrell (1995a) found that 12 × 1-m² plots in each of two blocks was an efficient design for tropical litter agarics.

## Extrapolation to Total Richness

Extrapolation methods estimate total richness based on the proportion of rare species (those encountered in only 1–2 samples):

### First-Order Jackknife

```
S* = S_obs + L[(n − 1) / n]
```

Where S_obs is the observed number of species, L is the number of species found in exactly one sample, and n is the number of samples.

### Chao's Second Estimator (Chao 2)

```
S* = S_obs + (L² / 2M)
```

Where L is the number of species found in exactly one sample and M is the number found in exactly two samples. Chao 2 is simple and generally reliable for small sample numbers, and it highlights the contribution of rare taxa to total diversity. However, it can produce infinite richness estimates when no species are found in exactly two plots.

### Reliability Concerns

Schmit et al. (1999) found that none of the current extrapolation techniques were robust for their macrofungal data, underscoring the continued need for methodological development in [[fungal-biodiversity]] assessment.
