# Food Systems, Distribution and Local Self-Reliance

[[david-holmgren]]'s analysis of food systems addresses the industrial food
chain, food miles, local production, and the relationship between
self-reliance and marketplace diversity. He argues that energy descent
will fundamentally reconfigure how food is produced and distributed.

## The Industrial Food Chain

Modern agriculture has moved down the "industrial food chain,"
resulting in explosive development of processing, transport and retail
systems. The application of self-reliance to the food supply has
enormous implications for diversity within production systems. In
high-energy systems, marketplace diversity provides for people's needs
even though production systems feeding those markets are almost all
monocultures.

By extending supply lines across bioregional boundaries and even
continents, marketplace diversity has increased dramatically. But this
diversity is fragile, depending on cheap energy and subsidised
transport. Long-distance, high-speed refrigerated transport of perishable
food has destroyed one of the last links to seasonal patterns in ordinary
people's lives.

## Food Miles and Local Production

At Melliodora, home-produced food combined with infrequent bulk purchase
dramatically reduces "food miles" and speed. Perishable food brought
from great distances demands fast transport. Production of perishable
foods from gardens adjacent to housing is listed among the small-scale,
minimal-movement alternatives associated with Permaculture.

## Gardening as Serious Agriculture

Holmgren argues that gardening must be regarded as a serious form of
agriculture, and that a major increase in skilled food-growers who can
feed their families and neighbours from food gardens is essential. If
garden agriculture is to begin replacing central market systems in
supplying fresh produce before energy and food costs force these changes,
the diversity of yields possible will be a key factor in success.

One sobering reality is that not even the Garden of Eden could provide
the diversity of ingredients that many people have grown accustomed to
in the fridge and supermarket culture. Therefore, gardening strategies
that maximise diversity of yield will be more successful in weaning
people from high-energy dependencies.

## Diversity of Yield and Garden Strategy

Gardening strategies that maximise diversity of yield will generally be
more successful in alluring jaded consumers away from dependencies on
high-energy systems. Much of what is called traditional gardens is small
versions of market gardens that produce a handful of varieties in great
abundance but with little variation. The popularity of "Permaculture
gardens" over more traditional food gardens may reflect the appeal of
diversity.

Similarly, the rapidly increasing popularity of heritage vegetable
varieties can be attributed to politics and the search for novelty as
much as practical need, but it is having the effect of maintaining
diversity abandoned by agribusiness. For the urban and landless poor in
developing countries, garden diversity can mean the difference between
bare survival and well-being.

## Subscription Farming and Barter

The enthusiasm for home-grown food has increased awareness of, and
support for, local commercial production on a modest scale through
subscription farming. This is a modest example of overshoot and
rebalancing in action. For many people, a home garden, a local market,
a subscription farm or barter with friends, plus some purchases of
organic produce from the central market system, can satisfy needs and
provide greater fresh food security.

## Cuba as a Model

The remarkable growth of urban and organic agriculture in Cuba since
the collapse of the Soviet Union and the US economic blockade provides
one of the best models for redevelopment of diverse fresh food
production to meet local needs. Although relatively poor, Cuba is
quite urbanised and has a long history of modern market-based
agriculture.

## Food Chain Hierarchy

In nature, long food chains and webs involve transformation of solar
energy into more concentrated forms. A simple Australian example
illustrates energy flow relationships:

- Reducing energy and mass contributed along the food chain
- Increasing power and value per unit energy and mass along the food
  chain
- Species high in the food chain encourage life of species lower in the
  chain

Holmgren notes that standing plant biomass, standing animal biomass,
and length of food chains are key indicators of biological productivity.

## Distribution of Surplus

The permaculture ethic of redistributing surplus is a major feature of
traditional societies. Volunteer work, philanthropy, and support for
activism and organisations with no strong marketplace value are current
expressions of this redistribution. The tripartite distribution of energy
(from Odum's framework) shows how energy flows between competitive,
shared, and cooperative uses in any system.

## Self-Reliance Fosters Local Markets

Holmgren argues that home food production has tended to foster, rather
than compete with, small commercial organic growers serving local
markets. Self-reliance works as an invisible consumer boycott,
undermining centralised systems while stimulating new local forms of
economic activity.
