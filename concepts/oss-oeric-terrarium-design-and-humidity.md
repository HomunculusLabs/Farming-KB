# Oss & Oeric: Terrarium Design and Humidity Control

Maintaining proper environmental conditions during the fruiting
stage is critical for successful mushroom cultivation. The 1976
guide by Oss & Oeric describes several approaches to creating
high-humidity growing environments using readily available materials.
The authors emphasize that humidity control is essential at every
stage of cultivation, from mycelial incubation through fruiting.

## Incubation Boxes for Mycelial Growth

During the colonization phase (both on agar and on rye grain),
the authors recommend using styrofoam boxes obtained from pet
stores or tropical fish dealers. These boxes are modified by
cutting a window into the lid and sealing clear plastic vinyl
over the opening with silicone marine glue. The completed boxes
serve as modular, insulated incubation chambers that retain the
heat generated by actively growing mycelium.

The mycelium gives off heat as it grows, and the styrofoam
construction helps retain this metabolic heat, creating a self-
incubating environment. This is particularly important for
maintaining the recommended temperature range of 70-80 degrees F
during rye grain colonization. The window allows visual monitoring
of colony progress without opening the box and introducing
contaminants.

## Fruiting Chambers for Cased Jars

After the casing step, the jars require a high-humidity
environment to support mushroom formation and development. The
authors recommend large styrofoam coolers with windows cut into
the lids and covered with clear or translucent polyethylene
sheeting. This creates a greenhouse-like environment that
maintains the high relative humidity needed for sustained
fruiting across multiple flushes.

The design is intentionally simple and accessible. Styrofoam
coolers were readily available, inexpensive, and provided
excellent insulation against temperature fluctuations. The
clear plastic window allows ambient light to reach the cultures,
which the authors found beneficial for proper mushroom
development.

## Humidity Maintenance

The primary method of humidity maintenance is daily fine-mist
spraying. Each cased jar requires approximately 2-3 good squirts
of water per day to compensate for moisture lost through
evaporation. The authors stress using a fine-mist spray to avoid
sealing the surface of the casing soil, which would impede air
exchange and mycelial respiration.

A key concept is "field capacity" for the casing soil. The soil
should be moistened thoroughly throughout but not saturated.
The test is that no water should pass through the casing soil
into the mycelium below. A properly maintained casing surface
should feel moist and spongy to the touch.

## Watering Guidelines and Pitfalls

The authors describe several important indicators for watering
adjustments. If jars are underwatered, the surface mycelium will
turn blue with thirst, providing a visible signal to increase
moisture. If overwatered, water passes through the casing into
the grain below, potentially promoting bacterial contamination
and mycelial rot. The daily spraying also serves to "knock down"
surface mycelial growth that might otherwise overgrow the casing
surface and impede pin formation.

## Lighting Requirements

The authors found that mushrooms respond favorably to light,
with a daily 10-hour Grow-lux fluorescent cycle producing
mushrooms with larger caps and shorter stems compared to those
grown without supplemental lighting. However, they caution
against placing cultures in direct sunlight for prolonged periods,
as approximately equal numbers of mushrooms will grow to half
size or less and then cease growing, producing "aborts."

## Temperature and Air Exchange

Proper aeration is essential throughout all stages. During rye
colonization, jar lids must be left loose enough to allow oxygen
diffusion from the surrounding air. The authors note that the
mushroom mycelium is quite aerobic, and that proper aeration
can even afford the fungus a competitive advantage against
anaerobic contaminants. During fruiting, the semi-permeable
polyethylene lid covering on the cooler provides passive air
exchange while maintaining humidity.

## Modern Terrarium Design Evolution

The basic principles established by Oss & Oeric have been refined
by subsequent cultivators. The key insight from the original design
is that a simple, enclosed, humidified container is sufficient for
fruiting [[psilocybe-cubensis]]. Modern designs have improved on this
with features such as:

**Automated Humidification:** Ultrasonic humidifiers connected to
hygrostat controllers maintain consistent humidity levels without
manual misting. Perlite-based passive humidification is another
popular approach that requires no electricity.

**Fresh Air Exchange (FAE):** The importance of regular air exchange
for proper fruitbody development was underappreciated in 1976. Modern
fruiting chambers incorporate regular FAE through fan-driven
ventilation or manual fanning schedules. High CO2 levels produce
long, stringy stems with small caps, while adequate fresh air
produces more compact, normally proportioned mushrooms.

**[[shotgun-fruiting-chamber]]:** The most widely used modern design
incorporates numerous small holes on all six sides of a plastic
storage container, providing continuous passive air exchange while
a layer of wet perlite on the bottom maintains high humidity. This
design requires no electrical equipment and can be constructed from
commonly available materials for under $20.

**Martha Stewart Grow Tent:** For larger-scale operations, modified
greenhouse tents with automated humidifiers and oscillating fans
provide excellent environmental control for dozens of fruiting
trays or monotubs simultaneously.
