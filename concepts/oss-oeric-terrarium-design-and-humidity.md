# Oss & Oeric: Terrarium Design and Humidity Control

Maintaining proper environmental conditions during the fruiting stage is critical for successful [[mushroom-growing-environments]] using readily available materials. The authors emphasize that humidity control is essential at every stage of cultivation, from mycelial incubation through fruiting.

## Incubation Boxes for Mycelial Growth

During the colonization phase (both on agar and on rye grain), the authors recommend using styrofoam boxes obtained from pet stores or tropical fish dealers. These boxes are modified by cutting a window into the lid and sealing clear plastic vinyl over the opening with silicone marine glue.

The completed boxes serve as modular, insulated incubation chambers that retain the heat generated by actively growing mycelium. The styrofoam construction helps retain metabolic heat, creating a self-incubating environment at the recommended temperature range of 70-80 degrees F during rye grain colonization.

The window allows visual monitoring of colony progress without opening the box and introducing contaminants.

## Fruiting Chambers for Cased Jars

After the casing step, jars require a high-humidity environment to support [[passive-humidity-fruit-chamber-polyethylene-sheeting]], creating a greenhouse-like environment that maintains high relative humidity needed for sustained fruiting across multiple flushes.

The design is intentionally simple and accessible. Styrofoam coolers were readily available, inexpensive, and provided excellent insulation against temperature fluctuations. The clear plastic window allows ambient light to reach the cultures.

## Humidity Maintenance

The primary method of humidity maintenance is daily fine-mist spraying. Each cased jar requires approximately 2-3 good squirts of water per day to compensate for moisture lost through evaporation. The authors stress using a fine-mist spray to avoid sealing the surface of the casing soil, which would impede air exchange and mycelial respiration.

A key concept is "field capacity" for the casing soil. The soil should be moistened thoroughly throughout but not saturated. The test is that no water should pass through the casing soil into the mycelium below. A properly maintained casing surface should feel moist and spongy to the touch.

## Watering Guidelines and Pitfalls

If jars are underwatered, the surface mycelium will turn blue with thirst, providing a visible signal to increase moisture. If overwatered, water passes through the casing into the grain below, potentially promoting bacterial contamination and mycelial rot.

The daily spraying also serves to "knock down" surface mycelium growth that might otherwise overgrow the casing surface and impede pin formation.

## Lighting Requirements

Mushrooms respond favorably to light, with a daily 10-hour Grow-lux fluorescent cycle producing mushrooms with larger caps and shorter stems compared to those grown without supplemental lighting. The authors caution against placing cultures in direct sunlight for prolonged periods, as approximately equal numbers of mushrooms will grow to half size or less and then cease growing, producing "aborts."

## Temperature and Air Exchange

Proper aeration is essential throughout all stages. During rye colonization, jar lids must be left loose enough to allow oxygen diffusion. The mushroom mycelium is quite aerobic, and proper aeration can afford the fungus a competitive advantage against anaerobic contaminants.

During fruiting, the semi-permeable polyethylene lid covering on the cooler provides passive air exchange while maintaining humidity.

## Modern Developments

Modern designs have improved on the original Oss & Oeric approach with automated humidification using ultrasonic humidifiers connected to hygrostat controllers, and perlite-based passive humidification requiring no electricity.

Fresh air exchange (FAE) was underappreciated in 1976 but is now recognized as critical for proper fruitbody development. High CO2 levels produce long, stringy stems with small caps, while adequate fresh air produces compact, normally proportioned mushrooms.

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

Several recurring issues arise with fruiting chamber cultivation:

- **Stalled pinning**: Often caused by insufficient fresh air exchange or
  inadequate temperature fluctuation between day and night cycles. A 5-10°F
  drop in nighttime temperature can trigger pinning in many species
- **Cobweb mold (Dactylium)**: A common contaminant that appears as wispy,
  greyish-white growth on casing surfaces. Increased FAE and light exposure
  help suppress it; severely affected casings should be removed to prevent
  sporulation
- **Overlay**: When casing mycelium overgrows the surface and forms a dense,
  matted layer, it impedes pin formation. Light scraping and increased misting
  can sometimes restore productivity
