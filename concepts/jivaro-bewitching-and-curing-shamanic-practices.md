# Jivaro Bewitching and Curing Shamanic Practices

## Overview

Among the [[jivaro]] of the Ecuadorian Amazon, shamanic practice encompasses two complementary but opposing functions: **bewitching** (sorcery/witchcraft) and **curing** (healing). These practices form a supernatural arms race in which bewitching shamans attack victims by projecting magical darts ([[tsentsak]]) into their bodies, while curing shamans attempt to extract these intruding objects and restore health. The entire system depends on the hallucinogenic brew [[natem]] ([[ayahuasca]]), which enables practitioners to perceive and interact with the supernatural reality underlying ordinary existence.

## The Bewitching Process

### Method of Attack

The bewitching ritual is conducted with careful secrecy. The shaman prepares by drinking natem and green tobacco juice, which enables him to regurgitate a tsentsak from his stomach. He then secretly approaches the house of his intended victim, positioning himself just out of sight in the forest.

When the victim emerges, the shaman throws the tsentsak at them. The outcome depends on the strength of the dart and the force with which it is thrown:

- **Full penetration**: The tsentsak passes completely through the victim's body, causing death within days to weeks. In this case, "there is nothing for a curing shaman to suck out."
- **Embedding**: More commonly, the magical dart lodges within the victim's body, causing illness of varying severity. This is theoretically curable through shamanic extraction.

If the intended victim does not appear, the shaman may bewitch any family member who emerges — typically a wife or child. The bewitcher takes great care to conceal his actions, as the victim must not become aware of the supernatural attack lest they immediately seek the protection of a curing shaman.

### Spirit Helpers in Bewitching

A bewitching shaman can deploy several categories of supernatural allies:

**[[pasuk]] (Chief Spirit Helper)**: The most powerful spirit helper available to a bewitcher. Each shaman possesses only one pasuk, which serves him exclusively. The pasuk has a distinctive defense — it appears to a curing shaman (under natem) as a human-sized figure "covered with iron except for its eyes." Its eyes are its sole vulnerability; a curing shaman can kill it only by shooting a tsentsak directly into them. To ordinary observers, the pasuk typically appears as a tarantula.

The pasuk remains near the victim after the initial bewitchment, positioned to shoot additional tsentsak into the patient if a curing shaman manages to extract the original dart. This makes the pasuk a persistent threat that must be neutralized before a cure can be completed.

**Wakani Birds**: Supernatural birds that any shaman can summon. The shaman blows on the wakani birds and sends them to the victim's house, where they fly around the person repeatedly, causing fear, fever, and ultimately insanity leading to death. The bewitcher can also position a wakani bird near the victim's dwelling as a resupply mechanism: when a curing shaman extracts a tsentsak, the bewitcher sends additional darts to the bird, which fires them from its beak into the patient. This creates a situation where the curer can never permanently rid the patient of magical darts.

**Anamuk (Supernatural Animals)**: Shamans can use tsentsak to create dangerous animals that attack victims. Specific examples include:

- A pointed armadillo bone tsentsak, shot into a river, manifests as an anaconda that overturns the victim's raft or canoe
- A tooth from a killed snake becomes a poisonous serpent
- Jaguars and pumas can be similarly created to kill victims

## The Curing Process

### Diagnosis

When a curing shaman is called to treat a patient, his first task is to determine whether the illness is caused by witchcraft. Diagnosis and treatment typically begin in the late afternoon or early evening. The shaman drinks natem, tobacco juice, and piripiri, which allow him to perceive the patient's body "as though it were glass." Through this hallucinogen-enhanced vision, he can see any intruding objects and determine whether the illness is within his capacity to cure.

### The Sucking Ceremony

The actual extraction takes place at night, in a dark area of the house, because it is only in darkness that the shaman can perceive the drug-induced visions constituting supernatural reality.

The ceremony follows a precise protocol:

1. The shaman whistles the tune of the curing song to alert his tsentsak
2. After approximately fifteen minutes, he begins singing
3. When ready to extract, he regurgitates two of his own tsentsak into his mouth — one positioned in the front and one in the rear of his throat
4. These two darts must be identical to the one he has identified in the patient's body
5. The front tsentsak is intended to catch the supernatural essence of the extracted dart
6. The rear tsentsak blocks the throat as a safety measure, preventing the hostile supernatural essence from entering the shaman's body if the first dart fails to capture it

If both defensive tsentsak were to fail, the hostile essence would pass into the shaman's stomach and kill him. When successfully captured, the essence is absorbed into one of the curing shaman's tsentsak, which he then "vomits" out and displays to the patient and family, declaring: "Now I have sucked it out. Here it is."

### The Social Performance of Curing

An important dimension of Jivaro curing is its performative aspect. The non-shaman participants believe that the material object displayed by the shaman is the actual projectile that was causing the illness. The shaman does not disabuse them of this belief. He is not considered to be lying, because he sincerely believes that the important component — the supernatural essence — has indeed been removed from the patient's body. However, explaining to laypeople that he already had the objects in his mouth would undermine the evidentiary basis of the cure and make it impossible to convince the family that payment is owed.

The ability to suck successfully depends on the quantity and strength of the shaman's own tsentsak. Under natem, these darts manifest as zoomorphic spirit helpers — hovering over the shaman, perching on his shoulders, protruding from his skin — and actively assist in the extraction process.

### Dealing with Persistent Threats

The curing process becomes more complex when the bewitcher has deployed spirit helpers:

**Confronting the Pasuk**: If tsentsak continue entering the patient's body after initial extractions, the curing shaman suspects a pasuk is present. He drinks additional natem to perceive it and engages in tsentsak duels with the armored spirit helper. The shaman's own tsentsak form a protective shield covering his body, closing together at the point of enemy attack to repel incoming darts.

**Eliminating the Wakani Bird**: If the pasuk is destroyed but darts continue appearing, the curing shaman suspects a wakani bird. He then drinks maikua (Datura arborea or suaveolens) — a hallucinogen described as even more powerful than natem — along with tobacco juice. He silently infiltrates the forest and kills the bird with a tsentsak. Upon returning, he blows throughout the patient's house to dispel the "atmosphere" created by the accumulated darts, then completes the extraction.

**Final Purification**: Even after all tsentsak are extracted, the shaman may remain an additional night to suck out any residual "dirtiness" (pahuri) still inside the patient. This final stage is described as a noisy process accompanied by deep but dry vomiting.

## Identifying the Bewitcher

While curing under natem's influence, the shaman typically "sees" the person who bewitched his patient and can usually identify them — unless the sorcerer lives far away or belongs to another tribe. The patient's family demands to know the identity of the bewitcher, particularly if the patient dies. This creates intense pressure on the curing shaman to name a specific individual, which can itself become a source of social conflict and accusation.

## Tsentsak Economy and Recycling

After extracting a tsentsak, the curing shaman places it in a small container. He does not swallow it because it belongs to another shaman and would be harmful. Later, he throws it into the air, and it flies back to its original owner. Tsentsak also return to their source shaman upon the death of an apprentice who had received them.

Shamans can also actively **reclaim** tsentsak from those to whom they have previously given them. The reclaiming shaman drinks natem and uses his tsentsak to create a "bridge" in the form of a rainbow between himself and the other shaman. He then shoots a tsentsak along this rainbow, causing an explosion and flash like lightning where it strikes the ground beside the target. The surprise causes the other shaman to momentarily forget to guard his magical darts, allowing the first shaman to suck them back along the rainbow.

A shaman who loses his tsentsak in this way discovers that "nothing happens" when he drinks natem — the supernatural world has become inaccessible. The sudden loss causes illness, though it is typically not fatal unless a bewitcher attacks while the shaman is weakened. Some men who lose their tsentsak choose to abandon shamanism entirely and can then be persuaded to reveal their knowledge, since they no longer have a vested interest in maintaining secrecy.

## Weakening and Decline

Shamans gradually lose power over time and must repeatedly purchase new tsentsak to maintain their effectiveness. They are especially vulnerable after curing a patient who was bewitched by a shaman with a fresh supply of magical darts. This creates a constant cycle of power acquisition, expenditure, and renewal that defines the career arc of a Jivaro shaman.

## Anthropological Significance

The Jivaro bewitching-curing complex represents a remarkably coherent supernatural system with internal consistency across its diagnostic, therapeutic, and defensive dimensions. The use of multiple psychoactive substances (natem, tobacco juice, piripiri, maikua/datura) for different specific purposes reveals a sophisticated ethnopharmacology. The social dynamics — the pressure to identify bewitchers, the economic relationships between shamans, the performative requirements of curing — demonstrate how hallucinogen-based shamanism is deeply embedded in the social fabric rather than existing as an isolated spiritual practice.
