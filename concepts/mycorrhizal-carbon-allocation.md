# Mycorrhizal Carbon Allocation

Mycorrhizal carbon allocation refers to the flow of photosynthetically fixed carbon from host plants to their mycorrhizal fungal partners. This carbon transfer is the fundamental basis of the mycorrhizal mutualism, with plants diverting a substantial portion of their photosynthate to fungal partners in exchange for enhanced nutrient uptake. Understanding carbon allocation patterns is critical for understanding ecosystem carbon cycling and the functioning of mycorrhizal symbioses.

## Magnitude of Carbon Transfer

Plants may divert up to 80% or more of net energy fixed as sunlight to below-ground processes. A high proportion of this energy feeds mycorrhizal fungi and other soil organisms. This carbon is not lost to the plant; rather, soil organisms in the root zone greatly influence plant establishment through effects on nutrient cycling, pathogen suppression, soil aeration, and water uptake. The carbon allocated to mycorrhizal fungi represents a significant component of total ecosystem carbon flow.

## Carbon Flow Pathways

Carbon flows from atmosphere to green biomass through photosynthesis, then below-ground to roots and is transferred to specific molecules. In arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) systems, carbon moves from the host plant through intraradical fungal structures to the extraradical mycelium (ERM). The pathway involves carbon fixation in leaves, translocation to roots via the phloem, transfer across the arbuscular interface, and transport through the ERM for fungal metabolism and growth.

## Signature Fatty Acid Analysis

The neutral lipid fatty acid (NLFA) 16:1omega5 is a useful biomarker for detecting and quantifying carbon flow to arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in roots and soil. Since total 13C incorporation in AM fungal hyphae correlates with enrichment in NLFA 16:1omega5, it is possible to estimate carbon uptake in AM fungi by analyzing this compound. Neutral lipids comprise around 20% of the biomass of hyphae, vesicles, and spores, making NLFA 16:1omega5 a practical field indicator of active mycorrhizal carbon allocation.

## Carbon Allocation and Soil Carbon Turnover

A significant fraction of the carbon allocated to mycorrhizal fungi enters soil organic matter pools through several pathways. These include fungal turnover and death, exudation of extracellular compounds, and deposition of necromass into soil. The biochemical composition of mycorrhizal fungal biomass, which is rich in chitin and glomalin-related soil proteins, contributes to the formation of stable soil carbon pools. Under conditions of high soil nutrient availability, the carbon drain to mycorrhizal fungi can exceed the nutritional benefit, resulting in a net cost to the host.
