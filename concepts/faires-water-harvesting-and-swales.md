# Permaculture Water Harvesting and Swales
**Source:** The Ultimate Guide to [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] & [[natural-farming-sustainable-living-principles]]
(Nicole Faires, 2012)
Water is the most important element on any piece of land. No other factor
impacts so many other things or is affected by so many other elements. How
much water you have depends on the rainfall, soil drainage characteristics,
existing vegetation, people and animals using the water, and the kinds of
plants you want to grow. The first step is to decide where water comes from
and devise a way to store it, utilizing gravity to move it where needed.
## Water Sources
Permaculture systems draw from several primary water sources:
- **Rain**: Surface runoff or groundwater recharge; captured from roofs,
  driveways, and compacted surfaces
- **Springs**: Natural groundwater discharge points; reliable year-round
  in many regions
- **Streams**: Permanent or seasonal surface water flows; can be diverted
  with proper design
- **Wells**: Access deep groundwater reserves; require energy for pumping
  unless equipped with hand pumps
These sources are diverted through various means to storage, irrigation
systems, and swales. Gravity is the primary force used to move water, making
gentle slopes and drains key design elements that lead water from collection
points to storage and use areas.
## Desert Water Strategies
Desert environments present the challenge of very little water with brief
rainfall events that often produce less than an inch of rain, running off
quickly from sandy soils. This runoff can be captured using several
techniques:
- Dirt drains and swales to redirect surface flow to planting areas
- Rock mulches to reduce evaporation and protect soil moisture
- Shade structures to reduce transpiration losses from crops
- Deep-rooted perennial plants that access water far below the surface
- Micro-catchments (small depressions) around individual plants to
  concentrate limited rainfall
The key principle in arid design is to slow, spread, and sink every drop
of water that falls on the site, maximizing infiltration and minimizing
runoff and evaporation.
## Swales: Design and Function
A swale is a shallow, level-bottomed ditch excavated along the contour of a
slope, with the excavated soil placed on the downhill side to form a berm.
Swales are one of the most important tools in the permaculture water
harvesting toolkit.
### How Swales Work
- Water flowing down a slope is intercepted by the swale and held
  temporarily in the ditch
- The water slowly infiltrates into the soil, recharging groundwater
  rather than running off the site
- The berm on the downhill side is planted with trees and perennials that
  access the stored moisture
- Swales transform runoff into groundwater, creating underground water
  reserves that sustain vegetation through dry periods
### Swale Design Considerations
- Spacing between swales depends on slope: steeper slopes require closer
  spacing to capture water before it gains destructive velocity
- Swales should be level along their entire length; use a laser level or
  water level for accurate construction
- Depth is typically 6-18 inches with a gentle, U-shaped cross-section
- The berm should be stabilized with mulch and planted immediately to
  prevent erosion
- Swales should never be placed where failure could threaten structures
  or roads below
### Swale Planting
The berm below a swale becomes a productive growing zone. Recommended
planting strategies include:
- [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]] and nitrogen-fixing support species on the berm
- Comfrey, chicory, and other deep-rooted [[comparison-dynamic-accumulators-vs-cover-crops]]
- [[permaculture-plants-food-forest-ground-covers-bamboos]] to protect the berm from erosion
- A diverse polyculture that mimics natural edge communities
## Rainwater Catchment Systems
Collecting rainwater from roofs provides a clean water supply for domestic
use and irrigation. Key components of a rainwater catchment system include:
- **Collection surface**: Roof area determines volume; metal roofs are
  ideal as they are smooth and non-toxic
- **Conveyance**: Gutters and downspouts direct water to storage; include
  first-flush diverters to exclude debris from the initial rainfall
- **Storage**: Tanks, cisterns, or ponds; size based on roof area, rainfall
  patterns, and demand
- **Distribution**: Gravity-fed irrigation or pumped systems to move water
  to points of use
A good rule of thumb is that 1 inch of rain on 1,000 square feet of roof
yields approximately 600 gallons of water. Storage capacity should be
sufficient to bridge dry periods between rainfall events.
## Greywater Systems
Greywater (water from sinks, showers, and laundry) can be recycled for
landscape irrigation, reducing overall water demand. [[holmgren-permaculture-twelve-design-principles-framework]]:
- Keep systems simple: avoid complex filtration and pumps when possible
- Use biocompatible cleaning products to protect soil biology
- Direct greywater to mulch basins around fruit trees and ornamentals
- Do not store greywater for extended periods; use it within 24 hours
- Check local regulations regarding greywater use before installing systems
