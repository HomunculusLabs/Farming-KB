Shulgin's Predictions on Future Psychoactive Research

In his 1975 paper "Drugs of Abuse in the Future," Alexander Shulgin offered a systematic survey of pharmacological families likely to produce novel substances of abuse. Rather than merely cataloging known drugs, Shulgin projected future directions by examining unexplored chemical families documented in the scientific literature. His core argument was that drug control authorities incorrectly assumed the drug problem had reached maximum complexity, when in fact new compounds would continually emerge to replace controlled ones.

The Predictive Framework

Shulgin identified the raw material for his predictions in pharmacology journals, where articles describing new centrally active compounds appear regularly. He noted that each such publication invites underground researchers to replicate the synthesis and evaluate the compound in humans outside any regulatory framework. Successful products enter the illicit market as new drug problems; failures are forgotten. He also warned that novel agents invented by illicit manufacturers themselves may go unrecognized for years.

Major Drug Families Analyzed

Opiates: Shulgin argued that heroin substitutes were economically inevitable as import restrictions increased. He documented thebaine-derived oripavine analogs with potencies 10,000 times morphine, morphinan phenethylamine derivatives 20-40 times morphine potency, and some 4,000 described meperidine analogs. He noted the paradox that clinical researchers sought to separate analgesia from euphoria, while the "unwanted side effects" they tried to eliminate were precisely what made drugs abusable.

Stimulants: Beyond amphetamine and methamphetamine (then costing roughly 2 cents per dosage unit to produce), Shulgin identified [[tihkal-oral-activity-mao-inhibition]]. The 6-methoxy dihydroharman group, not found in nature, was more potent than natural 7-methoxy counterparts. Alpha-methyl analogs of 5-methoxytryptamines were flagged as unexplored areas of future research.

Phenethylamines: Starting from mescaline as the parent, Shulgin observed that extending the alkyl chain to three carbons (phenylisopropylamines) with appropriate substitution changes could markedly amplify potency. He noted the para-position as particularly sensitive, citing DOM (STP) as a street-abused example and the 4-bromo analog as the most potent member yet studied. He stressed that position and substituent variables remained largely unexplored.

3. Atropine analogs (glycolate esters): These synthetic compounds, represented by the general formula of benzilate esters with basic nitrogen, produced visual and auditory phenomena beclouded by amnesia. JB-318 and JB-336 were already scheduled, but hundreds of additional compounds with similar human activity had been reported. Shulgin noted that benzilic acid and various ethanolamines were commercially available, making future abuse drugs from this family likely.

Qualitative Distinctions Between Hallucinogen Classes

Shulgin drew an important pharmacological distinction: indoles (DMT, harmaline, LSD) generate distortions arising from within the subject -- intuitive and interpretive changes. Phenethylamines (mescaline analogs) lead to sensory changes and perceptual syntheses involving the external world. Atropine analogs produce artificial visual and auditory phenomena frequently accompanied by amnesia -- disruptive and confusing rather than insightful.

Prescription and OTC Drugs as Future Problems

Shulgin also identified two additional vectors for future drug problems. First, prescription drugs with psychoactive properties distributed by physicians serve as initial distribution points. He noted that the PDR listed thousands of prescription drugs, many with side effects (dizziness, confusion, overstimulation) that served as clues to misuse potential. Second, over-the-counter drugs could be abused through intentional overdose or parenteral administration of drugs intended only for oral use, citing the dextromethorphan (Romilar) episode as precedent.

Polypharmacy and Synergism

He warned of unpredictable interactions from combined use of multiple drugs, noting that as new drugs emerge, the probability of dangerous synergistic effects increases dramatically. The mutual potentiation of alcohol and barbiturates was a known example, but such interactions were rarely appreciated by users.

Legacy and Accuracy

Many of Shulgin's 1975 predictions proved remarkably prescient. The emergence of novel fentanyl analogs in the opioid category, synthetic cathinones and novel stimulants, novel benzodiazepines, and the explosion of phenethylamine analogs (documented in his later PIHKAL) all followed the patterns he identified. His framework of chemical families with open-ended structural modification potential remains the standard model for understanding how new psychoactive substances emerge.
