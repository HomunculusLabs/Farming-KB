# Season Extension Timeline and Frost Protection

[[eliot-coleman]]'s season extension system is built on the principle of
layering protection to create progressively milder microclimates. By
combining the right crops with the right protection at the right time,
he has demonstrated that [[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]] can continue through winter
in any part of the continental [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], not just mild climates.

## The Layered Protection Principle

The core insight is that each protective layer shifts the effective
climate one and a half USDA zones warmer. A cold-tolerant vegetable grown
in the open field in USDA Zone 4, when protected by an unheated
greenhouse, experiences conditions equivalent to Zone 6. Adding a second
layer of lightweight floating [[coleman-double-layer-protection-greenhouse-row-cover]] inside the greenhouse shifts the
effective zone to approximately Zone 8. This simple arithmetic means that
growers in Zone 4 can achieve Zone 8 conditions with two layers of
protection, opening vast possibilities for [[coleman-beet-greens-cold-house-winter-production]].

Coleman's temperature records confirm the principle. On a 5°F (-15°C)
night, the temperature in a Zone 5 greenhouse would be 15°F (20°F
warmer), and under the inner layer of row cover it would reach 20°F
(10°F warmer again). By comparison, in a Zone 4 location the greenhouse
would be 10°F and the inner row cover zone 24°F.

## The Annual Season Extension Timeline

Coleman's year is organized around a precise timeline of protection
transitions. From the last frost date in spring through the first fall
frost, outdoor production proceeds with minimal protection. Simple hoop
houses, even without heat, provide a four- to twelve-week jump on outdoor
maturity depending on the crop. [[cervantes-season-extension-cloches-row-covers]] alone provide 4°F to 6°F of

harvesting. The floating row cover is the first layer applied to
greenhouse crops, typically just before the first hard frosts in autumn.

## Quick Hoops for Overwintering

For inexpensive overwinter protection of fall-planted crops such as
onions, spinach, and lettuce, Coleman developed "quick hoops." These
low tunnels are made from 10-foot lengths of half-inch electrical conduit
(plastic or metal), bowed into a half-circle covering two 30-inch beds
with a path between them. Each end is inserted about 10 inches into the
soil, forming a hoop about 30 inches high. Hoops are placed every 5 feet
along beds that are usually 50 or 100 feet long.

The hoops are initially covered with 10-foot-wide spun-bonded row cover
held down by sandbags. When real winter weather threatens in late
November or early December, a sheet of 10-foot-wide clear plastic is
added over the row cover for snow protection. The structure is stiffened
against wind and snow by driving stakes at each end and tying ropes to
pull the plastic taut lengthwise.

## Managing Quick Hoop Crops

Onions, scallions, spinach, and lettuce are the primary quick hoop
crops. Overwintering onions are planted during the last week of August,
with hoops and row cover placed over them in mid-October. Overwintered
onion varieties mature at the end of June, providing onions for the farm
stand five weeks or more before spring-planted crops are ready.

For spinach and lettuce, Coleman either direct-seeds or uses soil block
transplants seeded on October 1 and set out two weeks later. Overwintered
lettuce and spinach give outdoor crops to sell up to a month earlier than
the best spring transplants. Choosing varieties hardy enough to withstand
overwintering stress is critical.

## Ventilation and Spring Transition

When spring arrives, quick hoops need ventilation on sunny days. Coleman
removes sandbags along the southern edge and inserts notched props to
hold up the edges of the plastic and fabric. As outdoor temperatures
moderate, covers are progressively removed, allowing crops to acclimate
to outdoor conditions before the full harvest period begins.
