# Environmental Sensing and Signal Transduction in Filamentous Fungi

Filamentous fungi have evolved sophisticated systems for detecting and
responding to environmental signals. Because they are both non-motile
and heterotrophic, fungi must rapidly sense and adapt to the
heterogeneous microenvironments through which their hyphae grow. This
sensing capacity underpins all aspects of [[fungal-ecology]] including
[[fungal-mycelial-foraging-heterogeneous-environments]] and
[[fungal-mycelial-foraging-heterogeneous-environments]].

## The Supracellular Nature of Hyphae

The defining element of filamentous fungi is the hypha: a tip-growing,
typically multinucleate cellular element that undergoes regular branching
and possesses incomplete cross-walls (septa). When septal pores are
open, cytoplasm and organelles move freely between compartments. Hyphae
also fuse with one another in sub-peripheral colony regions, creating
interconnected networks. This supracellular organization, combining
features of both a coenocyte and a syncytium, enables long-distance
intracellular communication, translocation of water and nutrients, and
cooperative behavior spanning heterogeneous environments.

## Consequences of Non-Motility and Heterotrophy

Fungi are phylogenetically closer to animals than plants but combine
non-motility (like plants) with heterotrophy (like animals). This
combination has driven the evolution of rapid environmental response
mechanisms. Hyphae can change growth direction and branching patterns
within minutes, selectively growing from unfavorable to favorable
conditions. Sporulation can be initiated quickly to resist adverse
conditions or escape to better ones. Differentiated hyphal aggregates
such as sclerotia resist environmental stress, while rhizomorphs
transport water and nutrients across adverse environments.

## Categories of Environmental Signals

Environmental signals influencing fungi fall into four categories. First,
abiotic global signals such as light intensity, wavelength, and
periodicity, plus temperature and relative humidity. Second, abiotic
local signals experienced at the microscopic level as gradients:
nutrients, oxygen, carbon dioxide, pH, chemical signals, mechanical
stimuli, and osmotic conditions. Third, biotic signals from other
organisms including chemicals that promote or inhibit growth, compounds
modulating secondary metabolism, and host-derived cues for invasion — a topic explored in [[symbiotic-fungi-endophytic-fungi-in-plants]] and [[symbiotic-fungi-fungal-parasitism-and-pathogenicity]].
Fourth, autoregulatory signals produced by the fungus itself, including [[fungal-quorum-sensing-autoregulation]] mechanisms.

## Autoregulatory Signals

Autoregulators are the least understood class of fungal signals but play
critical roles in colony organization. During germination, some act as
self-inhibitors while others influence germ-tube emergence patterns.
Colony morphogenesis is regulated by signals causing hyphal avoidance at
the colony periphery, centrifugal growth away from the colony centre,
and hyphal homing for fusion. Some autoregulators function as quorum
sensors regulating yeast-hyphal dimorphism, chlamydospore formation,
and conidial fusion. Sex pheromones including trisporic acid in
Mucorales and peptide pheromones in ascomycetes and basidiomycetes
regulate sexual reproduction.

## Signal Transduction Machinery

Genomic analysis of Neurospora crassa, the first sequenced filamentous
fungus, revealed a signal transduction apparatus of remarkable
complexity. Compared with Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Neurospora possesses
expanded upstream sensory proteins with conserved downstream components,
suggesting extensive networking and novel signaling interactions not
found in yeasts — a theme also relevant to [[trichoderma]] biology.

### Two-Component Signaling

Two-component signaling systems consist of hybrid proteins containing
both histidine kinase and response regulator domains. N. crassa
possesses eleven histidine kinases (compared to one in S. cerevisiae),
but only one histidine phosphotransferase and two response regulators.
This conservation of downstream elements suggests integration of
multiple sensory inputs through few output channels. Two kinases are
involved in hyphal development and three in light sensing.

### G-Protein Coupled Receptors

N. crassa possesses 35 predicted GPCRs compared to only six in S.
cerevisiae. These fall into six classes including microbial opsins,
pheromone receptors, glucose sensors, nitrogen sensors, cAMP-receptor
homologs, and a novel PTH11-related class. G-protein signaling in
Neurospora regulates hyphal growth, conidiation, female fertility, and
stress responses through three alpha subunits, one beta, and one gamma
subunit.

### MAP Kinase Cascades

Nine MAPKKK/MAPKK/MAPK genes correspond to at least three MAP kinase
pathways. One regulates hyphal growth, fusion, aerial growth,
conidiation, and female fertility. A second mediates osmoregulation and
fungicide resistance. A third may maintain cell integrity. The scaffold
protein Ste5, which conditions specificity in yeast MAPK signaling, is
absent in Neurospora, suggesting greater signaling diversity and
cross-talk in filamentous fungi.

### Calcium and cAMP Signaling

Calcium signaling regulates spore germination, hyphal tip growth,
branching, and circadian clocks. Transient increases in cytosolic
calcium concentration serve as signals for multiple developmental
processes. The calcium-calmodulin system interacts with other pathways
to coordinate cellular responses. cAMP signaling controls aerial hyphal
growth and formation, conidiation, and thermotolerance. Neurospora
possesses two catalytic subunits and one regulatory subunit of protein
kinase A, compared to one of each in budding yeast, indicating greater
signaling capacity.

## Evolutionary Significance

The sophistication of [[fungal-environmental-sensing]] systems reflects
the evolutionary pressure faced by organisms that must locate and
exploit patchily distributed resources across complex soil
environments without the benefit of motility. Signal transduction
networks in fungi rival those of higher eukaryotes in their
complexity and integration capacity.
