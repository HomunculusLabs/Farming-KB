# US Climate Zones for Winter Harvest

## Overview

Understanding regional climate is essential for planning a successful winter harvest operation. The United States exhibits enormous variation in winter severity, and [[eliot-coleman]] provides four key climate maps that help growers compare their location to others and determine the feasibility of winter vegetable production under protection. Much of the U.S. has winter temperatures comparable to or warmer than southern France, where winter vegetable production in high tunnels is already common practice.

## The Four Key Climate Factors

### 1. Average January Temperature

The average January temperature map is the most immediately useful reference. It reveals that a surprisingly large portion of the continental United States experiences January temperatures at least as mild as those in southern France—a region where winter vegetable production in unheated high tunnels is standard practice. This comparison alone should encourage many U.S. growers to attempt winter harvesting.

Areas with average January temperatures above freezing have a particularly straightforward path to winter production. Even in areas where January averages are below freezing, cold-tolerant salad crops can thrive under the protection of greenhouses and row covers.

### 2. USDA Hardiness Zone Map

The standard USDA zone map divides the country by minimum winter temperatures. Coleman's farm in coastal Maine is rated Zone 5 (minimum temperatures of -10°F to -20°F). While useful for comparing how cold different regions get, this map has a significant limitation: it does not account for the **duration** of cold weather.

Two locations may share the same USDA zone but have vastly different winter lengths. This is a critical distinction for winter harvest planning, since the cumulative effect of prolonged cold affects both plant growth rates and the economics of protected cultivation.

### 3. Mean Annual Number of Days at or Below 32°F

This is perhaps the most practically useful map for winter harvest planning. It measures the **length** of the freezing period, which directly affects how many days crops must survive under protection.

Coleman's farm on the Maine coast sits on the **150-day** line—meaning temperatures reach 32°F or below on approximately 150 days per year. By contrast, a farm in northeastern Missouri also rated as Zone 5 sits on only the **90-day** line. That 60-day difference represents a substantially shorter winter, despite identical zone ratings.

For growers in different regions, this map provides the best single indicator of how challenging winter production will be. Fewer freezing days means:

- Less fuel or supplementary heat needed (if any)
- Faster crop growth during shoulder seasons
- More harvest days per season
- Greater economic viability

### 4. Average Depth of Frost Penetration

Frost penetration depth measures the severity of winter from a soil perspective. At Coleman's coastal Maine location, average frost depth reaches **48 inches**. The same northeastern Missouri farm with only 90 freezing days experiences an average frost depth of just **20 inches**.

Frost penetration matters for winter harvest because:

- Deeper frost makes it harder to harvest root crops in the field
- Soil that freezes deeply takes longer to thaw in spring
- Crops grown in raised beds or protected structures are less affected by deep ground frost
- The insulating effect of snow cover can dramatically reduce effective frost depth

## Comparative Examples

| Location | USDA Zone | Days ≤32°F | Frost Depth |
|----------|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Coastal Maine | Zone 5 | ~150 | 48 inches |
| Northeastern Missouri | Zone 5 | ~90 | 20 inches |
| Southern France | Zone 8+ | Very few | Minimal |

The Maine vs. Missouri comparison is particularly instructive. Both are Zone 5, but Missouri has a significantly shorter and less severe winter. A grower in Missouri has a substantial advantage for winter production despite the same zone rating.

## Implications for Growers

### Where Winter Harvest Works

Coleman's message is clear: if winter harvest can succeed in coastal Maine with 150 freezing days and 48-inch frost penetration, it can work in most of the continental United States. During the 1980s, Coleman also experimented successfully in the mountains of Vermont in **Zone 3**—an even harsher climate.

Growers should not be discouraged by their zone rating alone. The combination of zone, winter length, and frost penetration gives a more complete picture. Many areas of the country that growers assume are too cold for winter production are actually comparable to or milder than proven winter-harvest regions.

### Using the Maps for Planning

1. **Locate your farm** on all four maps
2. **Compare** to known successful winter-harvest regions (southern France, coastal Maine)
3. **Note the duration** of freezing days—this affects heating costs and harvest windows
4. **Consider frost depth** for field harvesting and soil management
5. **Plan protection levels** accordingly: areas with shorter, milder winters need less infrastructure

### Regional Adaptation

Growers in milder regions (fewer freezing days, shallower frost) can:

- Use simpler, less expensive structures ([[quick-hoops]] instead of full greenhouses)
- Extend the harvest season further into winter
- Grow a wider range of crops through winter
- Start spring production earlier

Growers in harsher regions (more freezing days, deeper frost) should:

- Invest in more robust protection (double-layer greenhouses with inner row covers)
- Focus on the most cold-tolerant crops
- Plan for slower growth rates during the coldest periods
- Consider mobile greenhouses to pre-warm soil before winter

## The Bottom Line

The climate maps demonstrate that winter vegetable production under protection is feasible across most of the United States. The key insight is that USDA zones alone are insufficient for planning—winter length and frost severity matter as much as minimum temperatures. Growers who combine the four climate factors with appropriate protective structures can achieve successful winter harvests in surprisingly cold climates.
