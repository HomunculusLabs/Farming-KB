# Psilocybe zapotecorum Section Zapotecorum: Americas Pacific Distribution

Section Zapotecorum of the genus Psilocybe represents one of the most
biogeographically significant groups of neurotropic fungi, with a
distribution spanning from Mesoamerica through South America to the
Pacific Islands, including a remarkable trans-Pacific disjunction between
the Americas and New Zealand. According to [[Guzmán, Allen & Gartz (1998)]],
this section bridges the mycobiotas of Mexico as a Northern and Southern Mycobiotic Convergence Zone and the Southern Hemisphere Origin Hypothesis for Neurotropic Fungi.

## Species Composition

The Section Zapotecorum includes the following principal species:

- **Psilocybe zapotecorum** Heim: The type species, distributed from Mexico
  through South America
- **Psilocybe aucklandii** Guzmán, Bandala & King: A New Zealand endemic
  closely related to P. zapotecorum
- **Psilocybe subzapotecorum** Guzmán: A recently described Mexican species
- **Psilocybe kumaenorum** Heim: Documented in New Guinea and New Zealand
- **Psilocybe ramulosa** Guzmán, Bononi & Picolo-Grandi: A Brazilian species
- **Psilocybe tasmaniana**: Found in Tasmania and Australia
- **Psilocybe eucalypta** Margot & Watling: Australian species

## Mexican Distribution

P. zapotecorum has deep cultural significance in Mexico, where it was first
documented by Wasson (1957) during his ethnomycological investigations. The
species name honors the Zapotec people of Oaxaca. The Guzmán, Allen & Gartz
survey documents an extensive publication history: Heim (1958a), Singer &
Smith (1958), Singer (1958, 1959, 1978), Guzmán (1975b, 1977a, 1983,
1990a, 1997), and many others. P. subzapotecorum was described by Guzmán
(1999a). The species grows in tropical and subtropical forests of southern
Mexico, particularly Oaxaca, Veracruz, and Chiapas.

## Central American Bridge

Section Zapotecorum species serve as a biogeographic bridge between Mexican
and South American populations through Central America:

- **Guatemala**: P. caerulescens recorded by Singer (1978)
- **Panama**: P. caerulescens var. caerulescens (Guzmán, 1983)
- **Colombia**: P. zapotecorum confirmed (Guzmán, 1983; Pulido, 1983)

This distribution supports the Central American isthmus as a dispersal
corridor for neurotropic fungi between North and South America.

## South American Distribution

P. zapotecorum and related species are well documented throughout tropical
South America:

- **Colombia**: Guzmán (1983), Pulido (1983), Stijve & de Meijer (1993)
- **Brazil**: Guzmán (1983), Stijve & de Meijer (1993), Stamets (1996).
  P. ramulosa described from Brazilian material by Guzmán et al. (1984)
- **Argentina**: Singer & Smith (1958), Singer (1978), Guzmán (1983)
- **Peru**: Guzmán (1983), Stamets (1996)
- **Bolivia**: P. yungensis (closely related) by Singer & Smith (1958),
  Singer (1978), Guzmán (1983)
- **Ecuador and Venezuela**: P. yungensis and P. caerulescens documented

The South American diversity of Section Zapotecorum is particularly rich in
Amazon and Atlantic forest regions, supporting the Southern Hemisphere Origin Hypothesis for Neurotropic Fungi.

## The Trans-Pacific Disjunction: New Zealand

The most biogeographically significant aspect of Section Zapotecorum is the
presence of closely related species in New Zealand, representing a
trans-Pacific disjunction of approximately 10,000 kilometers:

- **Psilocybe aucklandii**: Described by Guzmán, Bandala & King (1991,
  1993b) from New Zealand. Johnston & Buchanan (1995) and Stamets (1996)
  confirmed it. Closely related to P. zapotecorum, possibly a Gondwanan
  relict.
- **Psilocybe kumaenorum**: Originally from New Guinea (Heim et al., 1967),
  also in New Zealand (Allen et al., 1991)
- **Psilocybe tasmaniana**: Tasmania and New Zealand (Allen et al., 1991;
  Chang & Mills, 1992)
- **Psilocybe eucalypta**: Australia, also New Zealand (Guzmán et al.,
  1993b)
- **Psilocybe collybioides**: New Zealand (Allen et al., 1991), noted as
  synonym of P. zapotecorum
- **Psilocybe makarorae**: New Zealand endemic (Johnston & Buchanan, 1995)

Two main hypotheses explain this disjunction:

1. **Gondwanan vicariance**: Ancestral species distributed across Gondwana
   before breakup (~80-100 million years ago). However, this vastly
   predates Psilocybe evolution and is considered unlikely.
2. **Long-distance dispersal**: Spores transported across the Pacific by
   westerly wind currents, migratory birds, or other vectors. Prevailing
   westerlies could facilitate westward dispersal from South America to
   New Zealand.

## Australian Connection

Australia also harbors Section Zapotecorum species:

- **Psilocybe australiana**: Margot & Watling (1981), Guzmán (1983),
  Chang & Mills (1992)
- **Psilocybe subaeruginosa**: The most common Australian species, Picker
  & Richards (1970), Hall (1973), Southcott (1974), Cleland (1976)
- **Psilocybe tasmaniana** and **P. eucalypta**: Southeastern Australia

Australian species are adapted to temperate Eucalyptus and Nothofagus
forests rather than the tropical lowlands preferred by American species,
illustrating the ecological plasticity of the section.

## Biogeographic Significance

Section Zapotecorum provides crucial evidence for major biogeographic
hypotheses:

- **Southern Hemisphere diversity center**: Greatest species diversity in
  South America and Australasia supports the Southern Hemisphere Origin Hypothesis for Neurotropic Fungi
- **Trans-Pacific connections**: The Americas-Australasia disjunction
  mirrors patterns in other organism groups
- **North-South corridor**: Distribution from Mexico through South America
  demonstrates the American continent as a dispersal route
- **Habitat adaptation**: Shift from tropical forests (Americas) to
  temperate Eucalyptus/Nothofagus forests (Australasia) shows ecological
  plasticity
