# Soil Erosion and Rehabilitation

## Overview

Soil erosion and degradation represent the loss of productive capacity that underpins all agriculture and natural systems. In [[permaculture-design]], soil creation and conservation are central themes because all other productive elements depend on stable, living soil. Understanding erosion mechanisms and rehabilitation strategies is essential for any land designer.

## Types of Soil Erosion

Soils degrade through several primary mechanisms:

### Wind Erosion

Wind erosion manifests as **dust storms** and the **blow-out of dunes** and foreshores. It strips exposed topsoil particles and deposits them elsewhere, depleting the source area of its most fertile layer. Wind erosion is most severe on bare, flat, or recently cultivated land with minimal vegetative cover.

### Water Erosion

Water-driven erosion takes three distinct forms:

- **Sheet erosion**: A generalised surface flow of water off bare areas and croplands, removing a thin layer of topsoil uniformly across large areas. Often invisible until significant damage has occurred.
- **Gully erosion**: Caused by concentrated water flow over deep but unstable sediment. Gullies form where overland flow concentrates into channels, carving deep incisions into the landscape.
- **Tunnel erosion**: Sub-surface scouring of soils below the surface, where water flows through underground channels, undermining the soil structure from within. This is particularly dangerous because it can cause sudden surface collapse.

### Chemical Degradation

Soil **collapse or deflocculation** occurs following increased salt concentrations in clay-fraction soils. When salts accumulate, they disrupt the electrochemical bonds between clay particles, causing the soil structure to collapse and become impermeable.

## Causes of Erosion

Erosion follows several primary disturbances:

- **Deforestation**: Removal of tree cover eliminates the root systems and canopy interception that protect soil
- **Soil compaction**: From machinery, hooves, or overuse, reducing infiltration and increasing runoff
- **Disturbed soil-water balance**: Increased overland flow and rising water tables or salt seepages
- **Overgrazing**: Removing vegetative cover and compacting soil with livestock
- **Plough agriculture on broad scale**: Conventional tillage exposes soil to elements
- **Episodes of high winds or rains**: Especially during drought periods when cover is sparse
- **Severe disturbance**: From animal tracks, roading, and ill-advised earthworks

## Economic and Political Dimensions

Beyond physical factors, erosion is driven by economic and political pressures that require or permit inappropriate land use. Third-world debt and over-production mandates both force production onto fragile soil structures. A conservative approach to land use planning would encompass:

- Permitted or restricted use of soils, mapped by region
- Analysis of slope, soil stability, and minimal forest clearing
- Permitted maximum levels of crop production or livestock density
- Procedures of good soil husbandry

## The Role of Organic Matter

Research by Charles Arden-Clarke and David Hodges demonstrated that many severe erosion outbreaks are directly linked to **falling levels of organic matter in soil**. The more organic matter present, the more stable the soil, because:

- Good soil structure develops from [[humus]]
- Water infiltration is greatly improved
- Inorganic soils without organic matter break down under rain
- Increased overland flow follows structural collapse, leading to rills and gullies
- Surface soil can powder and blow away without organic matter to bond particles

## Control Strategies by Erosion Type

| Erosion Type | Control Methods |
|---|---|
| Wind | Windbreaks, tree crops, fast-spreading grasses |
| Sheet/Gully | Permanent crop, terracing, forestry, diversion drains, gabions |
| Tunnel erosion | De-stocking, contour drainage, deep-rooted plants |
| Salt/deflocculation | Reafforestation, deep interceptor drains |

## The Imperative for Organic Methods

On many delicate soils (particularly over chalks), the only sustainable answer is to **replace crops with pasture or forests**. Intensive arable use and winter cropping both accelerate erosion. The evidence leads to the conclusion that mulching, [[green-manure]], grass leys on rotation, hedgerows, and minimal cultivation are not merely beneficial but **imperative**.

As Mollison states: *"The time to examine the organic approach has passed; the time to adopt it has arrived."*

## Key Principles

1. **Soil stability is the foundation** of all productive systems — without it, everything else is at risk
2. **Organic matter is the primary stabiliser** of soil structure
3. **Permanent vegetation** is more effective than any structural intervention
4. **Prevention is far cheaper** than rehabilitation
5. **Economic pressures** must be addressed alongside physical remediation
