# Three-Dimensional Visible Language as Future Human Capacity: McKenna's Tryptamine Hypothesis of Linguistic Evolution

Among the most consequential speculative claims in McKenna's 1983 Esalen lecture
is his hypothesis that the tryptamine experience reveals a latent human capacity
for three-dimensional visible language — a mode of communication in which "the
intent of meaning is actually beheld in three-dimensional space." This hypothesis
bridges phenomenology, linguistics, and evolutionary biology, proposing that the
translinguistic objects encountered in DMT states are not aberrations but
anticipations of a future stage in human linguistic evolution.

## The Phenomenological Observation

McKenna's starting point is a specific phenomenological observation about the
tryptamine state: under the influence of DMT or psilocybin, one encounters
"extra-dimensional objects — the feeling-toned, meaning-toned, three-dimensional
rotating complexes of transforming light and color" that function as carriers of
information and meaning. These objects are not merely visual — they are
"translinguistic," meaning that they convey semantic content that transcends the
capacity of ordinary language to express. The experiencer "hears and beholds a
language of alien meaning that is conveying alien information that cannot be
Englished."

The critical feature of these translinguistic objects is that meaning is
perceived directly, as a visual-spatial phenomenon, rather than being decoded
from sequential auditory or visual symbols (as in spoken or written language).
The meaning is "beheld" — it is present in the object itself, available to
direct apprehension without the mediation of a symbolic code. This is what
distinguishes the tryptamine "language" from ordinary glossolalia or even
ordinary visual imagery: the semantic content is not projected onto the visual
form by the interpreter but is experienced as inherent in the form itself.

## The Evolutionary Hypothesis

From this observation, McKenna advances an explicit evolutionary hypothesis:
"These experiences strongly suggest that there is some latent ability of the
human brain/body that has yet to be discovered; yet, once discovered, it will be
so obvious that it will fall right into the mainstream of cultural evolution."
The tryptamine state, in other words, does not create a new ability but reveals
an existing one that has not yet been culturally developed — much as the
capacity for mathematics existed in the human brain for millennia before it was
culturally elaborated into the formal systems we now take for granted.

McKenna extends this hypothesis with a specific prediction about the timeline of
this development: "Given ten thousand years and high cultural involvement in
such a talent, does anyone doubt that it could become a cultural convenience in
the same way that mathematics or language has become a cultural convenience?"
This is a bold claim: that three-dimensional visible language is not merely a
curious artifact of altered states but a genuine communicative modality that
could, with cultural investment, become as foundational to human civilization as
spoken language, writing, or mathematics.

## Language as Shadow of This Ability

McKenna offers two complementary accounts of the relationship between ordinary
language and this latent capacity. In the first account, "language is the shadow
of this ability" — ordinary spoken and written language is a degraded, flattened
projection of a more fundamental capacity for direct semantic apprehension. Just
as a two-dimensional shadow preserves some information about the three-dimensional
object that casts it while losing dimensionality, ordinary language preserves
some information about the translinguistic reality it attempts to express while
losing the spatial, visual, and directly apprehensible qualities that make that
reality fully communicable.

In the second account, the latent ability will be "a further extension of
language" — not a replacement for existing linguistic forms but a new modality
that builds upon and transcends them. This account is more optimistic about the
value of ordinary language: it suggests that the evolution of communication
proceeds through stages, each of which preserves the achievements of its
predecessors while adding new capacities. Spoken language extended gesture;
writing extended speech; mathematics extended writing; and three-dimensional
visible language would extend all of these into a new domain of direct semantic
perception.

## The Assembly Language Behind Language

McKenna draws an analogy to computational linguistics when he describes the
tryptamine glossolalia as "simply a brain state that allows the expression of
the assembly language that lies behind language." In computing, assembly language
is a low-level programming language that corresponds directly to the machine
instructions executed by a processor. Higher-level languages (Python, Java, C++)
are compiled or interpreted down to assembly language before execution. McKenna
suggests that ordinary human languages are similarly "compiled" from a more
fundamental cognitive substrate — a pre-linguistic or proto-linguistic layer of
meaning that is closer to the brain's native processing format.

The tryptamine state, in this analogy, is a debugger or low-level access mode
that allows direct interaction with this assembly language, bypassing the
compilation step that normally translates raw cognitive content into the
sequential, symbolic form of ordinary language. The translinguistic objects
encountered in the DMT state are, in effect, the "machine code" of consciousness
— the native format in which the brain processes meaning before it is encoded
into the linear, symbolic structures of speech and writing.

## Primal Language Traditions and the Ursprache

McKenna connects his hypothesis to several traditions of primal or universal
language. He references Robert Graves's "The White Goddess," which posits a
primal poetic language underlying all human languages; the Kabbalistic "Zohar,"
which describes a primal "Ursprache" (original language) that is the language of
creation itself; and Philo Judaeus's description of the Logos moving "from things
heard to things beheld" — a progression from auditory to visual language that
mirrors McKenna's own phenomenological account of the transition from ordinary
speech to translinguistic visual objects.

These cross-cultural parallels suggest that the experience of a language beyond
language is not unique to McKenna or to the tryptamine experience but is a
recurrent feature of human encounters with the limits of linguistic expression.
The consistency of the motif across traditions — from Kabbalah to Romantic
poetry to psychedelic phenomenology — lends indirect support to McKenna's
hypothesis that the capacity for direct semantic perception is a genuine human
endowment rather than a pharmacological artifact.

## Implications for the Noosphere

McKenna's hypothesis has implications for his broader vision of the noosphere —
the sphere of human thought that surrounds the planet. If three-dimensional
visible language represents a genuine evolutionary capacity, then the noosphere
is not a static enclosure but a dynamic, expanding system whose boundaries are
defined by the limits of human communicative ability. The development of this
capacity would represent a phase transition in the noosphere analogous to the
transition from oral to written culture: a qualitative expansion in the
bandwidth and dimensionality of human communication that would fundamentally
transform the character of civilization.
