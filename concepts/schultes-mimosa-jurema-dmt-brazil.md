# Mimosa Jurema: DMT Tree of Northeastern Brazil

## Overview

[[entheogen-jurema-mimosa-hostilis-ethnobotany]] (syn. Mimosa tenuiflora), known as the **Jurema
Tree**, is a sparsely spiny treelet native to the dry caatingas of
eastern Brazil. It is one of the most significant hallucinogenic plants
of South America, valued for its root bark which contains high
concentrations of [[schultes-ayahuasca-magic-drink-amazon]] analog.

## Botanical Profile

Mimosa hostilis belongs to the Leguminosae (Pea Family). In the arid
caatinga ecosystems of eastern Brazil, this bushy, sparsely spiny
treelet flourishes abundantly. The spines are basally swollen,
approximately 3mm long. Its finely pinnate leaves measure 3 to 5 cm in
length. The flowers occur in loosely cylindrical spikes and are white
and fragrant. The legume pod is about 2.5 to 3 cm long, breaking into
4 to 6 sections when mature.

The plant is also known by its Mexican name **Tepescohuite**, where it
is used for various purposes. Several species of Mimosa are called
Jurema in eastern Brazil. M. hostilis is specifically known as **Jurema
Preta** ("black jurema"), while the related M. verrucosa, from the bark
of which a stupefacient is said to be derived, is called **Jurema
Branca** ("white jurema").

## Chemical Constituents

An alkaloid was isolated from the root of this treelet and initially
called **nigerine**. It was later shown to be identical with the
hallucinogenic N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT). The dried root bark of
Mimosa tenuiflora contains approximately 0.57% to 1% DMT by weight,
making it one of the richest natural sources of this compound.

The root bark is the primary plant part used for its psychoactive
properties. This concentration of DMT places Mimosa hostilis alongside
Psychotria viridis and Diplopterys cabrerana as one of the principal
DMT-containing plants utilized in the preparation of hallucinogenic
brews.

## Indigenous Use: Vinho de Jurema

Valued in eastern Brazil, several tribes in Pernambuco used the Jurema
plant in ceremonials. The beverage prepared from it was known as
**Vinho de Jurema** ("Jurema wine"). The preparation was employed by
various indigenous groups, some now extinct, of the same area.

The Pancaru Indians of Tacaratu in Pernambuco were documented using the
Vinho de Jurema in their ceremonial practices. The 1946 study by
Goncalves de Lima, "Observacoes sobre o vinho da Jurema," remains an
important early ethnobotanical record of this practice.

## Visionary Effects

The visionary effects of Juremahuasca often take the form of fireworks
or kaleidoscope-like designs, flashing colors, fantastic mandalas, or
travels to another world. The effects are described as equal to the
effects of the traditional ayahuasca preparations from the Amazon.

The DMT in Mimosa root bark, when combined with the beta-carboline
MAO inhibitors from [[mckenna-food-gods-peganum-harmala-soma]], creates the same pharmacological
synergy that makes [[harner-amazonian-ayahuasca-preparation-methods]] effective: the [[turner-harmala-alkaloids-preparation-and-dosage]]
inhibit monoamine oxidase, allowing the orally ingested DMT to reach the
central nervous system and produce its visionary effects.

## Other DMT-Containing Mimosa Species

Several other Mimosa species contain psychoactive alkaloids. Mimosa
scabrella bark has been documented as a DMT-containing plant suitable
for ayahuasca analog preparation. The genus Mimosa thus contributes
multiple species to the global pharmacopoeia of DMT-containing plants.

The related Acacia genus (also in the Leguminosae) provides additional
DMT sources, including Acacia maidenii (bark, 0.36% DMT), Acacia
phlebophylla (leaves, 0.3% DMT), and Acacia simplicifolia (leaves and
bark, 0.81% DMT).

## Cultural Significance

The Jurema tradition of northeastern Brazil represents one of the
earliest documented uses of a DMT-containing plant in the Americas. The
ceremonial preparation of Vinho de Jurema predates the modern discovery
of DMT's psychoactive properties and demonstrates sophisticated
indigenous knowledge of plant pharmacology.

The plant's enduring significance is evidenced by its continued use in
[[metzner-barquinha-umbanda-and-afro-brazilian-ayahuasca-syncretism]] churches such as [[metzner-syncretic-entheogenic-churches-brazil-santo-daime-udv-barquinha]] and Uniao do
Vegetal, and by its widespread adoption in global ethnobotanical and
psychonaut communities as a reliable source of DMT for ayahuasca analog
preparations.
