# [[psilocybin-mushroom-spore-print-identification]] Color Chart

Spore print color is the single most important macroscopic
identification feature for distinguishing psilocybin-containing
mushrooms from potentially dangerous lookalikes. The Psilocybin
Mushroom Image Guide documents species across several genera, each
with characteristic spore colors.

## Spore Print Colors by Genus

### Psilocybe Species

The core genus for psilocybin-containing mushrooms. All [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] and other Psilocybe
species documented in the guide produce dark spore prints:

- Spore print color: dark purple-brown to nearly black
- When fresh, spore deposits may appear purplish, becoming darker
  as they dry
- This dark spore print distinguishes Psilocybe from the lighter
  brown spore prints of many toxic lookalikes (Galerina,
  Conocybe)

Species in the guide with these spore characteristics:
P. azurescens, P. baeocystis, P. coprophila, P. crobulus,
P. cubensis, P. cyanescens, P. hoogshagenii, P. muscorum,
P. pelliculosa, P. samuiensis, P. semilanceata, P. subcubensis

### Panaeolus Species

The [[panaeolus-acuminatus]] and other Panaeolus genus documented in the guide includes:
P. campanulatus and P. subbalteatus (=cinctulus).

- Spore print color: black
- Gills are typically mottled due to uneven spore maturation,
  with dark mature spores alongside lighter immature ones
- The black spore print is distinct from the rusty brown of
  dangerous lookalikes like Panaeolina foenisecii

### Copelandia Species

Three Copelandia species are documented: C. bispora,
C. cambodgeniensis, and C. tropicalis.

- Spore print color: black
- Copelandia is sometimes treated as a section within Panaeolus
  due to shared spore characteristics
- C. cambodgeniensis is noted as the most potent of the
  Copelandia species

### Gymnopilus Species

[[gymnopilus-purpuratus-profile]] (Big Laughing Gym) and [[gymnopilus-genus-neurotropic-species]] are the Gymnopilus
species in the guide.

- Spore print color: rusty orange to reddish-brown
- This is notably different from the dark purple-brown to black
  spore prints of Psilocybe and Panaeolus
- The orange-brown spore print can help distinguish Gymnopilus
  from galerinas (which have rusty brown prints that may appear
  similar)
- Microscopic examination is more definitive for Gymnopilus
  identification

## How to Take a Spore Print

While the image guide is photographic, spore print analysis is a
standard companion technique. For [[psilocybin-mushroom-potency-and-dosage-guide]]
enthusiasts, accurate spore identification is essential safety practice:

1. Remove the cap from a fresh specimen
2. Place cap gill-side down on white paper (or half white, half
  black paper for dark-spored species)
3. Cover with a bowl or glass to prevent air disturbance
4. Allow 4-24 hours for spores to deposit
5. Compare resulting print color to known references

## Spore Color as a Discriminating Tool

[[gymnopilus-genus-neurotropic-species]] and other genera have distinct
spore colors that help discriminate species. The spore print
immediately eliminates many dangerous
possibilities:

- Dark purple-brown to black (Psilocybe, Panaeolus, Copelandia):
  consistent with psilocybin-containing species
- Rusty brown (Galerina): indicates potentially lethal amatoxin-
  containing species -- a critical warning signal
- White or cream (many other genera): not consistent with any
  psilocybin mushroom image guide spore color chart genus in the guide
- Rusty orange (Gymnopilus): requires additional identification
  beyond spore color alone

## [[sequestrate-fungi-taxonomy-geographic-distribution]] of Spore-Confirmed Species

The guide documents spore-characteristic species from these
regions: Hawaii (Copelandia), Germany's Black Forest
(P. cyanescens, P. semilanceata, P. subbalteatus), northern
California (P. cyanescens, G. spectabilis), Thailand-Koh Samui
(P. cubensis, P. samuiensis, P. subcubensis), Mexico
(P. hoogshagenii), Mendocino CA (G. spectabilis), and
Scandinavia-Norway (P. semilanceata).

## Interpreting Spore Print Results

When performing a spore print, consider these practical notes:

- Fresh specimens produce clearer, more abundant spore deposits
- Very young mushrooms may not have developed sufficient spores
- Humidity affects spore release; dry conditions may yield faint
  prints
- Dark-spored species (Psilocybe, Panaeolus) show best on white
  paper; lighter prints show better on dark surfaces
- The guide's dried specimens (especially P. cubensis) show
  residual spore deposits that can indicate print color even
  post-collection

## Spore Color and the Identification Hierarchy

Spore print color serves as the first branching point in most
[[mushroom-identification]] keys. For psilocybin mushroom hunters,
the decision tree from the guide's species works as follows:

1. Is the spore print dark (purple-brown to black)?
   - Yes: Proceed to Psilocybe, Panaeolus, or Copelandia
   - No, rusty brown: Could be Gymnopilus (check other features)
   - No, rusty brown with white ring: Warning -- could be
     Galerina (potentially lethal)
   - No, white or cream: Not a psilocybin mushroom from this
     guide's genera
