# Holzer Water Landscapes and Retention

[[comparison-sepp-holzer-vs-biodynamic]]'s concept of water landscapes is the foundational principle of his
approach to healing degraded land and restoring the hydrological-balance.
A water landscape consists of interconnected, decentralised retention spaces
built in cooperation with nature, designed to hold rainwater on the land and
allow it to seep slowly into the ground. Holzer argues that this is the single
most important step in earth restoration, representing 70% of the work needed
to heal a landscape.

## The Hydrological Foundation

Water is described as "the blood of the earth." Just as the human body needs
blood distributed evenly throughout, so does the body of the earth need water
spread across the landscape rather than concentrated in a few places. When
water is drained away on a large scale, as has happened through centuries of
land consolidation, road building, and river straightening, the subsoil suffers
and the entire ecological system degrades.

The [[fukuoka-natural-body-and-health]] of the earth acts as a storage organ for water. A naturally
built reservoir that allows water to seep into the ground has a balancing
effect on the climate. It stores rainwater and allows it to penetrate the
ground slowly. During the day the sun warms the water surface while deeper
layers stay cool. At night that heat is slowly released, and through dew
formation and evaporation the whole area is cooled and kept moist.

## Creating Decentralised Water Landscapes

Holzer advocates for many small retention spaces rather than large centralised
dams. A landscape containing several retention spaces has a reciprocal effect:
each retention space supports the others. A water landscape consisting of
several lakes, [[krameterhof-holzer-model-farm]] shows just how productive a water landscape is: fiscal
authorities determined the assessed tax value of the land to be ten times
higher than before. Projects in Spain and Portugal yield more vegetables from
the terraced banks than the whole properties used to produce before conversion,
with less work and at lower costs.

A water retention space serves multiple functions simultaneously:
- Fire protection for surrounding areas
- Flood prevention
- Irrigation reservoir for dry periods
- Watering place for animals
- Space for water gardening and aquaculture
- Fishery
- Habitat for waterfowl
- Recreational space for tourism

## Ten Percent Rule

For people wanting full restoration, Holzer estimates about 10% of the land
should be given to water. Depending on the landscape, climate, soil, and
geological conditions, possibly more. Annual rainfall needs to be factored in.
The building of water retention spaces and terraces using heavy machinery can
be considered part of transitional ethics.

## Relationship to Other Approaches

Holzer's water landscape approach shares principles with
[[mollison-permaculture-two-zone-planning-and-site-layout]]
planning by providing the water foundation that makes all other productive
zones possible.

The fundamental insight is that without water retention, no lasting fertility
or biodiversity is possible. Water is not just a resource to be used; it
is a living being that must be treated with respect and allowed to follow its
natural pathways through the landscape.
