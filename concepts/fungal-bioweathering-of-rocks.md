## Fungal Bioweathering of Rocks

## Overview

Fungi are among the most significant biological agents of rock weathering and mineral dissolution on Earth. Through a combination of mechanical and biochemical mechanisms, fungi contribute to the breakdown of rocks and minerals, playing a critical role in soil formation (pedogenesis), nutrient cycling, and the global carbon cycle. Their filamentous growth habit, ability to colonize rock surfaces and interiors, and production of diverse organic acids and chelating agents make them exceptionally effective at transforming mineral substrates.

## Mechanical Weathering

### Hyphal Penetration

Fungal hyphae can exert considerable physical force on rock and mineral surfaces. As hyphae grow into cracks, pores, and mineral grain boundaries, the turgor pressure within the hyphal tip — estimated at several atmospheres — can physically wedge apart mineral grains and widen existing fractures. This process is particularly important in porous rocks such as sandstone and limestone.

### Biomass Expansion

As fungal colonies expand within rock fissures and along mineral surfaces, the cumulative biomass creates mechanical stress. Swelling of hyphal cells, production of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), and the formation of dense biofilms all contribute to physical disruption of rock integrity. In some cases, the combined effect of hyphal penetration and biomass accumulation can dislodge mineral fragments visible to the naked eye.

### Limitations of Mechanical Action

Mechanical weathering alone is generally insufficient to explain the full extent of mineral dissolution observed in fungal colonization. Physical disruption primarily prepares rock surfaces by increasing surface area and exposing fresh mineral faces, but the actual chemical dissolution of mineral lattices requires biochemical mechanisms.

## Biochemical Weathering Mechanisms

### Organic Acid Production

Fungi secrete a wide range of low-molecular-weight organic acids that are primary agents of mineral dissolution:

- **Oxalic acid**: The most commonly produced fungal acid, oxalate forms strong soluble complexes with calcium, iron, and aluminum. Calcium [[crystal-structure-and-crystal-defects]] accelerates dissolution, releasing both iron and associated cations.

- **Phosphate minerals**: Apatite and other [[fungal-roles-in-phosphorus-solubilization]] from mineral sources is one of the most agriculturally significant aspects of fungal weathering, as plant-available phosphorus is frequently the limiting nutrient in terrestrial ecosystems.

- **Carbonate minerals**: Calcite and dolomite dissolve readily in the presence of fungal organic acids through acid neutralization and calcium complexation. While carbonates are relatively soft and weather quickly, fungal colonization accelerates the process substantially compared to purely abiotic dissolution.

## Lichen-Associated Weathering

Lichens — symbiotic associations between fungi (mycobionts) and photosynthetic partners (photobionts) — are among the most aggressive biological weathering agents on exposed rock surfaces. The fungal partner contributes biochemical weathering through organic acid secretion, while the photosynthetic partner supplies carbohydrates that fuel acid production and may contribute carbon dioxide, forming carbonic acid in micro-environments. Lichen weathering is responsible for significant mineral transformation in Arctic, alpine, and desert environments.

### Depth Profile Effects

Fungal weathering is not limited to the soil surface. Mycorrhizal hyphae extend meters into the soil profile and can access mineral horizons far below the organic-rich topsoil. Ectomycorrhizal fungi in particular are known to colonize mineral particles at significant depth, creating "weathering fronts" that contribute to the development of distinct soil horizons (O, A, E, B, C profiles) through differential mineral dissolution.

## Environmental Significance

### Carbon Cycle

Mineral weathering by fungi links the biological carbon cycle to the geological carbon cycle. The production of organic acids from photosynthetically fixed carbon drives mineral dissolution, which in turn consumes atmospheric CO₂ through the weathering reaction. Fungal carbon allocated belowground for weathering represents a significant — but still poorly quantified — carbon flux.
