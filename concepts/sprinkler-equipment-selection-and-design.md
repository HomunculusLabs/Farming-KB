## Sprinkler Equipment Selection and [[macrofungal-sampling-protocol-design-plot-selection]]

Choosing the right sprinkler equipment makes the difference between uniform irrigation and a garden with drowned spots alongside parched ones. Most consumer-grade sprinklers are designed for lawn convenience, not garden efficiency. Understanding sprinkler physics — nozzle diameter, operating pressure, throw radius, and overlap — allows gardeners to select or assemble systems that work properly.

## The Fundamental Problem: Circular Distribution

No sprinkler can uniformly spread water over a circular area. With a circular pattern, a sprinkler puts nearly ten times as much water on the perimeter as it does in the center. Every point in between receives a different amount. This is a geometric inevitability — the area of a circle grows with the square of the radius (πr²), so the outermost ring contains vastly more area than the innermost ring.

### The Solution: Overlap

The only way to achieve uniform coverage is to operate several correctly spaced sprinklers simultaneously, creating overlapping water patterns. The optimal spacing between sprinklers is about 60 to 70% of the sprinkler's throw radius.

### Square vs. Triangular Patterns

- **Triangular patterns**: Spread water slightly more uniformly
- **Square patterns**: May lend itself better to backyard layouts
- The shorter the sprinkler's throw radius, the smaller the differences between fringes, making short-radius, low-angle sprinklers preferable

## Nozzle Diameter Matters

### The Sweet Spot: 7/64 Inch (2.75 mm)

For [[dryland-vegetable-gardening]], sprinkler systems designed around this nozzle size cover the largest ground while using the smallest possible number of gallons per minute. This size allows the lowest practical [[compost-tea-application-rates]].

### Comparison of Nozzle Sizes

| Nozzle Diameter | Pressure (PSI) | Flow (GPM) | Suitability |
|----------------|---------------|------------|-------------|
| 3/64 inch | 30 | 0.45 | Very low rate, many heads needed |
| 5/64 inch | 30 | 0.79 | Low rate, good for heavy soils |
| 7/64 inch | 30 | 1.94 | **Ideal for veggie gardens** |
| 7/64 inch | 60 | 2.66 | Ideal with higher pressure |
| 3/16 inch+ | 30+ | 3.5+ | Too much — large droplets, compaction |

### Why Smaller Is Better (Within Reason)

Smaller bore nozzles:
1. Produce lighter droplets that don't compact soil or form crusts
2. Have shorter throw radius, keeping water where you need it and off buildings and noncritical vegetation
3. Result in lower [[ingham-compost-tea-application-rates-and-timing]], reducing leaching
4. Allow more precise coverage in tight backyard situations

### Why Bigger Is Worse

Larger bore nozzles (>7/64 inch):
1. Emit massive droplets that cause [[soil-compaction]], reducing root penetration and making cultivation and weeding more difficult
2. May contribute to forming soil crusts
3. Require larger water supply than most home gardeners have (5+ GPM per sprinkler)
4. Are designed for pastures, golf courses, and corn fields, not [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]]

## Operating Pressure

### Matching Pressure to Nozzle

Crop-sprinkler nozzles are designed to spray properly at pressures ranging from 10 to 100 PSI, with most requiring 30 to 60 PSI. Matching the nozzle bore to the water pressure is critical:

- **Too low pressure**: The stream doesn't break up and diffuse properly. An undiffused stream carries water to the fringes but little water is laid down between center and fringe. The throw may be longer than expected, but coverage is extremely uneven.
- **Too high pressure**: The stream breaks up too finely, shortening the throw and increasing the rate of application near the sprinkler.

### Typical Home Water Pressure

After piped water gets past the house's pressure regulator, pressure is usually between 30 and 45 PSI. Water mains can be considerably higher or sometimes lower. Homesteaders with their own electric pumps can choose their pressure.

**Rule**: Do not attempt to use sprinklers demanding higher pressure than you have.

## High-Angle vs. Low-Angle Sprinklers

### High-Angle Sprinklers

- Allow the stream to go its maximum distance
- Cover the largest area with fewest heads
- Draw the least GPM, resulting in lower application rates
- **Strongly affected by wind** — dispersion, off-course blowing, evaporation
- Best for: homesteaders with large gardens and low-yielding wells who can water at night or early morning

### Low-Angle Sprinklers

- Throw at only a few degrees above horizontal
- Radius is shortened, stream kept close to ground, out of strongest wind gusts
- Better for windy positions or daytime use
- Better in tight backyard situations
- More sprinklers needed to cover a given area, resulting in somewhat higher precipitation rates
- **Naan 501 series** sprinklers have low-angle nozzles

## Spacing Guidelines

| Sprinkler Type | Max Spacing (% of radius) |
|---------------|--------------------------|
| High-angle | ~65% of radius |
| Low-angle | ~75% of radius |

## Part-Circle Sprinklers

Some gardeners try to economize by locating sprinklers at garden edges, using part-circle heads. This practice has more disadvantages than advantages:

1. Cutting the arc in half doubles the rate of application — the nozzle puts out a powerful stream
2. Part-circle sprinklers have a larger bore nozzle to create enough force to operate the part-circle mechanism, producing high application rates and big droplets
3. When the sprinkler reverses at the end of its arc, it dumps a lot more water at the turnaround points

## The Fringe Strategy

The less-watered fringes between sprinkler patterns can be useful:
- For locating tall perennials (raspberries, climbing beans)
- Especially effective with low-angle sprinklers
- Putting a line of raspberries along the beginning of the dry fringe means the hedge gets about as much water as the middle of the sprinklers' patterns

## Impromptu Systems

Gardeners who know what they want to accomplish can reach their goal without ideal equipment. One good sprinkler head on a tall stand supplied by an ordinary hose, moved around the garden and run for equal periods in carefully determined positions, can achieve uniform irrigation.

### DIY Stand Construction

A simple stand can be made from:
- A sack of ready-mix concrete (base)
- A five-gallon plastic bucket (form)
- Four feet (120 cm) of galvanized pipe (riser)
- A few fittings

## Microirrigation

A hybrid between drip and sprinkling using low-pressure plastic tubing, cheap fittings, plastic spikes, and miniature short-radius sprinkler heads with emission rates measured in gallons per hour (not per minute). These are inexpensive alternatives for:
- Under orchard trees
- In vineyards
- Around ornamental beds
- Inside tunnel cloches

**Warning**: Use very effective filters — the nozzles are extremely fine and clog easily. Be wary about getting uniform water application.
