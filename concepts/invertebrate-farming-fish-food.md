# Invertebrate Farming for Fish Food

The single greatest ongoing cost of fish culture is food.
Pelleted commercial feeds typically represent 60–70% of total
production expenses.

By developing on-site systems for cultivating high-protein
invertebrates, fish farmers can dramatically reduce costs while
improving fish health and quality.

The key metric is the **food quotient (FQ)** — the ratio of food
weight given to fish weight gained.

## The Food Quotient

> FQ = weight of food given ÷ weight of fish gained

For comparison:

- **Insect larvae**: FQ = 1.8
- **Fish waste as feed**: FQ = 2.0
- **Guinea grass (terrestrial forage)**: FQ = 48.0

Insect larvae convert to fish flesh roughly 27 times more
efficiently than grass, making invertebrate cultivation arguably
the most important design element for economic aquaculture.

## Cockroaches, Mealworms, and Sowbugs

Food waste or flour scattered under leaves and seeded with
cockroaches or sowbugs creates a self-renewing protein source.

A proven Hawaiian system uses a Hibiscus-leaf cockroach mulch
heap: half-turning the pile every few days lets ducks harvest
cockroaches directly, and their manure stimulates pond plankton.

A "sandwich" mound of boards, paper, and leaves breeds sowbugs
and earwigs, which can be sieved out or harvested by chickens.

Eelgrass (Zostera) provides an excellent base for sowbug
colonies.

## Termites and Locusts

A perforated 200-litre drum filled with paper, old wood,
cardboard, and straw, then watered, attracts termites in
tropical areas or can be seeded with sowbugs in cool climates.

Plague locusts up to the 4th or 5th instar form flightless
ground swarms that can be vacuumed or trawled with a side-towed
bar and net through grasslands.

Frozen or dried, they are ideal protein food.

Fermented locusts store indefinitely.

Light-and-fan floats attract night-flying insects to the water
surface; yellow floats specifically attract grasshoppers.

## Pasture Grubs

Pasture grubs (larvae of Aphodius, Phyllophaga, and other
beetles) occur at extraordinary densities — 10 tonnes per
hectare is not uncommon in the top 3 cm of soil.

Strips of pasture can be skimmed, sieved, and grubs separated
by salt-water floatation.

At FQ 2–3, every hectare of pasture should produce 2–3 tonnes
of trout or other high-value fish.

Adult beetles cluster around lights and can be trapped in
funnel-and-drum systems.

## Snail Cultivation

A well-limed area planted with arum lily, nasturtium,
horseradish, brassica, broad beans, and cucurbit vines, seeded
with snails, builds dense populations over several years.

Arum or [[agapanthus]] clumps every 10 m² form permanent harbours.

The large tropical snail Achatina thrives on papaya with a
ground cover of cucurbits and nasturtium.

Desert snails gather on fence posts in swales to avoid soil
heat and can be collected from there.

## Zooplankton Culture

Water fleas, cyclops, ostracods, and rotifers can be cultured in
small ponds or tanks supplied with lettuce, potato, crushed sugar
cane, manioc, or legume leaves.

A shallow screened bay off fry ponds works well — plankton swim
through screens into fry ponds while enriched conditions produce
ample food.

Midge larvae and tubifex worms in rich, shallow, organic ponds
supply essential fry food.

Brine shrimp (Artemia) from salt pans are another critical fry
food — one of the very few productive uses of saline inland
ponds.

## Blow Flies and Worm Beds

Blow flies colonize waste meats or carcasses suspended over ponds.
Near-putrid shallows with kitchen sink water breed "gentles"
(Tubifera tenax fly larvae) in muddy bases at 1–2 cm depth.

Worm beds with hay, food scraps, or manure are invaluable —
worms are collected by periodic flooding.

The hessian covers can be immersed in or suspended over ponds,
allowing fish to feed directly.

## Aquatic Molluscs and Edge Plants

Molluscs (Physa, Limnaea, Bythinia, Vivipara, Pisidium,
Sphaerium) form a large part of fish diets and are easily
cultivated on vegetation in organic shallows.

For herbivorous species like [[tilapia]], semi-rampant plants
such as nasturtium, [[tradescantia]], [[dolichos]], and [[comfrey]] supply
hardy, palatable food directly.

In channel cultures, banks planted with these species become
a complete food supply, eliminating external feed inputs.
