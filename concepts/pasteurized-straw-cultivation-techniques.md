# Pasteurized Straw Cultivation Techniques

In commercial mushroom production, cereal straw is one of the most
frequently used [[bulk-substrate-preparation]]
materials. Not only does straw form the basis for mushroom composts,
but it is also used uncomposted as the sole ingredient for growing
various mushroom species. Chapter VI of The Mushroom Cultivator provides
detailed procedures for straw preparation and pasteurization.

## Straw Selection and Preparation

All types of straw are more or less suitable, but most growers use
**wheat straw** because of its coarse fiber and wide availability. The
straw should be:

-   Clean and free from molds.
-   Unspoiled by any preliminary decomposition.
-   Dry when stored; protected from moisture.

Preparation involves **chopping or shredding** the dry straw into 1-3
inch pieces. Tools include a wood chipper, garden compost shredder, or
power mower. Shredding increases moisture absorption by expanding the
available surface area and increases the density of the substrate mass.

## Pasteurization Methods

### Live Steam Method (Commercial Scale)

1.  Pre-soak straw to approximately 75% moisture content.
2.  Fill into a tunnel or steam room.
3.  Steam at 140-150°F for 2-4 hours.
4.  Cool to 80°F and spawn.

An **alternative program** extends the process: after the high-
temperature pasteurization, hold at 122°F for 12-24 hours. This promotes
beneficial microbial growth, giving the straw a higher degree of
selectivity for mushroom mycelium.

### Hot Water Bath Method (Home Cultivator)

The method best suited to the home cultivator uses simple equipment:

**Equipment needed:**
-   55-gallon drum (metal, suitable for heating).
-   Propane burner or heat source.
-   Wire mesh basket (hardware cloth) that fits inside the drum.
-   Weight to keep straw submerged.

**Procedure:**
1.  Fill the drum half-full with water.
2.  Heat water to 160-170°F.
3.  Place chopped dry straw into the wire mesh basket.
4.  Submerge the basket in the hot water. Use a weight to keep the straw
    fully underwater.
5.  Soak for 30-45 minutes at temperature.
6.  Remove the basket and allow the straw to drain thoroughly. It is very
    important to let all loose water run off.
7.  Spread the drained straw on a clean surface and cool to 80°F or less.
8.  Spawn when cooled.

## Spawning and Container Filling

Once cooled, the pasteurized straw is evenly mixed with [[grain-spawn-preparation]] and filled into:

-   **Trays** — traditional container for indoor cultivation.
-   **Shelves** — stationary growing surfaces in the growing room.
-   **Plastic bags** — the simplest and most efficient method.

### Plastic Bag Method

A five-gallon bag (1-2 mils thick) is well suited to most situations.
Two dozen nail-sized holes equally spaced around the bags provide
aeration. Some compression of the straw into the container is desirable
because cropping efficiency increases with substrate density.

Upon full colonization, the mycelia of species like *Pleurotus
ostreatus* and *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]* actually hold the straw together, at
which point the bag can be completely removed. Alternatively, perforate
or strip the bag from the top or side to allow easy cropping.

## Spawn Running Considerations

The same principles governing [[stamets-spawning-and-spawn-running]] apply to straw:

-   **Moisture content** is critical — deviations of more than 2% from
    optimum depress yields.
-   **Temperature** must be monitored in the substrate center (hottest
    point) and the room atmosphere.
-   **Dry weight per square foot** determines total yield potential.
-   Colonization must proceed as rapidly as possible to prevent
    competitors from becoming established.

The standard spawning rate is seven liters per ton of substrate or one
quart per eight square feet. Higher spawning rates lead to more rapid
colonization and greater protection against contaminants.

## Species Suited to Straw

Wheat straw prepared and pasteurized as described supports cultivation
of:

-   *[[pleurotus-ostreatus]]* (Oyster Mushroom)
-   *Stropharia rugoso-annulata* (Wine Cap)
-   *[[panaeolus-cyanescens]]* (Blue Meanies)
-   *Psilocybe cubensis* (Golden Teacher)

Many other species can likely utilize straw or modifications of it.
*[[volvariella-volvacea]]* ([[paddy-straw-mushroom]]) is traditionally grown on
rice straw in tropical climates.

## Supplementation and Yield Enhancement

Studies with *Pleurotus ostreatus* demonstrated yield increases with:

-   **20% grass meal** added prior to substrate pasteurization.
-   **Cottonseed meal** added after full spawn run — Bono (1978) obtained
    an 85% yield increase with *P. flabellatus* by adding 132 grams per
    kilogram of dry straw (approximately 22 grams crude protein per
    kilogram of straw).

Supplementation after full colonization is critical — adding nutrients
before the mycelium is established increases the risk of contamination.
Post-colonization supplementation also increased the protein content and
intensified the flavor of the mushrooms.

## Straw vs. Compost

Straw offers several advantages over [[stamets-non-composted-substrates]]:

-   Simpler preparation — no composting phase required.
-   Faster preparation — hours instead of weeks.
-   Lower equipment requirements.
-   Well suited to home cultivators and small-scale operations.

Disadvantages include lower nutrient density, less selectivity (fewer
beneficial microorganisms than compost), and generally lower yields per
pound of substrate compared to properly prepared compost.
