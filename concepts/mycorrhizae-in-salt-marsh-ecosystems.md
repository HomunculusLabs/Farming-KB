# Mycorrhizae in Salt Marsh Ecosystems

Salt marshes represent one of the most challenging environments for mycorrhizal symbioses.
Periodic tidal inundation, elevated soil salinity, and anoxic sediments create conditions that
generally suppress arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungal activity. Nevertheless, AM fungi persist
in these systems, colonizing key halophytic grasses and contributing to nutrient uptake, salt
tolerance, and ecosystem stability. This concept page synthesizes findings from Dighton's *Fungi
in Ecosystem Processes* on the distribution, function, and ecological significance of
mycorrhizae in salt marsh ecosystems.

## Distribution and Occurrence of AM Fungi in Salt Marshes

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] are present in salt marsh sediments, though their distribution is
heterogeneous and closely tied to sediment aeration. AM fungal structures are most commonly
found along natural channels and creek banks where faunal activity—particularly burrowing by
crabs, polychaetes, and other invertebrates—increases sediment aeration and promotes plant
litter decomposition. These oxygenated microsites create more favorable conditions for AM
fungal colonization than the bulk anoxic marsh sediment.

Mycorrhizal colonization extends to considerable depth in marsh soils. Cooke et al. (1993)
documented arbuscules to 37 cm depth and vesicles to 42 cm, indicating that AM fungi remain
active well below the surface despite the increasingly reducing conditions of deeper sediments.

## Species-Specific Colonization Patterns

Colonization levels vary markedly between dominant salt marsh grass species. *Spartina
alterniflora*, the foundational smooth cordgrass of Atlantic and Gulf coast marshes, exhibits
notably lower mycorrhizal colonization than *S. cynosuroides* (big cordgrass). In some studies,
*S. alterniflora* displayed periods of nonmycotrophy—essentially abandoning the mycorrhizal
symbiosis—during periods of maximal plant nutrient demand. This nonmycotrophic behavior
suggests that under certain environmental conditions, the carbon cost of maintaining AM fungi
may outweigh the nutritional benefits for this species.

## Salinity Effects on AM Fungal Function

Soil salinity is a primary constraint on AM fungal activity in salt marshes. Juniper and Abbott
(1993) demonstrated that elevated salinity directly reduces AM spore germination and hyphal
growth, limiting the ability of fungi to establish and spread in saline sediments. Despite these
inhibitory effects, mycorrhizae can confer significant benefits to host plants under salt stress:

- **Salt exclusion:** Rozema et al. (1986) found that mycorrhizal *Aster tripolium* plants
  maintained lower sodium (Na) concentrations in shoots at 300 mM NaCl compared to
  non-mycorrhizal controls, suggesting that AM fungi help regulate ion uptake and reduce
  sodium toxicity in aboveground tissues.

- **Enhanced oxygen uptake and salt resistance:** Hyde et al. (1998) reported that
  mycorrhizae enhance oxygen uptake capacity and overall salt resistance in host plants,
  a critical function in the frequently waterlogged and hypoxic conditions of marsh sediments.

## Phosphorus Dynamics and Nutrient Limitation

Phosphorus is widely recognized as the primary limiting nutrient in salt marsh ecosystems
(Valiela and Teal 1974). Given that P acquisition is a canonical function of AM fungi, the
symbiosis would seem especially relevant in these P-limited systems. However, the benefits
and costs of salt marsh mycorrhizae remain poorly understood (Brown and Bledsoe 1996).

Buresh et al. (1980) conducted a phosphorus fertilization study in Louisiana marshes and
observed that P addition increased tissue P content of *Spartina* but did not produce
corresponding increases in plant growth—a phenomenon described as "luxuriant" consumption.
This suggests that factors beyond P availability, such as nitrogen limitation or salinity
stress, may constrain growth even when P is abundant.

McHugh (2001) investigated the interacting effects of tidal inundation, salinity, phosphorus
addition, and AM inoculum on salt marsh plants in a greenhouse study. The results highlighted
the complex interplay of these factors: tidal regime and salinity modulated mycorrhizal
effectiveness, while P availability influenced the degree to which plants benefited from the
symbiosis.

## Nitrogen Assimilation and Tillering

Beyond phosphorus, mycorrhizal colonization has been shown to enhance total nitrogen (N)
assimilation in *S. cynosuroides*. Increased N uptake by mycorrhizal plants may be
particularly significant given that nitrogen can co-limit productivity in many marshes.

Mycorrhizal colonization also stimulates increased tillering (the production of additional
shoots from the base of the plant) in salt marsh grasses. This tillering response has been
observed across multiple coastal and wetland systems, including dune grasses (Gemma and Koske
1997) and wetland rice (Solaiman and Hirata 1998), suggesting that enhanced clonal growth is a
general benefit of mycorrhizal association in graminoids of water-stressed or periodically
inundated habitats.

## Role in Revegetation and Restoration

Mycorrhizae are increasingly recognized as important components of salt marsh restoration and
revegetation efforts. Jasper (1994) identified four key reasons why mycorrhizae are critical
in revegetation programs:

1. **Improved nutrient acquisition** during the critical establishment phase, when seedlings
   have limited root systems and are most vulnerable to nutrient stress.

2. **Enhanced drought and salinity tolerance**, enabling transplanted propagules to survive
   the harsh osmotic conditions characteristic of restored marsh sites.

3. **Increased plant diversity** by facilitating the establishment of mycorrhiza-dependent
   species that would otherwise fail to colonize bare sediments.

4. **Soil stabilization** through hyphal networks that bind soil particles and reduce erosion,
   accelerating the development of stable marsh surfaces.

## Interplant Linkages and Ecosystem Connectivity

The concept of interplant carbon and nutrient transfer through shared [[mycorrhizal-networks]],
first demonstrated by Bjorkman (1960) using radioactive carbon tracing from pine to the
achlorophyllous orchid *Monotropa*, may have implications for salt marsh plant communities.
If AM hyphal networks connect neighboring *Spartina* individuals or different plant species
within the marsh, they could facilitate resource sharing that buffers spatial heterogeneity in
nutrient availability—a possibility that warrants further investigation in these tidally
driven systems.
