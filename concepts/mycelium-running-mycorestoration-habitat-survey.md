# Mycorestoration Habitat Survey and Strategy

## Overview

Mycorestoration is the use of fungi to repair or restore weakened immune systems
of environments. Whether habitats have been damaged by human activity or natural
disaster, saprophytic, endophytic, mycorrhizal, and in some cases parasitic
fungi can aid recovery. The practice involves surveying damaged habitats,
selecting appropriate fungal species, and deploying mycelial inoculum to steer
ecological recovery. Mycorestoration encompasses four primary methods:
[[mycoforestry]], [[mycopesticides]].

## The Concept of Habitat Immune Systems

Stamets proposes that habitats, like people, have immune systems that become
weakened due to stress, disease, or exhaustion. On land, all life springs from
soil, which Stamets calls "ecological currency." Overspending or depleting soil
causes environmental bankruptcy. Mycorestoration recognizes the primary role
fungi play in determining the balance of biological populations and seeks to
reinforce the fungal foundations of ecosystem health.

As generations of mycelia cycle through a habitat, soil depth and moisture
increase, enhancing the carrying capacity and diversity of the environment. The
early introduction of primary saprophytes, which are among the first organisms
to rejuvenate the food chain after catastrophe, can determine the course of
biological communities through thoughtfully matching mycelia with compatible
plants, insects, and other organisms.

## Surveying the Habitat

The first step is a comprehensive habitat survey. Resident species are nature's
recommendations. Key questions include:

1. **What species are already present?** Mushrooms growing in damaged habitats
   tolerate existing conditions and may metabolize toxins as nutrients.
2. **Is the habitat mycologically neutral?** If no mushrooms are evident,
   species must be imported as spawn.
3. **What is the debris field?** Available organic material determines what
   substrate can carry mycelium.
4. **Is the problem seasonal or continuous?** Rain patterns affect contaminant
   outflow rates.
5. **What is the scale?** Mycelial mats must match the contamination source.

## Native Fungi as Restoration Guides

A field survey may reveal mushrooms already countering a pathogen. Mushrooms in
contaminated areas are naturally selected and predominate over less-adapted
species. These are prime candidates for propagation. In the laboratory, cultures
can be tested using standard agar well assays for antimicrobial activity or
toxin sensitivity. Often, a native fungus corrects imbalances more effectively
than imported species, having already adapted to local conditions.

## Post-Fire Recovery as a Model

Nature provides a model in post-fire landscapes. After forest fires, mushrooms
are among the first organisms to appear, particularly morels (Morchella) and cup
fungi (Auricularia). These fast-growing mushrooms release spores and fragrances
that attract insects and mammals. Each organism imports hitchhiking species from
afar. Flies deposit larvae in morels, attracting birds that defecate seeds of
plants from distant areas. As animals crisscross the terrain, they leave
invisible trails of spores, creating interlacing biological pathways with
mushrooms serving as biodiversity pioneers.

## Synergistic Species Combinations

Each mushroom species secretes unique mixes of extracellular enzymes and acids
for degrading organic matter. Using a plurality of species can have a synergistic
effect for more complete degradation of toxins than could be achieved with one
species alone. The art of mycorestoration lies in:

- **Species selection**: Matching fungal capabilities to specific contaminants
  or restoration needs
- **Timing**: Introducing species at the right moment in the ecological
  succession
- **Keystone species deployment**: Using fungi as keystone organisms to enable
  subsequent populations and create unique habitats

## Methods of Introducing Beneficial Fungi

Multiple techniques exist for expanding a helpful mushroom's domain in a
damaged habitat:

- **[[mycofiltration-silt-erosion-control]]
- **Stem butt transfers**: Harvested mushroom stem bases placed in new
  locations to regrow mycelium
- **Spore slurries**: Water suspensions of spores collected from mature
  mushrooms, spread over target areas
- **Pure culture spawn**: Laboratory-propagated mycelium on grain or sawdust
  for precise species deployment
- **Sheet mulch**: Shallow compost beds 6 inches to 2 feet thick, inoculated
  with mycelium to capture microbial outflow
