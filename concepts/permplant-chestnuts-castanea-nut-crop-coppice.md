## Chestnuts as Multipurpose Nut Crop in Permaculture

Chestnuts (Castanea spp) are close relatives of the oaks, belonging to
the family Fagaceae. They are long-lived, deciduous, drought-resistant
trees that thrive on well-drained and preferably light soils. With a
fair lime tolerance but inability to withstand salinity, chestnuts have
been valued for millennia as a |staple carbohydrate source and [[fungal-roles-in-old-growth-forests]] wood is of little use
except as firewood. This is the primary commercial chestnut species.

### Japanese Chestnut (Castanea crenata)
A smaller tree which coppices well. Strains have been developed which
produce excellent crops of nuts. Generally easier to establish and
earlier bearing than the European species.

### Chinese Chestnut (Castanea mollissima)
Grows to about 20 metres and has a sweeter tasting nut than the
European chestnut. It is much more resistant to the root fungus
Phytophthora spp, which can kill trees, making it a valuable rootstock
and breeding parent.

### American Chestnut (Castanea dentata)
Once one of the major deciduous trees of [[jadam-crop-rotation-critique]] Species: Chinquapins (Chrysolepis spp)

Chrysolepis chrysophylla (golden chinquapin) is very closely related to
chestnuts but is evergreen. Native to mountainous western North America,
it may grow to 40 metres. The sweet nuts are hard-shelled and about 10
mm long.

Chrysolepis sempervirens (bush chinquapin) is an evergreen shrub suited
to lower elevations. The sweet nuts resemble filberts and can be eaten
raw or roasted.

## Timber Uses

Young chestnut timber is tough and durable, making it valuable for
fencing, vineyard posts, and outdoor construction. In Europe, chestnut
coppice has been managed for centuries to produce poles, stakes, and
small timber on short rotations. The |coppice system
provides a sustainable yield of useful wood while maintaining nut
production from standard trees.
