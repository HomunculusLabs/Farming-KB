## Companion Cultivation with Garden Vegetables

The garden giant has a long history of use in intercropping systems.
In eastern Europe (particularly Hungary), farmers inoculate straw with
garden giant mycelium and mix it with corn stubble, ploughing it into
fallow fields every few years. When fields are replanted, garden giants
emerge between rows of corn and tend to perpetuate themselves.

## Ecological Benefits

The garden giant provides multiple [[biodiversity-of-fungi-ecosystem-services-agriculture]]:

- **Soil building**: Foot-deep beds of inoculated wood chips decompose
  into 1-2 inches of rich soil within 2-3 years
- **[[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]]**: Rhizomorphic mycelium grips substrates and
  stabilizes slopes
- **Water filtration**: Mycelial mats consume bacteria and filter
  pathogens from runoff
- **Biodiversity support**: Larvae in mature mushrooms feed fish and
  birds, creating food chain connections
- **Carbon cycling**: [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]] releases cool CO2 gradually,
  unlike combustion which releases hot CO2 rapidly

## As a Food Source

Beyond its ecological role, the garden giant is edible and produces
mammoth fruitings when well-established. Stamets reports harvesting
enormous mushrooms from patches that expanded from 4 feet to 30 feet in
diameter within 4 months. The mushrooms also served as fish food:
salmon fingerlings learned to bump floating mushrooms to dislodge
fly larvae living inside them.

## The Mycelial Wave Method

Stamets's preferred method for large-scale garden giant deployment is
creating parallel horizontal waves of mycelium combined with fragmented
mycelium interspersed between layers. As the mycelial planes and
fragments seek each other and connect, the organism strengthens.
Once a mycelial wave begins, its cellular momentum crosses over
inhospitable barriers, with the strength of projection proportional to
the mycelial mat's ability to sequester and channel nutrients to its
advancing tips.
