# Fungal Plant Diseases

Fungal pathogens are among the most common and destructive plant diseases in both home gardens and commercial agriculture. They spread via spores carried by wind, water, insects, and contaminated tools. Most fungal [[dighton-fungal-mediated-plant-competition]] diseases thrive in warm, humid conditions with poor air circulation.

## Major Fungal Diseases

### Powdery Mildew

Caused by Erysiphales order fungi. Appears as white-to-grayish powdery patches on leaf surfaces, stems, and occasionally fruit. Unlike most fungi, powdery mildew does not require free water on leaf surfaces -- it actually prefers dry conditions with high humidity around the plant. Common hosts include squash, cucumbers, roses, phlox, and lilacs.

Symptoms progress from isolated spots to full leaf coverage, causing yellowing, curling, and premature leaf drop. Severe infections reduce photosynthesis and yield significantly.

Management:
- Choose resistant varieties when available
- Ensure adequate spacing (12-18 inches minimum for most crops)
- Apply potassium bicarbonate spray (1 tbsp per gallon of water)
- Neem oil as a preventive foliar spray
- Remove and destroy infected plant debris -- do not compost

### Downy Mildew

Caused by oomycetes (Peronosporales), which are water molds rather than true fungi. Produces yellowish-green angular lesions on upper leaf surfaces with grayish-purple fuzzy growth on the undersides. Requires free moisture on leaves for infection.

Common on grapes, cucurbits, basil, and onions. Favored by cool, wet nights (below 65F) and warm days.

Management:
- Avoid overhead watering; use drip irrigation
- Improve air circulation with proper spacing and pruning
- Copper-based fungicides (Bordeaux mix) as preventive
- Remove infected leaves immediately
- Rotate crops -- oospores persist in soil for years

### Early Blight (Alternaria solani)

Characterized by dark brown concentric-ring lesions ("bullseye" pattern) on lower leaves, progressing upward. Common on tomatoes, potatoes, and eggplants. Favored by warm temperatures (75-85F) with alternating wet and dry periods.

Management:
- Mulch around base of plants to prevent soil splash
- Remove lower leaves showing first symptoms
- Apply copper fungicide at first sign
- Practice 3-year rotation with non-solanaceous crops
- Use drip irrigation to keep foliage dry

### Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans)

An oomycete responsible for the Irish Potato Famine. Causes rapid, water-soaked dark lesions on leaves, stems, and fruit that expand quickly in cool, wet conditions (60-70F). White fuzzy growth appears on lesion margins during humid conditions. Can destroy entire tomato or potato crops within days.

Management:
- Destroy infected plants immediately -- do not compost
- Plant resistant varieties (e.g., 'Defiant' tomato)
- Preventive copper or chlorothalonil sprays during cool wet periods
- Avoid overhead irrigation
- Harvest potatoes early if forecast is cool and wet

### Anthracnose

Caused by Colletotrichum species. Produces small, dark, sunken spots with pinkish-orange spore masses on fruit, leaves, and stems. Common on tomatoes, peppers, beans, strawberries, and mangoes. Favored by warm, wet conditions.

Management:
- Remove and destroy infected fruit and debris
- Apply sulfur-based fungicides preventively
- Use drip irrigation
- Harvest fruit promptly to reduce infection window
- Choose resistant varieties when available

### Botrytis (Gray Mold)

Caused by Botrytis cinerea. Produces gray-brown fuzzy mold on dying or damaged plant tissue. Common in cool, humid conditions (58-72F) with poor air circulation. Affects strawberries, tomatoes, grapes, beans, and many ornamentals. Often a secondary pathogen that colonizes wounded tissue.

Management:
- Reduce humidity in greenhouse/grow space
- Increase air circulation with fans
- Remove dead or dying plant material promptly
- Space plants adequately
- Avoid wounding fruit during harvest
- Biocontrol with Trichoderma species

## General Prevention Strategies

- Rotate crops by family every 3-4 years
- Select disease-resistant varieties
- Maintain proper plant spacing for airflow
- Water at the base of plants, not overhead
- Sanitize tools between plants with 70% isopropyl alcohol
- Apply preventive biological controls (Trichoderma, Bacillus subtilis)
- Keep garden debris cleaned up between seasons
- Monitor plants regularly for early symptoms
