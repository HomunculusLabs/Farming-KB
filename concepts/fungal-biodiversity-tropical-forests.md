# Fungal Biodiversity in Tropical Forests ## Overview Tropical forests represent
the greatest reservoir of fungal biodiversity on Earth, yet they remain the
least explored. The extraordinary plant diversity of [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-ecosystems]]
supports correspondingly diverse fungal communities, though the precise
relationship between plant and [[fungal-species-richness-and-diversity-indices]] remains debated. Evidence
accumulated since Hawksworth's (1991) seminal 1.5 million species estimate,
especially from tropical plant studies, suggests that even this figure may be
too low. No single site in the world has been comprehensively surveyed for
fungi, and [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] present the largest gap in mycological knowledge. ##
Host Plant Diversity and Fungal Richness The Neotropics contain the highest
known alpha diversity of trees, with some lowland forest sites near Iquitos,
Peru, supporting nearly 300 species of woody plants per hectare (Gentry 1992).
At a Gabon forest site, 34% of recorded species were trees, 25% climbers, and
37% herbs or shrubs. This plant diversity creates a vast array of ecological
niches for fungal associates. Parasitic fungi associated with epiphytic plants
are poorly known and may represent another potentially diverse group in the
tropics. Sillett et al. (1995) recorded 127 bryophyte species in the inner
crowns of just six Ficus trees in Costa Rica. Epiphytes, while frequently
infrequent at any given study site, may harbor unique fungi, adding to the
challenge of tropical fungal inventory. ## Plant-Parasitic Fungi: Scale of
Diversity The scale of tropical fungal diversity becomes apparent through

species per host - For herbs and epiphytes: 5.42 fungal species per host Applied

Collections from tropical regions consistently reveal high proportions of
undescribed taxa. Durán's 22-year Mexico survey found 17% of species were new to
science. Such figures suggest that the actual proportion of undescribed tropical
fungi may be far higher.

## Research Priorities for Tropical Mycology

- Comprehensive taxonomic guides for tropical microfungi — a high priority in
fungal systematics
- Multi-year sampling programs to account for aseasonal and unpredictable
fruiting
- Canopy access programs for macrofungal and endophyte surveys
- Integration of molecular methods with traditional morphology-based surveys
- Host-specificity studies across multiple plant families and tissue types
- Long-term [[mycorrhiza-bioassays-ecological-studies]] of [[dighton-fungal-succession-disturbance]] on
tropical litter
- Biogeographic surveys comparing fungal communities across tropical regions

## Canopy Fungal Communities

The forest canopy is a vast and largely unexplored fungal habitat. Tropical
canopy ecosystems contain epiphytic bryophytes, lichens, and accumulations of
organic matter in branch axils and tree holes that support diverse fungal
assemblages including endophytes, saprotrophs on dead twigs, and parasites.
Access limitations have historically restricted surveys, but canopy cranes,
rope climbing, and fogging have begun to reveal the extent of diversity. Canopy
fungal communities differ substantially from forest floor communities, with many
species restricted to the canopy environment.

## Molecular Approaches to Tropical Fungal Diversity

Culture-independent methods have revolutionized tropical fungal diversity
studies. High-throughput sequencing of environmental DNA routinely recovers
operational taxonomic units (OTUs) that cannot be assigned to described species.
In tropical soils, unassigned sequences can exceed 70%, compared to 30-50% in
[[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]]. Metabarcoding using ITS primers is the standard approach, but
many tropical species are absent from reference databases, and high diversity
means large sequencing efforts capture only a fraction of total communities.
Combining molecular surveys with voucher-based morphological studies remains
essential for building the taxonomic framework needed to interpret sequence data.

## Threats to Tropical Fungal Diversity

Tropical deforestation represents the greatest threat to undocumented fungal
diversity. When forest is cleared, entire fungal communities are lost before
cataloguing — species go extinct before being described ("centinelan
extinction"). Climate change adds a compounding threat: many tropical fungi have
