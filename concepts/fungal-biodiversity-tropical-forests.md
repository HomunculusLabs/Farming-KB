# Fungal Biodiversity in Tropical Forests ## Overview Tropical forests represent the greatest reservoir of fungal biodiversity on Earth, yet they remain the least explored. The extraordinary plant diversity of tropical ecosystems supports correspondingly diverse fungal communities, though the precise relationship between plant and fungal species richness remains debated. Evidence accumulated since Hawksworth's (1991) seminal 1.5 million species estimate, especially from tropical plant studies, suggests that even this figure may be too low. No single site in the world has been comprehensively surveyed for fungi, and tropical regions present the largest gap in mycological knowledge. ## Host Plant Diversity and Fungal Richness The Neotropics contain the highest known alpha diversity of trees, with some lowland forest sites near Iquitos, Peru, supporting nearly 300 species of woody plants per hectare (Gentry 1992). At a Gabon forest site, 34% of recorded species were trees, 25% climbers, and 37% herbs or shrubs. This plant diversity creates a vast array of ecological niches for fungal associates. Parasitic fungi associated with epiphytic plants are poorly known and may represent another potentially diverse group in the tropics. Sillett et al. (1995) recorded 127 bryophyte species in the inner crowns of just six Ficus trees in Costa Rica. Epiphytes, while frequently infrequent at any given study site, may harbor unique fungi, adding to the challenge of tropical fungal inventory. ## Plant-Parasitic Fungi: Scale of Diversity The scale of tropical fungal diversity becomes apparent through host-parasite ratio calculations: - Strong and Levin (1979) estimated 15.41 parasitic fungal species per tree species - For shrubs and vines: 7.05 fungal species per host - For herbs and epiphytes: 5.42 fungal species per host Applied to a 13.4-ha Costa Rican forest tract with 87 overstory trees, 38 shrubs, and 10 vines, this yields an estimated 2,833 plant-parasitic fungal species in that single small tract — exceeding the total number of then-described fungal species. At a 110,000-ha scale, the estimate reaches 81,457 species. These figures illustrate how dramatically tropical fungal diversity is underestimated. ## Rust Fungi as Indicators Rusts may be the most representative group for understanding tropical plant-parasitic fungal diversity. Nishida (1989) noted that in Buriticá and Hennen's (1980) Neotropical study, one in three plant species was parasitized by a unique rust species. In contrast, only 0.3% of the estimated 88,000+ vascular plant species in Latin America are known hosts for smut fungi — a figure that reflects sampling deficiency rather than true absence. Powdery mildews reach their greatest diversity in temperate regions, not tropics. ## Endophytes in Tropical Forests Tropical forests harbor exceptionally rich [[endophytic-fungi]] relative to saprotrophs
- More rapid decomposition rates creating ephemeral substrata
- Greater microhabitat complexity within individual leaves and stems

Collections from tropical regions consistently reveal high proportions of undescribed taxa. Durán's 22-year Mexico survey found 17% of species were new to science. Such figures suggest that the actual proportion of undescribed tropical fungi may be far higher.

## Research Priorities for Tropical Mycology

- Comprehensive taxonomic guides for tropical microfungi — a high priority in fungal systematics
- Multi-year sampling programs to account for aseasonal and unpredictable fruiting
- Canopy access programs for macrofungal and endophyte surveys
- Integration of molecular methods with traditional morphology-based surveys
- Host-specificity studies across multiple plant families and tissue types
- Long-term ecological studies of [[dighton-fungal-succession-disturbance]] on tropical litter
- Biogeographic surveys comparing fungal communities across tropical regions
