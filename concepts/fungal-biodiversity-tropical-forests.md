# Fungal Biodiversity in Tropical Forests

## Overview

Tropical forests represent the greatest reservoir of fungal biodiversity on Earth, yet they remain the least explored. The extraordinary plant diversity of tropical ecosystems supports correspondingly diverse fungal communities, though the precise relationship between plant and fungal species richness remains debated. Evidence accumulated since Hawksworth's (1991) seminal 1.5 million species estimate, especially from tropical plant studies, suggests that even this figure may be too low. No single site in the world has been comprehensively surveyed for fungi, and tropical regions present the largest gap in mycological knowledge.

## Host Plant Diversity and Fungal Richness

The Neotropics contain the highest known alpha diversity of trees, with some lowland forest sites near Iquitos, Peru, supporting nearly 300 species of woody plants per hectare (Gentry 1992). At a Gabon forest site, 34% of recorded species were trees, 25% climbers, and 37% herbs or shrubs. This plant diversity creates a vast array of ecological niches for fungal associates.

Parasitic fungi associated with epiphytic plants are poorly known and may represent another potentially diverse group in the tropics. Sillett et al. (1995) recorded 127 bryophyte species in the inner crowns of just six Ficus trees in Costa Rica. Epiphytes, while frequently infrequent at any given study site, may harbor unique fungi, adding to the challenge of tropical fungal inventory.

## Plant-Parasitic Fungi: Scale of Diversity

The scale of tropical fungal diversity becomes apparent through host-parasite ratio calculations:
- Strong and Levin (1979) estimated 15.41 parasitic fungal species per tree species
- For shrubs and vines: 7.05 fungal species per host
- For herbs and epiphytes: 5.42 fungal species per host

Applied to a 13.4-ha Costa Rican forest tract with 87 overstory trees, 38 shrubs, and 10 vines, this yields an estimated 2,833 plant-parasitic fungal species in that single small tract — exceeding the total number of then-described fungal species. At a 110,000-ha scale, the estimate reaches 81,457 species. These figures illustrate how dramatically tropical fungal diversity is underestimated.

## Rust Fungi as Indicators

Rusts may be the most representative group for understanding tropical plant-parasitic fungal diversity. Nishida (1989) noted that in Buriticá and Hennen's (1980) Neotropical study, one in three plant species was parasitized by a unique rust species. In contrast, only 0.3% of the estimated 88,000+ vascular plant species in Latin America are known hosts for smut fungi — a figure that reflects sampling deficiency rather than true absence. Powdery mildews reach their greatest diversity in temperate regions, not tropics.

## Endophytes in Tropical Forests

Tropical forests harbor exceptionally rich [[endophytic-fungal-diversity]]. Arnold et al. (2000) found 242 endophyte species in Heisteria concinna leaves and 259 in Ouratea lucens leaves at a single Panamanian site. Euterpe oleracea leaves in Brazil yielded 62 species with 21 common (Rodrigues 1994). Tropical palms support diverse endophyte communities (Rodrigues and Samuels 1992; Fröhlich and Hyde 2000). The proportion of novel taxa discovered in tropical endophyte surveys consistently exceeds that of temperate surveys.

The transition zone between healthy and moribund tissue in tropical plants supports particularly diverse fungal assemblages. Many endophytic species enter an intermediate necrotrophic stage as host tissue becomes senescent, and this transition — where endophytes, facultative pathogens, and saprobes co-occur — is a critical zone for fungal biodiversity.

## Saprobic Fungi on Plant Debris

Tropical forests generate enormous quantities and diversity of plant litter. Individual leaves in tropical litterfall can harbor up to 30 different species of hyphomycetes, only some of which grow in colonies well defined by zone lines. In humid tropical ecosystems, plant debris may decay fully and be incorporated into soil far more rapidly than in temperate systems (Anderson and Swift 1983), creating ephemeral but intensely competitive niches for saprobic fungi. This rapid turnover may contribute to high local species richness by constantly creating new colonization opportunities.

Primary colonizers of newly fallen leaves in tropical forests differ between upper and lower leaf surfaces, even of individual leaves (Rambelli, personal communication). Fungal succession varies even between upper and lower internodes of grass stems, and between standing and uprooted culms, demonstrating the microhabitat complexity that drives tropical fungal diversity.

## Sampling Challenges in Tropical Forests

Several factors make tropical fungal biodiversity particularly difficult to assess:
- Epiphytes are frequently infrequent at study sites but may harbor unique fungi
- Fruiting patterns are less seasonal and less predictable than in temperate forests
- Canopy access is logistically challenging for macrofungal surveys
- Moist chamber techniques may be less successful in very humid tropical forests
- Rapid decomposition rates mean dead plant material is ephemeral
- Plant diversity itself is poorly documented in many tropical regions
- Many species produce fruiting structures only at intervals of many years, which may then persist for only a few hours
- The same plant may support different fungi during different stages of its life cycle from flowering to decay
- Taxonomic literature for tropical microfungi is sparse; many species cannot be identified with existing guides

Tropical soils present additional challenges. Recent evidence indicates that species diversity in tropical soils is similar to that in temperate soils (Pfenning 1993; Bettucci and Roquebert 1995). However, the litter-soil interface in humid tropical forests is often abrupt: the overlying litter is species rich, whereas the underlying mineral soil is depauperate by comparison (Pfenning 1993; Bills and Polishook 1994). Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Trichoderma species usually are abundant in tropical forest soils, and many minor or rare genera appear to be primarily tropical in distribution.

## Tropical vs. Temperate Comparisons

Tropical forests differ from temperate forests in fungal community structure:
- Higher species richness per unit area
- Greater proportion of undescribed taxa
- Less pronounced seasonal fruiting patterns
- Greater dominance of ascomycetes over basidiomycetes in some groups
- More complex host-specificity patterns
- Higher proportion of [[endophytic-fungi]] relative to saprotrophs
- More rapid decomposition rates creating ephemeral substrata
- Greater microhabitat complexity within individual leaves and stems

Collections from tropical regions consistently reveal high proportions of undescribed taxa. Durán's 22-year Mexico survey found 17% of species were new to science. Such figures suggest that the actual proportion of undescribed tropical fungi may be far higher.

## Endemism and Conservation

The highest levels of endemism among plant-parasitic fungi should occur in areas with the highest host-species endemism. Gentry (1992) identified four such areas in the Neotropics, most representing biomes isolated as islands (intermountain regions, mountain ridges). Determining the true degree of fungal endemism requires much more complete distributional data — many apparently endemic species likely have wider ranges than currently documented.

Endemism is common among sequestrate fungi, whose ectomycorrhizal hosts harbor numerous species that do not fruit in association with other host genera. The diversity of sequestrate fungi appears to increase with the diversity of ectomycorrhizal hosts and the variability of soils and habitats.

## The Biodiversity Gap

The gap between estimated and described tropical fungal species represents perhaps the largest deficiency in mycological knowledge. We know that even in temperate areas we can expect about six times as many fungi as native plants to occur in a site and that 2,500 to 3,000 species can be found in about 200 hectares if a variety of specialists study the site for more than 25 years. In tropical areas, these figures are almost certainly much higher. This has profound implications for [[fungal-conservation-challenges]] — species cannot be protected if they have not been discovered.

## Research Priorities for Tropical Mycology

- Comprehensive taxonomic guides for tropical microfungi — a high priority in fungal systematics
- Multi-year sampling programs to account for aseasonal and unpredictable fruiting
- Canopy access programs for macrofungal and endophyte surveys
- Integration of molecular methods with traditional morphology-based surveys
- Host-specificity studies across multiple plant families and tissue types
- Long-term ecological studies of fungal succession on tropical litter
- Biogeographic surveys comparing fungal communities across tropical regions
