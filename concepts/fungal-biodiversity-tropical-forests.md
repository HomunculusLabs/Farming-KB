## Fungal Biodiversity in Tropical Forests ## Overview Tropical forests represent
the greatest reservoir of fungal biodiversity on Earth, yet they remain the
least explored. The extraordinary plant diversity of [[fungal-species-richness-and-diversity-indices]] remains debated. Evidence
accumulated since Hawksworth's (1991) seminal 1.5 million species estimate,
especially from tropical plant studies, suggests that even this figure may be
too low. No single site in the world has been comprehensively surveyed for
fungi, and [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] present the largest gap in mycological knowledge. ##
Host Plant Diversity and Fungal Richness The Neotropics contain the highest
known alpha diversity of trees, with some lowland forest sites near Iquitos,
Peru, supporting nearly 300 species of woody plants per hectare (Gentry 1992).
At a Gabon forest site, 34% of recorded species were trees, 25% climbers, and
37% herbs or shrubs. This plant diversity creates a vast array of ecological
niches for fungal associates. Parasitic fungi associated with epiphytic plants
are poorly known and may represent another potentially diverse group in the
tropics. Sillett et al. (1995) recorded 127 bryophyte species in the inner
crowns of just six Ficus trees in Costa Rica. Epiphytes, while frequently
infrequent at any given study site, may harbor unique fungi, adding to the
challenge of tropical fungal inventory. ## Plant-Parasitic Fungi: Scale of

Collections from tropical regions consistently reveal high proportions of
undescribed taxa. Durán's 22-year Mexico survey found 17% of species were new to
science. Such figures suggest that the actual proportion of undescribed tropical
fungi may be far higher.

Combining molecular surveys with voucher-based morphological studies remains
essential for building the taxonomic framework needed to interpret sequence data.

## Threats to Tropical Fungal Diversity

Tropical deforestation represents the greatest threat to undocumented fungal
diversity. When forest is cleared, entire fungal communities are lost before
cataloguing — species go extinct before being described ("centinelan
