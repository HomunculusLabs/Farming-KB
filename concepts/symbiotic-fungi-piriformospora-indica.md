# Piriformospora indica - A Cultivable Root Endophyte for Agriculture

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Symbiotic Fungi (Varma and Kharkwal, eds.), Chapter 1: Symbiosis - The Art of Living

## Introduction

Piriformospora indica (Hymenomycetes, Basidiomycota, family Sebacinaceae) is the only cultivable endophyte that colonizes roots. Unlike arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, which cannot be grown in the absence of a living plant, P. indica can grow on artificial medium and be multiplied in mass scale on cheap, industrially produced simplified nutrient medium. This makes it a unique tool for improving plant production systems.

Inoculation with the fungus and application of fungal culture filtrate promotes plant growth and biomass production. Due to its ease of culture, P. indica provides a model organism for the study of beneficial plant-microbe interactions and a new tool for improving plant production systems.

See also: [[piriformospora-indica-in-plant-fungal-co-culture]], [[fungal-endophytes-plant-fitness]]

## Growth Promotion Across Diverse Hosts

P. indica vastly improves the growth and overall biomass production of diverse hosts, including legumes, medicinally and economically important plants. A pronounced growth-promotional effect has also been seen with terrestrial orchids. Arabidopsis plants colonized with the fungus produce 22% more seeds than uncolonized plants.

### Medicinal Plants Tested

The medicinal plants which have been tested in laboratory conditions as well as in extensive field trials include:
- Bacopa moniera (Sahay and Varma 1999, 2000)
- Azadirachta indica (neem; Singh et al. 2002, 2003)
- Withania somnifera (Rai et al. 2001)
- Spilanthes calva (Rai et al. 2001)
- Adhatoda vasica (Rai and Varma 2005)
- Chlorophytum borivilianum (Mathur et al. 2008)

Rai et al. (2001) reported growth increases in Withania somnifera and Spilanthes calva when they were interacted with the fungus P. indica.

### Transplant Survival

The fungus provides protection when inoculated into tissue culture-raised plantlets, by overcoming the "transient transplant shock" on transfer to the field, leading to almost 100% survival (Mathur et al. 2008). This is particularly valuable for micropropagation industries where transplant mortality can be a significant cost.

## Colonization Pattern

P. indica forms inter- and intracellular hyphae in the root cortex, often differentiating into dense hyphal coils (arbuscule-like structures), spore and vesicle-like structures. As with AM fungi, hyphae multiply within the host cortical tissues and never traverse through the endodermis. Likewise, they do not invade the aerial portion of the plant (stem and leaves).

Fungal colonization increases with root tissue maturation:
- The root tip meristem shows no colonization
- The elongation zone shows mainly intercellular colonization
- The differentiation zone is heavily colonized by inter- and intracellular hyphae and intracellular chlamydospores
- The majority of hyphae are present in dead rhizodermal and cortical cells that become completely filled with chlamydospores

In some cases, hyphae penetrate cells and build a meshwork around plasmolyzed protoplasts, suggesting that the fungus either actively kills cells or senses cells undergoing endogenous programmed cell death.

## Multiple Functions as a Plant Growth Promoter

### Biofertilizer

Similarly to arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, P. indica:
- Stimulates nitrate assimilation in the roots
- Solubilizes insoluble phosphatic components in the soil
- Promotes growth of seedlings and stimulates nitrogen accumulation
- Stimulates expression of genes for nitrate reductase and the starch-degrading enzyme glucan-water dikinase (SEX1) in roots

See also: [[biofertilizers-and-soil-inoculants]], [[fungal-roles-in-phosphorus-solubilization]]

### Bioprotector (Disease Resistance)

P. indica has been reported to induce resistance to fungal diseases in the monocotyledonous plant barley, along with tolerance to salt stress. The beneficial effect on defense status is detected in distal leaves, demonstrating a systemic induction of resistance by a root-endophytic fungus.

The systemically altered "defense readiness" is associated with:
- An elevated antioxidative capacity due to activation of the glutathione-ascorbate cycle
- An overall increase in grain yield
- Local and systemic resistance to fungal diseases and abiotic stress

Since P. indica can be easily propagated in the absence of a host plant, the fungus could be exploited to increase disease resistance as well as yield in crop plants (Waller et al. 2005).

See also: [[mycopesticides-fungal-biocontrol]], [[mycorrhizal-pathogen-resistance]]

### Stress Tolerance

Recent experiments have amply demonstrated that P. indica provides resistance against:
- **Heavy metal contamination**: The fungus provides resistance against heavy-metal contamination in the soil
- **Salt stress**: Tolerance to salinity has been demonstrated in barley
- **Chemical stress**: Protection against various chemical stresses
- **Thermal and physical stress**: General stress tolerance enhancement

See also: [[fungal-stress-responses]], [[fungal-salt-tolerance-and-ion-homeostasis]]

### Bioregulator

P. indica interacts with plant hormonal systems:
- A leucine-rich repeat protein is required for growth promotion and enhanced seed production mediated by the endophytic fungus in Arabidopsis thaliana
- The fungus may modulate plant hormone signaling pathways
- Seven days after inoculation, expression of barley BAX inhibitor-1 (HvBI-1), a gene capable of inhibiting plant cell death, was attenuated

### Soil Fertility

The fungus preserves soil fertility and improves plant health. Through its role in nutrient cycling, stress tolerance, and disease suppression, P. indica contributes to overall soil ecosystem health.

## The Lifestyle Question: Mutualism vs Biotrophic Pathogen

To elucidate the lifestyle of P. indica, scientists have analyzed its symbiotic interaction and endophytic development in barley roots. The fungus has been described as a model organism to be used as a potential for biocontrol strategies. Its ability to increase biomass and grain yield of crop plants, combined with induced local and systemic resistance to fungal diseases and abiotic stress, positions it uniquely among root-associated fungi.

The endophyte appears to interfere with the host cell death program to form a mutualistic interaction with plants. Fungal proliferation was strongly inhibited in transgenic barley overexpressing GFP-tagged HvBI-1, which shows that P. indica requires host cell death for proliferation in differentiated barley roots.

## Applications in Agriculture

P. indica has great potential for application in:
- **Agroforestry**: Enhanced growth and stress tolerance of tree seedlings
- **Flori-horticulture**: Improved growth and flowering of ornamental species
- **Arboriculture**: Better establishment of transplanted trees
- **Viticulture**: Improved grapevine growth and stress tolerance
- **Tissue culture**: Almost 100% survival of tissue culture-raised plants on transfer to field
- **Medicinal plant cultivation**: Enhanced biomass and bioactive compound production

This would open up numerous opportunities for the optimization of plant productivity in both managed and natural ecosystems, while minimizing risks of environmental damage.

## Genetic Studies

Deactivating the Arabidopsis single-copy gene DMI-1, which encodes an ion carrier required for mycorrhiza formation in legumes, does not affect the beneficial interaction between P. indica and Arabidopsis. This suggests that P. indica uses different signaling pathways than arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, potentially allowing it to be used alongside AMF without interference.

## Limitations and Research Needs

While P. indica shows enormous promise, several research areas need further investigation:
- Long-term effects of P. indica on soil microbial communities
- Field performance across diverse soil types and climates
- Potential interactions with other beneficial microorganisms (AMF, PGPR)
- Optimal inoculation methods and timing for different crops
- Regulatory approval for commercial use as a bioinoculant
- Potential for non-target effects in natural ecosystems

The closest relatives of P. indica are members of the Rhizoctina group (Ceratobasidales). Preliminary studies indicated that P. indica was able to interact with orchids (Dactylorhiza purpurella and D. majalis) as a mycorrhizal partner, suggesting broader potential applications in orchid conservation and cultivation.
