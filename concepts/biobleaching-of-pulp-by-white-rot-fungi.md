# Biobleaching of Pulp by White Rot Fungi

Updated: 2026-04-18

Biobleaching is the use of fungal enzymes to replace or reduce chemical chlorine-based bleaching in the pulp and paper industry. Conventional bleaching produces toxic chlorinated organic byproducts, while fungal biobleaching offers a biological alternative that can simultaneously decolorize and detoxify effluents. The process exploits the [[ligninolytic-enzymes-bioremediation]] of white rot fungi to remove residual lignin from pulp fibers.

## Mechanism of Fungal Biobleaching

The major colour in pulping effluents results from dissolved derivatives or fragments of lignin, including lignin sulfonates, chlorolignins, chlorinated phenolics, and their partly oxidized derivatives such as quinones. White rot fungi degrade these lignin-derived chromophores through the same non-specific radical-based mechanisms used in natural [[lignin-degradation-mechanisms-wood-rot-fungi]]. The three principal enzymes involved are:

- [[lignin-peroxidase-mechanism]] -- demonstrated to be the most important enzyme for pulp delignification

- [[lignin-peroxidase-mechanism]] -- contributes to decolorization of chlorinated lignin fractions

- [[brown-rot-fungi-biodegradation]] -- induced by growth on pulping effluent in some species, notably Trametes versicolor

The radical-based attack on lignin is inherently non-specific, which is both its strength and its challenge. The enzymes cannot distinguish between lignin in wood and lignin-derived chromophores in effluent, making them broadly effective. However, this non-specificity also means that enzyme activity can be diverted by competing substrates in complex effluent streams. Understanding the [[bioremediation-ecosystem-restoration]] underlying these processes is essential for optimizing treatment efficiency.

## Role of Manganese Peroxidase

The role of MnP in pulp bleaching has been conclusively demonstrated through mutant studies. MnP-deficient mutants of Phanerochaete chrysosporium were unable to bleach Kraft pulp, and one of these mutants did not bleach wood pulp at all. Purified MnP from T. versicolor was shown to demethylate and delignify Kraft pulp (Paice, Reid, Bourbonnais, Archibald and Jurasek, 1993). MnP generates Mn(III)-organic acid chelates that are small enough to diffuse into pulp fibers and oxidize phenolic lignin fragments that the enzymes themselves cannot reach.

Jaspers, Jimenez and Penninckx (1994) correlated decolorization of Kraft effluent with MnP activity and could not detect LiP in decolorizing cultures. Purified MnP was capable of decolorizing Kraft effluent while LiP was not. Lackner et al. (1991) showed that purified P. chrysosporium MnP could decolorize and depolymerize chlorolignins from Kraft effluent.

The Mn(III)-organic acid chelate mechanism is particularly effective because the chelated Mn(III) acts as a low-molecular-weight, diffusible oxidant. Organic acids such as oxalate, malonate, and lactate serve as chelating agents, and their concentration significantly affects the rate and extent of delignification. The MnP system is central to [[mycoremediation-enzymology]] because it exemplifies how fungi solve the problem of enzyme access to sterically protected substrates.

## Role of Laccase

Laccase activity can be dose-dependently increased by the presence of pulping liquor in growth medium for T. versicolor (Manzanares, Fajardo and Martin, 1995). Laccase production was induced by growth on olive mill wastewater. In P. flavido-alba, both laccase and MnP were implicated in decolorization. The role of laccase is significant because it uses molecular oxygen as the oxidant (unlike peroxidases which require H2O2), potentially simplifying bioreactor design.

Laccase-mediator systems (LMS) represent a promising approach for industrial biobleaching. When combined with mediators such as HBT (1-hydroxybenzotriazole) or N-hydroxyacetanilide (NHA), laccase can oxidize non-phenolic lignin structures that constitute the majority of residual lignin in Kraft pulp. This dramatically extends the practical delignification capability of laccase beyond its native phenolic substrate range. The LMS approach is being actively developed for commercial pulp bleaching applications, as discussed in [[fungal-ligninolytic-enzymes-in-industrial-applications]].

## Molecular Mass Distribution Changes During Treatment

Treatment of pulping effluents by white rot fungi changes the molecular mass distribution of lignin fractions in several ways:

- Decrease in all fractions (depolymerization)

- Decrease in high-molecular-mass fractions with increase in low-molecular-mass fractions

- Transient polymerization, followed by depolymerization of newly formed high-molecular-mass fractions on prolonged treatment

The occurrence of polymerization or depolymerization depends on the fungal strain and the material being treated. For example, different strains of Schizophyllum commune caused polymerization, depolymerization, or no effect on the molecular mass distribution of lignosulfonates. T. versicolor caused polymerization of lignosulfonates but depolymerization of chlorolignins.

The transient polymerization phenomenon is important because it can temporarily increase effluent color before subsequent depolymerization reduces it. This has practical implications for reactor design and treatment time optimization. Understanding the [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] kinetics is crucial for predicting whether a given treatment will pass through a polymerization phase.

## Nutritional Requirements

Chromophores in pulping effluents cannot be degraded without a carbonaceous co-substrate. Glucose or sucrose are the most usual (1-10 g/L), though other sugars, polysaccharides, glycerol, and ethanol have also been used. Nitrogen requirements vary: P. chrysosporium ligninolysis is repressed by high nitrogen, while T. versicolor does not show nitrogen repression of effluent decolorization. Interestingly, high nitrogen concentrations repressed P. chrysosporium decolorization with glucose as co-substrate but promoted it with cellulose.

The co-substrate requirement represents a significant cost factor for industrial application. Research has explored using less expensive carbon sources including molasses, corn steep liquor, and effluent organic matter itself. The nitrogen effect differs among species because ligninolytic regulation pathways vary, with some fungi producing ligninolytic enzymes constitutively or under different metabolic triggers. These nutritional considerations are central to [[factors-affecting-mycoremediation-efficiency]].

## Dechlorination

White rot fungi can dechlorinate the chloroorganic components in pulping effluents. Pellinen et al. (1988a) correlated dechlorination and decolorization by P. chrysosporium, suggesting the processes are metabolically linked. Treatment achieves both dechlorination and detoxification simultaneously.

The dechlorination activity extends to individual chlorophenol compounds as described in [[chlorophenol-degradation-by-white-rot-fungi]]. Reductive dechlorination appears to occur alongside oxidative degradation, with quinone intermediates serving as electron acceptors. The simultaneous removal of chlorine atoms and aromatic ring structures is a distinctive advantage of fungal treatment over conventional approaches, which often address only one aspect of effluent toxicity.

## Bioreactor Configurations for Biobleaching

Biobleaching has been demonstrated in multiple reactor types:

- **Rotating biological contactors (RBCs)** -- discs partially submerged in effluent, providing alternating exposure to wastewater and atmosphere

- **Packed-bed reactors** -- fungal biomass immobilized on solid supports through which effluent flows

- **Fluidized-bed reactors** -- suspended carriers with attached biomass in upflowing liquid

- **Continuous stirred-tank reactors (CSTR)** -- well-mixed systems suitable for pellet-forming fungi

- **Trickle-bed reactors** -- effluent percolated over fixed fungal mats with passive aeration

Each configuration offers distinct advantages for different treatment scales and effluent characteristics. Reactor selection is a key component of [[mycoremediation-bioreactor-design]] and [[fungal-immobilization-bioreactor-systems]].

## Performance

Most studies report 60-90% colour removal in 3-6 days, with some reports exceeding 90% (Archibald, Paice and Jurasek, 1990a). Effective treatment has been demonstrated in multiple bioreactor configurations including rotating biological contactors, packed-bed reactors, fluidized-bed reactors, and continuous stirred-tank reactors.
