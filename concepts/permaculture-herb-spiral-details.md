# Permaculture Herb Spiral Details

The herb spiral is one of the most iconic and practical features of permaculture garden design. Described extensively by Toby Hemenway in Gaia's Garden, it is a three-dimensional, upward-coiling garden bed that packs a remarkable diversity of growing conditions into a compact footprint.

## What Is an Herb Spiral?

An herb spiral is a raised bed built in a spiral shape, typically 1.5 to 2.5 meters (5 to 8 feet) in diameter at the base, rising to about 1 to 1.5 meters (3 to 5 feet) at the center. Stones, bricks, or other materials form the retaining walls as the spiral climbs upward. The result is a mound with many different microclimates in a single structure.

## Why Build One?

The spiral design maximizes edge (see [[permaculture-edge-effect-design]]) and creates a gradient of conditions:

- The top is the **driest and warmest** zone, exposed to sun and wind
- The bottom is the **coolest and wettest**, near the water that drains down
- The **north side** (in the Northern Hemisphere) is shaded and cooler
- The **south side** receives more direct sun
- The **east side** gets morning sun (cooler)
- The **west side** gets afternoon sun (hotter)

This range of conditions allows you to grow herbs with very different needs in a single structure that might be only 2 meters across.

## Construction Steps

### 1. Choose the Site

- Select a location close to the kitchen (Zone 1) for easy harvest
- Preferably near a path or doorway for frequent access
- Needs at least partial sun; full sun is ideal
- Near a water source for easy irrigation

### 2. Prepare the Base

- Mark a circle on the ground approximately 1.5 to 2 meters in diameter
- Remove grass or weeds from the area (or [[permaculture-sheet-mulch-detailed-guide|sheet mulch]] them)
- Loosen the soil underneath to improve drainage
- Optionally lay down cardboard or weed barrier

### 3. Build the Walls

- Use stones, bricks, concrete blocks, or other durable materials
- Begin laying the wall material in a spiral pattern from the outside edge
- The spiral should make approximately 1.5 to 2.5 complete turns from the outside to the center
- Stack the wall progressively higher as you move inward, creating a dome shape
- Leave gaps between stones for drainage and beneficial insect habitat
- The center peak is typically 0.9 to 1.2 meters high

### 4. Fill With Growing Media

- Fill the bottom and lower portions with richer, moisture-retentive soil
- Use sandier, well-draining soil mix toward the top
- Incorporate compost throughout
- The bottom of the spiral can include a small pond or moisture zone
- Fill to just below the top of the wall stones

### 5. Plant According to Microclimate

Place herbs according to their preferred conditions:

**Top (dry, sunny, well-drained):**
- Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis)
- Thyme (Thymus spp.)
- Lavender (Lavandula spp.)
- Oregano (Origanum vulgare)
- Sage (Salvia officinalis)

**Middle slopes (moderate moisture and sun):**
- Basil (Ocimum basilicum)
- Tarragon (Artemisia dracunculus)
- Chives (Allium schoenoprasum)
- Savory (Satureja spp.)
- Marjoram (Origanum majorana)

**Lower levels and north side (moister, cooler, some shade):**
- Parsley (Petroselinum crispum)
- Cilantro/Coriander (Coriandrum sativum)
- Mint (Mentha spp.) -- plant in a separate contained section to prevent spreading
- Chervil (Anthriscus cerefolium)

**Bottom and pond area (wet):**
- Water mint (Mentha aquatica)
- Watercress (Nasturtium officinale)
- Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus)

**East side (morning sun, cooler):**
- Dill (Anethum graveolens)
- Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla)

**West side (afternoon sun, hotter):**
- Mexican tarragon (Tagetes lucida)
- Calendula (Calendula officinalis)

## Maintenance

- Water the top more frequently; the bottom retains moisture longer
- Harvest regularly to keep plants compact and productive
- Replace annuals as needed; perennials will persist for years
- Top-dress with compost annually
- Trim woody herbs to maintain shape and encourage new growth
- Check the pond area for mosquito larvae; add mosquito fish or Bacillus thuringiensis if needed

## Design Variations

- **Stone versus brick**: Stones create more habitat for beneficial insects in their gaps; bricks give a cleaner look
- **Double spiral**: Two interleaving spirals can hold even more plants
- **Herb snail**: A spiral laid flat rather than built up, creating a ground-level pattern
- **Combined with keyhole**: The spiral can incorporate a keyhole bed access path
- **Mandala integration**: The spiral can be the central feature of a larger [[mandala-garden-design|mandala garden]]

## Ecological Benefits

- Provides habitat for predatory insects and pollinators
- Creates multiple microclimates in minimal space
- Demonstrates the permaculture principle of [[permaculture-principle-use-small-and-slow-solutions|small and slow solutions]]
- Excellent educational tool for demonstrating microclimate variation
- Stone walls absorb heat during the day and release it at night, moderating temperatures
