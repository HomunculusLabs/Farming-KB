# Azurescens, Cyanescens, and Woodlovers Comparison

The Psilocybe cyanescens complex includes several closely related wood-loving species that share similar cultivation requirements but differ in potency, habitat preferences, and cultivation difficulty. This comparison draws on two detailed cultivation guides covering P. azurescens and the broader group of outdoor woodlovers.

## The Cyanescens Complex

P. azurescens belongs to the Psilocybe cyanescens complex, meaning it is closely related to:
- **P. cyanescens**: The wavy-capped psilocybe, the namesake of the complex
- **P. cyanofibrillosa**: Pacific Northwest species
- **P. subaeruginosa**: Australian/New Zealand species (cultivated in the woodlovers guide)

All share the core traits: lignicolous (wood-decomposing), cold-fruiting, temperate climate species that grow on decaying hardwood debris in half-shaded environments.

## Potency

P. azurescens holds the distinction of having the highest alkaloid concentrations of any known psilocybin mushroom. Gartz (1995) analysis of dried specimens:

| Sample | Psilocybin (%) | Psilocin (%) | Baeocystin (%) |
|--------|---------------|-------------|----------------|
| 1 | 1.40 | 0.31 | 0.28 |
| 2 | 1.56 | 0.30 | 0.32 |
| 3 | 1.68 | 0.28 | 0.38 |
| 4 | 1.71 | 0.34 | 0.41 |

These values translate to total tryptamine alkaloid concentrations approaching 25 mg per gram of dried material. No other psilocybin mushroom has been documented with higher levels.

## Cultivation Method Comparison

The two source documents describe overlapping but distinct approaches to outdoor woodlover cultivation:

### Azurescens-Specific Method
- **Spawn containers**: Glass jars (lower contamination risk)
- **Sterilization**: 121C for 1+ hours
- **Wood species**: Hazelnut twigs/chips preferred; beech and alder also suitable
- **Bed spawn ratio**: Minimum 1:5 (20% spawn by volume)
- **Bed depth**: 15-20 cm
- **Bed cover**: Wet cardboard + perforated plastic
- **Summer watering**: Minimal after first 2 weeks; critical not to overwater
- **Fruiting**: Late September to early October at 7-10C

### General Woodlovers Method
- **Spawn containers**: Polypropylene bags (higher volume, higher contamination risk)
- **Sterilization**: 121C for 3 full hours
- **Wood species**: Beech (Fagus sylvatica) as animal bedding
- **Bed spawn ratio**: 1:20 minimum; higher preferred
- **Bed depth**: Shallow hole
- **Bed cover**: Plain plastic sheet
- **Supplementation**: Oatmeal or boiled grass seed added to spawn bags
- **Fruiting**: When autumn temperatures reach the species' preferred range

### Key Differences

| Parameter | Azurescens Guide | Woodlovers Guide |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| Spawn ratio | 1:5 (4x more spawn) | 1:20 |
| Container | Jars | Bags |
| Sterilization | 1+ hours | 3 hours |
| Bed cover | Cardboard + plastic | Plastic only |
| Supplementation | None mentioned | Oatmeal/grass seed |
| Spawn chip size | Fresh cut hazelnut | Small beech bedding |
| Bed chip size | Fresh cut hardwood | Large beech bedding |

The azurescens guide's higher spawn ratio likely reflects the species' slower colonization compared to more aggressive woodlovers. The cardboard layer in the azurescens method provides both moisture retention and an additional colonizable medium.

## Morphological Distinction

P. azurescens is distinguished from its close relatives primarily by:
- **Greater size**: Caps to 100 mm, stems to 200 mm — significantly larger than P. cyanescens
- **Spore size**: 13-13.5 x 6.5-8.0 microns
- **Cystidia**: Fusoid-ventricose; cheilocystidia 23-28 x 6.5-8.0 microns; pleurocystidia 23-35 x 9-10 microns
- **Cap**: Ochraceous-brown, hygrophanous, viscid with separable pellicle, conic becoming convex to plane, umbonate
- **Stem**: White, strict and elongated, with bluish strigose fibers at base
- **Flesh**: Bluing on bruising

## Shared Cultivation Principles

Despite differences, both methods agree on core principles:
- Wood chip spawn is strongly preferred over direct grain inoculation
- Half-shaded outdoor locations are essential
- Cool autumn temperatures trigger fruiting
- Beds can produce for years with minimal maintenance
- Fresh chips added annually sustain long-term productivity
- Contaminated spawn discarded outdoors can still produce mushrooms
