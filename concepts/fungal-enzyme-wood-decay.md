## Fungal Enzyme Systems in Wood Decay
Fungi are the primary agents of wood decomposition in terrestrial ecosystems,
recycling the carbon locked in lignocellulose back into the global carbon cycle.
This process depends on complex enzyme systems that can break down the three main
components of wood: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. The efficiency and
specificity of these enzymatic arsenals determine decay type, decomposition rate,
and ecosystem-level carbon flux.

## Wood Composition and Recalcitrance

Wood is composed of approximately 40 to 50% cellulose, 20 to 30% hemicellulose,
and 20 to 30% lignin, with minor amounts of extractives and ash. Cellulose is a
linear polymer of beta-1,4-linked glucose units that forms crystalline
microfibrils of remarkable tensile strength. Hemicellulose is a heterogeneous
group of branched polysaccharides including xylan, glucomannan, and arabinan.
Lignin is an irregular, three-dimensional polymer of phenylpropanoid units that
encrusts cellulose and hemicellulose, providing rigidity and resistance to
degradation.

Lignin's irregular, non-repeating structure makes it extraordinarily resistant
to enzymatic attack. No single enzyme can cleave all the bond types in lignin,
requiring a coordinated suite of oxidative enzymes. This recalcitrance is the
primary reason lignocellulosic biomass persists in the environment and why fungal
decay is the rate-limiting step in carbon cycling.

## White Rot Decay

White rot fungi, predominantly Basidiomycota, are the only organisms capable of
substantial lignin degradation. They employ an oxidative enzymatic system

## Soft Rot Decay

Soft rot fungi, primarily Ascomycota, are the dominant wood decomposers in
extreme environments: waterlogged wood, arid conditions, and wood with high
preservative concentrations. They produce cellulase enzyme complexes that create
characteristic cylindrical cavities within the secondary cell wall. Two types
exist: Type I creates cavities aligned with the cellulose microfibrils, while
Type II causes a general erosion of the cell wall from the lumen outward.

Soft rot fungi are slower decomposers than white or brown rot fungi but tolerate
conditions that exclude basidiomycetes. Their importance in terrestrial carbon
cycling has historically been underestimated, but recent research shows they
contribute significantly to decomposition in tropical forests and aquatic
environments.

## Extracellular Electron Transfer

Emerging research reveals that some wood-decay fungi participate in direct
extracellular electron transfer, analogous to processes well-documented in
bacteria. Filamentous fungi like Phanerochaete can transfer electrons to humic
acids and other terminal electron acceptors in soil. This capability may extend
the metabolic reach of the fungus into anoxic zones within wood, where
oxygen-dependent peroxidases cannot function.
