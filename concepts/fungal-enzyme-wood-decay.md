# Fungal Enzyme Systems in Wood Decay

Fungi are the primary agents of wood decomposition in terrestrial ecosystems,
recycling the carbon locked in lignocellulose back into the global carbon cycle.
This process depends on complex enzyme systems that can break down the three
main components of wood: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. The efficiency
and specificity of these enzymatic arsenals determine decay type, decomposition
rate, and ecosystem-level carbon flux.

## Wood Composition and Recalcitrance

Wood is composed of approximately 40 to 50% cellulose, 20 to 30%
hemicellulose, and 20 to 30% lignin, with minor amounts of extractives and
ash. Cellulose is a linear polymer of beta-1,4-linked glucose units that
forms crystalline microfibrils of remarkable tensile strength. Hemicellulose
is a heterogeneous group of branched polysaccharides including xylan,
glucomannan, and arabinan. Lignin is an irregular, three-dimensional polymer
of phenylpropanoid units that encrusts cellulose and hemicellulose, providing
rigidity and resistance to degradation.

Lignin's irregular, non-repeating structure makes it extraordinarily resistant
to enzymatic attack. No single enzyme can cleave all the bond types in lignin,
requiring a coordinated suite of oxidative enzymes. This recalcitrance is the
primary reason lignocellulosic biomass persists in the environment and why
fungal decay is the rate-limiting step in carbon cycling.

## White Rot Decay

White rot fungi, predominantly Basidiomycota, are the only organisms
capable of substantial lignin degradation. They employ an oxidative enzymatic
system centered on lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidase (MnP), and
laccase. These enzymes generate radical intermediates that nonspecifically
attack lignin's aromatic structure, eventually mineralizing it to carbon
dioxide.

Lignin peroxidase, first characterized in [[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]],
catalyzes the oxidation of non-phenolic lignin units -- the most abundant
and recalcitrant fraction. It requires hydrogen peroxide as a co-substrate
and veratryl alcohol as a redox mediator. MnP oxidizes Mn(II) to Mn(III),
which diffuses into the wood matrix and oxidizes phenolic compounds. Laccase,
a multicopper oxidase, oxidizes phenolic subunits directly and can work with
low-molecular-weight mediators to extend its substrate range.

The coordinated action of these enzymes allows white rot fungi to remove
lignin preferentially, leaving behind bleached, cellulose-rich wood. Some
species, like Ceriporiopsis-subvermispora, are highly selective for lignin
and leave cellulose largely intact, making them candidates for biopulping
applications.

## Brown Rot Decay

Brown rot fungi, also primarily Basidiomycota, employ a fundamentally
different strategy. They do not degrade lignin significantly; instead, they
rapidly depolymerize cellulose through a non-enzymatic mechanism involving
the Fenton reaction (Fe2+ + H2O2 -> Fe3+ + OH- + OH.). Hydroxyl radicals
generated by this reaction attack cellulose chains nonspecifically, creating
soluble oligosaccharides that the fungus can absorb.

The brown rot strategy involves generating hydrogen peroxide and reducing
ferric iron to ferrous iron at the hyphal tip. Low-molecular-weight quinones
and other redox cycling compounds secreted by the fungus shuttle electrons
to create the reducing conditions necessary for Fenton chemistry. This
non-enzymatic approach is extremely energy-efficient for the fungus and
allows rapid colonization before competitors can establish.

The residual wood after brown rot is a brittle, brown, lignin-rich material
with reduced cellulose content. Brown rot causes enormous economic damage to
wood structures, with [[serpula-lacrymans]] (the dry rot fungus) being one
of the most destructive organisms in buildings.

## Soft Rot Decay

Soft rot fungi, primarily Ascomycota, are the dominant wood decomposers
in extreme environments -- waterlogged wood, arid conditions, and wood with
high preservative concentrations. They produce cellulase enzyme complexes
that create characteristic cylindrical cavities within the secondary cell wall.
Two types exist: Type I creates cavities aligned with the cellulose
microfibrils, while Type II causes a general erosion of the cell wall from
the lumen outward.

Soft rot fungi are slower decomposers than white or brown rot fungi but
tolerate conditions that exclude basidiomycetes. Their importance in
terrestrial carbon cycling has historically been underestimated, but recent
research shows they contribute significantly to decomposition in tropical
forests and aquatic environments.

## Extracellular Electron Transfer

Emerging research reveals that some wood-decay fungi participate in direct
extracellular electron transfer, analogous to processes well-documented in
bacteria. Filamentous fungi like Phanerochaete can transfer electrons to
humic acids and other terminal electron acceptors in soil. This capability may
extend the metabolic reach of the fungus into anoxic zones within wood, where
oxygen-dependent peroxidases cannot function.

## Industrial Applications

Fungal enzyme systems have been co-opted for numerous industrial processes.
Biopulping -- pretreatment of wood chips with white rot fungi before
mechanical pulping -- reduces energy consumption by 20 to 40%. Enzymatic
bleaching with laccase and MnP can replace chlorine-based bleaching agents
in paper production, reducing toxic effluent.

Biofuel production from lignocellulosic biomass requires efficient
deconstruction of plant cell walls. White rot fungal enzymes, particularly
laccase-mediator systems, can delignify biomass to improve enzymatic
saccharification yields. However, the cost of enzyme production remains a
barrier to commercial viability. See also [[lignin-degradation]] and
[[fungal-bioremediation]].
