# Permaculture Ethics Foundation

Ethics are the moral principles that guide action toward good and right outcomes and away from bad and wrong outcomes. In permaculture, ethics function as constraints on survival instincts and self-interest -- they are culturally evolved mechanisms for more enlightened self-interest, a more inclusive view of who and what constitutes "us," and a longer-term understanding of good and bad outcomes. This connects directly to [[permaculture-design]], [[permaculture-water-harvesting-earthworks]], and [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]].

## Ethics as Ecologically Functional

Holmgren advances an ecologically functional view of ethics: "The greater the power of human civilisation (due to energy availability) and the greater the concentration and scale of power within society, the more critical ethics become in ensuring long-term cultural -- and even biological -- survival." This view makes ethics central to the development of a culture for energy descent.

Unlike moral philosophy that seeks universal principles divorced from context, permaculture ethics are grounded in ecological realities. They were distilled from "research of community ethics as adopted by older religious and cooperative groups." They can be seen as common to all indigenous tribal peoples, whose cultures existed in relative balance with their environment for longer than any more recent experiments in civilisation.

## The Three Ethical Principles

Since the development of the Permaculture Design Course, ethics have generally been covered by three broad maxims:

1. **Care for the Earth**
2. **Care for People**
3. **Set Limits to Consumption and Reproduction, and Redistribute Surplus**

These principles were distilled from research into community ethics as adopted by older religious and cooperative groups. The third principle, and even the second, can be seen as derived from the first. Holmgren notes that while moral philosophers may argue that such a simple foundation is fraught with dangers, "the dangers of isolation of philosophical thought from an integrated existence are as great as the dangers of ignorance of the history of philosophy and ethics."

## Philosophical Frameworks

Holmgren grounds permaculture ethics in an awareness that ideas and values are never free of their ecological, economic and cultural context. We need to be suspicious of seeing the philosophy of individualism as the source rather than outcome of material well-being. Beliefs and values developed with a rising energy base are likely to be dysfunctional -- even destructive -- in a world of limited and declining energy.

The rejection of the primacy of energetic and ecological forces is, for Holmgren, a continuing expression of "Cartesian dualism" that separates mind and body, humanity and nature, thought and action. While productive alternatives have emerged within science -- dialectical materialism, systems theory, design science -- the fundamentalist adherence to reductionism that characterises much of the establishment will increase the likelihood of cultural revolutions.

## Permaculture as Design Science

Bill Mollison described permaculture as integrated design science, placing it within the culture of science. Permaculture is applied science concerned with improving long-term material well-being. In drawing together strategies from modern and traditional cultures, permaculture seeks a wholistic integration of utilitarian values. By using an ecological perspective, permaculture sees a much broader canvas of utility than reductionist perspectives, especially econometric ones, that dominate modern society.

## Spiritual Dimensions

Although permaculture can be seen as essentially materialist and scientific, it depends on an ecological perspective. Spiritual beliefs about a higher purpose in nature have been universal and defining features of all cultures before scientific rationalism. The more we understand the world through systems thinking and ecology, the more we see the wisdom in spiritual perspectives and traditions.

Permaculture attracts people raised in scientific rationalism because its wholism does not depend on a spiritual dimension. For others, it reinforces spiritual beliefs, even if these are simply a basic animism that recognises the earth as alive and, in some unknowable way, conscious. Holmgren maps the emergent union of materialism and spirituality along creative and destructive pathways, locating permaculture at the intersection of emerging holistic science and inclusive ecological spirituality.

## Care for the Earth

### Spaceship Earth and Gaia
People often associate Care for the Earth with planetary stewardship, reflecting the Spaceship Earth concept popularised by Stewart Brand. The Gaia hypothesis of Lovelock and Margulis provides a more powerful framing: the earth is a self-organised system, and if we seriously affect its life-support systems, we will be "neutralised" by co-evolutionary mechanisms. Care for the Earth in this global context is not only due to ethical restraint but also to fear of rejection and annihilation.

### Living Soil
In the most grounded sense, Care for the Earth means caring for living soil as the source of terrestrial life. There are good scientific and historical reasons for regarding soil state as the best measure of future health and well-being of society. How to care for soil remains controversial -- technical questions are entwined with ethical ones because we do not know how far it is possible to improve soil's capacity to support nature and provide for human needs.

### Stewardship
At the scale of place and territory, Care for the Earth means stewardship as understood by indigenous cultures and bioregionalism. Wendell Berry criticised planetary stewardship as a product of arrogant disconnection from nature: "The question is not how to care for the planet, but how to care for each of the planet's millions of human and natural neighbourhoods, each one of which is in some precious and exciting way different from all the others."

The stewardship concept demands: Will the resource be in better shape after my stewardship? This challenges the ethical validity of land ownership at the heart of our legal system. In a low-energy future, control of land will again become the primary focus for ethics, politics and culture.

### Biodiversity
Care for the Earth encompasses caring for all diverse lifeforms as valid parts of the living earth with intrinsic value, not dependent on current usefulness to us. Holmgren interprets the care of other lifeforms to mean: accepting all species as intrinsically valuable; reducing total environmental impact as the best way to care for all living things; and when harming and killing other living things, always doing so in a conscious and respectful way -- "not to use what we kill is the greatest disrespect."

## Care for People

### Self-Reliance as Ethical Foundation
Care for People makes permaculture an unashamedly human-centred environmental philosophy. It starts with self: accepting personal responsibility for our situation, focusing on opportunities rather than obstacles. This follows the pattern of almost all traditional ethical systems, expanding in widening circles from self to family to community.

The emphasis on providing for one's own needs first might appear to ignore gross disparities of wealth, but Holmgren reframes it: "As we reduce our dependence on the global economy and replace it with household and local economies, we reduce the demand that drives the current inequities. Thus 'look after yourself first' is not an invitation to greed but a challenge to grow up through self-reliance and personal responsibility."

### Non-Material Well-Being
One of the best ways to apply this principle is to focus on non-material values. Enjoying a sunset rather than watching a movie, walking rather than consuming medicine, playing with a child rather than buying them a toy -- these care for self and others without producing or consuming material resources. Rising consumption is not improving well-being in rich countries. For the United States, the Genuine Progress Indicator has been declining since 1978, despite huge rises in per capita consumption. Much new wealth is used to offset adverse effects of over-consumption.

## Set Limits to Consumption and Reproduction, and Redistribute Surplus

### Abundance and Limits
A sense of abundance emerges within a context of constraint -- the taste of the first strawberry is special when nature does not provide them year round. Luxuries lose their power when they become everyday realities. A sense of limits comes from mature understanding of how the world works. Everything has a limited lifespan and a limited place.

Recognition of limits does not come from the experience of scarcity. Except in extreme famine, scarcity is culturally mediated -- largely created by industrial economics and power rather than actual physical limits to resources. Manufactured scarcity encourages unrestrained consumption.

### Setting Limits
Setting limits requires considering what is enough, and sometimes making hard decisions. When we accept our own mortality and limited power, setting personal limits becomes a reasonable bargain with the world. We maintain autonomy and self-control by exercising self-restraint. The ecological footprint is one method to audit and reorganise personal demand on natural resources.

On population, the world is probably already overpopulated for the long-term good of humanity and other species. In rich countries, almost every child born accelerates consumption. Birth rates drop rapidly when three factors apply: women are economically secure, they have control over their own reproduction, and infant mortality rates are low.

### Redistribution of Surplus
Redistribution requires sharing surplus resources to help the earth and people beyond our immediate circle. What we choose to support with our surplus time, resources and wealth is an increasingly important life issue.

Traditional institutions of church and state are losing their authority while corporations have gained enormous power with little ethical constraint. Many traditional and materially poor societies redistribute relatively more of their material wealth. In indigenous societies, caring for the land beyond family needs was embedded in nature-spirit traditions requiring gifts, work, or devotion to maintain the living world.

In permaculture, the planting of long-lived and valuable trees and forests has been a traditional way of redistributing surplus for the benefit of future generations and the land itself. Soil management to increase long-term fertility -- especially humus content -- is regarded as stewardship where one leaves the land in better shape than when one received it.

However, social and environmental good works can sometimes be atonement rather than providing what people or nature really need. Sometimes giving creates dependency. "How to give in ways that show true altruism has always been problematic, but in the modern world it is an ethical question of great magnitude and complexity."

## Ethics as Design Tools

Permaculture design principles are conceptual tools for finding what is appropriate, practical, and yet of enduring value in chaotically changing times. Holmgren offers his thoughts "in the belief that discomfort, especially ethical discomfort, is a healthy alternative to ideological certainty."
