# Permaculture Duck Raising

Ducks are the gentlest and most versatile poultry in a [[permaculture-design]] system. They eat algae, weeds, slugs, snails, grubs, soft greens, grasses, water plants, small tree greens, and grains while fertilizing the water and soil.

## Role in the System

Ducks improve fish production by fertilizing pond water. They eat algae and weeds from ponds, slugs, snails, and grubs from the garden, and graze on greens and grasses. They need less care and feeding than [[permaculture-designers-manual-chicken-tractors]], but need more planning -- specifically lots of water and grazing space.

Key integration points:

- Enclosed area around a small pond with an island for nesting, typically in or on the edge of [[permaculture-zone-planning]]
- 25 ducks per acre of pond surface
- Forage garden around the pond
- Duck manure feeds algae-eating organisms and helps grow pond plants
- Can be let into the [[permaculture-zone-planning]] garden occasionally to eat slugs and pests, but only when plants are at least as big as the ducks
- Keep ducks separated from other animals' water sources or they will dirty them

## Duck Care and Feeding

If ducks have adequate water, a grassy yard with new grass, and a forage garden with bugs, minimal extra feed is needed. They need young grass; if their pasture is too small and unvaried, they will quickly destroy a grassy backyard.

Supplementary feed:

- Wheat is the best grain for ducks, goes well with oats
- Hard round fruits and vegetables need to be crushed first
- Liquid milk and hard-boiled eggs for protein (especially for laying birds)
- Calcium from eggshells or seashells
- Grit for digestion
- Extra niacin -- ducks need more than other poultry; fresh greens or peas usually suffice

### Homemade Poultry Chick Feed

- 30% grain (finely ground wheat, a little corn, and oats)
- 20% protein (fish meal, meat meal, yogurt, cottage cheese, worms, bugs, grubs)
- 50% greens (alfalfa meal, alfalfa leaves, or fresh greens)
- Extras: wheat germ, sunflower seeds, linseed meal, sand for grit, ground shells

### Homemade Poultry Adult Feed

- 30% grain
- 15% protein
- 55% greens

- Same extras as chick feed

## Housing

Ducks are social and need at least two or more. Each duck needs four square feet of housing. During the day a three-sided shelter near the pond suffices. At night they need protection from predators -- either a barn room with straw bedding or a simple shed. The shed should have a door for harvesting fertilized straw.

Ducks can usually fly and need wing clipping. Clip the ends of long feathers on one wing with big scissors when they first grow, and after each molting. Never clip during molting or you may cause fatal bleeding.

In winter they run out of forage and can turn the area around the pond to mud. Throw down mulch (fallen leaves or hay) as they stir up the earth. Ducks tolerate freezing temperatures as long as they can retreat to their shelter.

## Breeds

- Khaki Campbell: Most popular, first domestic duck breed, excellent mollison designers chicken

- Indian Runners: Excellent egg layers, not good meat birds

- Muscovy, Rouen, Pekin: Meat breeds

- Heritage dual-purpose breeds: Ancona, Appleyard, Buff, Magpie, Saxony

- Saxony: Probably the best foraging and egg-laying dual-purpose breed

## Breeding

Males and females are hard to tell apart -- females have a loud, raspy quack, males are quieter or sometimes silent. If you have a motherly duck, let her raise her own ducklings. Ducks start laying in spring at six to seven months old and keep laying for three years or longer. They always lay in the morning and are very scheduled. Let them out of the barn after 10:00 AM and lure them back in the evening with grain. Keep mother ducks and ducklings separate from the flock until chicks are six to eight weeks old.

## Egg Production and Processing

Duck eggs are larger than chicken eggs (about 3.5 ounces / 100 grams each) with richer yolks and higher protein content. They are prized by bakers for superior texture in cakes and pastries, and by chefs for their velvety richness in custards and pasta.

Egg production by breed:

- Khaki Campbell: 250-340 eggs per year; the most prolific layer

- Indian Runner: 200-300 eggs per year

- Saxony: 190-240 eggs per year

- Dual-purpose heritage breeds: 150-200 eggs per year

- Muscovy: 100-180 eggs per year (they lay in clutches with longer breaks between)

Egg and storage:

- Collect eggs daily; ducks lay in the morning before being let out

- Duck eggshells are more porous than chicken eggs, making them more susceptible to bacterial contamination -- do not wash unless necessary

- Store unwashed eggs at 45-55°F (7-13°C) for up to 6 weeks

- Duck eggs have a longer shelf life than chicken eggs due to thicker membranes

- Many customers at farmers markets will pay $6-8 per dozen for duck eggs

## Duck Health and Common Ailments

Ducks are generally hardy birds but require attention to a few specific health concerns.

Niacin deficiency is the most common nutritional problem in ducklings. Symptoms include bowed legs, reluctance to walk, and poor growth. Prevention is straightforward: add brewers yeast to feed at a rate of 1 tablespoon per cup of feed, or provide access to fresh green forage and peas. Duck starter feed should contain more niacin than chick starter.

Common health issues:

- Bumblefoot: bacterial infection of the foot pad; caused by walking on rough or dirty surfaces; treat by soaking in Epsom salt and applying antibiotic ointment

- Respiratory infections: ducks are susceptible to Aspergillosis from moldy feed or bedding; keep all feed dry and housing well-ventilated

- Botulism: caused by ingesting toxin from decaying mollison designers humus in stagnant water; prevent by keeping water sources fresh and moving

- Egg binding: more common in overweight ducks; ensure adequate exercise and balanced calcium intake

- Prolapse: usually follows egg binding; isolate the affected bird and keep the vent clean with warm water

Preventive care routines:

- Inspect ducks weekly for lice and mites; treat with food-grade diatomaceous earth in nesting areas

- Keep drinking water clean -- ducks contaminate water rapidly with feed and feces

- Provide deep water (at least 6 inches / 15 cm) for head-dipping; this keeps their eyes, nostrils, and feathers clean

- Maintain dry bedding to prevent foot problems and respiratory issues

## Predator Protection

Ducks are vulnerable to a wide range of predators including raccoons, foxes, owls, hawks, mink, weasels, and domestic dogs. Because they spend time both on water and on land, they need protection in multiple environments.

Water-based predators:

- Raccoons: the most common duck predator; they reach into the water and pull ducks under at night

- Mink and weasels: can squeeze through very small openings; they kill multiple birds in a single night

- Snapping turtles: can take ducklings from below; provide an island refuge where ducklings can rest out of the water
