# Soil Food Web Assessment (Ingham Field Guide)

The Ingham Field Guide frames soil health assessment around the complete soil foodweb -- bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms -- rather than single indicators. Assessment is the essential first step before any AACT application.

## Why Assess the Foodweb

Before applying compost tea, you must know what biology is already present in your soil. The assessment determines:
- Whether the soil is bacterial-dominated or fungal-dominated (and what the crop requires)
- Which organism groups are missing or deficient
- Whether disease organisms are present in problematic numbers
- What the fungal-to-bacterial (F:B) ratio is relative to the desired plant community

The Field Guide states: "First, establish what biology you have in your soil. Second, balance the foodweb. Third, monitor the foodweb with time."

## Assessment Methods Compared

The Field Guide evaluates three main approaches:

**Direct Microscopy (SFI method):**
- Dilutes soil, stains sub-samples, measures length, width, and number of individuals per group
- Uses activity stain to distinguish active (performing functions) from total organisms
- Identifies morpho-species based on morphology
- Measures active and total bacterial biomass, fungal biomass, protozoa numbers, nematode numbers by functional group
- Determines mycorrhizal colonization (VAM, ericoid, or ectomycorrhizal)
- Identifies root disease fungi and percent of root system affected
- Has published data correlating biomass levels to disease protection, nutrient cycling, and soil aggregation

**Plate Counts:**
- Grow organisms on specific nutrient media at one temperature and moisture
- Recover only 4 to 8 bacterial species out of approximately 25,000 per gram
- Cannot detect fungi, protozoa, or nematodes
- Do not differentiate beneficial from pathogenic bacteria
- No published correlation to plant response or disease suppressiveness
- Miss approximately 99.99% of soil bacteria and fungi

**Enzyme Assays:**
- Measure functional capacity of specific microbial processes
- Show changes in species composition across soils
- Useful for specific questions (e.g., cellulose-utilizing bacteria through a sprayer)
- Do not give biomass or organism group information

## Key Organism Groups to Assess

**Bacteria:** Active and total biomass. Bacteria immobilize nutrients, form microaggregates (soil structure), and compete with disease organisms for food and space.

**Fungi:** Active and total biomass, hyphal diameter. Fungi retain nutrients, form macroaggregates, and are the primary disease competitors on leaf surfaces and in soil. Fungal biomass above 2 micrograms per ml in tea shows good disease control.

**Protozoa:** Flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates. Protozoa consume bacteria and release plant-available nutrients (nitrogen cycling). It takes two weeks at 72 degrees F for protozoa to wake up and begin cycling nutrients after soil improvement. Worm compost is the best source.

**Nematodes:** Identified by functional group:
- Bacterial-feeders: relatively easy to establish; cycle nutrients
- Fungal-feeders: require fungal foods in the compost; most thermal compost lacks them
- Predatory nematodes: interfere with root-feeders; hard to establish but critical
- Root-feeders: indicate soil health problems; can be reduced from near 100 per gram to undetectable within 6 months with a complete foodweb

Forest soil is the best inoculum source for beneficial nematodes. A DIC microscope is needed for species identification.

**Mycorrhizal Fungi:** Assess colonization percentage of roots. VAM for annuals, deciduous trees, and grasses; ectomycorrhizal for conifers. Mycorrhizal inocula must be placed next to roots -- addition to tea works only if applied just before the plant goes near the spores (root dips, soil injections, aeration cores).

## The Assessment Process (Field Guide Protocol)

1. Take soil samples according to SFI protocol (call before taking samples)
2. Send soil samples for microbiological analysis to SFI ($252 covers active and total bacterial and fungal biomass, protozoa, and nematodes)
3. Send separate samples for nutrient/chemistry analysis (International Ag Lab, $25 for Test S-3 without recommendation)
4. Have chemistry results faxed to SFI or included with microbiology samples
5. Include lists of current practices and future goals with samples
6. Utilize an SFI Certified Advisor to interpret results and set up application programs

## What Assessment Reveals for Tea Selection

The assessment directly determines what kind of tea to brew:

- If soil is too fungal: brew a bacterial-dominated tea
- If soil is too bacterial (75% of cases): brew a fungal-dominated tea
- If specific organism groups are missing: add inoculants or select compost that contains them
- Foliage generally needs tea as fungal as possible
- The goal is to match the F:B ratio to the successional stage of the desired plant community

## Seasonal Monitoring

The foodweb changes through the year. SFI has published data showing how active and total organisms shift with soil type, season, and plant communities. Regular monitoring tracks whether management practices are moving the foodweb in the desired direction. After 4-5 years of healthy management, application rates can decrease to seasonal-only.

## Nutrient Cycling Assessment

The foodweb assessment also reveals nutrient dynamics:
- Total bacterial and fungal biomass indicates nutrient retention capacity
- Protozoa and nematode numbers indicate nutrient cycling rate
- If bacteria and fungi are present but protozoa are absent, nutrients are immobilized but not cycling to plants
- Compost provides approximately 25 lbs of N per acre; N is recycled through the foodweb until the plant takes it up
