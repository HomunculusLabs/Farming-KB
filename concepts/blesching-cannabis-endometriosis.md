# Cannabis and Endometriosis

## Overview

The endometrium is the interior lining of the uterus, governed by hormonal changes throughout the menstrual cycle. Endometriosis is a proliferation of endometrial cells outside the uterine cavity. These displaced cells continue to respond to hormonal changes and behave as they would inside the uterus; however, without an easy exit pathway, the tissue remains, causing growths and adhesions.

Growths and adhesions may spread throughout the pelvic cavity and form attachments on the ovaries, bowels, bladder, or any other surrounding tissue. The condition can be extremely painful and is a leading cause of chronic pelvic pain in women of reproductive age, significantly impacting quality of life and fertility.

### Primary Symptoms
- Generalized pelvic pain (often cyclical, worsening with menstruation)
- Difficult or painful periods (dysmenorrhea)
- Painful sexual intercourse (dyspareunia)
- Abnormal or heavy bleeding
- Infertility (in some cases, depending on lesion location and severity)
- Gastrointestinal symptoms if bowel is involved
- Urinary symptoms if bladder is affected

### Diagnosis
Tests to determine endometriosis include pelvic examination and ultrasound imaging; however, the only definitive way to confirm endometrial tissue growth outside the uterus is via laparoscopy. Under full anesthesia, a physician inserts a laparoscope through a small abdominal incision to visually inspect the pelvic cavity for endometrial implants, adhesions, and scar tissue.

### Orthodox Treatment
Pain management with NSAIDs and analgesics, hormonal supplementation (oral contraceptives, GnRH agonists, progestins), and surgery (laparoscopic excision of implants, hysterectomy in severe cases) are the standard approaches. Hormonal treatments aim to suppress the cyclical hormonal changes that drive endometrial tissue growth, but carry significant side effects including weight gain, mood changes, bone density loss, and menopausal symptoms.

The exact causes remain unclear, though retrograde menstruation — unexpelled menstrual blood containing endometrial cells returning via the fallopian tubes to the pelvic cavity — is the leading hypothesis. Other theories include immune system dysfunction, embryonic cell transformation, and surgical scar implantation.

## Cannabis Health Index Evidence

The Cannabis Health Index (CHI) rated evidence for cannabis in endometriosis with a total value of **5**, based on **2 reviewed studies**.

## Clinical Study Findings

### 2010 — Florida State University: CB1 Receptors and Endometrial Pain (CHI: 2)

Researchers discovered that CB1 cannabinoid receptors are present in nerve cells that innervate endometrial growths. This was a novel finding establishing a direct anatomical link between the endocannabinoid system and endometriosis-associated pain. CB1 activation was shown to reduce pain sensation (hyperalgesia) in the animal model.

Both CB1 agonists and CB1 antagonists were tested in the study, confirming the receptor-specific pathway. The authors wrote: "Together these findings suggest that the endocannabinoid system contributes to mechanisms underlying both the peripheral innervation of the abnormal growths and the pain associated with endometriosis, thereby providing a novel approach for the development of badly-needed new treatments."

This finding is significant because it identifies a specific mechanism — CB1-mediated pain reduction in the nerves specifically surrounding endometrial lesions — rather than a general systemic analgesic effect. The presence of CB1 receptors on nerves innervating the abnormal growths suggests the endocannabinoid system is intimately involved in both the innervation and the pain signaling of endometriotic tissue.

### 2010 — Université Paris Descartes: Anti-Proliferative Effects of WIN55,212-2 (CHI: 3)

A laboratory experiment conducted on human endometriotic cell lines, confirmed in vivo using rodents implanted with endometrial tissue, reported several important discoveries. The synthetic cannabinoid WIN55,212-2 abrogated the growth of endometriotic tissue and exerted a direct anti-proliferative effect in mice implanted with endometrial tissue.

This finding is particularly noteworthy because it suggests a dual therapeutic potential: cannabinoids may not only manage endometriosis-associated pain but also potentially slow or halt the progression of the disease itself by inhibiting the proliferation of endometrial cells outside the uterus. The anti-proliferative mechanism adds a disease-modifying dimension beyond simple symptomatic relief, which is rare in the current endometriosis therapeutic landscape.

## Mechanistic Significance

The two studies together reveal a compelling dual mechanism of action:

1. **Pain pathway modulation** — CB1 receptors on nerves innervating endometrial lesions provide a specific target for reducing endometriosis-specific pelvic pain, distinct from general analgesia
2. **Anti-proliferative action** — Cannabinoids (particularly CB2-preferring compounds) can inhibit the growth and spread of endometriotic tissue itself

This combination is rare in endometriosis therapeutics, where current treatments typically address either pain or tissue growth, but rarely both simultaneously. A single therapeutic approach that addresses both dimensions would represent a significant advance in endometriosis management.

## Strain-Specific Considerations

WIN55,212-2 binds with higher affinity to CB2 than CB1 receptors. Indicas and indica-dominant strains generally present with a lower THC:CBD ratio, thus favoring CB2 expression. Since the Florida State study identified CB1 involvement in pain signaling around endometrial lesions and the Paris study demonstrated anti-proliferative effects through a CB2-preferring cannabinoid, a balanced approach engaging both receptor pathways may offer the most comprehensive therapeutic strategy.

THC binds relatively equally to CB1 and CB2, making whole-plant cannabis preparations potentially advantageous over isolated cannabinoids for endometriosis patients seeking both pain relief and anti-proliferative effects. The entourage effect of multiple cannabinoids, terpenes, and flavonoids working together may provide synergistic benefits beyond any single compound.

Researchers have examined the psychological profiles of women with chronic pelvic pain and found connections between changes in hormone profiles and exposure to chronic stress or post-traumatic stress disorders. This association was especially pronounced in patients who were victims of physical or sexual abuse, suggesting that trauma history may influence both the development and severity of endometriosis symptoms through stress-mediated hormonal disruption.

## Relationship to the Endocannabinoid System

The endometrium naturally contains cannabinoid receptors and produces endocannabinoids as part of normal uterine function. The endocannabinoid system plays a well-documented role in reproductive processes including embryo implantation, ovarian function, and hormonal regulation. The discovery that this same system is involved in endometriosis — a condition of abnormal endometrial cell behavior — suggests that dysregulation of endocannabinoid signaling may contribute to the disease's development or progression.

Anandamide (AEA), the body's own cannabinoid, has been shown to play a critical role in determining whether a fertilized egg will implant in the uterine wall. The fact that cannabinoid signaling governs both the normal behavior and the abnormal proliferation of endometrial cells highlights the system's fundamental importance in uterine biology and provides a strong theoretical foundation for cannabinoid-based endometriosis treatments.

## Current Treatment Limitations

The limitations of existing endometriosis treatments underscore the need for new therapeutic approaches:

- **Hormonal suppression** — While effective for some patients, hormonal treatments carry significant side effects including menopausal symptoms, bone density loss, mood changes, and weight gain. They are also contraindicated for women seeking pregnancy.
- **Pain management** — NSAIDs and opioid analgesics address symptoms only and carry their own risks with long-term use.
- **Surgical excision** — While laparoscopic surgery can remove visible implants, recurrence rates are high. Hysterectomy is a radical option not appropriate for women desiring fertility.
- **No cure exists** — All current treatments manage symptoms rather than addressing underlying disease mechanisms.

Cannabinoid therapy's potential to both reduce pain and inhibit tissue proliferation would address two major treatment gaps simultaneously with a single intervention, potentially offering advantages over current multi-drug regimens.

## Research Gaps

Significant research gaps exist in understanding cannabis and endometriosis. The two available studies represent only preliminary evidence. Needed research areas include:

- Controlled human clinical trials with endometriosis patients measuring pain outcomes
- Longitudinal studies on cannabinoid effects on endometrial lesion progression
- Investigation of specific cannabinoid ratios optimal for endometriosis symptom profiles
- Studies comparing cannabis to standard hormonal treatments for quality of life outcomes
- Examination of cannabis effects on fertility in women with endometriosis

## Summary

Despite the limited number of studies (2), the findings for cannabis in endometriosis are mechanistically compelling. The identification of CB1 receptors in nerves specifically innervating endometrial lesions, combined with the anti-proliferative effects of cannabinoids on endometriotic tissue, suggests a dual therapeutic potential encompassing both pain management and disease modification. Given the limited effectiveness and significant side effects of current hormonal treatments and the invasive nature of surgical options, cannabinoid-based therapies represent a promising new avenue warranting expanded clinical research with larger patient populations.
