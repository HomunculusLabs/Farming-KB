# Enterprise Overhead and Gross Margin Analysis

Gross margin analysis, derived from Cambridge economist David Wallace,
helps determine the strongest links of product conversion in the chain
from sunlight energy to solar dollar. It assists in finding the
enterprise or combination of enterprises that brings in the most income
for the least additional cost each year, essential for ranch  [[homestead-financial-planning]].

## Fixed vs. Variable Costs

On most farms and ranches, money is tied up unproductively in fixed
costs (overhead) -- land payments, salaries, family withdrawings,
vehicles, and machinery. None of these creates new wealth; only
activities leading to a saleable product do. The key distinction:

- **Fixed costs**: Exist regardless of production volume. Examples:
  land payments, insurance, equipment payments on owned machinery.
- **Variable costs**: Are a function of production volume. Examples:
  seed, fuel for sowing and harvesting, livestock supplements.

Wallace originally used "gross profit" but found it confusing for
farmers computing positive figures while their bottom line was deeply
negative. "Gross margin" distinguishes his analysis from conventional
profit-and-loss statements.

## American vs. Wallace Approach

Conventional American analysis allocates portions of fixed costs to
individual enterprises. This can show identical margins for very
different enterprises. Wallace's approach excludes all overhead
allocations, revealing that an enterprise with low variable costs may
contribute far more toward covering overall farm expenses than an
alternative that looks equally profitable when overhead is included.

## The Bridge Analogy

Savory offers a visualization: picture yourself on a bridge looking
upstream. Any water (cost) that has passed under your feet is fixed;
any water (cost) upstream is still variable. The classification depends
on timing. In the very long term all costs are variable; in the very
short term all costs are fixed. No formula can sort this because any
item could be either depending on the situation.

## Comparing Different Enterprises

Gross margin analysis allows comparison of very different enterprises:
crops versus livestock, different crops, or livestock versus recreation.
To compare dissimilar enterprises, use consistent units:

- Gross margin per acre per year when land is the major capital
  commitment.
- Gross margin per dollar of capital or per man-hour of labor in other
  cases.

A single enterprise can be broken into components: should replacement
stock be bought or bred? Should hay be bought [[integrate-rather-than-segregate]] grown to free
land for livestock and decrease capital per animal unit?

## When to Apply Gross Margin Analysis

Enterprises benefit from analysis at three points during the year:

1. **Evaluating new enterprises** or when market shifts affect
  existing ones.
2. **Annual financial planning** when the main strategy takes shape.
3. **Year's end** when actual results feed back into future plans.

## Accounting for Risk

Advance analysis must consider major risk variables. Project worst,
average, and best cases, starting with the variable least within
control. For a dry farmer, weather is critical -- compute margins at
low, average, and high yields at average price. For irrigated farming,
price may be the critical variable.

## Shortcomings

Despite its benefits, gross margin analysis has limitations:

1. **It ignores [[fungi-in-ecosystem-processes-dighton]]**: A chemical monoculture may show
   the highest margin while damaging soil and biological communities.
2. **Scaling effects**: Doubling an enterprise may convert fixed costs
   to variable ones.
3. **Chemical dependency**: High fertilizer damages [[fukuoka-natural-fertility-cycle]]
   and creates spiraling costs not visible in short-term analysis.
4. **Pest vulnerability**: Monocultures invite outbreaks requiring
   pesticides that create long-term problems.
5. **External costs**: Pollution is borne by the broader community,
   not reflected in the analysis.

## Integration with Other Guidelines

Gross margin analysis must be used with other testing guidelines: the
cause and effect guideline, [[savory-weak-link-analysis]], [[animal-tractoring]] guideline, and the holistic goal itself. The ideal is
finding the enterprise combination where all tools pass all tests,
creating a holistically sound chain from sunlight to solar dollar.

## The Supplemental Feeding Example

Supplemental feed and salt expenses may have no justification at all.
The same funds invested in fencing to increase paddock divisions would
yield a higher marginal reaction. Supplements seldom pass the marginal
reaction test compared to fencing until paddock levels reach thirty or
more per herd with good biological planning.
