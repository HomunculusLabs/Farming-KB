## Sampling and Culture of Arbuscular Fungi

Studying [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] (AM) fungi requires specialized techniques for sampling, isolating, and culturing these obligate symbionts. Because AM fungi cannot be grown apart from their plant hosts, all [[mushroom-spawn-making-and-pure-culture-methods]] rely on maintaining living plant roots. This chapter covers the practical methodologies for extracting spores from soil, establishing trap-pot cultures, and culturing individual species from mixed communities.

## Spore Extraction from Soil

### Wet-Sieving and Sucrose Centrifugation

The standard method for extracting AM fungal spores from soil involves wet-sieving followed by sucrose-density gradient centrifugation (Daniels and Skipper 1982). The procedure includes:

1. **Visual inspection** of soil for epigeous sporocarps at the collection site
2. **Soil collection** to a predetermined depth (usually 15–20 cm), ensuring samples contain all available roots
3. **Homogenization** by mixing soil with water in a blender for 30 seconds
4. **Wet-sieving** through nested sieves with 500-, 250-, and 38- (or 45-) μm openings using a water spray
5. **Examination** of material from 500- and 250-μm sieves for small sporocarps and large spores
6. **Sucrose centrifugation** of material from the 38-μm sieve at 20% and 60% concentrations using 50-ml tubes with a swinging bucket rotor
7. **Rinsing** of supernatant through a fine sieve, rinsing in distilled water, and storage in glass Petri dishes

More than 80% of described AM species are nonsporocarpic, meaning they produce spores singly or in loose aggregates rather than in organized fruiting structures. This makes careful attention to sieve sizes critical — undescribed *Glomus* species producing spores less than 60 μm in diameter may remain undetected despite being dominant in some plant communities.

### Alternative Methods

- **Filter paper separation**: Sievings washed through prewetted filter paper in a Buchner funnel under vacuum; filter paper can be stored at 4°C for 5–7 days or dried for longer storage
- **Nested sieves of specific sizes**: For separating spores of different species, sizes, and colors

### Spore Counting

Spores can be counted directly in gridded Petri dishes under a stereomicroscope when numbers are low. When numbers are high, spores from 30–40 randomly chosen grid cells are counted, averaged, and extrapolated. Gridded filter paper eliminates movement but makes differentiation of similar morphotypes more difficult for inexperienced observers.

## Determining Sampling Depth and Strategy

### Vertical Distribution

Spores of AM fungi are highly aggregated, occurring in clusters separated by horizontal and vertical distances of only a few centimeters (Anderson et al. 1983; Friese and Koske 1991). The appropriate sampling depth is determined by the location of the active root system:

- Crop and pasture plants: roots most abundant in top 15 cm of soil, where most spores are found
- Sand dunes: spore abundance can be high at depths of 100 cm or more

A common mistake is sampling only at and just below the soil surface where organic matter may be high but roots and sporulation may be rare or absent.

### Horizontal Sampling

**Stratified random sampling** (Mueller-Dombois and Ellenberg 1974) involves collecting root and soil samples at randomly selected points along one or more transects in representative areas. For sites with sparse plant cover, it is useful to sample the plant nearest the random point.

### Number of Samples

The species-increment plot method estimates appropriate sample numbers for measuring species richness, while the spore-abundance plot method is used for population measurements. In New England sand dunes, 30 separate samples were sufficient for quantifying dominant and subdominant species (Tews and Koske 1986). Pooling or "bulking" of samples often results in loss of valuable information and is not recommended.

## Seasonal and Successional Considerations

Seasonal fluctuations in live spore abundance have been documented for many species, with sporulation frequently linked to host phenology. Different fungal species can have unique seasonal patterns, meaning samples from a single date may not accurately assess [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]].

- **Monthly sampling** over 1–2 years is most desirable
- **Quarterly sampling** may be more practical
- Even a single sampling coupled with trap cultures provides valuable information
- In arid or wet habitats where [[genomics-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-development]] is low, sporulation may not be detected for much of the year

The relationship between aboveground succession and AM fungal community composition has been studied in many habitats, but interpretation of field-collected data at minimum requires multiple seasonal sampling along with measurements of soil characteristics (pH, organic matter, [[cation-exchange-capacity]], nutrient concentrations).

## Trap-Pot Cultures

Trap-pot cultures are the primary method for recovering nonsporulating species and inducing sporulation of fungi that are present in field-collected roots but not producing spores. The procedure:

1. Collect at least 250 cm³ of field soil containing abundant roots; chop roots into small fragments
2. Mix soil-root material thoroughly with coarse sterilized sand (1:1 v/v) to fill a pot of at least 600 cm³ volume
3. Sow seeds of host plant; grow for minimum 4 months with low-phosphorus fertilizer applied only as needed
4. At harvest (ideally 30–40 days after shoot growth ceases), assess colonization and extract spores
5. If colonization and spore abundance are high, terminate [[solomon-watering-and-dry-farming]] pots slowly; store at 4°C (temperate) or room temperature (tropical) for minimum 30 days
6. If spore numbers are low, a second propagation cycle (reseeding without disturbing pot contents) often increases abundance and richness
7. In rare cases, a third cycle may be needed, with vigorous mixing of pot contents before reseeding

## Culturing Individual Species

### The INVAM Protocol

The procedure to establish a monospecific culture from a trap culture (Morton et al. 1993):

1. **Extract and group spores** by morphology; mount minimum 10 spores on slides as permanent vouchers
2. **Clean spores**: Place 50–100 spores of each morphotype in a watchglass, remove extraneous material with a pipette; store at 4°C for ≥24 hours and re-examine for unhealthy/parasitized spores
3. **Prepare seedlings**: Place a pot with 10–12-day-old sorghum seedlings growing in sterile medium into sterilized water; gently tease roots apart
4. **Inoculate**: Remove 3 seedlings, wash roots with a gentle water stream until entwined, then pipette spores directly along root length
5. **Plant**: Transfer seedlings to 3.8 × 21-cm cone-tainers with sterile growth medium; grow for ≥90 days
6. **Verify**: Examine for mycorrhizae and sporulation

For **single-spore cultures**, a minimum of ten replicates is needed to ensure at least one successful culture. An alternative method involves germinating individual spores on membrane filters placed over steamed soil, then transferring germinated spores with seedlings to pots.

### Contamination Control

Contamination by other AM fungi and antagonistic organisms is a persistent challenge. Critical measures include:

- Prohibit field soils in culture rooms
- Disinfect surfaces regularly to reduce airborne propagule movement
- Handle pots carefully to prevent cross-contamination
- Use bottom-watering methods to prevent splashing
- Maintain an immediate chemical control plan for insect infestations (especially fungal gnats and Collembola)
- Monitor with sterile control pots placed randomly on culture tables

### Scale-Up

Once a culture contains only one species, volume can be multiplied by transferring whole inoculum (spores, root fragments, hyphae, and growth medium) to larger pots. However, "internal" contaminants from the parent culture may not appear until the third or fourth propagation cycle — making it critical to check culture composition and prepare vouchers at the end of each cycle.
