# Fukuoka's Natural Farming Method: Clover, Barley, and Rice

updated: 2026-04-21

In [[fukuoka-natural-farming-method]] and represents decades of refinement on [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming]] — no tillage, no chemical fertilizer, no pesticides, and no weeding — are not abstract ideals but achievable practices that produce competitive yields.

## The Clover-Rice-Barley Cycle

Fukuoka sows clover seed in early October, scattering about a pint by pinching it between his fingertips. An hour covers a quarter-acre. The clover grows thick and fast with the arrival of spring. Rice seed is sown into this [[fukuoka-clover-cover-cropping-soil-ecology]], and the field is flooded to weaken the clover and favor the rice. The water is then drained and the field left to itself. The rice, barley, and clover work the soil biologically.

A week or two before harvesting rice, four to ten quarts of barley seed are scattered over the field from a basket. After harvesting and threshing, all rice straw is scattered back over the field uncut. Rice seed is then scattered from mid-November to January on top of the clover and barley. The farmer stays out of the fields until it is time to cut the barley.

The [[white-clover-living-mulch]] is the linchpin of the entire system, replacing nitrogen fertilizer, herbicide, and tillage in a single plant.

The [[fukuoka-scattering-straw-technique]] illustrates a recurring theme in Fukuoka's work: what seems messy and disorderly to the scientific mind is often precisely what nature requires. The [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-system]] came about when Fukuoka simply stopped removing straw from his fields and observed what happened. The clover thrived, weeds were suppressed, and soil fertility improved. This discovery, which took agricultural researchers decades to validate through controlled experiments, emerged from a single decision to stop interfering.

## The Problem with Composting

Fukuoka deliberately declared composting unnecessary and proposed scattering all fresh rice straw over the field. Agricultural technicians had always regarded rice straw as a source of rice blast disease. In Hokkaido, farmers were instructed to burn their straw, with incentive money given for composting. Converting 100 pounds of straw to compost doubles its weight to 200 pounds, requiring repeated turning with a pitchfork in hot, steaming conditions. Fukuoka calls this "hellish work" that young people today are incapable of performing.

The composting debate highlights a key difference between [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] that develops under continuous straw mulching is qualitatively different from the fertility provided by compost applications. Mulch feeds a diverse soil food web that includes fungi, bacteria, earthworms, and microarthropods, while compost primarily provides readily available nutrients that feed plants directly. Fukuoka believed the former approach produced healthier soil, healthier plants, and more nutritious food.

## Fertilization

For yields above 20 bushels per quarter-acre, Fukuoka suggests applying 400-500 pounds of chicken manure. He notes, however, that chemicals used in poultry raising may make chicken manure problematic. All grain should be taken home, but all straw and hulls must be returned to the field. Human wastes, though polluted, should not be overlooked as a valuable resource. The fields also get fertilized by sparrows, moles, and other small creatures.

Fukuoka's attitude toward fertilization reflects the [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] that eventually develops can sustain productive crops without any added fertility.

## Natural Farming Beyond Science

Fukuoka positions his method as going a step beyond science. He points out that over twenty years he had not read a single book on the topic, yet had managed to stay at the forefront of [[jadam-rice-cultivation-methods]] practices in Japan. His teacher is nature itself, which is always perfect in every case. Others have broken nature down and looked at it only as small fragments, learning only incomplete things.

Science, in Fukuoka's view, never does more than mimic a virtual image of nature that exists only in the human mind. Anything created by man with scientific knowledge will always be inferior to nature. The moment one becomes humble before nature and renounces the self, the self becomes assimilated into nature and nature allows it to live.

This philosophical stance connects the clover-barley-rice method to [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-philosophy]] holds that this minimalism is not a limitation but a strength.

## Irrigation and Water Management

Fukuoka notes that water management is the most critical part of rice cultivation. Farmers using direct-seeding methods irrigate at times different from neighboring farmers who transplant rice, which can lead to disputes since irrigation canals are communally controlled. He emphasizes that natural farming uses intermittent irrigation and that proper levee maintenance is essential.

Intermittent irrigation — flooding the field and then allowing it to dry partially — is critical for both rice health and soil biology. Continuous flooding, as practiced in conventional paddies, creates anaerobic conditions that can harm soil organisms and promote methane emissions. The [[fukuoka-weed-ecology]] that develops includes nitrogen-fixing legumes, deep-rooted plants that break up compacted soil, and shallow-rooted species that protect the soil surface. The [[fukuoka-natural-farming-method]] thus offers a path to agricultural viability that does not depend on economies of scale.
