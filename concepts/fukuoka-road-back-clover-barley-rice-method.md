## The Problem with Composting

Fukuoka deliberately declared composting unnecessary and proposed scattering all fresh rice straw over the field. Agricultural technicians had always regarded rice straw as a source of rice blast disease. In Hokkaido, farmers were instructed to burn their straw, with incentive money given for composting. Converting 100 pounds of straw to compost doubles its weight to 200 pounds, requiring repeated turning with a pitchfork in hot, steaming conditions. ## Irrigation and Water Management

Fukuoka notes that water management is the most critical part of rice cultivation. Farmers using direct-seeding methods irrigate at times different from neighboring farmers who transplant rice, which can lead to disputes since irrigation canals are communally controlled. He emphasizes that natural farming uses intermittent irrigation and that proper levee maintenance is essential.

Intermittent irrigation — flooding the field and then allowing it to dry partially — is critical for both rice health and soil biology. Continuous flooding, as practiced in conventional paddies, creates anaerobic conditions that can harm soil organisms and promote methane emissions. The [[fukuoka-natural-farming-method]] thus offers a path to agricultural viability that does not depend on economies of scale.
