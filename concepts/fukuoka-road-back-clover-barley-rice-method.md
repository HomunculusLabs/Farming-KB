# Fukuoka's Natural Farming Method: Clover, Barley, and Rice

updated: 2026-04-21

In [[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]], Masanobu Fukuoka describes in practical detail his method of growing rice and barley in succession without tillage, chemical fertilizers, or pesticides. This is a biological method that uses plants and animals rather than heavy machinery.

This system is the practical core of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-method]] and represents decades of refinement on [[natural-farming-fukuoka]]'s farm on Shikoku Island. It demonstrates that the [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming]] — no tillage, no chemical fertilizer, no pesticides, and no weeding — are not abstract ideals but achievable practices that produce competitive yields.

## The Clover-Rice-Barley Cycle

Fukuoka sows clover seed in early October, scattering about a pint by pinching it between his fingertips. An hour covers a quarter-acre. The clover grows thick and fast with the arrival of spring. Rice seed is sown into this clover cover, and the field is flooded to weaken the clover and favor the rice. The water is then drained and the field left to itself. The rice, barley, and clover work the soil biologically.

A week or two before harvesting rice, four to ten quarts of barley seed are scattered over the field from a basket. After harvesting and threshing, all rice straw is scattered back over the field uncut. Rice seed is then scattered from mid-November to January on top of the clover and barley. The farmer stays out of the fields until it is time to cut the barley.

The [[fukuoka-rice-barley-dual-culture]] creates a self-reinforcing cycle. Clover fixes atmospheric nitrogen, feeding both the rice and barley. Straw mulch suppresses weeds and feeds soil organisms. The continuous ground cover prevents erosion and maintains soil moisture. The [[white-clover-living-mulch]] is the linchpin of the entire system, replacing nitrogen fertilizer, herbicide, and tillage in a single plant.

The [[fukuoka-direct-seeded-no-tillage-rice-barley-method]] eliminates transplanting, the most labor-intensive operation in conventional rice farming. By scattering rice seed directly into the standing clover and barley, Fukuoka reduced the labor requirement for rice cultivation to a fraction of the conventional standard.

## Straw Mulching Philosophy

Fukuoka insists that straw must be scattered loosely and disorderly. The barley grows up through the spaces left between straw pieces. He tells researchers to scatter all the straw — three, four, or five hundred pounds — without hesitation. When a university professor cut the straw neatly and laid it in order, the barley failed to germinate.

He recounts that it took the Ehime Prefecture research facility nine years to adopt his straw method. They began by cutting straw into small pieces with a cutter, taking a whole day. It took three years for them to realize there was no need to chop the straw at all. Meanwhile, Fukuoka's method required only two or three hours of work.

The [[fukuoka-scattering-straw-technique]] illustrates a recurring theme in Fukuoka's work: what seems messy and disorderly to the scientific mind is often precisely what nature requires. The [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-system]] works because loosely scattered straw creates a heterogeneous environment with varying levels of light, moisture, and temperature — the very diversity that allows seeds to find their optimal germination conditions.

The [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-system]] came about when Fukuoka simply stopped removing straw from his fields and observed what happened. The clover thrived, weeds were suppressed, and soil fertility improved. This discovery, which took agricultural researchers decades to validate through controlled experiments, emerged from a single decision to stop interfering.

## The Problem with Composting

Fukuoka deliberately declared composting unnecessary and proposed scattering all fresh rice straw over the field. Agricultural technicians had always regarded rice straw as a source of rice blast disease. In Hokkaido, farmers were instructed to burn their straw, with incentive money given for composting. Converting 100 pounds of straw to compost doubles its weight to 200 pounds, requiring repeated turning with a pitchfork in hot, steaming conditions. Fukuoka calls this "hellish work" that young people today are incapable of performing.

The composting debate highlights a key difference between [[natural-farming-vs-organic-farming]]. Organic farming typically relies heavily on compost as a primary fertility source. Natural farming, as Fukuoka practiced it, returns organic matter directly to the soil surface as mulch, allowing decomposition to occur in situ with minimal labor and maximum benefit to soil biology.

The [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] that develops under continuous straw mulching is qualitatively different from the fertility provided by compost applications. Mulch feeds a diverse soil food web that includes fungi, bacteria, earthworms, and microarthropods, while compost primarily provides readily available nutrients that feed plants directly. Fukuoka believed the former approach produced healthier soil, healthier plants, and more nutritious food.

## Fertilization

For yields above 20 bushels per quarter-acre, Fukuoka suggests applying 400-500 pounds of chicken manure. He notes, however, that chemicals used in poultry raising may make chicken manure problematic. All grain should be taken home, but all straw and hulls must be returned to the field. Human wastes, though polluted, should not be overlooked as a valuable resource. The fields also get fertilized by sparrows, moles, and other small creatures.

Fukuoka's attitude toward fertilization reflects the [[fukuoka-soil-building-approach]] that develops over time. In the early years of transitioning to natural farming, some supplemental fertility may be needed. But as the soil ecosystem matures under continuous cover cropping and mulching, external inputs become increasingly unnecessary. The [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] that eventually develops can sustain productive crops without any added fertility.

## Natural Farming Beyond Science

Fukuoka positions his method as going a step beyond science. He points out that over twenty years he had not read a single book on the topic, yet had managed to stay at the forefront of rice cultivation practices in Japan. His teacher is nature itself, which is always perfect in every case. Others have broken nature down and looked at it only as small fragments, learning only incomplete things.

Science, in Fukuoka's view, never does more than mimic a virtual image of nature that exists only in the human mind. Anything created by man with scientific knowledge will always be inferior to nature. The moment one becomes humble before nature and renounces the self, the self becomes assimilated into nature and nature allows it to live.

This philosophical stance connects the clover-barley-rice method to [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-and-spiritual-life]]. The method works not because Fukuoka designed it perfectly but because he designed it minimally, leaving maximum room for nature to express its own intelligence. The [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-philosophy]] holds that this minimalism is not a limitation but a strength.

## Irrigation and Water Management

Fukuoka notes that water management is the most critical part of rice cultivation. Farmers using direct-seeding methods irrigate at times different from neighboring farmers who transplant rice, which can lead to disputes since irrigation canals are communally controlled. He emphasizes that natural farming uses intermittent irrigation and that proper levee maintenance is essential.

Intermittent irrigation — flooding the field and then allowing it to dry partially — is critical for both rice health and soil biology. Continuous flooding, as practiced in conventional paddies, creates anaerobic conditions that can harm soil organisms and promote methane emissions. The [[duck-rice-paddy]] method, developed by Takao Furuno, builds on this insight by using ducks to control pests and weeds in intermittently flooded rice fields.

## The Weed Community

Over time, the clover-barley-rice method produces a characteristic weed community that is not a problem but a benefit. The [[fukuoka-weed-ecology]] that develops includes nitrogen-fixing legumes, deep-rooted plants that break up compacted soil, and shallow-rooted species that protect the soil surface. The [[fukuoka-crop-rotation-and-natural-weeds]] emerge spontaneously from the interaction of scattered seeds and the residual seed bank.

Fukuoka argued that weeds should not be classified simply as "good" or "bad." Each weed species plays a role in the soil ecosystem, and the diversity of the weed community is itself a measure of soil health. When the weed community becomes dominated by a single species, it indicates an ecological imbalance that usually results from human interference.

## The Biological Soil Engine

The clover-barley-rice system creates what might be called a biological soil engine. The continuous presence of living roots feeds soil microorganisms through root exudates. The straw mulch provides carbon for decomposer organisms. The clover fixes atmospheric nitrogen. The intermittent flooding and drying cycles create conditions favorable for both aerobic and anaerobic soil organisms.

This biological engine replaces the mechanical and chemical inputs of conventional farming. Where conventional farmers use tractors to till, natural farmers rely on earthworms and root penetration to maintain soil structure. Where conventional farmers apply chemical nitrogen, natural farmers rely on clover and other legumes. Where conventional farmers spray herbicides, natural farmers rely on the competitive advantages of dense, diverse plantings.

## Labor and Economics

One of the most significant aspects of the clover-barley-rice method is its radical reduction in labor. Fukuoka estimated that his method required roughly one-tenth the labor of conventional rice farming. The scattered seeding took hours rather than days. There was no transplanting, no weeding, no pesticide application, and no composting. The harvest was the same as conventional farming, but everything else was dramatically simpler.

This labor reduction has important economic implications. Farmers using natural methods can work smaller areas intensively or larger areas extensively. They are less dependent on purchased inputs, making them more resilient to price fluctuations and supply disruptions. The [[fukuoka-natural-farming-method]] thus offers a path to agricultural viability that does not depend on economies of scale.

## Legacy and Influence

The clover-barley-rice method has influenced sustainable agriculture movements worldwide. The [[natural-farm-permaculture-ethics-principles]] incorporate many of its insights about ground cover, mulching, and minimal tillage. In Korea, the natural farming movement led by Cho Han-kyu adapted Fukuoka's methods with local modifications. In India, Subhash Palekar's zero-budget natural farming draws on similar principles.
