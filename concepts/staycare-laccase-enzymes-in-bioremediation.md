# Laccase Enzymes in Bioremediation

## Overview

Laccase (benzenediol:oxygen oxidoreductase, EC 1.10.3.2) is a multicopper blue oxidase that catalyzes the one-electron oxidation of diphenols, aromatic amines, and other phenolic compounds while reducing molecular oxygen to water. First described by Yoshida in 1883 from the lacquer tree Rhus vernicifera, laccases are now known to be widely distributed among fungi, plants, insects, and bacteria. Fungal laccases are of particular biotechnological interest due to their broad substrate specificity, use of molecular oxygen as the sole co-substrate, and ability to degrade a wide range of environmental pollutants.

## Biochemistry

### Structure and Mechanism

Laccases are glycoproteins typically with molecular weights of 60-90 kDa (glycosylated form). They contain four copper atoms per monomer organized into three spectral types:

- **T1 copper** (Type 1, blue copper): The primary electron acceptor site; responsible for the characteristic blue color. This is where the substrate binds and undergoes one-electron oxidation.
- **T2 copper** (Type 2, normal copper): Part of the trinuclear cluster; participates in electron transfer
- **T3 copper pair** (Type 3, coupled binuclear): Forms a trinuclear cluster with T2; this is where molecular oxygen binds and is reduced to water

The catalytic cycle: substrate donates an electron to T1 copper, which transfers it through T2/T3 cluster to O₂, producing water and a substrate radical.

### Substrate Range

Native laccase substrates include:
- Diphenols (guaiacol, catechol, hydroquinone)
- Aromatic amines (p-phenylenediamine, syringaldazine)
- Phenolic lignin model compounds
- Some non-phenolic compounds (limited by redox potential)

**Redox potential**: The oxidation potential of fungal laccases (0.5-0.8 V) limits their ability to directly oxidize non-phenolic compounds with higher redox potentials (>0.9 V). This constraint is overcome by mediator systems.

### Mediator Systems

The discovery that laccase, in the presence of low-molecular-weight mediators, can oxidize non-phenolic lignin model compounds (Bourbonnais & Paice, 1990) dramatically expanded the bioremediation potential of laccases.

**Synthetic mediators:**
- **ABTS** (2,2'-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonate)): First mediator discovered; enables oxidation of non-phenolic lignin; the ABTS cation radical acts as a diffusible oxidant
- **HBT** (1-hydroxybenzotriazole): Effective mediator for pulp delignification and dye decolorization; also induces laccase synthesis in T. versicolor (Collins & Dobson, 1997)
- **Violuric acid**: Nitrogen-containing compound that assists laccase in pulp delignification (Bourbonnais et al., 1997)
- **NHA** (N-hydroxyacetanilide): Another effective mediator

**Natural mediators:**
Simple, natural aromatics like phenol, aniline, and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (at concentrations as low as 0.1 mM) can mediate laccase oxidation of PAHs (Johannes & Majcherczyk, 2000). This is significant for practical applications as natural mediators are cheaper and more environmentally compatible than synthetic ones.

### pH Dependence

Laccase pH optima vary with substrate. For example, Pycnoporus cinnabarinus laccase has different optima for guaiacol, syringaldazine, and ABTS (McCarthy et al., 1999). For bioremediation applications, pH optima typically fall in the range 4-6, though some fungal laccases maintain significant activity at near-neutral pH.

### Temperature

Laccases generally have temperature optima of 30-60°C depending on the source organism. P. cinnabarinus laccase has an optimum of 65°C for dye decolorization but is unstable at this temperature, creating a trade-off between activity and stability.

## Laccase-Producing Fungi

The most studied fungal laccases come from:

| Fungus | Notable Characteristics |
|--------|------------------------|
| Trametes (Coriolus) versicolor | Strong constitutive laccase producer; widely used in bioremediation studies |
| Pycnoporus cinnabarinus | High laccase activity; mediators enhance dye decolorization |
| [[agaricus-bisporus]] | One of the first fungal laccases characterized |
| Coriolus hirsutus | Active laccase producer |
| [[neurospora-crassa]] | Model organism for laccase genetics |

Copper is a key inducer of laccase production: Cu²⁺ stimulates laccase synthesis in T. versicolor (Collins & Dobson, 1997) and P. ostreatus (Palmieri et al., 2000). Some white rot fungi (notably P. chrysosporium) produce little or no laccase under standard conditions, though laccase production has been reported using cellulose as carbon source (Srinivasan et al., 1995).

## Bioremediation Applications

### Dye Decolorization

Laccases decolorize a wide range of synthetic dyes including azo, triphenylmethane, anthraquinone, and indigo dyes. Key findings:

- Laccase from P. cinnabarinus, with HBT as mediator, decolorizes a range of dyes (McCarthy et al., 1999)
- T. versicolor laccase with mediators effectively treats dye-house effluents
- Laccase alone can decolorize phenolic dyes; mediator systems extend this to non-phenolic dyes
- Caution: laccase and MnP can also catalyze coupling reactions that create new chromophores; the residual red coloration observed during some decolorization processes may arise from such coupling of degradation products

### Pulp and Paper Bleaching

The combined use of laccase with low-molecular-weight mediators for bleaching kraft pulp has gained considerable attention since the discovery that laccase + ABTS can delignify pulp (Bourbonnais & Paice, 1990). This approach reduces chlorine consumption and the production of toxic chloroorganic wastes.

### PAH Degradation

Laccase-mediator systems can oxidize PAHs including anthracene, phenanthrene, and benzo[a]pyrene. The mediator extends the oxidative capability of laccase beyond its native redox potential, enabling attack on the non-phenolic aromatic rings characteristic of most PAHs.

### Chlorinated Compound Degradation

Laccases can dechlorinate and oxidize chlorinated phenols and related compounds. T. versicolor laccase polymerized 4-chloroaniline to produce oligomers (dimers to pentamers), which may represent a detoxification mechanism (Hoff, Liu & Bollag, 1985).

### Pesticide Transformation

Laccases from white rot fungi participate in the oxidative coupling of toxic intermediates of pesticide decomposition, potentially serving as a detoxification reaction (MacRae, 1989).

## Induction and Regulation

Laccase synthesis is regulated by multiple factors:

- **Copper**: Primary inducer in many species
- **Aromatic compounds**: 2,5-xylidene and 1-hydroxybenzotriazole induce laccase synthesis in T. versicolor (Collins & Dobson, 1997)
- **Nitrogen**: In P. chrysosporium, laccase production was observed with cellulose as carbon source (Srinivasan et al., 1995)
- **Carbon source**: Different carbon sources affect laccase production levels
- **Culture conditions**: Solid-state fermentation often produces higher laccase yields than submerged fermentation
