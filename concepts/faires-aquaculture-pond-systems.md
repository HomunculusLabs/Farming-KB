## Pond Design Principles

- Maximize edge with winding shorelines (increases habitat diversity)
- Divide into thirds: shallow end, mid-range, deep end (6-8 feet minimum)
- Include refuge areas: shallow shelves for plants, islands for ducks, deep areas for fish, hollow logs for hiding
- Stocking capacity relates to surface area, not depth -- 150+ sq ft can supply a family
- Plants should cover ~60% of surface; underwater plants at 1 bunch per 2 sq ft
- Keep large livestock out of aquaculture ponds
- pH should be maintained at 7 or above — regulate with fertilizer or lime additions
- Always source disease-free stock from a reputable supplier
- Choose species you want to eat and that can be sold locally

## Construction Steps

1. Lay rope as shape guide, dig the pond
2. Line with pond liner or gley (fermented manure layer)
3. Stabilize edges with rocks, bamboo, grass ledges, logs
4. Add 2 inches of straw trampled into bottom for insect habitat
5. Introduce water plants (lilies, duckweed, water chestnuts) -- wait 6 months
6. Plant grass at inlet to filter incoming water
7. Introduce baby fish in spring; add buckets of pond water from established ponds
8. Monitor pH (target 7-8); add lime if acidifying; drain every few years

## Polyculture System

Three biological levels keep the pond clean and productive:

1. **Aquatic vegetation** (duckweed) -- creates oxygen, feeds plant-eating fish
2. **Plant-eating fish** (tilapia) -- create fertilizer and debris; bass in netted areas control population
3. **Bottom feeders** (freshwater prawns) -- clean up debris; periodically dredged muck becomes compost

Oxygen is crucial to pond health. Solar pond aerators and fountains help maintain dissolved oxygen levels, especially in warmer months.

### Species Stocking Rates

| Species | Per Acre | Temp Range | Notes |
|---------|----------|------------|-------|
| Tilapia | 3,000 | Warm | 4 months fingerling to edible, fed duckweed, shade under water lilies |
| Catfish | 1,500 | Hardy | Winter hardy, clean pond, 1,000 minnows per 500 catfish |
| Bluegill | 500 | Warm | 3 years to edible, eats insects/fish eggs/crayfish, needs vegetation shade |
| Minnows | 2,000 | Variable | Food for bass and catfish, introduce 1 year before predators, 3 year lifespan |
| Bass | 100 | Warm | Population control, eats smaller fish, needs lots of plants |
| Trout | 400 | Cold (<60°F) | Big deep ponds or overwinter only, minnows as sole food |
| Crayfish | 200 | 65-85°F | Burrows if <45°F or >88°F, vulnerable when molting, needs hiding places |
| Shrimp/Prawns | 16,000-24,000 | >65°F | Fewer = larger, don't survive cold winters |
| Mussels | ~200 | 50-90°F | Natural filter, eats plankton at bottom, filters ~1 gallon/hour each |

### Sample Polyculture Yield (per quarter acre)

40 lbs bass, 80 lbs catfish, 120 lbs bluegill, 350 lbs tilapia

### Stocking Rules

- Reduce stocking rates by 1/3 to ensure adequate oxygen levels
- Do not grow species that compete for the same food supply in the same pond
- Introduce minnows at least one year before adding bass or other predator species

## Chinampa System

chinampas|Chinampas are an ancient Mesoamerican technique where canals between raised garden beds create slow-moving water for fish:

- Islands are never wider than 30 feet
- Canals run 100-300 feet per side, 5-8 feet deep
- Water level never closer than 3 feet to the garden surface
- Willows planted along banks prevent erosion
- Canal mud is dredged annually and used for seedling beds and mulch
- Fry can be stocked at the canal source and released into slow-flowing water

## Fry Management

- Use several small parallel ponds for raising young fish, then release when they are large enough
- Canal systems allow fry to be stocked at the source and naturally distribute through slow-flowing water
- Separate fry-rearing areas protect young fish from predators

## Dam and Wetland Design

Small-scale food forests are the best-understood example of the concept of broadacre permaculture design. Other broadacre strategies emphasise the advantages of slope. Poorly drained wetlands tend to be seen as unproductive, whereas recognition of aquaculture as a productive land use transforms their value.

The design of dams and wetlands follows natural models. Water should be held as high on the landscape as possible, released slowly through multiple use stages. Each release point provides opportunities for different productive uses.

## Reed Bed Water Treatment

The latest development in environmental engineering has been recognition that natural systems provide the best models for water purification. Artificial reed beds, living machines and other technologies are among the fastest-growing aspects of environmental technology. Swamps are the natural models for much of this technology.

Some of the best and lowest-cost water purifying systems have evolved where sewerage outfalls have been fed into natural swamps at rates moderate enough to allow adaptation of the swamp ecosystem. Beyond simple earthworks, ecological succession of streamside vegetation can effectively reinforce water-purifying functions.

## Soil Filtration

The extraordinary water-filtering capacity of deep, well-structured soils has been obscured by the flurry of research on artificial wetlands. A metre or two of free-draining, well-structured loam and clay loam soil can destroy pathogenic organisms in sewage water and free tied-up surplus minerals from clay and humus particles. Where such favourable soils exist, septic tank absorption systems work well. See [[aact-applications-guide-ingham]].
