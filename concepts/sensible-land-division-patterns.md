# Sensible Land Division Patterns

The way human societies divide and pattern land has profound implications for ecological function, resource availability, and social stability. A bird's-eye view of centralized and disempowered societies reveals strictly rectilinear networks of streets, farms, and property boundaries — patterns that serve survey instruments rather than human or environmental needs.

## The Problem with Rectilinear Boundaries

Straight-line property boundaries and grid-patterned landscapes:

- **Prevent sensible landscape planning strategies** by ignoring natural topography, water flow, and ecological zones
- **Result in neither aesthetically nor functionally satisfactory** landscapes or streetscapes
- **Once codified into law**, such patterning is stubbornly defended despite its dysfunction
- **Fragment ecosystems** by cutting across natural ridgelines, watersheds, and wildlife corridors
- **Increase infrastructure costs** by requiring straight roads across varied terrain

The rectilinear pattern is ultimately created by people and can therefore be dismantled by people — but doing so requires recognizing its inadequacy and having the will to replace it.

## Hawaiian Ahupua'a System

Hawaiian villagers developed a far more sensible approach by taking **natural ridgelines as their boundaries**. This land division system, known as the *ahupua'a*, produced stable and resource-rich landscapes extending from dense cloud forests to outer reef zones.

### Key Features

- **Water catchment containment**: Each ahupua'a contained within one water catchment, enabling the whole valley community to maintain clean water
- **Elevation gradient management**: Each unit spanned from mountain forests to sea, encompassing all ecological zones
- **Nutrient cycling**: Lost nutrients were caught in shallow ocean enclosures, converting first to algae, then to crabs and fish
- **Reef preservation**: By capturing nutrients before they reached the open ocean, offshore reef areas and marine environments were preserved
- **Water storage and diversion**: Mid-slope water resources could be stored and diverted for community needs

The radial nature of conic volcanic landscapes with their radial water lines particularly suits this method of land division. The entire system functions as an integrated resource management unit.

## Circular and Zonal Settlement Patterns

Other traditional societies adopted circular or zonal modes in their plains settlements:

- **Zulu settlements** in southern Africa used circular patterns that reflected social organization and provided defensive and functional advantages
- **American Indian plains settlements** similarly arranged dwellings and community spaces in patterns responsive to wind, sun, and social interaction

These patterns work with natural forces rather than against them, providing shelter from prevailing winds, optimizing solar exposure, and creating communal spaces that reinforce social bonds.

## Bioregional Land Division

Mollison proposes that such traditional models can be studied and adopted by future **bioregional societies** as sane and caring people become the majority in their regions. Sensible land division is described as a long-delayed but essential precursor to a stable society.

### Principles for Bioregional Division

1. **Follow natural boundaries**: Use ridgelines, watersheds, and ecological transitions as borders
2. **Maintain catchment integrity**: Each division should contain its complete water cycle
3. **Span ecological gradients**: Each unit should have access to diverse resources from different elevation zones
4. **Enable nutrient closure**: Design boundaries that allow cycling of nutrients within the system
5. **Preserve downstream systems**: Prevent nutrient export that degrades marine or lowland ecosystems
6. **Support self-reliance**: Each unit should be capable of meeting basic needs from its own resources

## Traditional Navigation as Pattern Application

The sophisticated pattern recognition used in traditional navigation illustrates how tribal peoples applied pattern understanding to practical challenges:

- **Helmsman** sings to time the log line and feels for current deflections through the steering oar
- **Lookouts** note star patterns, bird flight, cloud trains, and light refraction for course correction
- **Skilled listeners** in a "black box" near the keel listen to wave refraction from the hull, predicting storms or islands from wave periods timed by chants
- **Navigators** consult voyage "charts" of sticks and cowries representing the interaction of phenomena, singing navigation songs for specific search patterns
- **Water temperature** is gauged by submerging the testicular sac in the sea
- **Wind-carried scents** from forests, rock lichen, bird colonies, and fish schools are all recorded and interpreted
- **Indicator organisms** are caught in nets related to the water mass, and near shores a "lead line" is used to find banks and sample bottom fauna
- **Polarized light** may be used to predict rain or find the sun on overcast days

## Implications for Modern Design

These traditional approaches suggest several principles for modern land division and settlement design:

- **Work with natural patterns** rather than imposing arbitrary geometric grids
- **Use catchment boundaries** as the primary organizing principle for land tenure
- **Design for nutrient cycling** to prevent resource loss and pollution
- **Create multi-resource units** that provide diverse outputs across ecological gradients
- **Preserve the capacity for community-scale management** of shared resources

Sensible land division is not merely an aesthetic preference — it is a functional necessity for creating sustainable human settlements that maintain ecological integrity over the long term.
