## Early American Mycology and Julius A. Palmer

[[julius-auboineau-palmer]] Jr. was one of the earliest and most determined American
pioneers in the practical study of [[classification-of-edible-and-poisonous-mushroom-families]] fungi. His book *About
Mushrooms*, first published in 1894, was one of only two books on the subject
ever printed in America up to that time. Palmer's work bridged the gap between
European mycological knowledge and the practical needs of American mushroom
hunters, bringing scientific rigor to a field that was then riddled with
folklore, newspaper misinformation, and genuinely dangerous misconceptions about
which mushrooms were safe to eat.

## Acknowledgments and Intellectual Debts

Palmer opened his work with generous acknowledgments to the individuals and
institutions that had supported his decade-long study of fungi. His most
significant intellectual debt was to a mentor connected with the Havemeyer sugar
refineries — a man who directed twenty subordinates in his chosen profession yet
never neglected to provide Palmer with weekly encouragement over a period of
more than ten years, until his sudden death. Though this mentor made no pretense
to specific knowledge of mycology, Palmer credited his "clear, thorough, and
educated mind" with supplying ideas that Palmer's own "more superficial nature
lacked," so that for many conclusions reached, Palmer felt himself in debt.

Palmer also acknowledged debts to the medical profession — to such a degree that
he inscribed the work to them — as well as to the press, who had "always
welcomed my communications and allowed me the privilege of this reprint of their
columns." He thanked the superintendents and public officials in charge of
state, city, and hospital grounds for granting him repeated opportunities to
gather fungi from localities that, "for the best of reasons," had been closed to
the general public. "Without such courtesies, this book could never have been
offered to the people," he wrote.

## The State of American Mycological Knowledge

When Palmer began his studies, the landscape of American mycological literature
was essentially barren. His book was only the second ever printed in America on
the subject of edible and [[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]]. Its predecessor was a work
prepared by Palmer himself and published through the enterprise of Messrs. Louis
Prang & Co., the well-known lithographers. He had also contributed an article to
Appleton's *Cyclopedia* (vol. XXVI, p. 590, 1886), which he considered "the most
complete statement of the result of my labors," though Appleton declined
permission to reprint it.

The pamphlets issued by the United States Government drew Palmer's particular
criticism. He found their utility "but limited," noting that the text was drawn
almost entirely from the earlier and incomplete labors of Worthington G. Smith.
Most troublingly, these government publications recognized no distinction
between the word "poisonous" as applied to the deadly *Amanita* and the same
term used when writing of the *[[lactarius-piperatus]]* — which Palmer described as
"a perfectly edible mushroom" and called the confusion "an inexcusable error."

## Pioneering in Isolation

Palmer worked without the support of fellow mycologists or even sympathetic
companions. He described his solitary study in evocative terms: "Save for the
use of text-books this study was prosecuted by me without sympathy or
companionship; my long days in the woods were gladdened by some new development
on the part of my humble friends; but the moan of the wind through the lofty
pine or the rustle of oak-leaves above which the little toadstool showed its
modest head was the only echo upon which I could rely for any thought or theory
which that development suggested."

He acknowledged no jealousy of contemporary students and hoped they would
continue the study until his own labors "shall seem to be those of a pioneer."
But he was sensitive about intellectual independence: "if there is one thing on
which I am sensitive, it is that I am in the least indebted to others for my
conclusions, not only in this, but in all branches of study."

## Three Types of Mushroom Poisoning

Palmer's most significant contribution to mycology was his identification of
three fundamentally different types of mushroom poisoning, each requiring
different medical treatment — a distinction that the standard medical practice
of his era entirely failed to recognize:

1. **[[amanitine-poisoning-and-absorption-mechanisms]]**: The deadliest form, caused by species of *Amanita*. The
  absorption of amanitine by the human system produced symptoms that were not
  merely unresponsive to purgative or emetic treatment but were actually
  aggravated by it. This was a critical insight that ran counter to the universal
  practice of inducing vomiting for all suspected mushroom poisoning.

2. **Acridity poisoning**: Caused by hot, peppery members of the *Russula* genus
  and related families. The digestive disturbance from these fungi responded to
  purgative treatment. The sharp, biting taste served as nature's warning signal —
  but Palmer noted this guide was reliable only for certain families and "utterly
  worthless, even illusive" when applied to the deadly Amanite.

3. **Decomposition poisoning**: Caused by eating common edible mushrooms that had
  entered a state of fermentation or decomposition. This type responded to both
  purgative and emetic treatment. Palmer himself was poisoned this way when edible
  mushrooms were contaminated by decomposing *[[coprinus-deliquescens]]* packed in the
  same shipping container.

## Rejection of the Alkaline Excess Theory

An influential theory attributed to Berkeley held that mushrooms could become
poisonous through an excess of alkali — for instance, when forced with potash or
grown in alkaline soil. Palmer carefully considered this idea and rejected it in
a subsequent article contributed to the *Boston Medical and Surgical Journal*.
His rejection was based on direct experimentation: a gardener once sent him
horse-mushrooms that had been forced with potash, and they cooked "like pieces
of soap" with liquor "resembling strong lye." But this was a matter of
contaminated growing conditions, not a general principle of alkaline toxicity.

## The Truffle Question in America

Palmer devoted considerable attention to the truffle (*Tuber*), noting that it
differed from all other mushrooms in that both the spawn and the fruiting body
remain entirely below the soil surface. He proposed an interesting experiment:
importing from France the swine and dogs already trained to detect truffles
beneath the soil, and releasing them into American oak plantations to determine
whether the fungus was indigenous to the continent. Isolated cases of truffles
had been reported found in America, but with considerable uncertainty. Palmer
directed readers to the *Revue des Deux Mondes* for April 1875 for a
comprehensive review of the truffle's nature and cultivation.

## Charles H. Peck and American Classification

Palmer praised the work of Charles H. Peck, the State Botanist of New York,
whose reports issued by the Board of Regents of the University at Albany
contained much that was of value to the student. Peck had performed the most
thorough work in the classification or identification of American mushrooms and
toadstools. Palmer understood that Peck intended to publish an illustrated work
on the fungi of the United States, "for the success of which he has my hearty
good wishes."

## Errors and Corrections

Palmer candidly acknowledged errors in his earlier published work. His most
notable mistake was condemning the *[[coprinus-micaceus]]* as non-esculent in his
*Popular Science Monthly* article; he later recognized it as perfectly safe and
important for the mycologist to identify. He also regretted his apparent
endorsement of Berkeley's alkaline theory in that same article, having carefully
rejected it in a later publication. He noted that taste as a general guide to
edibility was "only applicable to certain families" and "utterly worthless" for
the deadly Amanite.
