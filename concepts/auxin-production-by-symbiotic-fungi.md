## Bioassay for IAA Detection

A plant-based bioassay using Arabidopsis thaliana provides a rapid screening
method for auxin production by symbiotic fungi:

### Single-Medium Bioassay

Square Petri dishes are filled with MSK medium (a modified plant growth
medium) slanted at approximately 30 degrees. Fungal mycelial plugs from the
growing colony margin are placed below surface-sterilised A. thaliana seeds.
Plants are grown at 20 C with 120 micromol photons per square meter per second
and a 16-hour light cycle for up to 15 days. Root branching and root length
are quantified under a binocular microscope, with image analysis using
software such as ImageJ.

### Dual-Medium Bioassay

For fungi requiring specialised growth media (such as truffles), a dual-culture
system is used. Half the Petri dish contains fungal medium, while the other
half contains plant growth medium. The fungus is inoculated on the fungal
medium side and allowed to grow before plant seeds are added to the plant
medium side. Diffusible metabolites, including auxin, can pass between the
two compartments.

### IAA Quantification

IAA is quantified by HPLC-ESI-MS/MS (high-performance liquid chromatography
coupled with electrospray ionisation tandem mass spectrometry). Agar from
bioassay plates is extracted with water/methanol (1:1) containing deuterated
IAA as an internal standard, followed by ethyl acetate extraction and
analysis on a reversed-phase C18 column. Culture filtrates can also be
directly extracted and analysed.

## Piriformospora indica

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], P. indica
grows readily on artificial media and can colonise cruciferous plants
including A. thaliana. For broader context on [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] forming symbioses with trees such as oaks
and hazels, and some shrubs including Cistus spp. See [[truffle-cultivation-basics]] for further details on truffle biology.
Production of IAA by
ectomycorrhizal fungi is well-documented and may drive ectomycorrhiza
genesis alongside other signalling molecules.

Using the dual-medium bioassay, exudates of two truffle species (Tuber
borchii and T. melanosporum) were shown to modify A. thaliana root
architecture, inducing root shortening, increased branching, and enhanced
root hair length. T. borchii produced 1.3 x 10^-7 M IAA after 10 days of
growth in the dark, approximately ten times more than control plates.
A dose-response curve demonstrated that the IAA exuded by T. borchii fully
accounts for the observed root shortening.

## Mechanism and Significance

The production of auxin by symbiotic fungi may serve several functions:

  - Modifying host root architecture to increase the surface area available
    for fungal colonisation
  - Stimulating lateral root formation that creates additional entry points
    for fungal hyphae
  - Enhancing root hair development that improves nutrient and water uptake
  - Acting as a signalling molecule in the early stages of mycorrhizal
    establishment

## Limitations

Bioassays provide an indication of auxin-like activity but cannot be
considered definitive proof of IAA production, because other substances
may cause similar root morphological effects, or inhibiting compounds may
interfere with auxin action. Chemical quantification by HPLC-MS/MS is
necessary to confirm IAA production and determine its concentration.

## Ectomycorrhizal Fungi

[[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]], Laccaria laccata, [[suillus-bovinus]] have all been shown to produce IAA in culture
- Production levels vary with culture conditions, media composition, and fungal species
- Auxin production is often higher in young mycelial cultures

## Additional Bioassay Methods

### Filter Disc Bioassay

For testing culture filtrates or extracted compounds:

1. Apply fungal culture filtrate or extract to filter paper discs
2. Place discs on MSK agar seeded with Arabidopsis
3. Compare root responses to IAA standard dose-response curves
4. For P. indica, 135 microliters of extract per disc was effective

### Culture Filtrate Testing

1. Grow fungus in liquid medium (e.g., M+ medium for P. indica)
2. Separate mycelium by filtration
3. Apply sterile filtrate directly to filter discs, or extract with ethyl acetate at pH 3
