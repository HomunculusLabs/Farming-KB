## Mushroom Casing and Fruiting

Casing is the process of covering fully colonized substrate with a layer of non-nutritive soil or soil-like material to induce [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] (fruiting). In commercial [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]], casing is a standard practice that triggers the transition from [[maitake-spawn-run-vegetative-mycelial-phases]] growth to the production [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]]. The technique was adapted by Oss & Oeric (1976) for indoor cultivation of *[[stropharia-cubensis]]* on rye [[grain-spawn]].

## The Biological Purpose of Casing

Mycelium growing on nutrient-rich grain remains in a vegetative state, continuously expanding and consuming nutrients without producing mushrooms. Casing provides several environmental cues that shift the fungus into reproductive mode:

1. **Reduced nutrients:** The casing layer contains minimal nutrition, signaling the mycelium that food resources are becoming limited — a natural trigger for reproduction
2. **[[holzer-climate-zone-microclimate-creation]]:** A thin, moist soil layer creates a high-humidity zone at the substrate surface with favorable gas exchange, mimicking the natural soil surface where mushrooms would fruit in the wild
3. **Physical barrier:** The casing provides a protective layer that buffers against drying, temperature fluctuations, and contamination while still allowing mycelial penetration
4. **CO₂ gradient:** The casing layer helps establish a carbon dioxide gradient — high CO₂ at the substrate surface, lower CO₂ above — which is one of the primary triggers for fruiting body initiation

## Casing Procedure for Jar Cultures

### Step 1: Apply the Casing

1. Remove the dome and ring lid from the fully colonized jar
2. Cover the surface of the colonized rye with ½ to ¾ inch of casing soil (approximately 1.5 cups for quart jars)
3. The soil can be applied dry, then immediately shaken level
4. Wet the casing to **field capacity** using chlorine-free tap or distilled water applied as a fine-mist spray

### Step 2: Moistening to Field Capacity

Field capacity is the moisture content at which the soil is thoroughly moistened throughout but no water passes through into the substrate below. A practical rule of thumb:

- Spray with a fine-mist sprayer until the casing soil is moistened throughout
- Stop immediately before any water drips through the soil into the mycelium
- The surface should feel moist and spongy to the touch

**Critical:** Do not use a coarse spray, as this will seal the surface of the casing soil, creating an impermeable crust that blocks [[mushroom-cultivation-requirements-food-water-gas-exchange-and-light]] mycelial penetration.

### Step 3: Maintenance

1. Discard the jar lids — they are no longer needed
2. Maintain cultures in a high-humidity environment
3. A large styrofoam cooler with a clear or translucent polyethylene window in the lid is ideal
4. Spray the cultures daily with a fine-mist spray to replace moisture lost through evaporation
5. Each cased jar requires 2–3 good squirts of water per day for continuous fruiting
6. **Never exceed field capacity** — overwatering is more damaging than underwatering
7. Store newly cased jars in chronological order for organized harvesting

## Casing Soil Recipes

### Primary Recipe (Recommended)

| Ingredient | Volume |
|---|---|
| Peat moss | 2.5 liters |
| Fine vermiculite | 3.5 liters |
| Washed fine sand | 4 liters |
| [[peat-calcium-carbonate-perlite-casing-psilocybe-cubensis-miniculture]] (finely crushed oyster shell) | 2 liters |

This mixture provides:
- **Peat moss:** Water retention and organic structure
- **Vermiculite:** Aeration, moisture buffering, and light texture for easy mushroom penetration
- **Sand:** Weight, drainage, and structural integrity
- **Calcium carbonate:** pH buffering — mushrooms generally fruit best in slightly alkaline conditions (pH 7.0–7.5)

### Alternative Recipes

- **Mica-peat mix:** One part 50/50 vermiculite-peat moss mixture to one part potting soil
- **Garden loam:** Plain rich garden loam works but carries higher contamination risk due to unsterile conditions
- **Dung-based:** Finely granulated horse dung or cow dung, alone or mixed with chopped wheat straw
- **Leaf mold:** Leaf-mold mulch may promote fruiting of smaller, more delicate *Psilocybe* species that struggle with the primary recipe

### Casing Soil Characteristics

An ideal casing soil should be:
- **Porous enough** to allow air to reach the mycelium beneath
- **Light enough** to allow young mushroom pins to penetrate through the surface easily
- **Moisture-retentive** enough to maintain high humidity at the surface
- **Nutrient-poor** to avoid encouraging competitor organisms

## Mycelial Run-Up Through the Casing

### Days 1–7: Colonization

After casing, the mycelium begins growing up from the grain substrate into the casing soil. During this period:

- Mycelium penetrates the casing to just beneath the surface
- ropy strands become visible at the interface of soil and glass
- The network gradually develops more intersecting nodes

### Days 7–14: Surface Growth Management

The mycelium may occasionally break through the casing surface and begin spreading across it. Part of the purpose of daily misting is to keep this surface growth "knocked down" — the fine spray physically disrupts surface mycelium, encouraging it to remain subsurface where it can form primordia rather than forming a dense mat on the surface.

**Underwatering indicator:** If surface mycelium turns blue, the casing is too dry. Increase misting frequency.

### Days 14–20: Primordia Formation

By the 14th to 20th day after casing, the ropy network has differentiated into tiny white dots distributed through the casing soil along the jar perimeter. These dots are **mushroom primordia** — the earliest stage of [[fruiting-body-development]].

Primordia gradually enlarge and incorporate more mycelium, taking on the appearance of tiny mushrooms with squat, fat stems and dark brownish heads. At this "pin" stage, primordia are approximately 1–2 mm in length.

## Pinning to Maturity

### Days 20–30: Fruit Development

Once young mushrooms have penetrated the casing surface (at both the sides and center of the jar), an additional 5–10 days is required for them to reach full maturity. At maturity:

- Mushrooms are several inches tall
- Caps range from one to several centimeters in diameter
- The cap is conic to bell-shaped, viscid, whitish to pale yellow

### Lighting

Mushrooms respond favorably to light during fruiting:

- A daily **10-hour Grow-lux cycle** produces mushrooms with larger caps and shorter stems than those grown without special lighting
- Direct sunlight for prolonged periods should be avoided — approximately half the mushrooms may reach only half size or less and then cease growing ("aborts")

### Aborts

Aborted mushrooms stop growing at some point before full maturity. They should be removed promptly because:

- They are still usable when dried (though less aesthetically appealing)
- Left in the culture, they become susceptible to bacterial attack that renders them both ugly and unusable
- They can block the development of healthy pins nearby

With practice, aborts can be spotted early and removed before they become problematic.

### Bottom Fruiting

Occasionally, primordia form at the bottom of the jar and grow to maturity but cannot break through the casing surface. Mitigation strategies:

- **Tinfoil wrapping:** Wrapping jars with aluminum foil up to the top of the casing soil can inhibit bottom fruiting by reducing light exposure to the lower portions
- **Jar breaking:** If bottom fruiting becomes extensive, the jar can be carefully broken and the mycelial block removed as a single piece, allowing side-pins to develop unimpeded

## Harvesting

### Timing

The optimal harvest time is at **veil rupture** — when the partial veil connecting the cap margin to the stem detaches to form the annulus (ring). This is when:

- Alkaloid content is at or near peak
- The mushroom is fully formed but spores have not yet been released
- The cap is still convex (not yet flattened)

If a spore print is desired, the mushroom should be allowed to mature further until the cap flattens to an umbrella shape.
