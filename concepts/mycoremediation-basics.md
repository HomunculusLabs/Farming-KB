# Mycoremediation Basics

Mycoremediation is the use of fungal mycelium to break down or sequester toxic contaminants in soil and water. Part of the [[mycorestoration-overview]] framework developed by [[paul-stamets]]. The term was popularized by Singh (2006) in the encyclopedic reference "Mycoremediation: Fungal Bioremediation."

## Why Fungi?

Fungi are among nature's most versatile organisms in structure, metabolism, ecology, and genomics. Key advantages (Singh 2006):

- Fungal mycelial networks may cover several hectares of forest floor — fungi are considered "true ecosystem engineers" (Lawton and Jones, 1995)
- Most fungal cells are totipotent — the entire organism can regenerate from spores or hyphal fragments
- Fungi tolerate extreme pH (acidic or alkaline) that would kill bacteria
- White-rot fungi produce nonspecific extracellular enzymes that degrade a wide range of pollutants without needing to metabolize the compound directly
- Fungi can grow on cheap waste substrates (wood chips, sawdust, agricultural residues) and establish themselves among indigenous microorganisms
- Degradation follows pseudo-first-order kinetics — rate depends on initial concentration, unlike Michaelis-Menten bacterial kinetics

## How It Works

Mycelium secretes extracellular enzymes and acids that can break down:
- **Petroleum hydrocarbons** — PAHs, diesel, motor oil, BTEX ([[mycoremediation-of-oil-spills]])
- **PCBs and dioxins** — persistent organic pollutants ([[mycoremediation-pcbs-and-dioxins]])
- **Heavy metals** — lead, mercury, cadmium, copper, zinc via biosorption ([[mycoremediation-of-heavy-metals]])
- **PAHs** — polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons ([[mycoremediation-pahs]])
- **Phenols and chlorophenols** — industrial wastewater contaminants
- **Agricultural chemicals** — pesticides, herbicides, nitrates, phosphorus
- **Dyes and textile effluents** — azo, anthraquinone, and triphenylmethane dyes
- **E. coli and coliforms** — from farm runoff

## Key Enzyme Systems

The three major enzyme systems driving mycoremediation (Singh 2006):

### Lignin Peroxidase (LiP)
- Oxidizes non-phenolic aromatic compounds with high redox potential
- Key enzyme in PCB and dioxin degradation
- Found in Phanerochaete chrysosporium, Trametes versicolor, Bjerkandera adusta
- Degrades via oxidative cleavage producing quinones and ring-fission products

### Manganese Peroxidase (MnP)
- Generates Mn(III) chelates that act as diffusible oxidants
- Important in PAH degradation and lignin breakdown
- Works synergistically with LiP in white-rot fungal systems
- Requires Mn(II) and hydrogen peroxide

### Laccase
- Heme-containing para-diphenol oxidase that uses molecular oxygen (not H2O2)
- Oxidizes phenols, chlorophenols, and PAHs
- Generates phenoxy radicals and semiquinones, forming quinones and coupling products
- Found in Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus ostreatus, Coriolus versicolor
- Can be used with mediators (ABTS, HBT) to extend substrate range

## Fungi vs Bacteria for Bioremediation

White-rot fungi offer several advantages over bacterial systems (Singh 2006):

| Feature | White-Rot Fungi | Bacteria |
|---------|----------------|----------|
| Enzyme induction | Under nutrient-limited conditions; does not depend on the pollutant | Must be exposed to the compound; requires adequate quantities for enzyme synthesis |
| Preconditioning | Not required to a specific compound | Often required |
| Growth substrate | Cheap waste sources (wood chips, sawdust) | Cannot utilize waste substrates as easily |
| Degradation mechanism | Extracellular, nonspecific free radicals; does not require metabolism | Intracellular; requires solubility and uptake |
| Competitiveness | Produce hydroxyl radicals that kill competing microbes; adjust surrounding pH | Often outcompeted in mixed communities |
| Kinetics | Pseudo-first-order (rate depends on concentration) | Michaelis-Menten |
| Insoluble compounds | Can convert insoluble organic mixtures via nonspecific mechanism | Limited by solubility |

## Key Discoveries

### Battelle Labs Collaboration
Stamets and Battelle scientists identified a fungus that metabolized **dimethyl methylphosphonate (DMMP)** — a key ingredient in VX nerve agent. The mycelium thrived on DMMP as its sole nutrient source, converting the toxin into unstable, nontoxic subderivatives within weeks.

### Oil Spill Remediation
- Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus) deployed on oil-contaminated soil
- Mycelium broke down petroleum hydrocarbons, producing mushrooms that attracted insects
- Insects attracted birds, which deposited seeds — kickstarting ecological recovery
- Within 8 weeks, the hydrocarbon content dropped dramatically; soil was rejuvenated

### Heavy Metal Biosorption
- Aspergillus niger removed 91% copper and 70% zinc from swine effluent (Price et al. 2001)
- Fungal biosorption uses living or dead biomass; cell wall components (chitin, glucans, proteins) bind metal ions via ion exchange, complexation, and adsorption
- Mycorrhizal fungi protect host plants from metal toxicity while accumulating metals in their tissues
- See [[mycoremediation-of-heavy-metals]] for detailed coverage

### PAH Degradation
- White-rot fungi degrade PAHs via cytochrome P450 and ligninolytic enzymes
- Penicillium, Fusarium, Cunninghamella elegans metabolize PAHs as sole carbon source
- Fungal-bacterial co-cultures show enhanced mineralization rates
- See [[mycoremediation-pahs]] for detailed coverage

### PCB/Dioxin Degradation
- Trametes versicolor achieved 95% degradation of chlorinated biphenyls (Zeddel et al. 1993)
- Phanerochaete sordida degrades nearly 60% of 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin
- Cordyceps sinensis shows novel dioxin degradation pathway producing catechol intermediates
- See [[mycoremediation-pcbs-and-dioxins]] for detailed coverage

## Fungal Biodeterioration and Biodegradation

Fungi are known to degrade a wide variety of materials (Singh 2006):

| Category | Examples |
|----------|---------|
| Wood | All wood types via white-rot and brown-rot fungi |
| Plastics | Polyethylene (Penicillium simplicissimum), pPVC (Aureobasidium pullulans), polyamide-6 (P. chrysosporium) |
| Library materials | 234 species of 84 genera isolated from library materials (1919-1977) |
| Textiles | Wool (Trichophyton simii, A. niger) |
| Concrete | Fusarium spp. cause calcium loss; A. niger acids increase cement porosity |
| Rubber | Resinicium bicolor most effective at detoxifying waste tire rubber |

## The Process

1. **Profile the site** — identify contaminants, resident species, water flow
2. **Survey native mycoflora** — mushrooms already present have been naturally selected for tolerance
3. **Select species** — match enzymes to contaminants (see charts in [[mycoremediation-techniques]])
4. **Deploy mycelial mats** — inoculate substrate (straw, wood chips, sawdust) with spawn
5. **Monitor** — test water/soil at intervals
6. **Harvest fruiting bodies** — mushrooms can concentrate toxins; collect and dispose properly

## Important Considerations

- **Strain variability** — not all strains of a species perform equally; lab testing recommended
- **Temperature and moisture** — mycelial activity is temperature-dependent; temperate species may need seasonal deployment
- **Toxin concentration** — some contaminants at very high concentrations kill mycelium; may need dilution or phytoremediation first
- **Disposal of fruiting bodies** — mushrooms that bioaccumulate heavy metals must be treated as hazardous waste
- **Scale** — mycelial mats must match the cubic size of the contamination source
- **Mineralization vs transformation** — fungi often transform pollutants into bound residues rather than fully mineralizing to CO2; monitoring must track metabolites

## Advantages Over Conventional Methods

- Low cost — uses agricultural waste (straw, wood chips) as substrate
- Self-propagating — mycelium grows and expands on its own
- Non-destructive — no excavation or chemical treatment needed
- Synergistic — kickstarts broader ecological recovery
- Can be deployed in-situ — no need to transport contaminated soil
- Extracellular degradation — does not require cellular uptake of pollutants
