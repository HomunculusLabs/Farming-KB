# Desert House Water and Energy Conservation

Desert house design in [[water-conservation]], and productive roof systems into a cohesive living system.

## The Human Cost of Poor Design

- Low-income families bear the greatest burden from inefficient desert housing
- Smoky indoor fires cause respiratory illness and reduced quality of life
- Cold nights lead to poor sleep and chronic tiredness, compounding poverty
- Energy costs for heating and cooling consume disproportionate household income
- Good design eliminates most of these costs through passive systems
- The financial strain of poor design perpetuates a cycle of poverty that
  proper [[permaculture-design]] addresses this primarily through solar systems:

### Solar Attic Systems

- Water tank with glass cover and aluminium foil reflector in the attic space
- Grid of plastic pipe laid in soot-covered sand under glass for heat absorption
- Pipe coil mounted on metal roofing for direct [[solar-water-heating]]
- These systems can be built from locally available materials at very low cost,
  making them accessible even in remote desert communities with limited resources

### Solar Ponds

- Dimensions of 4–5 meters per side, 1–2 meters deep
- A single pond can supply hot water and heating for 5–10 houses
- Salt-gradient or gel-stabilized designs store heat for extended periods

### Photovoltaic Cells

- Used for lighting and small electronics
- Sized to complement reduced overall energy demand from passive design

### Wastewater Recycling

- All household wastewater diverted to firewood plantations via trickle irrigation
- Nutrient-rich greywater supports biomass production for fuel and shade
- This closes the loop between water use, food production, and energy needs,
  turning what is normally treated as waste into a productive resource stream

## Water Conservation Strategies

Water is the limiting resource in desert environments. Conservation measures include:

- **Efficient shower heads** to reduce flow without compromising function
- **Greywater reuse** — shower and basin water directed to flush toilets or irrigate gardens
- **Dry pit toilets** function effectively for years without any water input, eliminating a major source of household water consumption entirely
- **Covered concrete water tanks** positioned on the shaded side of the house under trellis to keep drinking and cooking water cool through passive evaporation and shade
- Australian desert houses commonly maintain **5,000+ liters** of rainwater tank capacity, demonstrating that even low-rainfall areas can achieve meaningful water autonomy

## The Permaculture Approach: Arbor Living

The overarching philosophy of the desert house emphasizes **developing the arbor (shaded outdoor area) for daytime living** while keeping the house itself **compact for night occupancy only**.

This approach offers several key advantages:

- Dramatically reduces the volume that must be heated or cooled
- Concentrates energy use where it matters most — sleeping comfort
- Aligns human activity with the natural rhythm of desert temperatures
- Active under shade by day, sheltered and warm by night
- The arbor becomes the primary living space, blurring the boundary between
  house and garden and creating a resilient habitat that works with the desert
  rather than against it
