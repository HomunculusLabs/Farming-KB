# Windbreak Design and Establishment Methods

## Overview

Wind is one of the most powerful forces shaping landscapes, microclimates, and agricultural productivity. While we have least control over wind compared to other elements — it cannot be stored or generated on site — we can profoundly modify its behavior through strategic design. Windbreaks and shelterbelts represent some of the most impactful design interventions available to [[permaculture]] practitioners, capable of transforming unproductive exposed sites into productive food-growing systems.

## The Impact of Wind on Crops and Livestock

### Wind Speed and Damage Thresholds

| Beaufort Number | Speed (km/h) | Speed (mph) | Effect |
|----------------|-------------|------------|--------|
| 0-2 | 0-11 | 0-7 | Negligible — no damage to crops or structures |
| 3-4 | 12-19 | 8-12 | Damage to very susceptible species; useful wind energy |
| 5-6 | 20-38 | 13-24 | Mechanical damage to crops, some structural damage |
| 7-12 | 39-118+ | 25-74+ | Severe structural and crop damage; windmill damage |

Winds of 8 km/h are harmless to crops. At 24 km/h, production is reduced and animals lose weight. At 32-40 km/h, sheer mechanical damage to plants exceeds all other effects — plants can be uprooted, defoliated, or broken. Trees are severely wind-pruned through a combination of mechanical damage and, near coasts, salt burn and sandblast.

### Wind-Transported Hazards

Wind carries more than air. The most severe effects come from transported materials:
- **Salt spray**: On coasts, salt deposits burn and kill vegetation. Only species with fibrous, waxy, or fleshy leaves survive
- **Sand**: Desert and coastal sandblast buries fences, buildings, trees, and crops
- **Ice**: In cold climates, ice-blast damages and deforms vegetation
- **Sulphur and nitrogen particles**: Industrial pollution converts to acid rain, burning gardens and forests
- **Radioactive particles**: Downwind of nuclear facilities, tests, or accident sites
- **Spores, insects, and organisms**: Wind transports fungi, locust swarms, mosquitoes, fruit flies, and spiders across mountain and ocean barriers

### Salt Burn

On warm sea coasts, onshore winds carry salt that deposits on vegetation. This is often the limiting factor on species selection for coastal sites. Salt spray crystalizes on leaves and stems, burning tissue and killing many species. Even hardy plants that withstand years of normal gales can die in salty summer winds. The effect is so severe that on some coasts, salt crystalizes out in pinkish ponds mixed with volcanic earth — a visible testament to the loads carried by wind.

## Wind Patterns and Predictability

### Global Wind Belts

Wind patterns are broadly predictable based on latitude:

**Low latitudes (0-35 deg N/S):**
- Bi-modal, seasonal wind systems
- Southeast or northwest in the southern hemisphere
- Southwest or northeast in the northern hemisphere
- Trade winds dominate, with monsoon shifts creating two main seasons of winds and two short periods of relative calm

**Mid latitudes (35-50 deg):**
- Westerlies prevail in winter
- Easterlies occur sporadically as high and low pressure systems pass
- Cold winds blow from continental interiors in winter
- Chilling winter winds come from the seas

**High latitudes (>50 deg):**
- Very long summer days, short or no winter daylight
- Dramatic seasonal wind shifts

### Wind Harmonics

Ground winds do not blow steadily but vary as gusts and calms in predictable, locality-specific patterns. Key characteristics identified by wind researcher [[frank-bethwaite]]:

- **Gust frequency**: 49-60 minute cycles are typical at mid-latitudes
- **Gust intensity**: Gusts are typically 40% stronger than lulls
- **Direction change in lulls**: Wind shifts about 15 deg from the main direction during calm periods
- **Wave patterns**: Visible in grasslands and on water surfaces, with "fronts" arriving every ~14 seconds

Regional variations in gust periodicity:
- Bahrain: 5.25 minutes
- Sydney: 6-12 minutes
- Toronto: 10 minutes

### Prevalent vs. Energy Winds

In the westerly wind belts, two distinct wind categories can be identified:
- **Prevalent winds** (8-24 km/h): Blow five out of seven windy days
- **Energy winds** (16-40 km/h): Blow on the remaining two days, arriving from 15-20 deg off the direction of prevalent winds

## Windbreak Design Principles

### Species Selection

Wind-tolerant "frontline" trees share common adaptive features that resist desiccation and mechanical damage:

- **Fibrous stems**: Palms flex without breaking
- **Fleshy leaves**: Aloes, agaves, and *Euphorbias* store water and resist desiccation
- **Hard, needle-like leaves or stems**: Pines, tamarisks, *Casuarinas*, some *Acacias*
- **Hairy (tomentose) or waxy leaf surfaces**: *Coprosma*, eucalypts, some pines and acacias

Many fire-resistant plants are also wind-resistant, as both require strategies against desiccation. Some drought-resistant but fire-prone species (pines) also withstand wind.

### Penetrability

The most effective windbreaks are **40-50% penetrable**. Solid barriers create turbulence on the lee side that can cause more damage than no barrier at all. A semi-permeable windbreak reduces wind velocity gradually across a broad area, providing maximum protection.

### Wind Reduction in Forests

Wind velocity decreases progressively as it penetrates into forest. The reduction follows a predictable pattern where velocity diminishes with each meter of penetration through the canopy.

### Layout Patterns

**Predictable wind directions:**
- Plan directional, patterned windbreaks of earth banks and trees aligned to intercept prevailing winds
- Orient gardens and animal shelters to face the sun while being protected from wind

**Unpredictable or multi-directional winds:**
- In deserts where severe winds and sandblast may come from any direction, impose a close rectangular or network pattern of windbreaks

## Establishment Methods

### Initial Protection

Young trees cannot survive direct exposure to severe winds. Establishment protection includes:

- **Individual plastic bags**: Open-ended plastic bags around stakes are a common and effective method
- **Earth mounds**: Side-cast earth banks longer than the tree line provide immediate shelter
- **Brush fences**: Wire-mesh fences, or staked fences with 40% wind penetrability
- **Existing tussock grass**: Tough unmown grass to windward should be preserved if already present
- **Stone anchoring**: Stones or mulch around tree bases reduce root disturbance and desiccation

All of these can be combined in very hostile areas.

### Establishment Sequence

On coasts and in deserts, the windward rows of trees will be heavily wind-flagged (shaped by persistent wind). Taller species should be placed in their lee. It is not until after the **fourth or even fifth tree row** evolves that wind-prone fruit or nut-bearing trees will yield. Windbreak establishment is therefore the **first priority** for gardens in exposed coastal or desert situations.

### Alternative Windbreak Structures

**Trellis windbreaks:**
- More immediate than tree establishment
- Should be sinuous (brick or mud brick) or zig-zagged (timber) to withstand persistent forces
- Earth mounds can be better streamlined and less sensitive to windthrow

**Tyre walls:**
- Create warmth inside the tyres (dark rubber absorbs heat)
- Cheap and removable once tree shelter is established
- Most aesthetic when regularly arranged and planted

**Mesh fences with fedges:**
- Stoutly built with heavy top rail
- Basis for fence-hedges (fedges) of thick-leaved vines
- On coasts, may be completely mounded over with tough semi-succulents: *[[rhagodia]]*, *Tetragonia*, *Carpobrotus*, *Mesembryanthemum*

**Rock walls:**
- Can be mounded with scramblers or cacti
- Some species provide bee forage, berries, or edible fruits
- The hollow from which earth is taken for mounds can hold water or protect young plants

### Coastal Growth Rates

On dry salt-wind coasts, tree canopies rarely gain more than 46 cm height per 1 meter of width (18 inches per 3 feet). Considerable width must be given to pioneer windbreaks in these situations. Hardy pioneers like Norfolk Island pines can be nursed to grow to windward, but even these start from ground level. A fence, building, earth bank, or barrier gives them a critical head start with far less spread required.

### Practical Minimum Shelter

Even a 46-62 cm (18-24 inch) high fence or earth mound will grow sweet potato, strawberry, or cabbage in its lee. Hard-pruned canopies need not be barren — many dwarf fruit, vine, and flower crops will grow below windbreak trees if mulch and water are provided. In windbreak forests near coasts, small openings of 6-9 m (20-30 feet) provide garden shelter and admit light.

## Shelterbelt Benefits

### Energy Savings for Buildings

For glazed areas and hot water collectors, wind chill factors alone can remove 60% of collected heat. A shelterbelt (including thick vine trellis) around a house can achieve a **20-30% saving in heating fuels** in moderate to severe winters. In cold areas, earth banks plus shelterbelt plus sun-facing aspect form a critical integrated design strategy.

In deserts, where advected (wind-carried) heat is the most severe effect on human comfort, shelterbelt trees can **reduce ground temperatures by up to 15 deg C**.

### Livestock Protection

Blizzards can kill livestock and newborn lambs. Even hardy, adapted animals can lose 30% of body weight in 3 days of blizzard conditions. Critical design considerations:
- Shelterbelts in fields provide essential refuge
- **Fences must not form downwind or downslope traps** — herds escaping blizzards will pile up against barriers in fenced corners and smother
- All moorland and high plateau fences should allow easy downwind escape to woodlots, sheltered valleys, or lower elevations

## The Traditional Hawaiian Example

Across the flat peninsula of Kalaupapa on Moloka'i, Hawaiians built tiny stone fences only 25-50 cm (10-20 inches) high and 4.5-5 m (15-18 feet) apart. Behind these minimal barriers, they grew sweet potato crops with tough fern mulch. On the seaward coast, wild date palms still struggle to 4.5 m (15 feet) in the teeth of the trade winds — demonstrating that even minimal windbreak infrastructure can enable food production in extremely exposed sites.

## Key Principles

1. **Windbreaks are the first priority** on exposed sites — nothing else matters until wind is controlled
2. **40-50% penetrability** is optimal — solid barriers create damaging turbulence on the lee side
3. **Four to five tree rows** may be needed before fruit and nut trees will yield in exposed coastal or desert sites
4. **Combine multiple protection methods** — fences, mounds, bags, grass, and stone in very hostile areas
5. **Species with fibrous, fleshy, needle-like, or waxy leaves** are most wind-resistant
6. **Wind harmonics are predictable** — observe local patterns before designing permanent shelter systems
7. **Shelterbelts save 20-30% of heating fuel** and can reduce ground temperatures by 15 deg C in deserts
8. **Fences must allow livestock escape** — never create downwind traps that cause animals to pile up and smother in blizzards
9. **Even minimal barriers (46 cm)** enable productive growing — start with what you have and improve over time
10. **Salt, sand, ice, and pollution** carried by wind are often more damaging than the wind itself — species selection must address these specific hazards
