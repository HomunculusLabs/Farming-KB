Without immediate and truly useful yields, whatever we design and develop will tend to

attributed to nature, market forces or human greed, systems that most effectively
obtain a yield and use it most effectively to meet survival needs tend to prevail. A
yield functions as a reward that encourages, maintains and/or replicates the system that
generated it — a positive feedback loop that amplifies the original process.

## The Maximum Power Law

Holmgren draws on the thermodynamic Maximum Power Law to frame this principle. A heat
engine running free with no load consumes energy but produces no useful work; loaded
towards stalling, efficiency approaches 100% but power output falls to zero. Maximum
power is achieved at a balance — roughly half the energy converted to work, half
dissipated as waste. All biological and self-organising systems show this same pattern.

The modern consumer economy encourages consumption for its own sake — analogous to the
engine running with no load. The conservation ethic focuses on maximum efficiency —
analogous to the engine loaded to near-stalling. [[fukuoka-textdoc-direct-seeding-rice-barley-succession-evolution]] vigour and fitness. Direct seeding encourages the
fastest-growing individuals to prevail. Selective thinning acts as predation, improving
stock. In human systems, comfort and excessive protection can lead to self-satisfied,
lazy and dysfunctional behaviour.

## Hardy and Self-Reliant Species

Hardy, locally adapted and self-reproducing plants minimise resources required to
maintain gardens, farms and forests. Their minimal demands, relative efficiency, or
symbiotic microbes give them competitive advantage on poor soils. The obsession with
control limits development of systems less dependent on non-renewable inputs. By using
only species that don't grow or reproduce freely, we ignore some of the most useful
species.

The same applies to animal husbandry: intensive management has produced cattle that
cannot give birth unassisted, turkeys bred so large they cannot naturally reproduce.
During energy descent, species requiring large external input will be displaced by
those adapted to prevailing conditions.

## Increasing Fertility

While adapting to prevailing conditions is a classic strategy, increasing and
maintaining fertility to grow more useful and higher-yielding species is equally
important. Vegetable yields can vary two orders of magnitude depending on fertility.

The two strategies are in dynamic tension but the conflicts are less than they seem.
The area needing high fertility for substantial food production is small, so
concentrating soil-improving activities in garden [[holmgren-food-systems-and-local-self-reliance]] ories at
the cost of self-reliance and flexibility. "Loose systems last longer and work better"
— flexibility can be more important than efficiency. Specialisation comes at a cost
to flexibility; generalisation at a cost to efficiency. Many permaculture strategies
are generalist, allowing high flexibility with less emphasis on efficiency.

## Practical Considerations

Successful implementation of Permaculture Obtain a Yield Principle requires attention to
several practical factors including environmental conditions,
resource availability, and timing. Careful monitoring and
adaptive management help optimize outcomes across varying
conditions. Integration with other system elements enhances
overall effectiveness and creates beneficial synergies that
improve resilience and productivity over time.
