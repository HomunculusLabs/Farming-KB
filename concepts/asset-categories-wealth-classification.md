# Asset Categories and Wealth Classification

Asset categories and wealth classification form a foundational framework in [[permaculture]] economics for understanding how different types of capital affect the long-term health of a community and its environment. Drawing on the work of Turnbull (1975) and expanded by [[bill-mollison]], this system classifies assets according to their capacity to create, maintain, or destroy wealth over time. The framework provides a lens through which communities can evaluate expenditures, design financial institutions, and make decisions that either build or erode their resource base.

## Money as a Transport Agent

In [[pdc-right-livelihood-and-permaculture-economics]], money is understood as a **transport agent** analogous to water in a landscape. The critical metric is not the total volume of money present in a system, but rather the **number of times it cycles through local hands** before leaving the community. A small amount of money that circulates many times within a local economy creates far more wealth than a large sum that passes through once and departs.

This hydraulic metaphor has direct implications for community financial design:

- **Leakage** — the outflow of capital from a community to distant centers — must be actively prevented.
- **Recycling** of money within the local economy must be made the operating rule, not the exception.
- Just as water retention is a primary goal in permaculture [[mollison-country-permaculture-rural-land-design]], **capital retention** is a primary goal in permaculture economic design.

Time and money are, in this framework, interchangeable resources. Controlling the flow of cash through a community is tantamount to controlling the lives and livelihoods of its members. This makes financial literacy and community-governed financial institutions matters of fundamental importance.

## The Five Asset Categories

### Category 1: Degenerative Assets

Degenerative assets are those that inevitably decay, wear out, or depreciate over time. They represent a net draw on a region's resources and, when accumulated in excess, impoverish the community in the long term.

Examples include:

- Buildings and infrastructure (roads, bridges)
- Motor vehicles
- Furnishings and household appliances
- Consumer goods and electronics

Degenerative assets are not without value — they provide essential services and quality of life — but they are **terminal assets** that require constant replacement. A society that invests the majority of its capital in degenerative assets is on a path of resource depletion, because every building, road, and appliance must eventually be replaced with further expenditure of energy and materials.

### Category 2: Generative Assets

Generative assets are tools and equipment that can **manufacture, process, or transform raw materials** into useful products. Their defining characteristic is the capacity to maintain, repair, and even reproduce each other, creating a self-reinforcing productive capacity.

Examples include:

- Huskers, grinders, and blenders for food processing
- Lathes, milling machines, and metalworking tools
- Furnaces, kilns, and forges
- Sewing machines, looms, and woodworking equipment

A community well-supplied with generative assets can produce much of what it needs locally, reducing dependence on imported goods and external supply chains. These assets form the productive backbone of a resilient local economy.

### Category 3: Procreative Assets

Procreative assets are **living systems** that grow, reproduce, and yield ongoing returns without being consumed in the process. These are the highest-priority investments in a permaculture economy, as they represent self-renewing wealth that supports both generative tools and a modest complement of degenerative assets.

Examples include:

- Trees and [[perennial-vegetables-and-edible-perennial-crops]]
- Wildlife, fish, and invertebrate populations
- Mammals and domestic livestock
- Pastures, wetlands, and healthy soil ecologies

The instruction from Mollison is unambiguous: **maximise procreative assets**. A region rich in living systems can feed its people, supply raw materials for generative tools, and sustain itself indefinitely. These are the assets that actually *create* wealth rather than merely consuming or transforming it.

### Category 4: Informational Assets

Informational assets encompass the knowledge, data, and intellectual resources that enable a community to manage its physical assets wisely and adapt to changing conditions.

Examples include:

- Education and skills training
- Applied intelligence and traditional ecological knowledge
- Seeds, genetic stock, and plant varieties
- Books, manuals, databases, and design records
- [[maps]], climate data, and site assessments

Information is non-depletable when shared — it grows in value as it distributes through a community. Investment in informational assets multiplies the effectiveness of every other category and is essential for long-term adaptation and resilience.

### Category 5: Conservative Assets

Conservative assets are investments that **protect, preserve, and stabilize** existing resources against degradation, loss, or waste. They function as a form of systemic insurance, preventing the erosion of the wealth generated by other categories.

Examples include:

- Insulation and [[desert-house-water-energy-conservation]] measures
- Dams, swales, and water harvesting infrastructure
- Money recycling systems and [[local-currencies]]
- Good storage facilities for harvests and materials
- Strategic forests planted to arrest erosion and [[desertification]]
- Easements and conservation covenants on land

Conservative assets ensure that the gains made through procreative and generative investment are not lost to entropy, erosion, or economic leakage.

## The Wealth Equation

The relationship between these categories defines the trajectory of a community's wealth:

- Expenditure directed toward **categories 3, 4, and 5** (procreative, informational, conservative) **conserves and creates** wealth. These investments compound over time, yielding increasing returns in biological productivity, knowledge, and systemic stability.
- Societies that spend predominantly on **categories 1 and 2** (degenerative and generative) will inevitably pollute and then **extinguish their resource base**. The productive capacity of generative tools is wasted if the living systems that supply raw materials are depleted.
- The optimal strategy is to **prioritise procreative assets** (which create wealth), support them with **informational and conservative assets** (which protect and enhance wealth), maintain sufficient **generative assets** to process local production, and hold **degenerative assets** at a modest, sustainable level.

## Regional Wealth and Export Trade

Mollison distinguishes between the **regional wealth basis** and **export trade items**. The regional wealth basis is the foundational productive capacity of a bioregion — primarily its soil, water, and living systems. This is not a commodity for export but the substrate upon which all local prosperity depends.

Export trade items, by contrast, are genuinely surplus production — surplus water from well-managed catchments, surplus manufactured goods from generative tools, surplus food from productive landscapes. The key principle is that **surplus must never be based on the loss of irreplaceable resources**. Exporting timber harvested by clear-felling old-growth forest, or selling grain produced by mining topsoil, is not trade — it is the liquidation of capital disguised as income.

Financial institutions in a permaculture economy have a clear mandate: **prevent the leakage of essential resources** and **expedite the export of genuine local surplus**. This dual function protects the wealth base while enabling the community to benefit from productive exchange with other regions.

## Ethical Financial Institutions

Mollison identifies **two foundation stones** upon which all financial institutions in a permaculture economy must be built:

1. A **published ethical charter** that commits the institution to community benefit, ecological stewardship, and transparent governance.
2. A **restriction to appropriate resource development** — the institution must direct capital toward investments that build long-term regional wealth rather than extract it.

### Types of Financial Institutions

A resilient permaculture economy draws on a diversity of financial structures, each suited to different functions:

- **Credit unions and credit cooperatives** — member-owned, democratically governed savings and lending institutions that keep capital circulating locally.
- **Trusts and foundations** — entities that hold assets in perpetuity for specified community or ecological purposes, insulated from short-term market pressures.
- **Savings and loan banks** — institutions that channel local savings into local productive investment, particularly in housing and infrastructure.
- **Insurance agencies** — mutual or cooperative insurers that spread risk across a community and incentivise conservative asset investment.
- **Finance companies** — providers of capital for productive enterprise, ideally governed by ethical charter.
- **Commercial banks** — when ethically chartered, providers of transaction services and business credit within the community.
- **Investment brokers** — intermediaries that direct capital toward appropriate resource development, not speculative extraction.
- **Limited liability companies and trading companies** — business structures that enable productive enterprise while limiting individual risk.
- **Cooperatives** — member-owned enterprises for production, distribution, or consumption that distribute surplus equitably among members.

### Principles of Ethical Banking

The guiding principle of ethical banking in permaculture is that **financial institutions must belong to and be governed by the communities they serve**. They must not be open to distant or centralised control that diverts local capital to external priorities. This is both an economic imperative — to prevent leakage — and a democratic one, to ensure that financial power serves the people who are affected by its decisions.

## Democracy, Peace, and Lifetime

The economic framework of asset categories and ethical finance is grounded in a broader philosophy that Mollison describes as one of **true democracy, peace, and "lifetime."** True democracy means that communities have genuine control over the resources and institutions that shape their lives. Peace follows from equitable resource distribution and the elimination of extractive economic relationships. And "lifetime" refers to the long-view perspective — designing economies and institutions that function across generations, not fiscal quarters.

Personal independence, in this framework, **relies on cooperative human society**. The myth of the self-sufficient individual is replaced by the reality of interdependent communities where each person's wellbeing is supported by shared infrastructure, mutual financial institutions, and collectively managed living systems. The five asset categories provide the analytical tool for ensuring that community resources are directed toward genuine, lasting wealth rather than the illusion of prosperity built on resource depletion.

| Category | Type | Examples | Wealth Effect |
|----------|------|----------|---------------|
| 1 | Degenerative | Cars, buildings, appliances | Destructs wealth over time |
| 2 | Generative | Lathes, grinders, furnaces | Transforms but doesn't create wealth |

| 4 | Informational | Education, seeds, data | Multiplies effectiveness of all categories |

The central lesson is clear: **wealth is not money — it is the capacity of a landscape and its people to sustain themselves over time.** Financial systems, asset allocation, and economic policy must all be designed to maximise procreative, informational, and conservative assets while holding degenerative expenditure to the minimum necessary for a good quality of life.
