# Mycofiltration and Mycofiltration Systems Design

## Overview

Mycofiltration is the use of fungal mycelium to filter and remove contaminants from water, typically through biological filtration beds or mats containing fungal biomass. The concept extends the natural water-filtering role of forest fungal networks to engineered systems. Mycofiltration exploits the biosorption capacity of fungal cell walls, enzymatic degradation of dissolved organic contaminants, and physical filtration by dense mycelial mats. It is closely related to [[mycoremediation-biosorption-mechanisms]] and [[mycoremediation-white-rot-fungi-lignin-degradation-enzymes]] processes.

## Principles of Mycofiltration

Mycofiltration systems rely on three primary contaminant removal mechanisms:

1. **Physical filtration**: Dense mycelial mats physically trap particulate matter, sediments, and microorganisms as water passes through. Fungal hyphae form extensive networks that create a biological filter matrix.

2. **Biosorption**: Metal ions and organic compounds bind to functional groups on fungal cell walls, including carboxyl, phosphate, hydroxyl, amino, and sulfydryl moieties. This passive process does not require metabolic energy and works with both living and dead biomass.

3. **Enzymatic degradation**: White-rot fungi in filtration systems produce extracellular [[mycoremediation-white-rot-fungi-lignin-degradation-enzymes]] (laccase, MnP, LiP) that can degrade dissolved organic contaminants including [[mycoremediation-textile-dye-degradation]], phenols, and some [[mycoremediation-pesticide-herbicide-contamination]].

## Fungal Species for Filtration

Several fungal groups are suitable for mycofiltration applications:

- **White-rot basidiomycetes**: Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus ostreatus, and Phanerochaete chrysosporium produce both dense mycelial mats and degradative enzymes. T. versicolor is particularly effective for dye and phenol removal from water.
- **Zygomycetes**: Rhizopus arrhizus and Rhizopus nigricans have high biosorption capacities for heavy metals due to chitin-chitosan cell wall composition. R. arrhizus has been used in continuous-flow stirred-tank reactors for Cu(II) and Ni(II) removal.
- **Yeasts**: Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Candida spp. can be immobilized in filtration systems for metal removal. Crossflow filtration using S. cerevisiae as carriers removes heavy metal ions from water, with binding affinity Pb(II) > Cu(II) > Ni(II).
- **Aspergillus species**: A. niger is widely used for metal biosorption with high uptake capacities for lead, copper, cadmium, and nickel.

## System Design Considerations

### Reactor Configurations
Multiple reactor types are applicable to mycofiltration:

- **Packed-bed columns**: The most common configuration. Fungal biomass is packed into columns through which contaminated water flows. Pycnosporus sanguineus has been used in fixed-bed columns for heavy metal adsorption. Column design must account for pressure drop, channeling, and biomass compression.
- **Fluidized-bed reactors**: Biomass particles are suspended in upward-flowing water, providing better mass transfer and contact efficiency than packed beds.
- **Membrane bioreactors**: Fungal cells or enzymes immobilized on membrane surfaces. Laccase immobilized on modified PVDF microfiltration membranes enables continuous enzyme treatment of water.
- **Rotating biological contactors**: Fungal biomass attached to rotating discs provides alternating contact with contaminated water and air.
- **Constructed wetlands**: Pilot-scale constructed wetlands have been used to treat PAH-contaminated water, with 40 fungal species identified from contaminated wetland ecosystems.

### Biomass Preparation
Biomass pretreatment significantly affects filtration performance. Alkali treatment (NaOH) increases the number of active binding sites on cell walls. Formaldehyde treatment cross-links amino groups, improving structural stability. Drying and granulation produce uniform particles suitable for column packing. The choice between living and dead biomass depends on the application: dead biomass avoids toxicity issues and nutrient requirements, while living biomass provides ongoing enzymatic degradation.

### Operating Parameters
Key operating parameters include flow rate (affecting contact time), pH (affecting metal speciation and enzyme activity), temperature, and influent contaminant concentration. The pH must be optimized for both biosorption (typically pH 4-6 for metals) and enzyme activity (pH 3-5 for laccase, pH 4.5-5 for MnP). Temperature control adds cost but can improve performance within optimal ranges (25-35 degrees C for most species).

## Integration with Other Treatment Steps

Mycofiltration is most effective as part of multi-stage treatment systems:

- **Pretreatment**: Physical screening or sedimentation removes large particulates before fungal filtration.
- **Biological treatment**: Mycofiltration can serve as the primary or secondary biological treatment step. A two-step process with Candida utilis (biomass production) followed by Aspergillus niger (COD reduction) achieved 87% total COD reduction from cane stillage.
- **Polishing**: Fungal filtration can serve as a final polishing step after conventional biological treatment for removal of recalcitrant compounds.
- **Enzyme treatment**: Crude or purified fungal enzymes can be applied directly as a treatment step. Agaricus bisporus compost extract (crude laccase) achieves 100% phenol removal.

## Desorption and Regeneration

Regeneration of fungal biomass is critical for practical mycofiltration operation. Desorption recovers biosorbed metals and restores biomass capacity for repeated use cycles. Common metal eluants include salts of sodium, potassium, and calcium (counterion exchange), mineral acids such as HCl and H2SO4 (proton exchange), and complexing agents like EDTA. More than 90% of biosorbed metal can be eluted, equivalent to a 25-fold concentration increase, with copper recovered by electrolysis using a rotating cathode cell. Boiling of Saccharomyces uvarum biomass enhanced lead adsorption. Treatment with glucose increases energy supply to living yeast cells for greater metal accumulation. The Thomas model has proven suitable for column kinetics using immobilized Mucor rouxii in polysulfone matrix.

## Effluent Composition Effects

Real effluents contain multiple metal species, creating competition for limited binding sites on fungal biomass. In column experiments, metal affinity and concentration determine breakthrough order. Metals in trace amounts may reach breakthrough before more concentrated species. Biomass-polysulfone beads for immobilizing Aspergillus niger display good handling and settling qualities with negligible biomass loss. PVA matrix for inactivated Saccharomyces cerevisiae shows low mass-transfer resistance for copper biosorption. An integrated Cr(VI) remediation system using Fujino media leaching, immobilized Ganoderma lucidum bioreactor, and adsorption column achieved more than 80% Cr(VI) reduction at 50 mg/l within 4 hours, with over 95% Cr(III) removal as a polishing step.

## Specific Applications and Performance Data

Fungal biosorbents have been characterized by five key criteria: biosorption efficiency and uptake capacity, metal ion specificity, flocculation and sedimentation efficiency, desorption efficiency, and preservation of biosorptive capacity. Filamentous fungi of the Order Mucorales are particularly good biosorbents of uranium (up to 343 mg/g by Mucor javanicus). Metal enrichment selectivity by Mucor hiemalis follows decreasing order: Cr(III) > Cu(II) > Pb(II) > Ag(I) > Al(III) > Co(II) > Zn(II) > Ni(II) > Fe(II) > Mo(V) > Cd(II). Aspergillus flavus achieved 40 mg/g U(VI) uptake at pH 4.5. Dead Rhizopus nigricans biomass proved a potent biosorbent of Cr(VI). These performance metrics guide species selection for specific mycofiltration applications.

## Biomass Immobilization Methods

Multiple immobilization matrices have been developed for mycofiltration biomass. Biomass-polysulfone beads for Aspergillus niger display good handling and settling qualities with negligible biomass loss. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) matrix for inactivated Saccharomyces cerevisiae shows low mass-transfer resistance for copper biosorption. Immobilization in PVA Na-alginate and alkali-treated polyethylenimine:glutaraldehyde (PEI:GA) achieves highest Cu(II)-accumulating capacity (80%). Calcium-alginate beads with Fomitopsis carnea showed 80% gold removal, compared to PVA beads. Dead fungal biomass immobilized in polyacrylonitrile-B/75% removed metals from simulated wastewaters. The choice of matrix affects mass transfer, mechanical stability, and regeneration potential.

## Limitations and Research Needs

Mycofiltration systems face several challenges: biomass clogging in packed beds, limited long-term stability of living systems, variable performance with changing water chemistry, and lack of full-scale commercial installations. The main research needs include development of robust immobilization methods, scaling studies from bench to field, cost-benefit analyses compared to conventional treatment, and integration with existing wastewater infrastructure. The nonliving biomass approach avoids many operational challenges but cannot provide ongoing enzymatic degradation of organic contaminants. Metal affinity criteria for breakthrough in multimetal solutions remain poorly understood. Desorption and regeneration protocols need standardization for reliable long-term operation.
