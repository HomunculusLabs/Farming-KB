## Integrated Land Management and Village Design

Integrated land management treats the landscape as a whole system, from hilltop to shoreline, connecting forests, agriculture, water systems, settlements, and marine environments into a coherent and self-sustaining pattern. The traditional Maori marae and Hawaiian ohana serve as models of geomorphic and sociological units in which land and people were integrated for sustenance, having evolved from early errors of over-clearing, excessive burning, and species extinction before reaching equilibrium.

## The Hill-to-Shore Profile

The stable tropical landscape, often following a volcanic cone profile, requires a specific sequence of land use zones from summit to sea:

### Protected Skyline and Hill Forests

The uppermost slopes must remain under permanent forest cover. These forests protect soils and waters, mine and release plant nutrients from steep slopes, and can serve as limited forage and mulch resources — but only with iron-clad protection. Clearing these forests brings compound catastrophes: landslide, loss of nutrient in water and crop, [[humus]] soils accumulate on valley floors. Greywater and processed manures from settlement and livestock add to fertility, supporting extensive aquacultures or rich forest and orchard crops.

### Shorelines

Shorelines should be preserved for approximately 100 meters inland for essential windbreak, shore stability, and cloud generation over land. Selected quiet harbors accept fishing settlements. Shores with 15-20 year tsunami history need sacrificial palm and Casuarina barriers to reduce storm damage. Light temporary shore housing for coastal work should be supplemented by more substantial homes safely inland or behind earth embankments.

### Lagoon and Estuary Systems

Ancient sea-walls of semi-permeable coral blocks with entries, flood-tide gates, and inner pens create excellent warm-water lagoon systems. Restricted mangrove plantings stabilize banks, provide sea mulch, and catch silt. These lagoons are rich in algae, support cultivated seaweed, and impound mullet, mangrove fish, oysters, eels, shellfish, and prawns. Mobile phosphates are fixed in plants and mud within days, becoming available for growth. Such managed estuaries are invaluable as mariculture systems.

### Marine Constructs

Artificial reef systems of tires on sand, coral blocks, and boxes of palm trunks greatly enlarge habitat for sedentary fish and lobsters, consolidated by corals within a year. Weak electrolysis on metal mesh creates artificial but permanent coralline deposits more rapidly. In sandy shallows, log barriers filled with sand and stabilized with mangroves provide shore barriers, lagoon walls, and productive "edge" in the sea.

## Elements of a Village Complex

Based on traditional and modern villages, a complete humid tropics village complex includes:

- A well-arranged array of housing, often grouped around a compound with processing and storage areas, threshing floors, meeting houses, cooperative stores, bulk fuel depots, and firewood and mulch depots
- Integrated clean water systems (wells, piped water, tanks, or dams)
- A plant nursery serving gardens and forest plantings
- At each house, 0.25-1.0 hectare of home garden and orchard based on self-reliance
- Border ranges for domestic species: chickens, guinea pigs, ducks, rabbits, small pigs, and pigeons — serving as manure resources for the home garden
- Fish ponds (in areas of plentiful water) with some animals housed above them
- Fuelwood plantation sectors integrated with windbreaks
- Stock yards and sheds for cattle and pigs at commercial scale
- Careful zonation of tree polyculture
- Special facilities: log-trimming areas, boat-building areas, fish net drying racks, wharves, freezers, solar pond installations, power houses, community drying sheds, craft work areas, and vehicle or draught-animal parks

## The Ohana System

The Hawaiian ohana system illustrates integrated land management on a volcanic island"s radial valley. Key elements include: "taboo" forests on upper slopes (Zone A), stream diversion to ridges and high terraces serving the main village (Zone B), sacrificial forests of palm to modify hurricanes (Zone C), feet fish ponds that retain leached nutrients and grow mullet and shellfish (Zone D), and reef systems modified with coral blocks to shelter crayfish and fish (Zone E). Each zone supports and is supported by the others, creating a resilient integrated system.

## Key Principles

- **Design from summit to sea**: Every landscape zone has specific appropriate uses that connect to adjacent zones
- **Protect the high forests first**: Upper slope forests are non-negotiable; their loss cascades into every downstream system
- **Settlement at the keypoint**: Villages belong at the ecological transition zone where water, shelter, and access converge
- **Integrate water, food, and waste**: Village greywater feeds crops; livestock manure enriches gardens; food waste becomes compost
- **Shorelines need buffers**: 100 m inland preserves windbreak, stability, and cloud generation
- **Mariculture extends the farm**: Lagoons, estuaries, and artificial reefs are agricultural extensions into the sea
- **Build complete village systems**: Housing, water, nursery, livestock, fuelwood, and processing must all be planned together
