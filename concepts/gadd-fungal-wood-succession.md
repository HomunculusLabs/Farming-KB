# Fungal Succession in Decomposing Wood

**Source:** Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, *Fungi in the Environment* (2006), Chapters 6-7 (Boddy & Jones; Taylor & Fransson)

## Overview

[[wood-decomposition-by-fungi]] is a major pathway in forest carbon and nutrient cycling. The process involves predictable patterns of species succession driven by substrate quality changes, combative interactions between fungal species, and the functional differences between decay types (white rot, brown rot, and soft rot). Stable isotope analysis (d13C and d15N) provides powerful tools for investigating [[fungal-ecology]] during wood decomposition.

## Decay Types

### White Rot (Basidiomycota)
- Utilizes all cell-wall components (cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin)
- Produces lignin and manganese peroxidases
- Some species use laccase as sole lignin-oxidizing enzyme when redox-mediator molecules are present
- Examples: [[staycare-trametes-versicolor-bioremediation-applications]], Pycnoporus cinnabarinus
- d13C values of white-rot fungi differ from brown-rot fungi

### Brown Rot (Basidiomycota)
- Utilizes carbohydrates but degrades lignin only to a limited extent
- Higher %C in wood residues compared to white rot (43.5 +/- 0.9% vs 41.6 +/- 0.3%)
- Despite different substrate utilization strategies, d15N and d13C values show substantial overlap between brown- and white-rot fungi
- Growing isolates on the same substrate eliminates most differences

### Soft Rot (Ascomycota)
- Type 1: cavity formation in wood cell walls
- Type 2: very similar to white rot of basidiomycetes
- Ascomycetes use laccase for lignocellulolysis, sometimes with redox-mediator molecules

## [[fungal-succession-patterns]]

### Traditional Hypothesis
The classical model (Frankland, 1998) posits that each successive colonizer utilizes substrates that the preceding species either could not use or could only partly decompose. Later colonizers specialize on the partially degraded products of earlier species.

### Mycelial Feeding Hypothesis
More recent evidence suggests at least some late colonizers are specialized in using the mycelium of earlier colonizers (Holmer et al., 1997). Stable isotope analysis (d15N and d13C) can distinguish between fungi utilizing wood components versus those feeding on earlier colonizer mycelium.

### Combative Interactions
Outcomes of mycelial combat between wood-decay basidiomycetes include:
- **Deadlock:** Neither fungus gains territory
- **Replacement:** One fungus completely replaces the other
- **Partial replacement:** Some territory gained
- **Mutual replacement:** Both fungi gain some of the other's territory

Studies on Stropharia caerulea interacting with other cord-forming basidiomycetes (Agrocybe gibberosa, Hypholoma fasciculare, Phanerochaete velutina, Phallus impudicus) showed species-specific outcomes with localized changes in mycelial morphology reflecting defensive responses.

## Stable Isotope Ecology of Wood-Decomposing Fungi

### Nitrogen Isotopes (d15N)
- Wood-decomposing fungi show little 15N enrichment between substrate and fungus (e = 0.4 +/- 1.2 per mil)
- This contrasts with fungi growing on more N-rich substrates that show greater enrichment
- Low enrichment in wood decomposers reflects the very low N concentrations in woody substrates, creating a closed reaction system where all available N is transformed

### Carbon Isotopes (d13C)
- Wood decomposers show enrichment of 3.5 +/- 0.9 per mil between d13Csubstrate and d13Cfungus
- This enrichment appears consistent regardless of wood mass loss (up to 70%)
- Trametes versicolor showed constant wood d13C (-26.3 +/- 0.3 per mil) over 181 days despite 70% mass loss
- Enrichment likely relates to differences in cellular components (e.g., chitin is enriched in 13C relative to wood)

### Taxon-Specific Fractionation
- Different fungal species fractionate isotopes differently during uptake and metabolic processing
- A discriminant analysis of d13C in four amino acids (Thr, Ile, Phe, Val) could divide fungal species into their respective groups
- This has implications for investigating selective feeding by soil fungivores

## Functional Groups in Wood Decomposition

Field studies in central Swedish forests (Uppsala) identified approximately 74 saprotrophic species, 45 of which were lignicolous. Key findings:
- Total d15N range in saprotrophic and ECM sporocarps: -4.60 to +17.52 per mil
- Total d13C range: -29.20 to -19.79 per mil
- [[saprotrophic-fungi]] significantly different from ECM fungi: d15N (1.86 vs 5.83 per mil), d13C (-23.2 vs -25.6 per mil)

### Functional Categories
- Wood decomposers (lignicolous species)
- Litter decomposers (terricolous species)
- Ectomycorrhizal fungi

Fungi could be further classified by their preferred decay stage: early colonizers of fresh wood versus species utilizing more decomposed material.

## Insect-Fungus Interactions in Wood Decay

Many wood-boring beetles inoculate wood with spores of wood-decay fungi during egg laying. The fungal mycelium rapidly degrades wood, and insects may benefit by:
1. Feeding on more palatable, decayed woody tissue
2. Consuming fungal mycelium directly

Stable isotope analysis (d15N and d13C in larvae, wood, and mycelium) can help determine the relative importance of each pathway.

## Invertebrate Grazing Effects

- Mycelia are highly nutritious; Collembola, termites, nematodes, and Diptera graze on fungal mycelia
- Different fungi are preferentially grazed in different regions (hyphal tips, cords, fine mycelium)
- Grazing can alter mycelial morphology and foraging patterns
- Intense grazing can completely destroy mycelia
- Effects vary with grazing intensity, collembolan species, wood decay state, and nutrient status of inocula
