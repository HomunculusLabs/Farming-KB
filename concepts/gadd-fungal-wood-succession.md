# Fungal Succession in Decomposing Wood

**Source:** Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, *Fungi in the Environment* (2006), Chapters 6-7 (Boddy & Jones; Taylor & Fransson)

## Overview

interactions between fungal species, and the functional differences between decay types

## Decay Types

### White Rot (Basidiomycota)
[[staycare-trametes-versicolor-bioremediation-applications]], Pycnoporus cinnabarinus
- d13C values of white-rot fungi differ from brown-rot fungi

### Brown Rot (Basidiomycota)
- Utilizes carbohydrates but degrades lignin only to a limited extent
- Higher %C in wood residues compared to white rot (43.5 +/- 0.9% vs 41.6 +/- 0.3%)
- Despite different substrate utilization strategies, d15N and d13C values show substantial overlap between brown- and white-rot fungi
- Growing isolates on the same substrate eliminates most differences

### Soft Rot (Ascomycota)
- Type 1: cavity formation in wood cell walls
- Type 2: very similar to white rot of basidiomycetes
- Ascomycetes use laccase for lignocellulolysis, sometimes with redox-mediator molecules

## [[saprotrophic-fungi]] significantly different from ECM fungi: d15N (1.86 vs 5.83 per mil), d13C (-23.2 vs -25.6 per mil)

### Functional Categories
- Wood decomposers (lignicolous species)
- Litter decomposers (terricolous species)
- [[query-what-are-ectomycorrhizal-fungi-and-which-plants-do-they-associate-with]]

Fungi could be further classified by their preferred decay stage: early colonizers of fresh wood versus species utilizing more decomposed material.

## Insect-Fungus Interactions in Wood Decay

Many wood-boring beetles inoculate wood with spores of wood-decay fungi during egg laying. The fungal mycelium rapidly degrades wood, and insects may benefit by:
1. Feeding on more palatable, decayed [[bark-xylotropic-endophytes-woody-tissue-colonization]]
2. Consuming fungal mycelium directly

Stable isotope analysis (d15N and d13C in larvae, wood, and mycelium) can help determine the relative importance of each pathway.
