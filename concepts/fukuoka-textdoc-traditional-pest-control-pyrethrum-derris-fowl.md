## Overview

Before the widespread adoption of synthetic pesticides, Japanese vegetable
growers relied on a small toolkit of natural insect control methods.
Masanobu Fukuoka draws on his wartime experience at the Kochi Prefecture
Agricultural Testing Station to describe these traditional techniques in
detail, arguing that they remain not only viable but superior to chemical
approaches. His account reveals a sophisticated understanding of insect
behavior, plant resistance, and the role of biological balance that
predates modern integrated pest management by decades. This page explores
the specific methods of trapping, repulsion, and natural insecticide use
that Fukuoka recommends for pesticide-free vegetable cultivation.

## The Wartime Pest Control Manual

While working at the Kochi Prefecture Agricultural Testing Station during
the war, Fukuoka wrote a practical manual entitled "Proposal for the
Control of Disease and Pest Damage in Vegetables." It contained reference
tables on diseases and insect pests for different vegetables, and gave the
most detailed possible descriptions of individual diseases and pests, the
characteristics of pathogenic microbes, infection processes in plants, and
the stages of development and behavior of insect pests.

The methods of control described in this booklet were all primitive and
consisted almost exclusively of skillful trapping or some form of
repulsion. There was almost nothing to write about insecticides. The
agents most widely in use at the time were herbs such as pyrethrum,
tobacco, and derris root. Aside from this, lead arsenate was used in a
very minor amount. Bordeaux mixture was used as a universal remedy for
bacterial and fungal diseases, and sulfur preparations saw occasional use
against certain diseases and mites.

Fukuoka reflects that it was fortunate there were no pesticides at the
time, for this allowed farmers and agricultural technicians to learn the
characteristics of crop diseases and pests, and concentrate on preventing
damage through repulsion and sound farming practices. The absence of easy
chemical solutions forced a deeper understanding of pest ecology and crop
vulnerability.

## The Narrow Range of Truly Major Pests

Although from ten to twenty types of pests and diseases generally attack
any one kind of vegetable, Fukuoka observes that the only ones that are
really major pests are cutworms, borers, leaf beetles, certain types of
ladybugs, seed-corm maggots, and aphids. The others can generally be
controlled by proper management. This insight is crucial: the vast
majority of insect damage can be managed through cultural practices rather
than chemical intervention, because most insects are not significant
threats to crop productivity.

Farmers in the pre-pesticide era almost never used pesticides on
vegetables in their kitchen gardens. All they did was to catch insects in
the morning and evening using gummy earth at the end of a piece of split
bamboo. This simple but effective technique worked well for caterpillars
feeding on cabbage and other leaf vegetables, melon flies on watermelon
and cucumbers, and ladybugs on eggplant and potatoes. The gummy earth
traps required no special equipment, no protective clothing, and no
knowledge of toxicology. They were safe for the farmer, safe for the
consumer, and selective enough to avoid killing beneficial insects.

## Disease Prevention Through Farming Practice

Disease and pest damage to vegetables can usually be prevented by being
familiar with the nature and features of such damage rather than by
attempts at chemical control. Most problems can be taken care of by
practicing a method of natural farming that gives some thought to what a
healthy vegetable is. Because hardy varieties are used, the right crop is
grown at the right time in healthy soil, and plants of the same type are
not grown together in large monocultures.

Companion-planting vegetables of many different types in place of weeds in
an orchard or on idle land is an eminently reasonable method of
cultivation. The diversity of plant species creates a complex habitat that
confuses pest insects and supports populations of natural predators.
Monoculture, by contrast, creates ideal conditions for pest outbreaks by
providing an abundant, concentrated food source and a simplified
environment in which pests can reproduce without natural checks.

## Pyrethrum and Derris as Natural Insecticides

As an additional precaution, Fukuoka recommends planting pyrethrum and
derris root at the edge of the garden. Tests were conducted on different
varieties of derris root at the Kochi testing center before the war, and
those varieties that were cold-hardy, suited to outdoor cultivation, and
had a high content of the active ingredient were selected for use.

Pyrethrum flowers and derris root may be dried and stored as powders.
Pyrethrum is effective against aphids and caterpillars, while derris root
works well against cabbage sawflies and leaf beetles. However, both agents
may be used against all insect pests, including melon flies, by dissolving
the agent in water and sprinkling the solution onto the vegetable plants
with a watering can. Both are harmless to man and garden vegetables.

The preparation of these natural insecticides is straightforward and
requires no industrial processing. The dried flowers of pyrethrum contain
pyrethrins, natural neurotoxins that break down rapidly in sunlight,
leaving no harmful residues on food crops. Derris root contains rotenone,
which similarly degrades quickly in the environment. Unlike synthetic
pesticides, which can persist in soil and water for years, these natural
compounds have been used safely for centuries.

## Fowl as Living Pest Control

While working in Kochi Prefecture, Fukuoka observed local chickens, black
as ravens, strutting through a vegetable patch in a farmyard and deftly
picking at insects without scratching the earth or harming the vegetables.
This observation led him to advocate letting fowl loose in a vegetable
patch as an effective and natural method of insect control. Chickens,
ducks, and other poultry consume large quantities of insects, particularly
in the early morning and evening when pests are most active near the soil
surface.
