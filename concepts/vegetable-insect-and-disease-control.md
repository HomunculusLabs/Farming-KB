# Vegetable Insect and Disease Control

[[integrated-pest-management]] strategies for home vegetable gardens,
based on Purdue Extension guidelines. Emphasis is on reducing
pesticide dependence through cultural practices, resistant cultivars,
and good garden sanitation.

Related: [[vegetable-garden-guide]], [[vegetable-weed-control-and-mulching]],
[[vegetable-watering-and-thinning]]

## The First Step: Identify the Problem

The very first step in controlling diseases and insect pests is to
identify the cause of the problem. Then choose an appropriate control
strategy. Misidentification leads to ineffective treatments and
unnecessary pesticide applications. Consult your local county
Extension office for help with diagnosis.

## Pesticide Application

Commercially available garden sprays or dusts applied throughout the
growing season will control many vegetable insects and diseases. Spray
or dust regularly as directed on the label. Be sure to cover the
underside of the leaves as well as the tops. Controls may need to be
reapplied after rain.

## Reducing Pesticide Dependence

To reduce dependence on pesticides, use the following integrated pest
management (IPM) techniques. These cultural and preventive practices
form the foundation of a sustainable pest management program.

## Strategy 1: Disease-Resistant Cultivars

Plant only disease-resistant cultivars whenever possible. Many
vegetable varieties have been bred for resistance to common diseases.
Resistance is the most effective and economical form of disease
control. Examples include tomato varieties resistant to Fusarium wilt,
Verticillium wilt, and nematodes; cabbage resistant to yellows and
black rot; and cucumber resistant to mosaic, scab, and powdery mildew.

## Strategy 2: Crop Rotation

Plant crops in a different location from year to year. Crop rotation
breaks the life cycles of many soil-borne pathogens and insect pests
that overwinter in the garden. Do not follow a crop with another
member of the same family. Rotate at least every 2 to 3 years.

Example: avoid planting tomatoes where potatoes, eggplants, or peppers
grew the previous year, as all are in the nightshade family.

### Green Manure Rotation

Plant half the garden to a winter green manure crop (rye, wheat,
barley, or winter vetch) in September or October. Apply well-rotted
manure to the other half, fall-plow, and plant spring vegetables
there. The following spring, turn under the green manure and plant
summer vegetables. Rotate these areas each year.

## Strategy 3: Garden Sanitation

Practice good garden sanitation to reduce pest and disease pressure.
Remove plant debris from the garden at the end of the season. Keep
edges and fence rows free of trash and weeds. Destroy diseased plants
rather than composting them. Clean up fallen fruit and rotting
vegetables promptly.

Weeds in and around the garden serve as alternate hosts for many
insect pests and plant diseases. Maintaining clean borders reduces
the reservoir of pests that can move into vegetable crops.

## Strategy 4: Hand-Picking Insect Pests

Hand-pick insect pests when possible. This is effective for larger,
slow-moving pests such as tomato hornworms, cabbage worms, Japanese
beetles, and Colorado potato beetles. Check plants at least weekly.
Early morning is often best, when insects are sluggish. Drop picked
insects into a container of soapy water.

## Cultural Practices for Disease Prevention

### Proper Watering

Water at the base of plants rather than overhead to keep foliage dry.
Wet leaves promote fungal and bacterial diseases. Water early in the
day so foliage dries quickly.

### Proper Spacing and Thinning

Proper spacing promotes good air circulation, which helps foliage dry
faster after rain or dew. Thinning overcrowded seedlings reduces
humidity within the plant canopy.

### Soil Drainage

Ensure good drainage to prevent waterlogged conditions that favor
root rot diseases. Avoid planting in low-lying areas where water
collects.

## Additional Preventive Measures

### Avoid Walnut Proximity

Walnut trees produce juglone, toxic to some plants (particularly
tomatoes). Maintain at least 50 feet between walnut trees and
sensitive plants. Juglone toxicity can mimic disease symptoms
(wilting, yellowing), so proper identification is important.

### Frost Protection

Cover tender crops when frost is forecast using cardboard boxes,
blankets, hot caps, or fabric row covers. Do not use metal or plastic
sheets, which conduct cold. Frost-damaged tissue is more susceptible
to pathogen infection.

### Mulching

Mulches help prevent soil splash onto lower leaves, which can spread
soil-borne diseases. Both organic and plastic mulches provide this
benefit. Black plastic mulch also warms soil for heat-loving crops.

## Season-End Cleanup

Clean, repair, and sharpen garden tools, and store them in a dry
place. Wipe metal blades with an oily rag to prevent rust. Store
leftover pesticides protected from extreme temperatures and moisture,
out of reach of children and pets. Remove all crop residue to reduce
overwintering sites for pests and diseases.
