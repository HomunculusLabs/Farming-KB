## Forage Trees and Meadow Rehabilitation

## The Decline of Natural Grasslands

After cropping, the second major factor reducing natural grassland production is **private land ownership and fencing**. The enclosure of open rangelands into privately held parcels has fundamentally altered grazing ecology by preventing the natural migratory movements of herd animals.

In wild systems, large herbivores graze an area intensively for a short period and then move on, allowing vegetation to recover fully before their return. Fencing breaks this cycle, concentrating animals on limited ground and leading to chronic overgrazing, soil compaction, and the progressive degradation of grassland communities. The result is a steady decline in both the productivity and the species diversity of meadow systems worldwide.

## The Bimodal Growth Curve of Grasslands

Grasslands exhibit a characteristic **bimodal growth curve** — they produce biomass in pulses rather than continuously throughout the year. Between these two growth periods lie two distinct **deficit periods** during which animals on pasture face serious nutritional shortfalls:

1. **Late summer deficit**: Triggered by seed shedding, increasing drought stress, and the natural drying-off of grasses as they complete their annual cycle. By late summer, the nutritional quality of standing forage declines sharply as plants translocate nutrients back into roots and crowns.
2. **Winter deficit**: Caused by slow or zero growth as temperatures drop and day length shortens. In colder climates, snow cover may completely deny access to any residual forage. Even where grass remains visible, its nutritive value is minimal.

Designing effective grazing systems means addressing both deficits through a combination of stored feeds and living forage reserves drawn from diverse sources.

## Forage Trees: Bridging the Summer Gap

Forage trees are one of the most effective strategies for overcoming the late summer deficit. While grasses are drying off and shedding seed, deep-rooted tree plantations continue to produce **green leaf** well into the dry season. Trees access moisture and nutrients from soil horizons far below the reach of grass roots, allowing them to maintain growth when surface soils are exhausted.

**Tagasaste** (*Chamaecytisus proliferus*) is particularly notable in this role. It has been demonstrated to produce forage weights equivalent to those of irrigated lucerne — a benchmark high-performance forage crop — without the need for irrigation or the heavy nitrogen inputs that lucerne demands.

Tagasaste thus offers a low-input, high-output solution for summer feed gaps, especially in Mediterranean and semi-arid climates where summer drought is the primary limitation on pasture productivity. Its nitrogen-fixing ability further reduces external inputs while improving soil fertility for surrounding pasture species.

## Winter Forage Strategies

Winter feeding presents a different set of challenges. Forage trees can contribute here as well, but species selection is critical:

- **Evergreen species** must carry mature, frost-hardy leaf through the cold months. Young growth is vulnerable to frost damage, so trees need to be well-established and bearing mature foliage before winter arrives.
- **Willow and poplar** serve as summer-deciduous forage trees. While they lose their leaves in winter, their prolific summer leaf production can be processed and stored for cold-season feeding.
- **Wood chips** from deciduous forage trees can be **fermented** to produce winter concentrate feeds. This fermentation process makes the woody material more digestible and concentrates nutrients into a compact, storable form that animals can consume during periods when fresh forage is unavailable.

## Processing Forage from Trees

Tree foliage can be processed through several well-established methods to preserve its nutritional value for off-season feeding. Each method has distinct advantages depending on the available infrastructure and the intended feeding regime:

- **Hay**: Lopped foliage is sun-dried and baled for long-term storage, retaining much of its protein content. Simple and low-cost, hay production requires only drying racks and baling equipment.
- **Silage**: Fresh-chopped foliage is ensiled under anaerobic conditions, preserving protein content and digestibility while reducing dry-matter losses. Silage is particularly valuable in humid climates where sun-drying is unreliable.
- **Pelleting**: Dried, ground foliage is compressed into pellets for efficient handling, storage, and controlled ration feeding. Pellets are ideal for supplementing confined animals during winter housing.

These processing methods extend the utility of tree forage well beyond its growing season, creating reliable year-round feed security for grazing operations.

## Cultivating Tagasaste

Tagasaste is relatively straightforward to establish in the field. It can be **field-drilled** using a normal seedbox without requiring specialized equipment. For effective establishment, it is common practice to **mix the tagasaste seed with daikon radish or turnip**. These companion crops germinate quickly, and their broad leaves suppress weed grasses while also protecting the young tagasaste seedlings during their most vulnerable early stages of growth.

Seed is typically gathered from the **soil beneath mature trees** using shaker trays. Mature tagasaste drops seed freely throughout the season, and collecting it from the ground surface is more efficient than attempting to harvest directly from the plant canopy. A single mature tree can produce enough seed to establish a substantial planting, making seed collection a worthwhile investment.

### Matthew Carpenter's Canterbury Plain Work

On the Canterbury Plain of New Zealand, **Matthew Carpenter** demonstrated that tagasaste planted in strips provides benefits extending well beyond the trees themselves. Grasses growing between the tagasaste strips were observed to **dry out later** in the season and to **produce more total growth** than grasses in open, unsheltered pasture. The trees appear to create a favorable microclimate — reducing wind speed, moderating temperature extremes, and possibly redistributing soil moisture through hydraulic lift — that extends the productive life of the inter-row pasture and improves overall meadow output.

This finding is significant because it demonstrates that integrating forage trees into pasture does not merely replace grass production but can actively enhance it. The shelter effect alone can extend the growing season by several weeks, which in a marginal climate translates directly into additional weeks of animal weight gain or milk production.

### Spacing and Multiple Yields

Tagasaste spacing is determined by the intended use and management goals:

- **1.0–1.5 m spacing**: Used for cut forage systems. At this close density, stems reach harvestable height (approximately 1 m high) quickly, allowing frequent coppicing for sustained fodder production.
- **4–5 m spacing**: Used when the primary goals are seed production and bee forage. Wider spacing allows each tree to develop a full, open canopy, maximizing flower production per tree.

Beyond forage, tagasaste also produces **chicken seed** from dropped pods and supports **honey production** through its prolific nectar-rich flowers, making it a truly multi-yield element in any holistic grazing system design.

## Plant Groups for Mixed Meadows

A well-designed meadow incorporates plant species from five functional groups, each contributing different root structures, nutrient dynamics, and wildlife habitat value:

### 1. Grasses

Perennial grasses form the structural backbone of any meadow. Design should include a mix of **perennial, bunch, and tussock species** to provide year-round ground cover and diverse growth forms. **Ricegrasses** and species of *Panicum* offer diverse growth habits and high palatability for grazing animals. **Rushes** serve a dual role as thatch material for construction and craft use, adding economic value beyond the meadow itself. Selecting grasses across a range of heights and root depths ensures that no single soil layer is unoccupied, maximizing both nutrient cycling and resistance to erosion.

### 2. Legumes

Legumes fix atmospheric nitrogen, enriching the soil for surrounding grasses and forbs. Useful meadow legumes include **clovers, vetches, lupins, and tares**.

**Bulbous and deep-rooted legumes** are especially valuable because their deep taproots break compacted subsoil layers and access nutrients unavailable to shallow-rooted species, cycling them back to the surface through leaf decomposition. A healthy legume component can substantially reduce or eliminate the need for applied nitrogen fertilizers in the meadow system.

### 3. Bulbs, Corms, and Tubers

This group stores energy below ground and provides critical food for wildlife and livestock during lean seasons. Key species include **sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke), breadroot, crocus, camass, sego lily, various orchids, and onions**.

Many of these were staple foods for indigenous peoples and remain highly productive in undisturbed meadow conditions. Their root systems also improve soil structure through penetration and decomposition, creating channels for water movement and organic matter incorporation.

### 4. Herbaceous Perennial Forbs

Broad-leaved flowering plants add biodiversity and support pollinator populations. Important genera include **nettles, daisies, Umbelliferae (carrot family), Compositae (daisy family), poppies, forget-me-nots, and watercress**.

These species occupy varied ecological niches within the meadow sward, from wet seepage areas to dry ridges, each filling a specific role in the overall community. Their presence also improves the mineral profile of the meadow through deep nutrient cycling.

### 5. Spike-Rooted Flatweeds

Often dismissed as weeds, spike-rooted species are essential meadow components. **Dandelion, thistles, plantains, docks, and chicory** send deep taproots into the subsoil, mining minerals and bringing them to the surface where they become available to other plants through leaf fall and decomposition.

Their deep roots also create channels for water infiltration and soil aeration. These dynamic accumulators perform a function no other plant group can replicate, making them indispensable in any comprehensive meadow design.

## Critical Characteristics for Meadow Design

When selecting species for a meadow mix, the designer must carefully consider several critical characteristics:

- **Mode of occurrence**: Does the species grow in dense clumps or as scattered solitary individuals? Clumping species provide dense cover and protection; solitary species fill gaps between clumps and extend the sward into marginal microsites.
- **Method of propagation**: Understanding whether a species spreads by seed, rhizome, stolon, or bulb is essential for predicting how it will behave and spread within the meadow over time.
- **Preferred soils and sites**: Each species has specific requirements for drainage, soil texture, pH (from acidity to alkalinity), and aspect. Matching species to appropriate microsites within the meadow ensures the greatest possible diversity, resilience, and productivity.

## The Meadow as a Perennial Self-Seeding System

The ultimate goal of meadow rehabilitation is to establish a **perennial, self-seeding system** that requires minimal ongoing management once it is fully established. Achieving this demands significant upfront investment in **earth-shaping** — creating swales, ponds, and contour banks to manage water flow across the site — and thorough **initial weed removal** to give desirable species a competitive advantage during the critical establishment phase. Earthworks ensure that water is distributed evenly across the landscape rather than concentrating in gullies or running off the surface, which both wastes water and erodes topsoil.

Once established, a diverse meadow maintains itself through natural seed drop, vegetative spread, and the complex ecological relationships between its component species. The designer's role shifts from active management to observation and occasional intervention — such as controlled burns or rotational grazing adjustments — to maintain diversity and prevent any single species from becoming overly dominant.

## Essential Fauna Components

A meadow is not merely a plant community — it is a complex ecosystem that depends on the active presence of several animal groups for its health and function. Designing for these relationships is as important as selecting plant species:

- **Pollinators**: Butterflies and bees ensure the reproduction of flowering forbs and legumes, maintaining genetic diversity and reliable seed production across the meadow.
- **Browsers**: Appropriate grazing animals stimulate growth, prevent any single species from dominating, and cycle nutrients through dung and urine deposition.
- **Burrowers**: Worms aerate and enrich the soil through their castings; field mice and other small mammals create tunnel networks that improve water infiltration and seed burial.
- **Predators**: Owls and other raptors control rodent and insect populations, preventing outbreaks that could damage the meadow community.

Each group plays an irreplaceable role, and the removal of any one creates cascading imbalances that degrade the entire system over time.

## Soil Rehabilitation: Sodseeding

Where degraded pastures require rehabilitation without the expense and disruption of complete renovation, **sodseeding** is a highly effective technique. Using a **broad-flanged chisel point** at a depth of **6–10 cm** and a spacing of **0.5–0.6 m**, the operator creates narrow slits in the existing sod without fully disturbing it. Seed is dropped directly into these slits, where it enjoys good seed-to-soil contact and is protected from birds and desiccation. This approach introduces new species into an existing sward with minimal disruption, gradually shifting the species composition toward a more diverse, resilient, and productive meadow community.

The shallow depth of sodseeding is deliberate — it places seed in a zone where soil moisture is more reliable than at the surface but does not disturb the existing root mat. Over successive seasons, the new species establish, flower, set seed, and spread naturally into the surrounding sod, progressively transforming a degraded pasture into a rich, multi-species meadow without the need for ploughing and reseeding from bare soil.
