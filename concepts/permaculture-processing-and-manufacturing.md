# Permaculture Processing and Manufacturing

updated: 2026-04-18

Bill Mollison's [[permaculture-design]] course covers processing hardware needs and manufacturing policy as essential components of a self-reliant community. Processing adds value to raw products, extends shelf life, and creates local employment. Manufacturing policy determines what is made locally versus imported.

## Processing Hardware

### Essential Processing Equipment
Mollison identifies key hardware needs for a permaculture community:
- **Pressers**: Oil seed extractors, fruit presses, cider presses
- **Grinders/Mills**: Grain mills, nut butters, spice grinders
- **Juicers**: For fruits and vegetables
- **Stills**: For essential oils, distilled water, and spirits
- **Dryers**: Solar dehydrators, food dryers for preservation
- **Fermentation vessels**: For wine, beer, vinegar, lacto-fermented foods
- **Canning equipment**: For long-term food preservation
- **Bakery ovens**: For bread and baked goods
- **Cheese-making equipment**: For dairy processing

### Shared Equipment Models
Processing equipment is expensive individually but affordable when shared:
- **Community tool libraries**: Equipment available for community use
- **Cooperative ownership**: Groups purchase and share equipment
- **Mobile processing units**: Shared between communities on rotation
- **Ripple Flow rights**: The right to use equipment when not in use by owner (see below)

## Manufacturing Policy

### Local Manufacturing Principles
Mollison's manufacturing policy:
1. **Local within range of users**: Manufacture close to where products are used
2. **District facilities**: Shared processing facilities serving multiple settlements
3. **[[appropriate-technology]]**: Simple, repairable, locally-maintainable equipment
4. **Value-added processing**: Transform raw products into higher-value goods locally
5. **Avoid unnecessary transport**: Process where the raw material is produced

### Ripple Flow Rights
A concept from the Watts Self-Help Group:
- When you own a piece of equipment (e.g., a husker-grinder), others have the right to use it when you are not
- This maximizes equipment utilization
- Creates a culture of sharing and mutual support
- Reduces the capital investment needed by each household

### The "Hand Off" Concept
When one community member masters a skill or acquires equipment:
- They are expected to "hand off" knowledge and access to others
- Teaching and sharing become obligations of expertise
- Prevents dependency on single individuals
- Builds community resilience through distributed knowledge

## Examples of Processing Chains

### Olives to Oil
1. Harvest olives from permaculture tree systems
2. Press within 24-48 hours (community press)
3. Store oil in dark, cool conditions
4. Press cake becomes animal feed or mulch
5. Oil used locally or traded

### Grains to Bread
1. Grow grain (wheat, rye, oats) in field systems
2. Harvest, thresh, winnow
3. Mill at community mill
4. Bake at community bakery or home oven
5. Straw used for mulch, compost, animal bedding, mushroom substrate

### Fruit to Preserves
1. Harvest from food forests and orchards
2. Process in community kitchen
3. Can, jam, dry, or ferment
4. Extend shelf life from weeks to years
5. Trade preserved goods for other community products

### Bamboo to Products
1. Harvest from bamboo groves
2. Process (split, treat, shape)
3. Manufacture (furniture, tools, building materials, crafts)
4. Use locally or trade

## The Watts Self-Help Group Model

### Barney's Story
From Mollison's permaculture design course, the Watts Self-Help Group in Los Angeles:
- **Barney** took over approximately 70 street gangs
- Redirected gang activity into productive employment and community gardens
- Established food-growing operations in vacant lots
- Created economic alternatives to gang violence
- Was shot afterward (highlighting the risks of systemic change)

### Lessons
1. Economic alternatives can redirect destructive social patterns
2. Food-growing can be a vehicle for social transformation
3. Community organizing around productive activity builds social capital
4. The risks are real when challenging established power structures
5. Processing and manufacturing create jobs that anchor community development

## Appropriate Technology Selection

### Criteria for Equipment Selection
- **Repairable**: Can be fixed locally with available tools and skills
- **Affordable**: Within community financial means
- **Energy-efficient**: Can be powered by renewable energy or human power
- **Multi-purpose**: Serves more than one function
- **Scalable**: Works at household, community, and district scales
- **Locally-sourced**: Can be built or obtained locally

### Energy Sources for Processing
- **Solar**: Solar dryers, solar ovens, solar-powered mills
- **Human power**: Hand grinders, presses, bicycles for electricity
- **Animal power**: Draft animal-powered mills and presses
- **Biomass**: Wood gasifiers, biogas digesters
- **Micro-hydro**: Where water flow is available
- **Wind**: Mechanical wind power for grinding and pumping

## Integration with Community Economics

### Local Currency Support
Processing and manufacturing create goods for local trade:
- Products can be priced in local currency (LETS systems)
- Value-added processing increases economic activity
- Skills become tradeable services
- Equipment sharing builds social networks

### Employment Creation
Processing adds labor value to raw materials:
- Sorting, cleaning, grading
- Processing (pressing, milling, cooking, preserving)
- Packaging and labeling
- Distribution and marketing
- Equipment maintenance and repair
