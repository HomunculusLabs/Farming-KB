# Landscape Rehabilitation of Degraded and Mined Land

Rehabilitating degraded, eroded, and mined landscapes is among the most
important applications of [[permaculture-design]]. Mollison presents landscape
restoration as a practical demonstration of [[permaculture-principles]]: the
designer works with natural succession processes to rebuild soil, re-establish
vegetation, capture and hold water, and eventually restore productive capacity
to damaged land. The process requires patience, careful observation, and
systematic intervention at the appropriate scale and sequence.

## Assessment and Planning

Before any rehabilitation work begins, a thorough site assessment is
necessary. This includes: mapping existing erosion patterns (sheet, rill, and
gully erosion), analysing soil conditions (depth, texture, structure, pH,
toxicity), identifying remaining vegetation and seed sources, assessing
water flow patterns and drainage, cataloguing any contamination from mining
or industrial activity, and determining the slope, aspect, and elevation of
the site.

The rehabilitation plan follows a sequence of stabilisation, [[water-management]],
soil building, vegetation establishment, and productive system development.
Each phase creates conditions for the next. Attempting to establish productive
systems before stabilisation and water management are complete will fail.

## Erosion Control

Erosion control is the first priority on degraded land. Unstabilised soil
moves with every rainfall event, removing the medium in which plants grow and
carrying sediment into waterways. Mollison emphasises that erosion control
must be physical and immediate, before biological systems can establish.

**Physical structures:** Rock walls (dry stone or gabion), log check dams,
and brush barriers placed across gullies and watercourses slow water flow,
trap sediment, and create level areas where soil accumulates. On slopes,
contour bunds (earth banks on contour) break long slopes into shorter
segments, reducing the velocity of overland flow. Straw bale barriers provide
temporary erosion control while vegetation establishes.

**Contour earthworks:** Swales on degraded slopes capture runoff and allow
infiltration rather than concentrating flow into erosive channels. See
[[mollison-designers-contour-surveying-a-frame-bunyip-level]] for
techniques to accurately peg contours on degraded terrain. Swales on severely
degraded slopes may need to be reinforced with rock or geotextile until
vegetation stabilises the berm.

**Surface protection:** Immediate surface protection through mulching
prevents raindrop impact from sealing the soil surface and initiating
erosion. Straw, wood chips, or geotextile mats are applied at 5 to 10
centimetres depth over bare soil. On steep slopes, mulch is held in place
with jute netting or pegged brush.

## Soil Rebuilding

Degraded and mined soils typically lack organic matter, soil structure,
microbial life, and often contain toxic levels of heavy metals or
acidification. Soil rebuilding is a multi-stage process.

**Physical amelioration:** Compacted mine soils and degraded subsoils require
deep ripping or keyline ploughing to break hardpans and improve water
penetration. On slopes, this work follows contour to prevent concentrated
water flow. Rock dust and clay additions improve the water-holding capacity
and cation exchange capacity of sandy or skeletal mine soils.

**Organic matter addition:** Compost, manure, and [[green-manure-crops]] add
organic matter that improves soil structure, water retention, microbial
activity, and nutrient availability. On severely degraded sites, the first
organic matter inputs must come from off-site sources until on-site
production can sustain itself.

**[[mycorrhizal-inoculation]]:** [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] form symbiotic relationships
with plant roots, dramatically extending the effective root zone for water
and nutrient uptake. On degraded and mined land where soil biology has been
destroyed, inoculation with mycorrhizal fungi is essential for plant
establishment. Inoculant can be applied as a root dip at planting, mixed
into compost, or applied as a top-dressing. Protecting existing remnant
vegetation that hosts [[mycorrhizal-networks]] is critical, as these serve as
inoculation sources for adjacent areas.

## Pioneer Species Selection

Pioneer species are the first plants established on degraded land. They must
tolerate exposed conditions, low fertility, and often drought, while
beginning the process of soil building and microclimate creation.

**Nitrogen-fixing trees:** Acacia, alder, black locust, [[permaculture-tagasaste]], casuarina,
and leucaena fix nitrogen while providing shade, leaf litter, and wind
protection. They are the primary framework for rehabilitation of degraded
land. In arid zones, acacia species are the most reliable pioneers.

**Erosion-control grasses:** Vetiver grass (Vetiveria zizanoides) is the
single most effective plant for erosion control on steep slopes. Its deep,
dense root system binds soil to depths of 3 metres or more, tolerating a wide
range of soil conditions including acidity, salinity, and heavy metals. Other
useful grasses include kikuyu, buffalo grass, and native bunch grasses.

**Accumulator and soil-building species:** Comfrey and yarrow bring minerals
from subsoil to the surface through deep root systems. Their biomass, cut and
used as mulch, adds minerals and organic matter to the surface soil layer.

**Leguminous ground covers:** Clovers, vetch, and lablab cover the soil
surface, fix nitrogen, and add organic matter. They are sown beneath and
between pioneer trees to accelerate soil development.

## Water Management on Degraded Slopes

Water management on degraded land must address both erosion (slowing and
spreading water flow) and infiltration (capturing water into the soil
profile). The keyline system described by Yeomans and endorsed by Mollison
is particularly relevant: water is stored high in the landscape at the
keypoint (where valley slopes change from convex to concave) and distributed
along contour channels to dry ridges.

Dams in upper catchments provide irrigation reserves and stock water while
reducing peak flows. Spillways are designed as grassed or rock-armoured
waterways. On mine sites, clean water must be directed away from contaminated
areas and contaminated runoff contained for treatment.

Revegetated swales and contour banks progressively reduce runoff velocity
and increase infiltration as vegetation matures. The goal is to convert the
site from a net water exporter (erosion) to a net water accumulator.

## Establishing Productive Systems

As pioneer species establish and soil conditions improve, productive species
are progressively introduced. The transition follows the succession pattern
described in [[mollison-designers-food-forest-succession-design]]. Pioneer
nitrogen-fixing trees are thinned or retained as shelter, while fruit and nut
trees, timber species, and pasture are established in improving conditions.

On mine sites, careful monitoring of soil and water quality is essential before
food-producing plants are introduced. Heavy metal accumulation in plant tissues
must be assessed. Initial systems may focus on non-food products (timber, fibre,
biofuel, livestock fodder) until contamination levels are confirmed safe.
