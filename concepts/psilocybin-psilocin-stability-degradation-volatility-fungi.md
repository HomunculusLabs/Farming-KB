### Baeocystin as a Degradation Marker

The rapid loss of baeocystin in aged specimens suggests it may serve as a useful indicator of specimen freshness. Specimens retaining significant baeocystin are likely to have been collected relatively recently, while those showing only psilocybin and psilocin (without baeocystin) may have undergone significant degradation. This has implications for both forensic analysis of seized material and for the interpretation of published chemical studies that report alkaloid profiles without specifying specimen age or storage conditions.

### Temperature and Storage Medium Effects

While the primary studies focused on time-dependent degradation, the rate of alkaloid loss is almost certainly influenced by temperature and storage conditions. Higher temperatures accelerate chemical degradation of most organic compounds, and the indole alkaloids in fungi are likely no exception. Dark, cool storage in airtight containers would be expected to slow degradation significantly compared to room-temperature storage in open containers. The absence of systematic studies on storage conditions represents a gap in the mycochemical literature.

## Time-Dependent Loss of Baeocystin

Repke and colleagues showed that the related compound baeocystin exhibits age-dependent variation. In [[psilocybe-cyanescens]], specimens analyzed 20 to 60 days after collection showed **no trace of indole compounds** whatsoever. This finding has two important implications:

1. Baeocystin may degrade even more rapidly than psilocybin.
2. Chemical studies performed on specimens that are not freshly collected may produce misleadingly low or zero readings for baeocystin, even when the compound was originally present in significant quantities.

## Ethnobotanical Knowledge Confirms Laboratory Findings

Indigenous peoples of Mexico, who have used neurotropic fungi for centuries, possess traditional knowledge that aligns precisely with the laboratory findings on alkaloid degradation. Mexican Indian curanderos traditionally advise that **old dried specimens kept for more than one year are not good to use** and should be discarded. This practice likely evolved through empirical observation of diminished effects from aged material.

Guzman himself reported a personal experience supporting the instability of these compounds. In 1958, while exploring Oaxaca in search of neurotropic fungi, he slept in a small closed room filled with a large collection of fresh and semifermented specimens of mixed Psilocybe species (P. caerulescens, P. cordispora, P. cubensis, P. mexicana, and P. zapotecorum). The air was heavy with a strong acrid aroma, and despite never consuming any fungi, he experienced **colored hallucinations** from prolonged exposure to the volatile compounds. Upon leaving the room for fresh air, the hallucinations ceased immediately. This suggests that psilocybin and psilocin, or their precursors, may be **volatile** at least under conditions of high concentration and warm, enclosed spaces.

## Volatility and Environmental Exposure

The Guzman incident raises the possibility that psilocybin and related indoles may be released into the air under certain conditions. This has several implications:

- **Occupational exposure**: People who handle large quantities of fresh neurotropic fungi in enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces may experience mild psychoactive effects through inhalation alone, without any oral ingestion.
- **Storage recommendations**: The volatile nature of the compounds reinforces the need for airtight, cool storage to prevent both degradation and loss of active material to the surrounding air.
- **Forensic considerations**: The presence of psilocybin in the air of spaces where mushrooms have been stored could potentially be detected, providing an additional avenue for forensic investigation.
- **Ecological questions**: It remains unknown whether volatile psilocybin compounds play any ecological role, such as in deterrence of herbivores or attraction of spore-dispersing animals.

The acrid aroma described by Guzman is characteristic of many Psilocybe species and is often noted by field mycologists as a distinguishing feature. Whether this aroma is caused by psilocybin itself, by related indole compounds, or by other secondary metabolites is not definitively established.

## Implications for Chemical Studies

The instability of these compounds creates significant methodological challenges for mycological research:

### False Negatives from Aged Material

Several published chemical studies may have reported false negatives due to testing aged specimens. Hofmann, analyzing Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides collected from a Mexican market and preserved for a long period, found no indole compounds. Given what is now known about degradation, the original specimens may well have contained psychoactive substances that degraded during storage.

### Misidentification Compounding Stability Issues

The problem of degradation interacts with the separate problem of taxonomic misidentification. Several cases exist where researchers reported psilocybin from specimens that were later determined to be different species than assumed. The combination of misidentified material and aged specimens makes it extremely difficult to assess the reliability of many published chemical analyses.
