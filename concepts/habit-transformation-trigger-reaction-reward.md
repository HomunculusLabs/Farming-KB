## The Three-Phase Habit Model

Every habit operates through the same cycle. Consider the example of
shopping addiction: whenever a person feels anxious (the trigger), they
think about going shopping (the reaction). At the store, they
temporarily feel safe and in control (the reward).

However, the insecurity only intensifies over time as rising bills
become an additional source of anxiety, creating a reinforcing loop
where the "solution" generates more of the original problem.

Whether the habit involves tapping a foot incessantly, reaching for
substances, avoiding difficult conversations, or any other pattern,
these three phases are operational. The key insight is that the reward
can be preserved while changing the reaction.

Successful transformation means identifying the actual reward — feeling
safe, feeling in control, feeling connected — and finding new reactions
that deliver the same reward without the destructive side effects.

## Choosing Words That Matter

The words chosen to communicate about stressful situations have a
significant physiological impact. During crises such as emergencies or
scheduled surgeries, patients exhibit heightened attention and
increased susceptibility to suggestion.

This intensity-rich environment creates a trance-like state where
people are more vulnerable to both negative and positive words.
Hurtful words aggravate anxiety and pain; carefully chosen supportive
words offer a direct therapeutic opportunity.

"I am a bit anxious about the situation" feels physiologically
different from "I am terrified" or "I feel like I am going to die."
The words chosen change the meaning and emotional intensity of the
experience.
