# Werewolf Beliefs and Hallucinogenic Shape-Shifting

## Overview

Michael J. Harner proposed that the European werewolf belief—lycanthropy—may have its origins in the hallucinogenic effects of psychoactive plants. This concept page examines the historical evidence for lycanthropy, its pharmacological parallels with tropane alkaloid and beta-carboline intoxication, and the broader phenomenon of hallucinogen-induced shape-shifting across cultures.

## The Harmaline Shape-Shifting Experiment

### Claudio Naranjo's Research

The key piece of evidence linking hallucinogens to werewolf beliefs comes from the research of Claudio Naranjo, who administered harmaline (a beta-carboline alkaloid from *Banisteriopsis caapi*, a component of ayahuasca) to an experimental subject. The subject's experience progressed through three stages of transformation:

1. **Bird**: "He first believed he was a bird flying through the air"
2. **Fish**: "Then a fish"—experiencing aquatic freedom
3. **Tiger**: "I wasn't a fish anymore, but a big cat, a tiger. I walked, though, feeling the same freedom I had experienced as a bird and a fish, freedom of movement, flexibility, grace. I moved as a tiger in the jungle, joyously, feeling the ground under my feet, feeling my power; my chest grew larger."

### The Attack on the "Deer"

The most striking aspect of the experience occurred when the subject "approached an animal, any animal. He only saw its neck, and then experienced what a tiger feels when looking at its prey." The "neck" belonged to a woman in the room who had appeared to him to have changed into a deer. The subject "had to be restrained from attempting to bite her neck."

This progression—from bird to fish to predatory cat, culminating in an actual predatory attack—demonstrates that hallucinogen-induced shape-shifting is not merely a subjective belief but can produce behavior consistent with the assumed animal identity. The subject did not just *think* he was a tiger; he *acted* as a tiger, with potentially dangerous consequences.

## Historical Accounts of Lycanthropy

### Paulus Aegineta (4th-7th Century)

The Greek physician Paulus Aegineta described lycanthropy as follows:

"Those labouring under lycanthropia go out during the night imitating wolves in all things and lingering about sepulchres until morning. You may recognize such persons by these marks: they are pale, their vision feeble, their eyes dry, tongue very dry, and the flow of the saliva stopped; but they are thirsty, and their legs have incurable ulcerations from frequent falls."

### Pharmacological Parallels

Harner notes that the symptoms described by Aegineta "closely resemble those reported for the clinical effects of atropine":
- **Dry eyes**: Atropine causes extreme pupillary dilation and reduced lacrimation
- **Dry tongue / stopped saliva**: Atropine is a potent anticholinergic that dries all mucous membranes
- **Great thirst**: A consequence of the extreme dryness of mouth and throat
- **Impaired vision**: Atropine causes blurred vision, photophobia, and visual disturbances
- **Staggering gait**: Atropine causes ataxia and loss of motor coordination
- **Pale appearance**: Vasomotor effects of anticholinergic drugs
- **Leg ulcerations from falls**: The ataxia and delirium caused by tropane alkaloids would lead to frequent falls and injuries

The correspondence between the clinical symptoms of lycanthropy and the known pharmacological effects of atropine is too close to be coincidental. Harner argues that lycanthropy was not merely a psychological disorder but a toxic condition caused by the ingestion of solanaceous plants.

## LSD and Shape-Shifting

Beyond classical hallucinogens like harmaline and tropane alkaloids, LSD experiences in Western culture have also produced random accounts of shape-changing. This suggests that the phenomenon of hallucinogen-induced animal transformation is not specific to any one class of psychoactive compounds but may be a general feature of the psychedelic experience when it occurs in cultural contexts that include animal transformation as a recognized possibility.

The cultural expectation of shape-shifting appears to function as a "suggestive frame" that channels the hallucinogenic experience in a particular direction. In cultures where werewolf beliefs are prevalent, the experience of altered identity under the influence of psychoactive substances naturally takes the form of wolf transformation.

## The Jivaro Perspective

During Harner's fieldwork among the Jivaro Indians of eastern Ecuador, he found that they use both solanaceous plants (*Datura*) and non-solanaceous hallucinogens. The Jivaro use Datura in the vision quest simply to encounter the supernatural but do not use it in shamanism because it is "too strong" and prevents the shaman from being able to operate in both worlds simultaneously.

This distinction between vision quest (where the goal is to lose ordinary identity and merge with the supernatural) and shamanism (where the goal is to maintain a functional connection between ordinary and extraordinary reality) provides a framework for understanding the difference between the European witch's Sabbat experience and the shaman's more controlled engagement with altered states.

## Sabbats and Esbats

Harner proposes that the existence of both Sabbats (the major nocturnal gatherings involving "flight") and Esbats (minor, often local gatherings) in European witchcraft may reflect the difference between solanaceous-induced visionary experiences (Sabbats) and more ordinary ritual activities (Esbats). The solanaceous plants, being "too strong" for controlled shamanic work, were reserved for the dramatic Sabbat experiences, while Esbats involved the routine practice of folk magic without psychoactive enhancement.

## The Scope of Shamanism

The European case raises the question of whether shamans must be in a trance state while performing their manipulative activities. If not, it may be necessary to "revise our conceptions of the scope of shamanism and to extend it to include some of the central aspects of witchcraft as it was formerly practiced in Europe."

This suggests that European witchcraft was not a degenerate or corrupted form of shamanism but a legitimate variant of the shamanic tradition, adapted to the specific psychoactive plants available in Europe and the particular cultural context of late medieval Christianity.

## Trans-Cultural Regularities

The shape-shifting belief—found in European werewolf traditions, Amazonian ayahuasca experiences, and the broader shamanic concept of animal transformation—represents a "widespread and obviously long-standing thematic regularity" in human culture. Harner argues that such regularities are best explained not by cultural diffusion alone but by the common human response to psychoactive substances, which produce similar experiential patterns across cultures.
