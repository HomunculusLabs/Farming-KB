# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi — Diversity, Distribution, and Species Richness

## Range of Habitats

Fungi in the Order Glomales have been found in nearly every habitat in which plants are able to grow (Brundrett 1991). The occurrence of a species and its abundance are determined largely by the presence and abundance of compatible host plants that allow the fungus to grow and reproduce.

### Where AM Fungi Are Least Abundant

AM fungi are least abundant and least heterogeneous in:

- **Coniferous forests** (Pinus, Picea, Abies)
- **Hardwood forests** dominated by Quercus, Fagus, Thuja, or Sequoia
- **Eucalyptus forests** — largely ectomycorrhizal (Allen et al. 1995)
- **High arctic** (Bledsoe et al. 1990)
- **Tundra** — dominant ericaceous plants have ericoid mycorrhizae

Arctic habitats show little evidence of AM associations, although endemic plants are capable of forming mycorrhizal associations when inoculated with introduced fungi (DeMars and Boerner 1995). This suggests that the absence of AM in these regions is due to environmental constraints rather than host plant incompatibility.

### Where AM Fungi Are Most Diverse

The highest numbers and diversity of AM fungal species are found in:

- **Maritime and lacustrine sand dunes** (Koske 1987)
- **Prairie grasslands** (Bentivenga and Hetrick 1992)
- **Temperate and tropical forests** (Louis and Lim 1987)
- **Deserts** (Bethlenfalvay et al. 1984)
- **Salt marshes** (Hoefnagels et al. 1993)
- **Agricultural fields** (Allen et al. 1995)

Even in sites where sporulation is not detected, most plants remain mycorrhizal — the fungal symbionts persist as vegetative mycelium or as dormant propagules in soil or roots.

### Cosmopolitan Species

Several AM fungal species have been documented across most continents:

- *[[acaulospora-scrobiculata]]*
- *[[archaeospora-leptoticha]]*
- *Gigaspora gigantea*
- *Scutellospora calospora*

These cosmopolitan distributions likely reflect either long-distance dispersal capabilities or historical biogeographic patterns that predate continental separation.

## Generic-Level Habitat Preferences

At the generic level, no clear-cut habitat preferences are discernible. Similar ratios of species per genus are obtained from deserts, grasslands, tropical forests, and agricultural communities (INVAM Biogeography Database).

However, some patterns emerge:

- **Glomus dominance**: The number of *Glomus* species often exceeds those of other genera by an average of 3 to 1, but this reflects the larger number of described species in the genus rather than true ecological dominance.
- **Arid sites**: Fungal communities are composed mainly of small-spored species in the genera *Glomus*, *Acaulospora*, and *Entrophospora* (Stutz and Morton 1996; Stutz et al. 2000).
- **Tropical forests**: *Acaulospora* appears dominant in field settings, but this is an erroneous conclusion resulting from the great abundance of spores produced by *Acaulospora* species, not necessarily greater species diversity.

### Continental Comparisons

Comparative studies across continents with similar habitat characteristics reveal some selectivity in [[mineralogical-controls-fungal-community-structure]]. Arid sites from the Namib Desert (Africa) and the Sonoran Desert (North America) contain four to eight species in the suborder Glomineae (*Glomus*, *Acaulospora*, *Entrophospora*). Among 21 species recovered from both continents, 61% occurred on both (Stutz et al. 2000), suggesting considerable overlap in arid-adapted AM fungal communities.

## Patterns of Species Richness

### Measurement Challenges

Species richness of AM fungi is measured as the number of species whose spores are extracted from soil samples, with a few exceptions for species whose mycorrhizal roots are sufficiently well characterized for identification (McGee 1989; Abbott and Gazey 1994).

A minimum level of fungal biomass (mycorrhizal colonization) is needed before reproduction commences (Gazey et al. 1992). Many fungi colonize roots at low levels and grow through hyphal spread among plants without sporulating, making spore-based surveys incomplete.

### Trap Culture Yields

Trap cultures from arid sites in the southern United States and Namibia demonstrated progressive recovery:

- **First cycle**: sporulating species increased from zero to one
- **Third cycle**: four to eight species (Stutz and Morton 1996)

Species saturation in the root system of a single host plant can be as high as eight species (Morton et al. 1995). This highlights the importance of multi-cycle trap cultures for comprehensive species inventories.

### Field Richness Values

| Habitat Type | Species per Sample | Source |
|---|---|---|
| General field sites | 3–11 | Walker et al. 1982 |
| Temperate sites | 5–10 | Benjamin et al. 1988 |
| Temperate sites (intensive) | 12–22 | Johnson et al. 1991 |
| Tropical forests | 10–18 | Johnson and Wedin 1997 |
| Arid habitats | 0–2 | Rose 1981; Bethlenfalvay et al. 1984 |

Species composition varies considerably among communities from different habitats, especially those in which moisture availability is limited.

### Latitudinal Patterns

The widespread trend of increasing species richness from high to low latitudes among living organisms (Rohde 1992) is **not clear-cut** among AM fungi:

- Koske (1987): 2–8 species per site along 300 km Atlantic coast transect, with some latitudinal temperature gradient evidence
- North Carolina old field (36°N, 75 m²): 23 species (Bever et al. 1995)
- Costa Rica dry forest (10°N, 20 km²): 25 species (Johnson and Wedin 1997)

These nearly equivalent richness values at very different latitudes suggest that AM fungal diversity may not follow the classic latitudinal diversity gradient observed in many other organism groups.

### Polar and Subpolar Regions

- **Antarctic Peninsula**: *Deschampsia antarctica* associated with at least one AM species on the Danko coast (Cabello et al. 1994), but no AM detected on Anvers Island (DeMars and Boerner 1995)
- **Greenland and Northwest Territories**: virtually unexplored; many plant species are nonmycorrhizal (Bledsoe et al. 1990; Kohn and Stasovski 1990)
- **Alaska glacial front**: mycorrhizal associations based on sporulation of *Glomus etunicatum* and *Archaeospora trappei* in trap cultures; 32 species recovered from 400 m² using systematic methods

## Sporulation Dynamics and Sampling Considerations

Sporulation frequently correlates with host phenology — maximum spore production often occurs near the middle to end of a growing season. However, abiotic factors (especially [[mushroom-growing-temperature-and-moisture]]) strongly influence sporulation patterns.

Different fungal species can have unique seasonal patterns, meaning samples from a single date may not accurately assess fungal community composition. Monthly sampling over 1–2 years is most desirable, though quarterly sampling may be more practical.

In sites of low species richness, a great number of samples must be collected to validate preliminary observations. Trap cultures are essential to search for nonsporulating species, especially in arid habitats or saturated soils.
