# Faires Mushroom Growing and Foraging

While the Faires guide does not provide detailed mycological cultivation methods, it references mushrooms and fungi in several contexts throughout the book, highlighting their role in permaculture systems as food, medicine, soil builders, and ecological partners. Fungi play a central role in the cycles of life within any [[natural-farm-permaculture-ethics-principles]] system.

## Mushrooms in the Permaculture Garden

### Pond Ecosystem Mushrooms

Water features in the permaculture landscape serve as mushroom habitat. A well-designed pond or dam can grow:

- Water chestnuts, rice, bait fish, brine shrimp, snails, aquarium fish, water lilies
- Basketry materials such as reeds and rushes
- **Mushrooms** at the moist edges
- Crayfish, prawns, mussels, and clams

The pond edge — where water meets land — provides the constant moisture and organic matter that many mushroom species need. This is a natural application of the [[permaculture-zones-and-sectors]].

### Indoor Mushroom Growing

The guide mentions two simple methods for growing mushrooms without specialized equipment:

**Cupboard mushrooms:** A dark cupboard in the kitchen can serve as a growing space for mushrooms. This is Zone 0 growing — so small and intensive that the built environment itself determines productivity. The cupboard provides darkness, stable temperature, and proximity for daily monitoring.

**Sack of mushrooms in a dark place:** For those with very little space, a sack of inoculated substrate placed in any dark location can produce mushrooms. This is one of the simplest possible mushroom cultivation methods, requiring no more than a container, substrate, and spawn.

## Fungi in Soil Systems

### Hugelkultur and Fungi

Fungi are a critical component of [[faires-hugelkultur-and-forest-garden-strategy]] beds. When wood is buried in the ground to create raised beds:

- Fungi naturally colonize the decomposing wood
- They speed the decomposition process
- They help make nutrients available to plants growing above
- The [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] is enhanced by fungal networks connecting the wood to the surrounding soil

The guide identifies fungi as "the other key component" alongside urine for breaking down fresh wood in hugelkultur beds. Without fungi, the wood decomposition would be much slower and less efficient.

### Forest Ecosystem Fungi

The [[faires-mimicking-forests]] strategy explicitly references the role of fungi in tree-root relationships:

- Tree roots have fungi that benefit the soil
- These mycorrhizal relationships are essential to the self-sustaining nature of forest garden systems
- The massive biomass of trees affects everything around them partly through their fungal partnerships

### Disease Fungi

The guide also warns about pathogenic fungi:

**Silver leaf (Chondrostereum purpureum):** A fungal disease that attacks most deciduous trees. Recognizable by the silvery sheen on leaves when leaf cells are damaged. Spreads between species through bark wounds. Prevention through smart pruning strategies is more effective than treatment.

**Ergot fungus:** Can infect grain crops if grain gets damp, turning it hard, black, and purple inside. Never eat moldy grain or feed it to animals. If desperate, soak grain in very salty water — the ergot masses (sclerotia) will float and can be skimmed off.

**Peach leaf curl:** A fungus that turns almond and peach leaves brown and curled. Remove infected leaves and burn them.

## Chicken of the Woods

The guide specifically mentions **Chicken of the Woods** (Laetiporus sulphureus) as an edible fungus that plays an active role in the garden. This bracket fungus:

- Grows on dead and dying trees, particularly oaks
- Is edible when young and fresh (the texture resembles chicken, hence the name)
- Contributes to the decomposition of dead wood, cycling nutrients back into the system
- Represents the active role that fungi play in garden ecology beyond cultivation

## Fungi and Plant Health

### Soil Biology

The guide's discussion of soil management emphasizes the role of fungi as part of the beneficial soil ecosystem:

- Fungi and bacteria play a central role in the cycles of life in the soil
- Encouraging worms and other beneficial creatures (including fungi) is key to soil health
- The best cultivators of soil are the organisms living within it
- [[faires-soil-management-and-sheet-mulching]] strategies should support fungal networks

### Forest Garden Integration

In warm, humid climates where plants are spaced closer together, fungus problems can arise if airflow is insufficient. This is a design consideration for [[mollison-designers-temperate-zone-food-forest-design]] plantings:

- Maintain adequate spacing in humid climates
- Ensure airflow through the forest garden layers
- Monitor for fungal disease, especially in dense plantings
- Use fungi-aware species selection for humid zones

## Using Mushrooms as a Food Analog

The guide uses mushrooms as a reference point for other edible plants:

- **Daylily flowers:** Cut off the base with the ovary and use "like mushrooms" in stir-fry dishes
- **Moringa flowers:** Harvested and cooked, used "like mushrooms" in dishes
- This comparison indicates that mushrooms serve as a familiar culinary reference — their texture, umami flavor, and cooking applications make them a useful analog for introducing new foods

## Asparagus Bed Preparation

Traditional asparagus cultivation uses composted manure or mushrooms at the bottom of the planting trench. This references the spent mushroom substrate (mushroom compost) that is a byproduct of commercial mushroom growing — rich in organic matter and beneficial microorganisms. After [[natural-farm-composting-vermicomposting]], this material provides excellent bed preparation for demanding crops like asparagus.

## Design Integration

Mushrooms and fungi integrate into the permaculture landscape at multiple levels:

- **Zone 0:** Indoor cultivation in cupboards and dark spaces
- **Zone 1:** Kitchen garden edges, compost areas
- **Zone 2:** Pond edges, orchard understory
- **Zone 3:** Forest garden floor, hugelkultur beds
- **Zone 4-5:** Wild foraging, forest mushroom hunting

The presence of mushrooms in the system is generally an indicator of healthy soil ecology. Their mycelial networks connect plants, distribute nutrients, and build soil structure. Designing for fungal health means providing moisture, organic matter, and minimal soil disturbance.
