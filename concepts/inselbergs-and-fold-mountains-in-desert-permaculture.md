# Inselbergs and Fold Mountains in Desert Permaculture

Inselbergs and fold mountains are two of the most significant large-scale landforms in desert landscapes. While less complex than [[humus]] and can support trees, yams, and other vegetation. Occasionally, steep and shaded clefts present sites for rock dams, but for the most part, partly sealed tanks and infiltration systems must suffice.

### Scale of Occupancy

The larger systems (100 hectares or more) have often been occupied in historical times by careful and conservative peoples, usually as base camps for long-range foraging after rains. Each inselberg has a special character, and very sensitive and restricted occupancy is called for — developing and protecting natural resources rather than attempting extensive systems.

Very small rock slabs (1-10 hectares) present a useful area for a gutter and tank system, a hectare or two of trees, and a wayside house. Many have been guttered using rock walls, slab on edge, or concreted drains to serve the needs of houses or even steam trains for water in remote locations. Many would support a wildlife ramp-tank for desert birds and small mammals.

### Forest Regeneration

Where grazing animals such as camels and goats have been removed or controlled, as in parts of South Australia, dense Callitris pine forests have regenerated at the bases of these formations, demonstrating the productive potential that exists when browsing pressure is reduced.

## Fold Mountains

### Geological Characteristics

Fold mountains are the most extensive features in many deserts and combine characteristics of both inselbergs and scarps, but are generally far more complex than either. They form through tectonic processes where continental drift creates fold series, often inside coastal ranges.

Two primary structural features arise from folding:

- **Synclines**: Down-flexed sediments that may erode to great canoe shapes, with a few river gorges cutting their way out.
- **Anticlines**: May form great whalebacks, but over geological time, rivers form on their spines and produce long valleys in the hills, faced on each side by cliffs or steep tilted sediments.

Eventually, typical basin and range topography evolves from folded series, with alternating basins and ridgelines.
