# PF Tek Contamination Prevention and Troubleshooting

## Overview

Contamination by competing microorganisms is the primary challenge in amateur [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]. Bacteria, molds, and yeasts compete with the desired [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]] for substrate nutrients and can entirely colonize a jar before the mushroom mycelium becomes established. The PF TEK addressed contamination through a multi-layered prevention strategy involving [[gartz-kh2po4-phosphate-substrate-design-psilocybe-cubensis-cultivation]], sterilization, sterile [[inoculation-technique-and-alcohol-flaming]], and [[chen-maitake-growth-parameters-environmental-control]] during colonization and fruiting.

## Common Contaminants

### Mold Contaminants

Molds are the most frequently encountered contaminants [[pasteurization-in-mushroom-cultivation]]:

- **Trichoderma (Green Mold)**: The most common and aggressive contaminant. Produces a rapidly spreading green carpet of spores. Often indicates insufficient sterilization or contaminated inoculation equipment. Once established, Trichoderma releases massive quantities of spores that can contaminate nearby jars.
- **Aspergillus (Black/Green/Yellow Mold)**: Common environmental mold that produces dark-colored colonies. Some *Aspergillus* species produce mycotoxins that can be harmful if inhaled. Contaminated jars should be discarded without opening.
- **Penicillium (Blue-Green Mold)**: Produces a powdery blue-green growth. Less aggressive than Trichoderma but can outcompete slow-growing mushroom mycelium.
- **Rhizopus (Bread Mold)**: Fast-growing, fuzzy white mold that quickly turns dark as it produces sporangia. Common on substrates that are too wet.
- **Mucor**: Similar to Rhizopus, producing rapidly spreading, cottony white growth.

### Bacterial Contaminants

Bacterial contamination is often less visible but equally damaging:

- **Wet spot (Bacillus)**: Produces a slimy, foul-smelling area in the substrate, often appearing as a yellowish or brownish wet patch. Indicates that sterilization was insufficient to kill bacterial endospores.
- **Pseudomonas**: Produces a sour smell and slimy texture. Common in substrates with excessive moisture.
- **General bacteria**: Bacterial contamination often manifests as slowed or stalled mycelial growth, sour or rotting odors, and a slimy substrate texture.

### Yeast Contaminants

Yeasts are less common but can cause problems:

- Usually appear as a powdery or creamy growth on the substrate surface
- Often associated with contaminated spore syringes
- Produce a yeasty or fermented smell

## Contaminant Source Identification

The PF TEK emphasized identifying the source of contamination to prevent recurrence:

### Sources of Contamination

1. **[[mushroom-spore-inoculation-technique]]**: The most common source. Introducing the spore syringe needle through non-sterile air, touching the needle to non-sterile surfaces, or using a contaminated syringe.
2. **Spore syringe**: Commercial or homemade syringes may contain bacterial or fungal contaminants. Using syringes from reputable suppliers and inspecting them before use is essential.
3. **Substrate ingredients**: Brown rice powder or vermiculite may carry contaminant spores, though this is less common if ingredients are fresh and properly stored.
4. **Water**: Tap water may contain chlorine-resistant bacteria or other microorganisms. Distilled water is preferred.
5. **Jars and lids**: Improperly cleaned jars or lids with residual organic matter can harbor contaminants.
6. **Air exposure**: Opening jars during [[jar-lid-management-sterilization-cooling-vacuum-prevention]], colonization, or fruiting exposes the substrate to airborne spores.
7. **Environment**: Cultivation areas with high mold spore counts (damp basements, areas with houseplants) increase contamination risk.

## Sterile Inoculation Technique

### The Alcohol Flaming Technique

The PF TEK described the alcohol flaming technique as the primary method for maintaining needle sterility during inoculation:

1. Prepare a small alcohol lamp or candle
2. Shake the spore syringe to distribute spores evenly
3. Heat the needle until it glows red in the alcohol flame
4. Allow the needle to cool for 15–30 seconds (touching a sterile surface briefly to test temperature)
5. Insert the needle through the inoculation hole in the jar lid
6. Dispense spore solution against the inside glass wall of the jar (not directly into the substrate)
7. Withdraw the needle and re-flame before moving to the next jar

### Key Principles

- **[[flammability-hazard-alcohol-lysol-flame-sterilization-mycology]]** between each jar is the single most important contamination prevention step
- **Work quickly** after flaming to minimize airborne contamination exposure
- **Work in a still-air environment**: Turn off fans and air conditioning, close windows, avoid breathing directly over the inoculation site
- **Do not touch the needle** to any non-sterile surface after flaming
- **Inoculation depth**: The needle should reach into the jar to deposit spores near the substrate (not just at the surface)
- **Amount**: Typically 1–2 cc of spore solution per jar, distributed across 2–4 inoculation points

### Inoculation Without Lids

The PF TEK also described a method for inoculating jars without lids, using the dry vermiculite barrier as the primary contamination filter:

- The jar is opened briefly in a still-air environment
- Spore solution is dripped onto the dry vermiculite layer
- The spores migrate through the vermiculite to reach the moist substrate below
- This method is simpler but carries higher contamination risk than the through-lid method
