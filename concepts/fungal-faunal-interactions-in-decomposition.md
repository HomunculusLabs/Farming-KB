# Fungal-Faunal Interactions in Decomposition

The decomposition of organic matter is a collaborative process involving fungi, bacteria, and soil fauna whose interactions generate complex feedback loops that determine decomposition rates, [[nutrient-mineralization]], and soil fertility. Soil animals — particularly fungivorous microarthropods, nematodes, and earthworms — influence decomposition both directly through consumption of fungal biomass and indirectly through physical modification of substrates and alteration of fungal community composition.

## The Faunal Decomposer Food Web

Soil fauna participate in decomposition through multiple pathways:

1. **Comminution**: Physical breakdown of litter into smaller particles, increasing surface area for microbial colonization
2. **Grazing**: Selective consumption of fungi, regulating fungal biomass and community structure
3. **Gut passage**: Transport of fungal propagules and modification of organic matter during digestion
4. **Enzyme stimulation**: Enhancement of microbial activity through nutrient recycling in fecal deposits

These activities link fungal decomposer communities to higher trophic levels while simultaneously modifying the conditions under which decomposition proceeds.

## Direct Effects of Faunal Grazing on Decomposition

### Species Complexity and Decomposition Efficiency

Decomposition rates are paradoxically reduced as the number of fungal species competing for a resource increases. This occurs because the metabolic activity of competing fungi exceeds that of an equivalent biomass of a single species (Wicklow and Yocum, 1982; Robinson et al., 1993). Selective grazing by soil microarthropods typically reduces fungal species diversity, which can increase overall decomposition efficiency.

Lussenhop and Wicklow (1985) demonstrated this effect using rabbit dung as a substrate. As saprotrophic fungal species complexity increased, decomposition decreased and spore production declined. However, when they introduced the mycophagous fly larvae [[lycoriella-mali]], decomposition increased by 10% at high fungal diversity, and spore production surged by 1500%. Three mechanisms were proposed:

1. Larvae may directly compete with fungi for water-soluble compounds, intensifying as fungal interaction complexity increases
2. Larval grazing on mycelia may slow hyphal growth enough to reduce competitive interactions, allowing fungi to invest more resources in decomposition
3. Larvae may concentrate enzymes as the number of fungal species increases

### Nitrogen Mineralization and Immobilization

The balance between nitrogen mineralization and immobilization is strongly influenced by faunal activity. Beare et al. (1992) showed that removal of [[soil-arthropods]] reduced leaf litter decomposition by 5% in agricultural plots. The increase in fungal biomass resulting from grazing relief was correlated with 25% higher nitrogen retention compared to plots with intact faunal populations. Approximately 85% of the net immobilized nitrogen was associated with the saprotrophic fungal community.

This finding has profound implications: while fungal biomass may increase without grazing pressure, nutrient availability to plants actually decreases because nitrogen becomes locked in fungal tissue. The activities of soil fauna thus promote greater nutrient mineralization and soil fertility by maintaining microbial turnover rates.



### Nematode-Fungal Interactions in Decomposition

Chen and Ferris (1999) demonstrated that fungal-feeding nematodes increased decomposition of alfalfa residues and cellulose. When residues were colonized by the nematode-preferred fungus [[rhizoctonia-solani]], both nematode populations and nitrogen mineralization were significantly higher than when the less-preferred Trichoderma sp. was available. This suggests that nematode feeding stimulates biomass or activity of preferred fungi, translating into enhanced ecosystem function.

In contrast, Coleman et al. (1990) found that in forest ecosystems with high densities of soil fauna, reduction of microbial predators led to increased litter decomposition through grazing relief. The direction of the faunal effect thus appears ecosystem-dependent.



## Indirect Effects Through Substrate Modification



### Litter Comminution and Physical Processing

Anderson and Ineson (1984) showed that isopod activity enhanced leaf litter decomposition by comminuting litter, which increased the surface area available for fungal and bacterial colonization. Similarly, earthworm activity increases litter surface area through gut processing, enhancing saprotrophic fungal access to organic matter.

Ponge (1990, 1991) documented changes in fungal species invading pine needles in concert with faunal invasions during decomposition. While animals may carry specific fungal propagules, the dominant effect appears to be physical alteration of litter physicochemical properties through comminution, which shifts competitive dynamics among fungal species.



### Earthworm Effects on Fungal Communities

Earthworms are selective fungal feeders. Brown (1995) cited Cooke (1983) showing that earthworms select certain fungal species and reject others. Tiwari and Mishra (1993) found greater numbers and diversity of fungi in earthworm casts than in surrounding soil. Salmon and Ponge (2001) showed that earthworm feces attract entomobryid collembola, which feed on the mucus/urine mixture in the feces. The elevated nutrient loading at these sites promotes bacterial and fungal community development, contributing to [[soil-aggregate-formation]] — a process useful for restoring degraded soils (Scullion and Malik, 2000; Görres et al., 2001).



## Top-Down Regulation of Decomposition

The number of fungivorous nematodes in a community is itself regulated by predation. Laakso and Setaälä (1999) demonstrated that a specialist nematode-feeding mite (Parazercon radiatus) reduced both bacterial- and fungal-feeding nematode populations by half, decreasing nitrogen availability in soil. An omnivorous mite (Lysigamasus lapponicus), however, increased fungal-feeding nematode density relative to bacterial feeders, stimulating the microbial community and increasing nitrogen availability. These results demonstrate that "top-down" regulation processes are important drivers of ecosystem-level function.



## Canopy-Level Interactions

Herbivorous invertebrates in plant canopies indirectly affect decomposition processes through honeydew production. Aphids, processing large quantities of phloem sap to obtain nitrogen, excrete excess sugar as honeydew. This resource stimulates bacterial and filamentous fungal growth on leaf surfaces to densities two to three orders of magnitude higher than on control leaves (Stadler and Müller, 1996, 2000). The elevated microbial biomass on leaf surfaces increases nitrogen absorption from throughfall precipitation through microbial immobilization (Stadler et al., 1998; Stadler and Michalzik, 1999), directly affecting nutrient loading of soil beneath the canopy.



## Climate Change Considerations

Arid and semiarid ecosystems are fungal-dominated (Zak, 1993), with soil faunal communities correspondingly dominated by fungivores (Whitford, 1989). Some fungivorous mites can enter cryptobiosis, and nematodes can undergo anhydrobiosis during desiccation, allowing dispersal by wind. Couteaux and Bolger (2000) warn that climate change may alter fungal community composition, which would cascade through fungivore populations with multiple consequences for ecosystem processes including decomposition, nutrient cycling, and soil formation.

The interactive effects of climate change on fungal communities, their faunal grazers, and the resulting ecosystem processes remain poorly understood and represent an important frontier in decomposition ecology.
