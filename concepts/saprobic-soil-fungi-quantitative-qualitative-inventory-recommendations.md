# Saprobic Soil Fungi — Quantitative and Qualitative Inventory Recommendations

## Overview

Systematic inventory of [[saprobic-soil-fungi]] requires standardized protocols that balance sampling intensity with practical constraints. The Wisconsin survey program (Christensen 1969; Gochenaur and Woodall 1974) established a model framework for recognizing principal species and comparing diversity patterns among soil fungal communities. This page summarizes recommended procedures for both quantitative and qualitative inventories, including the complete workflow from site selection through data analysis.

## Quantitative Inventories

### Design Philosophy

Quantitative inventories aim to characterize the principal saprobic species at a site and enable statistically meaningful comparisons among sites, treatments, or environmental gradients. The approach is less isolate-intensive than qualitative surveys but demands rigor in standardization of sampling, isolation, and enumeration procedures.

A less isolate-intensive approach is appropriate when the primary interest lies in the initial segment of the species-abundance curve and the relative abundance of the 10–40 most common species. Those species are recognized most easily, and their responses to environmental or successional variables can be monitored most readily.

### Workflow Organization

The recommended workflow (Figure 13.4 in the source) proceeds through sequential stages:

1. **Selection of sites, collection of soils, habitat characterization** — guided by prior vegetation analysis and environmental gradients
2. **Analysis and preparation of soils** — drying, sieving, and suspension preparation
3. **Preparation of isolation plates, colony selection** — standardized plating with predetermined colony targets
4. **Sorting and counting of isolates** — morphological grouping on uniform agar slants
5. **Selection of unique or representative strains** — reduction of redundancy
6. **Identification in Petri dishes** — microscopic examination and taxonomic assignment
7. **Growth and purification for preservation of isolates** — transfer to fresh media
8. **Preservation and storage of representative strains** — lyophilization, ultra-cold freezing
9. **Data tabulation in electronic spreadsheet** — frequencies and relative densities
10. **Secondary analysis of sterile isolates** — physiological tests, molecular methods
11. **Analyses and reporting** — diversity indices, ordination, multivariate analysis
12. **Specimen database** — accession numbers, voucher documentation

### Step-by-Step Protocol

**Step 1 — Sampling intensity:** Use 6, 8, 10, or 20 soil samples per site so that the prominence of a species can be assessed quantitatively. Each sample of 5–20 g of soil is mixed thoroughly prior to preparation.

**Step 2 — Colony targets:** Isolate a standard number of colonies per soil sample — minimum 25–30 from temperate forest soils and 30–40 from temperate grassland soils, where densities for top-ranking species are often lower. Sampling should be repeated during several seasons to measure seasonal variation.

**Step 3 — Medium selection:** Use a standard, low-nutrient medium such as soil-extract agar (SEA) made with soil from the area under study, Gochenaur's glucose ammonium nitrate agar (GAN), carboxymethyl [[cellulose]] agar (CMC), or dilute [[malt-extract-agar]] with antibiotics. After a primary medium is selected, experimental trials determine the soil/water ratios yielding 5–30 colonies per plate and the medium/antibiotic combination that eliminates bacteria while maximizing fungal taxa.

**Step 4 — Incubation:** Incubate surface-inoculated plates at 15–20°C or at in situ [[soil-temperature]]; if a cooling incubator is unavailable, incubate at room temperature for 4–14 days or longer.

**Step 5 — Colony isolation:** Isolate all colonies by hyphal tip dissection in sequence until the predetermined "representative sample" has been obtained. For a target of 40 isolates per site, cut 42–44 tiny blocks of hyphal tips (allowing for unsuccessful transfers) and place each on an identical slant of nutrient agar (e.g., malt-yeast extract agar or PDA).

**Step 6 — Sorting:** After incubation for 5–10 days, sort equal-aged, identically grown cultures into apparent taxonomic entities based on appearance. Assign permanent culture numbers to representatives of each taxon and tabulate occurrences in an electronic spreadsheet.

**Step 7 — Identification and calculation:** After fungi are identified, combine or separate occurrences as appropriate. Calculate frequency (F) and relative density (RD) for all taxa on the final list.

### Key Quantitative Metrics

- **Frequency (F):** percentage of sites at which a species occurred

  F = (no. sites of occurrence / total no. sites sampled) × 100

- **Relative Density (RD):** percentage of total isolates contributed by a given species

  RD = (no. isolates of species / total no. isolates) × 100

These metrics were a key component of the Wisconsin studies (Christensen 1969; Gochenaur and Woodall 1974). They allow compilations of species ordered by relative commonness and comparisons among surveys despite differences in numbers of sites sampled and isolates examined.

Quantitative data enable:
- Depictions of equitability and diversity
- Quantitative prominence of single species relative to other variables
- Coefficients of similarity among communities (presence/absence or quantitative)
- Multivariate analyses correlating fungal composition with biotic and abiotic variables among sites or treatments

## Qualitative Inventories

### Goals and Scope

Qualitative inventories aim to produce the maximum species list per site, extending the inventory well beyond the principal species captured by quantitative protocols. They are less isolate-intensive overall but employ a wider array of techniques and media.

### Two-Phase Approach

A two-phase approach is recommended:

1. **Quantitative phase:** First identify the principal species using standardized protocols
2. **Qualitative phase:** Then apply diverse specialized techniques to extend the species list

### Specialized Techniques for Extending Species Lists

Qualitative inventories draw on a broader toolkit:

- **Selective media:** ethanol pasteurization of soil, benomyl-containing media for basidiomycetes, pimaricin-containing media for oomycetes
- **Varied incubation regimes:** low temperature (0–10°C) for psychrophiles and slow-growers; high temperature (40–55°C) for thermotolerant and thermophilic species
- **Particle filtration:** different particle size fractions to target species with specific microhabitat preferences
- **Baiting techniques:** organic substrata (cellulose, keratin, pollen, chitin) to attract specific functional groups
- **Direct plating:** Drechsler's method for microcosm creation; Warcup soil plates for basidiomycetes
- **Density-gradient centrifugation:** Percoll or sucrose gradients for separating propagule types

### Complementary Nature

Quantitative and qualitative approaches are complementary. The quantitative phase provides the statistical framework and community characterization, while the qualitative phase maximizes species discovery. Together they yield the most complete picture of [[soil-fungal-diversity]] at a site.

## Practical Considerations

### Managing Workload

The maximum number of isolates manageable by two full-time, experienced researchers at a single time in a well-equipped laboratory is approximately 2000–3000. This estimate assumes familiarity with a high proportion of genera and species. During the design phase, the number of soils, horizons, and isolation protocols must be balanced against resources available for media preparation, plate vigilance, identification, and culture preservation.

### Species-Isolate Curves

Species-abundance curves and species-isolate curves are the mycological analogues of species-area curves in plant ecology. By conservatively sorting isolates into morphological types and ranking them by decreasing abundance, one can rapidly foresee the complexity of a full-scale survey. Asymptotes for species-isolate curves range from about 60 to 150 species for 600–1000 isolates, but new species continue to be encountered indefinitely.

### Minimum Sampling Standards

- **For majority species:** minimum 600–1000 isolates per site using suspension plating or particle filtration
- **For quantitative comparison:** 180–250 isolates per site provide repeatable data for principal species
- **For common taxa determination:** 200–250 strains from 6–10 separate samples or a composite sample
- **For frequency/density metrics:** standard number of colonies per sample (25–40 depending on habitat)
