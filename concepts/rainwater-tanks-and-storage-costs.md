# Rainwater Tanks and Water Storage Costs

Rainwater harvesting and water storage are foundational elements of [[permaculture-design]].
Choosing the right storage method depends on site conditions, budget, and water needs.
This concept covers storage options ranked by cost, tank construction details, water [[rainwater-tanks-and-water-quality|quality]]
management, and the role of swales in landscape-scale water retention.

## Storage Methods: A Cost Hierarchy

Water storage costs vary enormously between methods. Understanding this hierarchy is essential
for making effective design decisions that maximise stored water per unit of investment.

- **Soil and humus water storage** — cheapest option by far and stores the greatest volume
- **Surface dams** — next cheapest, excellent for country sites with suitable topography
- **Above-ground tanks** — more expensive than dams but far cheaper than mains supply
- **Piped mains water** — the most expensive option over the long term

### Cost Comparison: Tanks vs. Earth Storage

The cost differential between tank storage and earth storage is dramatic:

| Storage Type       | Volume per 20 Money Units | Relative Cost |
|--------------------|--------------------------:|---------------|
| Concrete tank      | 22,500 L                  | 1× (baseline) |
| Earth storage      | 2,500,000 L               | 1/100×        |

Earth storage provides roughly **100 times** the volume for the same investment as a
concrete tank. This single comparison underscores why [[permaculture]] design prioritises
soil-based and earthworks storage wherever site conditions permit.

## Tank Types and Sizing

### Pre-manufactured Tanks

- Tanks below **22,500 litres** are typically carted from central manufacturing facilities
- These smaller units are practical for urban and suburban settings
- 22,500 L is a key threshold — it provides a family with **all needed water for one year**
- Every roof in most climates would fill many such tanks from rainfall alone

### On-site Poured Tanks

- Tanks above **22,500 litres** are usually poured on site using simple reusable forms
- Simple concrete tank forms cost only a few hundred dollars and can be reused hundreds
  of times, making them extremely economical over their lifespan
- Earth tanks excavated below grade cost approximately **one-tenth** the price of concrete
  tanks built above grade — a critical consideration for budget-conscious designs

## Dams: Siting and Cost Factors

Dams occupy the middle ground between cheap earth storage and expensive tank storage,
but their costs scale with engineering requirements.

- Dams begin to cost significantly more as **wall height increases**
- About **3 metres of retaining wall** is the practical limit for cheap [[dam-construction]]
- Dam sizes from **22,500 L to 4.5 megalitres** are best suited to good sites in country
  areas with appropriate landform and catchment
- Massive dams are appropriate **hardly anywhere** except rock-bermed or glaciated uplands
  where natural containment reduces construction costs

### Appropriate Contexts for Each Storage Type

- **Tanks** — isolated dwellings, flatlands, cities, and anywhere space is limited
- **Medium dams (22,500 L – 4.5 ML)** — good country sites with suitable topography
- **Massive dams** — rock-bermed or glaciated uplands only
- **Soil and humus storage** — universally applicable and should always be maximised

## Calculating Rainwater Yield

Estimating how much water a roof can harvest is straightforward:

> **Roof area × average rainfall = expected yield**

This formula allows designers to size tanks appropriately for household needs. Combined
with the 22,500 L per-family-per-year benchmark, it provides a reliable basis for
rainwater system planning.

## Water Quality Management

Maintaining water quality in stored rainwater requires attention to several design
details that prevent contamination and support biological purification.

### First Flush Rejection

- The **first flow-off** of water from roofs should always be rejected
- This initial runoff carries dust, debris, and contaminants accumulated on the roof
- Rejected water should be directed to gardens or swales rather than wasted
- Two methods for first flush rejection are commonly employed in permaculture [[mollison-designers-rainwater-harvesting-swale-systems|systems]]

### Exclusion of Pests

Keeping insects, birds, and rodents out of stored water is essential for health:

- **U-pipe entry and exit** — prevents crawling insects and rodents from accessing tank
- **Sealed tank roof** — eliminates bird access and prevents contamination from above
- **Overflow to gravel swale** — safely directs excess water while filtering contaminants

### Biological Film and Tap Placement

- Taps should be fitted **15–20 cm above the tank floor**
- This gap allows a **biological film** to develop on the tank bottom
- The biological film acts as a living filter, improving water quality over time
- Drawing water above this layer avoids disturbing the beneficial microbial community

### pH Adjustment and Health Benefits

- A net bag of **limestone, shell, or marble chips** can be suspended inside the tank
- These materials slowly dissolve, creating **alkaline water**
- Alkaline conditions help prevent **heavy metal uptake** from tank materials
- Studies suggest alkaline water consumption is associated with **decreased heart attacks**
- This simple intervention significantly improves the health quality of stored rainwater

## Gutter [[mollison-designers-soil-water-storage-in-forest-systems|Systems]]

Effective gutters are the critical link between roof catchment and storage tanks:

- Gutters must be **cleaned regularly** to prevent debris from entering tanks
- Alternatively, **leaf-free gutter systems** can be fitted to reduce maintenance
- Well-maintained gutters ensure maximum water capture and minimise contamination
- Gutter design should integrate with first flush rejection systems for best results

## Introduction to Swales

Swales are a complementary [[water-management]] strategy that works at the landscape scale,
storing water in soil rather than in containers.

### What Are Swales?

- **Long, level excavations** of varying width across the landscape contour
- Designed as **cross-flow dry channels** that intercept overland water flow
- Water is held in swales for **hours to days**, allowing infiltration into the soil
- The primary purpose is **groundwater recharge** — storing water in underlying soils

### Design Principles

- Swales should **not exceed the width of the crown spread** of fringing trees
- **Trees are essential components** of any swale system
- Without trees, swales risk causing **waterlogging** and potentially creating a
  **local rainfall deficit** due to excessive evaporation from saturated bare soil
- Trees transpire stored groundwater, cycling water back through the local hydrology
