---# Predator-Prey Relationships and Herding Behavior

In . Savory argues that
the removal of predators from ecosystems has been a primary driver of
[[desertification]], as it eliminated the natural soil disturbance that herding
animals once provided under threat of predation.

## The Discovery

Savory's university training taught him, as it did all scientists of that
era, that large animals such as domestic cattle could damage land. Only
keeping numbers low and scattering stock widely would prevent destructive
trampling and intense grazing. His field observations as a biologist in
Africa led him to the opposite conclusion: relatively high numbers of
heavy, herding animals, concentrated and moving as they naturally do in the
presence of predators, support the health of the very lands people thought
they destroyed.

## Tracking Revealed the Pattern

During years of tracking in Zimbabwe, Savory covered game areas, tribal
areas, European farms and ranches, and all different soil and vegetation
types. He realized that vast differences distinguished land where wildlife
herded naturally, where people herded domestic stock, and where stock was
fenced and not herded at all.

Most obvious was that where animals were present, plants were green and
growing. In areas without animals they were often grey and dying unless
they had been burned. The degree of disturbance had a proportionately
positive impact on the health of plants and soils.

## How Herding Animals Behave

Savory distinguished two fundamentally different behavior patterns in
herding animals:

When feeding, buffalo and similar herding animals tended to spread out,
although not too far for fear of predation, and to walk gently and slowly.
They placed their hooves beside coarse plants and not on top of them. They
also placed their full weight on their hooves, compacting the soil below
the surface but hardly disturbing the surface itself. While feeding they
had remarkably little impact on the plants and soil.

Once feeding was over and the herd began to move, or when predators
threatened, the animals behaved completely differently. They bunched
together and in their excitement kicked up dust. While bunched, each
animal stepped recklessly, and even very coarse plants were trampled down,
providing cover for the soil surface. The trotting and galloping hooves
left the soil chipped and broken as if a gardener had hoed it.

## The Dual Function of Herding Behavior

Savory became convinced that the disturbance created by hooves of herding
game populations was vital to the health of the land, and that mankind had
lost this benefit when domestic animals were protected from predators. Two
functions appear critical:

1. Creating necessary soil disturbance to provide a good seed bed for new
   plants
2. Protecting bare soil by trampling down old plant material
