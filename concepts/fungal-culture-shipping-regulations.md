## Fungal Culture Shipping Regulations and Biosafety

The transport of fungal cultures across domestic and international borders is subject to a complex framework of biosafety classifications, packaging standards, and regulatory requirements. These regulations exist to prevent the accidental release of pathogenic organisms into the environment and to ensure the safe handling of biological materials throughout the entire shipping chain, from the originating laboratory to the receiving facility.

Compliance is not optional — failure to adhere to applicable regulations can result in the seizure and destruction of biological materials, significant civil and criminal penalties, revocation of institutional shipping privileges, and reputational harm. All personnel involved in the packaging, documentation, and shipment of fungal cultures should receive appropriate training and stay current with regulatory changes.

## USPHS Biosafety Levels

The United States Public Health Service (USPHS) classifies etiological agents into four biosafety levels based on their potential to cause human or animal disease, the severity of those diseases, and the availability of effective treatment and preventive measures:

- **Biosafety Level 1 (BSL-1):** Agents that are not known to consistently cause disease in healthy adult humans and pose minimal hazard to laboratory personnel and the environment. Many common environmental and saprophytic fungi, including most molds encountered in indoor air quality investigations, fall into this category. Basic microbiological practices and no special containment equipment are required.

- **Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2):** Agents associated with human disease of moderate severity, where hazard may be present from percutaneous injury, ingestion, or mucous membrane exposure. Several medically important dimorphic fungal pathogens, including *Blastomyces dermatitidis*, *Histoplasma capsulatum*, *Coccidioides immitis*, and *Coccidioides posadasii*, are classified at BSL-2. Laboratory practices require biological safety cabinets, limited access, and biohazard signage.

- **Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3):** Indigenous or exotic agents with potential for aerosol transmission and that may cause serious or potentially lethal disease. Certain highly virulent fungal strains and drug-resistant isolates may require BSL-3 containment. Facilities must maintain negative air pressure, HEPA-filtered exhaust, and controlled access protocols.

- **Biosafety Level 4 (BSL-4):** Dangerous and exotic agents with a high risk of life-threatening disease for which no vaccine or treatment is generally available. No fungal organisms are currently classified at BSL-4, though this level exists for the most hazardous viral and prion agents.

The USPHS also maintains a list of select agents and toxins under the Public Health Security and Bioterrorism Preparedness and Response Act of 2002. Possession, use, and transfer of select agents require registration with the Federal Select Agent Program (FSAP), a joint program of the CDC and USDA-APHIS.

## APHIS/USDA Regulations for Plant Pathogens

The Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) of the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) maintains separate regulatory oversight for fungal plant pathogens. While the USPHS framework addresses human and animal health risks, APHIS focuses on preventing the introduction and spread of plant diseases that could devastate agricultural crops, forests, rangelands, and native plant ecosystems.

Organisms that pose a threat to agriculture or natural ecosystems require USDA permits for importation, interstate movement, and in some cases even intrastate transport. The USDA Regulated Plant Pest list includes numerous fungal pathogens:

- *Puccinia graminis* (wheat stem rust) and its various formae speciales
- *Ceratocystis fagacearum* (oak wilt fungus)
- *Ophiostoma novo-ulmi* and *Ophiostoma ulmi* (Dutch elm disease agents)
- *Cryphonectria parasitica* (chestnut blight)
- *Phytophthora ramorum* (sudden oak death pathogen)
- *Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp. cubense (Panama disease of banana, tropical race 4)

The USDA may require that cultures be shipped under specific containment conditions and that recipients hold appropriate facilities, expertise, and permits. APHIS inspectors examine shipments at ports of entry and may seize, treat, or destroy non-compliant materials. Shippers must also comply with state-level agricultural regulations, which can be more restrictive than federal requirements.

## Canadian Risk Groups and Containment Levels

Health Canada and the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) jointly regulate the import and transport of fungal cultures. Canada employs a risk group (RG) system that is analogous to but distinct from the US biosafety level framework:

- **Risk Group 1 (RG1):** Low individual and community risk; organisms unlikely to cause disease in healthy humans or animals. The majority of environmental and saprophytic fungi are classified here and can generally be handled with standard microbiological practices.

- **Risk Group 2 (RG2):** Moderate individual risk, low community risk; organisms that can cause disease but for which effective treatment and preventive measures are available. Many opportunistic fungal pathogens fall into this group.

- **Risk Group 3 (RG3):** High individual risk, low community risk; organisms that pose a serious disease risk with available treatment but potential for severe consequences. These require enhanced containment and specialized laboratory facilities.

- **Risk Group 4 (RG4):** High individual and community risk; organisms that cause serious or fatal disease with no available treatment or preventive measures. No known fungi are currently assigned to this group.

Containment levels (CL1 through CL4) correspond directly to these risk groups and specify physical and operational requirements for laboratories handling organisms at each level. Importers of fungal cultures into Canada must obtain permits under the Human Pathogens and Toxins Act (HPTA) for human pathogens or the Plant Protection Act for plant-associated fungi, depending on the nature of the organism being imported.

## European Federation of Biotechnology Risk Classifications

The European Federation of Biotechnology (EFB) provides a risk classification system widely adopted across European Union member states. The EFB framework categorizes biological agents into four groups based on infection risk:

- **Group 1:** No or very low risk to individuals and the community; unlikely to cause human disease.
- **Group 2:** Moderate risk to individuals, low risk to the community; can cause human disease but effective treatment and prophylaxis exist.
- **Group 3:** High risk to individuals, low risk to the community; can cause severe disease with potential for serious consequences, though effective treatment may exist.
- **Group 4:** High risk to individuals and the community; can cause severe, life-threatening disease for which no effective treatment or prophylaxis is available.

EU Directive 2000/54/EC on the protection of workers from risks related to exposure to biological agents at work incorporates these classifications and mandates appropriate containment measures, risk assessment, worker notification, and record-keeping. For mycological collections operating within or shipping to the EU, compliance with both the EFB classification system and the specific implementing legislation of the destination member state is required. Each member state may impose additional requirements beyond the EU baseline, and some countries maintain national lists of regulated organisms.

## USPS and Freight Carrier Shipping Requirements

Domestic shipment of fungal cultures within the United States is regulated under Title 42 of the Code of Federal Regulations (42 CFR Part 72), which governs the interstate shipment of etiological agents. The United States Postal Service (USPS) accepts packages containing biological materials only when they are properly packaged according to DOT specifications, correctly labeled with the appropriate hazard class markings, and accompanied by complete documentation including a shipper"s declaration of dangerous goods.

The USPS requires that biological specimens be shipped as either Priority Mail or Express Mail services. Packages must not be placed in street collection boxes or regular mail drops — they must be presented in person at a USPS service window or picked up by a postal carrier under a scheduled pickup arrangement.

Private freight carriers including FedEx, UPS, and DHL each maintain their own internal hazardous materials shipping policies that incorporate and in many cases exceed the minimum regulatory requirements. Shipments of etiological agents must use approved carriers and may require advance arrangement or special service agreements. Many carriers require that the shipper provide evidence of dangerous goods training certification before they will accept biological material shipments.

The Department of Transportation (DOT), through its Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA), enforces the Hazardous Materials Regulations (49 CFR Parts 171–180), which implement UN recommendations for all modes of domestic transport. DOT compliance is mandatory for any shipment of infectious substances, regardless of the carrier used. Violations of DOT hazmat regulations carry penalties of up to $75,000 per violation, or $175,000 per violation if the violation results in death, serious illness, or severe property damage.

## IATA International Air Transport Regulations

The International Air Transport Association (IATA) Dangerous Goods Regulations (DGR) govern the international air transport of biological substances and are updated annually. IATA classifies infectious substances into two primary categories:

- **Category A (UN 2814 or UN 2900):** Infectious substances capable of causing permanent disability, life-threatening, or fatal disease in otherwise healthy humans or animals when exposure occurs. Category A substances affecting humans are assigned UN number 2814 (Infectious Substance, Affecting Humans), while those affecting animals only receive UN 2900. Very few fungal organisms meet Category A criteria.

- **Category B (UN 3373):** Infectious substances that do not meet Category A criteria. The vast majority of fungal cultures shipped internationally fall under UN 3373 (Biological Substance, Category B), which has significantly less stringent packaging and documentation requirements compared to Category A.

All personnel involved in preparing and offering shipments of infectious substances by air must hold current IATA dangerous goods training certification, which must be renewed every two years. Training must be function-specific and cover the classification, identification, packaging, marking, labeling, and documentation requirements applicable to the materials being shipped.

## Packaging Requirements

Proper triple-container packaging is mandatory for shipping fungal cultures under all applicable domestic and international regulations. This system, codified in international standards and adopted by all major carriers, consists of three distinct layers, each serving a specific protective function:

1. **Primary Container:** The culture tube, plate, ampoule, or cryovial that directly contains the fungal specimen. It must be leak-proof, watertight, and properly sealed with appropriate closures. For liquid cultures, the container should not be filled beyond its rated capacity to allow for thermal expansion during transit. Screw-cap tubes should be secured with tape or parafilm as an additional safeguard. Slant cultures on agar should be packed in an upright position to prevent the medium from dislodging.

2. **Secondary Container:** A durable, watertight container that encloses and protects the primary container or containers. It must contain sufficient absorbent material (cotton, paper towels, or commercial absorbent pads) to absorb the entire volume of liquid in the event of primary container failure. Multiple primary containers may be placed in a single secondary container if they are individually wrapped or separated by cushioning material to prevent contact and breakage. The secondary container must also be capable of withstanding a pressure differential without leakage.

3. **Outer Shipping Container:** A rigid outer container of adequate strength for its capacity, mass, and intended mode of transport. It must bear the appropriate labels, markings, and shipper and receiver documentation on the outside surface. The outer container should be constructed of corrugated fiberboard, wood, or other rigid material capable of withstanding normal handling stresses. Cushioning material should fill any void space to prevent movement of the secondary container within the outer package.

For Category B (UN 3373) shipments, the complete packaging assembly must be capable of passing a 1.2-meter drop test onto a solid surface. Category A shipments require packaging that has been tested to more rigorous performance standards, including pressure differential tests, vibration tests, and temperature exposure tests as specified in the IATA DGR. Volume limits also apply: primary receptacles for Category A must not exceed 1 liter for liquids or 4 kg for solids, with a total outer package limit of 4 liters or 4 kg. Category B primary receptacles are similarly limited to 1 liter for liquids, with an outer package weight not exceeding 4 kg.

## Dry Ice and Liquid Nitrogen Shipping

Cultures requiring cold chain maintenance may be shipped with dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) or liquid nitrogen to preserve viability during transit. Dry ice is classified as a miscellaneous dangerous good under UN 1845, Class 9, and requires specific labeling including the net weight of dry ice in kilograms on the outer package along with the Class 9 miscellaneous dangerous goods label. Critically, packages containing dry ice must allow for the venting of carbon dioxide gas to prevent pressure buildup that could rupture the container. Outer packaging must never be airtight.

Liquid nitrogen shipments using specialized dry shippers are classified as UN 1977 (Nitrogen, Refrigerated Liquid) under Class 2.2 (Non-flammable, non-toxic gas). However, dry shippers that are specifically designed to absorb and retain liquid nitrogen without containing free liquid during transport are often exempt from many Class 2.2 dangerous goods requirements, provided the shipper design has been tested and certified. Shippers must verify exemption eligibility based on the specific shipper design, carrier policy, and applicable mode-specific regulations. Standard cryogenic dewars containing free liquid nitrogen are prohibited on passenger aircraft and are subject to additional restrictions on cargo aircraft.

## Export Control and Documentation Requirements

International shipments of fungal cultures may be subject to export control regulations that restrict the transfer of certain biological agents to specific countries, entities, or end-users. In the United States, the Export Administration Regulations (EAR) administered by the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) may apply to certain plant and animal pathogens, while the International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR) may apply to biological agents with potential weaponization concerns. A Shipper's Export Declaration (SED) or Electronic Export Information (EEI) filing through the Automated Export System (AES) may be required depending on the classification of the organism, the declared value of the shipment, and the destination country.

Key documentation for international fungal culture shipments includes:

- A completed customs declaration accurately describing the biological material, its quantity, its scientific name and strain designation, and its intended use (research, clinical diagnostics, commercial production, or educational).
- Import permits from the receiving country's competent national authority, which must be obtained in advance by the consignee.
- USDA/APHIS phytosanitary certificates for plant pathogenic fungi, issued after inspection of the material and its packaging.
- Material Transfer Agreements (MTAs) where applicable, particularly for patented strains, deposited type specimens, or biological materials subject to the Convention on Biological Diversity and its Nagoya Protocol on access and benefit-sharing.
- Proper UN classification labels (UN 2814 for Category A, UN 3373 for Category B, or UN 1845 for dry ice) affixed prominently to the outer packaging along with orientation arrows and the shipper's name, address, and telephone number.

## Labeling and Marking Standards

Proper labeling is critical for regulatory compliance and safe handling throughout the shipping chain. All packages containing infectious fungal materials must display the applicable UN number and hazard class markings:

- **UN 2814 (Class 6.2):** Affixed to packages containing Category A infectious substances affecting humans. The label features the standard biohazard symbol with "Infectious Substance, Affecting Humans" text.
- **UN 2900 (Class 6.2):** Used for Category A infectious substances affecting animals only, with corresponding text.
- **UN 3373:** Applied to Category B biological substances. Features a diamond-shaped mark with the biohazard symbol and "Biological Substance, Category B" notation.
- **UN 1845 (Class 9):** Marked on packages containing dry ice, with the net weight of dry ice in kilograms.
- **Orientation arrows:** Required on packages containing liquid cultures to indicate upright positioning during handling.

The CDC also requires that packages containing select agents be marked with the identity of the etiologic agent, the sender's name and address, and the recipient's name and address. All labels must be legible, durable, and positioned so they are not obscured by tape, strapping, or other packaging materials.

Institutions maintaining fungal culture collections should designate specifically trained personnel responsible for regulatory compliance, establish standard operating procedures for all aspects of biological material shipment, and maintain current knowledge of evolving international regulatory requirements through regular training and professional engagement.
