## What Is Root Beer

Root beer is a sweet, carbonated beverage traditionally made from the root bark of the sassafras tree (Sassafras albidum), sarsaparilla (Smilax spp.), and a blend of other herbs and spices. It originated in North America as a folk beverage brewed by Indigenous peoples and European settlers. Commercial root beer became popular in the 19th century, with Charles Hires introducing the first commercial brand at the 1876 Philadelphia Centennial Exposition.

Traditional homemade root beer is a lightly fermented beverage with natural carbonation, distinct from modern commercial root beers which are typically carbonated with pressurized CO2 and may contain artificial flavors.

## Historical Context

Root beer evolved from "small beer" — low-alcohol fermented beverages common in colonial America. These were brewed from locally available roots, barks, and herbs as a safer alternative to often-contaminated water. The primary flavoring, sassafras root, was widely used in Indigenous American medicine and cooking long before European contact.

In 1960, the FDA banned sassafras root and oil as food additives after studies showed safrole (the primary aromatic compound) caused liver cancer in rats. Modern commercial root beers use artificial sassafras flavoring or safrole-free extracts. Home brewers can use safrole-free sassafras extract or alternative roots.

## Key Ingredients

### Sassafras (Safrole-Free)

- Traditional sassafras root bark provides the distinctive root beer flavor — creamy, vanilla-like, and slightly medicinal
- Safrole-free sassafras extract is commercially available and legal for home brewing
- Some home brewers use small amounts of traditional sassafras root bark, understanding the safrole risk — the FDA ban applies to commercial products, not home use
- Sassafras leaves (file powder) do not contain significant safrole and are safe to use

### Sarsaparilla

- Sarsaparilla (Smilax regelii or other Smilax species) provides a similar but distinct flavor — earthier, more bitter, slightly medicinal
- Historically used as a medicinal tonic
- Available as dried root bark from herb suppliers
- Combines well with sassafras for a more complex flavor

### Supporting Herbs and Spices

A traditional root beer recipe includes a blend of:

- Sassafras root bark (or safrole-free extract): primary flavor
- Sarsaparilla root: secondary base flavor
- Sassafras root bark or extract: main flavor note
- Wintergreen ( Gaultheria procumbens): minty, sweet
- Birch bark: wintergreen-like, creamy
- Licorice root: sweet, anise-like, natural sweetness
- Vanilla bean or extract: rounds out the flavor
- Cinnamon bark: warmth and spice
- Nutmeg: warmth and complexity
- Cloves: strong, spicy — use sparingly
- Allspice: warm, aromatic
- Ginger root: heat and spice
- Burdock root: earthy, bitter
- Dandelion root: bitter, roasted-coffee notes
- Anise or star anise: sweet, licorice-like
- Molasses or honey: sweetener and color

## Making Root Beer Extract

Before brewing, prepare a concentrated root beer extract:

1. Combine 2 tablespoons each of dried sassafras (or safrole-free extract), sarsaparilla, licorice root, and burdock root
2. Add 1 cinnamon stick, 1 teaspoon whole cloves, 1 teaspoon allspice berries, 1/2 teaspoon nutmeg, and 1 vanilla bean (split)
3. Add 1 tablespoon dried wintergreen or 1/4 teaspoon wintergreen oil
4. Pour 4 cups of water into a saucepan
5. Bring to a boil, reduce heat, and simmer covered for 20-30 minutes
6. Remove from heat and let steep for 1-2 hours
7. Strain through a fine mesh strainer or cheesecloth — this is your root beer extract

## Brewing Root Beer

### Sweetener

- Cane sugar: 1-2 cups per gallon — provides food for yeast
- Molasses: traditional choice, adds depth and color; combine with sugar
- Honey: can be used but produces thinner body
- Brown sugar: adds caramel notes
- Do not use artificial sweeteners — yeast cannot ferment them

### Yeast

- Champagne yeast (Lalvin EC-1118): clean fermentation, minimal off-flavors, most popular choice
- Ale yeast: works but may produce slight beer-like flavors
- Baker's yeast: works in a pinch but produces bread-like off-flavors
- Ginger beer plant (GBP): natural symbiotic culture; more complex but slower
- Use only 1/8 to 1/4 teaspoon of yeast per gallon — too much creates excessive carbonation

### Process

1. Prepare root beer extract (above) or use commercial root beer extract
2. In a large pot, combine 4 cups of sugar (or sweetener blend) with 1 gallon of filtered water
3. Heat until sugar is fully dissolved — do not boil
4. Remove from heat and cool to 75-80F (24-27C)
5. Add the root beer extract and stir well
6. Dissolve 1/8 teaspoon of champagne yeast in a small amount of warm water (100F) — let bloom for 5 minutes
7. Add the yeast to the root beer mixture and stir gently
8. Siphon or pour into clean, sanitized bottles (glass swing-top or plastic PET bottles)
9. Leave 1-2 inches of headspace in each bottle
10. Cap tightly

### Carbonation and Fermentation

1. Store bottles at room temperature (70-75F / 21-24C) for 24-48 hours
2. Check carbonation by squeezing a plastic bottle or carefully opening a glass bottle
3. Carbonation typically develops in 24-48 hours
4. When the desired carbonation level is reached, refrigerate immediately to halt fermentation
5. Cold storage slows yeast activity and prevents over-carbonation

### Critical Safety Warning

Home-fermented root beer builds pressure in sealed bottles. Over-carbonated bottles can explode.

- Use plastic PET bottles for safety — they bulge visibly when pressurized
- If using glass, burp bottles daily to release pressure
- Never exceed 48 hours at room temperature without checking
- Refrigerate as soon as carbonation is achieved
- Keep bottles in a safe location during carbonation (away from children, in a box or cabinet)

## Bottling

- Glass swing-top bottles: traditional presentation, requires careful carbonation monitoring
- Plastic PET bottles: safest option, visual pressure indicator
- Grolsch-style bottles: good seal, reusable
- Beer bottles with crown caps: requires a capper
- Label bottles with the date and contents

## Storage and Serving

- Refrigerated: drink within 1-2 weeks for best flavor and carbonation
- Room temperature: carbonation continues to build — dangerous in sealed bottles
- Serve chilled over ice
- Root beer floats with vanilla ice cream are a classic serving method
- Unfermented root beer syrup (no yeast) can be stored refrigerated for months and mixed with carbonated water

## Flavor Variations

- Ginger root beer: increase ginger significantly for a spicy ginger beer variant
- Birch beer: emphasize birch bark for a wintergreen-forward flavor
- Cream soda: reduce herbal roots, increase vanilla
- Spiced root beer: add more cinnamon, nutmeg, and allspice
- Sarsaparilla: omit sassafras, increase sarsaparilla root
