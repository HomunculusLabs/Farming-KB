## Chronosequence Approaches

Succession of macrofungi on woody substrata is an important dimension of
biodiversity that can be studied through chronosequence approaches. Large
fallen tree trunks host fungi that fruit early in log decomposition and others
that fruit only later. Heilmann-Clausen (2001) studied a 30-year record of
aerial photographs of a Danish forest to determine when each tree exceeding
60 cm dbh had died, dividing 130 logs into seven age cohorts and revealing
clear successional patterns.

By substituting space for time through careful selection of logs of known ages,
investigators can reconstruct [[biodiversity-fungi-temperate-boreal-forests]].

## Canopy Access and Collection Techniques

A full species list of fungi in a given forest requires collecting many meters
above the ground. Canopy access techniques include semi-permanent platforms
reached by ropes and pulleys, human climbers using spiked foot griffes or foot
straps, and shotguns or rifles used to obtain samples from the upper canopy.
Pike and colleagues (1975) sampled epiphytes from a 450-year-old Douglas fir
by separating the tree into six zones and identified 74 species of agarics and
fleshy fungi from these canopy samples.

Fleshy fungi require a heat source for drying (38-55 degrees C with wire
screen shelves). For polypores, some Corticiaceae, and some ascomycetes,
air-drying is preferable because the fungus is not killed but merely goes
dormant, allowing later isolation of cultures. Large agarics and boletes should
be split in half or quartered before drying to speed the process.

For DNA analysis, subsamples of sporocarp tissue are excised and submerged in
2X CTAB buffer or supersaturated DMSO solution in cryovials. These fixatives
prevent degradation of DNA during long-term storage. Methods for smaller fungi
on wood overlap with those discussed in
[[malt-extract-agar]], though potato
dextrose agar and oatmeal agar are also used. Special agar containing organic
matter from a particular substratum can be used for hard-to-culture species.
Some wood-inhabiting basidiomycetes will fruit on wood-containing media with
sawdust as a base.
