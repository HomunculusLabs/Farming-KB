# [[sequestrate-fungi|Sequestrate Fungi]] — Truffles, False Truffles, and Hypogeous Fungi

Sequestrate fungi produce enclosed fruiting bodies in which spores are
not forcibly discharged into the air. They include true truffles (Tuber,
Ascomycota), false truffles (multiple Basidiomycota genera), and
various Zygomycetes. This lifestyle has evolved independently many
times across the fungal kingdom.

## Definition and Characteristics
Sequestrate fungi have:
- Spores not forcibly discharged -- enclosed within the fruiting body
  (gleba)
- Peridium (outer layer) surrounding the spore-bearing tissue
- Reduced or absent stipe (stem)
- Reduced or absent cap (pileus)
- Typically hypogeous (below ground), though some are emergent

## Distribution and Habitat
- Usually associated with trees or shrubs (mycorrhizal or saprobic)
- Found in forests, woodlands, and shrublands worldwide
- [[query-what-are-ectomycorrhizal-fungi-and-which-plants-do-they-associate-with|Ectomycorrhizal]] sequestrate fungi occur wherever ectomycorrhizal
  hosts occur (including many tropical forests)
- Some species in deserts (special [[mycorrhizal-types|mycorrhizal types]] with annual/
  perennial hosts)
- Diversity highest in subtropical to temperate forests
- Declines toward polar regions and at high elevations
- Absent from Antarctica

## Evolution — From Mushrooms to Truffles
Sequestrate forms evolved independently along several phylogenetic
lines:

### Boletales
- **Suillus** (mushroom with forcibly discharged spores from tubes)
- -> **Gastrosuillus** (modified mushroom; tubes contorted and
  plugged)
- -> **Truncocolumella** (chambers enclosed in peridium; vestigial
  stipe)
- -> **Rhizopogon** (chambers, no columella)
- -> **Alpova/Melanogaster** (gelatinous-solid mass of pockets with
  basidia and spores)

### Cortinariaceae
- **Cortinarius** (mushroom) -> **Thaxterogaster** (stipitate
  sequestrate) -> **Cortinomyces/Hymenogaster** (further reduced)

### Russulaceae
- **Russula** -> **Macowanites** -> **Gymnomyces**
- **Lactarius** -> **Arcangeliella** -> **Zelleromyces**

### Tricholomataceae
- **Laccaria** -> **Podohydnangium** -> **Hydnangium**

### Pezizales (Ascomycota)
- **Humaria** (epigeous cup fungus) -> **Geopora** (sequestrate
  chambers)
- -> **Stephensia** (hymenia embedded in solid tissue)
- -> **Phaeangium** (asci packed in pockets, not layers)
- **Peziza** -> **Hydnoplicata** -> **Hydnotryopsis** (further
  reduced)

### Zygomycetes
- May represent evolutionary progression in opposite direction
- Glomales fossil record suggests extremely early occurrence
- Present-day forms range from simple spore masses to complex
  fruiting bodies

## [[spore-dispersal|Spore Dispersal]] — Animal Vectors
Loss of forcible spore discharge required alternative dispersal:
- **Mammals:** Primary dispersers; dig up and eat sporocarps
- **Arthropods and gastropods:** Secondary dispersers
- **Birds:** Minor role

### Attraction Mechanisms
- Aromatic compounds including pheromones attract animals when spores
  are mature
- Each species produces unique aroma combinations
- Volatile compounds include dimethyl sulfide, alcohols, aldehydes,
  and various sulfur-containing molecules
- Aroma intensity peaks precisely when spores are viable, ensuring
  effective dispersal timing

### Dispersal Process
1. Animal digs up sporocarp, often moves to feeding spot
2. Peridium (leathery/carbonaceous) consumed
3. Sterile tissues digested; spores pass through gut unharmed
4. Spore-containing feces weather; spores wash into soil
5. Spores contact receptive host feeder roots

### Specialized Adaptations
- Some genera (Gummiglobus, Mesophellia) have powdery gleba with
  central columella
- Animals peel off peridium, releasing powdery spores to air/fur
- Others (Scleroderma, Elaphomyces) have thick peridia consumed by
  animals
- Desert species: some have delayed spore maturation, or produce
  during any season

## Mammalian Dispersers in Detail

### Mycophagous Marsupials
In Australia, marsupials are among the most important truffle
dispersers. The long-nosed potoroo (Potorous tridactylus) and the
greater glider (Petauroides volans) consume large quantities of
hypogeous fungi. Studies have found that up to 90% of potoroo
diet by volume can consist of sequestrate fungi during wet seasons.
Marsupial diggings create microsites that enhance water infiltration
and seed germination, providing additional ecological benefits beyond
spore dispersal.

### Rodent Dispersers
Small mammals in the family Sciuridae (squirrels and chipmunks) and
Geomyidae (pocket gophers) are major dispersers in North American
forests. Flying squirrels (Glaucomys spp.) are particularly
important: they forage primarily on hypogeous fungi, create
extensive cache sites, and carry spores between forest patches.
Research in Pacific Northwest forests shows that flying squirrel
density correlates strongly with ectomycorrhizal species richness.

### Small Marsupials and Shrews
Smaller mammals like shrews (Soricidae) and dunnarts (Sminthopsis)
consume smaller sequestrate fungi that larger mammals ignore. Their
role is often underestimated in dispersal studies because of their
small size and nocturnal habits, but they contribute significantly
to the dispersal of small-spored species.

### Large Herbivores
Pigs (Sus scrofa) are famous truffle hunters, but wild boars and
other large herbivores also consume sequestrate fungi incidentally
while rooting. Their destructive rooting behaviour can damage
forest soils, but they serve as effective dispersal vectors for
species with robust spores that survive gut passage.
