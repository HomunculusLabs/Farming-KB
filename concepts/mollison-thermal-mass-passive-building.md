# Mollison: Thermal Mass and Passive Building Design

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapters 7, 9, 12)

## Thermal Mass Principles

Mollison emphasises the critical role of thermal mass in house design and its interactions with solar gain, insulation, and ventilation.

In house design, the interactions of thermal mass, insulation, and solar orientation determine the energy efficiency and comfort of the dwelling.

The fundamental principle is to store heat when it is available (day, summer) and release it when needed (night, winter).

## Earth as Thermal Mass

Earth-compact ed walls provide enormous thermal mass.

Mollison describes machines that can raise and compact a complete house or barn wall in a morning's work.

The resulting structure is fireproof, silent, energy-conserving, and naturally sheltered.

Earth walls absorb heat during the day and release it slowly at night, creating stable interior temperatures without mechanical heating or cooling.

## Earth Banks for Climate Modification

Raised earth banks beside houses modify several external climatic factors simultaneously.

In flatlands or on high exposed sites, an earth crescent bowed into the cold winds creates shelter and warmth on its lee side.

High banks of 2.5-3.5 metres close behind house sites create instant shelter that may be further reinforced with trees.

This is a long-term and considerable heat energy saving in cold climates, and a shading or cooling wind director in warm climates.

## Ground Design for Flatland Sites

Mollison provides an actual ground design for a flatland site with cold southwest, hot northwest, and cooling northeast winds, all controlled by one earthbank and the pond formed by the excavation.

The earthbank simultaneously addresses multiple climatic factors while the pond provides thermal moderation and water storage.

## Passive Solar Heating

Solar heating is most effective in cold deserts and cool climates where the heating requirement is significant.

Building orientation should maximise solar gain on the winter-equinox sun angle.

Deciduous planting provides summer shade while allowing winter sun penetration.

Thermal mass floors and walls absorb and store solar radiation.

## Cool Air Space Design

Trellis systems on buildings create cool air spaces that modify the microclimate around the house.

In mesothermal climates, trellis on the north side of buildings (southern hemisphere) creates a shaded, cool zone adjacent to walls.

This cool air space reduces cooling loads in summer.

## Insulative Air Zones

Strategic placement of structures creates insulative air zones around the house.

A trellis crop against a building, combined with appropriate spacing, creates a buffer zone between the exterior climate and the building envelope.

Summer cool air spaces and winter insulative air zones reduce the energy needed for mechanical climate control.

## Greenhouse Integration

Greenhouses attached to the house provide both growing space and passive solar heating.

Ponds inside greenhouses provide thermal storage and humidity regulation.

The greenhouse acts as a solar collector, warming air that can be vented into the house.

## Pond-Based Thermal Systems

Mollison describes solar ponds used in conjunction with compact anaerobic ponds to supply low-grade heat for digestion processes.

Fish ponds and water features near the house moderate temperature extremes.

A special pond of 100 square metres can supply house heating and hot water as a solar pond, or be built just to heat a larger pond.

## Building Materials and Thermal Performance

Different building materials have different thermal properties.

Earth and stone have high thermal mass but low insulation value.

Straw bale and natural insulations have high insulation value but low thermal mass.

The ideal building combines both: thermal mass on the interior for heat storage and insulation on the exterior for heat retention.

## Design for Climate Zones

In cold climates, thermal mass is oriented to capture and store solar gain, with insulation preventing heat loss.

In hot climates, thermal mass is used to buffer temperature swings, with the building oriented to exclude midday sun.

In temperate climates, the design must accommodate both heating and cooling needs through orientation, thermal mass placement, and ventilation strategies.
