# Windbreak Effects on Soil Moisture and Extreme Weather

Windbreaks profoundly influence soil moisture dynamics, snow accumulation, water evaporation, and crop yields. Beyond these everyday benefits, understanding extreme wind events — hurricanes, tornadoes, and firestorms — is critical for [[permaculture-design]] in vulnerable regions.

## Soil Moisture Conservation

### Evaporation Reduction

Windbreaks significantly reduce water evaporation from soil and plant surfaces:

- In strong winds (32 km/h or more), water evaporation can be **halved** for distances up to **10 times the height** of the windbreak
- Over 24 km/h, a **30% gain in soil water conservation** is achieved
- Only in still-air conditions is evaporation loss about the same for sheltered and open field conditions

### Snow Moisture Accumulation

Snow moisture is increased by windbreaks of Type A (dense) or Type B (permeable zig-zag) configurations when snow is trapped on fields. The snow depth in winter bears a **close correlation to dry matter yields** in spring and summer, so crop yields are highest downwind from windbreak areas.

Wherever snow blows across the landscape, windbreaks of savannah configurations create **spring soil moisture traps**. This effect can also be achieved using open swales in snow-drift areas. Windbreaks in exposed snowfield areas can be better established in the lee of earth bunds or in natural cornices just poleward of ridges.

### Sea Air Condensation

In foggy climates or on sea-facing coasts, the effect of sea air condensation on windbreak foliage can add **80–300% of rainfall** as leaf drip. This makes coastal windbreaks extraordinarily valuable moisture collectors beyond their wind-reduction function.

### Hot Wind Effects

In hot deserts and hot winds, advected hot winds are the major factor in soil moisture loss. Such effects are produced over large treeless areas of dry grain crop as well as in deserts. The effects of windburn and seed shattering in hot winds are insignificant for up to **18 times the height** of the windbreak.

## Soil Conservation and Erosion Prevention

Very serious soil losses of up to **100 tonnes per hectare per day** in duststorm episodes (usually followed by torrential rain) are prevented by windbreaks combined with soil pitting using tussock grasses. Approximately **50–70% of dusts settle out of the air** within 100 m into tree clumps, making treelines essential companions to any pastoral or crop system in arid areas.

On coasts, removal of mangroves and coastal dune vegetation results in sudden acceleration of wind erosion on beaches and coastal soils. Following deforestation, up to **30% more silt per annum** flows into and reduces the useful life of water storages.

## Crop Yield Increases

Crop yields vary in increase following windbreak establishment on exposed sites:

| Crop | Yield Increase |
|------|---------------|
| Avocado | Up to 100% |
| Alfalfa | 60–70% |
| Corn | 45% |
| Wheat | 30% |
| Lettuce and low crops | 7–18% |

Almost all normal garden vegetables (cucurbits, tomato, potato) benefit greatly from wind shelter. Effects are of course less in naturally sheltered situations or areas of normally low wind speed.

### Spiral Windbreak Pattern

For garden vegetables and wind-affected pastures, a spiral pattern of low leguminous windbreak within the crop is recommended. The spiral pattern allows a continual tractor path while providing a very sheltered crop environment for all wind directions.

## Hurricanes (Cyclones, Typhoons)

Very stable and still-air calms near the equator produce fierce updraughts of air over warm oceanic areas, building over days or weeks into the great rising spirals of hurricanes.

### Hurricane Mechanics

- Hurricanes move slowly, usually at **24–32 km/h** across the ocean toward land
- Wind speeds around the vortex reach **128–192 km/h**
- Within the vortex, a "tidal bulge" rises up to **2.7 m (9 feet)** above sea level
- Vortices revolve anti-clockwise in the northern hemisphere, clockwise in the southern hemisphere
- Combined effects of rapidly fluctuating pressures, tidal bulge, wave and sea pile-up, and wave backwash create devastation on coasts
- In a "worst case" of high tides and prior rains, destructive wave attack can reach **6–9 m (20–30 feet)** above normal high-tide wave levels

### Coastal Effects

Coastal areas to the north side have the highest water and wave levels in the northern hemisphere, and to the south side in the southern hemisphere. Rain and inshore tidal effects are most severe to the poleward sides of the low-pressure cell.

### Wave Warning Signals

As wind strength increases at sea, wavelength increases. Normal wave fronts arriving at 8 per minute in calm Atlantic conditions slow down to a storm frequency of **5 per minute** before great winds. These wider-spaced waves travel fast, are larger, and create severe backwash undermining of shorelines.

Storm waves may arrive **long before** a cyclonic depression or hurricane. The change of wave beat gives warning to shore crabs, birds, fish, and turtles, who either take shelter inland or go to sea to escape the approaching hurricane.

### Design Implications

- Modern satellite photographs provide a few days' warning for coastal areas
- Well-built towns can withstand cyclones with minimal damage, but stoutness is usually only built after initial destruction
- It is possible to strictly regulate buildings for hurricane safety
- In areas with flimsy constructions, dig **refuge trenches and caves** for emergency shelter
- All emergency shelter must be in **well-drained hillside sites**

## Tornadoes

Unlike hurricanes, tornadoes occur over land and sea from shear effects at the junction of hot and cold air masses, creating intense low-pressure vortices.

### Differences from Hurricanes

- Tornadoes may occur in quite cold inland areas
- They last only seconds or minutes (vs. days for hurricanes)
- They affect only a few square kilometres (vs. hundreds of square miles)
- They usually escape detection by satellite and ground sensors

### Wind Speeds

Wind speeds may reach 120 km/h, at worst **280 km/h** — these speeds can exceed hurricane winds.

### Conditions for Tornado Formation

1. Thunderstorms with fast-growing cumulonimbus cloud
2. A persistent source of warm moist air to feed the updraught side of the front
3. An input of cold dry air entering the system from another direction
4. Vortex formation reaching the ground, caused by wind-shear effects at the border of the conflicting system (or as a frontal dust storm in deserts)

### Effects

- Trees twisted off and broken
- People and objects sucked out of cars and buildings
- "Rains" of soil, fish, and frogs may fall out ahead of the disturbance

## Firestorms

Intense wildfires (urban and rural) fanned by dry winds create powerful vortices due to conditions very much like tornadoes. The mass ignition of large areas of forests and buildings feeds a powerful updraught. Colder dry air rushes in to replace air consumed in burning, and **fire tornadoes (firestorms)** result.

### Firestorm Mechanics

- Large burning particles are carried aloft on "smoke nimbus" clouds
- Whole house sections pinwheel across the sky to drop out ahead of the main fire front
- These falling embers set up **secondary firestorm conditions** downwind
- Effects on people and property are very much like tornadoes, but with the additional danger of intense heat

### Firestorm Prone Locations

Fire tornadoes are especially common at **hill crests**, where wind shear is greatest. The combination of slope, dry vegetation, and wind creates the most dangerous conditions for firestorm development.

### Design Implications

- Firebreak design must account for ember transport, not just flame spread
- Hilltop structures face the greatest firestorm risk
- Fuel reduction on ridgelines is critical for preventing fire tornado initiation
- Refuge areas should be located away from ridge crests and in well-vegetated (fire-resistant) valleys
