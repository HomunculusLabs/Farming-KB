# Barquinha, Umbanda, and Afro-Brazilian Ayahuasca Syncretism

## Three Brazilian Ayahuasca Churches

In his 1998 review, [[ralph-metzner]] documented the existence of three organized churches in Brazil in which ayahuasca serves as the primary sacrament: Santo Daime, Uniao de Vegetal (UDV), and Barquinha.

Each was founded by rubber tappers working in the Amazon region in the 1950s, who came into contact with the hallucinogenic vine through mestizo or Indian *ayahuasceros*.

The founder of each reported a significant vision which instructed them to organize a church using the brew as the principal sacrament.

Members come from all walks of life and both urban and rural environments in Brazil.

Each has by now several thousand members in Brazil, and two of the churches have significant satellite centers in North America and Europe.

The churches are officially recognized and the use of ayahuasca is legal in Brazil within that framework.

## The Barquinha Tradition

The Barquinha church, less well known outside of Brazil than Santo Daime or UDV, has incorporated elements of Umbanda, an Afro-Brazilian religious movement.

Umbanda involves contact with deceased ancestors and deities known as *orixas*—spiritual entities derived from the Yoruba religious tradition of West Africa, transmitted to Brazil through the Atlantic slave trade.

Barquinha ceremonies involve both singing and dancing, blending the Amazonian ayahuasca tradition with the rhythmic, movement-based worship characteristic of Afro-Brazilian religious practice.

This syncretism represents a unique fusion of indigenous Amazonian shamanism with African spiritual traditions and Catholic iconography—a triple syncretism that is characteristic of Brazilian religious culture more broadly.

The Barquinha church, like Santo Daime, was founded by a Brazilian of African descent, which may partially account for the stronger African influence in its ceremonial forms.

## The Santo Daime Tradition

The Santo Daime, founded by Raimundo Irineu Serra, involves the singing of hymns by the entire congregation.

The hymns are led by a small group of women singers, reflecting the importance of female leadership in the tradition.

Some Santo Daime ceremonies also involve dancing, in simple rhythmic, swaying steps—the whole somewhat reminiscent of African-American gospel services.

This African-American comparison is Metzner's, and it highlights the Afro-Brazilian roots of the Santo Daime tradition despite its primarily indigenous (ayahuasca) sacrament.

The Santo Daime represents a middle ground between the purely shamanic ayahuasca ceremonies of the Amazon and the more formal, structured ceremonies of the UDV.

It preserves the communal singing and dancing that characterize Afro-Brazilian religious practice while maintaining the visionary focus of the ayahuasca tradition.

## The UDV Tradition

The UDV (Uniao de Vegetal) is the largest and most formal of the three churches.

Participants sit in rows in straight-backed chairs during the ceremony, listening to sermons and songs given by the *maestres* who sit around a table in the center.

There is also a question and answer period, and sometimes testimonials of life-transformations are offered by longer-term members, reminiscent of AA confessions.

The UDV's formality and structure reflect a more Western-influenced organizational model, with clear hierarchies of authority and a more didactic approach to the ceremonial experience.

The research of Charles Grob, [[dennis-mckenna]], and others has confirmed that membership in these churches and long-term drinking of the tea is associated with no adverse health effects—and indeed with a reduction of addictions and other psychopathologies.

## Umbanda: The Afro-Brazilian Spiritual Matrix

Umbanda, the Afro-Brazilian religious movement that influenced Barquinha, deserves attention in its own right as a context for understanding the syncretic ayahuasca churches.

Umbanda originated in the early [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]] in Rio de Janeiro and is characterized by the incorporation of African *orixas*, Catholic saints, and indigenous Brazilian spiritual entities into a single syncretic framework.

The *orixas* are spiritual forces associated with elements of nature—water, fire, earth, air—and with specific colors, foods, songs, and dances.

Each *orixa* has a corresponding Catholic saint (a legacy of the colonial period when African religious practices had to be disguised under Catholic forms).

Umbanda ceremonies typically involve mediumship, in which practitioners become "horses" for the spirits, allowing the entities to speak and act through them.

The incorporation of Umbanda elements into Barquinha ayahuasca ceremonies adds a dimension of spirit possession and mediumship to the ayahuasca experience that is absent from the more contemplative UDV ceremonies.

This fusion raises interesting questions about the interaction between the psychoactive effects of ayahuasca and the belief systems of Afro-Brazilian spirituality.

## Social and Protective Functions

Metzner emphasized the social functions of the syncretic ayahuasca churches.

An important social function of these religious ceremonies is to strengthen community bonds and give members a sense of participation and belonging.

Participation in the Native American Church in the United States, as well as the ayahuasca churches in Brazil, tends to reduce the incidence of alcoholism and drug addiction.

A further societal function of these churches is to provide a protective shield of traditional lore against the encroachments of Christian missionaries and the seductions of Western consumer culture.

These syncretic religious movements have brought the use of entheogenic plant substances out of the context of shamanic healing rituals, where only a very limited number of people came into contact with them.

They have made profoundly spiritually transforming experiences with entheogenic plant medicines accessible to a large number and wide spectrum of people in all walks of life.

As such, we may be seeing the beginnings of a broader cultural transformation movement with significant impact.

## The Consciousness-Expansion Model Applied to Addiction

Metzner proposed that the consciousness-expanding properties of all hallucinogens, including LSD, peyote, ayahuasca, and others, can serve to counteract the consciousness-contracting and fixating effect of addictive narcotics or stimulants.

This model provides a theoretical framework for understanding the anti-addictive effects observed in the ayahuasca churches.

The consciousness of the addict is contracted, fixated, and narrowed to a single compulsive loop. The entheogenic experience expands, opens, and liberates consciousness from these fixations.

The more plausible assumption is that all the "consciousness-expanding" hallucinogens can serve to counteract the consciousness-contracting and fixating effect of the addictive narcotics or stimulants.

This is not specific to ibogaine (which has been proposed as a chemical antidote to addiction due to its action on cocaine receptor sites) but is a general property of the class.

The communal, [[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] of the ayahuasca churches amplifies this anti-addictive effect by providing social support, community bonds, and a framework of meaning that supports recovery and transformation.

## Legal and Cultural Significance

The legal recognition of the Brazilian ayahuasca churches represents an important precedent in the relationship between indigenous spiritual practices and modern legal systems.

The fact that three distinct churches, each with thousands of members, have achieved legal recognition for their use of a scheduled substance in a religious context suggests that the legal framework is capable of accommodating entheogenic practice when it is embedded in a genuine religious tradition with a substantial membership and a clear organizational structure.

The expansion of these churches to North America and Europe raises questions about the portability of indigenous-syncretic religious traditions across cultural boundaries.

The fact that the churches have been able to establish satellite centers in these regions suggests that the appeal of the ayahuasca experience, when embedded in a ceremonial and communal context, transcends the specific cultural milieu in which it originated.
