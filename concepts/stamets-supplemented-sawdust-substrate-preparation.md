# Supplemented Sawdust Substrate Preparation

## Overview

Supplemented sawdust substrates form the foundation of modern indoor [[jar-shaking-grain-redistribution-mycelial-colonisation]] and high-yield fruiting. The preparation process—involving substrate [[straw-substrate-preparation-mushroom-cultivation]] is as important as its chemical composition. The ideal substrate is not uniform sawdust but a carefully proportioned matrix of particle sizes:

- **Small particles (fine sawdust)**: Stimulate rapid initial mycelial growth ("leap-off" from the spawn). Fine particles provide abundant surface area for colonisation and quick access to nutrients.
- **Large particles (wood chips)**: Encourage the formation of thick, cord-like strands called rhizomorphs. These larger pieces serve as nutritional reservoirs and fruiting platforms, giving rise to larger individual mushrooms.

The recommended ratio is a **50:50 mixture by volume of sawdust and chips** with varying particle sizes. This dual-particle-size approach creates an ideal habitat that balances rapid colonisation with sustained nutrition for fruiting. Disproportionate particle sizes—either too fine or too coarse—hinder full colonisation and reduce yields.

## Selecting Wood Materials

### Preferred Hardwoods

Fast-decomposing hardwoods with high ratios of starch-rich sapwood to heartwood are ideal:

- **Top choices**: Alder (*Alnus* spp.), cottonwood, poplar, birch (*Betula* spp.), beech (*Fagus* spp.), oak (*Quercus* spp.), sweetgum (*Liquidambar* spp.), willow (*Salix* spp.)
- **Good choices**: Ash (*Fraxinus* spp.), walnut (*Juglans* spp.), elm, chestnut (*Castanea* spp.), chinkapin (*Castanopsis* spp.), hornbeam (*Carpinus* spp.), hickory (*Carya* spp.)
- **Limited use**: Douglas fir (*Pseudotsuga*) and hemlock (*Tsuga*) can support some species but generally produce lower yields than hardwoods

### Woods to Avoid

- **Cedar and redwood**: Contain anti-rotting compounds that actively resist [[gourmet-mushroom-cultivation]] [[straw-substrate-preparation-mushroom-cultivation]] with unpredictable yields.

### Freshness and Contamination

Freshness of the wood material is critical. Old sawdust, chips, or base materials may already harbour competitor organisms. Wood from trees along busy roadways should be avoided because automobile exhaust and asphalt leachate contaminate surrounding soil with lead, aluminium, and other heavy metals that can be concentrated by [[comparison-enoki-vs-shiitake-cultivation]].
- **Grains**: Whole wheat, rye, millet, and other whole grains provide both nutrition and structure.
- **Legume meals**: Soybean meal and other legume flours are nitrogen-rich supplements that boost yields significantly.
- **Agricultural waste**: Hulls of almonds, walnuts, sunflower, pecans, and peanuts; soybean meal roughage (okara); artichoke waste.

### Supplementation Rates

Typical supplementation rates range from 5-20% of the total dry substrate weight, depending on the species and supplement type. Higher supplementation rates increase yield potential but also increase contamination risk, as the added nutrients are equally attractive to competitor organisms. This is why supplemented substrates require sterilisation (rather than pasteurisation) to achieve a fully clean slate.

## Hydration and Moisture Content

### Target Moisture

The goal moisture content for supplemented sawdust substrates is typically **55-65%** by weight. This range provides sufficient water for mycelial metabolism while maintaining adequate air-filled porosity for gas exchange.

### Testing for Moisture Content

Stamets recommends a simple field test: squeeze a handful of the hydrated substrate firmly. A few drops of water should appear between your fingers. If water streams out freely, the substrate is too wet. If no water appears and the substrate does not hold together when squeezed, it is too dry.

### Achieving Even Hydration

Uneven moisture distribution is a common problem, particularly with fine sawdust that can become waterlogged on the outside while remaining dry in the centre. Best practices include:

- Mixing substrate thoroughly during hydration, allowing time for water absorption.
- Using hot or warm water to accelerate hydration of wood particles.
- Breaking up clumps and checking moisture in multiple spots throughout the batch.
- Allowing the hydrated substrate to rest (1-24 hours) for moisture equilibration before bagging and sterilisation.

## Bagging and Sterilisation

### Container Selection

Supplemented sawdust is typically packed into autoclavable bags equipped with filter patches for gas exchange. The most common formats are:

- **Polypropylene bags with micropore filter patches**: The industry standard. Available in various sizes, typically holding 2-10 kg of substrate per block.
- **Bottle culture**: Used primarily in Asian shiitake cultivation, where substrate is packed into wide-mouth bottles.
- **Container bags with self-sealing injection ports**: Allow clean inoculation without opening the bag.

### The Sterilisation Imperative

Because supplements add readily available nutrients, supplemented substrates **must be sterilised** (not merely pasteurised) to eliminate all competing organisms. Pasteurisation (heating to 60-80°C) leaves heat-resistant bacterial endospores alive; these organisms can outcompete mushroom mycelium on the nutrient-rich supplemented substrate.

### Sterilisation Parameters

- **Autoclave/retort**: 15 PSI (121°C) for 1.5-2.5 hours, depending on bag size and density.
- **Atmospheric steam sterilisation**: 100°C for 12-24 hours in a dedicated steam chamber. This lower-tech approach is accessible to small-scale growers without pressure vessels.
