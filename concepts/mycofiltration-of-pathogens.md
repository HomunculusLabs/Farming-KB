Mycofiltration of pathogens is the use of fungal mycelial mats as biological membranes to filter, trap, and destroy disease-causing microorganisms from water and soil. This application of mycology demonstrates how mushroom mycelium can serve as a living filtration system, reducing populations of coliform bacteria, protozoa, and other pathogens that threaten human health, agriculture, and aquatic ecosystems.

## How Mycofiltration Works

Mycofiltration is the use of mycelium as a membrane for filtering out microorganisms, pollutants, and silt. More than a mile of threadlike mycelial cells can infuse a gram of soil. These fine filaments function as a cellular net that catches particles and, in some cases, digests them. As the substrate debris is digested, microcavities form and fill with air or water, providing buoyant, aerobic infrastructures with vast surface areas.

Water runoff, rich in organic debris, percolates through the cellular mesh and is cleansed. When water is not flowing, the mycelium channels moisture from afar through its advancing fingerlike cells. The Stropharia [[stropharia-rugoso-annulata-mycofiltration]] (garden giant) mycelium holds wood chips together tenaciously and thrives when it comes into contact with bacteria, compared to its slow growth under sterile conditions.

## Coliform Reduction: The Skookum Inlet Experiment

The most dramatic demonstration of mycofiltration's pathogen-destroying capability came from Paul Stamets' farm on Kamilche Point in Skookum Inlet, Washington. Two swales gradually narrowed over about 800 feet, dropping 120 feet from uplands to a saltwater bay downstream where neighbors grew clams and oysters commercially.

Stamets installed outdoor wood chip beds of garden giant mushrooms (Stropharia rugoso annulata) at the top of one of the sloping basins. Several truckloads of wood chips from utility company tree-trimming operations were dumped into the depression, inoculated with Stropharia spawn, and raked into a foot-deep layer. Within months, a mycelial bed approximately 50 feet wide and 200 feet long was established.

Most septic systems in the area were primitive cedar barrels or pits. Fecal coliform pollution directly threatened the shellfish industry on the inlet. Just one year after installing the mycelium beds, and before even repairing the septic system, analysis of outflowing water showed a hundredfold drop in coliform levels, despite having more than doubled the population of farm animals.

## Battelle Marine Science Laboratories Research

That backyard mycofiltration experiment reducing coliform populations drew the attention of researchers at the Battelle Marine Science Laboratories in Sequim, Washington. More formal studies ensued, demonstrating that other mushrooms, including oyster mushrooms and wood conk mushrooms, also functioned as microbiological filters.

This collaborative research showed that mycofiltration could have wide applications in the destruction of biological as well as chemical toxins. Working with Battelle, Stamets identified a fungus that broke down dimethyl methylphosphonate (DMMP), a key ingredient in the deadly neurotoxin VX and sarin. The mushroom thrived in a petri dish eating nothing but DMMP, metabolizing the majority into unstable subderivatives that soon became nontoxic.

## Mechanisms of Pathogen Destruction

Pathogens passing through the cellular nets of mycelia are digested by the fungi. If enzymes and antibiotics secreted by the mycelium do not kill all the pathogens, bacteria such as Bacillus subtilis are blocked from reproducing and are suspended in a state of dormancy. These types of bacteria may later revive when the mycelial mat dies and a hospitable habitat reemerges.

This dieback process is analogous to the decay of a fishing net that loses its cohesiveness and unthreads. The mycelium not only acts as a biofilter but also provides food for organisms at multiple trophic levels, including salmon that fed on fly larvae living in the Stropharia mushrooms.

## Antimicrobial Properties of Mushrooms

Mushroom mycelium has an unquenchable appetite for organic debris. The mycological landscaper can select mushroom species that target and consume the bacteria and protozoa in a habitat. Research has shown that [[growing-gourmet-polyporus-umbellatus-zhu-ling-cultivation]] (zhu ling) was 100 percent effective in vitro in inhibiting the malarial parasite Plasmodium falciparum.

Mycomulching with polypore species around a malaria-infected swamp could reduce the background population of malaria, since the fungus likely consumes the parasite and secretes antibiotics into the habitat. Combining these polypore species with pesticidal mycelia that attract and attack plague-carrying mosquitoes could amount to a double-pronged response to malaria.

## Malaria and Mosquito Control

Combining polypore mushroom species with pesticidal mycelia that could attract and attack plague-carrying mosquitoes could amount to a powerful response to malaria and other mosquito-borne diseases. Mycofilters and entire landscapes can be customized with mosaics of mycelial mats to prevent infection from coliform or staph bacteria and protozoa.

The mats can even trap disease-carrying insects, thwarting disease vectors and protecting human health. Multiple mushroom species have demonstrated specific antimicrobial effects against various pathogenic microbes, making them suitable for targeted biological filtration applications.

## Installation Methods

Installation of mycofiltration membranes can utilize debris from forests (brush, tree trimmings, wood chips), pulp and paper mills, city and rural recycling centers (yard waste), farms (straw, corncobs, cotton, coffee wastes), and breweries (washed grains). These materials are inoculated with toxin-specific or pathogen-specific mushrooms.

Creating a sheet mulch 6 inches to 2 feet thick provides mycofiltration properties where the mycelium and surface areas of the substrate particles capture microbial outflow. Placing myceliated burlap bags, or "bunker spawn," stacked like sandbags is one simple method of introducing beneficial mushrooms. Stem butt transfers or spore slurries can expand a helpful mushroom's domain.

Several factors affect the efficiency of mycofiltration: slope, flow rate, thickness, and the species of mushroom selected. Native fungi that already tolerate the pathogen load at contaminated sites are often the best choice for mycofiltration applications.

## Agricultural Applications

Around cattle and pig feedlots, the flow of coliforms, nitrates, and phosphorus affects the downstream watershed, especially during the rainy season. Farmers can build soils while creating mycofiltration membranes for trapping pollutants by using thick mats of myceliated wood chips or straw.

Corn farmers can first profitably grow oyster mushrooms on cornstalks and straw, and then use the spent substrate for mycofiltration. Once a mycofiltration habitat is constructed, ecological recovery unfolds and nature guides the course. Mycological landscapes must be replenished with carrier materials and sometimes recharged with spawn to preserve the saprophytic mushroom communities.

## Broader Significance

Mycofiltration represents an elegant convergence of ecology, engineering, and mycology. By harnessing the natural filtration properties of [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]], we can address water quality issues without the energy inputs and chemical treatments required by conventional filtration systems. The approach works with natural biological processes rather than against them.

The potential applications extend beyond rural settings to suburban developments, factory runoff treatment, and municipal water systems. The low cost and sustainability of mycofiltration make it particularly attractive for developing regions where conventional water treatment infrastructure may be unavailable or unaffordable.

The research at Battelle Marine Science Laboratories validated what Stamets had observed empirically on his farm, providing scientific credibility to the approach. Further research continues to expand the known capabilities of mycofiltration for destroying both biological and chemical contaminants.

## Merged: Mycoremediation of Pesticides

Mycoremediation is the use of fungi to degrade environmental toxins, including a wide range of synthetic pesticides that have accumulated in soils and waterways worldwide. Certain mushroom species produce enzymes capable of breaking down complex chemical compounds, including organochlorines, organophosphates, and other persistent organic pollutants (POPs), transforming them into less harmful or inert substances.

## The Enzymatic Mechanism

Mushroom mycelia can decompose hydrocarbons in petroleum products because lignin has similar hydrogen-carbon bonds. The extracellular enzymes that white-rot fungi secrete to break down lignin — particularly lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases — are nonspecific in their activity. This means they can attack a wide range of [[plants-of-the-gods-chemical-structures-hallucinogens]] beyond lignin, including many synthetic compounds that were designed to resist biological degradation.
