Mycofiltration of pathogens is the use of fungal mycelial mats as biological membranes to filter, trap, and destroy disease-causing microorganisms from water and soil. This application of mycology demonstrates how mushroom mycelium can serve as a living filtration system, reducing populations of coliform bacteria, protozoa, and other pathogens that threaten human health, agriculture, and aquatic ecosystems.

## How Mycofiltration Works

Mycofiltration is the use of mycelium as a membrane for filtering out microorganisms, pollutants, and silt. More than a mile of threadlike mycelial cells can infuse a gram of soil. These fine filaments function as a cellular net that catches particles and, in some cases, digests them. As the substrate debris is digested, microcavities form and fill with air or water, providing buoyant, aerobic infrastructures with vast surface areas.

Water runoff, rich in organic debris, percolates through the cellular mesh and is cleansed. When water is not flowing, the mycelium channels moisture from afar through its advancing fingerlike cells. The Stropharia [[growing-gourmet-polyporus-umbellatus-zhu-ling-cultivation]] (zhu ling) was 100 percent effective in vitro in inhibiting the malarial parasite Plasmodium falciparum.

Mycomulching with polypore species around a malaria-infected swamp could reduce the background population of malaria, since the fungus likely consumes the parasite and secretes antibiotics into the habitat. Combining these polypore species with pesticidal mycelia that attract and attack plague-carrying mosquitoes could amount to a double-pronged response to malaria.

## Malaria and Mosquito Control

Combining polypore mushroom species with pesticidal mycelia that could attract and attack plague-carrying mosquitoes could amount to a powerful response to malaria and other mosquito-borne diseases. Mycofilters and entire landscapes can be customized with mosaics of mycelial mats to prevent infection from coliform or staph bacteria and protozoa.

The mats can even trap disease-carrying insects, thwarting disease vectors and protecting human health. Multiple mushroom species have demonstrated specific antimicrobial effects against various pathogenic microbes, making them suitable for targeted biological filtration applications.

## Installation Methods

Installation of mycofiltration membranes can utilize debris from forests (brush, tree trimmings, wood chips), pulp and paper mills, city and rural recycling centers (yard waste), farms (straw, corncobs, cotton, coffee wastes), and breweries (washed grains). These materials are inoculated with toxin-specific or pathogen-specific mushrooms.

Creating a sheet mulch 6 inches to 2 feet thick provides mycofiltration properties where the mycelium and surface areas of the substrate particles capture microbial outflow. Placing myceliated burlap bags, or "bunker spawn," stacked like sandbags is one simple method of introducing beneficial mushrooms. Stem butt transfers or spore slurries can expand a helpful mushroom's domain.

Several factors affect the efficiency of mycofiltration: slope, flow rate, thickness, and the species of mushroom selected. Native fungi that already tolerate the pathogen load at contaminated sites are often the best choice for mycofiltration applications.

## Agricultural Applications

Around cattle and pig feedlots, the flow of coliforms, nitrates, and phosphorus affects the downstream watershed, especially during the rainy season. Farmers can build soils while creating mycofiltration membranes for trapping pollutants by using thick mats of myceliated wood chips or straw.

Corn farmers can first profitably grow oyster mushrooms on cornstalks and straw, and then use the spent substrate for mycofiltration. Once a mycofiltration habitat is constructed, ecological recovery unfolds and nature guides the course. Mycological landscapes must be replenished with carrier materials and sometimes recharged with spawn to preserve the saprophytic mushroom communities.

## Merged: Mycoremediation of Pesticides

Mycoremediation is the use of fungi to degrade environmental toxins, including a wide range of synthetic pesticides that have accumulated in soils and waterways worldwide. Certain mushroom species produce enzymes capable of breaking down complex chemical compounds, including organochlorines, organophosphates, and other persistent organic pollutants (POPs), transforming them into less harmful or inert substances.

## The Enzymatic Mechanism

Mushroom mycelia can decompose hydrocarbons in petroleum products because lignin has similar hydrogen-carbon bonds. The extracellular enzymes that white-rot fungi secrete to break down lignin — particularly lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases — are nonspecific in their activity. This means they can attack a wide range of [[plants-of-the-gods-chemical-structures-hallucinogens]] beyond lignin, including many synthetic compounds that were designed to resist biological degradation.
