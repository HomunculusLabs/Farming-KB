# Morphology and Anatomy of Agaricus campestris

> Based on B.M. Duggar's *Mushroom Growing* (1915), a foundational
> USDA bulletin that codified the descriptive morphology of the common
> cultivated field mushroom for American growers.

## Overview

Agaricus campestris, the common field mushroom, was the principal
cultivated species in Duggar's time and remains the archetype for
understanding agaric anatomy. Duggar provided careful descriptions of
each major anatomical feature — cap, stem, ring, gills, and spores —
as practical knowledge for growers who needed to identify their crop,
distinguish it from poisonous look-alikes, and understand its life
cycle. His treatment emphasizes observable macroscopic features while
also introducing key microscopic structures such as basidia and spores.

## The Cap (Pileus)

The cap (pileus) undergoes a predictable sequence of developmental
stages as the mushroom matures:

1. **Hemispherical** — In the young button stage the cap is
 dome-shaped, fitting tightly over the gills.
2. **Convex** — As expansion proceeds the cap broadens into a rounded
 profile.
3. **Nearly plane** — Full maturity flattens the cap, sometimes with
 a slight uplift at the margin.

The cap surface is **smooth**, ranging from **white to cream** in
color. Occasional specimens develop **brownish scales**, especially
near the center. The flesh (context) is **white and firm** throughout
all stages, a consistency valued in commercial grading.

## The Stem (Stipe)

The stem (stipe) is **cylindrical and nearly equal** in diameter from
base to apex, meaning it does not taper significantly. It is **solid**
— not hollow — and **white** in color. These features distinguish it
from many woodland agarics that develop hollow or tapered stipes. The
stem elevates the cap above the substrate, facilitating spore
dispersal by air currents.

## The Ring (Annulus)

The ring (annulus) is a **remnant of the partial veil** — a membrane
that originally connected the margin of the expanding cap to the stem
during the button stage. As the cap opens, the veil tears free and
remains as a collar-like ring on the upper stem.

- The ring **breaks away** from the stem as the mushroom reaches full
 expansion.
- In the variety **"Columbia"**, the ring is **particularly
 well-developed**, a notable cultivar characteristic.
- The presence, position, and persistence of the ring are important
 diagnostic features for agaric identification.

## The Gills (Lamellae) and Hymenium

The gills (lamellae) occupy the **underside of the cap**, **radiating
outward** from the stem. Their color progression signals maturity:

| Stage | Gill Color |
|-------|-----------|
| Young | Pink |
| Intermediate | Dark brown |
| Mature | Nearly black |

The gill surface is the **hymenium** — the spore-bearing layer. Under
magnification it is studded with **erect cells in palisade
arrangement** called **basidia**, each bearing **two or four** spores
on slender stalks (sterigmata). This palisade architecture maximizes
spore production per unit area of gill surface.

## Spores and Spore Prints

The dark powder shed from mature gills consists of **innumerable
minute simple cells** — **ovate in shape** — called **spores**. Duggar
emphasized several key points:

- Spores serve for **reproduction** and are functionally analogous to
 the green powdery substance produced by moulds on bread or cheese.
- Spores are **not seeds** — their structure is simple and their
 development is distinct from that of seed plants. Duggar cautioned
 growers against using the term "seed" for spores.
- The **color of the spore print** is critically important for species
 identification.

**Making a spore print** (Duggar's method):

1. Twist the stem gently until it separates from the cap.
2. Place the cap **gill-side-down** on white paper.
3. Cover with a vessel to exclude air currents.
4. After **24 hours**, a **brownish-black** spore deposit appears,
 faithfully reproducing the radial pattern of the gills.

## Distinguishing from Amanita (Volva)

A critical safety distinction Duggar addressed is the **absence of a
volva** in *Agaricus campestris*. A volva is a cup-like sheath at the
base of the stem, formed from remnants of the **universal veil** that
encloses the entire young mushroom in some genera.

- *Agaricus campestris* **lacks a volva entirely**.
- Deadly **Amanita** species (e.g., *A. phalloides*, the death cap)
 **possess a prominent volva** at the stem base.

This single feature is a key field character for separating the
edible cultivated mushroom from potentially lethal look-alikes. Duggar
stressed that cultivators and foragers must check the stem base of
every specimen.

## Commercial Grading and Varieties

Duggar's work reflected the emerging commercial [[mushroom-industry-african-countries]] of
the early [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]]:

- **Button stage** — Mushrooms graded **highest** commercially are
 those picked **just before the veil breaks**, when the cap is still
 hemispherical and gills are not yet exposed. This stage maximizes
 shelf life, texture, and appearance.
- **Variety "Columbia"** — Duggar specifically notes this variety for
 its **particularly well-developed ring**, suggesting it was a
 recognized commercial strain with desirable market characteristics.
