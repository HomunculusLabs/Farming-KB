# Mushroom Toxicity and Safe Foraging Practices

Julius A. Palmer's personal experiences with [[mushroom-poisoning-and-remedies]] his systematic
approach to testing new species provide a window into 19th-century understanding
of fungal toxicity. While some of his conclusions have been superseded by modern
mycological science, his core principles of caution, incremental testing, and
respect for the susceptibility of fungi to environmental contamination remain
relevant to foragers today.

## Palmer's Personal Poisoning Experience

Palmer was never deceived into making a meal of genuinely poisonous fungus, but
he was once poisoned through contamination. Edible mushrooms had been shipped in a
box containing specimens of *[[coprinus-deliquescens]]* that were in a state of
decomposition. The deliquescing coprinus eventually turns to ink independent of
contact with any fluid substance, and its decomposing juices contaminated the
edible specimens.

### Symptoms

The symptoms resembled those of a person using tobacco for the first time:
dizziness, nausea, purging, perspiration, and alternating cold spells. All
passed within an hour — so rapid was the effect of what Palmer called the
"mushroom virus." He noted that his "engines refused to reverse action,"
suggesting the [[emcdda-psilocybin-cardiovascular-risks-acute-physiological-effects]], while acute, could not be willed away.

### Treatment

Two wineglasses of equal parts whiskey and sweet-oil neutralized the poison, and
within a few hours Palmer was no worse for the experience. He prescribed this
remedy for all cases rather than emetics, allowing that the whiskey could be
omitted or vinegar substituted for spirits, but sweet-oil should be taken
liberally.

## The Sweet-Oil Hypothesis

Palmer believed that Italians could eat many dangerous fungi with impunity because
fresh fungal properties were changed by sweet-oil, and preserved fungi were
neutralized by pickle. He theorized that either element rendered harmless the
peculiar alkali to which mushrooms owe their noxious qualities. While this
hypothesis has not been validated by modern toxicology, the principle that
cooking methods and accompanying ingredients can affect fungal toxicity has
some scientific basis — thorough cooking does denature certain fungal toxins,
though by no means all of them.

## Environmental Contamination of Fungi

Palmer documented several cases demonstrating the extreme susceptibility of
mushrooms to their [[cannabis-growing-environment-setup]]:

### Potash-Forced Horse Mushrooms

A gardener sent Palmer a basket of horse-mushrooms that had been forced in growth
using potash. They cooked like pieces of soap, and the liquor resembled strong
lye. The potash had fundamentally altered the [[chemical-composition-of-hallucinogens]] the fungi,
rendering them unfit for consumption.

### Fish-Contaminated Boleti

A large number of a delicious boletus species (*[[boletus-aestivalis]]*) were found
impregnated so strongly with the odor of decayed fish as to be unfit for food.
The fungi had apparently absorbed environmental contaminants from their substrate.

### Flavor Transfer

Specimens of *[[boletus-pachypus]]* communicated their strong nutty flavor, without
direct contact, to potatoes placed beside them in Palmer's refrigerator —
demonstrating that [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]] from fungi can permeate nearby foods.

### Habitat Effects on Edibility

The maned mushroom (*[[coprinus-comatus]]*) was delicious when found in lawns, but
specimens from dust-heaps, brick-yards, or city offal had no flavor and would
"doubtless prove deleterious" in large quantities. Palmer drew the parallel to
other foods: who would willingly eat tainted meat, or has not been sickened by
bad oysters, stale fish, or overripe fruit?

## The Danger of Disguised Flavor

Palmer's most emphatic warning concerned the practice of masking mushroom flavor
during cooking. He argued that Nature provides warning signs at the "gateway" —
primarily through taste and smell. When mushrooms are mixed with gravies, fried
in batter, simmered in fat, or seasoned with black pepper and parsley, their
delicate natural flavor is destroyed along with these protective signals.

One of the most common signs of harmful fungus is a stinging sensation affecting
the tongue slightly, the throat and tonsils more, and probably having the most
effect upon the stomach and bowels. If the mushroom absorbs enough fat to cover
this sensation, or if the taste is disguised by spice or by mixing one variety
with another, a person may easily eat enough poisonous fungus to cause death.

## The Fermentation Hazard

Palmer identified a specific danger with the olive-red boletus (*Boletus
subtomentosus*), which he had eaten by the bushel from the same location. The
culprit in poisoning cases was not misidentification but chemical change in the
substance of the mushroom.

Under litmus paper, this mushroom shows a marked acid reaction. When heavily
charged with water from dew or rain, its spongy substance enters a state of
acidulous fermentation, exhibiting brilliant blue when pressed, cut, or broken.
Three cases of illness from this cause were brought to Palmer's attention
simultaneously, none more serious than excessive purging and vomiting.

### The Lesson

Palmer's conclusion was not that the mushroom was unfit for food, but that only
young specimens with substance too solid to admit of fermentation should ever be
eaten. This principle — that the condition and freshness of a fungus matters as
much as its species identification — remains sound mycological advice.

## Palmer's Incremental Testing Protocol

Palmer described his systematic approach to testing new fungal species:

1. **Trace to family**: Identify the specimen's family using morphological
   features (gills, pores, teeth, or puffball structure).
2. **Simple cooking**: Broil with salt and butter only — no pepper, no spices,
   no batter.
3. **Small initial dose**: Eat a small piece on an empty stomach.
4. **Gradual increase**: Increase quantity in successive experiments.
5. **Monitor reactions**: Watch for unpleasant sensations over the following
   hours.

Usually, the non-edible fungus revealed its character during this process:
nauseous slime weeping from the stem, a grassy and disagreeable odor arising as
it heated, or simply no desire to take another mouthful after tasting.

## Recklessness Versus Bravery

Palmer carefully distinguished between careful experimentation and foolhardy
consumption: "In whatever cause, reckless disregard of consequences is not
bravery, although no two elements are oftener confounded." His approach combined
intellectual rigor with physical caution — a model that modern foraging guides
continue to recommend, albeit with significantly more conservative boundaries
than Palmer employed.
