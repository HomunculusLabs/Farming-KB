# Fungal Biodegradation and Bioremediation

## Overview

Fungi possess extraordinary capabilities for degrading complex organic molecules, including many synthetic compounds that are resistant to bacterial degradation. The lignin-degrading enzyme systems of white-rot basidiomycetes are particularly noteworthy for their ability to attack a wide range of recalcitrant environmental pollutants. Fungal bioremediation (mycoremediation) exploits these natural degradative abilities to clean up contaminated soils, water, and industrial waste streams. The capacity of fungi to extend hyphal networks through soil, tolerate high concentrations of toxic compounds, and produce non-specific oxidative enzymes makes them uniquely suited for environmental biotechnology applications.

## Ligninolytic Enzymes and Pollutant Degradation

The same enzyme systems that white-rot fungi use to degrade lignin -- lignin peroxidases (LiP), manganese peroxidases (MnP), and laccases -- are responsible for the degradation of many environmental pollutants. These enzymes operate through non-specific radical-mediated reactions rather than substrate-specific catalysis, enabling them to attack a broad spectrum of structurally diverse compounds.

### Lignin Peroxidase (LiP)

Lignin peroxidase is a heme-containing glycoprotein that catalyzes the oxidation of non-phenolic aromatic compounds with redox potentials exceeding 1.4 V, making it one of the most powerful biological oxidants known. LiP can oxidize [[cervantes-hydrogen-peroxide-sterilization]] as a co-substrate and [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]] and other white-rot fungi can degrade PCP through MnP-mediated oxidation. Competitive RT-PCR studies have shown that specific lignin peroxidase genes are not necessarily expressed during PCP degradation in soil, highlighting the importance of MnP and other enzyme systems.

### Chlorinated Dioxins and Furans

These highly toxic, persistent organic pollutants are products of combustion and chemical manufacturing. White-rot fungi can dechlorinate and degrade dioxin congeners, although degradation rates are generally slow. Laccase-mediated oxidation with appropriate redox mediators shows promise for enhancing dioxin degradation.

### Explosives and Munitions

Fungi can degrade explosive compounds including TNT (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene), RDX, and HMX through both ligninolytic and non-ligninolytic mechanisms. The ability of fungal mycelia to penetrate soil and reach contaminants that are inaccessible to bacteria is particularly valuable for remediating contaminated military sites.

### Pesticides and Herbicides

Fungi contribute to the natural attenuation of many pesticide compounds in soil. Organophosphate, carbamate, and organochlorine pesticides can be degraded or transformed by fungal enzymes including esterases, hydrolases, and ligninolytic peroxidases.

## Gene Expression During Bioremediation

Studies using competitive RT-PCR and microarray-based transcript analysis have revealed that fungal gene expression during pollutant degradation differs substantially from expression in laboratory culture. In Phanerochaete chrysosporium, certain lignin peroxidase transcripts abundant in defined laboratory media were not expressed during organopollutant degradation in soil. Similarly, cellulase gene expression profiles differed dramatically between wood chips and soil substrates. These findings demonstrate that laboratory studies may not accurately predict which fungal enzymes are active during in situ bioremediation, underscoring the need for direct molecular monitoring of gene expression in contaminated environments.

## Bioavailability Considerations

A critical factor limiting fungal bioremediation is the bioavailability of hydrophobic pollutants. Many target compounds (PAHs, PCBs, dioxins) are strongly sorbed to soil organic matter or sequestered within soil aggregates, making them physically inaccessible to fungal enzymes. Fungal hyphae can extend into soil micropores and interact with pollutant molecules at the soil-fungus interface, potentially enhancing bioavailability compared to bacterial approaches. Surfactants and solubilizing agents have been investigated as amendments to increase pollutant bioavailability for fungal degradation.

## Practical Applications

Several mycoremediation strategies have been developed:

- **Land treatment**: Application of fungal inocula to contaminated soil, often with nutrient amendments and periodic tilling to enhance aeration and fungal colonization.
- **Biopiles and bioreactors**: Contained systems that optimize environmental conditions (moisture, aeration, nutrients) for fungal degradation of excavated contaminated soil.
- **Lignocellulosic substrate amendments**: Addition of wood chips, sawdust, or straw as growth substrates and nutrient sources for white-rot fungi in contaminated soil.
- **Mycofiltration**: Use of fungal mycelial mats to filter and degrade contaminants from water flowing through the substrate. Fungal biofilters have been applied to treat stormwater, agricultural runoff, and industrial effluents.
