# Fungal Taxonomy and the Kingdom Fungi

## Overview
Fungal taxonomy has undergone revolutionary changes in recent decades as
molecular phylogenetic methods have reshaped our understanding of fungal
evolutionary relationships. Modern systematics recognizes Kingdom Fungi as a
monophyletic group more closely related to animals than to plants, comprising
several major phyla. However, the traditional four-phylum classification does
not fully represent the phylogenetic complexity revealed by molecular
analyses, and several groups once considered fungi have been reassigned to
separate kingdoms entirely. Understanding [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity-patterns-distribution]], ecology, and evolution.

## The Tree of Life: Fungi and Their Allies

Contemporary phylogenetic analyses based on multiple protein-coding genes

## Phylum Chytridiomycota: The Flagellated Fungi

Chytridiomycota (chytrids) are the most basal lineage within Kingdom Fungi and
the only fungi that produce motile cells (zoospores or gametes) at some stage
in their life cycle. They possess a single posteriorly inserted smooth
flagellum, an ancestral trait shared with the opisthokont common ancestor of
fungi and animals. Chytrids are primarily aquatic organisms found in
freshwater, marine, and soil habitats, where they function as decomposers,
parasites of algae, invertebrates and other microorganisms, and occasionally
as pathogens of plants and animals.

Recent phylogenetic analyses have shown that the Chytridiomycota as
traditionally defined is not monophyletic — some chytrid lineages are more
closely related to non-flagellated fungal groups than to other chytrids. The
group is divided into five orders based on zoospore ultrastructure:
Blastocladiales, Monoblepharidales, Neocallimastigales, Spizellomycetales, and
Chytridiales, with phylogenetic analyses supporting the monophyly of several
but not all of these orders. The [[batrachochytrium-dendrobatidis]]* (Bd), a chytrid pathogen of amphibians, is a
