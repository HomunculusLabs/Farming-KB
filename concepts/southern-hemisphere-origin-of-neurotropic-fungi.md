# Southern Hemisphere Origin of Neurotropic Fungi

## Overview

Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998) proposed that the neurotropic species of Psilocybe likely originated in the
southern hemisphere, primarily in South America, based on the markedly higher species diversity there compared to
northern temperate regions. From this putative center of origin, these fungi are thought to have dispersed northward
into Central America, North America, and eventually Europe and Asia through forest corridor pathways. This
hypothesis challenges the common assumption that mycological diversity would be greatest in the better-studied
northern hemisphere.

## The Diversity Paradox

A striking pattern emerges from the global distribution data compiled by Guzmán (1983): despite North America and
Europe being far more thoroughly explored mycologically than South America, the southern hemisphere harbors
significantly more neurotropic species. Analysis of 85 neurotropic species of Psilocybe revealed that 59
species occur in South America and Mexico (southern hemisphere and tropical latitudes), compared to only 18 species
in the United States and Canada (northern temperate zone) and 9 species in Europe (northern temperate zone).

This ratio holds even when correcting for exploration effort. Mexico alone has 44 taxa of Psilocybe, exceeding
the combined total of the US, Canada, and Europe (approximately 35 taxa). This cannot be attributed solely to
sampling bias, because coniferous forests in Mexico, which have been far more intensively surveyed than mesophytic
forests, support only 4 neurotropic species compared to 34 in the less-explored cloud forests. The genuine
ecological preference for mesophytic habitats combined with the disproportionate diversity in less-explored regions
strongly supports a southern hemisphere origin.

## Dispersal Pathways

The movement of neurotropic fungi across the Americas involved multiple directional dispersal events documented by
Guzmán (1973, 1983) and Guzmán, Montoya, and Bandala (1988).

### The Northern Invasion

Northern temperate species such as [[psilocybe-caerulipes]], common in deciduous forests of eastern North America,
reach their southern distributional limit in northeastern Mexico, specifically in Zacualtipan, Hidalgo. They occur
there through the same forest type, known in Mexico as mesophytic forest but associated with Fagus (American
beech). This represents a southward movement of temperate-adapted species along continuous forest corridors.

Similar north-to-south movements are seen in species like [[psilocybe-semilanceata]], which reaches into the Andes
of Chile but is absent from tropical Mexico. This species' absence from Mexico despite being common in both Europe
and North America suggests it may have arrived in the Americas after the original peopling of the continent, or
that Mexico's neotropical environment lacks suitable temperate grassland habitat.

### The Southern Invasion

Southern species such as [[psilocybe-yungensis]] and P. subyungensis, native to the Andean region of South America,
reached Mexico through mesophytic forests associated with Alnus (alder) trees. These Andean species found
suitable habitat in Mexico's cloud forests, demonstrating successful long-distance dispersal across thousands of
kilometers. The species name "yungensis" refers to the Yungas forests of Bolivia, indicating the origin of this
particular dispersal event.

### Mexico as a Convergence Zone

Mexico functions as a biological crossroads where northern and southern mycobiotas overlap. This convergence
explains the extraordinary diversity of neurotropic species in the country: it receives species from both
hemispheres while also harboring numerous endemics that evolved in situ within its diverse volcanic landscapes. The
complex topography of central and southern Mexico, with its numerous volcanic peaks and intermontane valleys,
creates a mosaic of microclimates that can support species from multiple biogeographic origins in close proximity.

## Trans-Pacific and Gondwanan Connections

Horak (1983) documented remarkable distributional relationships between certain agarics and boletes in the South
Pacific hemisphere. South American and Mesoamerican species show close taxonomic affinities with those of
southeastern Asia, New Guinea, Indonesia, and Australia. Shared genera include Cystoagaricus, Galerina,
Mycena, and Paxillus, suggesting ancient continental connections predating the breakup of the southern
supercontinent Gondwana.

This pattern extends directly to neurotropic fungi. [[psilocybe-samuiensis]] from Thailand is closely related to
[[psilocybe-mexicana]] from Mexico and Guatemala, both belonging to Section Mexicanae. [[psilocybe-aucklandii]]
from New Zealand is closely related to [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]] from Mexico and South America, both belonging to
Section Zapotecorum. [[psilocybe-makarorae]] from New Zealand is also related to the Mexican Section Mexicanae.

These disjunct distributions across vast oceanic distances suggest either ancient Gondwanan connections dating back
80 to 100 million years, or long-distance dispersal events via spore transport by atmospheric currents, birds, or
other vectors, followed by independent evolution in isolated regions.

## Hongo's Distribution Type Classification

Following the nine distribution types proposed by Hongo (1978), the neurotropic fungi can be categorized:

1. **Cosmopolitan**: Panaeolus spp. and [[panaeolina-foenisecii]] occur worldwide in temperate regions.
2. **Northern hemisphere**: [[amanita]] spp., Cordyceps spp., Psilocybe pelliculosa, and P. silvatica.
3. **Eurasian**: Some [[amanita]] species including A. muscaria and A. regalis.
4. **North American and Eastern Asiatic**: Connections between Psilocybe graveolens, P. muliercula, P.
   pintonii, and P. zapotecorum from the Americas and P. argentipes and P. subcaerulipes from Japan.
5. **Southeastern Asiatic**: Psilocybe subaeruginascens var. subaeruginascens from Japan and Java.
6. **Tropical and subtropical**: [[psilocybe-cubensis]], P. subcubensis, [[copelandia-cyanescens]], and C.
   tropicalis.
7. **Arctic and alpine**: [[psilocybe-aztecorum]] var. aztecorum from Mexican alpine habitats above 3,200 meters.
8. **Endemic**: Numerous species restricted to single countries or small regions.

## Africa: The Continent That Remains Unknown

Africa presents a particularly challenging biogeographic picture for understanding neurotropic fungal evolution.
Despite having extraordinarily high overall biodiversity, only 6 to 8 neurotropic species of Psilocybe are known
from the entire continent, almost certainly reflecting minimal mycological exploration rather than genuine absence.

[[psilocybe-cubensis]] may grow in Kenya despite confusing early reports that could not be confirmed. The only
confirmed indigenous species from South Africa is [[psilocybe-natalensis]], described by Gartz, Reid, Eicker, and
Smith (1995). [[psilocybe-mairei]] is known from North Africa (Morocco and Algeria) and also from southern Europe.

Samorini has suggested that indigenous use of neurotropic fungi may exist in Africa, potentially paralleling
traditions in Mexico and New Guinea, but this remains largely uninvestigated. If such traditions exist, they could
dramatically expand the known distribution of neurotropic species on the continent.

## Australian and Oceanic Distributions

The Australasian region supports a distinctive neurotropic mycota with notable endemics. Australia has approximately
10 species of Psilocybe including P. australiana, P. eucalypta, P. subaeruginosa, P. tasmaniana, and P.
collybioides. New Zealand has P. aucklandii, P. makarorae, and several species shared with Australia.

The prevalence of species associated with Eucalyptus (P. eucalypta, P. australiana) suggests co-evolution with this
distinctive southern hemisphere plant genus. New Guinea presents a particularly interesting case with species
described by Heim including [[psilocybe-kumaenorum]] and several Boletus and Russula species associated with
Kuma ceremonial use, though the neurotropic status of some of these boletes remains disputed.
