## Overview

The formulation of fungal inocula for in situ bioremediation addresses the critical gap between laboratory-scale pollutant degradation studies and field application. While laboratory experiments demonstrate that fungi can degrade a wide range of persistent organic pollutants under optimal conditions, successful field deployment requires careful attention to inoculum preparation, carrier substrates, delivery systems, and strategies for ensuring fungal survival in competitive soil environments.

## Rationale for Fungal Formulation

Fungal remediation of contaminated soil relies on two primary strategies:

### Biostimulation

Stimulation of indigenous fungal and microbial populations through nutrient amendments. For petroleum-contaminated soils, this typically involves adding nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizers — e.g., marine oil spill cleanup (Atlas & Bartha, 1992) and PAH-contaminated soil treatment. Biostimulation avoids the cost and complexity of inoculum production but depends on the presence of competent degraders.

### Bioaugmentation

Introduction of specific fungal strains with demonstrated degradation capabilities. Bioaugmentation becomes necessary when indigenous populations lack the enzymes or pathways needed to degrade target pollutants. [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]] frameworks help determine when bioaugmentation is warranted.

## Inoculum Preparation

Fungal inocula for soil bioremediation are typically prepared on solid substrates rather than in liquid culture. Solid-substrate preparation offers several advantages:

- Higher spore/mycelial biomass per unit volume
- Better survival during storage and transport
- Easier handling and soil application
- More natural growth conditions for soil-adapted fungi

Common preparation substrates include grain kernels (wheat, rye, millet), wood chips, sawdust, straw, and specialized formulated pellets. Spent mushroom substrate from commercial cultivation operations provides a low-cost inoculum source with residual enzymatic activity.

## Carrier Substrates and Amendments

### Organic Amendments

Soil amendment with organic substrates improves fungal colonization and pollutant transformation:

- **Straw**: Increases hyphal length and provides the greatest initial rate of pollutant breakdown. Straw-to-soil ratios of 1:4 have been used effectively.
- **Corn cobs**: Ground corn cob-to-soil ratios of 4:1 supported good colonization and PCP degradation (Ryan & Bumpus, 1989).
- **Compost**: Composting contaminated soil with *Phanerochaete chrysosporium* inoculation created favorable conditions for fungal growth (McFarland et al., 1996).

Amendments serve dual purposes: stimulating fungal growth and sorbing pollutants to reduce toxicity. Importantly, fungal inoculation gives greater pollutant degradation than simple amendment alone — Rosenbrock et al. (1997) showed that inoculation plus amendment degraded more dibenzo-p-dioxin than amendment alone.

### Formulated Pellets

Specially prepared pellets enable good soil colonization by multiple fungal species (Lestan & Lamar, 1996). Pellet formulation standardizes inoculum delivery and protects fungal propagules during application. This approach is crucial for [[factors-affecting-mycoremediation-efficiency]].

## Delivery Systems

### Direct Soil Incorporation

The most common method — fungal inoculum (on substrate or in pellets) is mixed into the topsoil layer. This works best when combined with tilling to distribute inoculum and amendments throughout the contaminated zone.

### Root Zone Inoculation

For mycorrhizal applications, inoculum is placed in the root zone during planting. [[mycorrhizal-fungi-rhizosphere-remediation]] depends on establishing the plant-fungus symbiosis before or during contamination exposure.

### Slurry Application

Fungal spores or mycelial fragments suspended in liquid carrier (water, nutrient broth, or surfactant solution) and sprayed onto contaminated surfaces. Useful for large-area treatment but may offer less protection against desiccation and competition.

## Survival in Field Conditions

Field deployment introduces challenges not encountered in laboratory settings:

### Environmental Stressors

- **Moisture**: Soil moisture levels critically affect fungal activity. *Lentinula edodes* was more effective at lower moisture; *P. chrysosporium* at higher moisture (Okeke et al., 1996).
- **Temperature**: Most remediation fungi are mesophilic (optimal 25–30°C). *P. chrysosporium* has an unusually high optimum of 40°C, limiting its use in cooler climates.
- **pH**: Optimal transformation of PCP occurred at pH 4 for both *L. edodes* and *P. chrysosporium* (Okeke et al., 1996).
- **Aeration**: Aerated soils favor fungal growth but may reduce manganese availability, potentially limiting MnP activity.

### Biological Competition

Indigenous soil microorganisms compete with introduced fungi for nutrients and space. Microbes antagonistic to *P. chrysosporium* have been isolated from soil (Ali & Wainwright, 1994; Radtke, Cook & Anderson, 1994). *Pleurotus* species are better soil competitors than many other white-rot fungi (Lang, Eller & Zadrazil, 1997), though *Dichomitus squalens* may stimulate overall soil microflora degradation more effectively despite being a poorer competitor.

### Soil Surfactants and Additives

Surfactants have shown positive effects on POP transformation by increasing bioavailability. PAH transformation in both liquid culture and soil was enhanced by surfactants (Boyle, Wiesner & Richardson, 1998; Kotterman et al., 1998; Bogan et al., 1999). Manganese addition stimulates atrazine biotransformation by increasing membrane permeability and MnP activity (Masaphy, Henis & Levanon, 1996).

## Monitoring Fungal Activity in Soil

Several techniques track introduced fungi:
- Image analysis for hyphal length measurement
- Extracellular enzyme activity assays (LiP, MnP, laccase)
- In situ fungal gene expression via mRNA extraction and RT-PCR
- Ergosterol and phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis for fungal biomass

## Emerging Approaches

Molecular techniques are being explored to enhance fungal remediation potential:
- Laccase and MnP genes have been introduced into high-secretion fungi like *Aspergillus oryzae* (Stewart et al., 1996; Berka et al., 1997)
- Hybrid genes combining bacterial organophosphate transformation with fungal promoters have transformed soil fungi (Xu, Wild & Kenerley, 1996)
