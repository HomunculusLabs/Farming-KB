# Soil Block Transplant System — Growing Seedlings Without Plastic Pots

## Overview

The soil block system grows seedlings in cubes of compressed potting mix rather than plastic pots or cell trays. Developed in Europe and refined by Eliot Coleman for American organic growers, soil blocks eliminate the need for plastic containers, reduce [[query-how-to-prevent-transplant-shock-when-moving-cannabis-plants]], encourage superior root development, and allow seedlings to be spaced efficiently on greenhouse benches or floors. Coleman considers soil blocks one of the essential tools for the serious organic grower, alongside items like the right-angle trowel and flame weeders.

## How Soil Blocks Work

A soil block maker (or "blocker") compresses moist potting mix into self-contained cubes. The blocks hold together through the cohesive properties of the mix—primarily the binding action of peat or coconut coir combined with the right moisture content. No container is needed because the compressed soil maintains its shape.

Each block maker creates a specific size:

- **Mini-blocks** (~¾ inch): Used for initial germination of small-seeded crops
- **2-inch blocks**: First transplant stage; mini-blocks are inserted into the indentation
- **3-inch blocks**: Used for larger transplants (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers); feature a ¾-inch cubic indentation at center for receiving smaller blocks or [[clay-seed-pellets-direct-seeding]]
- **4-inch blocks**: For large transplants requiring extended greenhouse time

The indentation (or "dimple") in the top of each block serves two purposes: it provides a recessed spot for sowing seeds, and it accepts smaller blocks for potting-on without disturbing roots.

## Advantages Over Plastic Pots

### Root Development

In plastic pots, seedling roots reach the container wall and begin circling—a condition called root-bound. Circling roots must be untangled or cut at transplanting, causing transplant shock. In soil blocks, roots reach the air at the block surface and are naturally "air-pruned." This stops root extension and stimulates lateral branching within the block, producing a dense, fibrous root system that establishes rapidly when planted out.

### Elimination of Plastic

Soil blocks eliminate the need for plastic pots, cell packs, and inserts. This reduces:

- Upfront costs for containers
- Storage space for stacks of pots between seasons
- Plastic waste and [[fertilizer-pollution-environmental-impact]]
- The labor of washing and sterilizing containers between uses

### Transplant Efficiency

Soil blocks are faster to handle in the field than potted transplants:

- No pot to remove at planting
- Blocks can be spaced tightly on greenhouse benches or even grown on the floor
- The flat bottom of each block sits level on any surface
- Seedlings can be transplanted by simply placing the block in a hole

### Space Efficiency

Soil blocks can be placed directly on the greenhouse floor without benches, or spaced at exact intervals on benches. This maximizes use of available greenhouse space. The uniform cubic shape allows precise spacing calculations for each crop type.

## Soil Block Mix

The potting mix for soil blocks must have specific properties:

- **Binding ability**: Must hold together when compressed; peat or coconut coir provides this
- **Nutrition**: Balanced fertility for seedling growth without causing excessive vegetative growth
- **Moisture retention**: Must maintain adequate moisture between waterings
- **Drainage**: Must not become waterlogged

Coleman's standard soil block mix typically includes:

- Peat moss or coconut coir (base and binder)
- Compost (nutrition and microbiology)
- Perlite or sand (drainage and aeration)
- Lime (pH adjustment if using peat)
- [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof]] blend (balanced NPK with [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-trace-minerals]])
- Soil from the farm (inoculates blocks with local biology)

The mix must be moistened to the correct consistency—like wrung-out sponge—before blocking. Too dry and blocks crumble; too wet and they deform or won't release from the blocker.

## Crop-Specific Blocking Practices

### Greenhouse Cucumbers

Coleman's greenhouse cucumber system demonstrates advanced soil block technique:

- **Block size**: 3-inch blocks with ¾-inch cubic indentation
- **Indentation fill**: Fine seed-starting mix placed in the indentation
- **Seeding**: One seed per block, sown shallowly into the fine mix
- **Germination**: Heating pad at 85°F (29°C) to optimize expensive seed germination
- **Transplant timing**: 2 weeks after sowing into greenhouse
- **Night temperature**: 55-60°F (13-15°C) for best establishment

### Tomatoes

- Start in mini-blocks on heating pad
- Progress to 2-inch blocks
- Final stage in 5-inch pots before greenhouse transplanting
- Seven-week-old plants transplanted May 1

### Peppers and Eggplants

- Sown in mini-blocks on heating pad at 80°F
- Potted up to 2-inch blocks then 5-inch pots
- Peppers transplanted at ~7 weeks old (May 1)
- Eggplants sown April 1, transplanted at ~8 weeks (June 1)
- Both trained to two stems in the greenhouse, limiting to one fruit per node

## Seed Soil Blocks for Fall Planting

For crops that need to be established in the greenhouse for fall/winter harvest:

- Seed soil blocks on October 1 for fall transplanting
- Transplants may be better in some cases than direct seeding for precise spacing
- Soil blocks allow growers to start crops in a protected environment and move them to the field when conditions are right

## Floor Growing

Coleman notes that in his system, seedlings in soil blocks can be grown directly on the greenhouse floor rather than requiring benches. This is possible because:

- Blocks are self-supporting and don't need container support
- The flat bottom allows stable placement on any level surface
- Irrigation can be managed through floor-level watering or misting
- Spacing is controlled by block placement rather than container size

## Air Pruning Biology

When a root tip encounters air at the block surface, the exposed tip desiccates and dies. The plant responds by:

1. Releasing auxin from the root tip, which normally inhibits lateral root formation
2. Without the inhibiting auxin signal, lateral root primordia behind the tip are activated
3. Multiple new lateral roots emerge within the block, creating a branched root system
4. These lateral roots eventually reach the block surface and are also air-pruned
5. The cycle repeats, producing an increasingly dense, fibrous root mass

This natural process is far superior to the mechanical root pruning sometimes done at transplanting, because it occurs gradually during the entire growth period in the block.

## Tools and Equipment

Key soil blocking equipment includes:

- **Block makers** (multiple sizes: mini, 2-inch, 3-inch, 4-inch)
- **Mixing tub** for moistening the potting mix
- **Flat trays** for holding blocks during germination and growth
- **Heating pads** for bottom heat during germination
- **Trowel** (right-angle type preferred for efficient block handling)

Commercial sources offer complete lines of soil block makers, blocking mixes, and related equipment.

## Key Principle

Soil blocks represent a fundamentally different approach to [[coleman-sowing-seedling-production]] that works with plant biology rather than against it. By allowing natural air pruning of roots and eliminating the artificial container boundary, soil blocks produce seedlings with superior root systems that transplant with minimal shock. The system is particularly well-suited to organic operations where reducing plastic use and working with natural processes are core values.
