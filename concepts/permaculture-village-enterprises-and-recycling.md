## Permaculture Village Enterprises and Recycling Systems

A [[permaculture]] village is designed to be economically self-reliant, with residents earning their living primarily through enterprises that serve the village itself and the surrounding district. This model inverts the conventional pattern where residents commute to external employment and import most of their needs. Instead, the village becomes a productive economic unit where money circulates internally, building local wealth and reducing dependence on distant supply chains.

## Analyzing Village Economic Potential

For a typical **50-house (100 adult) village**, costs are highest in three areas: food, energy, and transport. Understanding how residents can earn their living in each of these sectors reveals the economic foundation of a self-reliant village.

### Food Enterprises (5-7 families)

About 20 adults can support 1 adult providing a food supply, meaning that 5 to 7 families can earn a living from food-related enterprises:

- **Open-air market gardens**: Fresh [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]] for village and district markets
- **Glasshouse crops**: [[mollison-designers-glasshouse-protected-cropping]] for higher-value produce and year-round production
- **Cooperative store**: A village store serving local trade and visitor sales
- **Domestic livestock and fishery**: Chickens, goats, pigs, sheep, rabbits, and aquaculture
- **Cafe and food processing**: Part-time livelihoods from prepared foods and value-added products
- **Baked goods**: Artisanal baking for village consumption and visitor sales
- **Village market**: A venue for selling surplus to visitors and locals from outside the village

All surplus production can be sold through the village market or cooperative store, creating income from the village"s productive capacity.

### Energy Enterprises (2-5 families)

Energy needs include space heating, cooking, electricity, hot water, gas, and appliances. To establish energy-efficient systems, plans must include excellent house design, retrofit capability for inefficient houses, insulation, and good appliances. Most investments amortize in 3-7 years.

Livelihoods in this sector come from:
- **House design and retrofit**: Designing energy-efficient homes and upgrading existing stock
- **Contract and extension work**: Extending services beyond the village
- **Hardware and appliance sales**: Supplying the village and district with [[appropriate-technology]]
- **Energy system installation and maintenance**: Installing, repairing, and tending [[village-energy-systems-and-conversion-efficiency]]
- **Local manufacturing**: Over time, developing balanced trade in appliances manufactured within the village

### Vehicle and Transport Enterprises (2-4 families)

Vehicles are often the most costly item in a household budget and require careful, planned management:

- **Vehicle service and repairs**: Maintenance of village and district vehicles
- **Bulk fuel and oil supply**: Leveraging collective purchasing power
- **Alternative fuel production**: Growing, distilling, or fermenting fuels for engines
- **Fleet lease and insurance**: Village-scale vehicle leasing systems
- **Tractor and special vehicle lease**: Sharing expensive equipment across the village
- **Alliance-level manufacturing**: Village alliances can establish special engine or chassis manufacture

### Financial Services (2 people)

Handling income, loans, and accounting of enterprises, and running a credit union is essential [[permaculture-village-infrastructure-and-dwelling-design]]:

- **Community credit union**: Holding insurance money as well as incomes, funding enterprises such as energy systems, dairies, and food processing centres
- **Computer-based accounting**: Managing the financial complexity of multiple village enterprises
- **Managerial services**: Forward planning, market research, and development support

### Medical and Pharmaceutical Services (1-2 people)

- **Medical services**: Prescription, massage, counselling, and local treatment
- **Local remedies**: Manufacturing medications that can be sold more widely
- **Pharmaceutical production**: Growing or manufacturing specific remedies
- **Prevention-based health**: Community health programs emphasizing illness prevention

### Building and Maintenance (1+ person)

A plumber-builder-mechanic can serve village establishment needs and later maintenance. With a woodworking area, useful furnishings and sale items can be produced for extra income.

## Essential vs. Extended Enterprises

The essential occupations listed above — covering shelter, food, economy, energy, transport, and health — would initially fund about **20 of the 50 households**, with more employment developing as manufacturing grows.

Beyond the essentials, a wide range of potential village enterprises can develop from both land resources and local skills:

### Land-Based Enterprises
- Glasshouse crops and special crops (cut flowers, herbs, pharmaceutical plants)
- Processed dairy products
- Fish and aquaculture
- Nursery and plant production (plant materials for site, fire control, bees, orchards, forages)
- Animal breeding programs (special poultry, pigeon, sheep, or goat breeds as small studs)

### Skill-Based Enterprises
- **Education**: Teachers for children, adult education, and applied workshops on site
- **Workshops**: Craft, medical, or design workshops employing 3-4 people
- **Business service centre**: Small business support for 20+ village enterprises
- **Consultancy**: Architecture, landscape, and design consultancy for other sites
- **Computer services**: Programming, databases, and data services to a network
- **Publishing**: Computer word processing and typesetting services
- **Trade and distribution**: Distribution rights, wholesale, import-export, and village trade networks
- **Craft products**: Metal or wood workshops, pottery, and art
- **Tourism**: Income from guests, visitors, and site tours (if the village concurs)
- **Physical therapy**: Massage and paramedical services, especially for stress-related problems

## Enterprise Development Principles

### Capital and Management
All village enterprises depend on two basic factors:

- **Capital**: Enough money to start up and develop. Many small enterprises can pre-sell their wares for initial capital. Others need no capital to start but require time to develop. Skills-only businesses (massage, paramedical) need only skills to begin, while others (market gardens, orchards, furniture making) need skills, capital, and time.
- **Management**: Careful accounting, forward planning, market research and development, product development, sensible costing and staffing, correct lease and rental agreements, and appropriate legal structures.

A small business service centre is economical and necessary for 20 or more businesses, providing shared administrative support.

### Priority Assessment
A careful assessment of skills and available capital indicates priorities in any village:
- **Income-earning for local development** is the top priority
- **Capital-intensive schemes** (e.g., commercial pottery) should wait until capital accumulates
- **Information-based income** (workshops and classes) provides the most reliable early income, though workshop income fades as students gain skills themselves
- **Workshops yield capital** for further local developments and can establish the village as a special education centre

### Job Sharing as a Principle

It should be the long-term aim of any village for residents to own and operate their own employment enterprises. In traditional villages, it was unusual for a person to hold just one job — the banker was also a part-time barber, trader, and perhaps gardener. Sharing occupations so that individuals have stakes in 2-3 enterprises is wise because:

- Total failure is unlikely when income comes from multiple sources
- Unemployment is virtually eliminated
- Holidays can be taken without disrupting essential services
- Wet days can be spent on indoor work
- Occupational diversity mirrors the ecological principle of polyculture — the total village structure is complex, but the work of any individual is simple

## Recycling in the Community: The Devonport Model

The borough of Devonport, in Auckland, New Zealand, provides a proven model for comprehensive [[community-recycling-and-waste-recovery-systems]]. Their system returns a **cash benefit to households** — the opposite of the cash payment (rates) that households in non-recycling areas pay to their councils.

### Key Features of the Devonport System

**1. Color-coded waste calendar**: The borough issues an annual calendar, color-coded by waste category. On any given day, only one category is collected — clean glass, metal, tyres, paper, organic waste, oils, etc. No mixed loads are accepted. This forces waste **separation at the source**, which is the critical first step in effective recycling.

**2. Supervised waste disposal site**: At the waste site, loads must also be in a single category. Here, materials are processed according to type:

- **Wood** is sorted into useful wood (for a community woodwork centre), firewood (issued to elderly people), and chipping or mulch wood
- **All organics** are composted using a small tractor windrow system
- **All oils** are collected, filtered, and re-sold as lubrication oils
- **[[compost]] and mulch** are sold commercially, with surplus used in community gardens

**3. The Ziggurat**: Surplus materials are used to construct a "ziggurat" — an ascending spiral ramp made of broken pipe, brick, clay, concrete, and clean fill. As the ziggurat ascends, community organic gardens follow the fill, creating productive growing space from what was formerly waste.

**4. Site supervision**: The waste site is supervised, loads are directed to appropriate areas, and mixed waste is sorted on-site. Saleable or recyclable items are grouped in clearly marked areas.

### Lessons for Village Design

The Devonport model demonstrates that comprehensive recycling is not only environmentally beneficial but **economically profitable** when properly organized. Key principles for village-scale recycling include:

- **Source separation is essential**: Single-stream collection makes recycling difficult; category-specific collection makes it efficient
- **Waste is a resource**: Every category of waste has value if properly sorted and processed
- **Community facilities enable recovery**: A woodwork centre, [[composting]] operation, and re-use areas turn waste into productive inputs
- **Supervision ensures compliance**: Without oversight, the system degrades into mixed waste
- **Creative reuse of inert materials**: The ziggurat concept demonstrates that even construction debris can become productive landscape
