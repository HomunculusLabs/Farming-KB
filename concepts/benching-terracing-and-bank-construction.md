## Benching, Terracing, and Bank Construction

## Overview

Banks, benches, and terraces are fundamental earthwork techniques in permaculture for converting steep, [[savannah-forestry-and-difficult-terrain-pioneering]] into productive, accessible, and stable landscapes. These structures manage water flow, [[query-how-do-i-garden-on-a-slope-and-prevent-erosion]], create planting surfaces on slopes, and provide access for cultivation, planting, and harvesting. Understanding their [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]] is critical for creating earthworks that are stable, functional, and long-lasting.

## Banks

### Construction Economics

It is cheaper to construct a bank than to step or retain the earth, provided the bank can be made stable. Wherever a four-way bucket (Bobcat or Drott) or power shovel can be used to dish the bank, they will remain stable at greater slopes than a straight cut, because the dished profile frustrates soil slump by giving it the profile it would achieve had it slumped naturally.

Banks or cuts more than 4 meters high need careful stabilization planning and execution.

### Bank Stability Factors

Unlike dam walls, cuts for roads are not normally compacted and do not necessarily have much pressure on them. The greatest causes of bank slump are:

- **Water flow** through or over banks — the primary cause of failure
- **Lie of strata** in shaly ground — tilted rock layers create planes of weakness
- **Straight-cut profiles** — which will slump to concave over time regardless

All normal soils with some clay content and stony banks are fairly stable when:
- The angle of repose is preserved
- Banks are cut with concave profiles
- Adequate drainage is provided

### Retaining Wall Essentials

In severe slump areas, options include re-routing roads, expensive drainage systems, or concrete retaining walls as last resorts. Retaining walls should incorporate:

- **Drainage behind the wall** — weep holes or gravel drainage layers to relieve hydrostatic pressure
- **Proper foundation** — footing depth below the frost line or failure plane
- **Tie-backs or anchors** — for walls over 1 meter height
- **Vegetation above and below** — plant roots stabilize the soil mass that the wall retains

## Benching

### What Is a Bench?

A bench is a flat, near-contoured cut made in a slope. It creates a shelf or ledge that provides a level working surface on otherwise unusable steep terrain. Very severe slopes of 30–40° can be bench-cut if a bulldozer can start on safe ground (typically from a ridge of 20° or so).

### Uses of Benches

- **Roads and access tracks** — providing traversable surfaces across steep terrain
- **House sites** — creating level building platforms on hillsides
- **Forestry** — benches every 100 m or so on steep hills greatly aid access, planting, and eventual harvest
- **Tree shelves** — narrow benches that serve as planting platforms for orchard or timber trees
- **Garden terraces** — creating cultivable land on steep properties

### Forestry Bench Design

When benching for forestry on steep hills:
1. Plant fast-growing trees on the lower (loose soil) side of benches first — these can be nut or fruit trees for harvest, or seed crop trees
2. Benches greatly aid access, planting, and harvesting operations
3. A quiet traverse with a donkey or cart serves to distribute plants, fertilizer, and mulch, and to gather in the crop

### The Kick-Down System

Small-holders on very steep hillsides must work with slopes rather than against them, or expend much energy carrying water and fertilizer uphill. The most efficient system is a gravity-fed cascade:

**Orchard and mulch above → poultry above → garden below**

The ridge or hill above this system should be planted to mulch-producing trees such as Casuarina, pine, oak, and beech. Mulch and bedding is thrown downhill as greenfeed and seed for chickens, and they kick down to the lower fence where the gardener accepts the manured and shredded mulch for the essential terraced garden beds.

### Bench Construction

Benches are quickly made to a survey line pegged every 6 m (20 feet) or so, followed by a bulldozer with blade set to side-cast (angled). Key construction steps:

1. **Survey and peg** the bench line on contour at the desired vertical interval
2. **Side-cast** with an angled blade — the blade is angled to the direction of travel so dirt is cast to one side rather than pushed ahead
3. **Raise the blade** on a second pass to shave back or slightly step the uphill bank
4. **Cross-walls** every 20–30 m to prevent gutter flow along the bench surface

### Bench Drainage and Design

- Benches on very steep slopes (rarely traveled) can slope slightly outwards to drain
- In stable soils, benches can slope into the hill to form swales that infiltrate water for trees below the bench
- Benching should not necessarily continue across water runnels unless pipes, gabions, or culverts are fitted in the watercourses
- Small watercourses can be bridged rather than benched through
- Wherever benches cross drainage channels, pipes or rock-lined swales must carry water across — these and their maintenance are the greatest long-term expense

### Narrow Benches

Narrow benches (1.5–3.0 m wide) are particularly effective:
- Quickly overhung by tree crop canopy, providing shade
- Stable due to their small cross-section
- Accessible for maintenance and harvest
- Quick and inexpensive to construct

## Terracing

### Terraced Production Systems

Terraced lands, given a reserve of local [[cover-crops]] or composts and adequate water, are potentially very stable and productive systems. However, terraces can fail when designers:

- **Attempt to terrace in unstable soils or sediments** — slopes with active mass movement, quick clays, or hydrophobic post-fire soils
- **Risk hydraulic pressures** from impounded or infiltrated water building up behind terrace walls
- **Create unstable bund or wall faces** — walls that are too steep for the soil material or without proper drainage
- **Extend terracing as annual crop over too large an area** — requiring excessive external inputs to maintain fertility

### Terracing vs. Benching

While related, terraces and benches differ in important ways:
- **Terraces** typically have retaining walls or bunds to hold soil on steep slopes for [[latitude-effects-on-photosynthesis-and-crop-production]], often with [[water-management]] features
- **Benches** are simpler cuts into the slope, often without retaining walls, used more for access and [[tree-establishment-in-deserts]]

## Machine Operation Principles

### Side-Casting Efficiency

Side-casting is the most efficient method for creating benches over long distances. Graders and bulldozers can bench soils effectively for kilometers because no dirt is carried — it is cast to one side of the angled blade. This saves enormous amounts of time, fuel, money, and timber compared to pushing soil ahead.

### Machine Selection

- **Bulldozers** can loosen and pile up material but cannot load it; they bench but cannot trench (at least not smaller than blade width)
- **Bucket diggers** can load material and work effectively upslope, unlike bulldozers
- **On complex jobs**, two or more machines may be needed — e.g., preparing a house site and its sewage lines simultaneously, or digging and carting a clay deposit

### Designing for Machine Efficiency

Designers should imagine themselves as the machine driver and make it as easy as possible to cut the desired shapes:

- **Maximum push distance** — about 6–9 machine lengths before the blade, so plan spoil areas accordingly
- **Direction of work** — much faster and better work is done cross-slope and downhill than upslope by any machine except a bucket digger
- **Peg clearly** — well-placed pegs with visible markers allow the operator to work efficiently without constant supervision
- **Designate stages** — plan the sequence of earthworks to minimize machine movement and re-handling of material
- **Plan spoil areas and topsoil stores** — designate these before work begins so the operator knows where to place material
- **Specify bank slopes** — clearly communicate desired slope angles for cuts and fills
