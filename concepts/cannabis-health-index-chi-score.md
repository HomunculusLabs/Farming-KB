# Cannabis Health Index (CHI) Score

The Cannabis Health Index (CHI) is an evidence-based scoring system developed by Uwe Blesching, PhD, for evaluating the scientific evidence supporting cannabis as a treatment for specific diseases and symptoms. Published in *The Cannabis Health Index* (North Atlantic Books, 2015), the CHI provides a systematic framework for patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers to assess the strength of available evidence and make informed decisions about cannabinoid therapy. The CHI covers more than 100 chronic symptoms and diseases, drawing on over 15,000 entries in the scientific literature on medical cannabis.

## Purpose and Context

The CHI was created to address a fundamental problem in medical cannabis: the lack of a systematic, evidence-based model for the plant's safe and targeted use. While the available scientific literature on medical cannabis has grown enormously (more than 15,000 studies indexed in the U.S. National Library of Medicine), most patients and practitioners lack the tools to evaluate the quality and relevance of this evidence for specific conditions. The CHI fills this gap by:

1. Compiling and reviewing the available literature for each condition
2. Assigning an evidence score based on the type and quality of research
3. Providing strain-specific and form-specific considerations
4. Integrating mind-body medicine perspectives alongside pharmacological evidence

## How the CHI Score Works

The CHI score evaluates the quality of scientific evidence for cannabis as a treatment for a specific condition. The score takes into account the hierarchy of research study types, with higher-quality evidence receiving higher scores. The importance of the type of research study is fundamental to understanding the CHI system.

### Study Type Hierarchy

The CHI follows the standard evidence-based medicine hierarchy, where certain types of studies provide stronger evidence than others:

1. **Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)**: The gold standard of clinical research. Patients are randomly assigned to treatment or control groups, minimizing bias. RCTs provide the strongest evidence for treatment efficacy.

2. **Controlled clinical trials**: Similar to RCTs but may lack randomization or adequate blinding. Still provide valuable evidence but are more susceptible to bias.

3. **Observational studies**: Cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies that observe outcomes without experimental intervention. Useful for identifying associations but cannot establish causation.

4. **Case series and case reports**: Descriptions of individual patients or small groups who received a treatment. Useful for generating hypotheses but providing the weakest evidence for treatment efficacy.

5. **Preclinical studies**: Animal studies and in vitro (laboratory) experiments. Essential for understanding mechanisms of action but their results cannot be directly extrapolated to humans.

6. **Reviews and meta-analyses**: Systematic reviews that synthesize the results of multiple studies. A well-conducted meta-analysis of RCTs provides the strongest possible evidence.

### CHI Score Ranges

The CHI score reflects both the quantity and quality of available evidence for a given condition. Higher scores indicate stronger evidence:

- **Low score**: Limited or weak evidence (primarily case reports, preclinical studies, or small uncontrolled studies)
- **Moderate score**: Moderate evidence (controlled studies with some limitations, or multiple consistent observational studies)
- **High score**: Strong evidence (well-designed RCTs, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses supporting efficacy)

The specific scoring methodology allows users to quickly assess how much confidence they can place in the evidence for cannabis as a treatment for their particular condition.

## Using the CHI Score in Practice

### General Guidelines

The CHI is designed to be used as a decision-support tool, not as a substitute for professional medical advice. General usage principles include:

1. **Higher scores = more confidence**: A higher CHI score for a particular condition means there is stronger scientific evidence supporting cannabis as a treatment. This does not guarantee efficacy for any individual patient, but it does indicate that the evidence base is more robust.

2. **Consider the full picture**: The CHI score should be considered alongside other factors including the patient's overall health, current medications, previous treatment experiences, and the potential risks and benefits of cannabis use.

3. **Evidence evolves**: The CHI is a snapshot of the evidence available at the time of publication (2015). New studies are published regularly, and the evidence base for many conditions continues to grow. Users should seek updated information when possible.

### For Specific Studies

When evaluating a specific research study within the CHI framework, users should consider:

- **Study design**: Was the study an RCT, an observational study, or a case report? The study type determines the weight of its contribution to the CHI score.
- **Sample size**: Larger studies generally provide more reliable results than smaller ones.
- **Outcome measures**: What endpoints were measured, and were they clinically meaningful?
- **Cannabis preparation**: What form of cannabis was used (smoked, vaporized, oral, topical)? What was the THC/CBD ratio? Was it a whole-plant product or an isolated cannabinoid?
- **Dosing**: What dose was used, and was it optimized through dose-finding studies?

## Evidence-Based Strain and Form Considerations

The CHI incorporates considerations for the specific type of cannabis preparation used in the supporting studies:

### Strain Considerations
- **Sativa vs. Indica**: Different cannabis strains (and sativa/indica classifications) may produce different therapeutic effects due to their distinct cannabinoid and [[blesching-cannabis-emotional-processing-expansive-constricting-emotions]], and personal growth.

### Powerful Questions
The CHI includes self-guided questions designed to help patients deepen their healing process, including inquiries about emotional blocks, limiting beliefs, lifestyle factors, and the psychosocial dimensions of their illness.

## Mind-Body Connections to the ECS

The CHI draws explicit connections between emotional states and the endocannabinoid system, proposing that emotional patterns influence ECS function:

- **Expansive emotions** (associated with molecules like acetylcholine, anandamide, [[expansive-constricting-emotions-neurotransmitter-associations-blesching]]** (associated with epinephrine, dopamine, norepinephrine, cortisol, glutamate, and vasopressin) may impair ECS function and hinder healing

This framework suggests that emotional healing and pharmacological treatment are complementary rather than alternative approaches, and that optimizing emotional well-being may enhance the therapeutic effects of cannabinoid therapy.

## Conditions Covered

The CHI evaluates evidence for cannabis across a comprehensive range of conditions organized into categories including:

- **Cancer** (general and site-specific: brain, breast, colon, liver, lung, pancreatic, prostate, and others)
- **Cardiovascular disease** (heart disease, hypertension, stroke)
- **Infectious diseases** (bacterial, viral including HIV/AIDS, hepatitis, herpes)
- **[[blesching-cannabis-inflammatory-bowel-disease]])
- **Mental disorders** (anxiety, depression, PTSD, schizophrenia, ADHD, autism)
- **Neurological diseases** (Alzheimer's, ALS, epilepsy, Huntington's, MS, Parkinson's, Tourette's)
- **Pain** (chronic, migraine, neuropathic, cancer-related)
- **Gastrointestinal conditions** (GERD, IBD/IBS, pancreatitis)
- **Respiratory diseases** (asthma, COPD)
- **Skin diseases** (acne, dermatitis, psoriasis)
- **Other conditions** (diabetes, fibromyalgia, insomnia, osteoporosis, sickle cell disease)

## Limitations

- The CHI was published in 2015; the evidence base has grown significantly since then
- The quality of individual studies varies, and even a high CHI score does not guarantee that cannabis will be effective for any particular individual
- Much of the research uses synthetic or isolated cannabinoids rather than whole-plant cannabis, limiting the applicability of findings to natural cannabis products
- Publication bias (favoring positive results) may inflate the apparent strength of evidence for some conditions
