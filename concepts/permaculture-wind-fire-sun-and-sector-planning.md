## Overview

Sector planning is the process of mapping and managing the energies that flow into and across a site from outside — wind, fire, sun, water, noise, privacy, and views. Combined with zonation (which manages distances from the core), sector analysis creates the complete spatial framework of a [[permaculture-design]]. Mollison emphasizes that the designer must define these extrinsic energies for each site regarding direction, intensity, and frequency before placing any elements.

## Defining Extrinsic Energies

Every site receives energies from outside its boundaries. These include:

- **Sunlight and heat**: Direct radiation, the elevation of the sun, and seasonal variation
- **Cold air masses**: Lateral transfer of colds traveling across the surface
- **Hot air masses**: Continental interior winds, desert winds
- **Fire**: Catastrophic [[wildfire]] approaching from specific directions
- **Water flow**: Both surface runoff and groundwater movement
- **Air flow**: Wind patterns including circulation cells and channeling effects
- **Noise**: Roads, industrial areas, neighbors
- **Views**: Distant mountains, valleys, water features
- **Privacy**: Visual exposure from roads, neighbors, public areas

The evaluation rule: if both intensity and frequency are low, the energy may not even warrant consideration. If either is high — high frequency at any intensity, or high intensity at any frequency — the designer takes note. It is up to the designer to define the number of influences that affect any center point on the site.

## Wind Analysis

### Reading Wind on Site

Forget the local weatherman — the station sits in a flat location and makes abstract observations that do not apply to specific sites. The site itself tells you what happens through wind-flagged trees, vegetation patterns, erosion, and microclimatic evidence. A brisk southwesterly wind hitting a mountainside may wrap around due east. Every southwesterly produces a screaming easterly on the lee side. These channeling effects must be mapped on-site.

### Cold Winds

Cold winds require sector-based defense. The designer must treat the whole sector, not just a single point. Access roads should be brought to the side of the house site so it is possible to defend the house from the wind path created by the access. Placing the house on the edge of the property where cold winds arrive is a type-one error — move the client inward if possible.

### Hot Winds

Hot desert winds can be used for cooling. A wind-dodger (a small sail) brings hot wind down to pots of water with wicks — the hotter the wind blows, the faster water rises in the wicks, producing a profound cooling effect through evaporation.

### Wind for Heating

A vertical-axis windmill stirring a water kettle with fixed vanes creates tremendous turbulence. The force of wind generates heat — the colder it blows, the hotter the kettle gets. Canadians use this technique for winter heating.

### Wind-View Conflicts

One of the worst design problems is the wind-view conflict. Even a small hole through vegetation creates a draft. Leaving a gate open into a garden is very destructive. When a client demands a view that exposes them to severe wind, the solution is to build a small retreat shelter (with cupola and seating) in zone three with the view, and keep the dwelling sheltered. Clients respond well to the idea of making "an expedition to the view" — they actually look at it more carefully when they go there specifically for that purpose.

## Sun and Thermal Position

The elevation of the sun, the slope of the surface, and the flow of air all affect placement. A house on a bare ridge top changes hands every four years because of energy expense. A happy place with good thermal positioning rarely changes hands — perhaps three or four times in 200 years. The fewer trees around a dwelling, the more often it resells. Lack of trees is miserable for both cattle and people.

For houses where winters are long and cold, bring pines or columnar evergreens tightly against the house for warmth, then zone outward. These plantings provide mulch, winter bird and animal refuge, and fire protection.

## Fire Sector Planning

### Fire-Immune Elements

Several zonation elements can intercept fire:

- **Mud crops**: Wet areas, ponds, swales
- **Mulched gardens**: Moist, low-fuel surfaces
- **Roads**: Bare mineral earth breaks
- **Short grazers**: Wallabies and other animals that keep vegetation low
- **Summer-green systems**: Deciduous plants that drop leaves (reducing fuel load)
- **Low-litter plants**: Species that do not accumulate flammable debris

### Fire and Access Design

The windbreak for fire can also serve as the setup for feeding pigs. A tall forest of marsh species on rises (where no high hills exist on the far side) provides both wind protection and a radiation break close to the house. The same plantings can be mulch-productive and serve as winter refuge for birds and animals.

### Strategic Placement

Verify that the house site is not in a fire funnel — valleys that channel wildfire with catastrophic speed. A house on a ridge in a valley is doubly exposed: the valley funnels wind and the ridge catches fire from any direction. Place the client just below the ridge, backing into the mountain and facing the sun.

## The Three-Dimensional System

Design is a three-dimensional system incorporating:

- **Length, breadth, depth**: Physical dimensions of the site
- **Elevation**: Sun angle, slope, water flow, air movement
- **Time**: Evolution of the design, seasonal variation, succession

All elements must be used to greatest benefit. The designer should work with architects, as the interaction between landscape designer and architect is fruitful. Orientation, surfaces around dwellings, and the whole site pattern all factor into building placement.

## Time as a Design Element

The time element requires planning for evolution. Set priorities since no client has unlimited resources:

1. **Energy-productive systems** first
2. **Energy-conserving systems** second or concurrently
3. **Energy-consuming systems** last

Design for caloric efficiency. Understand that the near view (quail in the greenhouse, bird-feeding table outside a child's window, a fuchsia half-covering a professor's window) will occupy far more of the client's attention than the distant panorama. The best views for children, elderly, or sick people come from bird-attracting shrubs placed directly against their windows.

## Practical Sector Solutions

Mollison provides multiple specific solutions demonstrating the [[permaculture]] principle that solutions should be multiple and every element should serve many functions:

- A windbreak that also collects manure, keeps grass down, and defends against fire
- A pond that grows watercress, breeds frogs for pest control, provides wildlife habitat, and creates a firebreak
- An access road that drains properly, requires minimal maintenance, protects against fire, reduces snow drifting, and provides solar exposure in winter

The ironclad rule: for every element placed, the designer must have answers when asked "Why did you put that there?" Multiple answers indicate good design.

## Circulation Cells and Complex Patterns

In some regions, winds start circling in steadily from a specific sector, creating a circulation cell. Defenses must treat the whole sector, not just a point. Understanding these patterns — which become apparent only through direct site observation — is essential for effective sector planning.

## Aboriginal Ecological Management

Aboriginal Australians managed their landscape through controlled burning, creating specific fire regimes that produced the edge-rich patterns Mollison admired. Their ceremonial stone patterns and totemic garden layouts represent another form of sector planning — one where cultural practice, ecology, and design are unified.

## The Client and the Environment

The designer's ultimate responsibility is to make the marriage between what the client wants and what the environment needs, looking out for the environment before looking after the client. Keep the swan, protect the emus, and still look after the client — using useful corridors, guarded gardens, and controlled access for wild elements rather than extermination.
