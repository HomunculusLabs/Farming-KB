# Dark Night of the Soul: Meditation Adverse Effects and [[blesching-cannabis-mind-body-medicine|Mind]]-Body Risks

**Source:** [[blesching-cannabis-health-index-mental-health-applications|Blesching]], U. — *The [[cannabis-mind-body-medicine-integration-framework-blesching|Cannabis]] Health Index*
**Concept:** The potential dangers of mind-body medicine practices including meditation, mindfulness, and [[blesching-cannabis-epigenetics-and-emotional-trauma|emotional]] release work — from "the dark night of the soul" to Stanislav Grof's "unresolved ego-deaths."

---

## Overview

After acknowledging the vast and significant potential health benefits of mind-body medicine, it is equally important to examine the rare but potential downside. Observations from long-term mindfulness practitioners, psychotherapists, meditation teachers, and shamanic practitioners suggest that a very small percentage of people using mind-body modalities may experience significant disturbances.

These disturbances are not widely discussed in popular mindfulness literature, yet they represent a recognized clinical phenomenon across multiple contemplative traditions and modern [[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]]. Understanding these risks is essential for safe practice, proper facilitation, and informed consent.

---

## Forms of Disturbance

The disturbance may express itself through various manifestations:
- Confusion and disorientation
- Feeling lost or untethered
- A psychotic episode
- The end of a significant relationship
- Difficulty concentrating during daily work
- Spiritual crisis or existential emergency
- Dissociation from ordinary reality
- Overwhelming emotional release that cannot be contained
- Physical symptoms without clear medical cause

These reactions can be triggered by meditation, breathwork, emotional release techniques, bodywork, or any practice that alters ordinary consciousness and opens suppressed psychological material.

## Historical and Cross-Cultural Recognition

### The Christian Tradition: "Dark Night of the Soul"

In Christian spiritual traditions, this type of disturbance — brought on by prayer or visionary awareness — is referred to as "the dark night of the soul." The concept was articulated by St. John of the Cross (1542–1591) and describes a spiritual crisis on a person's journey toward union with God. It represents the painful transition where old attachments and identity structures dissolve before new spiritual maturity emerges.

The dark night is not considered a failure of practice but rather a necessary — though extremely challenging — phase of deep spiritual development. However, without proper support and guidance, practitioners can become stuck in this phase indefinitely.

### The Buddhist Tradition: "Enlightenment's Evil Twin"

In meditation communities, this type of disturbance may be spoken of as "enlightenment's evil twin" — a phenomenon where meditative insights go wrong. It is often related to:
- Irreversible insights such as staring into "emptiness" (śūnyatā)
- Experiencing "no-self" (anattā) in a way that is destabilizing rather than liberating
- The dissolution of ego boundaries before the practitioner has developed sufficient psychological integration

The term captures the paradox that the very insights that lead to enlightenment can, when encountered prematurely or without support, produce states that mirror mental illness.

### Modern Psychedelic Research: "Unresolved Ego-Deaths"

[[stanislav-grof]], MD, who conducted extensive research into extraordinary [[mckenna-food-gods-designer-states-of-consciousness]] — first using [[lsd]] and later [[metzner-holotropic-breathwork-and-grof-perinatal-matrices]] — called these events "unresolved ego-deaths." Grof's framework recognizes that:
- The psyche has a natural tendency toward wholeness and healing
- This healing process can involve confronting traumatic material stored in the unconscious
- When the ego structure temporarily dissolves during these confrontations, it can be experienced as a form of "death"
- If this process is not completed — if the ego does not successfully reconstitute in a healthier form — the result is an "unresolved ego-death"
- The person may remain in a fragmented state, unable to function normally

## The NIH Perspective

The U.S. National Institutes of Health addresses meditation risks on their website:

> Meditation is considered to be safe for healthy people. There have been rare reports that meditation could cause or worsen symptoms in people who have certain psychiatric problems, but this question has not been fully researched. Individuals with existing mental or physical health conditions should speak with their health care providers prior to starting a meditative practice and make their meditation instructor aware of their condition.

Key points from the NIH advisory:
- Meditation is "considered safe for healthy people" — but this qualifier leaves significant ambiguity
- "Certain psychiatric problems" is undefined, leaving practitioners to self-assess vulnerability
- The relationship between meditation and psychiatric worsening "has not been fully researched"
- Pre-existing conditions require both medical and instructor consultation

## The Four Approaches to Mind-Body Medicine

Blesching identifies four basic approaches that attempt to integrate mind-body medicine in different ways:

### 1. Psychiatry
The psychiatrist (viewed as expert) diagnoses the problem and primarily prescribes mind/mood-altering pharmaceuticals with little focus on underlying influences. Expediency drives this approach — waiting rooms are full and lists are long, so prescriptions are faster than depth work.

### 2. Psychology
A psychologist or therapist (viewed as expert) conducts an initial interview, identifies a problem area including underlying causes, and employs specific techniques to work with the client toward desired changes. The therapist guides the process.

### 3. Transpersonal Psychology
An Eastern-influenced psycho-spiritual approach where the patient embarks on a therapeutic journey emphasizing self-responsibility for problem discovery, origins, and self-guided treatments such as mindfulness and spiritual practice. If a therapist is employed, they act as a catalyst for the patient's own truth-finding.

### 4. Shamanic Approach
The person engages the assistance of a plant ally (such as cannabis) to find and make conscious that which obstructs healing, and to explore ways of replacing it with what supports health and well-being. The emphasis is on direct experience and plant-assisted self-discovery.
