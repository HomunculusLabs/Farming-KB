# Community Design in Permaculture

Updated: 2026-04-18

Permaculture design extends beyond individual properties to encompass community-scale systems. The PDC course material treats community as essential to permaculture because natural systems are maintained by communities of species working in symbiosis, and human-designed systems reach their highest potential when plugged into community networks.

## Why Community Matters

- A permaculture system in your backyard should benefit the Earth, other people, and be shareable.
- Natural systems benefit from and depend on the "network effect" -- the more individuals involved, the more value for each.
- Humans have lost the instinct to involve themselves in natural networks and need organizational systems to assume their role.
- With community, you can: swap seeds, trade for supplies, coordinate food strategies, organize work parties, and access pools of talent and knowledge.

## Creating Intentional Community

### Step 1: Setting Intention

The community should create organized Mission and Vision statements:

- Mission statements articulate what you want to accomplish and why (goal-oriented).
- Vision statements describe what the mission will look like when manifest (often covers the "how").
- Process: each person writes one sentence, reduce redundancies, synthesize into a mission statement (about three sentences) and a vision statement (three to four sentences).
- Apply permaculture principles: use and value diversity, integrate rather than segregate.
- The question is not whether you get your way entirely, but whether you can live within the shared intention.

### Step 2: Organizational Structure -- The Stewardship Mandala

The PDC presents a non-hierarchical organizational structure:

**Structure:**
- Composed of individual "lobes" each headed by a "Steward."
- Stewards are chosen based on experience, leadership skill, and willingness.
- Stewardship is not a position of personal gain but of responsibility for a resource for the benefit of the whole group.

**Lobe Functions:**
- Each lobe represents a specific community function (analogous to departments).
- Stewards don't necessarily do all jobs in their lobe but ensure they get done.
- Stewards set goals, strategies, policies, and outputs based on agreed community goals.
- Sub-circles can exist within lobes for specific functions.

**Example Structure:**
A community might have a "Food Strategy" lobe with sub-circles of: Gardens, Kitchen, Food Co-Op, and Food Not Bombs. The Garden Circle designs, creates, and maintains community gardens, and may have sub-circles for individual garden plots.

### Step 3: Decision-Making

Consensus-based decision making is preferred but can be slow. Alternatives include:
- Modified consensus (supermajority thresholds)
- Sociocracy (consent-based decision making)
- Democratic voting with protections for minority positions
- The key is that the decision-making process should be agreed upon before decisions need to be made.

## Types of Permaculture Communities

### Ecovillages

Intentional communities designed from the ground up with permaculture principles:
- Clustered housing to preserve land for agriculture and habitat.
- Shared facilities (community kitchen, laundry, workshop).
- Collective food production systems.
- Common water, energy, and waste management infrastructure.

### Urban Permaculture Communities

People living dispersed within a city who coordinate:
- Seed swaps and plant sharing networks.
- Cooperative buying of bulk materials.
- Shared tool libraries.
- Community gardens on vacant lots.
- Urban food forests and edible landscaping.
- Neighborhood composting systems.

### Rural Community Design

Farm-scale permaculture with community integration:
- Community Supported Agriculture (CSA) models.
- Farm-to-table networks connecting producers and consumers.
- Shared equipment and labor cooperatives.
- Watershed-level design coordinating multiple properties.
- Agroforestry systems that serve both ecological and community functions.

## The History of Community Destruction and Recovery

The PDC traces how modern development destroyed community food systems:

- The International Style of architecture and LeCorbusier-inspired city centers made urban land too expensive for homesteading.
- Frank Lloyd Wright's "Broad Acre City" vision (one-acre homesteads per family) was bastardized into Levittown-style suburbs with tiny lawns replacing food-producing land.
- Agricultural land was pushed further from cities, creating petroleum-dependent food delivery infrastructure.
- Suburbs destroyed local ecologies and food security simultaneously.
- The Green Revolution replaced traditional food systems with industrial monoculture dependent on chemical inputs and fossil fuels.

Permaculture community design seeks to reverse this by reintegrating food production into human settlements at every scale.

## Scaling Permaculture Systems

Permaculture systems reach higher potential when:

- Multiple gardeners coordinate food strategies and grow complementary crops.
- Work parties share labor for large projects.
- Pools of talent, knowledge, and skill exceed any individual's capacity.
- Succession plans extend beyond one person's involvement -- the community continues the work.
- Human "birds and bees" propagate and spread permaculture systems to new locations.
