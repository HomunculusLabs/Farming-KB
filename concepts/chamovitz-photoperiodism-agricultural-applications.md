## The Maryland Mammoth Discovery

### The Problem

In 1906, tobacco farmers in southern Maryland noticed a new strain
that never stopped growing. It could reach 4.5 metres in height and
produce nearly a hundred leaves, but rarely flowered. This was a
serious problem: farmers needed seed from flowers for the next year's
crop.

### The Experiment

In 1918, Garner and Allard at the US Department of Agriculture
planted Maryland Mammoth in pots and divided them into two groups:

- **Group 1**: Left outside in the fields with natural long summer
  days → kept growing leaves, did not flower
- **Group 2**: Put outside during the day but moved to a dark shed
  every afternoon (artificially shorter days) → stopped growing and
  began flowering

The conclusion was revolutionary: the plant was measuring [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] to reproduction.

### The Concept of Photoperiodism

This phenomenon, named photoperiodism, provided the first strong
evidence that plants quantitatively measure light exposure. It led
to the classification of plants into categories:

- **Short-day plants**: Flower when days are short (nights are long)
  — e.g., chrysanthemums, soybeans, Maryland [[coleman-persephone-months-winter-day-length]]

### Immediate Agricultural Exploitation

Florida farmers quickly realised they could grow Maryland Mammoth for
many months without frost (unlike in Maryland) and that the plants
would eventually flower in midwinter when days reached their
shortest. This was the first commercial exploitation of
photoperiodism knowledge.

## Night Length, Not Day Length

### The Critical Insight

Subsequent experiments revealed that plants measure the length of
continuous darkness, not the length of daylight:

- A few minutes of light in the middle of a long night is enough to
  convince a short-day plant that the night was short (suppressing
  flowering)
- Conversely, a flash of light during a short night can convince a
  long-day plant that the night was even shorter (promoting flowering)

This means "short-day plants" are more accurately "long-night plants,"
and "long-day plants" are "short-night plants," though the original
terminology persists.

### The Challenge

Chrysanthemums are short-day plants that naturally flower in autumn
as days shorten. However, Mother's Day falls in spring (May in the
US and many European countries) — the opposite end of the
