# Fungal Nitrogen Deposition and Community Responses

## Overview

Atmospheric nitrogen deposition, resulting from industrial processes and automobiles,
acts as both an acidifying pollutant and a fertilizer that causes profound changes in
fungal community structure and ecosystem function. The responses of fungal communities to
elevated nitrogen represent one of the best-documented examples of anthropogenic impacts
on [[molecular-methods-fungal-taxonomy-and-diversity]] and have significant implications for
[[dighton-fungal-ecosystem-services-framework]] including nutrient cycling and carbon
storage.

## Impacts on Ectomycorrhizal Fungi

Ectomycorrhizal fungi are particularly sensitive to nitrogen deposition. Arnolds (1988)
reported that in most healthy forest ecosystems, fruit bodies of mycorrhizal fungi formed
between 45-50% of all fruit bodies found, whereas in polluted stands only about 10% of
fruit bodies were of mycorrhizal origin. This dramatic shift in the
mycorrhizal:saprotrophic ratio has been used as a biological indicator of forest
ecosystem health (Fellner, 1988; Colpaert and Van Tichelen, 1996).

Termorshuizen (1990) showed that nitrogen addition to the forest floor significantly
reduced the number of fruit bodies of basidiomycete ectomycorrhizal fungal species,
although this was not always reflected in changes in mycorrhizal formation on roots. In
the NITREX experiment in Sweden, Brandrud (1995) demonstrated that dominant genera
including Cortinarius and Russula were reduced in abundance by additional nitrogen, while
Lactarius showed little change and specific species such as Paxillus involutus and
Lactarius rufus increased.

## Mechanisms of Nitrogen Deposition Effects

Dighton and Jansen (1991) proposed a two-directional impact model for acidifying
pollutants on mycorrhizal development. The first mechanism operates through reduction in
photosynthesis in the tree canopy, reducing the carbohydrate energy supply to roots and
their mycorrhizae. Reduced energy supply both reduces overall mycorrhizal colonization
and favors those fungal species that can survive on low carbohydrate supplies.

The second mechanism operates through acid-induced increases in the availability of toxic
metal ions in soil, resulting in root damage and reduced mycorrhizal fungal growth. The
overall effect of nitrogen deposition is a shift from diverse, carbohydrate-demanding
mycorrhizal communities toward communities dominated by a few tolerant species with lower
metabolic requirements.

## Effects on Mycorrhizal Community Structure

The addition of ammonium sulfate to Norway spruce forests decreased fine root biomass but
not the degree of ectomycorrhizal colonization of roots (Kareen and Nylund, 1997).
However, changes in the structure of the ectomycorrhizal community were observed.
Markkola and Ohtonen (1988) found that Piloderma, Dermocybe, Hebeloma, and a type-03
ectomycorrhizal morphotype were significantly reduced in the presence of acidifying
pollutants, whereas Cenococcum was found to increase.

In Scots pine forests, nitrogen fertilization reduced total mycorrhizal colonization from
70% to 55% over a 13-year period with additions of 1700 and 950 kg N per hectare
(Arnebrant and Soderstrom, 1992). The below-ground ectomycorrhizal community composition
showed corresponding shifts, with sensitive species declining and tolerant species
persisting.

## Saprotrophic Fungal Responses

Kuyper (1989) showed that nitrogen addition and liming stimulate saprotrophic fungi and
leaf litter decomposition where nitrogen levels in the leaf litter are low, but suppress
saprotrophic activity where leaf litter nitrogen content is high. This non-linear
response reflects the complex relationship between resource quality, nitrogen
availability, and microbial activity.

Arnolds (1989a, 1989b, 1989c) documented changes in fungal species composition in a
nutrient-poor acidic-soil Dutch pasture under long-term fertilization. The total number
of fungal species remained similar between 1974 and 1980, but the species composition
shifted dramatically. Species including Hygrocybe ceracea, Entoloma conferendum, Mycena
cinerella, and Geoglossum glutinosum were lost, while Marasmius oreades, Panaeolina
foenescii, and Clitocybe amarescens increased between six- and 400-fold.

## Implications for Forest Decline

The stages of forest decline have been identified according to the macrofungal ratio of
saprotrophic to mycorrhizal forms (Fellner and Peskova, 1995). At the latent disturbance
level, ectomycorrhizal fungi decrease to 40% while lignicolous species increase to more
than 30%. At the acute level, ectomycorrhizal species fall below 40% and lignicolous
species exceed 40%. At the lethal level, ectomycorrhizal species decrease to below 20%
while lignicolous species rise above 55%.

These changes have cascading effects on [[fungal-nutrient-cycling-mineral-transformations]], as the loss of mycorrhizal diversity reduces the functional capacity
of the mycorrhizal community to access organic forms of nutrients from diverse substrate
types. The consequences for [[fungal-contributions-soil-structure]]
may be significant, as reduced mycorrhizal activity could alter the balance between
decomposition and carbon sequestration.

## Conservation Implications

Arnolds' observations have led to the adoption of red data lists for the conservation of
fungal species threatened by atmospheric deposition (Arnolds, 1989b, 1997). The inclusion
of fungi in lists of species for conservation has been adopted in the United Kingdom
(Watling, 1999). These conservation measures reflect growing recognition that
anthropogenic nitrogen deposition represents a major threat to
[[fungal-biodiversity-conservation-status]], particularly in nutrient-poor
ecosystems where mycorrhizal fungi play critical roles in plant nutrition and ecosystem
function.
