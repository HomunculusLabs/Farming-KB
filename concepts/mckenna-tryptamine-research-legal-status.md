# Tryptamine Research and Legal Status

McKenna addresses the scientific, cultural, and legal barriers to tryptamine research in "Tryptamine Hallucinogens and Consciousness" (Esalen lecture, December 1983; published in The Archaic Revival, 1992). His analysis situates the suppression of tryptamine research within a broader pattern of what he calls Western civilization's alienation from shamanic knowledge and non-ordinary states of consciousness. The essay was delivered at the Lilly/Goswami Conference on Consciousness and Quantum Physics at Esalen Institute, placing McKenna's critique of scientific orthodoxy alongside quantum mechanical discussions of consciousness.

## Legal Conflation of Distinct Compounds

McKenna observes that in the eyes of both the "uninformed public and the law," [[psilocybin]] is "lumped together with LSD and mescaline, when in fact each of these compounds is a phenomenologically defined universe unto itself." This legal conflation, he argues, prevents meaningful scientific investigation because the unique properties of each compound -- particularly the tryptamines' distinctive phenomenology of alien intelligence, translinguistic information, and direct action on language centers -- are obscured by categorical prohibition.

The legal framework fails to distinguish between compounds with radically different pharmacokinetic profiles: DMT's forty-five-second onset and five-minute duration versus LSD's extended body retention, or psilocybin's hour-long buildup and multi-hour plateau. For McKenna, these pharmacological differences reflect fundamentally different relationships between the compounds and human consciousness. Treating them as equivalent is, in his view, an epistemological error of the first order -- comparable to classifying all books together without regard to their content, or all musical instruments together without regard to the sounds they produce.

## The Insufficient Data Problem

McKenna calls himself "an explorer rather than a scientist, because the area that I'm looking at contains insufficient data to support even the dream of being a science." He compares the state of tryptamine research to that of early cartographers: explorers who "map one river and only indicate other rivers flowing into it; we must leave many rivers unascended and thus can say nothing about them."

This "Baconian collecting of data, with no assumptions about what it might eventually yield" is McKenna's proposed methodology. He argues that the field requires basic phenomenological mapping before theoretical frameworks can be constructed -- a task that legal prohibition makes nearly impossible. The essay itself represents an attempt at such mapping: McKenna presents detailed descriptions of subjective experience as "primary data for being," arguing that these experiences are as valid as any data produced by sanctioned instrumentalities.

The insistence on insufficient data is itself a methodological stance. McKenna refuses to advance grand theories about the nature of the tryptamine experience, preferring instead to present the raw phenomenological data and allow others to draw their own conclusions. This approach stands in marked contrast to both the reductionism of laboratory psychopharmacology and the dogmatism of many spiritual traditions that claim definitive knowledge of the psychedelic dimension.

## The Inadmissibility of Shamanic Evidence

A central theme in McKenna's analysis is the systematic exclusion of subjective psychedelic experience from scientific discourse. He argues that "the contents of shamanic experience and of plant-induced ecstasies are inadmissible" by the guardians of scientific truth, even though they represent "the source of novelty and the cutting edge of the ingression of the novel into the plenum of being."

McKenna critiques the assumption that only experiences "validated through consensus via certain sanctioned instrumentalities will be accepted as a signal." He points out that psychics, shamans, mystics, and schizophrenics have long reported experiences of other dimensions, but these have been "ruled a priori irrelevant, incoherent, or mad." The problem, as McKenna frames it, is not that these experiences lack validity but that "we are so inundated by these signals -- these other dimensions -- that there is a great deal of noise in the circuit."

The parallel with the early history of science is instructive. Galileo's telescopic observations were initially dismissed by the Aristotelian establishment because they contradicted the accepted framework. McKenna suggests that tryptamine experiences may occupy a similar position: genuine observations of reality that are excluded because they do not fit within the current paradigm. The difference is that Galileo's observations could be verified by anyone with a telescope, while tryptamine experiences require a willingness to ingest a prohibited substance -- a barrier that is simultaneously legal, cultural, and psychological.

## The SETI Analogy

McKenna uses the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI) as a pointed analogy for the blindness of scientific orthodoxy. He calls the question of extraterrestrial contact "a kind of red herring premised upon a number of assumptions that a moment's reflection will show are completely false." Searching for radio signals from space is "probably as culture bound a presumption as to search the galaxy for a good Italian restaurant."

Meanwhile, "there are people all over the world -- psychics, shamans, mystics, schizophrenics -- whose heads are filled with information, but it has been ruled a priori irrelevant, incoherent, or mad." McKenna suggests that the tryptamine experience may represent a form of communication with non-human intelligence that is far more accessible than any radio signal, but which the scientific framework is structurally incapable of recognizing. The irony is that the very phenomenon science seeks through billion-dollar instruments may be available through a few milligrams of a compound the human brain itself produces.

## The Cultural Black Hole Around DMT

McKenna proposes that DMT suffers from what he calls "the black hole effect" -- not only at the individual level of communication (where the experience resists articulation) but at the cultural level of research attention. He asks: "Why the phenomenon of tryptamine ecstasy has not been looked at by scientists, thrill seekers, or anyone else, I am not sure, but I recommend it to your attention."

Despite living in a society "absolutely obsessed with every kind of sensation imaginable and that adores every therapy, every intoxication, every sexual configuration, and all forms of media overload," McKenna observes that DMT is experienced as "too much" -- "bastante, it's enough -- so much enough that it's too much." This intensity, rather than attracting investigation, seems to repel it. The more extraordinary the phenomenon, the less capable the culture seems to be of examining it.

The black hole metaphor captures something essential about the cultural dynamics of DMT. Like a gravitational singularity, the DMT experience exerts a pull that is proportional to proximity -- those who have experienced it are drawn inexorably toward it, while those at a distance cannot perceive it at all. There is no comfortable middle ground: one is either inside the event horizon, unable to communicate what one has seen, or outside it, unable to comprehend what the insiders are talking about.

## Science as Potentially Culture-Bound

McKenna raises the possibility that "science and mathematics may be culture-bound," arguing that "we have never dealt with an alien mathematics or an alien culture except in the occult realm, and that evidence is inadmissible by the guardians of scientific truth." This epistemological critique suggests that the very framework used to evaluate tryptamine experiences may be inadequate to the task, creating a Catch-22 where the tools for investigation are themselves shaped by assumptions that preclude the phenomena under investigation.

He argues that "the structure of the Western intellectual enterprise is so flimsy at the center that apparently no one knows anything with certitude." This radical skepticism about the foundations of Western epistemology leads McKenna to privilege direct experience over theoretical frameworks: "The only intellectual or noetic or spiritual path worth following is one that builds on personal experience." He challenges the utility of abstract theoretical physics: "What good is a theory of how the universe works if it's a series of tensor equations that, even when understood, come nowhere tangential to experience?"

## Caution and Risk Acknowledgment

McKenna does not advocate recklessness. He includes explicit cautionary notes: "Experimenters should be very careful. One must build up to the experience. These are bizarre dimensions of extraordinary power and beauty. There is no set rule to avoid being overwhelmed, but move carefully, reflect a great deal, and always try to map experiences back onto the history of the race and the philosophical and religious accomplishments of the species." He states that "all the compounds are potentially dangerous, and all compounds, at sufficient doses or repeated over time, involve risks." He recommends that "the library is the first place to go when looking into taking a new compound."

McKenna also warns about the seductive nature of laboratory-synthesized compounds: "There is some certainty that one is dealing with a creature of integrity if one deals with a plant, but the creatures born in the demonic artifice of laboratories have to be dealt with very, very carefully." This distinction between plant-derived and synthetic compounds reflects a broader epistemological preference for naturally occurring substances that have been tested by evolutionary time.

## The Tragedy of Lost Shamanic Tradition

McKenna frames the research problem as fundamentally cultural: "The tragedy of our cultural situation is that we have no shamanic tradition." He defines shamanism as "primarily techniques, not ritual" -- a set of methods "worked out over millennia that make it possible, though perhaps not for everyone, to explore these areas." The absence of this institutional infrastructure means that Western explorers of non-ordinary states operate without the accumulated wisdom of thousands of years of careful experimentation.

In archaic societies, individuals showing signs of shamanic potential are identified and trained. McKenna describes the signs: "oddness or uniqueness in an individual. Epilepsy is often a signature in preliterate societies, or survival of an unusual ordeal in an unexpected way." Those struck by lightning and surviving "are thought to make excellent shamans." People who nearly die and fight their way back to health after weeks in an "indeterminate zone" are thought to possess "strength of soul." Shamans undergo training in "breathing and plant usage and other things" -- "the fact is that we don't really know all of what goes on. These secret information systems have not been well studied."

McKenna also notes that "shamanism is not, in these traditional societies, a terribly pleasant office." The shaman occupies an ambivalent social position: "Shamans are not normally allowed to have any political power, because they are sacred." They sit beside the headman in council but return to their hut at the village's edge. They are called upon in crisis -- death, illness, psychological difficulty, marital quarrels, theft, weather prediction -- but excluded from ordinary social life.

## The I/Thou Approach to Non-Ordinary Experience

McKenna advocates for a particular epistemological stance toward psychedelic experience that has implications for research methodology. He argues against both uncritical acceptance and premature dismissal. "There is no dignity in the universe unless we meet these things on our feet," he writes, calling for an "I/Thou relationship" with the entities and experiences encountered. One must say to the Other: "You say you are omniscient, omnipresent, or you say you are from Zeta Reticuli. You're long on talk, but what can you show me?"

This demand for demonstrated evidence -- "What can you show me?" -- is McKenna's fundamental epistemological criterion. It applies equally to the entities encountered in the tryptamine trance and to the claims of scientific materialism. Neither revelation nor reductionism is sufficient; what matters is what can be shown, what can be experienced directly. This criterion could, in principle, form the basis of a rigorous research methodology for investigating psychedelic phenomena -- one that takes subjective experience seriously as data while maintaining the critical standards of genuine inquiry.

## The Decentralizing Bender of Western Thought

McKenna contextualizes the suppression of tryptamine research within a broader historical pattern. "Western societies have been on a decentralizing bender for five hundred years, concluding that the Earth is not the center of the universe and man is not the beloved of God. We have moved ourselves out toward the edge of the galaxy." This progressive demotion of human significance has, in McKenna's view, created an intellectual climate in which the extraordinary contents of tryptamine experience cannot be taken seriously -- because to do so would require re-centering human consciousness in a way the post-Copernican worldview forbids.

The double bind is this: the intellectual framework that makes scientific investigation possible also renders it incapable of engaging with the most significant phenomena it might investigate. A science that has defined itself through the exclusion of subjectivity cannot investigate states of consciousness that are, by definition, subjective. A culture that has relegated human beings to the periphery of the cosmos cannot take seriously experiences that suggest human consciousness is central to the nature of reality.

## The Path Forward: Mapping the Territory

McKenna concludes with a call for systematic exploration of the tryptamine territory. He advocates what he calls a "Baconian" approach -- the patient collection of data without premature theorizing. "I don't know what we can do with this phenomenon, but I have a feeling that the potential is great." He urges that explorers "always try to map experiences back onto the history of the race and the philosophical and religious accomplishments of the species."

The essay's final call is to form "a relationship with these hallucinogenic plants" and to recognize that "the gnosis comes from plants." The research program McKenna envisions is not one of laboratory pharmacology alone but of direct, careful, systematic exploration of consciousness using the tools that nature has provided -- an approach that the current legal and institutional framework makes extraordinarily difficult. The first step, as McKenna sees it, is simply to pay attention: to look at the phenomenon that is right in front of us but which our cultural assumptions have rendered invisible.
