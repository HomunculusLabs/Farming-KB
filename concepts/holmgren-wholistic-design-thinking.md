# Holmgren Wholistic Design Thinking

Wholistic design thinking is the foundational cognitive framework underlying David Holmgren's permaculture design principles. It draws from systems ecology, cybernetics, landscape geography, and ethnobiology to create an approach to design that is inherently integrative rather than reductionist. This framework is what distinguishes permaculture from collections of techniques and makes it a coherent design discipline.

## Foundations in Systems Ecology

The scientific foundation for permaculture design principles lies generally within the modern science of ecology, and more particularly within the branch of ecology called systems ecology. Other intellectual disciplines, most particularly landscape geography and ethnobiology, have contributed concepts that have been adapted to design principles.

Fundamentally, permaculture design principles arise from a way of perceiving the world which is often described as "systems thinking" and "design thinking." Apart from the ecological energetics of Howard Odum, the influence of systems thinking in Holmgren's development of permaculture has not come through extensive study of the literature, but more through an osmotic absorption of ideas in the "cultural ether" which strike a chord with practical experience in permaculture design.

## Key Intellectual Lineages

### Howard Odum and Ecological Energetics

The work of Howard T. Odum on ecological energetics has been the single most important intellectual influence on Holmgren's thinking. Odum's Environment, Power and Society (1971) was a book which influenced many key environmental thinkers in the 1970s and was the first listed reference in Permaculture One. Odum's prodigious published output over three decades has continued to inform Holmgren's work.

Odum's concept of EMERGY (embodied energy) -- all the available energy that was used in the work of making a product -- provides a quantitative framework for understanding the real energy cost of goods and services. The maximum power principle -- that self-organising systems tend to maximise power throughput -- informs the understanding of optimal design that runs through all twelve principles. See [[holmgren-solar-cell-emergy-critique]] for applications and [[holmgren-trophic-pyramid-energy-hierarchy]].

### Cybernetics

Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics (1948) -- control and communication in the animal and the machine -- provides the theoretical framework for understanding feedback loops, self-regulation, and system stability. John Gall's General Systematics (1977) offers an accessible and useful guide for permaculture designers. The cybernetic concepts of positive and negative feedback, self-organisation, and homeostasis are embedded throughout the design principles, especially [[holmgren-self-regulation-feedback-detailed]].

### Design Science

Victor Papanek defines design as "the conscious and intuitive effort to impose meaningful order." This emphasises that design is not simply the result of rational, analytical, and reductionist thinking, but also depends on our intuitive and integrative capabilities. To design requires familiarity with models generated by nature and humanity as well as an ability to visualise some new adaptation, variation or possibility.

Papanek's "imposition of meaningful order" recognises the powerful nature of designing. The dangers of "playing god" inherent in this definition remind us of the potent nature of design. As Stewart Brand said in the original Whole Earth Catalogue (1968), "We are as gods so might as well get good at it."

From a systems ecology perspective, "design by nature" is not simply a metaphor but a result of the forces of self-organisation which can be observed everywhere in the living and wider universe. This imposition of meaningful order is a counter-flow to the prevailing entropic forces of disorder within nature and the wider universe. Self-organisation occurs wherever energy flows are sufficient to generate storages. Designing is as natural as breathing, and, like breathing, most of us can learn to do it better.

### Christopher Alexander's Pattern Language

Alexander's Pattern Language was a milestone in the recognition and organisation of classic patterns of human-scale built environments. Developing a similar pattern language for the much broader scope of permaculture design is a need that several permaculture designers have recognised. See [[holmgren-pattern-thinking-and-pattern-language]].

## The Design Revolution

Holmgren identifies a broader "thinking and design revolution" as part of the information economy. The information and knowledge systems that direct and organise the physical economy of goods now have the greatest value and power. Computers are the most obvious feature of the information economy, but changes in the way we think, especially the emergence of design thinking, are more fundamental to the information economy than the hardware and software we use.

A large part of the thinking revolution involves the emergence of design as a universal skill alongside those of literacy and numeracy. It is not so much that we are just beginning to design; rather, we are becoming more conscious of the power of our individual and collective design processes and how to improve them. Permaculture itself is part of this thinking revolution.

The most creative design involves the promiscuous hybridisation of possibilities from apparently disconnected, or even discordant sources to create a new harmony. This capacity for creative recombination is essential for [[holmgren-energy-descent-planning]], where new solutions must often be assembled from available resources rather than manufactured from scratch.

## Beyond Reductionism

Holmgren argues that reductionist science, while powerful in dealing with the disintegrated world created by the rising energy base, is now an impediment to human survival. Reductionism both explained the physical reality of the industrial world and reflected its fundamental ideology. Despite the substantial philosophical critiques and alternatives to the world-view created by reductionism, it has held sway partly because it is powerful in dealing with the increasingly disintegrated world created by the rising energy base. Through it we have achieved much. But, despite the hubris about new contributions to humanity's well-being, Holmgren believes reductionist science is now an impediment to human survival.

The [[holmgren-ethical-philosophical-framework]] of permaculture challenges the "Cartesian dualism" that separates mind and body, humanity and nature, thought and action, subject and object. Learning to think wholistically requires recognising the degree to which energetically novel conditions of recent centuries are primary factors in creating and spreading much of what we hold precious in human thinking and culture.

Some productive alternatives have emerged within the culture of science, such as dialectical materialism, systems theory and design science. The development of a truly wholistic science is important; otherwise we will see a wholesale rejection of the culture of science in the new millennium as it increasingly fails to explain and predict the novel phenomena of energy descent.

## Indigenous Knowledge and Systems Thinking

Many of the insights of systems thinking that are difficult to grasp as abstractions are truths that are embodied in the stories and myths of indigenous cultures. This does not romanticise indigenous knowledge but recognises that cultures which have existed in relative balance with their environment for millennia must embody effective systems thinking, even if not expressed in scientific language.

Permaculture's focus on learning from indigenous tribal cultures is based on the evidence that these cultures have existed in relative balance with their environment and survived for longer than any of our more recent experiments in civilisation. Their stories, patterns, and practices encode systems understanding that modern people often lack.

## Practical Manifestations

Wholistic design thinking in permaculture practice manifests as:

- **Reading the landscape** as an integrated system rather than a collection of separate elements, developing skills in interpreting topography, vegetation patterns, soil indicators, and wildlife signs
- **Designing from patterns to details** rather than from components upward, ensuring that overall system structure is appropriate before filling in specific elements
- **Considering multiple scales** simultaneously, from microscopic soil biology to global climate patterns, recognising that systems at different scales interact
- **Recognising feedback loops** and designing for self-regulation, building systems that maintain their own stability without constant human intervention
- **Valuing emergent properties** that arise from system interactions, properties that cannot be predicted from understanding components alone
- **Accepting uncertainty** and designing for adaptive capacity rather than fixed outcomes

## The Teaching Challenge

Despite the powerful applications of systems thinking in the modern world, it has been slow to change the fundamental aspects of our everyday thinking patterns. Holmgren recounts being quizzed about teaching systems thinking by an American academic nearing retirement after a career spanning labouring, mining, engineering, and academia. This academic had begun to wonder whether such thinking was an almost innate ability, to which teaching could contribute little. His experience was that most people seem stuck in simplistic understandings and reactive responses to complexity, unable to grasp the broader understandings of systemic thinking.

Permaculture has contributed to the spread of some innovative design solutions that illustrate permaculture principles, but it has been less effective in spreading the systems and design thinking which underlies those solutions. This gap between solution and understanding remains a critical challenge for the movement and for effective [[holmgren-energy-descent-culture-adaptation]].

## The Twelve Principles as Design Tools

Holmgren's twelve permaculture design principles function not as prescriptive rules but as cognitive tools for thinking wholistically about any design problem. Each principle describes a pattern observable in natural systems that can be applied to human design contexts. Together, they form a framework that resists the reductionist tendency to optimize single variables in isolation.

The principles — from Observe and Interact through Creatively Use and Respond to Change — are deliberately interdependent. Applying them sequentially or in combination reveals different aspects of a design challenge. For example, using Principle 1 (Observe and Interact) alongside Principle 8 (Integrate Rather Than Segregate) leads the designer to notice relationships between elements that a reductionist analysis would treat as separate systems. This is the essence of wholistic design: the recognition that the relationships between components are as important as the components themselves.

## Wholistic vs. Reductionist Approaches in Agriculture

Reductionist agriculture decomposes farming into isolated variables — nitrogen availability, pest pressure, yield per hectare — and optimizes each independently, typically through chemical or mechanical intervention. This approach has produced remarkable short-term yield increases but at the cost of degraded soils, polluted waterways, vulnerable monocultures, and dependency on external inputs.

Wholistic design thinking in agriculture treats the farm as an ecosystem rather than a factory. Rather than asking "how do I eliminate this pest?" the wholistic designer asks "what conditions allow this pest to proliferate, and how can I redesign the system to restore balance?" This shift from symptom management to causal intervention is fundamental. It draws on [[holmgren-self-regulation-feedback-detailed]] (Principle 4) and the recognition that every intervention has cascading effects throughout the system.

In practice, this means designing polycultures that provide their own pest control through biological interactions, building soil fertility through closed-loop nutrient cycling rather than imported fertilizers, and selecting for genetic diversity rather than uniform high-yielding varieties. The wholistic approach accepts lower maximum yields in exchange for greater system resilience, reduced input costs, and long-term productivity stability.

## Case Studies of Wholistic Design in Practice

Holmgren's own property at Melliodora in Hepburn Springs, Victoria, demonstrates wholistic design thinking applied over decades. The 1-hectare property integrates a passive-solar house, food gardens, orchards, poultry, and water harvesting into a system where waste from one element becomes the resource for another. House greywater irrigates food gardens; poultry control orchard pests while providing manure; food forest canopy moderates house temperatures. No single element functions in isolation.

The transition town movement, which Holmgren has strongly supported, applies wholistic thinking at the community scale. Rather than addressing energy descent through isolated technical fixes, transition initiatives redesign food systems, transport, economics, and social organization as interconnected domains. This mirrors the permaculture design principle that the problem is the solution — the challenges of energy descent become the driver for building more resilient, localized systems.

Broader applications include regenerative agriculture projects that integrate livestock with cropping through adaptive multi-paddock grazing, where the grazing animal's disturbance of pasture stimulates root exudation and soil carbon sequestration while simultaneously controlling weeds and cycling nutrients. These systems only function when understood wholistically — the soil, plants, animals, and grazing schedule form a single system whose behavior cannot be predicted from any single component.

## Thinking in Systems: Skills and Obstacles

Wholistic design thinking requires developing specific cognitive skills that differ from those emphasized in conventional education. The ability to hold multiple variables in mind simultaneously, to recognize non-linear cause-and-effect relationships, and to perceive feedback loops are all skills that require deliberate cultivation. Holmgren notes that many people find these ways of thinking counterintuitive because formal education trains us in linear, reductionist reasoning from an early age.

A key obstacle is the human tendency to break complex problems into discrete parts and solve each part independently — precisely the approach that wholistic thinking rejects. In agriculture, this manifests as the agronomist optimizing nitrogen, the pathologist treating disease, and the engineer designing drainage as separate problems, when in reality they are interacting aspects of a single system. The wholistic designer must resist this fragmentation and maintain a view of the whole system even while working on specific details.

Holmgren suggests that direct engagement with nature — through gardening, farming, or simply extended observation of natural systems — is the most effective way to develop wholistic thinking. The complexity of real ecosystems teaches patterns of thought that cannot be acquired from textbooks alone. This emphasis on experiential learning connects wholistic design thinking to indigenous knowledge traditions, where understanding of ecological relationships is built through generations of direct interaction with place.

## Connection to Energy Descent

Wholistic design thinking becomes especially critical in the context of energy descent, where the reductionist approach of simply substituting one energy source for another (e.g., replacing fossil fuels with renewables while maintaining all other systems unchanged) is fundamentally inadequate. Energy descent requires redesigning entire systems — food, shelter, transport, economy — as integrated wholes. The twelve principles of permaculture provide the thinking tools for this redesign, but only when applied wholistically rather than as a checklist of techniques.

Holmgren argues that the energy descent transition will reward those who can think wholistically and penalize those who remain trapped in reductionist frameworks. Industrial agriculture, with its dependence on fossil fuel inputs at every stage, is particularly vulnerable because its reductionist structure cannot adapt incrementally — it requires a wholistic redesign that the prevailing paradigm is ill-equipped to envision. Permaculture design thinking, grounded in systems ecology and informed by indigenous knowledge, offers the cognitive framework needed for this unprecedented challenge.
