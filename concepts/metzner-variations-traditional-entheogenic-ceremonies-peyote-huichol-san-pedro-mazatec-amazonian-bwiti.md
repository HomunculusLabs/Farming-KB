# Variations on Traditional Entheogenic Ceremonies: Peyote, Huichol, San Pedro, Mazatec, Amazonian, and Bwiti Ritual Forms

## Three Distinctive Features of Traditional Ceremonies

Ralph Metzner identifies three features that consistently distinguish traditional shamanic
entheogenic ceremonies from Western [[metzner-psychedelic-psychotherapy-clinical-history]] sessions. First, traditional
rituals involve very little or no talking among participants, except perhaps during a
preparatory phase or afterward to clarify teachings and visions received. Second, singing —
particularly the shaman's singing — is "invariably considered essential to the success of the
healing or divinatory process." The singing typically has a fairly rapid beat, similar to the
rhythmic pulse of shamanic drumming journeys. Third, traditional ceremonies are almost always
conducted in darkness or low light, which facilitates the emergence of visions.

The rhythmic chanting, like the drum pulse, "seems to give support for moving through the flow
of visions, and minimizes the likelihood of getting stuck in frightening or seductive
experiences." This functional understanding of ceremony elements connects them to the set-and-
setting model: ceremony is the intentional arrangement of [[lsd-set-and-setting-framework]] for purposes of
[[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]].

## Native American Church Peyote Ceremony

The peyote ceremonies of the Native American Church (NAC) in North America follow a
consistent format. Participants sit on the ground in a circle around a blazing central fire
inside a tipi. The ceremony lasts all night and is conducted by a "roadman," assisted by a
drummer, a firekeeper, and a cedar-man responsible for purification. A staff and rattle are
passed around the circle, and participants sing traditional peyote songs involving a rapid,
rhythmic beat.

The NAC was legally incorporated as an organized religion that uses peyote as a sacrament.
For most of the [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]], it enjoyed legal access to the entheogen in most Western
states, though legal protections have been eroded by recent court decisions. NAC ceremonies
are legally limited to persons with 25% or more Indian ancestry, although some ceremonial
leaders have opened ceremonies to non-Indians as well. There is virtually no discussion of
healing processes or visions during or after the ceremony.

## Huichol Peyote Pilgrimage

The Huichol Indians of northern Mexico maintain a more traditionally shamanic model of peyote
use. Their practice involves careful and lengthy apprenticeships for curanderos and a group
pilgrimage to the sacred land of Wirikuta to find the rare peyote cactus. The actual
ceremonies are accompanied by much singing and the telling of creation myths and other sacred
stories.

The pilgrimage itself is an integral part of the ceremonial practice — the journey to the
sacred land, the search for the cactus, and the return journey all form part of the
transformative process. This contrasts with the NAC model, where peyote is typically
obtained through supply networks and brought to a fixed ceremonial location. The Huichol
model emphasizes the relationship between the seeker, the land, and the plant as a
triadic sacred complex.

## San Pedro Cactus Altar Ceremonies

The ceremonies of the San Pedro cactus (*Echinopsis pachanoi*) in Andean regions show
variation in form. Some are conducted around a fire with singing, similar to peyote
ceremonies. In other cases, the curandero sets up an altar on which different symbolic
figurines and objects are placed, representing the light and dark spirits that one is
likely to encounter during the ceremony. This altar arrangement creates a structured
symbolic landscape through which the participant navigates during the experience.

The use of symbolic objects on the altar provides a kind of externalized map of the
inner territories to be explored. The figurines and objects serve as anchors and
reference points, allowing the curandero to guide the experience by directing
attention to specific symbolic elements. This practice represents a sophisticated
integration of visual symbolism, ceremonial space, and visionary experience.

## Mazatec Mushroom Veladas

The [[stamets-psi-mazatec-mushroom-ceremonies-ethnobotany]] (velada) of the Mazatec Indians of Mexico involve participants
sitting or lying in a very dark room with only a small candle. The healer — who may be a
woman or a man — sings almost uninterruptedly throughout the night. The chants weave
together the names of Christian saints, spirit allies, and the spirits of the Earth, the
elements, animals and plants, the sky, the waters, and the fire.

The Mazatec velada represents a particularly rich example of syncretism, in which
pre-Columbian shamanic practices have been integrated with Catholic devotional elements.
The darkness of the ceremony is not incidental but functional: by minimizing external
sensory input, it allows the inner visionary content to emerge with maximum clarity. The
small candle serves as a fixed point of reference in the darkness, preventing complete
disorientation while not providing enough light to suppress visions.

## Amazonian Ayahuasca Ceremonies

Traditional Amazonian Indian or mestizo ayahuasca ceremonies involve a small group sitting
in a circle in semidarkness. Initiated healers sing the icaros — healing songs — through
which the diagnosis and healing takes place. These songs also have a rapid rhythmic pulse
that keeps the flow of the experience moving along. Some traditions include shamanic
"sucking" methods for extracting toxic psychic residues or sorcerous implants.

The icaros function as the primary therapeutic technology in Amazonian ayahuasca practice.
Each icaro is understood to have specific healing properties — some for calling protective
spirits, others for extracting illness, still others for establishing boundaries. The
healer's repertoire of icaros, often acquired through dietas (dietary retreats in
isolation), constitutes the core of their therapeutic expertise. The rapid rhythmic pulse
provides a temporal structure for the experience, preventing stagnation in difficult
emotional or visionary territory.

## Bwiti Iboga Ceremonies

The Bwiti cult among the Fang people in Gabon and Zaire uses the powerful hallucinogen
iboga (*Tabernanthe iboga*), from which ibogaine is derived. The Fang say they originally
learned about iboga from the Pygmies, the deep forest dwellers. Ceremonies involve an
altar with ancestral and deity images, with initiate men and women daubed with white mud
sitting and singing in lengthy ceremonies.

A distinctive feature of Bwiti ceremonies is the presence of a mirror in the assembly
room, in which initiates may "see" their ancestral spirits. The mirror serves as a
literal and symbolic portal between the visible and invisible worlds. The death-rebirth
experience is central to Bwiti initiation: the initiate's "head is opened up," after
which they are able to converse with ancestor spirits who can guide them in their lives.

## Common Elements Across Traditions

Despite their diversity, these ceremonial traditions share common structural elements:
the central role of an experienced guide, the use of singing or rhythmic sound,
preference for darkness or low light, group participation in a circle, and the
understanding that the plant medicine communicates hidden knowledge. These convergent
features suggest that they represent not arbitrary cultural inventions but
discoveries about the optimal conditions for productive engagement with entheogenic
states — a kind of phenomenological technology refined over millennia.
