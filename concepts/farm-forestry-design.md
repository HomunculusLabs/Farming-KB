# Farm Forestry Design

**Farm forestry design** is a permaculture discipline concerned with the integration of tree-based systems into agricultural landscapes for the simultaneous production of timber, forage, food, and ecological services. As articulated by [[green-manure]]** from leaf fall and prunings
- **Stickwood** for kindling, craft materials, and garden supports
- **Windbreak and shelter** effects for adjacent fields and pastures
- **Livestock containment** as living fences when appropriately designed

The species diversity within hedgerows ensures resilience against pest and disease outbreaks and provides yields across multiple seasons and years. Hedgerows also function as **biological corridors**, connecting fragmented habitat patches and allowing wildlife to move across agricultural landscapes. In [[permaculture-design-principles]]:

- **Multiple function** — Every tree planting should serve at least three functions (e.g., timber production, erosion control, and wildlife habitat) to justify the space and time investment
- **Diversity of species and yields** — Mixed plantings spread economic risk, extend harvest seasons, and reduce vulnerability to pests and diseases
- **Integration with existing farm systems** — Tree plantings should complement, not compete with, existing livestock and crop enterprises
- **Zoning for efficiency** — High-maintenance tree crops (fruit, nut) are placed in Zone 1–2 near the homestead, while timber and fuel wood are positioned in outer zones
- **Use of biological resources** — Trees are selected for nitrogen fixation, pest predator habitat, mulch production, and other biological services that reduce external inputs

## Critique of Monoculture Forestry

Mollison identifies several systemic problems inherent in **conventional monoculture forestry** that farm forestry design seeks to address:

- **Glut conditions** — Large-scale single-species plantations produce uniform timber volumes that reach market simultaneously, depressing prices and destabilizing regional timber economies
- **Land ownership instability** — Monoculture forestry is often practiced by absentee corporate owners whose land management decisions are driven by short-term financial returns rather than long-term ecological stewardship
- **Catastrophic fire risk** — Even-aged, single-species stands create continuous fuel loads that are highly vulnerable to wildfire
- **Displacement of bioregional needs** — Monoculture timber operations override local needs for diverse products, replacing them with export-oriented commodity production that serves distant markets

## Small Forest Farms

Mollison advocates for **small-scale forest farms** of approximately 12–16 hectares as a particularly productive and ecologically sound model. These compact holdings, when managed with permaculture principles, can sustainably yield a remarkably diverse array of products:

- **Edible fungi** from forest floor and inoculated logs
- **Tree seed** for nursery stock and direct sale
- **Small fruits** and berries from understory plantings
- **Fur and meat** from integrated livestock and small game management
- **Wild fruits** harvested from semi-wild forest margins
- **Honey** from managed apiaries within the forest canopy
- **Poles and coppice** for fencing, construction, and fuel
- **Bark and medicinals** harvested sustainably from managed tree crops
- **Mulches and organic matter** from pruning, leaf fall, and coppice residues

The diversity of yields from small forest farms provides economic resilience, ecological stability, and a degree of self-sufficiency that is unattainable in conventional single-enterprise farming systems. Crucially, these yields can be harvested **continuously and sustainably** across seasons and years, providing a stable income stream that does not depend on a single harvest cycle or market price.
