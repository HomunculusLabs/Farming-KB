## The Genus Iochroma

The genus Iochroma comprises approximately two dozen species, all native to
the highlands of South America. These plants are shrubs or small trees with
tubular flowers that are often brightly colored, making several species
popular as ornamental plants in temperate gardens. The genus belongs to the
nightshade family, Solanaceae, which includes some of the most potent
psychoactive plants known, such as Datura, Atropa, Hyoscyamus, and
Mandragora.

The systematic placement of Iochroma within the Solanaceae places it within
a tribe that includes several other genera with reported psychoactive
properties. The evolutionary proximity to known hallucinogenic and toxic
Solanaceous plants supports the possibility that Iochroma species may
produce similar [[ayahuasca-pharmacology-and-cultural-context]].
The valley, inhabited primarily by the Kamsa and Inga peoples, is home to
an unparalleled concentration of psychoactive plant use. The Kamsa medicine
men cultivate and employ numerous psychoactive species, including
[[tree-daturas-brugmansia-south-american-ethnobotany]], and Iochroma species.

Reports that Iochroma is taken in hallucinatory drinks, either alone or
mixed with other narcotic plants, are consistent with the general pattern of
psychoactive plant use in the Sibundoy Valley, where complex mixtures of
multiple plant species are common. The practice of combining plants to
enhance, modify, or direct psychoactive effects is a sophisticated
pharmacological technique well-documented among indigenous peoples of the
northwestern Amazon and adjacent Andean highlands.

## The Name Borrachera

The application of the name borrachera to Iochroma fuchsioides places it
within a broad category of [[cordyceps-cancer-bioactive-compounds]] is a reasonable hypothesis. However, the
absence of chemical data means that the active constituents, mechanism of
action, and toxicity profile of Iochroma all remain unknown.

The lack of chemical investigation is somewhat surprising given the
relatively accessible location of the Sibundoy Valley and the extensive
ethnobotanical work conducted there by Schultes and others. It may reflect
the difficulty of obtaining sufficient plant material for analysis, the
challenge of identifying which of the approximately two dozen Iochroma
species are actually used, or simply the limited resources available for
comprehensive phytochemical screening of all reported psychoactive plants.
