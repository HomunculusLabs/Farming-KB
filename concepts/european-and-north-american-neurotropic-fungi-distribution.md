### Country-by-Country Highlights

**Germany** has the most diverse documented neurotropic mycota in Europe. [[galerina-steglichii]],
described from Germany by Besl (1994), and [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] was recorded as early as Sowerby (1797-1809), and [[gymnopilus-purpuratus]], G. sapineus, and G. spectabilis.

**Italy** has a particularly rich documented neurotropic mycota studied extensively by Giorgio Samorini (1989,
1992, 1996), including rare species such as Mycena cyanorhiza and [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] found above the Arctic Circle and [[amanita-muscaria-entity]] distributed across its vast territory, and [[psilocybe-azurescens]] is one of the most potent psilocybin-containing species, discovered by Stamets and Gartz
(1995) along the Oregon coast. It grows on decaying wood in coastal dune grasses. [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] is widespread in
the Pacific Northwest and also found in California, Ohio, Kentucky, and Europe. [[psilocybe-caerulipes]] is
found in deciduous forests from Georgia and Florida north to Maine and Ontario, Canada. [[psilocybe-quebecensis]] is a Canadian endemic
from Quebec, related to the Mexican P. aztecorum. [[psilocybe-cubensis]] is found along the Gulf Coast from Texas to Florida and north to South Carolina. [[copelandia-cambodginiensis]] is
recorded from Texas.

### Canadian and Alaskan Records

Canada has confirmed records from every province. British Columbia has the richest mycota with more than 14
neurotropic species. More northern territories (Yukon, Northwest Territories, Newfoundland) primarily host only
[[psilocybe-semilanceata]] extends to Prince Edward Island

widespread Panaeolus species. This pattern reflects the drier continental climate, which is less suitable for
most neurotropic fungi adapted to humid environments.
