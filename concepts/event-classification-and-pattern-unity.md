## Overview

Event classification and pattern unity form a foundational framework in [[permaculture]] for understanding how all phenomena in the natural world relate to one another. This system provides a universal language for describing events — from the growth of a seedling to the eruption of a volcano — through a shared set of categories. By classifying events along multiple axes, designers gain the ability to perceive the deep connections between seemingly disparate processes and to recognize that all natural phenomena are expressions of a single, underlying master pattern.

## The Event Classification System

Every event in nature can be annotated using four complementary axes: Nature, Stage, Dimension, and Location. Together, these axes produce a compact designation that captures the essential character of any phenomenon.

### A. Nature — What Kind of Event Is Occurring?

The Nature axis describes the intrinsic quality or character of the event.

- **A1 — Explosive / Disintegration / Erosion / Impact**: Events that break down, release energy, or cause physical disruption. Examples include landslides, explosions, coastal erosion, and meteor impacts.
- **A2 — Growth / Integration / Construction / Translation**: Events that build, assemble, or transform energy into structured form. Examples include plant growth, soil formation, and the weaving of a spider's web.
- **A3 — Conceptual / Idea / Creative Thought / Insight**: Events that occur in the realm of thought, inspiration, or abstract understanding. This category acknowledges that ideas and insights are real events with tangible consequences in the world.

### B. Stage — Where Is the Event in Its Life Cycle?

The Stage axis places the event along a temporal arc from latent potential through active expression to eventual decay.

- **B1 — Potential Only**: The event exists as latent possibility — a seed in dormancy, an unlit fuse, an idea not yet acted upon.
- **B2 — In Process of Evolution**: The event is actively unfolding, changing, or developing toward its completed form.
- **B3 — Completed**: The event has reached its fully expressed state — a mature forest canopy, a finished structure, a realized design.
- **B4 — Decaying / Disintegrative**: The event is in decline, releasing stored energy and materials back into the broader system.

### C. Dimension — What Spatial or Temporal Form Does the Event Take?

The Dimension axis classifies the geometric or topological character of the event.

- **C1 — One (Linear)**: Events that proceed along a single axis — a root tip pushing downward, water flowing in a channel, a thought following a chain of logic.
- **C2 — Two (Surface / Tessellae / Dendrites)**: Events that spread across a plane — [[mycelial-networks]], ripples on water, branching river systems viewed from above.
- **C3 — Three (Solid / Trees)**: Events that occupy volume — a standing tree, a boulder, a soil profile.
- **C4 — Four (Moving Solid + Time)**: Events that involve a three-dimensional object changing through time — a falling boulder, a growing organism, a migrating herd.
- **C5 — More (Conceptual / Models of Particles)**: Events that transcend conventional spatial dimensions — ecosystem models, theoretical frameworks, multi-variable interactions.

### D. Location — Where Does the Event Occur Relative to Its Context?

The Location axis describes the positional relationship between the event and its surrounding medium or field.

- **D1 — Across Equi-Potential Surfaces**: Events that distribute energy or material evenly across a surface of equal potential — water seeking its own level, heat radiating across a uniform plane.
- **D2 — Within Media**: Events that occur within a substance or medium — groundwater percolation, nutrient diffusion through soil, sound traveling through air.
- **D3 — Through Surfaces at Approximately 90°**: Events that cross boundaries roughly perpendicular to those boundaries — roots penetrating a soil horizon, light passing through a leaf surface, a nail driven into wood.
- **D4 — Englobements**: Events that surround, encapsulate, or contain — a forest canopy englobing the understory, a pond englobing aquatic life, a cell membrane containing its contents.
- **D5 — Out of Normal Space-Time Dimensions**: Events that operate beyond ordinary spatial or temporal frameworks — quantum phenomena, deeply abstract pattern processes, emergent system-level behaviors.

### Annotation Examples

By combining designations from each axis, any event can be compactly described:

- **A sapling (young tree)**: A2, B2, C3, D3 — an integrative growth event (A2), still in the process of evolution (B2), occupying three-dimensional space (C3), pushing through surfaces at roughly perpendicular angles (D3).
- **A falling bomb**: A1, B1, C4, D2 — an explosive event (A1) with destructive potential (B1), a moving solid through time (C4), traveling within a medium (D2).

These annotations make it possible to compare, contrast, and find pattern resonance between events that appear on the surface to have nothing in common.

The power of this system lies in its ability to reveal hidden similarities. A sapling and a coral polyp, though separated by kingdom and habitat, share the same A2-B2-C3-D3 signature — both are integrative growth events occupying volume and crossing boundaries at roughly perpendicular angles. Such cross-domain pattern recognition is essential to the permaculture designer, who must constantly draw lessons from one system and apply them to another.

## Time, Relativity, and the Implicate Order

### Seeds, Trees, and Temporal Focus

A seed is not merely a small container of genetic information — it is an **origin point** in time. The tree that grows from it is the **time-focus of its entire genealogy**, concentrating the evolutionary history of its lineage into a living, breathing organism. Every tree is thus a temporal event as much as a spatial one: it is the visible expression of centuries of accumulated adaptation, compressed into a single organism unfolding in real time.

### The Implicate Order

The present moment is not a thin boundary between past and future — it is the **enfolding point** where both past and future are contained. Drawing on Buddhist philosophy and the work of David Bohm, this perspective holds that all time is present in every moment. The past is not gone; it is **implicated** — folded into — the current state of every living system. Similarly, the future is not absent; it exists as the set of potentials enfolded within the present.

This implicate order means that no event is truly isolated in time. A forest clearing carries within it the history of the trees that once stood there, the succession that follows is already present as potential in the soil seed bank, and the designer who reads this landscape is, in effect, reading a text in which all moments are simultaneously present.

This means that we ourselves exist at an **ever-changing origin** on the boundary between past and future. We are not passengers moving through time — we are participants in the continuous unfolding of potential. Our bodies contain the potential for future generations, encoding biological information that extends far beyond our individual lifespans.

### All Origins Can Be Located in the Matrix

Within the universal pattern matrix, every origin — every seed, every spark, every creative impulse — can be located and understood in relation to every other. There is no true isolation; every event connects to a web of causes and consequences that extends across all scales of time and space.

## The Role of the Designer

### Moving Sideways in Waves of Time

Designers do not stand outside the flow of events — they **move sideways in waves of time**, like a surfer riding a wave-front. The designer's task is not to control time but to read it, to position themselves at the optimal point on the advancing edge of change, and to intervene with precision and sensitivity. This requires a deep understanding of the temporal stage of every element in a system and the ability to anticipate what is about to unfold.

### Mankind as Translator

Humanity occupies a unique position in the pattern matrix. Mankind serves as an **active translator of life and death** — the species most capable of consciously directing the flow between integrative (A2) and disintegrative (A1) processes. Every design decision is, at its root, a choice about where to encourage growth and integration and where to allow or direct release and renewal.

## Pattern Unity

### The World as a Tessellation of Events

The world is not a collection of separate objects — it is a **tessellation of events**, each one fitting together with its neighbors in a continuous mosaic. An ancient volcanic crater, for instance, is not merely a geological feature; it is an event that continues to unfold through time, influencing soil composition, water flow, microclimate, and the succession of living communities that establish themselves upon it. Every square meter of the planet is part of this ongoing event-tessellation.

### The Single Master Pattern

Despite the apparent diversity of natural phenomena, all events are expressions of a **unity of patterns** based on one master pattern. Branching patterns appear in rivers, trees, blood vessels, and lightning bolts. Spiral patterns appear in shells, galaxies, and the growth of fern fronds. These repetitions are not coincidental — they reflect the operation of a single set of underlying principles that govern the flow of energy and the organization of matter at every scale.

### Life as the Integrative Force

Among all the forces in the universe, **life is the only integrative force** — the only process that actively gathers dispersed energy and matter and weaves it into more complex, more organized, more resilient structures. Where physical processes tend toward entropy and dispersal, biological processes move toward integration, connection, and increasing complexity. This is the fundamental insight that makes permaculture possible: by aligning human design with the integrative tendencies of life, we can create systems that grow more productive and more stable over time rather than degrading.

### Patterning as the Template for Design

Patterning — the recognition and application of these universal patterns — is the **template for design**. When a designer recognizes that the branching pattern of a watershed mirrors the branching pattern of a tree, or that the spiral pattern of a nautilus shell reflects the same mathematics as the spiral of a plant's leaf arrangement, they gain access to a design language that is universally applicable.

The practical consequence is that good design in one domain suggests good design in another. The way water is distributed across a landscape (D1), the way a forest canopy englobes its understory (D4), the way a root system penetrates soil horizons (D3) — all of these spatial and temporal patterns can be translated into the layout of gardens, buildings, and human settlements. The event classification system provides the vocabulary; pattern unity provides the grammar.

### The Projections of a Single Totality

The physicist David Bohm proposed that all things are **projections of a single totality** — that the apparent separateness of objects and events is an illusion generated by our limited perspective, and that in reality, everything is an expression of one undivided whole. Permaculture's pattern understanding aligns directly with this view. When designers work with patterns, they are not imposing artificial order on nature; they are cooperating with the inherent order that already exists.

## Conclusion

**Pattern is design, and design is the subject of permaculture.** The event classification system and the philosophy of pattern unity together provide the conceptual foundation upon which all [[permaculture-design]] rests. By learning to see every phenomenon as an event with a nature, a stage, a dimension, and a location — and by recognizing that all events are expressions of a single, unifying pattern — the designer gains the ability to create systems that are in harmony with the deepest principles governing life on Earth.

The journey from observation to classification to pattern recognition to design is, in essence, the journey of permaculture itself. It begins with paying attention, proceeds through systematic understanding, and culminates in the creative act of shaping systems that sustain and regenerate the living world.
