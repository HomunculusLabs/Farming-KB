## Fungal Pathogens of Animal Populations

## Overview

Fungal pathogens affect virtually all animal groups, from invertebrates to vertebrates, and play significant roles in regulating wild populations, causing disease in aquaculture and agriculture, and driving conservation concerns. This page synthesizes research on the diverse ways fungi act as pathogens across animal taxa, their population-level effects, and their applications in biological control.

## Amphibian Decline and Chytrid Fungi

One of the most dramatic examples of fungal impacts on animal populations is the decline of amphibian species worldwide:

- **Chytridiomycosis** caused by *[[batrachochytrium-dendrobatidis]]* has been linked to mass mortality events in frogs and other amphibians
- **Fellers et al. (2001)** documented oral chytridiomycosis in the mountain yellow-legged frog (*[[rana-mucosa]]*), demonstrating direct pathogenic effects
- **Kaiser (1998)** reported that a fungus may drive frog "genocide" in declining tropical populations
- **Lips (1999)** documented mass mortality and population declines of anurans at an upland site in western Panama, with fungal pathogens implicated
- **Morell (1999)** raised the question of whether pathogens are felling frogs, highlighting the conservation urgency
- **Warkentin et al. (2001)** discovered that an egg-killing fungus induces early hatching of red-eyed treefrog eggs, demonstrating sophisticated host behavioral responses to fungal infection

These declines are particularly concerning because tropical areas serve as biodiversity havens, and amphibians are considered indicator species for ecosystem health. The scenario of climate change producing conditions more conducive for fungal growth raises the possibility that fungal diseases of animals may become increasingly important.

## Fish and Aquatic Animal Pathogens

Fungal diseases affect both wild fish populations and aquaculture operations:

- **Noga (1990)** provided a comprehensive synopsis of mycotic diseases of marine fishes and invertebrates
- **Ogbonna and Alabi (1991)** studied species of fungi associated with mycotic infections of fish in Nigerian freshwater fish ponds
- **Reed et al. (2000)** documented *Chlamydiapneumoniae* infection in a breeding colony of African clawed frogs (*[[xenopus-tropicalis]]*)
- **Norton et al. (1994)** isolated pathogenic dematiacious fungi from juvenile clams, showing economic impacts on shellfish cultivation
- Oomycetes are particularly destructive pathogens of finfish, mollusks, and shellfish

## Entomopathogenic Fungi

Fungi are among the most important natural regulators of insect populations:

### Population Regulation
- **Kamata (2000)** demonstrated that population dynamics of the beech caterpillar (*Syntypistis punctatella*) are influenced by fungal pathogens, with effects following density-dependent patterns
- **Hicks et al. (2001)** showed the influence of weather on populations of pine beauty moth and its fungal diseases in Scotland
- The effect of [[entomopathogenic-fungi]] typically follows density-dependent interaction rules, where impact on the host population is maximal when the animal population is at its peak

### Biocontrol Applications
- **Johnson and Goettel (1993)** achieved reduction in grasshopper populations following field application of *Beauveria bassiana*
- **Onofre et al. (2001)** tested pathogenicity of entomopathogenic fungi against the bovine tick *Boophilus microplus*, showing potential for tick control
- **Freimoser et al. (2000)** developed in vitro cultivation methods for *Entomophthora thripidum*, enabling mass production for biocontrol
- **Leite et al. (2000)** produced hyphal bodies of the mite-pathogenic fungus *Neozygites floridana* in vitro

### Fungal-Pathogen-Mite Interactions
- **Norton et al. (2000)** demonstrated that mycophagous mites mediate tritrophic interactions in grapes through leaf domatia, where mites that consume foliar pathogens are harbored in specialized leaf structures

## Nematode-Trapping Fungi

Nematophagous fungi represent an important group with both ecological and agricultural significance:

- **Larsen et al. (1997)** reviewed biological control of gastrointestinal nematodes in livestock, assessing whether approaches were facts, future, or fiction
- **Bird et al. (1998)** showed that dung-derived biological agents were associated with reduced numbers of infective larvae of equine strongyles in faecal cultures
- **Manueli et al. (1999)** screened fresh dung of small ruminants in Fiji for the presence of nematophagous fungi, finding potential for biological control of nematode parasites
- Nematode-trapping fungi can be introduced into pastures through cattle feces to densities that significantly reduce populations of juvenile gut-parasitic nematodes in soil, thereby reducing the incidence of parasitic nematodes in cattle and improving yields

## Soil-Borne Pathogen Interactions with Fauna

Soil animals can influence the dynamics of fungal plant pathogens:

- **Sabatini and Innocenti (2000)** studied soil-borne plant pathogenic fungi in relation to collembolan species under laboratory conditions
- **Shew and Beute (1979)** demonstrated involvement of soil-borne mites in *Pythium* pod rot of peanut
- **Clapperton et al. (2001)** showed that earthworms indirectly reduce the effects of take-all (*Gaeumannomyces graminis*) on soft white spring wheat
- **Dromph (2001)** demonstrated dispersal of entomopathogenic fungi by collembolans, showing that soil arthropods can spread beneficial fungi

## Climate Change Implications

The relationship between environmental conditions and fungal pathogenicity has important implications under climate change:

- Fungi generally thrive in warm, moist conditions—precisely the conditions predicted to become more prevalent under many climate change scenarios
- When environmental conditions are favorable for fungal growth, pathogens can cause devastating effects on host animal populations
- The decline in anuran populations due to fungal pathogens is especially disturbing from a biodiversity conservation perspective
- The density-dependent nature of many fungal-animal pathogen interactions suggests that stressed populations may be particularly vulnerable

## Implications for Biological Control

The study of fungal pathogens has practical applications in pest management:

1. **Mass production**: Development of in vitro cultivation methods enables commercial-scale production of entomopathogenic fungi
2. **Targeted delivery**: Earthworms and other soil animals can serve as delivery systems for biocontrol fungi to specific soil locations
3. **Integrated approaches**: Combining fungal biocontrol agents with existing management practices can enhance efficacy
4. **Species specificity**: Many fungal pathogens show host specificity, reducing non-target impacts compared to broad-spectrum chemical pesticides

## Key Research Needs

- Better understanding of fungal pathogen impacts under realistic field densities (most studies use unrealistic laboratory concentrations)
- Long-term monitoring of fungal disease prevalence in wild animal populations
- Assessment of climate change effects on the geographic range and virulence of animal-pathogenic fungi
- Development of sustainable biocontrol strategies using fungal pathogens
- Investigation of fungal pathogen roles in species extinction events
