# Natural Culture Community and the Vision of Green Earth

In the closing philosophical chapters of *Sowing Seeds in the Desert*,
Masanobu Fukuoka articulated his vision for an alternative civilization he
called "natural culture and community." This was not a retreat to primitivism
but a proposal for a fundamentally different relationship between humanity
and the natural world, grounded in his experience of restoring degraded land
through [[fukuoka-natural-farming]].

## The Definition of Natural Culture

Fukuoka defined natural culture as a way of life in which people enjoy the
truth and beauty of nature with freedom in their hearts: climbing mountains,
playing in meadows, bathing in warm sunlight, breathing pure air, drinking
crystalline water, and experiencing the true joy of life. The society he
described would be one in which people create a free and generous community,
distinct from both the privation of subsistence living and the anxiety of
consumer culture.

He acknowledged that seen through the eyes of modern civilization, this life
must appear monotonous and primitive. He disagreed completely. The richness
he experienced on his own farm, with its fruit tree jungle, diverse wildlife,
and fukuoka orchard ground cover crop classification, represented an
abundance that modern systems could not replicate.

## The Urgency of the Present Moment

Fukuoka warned that if the primal source of nature is destroyed, it will no
longer restore itself, and the image of natural culture will become obsolete.
Many species of plants and animals become extinct each day, and the meaning
of each disappearance is not merely the death of one organism but the
destruction of the harmony of all living things.

He directed his message to the growing number of people who question the
path of modern society and wonder whether the environmental crisis can be
solved or evaded. He cited scientists who believed the long-term
sustainability of life on earth would be decided in the next twenty to thirty
years. These were the people to whom he spoke directly.

## The Dragonfly as Messiah

One of Fukuoka's most striking and often-quoted statements appears in this
context: "I look forward to the day when there is no need for sacred
scriptures or sutras. The dragonfly will be the messiah." This encapsulates
his belief that spiritual fulfillment comes not from religious doctrine but
from direct experience of nature. The dragonfly, an insect dependent on clean
water and healthy ecosystems, symbolizes the natural world itself as the
source of salvation.

He criticized religious movements that flourished during times of social
disorder, describing founders who used physiognomy, mind reading,
fortune-telling, palmistry, hypnotism, and other techniques to attract and
control believers. These imposters held both deities and people captive
while acquiring money and power.

## The Path of Regreening

Fukuoka's travels around the world, documented in [[fukuoka-world-travels-global-influence]], convinced him that regreening the earth and sowing seeds in the desert is the path society must follow. His visits to India, Africa, Europe, and the United States revealed both the severity of environmental degradation and the potential for recovery through natural methods.

The [[fukuoka-sowing-desert-greening-worldwide-results]] from aerial seeding
with [[fukuoka-clay-seed-pellets]] demonstrated that large-scale revegetation
was technically feasible with minimal resources. The aerial mangrove seeding
at the mouth of the Ganges River, where seedlings of twenty to thirty
varieties sprouted and grew as far as the eye could see, provided a concrete
example of what could be accomplished.

## The Philippines Natural Farm Example

Fukuoka described Ms. Aveliw's natural farm in the Philippines as a concrete
realization of natural culture. She read The One-Straw Revolution, conducted
practical research for almost ten years, then established her farm in four
years mainly by scattering seeds and planting trees. The result was an
assortment of fruit trees (banana, papaya, guava, durian, coffee) beneath
thick ground cover of perennials and green manure, with orchids, birds, and
fish ponds. Despite the Philippines' generally poor soils and widespread
deforestation, she created abundance through harmony with nature.

## No God or Buddha Will Rescue Humanity

Fukuoka's message carried an uncompromising urgency: the destruction of
nature will lead to the destruction of the human race, and no divine
intervention will prevent it. The human race will not be born again. When the
people on earth have died out, there will be no God or Buddha to rescue
them. This is not nihilism but a call to immediate action based on the
recognition that humanity's fate is in its own hands.

He acknowledged that some people most easily perceive the sacredness of
nature: a few religious practitioners, artists of great sensitivity, and
children. Their aesthetic sense, love, receptivity, and understanding
represent people's most basic instincts, their true nature. But even an
artist with keen sensitivity will become lost if their understanding of
nature is unclear.

## The Natural Farm as Model

Fukuoka pointed to his own farm as evidence that the vision was achievable.
The land had been occupied since the Stone Age and was once covered with
ancient forest of at least eight Metasequoia species. Over centuries, the
soil eroded to clay subsoil. His forty-five years of natural farming
restored it to forest-level fertility without external inputs, creating the
"fruit tree jungle" that Larry Korn encountered in 1973.

The techniques he perfected, from [[fukuoka-natural-protective-forests-appendix]]
to [[fukuoka-ground-cover-strategy]], could be applied to regreening the
deserts. His approach was strictly intuitive and based on observation,
arrived at deductively: if the causes of desertification are misguided human
knowledge and action, then eliminating those causes allows nature to heal
itself.
