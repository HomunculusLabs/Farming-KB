## Ectomycorrhizal Succession and Mycelial Foraging Strategies

## Overview

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungal communities are not static assemblages but dynamic systems that change in composition over time and space. This succession is driven by shifts in available nutrient resources, forest stand age, and local environmental conditions. Understanding these successional patterns and the foraging strategies of ECM fungi is critical to predicting forest ecosystem responses to disturbance and environmental change.

## Mycorrhizal Succession During Forest Development

### The Concept of Mycorrhizal Succession

Fleming et al. (1986) first proposed the concept of mycorrhizal succession based on observations of concentric bands of different ECM fungal species around the base of aging birch trees. The outermost ring consisted of "early-stage" fungi, while those nearer the tree base represented "late-stage" fungi. This spatial pattern reflected temporal changes in the mycorrhizal community as the tree and its root system matured.

### Linking Succession to Nutrient Resource Changes

Dighton and Mason (1985) and Last et al. (1987) linked mycorrhizal succession to changes in the nutrient resources available in the forest floor. The general pattern agrees with Read's (1991a) framework:

- **Early-stage forests**: High-quality litter inputs with low C:N ratios favor ECM fungi adapted for inorganic nutrient uptake
- **Late-stage/mature forests**: Accumulation of recalcitrant materials favors ECM fungi with greater enzyme competency for organic nutrient access

Surveys of ECM fruit body production in Sitka spruce and lodgepole pine stands of different ages revealed consistent patterns of mycorrhizal succession (Dighton et al., 1986). However, there is ongoing debate about whether fruit body surveys accurately reflect actual mycorrhizal root tip abundance (Termorshuizen and Schaffers, 1989; Egli et al., 1993; Yamada and Katsuya, 2001).

### Succession on Decomposing Litter

Ponge (1990, 1991) demonstrated that ECM invasion of pine needles occurs during the latter stages of decomposition. Saprotrophic fungal and faunal activity first renders the organic matrix more penetrable by roots and [[mycorrhizal-fungi]], and nutrients become more available in inorganic form. This sequential colonization mirrors the successional patterns seen in saprotrophic fungal communities exploiting changing resources.

## Nutrient Patch Exploitation and Local Succession

### Animal Remains and Waste Patches

Sagara (1995) documented clear successions of mycorrhizal fungal fruit bodies in response to localized nutrient additions from urine, feces, and dead animal bodies in Japanese pine forests:

**Human feces succession:**
- *Ascobolus hansenii*, *Peziza* sp. (immediate, ~day 0)
- *[[laccaria-laccata]]* (later, ~day 100, persisting to day 600+)

**Dead cat succession:**
- *Ascobolous denudatus*, *Tephrocybe tesquorum* (immediate)
- *[[hebeloma-spoliatum]]* (days 100-200)
- *[[lactarius-chrysorrheus]]*, *Mitrulia* sp. (days 300-1700)

**Ammonia-treated soil succession:**
- Coprophilous and early decomposer fungi (days 0-200)
- *[[laccaria-bicolor]]* and *Hebeloma* spp. (days 300-1700) — species with affinity for high ammonium content

### Specialized Exploitation of Mole Middens

The ECM fungus *Hebeloma radicosum* specializes in exploiting subterranean mole middens, where it can "defend" its site of occupancy against the more common *H. spoliatum*. This competitive advantage is attributed to exploitation of local environmental variables such as available nutrients, demonstrating how microhabitat specialization can maintain species diversity within ECM communities.

### Temporal and Spatial Fruiting Patterns

The appearance of different mushroom species in *Abies firma* forests of Japan shows both spatial and temporal phenological patterns (Matsuda and Hijii, 1998), reflecting the combined effects of seasonal resource availability and competitive interactions among ECM species.

## Mycelial Cord Systems and Nutrient Translocation

### Rhizomorphs and Mycelial Mats

Many ECM species with high enzyme activity also possess rapid hyphal growth and large mycelial networks aggregated into cords or rhizomorphs (Read, 1991a). These structures allow translocation of nutrients from distal parts of the extraradical mycelial network to the root, analogous to mycelial cord systems of wood-rotting fungi (Rayner et al., 1985; Wells and Boddy, 1990, 1995; Cairney, 1992; Boddy, 1999).

ECM mycelium can be so dense in humic soil horizons that they form "mats":
- Comprising 10-20% of the top 10 cm of soil in temperate forest ecosystems (Cromack et al., 1988)
- Accounting for 45-55% of total soil biomass (Cromack et al., 1979)

### Nitrogen Immobilization in Mats

Aguilera et al. (1993) showed that mat-forming ECM communities in Douglas fir forests increasingly remove organic nitrogen from the soil pool and immobilize it into high C:N ratio fungal tissue as forest growth progresses. While forest soil becomes enriched with organic nitrogen as it matures, this nitrogen becomes increasingly less available to plant growth — a potential mechanism of nitrogen limitation in old-growth forests.

## Hyphal Foraging Plasticity

### Morphological and Physiological Plasticity

Tibbett (2000) emphasized that both ericoid and ectomycorrhizal extraradical hyphae exhibit significant morphological and physiological plasticity (Bending and Read, 1995a, 1995b; Cairney and Burke, 1996), making them ideally suited for exploiting patchily distributed nutrient resources.

### Density-Dependent Foraging

Dickie and Koide (1998) demonstrated that hyphal foraging responds to nitrogen concentration and form. At low nitrogen concentrations (either inorganic or organic), ECM fungi produce less dense but more extensive hyphal growth, increasing the soil volume explored. This response, similar to that seen in saprotrophs (Ritz, 1995; Rayner, 1991; Rayner et al., 1994), maximizes the probability of encountering nutrient-rich patches.

## Phosphate Acquisition in Deep Soil Layers

Newbery et al. (1997) demonstrated that ECM fungal communities on tropical legume tree species can exploit phosphorus in deeper soil layers beyond the range of surface feeder roots. They proposed the PACER concept (Phenological and Climatic Ectomycorrhizal Response): a strong interaction among phosphate acquisition capacity, environmental controls of phosphate release, and seasonal tree demands — particularly during mast years — that optimizes phosphate utilization while minimizing leaching losses.

## Differential Function of Arbuscular vs. Ectomycorrhizae

Van der Heijden (2001) demonstrated functional differences between AM and ECM associates of willow (*Salix repens*):
- **AM fungus (*Glomus mosseae*)**: Low colonization rate, significant short-term effects on shoot growth and root length, higher shoot P uptake
- **ECM fungus (*Hebeloma leucosarx*)**: High colonization rate, improved host growth over longer term

AM colonization showed greater effects on plants collected in December than March, while ECM effects showed no seasonal difference — suggesting that AM function is more temporally variable.

## Research Priorities

Vogt et al. (1991) identified four key research priorities for ECM ecology:
1. Cost-benefit analyses of maintaining mycorrhizal associations
2. The role of mycorrhizae in nutrient and carbon storage
3. The significance of mycorrhizal linkages between host plants
4. The role of mycorrhizae in acquiring nutrients from organic sources

The paradigm has shifted from viewing mycorrhizae as passive nutrient uptake enhancers to recognizing them as active participants in decomposition processes, with synergistic interactions between saprotrophic and mycorrhizal communities (Rayner, 1998).
