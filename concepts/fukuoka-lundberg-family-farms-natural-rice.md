# Lundberg Family Farms and Natural Rice in California

Lundberg Family Farms, located near Chico, California, in the Sacramento Valley,
represents one of the most significant case studies of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-vs-organic-farming]] on a large commercial scale. Masanobu Fukuoka visited the
7,500-acre farm twice during his American travels, observing a remarkable
transformation in both agricultural practice and philosophical outlook.

## First Visit and Initial Impact

Fukuoka first visited Lundberg Family Farms seven years before his 1986 American
tour. At that time, the farm was being operated organically, growing rice in
each field every two or three years, with fields lying fallow for one year and
planted with summer wheat or barley during the second. When Fukuoka presented
his [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming]] to the owner, Mr. Lundberg,
the response was enthusiastic: "This is wonderful! It is a revolution!"

Following this initial meeting, Lundberg took Fukuoka's words to heart in a
tangible way. He disposed of six of his tractors and began transitioning the
massive operation toward natural farming methods. This act of removing
machinery was significant: it signaled a willingness to abandon the
mechanized, energy-intensive paradigm that characterizes
[[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] in favor of
approaches that work with natural processes.

## The 1986 Reunion

When Fukuoka returned in 1986, he found a transformed operation. Lundberg
reported that he and his three brothers had all adopted natural farming. The
farm now featured four large grain elevators standing in different locations on
the property, and nearly one hundred farmers had gathered that day to celebrate
the founding of an association dedicated to increasing production of naturally
grown rice in the Sacramento Valley and making it available nationwide. Lundberg
presented Fukuoka with a large silver medal for distinguished service.

## The Barnyard Grass Revelation

One of the most striking observations Fukuoka made during his tour was the
presence of barnyard grass growing abundantly throughout thousands of acres of
rice fields. Neither Lundberg nor any of the visiting farmers seemed concerned.
Fukuoka recognized this as a profound shift in mindset. In Japan, such fields
would have been considered a failure, with critics arguing that the weeds
proved [[fukuoka-natural-farming-weed-and-insect-philosophy]]. Lundberg's calm acceptance
of the weeds over seven years demonstrated genuine openness to letting nature
find its own balance.

This tolerance reflected a core Fukuoka principle: that [[fukuoka-bushman-world-ecological-balance]]
emerges when human intervention is reduced. The barnyard grass, far from being
a purely negative presence, contributed to the overall [[fukuoka-living-soil]]
by adding organic matter and supporting microbial activity.

## Yields and Productivity

Despite the apparent disorder of weedy fields, Lundberg's natural rice produced
sixteen to eighteen bushels (960 to 1,080 pounds) per quarter acre, which is
approximately the average yield from conventional paddies in Japan. This result
is particularly impressive because Lundberg achieved it using large harvesting
equipment, whereas Japanese farmers have the advantage of managing small fields
more intensively.

Furthermore, the transition from organic to natural methods enabled Lundberg to
grow rice in each field every year, eliminating the fallow periods that had
previously been necessary. This represented a significant increase in both
land-use efficiency and total production. Combined with the premium price that
naturally grown rice commands over ordinary brown rice, the economic success of
the operation was assured.

## Marketing Innovation

Lundberg also demonstrated creative approaches to marketing. He combined brown,
black, and long-grained varieties into diverse blends, appealing to consumers
seeking variety and nutritional complexity. He expressed a firm resolve not to
surrender to oil companies or the capitalists who back them, regardless of
difficulties encountered. This stance aligned with Fukuoka's broader critique
of [[fukuoka-food-and-human-health]].

## Significance for the Natural Farming Movement

The Lundberg case is significant for several reasons:

1. **Scale**: At 7,500 acres, it demonstrated that natural farming principles
   could be applied far beyond the [[fukuoka-quarter-acre-farmer-do-nothing-farming]] Fukuoka
   typically advocated. While Fukuoka's ideal remained the small family farm,
   Lundberg proved that the philosophy could influence large operations.

2. **Transition pathway**: It showed a viable path from conventional to
   organic to natural farming, supporting Fukuoka's belief that
   [[fukuoka-natural-farming-vs-organic-farming]].

3. **Community spread**: The formation of a hundred-farmer association
   illustrated how one farm's success could catalyze regional adoption,
   contributing to Fukuoka's vision of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-global-influence]].

4. **Soil vitality**: The seven-year journey showed that [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] under natural management, even on a large scale.

Fukuoka concluded that the enthusiasm for raising new rice varieties among
growers near Chico, in the heartland of California, convinced him that natural
farming would expand rapidly and contribute greatly to making wholesome brown
rice an everyday part of the American diet.

## Broader Context: American Agriculture at a Crossroads

Fukuoka's visits to Lundberg and other American farms took place during a
period when roughly half the land in the United States was, or was becoming,
desert. He observed that [[fukuoka-america-drying-up]], with
agricultural experts and agribusiness remaining committed to the belief that
petroleum energy, chemicals, and irrigation could compensate for declining
natural vitality. Against this backdrop, Lundberg's success represented a
tangible alternative and a reason for hope that [[fukuoka-natural-farming-new-age]]
might emerge from the American heartland.
