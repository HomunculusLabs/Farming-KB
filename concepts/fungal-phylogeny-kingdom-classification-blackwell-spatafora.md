# Fungal Phylogeny and Kingdom Classification

## Historical Context and the Polyphyly of Fungi

The concept of "fungi" as understood historically encompassed a far broader assemblage of organisms than the monophyletic Kingdom Fungi recognized today. Historically, mycologists studied not only true fungi but also a range of fungus-like organisms, including water molds (Oomycota), [[slime-molds]] ds]] ds]] (Myxomycota, Dictyosteliomycota, Acrasiomycota), and hyphochytrids. These groups were grouped together primarily on the basis of convergent morphologies, particularly filamentous or absorptive growth forms and spore production. Early fungal herbaria, such as those of E. Fries and C. H. Persoon, contained collections that defined taxa broadly, without the benefit of evolutionary concepts that now guide systematics.

Contemporary molecular phylogenetic studies have demonstrated that these organisms are phylogenetically unrelated and that their similar appearances represent independent evolutionary solutions to similar ecological niches. Blackwell and Spatafora emphasized that this polyphyly of the historical "fungi" concept is one of the most striking examples of convergent evolution among eukaryotes. A monophyletic group, exclusive of slime molds and oomycetes, is now well defined and supported as "true fungi," a kingdom-level taxon. This reclassification was made possible by advances in molecular systematics, particularly the analysis of ribosomal RNA genes and protein-coding gene sequences.

Fungi are heterotrophic organisms that permeate our environment. With few exceptions, fungi have filamentous bodies enclosed by cell walls, are nonmotile, and reproduce both sexually and asexually by spores. They function as primary decomposers in virtually all ecosystems, are critical mutualists forming mycorrhizal and endophytic associations with plants, and act as devastating parasites and pathogens of all major groups of organisms. During the last decade of the [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]], mycologists made unprecedented progress toward producing a phylogenetic classification of fungi, a framework that continues to be refined as new data and analytical methods become available.

The recognition of Kingdom Fungi as a distinct monophyletic lineage separate from plants and animals represented a paradigm shift in biological classification. This reclassification carried profound implications for understanding the evolution of eukaryotic life and required restructuring of long-standing taxonomic conventions. Prior to molecular evidence, the shared absorptive nutrition and cell-wall-bearing growth forms led to the grouping of fungi with plants, obscuring their true evolutionary affinities.

## Molecular Phylogenetics and the Revolution in Fungal Taxonomy

The phylogenetic classification of Kingdom Fungi was revolutionized by molecular data, beginning with analyses of small subunit ribosomal RNA genes (SSU rDNA). Bruns and colleagues (1991) developed a relatively early skeleton phylogeny based on analyses of DNA characters, and subsequent studies by numerous researchers expanded this framework. However, analyses based solely on SSU rDNA often suffered from limited taxon sampling and insufficient resolution of deep branches, particularly because the so-called "crown eukaryotes" appear to have undergone an ancient rapid radiation that is difficult to reconstruct from any single gene.

A landmark study by Baldauf and colleagues (2000) addressed these limitations by analyzing deduced amino acid sequences from four protein-coding regions — alpha-tubulin, beta-tubulin, actin, and elongation factor 1-alpha (EF-1a) — in addition to rDNA. This multi-gene approach obtained better resolution of deep branches representing the earliest divergences in fungal evolution. The large data set supported several controversial or conflicting tree branches depending on whether rDNA or protein-coding genes were used. The incorporation of both molecular and non-molecular phenotypic characters, including flagellum type and mitochondrial crista arrangement, has further strengthened phylogenetic hypotheses and provided additional support for clades identified by DNA sequence data alone. Mitochondrial ultrastructure, particularly the type of mitochondrial cristae — flat, plate-like cristae in true fungi versus tubular cristae in straminipiles — provides non-molecular corroboration of molecular phylogenies and offers important characters for resolving the placement of ambiguous groups.

Fungal classification, as opposed to phylogeny, remains in a state of flux. Ideally, classifications should reflect phylogenetic relationships, but the relationships of all groups have not been resolved, nor have all groups been represented in analyses. Hibbett and Donoghue (1998) suggested the practical use of clade names rather than formal taxon designations for higher taxa until relationships are better resolved. One reactionary suggestion even called for a moratorium on publication of higher-level taxonomic systems until 2050, reflecting the magnitude of remaining uncertainty.

## The Four Traditional Phyla of Kingdom Fungi

Historically, the monophyletic Fungi have been classified in four phyla: Chytridiomycota, Zygomycota, Basidiomycota, and Ascomycota. However, current studies have shown that such a simple classification does not accurately represent the phylogeny of those organisms, as neither Chytridiomycota nor Zygomycota emerge as monophyletic groups in modern analyses.

### Phylum Chytridiomycota (Zoosporic Fungi)

The Chytridiomycota has been traditionally defined by the presence of a single posteriorly inserted smooth flagellum, a plesiomorphic or ancestral trait. At the time molecular methods came into use, some mycologists doubted that chytrids were true fungi, but their inclusion in Kingdom Fungi was confirmed based on rDNA evidence from studies by Förster and colleagues (1990), Bowman and colleagues (1992), and Bruns and colleagues (1993). Subsequent studies with increased taxon sampling demonstrated that the Chytridiomycota is not monophyletic, though some clade of the group occupies the most basal branch of Kingdom Fungi — a finding consistent with a choanoflagellate-like ancestor.

Barr and Desaulniers (1988) established five orders based on zoospore ultrastructure: Blastocladiales, Monoblepharidales, Neocallimastigales, Spizellomycetales, and Chytridiales. Their evolutionary hypothesis is congruent with phylogenies based on mitochondrial DNA. Phylogenetic analyses using SSU rDNA by James and colleagues (2000) distinguished four monophyletic clades consistent with these groupings, though the relationships among the orders and clades were not fully resolved. The core Chytridiomycota forms a clade, but the Blastocladiales may represent a lineage independent of the other orders. Notably, [[batrachochytrium-dendrobatidis]], the chytrid associated with global [[bloomfield-amphibian-decline-fungal-disease-climate-interactions]], does not fall within any of the groups defined by DNA characters. James and colleagues (2000) predicted that additional chytrid diversity will be uncovered when more taxa with unique zoospore types are included in phylogenetic analyses.

The basal position of chytrid-like organisms in the fungal tree is consistent with the hypothesis that the ancestor of all fungi was flagellated and aquatic, with the loss of flagella and adaptation to terrestrial habitats occurring later in fungal evolution. The Neocallimastigales, anaerobic chytrids from the guts of herbivorous mammals, represent an ecologically specialized group that may have diverged relatively early. Their unique cell biology and obligate anaerobic metabolism suggest a long independent evolutionary history.

### Phylum Zygomycota

The Zygomycota, comprising more than 1000 described species, is defined by the production of a meiospore known as the zygospore and the absence of a flagellum. Molecular phylogenetic analyses have revealed that the Zygomycota, as traditionally circumscribed, is polyphyletic. Several traditional zygomycete lineages are considered monophyletic, including Entomophthorales, Harpellales, Kickxellales (excluding Spiromyces), Mucorales, Mortierellales, Dimargaritales, and Zoopagales. However, the relationships among these monophyletic lineages have not been well supported in most analyses.

The genus Basidiobolus has been placed within core chytrids in some rDNA-based analyses, possibly due to long-branch attraction artifacts from accelerated rates of sequence evolution in zygomycete SSU rDNA. In trees based on protein-coding genes, Basidiobolus is retained within the zygomycetes, highlighting the conflicts that arise when trees based on different character systems disagree. The Harpellales and Kickxellales are sister taxa, sharing the ultrastructural feature of plugged, flared septal pores. O'Donnell and colleagues (2001) found that several traditionally recognized families within the Mucorales, including Mucoraceae, Thamnidiaceae, and Pilobolaceae, are polyphyletic. The Amoebidiales, once considered trichomycetes, has been determined to represent a protozoan lineage and has been excluded from the Zygomycota.

Zygomycetes are ecologically diverse, including the pin molds of the Mucorales commonly encountered on decaying fruits, the insect-pathogenic Entomophthorales, and the obligately commensal trichomycetes inhabiting arthropod guts. Their ecological versatility and relatively simple morphology have historically made them difficult to classify, a problem that molecular data has partially resolved while also revealing their deep polyphyly. The dissolution of the Zygomycota as a formal phylum may ultimately require recognition of multiple independent phylum-level lineages.

### Phylum Ascomycota

The Ascomycota is the largest phylum of Kingdom Fungi, with approximately 32,000 described species, characterized by the production of meiospores (ascospores) within sac-shaped cells called asci. The phylum includes organisms of immense importance to human civilization, including [[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]] (baker's yeast), [[penicillium-chrysogenum]] (source of penicillin), and Pneumocystis jiroveci (agent of pneumonia). Three major classes are generally recognized: Archiascomycetes (a paraphyletic assemblage of basal taxa including the fission yeasts, Pneumocystis, and the plant-pathogenic Taphrinales), Saccharomycetes (true yeasts), and Euascomycetes (filamentous, sporocarp-producing taxa and their diverse asexual relatives).

The Euascomycetes is arguably the most successful group of fungi, encompassing parasites, pathogens, and mutualists of plants, algae, and animals, as well as saprobes capable of decomposing virtually all known organic substrata. The poorly resolved base of the euascomycete clade may represent a radiation event in which many major clades originated over a relatively short period. Convergent morphological features, particularly in ascus morphology and dehiscence mechanisms, are common throughout the Euascomycetes, leading to the description of numerous polyphyletic taxa. Lichenization occurred early and infrequently during the evolution of the Euascomycetes, and some nonlichenized lineages, such as the Chaetothyriales, may be derived via the loss of lichenization — a novel hypothesis not predicted from previous morphological studies.

The remarkable morphological plasticity of ascomycetes, which includes unicellular yeasts, filamentous molds, elaborate cup- and flask-shaped fruiting bodies (apothecia and perithecia), and closed stromatic structures (cleistothecia), has historically made morphological classification unreliable. The teleomorph-anamorph connection problem — linking sexual and asexual morphs of the same species — further complicated ascomycete taxonomy until molecular methods enabled direct genotype comparisons across morphologically dissimilar stages.

### Phylum Basidiomycota

The Basidiomycota is the second largest phylum, with approximately 23,000 species, including many common macroscopic forest fungi such as mushrooms and shelf fungi. The group is characterized by meiospores (basidiospores) produced on club-shaped cells called basidia. Three classes are currently recognized: Urediniomycetes (rusts and relatives), Ustilaginiomycetes (smuts), and Hymenomycetes (mushrooms and relatives). Molecular studies have revealed a greater degree of convergent evolution in morphological traits than previously appreciated. The traditionally recognized Phragmobasidiomycetes (fungi with septate basidia) is not monophyletic; the septate basidium is now considered an ancestral character state for the Basidiomycota. The teliospore and "smut" morphology have also arisen independently in multiple lineages.

Within the Hymenomycetes, the mushroom-forming Agaricales, polyporoid aphyllophorales, and corticioid fungi form the most conspicuous elements of the phylum. Molecular phylogenies have dramatically reshaped our understanding of relationships within these groups, showing that gilled mushrooms evolved multiple times independently and that many polypore-like forms are actually more closely related to gilled taxa than to other poroid fungi. The ecological roles of basidiomycetes — as wood decomposers, ectomycorrhizal symbionts, plant pathogens, and litter saprobes — are reflected in their phylogenetic diversification, with major ecological transitions appearing to have driven speciation events throughout the history of the phylum.

## The Glomales Clade and the Crown Fungi

The Glomales comprises arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi associated with an estimated 80% of the world's plant species. Based on an early molecular study by Simon and colleagues (1993), the Glomales was placed in a monophyletic clade sister to the Ascomycota-Basidiomycota lineage, a placement supported by numerous subsequent analyses. Together, Glomales, Ascomycota, and Basidiomycota constitute a large monophyletic clade of "crown fungi," representing the most derived, higher monophyletic grouping within Kingdom Fungi. The Ascomycota and Basidiomycota alone comprise more than 95% of all known fungal taxa.

The timing of the appearance of this clade on Earth probably coincided with the origin and diversification of land plants, underscoring the ecological significance of mycorrhizal associations for terrestrial life. The Glomales includes two major lineages, Glomaceae and Gigasporaceae, as well as two previously unrecognized families, Archaeosporaceae and Paraglomaceae, identified through molecular evidence. Differences in mycorrhizal morphology, fatty acid profiles, and immunological reactions against monoclonal antibodies all agree with the molecular evidence. Geosiphon, a cyanobacterial symbiont, may be derived from within the Glomales rather than being ancestral to it.

## The Fungi-Animal Connection and Microsporidia

Contemporary molecular studies indicate that members of Kingdom Fungi are most closely related to animals, not plants, possibly through a choanoflagellate-like common ancestor. This relationship, supported by analyses of both rDNA and multiple protein-coding genes by Baldauf and Palmer (1993), Wainright and colleagues (1993), and others, was one of the most significant findings to emerge from modern phylogenetics. Historically, fungi had been compared with plants and included in the study of botany, but molecular evidence has fundamentally changed this view.

The placement of Microsporidia — once classified as protozoa due to their reduced cellular organization — as a sister group to Fungi was another important discovery. Microsporidia and Fungi together form a monophyletic grouping with Metazoa (animals), all linked through the choanoflagellate lineage. The relationship identified between Fungi and Microsporidia was one of the key findings strengthened by the multi-gene approach of Baldauf and colleagues (2000), as single-gene analyses sometimes failed to recover this connection with strong support. Microsporidia possess several fungal-like characteristics that support their placement near the fungal lineage, including chitinous spore walls, a trehalose-based energy storage system, and similarities in mitochondrial function, although they have undergone extreme genome reduction as an adaptation to their obligate intracellular parasitic lifestyle. This genome reduction initially obscured their phylogenetic affinities and contributed to their historical classification among the protozoa.

## Fungus-Like Organisms Excluded from Kingdom Fungi

### Kingdom Straminipila (Heterokont Organisms)

The Straminipila includes organisms with a heterokont or derivative flagellar condition, encompassing the Oomycota (water molds), Hyphochytriomycetes, Labyrinthulales, and Thraustochytriales. These organisms form a sister group to Kingdom Fungi in broad eukaryotic phylogenies but belong to a completely different evolutionary lineage more closely related to brown algae, diatoms, and chrysophytes. The Oomycota, which includes economically devastating plant pathogens such as phytophthora infestans, are particularly notable for the damage they cause to agriculture worldwide despite not being true fungi. Cell wall composition differs fundamentally between the two groups: true fungi possess walls of chitin and glucan, whereas straminipiles synthesize cellulose and beta-glucans. Their distinct biochemistry, including differences in lysine synthesis pathways and mitochondrial respiratory chain composition, further supports their separate psychoactive tryptamines in nature distribution ecology and evolutionary origins and underscores why treatments targeting straminipile pathogens must differ fundamentally from those used against true fungal infections.

### Slime Molds

Slime molds represent multiple independent lineages scattered across the eukaryotic tree. Myxomycete (myxogastrid) and dictyostelid slime molds may form a monophyletic group that, together with certain amoebae, constitutes the sister group to Fungi, Microsporidia, and Metazoa. The Plasmodiophorales has been placed in a clade of protozoans near alveolates. Acrasid slime molds occur in a clade with predaceous vahlkampfiid amoebae, remote from other slime-mold groups. These diverse placements underscore the polyphyly of organisms once grouped under the broad concept of "slime molds." Their exclusion from Kingdom Fungi is well supported by molecular data, and the shared traits of spore production and amoeboid motility represent convergent adaptations to similar saprotrophic lifestyles rather than evidence of common ancestry.

## Conclusions and Future Directions

The phylogenetic framework for Kingdom Fungi, while significantly advanced by molecular data, remains incomplete. Fewer than 800 fungal or fungus-like taxa have been included together in any single phylogenetic reconstruction, representing less than 1% of approximately 80,000 currently listed species. Estimates of up to 1.5 million fungal species present a daunting challenge. Future progress will depend on expanded taxon sampling, incorporation of additional independent loci beyond rDNA, and integration of morphological and molecular data to resolve conflicting phylogenetic hypotheses, particularly at the base of the fungal tree where rapid ancient radiations have left a challenging signal for systematists to decipher.

The transition from morphology-based classification to sequence-based phylogenetics has fundamentally altered the practice of mycology, requiring reconciliation of centuries-old nomenclatural codes with newly revealed evolutionary relationships. One of the most consequential outcomes of molecular phylogenetics has been the discovery that morphological simplicity — as seen in yeasts, microsporidia, and many zygomycetes — often masks deep evolutionary divergence, while complex morphological structures in ascomycetes and basidiomycetes frequently arose through convergent evolution. This pattern complicates efforts to identify synapomorphies that define major clades and highlights the need for continued integration of ultrastructural, biochemical, and ecological data alongside molecular sequences.

Emerging genomic technologies, including whole-genome sequencing and transcriptomics, promise to overcome many of the limitations inherent in single-gene and even multi-gene phylogenetic approaches. Phylogenomic analyses of hundreds or thousands of genes from across the fungal tree of life are beginning to resolve the deepest branches with unprecedented confidence. At the same time, environmental DNA surveys are revealing vast pools of undocumented fungal diversity that have never been cultured or observed as sporocarps, suggesting that the true scope of Kingdom Fungi may far exceed even the most generous current estimates. The challenge for the next generation of fungal systematists will be to integrate these molecular discoveries into a stable, phylogenetically informed classification that serves both taxonomic specialists and the broader biological community.
