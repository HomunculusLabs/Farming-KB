## Overview

Designer Compost, commonly referred to as Bokashi, is a cornerstone practice in natural farming that combines organic matter with biodiversed [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]] and targeted bionutrients to create a living, nutrient-rich soil amendment. Unlike conventional composting, which relies on aerobic decomposition alone, Bokashi is an anaerobic fermentation process that preserves more nutrients and produces a more biologically diverse end product. The "designer" designation reflects the ability to tailor the nutrient and microbial profile of the compost to specific crop needs.

## Bionutrients: Fermentation as Extraction

### Why Fermentation Over Simple Extraction

Traditional fermentation is superior to simple extraction methods such as boiling or infusion. When plant or animal materials are merely boiled or steeped, only a fraction of their nutrients and bioactive compounds are released. Fermentation, by contrast, employs microorganisms that actively break down complex plant tissues using sugar as an energy source. This microbial action produces enzymes, organic acids, vitamins, and other metabolites that are not present in simple extracts.

The analogy to food is instructive: fermented foods such as yogurt, kimchi, and miso are demonstrably more nutritious and bioavailable than their unfermented raw ingredients. The same principle applies to [[bionutrients-fermented-plant-extracts]] — the microbial breakdown liberates compounds that would otherwise remain bound within plant cell walls, creating a richer, more accessible nutrient solution for crops.

### General Fermentation Formula

A standard fermentation protocol applies across most bionutrient preparations:

- Fill a container with chopped plant or animal material
- Add crude sugar or molasses at a ratio of approximately 1 part sugar to 3 parts material (roughly 1/3 by volume)
- Leave a 50-75% air gap at the top of the container to allow gas escape
- Ferment for 5-7 days at ambient temperature
- Strain the liquid extract and store in a cool, dark place
- Dilute at approximately 1:20 for foliar or soil application
- Apply at a rate of 2-4 tablespoons per gallon of water

## Key Bionutrient Preparations

### Papaya Fermented Extract

Papaya is valued for its high concentration of the enzyme papain, a proteolytic enzyme that aids in protein digestion [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]] in the soil. Fermented papaya extract also delivers beta-carotene (a precursor to Vitamin A) and Vitamin C, both of which function as antioxidants and plant growth cofactors. This extract is especially useful for improving soil biological activity and supporting seedling establishment.

### Kangkong (Water Spinach) Fermented Extract

Kangkong serves as a growth promotant owing to its natural content of plant growth hormones, including gibberellins, auxins, and cytokinins. A general principle holds that fast-growing plant species accumulate higher concentrations of growth hormones. Kangkong, being a vigorous aquatic vine, is therefore an excellent source material for extracts intended to stimulate vegetative growth in a wide range of crops.

### Banana-Squash-Papaya (BSP) Fermented Extract

The BSP combination is formulated specifically as a flowering and fruiting inducer, with high potassium content driving reproductive development. The blend leverages the complementary nutrient profiles of its three ingredients: banana provides potassium and quick-energy sugars, squash contributes additional potassium and carotenoids, and papaya supplies papain and vitamins. Where these tropical crops are unavailable, suitable substitutes include comfrey, squash, and carrot.

### Calcium Phosphate and Nutrioperiodism

[[cho-water-soluble-calcium-phosphate-preparation]] is grounded in the theory of Nutrioperiodism, developed by Japanese researcher Yasushi Inoue in the 1930s. This theory holds that plants require specific nutrients at specific developmental stages. Inoue identified three principal periods:

1. **Vegetative period** — characterized by leaf and stem development
2. **Changeover period** — the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth
3. **Reproductive period** — flowering, fruiting, and seed production

Calcium phosphate is the critical input during the changeover period, when the plant shifts its energy allocation. The preparation method involves roasting clean eggshells until brittle, soaking them in natural vinegar for approximately two weeks, then diluting the extract at 1:20. Ash from burned soybean stems may serve as an alternative calcium and phosphorus source where eggshells are scarce.

### Ginger-Garlic Extract

Ginger-Garlic Extract functions as a natural antibiotic and fungicide. The preparation involves soaking chopped ginger and garlic in beer or wine for 12 hours, adding sugar at the standard 1/3 ratio, fermenting for 5-7 days, and stabilizing the final product by adding 40% alcohol. This alcohol preservation step is analogous to herbal tincture preparation. Garlic"s sulfur compounds provide potent antifungal properties. Applications include poultry health (added to drinking water), treatment of [[beneficial-indigenous-microorganisms-bim|BIM]]

### Philippine Standard Recipe

The following recipe represents a standard Philippine formulation, scaled to approximately 100 kg total dry weight:

| Ingredient | Amount | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Rice bran | 10 kg | Carbohydrate source |
| Copra meal | 20 kg | Protein source |
| Coco peat | 20 kg | Carbon and moisture retention |
| Chicken manure | 30 kg | Primary nitrogen source |
| Charcoal dust | 20 kg | Carbon substrate for microbes |
| BIM | 1 L | Microbial inoculant |
| Molasses | 1 kg | Energy source for fermentation |
| Bionutrient (mixed) | 1 L | Targeted micronutrient input |

### Basic Formulation Ratio

The underlying nutritional framework follows this approximate ratio:

- **80%** carbohydrate/carbon sources (rice bran, coco peat, charcoal dust)
- **17%** protein/nitrogen sources (chicken manure, copra meal)
- **3%** vitamin, mineral, and micronutrient sources (bionutrients, BIM, molasses)

### US Substitutions

For practitioners outside the tropics, the following substitutions maintain the same functional roles:

- Wheat bran for rice bran (carbohydrate source)
- Corn meal or soybean meal for copra meal (protein source)
- Peat moss for coco peat (moisture retention and carbon)
- Sawdust for charcoal dust (carbon substrate)

### Role of Charcoal Dust

Charcoal dust serves as a carbon substrate for microbe proliferation. Its highly porous structure provides vast surface area for microbial colonization, while also improving aeration, water retention, and [[cation-exchange-capacity]] in the finished compost. The charcoal acts as a refuge and [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] microorganisms, helping to sustain their populations long after the compost has been incorporated into the soil.

### Customizing Bionutrient Emphasis

The "designer" aspect of Bokashi allows the producer to emphasize specific nutrients based on crop requirements. For fruiting crops, high-potassium bionutrients (such as BSP extract) are emphasized. For leafy vegetables or crops in the vegetative stage, high-nitrogen inputs (such as [[fish-amino-acid]] or kangkong extract) are prioritized. The BIM and molasses components remain constant across all formulations.

## Key Principles Summary

The practice of Designer Compost and Bokashi in natural farming rests on three interdependent pillars:

1. **Biodiversed beneficial microorganisms** — a broad community of bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes, and yeasts working in synergistic balance
2. **Targeted bionutrients** — fermented plant and animal extracts supplying specific macro- and micronutrients at appropriate growth stages
3. **Quality carbon and organic matter** — providing the physical structure, energy source, and habitat for sustained microbial activity

Living soil is fundamentally dependent on microbial diversity and balance. Bokashi compost, when properly prepared and applied, introduces and sustains this diversity, creating a self-regulating soil ecosystem that reduces dependence on external chemical inputs.
