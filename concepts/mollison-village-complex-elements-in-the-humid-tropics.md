# Village Complex Elements in the Humid Tropics

Based on traditional and modern village models, Mollison presents a
comprehensive checklist for building a village complex in humid tropical
regions. This framework integrates housing, food production, water
management, and community facilities into a coherent and self-reliant
settlement design. The elements described draw from centuries of
traditional practice refined by permaculture design principles.

## Core Housing and Compound Design

The village complex begins with a well-arranged array of housing,
often grouped around a central compound. The compound itself serves as
the social and functional heart of the settlement, containing:

- Processing and storage areas for harvested crops
- Threshing floors for grain processing
- Dancing areas and meeting houses for community gatherings
- Cooperative or retail stores for local trade
- Bulk fuel depots and firewood storage
- Mulch depots for garden and orchard maintenance

This compound-centred layout ensures that daily activities cluster
efficiently around shared resources, reducing travel time and energy
expenditure while strengthening community bonds.

## Water Supply Systems

A reliable clean water supply is essential to any village complex.
Mollison emphasises that the specific water system must be adapted to
local skills, resources, water availability, and site characteristics.
Options include:

- Wells with hand or mechanical pumps
- Piped water systems from springs or catchments
- Rainwater tanks at individual houses and community buildings
- Dams and reservoirs for larger-scale water storage

The water system must be sited and integrated with overall village
planning, ensuring that clean water sources are protected from
contamination by animal ranges, processing areas, or waste disposal.

## Home Gardens and Orchards

Each house should have between 0.25 and 1.0 hectares of home garden
and orchard, designed around principles of self-reliance. These gardens
are the most intensively managed zones of the village system, producing
a diverse range of foods, medicines, and useful materials. The home
garden is where the highest species diversity is found and where
householders can produce most of their subsistence needs.

## Domestic Animal Integration

At the borders of gardens, ranges are established for domestic species
including chickens, guinea pigs, ducks, rabbits, small pigs, and
pigeons. These animals serve as a manure resource for the home garden,
closing nutrient cycles and reducing the need for external fertiliser
inputs. In areas of plentiful water, fish ponds can be constructed with
some animal housing placed directly over the ponds, so that manures
feed the aquatic system directly.

## Forest and Fuelwood Systems

A sector or zone of fuelwood plantation should be established, ideally
integrated with windbreak plantings that protect the village from
prevailing winds. The fuelwood zone serves multiple functions: providing
cooking and heating fuel, construction materials, mulch, and shelter.
Species selection should favour fast-growing, coppicing trees that
can be managed on a sustainable rotation.

## Commercial Livestock Areas

A strong yard and sheds for cattle and pigs at a commercial level
should be placed at the outer edges of the village complex, where they
have access to grazing or forage without conflicting with intensive
garden zones. These commercial livestock areas can generate surplus for
trade while their manures are directed toward crop production.

## Tree Polyculture Zonation

A careful zonation of tree polyculture is essential, organising tree
plantings from intensive food-producing species near the house to
harder managed timber and fuelwood species at greater distances. This
zonation follows the same [[mollison-zone-planning-frequency-distance-and-placement]] that apply at broader scales,
creating concentric rings of decreasing management intensity.

## Special Facilities

Beyond the basic elements, Mollison identifies special facilities that
enhance village function and resilience:

- Log-trimming or boat-building areas for coastal or riverine sites
- Fish net drying racks and canoe or small boat landings
- Wharf facilities and freezer storage
- Solar pond installations for energy production
- Power house for community energy systems
- Large community drying shed for crop preservation
- Craft work areas for value-added production
- Vehicle or draught-animal parking areas

## Species Richness in Village Systems

The gardens, walls, roof areas, trellis systems, and compounds of
villages are the most complex and rich areas of cultivated species.
Mollison estimates that 200-400 species can be managed and utilised
in such situations. The usage classes break down approximately as:

- Basic food species: 70-90
- Fodch and fodder provision: 10-30
- Medicinals and biocides: 20-50
- Structural and craft materials: 5-20
- Culinary herbs: 20-30
- Beverages and export specialities: 10-15
- Fuelwood and coppice: 12-30
- Special and sacred uses: 8-15
- Total cultivated species: 155-280
- Plus wild-gathered species: 40-80
- Total species utilised: 195-360

While such complex polycultures delight naturalists and householders,
they work best at small scale with close attention from people. The
depopulated wastelands of modern agriculture cannot cope with complex
intercrop systems, thus sacrificing yield, quality, stability, and
people. See also [[mollison-wet-tropical-forest-structure-and-polyculture]] and [[mollison-tropical-home-garden-design]].
