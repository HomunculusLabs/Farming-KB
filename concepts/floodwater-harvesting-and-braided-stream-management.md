# Floodwater Harvesting and Braided Stream Management

## Overview

Floodwater harvesting is the deliberate capture, diversion, and infiltration of flood flows for agricultural and ecological benefit. Traditional societies across [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-arid-regions]] developed sophisticated floodwater farming systems that sustained communities for millennia. Modern [[braided-stream]] management and scour hole creation to maximize the productive use of ephemeral water resources.

## Traditional Floodwater Farming

### Aboriginal Australian Systems

Aboriginal Australians made earthwall floodwater dams to increase Panicum grass and their seed harvest (Stevenson, 1978). These simple structures redirected seasonal flood flows onto productive grasslands, demonstrating an understanding of how to work with natural hydrology rather than against it.

### Papago and Sonoran Desert Systems

The Papago and neighbouring groups in the Sonora regions of the USA (Nabham, 1979) worked on river floodplains and deltaic deposits at the mouths of wadis. Key observations from their systems include:

- **Summer storms** bring 40-60% of water but contribute only 15% of runoff to mainstream reservoirs
- **Most runoff is localized** in intermittent streams, available only for local headwater agriculture
- **Minimum rainfall threshold:** Approximately 8 cm of rain is needed to produce useful runoff
- **Catchment ratio:** 15-27 times the catchment area to the field area
- **Hardy crops:** Sorghum can be produced with runoff only 4 times that of the field area

### Field Construction Techniques

Along the Colorado River, floodplain fields were constructed for major floods using:
- Temporary basins for ponding floodwater
- Irrigation canals for distributing captured water
- Field boundaries of stone and earth reinforced with posts, brush, and vegetation
- Planted and wild weed species grown together as a crop complex over areas of 4 hectares or less per field

The system requires minimal management once established. These techniques formed the basis of the rich Nilotic agriculture along the Nile floodplain.

## Gridiron Floodwater Systems

### Design Specifications

Where flooded land is relatively flat, a gridiron of low banks keeps floodwater and silts settled and absorbed:

- **Bank height:** 0.5-1 m
- **Stabilisation methods:**
  - Stone on the upstream side
  - Tussock grasses or tough low shrubs
  - Live-set sticks and brush
  - Heavy rock walls from the main river bed

### Nutrient Cycling

Gridiron systems in Papago Indian areas maintain high levels of major plant nutrients. The floodwaters carry dissolved minerals, [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]], and silt that deposit in the grid cells, creating naturally fertile growing beds.

### Cropping Strategy

When rivers flood, fields of millet, sorghum, and legumes can be broadcast-sown into the wet silt and later harvested as stored crop. Further from the main stream, broader and deeper dykes hold water in narrower and deeper channels for [[mollison-designers-avocado-mango-and-subtropical-tree-crops]]. The trees should overshade the deep absorption swales that develop below these dykes.

## Braided Stream Management

### The Principle of Braiding

A close study of braiding patterns in streams reveals an inverse relationship between channel width and the land area wetted or saturated. Small multiple streams produce much larger wetted areas than simple main channels. This principle suggests a powerful design strategy for arid floodplains.

### Natural Braiding Process

Braiding depends on shallow water flow over extensive deep sand sheets; it does not occur underwater. Each braid-form is initially a rough diamond shape with plunge-pools at each corner, caused by the confluence of side streams.

### Engineering Braided Streams

The untried but promising strategy involves:
1. Building **concreted, deeply bedded deflections** in main channels
2. Setting these out to split flood flow as often as there is space to create small channels
3. Using **notched weirs**—level walls with notches that imitate natural plunge-pools—to initiate finer braid patterns across flood plains

In low flow conditions, only the channel flow persists and sheet flow across the interfluve is absent. The aim is to widen and slow floodwaters, wetting as much alluvium as possible before water is lost to saltpans downstream.

### Experimental Approach

Methods of achieving multiple stream braiding would best be modelled in experimental sand channel flows before full-scale implementation. However, the ground effects on [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] are evident in many [[animal-systems-arid-areas-permaculture]], where wetted areas support strong tree growth compared to dry interfluves.

## Mulch Fences on Floodplains

As headwater streams coalesce and enter broad valley systems, braided rivers drop silt and leave lines of organic mulch at their high-water marks. This mixture of seed, manure, twigs, molluscs, and debris is excellent garden material and can be trapped using a sequence of **stout scoop fences** placed across the floodplain.

The fences serve multiple functions:
- **Silt trapping:** Capturing nutrient-rich sediments from flood flows
- **Mulch collection:** Harvesting organic debris for garden use
- **Flow modification:** Slowing water and promoting infiltration
- **Scour hole creation:** When properly positioned, creating permanent lagoons

## Scour Hole Lagoons

### Formation Mechanism

Permanent desert lagoons within 10-50 km of ranges form where a strong natural ridge (often unnoticed by visitors) approaches the river from one or both banks. This constricts flood flow, forcing increased velocity and powerful hydraulic digging action that excavates deep scour holes.

### Ecological Value

These lagoons are often of great beauty, supporting:
- Crustaceans and fish
- Molluscs
- Water plants such as lotus, kangkong, water chestnut, and submerged weeds
- Birds and mammals visiting by day and night

### Artificial Construction

Scour holes can be constructed or enhanced using a combination of rock-cement walls and bulldozed banks, stronger and deeper near the river bed itself. The ponds remain full in drought because deep river sands contain millions of litres of slow seepage, which can be increased by installing sand dams higher up in the system.

### Design Caution

Open-water systems should not be created too frequently unless silt dams of up to 9-10 times the capacity of each pool have been constructed upstream, as open water evaporates over time. The profile should follow natural scour bed geometry, with deeper sections near the constriction point.
