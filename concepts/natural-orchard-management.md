# Natural Orchard Management and Ground Cover

Natural orchard management is Masanobu Fukuoka's approach to growing fruit trees without pruning, fertilizers, pesticides, or conventional weeding. Instead, the orchard functions as a three-dimensional ecosystem where fruit trees coexist with green manure crops, nitrogen-fixing trees, poultry, and semi-wild vegetables. The system relies on ground cover cultivation, particularly ladino clover, to build and maintain soil fertility while suppressing unwanted weeds.

## Establishing a Natural Orchard

### Site Preparation Without Bulldozers

Fukuoka strongly advised against clearing and smoothing land with a bulldozer when establishing an orchard. Bulldozing disturbs the humus-rich topsoil built up over long periods, and land developed this way is washed free of its topsoil within ten years, greatly shortening the economic life of the farm. Instead, he recommended:

- Arranging felled branches, leaves, and trunks along contour lines to decompose naturally
- Allowing organic matter to serve as a nutrient source while curbing weed growth and preventing soil erosion
- Preserving tree roots in the ground, as they work down to the deepest soil strata, contribute to soil aggregation, and have chelating action that solubilizes insoluble nutrients

Burning branches and leaves, or digging up and disposing of tree roots, drastically changes natural conditions and damages soil beyond recovery.

### Soil Fertility Without Inputs

One foot of natural topsoil holds enough nutrients to sustain fruit trees for ten years without fertilization; three feet of rich soil can supply enough nutrients for about thirty years. Trees grown from seed in such conditions develop deep root systems, resist temperature extremes, and live two to three times longer than trees grown by scientific methods with artificial fertilizers.

### Planting

Fruit saplings should be planted at equal intervals along hill contours. Fukuoka recommended digging a fairly deep hole, filling it with coarse organic matter, and planting the sapling over this material. He preferred trees grown from seed over grafted nursery stock, noting that grafted trees have blocked sap flow at the graft juncture, resulting in either dwarf trees requiring heavy fertilization or trees with short lifetimes and poor resistance.

## Three-Dimensional Orchard Design

### Mixed Planting

Fukuoka advocated avoiding monoculture in orchards. A natural orchard should include:

- **Deciduous fruit trees** alongside evergreen fruit trees for year-round biological activity
- **Green manure trees**: Acacias (pea family, produce nitrogen), myrtle (produces phosphoric acid and potash), alder, and podocarpus
- **Climbing fruit vines**: Grapevine, akebia, and Chinese gooseberry
- **Leguminous ground covers**: Clover, alfalfa, lupine, and bur clover
- **Semi-wild vegetables**: Grown as orchard undergrowth
- **Poultry and livestock**: Allowed to graze freely in the orchard

This full three-dimensional use of space creates an entirely different environment from conventional orchards that employ high-production techniques.

## Ground Cover System

### Why Ground Cover Matters

The purpose of soil management in a natural orchard is to promote the conversion of weathered bedrock material into living organic soil. Conventional soil management consists of clean cultivation that turns soil into mere mineral matter. Ground cover cultivation enables soil to become naturally enriched without external inputs.

Fukuoka's motivation for developing ground cover cultivation was both ecological and economic. His father's citrus orchard, once regarded as one of the best locally, had failed to make a net profit over more than forty years of operation. During profitable years, the sturdy trees and growing wealth masked the fact that orchard soil was steadily becoming depleted. Fukuoka set out to raise fruit trees that grow as the soil enriches.

### Ladino Clover as Primary Cover

Ladino clover (Trifolium repens latum) became the primary ground cover in Fukuoka's orchard. Its advantages include:

- **Weed elimination**: Annual weeds are displaced in one year; biennials disappear in two years. Within two to three years, almost all garden weeds vanish, leaving a solid clover field.
- **Soil improvement**: Improves soil to a depth of 16 to 18 inches.
- **Persistence**: Seed does not need to be resown for 6 to 8 years.
- **Low competition**: Does not compete strongly with fruit trees for fertilizer or moisture.
- **Resilience**: Grows back easily after cutting and remains healthy even when trampled.
- **Farm compatibility**: Does not hinder farming operations.

The disadvantages are susceptibility to summer-killing and sclerotium disease during hot, dry weather, and retarded growth in shade and under trees.

### Seeding and Managing Clover

Clover seed should be drilled in the first autumn. Delayed seeding invites insect damage. Seeds should not be covered with soil, as this often hampers germination; the soil should merely be firmed after drilling. When broadcast in late autumn among dying weeds and grasses, clover growth gradually thickens.

Proper management is essential. Clover does not choke out other vegetation but becomes dominant by growing thickly enough to prevent weed germination. If left entirely alone after taking well, clover becomes excessively luxuriant, leaving it open to leaf spot disease and the reemergence of weeds within five to six years. Clover requires the same meticulous care given a lawn, with more frequent cutting in areas where perennial weeds such as sorrel, dandelion, bindweed, and bracken grow abundantly, supplemented by scattering wood ashes or coal ashes.

### Alfalfa for Deep Soil Improvement

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa) complements clover in warm and dry regions. Sending roots down to depths of six feet or more, alfalfa improves deeper soil strata that clover cannot reach. A hardy perennial resistant to drought, cold, and high temperatures, it also serves as a feed and forage crop. When mixed with clover, alfalfa helps eliminate other herbs and grasses.

### Bur Clover for Seasonal Coverage

Bur clover (Medicago polymorpha) controls spring weeds, withers in summer, and grows back in fall to suppress winter weeds. It serves as a useful orchard cover crop and a valuable rotation crop preceding summer vegetables.

## Black Wattle: The Multipurpose Fertilizer Tree

### Soil Conditioning

The black wattle (Acacia mearnsii), a type of acacia, is central to Fukuoka's orchard system. Up to ten trees should be planted per quarter-acre among the fruit trees. As a member of the pea family, it provides:

- Rapid improvement of deep soil layers
- Shelterbelt formation and windbreak protection
- Shade during summer in warm regions, protecting soil from depletion
- Effective prevention of orchard pests, especially mites

Five to six years after planting, an area of more than 100 square yards of what had been hard, lean soil around each tree becomes soft and porous, far more effectively than mechanical methods. When cut, each tree yields approximately half a ton of high-quality organic material for burying in trenches.

### Protection of Beneficial Insects

The black wattle serves a critical ecological role as a protective tree for beneficial insects. It grows constantly throughout the year, sending out new shoots that attract aphids and scales, which in turn support growing populations of ladybugs. Planting five or so per quarter-acre keeps scales and mites to a minimum.

### Economic Value

Beyond its ecological functions, the black wattle produces bark rich in tannin (marketable at good prices), excellent wood for furniture making, and nectar for honey production. It grows five or more feet per year, creating a shelterbelt in three to four years and reaching the size of a telephone pole in seven to eight years.

## Orchard Development Timeline

### Years 1 to 5: Establishment

After planting fruit saplings among tree stumps, suckers grow from cut stumps and weeds and low brush flourish. Orchard management consists primarily of cutting weeds and underbrush with a large sickle twice a year. Some pinching back of young saplings may be necessary to correct branch arrangement, especially if dieback occurs or too many suckers emerge.

### Years 5 to 6: Terracing

When trees begin bearing fruit, terraces and roads are constructed on the orchard slope by digging earth on the uphill side of the fruit trees with a hoe. Original weeds are gradually replaced, first with soft weeds such as chickweed, knotweed, and crabgrass, then with clover. The orchard begins to look like a conventional orchard.

### Years 6 to 10: Soil Building

Black wattle trees are felled and buried in trenches within the orchard, contributing massive amounts of organic matter. Clover cover becomes fully established. Soil fertility improves measurably as the ecosystem matures.

### Years 10 Plus: Maturity and Replanting

After about ten years, the clover crop begins to decline and weeds reemerge. This is natural and not necessarily problematic, as the emergence and succession of different herbs is more conducive to long-term soil enrichment than maintaining a single plant species. If weeds become too thick to manage, clover is reseeded or replaced with vegetable crops.

For old, rundown orchards, Fukuoka recommended planting many acacia trees among the fruit trees, then felling everything after five to six years and replanting with fresh saplings. This is far superior to bulldozing and replanting, as it rejuvenates the land.

## Soil as Indicator: Reading Weeds

Fukuoka believed that one can understand the nature of the soil more quickly by examining the weeds growing in it than by examining the soil itself. Weeds solve the problems of both the soil and the weeds. By observing which weeds emerge and succeed one another, the farmer can diagnose soil conditions and select appropriate cover crops. This principle of reading nature's signals rather than applying laboratory analysis is central to the natural farming approach.
