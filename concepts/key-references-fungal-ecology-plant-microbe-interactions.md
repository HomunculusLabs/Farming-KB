## Key References in Fungal Ecology and Plant-Microbe Interactions

This page synthesizes the major research themes from the reference literature of Chapter 5 ("Fungi and Population and Community Regulation") in John Dighton"s *Fungi in Ecosystem Processes*. The cited works span mycorrhizal ecology, fungal succession, biological control, endophyte-plant mutualisms, phyllosphere microbiology, and nematophagous fungi, collectively forming a comprehensive map of fungal roles in terrestrial ecosystems.

## Mycorrhizal Ecology and Community Structure

Mycorrhizal research forms the backbone of this reference collection. The foundational text is Smith and Read (1997), *Mycorrhizal Symbiosis*, which serves as the definitive overview of the field. Key early contributions include Pirozynski and Malloch (1975), who proposed the "mycotrophism" hypothesis for the origin of land plants, suggesting that fungal associations were critical to terrestrial colonization.

Francis and Read (1994, 1995) demonstrated that mycorrhizal fungi are determinants of plant community structure, showing that the mutualism and antagonism within mycorrhizal symbiosis directly impacts which plant species coexist. Gange, Brown, and Sinclair (1993) extended this finding to early succession, establishing vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi as a controlling factor in plant community assembly during primary succession.

Johnson, Tilman, and Wedin (1992) explored how both plant and soil factors control mycorrhizal communities, while van der Heijden et al. (1998) provided experimental evidence that different arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal species act as potential determinants of plant community structure. This work directly contributed to the diversity-function debate in ecology.

Koide and collaborators produced an important body of work on mycorrhizal effects on plant fitness and offspring vigor. Koide, Li, Jewis, and Irby (1988) compared wild versus cultivated oats, while Koide and Lu (1992) showed nutritional effects on offspring growth and reproduction. Lewis and Koide (1990) linked phosphorus supply and mycorrhizal infection to plant offspring vigor, and Heppell, Shumway, and Koide (1998) examined how mycorrhizal infection affects competitiveness of offspring.

## Fungal Succession and Ecosystem Development

The literature documents extensive work on fungal succession during primary and secondary succession. Last, Dighton, and Mason (1987) described successions of sheathing (ectomycorrhizal) fungi, while Jumpponen and colleagues conducted landmark studies on glacier forefronts: Jumpponen, Mattson, and Trappe (1998) examined mycorrhizal functioning of *Phialocephala fortinii* with *Pinus contorta* on glacier forefront soil, and Ohtonen et al. (1999) tracked ecosystem properties and microbial community changes during primary succession on glacier forefronts.

Janos (1980) established that mycorrhizae influence tropical succession, a finding later expanded by Holah, Wilson, and Hansen (1997), who showed impacts of native root-rotting pathogens on successional development of old-growth Douglas fir forests.

## Biological Control and Mycoparasitism

The reference list contains substantial literature on fungal biological control of plant pathogens. Jeffries (1997) reviewed mycoparasitism in *The Mycota IV*, while Kerry and Jaffee (1997) surveyed fungi as biological control agents for plant-parasitic nematodes. Specific biocontrol studies include:

- Liu (1995): VAM fungi effects on Verticillium wilt of cotton
- Mandeel and Baker (1991): Mechanisms of nonpathogenic *Fusarium oxysporum* strains controlling fusarium wilt on cucumber
- Pieckenstain et al. (2001): *Epicoccum purpurascens* for biocontrol of Sclerotinia head rot of sunflower
- Traquair (1995): Endomycorrhizal suppression of cylindrocarpon root rot
- Tsahouridou and Thanassoulopoulos (2002): *Trichoderma koningii* suppression of damping-off by *Sclerotium rolfsii*
- Van den Boogert and Deacon (1994): Biotrophic mycoparasitism by *Verticillium biguttatum* on *Rhizoctonia solani*

## Fungal Endophytes and Grass Mutualisms

The coevolution of fungal endophytes with grasses is well-represented. Lane, Christensen, and Miles (2000) examined the significance of secondary metabolites in endophyte-grass coevolution, while Richardson (2000) questioned whether endophyte alkaloids are defense chemicals or biological anomalies. Marks and Lincoln (1996) demonstrated that antiherbivore defense mutualism between fungal endophytes and grasses persists under elevated CO₂ levels. Wilson and Faeth (2001) investigated whether endophytes result in selection for leafminer oviposition preference, and Moy et al. (2000) identified epiphyllous mycelial nets on leaves of grasses infected by clavicipitaceous endophytes.

## Nematophagous Fungi and Soil Food Webs

Several references address the role of fungi in controlling nematode populations. Hay et al. (1998) studied invasion of sheep dung by nematophagous fungi and soil nematodes in New Zealand pastures. Kerry (1988) reviewed fungal parasites of cyst nematodes, while Manueli et al. (1999) screened fresh dung of small ruminants in Fiji for nematophagous fungi. Mankau (1981) provided an early review of microbial control of nematodes, and Larsen et al. (1997) assessed whether biological control of gastrointestinal nematodes was fact, future, or fiction.

## Ectomycorrhizal Networks and Carbon Transfer

The phenomenon of carbon transfer between ectomycorrhizal tree species through [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]] is represented by the work of Simard and colleagues. Simard, Perry, Smith, and Molina (1997a) examined effects of soil trenching on ectomycorrhizal occurrence, while Simard, Jones, Durall, Perry, Myrold, and Molina (1997b) demonstrated reciprocal transfer of carbon isotopes between ectomycorrhizal *Betula papyrifera* and *Pseudotsuga menziesii*, and (1997c) showed net carbon transfer between ECM tree species in the field. Read (1998) later popularized this concept as "plants on the web."

## Phyllosphere and Leaf Surface Fungi

The ecology of fungi on leaf surfaces is documented through several key references. Last and Deighton (1965) provided foundational work on the non-parasitic microflora on living leaf surfaces, while Preece and Dickinson (1971) published *Ecology of Leaf Surface Micro-organisms*. Garbaye (1991) described biological interactions in the mycorrhizosphere, and Omar and Heather (1979) showed how saprophytic phylloplane fungi affect germination of *Melampsora larici-populina*.

## Forest Pathogens and Stand Dynamics

Hansen and Goheen (2000) reviewed *Phellinus weirii* and other native root pathogens as determinants of forest structure in western North America. Hiers and Evans (1997) showed how anthracnose affected dogwood mortality and forest composition on the Cumberland Plateau. Packer and Clay (2000) demonstrated that soil pathogens create spatial patterns of seedling mortality in temperate trees, and van der Putten (2000) discussed pathogen-driven forest diversity.

## Allelopathy and Polyphenols

Hättenschwiler and Vitousek (2000) reviewed the role of polyphenols in terrestrial ecosystem nutrient cycling, while Golovko (1999) examined allelopathic soil sickness from basic and methodological perspectives.

## Fungal Conservation and Biodiversity

The reference collection signals emerging awareness of fungal conservation needs. Arnolds" work on changes in fungal communities under nitrogen deposition (1989a-d) led to the development of "red data" lists for fungal species conservation. Newsham, Fitter, and Watkinson (1995) explored multi-functionality and biodiversity in arbuscular mycorrhizas, connecting fungal diversity to ecosystem function.

## Fungal Dispersal by Animals

Animal-mediated dispersal of fungal spores is represented by Kotter and Farentinos (1984a,b), who showed that tassel-eared squirrels act as spore dispersal agents for hypogeous mycorrhizal fungi through their feces. Trappe and Maser (1976) demonstrated germination of *Glomus macrocarpus* spores after passage through a rodent digestive tract.
