    10|# Phyllotaxy and Natural Tree Form: The Case Against Pruning
    11|
    12|## Overview
    13|
    14|Masanobu Fukuoka argued that fruit tree pruning is unnecessary when trees are
    15|allowed to grow in their natural form from the start. He based this argument
    16|on the botanical principle of phyllotaxy: the mathematically precise
    17|arrangement of leaves and branches on a plant. Since natural trees never
    18|produce crossing branches, dying lower limbs, or uneven foliage, the
    19|problems that pruning supposedly corrects are actually caused by pruning
    20|itself. A tree grown under natural conditions attains the goals that
    21|pruning seeks to achieve through human intervention.
    22|
    23|## The Problem Pruning Claims to Solve
    24|
    25|When pruning is discontinued on a fruit tree, growers observe:
    26|
    27|- The form of the tree becomes confused
    28|- Primary scaffold branches entangle
    29|- Foliage grows dense, complicating orchard management
    30|- Heavy spraying of pesticides becomes ineffective
    31|- Branches grow ridiculously long, crossing with neighboring trees
    32|- Sunlight ceases to penetrate to lower branches, which weaken
    33|- Ventilation is poor, encouraging disease and insect infestation
    34|- Dead and dying branches abound
    35|- Fruit forms only at the surface of the tree
    36|
    37|Growers concluded from these observations that pruning is absolutely essential.
    38|Fukuoka argued they were observing the effects not of neglect but of prior
    39|pruning followed by abandonment.
    40|
    41|## Phyllotaxy: The Mathematical Order of Nature
    42|
    43|Fukuoka drew attention to phyllotaxy, the fixed law governing leaf and branch
    44|arrangement on plants. In a given plant, leaves always grow either alternately
    45|or oppositely. The direction and angle at which a leaf grows is always the
    46|same; there is never even the slightest deviation.
    47|
    48|For example, on branches of peach, persimmon, mandarin orange, orange, and
    49|cherry trees, the angle between consecutive leaves is 72 degrees. This means:
    50|
    51|- The sixth leaf is always located directly above the first leaf
    52|- The eleventh leaf is always directly above the sixth leaf
    53|- If the distance between consecutive buds is one inch, the distance from
    54|  one leaf to the next leaf directly above it is always five inches
    55|- Two leaves will not overlap or two branches emerge within any five-inch
    56|  length along the branch
    57|
    58|## Evidence from Forest Trees
    59|
    60|Fukuoka pointed to pines and cedars growing in natural forests as proof that
    61|trees do not need pruning:
    62|
    63|- Trunks never branch or twist as long as they are not cut or harmed
    64|- Branches on the right and left sides never run up against each other or
    65|  cross
    66|- There are no dense lower branches that die back
    67|- Upper and lower branches do not grow so close that sunlight cannot reach
    68|  some of the leaves
    69|- Every leaf, shoot, and branch grows in an orderly and regular arrangement
    70|- No part of the plant is in disarray or confusion
    71|
    72|This orderly arrangement is abundantly clear when one looks carefully at a
    73|mountain pine. The central trunk rises straight, putting out branches at
    74|equal vertical spacings in a radial arrangement. One can clearly see the
    75|chronology of branch emergence, with spacing and angle both regular and
    76|orderly. Never does one branch grow too long or cross with another.
    77|
    78|## The Bamboo, Cryptomeria, and Camphor Pattern
    79|
    80|The same principle applies across species. In bamboo, the emergence of a
    81|branch or leaf follows a fixed law for that type. Cryptomeria, Japanese
    82|cypress, camphor tree, camellia, Japanese maple, and all other trees observe
    83|the phyllotaxy and divergence specific to that species.
    84|
    85|Fukuoka posed the question: if we simply let fruit trees and mountain pines
    86|grow to their full size under natural conditions, would the very goals at
    87|which the gardener aims through pruning not be attained naturally? Would the
    88|persimmon, peach, and citrus tree ever have needed cutting with a saw or
    89|lopping off branches to control erratic growth if left to grow of their own
    90|accord?
    91|
    92|## Misconceptions About the Natural Form
    93|
    94|Fukuoka identified a fundamental error in how orchardists conceive of the
    95|natural form:
    96|
    97|- **Neglected tree fallacy:** Many have a vague idea of the natural form as
    98|  something akin to the shape of a neglected tree. There is a world of
    99|  difference between a tree left to grow naturally and a tree that was
   100|  pruned haphazardly then abandoned.
   101|- **Environmental variation confusion:** Trees grow under incredible diversity
   102|  of conditions. A low, twisted pine on a seashore is not the natural form
   103|  any more than a lone cryptomeria standing tall in a meadow. The form
   104|  depends on the growing conditions.
   105|- **Scientific uncertainty:** Scientists say the natural form of a citrus
   106|  tree is hemispherical with primary scaffold branches at 40 to 70 degrees,
   107|  but no one knows whether the true form is a large upright tree or a low
   108|  bush.
   109|
   110|## Pruning as Self-Perpetuating Error
   111|
   112|The most devastating argument against pruning is that it creates the very
   113|conditions that make it seem necessary:
   114|
   115|1. Pruning disrupts the natural phyllotactic arrangement
   116|2. The tree responds with erratic growth to compensate for lost branches
   117|3. New growth is dense and tangled because the natural branching pattern has
   118|   been broken
   119|4. The grower prunes again to correct the problems caused by the previous
   120|   pruning
   121|5. Each pruning cycle makes the tree more dependent on human intervention
   122|
   123|Fukuoka compared this to striking one's left hand with one's right. No
   124|persimmon or chestnut tree has branches on the right that compete with those
   125|on the left and must be cut back because they grow too long. A branch on the
   126|east side does not wander over to the south side, cutting off light.
   127|
   128|## The Real Reason Trees Are Pruned
   129|
   130|Fukuoka argued that the true reasons trees are pruned have nothing to do with
   131|the tree's welfare:
   132|
   133|- **Operational convenience:** Trees are shaped to facilitate tillage,
   134|  weeding, fertilization, spraying, and harvesting
   135|- **Compensation for artificial cultivation:** Trees weakened by chemical
   136|  fertilizers and pesticides develop the problems that pruning claims to fix
   137|- **Ignorance of natural form:** Growers prune because they have never seen
   138|  a truly natural fruit tree and cannot imagine one
