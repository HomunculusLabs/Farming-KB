## Death Cap (Amanita phalloides) — Amatoxin Toxicology and Pathology

## Overview

*Amanita phalloides*, commonly known as the death cap, is responsible for the vast majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. It contains amatoxins — bicyclic octapeptides that are among the most potent natural toxins known. A single mature cap can contain a lethal dose for an adult, and the insidious nature of [[bloomfield-destroying-angel-amanita-virosa-amatoxin-poisoning-clinical-course]] is essential for mycologists, foragers, and medical professionals.

## Species Description

*Amanita phalloides* is a medium to large agaric with the following morphological features:
- **Pileus**: 5–15 cm, convex to flat, smooth, greenish-yellow to olive-green (but can be white in some populations), often with a silky sheen when wet
- **Gills**: white, free, crowded
- **Stipe**: white, 6–15 cm, with a membranous skirt-like annulus (ring) near the top
- **Volva**: white, sac-like cup at the base, often buried in soil — this is a critical identification feature
- **Spore print**: white
- **Flesh**: white, does not change color when cut
- **Odor**: initially pleasant, sometimes described as honey-like, becoming sickly-sweet in age
- **Habitat**: ectomycorrhizal with oaks, beeches, and other hardwoods. Originally native to Europe, now established in [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], and other regions through introduction with host trees.

### Enterohepatic Circulation
A critical factor in amatoxin toxicity is enterohepatic circulation. After absorption from the gastrointestinal tract, amatoxins are transported to the liver via the portal vein. The liver processes and excretes them into bile, which is released into the duodenum. The amatoxins are then reabsorbed from the intestine, creating a recycling loop that repeatedly exposes the liver to toxin. This enterohepatic circulation prolongs the effective half-life of amatoxins and amplifies hepatic damage.

### Gastrointestinal Phase Mechanism
The severe gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) during the initial phase are primarily caused by phallotoxins and possibly by direct irritant effects of amatoxins on the intestinal mucosa.
