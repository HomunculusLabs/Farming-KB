# Death Cap (Amanita phalloides) — Amatoxin Toxicology and Pathology

## Overview

*Amanita phalloides*, commonly known as the death cap, is responsible for the vast majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. It contains amatoxins — bicyclic octapeptides that are among the most potent natural toxins known. A single mature cap can contain a lethal dose for an adult, and the insidious nature of amatoxin poisoning — with a characteristic latency period before symptoms — contributes to its lethality. Understanding the toxicology, clinical presentation, and treatment of amatoxin poisoning is essential for mycologists, foragers, and medical professionals.

## Species Description

*Amanita phalloides* is a medium to large agaric with the following morphological features:
- **Pileus**: 5–15 cm, convex to flat, smooth, greenish-yellow to olive-green (but can be white in some populations), often with a silky sheen when wet
- **Gills**: white, free, crowded
- **Stipe**: white, 6–15 cm, with a membranous skirt-like annulus (ring) near the top
- **Volva**: white, sac-like cup at the base, often buried in soil — this is a critical identification feature
- **Spore print**: white
- **Flesh**: white, does not change color when cut
- **Odor**: initially pleasant, sometimes described as honey-like, becoming sickly-sweet in age
- **Habitat**: ectomycorrhizal with oaks, beeches, and other hardwoods. Originally native to Europe, now established in North America, Australia, South America, and other regions through introduction with host trees.

The death cap is responsible for an estimated 90–95% of all mushroom fatalities globally. It is frequently confused with edible species, particularly the paddy straw mushroom (*Volvariella volvacea*) in Southeast Asia and various edible *Amanita* species in Europe.

## Amatoxin Chemistry

### Structure and Classification
Amatoxins are bicyclic octapeptides (8 amino acid cyclic peptides) containing a unique tryptathionine bridge (a cross-link between a tryptophan and a cysteine residue). The major amatoxins found in *A. phalloides* are:

- **α-amanitin**: the most abundant and toxic amatoxin, primary inhibitor of RNA polymerase II
- **β-amanitin**: slightly less potent than α-amanitin, similar mechanism
- **γ-amanitin**: minor component, lower toxicity
- **ε-amanitin**: minor component
- **amanullin, amaninamide, amanullinic acid**: minor amatoxins with lower toxicity

### Concentration in Tissues
Amatoxin distribution within the fruiting body is not uniform:
- **Pileus (cap)**: highest concentration, particularly in the skin
- **Gills**: high concentration
- **Stipe**: moderate concentration
- **Volva**: variable concentration
- **Total content**: a single mature *A. phalloides* cap can contain 5–8 mg of total amatoxins; the estimated lethal dose for an adult human is 0.1 mg/kg body weight (approximately 7–10 mg for a 70 kg adult)

### Other Toxins
*Amanita phalloides* also contains:
- **Phallotoxins** (phalloin, phalloidin, phallisin, phallacidin): bicyclic heptapeptides that are toxic when injected but poorly absorbed orally. They stabilize actin filaments, preventing depolymerization. Phallotoxins contribute to the severe gastrointestinal symptoms but are not responsible for the delayed hepatic and renal failure that characterizes lethal amatoxin poisoning.
- **Virotoxins**: monocyclic peptides similar in action to phallotoxins, found in some *Amanita* species.

## Mechanism of Toxicity

### Primary Target: RNA Polymerase II
α-Amanitin is an extremely potent and selective inhibitor of eukaryotic RNA polymerase II (Pol II), the enzyme responsible for transcribing messenger RNA (mRNA) from DNA. The binding is essentially irreversible at physiological temperatures:

- α-Amanitin binds to the bridge helix of the RPB1 subunit of Pol II
- This blocks the translocation step of transcription, preventing mRNA elongation
- Without new mRNA synthesis, protein synthesis ceases
- Cells with high metabolic rates and protein turnover are most affected

### Secondary Effects
The interruption of mRNA synthesis triggers cascading cellular pathology: depletion of short-lived proteins (enzymes, structural proteins), mitochondrial dysfunction with impaired oxidative phosphorylation, oxidative stress from disrupted antioxidant defenses, and eventual necrosis and apoptosis from severe energy depletion.

### Organ Specificity
The liver and kidneys are primarily affected because:
- **Liver hepatocytes**: extremely high metabolic rate and protein synthesis demands. Hepatocytes process absorbed toxins through the portal circulation, receiving the highest initial concentration of amatoxins from the gut.
- **Renal tubular epithelial cells**: high metabolic activity and involvement in toxin excretion concentrate amatoxins in the kidneys.

### Enterohepatic Circulation
A critical factor in amatoxin toxicity is enterohepatic circulation. After absorption from the gastrointestinal tract, amatoxins are transported to the liver via the portal vein. The liver processes and excretes them into bile, which is released into the duodenum. The amatoxins are then reabsorbed from the intestine, creating a recycling loop that repeatedly exposes the liver to toxin. This enterohepatic circulation prolongs the effective half-life of amatoxins and amplifies hepatic damage.

### Gastrointestinal Phase Mechanism
The severe gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) during the initial phase are primarily caused by phallotoxins and possibly by direct irritant effects of amatoxins on the intestinal mucosa. The phallotoxins act on intestinal smooth muscle and mucosal cells, though their oral bioavailability is low.

## Clinical Presentation

Amatoxin poisoning follows a characteristic four-phase clinical course:

### Phase 1: Gastrointestinal Phase (6–24 hours post-ingestion)
- Abrupt onset of severe nausea, persistent vomiting, profuse watery diarrhea, and abdominal cramping
- Significant fluid and electrolyte loss leading to dehydration, hypotension, and tachycardia
- Duration: 12–24 hours
- **Critical danger**: patients often improve after this phase and may seek or accept medical discharge, thinking they have recovered. This "false recovery" is a hallmark of amatoxin poisoning.

### Phase 2: Latent/Appearance of Recovery Phase (24–72 hours)
- Apparent clinical improvement with resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms
- The patient feels relatively well despite ongoing, silent hepatic and renal damage
- Laboratory values begin to show abnormalities: rising transaminases (AST, ALT), bilirubin, prothrombin time (PT/INR)
- Duration: 1–2 days
- This deceptive lull period contributes to mortality, as patients and sometimes clinicians underestimate the severity of poisoning.

### Phase 3: Hepatic/Renal Failure Phase (72–96 hours)
- Rapid onset of fulminant hepatic failure: AST and ALT rise to thousands of IU/L
- Jaundice develops as bilirubin rises
- Coagulopathy: prolonged PT/INR due to impaired hepatic synthesis of clotting factors
- Hepatic encephalopathy: confusion, asterixis, progressing to coma
- Acute kidney injury: rising creatinine, oliguria or anuria
- Hypoglycemia: impaired gluconeogenesis
- Lactic acidosis: from hepatic and tissue hypoperfusion
- This phase determines survival — patients who progress to multi-organ failure have very high mortality.

### Phase 4: Recovery or Death (5–16 days post-ingestion)
- **Recovery**: patients who survive the hepatic failure phase typically recover fully over weeks to months. Hepatocytes have remarkable regenerative capacity, and liver function usually returns to normal.
- **Death**: occurs in 10–30% of treated cases and up to 50–60% of untreated cases. Cause of death is typically multi-organ failure, cerebral edema, or catastrophic hemorrhage from coagulopathy.

## Treatment

### Early Interventions
- **Gastric decontamination**: activated charcoal (if within 1–2 hours of ingestion), gastric lavage
- **Aggressive fluid resuscitation**: critical during the gastrointestinal phase to correct dehydration and electrolyte imbalances
- **Multiple-dose activated charcoal (MDAC)**: may interrupt enterohepatic circulation by binding amatoxins in the gut lumen

### Specific Therapies
- **Silibinin (silybin)**: a water-soluble derivative of silymarin from milk thistle. The most evidence-supported specific therapy. Mechanisms include: competitive inhibition of amatoxin uptake into hepatocytes (blocking OATP1B1/B3 transporters), interrupting enterohepatic circulation, stimulating RNA polymerase I activity, and antioxidant effects. IV silibinin (Legalon SIL) is available in Europe.
- **N-acetylcysteine (NAC)**: provides antioxidant support and may replenish glutathione stores in the liver.
- **Penicillin G**: high-dose intravenous penicillin G has been used historically to competitively inhibit amatoxin uptake into hepatocytes, though evidence for its efficacy is weaker than for silibinin.
- **Cimetidine**: H2 blocker that may inhibit amatoxin metabolism by CYP450 enzymes.

### Supportive Care
Serial monitoring of liver function (transaminases, bilirubin, PT/INR, ammonia), renal function (creatinine, urine output), coagulopathy correction (fresh frozen plasma, vitamin K), hepatic encephalopathy management (lactulose, rifaximin), blood glucose correction, and liver transplantation evaluation for fulminant failure.

### Liver Transplantation
Orthotopic liver transplantation is the definitive treatment for patients with fulminant hepatic failure who do not respond to medical therapy. Indications include: progressive coagulopathy, worsening encephalopathy, rising bilirubin, and multi-organ failure despite maximal medical support.

## Epidemiology

*Amanita phalloides* accounts for the majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. The highest incidence occurs in Europe (particularly Eastern Europe, France, Italy, and Spain), where foraging is popular and *A. phalloides* grows abundantly. In the United States, the death cap was introduced with ornamental trees and is now established on both coasts. Cases occur annually, particularly among recent immigrants who confuse death caps with edible paddy straw mushrooms from their countries of origin.

Mortality has decreased significantly with modern treatment protocols (particularly the use of IV silibinin), from approximately 50–60% historically to 10–30% in contemporary series. However, delayed presentation — a consequence of the characteristic latency period — remains a major contributor to mortality.
