## Woronin Bodies: HEX-1 Protein and Septal Pore Sealing

## Overview

Woronin bodies are dense, membrane-bound organelles unique to
filamentous Ascomycete fungi (Euascomycetes) that function as
emergency plugs for septal pores. When a hyphal compartment is
damaged, Woronin bodies rapidly move to adjacent septal pores and
seal them, preventing catastrophic loss of cytoplasm. Gregory Jedd"s
chapter in *Fungi in the Environment* provides the definitive account
of Woronin body biology, from historical discovery through molecular
characterization of the HEX-1 protein and its crystal lattice
structure.

## Historical Discovery

Woronin bodies were first described in 1864, but their function
remained mysterious for nearly a century. A.H.R. Buller conducted
pioneering experiments in the 1930s, severing hyphae with a drawn
needle and observing that septal pores became plugged by a
"homogeneous, colourless, and highly refractive" material. Buller
incorrectly concluded the plug was a "coagulum of protoplasmic
origin" rather than recognizing it as the Woronin body. It took
roughly 50 more years before Collinge and colleagues established
the connection between Woronin bodies and septal-pore plugging.

## Structure and Composition

Woronin bodies are generally oval, centered on an electron-dense
proteinaceous matrix circumscribed by a single membrane. Diameter
varies between 100 nm and greater than 1 micrometer, generally
exceeding septal pore diameter. In most Euascomycetes the Woronin
