# Food Systems in [[appropriate-technology]] to create
abundant, resilient sources of balanced nutrition.

## The Untapped Potential of Urban Food Production

Permaculture teacher Andrew Millison once pointed out that if you
look around any city you will see ornamental landscaping and trees
for miles around, all consuming water, fertilizer, and labor, and
among all of that not a speck of food in sight. Imagine the food
abundance in your city, town, or neighborhood if all of the trees
and shrubs were food producers of some sort, and all of the lawn
space was used for gardens. Theoretically this could be accomplished
since we are already devoting the resources to keep those plants
alive. As you can see there is more than abundant space for food
security within every city, town, and neighborhood.

In America approximately ten thousand square miles of land are used for
agricultural production, while about forty thousand square miles are used
for lawns and ornamental landscaping. Ornamental landscaping and edible
food production can and should be one and the same. Municipal
landscaping could be filled with fruit trees, berry bushes, and edible
ground crops.

## Expanding the Edible Plant Palette

When most people think of food systems the first thing they think of
is gardens, and when choosing edible plants most people instinctively
jump to what might be called the salad plants: lettuce, tomatoes,
carrots, beans, potatoes, peppers, and spices. The typical salad
plants probably make up about one percent of the total palette of
edible plants. Most everyone knows what a dandelion is, yet very few
people know that they are edible and a good source of vitamin C. There
are edible flowers in great multitude. When most people think of
greens only a few varieties come to mind, but there are dozens upon
dozens of types of edible greens often thought of only as weeds.

You could never use a salad plant in your life and still fill a
design with dozens upon dozens of edible plants. When looking for
edible plants for a design remember to open your mind to what edible
plants really means. Gardens in the dirt should only be made with
local edible plants. Plants not native to your local area should be
grown in container gardens, architectural food systems such as
greenhouses, or appropriate food technology such as hydroponics.

## Vertical and Container Gardening

A good permaculture food system designer takes advantage of every
available opportunity to produce food. The vertical dimension is often
ignored and marginalized in most designer's thinking. Trellises allow
food vines to be grown on the sides of structures. Even a standard
chain link fence can be used as a trellis system for vertical
gardening. When you integrate the vertical dimension even a small
back yard can produce a high yield of food.

Food can also be grown in container gardens, which are simply any kind
of container with food growing in it, from buckets to barrels. One
favorite system is the kiddie pool garden, where cheap plastic wading
pools were converted into garden containers. Advanced techniques use
barrels where holes are poked in the side, enabling you to grow food
out the top and along the side simultaneously.

## Mechanical and Architectural Food Systems

Appropriate Technology can be used to create mechanical food systems
that have many advantages over gardens. These systems allow crops to
be better protected against critters and diseases, and you can
control the environment better. Some mechanical food systems can
themselves be [[aquaponics]].

Hydroponics suspend the roots of plants in water without soil.
Aeroponics systems use misters to spray atomized, nutrient-rich water
directly on root systems dangling in mid-air. While hydroponics save
on water compared to conventional gardening, aeroponics provide an
even greater reduction, though the spray misters can clog and are
harder to maintain. Aquaponics combines growing fish with a
hydroponics system, where fish water is siphoned off and used as the
nutrient-rich water for plant growth.

Architecture can also provide growing space for food systems. The
most common is the greenhouse. One of the simplest architectural
food systems is the hoop house, made from rebar or PVC pipe formed
into arches about eight feet tall with plastic stretched over it.
Hoop houses greatly extend the growing season and protect crops. Cold
frames are essentially very small greenhouses that go over individual
plants to protect them from freezing. Full bio-shelters and solar
greenhouses represent more advanced options.

## Integrating Animals into Food Systems

Animal systems play an important role in food production beyond
raising animals for slaughter. Chickens can provide eggs and
occasionally meat. Ponds can be used to grow fish. Bee hives can
provide honey. Goats can provide milk. These animal systems can be
integrated into the broader food production design to create
additional yields while the animals perform other functions such as
pest control, soil aeration, and nutrient cycling.

## Beyond the Home Garden

Food systems extend even beyond what you grow at home. They include
where you purchase food, such as CSA (Community Supported
Agriculture) programs and other local sources of food. Food
preservation can be an important part of your food system strategy
as well. These extended food systems require skill and some
infrastructure, so if the permaculture designer has a client with
these skills they should know how to design the appropriate
infrastructure systems that will be needed.

## Key Principles

- [[integrate-rather-than-segregate]]: food production should be woven
  throughout the entire design, from site edges to building interiors
- Use and value diversity: the edible plant palette extends far
  beyond common garden vegetables to include dozens of wild edibles,
  flowers, and lesser-known crops
- Stack functions vertically: trellises, fences, and walls provide
  additional growing surfaces beyond ground-level gardens
- Every element performs multiple functions: hoop houses extend
  seasons, aquaponics combines fish and plant production, container
  gardens use waste materials for food growing

## Applications

- Replacing ornamental municipal landscaping with fruit trees, berry
  bushes, and edible ground covers
- Building barrel systems with side plantings for maximum vertical
