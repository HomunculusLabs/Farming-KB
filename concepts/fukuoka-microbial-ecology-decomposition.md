# Microbial Ecology and Natural Decomposition

In Chapter 5 of [[fukuoka-natural-way-of-farming-theory-of-natural-farming]], Masanobu Fukuoka presents a detailed account of microbial ecology and the decomposition process, drawing on his wartime research at the Kochi Prefecture Agricultural Testing Station and decades of observation on his natural farm.

## Microbes as Scavengers

Fukuoka opens with a meditation on death and decomposition. While the farmer dreads being caught loafing, he notes that "it takes more than four people to dispose of a corpse. Countless microbes and small animals in the soil are engaged in what could almost be called an assembly-line operation consisting of the dismantling, decomposition, rotting, and fermentation of the body."

He traces the transformation: "After death, the corpse is decomposed as food by bacteria, so one's ultimate form may be that of a microbial cell. And since the last to offer up incense to the departed soul are probably lactic acid bacteria, a person vanishes as a sweet, acrid aroma through lactic fermentation."

This leads to a broader principle: "Thanks to the microbes that dispose of the remains of animals and plants, the earth's surface is always clean and beautiful. If animals died and the carcasses just remained there without decomposing, a couple of days is all it would take to make the world an intolerable place to be."

### Decomposition Pathways
The decomposition of organic matter proceeds through distinct biochemical pathways, each mediated by specific microbial functional groups:
- **Proteolysis**: Proteins are broken down by extracellular proteases (produced by Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and fungal species) into amino acids, then deaminated to release ammonia (NH3), which is either volatilized or converted to ammonium (NH4+) for plant uptake
- **Lipid hydrolysis**: Fats and oils are cleaved by lipases into glycerol and fatty acids, which are further broken down through beta-oxidation to yield acetyl-CoA entering the Krebs cycle
- **Cellulolysis**: Cellulose, the most abundant organic polymer on Earth, is depolymerized by cellulase enzyme complexes (endoglucanases, exoglucanases, and beta-glucosidases) produced by fungi (Trichoderma, Aspergillus) and bacteria (Cellulomonas, Clostridium)
- **Lignin degradation**: The most recalcipnt polymer is broken down primarily by white-rot fungi (Phanerochaete, Pleurotus) using lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase enzymes; this oxidative process cannot be performed by bacteria alone
- **Hemicellulose degradation**: Hemicellulose (xylan, mannan, arabinan) is hydrolyzed by xylanases and mannanases produced by diverse bacteria and fungi, yielding pentose and hexose sugars for fermentation
Each pathway operates simultaneously but at different rates, with sugars and proteins decomposing most rapidly (days to weeks) and lignin persisting for months to years. Fukuoka's observation of the straw decomposition cascade reflects this ordered progression: simple sugars are consumed first, followed by cellulose and hemicellulose, with lignin the final substrate to yield to microbial attack.

The speed of microbial reproduction is staggering -- a single bacterium such as Escherichia coli could leave a mass of progeny equivalent to all living organisms on Earth in two or three days if unchecked. This means that if the self-governing mechanisms by which nature regulates the reproductive power of one type of bacteria were to cease operating for just several days, the earth would become a morass of bacterial remains. The self-governing mechanisms by which nature regulates reproduction are "indeed a mystery."

## The Natural Balance

Fukuoka expands on the ecological balance that decomposition maintains: "No species of bird flying in the air should become extinct. The earthworms that burrow through the soil must not vanish. Nor should the mice and spiders proliferate too greatly. If one type of fungus thrives just a little too well, this throws everything out of balance. Tens of millions of species live on in perfect harmony without increase or decrease; they are born and they die unseen by man."

The balance between multiplication and destruction, the equilibrium between production and consumption, the fact that nature has seen to the growth and propagation of organisms and also to the treatment of their wastes and remains, carrying out both rapidly and harmoniously without the least hitch for untold thousands and millions of years -- "all this is of enormous consequence. It is here that one must turn for a just comparison of the powers of man with those of nature."

### Self-Regulation Mechanisms
Nature employs several elegant mechanisms to maintain population balance without external intervention:
- **Predator-prey dynamics**: Population cycles between predators and prey naturally prevent either from dominating. When decomposer bacteria multiply rapidly, their bacterial-feeding nematode predators multiply in response, creating a lagged oscillation that stabilizes populations
- **Resource limitation**: Microbial populations are naturally constrained by substrate availability; as straw is consumed, the carrying capacity for decomposers declines and populations self-limit
- **Competitive exclusion**: Multiple microbial species competing for the same resource niche prevent any single species from monopolizing decomposition, maintaining community diversity
- **Quorum sensing**: Many bacteria communicate through chemical signals (autoinducers) to coordinate group behaviors including population density regulation, biofilm formation, and enzyme production -- a natural form of microbial self-governance
- **Parasitism and cannibalism**: As Fukuoka observed, when populations become dense, organisms begin consuming each other, creating an intrinsic population check that requires no external predator

## Man as Heretic in the Natural Order

Fukuoka identifies man as "the sole heretic in the natural order." While all other organisms participate in natural cycles, man disrupts them: "Instead of burying his corpses in the earth, he douses them with heavy fuel oil and cremates them." The sulfurous gases from crematorium chimneys pollute smaller animals and plants more than humans.

People think of cremation as fast, convenient, and hygienic because a corpse can be entirely disposed of in just two hours. But what about the fuel oil that is mined, transported, and burned in the crematorium furnace? If this and antipollution treatment of the stack gases are taken into account, cremation is neither fast nor clean. "Perhaps simple burial or open burial in which the corpse is left exposed to the elements may seem primitive and inefficient to the short-sighted, but these are the most sensible and complete ways to dispose of a corpse."

"Designs for the most advanced refuse processing plants are child's play compared with the infinitely elaborate methods of treating garbage used by nature."

## The Straw Decomposition Cascade

Fukuoka provides a remarkable account of the natural decomposition of a single piece of rice straw scattered on the ground, which serves as his primary argument against composting:

1. The straw draws flies and other insects that lay eggs, producing maggots

2. Rice blast disease, leaf blotch, and rot-causing fungi spread rapidly over the straw

3. Spider mites crawl over this fungal growth

4. Different microbes proliferate: yeasts, blue mold, bread mold, and trichoderma fungi, which destroy the pathogens and begin decomposing the straw

5. Nematodes feed on the fungi, bacteria feed on nematodes, mites consume bacteria, predaceous mites feed on those mites, joined by spiders, ground beetles, earwigs, mole crickets, and slugs

6. The straw undergoes a succession of "tenants" as it gradually decomposes

7. Fibrin-decomposing fungi are supplanted by lipoid- and lignin-decomposing bacteria

8. Parasitism and cannibalism set in among aerobic bacteria, gradually replaced by anaerobic bacteria

9. Lactic acid bacteria complete the process with lactic acid fermentation, at which point all trace of the straw disappears

### Key Microbial Players in the Cascade
Several microbial groups play critical roles at different stages of the decomposition cascade:
- **Trichoderma species**: filamentous fungi that act as mycoparasites, actively attacking and consuming the pathogenic fungi (Pyricularia oryzae causing rice blast, Helminthosporium causing leaf blotch). Trichoderma produces chitinases and glucanases that degrade the cell walls of competing fungi
- **Bacillus subtilis**: a common soil bacterium that produces antibiotics (bacitracin, subtilin) suppressing competing microorganisms while contributing to straw decomposition through cellulase production
- **Saccharomyces and other yeasts**: early colonizers that ferment simple sugars released from the straw, creating an acidic microenvironment that inhibits many pathogens
- **Lactobacillus species**: facultative anaerobes that dominate the final fermentation stage, producing lactic acid as a metabolic byproduct. This lactic acid lowers the local pH to approximately 3.5-4.0, effectively sterilizing the remaining material
- **Cellulomonas and Cytophaga**: bacteria specialized in cellulose degradation, breaking down the structural polysaccharides of the straw cell walls
- **Streptomyces species**: actinomycete bacteria that decompose the more recalcitrant lignin and chitin components in the later stages of decomposition, producing the characteristic "earthy" smell of healthy soil (geosmin)

### The Soil Food Web Connection
The decomposition cascade Fukuoka describes exemplifies the soil food web in microcosm. Energy flows from the organic substrate (straw) through successive trophic levels: bacteria and fungi (primary decomposers) → nematodes and mites (bacterial/fungal feeders) → predatory mites and ground beetles (predators) → spiders (top predators). Each trophic transfer results in nutrient mineralization -- carbon is respired as CO2 while nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients are converted from organic to plant-available inorganic forms. Fukuoka's observation that this cascade proceeds "without the least hitch" underscores that the soil food web is a self-organizing system requiring no external management. The rapid succession of microbial communities on the straw mirrors ecological succession at the landscape scale, but compressed into days rather than decades.

This entire process takes place "over a period of several days" without any human intervention. Fukuoka emphasizes that a look at how nature disposes of the carcass of an animal will show a method that is perfect in every sense -- biologically, physically, and chemically. "If man were to try the same thing himself, his method would be plagued with problems and would invariably create pollution in some form."

## The Compost Experiment

At the Kochi testing center, Fukuoka once searched for a beneficial bacterium to prepare compost from straw and brush. He collected refuse from garbage sinkholes and various animal droppings -- cattle, hog, chicken, rabbit, and sheep. From these, he isolated and cultured microorganisms, obtaining samples of many different bacteria, fungi, slime molds, and yeast. He then inoculated these into straw in test tubes or within concrete enclosures and observed the rotting rates.

"Later, however, I realized that such an experiment was really quite worthless." Rather than isolating beneficial microbes, "all I had to do was scatter a handful of chicken droppings or clumps of soil over the straw. Not only was this the quickest way, it also gave the most completely rotted compost."

"There is no call for making a lot of fuss over 'microbial' and 'enzymatic' farming methods." The natural process is always superior. See [[fukuoka-compost-unnecessary-philosophy]] for the full critique.

### Why Inoculation Failed
Fukuoka's experience reflects a fundamental principle of microbial ecology: isolated microbial cultures perform poorly outside the complex community context of natural soil. When a single beneficial bacterium is introduced to sterile straw, it lacks the syntrophic partners, signaling molecules, and competitive community structure that enable efficient decomposition in nature. The handful of soil or chicken manure succeeded precisely because it introduced a complete microbial community -- bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and their associated enzymes -- in the natural proportions that had co-evolved to function together. Modern microbiology has confirmed this principle: defined microbial consortia outperform single-strain inoculants for decomposition tasks, and the most diverse inocula consistently produce the most complete and rapid substrate breakdown.

## Implications for [[fukuoka-compost-unnecessary-philosophy]]

Fukuoka extends this to his broader critique of composting: "Microbiologists are well aware of how rapidly and perfectly the processes of decomposition and rotting break down garbage in the natural world. Yet man, believing that he has to make intensive use of beneficial microbes to speed up putrefaction or that he must raise the temperature to promote bacterial growth, prepares compost."

The natural farmer simply returns straw and organic matter to the field surface and lets nature handle the rest. The following transformations take place on a rice straw casually dropped onto the earth: first, disease-causing fungi spread rapidly, but are soon consumed by beneficial trichoderma fungi. Then nematodes feed on the fungi, and bacteria feed on the nematodes. Predaceous mites consume the bacteria-eating mites. Before long, parasitism and cannibalism set in among the aerobic bacteria, and these are gradually replaced by anaerobic bacteria. Lactic acid bacteria round off the process, and all trace of the straw disappears.

### Natural vs. Managed Decomposition
The fundamental distinction Fukuoka draws is between decomposition as a self-organizing ecological process and decomposition as a managed industrial process. In natural decomposition, microbial communities self-assemble in the correct sequence, regulate their own populations, and complete the full mineralization cycle without intervention. In managed composting, human control over temperature, moisture, aeration, and inoculation inevitably disrupts some aspect of the natural succession, typically reducing overall microbial diversity and producing intermediate decomposition products (humic and fulvic acids) that differ from those generated by the unmanaged process. Fukuoka's position is not that compost is harmful, but that the effort of making it is unnecessary and the natural process -- scattering organic matter on the soil surface -- produces superior results with zero energy input.
