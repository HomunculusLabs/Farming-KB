# [[permaculture-design]], beekeeping integrates with the broader landscape through
strategic hive placement, forage planning, and coordination with other system
elements such as orchards, water features, and animal systems.

## Hive Placement

Choosing the right location for hives requires understanding the sector
analysis of your site. Bees prefer to fly at least 300 feet to their food
source and will not forage efficiently when facing a cold headwind. Using the
sector map, place hives away from prevailing winds and use hedges of herbs
to shelter them in the direction you want them to travel. Point the hive
entrance toward forage areas and away from houses, barns, and loud motors.

There are two types of forage that bees need: pollen and nectar. Pollen-
producing species should be planted within 100 feet of the hives, while
nectar species are planted at least 300 feet or more away. A minimum of 30
species foraging throughout the season provides insurance for hive survival.
Good forage plants include gooseberries, apples, [[fukuoka-white-clover-cover-crop-system]], blackberries,
citrus, buckwheat, mustard, willow, [[acacia]], pine, and grapes. Species should
be planned to flower in succession for a continuous food supply.

## Choosing Bee Breeds

Three main types of honeybees are available to backyard keepers. Italian bees
are the hardest workers but can be more defensive. Caucasian bees are the
least likely to sting. Carniolan bees are the gentlest overall. Honeybees
can only sting once because they die afterward, which makes them
considerably less aggressive than wasps. You can purchase bees from a
supplier or buy an established hive from a local beekeeper. The latter is
preferable because the colony will already be well established.

## Essential Equipment

A basic beekeeping setup requires: a hive with stand, bottom board, brood
chamber, and honey supers; a smoker for calming bees; a hive tool (a small
hooked lever for removing frames); protective bee clothing; a bee brush;
and a feeder. Shallow supers are preferred over deep ones because they are
easier to carry when full of honey. Each super holds 10 removable frames
with wax foundation imprinted with hexagonal cells as a building template.
In most regions, used bee equipment is illegal due to disease transmission
risk, so always check local regulations before purchasing.

## Handling Bees

To inspect the hive, start a fire in the smoker with crumbled paper and dry
tinder such as pine needles. Stand to one side of the entrance, blow smoke
into the door, wait a minute, then remove the cover and blow more smoke
into the top. Use more smoke whenever bees become agitated. Avoid crushing
bees, which releases a panic odor that triggers stinging. The hive can be
inspected when temperatures exceed 50 degrees Fahrenheit and weather
conditions are calm.

## Understanding the Hive

Knowing the different cells and bee types helps with [[bee-forage-hive-management-mollison]].
Brood cells have dark-colored caps containing developing bees. Queen cells
are one inch long and resemble peanut shells hanging from the comb. Drone
cells protrude slightly with bullet-shaped tops. Worker cells are the
smallest and sit level with the comb. The queen bee is about one inch long
with a tapered body, and other bees do not crowd around her. Drones are
fat with large eyes and no stingers. Worker bees are the smallest and
perform all hive maintenance, foraging, and defense tasks.

## Water and Climate Management

Bees always need fresh, clean water. Large ponds are unsuitable because bees
can drown or fall prey to dragonflies. For one or two hives, an outside
faucet dripping onto a slanted board works well. If bees cluster outside
the hive entrance in [[stamets-cultivator-agaricus-bitorquis-warm-weather-cultivation]], they may be overheating. Solutions
include moving the hive into shade, enlarging the entrance, and staggering
supers for ventilation. In winter, reduce the entrance size to conserve
heat and prevent mice from entering.

## Making New Hives

To create additional colonies, take four frames with brood cells from your
strongest hive in early May, along with some honey and bee bread. Brush
worker bees into the new hive and ensure at least one queen cell is present.
Stuff the entrance loosely with grass. It takes two weeks for the colony to
produce a queen, and another four weeks for her to mature and mate. No
honey should be harvested from either the source or new hive that year.
