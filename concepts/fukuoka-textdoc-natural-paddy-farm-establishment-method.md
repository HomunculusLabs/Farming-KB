# Fukuoka's Method of Natural Paddy and Farm Establishment Without Machinery

Masanobu Fukuoka's *The [[fukuoka-natural-way-of-farming-theory-of-natural-farming]]* provides detailed practical guidance for establishing farms, rice paddies, orchards, and gardens without the use of heavy machinery, chemical inputs, or conventional land-clearing methods. His approach preserves topsoil, builds long-term fertility, and creates productive agricultural landscapes that improve with time rather than degrading.

## Why Not Bulldozers

Fukuoka strongly opposes the use of bulldozers for land clearing. When land is cleared with a bulldozer:

1. Irregular surface features on a slope are flattened and smoothed, destroying natural contour variation.
2. The humus-rich topsoil built up over long periods is disturbed. Land developed with a bulldozer and left virtually bare for ten years is washed free of its topsoil, greatly shortening the economic life of the farm.
3. Heavy machinery causes excessive soil settling, inducing ground water stagnation, root rotting, and suppression of initial crop growth.
4. Concrete levees and walkways destroy the community of soil microbes, gradually turning soil into dead mineral matter.

Instead, vegetation on the hillside is cut in lateral strips, and large trunks, branches, and leaves of felled trees are arranged or buried in trenches running along hill contours, covered with earth, and allowed to decompose naturally. None of the vegetation cut down should be carried away.

### Growing a Wood Preserve

Hill and mountain tops, being infertile and dry, are highly susceptible to denudation. The sequence is:

1. Plant a vine such as kudzu to prevent soil washing away.
2. Sow seeds of a low conifer such as moss cypress to create a mountain cover of evergreens.
3. Initial vegetation of cogon, bracken, lespedeza, eurya, and moss cypress gradually gives way to urajiro fern, kudzu, and a mix of trees that further enrich the soil.
4. Plant evergreens such as Japanese cypress and camphor tree on hillsides, together with deciduous trees such as Chinese hackberry, zelkova, paulownia, cherry, maple, and eucalyptus.
5. Plant fertile land at the foot of hills and in valleys with oak and evergreens such as cryptomeria and live oak, interplanting with walnut and ginkgo.

Bamboo groves are especially valuable because bamboo shoots grow to full size in one year, providing abundant coarse organic material. Bamboo is hollow with a large void ratio, decomposes slowly, and helps retain air and water in the soil.

## Traditional Paddy Construction

Fukuoka contrasts modern rice paddy construction with traditional methods. Modern preparation uses large machinery to clear land, remove rocks, and level the surface, which has several drawbacks including uneven topsoil thickness and destruction of [[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]].

Traditional Japanese farmers chose mountain valleys rather than flat riverbanks, building small fields in valleys or terraced rice fields on hillsides. The work of digging channels, constructing rice fields, and building rock walls was not regarded as hardship. By spreading fields with cuttings from ridge grasses, border weeds, and young foliage from trees, rice could be grown each year without fertilizers. A tiny field of perhaps 100 square yards supplied one person's food needs indefinitely.

## Starting a Garden

Land for fruit trees intercropped with grains or vegetables is prepared by burying coarse organic material in the ground. Initial goals are prevention of weed emergence and maturation of the soil:

- First summer: grow buckwheat.
- First winter: sow rapeseed and Indian mustard.
- Second summer: plant adzuki bean and mung bean.
- Second winter: hairy vetch and other hardy leguminous plants.

Soil movement is the greatest concern. The garden should be built in terrace fashion with the surface level on each terrace. The first task is building lateral embankments or stone walls running across the slope. Good soil preparation and the ability to build earthen embankments that do not crumble or skillfully lay stones from the field are determining factors in success.

A good method for rapid soil improvement is piling soil up to create high ridges using soil brought up from digging contour trenches, piling it around coarse organic material. Better aeration allows piled soil to mature faster than soil in a trench.
