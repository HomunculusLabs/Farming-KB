# True Breeding Strain Development and Stabilization

Vic High defines the goal of creating a true breeding strain (IBL -- Inbred Line) not as eliminating all variation, but as achieving overall phenotypic uniformity across a population for traits the breeder considers important. This distinction is fundamental to understanding what stabilization actually means in practice.

## What True Breeding Means

A true breeding individual is homozygous for a particular trait -- its gene pair carries two identical alleles, so all its gametes carry the same genetic information. A true breeding strain (IBL) extends this concept to a population: all individuals in the population are homozygous for the target trait or set of traits.

Vic High uses the analogy of dog breeds to illustrate the concept. A German Shepherd is recognisable as a discrete breed, yet individual dogs vary in height, width, temperament, colour, and build. They fall within an acceptable range for each trait rather than being identical. Similarly, a true breeding cannabis strain does not need zero variation -- it needs acceptable uniformity across key phenotypic traits.

The breeder's task is to make all the genes in the population's gene pool code for the same trait. In a population of 50 plants with one gene pair each for flavour, there are 100 flavour genes. Making all 100 code for the same flavour is the goal. The closer the population gets, the more homozygous or true breeding it becomes.

## Gene Frequency as the Measure of Progress

Gene frequency is the central metric for tracking stabilization:

- Gene frequency = percentage of the population's gene pool carrying the desired allele.
- A **fixed trait** has a gene frequency of 100%.
- The higher the gene frequency, the more true breeding the population.

Vic High emphasises that ALL breeding -- whether creating IBLs, F1s, F2s, selecting for this or that -- is fundamentally about manipulating gene frequencies. A breeder who does not track gene frequencies is breeding without a compass.

## Phenotype, Genotype, and Environment

A critical insight from Vic High is that phenotype (what we observe) is half genotype and half environment. This has several important consequences:

- Even in a perfectly stable population, growing under new conditions can reveal previously unobserved heterozygous traits.
- Complete elimination of all variation is impractical because the genetic code is too complex.
- Stabilization must focus on the most important phenotypic traits rather than attempting total uniformity.

Every time a population is grown under new conditions, new heterozygous traits may be observed. This is why Vic High frames the goal as overall uniformity rather than perfect uniformity.

## Partial vs Complete True Breeding

Vic High distinguishes between two scenarios that differ in scope:

1. **True breeding strain (IBL)**: The population is uniform across multiple important phenotypic traits simultaneously. This is the full ideal goal of a breeding programme.

2. **True breeding for a specific trait**: The population is uniform for only one trait (e.g., outdoor harvest date or mite resistance) while remaining variable for all others. Such a population would not be considered a true breeding strain per se.

The distinction matters because breeding strategy differs. A single-trait programme can be completed more quickly but may leave the population genetically diverse in other respects. In genetics, wording plays a big part in meaning and understanding.

## Stabilization Through Generational Inbreeding

Generational inbreeding is Vic High's preferred strategy for achieving full stabilization. The process involves:

1. Create F1 offspring from the P1 parents.
2. Cross F1 individuals together to create F2.
3. Select F2 individuals matching the desired phenotype as parents for F3.
4. Continue for F4, F5, and subsequent generations.

### Results for Dominant Traits (Full-Sib Selection)

- F2: 75% express desired phenotype, gene frequency 50%.
- F5: 96% express desired phenotype, gene frequency 80%.

### Results for Recessive Traits (Full-Sib Selection)

- F2: 25% express desired phenotype, gene frequency 50%.
- F3: 100% true breeding (all pp), complete fixation achieved.

The recessive case is dramatically faster because any individual expressing the trait is guaranteed homozygous.

### Half-Sib Limitations

When males cannot be phenotyped for the target trait, only females are selected:

- F5 (dominant trait): 87% express phenotype, gene frequency 63.5% (vs 96% and 80% for full-sib).
- F5 (recessive trait): 87.5% express phenotype, gene frequency 93.75%.

The difference between full-sib and half-sib results illustrates the enormous impact of male selection.

## Stabilization Through Backcrossing (Cubing)

Backcrossing increases the proportion of the original parent's genes in the gene pool progressively:

- F1: 50% of P1 genes.
- First backcross (B1): 75%.
- Second backcross / Squaring (B2): 87.5%.
- Third backcross / Cubing (B3): 93.75%.

### Cubing Effectiveness by Trait Type

- Cubing a homozygous recessive source: 88% true breeding after 3 backcrosses.
- Cubing a heterozygous dominant source (no selection): only 22% true breeding.
- Cubing a heterozygous dominant source (with selection): 35% true breeding.

Critical limitation: cubing a heterozygous dominant source can never produce a true breeding strain regardless of how many backcrosses are performed.

## The Limits of Stabilization

Vic High identifies important theoretical and practical limits:

- Heterozygous dominant traits cap at 50% gene frequency under backcrossing.
- Polygenic trait probabilities multiply: two recessive genes yield 76.6%, three genes yield 55%.
- Half-sib breeding significantly reduces stabilization rate compared to full-sib.
- Gene linkage means selection for one trait inadvertently affects hundreds of others.

## Starting Points for Stabilization Projects

Vic High describes several possible starting scenarios:

1. **A single special individual (selfing route)**: The plant is self-pollinated to create seed.
2. **A single special individual (F1 route)**: Crossed with another plant, then either backcrossed or generationally inbred.
3. **Multiple similar individuals**: Several outstanding plants form the starting population for direct generational inbreeding.

Each starting point leads to different optimal strategies and timelines.

## Key Takeaways

- True breeding means population-level homozygosity for target traits, not zero variation.
- Gene frequency is the breeder's primary measure of stabilization progress.
- Phenotype reflects both genotype and environment, so some variation is inevitable under new conditions.
- Generational inbreeding can achieve complete fixation; backcrossing alone cannot when the source is heterozygous.
- Full-sib selection dramatically outperforms half-sib selection at every generation.
- Practical stabilization focuses on acceptable uniformity across key traits.
- Methodical, single-trait approaches yield better results than attempting to fix many traits at once.
- Recessive traits can be fixed in as few as 3 generations with full-sib selection.
