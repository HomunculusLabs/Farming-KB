## Dark-Septate Mycorrhizae and Primary Succession on Glacial Forefronts

## Overview

Glacial forefronts provide natural laboratories for studying primary succession—the colonization of newly exposed substrates by living organisms. As glaciers retreat, they expose barren mineral substrates devoid of organic matter and biological activity. The establishment of plant communities on these surfaces depends critically on microbial colonization, particularly by [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] that facilitate plant nutrient acquisition in nutrient-poor glacial soils. Dark-septate endophytes (DSE), especially *[[phialocephala-fortinii]]*, have emerged as key fungal players in early glacial succession, bridging the gap between initial bacterial-dominated communities and later ectomycorrhizal-dominated ecosystems.

## The Lyman Glacier System

### Physical Environment and Safe Sites

The forefront of Lyman Glacier in the North Cascade Mountains serves as a model system for studying primary succession. As the glacier retreats, it exposes glacial till composed of crushed rock particles with virtually no organic matter. Plant colonization of this harsh substrate requires:

- Seed arrival and trapping mechanisms
- Protection from desiccation
- Access to limiting nutrients (particularly nitrogen and phosphorus)
- Mycorrhizal associations for enhanced nutrient acquisition

Jumpponen et al. (1999) identified "safe sites" on the glacier outwash—concave surfaces of coarse rocky particles that are ideal for trapping tree seeds and protecting them from desiccation. These microtopographic features also serve as foraging foci for small mammals, creating synergistic conditions where seed deposition, mycorrhizal spore deposition, and moisture retention co-occur.

### Mammalian Inoculum Sources

A diverse assemblage of mammals contributes mycorrhizal spores to the glacial forefront:

- **Pika** (*Ochotona* spp.)
- **Voles** (*Microtus* spp.)
- **Chipmunks** (*Tamias* spp.)
- **Marmots** (*Marmota* spp.)
- **Mountain goats** (*Oreamnos americanus*)
- **Mule deer** (*Odocoileus hemionus*)

Their fecal deposits create patchy but critical sources of mycorrhizal inoculum, allowing colonization of newly developing soils by early successional and slow-growing tree species including subalpine fir (*[[abies-lasiocarpa]]*), subalpine larch (*Larix lyallii*), mountain hemlock (*Tsuga mertensiana*), and willows (*Salix* spp.).

## Dark-Septate Endophytes in Early Succession

### *Phialocephala fortinii* and Lodgepole Pine

Jumpponen et al. (1998) investigated the effects of the dark-septate mycorrhizal fungus *Phialocephala fortinii* on lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*) seedlings in glacial forefront soil. Their experimental design tested the effects of mycorrhizal colonization with and without added organic matter and nitrogen.

#### Key Findings Without Added Nitrogen:

| Treatment | Plant Dry Weight (mg) | Total N (% dry wt.) | Total P (% dry wt.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| No OM, No Myco | 52.9 | 0.69 | 0.074 |
| OM, No Myco | 40.3 | 0.63 | 0.076 |
| No OM, Plus Myco | 48.8 | 0.60 | 0.087 |
| OM, Plus Myco | 43.1 | 0.62 | 0.100 |

Without added nitrogen, organic matter had a negative effect on biomass (significant), while mycorrhizal colonization alone significantly enhanced phosphorus content but not plant biomass.

#### Key Findings With Added Nitrogen (100 kg N/ha):

| Treatment | Plant Dry Weight (mg) | Total N (% dry wt.) | Total P (% dry wt.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| No OM, No Myco | 81.7 | 1.41 | 0.072 |
| OM, No Myco | 104.1 | 1.78 | 0.066 |
| No OM, Plus Myco | 129.9 | 1.64 | 0.092 |
| OM, Plus Myco | 146.2 | 2.11 | 0.128 |

With added nitrogen, mycorrhizal colonization significantly enhanced both biomass and phosphorus content. The combination of mycorrhizae and organic matter produced the largest seedlings with the highest nutrient content.

### Ecological Interpretation

These results reveal several important principles:

1. **Nitrogen limitation**: In severely nitrogen-limited glacial soils, mycorrhizal enhancement of phosphorus uptake alone does not translate to growth benefits, as nitrogen remains the primary limiting factor
2. **Synergistic effects**: When nitrogen is available, mycorrhizal fungi dramatically improve both nutrient uptake and plant growth
3. **Organic matter interaction**: The interaction between mycorrhizae and organic matter is context-dependent, producing different outcomes under different nitrogen regimes
4. **Successional significance**: As nitrogen accumulates through biological fixation and atmospheric deposition during succession, the benefit of mycorrhizal association increases progressively

## Ectomycorrhizal Succession on Glacial Forefronts

### Species Diversity Patterns

Jumpponen et al. (1999; 2002) documented ectomycorrhizal species composition across different successional stages on the Lyman Glacier forefront. Key findings include:

- **68 ectomycorrhizal species** identified belonging to **25 genera**
- **No single species** occurred on all three successional sites
- Ectomycorrhizal diversity increased to maximum at **canopy closure**
- Species turnover was high between successional stages

### Drivers of Diversity Change

The increase in ectomycorrhizal diversity at canopy closure has been related to:

1. **Nutrient paucity**: Declining availability of phosphorus as biomass accumulates (Dighton and Harrison, 1990)
2. **Organic nutrient forms**: Increasing proportion of nutrients locked in organic compounds requiring specialized enzymatic capabilities
3. **Functional complementarity**: Greater diversity allows expression of diverse enzymatic functions (Dighton and Mason, 1985)

### Enzyme Specialization

Conn and Dighton (2000) demonstrated that ectomycorrhizal community composition reflects appropriate enzyme functions relative to resource availability. Where phosphorus is immobilized during early leaf litter decomposition, the ectomycorrhizal community shifts toward a greater proportion of acid phosphatase-producing types. This functional specialization supports the hypothesis that mycorrhizal diversity serves an adaptive purpose in accessing heterogeneous nutrient resources.

## Microbial Community Succession

### From Bacterial to Fungal Domination

During plant succession on glacial till, fundamental shifts occur in the soil microbial community (Ohtonen et al., 1999):

1. **Early stage**: Bacterial-dominated communities with high respiration rates and rapid carbon turnover
2. **Transitional stage**: Increasing fungal biomass and establishment of mycorrhizal networks
3. **Late stage**: Fungal-dominated communities with net carbon accumulation

This transition in carbon-use efficiency—from high respiration to carbon accumulation—indicates that fungi serve as **stabilizing forces** in developing ecosystems. By facilitating the shift from carbon loss to carbon storage, fungi promote the net primary productivity needed for ecosystem development and soil formation.

### Carbon Sequestration Implications

The role of fungi in carbon cycling during succession has implications for understanding carbon sequestration in developing soils:

- Fungal hyphae contribute to [[soil-aggregate-formation]], physically protecting organic carbon
- Mycorrhizal fungi allocate significant carbon to soil through hyphal turnover and exudation
- The shift to fungal-dominated decomposition pathways increases carbon-use efficiency
- Net carbon fixation into biomass accelerates as fungal communities mature

## Tree Species Colonization

The glacier forefront succession supports colonization by tree species with different mycorrhizal strategies:

- **Subalpine fir** (*Abies lasiocarpa*): Ectomycorrhizal
- **Subalpine larch** (*Larix lyallii*): Ectomycorrhizal
- **Mountain hemlock** (*Tsuga mertensiana*): Ectomycorrhizal
- **Willow** (*Salix reinii*): Heavily ectomycorrhizal across all sites
- **Lodgepole pine** (*Pinus contorta*): Associates with both ectomycorrhizae and dark-septate endophytes

The successional appearance of different mycorrhizal fungal communities creates a dynamic belowground landscape that influences aboveground plant community assembly through differential effects on host plant nutrition and fitness.

## Broader Implications

### Climate Change and Glacier Retreat

As climate change accelerates glacial retreat worldwide, understanding the fungal mechanisms facilitating primary succession on newly exposed substrates becomes increasingly important. The rate and trajectory of vegetation establishment on deglaciated terrain will depend on:

- Availability of mycorrhizal inoculum
- Presence of mammalian dispersal vectors
- Soil nitrogen accumulation rates
- Establishment of functional mycorrhizal networks

### Ecological Restoration on Barren Substrates

The glacial forefront model provides lessons for restoring vegetation on human-disturbed barren substrates such as mine tailings, volcanic deposits, and construction sites. Key principles include maintaining or introducing mycorrhizal inoculum, preserving habitat for dispersal vectors, and managing nutrient availability to match the successional stage of mycorrhizal fungal communities.
