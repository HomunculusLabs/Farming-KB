# Fungicolous Fungi Taxonomic Groups

Fungicolous fungi—species that consistently associate with other fungi, including lichenicolous fungi that grow on lichens—are distributed across nearly all major fungal lineages. This page details the taxonomic distribution of mycoparasitic and fungicolous species across the lower fungi (Oomycota through Zygomycota), based on Chapter 17 of the fungal biodiversity compendium by Gams, Diederich, and Põldmaa. An estimated 2,175+ species of fungicolous fungi have been described, distributed across five major ecological categories: sporocarp-inhabiting, lichenicolous, biotrophic plant parasite mycoparasites, hyphal parasites, and aquatic fungicolous species.

The fungicolous habit is widespread throughout the fungi but is particularly concentrated in certain groups, notably the Hypocreales among ascomycetes and Tremellales among basidiomycetes.

## Oomycota

Although Oomycota are fungus-like organisms (stramenopiles) rather than true fungi, several species function as mycoparasites of other Oomycota and of true fungi.

### One-celled Biotrophic Parasites

Members of the Olpidiopsidales (*Olpidiopsis*) and Rozellopsidales (*Rozellopsis*, *Dictyomorpha*) form biotrophic associations with a diversity of other Oomycota (particularly Saprolegniales and Pythiales), some Chytridiomycetes, and many algae. Sparrow (1960) and Karling (1981) collectively listed approximately 18–22 fungicolous species of *Olpidiopsis*, 4 of *Rozellopsis*, and several other genera.

Walled endoparasites typically form several zoosporangia within a single hypertrophied host compartment. Wall-less endoparasites (e.g., some *Rozellopsis* species) form a single thallus per host compartment, which is then delimited by a septum induced in the host. The wall-less *Rozellopsis* species fall into several groups analogous to the subgroups composing the genus *Rozella* (Held 1981).

### Pythium Mycoparasites

In soil, several specialized *Pythium* species parasitize congeneric plant pathogens as well as other fungi. The mycoparasitic species include four with spiny oogonia (*P. oligandrum*, *P. acanthicum*, *P. periplocum*, *P. acanthophoron*) and two with smooth oogonia (*P. nunn*, *P. mycoparasiticum*).

These species differ from plant-pathogenic *Pythium* species in several important respects: they require organic nitrogen and thiamine for growth, they are noncellulolytic, and they function as "secondary sugar fungi"—profiting from surplus reducing sugars liberated by cellulolytic fungi from cellulose (Foley and Deacon 1986; Jones and Deacon 1995). Zoospores of mycoparasitic *Pythium* species tend to encyst on chitinous substrata, whereas those of plant-pathogenic species preferentially encyst on cellulose; the form of cyst attachment is a particularly sensitive indicator of taxonomic affinity and determines host–parasite specificity (Deacon 1988a; Jeffries and Young 1994).

## Hyphochytridiomycota

Two genera of Hyphochytridiales include mycoparasitic species: *Hyphochytridium* (one species) and *Rhizidiomyces* (two species), both of which parasitize oospores of *Phytophthora* species. These represent a small but ecologically significant group of fungicolous organisms at the interface between true fungi and fungus-like protists.

## Plasmodiophoromycota

A few species of *Woronina*, *Octomyxa*, and *Sorodiscus* in the Plasmodiophorales parasitize species of the Oomycota, particularly genera of the Saprolegniales and *Pythium*. They form intracellular naked plasmodia that later transform into cystosori and sporangiosori (Batko 1975; Held 1981). These protist parasites add another trophic layer to the complex web of fungicolous interactions.

## Chytridiomycota

One-celled members of the Chytridiomycota engage in intracellular or epibiotic parasitic associations, mostly with algae, occasionally with other Chytridiomycota and Oomycota, and rarely with Zygomycota. They cause some host fungi to hypertrophy.

### Epibiotic Taxa

*Caulochytrium* and *Sparrowia* grow on a diversity of hosts (Karling 1977). These taxa remain external to the host cell wall, absorbing nutrients through specialized structures.

### Endobiotic Taxa

Endobiotic Chytridiomycota form an intracellular thallus that either:

- **Is surrounded by a wall** ensheathed in the endoplasmic reticulum of the host (e.g., *Catenaria allomycis* in *Allomyces*; Sykes and Porter 1980; Powell 1982).
- **Is naked**, surrounded only by the host plasmalemma (e.g., *Rozella allomycis* in *Allomyces*, Karling 1942; *Rozella polyphagi* in *Polyphagus euglenae*, Powell 1984).

Naked endobionts consume part of the host cytoplasm by phagocytosis. Their thallus is surrounded only by the host plasmalemma, which disintegrates when sporogenesis of the parasite begins, leaving the mature endoparasitic thallus in direct contact with the host cytoplasm. The developing thallus induces a septum to form in its vicinity so that each host compartment contains only one zoosporangium. The segmented host of *R. allomycis* then appears to have multiple infections (Held 1980).

### Host Defense Reactions

When *R. allomycis* first invades an *Allomyces* hypha, it elicits a host reaction at the penetration site. The host forms lomasomes to which wall material is apposed, giving rise to an internal papilla. The parasite enters through the center of the papilla with minimal cell disruption. This reaction does not occur in already-infected hyphae, preventing renewed penetration (Held 1972).

### Taxonomic Groups Within Chytridiomycota

Held (1981) assigned approximately 25 known mycoparasitic *Rozella* species to five groups based on morphology and host affinities: (1) monocentric Chytrids, (2) *Blastocladia* and Leptomitales, (3) *Monoblepharis* and Pythiaceae, (4) *Allomyces* and Saprolegniaceae, and (5) miscellaneous hosts. Sparrow (1960), Karling (1960, 1977), and Batko (1975) described and illustrated approximately 45 potentially mycoparasitic Chytridiomycota taxa.

## Zygomycota

### Zoopagales

Obligate, biotrophic, contact parasites are found in the orders Zoopagales and Dimargaritales. These haustorial mycoparasites grow on other members of the Mucorales or, exceptionally, on nonmucoralean fungi (Benjamin 1979). The biotrophic zygomycete associations have been reviewed by Jeffries (1985) and Jeffries and Young (1994).

On contact, the mycoparasite forms a haustorium that induces gall formation in the host. Burgeff (1924) noted that mating type factors often determine the compatibility of a host and its parasite, and also observed plasmatic continuity between sikyotic cells (contact cells) of the parasites *Parasitella parasitica* and *Chaetocladium brefeldianum* and their hosts.

#### Piptocephalidaceae

Members of Piptocephalidaceae are obligate haustorial mycoparasites:

- ***Piptocephalis* and *Kuzuhaea***: Purely biotrophic, forming small haustoria in the host cell. *P. xenophila* can grow on *Penicillium* and *Chaetomium* species (Dobbs and English 1954; Shigo 1960b), but all other species in the family grow only on Mucorales sensu lato. They develop best on media containing organic nitrogen sources and low sugar concentrations (Berry 1959). The haustoria are enucleate and delimited from host cytoplasm by a very thin electron-transparent wall layer (Jeffries and Young 1976).

Host morphology is hardly affected by *Piptocephalis* apart from increased branching and decreased marginal hyphal density (Evans and Cooke 1981), though overall host growth may be inhibited. Some parasitic species stimulate growth of certain host species (Curtis et al. 1978); such cases are assumed to be mutualistic. *P. virginiana* apparently can only parasitize fungi in which linolenic acid concentration exceeds a certain threshold, though other nutrients are also required (Manocha and Deven 1975). Trehalase activity in sporelings of the parasite increases during interaction, indicating exploitation of host trehalose reserves (Evans et al. 1981). Axenic growth of *P. virginiana* on malt extract-yeast extract medium was very limited and could not be maintained (Manocha and Deven 1975).

- ***Syncephalis***: More necrotrophic than *Piptocephalis* (Hunter et al. 1977). They form a highly branched haustorial system in host hyphae and impair host growth and sporulation. *S. californica* was successfully cultivated axenically after transfer from monoxenic culture onto liver medium (Emerson 1958; Ellis 1966).

### Dimargaritales

This order comprises mycoparasitic genera *Dimargaris*, *Dispira*, and *Tieghemiomyces*. *Dispira cornuta* (Ayers 1935; Brunk and Barnett 1966) is a biotrophic parasite that forms nucleate haustoria from gall-like swellings and is found on rat dung, growing on Mucor species and other Mucorales sensu lato.

## Gene Transfer and Evolutionary Implications

Horizontal gene transfer has been demonstrated experimentally in the *Absidia glauca*–*Parasitella parasitica* system (Kellner et al. 1993; Wöstemeyer et al. 1995). A plasmatic connection is established between the sikyotic ("sucker" or "anchor") cell of the parasite and the host (Burgeff 1924). The prototrophic parasite compensates for artificially induced deficiencies in the host, with compensation established in 0.4% of uninucleate sporangiospores of the offspring. Transfer of a neomycin-resistance-conferring plasmid was also observed.

Although the "pararecombinants" were unstable, such a natural gene transfer mechanism could have important evolutionary implications (Jeffries 1995). Setliff (1984) speculated that the unusual morphology of *Aporpium caryae* (features of both Tremellales and polypores) may have resulted from horizontal transfer of genetic material from host fungus to mycoparasite.

## Host Specificity Considerations

Most mycoparasites are unspecialized and can infect a wide range of host fungi across diverse systematic groups. Not only can certain fungi parasitize other fungi belonging to diverse orders, but some are also entomogenous or nematophagous. More specific associations also exist. Lichenicolous fungi, for example, generally are specialized and rarely observed on nonlichenized host fungi. Several fungicolous fungi are hyperparasites of other fungicolous species.

The production of inhibitory metabolites and wall-degrading hydrolytic enzymes is probably less important for fungi that can make physical contact with a host than for fungi whose hyphae do not directly infect. Production of inhibitors may be positively correlated with pigmentation intensity in Hypomyces species: strains with only weak pigmentation or none had no inhibitory effect, whereas typically pigmented strains of *H. odoratus* lost inhibitory capacity when transferred to a medium causing pigment loss (Lerner and Sidorova 1978).
