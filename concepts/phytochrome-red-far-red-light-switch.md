# Phytochrome: The Red/Far-Red Light Switch

## Overview

[[phytochrome]] is a photoreceptor protein found in plants that functions as a molecular
light-activated switch, toggling between two forms in response to red and far-red light.
Discovered by Warren L. Butler and his colleagues in the early 1960s, phytochrome
explains how plants can "remember" the last colour of light they saw and use that
information to regulate critical processes including flowering, germination, and shade
avoidance. The name — from the Greek *phyto* (plant) and *chrome* (colour) — captures
its essence: a protein enabling plants to perceive and respond to specific colours of
light with remarkable precision.

## The Discovery of Red and Far-Red Effects

The story of phytochrome begins with experiments on [[photoperiodism]] — the phenomenon
where plants use [[coleman-persephone-months-winter-day-length]] to control flowering. Around World War II, scientists
discovered they could manipulate flowering by briefly turning lights on and off in the
middle of the night. Only red light flashes could disrupt or trigger flowering; blue or
green flashes had no effect. This was surprising because [[blue-light-phototropism-red-light-photoperiodism-dual-plant-vision-system]] was already known
to drive [[phototropism]]. Plants, it seemed, were using different colours for different
purposes.

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and his colleagues at the US Department of
Agriculture made an extraordinary discovery: far-red light — wavelengths slightly
longer than bright red, visible just barely at dusk — could cancel the effect of red
light on plants. If you gave irises (a long-day plant) a pulse of red light in the
middle of the night, they would flower. But if you followed that red pulse with a
flash of far-red, it was as if the red pulse had never happened. The irises would not
flower. If you then shone red light again after the far-red, they would flower. Hit
them with far-red again, and they would not. This back-and-forth could be repeated
indefinitely with just a few seconds of either colour. The plant was acting as if it
contained a reversible light-activated switch.

## The Phytochrome Switch

By the early 1960s, Warren L. Butler and his colleagues had demonstrated that a single
photoreceptor protein was responsible for both the red and far-red effects. They named
it phytochrome. In its simplest model, phytochrome exists in two interconvertible
forms. Red light converts phytochrome from an inactive form (Pr) to an active form
(Pfr). Far-red light converts the active Pfr back to inactive Pr. The system is fully
reversible: red activates, far-red deactivates, and only a few seconds of illumination
are needed to flip the switch.

On a philosophical level, this reversibility means the plant "remembers" the last
colour it saw. If the last light received was red, phytochrome remains in its active
Pfr form and the plant behaves as if it has seen daylight. If the last light was
far-red, phytochrome reverts to inactive Pr and the plant behaves as if in darkness.
This molecular memory is the basis for some of the most important timing mechanisms in
plant biology.

## Ecological Significance: Dawn and Dusk

The red/far-red switch has deep ecological meaning. In nature, the last light a plant
sees at the end of the day is far-red light. As the sun descends, its light passes
through more atmosphere, scattering shorter blue wavelengths and allowing longer red
and far-red wavelengths to dominate. The far-red light at dusk converts phytochrome to
its inactive Pr form, signalling to the plant that night is falling and certain daytime
processes should cease.

In the morning, sunlight is rich in red wavelengths. This red light activates
phytochrome, converting it to Pfr and signalling that daytime processes should begin.
Phytochrome thus acts as a daily clock, allowing the plant to measure how long ago it
last saw red light and adjust its growth accordingly. The plant does not merely respond
to light — it tracks the quality of light over time.
