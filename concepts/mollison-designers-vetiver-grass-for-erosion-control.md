# Vetiver Grass for Erosion Control and Mulch

Vetiver grass (Vetiveria zizanoides) is one of the most frequently cited
species in the Permaculture [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-animal-systems-in-permaculture]] for erosion control, slope
stabilisation, and mulch production. Mollison recommends it across
tropical, subtropical, and dryland contexts as a primary tool for
replacing engineered contour banks with living biological structures.

## Erosion Control on Contour

Contour strips of vetiver grass, lemongrass, and banna grass with tree
legumes not only replace contour ridges but trap soil particles, serving
as a cheap way to "terrace" even on steep slopes. These strips provide
mulch for trees and intercrop (Figure 10.13).

Mollison emphasises that vetiver strips are cheaper to establish than
earth contour banks, more permanent, and produce a continuous yield of
mulch material. The dense clumping root system of vetiver penetrates deep
into the soil profile, binding earth on slopes that would otherwise wash
away during heavy rains.

## Slope Stability

In the context of [[mollison-wet-dry-tropical-design-strategies]], Mollison describes
the use of vetiver grass for stabilising gullies and check dams. All weirs
need vetiver grass or some such tough plant barrier to hold erosion on the
gully sides. At the Assomada project in Cape Verde (Sahel), terrace
building and check dams built of stone-filled wire mesh gabions in stream
beds effectively checked and stabilised soil erosion.

Cross-slope vetiver grass lines on steeper ground help to slow and spread
surface water flows that would otherwise concentrate and deepen erosion
channels. The grass also traps silt, gradually building terrace-like
benches behind the grass lines.

## Dryland Applications

In desert and dryland systems, vetiver grass appears in recommended
plantings for windbreak edges, mulch production, and weed barrier
functions. Mollison lists it alongside comfrey, lemongrass, Echium
fastuosum, Acacia, and Pennisetum as species used for shelter, edge, weed
barrier, and hedgerow purposes.

The deep root system of vetiver makes it particularly valuable in drylands
where shallow-rooted grasses fail to persist through dry seasons. The
roots can penetrate several metres into the soil, accessing water and
nutrients unavailable to other [[permaculture-plants-food-forest-ground-covers-bamboos]].

## Integration with Earthworks

After earthworks operations, Mollison advises planting vetiver grass as
part of the stabilisation mix. Cross-swales of lemongrass or vetiver grass
trap silt and spread water across disturbed slopes. Small wire netting
fences with stone-weighted hay uphill can also trap silt, supplemented by
living vetiver barriers.

For dam spillways and watercourse edges, vetiver grass prevents scour
erosion that could undermine earthworks structures. Its toughness and
ability to withstand both flooding and drought make it one of the most
versatile stabilisation species.

## Establishment

Vetiver grass is propagated by division, not by seed. Clumps are split and
the tillers planted on contour at close spacing (10-15 cm apart in the
row). Initial mulching, manuring, and watering help establishment. Once
established, the clumps persist for decades and spread slowly outward,
forming a dense continuous hedge.
