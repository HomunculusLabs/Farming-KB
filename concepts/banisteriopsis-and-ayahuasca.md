# Banisteriopsis and Ayahuasca

Banisteriopsis is a genus of woody vines belonging to the Malpighiaceae family that serves as the primary ingredient in one of the most widely used hallucinogenic brews in the indigenous Americas. The drink, known by many regional names, forms the centerpiece of shamanistic practice across much of the Upper Amazon basin.

## Regional Names and Distribution

The Banisteriopsis brew is known by numerous names across its vast range:

- **Yagé** or **Yajé** in Colombia
- **Ayahuasca** (Quechua: "vine of the dead" or "vine of souls") in Ecuador and Peru
- **Caapi** in Brazil
- **Natem** or **Natemd** among the Jivaro of eastern Ecuador
- **[[nixi-pae]]** ("the vine drunkenness") among the Cashinahua of eastern Peru

The native use of Banisteriopsis extends from northwestern Colombia in the north to lowland Bolivia in the south, occurring both east and west of the Andes, and reaching eastward into the upper Orinoco area. The plant has also been reported from British Guiana and as far east as Pará, Brazil, at the mouth of the Amazon, though indigenous use in those eastern areas is less well documented.

Banisteriopsis species exist throughout Central America and Mexico, including the Yucatan Peninsula, and at least one species is reported from the southeastern United States. However, hallucinogenic use has apparently not been recorded in those areas.

## Botanical Identification and Preparation

All members of the genus grow wild as tree-climbing forest vines, but some tribes also cultivate the plants in their gardens. The original botanical identification was made by Spruce (1908), who reported that the vine was used among the Indians of the Rio Negro region of Brazil.

Preparation typically involves cutting the vine into segments, pounding them lightly, and boiling them in water for approximately one hour. The resulting brew is then ladled off to cool before consumption. While Banisteriopsis may be taken alone, it is commonly prepared with admixture plants that are believed to provide "added strength."

## Admixture Plants

A variety of plants may be added to the Banisteriopsis brew depending on the tribe and region. The second plant is typically considered essential to the success of the drink:

- **Prestonia** (formerly Haemadictyon) — reported among the Rio Negro Indians of Brazil and in the Rio Napo region of eastern Peru
- A **second species of Banisteriopsis** — used by the Cofan, Inga, and Sione Indians of the Rio Putumayo area of eastern Colombia
- **Psychotria viridis** — containing the powerful hallucinogen N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), used regularly by the Cofan Indians of eastern Ecuador and by the Cashinahua of eastern Peru (who call it *nai kawa*)
- **Altenanthera** and other unidentified plants — reported by Garcia-Barriga in the Rio Putumayo region
- **Cawa** leaves — used by the Shipibo-Conibo of the Rio Ucayali in eastern Peru, presumably the same Psychotria used by the linguistically related Cashinahua

Among the Amahuaca, additional plants include *chuchupawa*, *cha'i* (sometimes drunk before ayahuasca to increase hallucinations), and *chuchupano*.

## Biochemistry

The biochemistry of Banisteriopsis drinks is complex due to the variety of admixtures involved. The following alkaloids have been identified:

- **Harmine** — known for some time as a constituent of Banisteriopsis caapi
- **Harmaline** — more recently identified in B. caapi by Hochstein and Paradies
- **d-Tetrahydroharmine** — also found in B. caapi
- **N,N-Dimethyltryptamine (DMT)** — found in significant amounts in B. rusbyana and in the Psychotria viridis admixture

All four alkaloids are structurally related indole derivatives, related to other well-known hallucinogens including mescaline (found in peyote), psilocybin (found in Mexican psychotropic mushrooms), and LSD. There exists little information on the alkaloid constituents of complete brews embodying two or more plants.

## Cultural Context of Use

Banisteriopsis is typically taken by South American Indian shamans of the tropical forest to perceive the supernatural world and to contact and affect the behavior of particular supernatural entities—for example, locating and withdrawing a supernatural object from a bewitched patient. In many tribes, non-shamans (most commonly males) also take ayahuasca to obtain information from the spirit world, to receive visions, to achieve supernatural power, or to accompany a shaman in a curing or other ritual.

Different tribes exhibit distinct patterns of use:

- The **Jivaro** prefer to take the drug individually, using it to cope supernaturally with enemies in their individualistic and feuding culture
- The **Cashinahua** and **Sharanahua** tend to participate together in group sessions, sharing revelatory content of portent for the common good
- The **Campa** join together for Banisteriopsis sessions with the shaman playing a directorial role as leader of the ceremonies

The brew is almost always consumed after dark, typically beginning in the evening and lasting until the early morning hours. It is generally considered an unpleasant and fearsome experience, undertaken not for recreation but for serious supernatural purposes.

## Significance

The common denominator across all the varied preparations is Banisteriopsis. The plant's role in indigenous Amazonian cultures illustrates how hallucinogenic experiences may be deeply integrated into the supernatural life and total culture of a people. The widespread use of Banisteriopsis brews in the Amazon makes the Upper Amazon one of the most important regions in the world for the study of the relationship between hallucinogens and shamanism.
