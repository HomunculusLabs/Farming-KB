# Co-Action Analysis and Species Interaction in [[guild-design-methodology]]

In *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]] develops a
rigorous analytical framework for understanding how species interact
within guilds. Drawing on the work of Haskell (1970), Mollison
presents a co-action matrix system that classifies all possible
interactions between species pairs, providing designers with a
systematic method for building productive plant and animal guilds.

## The Interaction Matrix of Two Species

Mollison identifies three fundamental possibilities when two species
interact:

1. **One acts on the other** (co-action or unilateral action)
2. **Both act on each other** (interaction or mutual action)
3. **Neither acts** (inaction or neutrality)

These possibilities are organised into a matrix of outcomes using a
simple notation system where each position can be positive (+),
negative (-), or neutral (0).

## The Nine Interaction States

Mollison provides detailed [[aact-definitions-and-types-ingham]] examples for each state:

### Symbiosis (++)

Both species benefit. This is the ideal "win-win" situation best
suited to guild development. The classic example is the mycorrhizal
associates of higher plants, where mutualism or fair trade occurs
between a plant and its root associate. Both organisms gain from the
relationship.

### Synnecrosis (--)

Both species are harmed. Coined by Haskell, this "lose-lose"
situation is uncommon in nature but includes chemical warfare between
plants competing for light, nutrients, and space. In design, this is
the outcome to avoid.

### Amensalism (-o)

The actor is harmed, not the other. A butterfly attacking a
rhinoceros, or a wasp parasite glued to a tree it attacks, as [[allegro-death-and-resurrection-in-the-mushroom-cult]]
case of some pine [[trees-and-the-water-cycle]] Sirex wasps. The actor wastes energy while
the other is unaffected.

### Allolimy (o-)

The actor is unaffected but hurts the other. The critical example for
guild designers is a walnut tree beside an [[apple-tree-care]]: the walnut
yields well, but juglones secreted by its roots kill or weaken the
apple. Similarly, grasses act to weaken most deciduous fruit trees.

### Commensalism (+o)

The actor benefits, the other remains unaffected. An epiphyte attached
to a sturdy tree, such as vanilla on a coconut trunk, takes nothing
from the host while gaining structural support.

### Allotrophy (0+)

The actor is unaffected, the other benefits. This includes
teacher-student relationships and charity. In ecological terms, a tall
tree providing shade to an understory plant without being affected
fits this category.

### Parasitism (+-)

The actor benefits, the other loses. All pathogens and parasites tend
to weaken or take from the host. In design, parasitic plants or
over-shading vines may need to be managed.

### Self-Sacrifice (-+)

The actor loses to benefit others. The reverse of parasitism, this
self-deprivation to help others is common in nature, mostly as
individuals helping members [[allegro-star-of-the-morning-venus-and-the-sacred-mushroom]] same family or species. Nitrogen-
fixing trees that allocate resources to root nodules at their own
expense exemplify this in the plant kingdom.

### Neutrality (00)

Neither species affects the other. Many plant species appear to be
basically neutral to each other. Critical areas in nature such as
[[water-management]] holes, salt licks, and grooming stations may function as
neutrality zones where antagonistic species coexist.

## Building Guilds from Co-Action Analysis

Mollison demonstrates the guild-building process with a detailed
example using apple trees. The key insight is that designers can use
two-species results to achieve better outcomes with three or more
species, going beyond accepting primary conflicts.

### The Apple-Walnut Problem

Apple next to walnut produces (-0): the apple sickens or dies due to
juglone toxicity. This is not desirable and must be resolved.

### The Intervention Strategy

- Apple next to mulberry produces (+0): a good result.
- Mulberry next to walnut produces (00): mutual inaction.

Thus, apple-mulberry-walnut gives (+00). By adding a mulberry as an
intervenor or critical species, the primary conflict between apple and
walnut is cancelled out.

### Expanding the Guild

Mollison extends this further:
- [[acacia]] next to walnut gives (0+): the walnut benefits.
- Acacia next to mulberry gives (0+): the mulberry benefits.

Now, apple-mulberry-Acacia-walnut gives (++0+), which is much better
again. This process of isolating and arranging guilds to maximise
benefits and eliminate conflicts is the core skill of planning strip
or zone placements of mixed species.

## Field Methods for Co-Action Observation

Mollison describes a practical method for gathering co-action data:

1. **Score each species** for health, growth, and yield
2. **Walk from yard to yard** observing which species grow well
  together and which do not
3. **Draw up a co-action matrix** on paper with the target species
  (e.g., apple) at the top and associated species down the left
