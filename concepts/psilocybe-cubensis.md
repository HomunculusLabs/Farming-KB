# Psilocybe cubensis

Psilocybe cubensis (also known as Stropharia cubensis) is the most commonly cultivated and commercially sold hallucinogenic mushroom species in the world. It belongs to the Strophariaceae family within the [[Psilocybe]] genus. Unlike several other psilocybin-containing species that grow wild throughout Europe, P. cubensis is a tropical and subtropical species that does not grow naturally in Europe and must be cultivated under controlled conditions for commercial sale.

## Taxonomy and Classification

P. cubensis is classified within the following mycological hierarchy:

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Family**: Strophariaceae
- **Genus**: Psilocybe
- **Species**: P. cubensis

The genus Psilocybe is predominant in terms of recreational use, followed by genus Panaeolus (from the Bolbitiaceae family). Other genera containing psilocybin mushrooms include Copelandia and Panaeolus (Coprinaceae family) and Inocybe (Cortinariaceae family). Within the Psilocybe genus, P. cubensis is the most frequently marketed species, particularly through smartshops and online retailers.

There are more than 100 known hallucinogenic mushroom species worldwide, and the complexity of their mycological classification, together with their different chemical makeup and varying effects, may lead to inconsistencies in their description. The list of species and their geographical distribution is constantly revised by mycologists.

## Description and Identification

Psilocybe cubensis is a small to medium-sized brown or tan mushroom. Like nearly all psilocybin-containing mushrooms, it can be mistaken for numerous non-psychoactive, inedible, or poisonous mushrooms in the wild. The primary distinguishable feature of most psilocybin-containing mushrooms is that they bruise blue when handled, caused by the oxidation of psilocin.

This blue-bruising reaction is not unique to P. cubensis but is a useful initial field identification marker. However, it should not be relied upon as the sole criterion, as some toxic mushrooms can also exhibit bluing, and some psilocybin-containing mushrooms may show minimal bluing. Proper identification requires careful examination of multiple features including cap shape, gill structure, spore print colour, and habitat.

## Natural Habitat and Distribution

P. cubensis is native to tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. It grows naturally on dung in grassy fields, particularly in regions of Central and South America, Southeast Asia, and Australia. It does not grow wild in Europe, which distinguishes it from species like [[Psilocybe semilanceata]] (liberty caps) and [[Psilocybe cyanescens]] (wavy caps), both of which are found throughout central and northern Europe.

A variety of psilocybin-containing mushroom species are found naturally in Europe, particularly throughout central and northern Europe. Their habitats include wet grassy fields and uncultivated pastures. The most common wild European species is Psilocybe semilanceata, which can be found in the UK, Norway, and Germany. Other wild European species include Psilocybe cyanescens, Psilocybe bohemica, and Psilocybe moravica, particularly reported in the Czech Republic.

## Commercial Varieties

Numerous cultivated varieties of P. cubensis are marketed under geographic or cultural names, reflecting their purported origin or the strain line selected by cultivators. Common commercially available varieties include:

- Mexican
- Thai
- Colombian
- Amazonian
- Ecuadorian
- Hawaiian

These varieties differ primarily in growth characteristics, appearance, and potency rather than representing distinct species. The "Mexican" variety was reported as the most commonly used in a 2004 UK clubbing magazine survey. Most of these cubensis varieties do not grow wild in Europe and are cultivated specifically, mostly in the Netherlands, in order to produce a marketable product.

## Cultivation

P. cubensis is the preferred species for commercial cultivation due to several advantageous characteristics:

- **Ease of growth**: It grows readily in terrariums and indoor cultivation setups.
- **Low water content**: Fresh mushrooms stay viable for 7 to 10 days, facilitating retail distribution.
- **Predictable yields**: Reliable fruiting under controlled conditions makes it commercially viable.
- **Established cultivation knowledge**: Extensive information on growing techniques is available online.

### Cultivation Methods

Home cultivators and commercial growers use several approaches:

- **Spore prints**: Created by pressing the gills of a mature mushroom onto paper, or spore syringes where spores are transferred into a syringe under sterile conditions. Spores are used to inoculate a substrate of nutrients free of bacteria and molds, similar to how a common house plant is grown in a pot of soil. Spore prints are available for many magic mushroom species at prices ranging from 9 to 28 EUR.
- **Spawn bags**: Contain sterile hardwood chips inoculated with mushroom mycelium. Available for species like Psilocybe azurescens at approximately 24 to 25 EUR each.
- **Growkits**: Complete cultivation packages typically including a box with colonised substrate, a bag with an air filter, and paperclips. Available for various species and ranging from 25 to 100 EUR depending on species and complexity.
- **Free Spore Ring Europe**: A non-profit organisation distributing various strains (including P. cubensis and Panaeolus cyanescens) for approximately 1 EUR per print.

Instructions on how to grow hallucinogenic mushrooms using a spore print and a substrate can be easily found on the internet, making home cultivation accessible to a wide audience.

## Potency and Active Compounds

P. cubensis typically contains [[psilocybin pharmacology|psilocybin]] and psilocin at concentrations up to 0.6% combined. While this is lower than the potency of some other species (such as [[Psilocybe semilanceata]], which can contain up to 1% psilocybin, and higher potency reported in specific species like Psilocybe azurescens and Psilocybe bohemica), P. cubensis is sufficiently potent for recreational use at standard doses of 1 to 5 grams of dried material.

The mushroom may also contain baeocystin and norbaeocystin, which are less active tryptamine alkaloids thought to contribute to the overall psychoactive effect.

## Market and Commercial Significance

P. cubensis dominates the hallucinogenic mushroom retail market. In the Netherlands, it was estimated that magic mushrooms constituted approximately 50% of smartshop turnover, with P. cubensis varieties being the primary products sold. In the UK, approximately 300 shops and market stalls sold hallucinogenic mushrooms until prohibition in July 2005.

### Pricing

As of January 2006, the retail price for fresh P. cubensis (Mexican variety) ranged from 8.50 to 45.28 EUR per 30 grams. This wide price range reflects differences between retailers, product quality, and market positioning.

## Sclerotia-Forming Relatives

Some Psilocybe species related to P. cubensis form sclerotia, which are hardened masses of mycelium that serve as a defence mechanism against adverse environmental conditions such as dryness, cold, heat, or excessive moisture. Notable sclerotia-forming species include:

- Psilocybe mexicana
- Psilocybe tampanensis

The sclerotia of these species are sold commercially under names such as "truffles" or "philosopher's stone" at approximately 10 to 17.50 EUR per 15 grams.

## Wild Harvesting and Foraging

While P. cubensis is primarily a cultivated species, it is worth noting the broader context of wild hallucinogenic mushroom harvesting in Europe. Evidence on the extent of wild collection (known as "magic mushroom hunting" among users) is sparse but exists across several EU countries.

Websites provide users with information on identifying naturally growing mushrooms as well as sharing known locations. Qualitative reports from Poland, Ireland, and the Czech Republic confirm that users hunt for wild psilocybin-containing mushrooms, primarily [[Psilocybe semilanceata]]. In Poland, young persons aged 15 to 19 were observed seeking magic mushrooms in the south of the country. In Ireland, magic mushrooms were reported as "popular when in season" in the Kilkenny region.

The health risks associated with picking mushrooms in the wild are significant. Hallucinogenic mushrooms are easily mistakable for any number of non-psychoactive, inedible, or poisonous mushrooms, which may limit the number of people willing to risk consuming wild-harvested specimens.

## Prevalence and Patterns of Use

Surveys in 12 EU member states indicate that among young people aged 15 to 24 years, lifetime prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use ranges from less than 1% to 8%. The highest rates are found in the Czech Republic, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands, and the UK. Prevalence is generally much lower than for cannabis but comparable to ecstasy in some countries.

Among school students aged 15 to 16, lifetime prevalence ranges from 0% to 8% across 27 European countries surveyed. The Czech Republic, Netherlands, France, and Belgium show the highest estimates. Notably, lifetime use equals or exceeds ecstasy use in nine countries.

Clubbing surveys reveal substantially higher prevalence: estimates range from 9% in Budapest to 55% in France among club and dance music attendees. Within club populations, polydrug use tends to cluster, and hallucinogenic mushroom use is strongly associated with prior use of LSD, ecstasy, amphetamines, and cocaine.

## Legal Status

The legal status of P. cubensis varies by country. It is explicitly controlled in many EU member states following legislative changes between 2001 and 2006. See [[hallucinogenic mushroom legislation in Europe]] for detailed country-specific information. In countries where fresh mushrooms were not controlled (such as the Netherlands prior to further legislation), P. cubensis was sold openly through retail channels including smartshops and online shops.
