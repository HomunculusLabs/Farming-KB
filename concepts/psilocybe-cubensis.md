# Psilocybe cubensis

Psilocybe cubensis (also known as Stropharia cubensis) is the most widely cultivated and
commercially marketed species of hallucinogenic mushroom in the world. It belongs to the
Strophariaceae family and the [[Psilocybe]] genus, and is the predominant species in terms
of recreational use. Unlike many other psilocybin-containing species that are primarily
foraged from the wild, P. cubensis is overwhelmingly cultivated for the commercial market,
particularly in the Netherlands.

## Description and Identification

Psilocybe cubensis is a small brown to tan mushroom. Like nearly all psilocybin-containing
mushrooms, it bruises blue when handled, which is the primary distinguishable feature
separating it from many non-psychoactive, inedible, or poisonous mushrooms that it could
otherwise be mistaken for in the wild. This blue bruising reaction is caused by the
oxidation of [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] upon damage to the fungal tissue.

Nearly all psilocybin-containing mushrooms are small brown or tan fungi that could be
mistaken for a number of non-psychoactive, inedible, or poisonous mushrooms in the wild.
This visual similarity between psychoactive and toxic species is a significant risk factor
for wild mushroom foragers and represents one of the barriers to widespread wild harvesting
as a source of hallucinogenic mushrooms.

## Natural Distribution

The species does not grow wild in Europe. Its natural distribution is tropical and
subtropical. Despite this, it has become the most commonly encountered hallucinogenic
mushroom in European retail settings due entirely to its suitability for commercial
cultivation. The contrast between P. cubensis (cultivated, tropical, non-native) and
[[Psilocybe semilanceata]] (wild, temperate, native) illustrates the two distinct supply
chains through which hallucinogenic mushrooms reach European consumers.

## Commercial Varieties

Multiple cultivated varieties of P. cubensis are marketed under geographic or cultural names,
including Mexican, Thai, Colombian, and Amazonian varieties. These strains differ in
appearance and relative potency but all belong to the same species. Among the recreational
varieties, the Mexican strain has been reported as the most commonly used, according to the
2004 Mixmag survey of UK clubbers where over half of mushroom users who planned to use again
named Mexican as their preferred variety. These named varieties are primarily marketing
distinctions used by [[smartshops]] and online retailers rather than formally recognised
taxonomic subspecies.

Some [[Psilocybe]] species, such as Psilocybe mexicana and Psilocybe tampanensis, form
sclerotia, hardened masses of mycelium that are more resistant to adverse environmental
conditions than normal mycelium. This is a defence mechanism against dryness, cold, heat,
or excessive moisture. These sclerotia are marketed commercially as philosopher's stones
or truffles and are sold alongside fresh and dried P. cubensis mushrooms in smartshops.

## Cultivation and Market

The vast majority of recreationally used P. cubensis mushrooms are cultivated rather than
picked wild. Cultivation is primarily carried out in the Netherlands, where the mushrooms
are grown specifically to supply the smartshop market. P. cubensis is exceptionally easy to
cultivate in terrariums, and it possesses a low water content that allows fresh specimens
to remain viable for 7 to 10 days after harvest, making it well suited to commercial
distribution through retail channels.

P. cubensis makes up the majority of smartshop turnover in the Netherlands, estimated at
approximately 50% of total sales according to the Dutch Risk Assessment Report. Home
cultivation supplies are widely available through online retailers. Products include spore
prints (created by pressing the gills of a mature mushroom onto paper), spore syringes,
spawn bags (containing sterilised hardwood chips inoculated with mycelium), and complete
growkits (typically containing a box with colonised substrate, a filter bag, and
paperclips). The non-profit organisation Free Spore Ring Europe distributed spore prints
for approximately 1 EUR per print to facilitate home cultivation.

## Pricing

Growkits for P. cubensis typically ranged from 25 to 100 EUR depending on the species or
variety available, while spore prints sold for 9 to 28 EUR per print or syringe. Fresh
Psilocybe cubensis (Mexican variety) sold for approximately 8.5 to 45 EUR per 30 grams.
Fresh Copelandia cyanescens, a more potent species, sold for 13 to 17.5 EUR per 10 grams.
Philosopher's stones (Psilocybe mexicana sclerotia) sold for 10 to 17.5 EUR per 15 grams.
Spawn bags for outdoor cultivation of species such as Psilocybe azurescens sold for 24 to
25 EUR per bag. These price ranges were observed in a January 2006 survey of online shops.

## Potency

Psilocybe cubensis contains up to approximately 0.6% combined psilocybin and psilocin by dry
weight. This places it in the moderate potency range among hallucinogenic mushrooms, being
less potent than species such as [[Psilocybe semilanceata]] or Psilocybe azurescens, which
can contain up to 1% or more. Despite its relatively moderate potency, P. cubensis remains
the most popular species for recreational use because of its ease of cultivation, reliable
yields, and widespread commercial availability. The psychoactive dose of psilocybin is
believed to be between 45 micrograms per kilogram of body weight and 315 micrograms per
kilogram, making dose estimation somewhat more predictable with cultivated P. cubensis
than with wild-foraged species of variable potency.

## Legal Status and Market Impact

When the UK prohibited psilocybin-containing fungi in July 2005, online retailers quickly
adapted. Several UK-based internet shops stopped selling P. cubensis products entirely,
with some announcing they were sold out while simultaneously posting notices that fresh
mushrooms, truffles, and growkits were about to become Class A controlled drugs. The Irish
online headshop THC placed a notice on their site that hallucinogenic mushroom products
were no longer available since they became illegal on 31 January 2006.

Some retailers pivoted to selling [[Amanita muscaria]] (fly agaric) and [[Amanita pantherina]]
(panther cap) as legal alternatives, or products such as Purple Ohms capsules containing
Hawaiian Baby Woodrose (Argyreia nervosa) seeds with lysergic acid amide (LSA). This
substitution effect raised public health concerns, as Amanita species carry substantially
greater toxicity risks than psilocybin-containing mushrooms, and some closely related Amanita
species are highly toxic and potentially fatal.
