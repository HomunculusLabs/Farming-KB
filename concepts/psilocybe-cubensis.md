# Psilocybe cubensis

Psilocybe cubensis (also known as Stropharia cubensis) is the most commonly cultivated and marketed
hallucinogenic mushroom species worldwide. It belongs to the Strophariaceae family and is the
predominant species in terms of recreational use. A wide range of cultivated varieties are sold
commercially, most of which do not grow wild in Europe.

## Taxonomy and Classification

Hallucinogenic mushrooms span several families:
- **Strophariaceae** — Psilocybe genus (including P. cubensis)
- **Bolbitiaceae** — Conocybe genus
- **Coprinaceae** — Copelandia and Panaeolus genera
- **Cortinariaceae** — Inocybe genus

The list of species and their geographical distribution is constantly revised by mycologists. More
than 100 known hallucinogenic mushroom species have been identified worldwide. The genus Psilocybe
is predominant in recreational use, followed by genus Panaeolus.

## Common Species in the Psilocybe Genus

- **Psilocybe cubensis** — most commercially marketed species
- **Psilocybe semilanceata** (liberty caps) — most common wild European species
- **Psilocybe cyanescens** (wavy caps) — also found wild in Europe
- **Psilocybe mexicana** — forms sclerotia sold as "philosopher's stones"
- **Psilocybe tampanensis** — also forms sclerotia
- **Psilocybe bohemica** — reported primarily in the Czech Republic
- **Psilocybe moravica** — reported primarily in the Czech Republic
- **Psilocybe azurescens** — among the most potent species known

## Cultivated Varieties

Most recreationally used mushrooms are cultivated rather than picked wild. Cubensis varieties are
cultivated specifically for the commercial market primarily because they are easy to grow in
terrariums and possess a low water content that allows them to stay fresh for 7 to 10 days.
Commercially available varieties include Mexican, Thai, Colombian, Amazonian, and Hawaiian.

According to UK Mixmag 2004 survey data, the "Mexican" variety was the most commonly used among
respondents who had consumed hallucinogenic mushrooms. These varieties are sold both as fresh and
dried products through smartshops and online retailers.

## Sclerotia-Forming Species

Some Psilocybe species form sclerotia — hardened masses of mycelium that are more resistant to
adverse environmental conditions (dryness, cold, heat, excessive moisture) than normal mycelium.
This is a defense mechanism against environmental stress.

Psilocybe mexicana and Psilocybe tampanensis sclerotia are sold commercially as "truffles" or
"philosopher's stone." The term "philosopher's stone" emerged as a marketing label during the
late 1990s commercialization of hallucinogenic mushrooms by [[smartshops and hallucinogenicmushroom retail|smartshops]].

## Identification Characteristics

Nearly all psilocybin-containing mushrooms are small, brown, or tan. They can easily be mistaken
for a number of non-psychoactive, inedible, or poisonous mushrooms in the wild. This similarity to
toxic species poses a significant health risk for wild foraging.

The primary distinguishable feature of most psilocybin-containing mushrooms is that they **bruise
blue when handled**. This blue bruising reaction is caused by the oxidation of [[psilocybin]] and
psilocin. However, this characteristic alone is not sufficient for safe identification, and some
non-psychoactive species also exhibit blue bruising.

## Potency

Psilocybe cubensis typically contains up to 0.6% combined psilocybin and psilocin by dry weight,
making it moderate in potency compared to other species. More potent species include:
- [[Psilocybe semilanceata]] — up to 1% psilocybin
- [[Psilocybe azurescens]] — reported higher than average potency
- Psilocybe bohemica — reported higher than average potency

Actual potency varies with species, variety, origin, growing conditions, and age, making precise
dosing difficult.

## Cultivation Methods

### Spore Prints and Syringes

Spore prints are created by pressing the gills of a mature mushroom onto paper. Spore syringes
transfer spores under sterile conditions. Spores inoculate a substrate of nutrients (free of
bacteria and molds) to grow mushrooms. The "Free Spore Ring Europe" (FSRE) distributes various
strains for approximately 1 EUR per print.

### Spawn Bags

Spawn bags contain sterile hardwood chips with mushroom mycelium already grown through the
substrate, ready for further cultivation. Available commercially through online retailers.

### Grow Kits

Growkits typically contain a box with colonized substrate, an air filter bag, and paperclips.
Available for various species including Psilocybe cubensis and Psilocybe tampanensis. Instructions
for home cultivation using spore prints and substrate are widely available on the internet.

## Geographic Distribution

Unlike Psilocybe semilanceata, which grows wild throughout central and northern Europe, P. cubensis
is not native to Europe. The varieties sold commercially in European smartshops are cultivated
mostly in the Netherlands. In the wild, P. cubensis is found in tropical and subtropical regions.

European wild-growing hallucinogenic mushrooms include primarily P. semilanceata (UK, Norway,
Germany, and widely across central/northern Europe), P. cyanescens, and P. bohemica and P. moravica
(Czech Republic). Habitats include wet grassy fields and uncultivated pastures.

## Distinguishing from Amanita muscaria

Psilocybe cubensis is fundamentally different from Amanita muscaria (fly agaric). The active
chemicals in Amanita — muscimol, ibotenic acid, and muscarin — are entirely different from psilocybin
and psilocin. Amanita species carry substantial toxicity risks, and some closely related species are
highly toxic and can cause fatal poisoning. After psilocybin mushrooms were banned in the UK, some
retailers began marketing Amanita muscaria as a legal alternative, raising significant safety
concerns among drug policy experts.
