## Overview

Urban farming brings food production into cities and densely populated areas. With creative approaches to space, light, and soil, urban farmers can produce significant amounts of food on rooftops, balconies, vacant lots, and small yards. This guide covers container gardening, vertical growing, rooftop gardens, SPIN farming, and community gardens.

## Container Gardening

Container gardening is the most accessible form of urban food production — it requires no land, only a sunny spot.

### Container Selection
- **Fabric grow bags**: Excellent drainage, air-pruning roots, affordable
- **Plastic pots**: Lightweight, retain moisture; ensure drainage holes
- **Self-watering containers**: Reservoir system reduces watering frequency
- **5-gallon buckets**: Inexpensive DIY; drill drainage holes

### Potting Mix
- Use a quality potting mix, not garden soil (garden soil compacts in containers)
- Recipe: 1/3 peat moss or coconut coir + 1/3 compost + 1/3 perlite or vermiculite
- Add worm castings (10-20% by volume) for additional fertility
- Refresh or replace potting mix annually — nutrients deplete over time

### What Grows in Containers
- **Tomatoes**: Need 5-10 gallon minimum; dwarf varieties for smaller pots
- **Peppers**: 3-5 gallons; sweet and hot peppers both thrive
- **Lettuce and greens**: 6-12 inch pots, 4-6 inches deep; see [[lettuce-and-leafy-greens]]
- **Herbs**: Most herbs thrive in 6-12 inch pots; see [[herb-growing-guide]]
- **Carrots**: Need 10-12 inch depth; choose short varieties; see [[carrot-growing-guide]]
- **Beans**: Bush beans in 5-gallon pots; pole beans with a trellis; see [[bean-growing-guide]]
- **Cucumbers**: Bush types in 5-gallon pots; vining types with a trellis; see [[cucumber-growing-guide]]
- **Strawberries**: Excellent in containers and hanging baskets; see [[strawberry-growing-guide]]
- **Blueberries**: Require acidic potting mix (peat-based); see [[blueberry-growing-guide]]

### Water Management
- Containers dry out fast — daily watering in summer; water until drainage
- Self-watering containers reduce to every 2-5 days
- Weekly liquid feed: fish emulsion, kelp extract, or compost tea at half strength

## Vertical Growing

Vertical growing maximizes production in limited square footage by using walls, fences, and purpose-built structures.

### Trellis Systems
- **Wall-mounted trellis**: Attach lattice or wire grid to walls or fences
- **Freestanding trellis**: A-frame, T-frame, or panel trellis between beds
- **String trellis**: Strings from overhead structure — ideal for tomatoes, cucumbers, beans
- **Cattle panels**: Rigid wire panels attached to T-posts — sturdy and versatile
- See [[bean-growing-guide]] and [[cucumber-growing-guide]] for specific trellis applications

### Vertical Tower Systems
- **Stacked container towers**: Pots stacked with openings for side-planting (strawberries, herbs)
- **PVC tower gardens**: Vertical pipes with planting holes; hydroponic or soil-based
- **Pocket planters**: Fabric or felt wall pockets — good for shallow-rooted plants
- **Gutter gardens**: Rain gutters mounted on walls for lettuce and herbs
- **Shoe organizers**: Canvas pocket organizers mounted on walls for herbs and greens

### Plants for Vertical Growing
- **Vining vegetables**: Tomatoes, cucumbers, pole beans, peas, malabar spinach
- **Trailing plants**: Strawberries, nasturtiums, thyme, oregano
- **Lightweight plants for wall systems**: Lettuce, herbs, spinach, radishes
- Avoid heavy plants (winter squash, melons) on vertical systems unless very sturdy

### Structural Considerations
- Ensure walls support the weight of wet soil and mature plants
- Provide adequate light — vertical surfaces may be shaded
- Plan drip irrigation; upper levels dry out faster

## Rooftop Gardens

Rooftop gardens turn underutilized urban roof space into productive growing areas.

### Structural Assessment
- Consult a structural engineer before starting — roofs have weight limits
- Wet soil weighs 60-100 lbs per cubic foot
- Determine the roof's live load capacity (typically 20-60 lbs per sq ft)
- Distribute weight evenly; place heaviest containers near load-bearing walls
- Avoid placing planters on parapets or near roof edges

### Wind Exposure
- Rooftops are significantly windier than ground level
- Wind causes rapid drying, physical damage to plants, and toppled containers
- Install windbreaks: trellises, screens, or taller plants on the windward side
- Choose wind-tolerant plants: kale, chard, rosemary, thyme, strawberries
- Secure all containers and structures — lightweight items become projectiles

### Sun and Heat
- Rooftop temperatures 10-15F higher than ground level
- Use shade cloth (30-50%) for heat-sensitive crops
- Use light-colored containers to reduce heat absorption

### Container Systems for Rooftops
- Fabric grow bags ideal — lightweight, good drainage
- Raised beds with lightweight soil mix (add perlite, vermiculite)
- Self-watering containers reduce maintenance frequency

### Water Management
- Drip irrigation with timer reduces maintenance; mulch all containers

## SPIN Farming (Small Plot INtensified)

SPIN farming is a commercial-scale urban farming method that generates income from small plots (as small as 1/2 acre).

### Core Principles
- **Sub-acre scale**: Profitable farming on less than one acre
- **Minimal infrastructure**: Low startup cost; uses hand tools and small equipment
- **Intensive relay cropping**: Multiple crops per bed per season
- **Direct marketing**: Sell directly to customers, restaurants, CSAs, farmers markets

### SPIN Farming Workflow
1. **Siting**: Find backyard, front yard, vacant lot, or shared land
2. **Land preparation**: Create raised beds or intensive planting areas
3. **Crop planning**: Choose high-value, quick-turn crops for maximum revenue per square foot
4. **Relay cropping**: As one crop finishes, the next goes in immediately
5. **Harvest and market**: Sell fresh produce at premium prices

### Best Crops for SPIN Farming
- **High-value, fast-turn**: Mesclun mix, radishes, arugula, baby carrots, scallions
- **Premium crops**: Heirloom tomatoes, specialty peppers, fresh herbs, microgreens
- **Storage crops (fall sales)**: Winter squash, potatoes, garlic, onions
- See [[lettuce-and-leafy-greens]] and [[carrot-growing-guide]] for crop-specific production

### Economics
- Revenue potential: $5,000-20,000+ per half-acre depending on crop mix and markets
- Startup cost: $500-5,000 (hand tools, seeds, irrigation, row covers)
- Labor: 20-40 hours per week during peak season (1/2 acre)
- Key to profitability: high-value crops, minimal inputs, direct sales

## Community Gardens

Community gardens are shared growing spaces where individuals or groups manage plots within a larger garden area.

### Benefits
- Access to growing space for apartment dwellers and those without yards
- Community building and social connection
- Shared knowledge and resources (tools, compost, water)
- Fresh food production in food deserts
- Green space and biodiversity in urban environments
- Educational opportunities for children and adults

### Getting Started
- Find existing gardens through local parks departments or the American Community Garden Association
- Typical plot size: 10x20 to 20x30 feet; fees: $20-100 per year

### Community Garden Best Practices
- Establish clear rules: plot maintenance standards, organic-only policies, tool care
- Create common areas: compost bins, tool sheds, pollinator gardens
- Organize work days; plan crop rotations to avoid soil depletion

## Balcony and Patio Growing

The smallest urban growing spaces can still be productive.

### Balcony Considerations
- Weight limits and sun exposure (south-facing: full sun; north-facing: shade)
- Wind exposure common on upper floors; use windbreaks and secure containers
- Dripping water on neighbors below — use saucers or self-watering containers

### Productive Balcony Crops
- Hanging baskets: Strawberries, cherry tomatoes, trailing herbs
- Railing planters: Lettuce, herbs, radishes, edible flowers
- Floor containers: Tomatoes, peppers, beans, cucumbers with trellis

## Season Extension in Urban Spaces

Small urban growing spaces benefit greatly from season extension techniques.

- **Row covers and low tunnels**: Extend the season by 4-6 weeks in spring and fall
- **Cold frames**: Simple boxes with glass or plastic lids for hardening off and winter growing
- **Mini hoop houses**: Cover raised beds or container areas for winter greens
- **Indoor growing under lights**: Year-round production of herbs, microgreens, and salad greens
- See [[herb-growing-guide]] for indoor herb growing details
