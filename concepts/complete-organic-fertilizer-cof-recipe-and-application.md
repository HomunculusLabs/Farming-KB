## Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) Recipe and Application

## Overview

The [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof]] (COF) is [[steve-solomon]]'s carefully formulated blend of organic amendments designed to provide vegetables with all essential major and trace minerals in balanced proportions. Unlike simple compost or manure, the COF addresses the specific mineral balance needed for maximum nutritional quality in harvested vegetables.

## Why COF Instead of Compost or Manure Alone

Compost and manure are valuable for soil organic matter and biological activity, but they cannot reliably provide balanced mineral nutrition. The mineral content of compost depends entirely on what went into it, and manure quality varies wildly depending on the animal's diet. COF provides a consistent, complete mineral supplement that works alongside (not instead of) reasonable amounts of compost or manure.

## The COF Recipe

All measurements are by **volume** (scoops, bucketfuls, jarfuls — not by weight). Proportions are forgiving; small variations will work fine.

### Base (choose one)

**Standard option:**
- 4 parts any kind of seedmeal **except** coprameal

**Higher-nitrogen option** (better for leafy crops in spring):
- 3 parts any seedmeal except coprameal
- 1 part coprameal
- 1 part "tankage" (blood-and-bone, meatmeal)

**Coprameal-based option:**
- 4 parts coprameal
- 2 parts tankage (to boost nitrogen, since coprameal is lower in N)

### Blend With

- 1 part ordinary agricultural lime (finely ground)
- 1 part gypsum (calcium sulfate) — if not available, double the agricultural lime
- 1 part dolomite lime

### Plus

- 1 part phosphorus source, any one of:
  - Finely ground rock phosphate (hard or soft rock)
  - Bonemeal
  - High-phosphate guano

### Plus (for best results)

- 1 part kelpmeal
- **OR** 1 part basalt dust

## Ingredient Details

### Seedmeals

Seedmeals are the byproduct of pressing oil from oilseeds (linseed/flax, sunflower, canola, cottonseed, soybean, safflower). When chemically analyzed, most seedmeals test around **6-4-2 NPK**, though coprameal (coconut) is about one-third weaker.

Key points:
- Buy whichever seedmeal offers the most protein per dollar
- All are roughly equivalent in fertilizing value — the difference is cost
- Must be kept dry and protected from moisture in a sealed container with a tight lid
- Most seedmeals in commercial trade probably contain GMO material — relevant for organic purists

### Tankage

Tankage is a slaughterhouse byproduct — the whole animal minus the primary meat cuts, rendered and dried. It analyzes at approximately **10-4-0 NPK** and provides:

- Higher nitrogen content than seedmeals alone
- A broad range of trace minerals from bone
- Can be stored dry for over 20 years

If tankage is unavailable, simply increase the seedmeal portion slightly and accept that the COF will be somewhat lower in nitrogen.

### Lime Types

Three types of ground natural rock provide [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]]:

| Type | Provides | Notes |
|------|----------|-------|
| Agricultural lime | Calcium | Relatively pure [[peat-calcium-carbonate-perlite-casing-psilocybe-cubensis-miniculture]] |
| Dolomite lime | Calcium + Magnesium | Both minerals, usually in roughly equal amounts |
| Gypsum | Calcium + Sulfur | Calcium sulfate — does not affect pH |

**Never use** quicklime, burnt lime, hydrated lime, or other chemically processed limes.

Using all three types together gives the best result. If forced to choose one, gypsum is probably the best single choice, but a mixture is far superior.

### Phosphorus Sources

All three options (rock phosphate, bonemeal, high-phosphate guano) boost phosphorus equally well. Rock phosphate is the most economical for large gardens. Bonemeal releases fastest; hard rock phosphate releases slowest.

### Kelpmeal vs Basalt Dust

- **Kelpmeal**: Rich in trace elements, growth regulators, and natural hormones. Adds considerable fortitude to plants — increased resistance to cold, frost, and other stresses. Can be expensive; Korean kelp is often cheaper than meal from North American or European sources.
- **Basalt dust**: A broad-spectrum trace mineral source from volcanic rock. Less expensive than kelpmeal but does not contain the growth regulators and hormones.

## Application Rates

### Basic Annual Application

- Spread **4 to 6 quarts (4 to 6 liters)** of COF per 100 square feet of garden bed
- This equals roughly **1 gallon per 25 square feet**
- Work into the top few inches with a hoe or spade
- If practicing no-dig methods, simply spread on top — soil organisms will incorporate it

### For Raised Beds

- Apply the same rate down each 50-foot row of bed, 12–18 inches (30–45 cm) wide

### For Hills

- Mix an additional portion of COF into each hill when forming

### Side-Dressing for High-Demand Crops

For medium and high-demand vegetables, additional side-dressings boost performance:

1. After seedlings emerge or transplants are established, sprinkle small amounts of COF around each plant
2. Cover the area the root system will eventually occupy
3. Repeat every 3–4 weeks as plants grow, placing each dusting slightly further from the stem
4. A rough guide: about 1 quart per 100 square feet per application

## How COF Works: Slow Release Biology

COF ingredients are not immediately soluble — they release nutrients as soil microorganisms decompose them. This is fundamentally different from [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]]:

- **Chemical fertilizers** dissolve rapidly in water, causing a growth spike followed by a nutrient crash, requiring repeated applications. Much of the dissolved nutrients leach away before plants can absorb them, especially in sandy soils.
- **COF ingredients** decompose slowly through soil biology. The rate is determined by soil temperature — roughly doubling for each 10°F (5°C) increase. Full decomposition of a COF application takes about 2 months in warm soil.
- In hot climates with sandy soils, COF releases faster and the effect lasts shorter
- In cool climates with heavy soils, COF releases more slowly but lasts longer

This slow-release biology means nutrients are available as plants need them, with minimal waste through leaching. It also feeds the soil ecosystem, which in turn provides "biological vitamins" (Krasil'nikov's term) that enhance plant nutrition beyond what mineral analysis alone would predict.

## Why Chemical Fertilizers Are Problematic

1. **Incomplete nutrition**: Even so-called "complete" chemical fertilizers (like 16-16-16) contain only NPK plus possibly sulfur. They lack calcium, magnesium, and all trace minerals.
2. **Potassium excess**: Most chemical blends push potassium too high, increasing starch/fiber yield while diluting protein, vitamin, and mineral content.
3. **Rapid leaching**: Water-soluble nutrients wash through the soil profile, wasting money and polluting groundwater.
4. **Boom-and-bust growth**: Rapid [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]] causes flush growth that is more susceptible to [[mushroom-pests-and-diseases]].
5. **Soil biology damage**: Excess soluble salts harm beneficial soil microorganisms over time.

## Cost Considerations

COF ingredients purchased in bulk from farm supply stores and grain dealers are far more economical than small bags from garden centers. A 50-pound sack of each major ingredient will supply a typical 2,000-square-foot garden for several years at very low cost per season.
