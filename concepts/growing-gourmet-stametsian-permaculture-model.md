# The Stametsian Permaculture Model for Mushroom Integration

Permaculture is a concept pioneered by Australian Bill
Mollison and literally means "permanent agriculture." His
model of biological diversity and complementary agricultural
practices promotes a sustainable environment via the
interplay of natural ecosystems.

When gourmet and medicinal mushrooms are involved as key
organisms in the recycling of agricultural and forest
by-products, the biodynamics of permaculture soar to
extraordinary levels of productivity. Not only are mushrooms
a protein-rich food source for humans, but the by-products
of mushroom cultivation unlock nutrients for other members
of the ecological community.

## Key Integration Strategies

### 1. Oyster Mushrooms in Permaculture

Oyster mushrooms can be grown indoors on pasteurized corn
stalks, wheat, rice, and rye straw and a wide range of
other materials including paper and pulp by-products.
Soaking bulk substrates in cold water creates a residual
"tea" that is a nutritious fertilizer and potent
insecticide. Submerging the bulk substrate in hot water
produces a different brew of "tea": a naturally potent
herbicide.

Pumping CO2 from mushroom growing rooms into greenhouses
to enhance plant production makes good sense. The waste
substrate from Oyster production is useful as fodder for
cows, chickens, and pigs.

### 2. King Stropharia as Ecosystem Engineer

King Stropharia (Stropharia rugoso-annulata) is an ideal
player in the recycling of complex wood debris and garden
wastes. Vigorously attacking wood (sawdust, chips, twigs,
branches), King Stropharia also grows in wood-free
substrates, particularly soils supplemented with chopped
straw.

#### Bee Interaction

For 6 weeks one summer, bees attacked a King Stropharia
bed, exposing the mycelium to the air. A continuous convoy
of bees could be traced from morning to evening from
beehives to the mushroom patch, sucking the sugar-rich
cytoplasm from the mycelial wounds. Bee keepers across
North America had long been mystified by bees' attraction
to sawdust piles, now explained by the underlying sweet
mushroom mycelium.

#### Water Filtration

King Stropharia beds installed at the heads of ravines
draining onto waterways can eliminate fecal coliform
bacteria. The mycelium, in effect, becomes a
micro-filtration membrane. By properly locating mushroom
beds, "gray water" run-off can be cleaned of bacteria and
nitrogen-rich effluent. Overall water quality improves.

After three to four years, chunks of wood are totally
reduced into a rich, peat-like soil ideal for the garden.

#### Fish Food Generation

King Stropharia mushrooms serve as a base medium for
generating fish food. Fish learn to strike the large
mushrooms to dislodge swollen insect larvae into the
water where they are eagerly consumed.

### 3. Shiitake in Forest Ecosystems

Shiitake cultivation on logs fits naturally into forested
permaculture designs. Logs can be stacked along hillsides
and in forest clearings, generating secondary income for
families while recycling wood debris.

### 4. Garden Giant for Soil Building

The massive size of King Stropharia fruitbodies -- some
exceeding 5 pounds -- demonstrates the potential for
using mushrooms as primary soil-builders. The substrate
matrix concept of mixing sawdust and chips creates
ideal habitat, with smaller particles for quick
colonization and larger particles as fruiting platforms.

## Ecological Benefits

The rapid return of nutrients back into the ecosystem
boosts the life cycles of plants, animals, insects (bees),
and soil microflora. Mushrooms participate in permaculture
through:

- Nutrient cycling and decomposition of wood and plant
  debris
- Water filtration and soil remediation
- Insect control through natural pesticide teas
- Protein production for human and animal consumption
- CO2 enrichment for plant growth in greenhouses
- Soil building and structure improvement

## Species Diversity and Sequencing

The model encourages mixing and sequencing species in
outdoor environments. A high rate of inoculation (25% by
volume) is recommended when establishing new patches.
Permaculturally oriented farms could inoculate hardwood
stumps interspersed amongst multi-canopied shade trees.

Those with compromised immune systems would be wise to
establish their own medicinal mushroom patches utilizing
Maitake, Reishi, and other medicinal species. Clear
economic, ecological, medicinal, and moral incentives
support such models.
