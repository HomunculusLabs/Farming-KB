# Soil Structure and Composition

## Overview

Soil is a complex, living material composed of minerals, water, gases, life forms, and organic remains. Understanding its structure and composition is essential for permaculture designers working with dams, gardens, agriculture, and land rehabilitation. Soil structure determines water retention, nutrient availability, root penetration, and overall productivity.

## Tribal and Traditional Soil Classifications

Before modern scientific classification systems, agricultural societies developed sophisticated assessments of their soils based on practical characteristics:

- **Colour**: Indicates humus content (darker = more organic matter)
- **Taste**: Agrees with pH measures
- **Moisture capacity and water retention**: Critical for crop selection
- **Sand content**: Affects drainage and nutrient holding
- **General texture**: The feel of soil between fingers
- **Firmness**: Resistance to compression
- **Structure (dry soils)**: How soil holds together when dry
- **Wet-season structure**: Behaviour when saturated
- **Vegetative indicators**: Health of specific crops as soil quality markers
- **Drainage**: Rate of water movement through soil
- **Slope**: Affects erosion risk and water retention
- **Elevation**: Temperature and rainfall implications
- **Animal indicators**: Termite mound shape and size, earthworm presence
- **Plant indicators**: Species indicating acidity, drainage, or fertility
- **Catena**: Soil types based on slope relationships and position in landscape
- **"Hot" and "cold" soils**: Relative fertility (not temperature); also indicates water retention
- **Usage**: Pigments, pottery, salt extraction from reeds
- **Work needed for crop**: An energy input classification
- **Suitability for specific crop**: Soils ranked for up to twelve crop types

These traditional systems demonstrate that sophisticated soil assessment has always been available from agricultural peoples. Modern classifications are more complex in nomenclature and physical categories, using standard colour charts (Munsell and others), standard comparisons, and standard sieves.

## Compacted vs. Uncompacted Soil

If soil has enough plasticity (usually clay), or glue or fibre from organic sources, it can be pressed or compacted into mudbricks, hard pisé, or baked to clay or stoneware. In any of these forms, it is of little use to plants.

Uncompacted soils are open, crumbly, or soft unless concreted by chemical solutes or compacted by ploughs, hooves, or traffic. Crumbly soils have a definite structure—the soil particles are in nodules or clumps held together by roots, clay minerals, and chemical bonds.

### Destruction of Crumb Structure

When a plough or drag harrow passes through fragile soil assemblies, they may powder up as they do in a potter's ballmill or on outback roads. Dryland soils with high salt content are particularly susceptible to loss of [[crumb-structure]], only partly relieved by gypsum application.

## The Soil Mantle

The mantle of soil and subsoil that covers the earth is as thin as the shine on the skin of an orange. This mantle extends as living mud below the waters of earth as well as on land. It is composed of five elements:

1. **Minerals**: Mainly silica, oxides of iron and aluminium, and complex minerals
2. **Soil waters**: Fresh, saline, with dissolved minerals and gases
3. **Gases**: Some from the atmosphere, others emitted by rock breakdown and the earth's interior
4. **Life forms**: From fungal spores and bacteria to wombats and ground squirrels, from massive roots to minute motile algae
5. **Once-living remains**: The humus of the earth—decayed, compressed, and fossil organic material

Soils rarely extend much below 1-2 metres, and are more often a living system only 6-12 cm deep. Subsoils, lacking the life components, and buried soils or deep washed silts are rare and confined to valley floors, deltas, and glacial mounds.

## Particle Size Classification

### The Jar Test Method

To estimate the proportion of clay, silt, sand, and coarse particles in soil:

1. Mix a sample of soil from a few typical sites
2. Pour a cup of soil in a tall jar, filled almost with water
3. Shake vigorously and let the soil fractions settle out over a day or a week (clay can remain in suspension for up to a week)
4. Observe the distinct layers that form

### Particle Size Fractions (USDA Classification)

- **Coarse particles**: Inert but useful in fine soils as a wind-erosion deterrent
- **Sand**: 0.05-2 mm
- **Silt**: 0.02-0.05 mm
- **Clay**: Less than 0.002 mm (1 gram of clay has a surface area of up to 1,000 times that of 1 gram of sand)

### Practical Applications

- **40% or more clay** is needed for dam walls
- **Less clay** is needed for good mud bricks (without lime or cement added)

## Soil Crumb Structure

Soil crumb structure, aided by lime (calcium), bonds the various fractions together and creates 20-60% pore space. The organic materials and gels hold the structure open in rain, allowing plant nutrients to become soluble for absorption by roots.

Adequate pore space is critical because it determines:
- Water infiltration and retention
- Root penetration and growth
- Gas exchange (oxygen in, carbon dioxide out)
- Microbial habitat and activity

## Soil Elements

Of the 103 known elements, only some are commonly dealt with in soil and water literature. Soil itself is predominantly composed of:

- **Aluminium**: Major component of clay minerals
- **Silica**: Primary constituent of sand and many rock types
- **Iron**: Contributes to soil colour and redox chemistry
- **Calcium**: Important in calcareous areas, shell deposits, limestone regions

Only mineral ore deposits have large quantities of other elements. Designers in any field need a basic knowledge of nutrients, poisons, and tolerable or essential levels of trace elements in food, water, and the built environment.

### Environmental Health Concerns

Of particular concern are:
- Radioactives in clays, bricks, paints, and stone
- Emissions from domestic appliances (TV, microwave ovens)
- Microwave radiation from TV transmission and power lines
- Heavy metals in industrial soils

The health of plants, animals, the environment, soils, and buildings all depend on the balance of elements, radioactive substances, radiation, and water quality.
