# Soil Biological Indicators and Test Strips

## Mineral Fertilisers and Soil Amendments

### The Test Strip Method

The practical method for testing specific soil types involves sowing a mixed legume (clover), Brassica, and grass crop (or any important crop to be grown). This sowing is then divided into **trial plots** treated at varying levels with soil or foliar spray amendments, testing plant health and response based on soil test data for pH and mineral availability, or on leaf analysis.

### Scale of Application

- **Home gardeners**: A deliberate walk through the system and a good key to mineral deficiencies may be all that is needed. Problems are rare in well-drained garden beds using composts and organic moulds where monoculture cropping is not constantly practised.
- **Broad scale** (prairie re-establishment, erosion control, land reclamation, plantation forestry): Every practical farmer and forester uses **test strips** of light to heavy soil treatments.

### Test Strip Design

Test strips should include treatments ranging from soil loosening to fertiliser, micronutrient, and grazing/cutting/culling trials. Important guidelines:

1. Include typical areas of soil and drainage
2. **Avoid** areas under trees, old stockyards, hay-stacks, intense fire scars, watering points, gateways, and roads — all have special features needing separate assessment
3. Observe colonisation patterns: chicory, thistles, and tough deep-rooted weeds often colonise inhospitable areas like old roads and trafficked areas
4. Such data is useful for specific cases but doesn't mean a whole field should be compacted to grow chicory — rather, chicory is a useful pioneer of compacted soils

### Assessment

- Test strips can be as little as 1% of total acreage
- Assessment can be **casual** (in clear-cut cases) or **analytic and careful** where only slight differences appear
- Securely mark test strips with stout pegs for long-term visits, as effects of some treatments persist or become evident over several seasons
- Do not widen the treated area until trials are assessed

### Universal Pre-Treatments

- Always add [[dolomite]] to acid sands
- In alkaline and heavy clay soils, trace elements may become insoluble — best added as foliar sprays to mulch or green crop, or to trees

## Biological Indicators of Soil and Site Conditions

In any local area, the composition, shape, size, and distribution of plants give many clues to soil type, depth, and extrinsic factors. The following ten factors can be read from biological indicators:

### 1. Soil Depth

Shallow soils dry out quickly and hold few nutrients. An excellent indicator is to observe one tree species (e.g., [[compost]], all plants thrive in high humus soils at modest lime levels. Perennial species may need more care in site selection. Almost all pollutants and many fertilisers tend to make soils acid, as does continued cropping or over-grazing.

### 4. Fire Frequency

East-west ridges often reveal abrupt species changes at the ridge wherever fire occurs. Fire produces dry, scrabbly, summer-deciduous, thick-seeded species. Lack of fire develops broadleaf, winter-deciduous, small-seeded plants with thin seed capsules and deep litter fall.

Cross-sectional cuts of trees reveal fire scars as gum pockets or charred sections, which can be counted to determine the fire frequency of the site. If tree stem sections are marked for directions before sampling, the direction of fires can also be judged.

### 5. Frost Lines

Many tree and plant species indicate frost-lines. It is a matter of observing local flora, or planting frost-susceptible species (tomatoes, bananas, potatoes) down a hill profile to measure frost intensity in subtropics and deserts.

### 6. Drainage

- **Poor drainage**: Mosses, sundews, and fine-leaved heaths
- **Good drainage**: Large trees such as chestnuts (which require 2 metres of well-drained soil)

These indicators assist survey before pits are dug or drainage measured.

### 7. Mineral Deposits

Analysis of plant residues often indicates concentration of ores in underlying soil or rock. Lead and copper-molybdate deposits are indicated by specific plant assemblages. Leaves or humus from birch, cherry, honeysuckle, St. John's Wort, wormwood, juniper, and heather reveal lodes plus tungsten and tin concentrates.

**Specific indicator plants**:
- Rue of Violets → zinc
- Catchfly → cobalt
- Astragalus → selenium
- Milk vetch → selenium and uranium
- Russian thistle → boron
- Alyssum → nickel
- Honeysuckle → silver and gold
- Horsetail → gold and silica

### Phytomining and Phytoremediation

Plants can both concentrate and tolerate unusually high levels of specific minerals, opening possibilities for:
- **Biological concentration and removal** of metallic soil pollutants (lead, uranium)
- **Phytomining** of locally rare trace elements — some patents have been granted for mining gold deposits using banana or citrus plants deprived of common elements (potash, phosphate); their leaves then concentrate sparse gold deposits
- **Bioaccumulation monitoring**: Oysters concentrate zinc to 11% dry weight (an emetic dose), abalone concentrate cadmium, and several large fish concentrate mercury and biological poisons from corals to inedible levels

### 8. Overgrazing and Soil Compaction

High grasshopper and pasture grub activity (prevalent on overgrazed landscapes) and patches of poisonous, inedible, thorny, and unpalatable plants (e.g., Sodom apple, oxalis, capeweed) indicate overstocking or range mismanagement. This is a synthesis between changing soil conditions, plant stress, and heavy livestock selection favouring spiny or inedible species survival.

The recommended approach combines:
1. Sensible utilisation of grasshoppers and grubs as a dried-protein supplement for fish or food pellets
2. Soil conditioning, slashing, and de-stocking or re-seeding to restore species balance

Too often pastoralists blame weeds and seek chemical solutions rather than management solutions.

### 9. Macrofaunal Effects

The site of a sea-bird rookery, rabbit warren, goose or eider ground nest, owl pellet-pile, or large carcass decay causes sudden and often long-term vegetation change, as do termite mounds and harvester-ant colonies. Uses of this data:
- Aid to conservation
- Indication of soil drainage (rabbits choose good drainage)
- Result of specific nutrient supply (guano on seabird rookeries)
- Guide for establishing tree clumps following natural indicators

A large proportion of wind-blown, nitrogen-loving, and inedible plants, or plants carried by birds as seed, depend on specific animal habits, dung, or soil disturbances. The role of animals in plant seed distribution and root associations is well-recognised; their role in soil change is less commonly noted.
