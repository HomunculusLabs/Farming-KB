# Permaculture Design Process

From William Ozier's "William's PDC: A Permaculture Design Course" (2011). There is no one true "design process" -- design is a creative endeavour. However, having a structured process helps beginners and ensures design teams can work together efficiently.

## Step 1: Get a Client and a Site

The best way to find permaculture clients is to be part of a community (join your local permaculture guild).

## Step 2: Assemble Team

Teams should include people with expertise in the various permaculture elements: gardeners, builders, landscapers, illustrators, community organisers. Even without a PDC, team members should have interest and passion for the necessary research and design work.

## Step 3: Organise Design Team

Follow the Organizational Mandala from the Community section, adapted for design teams:
- Each team member is a "Design Steward" (a lobe of the mandala)
- Sub-tasks under each steward represent steps of the design process
- Every task must be assigned to someone
- Consider a central coordinator to track progress

## Step 4: Analysis

Before making ANY design decisions, analyse the site. Analytical tools used in permaculture design:

### Base Map
The map of the entire site within legal property limits. Obtain from local county/city office, or create using Google Earth. Keep an original aside as reference; work on copies. Use tracing paper or transparencies for overlays, or create a layered digital base map.

### Broad Scale Site
Analysis begins at the macroscale and works inward (from patterns to details):
1. Start globally -- what hemisphere, continent, country?
2. Zoom in -- region, state, bioregion, watershed, county
3. Finally zoom to the site itself

Consider: nearby roads, towns, toxic sources (trash dumps), forests, neighbour situations.

### Client Interview
The goal is to get into the client's head and think like them. Design is a manifestation of philosophy -- you are attempting to manifest the client's philosophy, not yours. Key questions:
- Understanding of permaculture?
- Willing to let design progress through ecological succession?
- Health needs and dietary requirements?
- Physical/mental limitations?
- Budget and time frame?
- Vision for life on the site?
- Mission statement and goals?
- How "hands on" do they want to be?
- How many people served? Pets?
- Social habits and events?

### General Climate Data
Solar altitude and azimuth, prevailing seasonal winds, flood plains, average temperatures, average annual rainfall. Identify microclimates on the base map.

### History of the Site
Research historical patterns -- major climactic events AND human habitation. Look for historical plants and crops used, design precedence, and other historical factors. Compile "the story of the site."

### Indicator Species
Observe the site for plant and animal indicator species that reveal soil conditions, water patterns, and ecosystem health.

### Inventories
Lists of available resources: plants, animals, building materials, salvageable materials, water sources, available people power, community resources.

### Observation and Interaction
Spend time on the site observing patterns, processes, and relationships before making design decisions.

### Soil Samples
Test infiltration rates across the site. Take samples where differences are observed (different grasses, ground covers, soil colourations, slopes). Colour-code areas on the base map.

### Surface Water Flow
Chart water flow across the site using topographical information. The ultimate goal: all rainwater falling on the site stays on the site and infiltrates into the ground.

### [[permaculture-zone-and-sector-analysis|Zones and Sectors]]
Map zones (0-5) based on frequency of human use. Map sectors (sun, wind, fire, noise, views, wildlife) based on external energies.

### Zoning Ordinances
Research local regulations that may constrain or enable design options.

## Step 5: Design

Once analysis is complete, use the collected data to create the design:
- Apply permaculture principles
- Place elements according to zone and sector analysis
- Design from patterns to details
- Integrate elements so each serves multiple functions
- Use the charette process for team-based design development

## Step 6: Presentation

Organise a practice run and formal presentation of the design to the client.

## Design Elements to Consider

Animal Systems, Appropriate Technology, Community, Composting, Earthworks, Electricity, Food Systems, Forests, Hydro Cycle, Invisible Structures, Passive Design, Plants, Rain Water, Right Livelihood, Soils, Structures, Trees, Water Courses, Water Treatment.
