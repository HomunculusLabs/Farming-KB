# Monotropoid Mycorrhiza

Monotropoid mycorrhiza is a specialized mycorrhizal association formed by non-photosynthetic (achlorophyllous) plants in the Monotropoideae subfamily (Ericaceae) with [[symbiotic-fungi-ectomycorrhizal-network-carbon-trading]].

## Host Plants

Monotropoid mycorrhizae are formed by plants in Monotropoideae:

- **Monotropa** (Indian pipe): M. uniflora, M. hypopitys
- **Monotropsis**: M. odorata (sweet pinesap)
- **Pterospora** (pinedrops): P. andromedea
- **Hemitomes**: H. congestum (giant coneplant)
- **Allotropa**: A. virgata (candystick)
- **Sarcodes**: S. sanguinea (snow plant)

These plants are completely achlorophyllous — they lack chlorophyll and cannot photosynthesize. They are often mistaken for fungi due to their pale, fleshy appearance. They are found in forest understories, typically in deep shade under coniferous trees.

## The Tripartite Carbon Flow

Monotropoid mycorrhiza involves a three-way carbon exchange:

1. **Autotrophic tree** (e.g., Pinus, Tsuga, Pseudotsuga) fixes carbon through photosynthesis
2. **Ectomycorrhizal fungus** receives carbon from the tree in exchange for soil nutrients
3. **Monotropoid plant** receives carbon from the shared fungus, acting as a carbon sink

This makes monotropoid plants epiparasites (or cheaters) on the mutualistic ectomycorrhizal relationship between trees and fungi. The monotropoid plant provides nothing known to the fungus in return — it is a fully mycoheterotrophic parasite of the common [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]]:

- **Tricholoma**: T. magnivelare is the primary fungal partner of [[host-recognition-mycoparasitism-fungal-specificity]] varies among monotropoid species:
- Some are generalists, connecting with multiple EMF species
- Others are highly specific, associating with only one or a few fungal species
- The degree of specificity often correlates with the monotropoid plant's habitat specialization

## Structural Features

Monotropoid mycorrhizae share some features with ectomycorrhizae but have distinctive characteristics:

### Fungal Mantle

- A well-developed fungal mantle surrounds the root, similar to ectomycorrhizae
- The mantle may be thick and conspicuous
- Hyphal connections link the mantle to the external [[singh-mycorrhizal-network-carbon-transfer-plants]] from fungus to plant

### Fungal Pegs

The most distinctive feature of monotropoid mycorrhizae:

- Fungal pegs are short, knob-like intrusions that penetrate from Hartig net hyphae into cortical cells
- Unlike arbuscules (which are profusely branched intracellular structures), fungal pegs are simple, undivided projections
- Each peg is surrounded by the host cell plasma membrane
- Pegs may be the primary site of [[comparison-orchid-mycorrhizae-vs-ericoid-mycorrhizae]]

### Root Morphology

- Roots are fleshy, coralloid, and often thickened
- Color varies from white to cream to pinkish depending on species and fungal partner
- Roots are often densely clustered in the organic soil horizon
- Root systems are reduced compared to photosynthetic relatives

## Physiological Ecology

### Carbon Flow

Carbon flows through the tripartite network:

- Trees allocate 10-20% of net photosynthate to their ectomycorrhizal fungi
- A fraction of this carbon is intercepted by the monotropoid plant through the shared fungal network
