## The Paradox of Fermentation

A central puzzle in mushroom nutrition is that fermentation is biologically
necessary but chemically detrimental to the manure. When the mycelium of
Agaricus campestris is grown in sterilized fresh manure in pure culture, it
grows as rapidly and even more profusely than in fermented manure similarly
treated. This demonstrates conclusively that fermentation does not improve
the chemical quality of the manure for the mushroom. The necessity of
fermentation is therefore biological rather than chemical.

## Why Fermentation Is Required

Fresh manure contains very readily soluble and relatively unstable organic
products. These products are ordinarily the seat of pronounced and abundant
bacterial activity, and perhaps also of some important direct chemical
rearrangements. In the presence of this rapid bacterial activity, which
occurs during the first stage of fermentation, the mycelium of the mushroom
will not grow.

This failure is not merely due to the increased temperature that accompanies
bacterial activity. Even when the manure is present in quantities small enough
that no noticeable temperature increase occurs, the mycelium still fails to
grow. This demonstrates that the bacterial community itself, rather than heat
alone, is the inhibiting factor.

## What Fermentation Accomplishes

Fermentation acts not by improving the quality of the manure but by effecting
a complete change in the bacterial activity within it. Specifically:

- It removes or transforms rapidly fermentable products that support
  competitive bacterial growth
- It induces certain changes in the insoluble material and straw, making
  the substrate accessible to mushroom mycelium
- It creates conditions where the mushroom mycelium can grow slowly and
  compete with the remaining microorganisms

The nutrients used by the mushroom are both the gradually soluble organic
products and the necessary inorganic salts present in the compost. The
once-prevalent belief that the ammonia of the manure might constitute the
only important fertilizer in mushroom culture is erroneous. However, manure
so treated as to effect the loss of much free ammonia is injured for
manurial purposes.

## Growth on Synthetic Media

Duggar demonstrated that Agaricus campestris mycelium grows quite well upon
synthesized media prepared from known chemical substances, such as ordinary
nutrient fertilizer salts with the addition of casein or peptone. Laboratory
experiments further indicated that all of the nitrogen may be supplied as
inorganic salts, provided that a suitable organic compound as a source of
carbon is furnished. This finding was significant because it showed that
the mushroom is not nutritionally dependent on the complex mixture found in
manure, but rather requires specific combinations of carbon and nitrogen
sources that happen to be provided by properly prepared compost.
