# cannabis grafting intergeneric humulus clarke

Grafting is an asexual propagation technique that joins the tissues of two
plants so they grow as one. In Cannabis, grafting has attracted both
scientific curiosity and practical interest, particularly the intergeneric
grafting of Cannabis with its closest botanical relative, Humulus (hops).
[[robert-clarke]] documents the history, science, and technique of Cannabis
grafting in [[clarke-marijuana-botany-cannabinoid-biosynthesis-pathway]], providing a thorough examination of what
grafting can and cannot accomplish with this species.

## Intergeneric Cannabis and Humulus Grafts

Intergeneric grafts between Cannabis and Humulus (hops) have fascinated
researchers and cultivators for decades. The two genera belong to the same
family, Cannabaceae, which makes them botanical relatives and raises the
question of whether grafted combinations might share cannabinoid-producing
properties.

### The Warmke and Davidson Claim (1943)

Warmke and Davidson (1943) claimed that Humulus tops grafted upon Cannabis
roots produced "...as much drug as leaves from intact hemp plants, even
though leaves from intact hop plants are completely nontoxic." According to
this research, the active ingredient of Cannabis was being produced in the
roots and transported across the graft union to the Humulus tops. This
finding implied that [[cannabis-trichome-development-cannabinoid-biosynthesis-sites]] might occur in the root system
and that psychoactive compounds could be translocated through the vascular
tissue of the graft to an entirely different genus.

If true, this would have had profound implications for the production of
cannabinoids, suggesting that hops plants grafted onto Cannabis rootstock
could produce psychoactive material. The claim circulated widely among both
researchers and cultivators, fueling interest in Cannabis grafting as a
potential means of expanding cannabinoid production.

### The Crombie and Crombie Refutation (1975)

Later research by Crombie and Crombie (1975) entirely disproved the
Warmke and Davidson theory. Their experiments were more rigorous and
thoroughly controlled. Grafts were made between:

- High-THC and low-THC strains of Cannabis (intrageneric grafts)
- Cannabis and Humulus (intergeneric grafts)

Detailed chromatographic analysis was performed on both donors for each
graft combination and their corresponding control populations. The results
showed "...no evidence of transport of intermediates or factors critical to
cannabinoid formation across the grafts."

This finding definitively established that:

1. Cannabinoids are not produced in the roots and transported upward
2. [[cannabinoid-biosynthesis-pathway]] occurs locally in the tissues where the
   compounds are found
3. Grafting cannot transfer psychoactive properties between species
4. The cannabinoid profile of a grafted shoot reflects its own genotype,
   not the genotype of the rootstock

The Crombie and Crombie study is considered authoritative and has not been
successfully challenged. It underscores the importance of genotype in
determining cannabinoid production, a theme that runs throughout Clarke's
work on [[oner-cannabis-breeding-fundamentals]] and genetics.

## Practical Grafting Technique for Cannabis

Despite the limitations revealed by the scientific studies, grafting
Cannabis remains a simple and interesting horticultural technique. Several
seedlings can be grafted together into one to produce unusual specimen
plants with multiple genetic identities sharing a single root system.

### Materials and Preparation

The procedure begins by planting one seedling each of several separate
strains close together in the same container. The stock (root plant) for
the cross is placed in the center, surrounded by the scion (shoot) plants
that will be grafted onto it. The seedlings are allowed to grow until they
are four weeks old, at which point they are ready to be grafted.
