# EMCDDA Brand Names, User Terms, and Smartshop Marketing of Magic Mushrooms

## Overview

The commercialization of hallucinogenic mushrooms through European smartshops in
the late 1990s and early 2000s created a distinctive marketing and nomenclature
ecosystem. The EMCDDA thematic paper (2006) documented the overlap between
retailer brand names and user-generated terminology, noting how these names
reflected changing market strategies, evolving consumer demographics, and broader
cultural trends in the "natural products" marketplace.

## Common User Terms

The EMCDDA recorded the most widely used terms for hallucinogenic mushrooms
across European languages and user communities:

**English terms**: magic mushrooms, mush, mushies, boomers, psilocybes, cubes,
liberty caps, caps, philosopher's stone, sacred mushrooms, teonanacatl, Mexican
mushrooms, and moon children. These terms range from descriptive (liberty caps
referring to the shape of P. semilanceata) to culturally specific (teonanacatl
being the Aztec name meaning "divine flesh" or "flesh of god") to slang (boomers,
mushies).

**European language equivalents**: The EMCDDA compiled translations of
"hallucinogenic mushrooms" across numerous European languages, including:
- Dutch: paddos
- German: Zauberpilze, Psychoaktive Pilze
- French: champignons hallucinogènes, champis
- Hungarian: varázsgombák
- Italian: funghi magici
- Norwegian: fleinsopp
- Spanish: hongos alucinógenos, honguitos
- Swedish: magiska svampar, psykedeliska svampar
- Polish: magiczne grzybki, grzyby halucynogenne
- Czech: magické houby
- Danish: psilocybinsvampe, magiske svampe

## Smartshop Brand Names and Marketing Evolution

The late 1990s saw the emergence of smartshops as commercial retailers of
hallucinogenic mushrooms, primarily in the Netherlands and the UK. This
commercialization introduced new product names and marketing strategies that
reflected the evolving demographics of mushroom consumers.

### The "Philosopher's Stone" Brand

One of the most notable marketing innovations was the branding of Psilocybe
[[psilocybe-mexicana-sclerotia-timeline-dark-incubation-flush-pattern]] as "Philosopher's Stone." Sclerotia — dense, hardened masses
of mycelium that serve as survival structures for the fungus — are sold by many
smartshops as an alternative to fresh or dried mushrooms. The name "Philosopher's
Stone" evokes alchemical symbolism and positions the product as a tool for
introspection and philosophical exploration rather than mere recreation.

### The "Herbal Highs" Category

Hallucinogenic mushrooms were increasingly marketed under the umbrella label of
"Herbal highs" during the late 1990s and early 2000s. This categorization
aligned [[cotter-mushroom-products-and-value-added-applications]] with the broader trend toward organic and herbal
products and positioned them within a product category that consumers perceived
as "natural" and therefore potentially safer than synthetic drugs.

The "Herbal highs" label encompassed numerous other [[bee-products-beyond-honey]] mushrooms,
including:
- "Herbal xtc" products containing ingredients such as Ephedra alkaloids
  (Sida cordifolia), guarana, caffeine, Siberian ginseng, and kola nut
- Salvia divinorum, a sprawling perennial herb containing the potent
  psychoactive compound salvinorin-A, native to the Sierra Mazatec region
  of Mexico
- Various other herbal products marketed for their stimulant or psychoactive
  effects

## Overlap Between Brand Names and User Terms

The EMCDDA noted that in the case of hallucinogenic mushrooms, there is a
significant overlap between brand names used by retailers and terms used by
consumers. This overlap reflects several dynamics:

1. **Marketing feedback loop**: User terms that gain popularity are sometimes
   adopted by retailers as product names, and vice versa.
2. **Market signaling**: New brand names can signal changes in the target
   consumer demographic or the perceived nature of the product.
3. **Cultural currency**: Terms that resonate with particular subcultures
   (psychonauts, festival-goers, spiritual seekers) can enhance product appeal.

The term "teonanacatl" exemplifies this phenomenon. Originally the Aztec name
for sacred mushrooms, it was adopted by modern users and retailers as a product
name that evokes indigenous authenticity and spiritual significance.

## The Aztec Connection: Teonanacatl

The term "teonanacatl" deserves special attention as both a user term and a
brand name. Teonanacatl is the Nahuatl (Aztec) word meaning "divine flesh" or
"flesh of the gods." It was the name used by the Aztecs for the psychoactive
mushrooms employed in their religious ceremonies, as documented by the 16th-
century Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún. The adoption of this term by
modern users and retailers reflects a romanticization of indigenous mushroom
use and positions contemporary mushroom consumption within an ancient spiritual
tradition.

The EMCDDA noted that "teonanacatl" had gained particular popularity among
users, indicating a consumer preference for terms that connect modern mushroom
use to its historical and cultural roots. This phenomenon parallels the
adoption of other indigenous terms (such as "ayahuasca") by Western
psychoactive substance communities.

## Impact of the "Natural" Marketing Frame

The positioning of magic mushrooms within the "natural" and "herbal" product
categories had significant implications for consumer perception and market
dynamics. By framing mushrooms as "natural" products, smartshops were able to
market them to consumers who might avoid "drugs" but were open to "herbal"
products. This marketing frame contributed to the expansion of the mushroom
market beyond traditional drug-using communities.

The EMCDDA contextualized this within the broader "return to nature" trend of
the late 1990s, which saw increased consumer interest in organic products,
natural remedies, and plant-based psychoactive substances. Hallucinogenic
mushrooms, with their long history of natural use in indigenous cultures, were
well-positioned to benefit from this trend.

## Legal Implications of Brand Names

The evolution of brand names and marketing language was closely tied to the
changing legal landscape. As various European countries moved to restrict or
prohibit the sale of hallucinogenic mushrooms, retailers adapted their product
names and marketing strategies to navigate legal frameworks. The use of terms
like "Herbal highs" and "Philosopher's Stone" can be seen partly as attempts
to distance mushroom products from drug legislation by framing them as
something other than "drugs."
