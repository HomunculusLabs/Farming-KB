# Mycorrhizal Biocontrol of Phytophthora Through Root Exudate Modification

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF) can protect host plants against soilborne pathogens including Phytophthora species through multiple mechanisms. A key mechanism involves modification of root exudation patterns, which alters the chemotactic attraction of pathogen zoospores to roots, changes the composition of the rhizosphere microbial community, and primes plant defense responses. The interaction between AMF, root exudates, and Phytophthora nicotianae in tomato has been extensively studied as a model system.

## Phytophthora Zoospore Chemotaxis

Phytophthora species produce motile zoospores that swim through soil solution towards host roots, guided by chemical gradients. Zoospores are attracted to root exudates containing sugars (sucrose, dextrose, fructose, rhamnose, maltose), amino acids (asparagine, glutamine), and other compounds. The subapical regions of roots, where most exudates are released, are the primary targets for zoospore attraction. Chemotaxis depends on pH, experimental conditions, and the degree of host specificity: more host-specific species show attraction to more specific molecules.

## AMF-Mediated Changes in Root Exudation

Mycorrhizal colonization significantly alters the quantity and quality of root exudates. Among 27 sugars, amino acids, and organic acids quantified in root exudates from mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal tomato roots, proline and isocitric acid concentrations differed significantly. Proline accumulates in mycorrhizal roots and may play a role in reducing zoospore attraction. Secondary metabolites including blumenin, mycorradicin, and nicoblumin accumulate in mycorrhizal roots. Carotenoid-derived isoprenoids and flavonoids such as acacetin and rhamnetin are also produced in response to mycorrhizal colonization.

## Age-Dependent Exudate Effects

The effect of mycorrhizal root exudates on zoospore chemotaxis depends on the developmental stage of the mycorrhiza. Exudates from young, actively growing mycorrhizal roots attract more zoospores than exudates from non-mycorrhizal roots, possibly due to higher amounts of attractive molecules released during early colonization. Conversely, exudates from mature mycorrhizal roots attract significantly fewer zoospores than water or exudates from non-mycorrhizal roots, suggesting accumulation of repulsive compounds with mycorrhizal maturation.

## Demonstrated Biocontrol Effects

Colonization of tomato with Glomus mosseae (BEG 12) or Glomus intraradices (DAOM 181,602) significantly protects against subsequent Phytophthora nicotianae infection. Measured effects include reduced numbers of infection loci, lower levels of root necroses, decreased intraradical pathogen development, and reduced biomass loss compared to non-mycorrhizal plants. The biocontrol effect is systemic: protection occurs not only in mycorrhizal root tissues but also in non-colonized parts of the root system.

## Induced Defense Responses

Mycorrhizal colonization induces defense-related responses in tomato plants. Constitutive synthesis of beta-1,3-glucanase and new isoforms of beta-1,3-glucanase and chitinase have been detected in mycorrhizal roots. Induced systemic resistance (ISR) is evidenced by accumulation of PR-1 proteins and cell wall thickenings rich in pectin in non-mycorrhizal root tissues of colonized plants. Jasmonic acid accumulates in cortical cells containing arbuscules, which may regulate both the symbiosis and defense against pathogens.

## Rhizosphere Microbial Community Effects

Mycorrhizal colonization modifies the rhizosphere microbial community, which contributes to pathogen suppression. Changes in root exudation patterns select for different bacterial and fungal populations in the rhizosphere. The mycorrhizosphere (zone influenced by mycorrhizal hyphae) harbors distinct microbial communities compared to the bulk soil or non-mycorrhizal rhizosphere. These microorganisms may contribute to biocontrol through competition, antibiosis, or additional induction of plant defense responses.

## Compartmented Experimental Systems

Two-compartment experimental systems have been designed to separate the effects of root exudate modification from other biocontrol mechanisms. Using in vitro transformed root cultures, exudates from mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal roots can be collected without interference from soil microorganisms. These systems demonstrate that changes in exudation alone are sufficient to significantly affect zoospore behavior, confirming root exudate modification as an independent biocontrol mechanism.

## Synergistic Mechanisms

AMF-mediated biocontrol operates through multiple synergistic mechanisms: physical barriers created by the fungal sheath around roots, altered root exudation reducing pathogen attraction, induced systemic resistance priming plant defenses, competition for root colonization sites, and modification of the rhizosphere microbial community. No single mechanism fully explains the observed biocontrol effects, and the relative importance of each mechanism may vary with the AMF species, host plant, pathogen, and environmental conditions.
