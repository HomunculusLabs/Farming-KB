# Mushroom Bioremediation and Mycofiltration

## Overview
Fungi possess remarkable abilities to degrade and transform
environmental pollutants, capabilities that are increasingly being
harnessed for environmental cleanup. Bioremediation using fungi,
sometimes called mycoremediation, exploits the powerful
extracellular enzymes that wood-decomposing fungi produce to break
down lignin — the complex polymer that gives wood its structural
rigidity. These same enzymes, including lignin peroxidases,
manganese peroxidases, and laccases, can also degrade a wide range
of anthropogenic pollutants including petroleum hydrocarbons,
polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), pentachlorophenol (PCP),
pesticides, and even some explosives. Mycofiltration, a related
concept, uses the dense [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]] and
species of Pleurotus) are the most extensively studied organisms for

radicals) to depolymerize cellulose and modify lignin. These
hydroxyl radicals are among the most powerful oxidants found in
biological systems and can also degrade many synthetic pollutants.
The combination of enzymatic and non-enzymatic oxidative mechanisms
gives fungi a uniquely broad capacity for pollutant degradation.

## Applications of Mycoremediation

Fungal bioremediation has been applied or proposed for a variety of
environmental contamination scenarios. Petroleum-contaminated soils
can be treated by inoculating polluted sites with lignin-degrading
fungi that break down complex hydrocarbons into simpler, less toxic
compounds. Paul Stamets has demonstrated that oyster mushroom
mycelium can colonize petroleum-contaminated soil and stimulate the
growth of other decomposer organisms, leading to a cascading
ecological recovery. After mushroom inoculation, contaminated sites
that were previously barren have been observed to support plant
growth, insect activity, and eventually bird colonization — a
sequence Stamets describes as [[fungal-industrial-wastewater-treatment]].
