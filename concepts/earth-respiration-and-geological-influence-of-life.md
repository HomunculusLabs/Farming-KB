# Earth Respiration and the Geological Influence of Life

The concept of earth respiration describes the continuous exchange of gases, fluids, and dissolved materials between the soil, atmosphere, and hydrosphere. This is not a metaphor — it is a literal, measurable process driven by biological activity, weather patterns, tidal forces, and geological processes. Understanding earth respiration provides a framework for comprehending how soil health, [[earthworms]] to prairie dogs, continuously move material from deeper layers to the surface and vice versa.

### The Hydraulic Effect of Forests

The hydraulic weight of great forests, such as those that once covered the Americas, would have exceeded any water catchment weight that modern engineering can now afford to build. These forests dispersed this weight over far greater areas, moderating climate and with it geological processes including erosion, sediment transport, and weathering rates.

## The Consequences of Life Destruction

It is possible — and observable — in Iran, Greece, North Africa, the USA, Mexico, Pakistan, and Australia to see how the destruction of biological systems has brought the hard bones of the earth to the surface. By stripping the living skin from landscapes for ephemeral uses, humans have exposed raw geological substrates that were previously protected by vegetation and soil.

Mollison describes this process poignantly: poor goatherds wander where lake-forests once stood and forest deities were worshipped. Religions of resignation and fanaticism follow those of the nature gods, and human-built temples replace trees and tree spirits. This cultural and spiritual transition parallels and reflects the physical degradation of the landscape.

If the destruction persists, the ultimate outcome is a moon-landscape — a surface stripped of soil, vegetation, and the biological processes that create and maintain the thin living layer that makes complex life possible on Earth's surface.

## Life as an Erosion Moderator

Life moderates every erosion process, every river basin, every cliff and rock fall. It shapes and reshapes earth in a thousand ways. Vegetation intercepts rainfall energy, roots bind soil particles, biological crusts stabilize surfaces, and organic matter maintains soil structure. When these biological protections are removed, erosion accelerates dramatically.

## Soil-Structure Interaction

### Soils in House Foundations

The practical implications of soil behavior extend beyond agriculture into the built environment. Soils cause approximately 60–80% of all house cracks and insurance claims for faulty construction and "tree damage." About 20% of the soils that buildings are constructed on will subside or heave depending on water content.

Black cracking clay, surface clays, and red-brown clay loams are particularly subject to swelling and shrinking cycles. Solid stone and brick houses are most vulnerable to structural failure from soil movement, while wood-frame and veneer construction is less susceptible (by approximately 50%).

Over-irrigation of gardens, which raises the water table, is a primary cause of soil swelling beneath foundations. The removal of trees exacerbates this process, as does paving (which concentrates water) and burst or leaking sewage and water pipes. Some notorious white or yellow clays collapse as dam walls when wetted.

### Foundation Design Considerations

While the effects of soil movement are most noticeable to 2 meters depth, probes to 10 meters need to be monitored for groundwater levels. Soils subside and shrink with excessive drying (too many trees too near the house) and swell and heave with excessive watering and no trees. In areas with highly reactive clay soils, such as Adelaide, Australia, large buildings may need to be built on foundations capping deep piles sunk 20 meters to bedrock or deep into the permanent water table.

### Tree Placement Near Buildings

Most Australian native trees have efficient water removal via roots, with eucalypts removing 2–3 times the water of pines or pasture within a 10-meter radius. As a general guideline, householders should keep large trees at least one half the mature height from the house when building on high clay-fraction soils. Sands and sand-loams are usually stable, as are weathered or fractured rock and sandstones.

### Mound Development Under Houses

The interaction between buildings, soils, and biological activity creates specific patterns. Stilt-plus-beam construction helps stability by allowing air circulation beneath the structure and reducing direct soil contact. Understanding these interactions is essential for durable construction in areas with reactive soils.

## Implications for Permaculture Design

The understanding of earth respiration and the geological influence of life leads to several key design principles:

1. **Maintain and enhance biological activity** in all soil management, as this supports the essential gas exchange and fluid transport processes of the earth
2. **Preserve and extend vegetation cover** as the primary mechanism for erosion control and geological moderation
3. **Work with biological processes** rather than against them — using root penetration, burrowing organisms, and microbial activity rather than mechanical force wherever possible
4. **Consider the long-term geological implications** of design decisions, recognizing that biological systems operate on timescales of centuries and millennia
5. **Design for soil breathability** in all earthworks and construction, ensuring that compaction, sealing, and impermeable surfaces are minimized

The fundamental insight is that the earth is a living system whose respiratory and geological functions depend on the continued health and diversity of biological communities. Any design that supports these communities supports the long-term productivity and stability of the landscape as a whole.
