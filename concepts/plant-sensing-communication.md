# Plant Sensing and Communication

Plants perceive and respond to their environment through sophisticated sensory systems that, while fundamentally different from animal senses, achieve many of the same functions. Research by Chamovitz and others reveals that plants detect light (color, direction, duration), chemical signals (volatile organic compounds), mechanical stimuli (touch, vibration), gravity, and moisture -- and use this information to coordinate growth, defense, and reproduction.

## What a Plant Sees

### Photoreceptors

Plants do not form images, but they possess multiple families of photoreceptors that detect light quality, quantity, direction, and duration:

- **Phytochromes**: Detect red and far-red light (660nm and 730nm). Exist in two interconvertible forms: Pr (absorbs red) and Pfr (absorbs far-red). Red light converts Pr to Pfr ("active" form); far-red light converts Pfr back to Pr. This is a biological light-activated switch. In nature, the last light at dusk is far-red, signaling the plant to "turn off"; morning red light "wakes it up." Phytochrome measures the length of continuous darkness to regulate flowering (photoperiodism).

- **Phototropins**: Detect blue light (450nm). Responsible for phototropism (bending toward light). Darwin and his son proved in 1880 that the "eye" of the plant for phototropism is in the shoot tip, which senses light direction and signals the stem to bend. Also controls stomatal opening, chloroplast movement within cells, and leaf expansion.

- **Cryptochromes**: Detect blue and UV-A light. Regulate circadian rhythms, de-etiolation, and flowering time. Mutants lacking cryptochrome cannot maintain proper day/night cycles.

- **UV-B receptors (UVR8)**: Detect ultraviolet-B radiation. Trigger production of protective pigments (flavonoids, anthocyanins) and DNA repair enzymes.

### Photoperiodism

Plants measure the length of continuous darkness (not day length) to determine when to flower. Discovered by Garner and Allard (1918) with Maryland Mammoth tobacco:

- **Short-day plants**: Flower when nights exceed a critical length (chrysanthemums, soybeans, cannabis). A brief flash of red light in the middle of the night prevents flowering -- the plant "resets" its darkness counter.
- **Long-day plants**: Flower when nights are shorter than a critical length (irises, barley, spinach). A flash of red light in the night induces flowering.
- **Day-neutral plants**: Flower regardless of photoperiod (tomatoes, rice).

Practical application: Greenhouse growers manipulate photoperiod to control flowering. Cannabis is a short-day plant -- switching to 12/12 light cycle triggers flowering. See [[cannabis-lighting-guide]].

### Shade Avoidance

Plants detect the ratio of red to far-red light. Chlorophyll absorbs red light but not far-red. When a plant is shaded by neighbors, the reflected light is enriched in far-red. A low red:far-red ratio triggers stem elongation, leaf repositioning, and accelerated flowering -- the plant is "reaching" for light.

## What a Plant Smells

### Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs)

Plants produce and detect a wide array of volatile chemical signals:

- **Ethylene**: The gaseous plant hormone. Regulates fruit ripening (one rotting apple produces ethylene that ripens nearby fruit), leaf abscission, flower senescence, and stress responses.
- **Methyl jasmonate**: Airborne defense signal. When one plant is attacked by herbivores, it releases methyl jasmonate that neighboring plants detect, priming their own defenses before attack arrives.
- **Methyl salicylate**: Another airborne defense signal, acts systemically within and between plants.
- **Green leaf volatiles (GLVs)**: Released immediately upon tissue damage from herbivory. The characteristic "fresh cut grass" smell is actually a plant distress signal.

### Plant-to-Plant Communication

- Damaged plants release VOCs that undamaged neighbors detect and respond to by upregulating defense genes (production of tannins, alkaloids, protease inhibitors)
- Parasitic plants like dodder (Cuscuta) detect volatile cues from host plants to locate and grow toward preferred hosts
- Root exudates carry chemical information -- plants can detect nearby root systems of competitors vs. kin, and adjust growth accordingly (kin recognition)

### Interspecies Signaling

- Some plants release VOCs that attract predators of the herbivores attacking them -- an indirect defense (e.g., corn releases volatiles that attract parasitic wasps when attacked by caterpillars)
- Mycorrhizal networks ([[mycorrhizal-networks]]) serve as underground communication channels, transferring chemical signals and nutrients between plants

## What a Plant Feels

### Thigmomorphogenesis

Plants detect and respond to mechanical stimulation. This is not "feeling" in the emotional sense but a sophisticated mechanosensory system:

- **Touch response**: Repeated touching or wind stimulation causes stems to grow shorter and thicker (thigmomorphogenesis). Houseplants that are regularly touched or brushed grow more compact. This is an adaptive response to mechanical stress.
- **Thigmonasty**: Rapid movement in response to touch. Mimosa pudica (sensitive plant) folds its leaves within seconds of being touched. Venus flytrap snaps shut when trigger hairs are touched twice within 20 seconds. These rapid movements are driven by changes in cell turgor pressure, not nerve impulses.
- **Gravitropism**: Plants detect gravity through statoliths (starch-filled organelles) in root cap cells that settle to the bottom of the cell, signaling which way is "down." Roots grow downward (positive gravitropism), shoots grow upward (negative gravitropism).

### Proprioception (How a Plant Knows Where It Is)

Plants sense their own posture and mechanical state:

- **Antigravitropic bending**: If a stem is displaced from vertical, statoliths shift, triggering differential growth that bends the stem back upright
- **Self-thinning**: Plants sense mechanical contact with neighbors and adjust growth patterns to avoid overcrowding
- **Root obstacle avoidance**: Roots grow around rocks and barriers by sensing physical contact and redirecting growth

## What a Plant Hears

### Vibration Response

Research on plant responses to acoustic vibration is emerging:

- **Vibration detection**: Plants can sense vibration through mechanoreceptor cells. Research has shown that roots grow toward water sources by detecting the acoustic properties of water movement in pipes -- even in the absence of moisture gradients
- **Predator detection**: Some studies suggest plants can detect the acoustic signatures of herbivore feeding vibrations and respond with defensive chemistry
- **Wind response**: Plants detect wind through mechanosensory systems, triggering thigmomorphogenic responses (shorter, thicker stems)

Note: This is the least understood area of plant sensing. Much research is preliminary and results should be interpreted cautiously.

## What a Plant Remembers

### Priming and Epigenetic Memory

Plants can "remember" past experiences through epigenetic mechanisms:

- **Stress priming**: A plant that survives drought, herbivory, or pathogen attack often responds more quickly and strongly to subsequent stresses. This "primed" state can persist for weeks to months and sometimes across generations.
- **Vernalization**: Some plants require a period of cold exposure to flower. The "memory" of winter is stored epigenetically through histone modifications at flowering genes (FLC locus in Arabidopsis). This memory is reset each generation.
- **Light memory**: Phytochrome-based "memory" of the last red/far-red light state persists through the night. The plant essentially remembers whether the last light it saw was red or far-red.

## Implications for Growing

Understanding plant sensing directly informs cultivation practices:

- **Light spectrum matters**: Using the right wavelengths for each growth stage (blue for vegetative structure, red for flowering) leverages plants' photoreceptor systems. See [[cannabis-lighting-guide]]
- **Mechanical stress**: Gentle air movement (fans) produces stronger, sturdier stems via thigmomorphogenesis
- **Companion planting VOCs**: Interplanting aromatic herbs may prime neighboring crops' defenses
- **Mycorrhizal networks**: Building healthy soil mycorrhizae creates communication channels between plants. See [[mycorrhizal-networks]]
- **Seed treatment**: Cold stratification and light exposure during germination use plants' sensory systems to break dormancy. See [[seed-starting-reference]]
