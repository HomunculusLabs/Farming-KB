## Overview

[[pyridoxal-phosphate]], usually abbreviated PLP, is the active aldehyde-phosphate coenzyme form of vitamin B6.

It is best known for amino acid chemistry, where it helps enzymes move, remove, or transform groups attached to the alpha carbon.

PLP-dependent enzymes participate in transamination, decarboxylation, racemization, elimination, replacement, and one-carbon reactions.

Because amino acids sit at the intersection of protein synthesis, neurotransmitter production, nitrogen disposal, and energy metabolism, PLP biology is unusually broad.

A useful shorthand is that PLP acts as an electron sink for reactions that would otherwise form unstable carbanion-like intermediates.

## Structure and Binding
PLP contains a substituted pyridine ring, an aldehyde group, and a phosphate ester.

The phosphate group anchors the coenzyme through ionic and hydrogen-bonding interactions in the enzyme active site.

The pyridine ring stacks against aromatic residues and can be protonated to increase electron withdrawal.

The aldehyde forms a Schiff base with an active-site lysine in the resting enzyme.

This enzyme-bound imine is called the internal aldimine.

Substrate entry usually replaces the lysine linkage with a new imine between PLP and the amino acid substrate.

That substrate-bound imine is the external aldimine and is the branching point for most PLP reaction types.

## Electron Sink Mechanism
PLP stabilizes negative charge by delocalizing electrons from the substrate into the conjugated imine-pyridine system.

When the enzyme removes a proton or breaks a bond at the amino acid alpha carbon, the developing carbanion character is distributed through PLP.

The quinonoid intermediate often appears in mechanistic schemes as a resonance-stabilized species.

The direction of chemistry depends on which bond around the substrate alpha carbon is aligned perpendicular to the PLP ring.

This stereoelectronic rule is known as the Dunathan hypothesis.

By choosing substrate orientation, the enzyme decides whether the reaction becomes transamination, decarboxylation, racemization, or elimination.

PLP therefore supplies general chemical capability, while the protein supplies reaction specificity.

## Transamination
Aminotransferases use PLP to transfer an amino group from an amino acid to an alpha-keto acid.

In the first half-reaction, the amino acid donates its amino group to PLP, converting PLP into [[pyridoxamine-phosphate]], or PMP.

The carbon skeleton of the amino acid leaves as a corresponding keto acid.

In the second half-reaction, PMP donates the amino group to another keto acid, regenerating PLP.

Alanine aminotransferase links alanine and pyruvate, while aspartate aminotransferase links aspartate and oxaloacetate.

These reactions are reversible and respond to substrate concentrations, which makes them central to nitrogen redistribution.

Clinically, serum ALT and AST are biomarkers of hepatocellular injury and tissue damage.

## Decarboxylation and Neurochemistry

PLP-dependent decarboxylases convert amino acids into biogenic amines by removing carbon dioxide.

Glutamate decarboxylase makes gamma-aminobutyric acid, a major inhibitory neurotransmitter.

Aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase participates in dopamine and serotonin biosynthesis.

Histidine decarboxylase makes histamine, which connects PLP chemistry to immune signaling and gastric physiology.

Vitamin B6 deficiency can therefore produce neurological symptoms, including seizures in severe cases.

Drug interactions that impair PLP availability can also affect neurotransmitter metabolism.

## Elimination and Replacement Reactions

PLP enzymes can remove groups from beta or gamma positions of amino acid substrates.

Cystathionine beta-synthase and cystathionine gamma-lyase are important in sulfur amino acid metabolism.

Serine dehydratase and threonine dehydratase illustrate dehydration chemistry that produces keto acid products.

Some PLP enzymes catalyze replacement reactions in which one substituent leaves and another nucleophile enters.

This chemistry underlies parts of cysteine, methionine, and bacterial cell-wall precursor metabolism.

## Biosynthesis and Vitamin B6 Forms

Humans obtain vitamin B6 from diet as pyridoxine, pyridoxal, pyridoxamine, and their phosphate forms.

Cellular enzymes interconvert these vitamers and generate PLP by phosphorylation and oxidation steps.

Many bacteria, fungi, and plants synthesize PLP de novo through pathways not present in humans.

That difference makes some PLP biosynthetic enzymes potential antimicrobial or herbicide targets.

PLP homeostasis is controlled because free aldehyde chemistry can be reactive outside enzyme active sites.

Transport, dephosphorylation, albumin binding, and tissue uptake all shape vitamin B6 status.

## Pharmacology and Inhibition

Several drugs interact with PLP metabolism or PLP-dependent enzymes.

Isoniazid can form hydrazone adducts with PLP and is associated with functional vitamin B6 deficiency unless pyridoxine is supplemented.

Levodopa therapy can be affected by peripheral aromatic amino acid decarboxylase, a PLP-dependent enzyme targeted by carbidopa or benserazide.

Vigabatrin is an antiepileptic that irreversibly inhibits GABA transaminase, raising brain GABA levels.

Eflornithine inhibits ornithine decarboxylase and shows how PLP [[enzyme-inhibition]] can be therapeutically useful.

Because PLP enzymes are widespread, selectivity is a major medicinal chemistry challenge.

Reactive inhibitors must distinguish one active site from many enzymes that use similar aldimine chemistry.

## Toxicology and Deficiency

Vitamin B6 deficiency affects amino acid metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, heme synthesis, and immune function.

Neurologic signs can include irritability, peripheral neuropathy, confusion, and seizures in severe deficiency.

Excess pyridoxine supplementation can also cause sensory neuropathy, so more vitamin is not always safer.

Toxicology depends on dose, duration, individual metabolism, and the distinction between dietary sufficiency and pharmacologic exposure.

Hydrazines, carbonyl-reactive compounds, and some drugs can sequester PLP or disrupt PLP-dependent pathways.

It connects to [[glycolysis-embden-meyerhof-parnas-pathway]] and the [[citric-acid-cycle-tca-krebs-cycle]] through amino acid carbon skeletons.

It also connects to pharmacology through drug-cofactor adducts, enzyme-targeted inhibitors, and biomarker interpretation.

The coenzyme is a prime example of how vitamins are converted into chemically specialized tools rather than serving only as nutritional labels.

For biochemistry, PLP is one of the clearest demonstrations that a single cofactor scaffold can support a large family of mechanistically related reactions.
