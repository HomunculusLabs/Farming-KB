# Plant Primary Nutrients and Mineral Sources

## Overview

Soil fertility in permaculture design depends on understanding the three primary macronutrients required by plants—phosphates, potash, and nitrogen—and the diverse natural sources from which these minerals can be obtained. Rather than relying on synthetic fertilizers, permaculture emphasizes closed-loop nutrient cycling, biological fixation, and locally-sourced mineral amendments to maintain soil health over the long term.

## Primary Nutrients for Plants

### Phosphorus (Phosphates)

Phosphates are among the most commonly deficient nutrients in heavily leached soils. Natural sources include:

- **Guano**: Bird manures from dry islands, concentrated by seed-eating birds. Marine guano, however, may contain elevated levels of cadmium and uranium absorbed by oceanic fish and shellfish in marine bird diets, making heavy use potentially polluting to soils.
- **Sedimentary rock phosphate**: Distributed in deposits containing 8–15% phosphorus in various combinations with oxygen or water (hydrated forms). Rock phosphate is very slow to release nutrients and may be completely absorbed onto leached clays and clay-loams, requiring amendment with super-acid phosphate added to compost.

Phosphates can also be **locally produced** where bird populations are plentiful and their manures are systematically collected and returned to gardens and fields.

### Potassium (Potash)

Potash occurs naturally in the mineral **kainite**, formed in areas of evaporated waters. Desert salts typically contain 20–25% potash. Large reserves also exist in common minerals such as **orthoclase**, a major constituent of granite.

Potash is readily obtained from burnt and rotted plant material or compost, making it one of the easier nutrients to recycle within a permaculture system through controlled burning or composting of green waste.

### Nitrogen

Nitrogen can be supplied through biological fixation rather than imported inputs:

- **Rhizobia-inoculated plants**: Water or land plants inoculated with rhizobial bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen. Species include *Acacia*, *Albizia*, and *Eleagnus*.
- **Aquatic fixation**: Algae and water plants such as *Scirpus* and *Azolla* fix significant quantities of nitrogen in aquatic systems.
- **Persistent root-zone release**: Even after nitrogen-fixing trees are cut, the root zone continues to release nitrogen for up to **6 years**. This makes pioneer legumes and nitrogenous trees effective cover crops that release nitrogen during their lifetimes and for years afterward.
- **Forest succession dynamics**: Legumes typically die out under closed forest canopy. Only a few larger leguminous trees (e.g., *Samanea*, *Acacia melanoxylon*) persist as forest trees in mixed forest systems.

## Sources of Minerals in Soils

### The Sea as a Mineral Reservoir

As soils are leached and made mineral-poor, those minerals become concentrated in the sea, in marine organisms, and in inland saltpans. Seaweeds, seagrasses, and fish residues have historically been core agricultural fertilizers. As seawater evaporates, minerals precipitate in a predictable sequence:

1. **Calcite and dolomite** — used for soil conditioning and pH adjustment
2. **Gypsum and anhydrite** — used for restoring soil crumb structure
3. **Rock salts** — crystallize next; deficiency is rare except in wet tropical uplands
4. **Potash, magnesium salts, and trace elements** — remain in the final concentrated liquid known as **bitterns**

**Bitterns** are cheap, easily transported to leached areas, and can be used diluted (100–500:1) as foliar sprays at 1–20 liters per hectare. They contain bromine and many early periodic table elements plus rare minerals, but are highly corrosive and require non-corrosive vessels (polyethylene pipes and drums) for storage and distribution. Their effects must be established by local field trials on specific crops.

### Rocks and Rock Dusts

Crushed and finely ground rocks provide slow-release mineral nutrition:

- **Granites** yield feldspars that provide potash or sodium salts
- **Limestone and dolomite** supply calcium and magnesium
- **Basalt dusts** are particularly helpful on leached tropical soils
- **Mineral deposits and ores** provide trace elements such as zinc, copper, cobalt, and molybdenum (needed in quantities as low as 5–7 kg per 100 hectares)

Field trials (Leeper, 1982) have established that cheap ores, finely ground, are as effective as more refined sulphates or oxides. Minerals can also be delivered to animals via salt licks, molasses, water supplements, or slow-release rumen pellets, and then cycled back to soils through manure.

**Caution**: Rock dust applied indiscriminately can do as much harm as good, potentially adding excessive, poisonous, or imbalanced micronutrients to soils. Always match mineral amendments to demonstrated local deficiencies.

## Distribution of Elements in the Soil Profile

The vertical distribution of nutrients through the soil profile (surface to approximately 2 meters deep) is governed primarily by **water movement**. Water acts as a universal solvent, enabling compounds to dissociate into ions and transporting them to various microsites throughout the soil. This movement operates in three dimensions:

- **Infiltration**: Water soaks in from the surface downward, carrying dissolved nutrients to depth
- **Capillary rise**: Water moves upward from the water table through capillary action
- **Lateral flow**: Water moves horizontally through the soil, distributing nutrients across the landscape

Understanding these vertical and lateral transport mechanisms is essential for designing nutrient-retentive landscapes—swales, contour planting, and groundcover systems all work to slow water movement and maximize the opportunity for nutrient retention within the root zone.

## Permaculture Design Principles for Nutrient Management

1. **Maximize biological nitrogen fixation** through strategic placement of leguminous trees and ground covers
2. **Close mineral loops** by returning all organic residues (plant, animal, human) to productive systems
3. **Use locally appropriate rock dusts** matched to demonstrated soil deficiencies
4. **Harvest and cycle marine resources** (seaweed, fish waste) where coastal access exists
5. **Design water flow** to retain rather than leach soluble nutrients from the root zone
6. **Use conservative application rates** of concentrated amendments like guano and bitterns to avoid heavy metal accumulation
