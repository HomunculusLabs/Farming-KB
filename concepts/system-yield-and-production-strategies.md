# System Yield and Production Strategies

Yield in [[permaculture]] extends far beyond simple crop output. It encompasses energy, health, social well-being, and the creative capacity of designers to multiply useful outputs from any system through intelligent intervention.

## Types of Yield

### Product Yield

The sum of primary and derived products available from, or surplus to, the system. Some are intrinsic (preceding design), while others are created by design intervention.

### Energy Yield

The sum of conserved, stored, and generated energy surplus to the system — both intrinsic and those created by design.

### Impalpable Yields

These are the less tangible but often most important yields: health and nutrition, security, and a satisfactory social context and lifestyle. The search for these invisible yields most often drives people to seek good design or take up life on the land. These invisible yields are understood in terms of values and ethics — they govern our concept of needs and set the limits of "enough."

## System Yield Defined

> **System yield** is the sum total of surplus energy produced by, stored, conserved, reused, or converted by the design. Energy is in surplus once the system itself has available all its needs for growth, reproduction, and maintenance.

This definition deliberately broadens the concept beyond the narrow views of traditional biology or agricultural economics. Biologists may define yield as a finite upper limit of surplus due to substrate conditions and available energy — a passive approach inapplicable to active design or management. Agricultural economists may regard a single product (peaches per hectare) as the yield — an approach that may itself be the true limit to yield.

A true accounting of yield considers both **upstream costs** (energy inputs) and **downstream costs** (health impacts, pollution, soil mineral loss). Product yield may create problems that cost more than it can replace.

## The Role of Life in Yield

Living things, including people, are the only effective intervening systems to capture resources on this planet and produce yield. The sum and capacity of life forms decide total system yield and surplus. Only life and flow can yield continually — coal and rock have a finite product. Thus, the energy stocks of any system are the flows and lives within it.

Technology depends on the continuation of life, not the opposite. On the moon, where life does not exist, only technology can intervene to obtain a yield. On Earth, life is the primary intervening strategy for capturing flow and producing yield.

## Yield as a Measure of Design Ability

> **Yield is not a fixed sum in any design system. It is the measure of the comprehension, understanding, and ability of the designers and managers of that design.**

Defined this way, yield has no known limits. There is always room for another plant, another cycle, another route, another arrangement, another technique or structure. We can continually shrink the area we need to survive. The critical yield strategy is in governing our own appetites.

Conversely, yield can be decreased by cultural and political limitations. Some politicians, developers, and religious dogmatists limit yield by disallowing certain products. Cultural taboos against eating snails, blackbirds, rabbits, or keeping certain animals in urban gardens are examples of self-imposed yield restrictions.

## Strategies That Create Yields

Permaculture organizes yield-creating strategies into several broad categories:

### Foundational Strategies

- **Creation of a niche in space** — providing a critical resource that enables new production
- **Rehabilitation and creation of soils** — building the foundation for all biological yield
- **Diversion of water and water recycling** — capturing and reusing the most limiting resource
- **Integration of structures and landscape** — buildings that produce, landscapes that shelter
- **Selection of low-maintenance cultivars and species** matched to the particular site
- **Investigation of other species for usable yields** — expanding the portfolio of useful organisms
- **Supplying key nutrients** through biological waste recycling (mulch, manure)
- **Assembly of beneficial and cooperative guilds** of plants and animals

### Organizational Strategies

- **Annidation** — nesting or stacking one thing within another (a vine in a tree, a bowl in a bowl)
- **Tessellation** — forming or arranging a mosaic of complementary parts
- **Innovative spatial geometry** — using edge effects and harmonics in design layouts

### Sequential Strategies

- **Routing of materials or energy** to their next best use
- **Zone, sector, slope, orientation, and site strategies**
- **Use of special patterns** suited to irrigation, crop systems, or energy conservation
- **Sequential annidation** — interplanting and intercropping
- **Increasing cyclic frequency** — more rapid turnover of productive cycles
- **Tessellation of cycles and successions** — as in browsing sequences

### Conservation Strategies

- **Routing of resources to next best use**
- **Recycling at the highest level** — extracting maximum value before discarding

### Cultural Strategies

- Removing cultural barriers to resource use
- Making unusual resources acceptable
- Expanding choices within a culture

### Productive Strategies

- Removing socio-legal impediments to resource use
- Creating effective structures to aid resource management
- Costing and adjusting systems for all energy inputs and outputs
- Cooperative endeavours, pooling of resources, sharing
- Financial recycling within the community
- Positive action to remove impeding systems
- Making harmonious connections between components and sub-systems

## The Permaculture Difference

The profound difference between [[permaculture-design]] and nature alone is that in permaculture we **actively intervene** to supply missing elements and guide system evolution. Rather than accepting natural limitations passively, the designer consciously adds elements, creates connections, and applies information to multiply yields beyond what unassisted natural processes would produce.

This approach is theoretically unlimited in its potential, as system yield results from the number of strategies applied, what connections are made, and what information is applied to a particular design.
