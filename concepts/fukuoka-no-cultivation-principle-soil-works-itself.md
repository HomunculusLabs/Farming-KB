## Plowing Ruins the Soil

The conventional wisdom holds that plowing loosens the soil, aerates it, increases nitrogen through nitrification, and introduces fertilizer deeper into the ground where roots can access it. These supposed benefits are taught in every agricultural school and practiced on virtually every conventional farm around the world. Yet Fukuoka argues that this entire framework rests on a narrow and self-referential set of conditions — it is true only because prior interventions have made it true.

The apparent effectiveness of plowing holds only for fields that have already been cleanly plowed, thoroughly weeded, and treated with applied fertilizer. In such fields, the soil has already been degraded by decades or centuries of prior intervention, and plowing temporarily compensates for the damage that previous plowing caused. It is a self-perpetuating cycle. But grassed fields and fields cultivated without [[jeavons-surface-cultivation-technique]] for the water and nutrients their shallow, distorted roots can reach. What begins as a convenience — moving a tree to a preferred location — becomes a permanent limitation on that tree"s capacity to sustain itself.

[[cervantes-fertilizer-application-strain-feeding]] and weeding halt the normal aggregation and enrichment of topsoil. Weeding removes the very plants whose roots would loosen and aerate the earth. Fertilizer replaces the organic processes that would naturally build humus and soil structure. Each intervention eliminates a natural function and replaces it with a human-dependent substitute that must be applied again and again.

Clearing land by pulling up trees and bushes robs the deeper soil of its primary source of humus. When the deep-rooted plants are removed, the cycle of root growth, microbial decomposition, and soil softening comes to a halt. The microorganisms that depended on those roots die off, and the soil begins to compact and harden from the surface downward. What was once living, porous earth becomes something closer to concrete — a manufactured substrate that can support plants only with constant artificial maintenance.

Each of these practices — transplanting, fertilizing, weeding, clearing — follows the same pattern: an intervention that appears beneficial in the short term creates a dependency that deepens over time.

Fukuoka draws the unavoidable conclusion:

> "These very actions are what make plowing and turning the soil necessary in the first place."

Man creates the problem through intervention, then labors endlessly to solve the very problem his intervention created. The plow is not a tool of improvement — it is a tool of compulsion, required only because the soil has been stripped of its natural capacity to care for itself.

> "There is no need to plow or improve a soil because nature has been working at it with its own methods for thousands of years."

Nature has had far more time and far greater intelligence at its disposal than any agricultural scientist. The soils of wild forests and grasslands are the product of millennia of iterative refinement by organisms whose collective understanding of soil chemistry and structure dwarfs anything humans have achieved through research.

> "Man has restrained the hand of nature and taken up the plow himself. But this is just man imitating nature."

And a poor imitation at that. The plow can turn the soil, but it cannot replicate the complex, multi-layered processes by which roots channel air and water to depth, microorganisms decompose organic matter into stable humus, earthworms create persistent burrows that endure for generations, and countless other organisms cooperate to maintain soil structure and fertility.

Human cultivation is a crude mechanical gesture compared to the living, self-organizing systems it displaces. It addresses one variable — physical loosening — while disrupting dozens of interconnected biological processes that no machine can restore.

> "No amount of research can teach man everything there is to know about the soil, and he will certainly never create soils more perfect than those of nature."

This is not anti-science — it is humility in the face of systems far more complex than our current understanding. Fukuoka does not deny the value of understanding soil mechanics or [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] — no cultivation — is not merely a technique. It is an act of trust in the intelligence of the earth itself.
