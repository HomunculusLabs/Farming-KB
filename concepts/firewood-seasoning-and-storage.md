# Firewood Seasoning and Storage

Properly seasoned firewood is essential for efficient heating, reduced creosote buildup, and a cleaner burn. Green (freshly cut) wood can contain 50% or more moisture by weight and produces excessive smoke, low heat, and dangerous chimney deposits. This page covers wood species selection, BTU values, splitting techniques, stacking methods, seasoning timelines, and moisture measurement.

## Wood Species BTU Comparison

BTU (British Thermal Unit) values are measured per cord (128 cubic feet) of dried wood. Higher BTU means more heat output per volume.

**Hardwoods (preferred for heating):**
| Species | BTU per Cord (million) | Splitting Difficulty | Seasoning Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hickory | 27.7 | Hard | 12+ months |
| Oak (White) | 26.4 | Hard | 12+ months |
| Beech | 24.0 | Moderate | 12 months |
| Maple (Sugar) | 24.0 | Moderate | 12 months |
| Ash | 23.6 | Easy | 6-12 months |
| Birch (Yellow) | 21.8 | Moderate | 6-12 months |
| Cherry | 20.0 | Moderate | 6-12 months |
| Elm | 20.0 | Very hard | 12+ months |
| Black Walnut | 20.0 | Moderate | 12 months |

**Softwoods (good for kindling and quick fires):**
| Species | BTU per Cord (million) | Splitting Difficulty | Seasoning Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[douglas-fir]] | 21.3 | Moderate | 6-12 months |
| Pine (Ponderosa) | 16.2 | Easy | 6 months |
| Cedar | 12.8 | Easy | 6 months |
| Spruce | 14.5 | Easy | 6 months |

## Splitting Firewood

### When to Split

Split wood seasons 2-3 times faster than round wood because surface area is dramatically increased. Split as soon as possible after felling — within weeks at most.

### Splitting Techniques

**Maul and Wedge:**
- Use an 8-pound maul for most hardwoods
- Start with the maul, switch to wedges for stubborn pieces
- Strike with the grain, not across it
- Let the weight of the maul do the work

**Hydraulic Splitter:**
- Worth the investment if processing more than 5 cords per year
- 20-25 ton splitter handles most hardwoods
- 30+ ton for knotty elm or large oak

**Felling Axe vs. Splitting Maul:**
- Felling axes are too thin — they wedge in the wood
- Splitting mauls have a wider, heavier head designed to force wood apart
- Never use an axe to split on a block — it damages the handle

### Split Size

Aim for pieces 3-6 inches across the widest face. This size dries efficiently and fits most wood stoves. Oversized pieces take too long to season; undersized pieces burn too fast.

## Stacking Methods

### End-Stacked (Traditional)

The most common method. Wood is stacked in parallel rows with the ends visible.

**Best practices:**
- Stack on treated wood pallets or rails — never directly on dirt
- Stack in single rows with 2-3 inches of space between for airflow
- Cover only the top with a tarp — never wrap the sides
- Face the stack to maximize sun and wind exposure
- Stack away from buildings (minimum 30 feet for fire safety)
- Place the stack where prevailing winds blow through it

### Holz Hausen (Holzhaufen)

A German method of stacking wood in a circular, self-supporting mound. This method sheds water naturally and requires no top cover.

**Construction:**
- Start with a circular base 6-8 feet in diameter
- Stack the first course radially like spokes
- Each subsequent course leans slightly inward
- Build up to 6-10 feet high
- Top with bark or shingles to shed water
- Interior seasoning is enhanced by chimney effect airflow

### Criss-Cross Stacking

Alternate layers at 90 degrees for maximum stability and airflow. Ideal for shorter sections or windbreak locations.

## Seasoning Time

Wood must dry to below 20% moisture content for optimal burning. Target seasoning times vary:

- **Softwoods:** 6 months minimum, 12 months ideal
- **Hardwoods:** 12 months minimum, 18-24 months ideal
- **Oak and hickory:** 18-24 months for best results

**Factors that speed seasoning:**
- Splitting to small pieces (3-5 inches)
- Stack in full sun with wind exposure
- Elevate off the ground
- Top cover only — open sides for airflow
- Stack in a single row, not a heap
- Avoid stacking against walls or fences

**Factors that slow seasoning:**
- Large or unsplit pieces
- Shade or north-facing stacks
- Stacking in deep piles without airflow
- High humidity climate
- Covering the entire stack with a tarp

## Using a Moisture Meter

A pin-type moisture meter is the only reliable way to determine if wood is ready to burn. Relying on visual cues (cracks, weight) is inaccurate.

**How to use:**
- Push pins into the split face, not the bark side
- Take readings from multiple pieces in the stack
- Measure the freshly split face for the most accurate reading
- Target: 15-20% moisture content
- Above 25%: wood will hiss and sputter — not ready
- 20-25%: usable but not optimal
- Below 15%: excellent, but may burn too fast

**Cost:** $15-40 for a basic pin-type meter. Digital models with LCD readouts are easier to use.

## Storage for Seasoned Wood

Once wood is seasoned, store it:
- Under a covered shed or well-ventilated structure
- At least 30 feet from any building or structure
- Elevated off the ground
- Accessible in winter — plan your stacking location for snow access
- Separate from green/seasoning wood to avoid confusion

## Firewood Volume Measurements

- **Cord:** 128 cubic feet (4x4x8 feet stacked)
- **Face cord:** 4x8 feet, one-third the depth of a full cord (about 42 cubic feet)
- **Rick:** Regional term, usually refers to a face cord
