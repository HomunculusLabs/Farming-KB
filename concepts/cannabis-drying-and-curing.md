## Cannabis Drying and Curing Methods

Drying and curing are the final and arguably most critical stages of [[query-how-do-i-know-when-my-cannabis-is-ready-to-harvest]] for harvest.

Approximately 50% to 70% of the pistils (hairs) should have changed color from white to brown or orange.

The plant will have stopped producing new crystals and resin on the bud surfaces.

Fan leaves and lower leaves will have turned yellow and begun to drop off naturally.

The smell will have reached its peak intensity.

Bud mass will not have increased in the past several days, indicating that the plant has redirected energy away from floral production.

These indicators together signal that the plant has reached peak maturity and should be harvested promptly for best results. Growers should follow the breeder"s stated flowering times as a general guide, but visual assessment of the plant itself provides the most reliable [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]]. Fan leaves are removed with clippers, followed by secondary leaves, and finally the resin-covered trim is collected separately. This yields four different grades of material: fan leaves (lowest quality), secondary leaves, trim (high quality due to resin), and the premium bud.

For Sativa plants, which can grow up to 12 feet tall and carry 20 or more ounces of bud, the process is more labor-intensive. The plant is chopped at the base and spread onto a canvas for transport. Because of the plant's size and bushiness, branches may need to be cut and hung separately rather than hanging the whole plant. The same leaf and trim removal process applies to each branch.

It must be emphasized that light degrades THC quantities. The drying room does not need to be as light-proof as the grow room, but harvested material should never be exposed to direct light. Any light exposure during the drying period will reduce final potency through photodegradation of cannabinoid acids.

## Drying Phase

After harvesting, plants are hung upside down and allowed to dry slowly over a period of two to three weeks. The ideal drying environment is cool (60-70 degrees Fahrenheit), dark, and well-ventilated with moderate humidity (45-55% relative humidity). Proper airflow is essential — stagnant air promotes mold growth, while excessive airflow can cause too-rapid drying.

The goal during drying is to remove moisture gradually. Drying too quickly (using ovens, microwaves, or excessive heat) locks chlorophyll in the plant tissue, resulting in harsh-tasting, green-smoking material that is inferior in every way to properly dried cannabis. The branches are ready for the curing phase when they snap easily between the fingers rather than bending, which typically takes two to three weeks.

Fan leaves, secondary leaves, and trim are dried on flat surfaces in the same environment, separated from the hanging branches. These materials typically dry faster than the dense buds and can be processed separately for smoking or hashish production. Trim in particular is valuable for hashish making due to its high resin content.

## Curing Phase

Curing is the process of slowly breaking down remaining chlorophyll and allowing residual moisture to redistribute evenly through the bud material. The most common curing technique is called "canning."

Once branches are brittle enough to snap, the bud is clipped from the stems and placed into airtight containers such as glass jars with removable lids.

The sealed containers are stored in a dark location at room temperature.

Every day, the lids are opened for several hours (six hours is recommended) to allow fresh air exchange and release any built-up moisture.

The buds should be gently shaken or rearranged every couple of days to ensure even curing and prevent moisture pockets from forming.

The canning process should continue for at least three to four weeks before the cured bud is sampled. Bud that is eight weeks into the curing process smokes extremely well and represents the pinnacle of cured quality. After approximately one year, some cannabinoid compounds begin to change composition and potency may decline. Fresh bud from eight weeks of curing is superior to aged material.

## Curing Chemistry

During curing, several important chemical transformations occur.

Chlorophyll and other plant pigments break down, eliminating the harsh "green" taste that characterizes improperly cured material.

Remaining sugars and starches are metabolized, smoothing the smoke considerably.

Cannabinoid acids continue their slow decarboxylation, converting THCA to active THC.
