## The Aquatic Edge Zonation

Mollison describes a detailed zonation of aquatic plants, each adapted to specific water depths and conditions:

- **Dry bank (above water table)**: Bamboos, willows, poplars -- trees that send feeder roots down to water but cannot tolerate waterlogged conditions. Bamboos "won't take sodden conditions" but send roots to the water table.

- **Wet bank (soaked soils)**: Fly-catching plants (Venus fly traps, pitcher plants, sundews), arrowheads, duck potatoes (Sagittaria, with 80 species across a wide climate range), wild rice.

- **Shallow water (0-18 inches)**: Water lilies, Chinese water chestnuts (grown as mud rushes), lotus species. Planting technique: wrap roots in clay balls with manure, weight with stone, drop into position.

- **Mid water (2-6 feet)**: Floating species including duckweed (Lemna), water hyacinth (in restricted locations to control pollution), and various algae-producing species.

- **Deep water (6-15 feet)**: At depths below 12 feet, algae production practically ceases. The main value of deep zones is providing refuge for fish during low-oxygen or temperature extremes.

## Water Storage as Production System

Mollison emphasizes that ponds should be designed as productive systems from the start. "While in most places, ponds are made for cattle and stock watering, we design the water storages in themselves as highly productive systems." Even a small pond -- six feet in diameter -- is "well worth having as a production pond."

The pond should have a sloping or stepped floor to allow complete draining, ideally draining into a second pond to create a dryland rotation cycle. After several years as a productive pond, the nutrient-rich mud supports three to four years of dryland crops without additional fertilization.

## Pond Management

Key management considerations include:

- **pH**: Ponds naturally maintain pH between 6 and 8, much less acidic than garden soils. Lime the base when constructing new ponds. "Good garden soil will go from pH 5 to 6.5. It is common to lime ponds."
- **Fertility management**: Use the Secchi disc test -- if a white disc disappears at about 2.5 feet in soft green water, the pond is well-manured. If visible at 5 feet, add chicken manure. If it blacks out at 1 foot, the pond is over-manured.
- **Species selection**: Introduce species carefully. Exclude spiral mollusks (they eat green plants). Include crawfish with restricted burrowing capacity. Don't let children introduce snails -- "they eat green plants, and they can wipe out a pond."
- **Pond life**: Include about 20 species of plants and small animals low on the trophic ladder: shrimps, yabbies, crayfish, catfish, edge plants, pond plants, and ducks.

## Gley Ponds

A traditional technique for creating watertight ponds in porous soils. The process involves laying organic material (leaves, manure, green matter) in layers, compacting it, and allowing it to decompose anaerobically. This creates an impermeable biological barrier. Mollison notes that "it is quite possible for a pond to grow its own gley" -- the process of a crop growing within the pond and rolling down creates the seal naturally. Once established, the gley "goes down in the soil quite a ways and perpetuates itself."

## Mussels: The Superior Phosphorus Fixer

Mollison gives special attention to freshwater mussels as "the superior phosphorus fixer" in aquatic systems. A single mussel filters about 200 gallons of water per day, drawing in living organisms and particles and burying them in the pond mud. This injects nitrogen and phosphorus into the benthic ecosystem. The mud surface has "fantastic ability to fix passing nutrients from the water." Mussels should be "harvested only modestly for chicken grit" to maintain the pond's nutrient cycling capacity.
