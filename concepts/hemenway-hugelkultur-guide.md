# Hugelkultur Guide Hugelkultur, or mound culture, is a traditional European gardening technique that uses rotting wood as a core component of raised beds. The method harnesses the remarkable water-holding capacity of decomposing wood to create self-fertilizing, low-maintenance garden beds that improve over time. ## Origins and Concept In the carefully tended forests of central Europe, no scrap of wood was ever wasted. Branches and brushy prunings were used in a gardening technique called hugelkultur. The technique was born from the observation that in nature, fallen trees and rotting logs serve as moisture reservoirs, nutrient banks, and nursery sites for new plants. ## How Wood Holds Water Rotting wood possesses a remarkable talent for holding water. Some naturalists theorize that roots and fungal mycelia may translocate water from these woody moisture caches to plants and fungi many feet away. The spongy, decomposing wood acts like a buried reservoir that slowly releases moisture to surrounding plant roots during dry periods. ## Classic Hugelkultur Mound Construction To create a traditional hugelkultur mound: 1. **Lay the foundation:** Pile up branches or brush a foot or two deep in a mound 4 to 8 feet long. Larger logs can form the base, with smaller branches layered on top. 2. **Compact the pile:** Stomp on the pile to compact it somewhat. This removes large air gaps and ensures the mound won't settle dramatically. 3. **Add compostables:** Toss compostable materials onto the pile, including grass clippings, sod, straw, leaves, and other organic matter. These materials fill gaps between the woody material and jumpstart decomposition. 4. **Sprinkle compost:** Add a layer of finished compost over the organic matter to introduce beneficial microorganisms. 5. **Top with soil:** Cover the mound with an inch or two of topsoil. This provides a seedbed for immediate planting. 6. **Plant:** Sow seeds or set out starts directly into the soil layer. The mound is now ready to grow. ## Inverted Hugelkultur (Buried Wood Swale) Permaculturist Tom Ward developed a variation well-suited to drier climates: digging trenches about 18 inches deep, tossing in woody trunks or rotten firewood, and backfilling with soil. He then plants blueberries on top, mimicking how blueberries often root on floating logs in ponds and bogs. The buried wood acts like a huge sponge sunk into the ground, soaking up and holding soil moisture that roots infiltrate during drought. ## Nitrogen Concerns Some people worry that decomposing wood will lock up nitrogen, making it unavailable to plants. As a precaution, some practitioners toss a nitrogen source into the swale or mound, such as green compost materials or a slow-release fertilizer. However, Hemenway suspects that the wood decomposes so slowly that very little nitrogen is bound up by the microbes gnawing at the logs, making this concern largely overstated. ## Benefits of Hugelkultur The advantages of hugelkultur beds are substantial: - **Water retention:** The decomposing wood holds large quantities of water, reducing or eliminating the need for irrigation - **Nutrient release:** As woody brush rots, it releases nutrients slowly over many years, reducing fertilization needs - **Temperature moderation:** The decomposing organic matter raises the bed temperature just enough to boost plant growth and extend the season - **Soil building:** Over time, the mound transforms into incredibly rich, deep topsoil teeming with microbial life - **Waste recycling:** Branches, prunings, brush, and waste wood that might otherwise be burned or sent to landfill are put to productive use ## Best Crops for Hugelkultur - **Potatoes** are particularly enthusiastic about hugelkultur. They can be started a month earlier than in garden beds because the warming effect of decomposition - **Squashes and melons** thrive on the mounds, with vines cascading down the sides - **Blueberries** do well on buried wood swales, as their shallow root systems benefit from consistent moisture - Nearly any plant will grow well on a buried wood swale once established ## Design Integration Hugelkultur integrates naturally with other permaculture techniques. The mounds can be placed on contour to function as swales, catching and infiltrating runoff. They pair well with hemenway-cover-crops-guide planted between mounds.
