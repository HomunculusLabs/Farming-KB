# PDC Yield Harvest and Seasonal Calendar
The principle of obtaining a yield is central to [[pdc-urban-permaculture-design-principles]]. Every system in nature is prolific in its bounty, and permaculture systems should produce useful things -- edible, medicinal, construction materials, beneficial microclimates, or any other type of useful output. A well-designed [[permaculture-mandala-garden-design]] produces all these different products at the same time. The Williams PDC course explores yield broadly, encompassing not just food but all useful outputs from a design, and emphasises the importance of planning harvests around seasonal calendars.

## Understanding Yield

### Beyond Food

In a permaculture sense, yield is not limited to edible crops. The principle of stacking of functions implies that you try to produce as many yields as possible from any one design element. A [[query-how-to-start-a-backyard-orchard]], for example, yields:
- Edible fruit for humans
- Oxygen and clean water through its biological processes
- Habitat for insects and animals
- Shade and microclimate modification
- Leaf litter for mulch and soil building
- Timber and pruning material
- Bee forage from blossoms
- Wind protection
- Aesthetic and psychological benefits
A chicken-keeping|chicken yields:
- Eggs and meat
- Manure for fertiliser
- Pest control through insect consumption
- Weed control through scratching and grazing
- Heat generation (body warmth in enclosed spaces)
- Entertainment and psychological benefit
- Education for children

### Yield as Feedback

Yield also serves as feedback. The quantity and quality of yields tell the designer whether the system is functioning well. Declining yields signal problems that need attention -- perhaps a nutrient deficiency, water stress, pest pressure, or design flaw. Increasing yields indicate that the system is maturing and improving.
Observing the effects that designs have on existing patterns on the site may be one of the best methods of obtaining feedback. If a design disrupts the patterns on the site in unexpected ways, that may very well be the feedback that the design is not compatible with the ecology of the site.

### Sustainable Yield Rates

The Williams course emphasises that systems should yield at a sustainable rate. This means harvesting no more than the system can replace. For wild harvesting (wildcrafting), the rate of harvest must be self-regulated to ensure the plant population is not depleted.
Understanding how much you can harvest without damaging the productive capacity of the system requires knowledge of:
- The growth rate and reproductive capacity of each harvested species
- The current population health and density
- The impact of harvest methods on future productivity
- The role of the harvested species in the broader ecosystem

## Harvest Calendar Planning

### The Seasonal Rhythm

Permaculture systems are designed around seasonal rhythms. In temperate climates, the year divides into distinct periods with different harvest opportunities. A [[beekeeping-seasonal-calendar]] exists for beekeeping operations.

### Spring (Equinox to Solstice)
- Asparagus, rhubarb, and early perennial vegetables
- Overwintered greens (kale, chard, spinach)
- Early berries (strawberries)
- Green garlic and spring onions
- Herb harvests begin (perennial herbs)
- Seed starting and nursery work
- Livestock birthing season begins
- Maple sap collection (late winter/early spring)

### Summer (Solstice to Equinox)
- Peak vegetable harvest: tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, squash, beans
- Soft fruit: berries, currants, gooseberries
- Stone fruit: cherries, plums, peaches, apricots
- Grain harvest begins
- Herb harvest at peak potency
- Maximum egg production from poultry
|- Peak honey flow from beekeeping-basics|beekeeping
|- Food preservation season begins in earnest, including [[lacto-fermentation-and-food-preservation-methods]] and drying

### Autumn (Equinox to Solstice)
- Root vegetable harvest: potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips
- Late fruit: apples, pears, grapes, late berries
- Nut harvest: walnuts, hazelnuts, chestnuts
|- Seed saving and selection via [[comparison-seed-saving-techniques-vs-tissue-culture-cloning]]
- Grain harvest completion
- Livestock processing
- Firewood collection and processing
- Final food preservation push (fermentation, drying, storage)

### Winter (Solstice to Equinox)
|- Stored food consumption from [[mollison-designers-cold-caves-root-cellar-storage]] and preserved stores
|- Winter greens under cover ([[cold-frame-construction]], [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-greenhouse-gardening]])
- Citrus harvest (in suitable climates)
- Planning and design work for next season
- Equipment maintenance and repair
- Skill development through study and workshops
- Community gatherings and celebrations

## Harvest Methods and Timing

### Determining Harvest Readiness

Different crops have different signs of harvest readiness:
- Colour change (fruits, vegetables)
- Size and firmness
- Ease of separation from the plant
- Seed maturity (for grains and seed saving)
- Sugar content (for sap collection and fruit)
- Weather conditions (dry weather for grain harvest)

### Post-Harvest Handling

How food is handled after harvest affects quality and storage life:
- Harvest in the cool of morning or evening when possible
- Handle gently to avoid bruising
- Cool harvested produce quickly
- Process or store within hours of harvest
- Remove field heat before storage
- Sort for quality -- damaged items should be processed immediately or composted

## Processing Calendar Integration

The harvest calendar must be integrated with the food preservation calendar. Peak harvest times require intensive processing activity. The Williams course context emphasises that any processing of farm products (like cheese or bread) should be done on site. This requires:
- Adequate processing facilities (kitchen, pantry, workshop)
- Preservation equipment and supplies
- Knowledge of multiple preservation methods
- Labour availability during peak times
- Storage capacity for preserved products

## Record-Keeping for Yield Improvement

Keeping records of yields is essential for improving the system over time:

### What to Record
- Planting dates and varieties
- Harvest dates and quantities
- Quality observations (flavour, size, disease incidence)
- Weather conditions during the growing season
- Inputs applied (water, compost, mulch)
- Pest and disease observations
- Consumer feedback and preferences

### Using Yield Data

Yield data informs future design decisions:
- Which varieties perform best in specific locations
- Which planting combinations produce the highest total yield
- Where there are gaps in the harvest calendar that need filling
- Which preservation methods work best for specific crops
- How yields change as the system matures
- Where labour bottlenecks occur during peak harvest times
