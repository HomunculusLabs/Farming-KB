# Mushroom Contamination Troubleshooting

Contamination is the primary cause of failure in mushroom cultivation. There are five primary sources (Stamets): the immediate external environment, the culture medium, the culturing equipment, the cultivator, and the spores/mycelium themselves. This page complements [[mushroom-sterile-technique]] with identification and recovery strategies.

## Common Contaminants

### Trichoderma (Forest Green Mold)
The most prevalent and aggressive contaminant. Appears as rapidly expanding white mycelium that turns vivid green as spores mature (T. harzianum is the most common species). Thrives in substrates that are too wet or insufficiently sterilized. Once sporulating, spores spread easily — early detection and immediate disposal are essential. Do not open contaminated jars indoors.

**Stamets detailed ID**: Cottony mold growing in circular colonies on casing soil or compost; grayish and diffuse at first, rapidly growing, soon forest green from spore production. On malt agar, aerial cottony brilliant forest green mycelium (contrast with Penicillium's appressed granular blue-green). Parasitized mushrooms have dry brownish blotches or sunken lesions on cap/stem, often enveloped by fine downy mildew becoming greenish. Spores are 3-5 x 3-4 microns, sticky (encased in mucus-like substance), in loose clusters (not linear chains like Aspergillus/Penicillium).

**Spread**: Primarily airborne (agar/grain), introduced through peat/humus on casing soils, spread during harvesting/bed cleaning/watering. Prefers acid pH 4-5.5. Spores utilized by red pigmy mites as food.

**Stamets control**: Careful picking; dispose of dead/diseased mushrooms; lower humidity; lower CO2; increase air circulation to eliminate dead air pockets. Use clean casing materials lacking undecomposed woody tissue. Since Trichoderma thrives in acid habitats, raise pH by covering colony with salt, sodium hypochlorite, or baking soda. Steam pasteurization at 160F for 1 hour kills spores. Treat in earliest stages before spore production to prevent satellite colonies. Sanitize all tools/worker contact. Trichoderma secretes toxins that inhibit primordia formation.

### Aspergillus (Green Mold)
Green, yellow, or black powdery colonies. Conidiophore swollen at apex in a bulb-like capitate shape. Multiple chains of lightly pigmented single-celled spores. Spores spread easily through air — discard immediately and clean the area.

### Penicillium (Blue-Green Mold)
Blue-green, powdery growth. Conidiophore diverges at apex into multiple chains of spores. Very common on agar plates. Indicates airborne contamination during pouring or transfer work.

### Dactylium (Cobweb Mold)
Fine, grayish-white, spiderweb-like mycelium draped over substrate or fruiting bodies. Distinguished from healthy mycelium by its wispy, aerial appearance. Grows faster than mushroom mycelium in high-humidity, stagnant-air conditions. Responds well to 3% hydrogen peroxide spray — mushroom mycelium has peroxidase enzymes that tolerate H₂O₂.

**Stamets detailed ID**: D. dendroides — cobweb-like, first appearing as small scattered patches rapidly running over casing soil surface, then overwhelming all mushrooms in its path. Initially grayish, sometimes whitish, becoming pinkish-tinged with age. Afflicted mushrooms covered with fluffy down of delicate mycelium; cut open, they are rotting flesh, young buttons reduced to formless masses of soft tissue. Conidia multicelled (3+ connected cells), clear or slightly yellowish, 20 x 5 microns.

**Spread**: Air; casing soil; water; insects. Spores germinate upon contact with a mushroom and envelope it with soft mildewy mycelium. Spores killed at 115-122F for only 30 min (Anderson 1956).

**Stamets control**: Immediate isolation of parasitized fruitbodies; lower relative humidity; increase air circulation. Examine casing soil components for hygienic quality. Pasteurization of casing soil generally prevents occurrence. Growth stopped by covering with salt, baking soda, or any highly alkaline compound. Dactylium is the conidial form of Hypomyces rosellus.

### Bacillus (Wet Spot / Sour Rot)
Slimy, foul-smelling patches — often pinkish, yellowish, or milky. Grain kernels appear matted with a wet, glossy sheen. Originates from unsterilized grain, contaminated water, or poor inoculation technique. Grain jars with wet spot should be discarded carefully.

### Mucor / Rhizopus (Pin Mold)
Fast-growing, coarse mold erupting vertically from substrate. "Pin cushion" appearance with spherical sporangia atop tall stalks. Grayish-white initially, turning dark gray or black as spores mature. Often appears on agar or excessively wet substrates.

### Pseudomonas (Bacterial Blotch)
Causes brown, sunken lesions on mushroom caps. Favored by high humidity and water pooling on cap surfaces. Reduce humidity and improve air exchange to control.

### Geotrichum (Lipstick Mold)
Mycelium whitish at first with a "frosty" appearance, forming whitish balls of mycelium. With age becomes pinkish then reddish from spore maturation. Older colonies fade to dull orange. Conidia cylindrical, 5-10 x 3-6 microns. Mesophilic thermophile -- sensitive to pasteurization temperatures.

**Spread**: Air; soil; old straw and spent composts.
**Stamets control**: Compost on concrete surface; thorough pasteurization of compost and straw; use clean casing materials. Inhibits primordia formation and development. Virtually eliminated from modern farms using concrete composting surfaces and peat-based casings. Closely allied to Sporendonema purpurescens.

### Doratomyces (Black Whisker Mold / Smoky Grey Mold)
Heavily sporulating grayish to blackish mold permeating compost; emits clouds of grayish spores when disrupted. Contaminated compost regions are darker and damper than surrounding material. Grows on inadequately pasteurized compost, wooden trays, and spent compost debris.

**Stamets control**: Air filtration; correct preparation and pasteurization of compost; strict hygiene schedule. Most common source is spent compost or newly turned soils. This is an **indicator mold** -- its presence suggests poor composting, pasteurization, or spawn running practices. Undercomposted, poorly pasteurized, and/or wet composts favor it. D. microsporus causes asthma-like respiratory response (coughing, sore throat, nosebleeds) from spore exposure.

## Prevention

- **Proper sterilization**: Grain jars 90+ min at 15 PSI; bulk substrates pasteurized 140–160°F for 60–90 min. See [[mushroom-substrates]].
- **Sterile technique**: Work in SAB or flow hood. Flame-sterilize tools between every transfer. See [[mushroom-sterile-technique]].
- **Filter integrity**: Proper gas-exchange filters (polyfill, micropore tape, filter discs) on all jars and bags.
- **Moisture control**: Substrate at field capacity — holds together when squeezed, releases no more than a drop or two. Overly wet substrates favor bacteria and Trichoderma.
- **Clean cultures**: Start from verified clean agar. Isolate clean sectors away from contamination before moving to grain.
- **Seasonal awareness**: Spore load increases dramatically in spring and summer (Stamets). Increase cleaning frequency during peak periods.
- **Clean workspace**: Remove contaminated cultures promptly. Wet-mop floors. Avoid working during cleaning activities that stir dust.

## Recovery Options

### Salt Treatment
Apply thin layer of non-iodized table salt directly to contaminated casing area. Suppresses mold growth as a delaying tactic while mycelium colonizes past the affected zone. Works best on surface contamination of casing layers.

### Hydrogen Peroxide (3%)
Mist directly onto cobweb mold — kills *Dactylium* on contact while mushroom mycelium tolerates H₂O₂ via peroxidase enzymes. Also useful for surface sterilization during cutting-away procedures.

### Cutting Away
For fully colonized substrates with small localized contamination: cut away affected area plus 1–2 inches of surrounding substrate. Perform outside the grow area. Seal exposed surface with salt or H₂O₂. A gamble — invisible spores may have already spread.

### Agar Sector Transfer
Transfer clean mycelium sector (as far from contaminant as possible) to fresh plate. Repeat 2–3 transfers to ensure purity before moving to grain spawn.

### Microwave Surface Sterilization
Research shows 2.45 GHz microwave irradiation effectively sterilizes surfaces contaminated with bacteria, yeasts, and molds within closed systems. Useful for sterilizing tools and small surfaces.

## When to Toss vs. Salvage

**Toss immediately**: Entire jar/bag contaminated, spores released in confined space, unidentified contaminant spreading rapidly, any doubt about safety for edible/medicinal grows.

**Attempt salvage**: Small surface spot on otherwise fully colonized substrate, mycelium significantly ahead of contaminant, cobweb mold (responds to H₂O₂), acceptable risk for learning project.

**Rule of thumb**: If more than 25% of substrate surface is contaminated, discard. Create conditions so favorable to mushroom mycelium that competitors cannot establish — achieved through proper preparation, not reactive treatments (Stamets).
