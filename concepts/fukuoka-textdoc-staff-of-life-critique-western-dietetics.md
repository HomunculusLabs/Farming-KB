## Overview

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] delivers a sustained critique of Western dietetics and
its foundational premise that health requires a scientifically calculated
balanced diet. He argues that taste and nutrition must be one, that the
analytical approach to food fragments what should be whole, and that the
four-group scoring method used at Japanese nutrition colleges exemplifies
everything wrong with modern [[fukuoka-critique-western-dietetics-nutritional-science]]. This page examines his
argument that the quest for nutritional completeness through science has
produced the opposite of its intended result.

## The Unity of Taste and Nutrition

Fukuoka opens by challenging the common separation of taste and nutrition.
Mothers tell children to eat food because it is "good" for them, even
if it does not taste good. This represents a reversal in human thinking,
the same as saying we nourish ourselves so we can work harder and live
longer. Fukuoka insists that what is nourishing and good for the human
body should stimulate the appetite of its own accord and serve as
delicious food. Flavor and nutrition must be one. He contrasts the simple
Eastern diet of [[cho-brown-rice-vinegar-preparation]] and vegetables, which provides everything the
body needs, with Western dietetics that teaches health cannot be
maintained without a full complement of nutrients.

## Three Fatal Flaws of Western Dietetics

Fukuoka identifies three fundamental problems with Western dietetics.
First, it lacks any awareness of man as a living, breathing creature.
Menus leave the impression that one is merely supplying energy to
mechanical humans cut off from the source of life. There is no attempt
to approach closer to a natural existence or conform to natural cycles.
Second, dietetics fails to account for man as a spiritual animal that
cannot be fully explained in organic, mechanical, and physiological
terms. The food that man eats is linked directly and indirectly with
human emotion, so a diet devoid of feeling is meaningless. Third,
Western dietetics operates within a narrow temporal and spatial framework
and cannot grasp things in their entirety.

## The Butterfly Dissection Metaphor

Fukuoka uses a vivid metaphor to illustrate the limits of analytical
science: man can dissect a butterfly and examine it in the greatest
detail, but he cannot make it fly. And even were this possible, he
cannot know the heart of the butterfly. Unmindful of the simple truth
that the whole is greater than the parts, [[fukuoka-road-critique-of-modern-science]] commits blunder
after blunder. No matter how the scientist may attempt to assemble a
full array of ingredients, this will never approach a complete diet. The
powers of the intellect will succeed only in creating an incomplete diet
far removed from nature.

## The Four-Group Scoring Method Analyzed

Fukuoka examines the four-group scoring method used at the Kagawa
Nutrition College in Japan as a case study. Group 1 includes milk and
eggs for complete nutrition, worth 3 points. Group 2 covers bluefish,
chicken, and tofu for building muscle and blood, worth 3 points. Group 3
provides light-colored and green-yellow vegetables, potato, and mandarin
oranges for vitamins, minerals, and fiber, worth 3 points. Group 4
includes white rice, bread, sugar, and oils for energy, worth 11 points.
Each point represents 80 calories, totaling 1,600 calories per day.
Eighty grams of beef equals one point, as do 500 grams of bean sprouts
or 200 grams of mandarin oranges.

The four-group system appears sensible, but Fukuoka reveals its hidden
consequences. A year-round supply of high-grade meat, eggs, milk, bread,
