# The Problem Is the Solution — Permaculture Design Maxim

"The problem is the solution" is one of the most widely recognised and distinctive maxims in permaculture. David Holmgren explores this idea extensively within Principle 1 (Observe and Interact), presenting it as a fundamental shift in perception that underlies much of permaculture's creative potential. Rather than seeing elements as inherently good or bad, the maxim encourages designers to reframe perceived problems as unrecognised resources or indicators of systemic change.

## Core Concept

The maxim expresses the idea that things are not always as they seem: elements we view negatively may have a positive aspect more important than, or at least compensating for, the predominant negative perception. In Holmgren's words: "An open, inquiring attitude to problems is almost always more fruitful than an urgent demand for solutions."

The demand for solutions is often driven by fear and unquestioned consensus about the nature of the problem. By pausing to observe more carefully, we may discover that the problem itself contains the seed of its resolution. This requires a fundamental shift from reactive problem-solving to creative pattern recognition.

## Weeds as the Primary Example

The most common applications of "the problem is the solution" relate to weeds and other lifeforms regarded as pests. Holmgren identifies three positive dimensions of weeds:

1. **Environmental indicators**: Weeds signal management changes that are needed. Their presence reveals soil conditions, nutrient imbalances, overgrazing patterns, or hydrological issues that might otherwise go unnoticed. Thistles indicate compacted soil; dock grows in wet clay; dandelions signal low calcium.

2. **Agents of repair**: Many weeds actively repair damaged soil. Deep-rooted pioneer species break up compacted subsoil, bring minerals to the surface, and begin the process of succession toward more complex plant communities. Blackberry thickets can stabilise eroding gullies while building soil organic matter.

3. **Undervalued resources**: Weeds represent biological productivity that is being wasted because economic or cultural prejudices prevent us from harvesting it. Holmgren argues that "pests can be viewed as the surpluses of nature, which need to be used rather than destroyed."

This idea of pests as surplus natural abundance has been a major theme in Holmgren's work over many years. In his Collected Writings, multiple articles expand on replacing negative responses to weeds with more positive approaches. Rather than the question "how do we get rid of this pest?", the permaculture approach asks "what need is this organism fulfilling, and how can we work with it?"

## Practical Applications

Holmgren provides concrete examples of reframing problems at his home site, Melliodora:

- **Gully floods**: Occasional flooding becomes a source of nutrients and sediment rather than a threat
- **Hot drying winds**: A nuisance becomes an opportunity to dry fruit and other produce
- **Blackberries**: A declared noxious weed serves as mulch, animal fodder, and a soil stabiliser
- **Snails**: Rather than an excess of snails, there is a deficiency of ducks — Bill Mollison's famous response to a gardener complaining about snail plagues

The slogan "the problem is the solution" helps recognise opportunities to harvest energy sources that are specific to each site and situation. Every sunny winter day charges a passive solar house so occupants stay warm overnight. These examples reflect the way ecosystems develop in response to the diverse and irregular energy availability across landscapes.

## The Geography of Solutions

Another dimension of the maxim is that the best solutions to problems can be found in places and cultures where the problem is most extreme. In these situations, co-evolution over time develops the most effective responses. In places where the design problem is less severe, people often ignore it or develop solutions that use more effort or resources than necessary.

Holmgren contrasts the Amalfi coast of southern Italy, where centuries of cultivation on precipitous terrain produced creative solutions to steep-land limitations, with Mediterranean countries generally, where the prevalence of permanent water sources reduced the stimulus to develop rainwater harvesting that was strong in Australia.

## Breaking Out of Design Cul-De-Sacs

"The problem is the solution" connects to a broader pattern Holmgren calls design cul-de-sacs. When we recognise the potential to turn problems into solutions, we encounter the inertia of fundamental beliefs, system architecture, and power structures that stand to lose by innovation.

In nature, proven solutions tend to become entrenched, while recent innovations are easily swept away by unfavourable conditions. In families, parents often teach children the lessons they learned in childhood rather than their own adult insights. In human culture, patterns of proven behaviour become entrenched through tradition and institutions. The more stressed parents are, the more this becomes true.

This conservatism is functional when rates of change are slow. Incremental small improvements are integrated where they can be implemented without altering fundamental structure. However, rapid change can make the "optimum solution" a dead end with no way forward and no going back. The story about the frog that fails to leap out of slowly heating water illustrates how incremental adjustment fails to escape the trap.

## Lateral Thinking and the Tunnel Through

Amory Lovins describes the need to "tunnel through the cost barriers" to gain large improvements in energy and resource efficiency, rather than fiddling with small improvements. Although common sense suggests this will be very costly, many examples in industrial design and manufacturing show the costs can be less than those produced by small incremental adjustments.

What is needed in these situations is the capacity to think laterally, readiness to abandon the proven and take risks, and the incentive to get to something better. Broad experience and observation of the world outside the particular situation can allow recognition of the patterns of design cul-de-sacs and the general nature of transformative solutions.

## The Maxim as Observational Tool

"The problem is the solution" is not just a design strategy but an observational discipline. It requires us to suspend judgment, look beyond first impressions, and ask what function an element is performing in the larger system. Ethics and ideology act as filters that determine what and how we see. These filters are unavoidable — in fact, essential — but the rush to judgment of right and wrong frequently clouds our observation and prevents understanding.

This commonly occurs in our attitude to pest plants and animals. A pest designation often reflects economic interests rather than ecological understanding. Holmgren notes that traditional Aboriginal people in central Australia see introduced animals like donkeys and rabbits as mwerranye (belonging to the land), while mass extermination programs are seen as immoral waste.

## Connection to Action Learning

The maxim is embedded in the action learning loop that Holmgren presents: observe, act, reflect, adjust. Failure is useful so long as we learn. When we try some action, we should not assume we were the reason for any success. We should conduct small trials and think about other possible causes for success or failure.

## Scepticism as a Design Tool

Closely related to "the problem is the solution" is the value of scepticism. Personal, political, and religious freedom provides a foundation for questioning current beliefs. Adaptation to energy descent depends on a dynamic balance of values and contextual knowledge rather than crusades of good over evil or simplistic universal solutions.

Nature is always providing myriad indicators, warning signs, and questions in response to actions driven by certainty and belief. The maxim reminds us to remain open to these signals rather than rushing to judgment.

## Connection to Other Principles

"The problem is the solution" resonates with several other Holmgren principles:

- **Observe and Interact**: Careful observation reveals the positive aspects of perceived problems
- **Use and Value Diversity**: Biodiversity provides the raw material for creative problem-solving
- **Creatively Use and Respond to Change**: Reframing problems is a form of creative response
- **Produce No Waste**: Transforming "waste" organisms into resources eliminates waste
- **Use Edges and Value the Marginal**: Solutions often come from the margins

## Application to Cultural and Social Problems

Beyond ecological design, "the problem is the solution" applies to social and cultural challenges. The breakdown of community institutions can be reframed as an opportunity to build new, more resilient social structures. The loss of corporate employment can be reframed as freedom to develop self-reliant livelihoods. Population decline in rural areas can be reframed as an opportunity for ecological restoration.

The key is maintaining the open, inquiring attitude that the maxim demands. This is harder when the problem directly affects our livelihood, identity, or sense of security. The maxim asks us to suspend our fear and our certainty long enough to see the larger pattern.

## Connection to Observe and Interact

"The problem is the solution" is embedded within Holmgren's first design principle, Observe and Interact. Good observation reveals aspects of reality that our preconceptions filter out. The maxim is the payoff of good observation: by looking carefully at what we initially perceive as a problem, we may discover resources, indicators, and functions that transform our understanding entirely.

Holmgren emphasises that observation is not passive. It involves active engagement with the system: touching the soil, watching the water flow, noting which plants thrive where, tracking seasonal patterns. This embodied knowledge cannot be obtained from books or plans. It is the foundation upon which creative reframing becomes possible.

## Relevance to Energy Descent

In an energy descent context, "the problem is the solution" becomes more than a design maxim — it becomes a survival strategy. As conventional supply chains break down and resources become scarce, the ability to see waste as resource, pest as food, and problem as opportunity will be essential. Those who have developed this perceptual skill through permaculture practice will be better prepared for the challenges of transition.

The maxim also provides psychological resilience. By reframing difficult circumstances as opportunities for creative response, it helps maintain agency and hope in the face of overwhelming systemic change. This is not naive optimism but a disciplined practice of observation and pattern recognition.

## The Designer as Scavenger

Holmgren extends the maxim to the broader task of cultural transition. In a low-energy future, most of today's technology and culture will end up in the dustbin of history; but some aspects of modernity may provide critical components of an energy descent culture. Permaculture principles provide one lens for helping to identify the useful pieces of modernity and combine them with those from nature and from pre-modern cultures in a new designed synthesis.

Holmgren suggests the image of a scavenger at the rubbish tip looking for gems among the possibilities — shocking to some, but one that many permaculturalists relate to. It is as if we are wandering about a landscape littered with the pieces of many different jigsaw puzzles. Our task is to pick up as many pieces as seem possibly useful and carry them to a place we do not yet know, where we must construct a new jigsaw puzzle from what we have.
