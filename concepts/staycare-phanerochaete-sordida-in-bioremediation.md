# Phanerochaete sordida in Bioremediation

P. sordida is a white rot basidiomycete fungus that has
demonstrated exceptional capabilities as a bioremediation agent,
particularly in field-scale applications for the treatment of wood
preservative contaminated soils. While [[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]] in Bioremediation has historically
received more laboratory attention, P. sordida has proven to be more
effective in practical field deployments for degrading pentachlorophenol
(PCP) and creosote components.

## Taxonomic Position and Ligninolytic System

P. sordida belongs to the same genus as P. chrysosporium but possesses
a distinct ligninolytic enzyme complement that differs in important ways.
This distinct enzymatic profile gives P. sordida unique advantages for
certain bioremediation applications compared to P. chrysosporium.
It produces both manganese
peroxidase (MnP) and laccase, placing it in the MnP-laccase enzyme
group as classified by Hatakka. This enzymatic profile differs from
P. chrysosporium, which is primarily known for its lignin peroxidase
(LiP) and MnP production. The presence of laccase in P. sordida may
contribute to its robust performance in soil environments where
conditions are less controlled than laboratory cultures.

## Field Trials for PCP Remediation

The most significant applications of P. sordida have been in field-scale
bioremediation of soils contaminated with pentachlorophenol (PCP), a
wood preservative widely used in telephone poles, fence posts, and
other timber products. PCP contamination is a persistent environmental
problem at wood treatment facilities and utility pole storage sites.

Field studies have demonstrated that P. sordida can be successfully
introduced into contaminated soil systems and can maintain degradative
activity over extended periods. Key findings from field deployments
include:

- Effective colonization of contaminated soil when inoculated with
  appropriate substrate amendments (typically lignocellulosic materials
  such as wood chips, corn cobs, or straw).
- Significant reductions in PCP concentrations within treatment
  periods ranging from weeks to months.
- Better survival and competitive ability in non-sterile soil
  environments compared to P. chrysosporium, which often struggles
  to establish in the presence of indigenous microorganisms.

## Creosote and Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbon Degradation

Beyond PCP, P. sordida has demonstrated the ability to degrade creosote
components, which include a complex mixture of polycyclic aromatic
hydrocarbons (PAHs), phenolic compounds, and heterocyclic aromatics.
Creosote-contaminated soils present a particular challenge because of
the diverse chemical structures present and their varying degrees of
recalcitrance.

P. sordida attacks creosote constituents through its ligninolytic
enzyme system, which operates through a non-specific free-radical
mechanism capable of oxidizing a broad spectrum of aromatic compounds.
The MnP-laccase complement of P. sordida generates diffusible oxidants
(manganese(III)-chelates and laccase-mediated radicals) that can
penetrate soil matrices and attack both low and high molecular weight
PAHs.

## Advantages Over P. chrysosporium

While P. chrysosporium is the most extensively studied white rot fungus
in laboratory settings, P. sordida offers several practical advantages
for field applications:

- **Environmental robustness**: P. sordida demonstrates better survival
  and activity in variable field conditions including fluctuating
  temperatures, moisture levels, and nutrient availability.
- **Competitive ability**: Unlike P. chrysosporium, which often fails
  to compete with indigenous soil microorganisms, P. sordida can
  establish and maintain populations in non-sterile environments.
- **Nutrient flexibility**: P. sordida shows effective ligninolytic
  activity across a wider range of nutrient conditions, including
  those that are less strictly nitrogen-limited than required by
  P. chrysosporium for optimal LiP production.
- **Laccase production**: The laccase enzyme produced by P. sordida
  provides an additional oxidative pathway for pollutant degradation
  and may contribute to its resilience in competitive environments.

## Dioxin and Furan Degradation

P. sordida has been shown to degrade mixtures of polychlorinated
dibenzo-p-dioxins (PCDDs) and polychlorinated dibenzofurans (PCDFs)
in liquid culture. In studies alongside P. chrysosporium, P. sordida
produced various metabolites including chlorocatechols from these
highly recalcitrant compounds. Notably, P. sordida showed no clear
structural dependence in its degradation of PCDDs and PCDFs, suggesting
a free-radical degradation mechanism typical of white rot fungi with
little substrate specificity. This broad-spectrum activity is
particularly valuable for treating mixtures of chlorinated contaminants
commonly found at industrial sites.

## Inoculation Strategies

Successful field deployment of P. sordida typically involves:

- Pre-culturing the fungus on lignocellulosic substrate (wood chips,
  sawdust, or corn cobs) to establish robust mycelial growth before
  soil application.
- Mixing inoculated substrate with contaminated soil at ratios
  optimized for the specific site conditions.
- Maintaining adequate moisture content (typically 40-60% of water
  holding capacity) to support fungal growth.
- Monitoring contaminant concentrations and fungal biomass over the
  treatment period.
