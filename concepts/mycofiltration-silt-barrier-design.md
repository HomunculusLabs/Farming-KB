# Mycofiltration Silt Barrier Design

Mycofiltration is the use of mycelium as a membrane for filtering out microorganisms, pollutants, and silt from water runoff. Habitats infused with mycelium reduce downstream particulate flow, mitigate erosion, filter out bacteria and protozoa, and modulate water flow through soil. More than a mile of threadlike mycelial cells can infuse a gram of soil, functioning as a cellular net that catches particles and digests them. As substrate debris is digested, microcavities form and fill with air or water, providing buoyant, aerobic infrastructures with vast surface areas.

## Core Filtration Mechanisms

The mycelial mesh works through multiple simultaneous mechanisms. Physical filtration occurs as the netlike fabric of interconnected hyphae catches silt, particulate matter, and debris. Rhizomorphic mycelium holds substrates with extraordinary tenacity — a single thread of Stropharia rugoso-annulata weighing 0.002 grams supported dowels weighing 6.079 grams, over 3,000 times its own mass; when 90% of the rhizomorph was cut away, it still supported the dowels, meaning it can hold more than 30,000 times its mass. Biological digestion uses fungal enzymes to break down organic debris and certain chemical toxins into fungal biomass or harmless byproducts. Biochemical antagonism occurs through secreted antibiotics and [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] that inhibit or kill pathogenic bacteria and protozoa; if enzymes and antibiotics don't kill all pathogens, bacteria such as [[bacillus-subtilis]] are blocked from reproducing and enter dormancy (a process called dieback, analogous to a fishing net losing its cohesiveness). Moisture channeling allows mycelium to draw water from surrounding areas through advancing hyphal tips, maintaining filter function even when surface water is not flowing.

Key [[factors-affecting-mycoremediation-efficiency]] filtration efficiency include slope gradient, flow rate, turbidity, substrate particle size (straw shaft diameter), [[mushroom-species-selection]], degree of mycelial colonization, and ambient microbial populations. Ecological engineers can customize mycofilters by matching mushroom species to specific problematic bacteria, preventing upstream pathogens from passing into downstream environments.

## Species Selection for Filtration Barriers

Given the number of mushroom species with specific antibacterial properties documented in research, practitioners can grow hundreds of species in mycomulches to buffer threats from upstream microbes. The book includes a comprehensive chart mapping mushroom species against specific microbial targets:

- **Stropharia rugoso-annulata** (Garden Giant): Premier species for farm and watershed mycofiltration. Produces tenacious rhizomorphic mycelium. Growth is stimulated by bacterial contact — it does not produce rhizomorphs under sterile laboratory conditions. Reduced fecal coliforms over 100-fold at Skookum Inlet, Washington. Also provides food through insect larvae that salmon learn to dislodge from floating mushrooms.

- **[[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]** (Oyster Mushroom): Indigenous primary saprophyte, ideal for road reclamation and cellulose-rich substrates. Used as keystone species at Tahuya State Forest test site. In field trials with straw bales channeled into a slough, shifted bacterial dominance from pathogenic Pseudomonas fluorescens to benign Bacillus subtilis downstream.

- **[[growing-gourmet-polyporus-umbellatus-zhu-ling-cultivation]]** (Zhu Ling): 100% effective in vitro at inhibiting Plasmodium falciparum (Lovy et al. 1999). Combining polypore species with pesticidal mycelia that attract mosquitoes could mount a double-pronged malaria response. Mycomulching with this species around malaria-infected swamps could reduce background parasite populations.

- **[[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]]** (Turkey Tail): Broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. Demonstrated inhibition of influenza A, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), herpes simplex 1 and 2, and HIV-1.

- **[[ganoderma-lucidum]]** (Reishi): Effective against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria including Staphylococcus aureus, plus herpes simplex viruses 1 and 2 and vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV).

- **[[ganoderma-applanatum]]** (Artist's Conk): Effective against Aspergillus niger, Bacillus subtilis, and [[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]].

- **Aspergillus niger**: Removed 91% of copper and 70% of zinc from treated swine effluent (Price et al. 2001), applicable for heavy-metal accumulation at factory farming operations.

## Farm Mycofilter Construction

A gently sloped area below a feeding lot or manure pond, where effluent continually seeps through, provides an ideal installation site. Layered construction proceeds as follows: (1) Bottom layer of sawdust or wood chips 3-4 inches deep. (2) First spawn layer of inoculated sawdust at 1/4 pound per square foot, spread by hand or silage spreader. (3) Middle layer of corncobs 4 inches deep — their fiber and cavities allow aeration and provide food for rapid mycelial colonization. (4) Second spawn layer of inoculated sawdust over the corncobs. (5) Cardboard cover for wind and sun protection. (6) Finish layer of straw 4-6 inches deep for shade and moisture retention.

Surface area should be several times larger than the pollution source, depending on depths, slope, and flows. Best built in early spring with sprinkler supplementation for the first few weeks if needed. Once established, the filter matures in a few months and remains viable for years with periodic addition of fresh organic debris. Spawn will likely be the biggest expense, but once established and cared for, the mycelium regenerates itself.

## Logging Road Reclamation Design

Abandoned logging roads are primary vectors of watershed siltation and pollution. Thousands of miles of unpaved roads channel runoff from uplands, clogging downstream spawning grounds with sediment. Mycofiltration offers a cost-effective alternative to conventional decommissioning (tank traps, ditches, scarification). Installation involves: (1) Spread a 6-inch-deep matrix of bark, wood chips, and conifer needles over the road surface. (2) Toss handfuls of selected mushroom spawn on top. (3) Cover with wheat straw bales to retain moisture. (4) Broadcast mycorrhizal inoculum and erosion-control grass seed. Adding spores to chain-saw bar oil ensures immediate inoculation upon cutting, jump-starting decomposition. Cost is approximately 50% less per mile than conventional methods ($4,100–$15,500/mile in Northern Rockies; $21,000–$105,600/mile in Olympics and Cascades). Bunker spawn — mycelium in burlap sacks — provides portable pre-colonized modules for rapid deployment at construction sites and emergency erosion control.

## Advantages Over Conventional Methods

Mycofiltration provides multiple simultaneous benefits over grass-only reclamation or engineered sediment traps: sediment reduction into streams and fisheries; enhanced moisture retention and groundwater recharge; reestablishment of native mycoflora (mycorrhizal and saprophytic types); reduction of hydrocarbon contaminants from diesel, oil, herbicides, and pesticides; filtration of coliform bacteria, E. coli, Pfiesteria, and protozoa; cooling of water entering streams, benefiting fisheries and marine systems; low collateral disturbance to adjacent ecosystems; subsurface mycelial growth enabling water and mineral transport plus aeration; insectary [[amphibian-habitat-creation]] for grubs supporting fish, bird, and bat food chains; mycopesticidal barriers for wood-boring beetles; and eventual transformation of reclaimed roads into nature trails.

## Case Study: Skookum Inlet, Washington

At Stamets' farm on Kamilche Point, utility-company wood chips were spread in a natural depression and inoculated with Stropharia rugoso-annulata spawn over multiple truckloads. Springwater saturated the wood chips — a perfect environment for running mycelium. Several months later, a 50-by-200-foot garden giant mycelial bed had established. Despite doubling farm animal populations without repairing the septic system, coliform levels in outflowing water dropped over 100-fold within one year. Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife inspectors confirmed the dramatic improvement, noting the spongy duff-like texture of the myceliated wood chip bed.

## Case Study: Tahuya State Forest, Washington

At a 500-foot test site alongside a salmon spawning stream in Tahuya State Forest, waste wood from North Mason Fiber was spread 6 inches deep, inoculated with native [[oyster-mushroom-spawn-preparation]], covered with wheat straw, and seeded with mycorrhizal inoculum (Mycogrow) and erosion-control grass (Regreen). Three zones were established based on slope gradient: the lower section with greatest slope and erosion risk, the middle section with gentler grade, and the upper portion with gentlest slope. A previous grass-only reclamation at the same site had shown minimal recovery after 7 years, lacking healthy soil, forest understory, and biological diversity.

## Mycofiltration Applications

Mycofiltration membranes can filter pathogens (protozoa, bacteria, viruses), silt, and chemical toxins. They can be installed around farms and suburban/urban areas, watersheds, factories, and stressed habitats. Installation can utilize debris from forests, pulp and paper mills, recycling centers, farms (straw, corncobs, cotton, coffee wastes), breweries (washed grains), and other industries, inoculated with toxin-specific mushroom species. Mycofilters can be customized with mosaics of mycelial mats to prevent infection from coliform or staph bacteria and protozoa, and can even trap disease-carrying insects, thwarting disease vectors.

## Maintenance and Longevity

Mycofilters regenerate until the debris base is reduced to soil. Mature areas become colonized by native grasses that play additional remediative roles. Red worms arrive and transform myceliated material, cardboard, and debris into rich soil. Every 2-3 years, newly formed soil can be harvested with a front loader tractor and used elsewhere. Gourmet mushrooms may fruit after rains, naturally re-seeding beds provided sufficient food remains. Corncobs offer dual utility: farmers can first grow oyster mushrooms commercially on corncobs, then use the spent substrate as inoculum into sheet mulch filtration barriers.

## Integration with Restoration Frameworks

Mycofiltration bridges with John Todd's "living machines" (estuary ecosystems for waste breakdown) and Bill Mollison's permaculture practices. Stamets envisions mycorestoration, permaculture, and living machines as essential synergistic components in a new model of habitat restoration, all gaining strength from biodiversity.
