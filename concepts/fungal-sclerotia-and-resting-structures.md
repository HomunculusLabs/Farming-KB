Sclerotia are compact, hardened masses of fungal hyphae that serve as survival structures, enabling fungi to persist through periods of adverse environmental conditions.

These differentiated hyphal aggregates are among the most resilient biological structures produced by fungi, capable of surviving desiccation, extreme temperatures, and microbial attack for years or even decades.

## Structure and Formation

Sclerotia form through the process of hyphal aggregation, in which individual hyphae become glued together to form conglutinate cells.

These conglutinate cells develop into true tissues that are either pseudoparenchymatous (losing their hyphal appearance) or prosenchymatous (retaining recognizable hyphal organization).

The resulting structures are composed of different tissue types with distinct functional roles.

A typical sclerotium consists of an outer rind of heavily melanized, thick-walled cells that provides mechanical strength and chemical protection, and an inner medulla of thinner-walled cells that serves as a food reserve.

The melanized rind is particularly resistant to microbial degradation and to environmental stressors including UV radiation, desiccation, and temperature extremes.

The combination of protective rind and nutrient-rich medulla allows sclerotia to survive prolonged dormancy and to germinate rapidly when favorable conditions return.

## Types of Resting Structures

Fungi produce a diverse array of resting structures adapted to different ecological contexts.

Sclerotia represent one of the most complex and resilient types, but other important resting structures include:

Rhizomorphs are differentiated, root-like aggregates of hyphae that allow long-distance transport of water and nutrients within adverse environments.

They function as both exploratory organs and perennating structures, enabling fungi to bridge between spatially separated resources.

Mycelial cords are linear aggregations of hyphae that serve as conduits for translocation of nutrients and water between resource patches.

Unlike rhizomorphs, cords are generally less differentiated but can persist for extended periods in soil.

Chlamydospores are thick-walled, terminal or intercalary spores produced by many fungi as survival structures.

They are simpler in structure than sclerotia but provide similar resistance to environmental stress.

Fruit bodies (sporocarps), including ascomata, basidiomata, and conidiomata, are hyphal aggregates that function primarily in spore production but can also serve as perennating structures for the fungal mycelium.

## Ecological Functions

The primary ecological function of sclerotia is survival during unfavorable conditions.

In temperate and Mediterranean climates, sclerotia allow soil-borne pathogens such as Sclerotinia sclerotiorum and Sclerotium rolfsii to persist between cropping seasons.

In natural ecosystems, sclerotia of saprotrophic fungi survive periods of drought, freezing, or resource scarcity buried in soil or leaf litter.

Sclerotia also serve as a means of initiating colonization when conditions become favorable.

Germination of sclerotia produces hyphae that can rapidly exploit newly available resources.

In many species, sclerotia can germinate either carpogenically (producing fruiting bodies and spores) or myceliogenically (producing vegetative hyphae directly), providing flexibility in the mode of colonization.

The long-term persistence of sclerotia in soil contributes to the maintenance of fungal species diversity.

Soil banks of sclerotia can contain viable propagules of many species, providing a reservoir of genetic diversity that can respond to environmental change or disturbance.

## Sclerotia in Plant Pathogens

Sclerotia are particularly important in the life cycles of many plant pathogenic fungi.

Sclerotinia species cause diseases of numerous crop plants, and their sclerotia can persist in soil for 5-10 years or more.

The melanized rind protects the sclerotium from soil microorganisms, while the medullary tissue provides energy reserves for germination.

Sclerotium rolfsii, the causal agent of southern blight, produces sclerotia that survive in soil and initiate infection of a wide range of host plants.

The sclerotia of this species are small (1-2 mm diameter), abundant, and highly resistant to environmental extremes.

Control of Sclerotium diseases often targets the sclerotial stage, as elimination of sclerotia from soil is extremely difficult.

## Sclerotia in Ecosystem Processes

In natural ecosystems, sclerotia of saprotrophic basidiomycetes contribute to soil carbon storage.

The heavily melanized hyphae and sclerotia of species such as Cenococcum geophilum are produced abundantly in forest soils and persist for extended periods.

Their resistance to decomposition means they contribute to the formation of stable soil organic matter.

The production of sclerotia represents a significant carbon allocation strategy.

Resources invested in sclerotium formation are diverted from active growth and reproduction but provide insurance against future environmental stress.

The balance between investment in active growth versus survival structures is a key life-history tradeoff that differs among fungal species and reflects their ecological strategy.

## Environmental Regulation of Sclerotium Formation

The formation of sclerotia is regulated by environmental signals including nutrient availability, temperature, light, pH, and the presence of other organisms.

Nutrient stress, particularly carbon limitation, often triggers sclerotium production as fungi shift resources from growth to survival.

Autoregulatory signals produced by the fungus itself play critical roles in regulating colony morphogenesis and the decision to form sclerotia versus other structures.

Temperature and moisture cycles can synchronize sclerotium germination with favorable environmental conditions.

Many sclerotia require a period of conditioning (exposure to cold or wet conditions) before they become germinable, preventing premature germination during brief periods of favorable conditions.
