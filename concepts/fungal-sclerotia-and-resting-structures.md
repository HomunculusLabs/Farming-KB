Sclerotia are compact, hardened masses of fungal hyphae that serve as survival
structures, enabling fungi to persist through periods of adverse environmental
conditions. These differentiated hyphal aggregates are among the most resilient
biological structures produced by fungi, capable of surviving desiccation,
extreme temperatures, and microbial attack for years or even decades.
## Structure and Formation
Sclerotia form through the process of hyphal aggregation, in which individual
hyphae become glued together to form conglutinate cells. These conglutinate
cells develop into true tissues that are either pseudoparenchymatous (losing
their hyphal appearance) or prosenchymatous (retaining recognizable hyphal
organization).
A typical sclerotium consists of an outer rind of heavily melanized,
thick-walled cells that provides mechanical strength and chemical protection,
and an inner medulla of thinner-walled cells that serves as a food reserve. The
melanized rind is particularly resistant to microbial degradation and to
environmental stressors including UV radiation, desiccation, and temperature
extremes. The combination of protective rind and nutrient-rich medulla allows
sclerotia to survive prolonged dormancy and to germinate rapidly when favorable
conditions return.
## Types of Resting Structures
Fungi produce a diverse array of resting structures adapted to different
ecological contexts. Sclerotia represent one of the most complex and resilient
types, but other important resting structures include:
Rhizomorphs are differentiated, root-like aggregates of hyphae that allow
long-distance transport of water and nutrients within adverse environments. They
function as both exploratory organs and perennating structures, enabling fungi
to bridge between spatially separated resources.
[[dighton-fungal-rhizomorphs-mycelial-cords]] are linear aggregations of hyphae that serve as conduits for
translocation of nutrients and water between resource patches. Unlike
rhizomorphs, cords are generally less differentiated but can persist for
extended periods in soil.
Chlamydospores are thick-walled, terminal or intercalary spores produced by
many fungi as survival structures. They are simpler in structure than sclerotia
but provide similar resistance to environmental stress.
Fruit bodies (sporocarps), including ascomata, basidiomata, and conidiomata,
are hyphal aggregates that function primarily in [[spore-production-geotropic-sensitivity-coupling-basidiomycete-badham-1982]] but can also
serve as perennating structures for the [[fungal-mycelium-hyphae-and-the-feeding-phase]].
## Sclerotial Development Stages
Sclerotium formation proceeds through distinct developmental phases. In the
initiation phase, hyphal tips undergo branching pattern changes, switching from
apical extension to lateral branching. This is followed by an aggregation phase
where hyphae adhere together through extracellular matrix polysaccharides.
During the consolidation phase, the aggregated hyphae differentiate into the
rind and medulla tissues. Melanin deposition in the rind cells provides
pigmentation and chemical resistance. Finally, maturation involves desiccation
and the accumulation of storage compounds including trehalose, glycogen, and
lipids.
The genetic regulation of sclerotium development involves multiple signaling
pathways. The MAP kinase and cAMP signaling cascades regulate the transition
from [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] to sclerotial initiation. Genes encoding hydrophobins
are expressed during early development, contributing to the hydrophobic surface
properties that protect sclerotia from waterlogging.
## Germination Patterns
Sclerotia can germinate through two distinct pathways. Carpogenic germination
produces [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]] (apothecia or stromata) that bear sexual spores. This
mode is characteristic of [[sclerotinia-sclerotiorum]], where apothecia arise from
sclerotia and release ascospores that initiate new infections.
Myceliogenic germination produces vegetative hyphae directly from the sclerotium
surface, bypassing the sexual stage. This mode allows rapid colonization of
substrate but does not produce airborne spores for dispersal. Many species can
use both pathways, with environmental conditions determining which predominates.
[[spore-germination-triggers-and-dormancy-breaking]] include temperature shifts, moisture availability,
nutrient cues, and exposure to host root exudates. Conditioning periods
(cold stratification or moist incubation) are required by many species to break
dormancy and ensure germination occurs under favorable conditions.
## Sclerotia in Plant Pathogens
Sclerotia are particularly important in the life cycles of many plant pathogenic
fungi. Sclerotinia species cause diseases of numerous crop plants, and their
sclerotia can persist in soil for 5-10 years or more. The melanized rind
protects the sclerotium from soil microorganisms, while the medullary tissue
provides energy reserves for germination.
Sclerotium rolfsii, the causal agent of southern blight, produces sclerotia
that survive in soil and initiate infection of a wide range of host plants.
The sclerotia of this species are small (1-2 mm diameter), abundant, and
highly resistant to environmental extremes. Control of Sclerotium diseases
often targets the sclerotial stage, as elimination of sclerotia from soil is
extremely difficult.
