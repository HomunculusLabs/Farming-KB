# Victorian Mycology and the Discovery of Psilocybin Mushrooms

The Victorian era (1837-1901) was a pivotal period in the history of mycology. The systematic study of fungi as distinct species, the founding of mycological societies, and the first encounters with psychoactive mushroom effects all occurred during this period, as documented extensively by Andy Letcher in "Shroom" (2006).

## The Problem of Mushroom Poisoning

Before the 19th century, mushrooms were generally treated as a single category. As naturalist William Hay complained, "the singular popular incapacity for individualizing fungus species" meant that "the ordinary Englishman's only idea of gathering wild Fungi is to make a heterogeneous collection of everything fungoid that comes in his way." This led to frequent poisonings and a general climate of fear.

Charles Badham (1806-1857) described the hysteria: "Persons have fancied themselves poisoned when they were not; indigestion produced by mushrooms is looked upon with fear and suspicion, and if a medical man be called in, the stomach-pump used, and relief obtained, nothing will persuade either patient or practitioner that this has not been a case of poisoning."

## Taxonomic Revolution

Fungal taxonomy was systematically placed on an equal footing with that of plants during the 19th century, primarily through the work of Elias Magnus Fries, who first separated species on the basis of spore colour, and later Paul Kummer (1834-1912), who revised Fries's work on gilled fungi. Between them, they extracted Psilocybe semilanceata from the heterogeneous Agaricus glutinosus, as part of a process by which the number of recognised species doubled between 1860 and 1890.

## The Woolhope Club and Fungus Forays

The Herefordshire Woolhope Naturalists' Field Club, of which most pioneering British mycologists were members, began organising "fungus forays" in 1868. Members would stroll through the autumn countryside, basket in one hand and mushrooming knife in the other, collecting and identifying fungi. At the end of the day, specimens were displayed in the nearest village hall and discussed over tea. These forays were instrumental in disseminating the new taxonomic understanding to the popular consciousness.

## Mordecai Cubitt Cooke

Mordecai Cubitt Cooke deserves special mention as the Victorian mycologist who came closest to starting a magic mushroom craze. Cooke began his writing career with "The Seven Sisters of Sleep" (1860), a defense of drug use that treated each narcotic plant as a "sister" ruling over a different part of the world. Of the seven plants described (tobacco, opium, hashish, betel, coca, belladonna, and fly-agaric), Cooke devoted significant space to the fly-agaric.

Cooke not only popularized the myths surrounding Amanita muscaria use in Siberia but retold the story of Mr Glen's encounter with a bemushroomed man staggering through Knightsbridge. He recognized that this must have been caused by a mushroom similar to Amanita in its intoxicating properties, and wrote prophetically that "if future generations do not deem it desirable to indulge in a narcotic of this kind for the purposes of producing pleasurable sensations... yet they may learn more than we do at present know of the peculiar characteristics which distinguish this [mushroom] from all the others."

Despite knowing all about the Liberty Cap and how to identify it, Cooke almost certainly never consumed it himself. By the time he published "Poisonous Mushrooms" in 1894, he had completely reversed his earlier positions, warning the public to avoid Liberty Caps.

## The Fly-Agaric in Victorian Art

The Victorian obsession with fairies gave rise to a series of fairy paintings featuring mushrooms, most notably by Noel Paton, John Anster Fitzgerald, Richard Doyle, and Richard Dadd. Mushrooms appeared as signifiers for the otherworldly realms fairies were thought to inhabit. Fitzgerald's "The Fairy's Barque" (1859) shows fairies dining around a mushroom table; Doyle painted "Elves Playing Leapfrog over Red Toadstools." On the Victorian stage, Puck appeared atop a mechanical mushroom in the Princess Theatre's 1856 production of A Midsummer Night's Dream.

By the end of the century, the fly-agaric found its way into children's books, Christmas cards, and greeting cards (especially in Germany, the Baltic countries, and Eastern Europe). Cartoon fly-agarics appeared in Walt Disney's Snow White and performed a memorable Danse Chinois in Disney's Fantasia. From there they entered the enchanted forests of Enid Blyton, forever conjoined with the garden gnome.

## Mr W. and the Knightsbridge Incident

The case of "Mr W." (possibly a Mr. White or Mr. Wright) is one of the earliest documented accidental intoxications with psilocybin mushrooms in Britain. A man in Knightsbridge gathered what he believed to be common field mushrooms but instead consumed Liberty Caps (Psilocybe semilanceata). He was subsequently found staggering through the streets in a state of extreme intoxication, with dilated pupils and vivid hallucinations. The case was widely reported and became a cautionary tale in Victorian mycological circles.
