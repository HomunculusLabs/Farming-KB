# Bioregional Organization and Political Affiliation Strategies

In Chapter 14 of Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Bill Mollison provides detailed guidance on forming bioregional associations, evolving ecological policy, and engaging with political systems from a permaculture perspective. This section bridges the ethical framework of the alternative nation concept with practical organizational strategies for grassroots ecological governance.

## Bioregional Association Defined

A bioregional association is an association of the residents of a natural and identifiable region. The region may be defined by various boundaries:

- **Watershed boundaries** — the most ecologically coherent delineation
- **Remnant or existing tribal or language boundaries** — culturally embedded territories
- **Town boundaries, suburban streets, or districts** — existing administrative or social units
- **Combinations of the above factors** — hybrid approaches drawing on multiple criteria

The acid test of a bioregion is that it is recognized as such by its inhabitants.

### Optimal Scale

Ideally, a bioregion encompasses 7,000 to 40,000 people. Of these, perhaps only a hundred will initially be interested in any regional association, and fewer still will be actively involved. This small active core can nevertheless accomplish significant work through systematic resource assessment and targeted action.

## Work of the Bioregional Group

The primary work involves:

1. **Assessing resources** — cataloguing natural, technical, service, and financial resources
2. **Identifying leakages** — finding where resources (water, soil, money, talent) leave the region
3. **Developing self-reliance strategies** — closing leakage gaps through local solutions

### Specialist Associations

People are called upon to write accounts of their specialties as they apply to the region. Regional newsletters publish results as they emerge. Once areas of action are defined, specialist associations form:

- **Food:** Consumer-producer associations, gardening and soil societies
- **Shelter:** Owner-builder associations
- **Energy:** Appropriate technology associations
- **Finance:** "Earthbank" associations
- **Additional areas:** Crafts, music, markets, livestock, nature study, and any other community interest

### The Regional Office

The bioregional office requires 4–6 people acting as consultants and coordinators, with others on call when needed. Functions include:

- **Registration, address, phone, and newsletter services** for all member associations
- **Land access centre** — operating strategies for connecting people with land
- **Leasehold and title register** — servicing agreements for clubs and societies
- **Community self-funding schemes** — collecting monies for trusts and societies
- **Contact centre** to other regions — serving as trade and coordination hub
- **Accounting services** — contracted to service multiple groups efficiently
- **Educational programming** — inviting craftspeople and lecturers, sharing income

## The Tension Between Local and Global

Mollison identifies a fundamental tension between bioregional centrality (living responsibly within one's region) and global outreach (integrating with people elsewhere). He extends the familiar slogan "think globally, act locally" to "act and think globally and locally."

The region serves as home address, cultural development ground, and node in bioregional networks. Global associations and families of common interest cross regional, state, and national borders to create multicultural alliances.

### Intercultural Exchange

Tribal maps often defined bioregions well: totems and clan groups took as their totemic mothers particular trees or animals, themselves limited in distribution by topographic and climatic factors. Today, minority language groups (Saami, Basque, Pitjatjantjara) claim territories that are ancient and specific to their life mode.

Mollison emphasizes that intermarriage, visits, mutual trade and aid, skills exchange, and educational exchange between regions of very different cultures enriches both. This is explicitly positioned as the antithesis of "integration" (which Mollison calls "bureaucratic genocide") promulgated by majority groups that disallow minority language use and cultural life. Reciprocal education values both sets of knowledge and respects others' lifestyles.

## Political Affiliation Strategies

Two approaches to achieving ecological change through political systems:

### Approach 1: Ground Mobilization

Mobilize ground support in every electorate where a candidate of any party takes a stand on good ecology or against nuclear and polluting industry. This cross-party approach focuses on ecological positions rather than party loyalty.

### Approach 2: Form Ecological/Green Parties

Form a local Ecological or Green Party, or a bioregional group. Mollison references early Green Party development, noting 23 federal and 48 state seats in Germany by 1983.

### The Value of Affiliation

Affiliation and subscription to a common policy is easier for small groups than evolving policy in isolation. A common policy statement sums up the skills of all groups while leaving room for local issues. Broad aims include being ecological, socially egalitarian, grassroots democratic, and non-growth oriented.

## Evolving a New Policy Base

Mollison outlines a structured approach to developing common global policy:

1. **Define the problem or concern** — give weight to priorities on a 1–10 scale
2. **State intent** — what the policy is intended to achieve in the region (principles)
3. **Collect proven strategies** — compile case histories of working solutions
4. **Frame policies** — base them on all successful strategies documented
5. **Set priorities** — weight for urgency, public cost, global spread, long-term effect, and threats to basic resources or life systems

Mollison cautions against framing policy on purely local or trivial matters initially, unless as a case history applicable to a broad principle. Policy should start with a general ethic and proceed to specifics for particular cultures, regions, and landscapes.

## Federal and Global Congresses

Just as bioregions need periodic federal congresses, they also need global congresses. Societies and families need global meetings to break down the idea of defended regional boundaries. Ethics and principles of self-governance, interdependence, and voluntary simplicity apply at both regional and global levels.

## Practical Implementation Notes

- **Start small** — a hundred interested people can drive significant change
- **Build the directory incrementally** — topic by topic, sold by the page, compiled as looseleaf
- **Use existing networks** — global seed exchanges, technology groups, and gardening forums already cooperate
- **Document case histories** — each section should include proven successful strategies
- **Computerize retrieval** — the resource index system is designed for database management
