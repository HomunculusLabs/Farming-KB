# Armillaria gallica: The Humongous Fungus and the Nature of Individuality

## Overview

In the Malheur National Forest of eastern Oregon, a single organism of
*Armillaria gallica* spreads beneath the forest floor across approximately
**2,385 acres** (965 hectares). Estimated to weigh **6,010 metric tons** and
aged between **2,400 and 8,650 years**, this individual has become one of the
most celebrated biological discoveries of the modern era. Dubbed the
"humongous fungus" by the popular press, it forced biologists, philosophers,
and the general public to reconsider the meaning of individuality, aging, and
the boundaries of life. The story is recounted in [[bloomfield-fungal-biology-and-hyphal-growth]] and how fungi confound our categories for understanding the
living world.

## The Discovery

The humongous fungus was identified through a combination of forest pathology
investigation and modern molecular genetics. *Armillaria* species are root rot
pathogens of trees, and researchers studying dieback in the Malheur National
Forest began mapping the extent of [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]] (mushrooms)
from widely separated locations and comparing their DNA using molecular
markers. When genetic fingerprints matched across all samples, researchers
realized they were dealing with one genetic entity rather than many separate
colonies.

## What Constitutes an Individual?

The humongous fungus raises profound questions about individuality in biology.
In animals, individuality is defined by physical continuity—a single body
with a bounded integument and nervous system. In plants, clonal organisms
like **Pando** (covering 106 acres in Utah) have stretched this definition,
but *Armillaria* pushes it further. Unlike Pando, whose above-ground stems
are visibly connected by shared roots, the humongous fungus exists almost
entirely underground, visible only when its mushrooms emerge after autumn
rains. It has no centralized nervous system, circulatory system, or organs.

The situation is complicated by the fact that parts of the mycelium can
become physically disconnected and continue growing independently. If a
fragment breaks off and establishes a new colony, is it still part of the
same individual? Is genetic identity sufficient, or must there be physical
continuity? These questions have no easy answers.
