# Cheese Making Basics

Homestead cheese making transforms fresh milk into a shelf-stable, value-added product. Soft cheeses require minimal equipment and skill, making them the ideal entry point for beginners. As experience grows, cheesemakers can progress to aged and pressed cheeses. This guide covers soft cheeses: ricotta, chevre, and mozzarella.

## Milk Selection

The quality of milk directly determines the quality of cheese:

- Raw goat milk: Excellent for chevre and soft cheeses. Small fat globules produce a creamy texture. Use within 2-3 days of milking.
- Raw cow milk: Versatile for all cheese types. Higher volume per gallon. Use within 3-5 days.
- Pasteurized milk: Works but produces less flavorful cheese. Avoid ultra-pasteurized (UHT) -- proteins are denatured and will not form curds properly.
- Homogenized milk: Works but produces less cream separation. Non-homogenized is preferred.

Always use the freshest milk possible. Milk for cheese should taste clean and sweet -- any off-flavors will concentrate in the finished cheese.

## Essential Equipment

- Stainless steel pot (non-reactive, holds at least 2 gallons)
- Dairy thermometer (accurate from 50-220 F)
- Cheesecloth or butter muslin (butter muslin has a tighter weave for soft cheeses)
- Slotted spoon or skimmer
- Colander for draining
- Cheese molds (for shaping)
- Cheese mat or drying rack
- pH strips or meter (helpful but not essential for beginners)
- Long knife for cutting curd
- Drainage container (catches whey)

## Cultures and Rennet

### Cultures

Cheese cultures are specific strains of lactic acid bacteria that acidify milk:

- Direct-set cultures (mesophilic): Add directly to milk. Convenient, no maintenance. Used for chevre, fromage blanc, and most soft cheeses.
- Thermophilic cultures: Require higher temperatures (90-110 F). Used for mozzarella, parmesan, and other Italian-style cheeses.
- Buttermilk or kefir: Can substitute for mesophilic culture in a pinch (use 1/4 cup per gallon of milk).

### Rennet

Rennet contains enzymes (chymosin) that coagulate milk proteins, forming curds:

- Animal rennet: Traditional, from calf stomach. Strongest coagulation.
- Vegetable rennet: From cardoon thistle or fig sap. Weaker coagulation.
- Microbial rennet: Fermentation-derived. Consistent and widely available.
- Rennet tablets vs liquid: Tablets have longer shelf life. Liquid is more precise for dosing.

## Ricotta

Ricotta (Italian for "recooked") is made from the whey leftover from other cheese making, though whole-milk ricotta is common on homesteads.

Whole milk ricotta recipe:
- 1 gallon whole milk
- 3 cups cultured buttermilk or 1/4 tsp mesophilic culture
- 1/2 tsp liquid rennet diluted in 1/4 cup cool water
- Salt to taste

Process:
1. Heat milk to 90 F. Remove from heat.
2. Add buttermilk or culture and stir gently.
3. Add diluted rennet and stir gently for 30 seconds (top to bottom motion).
4. Cover and let sit at room temperature for 12-16 hours.
5. The milk will form a single curd mass.
6. Cut curd into 1-inch cubes.
7. Heat slowly to 110 F, holding for 15 minutes.
8. Ladle curds into cheesecloth-lined colander.
9. Drain for 1-4 hours depending on desired consistency.
10. Salt to taste. Add herbs if desired. Refrigerate.

## Chevre

Chevre is a fresh goat cheese -- spreadable, tangy, and versatile. It is one of the simplest cheeses to make.

Recipe:
- 1 gallon goat milk
- 1 packet direct-set chevre culture (or 1/4 cup cultured buttermilk)
- 1/4 tsp liquid rennet diluted in 1/4 cup cool water (optional, for firmer curd)
- Salt to taste

Process:
1. Heat milk to 72 F (room temperature milk works if it is fresh).
2. Sprinkle culture over surface. Wait 2 minutes. Stir gently to combine.
3. Add diluted rennet if using. Stir gently for 30 seconds.
4. Cover and let sit at room temperature (70-75 F) for 12-18 hours.
5. The milk will have formed a firm curd with clear whey separation.
6. Ladle curds into cheesecloth-lined colander (do not cut the curd).
7. Drain at room temperature for 6-12 hours, or refrigerate and drain for 24-48 hours.
8. When desired consistency is reached, salt to taste (1/2 tsp per pound).
9. Mix in herbs, garlic, honey, or cracked pepper.

Chevre stores in the refrigerator for 1-2 weeks.

## Mozzarella

Mozzarella is a stretched-curd cheese that requires more active temperature management but is still approachable for beginners.

Recipe (30-minute mozzarella):
- 1 gallon whole milk
- 1.5 tsp citric acid dissolved in 1/4 cup cool water
- 1/4 tsp liquid rennet diluted in 1/4 cup cool water
- 1-2 tsp flaky salt

Process:
1. Pour milk into pot. Stir in citric acid solution.
2. Heat milk to 90 F. Remove from heat.
3. Stir in rennet solution with an up-and-down motion for 30 seconds.
4. Cover and let sit for 5-10 minutes until curd forms and pulls away from pot edges.
5. Cut curd into 1-inch cubes. Let rest 5 minutes.
6. Heat to 105 F while gently stirring curds.
7. Remove from heat and continue stirring for 2-5 minutes.
8. Pour curds into colander, draining whey.
9. Heat whey (or water) to 170 F.
10. Place curd in a bowl. Pour hot water over curds to reach 135 F.
11. Stretch and fold the curd like taffy until smooth and glossy.
12. Form into balls and drop into ice water to set.
13. Store in brine (1 cup salt to 1 gallon water) or wrap and refrigerate.

## Using Whey

Whey is a valuable byproduct of cheese making:

- Feed to [[duck-keeping]] or other livestock (high in protein)
- Use in bread baking (replace water in [[sourdough-bread-guide]] recipes)
- Ferment into ricotta (traditional method)
- Add to compost as a nitrogen source
- Use as a base for lacto-fermented vegetables (see [[food-fermentation-guide]])

## Troubleshooting

- Curd will not form: Milk may be ultra-pasteurized, or rennet is expired/weak
- Bitter cheese: Overheating during curd formation or excessive rennet
- Crumbly cheese (when not desired): Insufficient acid development or too-low temperature
- Rubbery mozzarella: Curd not hot enough during stretching (must reach 135 F)
- Off-flavors: Old milk, unclean equipment, or contamination
