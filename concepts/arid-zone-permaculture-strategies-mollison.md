## Vitamin C Testing

The Ames "C" stick (dip indicator for milk, urine, plant juices) enables advisors and camp gardeners to:

- Test crop success for vitamin C content
- Check urine of mothers and children for deficiency
- Identify which fresh fruits needing minimal care (no artificial fertilizers, sprays, forcing) are best for vitamin C

Reference: "Modern Urine Chemistry," Ames and Co. 1976, Miles Laboratories, 13 Spring St., Chatswood NSW 2067.

## The Nutrient Pump

Trees serve as "nutrient pumps" in arid environments:

- Both leaves and roots trap minerals from air and weathered stone
- Leaves recycle nutrients to topsoil
- Prosopis cineraria penetrates to 30 meters depth
- Acacia tortilis spreads a root net 40-50 meters
- Relatively few of these efficient root networks ensure a large safety web under gardens

## Run-Off Farming

Evenari, Shanan, and Tadmor (Agronomy Journal, Vols. 60, 62, 63) documented run-off farming techniques in arid areas. These are integrated with permaculture design through:

- Contour banks directing runoff to planted areas
- Micro-catchments for individual trees
- Diversion channels from bare rocky slopes to garden areas

## Broadscale Climate Modification

Large-scale tree planting in arid zones modifies local climate through:

- Wind speed reduction
- Increased humidity and dew formation
- Shade creation
- Soil improvement through leaf litter
- Frost mitigation in cold desert nights

## Aboriginal Land Management

Mollison's acknowledges that the eventual inheritors of the arid regions will be (almost solely) Aborigines. Aboriginal people are "when fit and well, active, intelligent, marvellously adapted to their environment, quick to learn, and capable of every sort of craft and technical task." The need for "barefoot doctors" and "barefoot gardeners" who strike at causes rather than effects is emphasized. Millions are wasted on sophisticated buildings which do not work and give nothing to thoughtful design.

## Useful Native Species for Arid Zones

- Kurrajong (Brachychiton gregorii) -- Nectar, pollen, fodder, bark fibre
- Bottletree (Apophyllum anomalum) -- Fodder, possible spice
- Ebony (Bauhinia carronii) -- Leaves and pods edible stock food
- Lime bush (Eremocitrus glauca) -- Small preserving fruit, grafts
- Native currant (Canthium latifolium) -- Fruit and fodder, poultry
- Wild oranges (Capparis mitchelli, C. ambonata)
- Berrigan (Eremophila longifolia) -- Emu and pigeon food
- Emu apple (Owenia acidula) -- Edible fruit, emu forage

# How Do I Create a Permaculture Zone Plan for My Property

## Short Answer
A permaculture zone plan organizes your property into five concentric zones based on the frequency of human attention
and maintenance each area requires. Zone 0 is your home and immediate living space, Zone 1 contains intensively managed
gardens and daily-use elements, Zone 2 holds less frequently visited food production systems, Zone 3 encompasses larger-
scale farming or orcharding, Zone 4 is managed woodland or foraging area, and Zone 5 is wilderness left for nature. This
zoning framework ensures that high-maintenance elements are placed where you visit most often.
