## The Limits of Scientific Method in Biology

Mollison presents a critical examination of the applicability of strict scientific method to living systems. While "hard" sciences such as physics, mathematics, and inorganic chemistry deal with material systems that can be precisely controlled and measured, biological sciences—botany, zoology, psychology—attempt to study systems that resist such treatment for two fundamental reasons: life is always in process of change, and life systems react to investigation and experiments.

Rigorous scientific method demands rigorous control of variables, which presumes either knowing all variables before starting, or being able to control all variables without creating disorder in the living system. Both presumptions are impossible when studying life. Every experiment is conducted by people, and results are communicated by people—living things that are themselves part of the system under study. To ignore the life in the system studied is to ignore oneself.

## The Problem of Control in Living Systems

Living things exist in conditions of flux, not imposed control, and respond to any form of control in unpredictable new ways. Mollison observes that living systems respond to strict control—whether by removal of stimuli or by constant input of stimuli—by becoming uncontrolled. In the case of people and rats at least, the response to strict control is dysfunction or madness.

Experiments in biology are therefore not decisive, rigid, or true findings but rather an eternal search for variables that have not been accounted for previously. Mollison draws an analogy to religious seekers chanting all possible names of God: scientists simply keep testing variables until perhaps they hit on the right one. This process never truly terminates because living systems are infinitely complex.

## The Gap Between Knowing and Acting

A persistent problem identified by Mollison is the separation between those who know and observe, and those who act. Scientists who "know" and observe typically do not apply their knowledge in the world, while those who "act" often do not know or observe. This disconnect has produced tragic outcomes: productive natural ecosystems destroyed to create unproductive cultivated systems, energy-efficient wild animals (deer, kangaroo, fish) displaced by inefficient domestic animals (sheep, cattle), and widespread agricultural systems that require enormous energy inputs while destroying basic resources and denying future yields.

[[edward-goldsmith]], in his 1981 essay "Thermodynamics or Ecodynamics" in *The Ecologist*, argues that many scientists refuse to consider the function of life in the systems they study. As natural systems disintegrate under reductionist management, they produce increasingly helpless plants, animals, and people. The state or the farmer takes over functions that natural processes once performed—Mollison describes this as "the State becomes the father of the orphaned child, the farmer the father of the orphaned chicken." Only by returning self-regulating function and responsibility to living things can a stable life system evolve.

## Approaches That Work in Design

Despite his critique, Mollison identifies several approaches that serve designers well in finding solutions to specific problems:

1. **Improving tools:** Inventing new tools for specific jobs
2. **Collecting large observation sets:** Sorting occurrences by likeness and unlikeness to establish categories, system boundaries, and keys—often revealing common characteristics of diverse elements and suggesting design strategies by analogy
3. **Insight:** The "Aha!" or "Eureka!" response to observation, which while seemingly spontaneous is likely the endpoint of extensive prior observation and pattern recognition
4. **Trials:** The empirical approach of "give it a try and see if it works"—eliminating what doesn't work, though not necessarily establishing how or why something works, or whether it works in the long run
5. **Guessing:** The best guesses being based on trials already known to work
6. **Observing unique events:** Noting singular occurrences, as with the accidental discovery of penicillin
7. **Accident:** Trials set up for one purpose that work in unpredicted ways
8. **Imitation:** Testing effects already discovered by others
9. **Patterning:** Seeing patterns across events of very different natures, producing insights into underlying effects—rare in formal science but essential to design
10. **Commonsense:** Often called "management" in business, consisting of staying with and steering a system through constant adjustment to a successful conclusion—particularly suited to evolving systems and continuous change

## Observation Versus Experimentation

Scientific method is one way to know about the real world, but observation and contemplative understanding constitute another, equally valid approach. Humans can find out about many things by timing, measuring, and observing—enough to build calendars, computers, clocks, meters, and rulers—but never enough to understand the complex actions in even a simple living system.

The distinction is fundamental: mechanical systems can be predicted. Hit a nail on the head and the result is fairly predictable. Hit a dog on the head and it will either dodge, bite back, or die—but it will never again react in the same way. Humans can predict only those things they set up to be predictable, not what they encounter in the real world of living and reactive processes.

## The Ecologist's Paradox

Ecologists and "whole systems" thinkers struggle to understand open and complex systems while recognizing that they too are part of the system they study. Mollison offers a wry observation: given enough limnologists studying freshwater lakes, these researchers themselves become the most important factor in the spread of lake organisms via their boats, boots, and nets. The act of studying a system inevitably changes it—a fundamental limitation that applies equally to students of human communities.

## Value-Free Science as Abdication

Mollison condemns what he sees as abdication of personal responsibility: taking drugs, watching television, retreating to a cave as a fakir, or reading only in pure science. All represent, in his view, an avoidance of engagement with life on earth. Value- and ethic-free lifestyles are as aberrant in science as in society.

He further critiques perverse planning in everyday life: houses facing the road rather than the sun, lawns replacing productive gardens, trees planted only to be pruned and tended. "Make-work is the rule," Mollison observes, suspecting that most theoretical scientists inhabit what he calls demented domestic environments, just as many psychiatrists are inhabitants of mental institutions—those who study dysfunction surround themselves with it.
