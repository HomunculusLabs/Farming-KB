## Succession and Priority Effects

Fungal community assembly during decomposition follows predictable successional patterns, with early colonizers modifying substrate conditions in ways that either facilitate or inhibit subsequent colonizers. Priority effects—where the identity of early colonizers influences the trajectory of community development—are particularly strong in fungal communities. On decomposing wood, for example, initial colonization by [[composting-systems]] systems, managing the succession of fungal decomposers can improve the efficiency and quality of the final product. In forest management, retaining dead wood of different sizes and decomposition stages supports diverse fungal communities by providing substrates at multiple successional stages.

## Ecological Significance

Understanding fungal community assembly is essential for predicting how fungal-mediated ecosystem processes will respond to environmental change, land use intensification, and biological invasions. The processes that structure fungal communities—environmental filtering, dispersal limitation, biotic interactions, and historical legacy—determine which fungal species are present in a given environment and, consequently, which ecosystem functions they perform. Shifts in community assembly processes under global change could alter the functional composition of fungal communities, with cascading effects on decomposition, nutrient cycling, and plant-fungal interactions.

The assembly of fungal communities has direct practical relevance for ecological restoration, biological control, and sustainable agriculture. In restoration projects, the assembly trajectory of fungal communities can determine the success or failure of ecosystem recovery. In agricultural systems, management practices that influence assembly processes—such as tillage, crop rotation, and organic amendments—can be optimized to promote beneficial fungal communities that enhance nutrient cycling and suppress pathogens.

## Research Methods

Fungal community assembly is studied through DNA metabarcoding of environmental samples from spatially explicit sampling designs, which reveal patterns of beta diversity and distance decay. Null model approaches partition the relative contributions of deterministic and stochastic processes to community structure. Common garden and reciprocal transplant experiments test the relative importance of environmental filtering versus dispersal limitation. Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses link community assembly patterns to functional gene content and expression.
