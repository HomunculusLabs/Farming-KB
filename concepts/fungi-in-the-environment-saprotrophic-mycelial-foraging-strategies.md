# Saprotrophic Mycelial Foraging Strategies in Heterogeneous Environments

Cord-forming basidiomycetes are the primary agents of decomposition and
nutrient cycling in forest ecosystems. Unlike resource-unit restricted fungi
that complete their life cycle on a single substrate, these non-resource-unit
restricted saprotrophs develop extensive mycelial networks that interconnect
multiple organic resources across the forest floor. The distribution of
resources in soil is extremely heterogeneous and unpredictable in both space
and time, and these fungi have evolved species-specific strategies to search
for new resources and capitalize on resources landing on their existing
mycelial systems.

## Mycelial Pattern Formation and Fractal Geometry

The pattern of mycelial outgrowth from a resource into soil and litter varies
between species and is modified by the quantity and quality of the initial
resource, soil nutrient status, and microclimate. These patterns have been
quantified using fractal geometry, specifically the box-count method, which
describes space-filling characteristics on a scale between one and two
dimensions. Mass fractal dimension and surface fractal dimension allow
distinction between mycelial systems with gaps in their interiors and those
that are completely plane-filled except at boundaries.

Two broad foraging morphologies are recognized. Mass fractal mycelia, produced
by species such as Agrocybe gibberosa, Coprinus picaceus, Phallus impudicus,
Phanerochaete velutina, and Resinicium bicolor, are open systems characterized
by well-defined, rapidly extending cords throughout. These function as
long-range foragers suited to discovering large, sparsely distributed
resources like fallen branches and trunks. Surface fractal mycelia, produced
by Hypholoma fasciculare and Stropharia caerulea, display diffuse, slowly
extending search fronts that operate as short-range foragers, more successful
at discovering abundant, relatively homogeneous resources such as leaf litter
and nutrient-rich substrates.

## Temporal Dynamics of Mycelial Architecture

Mycelial pattern characteristics change substantially over time. Systems tend
to become more open, with lower fractal dimension, as individual hyphae and
minor cords regress, leaving behind a persistent network of thick cords. Even
mycelia that were initially surface fractal for several months eventually
become increasingly mass fractal. This regression is not degenerative but
strategic: regressing mycelium allows re-distribution of biomass and nutrients
to support continued exploration by foraging fronts. Radiotracer studies have
confirmed that nutrients are scavenged at foraging fronts and translocated
from the resource base to sustain outward growth.

## Search Strategies

Two complementary approaches govern resource acquisition. In the sit-and-wait
strategy, a large mycelial network remains in place and capitalizes on new
resources when they arrive through litter fall or root death. In tropical
forest canopies, rhizomorphic fungi form suspended networks that trap and
colonize falling litter, analogous to animal filter feeders. In the active
search strategy, mycelia grow outward to seek new resources, with an array of
morphological and physiological responses triggered upon encounter.

When a new wood resource is added to an established Phanerochaete velutina
mycelium, the network responds by establishing mycelial growth to colonize the
resource, sometimes by supplementary growth elsewhere to acquire additional
nutrients, and by coordinated movement of carbon and mineral nutrients.
Experiments using radiolabelled phosphorus demonstrated sophisticated spatial
and temporal coordination: the total phosphorus allocated to new resources
increased linearly with time, but the proportion reaching each resource
depended on how long it had been in contact with the mycelial system. Better
colonized resources became the main sinks rather than the most recently added
ones, suggesting a system-wide translocation stream from which nutrients are
extracted at points of active use or storage.

## Responses to Abiotic Factors

Mycelial growth, extension, biomass, and fractal dimension are affected by
inoculum size and nutrient status, soil nutrient composition, and non-nutrient
abiotic variables including temperature, gaseous regime, water potential, and
pH. Most species produce mycelia with greater space-filling from larger
inocula. Higher nutrient concentrations in encountered resources generally
increase both extension rate and hyphal coverage, indicating that
space-filling is not achieved at the expense of exploration. Denser mycelium
reflects greater carbon and nutrient availability for generating
extra-resource mycelial biomass.

## Interactions with Other Fungi

When mycelial systems of different species meet, outcomes range from
replacement of one by another to deadlock, with both producing dense
defensive barricades at the interaction zone. Morphological changes including
altered fractal dimension and biomass production rates reflect defensive
responses that can sometimes result in territorial ingress. The outcomes of
fungal interactions parallel the four theoretical outcomes of competition
between plant or animal species: competitive exclusion in either direction,
unstable equilibrium, or stable coexistence through niche separation.

## Invertebrate Grazing Effects

Mycelia are highly nutritious and many invertebrates including Collembola,
termites, nematodes, and Diptera feed on them. Grazing causes dramatic
morphological changes analogous to herbivory on plants. Intense grazing by
Collembola can shift mycelial growth from dense, exploitative patterns to
less dense, explorative patterns. Different fungal species are preferentially
grazed in different regions: hyphal tips at growing margins, cords near the
inoculum, or fine mycelium throughout the colony. The browse line created
around mycelial margins mirrors that seen on trees in grazed wood pastures.
Enzyme expression changes can occur distant from the grazing site, indicating
system-wide communication within the network.

## Network Connectivity and Resilience

Mycelial networks are continuously remodelled through growth, branching,
hyphal fusion (anastomosis), and regression. Both radial and tangential
connections create systems with many connected loops. Tangential connections
shorten communication pathways between peripheral regions. Network architecture
determines resilience to disturbance: highly connected networks withstand
invertebrate grazing better because alternative pathways remain when some
connections are severed. Resilience also depends on the ease and efficiency of
reconnection through regrowth following damage.
