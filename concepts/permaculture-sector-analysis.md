# Permaculture Sector Analysis

Sector analysis is a core analytical tool in permaculture design that maps the flows of energy, forces, and influences that move across a site from outside. Developed by Bill Mollison and expanded by subsequent teachers including Williams, sector analysis helps the designer place elements to either intercept, channel, or block these external forces.

## What Is a Sector?

A sector is a directional zone of influence on the site. Unlike zones (which are based on distance from the center of activity), sectors are based on direction and the forces that flow across the landscape. Sectors are typically represented as wedge-shaped segments on a map, centered on the main house or Zone 0.

## Common Sectors to Map

### Sun Sectors

- **Summer sun sector**: Maps the solar azimuth during the summer solstice. The wedge should reflect the actual angle range of the sun's path. In the Northern Hemisphere, the sun arcs higher and wider in summer.
- **Winter sun sector**: Maps the solar azimuth during the winter solstice. The sun is lower and narrower in winter. This sector is critical for placing buildings, greenhouses, and frost-sensitive plants.

### Wind Sectors

- **Summer wind sector**: Prevailing wind direction during summer (approximately 80 degrees wide on the sector diagram). May bring cooling breezes or hot dry winds depending on location.
- **Winter wind sector**: Prevailing wind direction during winter (approximately 80 degrees wide). Often carries cold air that needs to be blocked or deflected.
- Williams notes that summer and winter winds typically come from different directions, creating two separate sectors.

### Fire Sector

- Direction from which wildfire is most likely to approach
- Should be noted on every design, even in relatively fire-safe areas
- Mollison identifies fire-immune elements: mud crops, mulched gardens, roads, short grazers, summer-green systems, low-litter plants

### Water Flow Sector

- Direction of surface water flow onto and across the site
- Includes seasonal flood patterns and stormwater runoff
- Guides placement of swales, ponds, and diversion drains

### Noise Sectors

- Roads, highways, industrial areas, schools, or other noise sources
- Each significant noise source gets its own sector wedge
- Angular width depends on how much of the site is affected

### View Sectors

- **Desirable views**: Scenery you want to preserve and frame in the design
- **Undesirable views**: Features you want to screen with plantings or structures

### Privacy Sectors

- Areas where neighbors or passersby can see into the site
- Guides placement of screening plantings and structures

### Pollution Sectors

- Direction of prevailing air pollution, dust, or chemical drift
- May come from roads, agriculture, or industrial sources

## Creating a Sector Diagram

Williams provides a practical method:

1. Draw a large circle centered on Zone 0 (the house or center of activity)
2. Create concentric rings equal to the number of sectors you plan to map
3. Starting from the center, draw angular wedges for each sector outward to the appropriate ring
4. Use different colors for each sector type
5. Typical order (outer to inner): summer sun, winter sun, prevailing winds, then other sectors

Mollison emphasizes that extrinsic energies should be defined for direction, intensity, and frequency. If both intensity and frequency are low, you may not need to consider them at all. High intensity or high frequency demands attention.

## Using Sector Analysis in Design

Once the sector diagram is complete, it tells the designer where to place elements:

- **Block** undesirable forces: Windbreaks on the cold wind sector, firebreaks on the fire sector, noise buffers on the noise sector
- **Allow access** to desirable forces: Open the winter sun sector for solar gain, direct cooling summer breezes into living spaces
- **Catch and store** useful flows: Place water harvesting on the water flow sector, position solar panels in the sun sector
- **Redirect** forces: Deflect cold wind over or around structures, channel water to storage

## Mollison's Sector Design Insights

- Access roads should approach from the side that allows defending the house site from cold winds
- It is a "Type 1 error" to place the house on the edge of the property where it is most exposed
- Views should be managed: create destinations (lookouts in Zone 3) rather than trying to see everything from the house
- For every element placed, the designer must be able to answer "Why did you put that there?" with multiple reasons
