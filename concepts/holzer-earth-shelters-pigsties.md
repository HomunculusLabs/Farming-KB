# Holzer Earth Shelters - Pigsties and Animal Housing

updated: 2026-04-18

[[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]] uses simple earth-sheltered structures for housing livestock, particularly [[backyard-poultry-and-integrated-animal-systems]]. These [[holzer-roundwood-shelter-construction]] with earth-covered roofs create a draft-free and warm environment in which all livestock can be kept without supplementary heating, even through severe alpine winters at 1,100 metres elevation.

## Design Philosophy

The earth shelter design follows Holzer's core principle of working with nature rather than against it. By building into or against the earth, the structure exploits the ground's thermal mass to moderate temperature extremes. The earth acts as insulation, keeping the interior warm in winter and cool in summer. This eliminates the need for artificial heating or cooling systems.

The shelters are built using roundwood timber -- branches and small-diameter logs that would otherwise be considered waste material from forest management. This makes construction extremely low-cost and uses locally available materials. The design is deliberately simple and robust, requiring no specialised skills or tools.

## Construction Method

Building earth shelters for pigs is straightforward. The basic structure consists of:

**Frame**: Roundwood posts and beams, typically from thinnings or fallen trees. The wood does not need to be milled or squared; whole round timbers are used as found. Hardwood species are preferred for durability, especially where posts contact the ground.

**Walls**: Can be solid timber, wattle (woven branches), or a combination. The walls do not need to be perfectly airtight, as some ventilation is necessary for animal health. The earth covering on the roof provides the primary weather protection.

**Roof**: Roundwood rafters covered with a thick layer of earth (typically 30-50 cm or more). The earth layer is planted with grass or groundcover plants, which prevent erosion and add to the insulation value. The living roof also blends the structure into the landscape aesthetically.

**Entrance**: Oriented away from prevailing winds. The entrance is kept low and relatively small, which helps retain heat while still allowing animals to enter and exit freely. A simple gate or barrier can be used to confine animals when needed.

**Drainage**: Critical to prevent water accumulation around or under the structure. The site should be well-drained or graded to direct water away from the shelter. Gravel or stone drainage layers may be incorporated beneath the floor.

## Benefits for Livestock

The earth shelters provide several advantages for animal welfare and farm economics:

**Temperature regulation**: The earth-insulated interior maintains a stable temperature well above outside conditions during winter. Holzer has observed that pigs in earth shelters remain comfortable and healthy through conditions that would severely stress animals in conventional sheds.

**Draft-free environment**: The solid construction and earth covering eliminate cold drafts, which are a major source of stress and illness in conventionally housed livestock. Animals can choose their position within the shelter to find their preferred temperature.

**Natural behaviour**: The shelter provides a dark, quiet, cave-like environment that suits the natural instincts of pigs and other livestock. Animals feel secure and are less stressed, which translates to better health and productivity.

**Bedding**: Deep bedding of straw or other organic material inside the shelter provides additional insulation. As the bedding decomposes, it generates heat through composting, further warming the shelter. The resulting composted material can later be spread on fields or [[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]] as fertiliser.

## Earth Cellars

Holzer also constructs earth-sheltered structures for [[sepp-holzer-earth-cellars-and-philosophy]], using similar principles. These earth cellars maintain cool, stable temperatures year-round for storing root crops, preserved foods, and other produce without refrigeration. The earth-sheltered approach extends to other farm buildings and even human dwellings on some of Holzer's projects.

The earth cellar design uses the same roundwood frame construction with earth-covered roofs, but with more attention to moisture control and ventilation to prevent spoilage of stored crops. The temperature inside remains naturally cool in summer (preventing stored food from rotting) and above freezing in winter (preventing frost damage).

## Economic Advantages

The cost advantages of earth shelter construction are significant:

- Materials are essentially free: roundwood from forest management, earth from the site
- No specialised labour required: any farmer can build these structures
- No ongoing energy costs for heating or cooling
- Minimal maintenance: the living roof is self-maintaining once established
- Long lifespan: earth-sheltered structures are protected from UV degradation and weathering
- The structures blend into the landscape and can serve multiple purposes over their lifetime

## Siting Considerations

When choosing a site for an earth shelter, Holzer considers:

- **Drainage**: The most critical factor. Poor drainage will undermine the structure and create unhealthy conditions for animals
- **Wind protection**: Building into a bank or hillside provides natural wind protection on at least one side
- **Sun exposure**: Some solar gain on the entrance face helps warm the interior during winter
- **Access**: The shelter needs to be accessible for feeding, cleaning, and animal management, but Holzer notes they do not need to be "easily accessible" in the conventional sense -- a walk across a field is acceptable and preferable to compromising the siting
- **Proximity to paddocks**: The shelter should be centrally located within the paddock system to minimise animal movement
- **Water supply**: A water source should be nearby for drinking water

## Integration with Paddock Systems

The earth shelters are designed as integral components of Holzer's [[holzer-livestock-philosophy-paddock-system]]. Each paddock ideally has its own shelter, or shelters are positioned so that animals in any paddock can reach one without excessive movement. As pigs or other animals are rotated through paddocks, they always have access to appropriate shelter.

The deep bedding from the shelters becomes a valuable resource. When cleaned out, the composted bedding is spread on the land or incorporated into raised beds, closing the nutrient cycle between animals and crops.
## Adaptation for Other Livestock

While pigs are the most commonly discussed occupants, Holzer's earth shelter principles apply broadly to other livestock. Poultry shelters use the same roundwood frame and earth-covered roof but with smaller dimensions, wire mesh to exclude predators, and elevated perches. The [[holzer-poultry-keeping-ducks-geese-and-chickens]] benefit equally from the thermal mass of the earth covering, keeping birds warm through winter without supplementary heat. For cattle and horses, larger shelters follow identical principles: roundwood posts, earth-covered roof, wind-protected entrance, and deep bedding. The scale changes but the core design logic remains the same.
## Maintenance and Longevity

Earth shelters require remarkably little maintenance once established. The living roof, planted with hardy grasses and groundcovers, is largely self-sustaining. Any bare patches that develop from erosion should be replanted promptly with tough, low-growing species. The roundwood frame will gradually decay over decades, but individual members can be replaced as needed without dismantling the entire structure. Holzer has shelters on the [[krameterhof-holzer-model-farm]] that have been in continuous use for over thirty years with only minor repairs. The key to longevity is ensuring that the posts are made from durable hardwood species where they contact the ground, and that drainage remains effective throughout the life of the structure.
## Climate Adaptation Beyond Alpine Conditions

Although developed for severe alpine winters, the earth shelter concept transfers readily to other climates. In hot, arid regions, the earth covering provides cooling rather than heating, keeping the interior significantly below ambient temperatures during the day. In humid tropical climates, the shelters offer protection from heavy rain and cyclonic winds while maintaining airflow through appropriately sized and positioned openings. The fundamental principle -- using earth mass to moderate temperature extremes -- works in every climate zone, though the specific design details must be adapted to local rainfall patterns, prevailing winds, and temperature ranges.
## Integration with Water Systems

Holzer often positions earth shelters in relation to water features on the farm. A shelter built into a slope above a pond or wetland gains additional thermal moderation from the nearby water body. In some designs, the roof drainage from the shelter feeds directly into adjacent [[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]] or irrigation channels, making the shelter a functional part of the overall water management system. This integration exemplifies the permaculture principle of connecting elements so that the output of one becomes the input of another, increasing overall system efficiency.
## Winter Management and Deep Bedding Systems

Managing earth shelters through winter requires attention to the deep bedding system that provides both insulation and composting heat. As winter progresses, the bedding layer builds up through the addition of fresh straw or leaves. The lower layers begin to compost, generating heat that supplements the earth-shelter effect. Holzer recommends maintaining at least 30-40 cm of bedding material through the coldest months, adding fresh material as the existing bedding compresses. The composting process is primarily anaerobic at the bottom of the deep litter, which generates more heat than aerobic composting. By spring, the accumulated bedding has converted to rich compost that can be spread on fields or used in [[holzer-raised-bed-techniques-hugelkultur]]. This annual cycle transforms what would be a waste management problem in conventional systems into a valuable resource, while keeping animals warm through the coldest months without any external energy input.
## Building on Slopes and Hillsides

The most effective earth shelter placements take advantage of natural topography by building into a slope or hillside. A shelter cut into an earth bank has one or more walls formed by the earth itself, requiring less timber for construction and providing superior insulation compared to a free-standing structure. The earth bank protects the shelter from prevailing winds on at least one side and simplifies roof construction since the roof can transition directly from the earth bank to a gently sloping surface. When building into a slope, Holzer recommends excavating horizontally into the bank rather than cutting a vertical face, which provides a more stable earth wall and a natural transition from the exterior to the shelter interior. The excavated soil can be used to build up the earth covering on the roof, creating a structure that appears to grow out of the landscape. Sites with 1:3 or gentler slopes are ideal for this approach; steeper slopes may require retaining walls or terracing to ensure stability.

## Shelter Design Variations

Beyond the basic rectangular pigsty, Holzer has developed several variations on the earth shelter theme that serve different functions. A farrowing shelter provides a more protected, warm environment for sows giving birth, with an inner compartment separated from the main shelter that can be bedded more deeply and heated by the sow's body heat. A mobile earth shelter on skids allows the structure to be moved between paddocks as the grazing rotation progresses, reducing the need for multiple permanent structures while still providing the thermal benefits of earth-sheltered construction. A combination shelter and storage structure uses one end for livestock and the other for [[sepp-holzer-earth-cellars-and-philosophy]], with a dividing wall that allows heat from the animal end to moderate temperatures in the storage end without contaminating stored crops with manure odours. Each variation demonstrates Holzer's principle that the basic design concept is flexible and can be adapted to the specific needs of the site, the livestock, and the farmer.
## Animal Behaviour and Shelter Use

Understanding the natural behaviour patterns of the animals being housed is essential for successful earth shelter design. Pigs are naturally burrowing animals that instinctively seek enclosed, dark spaces for rest and farrowing. The cave-like interior of an earth shelter appeals directly to these instincts, which is why pigs typically adapt to earth shelters immediately without the stress and behavioural problems that can occur when introducing animals to conventional metal or concrete structures. Pigs will naturally organise their shelter use, designating specific areas for sleeping, dunging, and feeding if given sufficient space. The [[holzer-reading-nature-philosophy]] that underpins all of Holzer's work emphasises observing these behavioural patterns before and after building, and adjusting the design to better accommodate the animals' preferences. For example, if pigs consistently dung in one corner, that corner can be designed with better drainage and easier access for cleaning, while the sleeping area can be given extra bedding and wind protection. This responsive, observation-based approach to animal housing design produces shelters that work better for both the animals and the farmer.
## Cost-Effectiveness of Earth Shelter Construction

The economic case for earth-sheltered pigsties is one of their strongest advantages. Conventional livestock buildings require expensive manufactured materials: steel framing, corrugated roofing, concrete floors, and insulation panels. Earth-sheltered construction uses primarily on-site materials: earth, timber, and stone, with the earth itself providing both structure and insulation. Holzer estimates that an earth-sheltered pigsty costs roughly one-third to one-half of a comparable conventional structure, while providing superior thermal performance that reduces winter feeding costs. The [[holzer-irrigation-principles]] that guide all of Holzer's work are exemplified in this construction approach: minimal purchased materials, maximal use of locally available resources, and long service life with minimal maintenance. Over a 20-year lifespan, the total cost of an earth-sheltered pigsty -- including construction, maintenance, and the additional feed savings from reduced thermal stress on the animals -- is typically 40-60% lower than an equivalent conventional building.
