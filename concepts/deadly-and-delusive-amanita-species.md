# Deadly and Delusive Amanita Species

## Amanita Verna — Fool's Mushroom

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 3 inches; usually entirely white, though centre may be slightly tinted ochraceous |
| **Stem** | Height ~5 inches, width ~3/8 inch; fairly long and slender |
| **Volva** | Usually more sheathing than in *A. phalloides* |
| **Habitat** | Woods, especially beech; summer and early autumn |
| **Status** | Rare in Britain |

*Amanita verna* is closely allied to *A. phalloides* and often regarded as a variety or subspecies. It is equally poisonous and probably **more dangerous** because, being white, it is more readily mistaken for edible mushrooms. The specific epithet *verna* (spring) is misleading — it rarely appears in spring despite what the name suggests.

J. Bauhin called it *Fungus stultorum* — "the fungus of fools" — presumably because in its young egg-like stage it was mistaken for *Amanita caesarea*, the prized Caesar's Mushroom of the Continent. The white colouration makes it particularly insidious: gatherers accustomed to the greenish tinge of *A. phalloides* may not recognize this all-white relative as equally deadly.

Distinguishing features from the Field Mushroom include the persistently white gills (the Field Mushroom's gills turn pink, then brown) and the membranous volva at the stem base — though this critical identifying feature may be left in the ground if the specimen is gathered carelessly.

## Amanita Virosa — Destroying Angel

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 5 inches; conical at first, often somewhat asymmetrical, always rather umbonate when expanded |
| **Stem** | Height ~7½ inches, width ~5/8 inch; slender, floccosely scaly |
| **Ring** | Incomplete, silky, usually lower on one side, often remains attached to the cap edge |
| **Volva** | Lax and wide |
| **Habitat** | Damp woods; summer and autumn |
| **Status** | Rare in Britain |

*Amanita virosa* shares the deadly toxicity of *A. phalloides* and *A. verna*. Its common name "Destroying Angel" is apt. Like *A. verna*, its predominantly white colouration makes it dangerous for inexperienced collectors. The asymmetrical, conical cap with a persistent umbo is a useful distinguishing character, as is the incomplete ring that often stays attached to the cap margin rather than forming a clean annulus on the stem.

The combination of rarity in Britain and extreme toxicity means that most mushroom poisonings in the UK are caused by *A. phalloides* rather than these white species — but in continental Europe, *A. virosa* accounts for a significant proportion of fatal poisonings.

## Amanita Mappa — False Death Cap

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 3 inches; usually lemon-yellow or white with no trace of green; no dark radiating fibrils; retains irregular patches of the volva |
| **Stem** | Height ~3¾ inches, width ~½ inch; slender, solid then hollow, white or yellowish, striate above the white membranous ring |
| **Base** | Bulbous and truncate with short thick margin, often showing projecting torn edge of the enclosing volva, separated from the rest of the stem by a distinct groove |
| **Gills** | White, often with yellowish edge, crowded and narrow |
| **Flesh** | White, often yellowish below the separable skin of the cap |
| **Smell** | Resembles raw potatoes |
| **Taste** | Insipid then disagreeable |
| **Habitat** | Woods, especially beech; summer and autumn |

The fancied resemblance of the volval patches on the cap to a map accounts for the specific epithet *mappa*. The truncate bulbous base with its distinct groove is the chief difference from *A. phalloides*.

**Important**: Until recently *Amanita mappa* was generally thought to be very poisonous — hence its common name "False Death Cap." It is now known to be **harmless**, though worthless as food because of its offensive flavour. This represents one of the more significant corrections in mycological knowledge: a species long feared and avoided is in fact benign, illustrating how slowly and uncertainly the edibility of fungi was established.

## Amanita Muscaria — Fly Agaric

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 7 inches; scarlet or orange-red, slightly viscid, shiny, dotted with thick white or yellowish wart-like patches often arranged concentrically; roundish when young, expands to flat with striate edge |
| **Gills** | Crowded, white or tinted yellow, thick, minutely toothed edge |
| **Stem** | Height ~10 inches, width ~1 inch; white or yellow-tinged, stuffed then hollow, often scaly, striate above the ring; bulbous base encircled by several zones of white or yellowish squamules |
| **Ring** | White or edged with yellow |
| **Flesh** | White except under the separable cap skin where orange-yellow |
| **Taste** | Pleasant |
| **Smell** | Not appreciable |
| **Habitat** | Mostly under birches, also under pines; autumn |

### Cultural Significance

The Fly Agaric is one of the most recognizable fungi in the world, figuring in nursery pictures, table decorations, films (such as Disney's *Fantasia*), and children's toys. Its specific epithet *muscaria* refers to the observation by Albertus Magnus in the 13th century that the fungus, broken up in milk, kills flies. The method is still used in country districts on the Continent — in Poland and Czechoslovakia, sugar solution replaces milk. In Romania, the fungus is placed on window-sills to discourage flies. It was formerly employed in Britain and Sweden for getting rid of bugs, accounting for the alternative name "Bug Agaric."

Among the Koryak tribes of northeast Siberia, the fungus is eaten deliberately to produce a state of excessive emotion. The stimulant is eliminated by the kidneys, allowing the effects to be recycled — urine containing the active compounds is consumed to prolong the intoxication. According to Scandinavian tradition, Viking berserkers ate *Amanita muscaria* before battle. It also figures in homeopathy as *Agaricus* or *Aga*, formerly classified among the antipsorics.

### Toxicity and Symptoms

Despite being "popularly regarded as the most poisonous toadstool," *Amanita muscaria* **never causes death in healthy people**. The poison resides principally in the skin of the cap, with considerable variation in potency between specimens.

Symptoms typically appear one to three hours after ingestion:
1. **Delirium and hallucinations**, sometimes accompanied by intestinal disturbances
2. **Intense stupor** following the excitation phase
3. **Complete awakening to total forgetfulness** of the experience

The fungus is eaten in some regions apparently without ill effect; however, the price on the barren Steppes — three or four reindeer for a single specimen — suggests considerable potency in those populations.

### Toxic Principles

Three alkaloids have been isolated:

1. **Muscarine (mycomuscarine)**: Present in small amounts; produces well-marked pharmacological effects, but these are *not* the characteristic symptoms of *A. muscaria* poisoning.

2. **Mycetoatropine (muscaridine)**: An alkaloid whose effects on the central nervous system resemble those of atropine; precise chemical constitution not known. This is likely responsible for the hallucinatory and deliriant effects.

3. **Choline**: Occurs in fairly large amounts; probably responsible for the gastrointestinal symptoms that sometimes accompany poisoning.

## Amanita Pantherina — False Blusher

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cap** | Average width 4 inches; convex then spread out, fleshy, brownish grey, brown or yellowish brown, darker in centre; slightly viscid when damp, shiny when dry, striate edge |
| **Gills** | White, crowded |
| **Stem** | Height ~5 inches, width ~½ inch; white, stuffed then hollow, often narrower and striate above; bulbous base surrounded by membranous border with one or more scaly bracelets (volva remnants) |
| **Ring** | Attached obliquely about middle of stem, thin and often fugaceous (evanescent) |
| **Flesh** | White |
| **Smell** | Slight radish-like |
| **Taste** | Mild |
| **Habitat** | Woods, heaths and pastures; summer and autumn |

*Amanita pantherina* is liable to be confused with the edible *A. rubescens* (The Blusher). Critical distinguishing features: the flesh and gills of *A. pantherina* do not redden (unlike *A. rubescens*, which turns pink-red when cut); the cap always has some shade of brown with numerous small white warts; and the stem base is surrounded by circular remnants of the volva rather than the loose scales of *A. rubescens*.

*A. pantherina* is poisonous, with symptoms similar to *A. muscaria* but apparently more serious.
