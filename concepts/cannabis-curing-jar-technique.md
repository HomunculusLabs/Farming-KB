# Cannabis Curing Jar Technique

Curing is the post-drying process that transforms freshly dried cannabis into a smooth, potent, and flavorful product. While drying removes the bulk of moisture from the harvested plant material, curing allows the remaining moisture to redistribute evenly, breaks down chlorophyll and other undesirable compounds, and promotes the degradation of non-psychoactive acidic cannabinoids into their active forms. The jar curing method is the most common and effective approach.

[[cannabis-drying-and-curing]]

## Why Curing Matters

Freshly dried cannabis often has a harsh, green, or grassy taste when smoked. This is because chlorophyll and other plant pigments have not fully broken down, and the moisture distribution within the buds is uneven. The outside of the bud may feel dry while the interior retains significant moisture.

During the curing process, residual enzymes continue to break down chlorophyll and starches. The remaining moisture slowly migrates from the interior to the exterior of the buds and is released. This redistribution prevents the growth of mold while allowing the chemical transformations that improve flavor and smoothness.

Cannabinoid acids such as THCA and CBDA are the primary forms present in fresh and dried cannabis. These acidic cannabinoids are not psychoactive in their raw form. Over time, through a natural process of decarboxylation, THCA converts to THC and CBDA converts to CBD. Curing facilitates this conversion gradually, which many experienced consumers believe produces a smoother, more nuanced effect than rapid decarboxylation through heat alone.

Terpenes, the aromatic compounds responsible for cannabis flavor and aroma, also mature during curing. Some terpenes may evaporate if the drying process is too rapid, while proper curing preserves and develops the terpene profile.

## When to Begin Curing

Curing should begin when the harvested branches are sufficiently dry that a small branch snaps cleanly rather than bending. If a branch bends without breaking, additional drying time is needed. If a branch crumbles to dust, the material has been over-dried.

The typical drying period before curing is two to three weeks for whole plants hung upside down. Environmental conditions during drying affect this timeline. Warmer temperatures and lower humidity accelerate drying, while cooler temperatures and higher humidity slow it.

Buds that are moved to jars too wet risk developing mold. Buds that are over-dried before jarring will not benefit as much from curing because there is insufficient residual moisture to support the enzymatic and chemical processes that improve quality.

## The Jar Curing Process

The basic jar curing method involves placing dried, trimmed buds into airtight glass jars and storing them in a cool, dark place with periodic burping (opening the jars to release accumulated moisture and gases).

Wide-mouth Mason jars are the standard container choice. Glass is preferred over plastic because it does not impart odors or flavors and creates an airtight seal. Quart-sized jars are commonly used, with buds filled to approximately three-quarters full to leave some headroom.

During the first two weeks of curing, the jars should be opened daily for approximately 10-15 minutes. This burping process releases built-up humidity and allows fresh air exchange. If condensation is visible on the inside of the jar, the buds were too moist when jarred and should be removed and allowed to dry further before returning to the jar.

After the first two weeks, burping frequency can be reduced to once every few days. By the fourth week, burping once per week is typically sufficient. The buds should be gently shaken or rotated in the jar every few days to redistribute moisture and prevent pockets of dampness from forming.

## Curing Duration

The minimum curing period before cannabis reaches acceptable smoking quality is approximately two weeks from the start of jar curing. However, significant improvement continues through the fourth to eighth week.

Cannabis cured for three to four weeks represents the pinnacle of quality for most consumers. At this stage, chlorophyll has sufficiently broken down, the smoke is smooth, flavors are well-developed, and potency has peaked. This is the point at which the balance of active cannabinoids, terpenes, and absence of harsh compounds is optimal.

Cannabis cured for eight weeks or longer can smoke extremely well but may begin to lose some potency as THC gradually converts to CBN, which has more sedative properties. Year-old cured cannabis is considered vintage quality by some enthusiasts but will have noticeably reduced THC content compared to fresh-cured material.

If the drying period before jarring was three weeks, this time can be subtracted from the recommended jar curing time. For example, if buds dried for three weeks before jaring, they may reach optimal quality after two to three weeks in jars rather than four to six.

## Monitoring During Curing

Regular inspection during the curing process allows the grower to catch problems early and assess progress. When opening jars for burping, the grower should note the aroma, texture, and appearance of the buds.

The aroma should evolve from a grassy, green smell toward the characteristic scent of the strain. If buds smell ammonia-like or like rotting vegetation, this indicates anaerobic conditions and potential mold problems. Such material should be discarded.

Buds should feel slightly springy when gently squeezed, not brittle or wet. If buds feel crispy and crumble easily, they may be too dry and the jar should be opened less frequently to preserve remaining moisture. If buds feel damp or soggy, they need more drying time outside the jar.

Visual inspection should reveal no white fuzzy growth (mold) or dark spots. Any buds showing signs of mold should be removed immediately to prevent contamination of the entire jar.

## Humidity Control with Two-Way Packs

Two-way humidity control packs, such as Boveda packs, can simplify the curing process by maintaining a specific relative humidity within the sealed jar. Packs rated at 58-62% relative humidity are commonly recommended for cannabis curing.

These packs absorb excess moisture when humidity rises above the target level and release moisture when humidity drops below it. This passive regulation reduces the risk of both mold (from excess moisture) and overdrying (from insufficient moisture).

When using humidity packs, the buds should still be sufficiently dry before jarring, as the packs have a limited capacity and cannot compensate for very wet starting material. The packs are most useful for maintaining optimal conditions during the later stages of curing and for long-term storage.

## Common Curing Mistakes

Jarring buds too wet is the most common and dangerous curing mistake. The trapped moisture creates conditions favorable for mold growth, which can spread rapidly through an entire jar. When in doubt, allow buds to dry longer before jaring.

Not burping jars frequently enough, especially during the first two weeks, leads to anaerobic conditions that produce off-flavors and potential mold. Even with humidity packs, regular air exchange during early curing is important.

Storing curing jars in warm or light-exposed locations degrades cannabinoids and terpenes. Curing jars should always be stored in a cool, dark place. A cupboard, closet, or dedicated storage area is ideal.

Overfilling jars prevents proper air circulation and makes it difficult to monitor the buds. Filling jars to approximately three-quarters capacity allows adequate air space for moisture management and easy inspection.
