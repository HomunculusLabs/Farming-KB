# Goat Management and Browsing Systems

Bill Mollison's [[permaculture-designers-manual-bamboo]] presents goats as
among the most versatile and useful domestic animals in permaculture
systems. Their natural browsing behavior, adaptability to marginal
land, and multiple products (milk, meat, fiber, manure, and land
clearing) make them ideally suited to the [[permaculture-principles]]
of multiple functions and biological resource management.

## Goat Breeds and Selection

Mollison discusses several breed categories suited to different
climates and production goals:

- **Dairy breeds:** Saanen, Toggenburg, Alpine, Nubian, and La Mancha
  for high milk production. These require good nutrition and
  management but yield 2-4 liters daily during lactation.

- **Meat breeds:** Boer, Kiko, and Spanish goats for efficient weight
  gain on browse. These are hardier and more self-sufficient than
  dairy breeds, making them suitable for extensive systems.

- **Fiber breeds:** Angora and Cashmere goats produce mohair and
  cashmere respectively. These thrive on rough browse and provide a
  valuable non-food product.

- **Dual-purpose and landrace breeds:** Local adapted breeds that may
  not excel in any single output but demonstrate superior resilience
  to local conditions, parasites, and climate. Mollison consistently
  favors locally adapted genetics over imported high-production stock.

## Browsing Behavior and Diet

Goats are browsers rather than grazers, a distinction Mollison
considers critically important. While cattle and sheep primarily
consume grasses, goats preferentially select woody plants, forbs,
shrubs, and the growing tips of trees. This makes them complementary
to grazing animals in a multi-species system and uniquely valuable
for land clearing and weed management.

Their dietary preferences include many plants considered invasive or
undesirable: blackberry, gorse, thistle, bracken, multiflora rose,
kudzu, and saplings of weed tree species. A herd of goats can
substantially reduce woody encroachment on pasture and restore grass
dominance without herbicides or mechanical clearing.

Mollison warns that goats will also browse desirable trees and crops
if given access, so fencing and timing are essential management tools.
In [[mollison-designers-mixed-orchard-design-species-selection]], goats should be
excluded during fruit set and only introduced for post-harvest
cleanup when fallen fruit needs to be removed to break pest cycles.

## Rotational Grazing Systems

Mollison advocates rotational browsing as the foundation of goat
management. The basic principle is to confine goats to a defined
paddock for a short period (1-7 days depending on stocking density
and season) and then move them to fresh paddock, allowing the
previously browsed area to recover.

Key design considerations for rotational systems:

- **Paddock size:** Calculated based on stocking rate and desired rest
  period. Mollison recommends a minimum of 8-12 paddocks to achieve
  60-90 day rest periods during the growing season.

- **Water access:** Each paddock must have reliable water. Gravity-fed
  systems using elevated tanks reduce labor compared to hauling water.

- **Shade and shelter:** Goats are susceptible to heat stress and
  rain. Permanent or portable shade structures should be available in
  every paddock.

- **Internal fencing:** Electric fencing (single or double wire) is
  the most practical and cost-effective method for subdividing
  larger pastures. Goats respect electric fencing once trained.

## Fencing Design

Mollison describes several fencing options:

- **Perimeter fencing:** Woven wire or page wire at 1.0-1.2m height,
  with a strand of barbed or electric wire at the top and bottom.
  Goats test fences relentlessly, so perimeter must be robust.

- **Electric internal fencing:** Portable polywire or electro-net
  fencing for temporary paddock subdivision. These systems allow
  rapid reallocation of browsing area in response to forage
  availability.

- **Living fences:** Hedge fences of thorny species (osage orange,
  hawthorn, prickly pear) that provide browse, windbreak, and
  wildlife habitat in addition to stock control. Mollison considers
  living fences a superior long-term investment where establishment
  time permits.

## Milk Production

For dairy goats, Mollison outlines a management system tied to the
seasonal breeding cycle. Does kid once yearly, producing milk for
8-10 months before a dry period before the next kidding. Freshening
is timed to coincide with spring forage availability, reducing
supplemental feed costs.

Milking hygiene is critical. Mollison recommends a dedicated milking
stand, stainless steel equipment, and immediate cooling of milk.
Goat milk can be consumed fresh, cultured into cheese and yogurt, or
used as animal feed for poultry and pigs.

## Land Clearing Applications

One of the most valuable functions of goats in permaculture
establishment is initial land clearing. Mollison describes using
densely stocked goat herds (50-100 animals per hectare for short
periods) to clear regrowth, blackberry, and woody weeds from
degraded land prior to planting. This approach:

- Eliminates the need for herbicides or burning
- Returns manure to the site during clearing
- Reduces fire hazard by consuming fine fuels
- Leaves root systems intact, preventing erosion
- Converts unproductive weed biomass into animal weight gain
