Contaminant molds appear in various colors ranging from pastels (greens, pinks, yellows, oranges) to dark colors (black, dark green, gray). Bacterial contamination may manifest as slimy patches, wet-looking spots, or discoloration of the substrate that differs from the normal appearance.

Any visible growth of any color other than the white of healthy mycelium in an uninoculated jar definitively indicates that the sterilization process failed to kill all contaminant organisms present in the substrate, or that the jar was contaminated after sterilization through a breach in the tape or vermiculite barrier.

Since the control jar was never opened for inoculation, the only possible contamination pathways are through the lid holes (if the masking tape failed during or after sterilization) or through incomplete sterilization of the substrate itself.

### Step 4: Bacterial Odor Detection

Bacterial contamination can be detected through the dry vermiculite layer by carefully loosening the jar lid and checking for odor. Bacteria produce a distinctive sour, foul, or rancid smell that is unmistakable and immediately recognizable to anyone who has encountered it.

This odor can typically be detected within two days of the sterilization cycle if bacterial contamination is present. The speed of bacterial growth means that odor detection is often the earliest indicator of bacterial problems, sometimes preceding any visible signs.

When performing an odor check, loosen the lid only slightly, keep a safe distance from the jar opening, do not inhale deeply or directly over the jar, and wash hands thoroughly with soap after handling. Bacterial contamination can be dangerous to human health and should be treated with appropriate caution.

If the jar contains bacterial contamination, clean it carefully at a safe distance and dispose of the substrate in a sealed bag. The jar itself can be cleaned and reused after thorough washing.

## Interpreting Results

### Scenario A: Control Jar Shows Contamination

If the uninoculated control jar develops any colored growth or bacterial odor, the sterilization process itself is the confirmed problem. The spore syringe and inoculation technique may be perfectly fine, but they cannot compensate for substrate that was never rendered sterile in the first place.

The most likely cause is a sterilization time that is too short. The standard PF TEK specifies a minimum of one hour of gentle steaming, but some situations may require longer. The remedy is to extend the [[growing-gourmet-atmospheric-steam-sterilization]].

### Scenario B: Control Jar Clean, Production Jars Contaminated

If the control jar stays completely clean and unchanged for several days but one or more of the inoculated jars develop contamination, the sterilization was successful and the contaminant was introduced during the inoculation procedure. This narrows the investigation to inoculation-specific causes.
