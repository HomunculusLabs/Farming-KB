# Mollison on Zone One Garden Design

**Source:** Bill Mollison, Permaculture Design Course, Pamphlet IX (Permaculture Techniques), 1981
**Last Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Overview

Mollison provides extremely detailed practical guidance for designing the intensive zone one garden -- the area of highest energy throughput closest to the dwelling. This extends the zone concepts in [[mollison-pdc-introduction-principles]].

## Zone One Layout Principles

- Most intensive garden section around garden entries (mud room to kitchen path)
- Herb spiral and parsley mass near door; chives as second critical herb
- Garlic scattered throughout; comfrey for later plantings
- Plucking herbs border paths; harvesting vegetables (tubers, heads) lie behind
- "Nothing more stupid than wading through cabbages to get to parsley"

## Mulch vs Compost

- **Compost decreases nutrients** -- sometimes to 1/20th of original; hot conditions consume them
- **Sheet mulch** preserves nutrients; slow surface decomposition leaches into soil
- Worm castings: highest tonnage per acre, best manure, test alkaline
- Mulch stratification creates pH ranging from 6 to 3 over 2-3 years
- Kitchen wastes go directly under mulch (no composting needed)
- Weeds lifted, reversed (roots in air), dropped back under mulch

## Specific Plant Systems

### Potato Box
- Permanent 8x5 foot frame, no soil/bottom; poles as frame (bark rots, adds nutrient)
- Set potatoes 9x9 inches, scatter ashes, fill with straw; 12+ years continuous production
- Comfrey leaf wraps potato before planting (nutrient package)
- Mint pot beside for cooking

### Tomato System
- Permanent beds; south-facing shelter (Jerusalem artichoke windbreak)
- Basil on hot side, parsley on cool side, nasturtium for white fly control
- Gooseberries for specific tomato pests on cool side
- Marigolds in random clumps; mulch tomatoes under in autumn (200 seedlings per spot)
- Never re-buy seed; always save from mulched plants
- Pinch axil shoots for succession planting

### Corn-Bean-Squash System
- Band of corn 4 feet across, hollow in middle for manure and cucurbits
- Climbing beans around edge; harvest order: corn/beans first, then pumpkins
- Provides edge effect within the band

## Greenhouse Design

- Adjust to use reflection from winter snow or white quartzite
- Focus sun to mid-sky position (not due south)
- Grow vanilla, cinnamon (one pot supplies 100 households), tea, pineapple
- Pineapple needs ethylene from apples to set fruit
- Two-story or sunken glasshouses for bananas in cool climates
- West walls: storage/insulation only (zero net gain from windows)

## Mollison's Inventions

- **Black sand solar heater**: water pipes through black sand box under glass; far more efficient than metal collectors
- **Titanium oxide nitrogen fixer**: TiO2 catalyzes atmospheric nitrogen to ammonia in sunlight (endless fertilizer)
- **Soapstone thermal storage**: heat with focusing mirror, plug into house for cooking/heating
- **Sky-focusing saline pond**: evaporated seawater creates lens effect, concentrating heat
## Path Design and Access Patterns

The layout of paths in zone one determines how efficiently the garden can be managed and harvested. Mollison advocates for paths that follow the natural flow of movement between the house and garden, with the most frequently visited elements closest to the door. Wide main paths (at least 1 metre) allow wheelbarrow access for moving mulch materials and harvested produce, while narrow secondary paths between beds reduce wasted space. Keyhole bed designs, where a circular or U-shaped bed is accessed from a single central path, maximise growing area while minimising path space and ensure that all plants are within arm's reach from the path. The [[mollison-pdc-zone-one-vegetable-bed-design]] principles emphasise that paths should be mulched or paved with stepping stones to prevent mud and weed growth, and should never be left as bare soil that will erode and compact. Path layout should also consider the [[mollison-house-and-garden-layout]] to create a seamless transition between indoor and outdoor living spaces.
## Season Extension in Zone One

Zone one gardens can produce food year-round in most climates with appropriate season extension techniques. Cold frames placed over existing beds create a simple, low-cost method for extending the growing season by 4-8 weeks in both spring and autumn. Mollison's [[greenhouse-cool-climates-mollison]] integrate closely with zone one, providing protected growing space for tender perennials, early seedlings, and winter greens. The combination of [[coleman-unheated-greenhouse-design]] with intensive zone one beds allows continuous harvest even through cold winters. Succession planting -- sowing new crops as previous crops are harvested -- ensures continuous production throughout the growing season. Mollison emphasises planning the zone one garden so that something is always ready to harvest, creating a garden that feeds the household rather than producing a single glut followed by scarcity.
## Vertical Growing and Microclimates

Making full use of vertical space in zone one dramatically increases productivity per square metre. Trellises, arbours, and climbing frames support beans, peas, cucumbers, and climbing tomatoes while leaving ground-level space available for other crops. The [[mollison-pdc-trellis-systems-climate-control]] Mollison describes serve multiple functions: they increase growing area, create shade for heat-sensitive crops, and define garden spaces with vertical elements that add beauty and structure. Careful microclimate management within zone one allows growing species that would not normally thrive in the local climate: south-facing walls absorb heat during the day and release it at night, creating warm microclimates for tender species; north-facing walls provide cool, shaded conditions for salads and herbs during hot summers; and windbreaks from permanent plantings protect the entire zone one garden from desiccating winds. The [[mollison-microclimate-creation]] described in Mollison's work are particularly effective at the small scale of zone one, where even modest interventions can significantly modify growing conditions.
## Pest Management Integration

Zone one's intensive planting naturally supports integrated pest management through diversity and companion planting. The [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]] principles that Mollison incorporates into zone one design include: marigolds to deter nematodes and attract beneficial insects; nasturtiums as a trap crop for aphids and whitefly; alliums near roses and brassicas to repel specific pests; and flowering herbs like dill, fennel, and coriander to attract predatory wasps and hoverflies. The [[mollison-pdc-poultry-forage-systems]] can be integrated into zone one through chicken tractors that move across beds between crops, cleaning up pest insects and adding fertility. Zone one's proximity to the house makes frequent observation easy, allowing pest problems to be detected and addressed early before they become severe. This constant observation is itself a form of pest management, as the gardener who visits the garden daily will notice the first signs of infestation and can respond with targeted interventions rather than blanket treatments.
## Herb Spiral and Keyhole Design Details

The herb spiral is one of the most iconic zone one design elements, and Mollison provides detailed guidance on its construction and planting. A typical herb spiral is 1-1.5 metres in diameter at the base and rises to approximately 1 metre at the centre, creating a range of microclimates from a hot, dry south-facing slope at the top to a cool, moist shaded area at the base. The top of the spiral is planted with drought-tolerant Mediterranean herbs like rosemary, thyme, and oregano; the middle section with herbs that prefer moderate conditions like sage, coriander, and basil; and the bottom and north side with moisture-loving herbs like mint, chives, and parsley. Rocks placed in the spiral structure absorb heat during the day and release it at night, moderating temperature fluctuations and extending the growing season for heat-sensitive herbs. The [[mollison-gangammas-mandala-and-banana-circle-gardens]] extend the keyhole concept to larger scale, creating circular garden beds with radial paths that provide efficient access to all planted areas.
## Soil Health in Intensive Zone One Gardens

The intensive nature of zone one gardening demands particular attention to soil health. Constant harvesting removes nutrients that must be replaced, and the high density of planting can deplete soil organic matter faster than in less intensive zones. Mollison's approach to maintaining soil health in zone one centres on the [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]] system: continuous application of organic mulch materials feeds soil organisms, suppresses weeds, moderates soil temperature, and conserves moisture. The mulch layer also provides habitat for predatory beetles and spiders that help control pest populations. Mollison specifically warns against the conventional practice of digging and turning garden beds, which destroys soil structure, disrupts fungal networks, and releases carbon dioxide from the soil. Instead, he advocates for permanent beds that are never tilled, with organic matter added as surface mulch and incorporated by soil organisms. The [[mollison-humus-and-organic-matter-in-soils]] principles from the Designers' Manual provide detailed guidance for maintaining the biological vitality of intensively managed garden soils.

## Perennial Vegetables in Zone One

Zone one is not limited to annual vegetables; Mollison strongly advocates including perennial vegetables and herbs that provide reliable harvests year after year with minimal maintenance. Perennial species such as asparagus, globe artichokes, sea kale, perennial kale, walking onions, Good King Henry, and sorrel occupy permanent positions in the zone one garden and produce earlier in spring than most annual crops. The [[mollison-plant-nursery-species-selection]] for zone one perennials should prioritise varieties that are productive, disease-resistant, and suited to the local climate. Perennial vegetables also contribute to soil health through their permanent root systems, which create channels for water infiltration and habitat for soil organisms. The combination of perennial staples with annual successional plantings creates a zone one garden that provides continuous harvest with decreasing maintenance effort over time as the perennial component matures and establishes.
## Kitchen Garden Integration with Zone One

Mollison's zone one design extends beyond the vegetable garden to include the kitchen itself as an integral element of the food production system. The zone one [[holzer-kitchen-garden-herbs-and-medicinal-plants]] is designed as a direct extension of the kitchen, with the most frequently used herbs and salad greens growing immediately outside the door where they can be gathered fresh during meal preparation. The [[mollison-domestic-small-animals-zone-one]] in zone one, including chickens, rabbits, and bees, provide eggs, meat, honey, and pollination services while recycling kitchen scraps into fertiliser. The worm farm or compost bin that processes kitchen waste is positioned for convenient access from the kitchen door, and the finished compost is applied directly to the adjacent garden beds. This tight integration of food production, food preparation, and waste recycling in zone one creates a highly efficient micro-system where the distance between harvest and plate is measured in metres rather than kilometres. The [[mollison-house-and-garden-layout]] principles that Mollison describes ensure that the physical arrangement of buildings, paths, and garden beds supports this integration by minimising unnecessary movement and maximising the convenience of daily garden tasks.
## Zone One as a Learning Laboratory

For permaculture students and new practitioners, zone one serves as a manageable scale at which to learn and experiment with permaculture principles before applying them to larger areas. The [[holzer-self-sufficiency-garden-plan]] that Mollison outlines demonstrates how a relatively small area close to the home can produce a significant proportion of a household's food needs while serving as a testing ground for polyculture design, succession planting, integrated pest management, and water harvesting techniques. Because zone one is visited daily, experiments are observed closely and results are apparent quickly, providing rapid feedback that accelerates the learning process. Mollison recommended that every permaculture student establish a zone one garden as their first practical project, arguing that the skills and observations gained from managing an intensive home garden provide the foundation for all subsequent design work at larger scales. The [[mollison-pdc-introduction-principles]] that can seem abstract in a classroom become concrete and experiential when applied to the daily reality of a productive zone one garden.
## Microclimate Creation in Zone One

Mollison's zone one designs make extensive use of microclimate creation to extend growing seasons and increase the diversity of species that can be grown close to the home. Walls, fences, and hedges create warm, sheltered microclimates on their south-facing sides while providing wind protection on their lee sides. Water features such as small ponds or [[mollison-pdc-small-pond-frog-habitat-design]] moderate temperature extremes through their thermal mass and evaporation. Pergolas and trellises provide structure for climbing plants while creating shade patterns that can be used to position sun-loving and shade-tolerant species in close proximity. The [[faires-climate-zones-and-microclimate-design]] principles from the Designers' Manual show how these microclimate modifications can shift the effective climate zone of a zone one garden by a full zone or more, allowing subtropical species to be grown in temperate climates with appropriate protection. This intensive microclimate management is only practical in zone one, where the high visit frequency justifies the maintenance investment required to sustain these structures.
## Record Keeping and Seasonal Planning

Mollison emphasises the importance of systematic record keeping in zone one gardens, particularly for tracking planting dates, harvest yields, variety performance, and pest and disease observations. The [[mollison-domestic-small-animals-zone-one]] system that he recommends is deliberately simple: a garden notebook or calendar that records what was planted where, when it was harvested, and how it performed. Over successive seasons, these records build into an invaluable site-specific database that takes much of the guesswork out of variety selection, planting timing, and crop rotation decisions. Seasonal planning for zone one should begin with an assessment of the household's food needs and preferences, followed by a planting calendar that ensures continuous harvest throughout the growing season. The key is to plan for succession: as one crop is harvested, the next crop is already established and growing, ensuring that zone one beds are never left bare and unproductive for extended periods.
## Mulch Materials for Zone One

The choice of mulch materials in zone one has both practical and aesthetic dimensions. Mollison recommends using whatever organic materials are most readily available locally: straw, leaves, grass clippings, shredded newspaper, seaweed, or compost. In zone one, where appearance matters more than in outer zones, mulch can be selected for visual appeal as well as functionality. A layer of clean straw over a base of compost provides excellent weed suppression while looking neat and tidy. Living mulches such as white clover or sweet alyssum can be established between crop rows, providing nitrogen fixation, beneficial insect habitat, and weed suppression simultaneously. The [[mollison-mulching-and-soil-health]] principles from the Designers' Manual emphasise that the mulch layer should be maintained at 5-10 centimetres depth and replenished as it decomposes, ensuring continuous soil protection and organic matter input throughout the growing season.
