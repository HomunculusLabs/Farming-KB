## Zone One Layout Principles

- Most intensive garden section around garden entries (mud room to kitchen path)
- Herb spiral and parsley mass near door; chives as second critical herb
- Garlic scattered throughout; comfrey for later plantings
- Plucking herbs border paths; harvesting vegetables (tubers, heads) lie behind
- "Nothing more stupid than wading through cabbages to get to parsley"

## Mulch vs Compost

- **Compost decreases nutrients** -- sometimes to 1/20th of original; hot conditions consume them
- **Sheet mulch** preserves nutrients; slow surface decomposition leaches into soil
- Worm castings: highest tonnage per acre, best manure, test alkaline
- Mulch stratification creates pH ranging from 6 to 3 over 2-3 years
- Kitchen wastes go directly under mulch (no composting needed)
- Weeds lifted, reversed (roots in air), dropped back under mulch

## Specific Plant Systems

### Potato Box
- Permanent 8x5 foot frame, no soil/bottom; poles as frame (bark rots, adds nutrient)
- Set potatoes 9x9 inches, scatter ashes, fill with straw; 12+ years continuous production
- Comfrey leaf wraps potato before planting (nutrient package)
- Mint pot beside for cooking

### Tomato System
- Permanent beds; south-facing shelter (Jerusalem artichoke windbreak)
- Basil on hot side, parsley on cool side, nasturtium for white fly control
- Gooseberries for specific tomato pests on cool side
- Marigolds in random clumps; mulch tomatoes under in autumn (200 seedlings per spot)
- Never re-buy seed; always save from mulched plants
- Pinch axil shoots for succession planting

### Corn-Bean-Squash System
- Band of corn 4 feet across, hollow in middle for manure and cucurbits
- Climbing beans around edge; harvest order: corn/beans first, then pumpkins
- Provides edge effect within the band

## Greenhouse Design

- Adjust to use reflection from winter snow or white quartzite
- Focus sun to mid-sky position (not due south)
- Grow vanilla, cinnamon (one pot supplies 100 households), tea, pineapple
- Pineapple needs ethylene from apples to set fruit
- Two-story or sunken glasshouses for bananas in cool climates
- West walls: storage/insulation only (zero net gain from windows)

## Mollison's Inventions

- **Black sand solar heater**: water pipes through black sand box under glass; far more efficient than metal collectors
- **Titanium oxide nitrogen fixer**: TiO2 catalyzes atmospheric nitrogen to ammonia in sunlight (endless fertilizer)
- **Soapstone thermal storage**: heat with focusing mirror, plug into house for cooking/heating
- **Sky-focusing saline pond**: evaporated seawater creates lens effect, concentrating heat
## Path Design and Access Patterns

The layout of paths in zone one determines how efficiently the garden can be managed and harvested. Mollison advocates for paths that follow the natural flow of movement between the house and garden, with the most frequently visited elements closest to the door. Wide main paths (at least 1 metre) allow wheelbarrow access for moving mulch materials and harvested produce, while narrow secondary paths between beds reduce wasted space. Keyhole bed designs, where a circular or U-shaped bed is accessed from a single central path, maximise growing area while minimising path space and ensure that all plants are within arm's reach from the path. ## Season Extension in Zone One

Zone one gardens can produce food year-round in most climates with appropriate season extension techniques. Cold frames placed over existing beds create a simple, low-cost method for extending the growing season by 4-8 weeks in both spring and autumn. Mollison's [[coleman-unheated-greenhouse-design]] with intensive zone one beds allows continuous harvest even through cold winters. Succession planting -- sowing new crops as previous crops are harvested -- ensures continuous production throughout the growing season. Mollison emphasises planning the zone one garden so that something is always ready to harvest, creating a garden that feeds the household rather than producing a single glut followed by scarcity.
## Vertical Growing and Microclimates

Making full use of vertical space in zone one dramatically increases productivity per square metre. Trellises, arbours, and climbing frames support beans, peas, cucumbers, and climbing tomatoes while leaving ground-level space available for other crops. The [[mollison-microclimate-creation]] described in Mollison's work are particularly effective at the small scale of zone one, where even modest interventions can significantly modify growing conditions.
## Pest Management Integration

Zone one's intensive planting naturally supports integrated pest management through diversity and companion planting. The [[mollison-pdc-poultry-forage-systems]] can be integrated into zone one through chicken tractors that move across beds between crops, cleaning up pest insects and adding fertility. Zone one's proximity to the house makes frequent observation easy, allowing pest problems to be detected and addressed early before they become severe. This constant observation is itself a form of pest management, as the gardener who visits the garden daily will notice the first signs of infestation and can respond with targeted interventions rather than blanket treatments.
## Herb Spiral and Keyhole Design Details

The herb spiral is one of the most iconic zone one design elements, and Mollison provides detailed guidance on its construction and planting. A typical herb spiral is 1-1.5 metres in diameter at the base and rises to approximately 1 metre at the centre, creating a range of microclimates from a hot, dry south-facing slope at the top to a cool, moist shaded area at the base. The top of the spiral is planted with drought-tolerant Mediterranean herbs like rosemary, thyme, and oregano; the middle section with herbs that prefer moderate conditions like sage, coriander, and basil; and the bottom and north side with moisture-loving herbs like mint, chives, and parsley. Rocks placed in the spiral structure absorb heat during the day and release it at night, moderating temperature fluctuations and extending the growing season for heat-sensitive herbs. The mulch layer also provides habitat for predatory beetles and spiders that help control pest populations. Mollison specifically warns against the conventional practice of digging and turning garden beds, which destroys soil structure, disrupts fungal networks, and releases carbon dioxide from the soil. ## Perennial Vegetables in Zone One

Zone one is not limited to annual vegetables; Mollison strongly advocates including perennial vegetables and herbs that provide reliable harvests year after year with minimal maintenance. Perennial species such as asparagus, globe artichokes, sea kale, perennial kale, walking onions, Good King Henry, and sorrel occupy permanent positions in the zone one garden and produce earlier in spring than most annual crops. The [[holzer-kitchen-garden-herbs-and-medicinal-plants]] is designed as a direct extension of the kitchen, with the most frequently used herbs and salad greens growing immediately outside the door where they can be gathered fresh during meal preparation. The [[mollison-house-and-garden-layout]] principles that Mollison describes ensure that the physical arrangement of buildings, paths, and garden beds supports this integration by minimising unnecessary movement and maximising the convenience of daily garden tasks.
## Zone One as a Learning Laboratory

For permaculture students and new practitioners, zone one serves as a manageable scale at which to learn and experiment with permaculture principles before applying them to larger areas. The [[mollison-pdc-introduction-principles]] that can seem abstract in a classroom become concrete and experiential when applied to the daily reality of a productive zone one garden.
## Microclimate Creation in Zone One

Mollison's zone one designs make extensive use of microclimate creation to extend growing seasons and increase the diversity of species that can be grown close to the home. Walls, fences, and hedges create warm, sheltered microclimates on their south-facing sides while providing wind protection on their lee sides. Water features such as small ponds or [[faires-climate-zones-and-microclimate-design]] principles from the Designers' Manual show how these microclimate modifications can shift the effective climate zone of a zone one garden by a full zone or more, allowing subtropical species to be grown in temperate climates with appropriate protection. This intensive microclimate management is only practical in zone one, where the high visit frequency justifies the maintenance investment required to sustain these structures.
## Record Keeping and Seasonal Planning

Mollison emphasises the importance of systematic record keeping in zone one gardens, particularly for tracking planting dates, harvest yields, variety performance, and pest and disease observations. The [[mollison-mulching-and-soil-health]] principles from the Designers' Manual emphasise that the mulch layer should be maintained at 5-10 centimetres depth and replenished as it decomposes, ensuring continuous soil protection and organic matter input throughout the growing season.
