# Vegetableatarian Experiment — Calorie Production from Home Gardens

## Overview

[[steve-solomon|Steve Solomon]]'s *Gardening When It Counts* (2005) includes a remarkable personal account of living primarily from his vegetable garden and seed company trials for three years (1980-1983). During this period, he demonstrated that a home garden can provide the majority of a household's food supply, including caloric needs, at extremely low cost. His "vegetableatarian" experiment provides concrete data on the relationship between garden size, calorie production, and food costs.

## The Experiment: 1980-1983

### Context and Motivation

After founding Territorial Seed Company in 1979, Solomon operated a half-acre variety trial ground alongside his five-acre homestead. In 1980 and 1981, he "mainly ate the trials" — consuming the vegetables grown for seed company testing purposes. This was not a planned dietary experiment but a pragmatic response to economic necessity combined with an abundance of fresh produce.

### Income and Budget

Solomon's financial situation during this period was instructive:

- **Initial salary:** Unable to take any salary from the seed company initially
- **Living expenses:** $4,000 per year for absolute necessities and property taxes
- **Income source:** Savings account used as reserve against "[[fukuoka-textdoc-what-plowing-really-is|really]] hard times"
- **Monthly draw after 1983:** $1,000/month (doubled the next year)
- **Business profitability:** Achieved by 1983 after years of reinvestment

The fact that Solomon could sustain himself on $4,000/year (approximately $333/month in early 1980s dollars) was directly attributable to garden food production displacing the majority of grocery purchases.

## Diet Composition

### Primary Food Sources

The household diet during the experimental period consisted of:

- **Vegetables (majority of calories):** Grown in the home garden and trials ground
- **Apples:** From the homestead's old orchard
- **Blackberries:** Picked during high summer, stored frozen in a chest freezer in the woodshed
- **Frozen bananas:** Purchased as "overripes" at super-bargain prices
- **Purchased supplements ([[coleman-cool-house-minimal-supplementary-heat|minimal]]):**
  - Occasional brown rice or millet
  - Sometimes a chunk of ordinary cheese
  - Butter or milk from a neighbor
  - Olive oil
  - Occasional pineapple from Queensland and imported luxuries

### Seed Company Leftovers

An important and often overlooked food source was the seed company's leftover stock:

- **Below-germination-threshold seeds:** When germination percentage dropped below what was ethical to sell, Solomon brought the seeds home
- **Cooked as food:** Bean, pea, grain, and other crop seeds were cooked and eaten
- **Zero waste:** Nothing from the trials was discarded — everything edible was consumed

This practice represents an efficient integration of seed business operations with household food security, though it is specific to running a seed company and not generally replicable.

## The "Vegetableatarian" Concept

### Not Vegetarian

Solomon explicitly states: "You have to be a pacifist or a vegan? It's not an absolute thing. There's no halfway." His own dietary evolution was gradual and pragmatic rather than ideological:

- **Youth:** "Meat and potatoes man"
- **Age 31:** Began shifting toward vegetable-heavy diet
- **Experimental period:** Diet became primarily garden-grown vegetables
- **Post-experiment:** Maintained substantial home food production but added back some animal products

### Caloric Adequacy

A critical question for any vegetable-based diet is whether it can meet caloric needs. Solomon's data suggests:

- **Garden area:** ~2,000 square feet of growing beds (later doubled to 4,000+ with [[solomon-garden-ley-rotation-soil-restoration]])
- **Household size:** Two adults
- **Caloric contribution:** Vegetables made up approximately half the daily calories
- **Supplementation:** Potatoes and sweet potatoes can provide the remaining caloric base

Solomon notes that to produce nearly all calories (not just half), approximately 5,000+ square feet (465 m²) of growing area per adult is needed, assuming potatoes or sweet potatoes as the primary caloric crop.

## Cost Efficiency Analysis

### Food Budget Reduction

Solomon presents a compelling cost-efficiency framework:

> "You can do that or more, and you may want to seriously consider the return on your investment in time and money"

His analysis is explicitly input-based: comparing total calories consumed against the cost of producing them. The key insight is that when you grow your own food, the "cost" is primarily your time and the cost of inputs (seeds, tools, fertilizer), not the retail price of the food.

### Cost Categories

During the experimental period, food costs were reduced to:

| Category | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Garden inputs (seeds, tools) | Minimal | Seed company provided seed at cost |
| Purchased staples | Very low | Occasional rice, cheese, oil |
| Imported luxuries | Discretionary | Pineapple, specialty items |
| **Total annual food budget** | **Well under $1,000** | Excluding the garden itself |

### The $4,000 Annual Budget

The $4,000/year that covered "absolute necessities" including food, property taxes, and all other expenses demonstrates that a garden-based food system can dramatically reduce household costs. In modern terms (adjusted for inflation), this would be roughly equivalent to $12,000-15,000/year, still remarkably low for a two-adult household.

## Practical Lessons

### Garden Scale vs. Caloric Output

Solomon's experience reveals an important scaling relationship:

- **2,000 ft² growing beds:** ~50% of household calories
- **4,000 ft² growing beds (with ley rotation):** Can approach 100% with potato-centric strategy
- **Diminishing returns:** The first 2,000 ft² provides the highest return per square foot; additional area adds calories but at lower efficiency

### The Role of Potatoes

Solomon repeatedly emphasizes potatoes as the key to garden-based calorie self-sufficiency:

- **Caloric density:** Potatoes provide more calories per unit area than any other temperate-climate vegetable
- **Storage:** [[comparison-root-cellaring-guide-vs-canning-and-preserving]] preserves potatoes for months without refrigeration
- **Nutrition:** Complete protein complemented by dairy, plus vitamin C, potassium, and fiber
- **Yield:** Good potato varieties can produce 10-20 pounds per 10-foot row
- **Land efficiency:** Producing the same calories from cereal grains would require 5-10 times more land

### Seasonal Eating

The experimental diet was inherently seasonal:

- **Summer abundance:** Fresh vegetables, berries, tree fruit
- **Winter scarcity:** Stored potatoes, frozen berries, purchased staples
- **No greenhouse:** Solomon's Tasmanian garden relied on seasonal production without artificial [[cervantes-season-extension-cloches-row-covers]]
- **Preservation:** Minimal — primarily freezing berries; root cellaring for potatoes and root crops

### Integration with Livestock

Solomon's "vegetableatarian" experiment was nearly but not entirely animal-product-free. The occasional butter, milk, and cheese from a neighbor suggest that small-scale [[mollison-animal-integration-permaculture-design]] (even if just purchasing from nearby producers) provides important nutritional supplementation that is difficult to achieve from vegetables alone:

- **Vitamin B12:** Not available from plant sources
- **Fat-soluble vitamins:** A, D, K more bioavailable from animal sources
- **Complete protein:** Easier to achieve with some animal protein
- **Caloric density:** Fats and oils from animal sources are energy-dense
