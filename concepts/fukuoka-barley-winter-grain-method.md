# Fukuoka Barley and Winter Grain Method

updated: 2026-04-18

Masanobu Fukuoka's barley and wheat cultivation evolved through three stages: (1) tillage and ridge cultivation, (2) level-row, light-tillage or no-tillage cultivation, and (3) natural cropping based on no-tillage cultivation. The final method requires no plowing, no chemical fertilizers, and minimal labor.

## Historical Context

Barley and wheat were second only to rice in importance as Japanese food staples. As recently as twenty years before Fukuoka's writing, paddy fields were always double-cropped with a winter grain after the rice harvest. The traditional process was extremely laborious:

1. Plow the field
2. Break up clods of earth
3. Make seed furrows
4. Sow seed in furrows
5. Cover seed with dirt
6. Apply prepared compost
7. First weeding before year's end
8. Second and third weeding in the new year
9. Loosen soil with a hoe between rows
10. Gather soil around plant bases to prevent frost damage
11. Trample shoots to promote root growth
12. Spray pesticide twice
13. Harvest at end of May (often during rainy season, requiring grain drying)

This arduous process was driven not by money or labor efficiency but by pride -- the farmer was afraid of being called lazy for leaving fields fallow over winter.

## Fukuoka's Three Stages of Development

### Stage 1: Tillage, Ridging, and Drilling
Naked barley and wheat seed was normally drilled at 6-7 inch seeding width on ridges spaced 3 feet apart. Fukuoka experimented with wider seeding (25-40% increase) but found no yield improvement and reduced stability. He then tried multiple rows per ridge (2, 3, 4 rows) which eventually led to low ridges almost level with the ground.

### Stage 2: Light-Tillage, Low-Ridge Cultivation
Switched to light-tillage and drilled individual seeds in straight, narrow rows. Discovered that naked barley susceptible to moisture damage during light-tillage actually performed better with no-tillage. Began studying seeding techniques for unplowed fields.

Key discovery: sowing ladino clover as ground cover together with barley, and scattering rice straw over the planted field, showed great promise for weed control. At the time, agricultural experts strictly forbade leaving straw on paddy fields for fear of disease, but Fukuoka confirmed that rice straw left on the ground during autumn decomposes entirely by spring, leaving no trace of pathogenic microbes.

### Stage 3: No-Tillage, Direct-Seeding Cultivation
Built an experimental seeding device; tried dibbling, drilling, and individual seeding in furrows. Combined with full straw cover, became convinced of direct seeding without tillage. Went from sparse to dense seeding, then returned to sparse before settling on broadcast seeding.

Conclusions from experiments:
- No-tillage cultivation not only does not degrade the land, it improves and enriches it (demonstrated by 10+ years of continuous no-tillage rice/winter grain succession)
- The method is extremely simple, yet provides total germination and weed control with less labor and higher yields than other methods
- Full potential can only be tapped by combining with natural farming rotation and direct-seeded rice

## The Natural Method

The realization that rice and barley, both grass family members, should be grown the same way was key. Fukuoka found that mixed seeding and even simultaneous seeding were possible. Barley seed is broadcast directly over standing rice or immediately after rice harvest, covered by scattered rice straw, and left until the late spring harvest.

### Fertilization
- After rice harvest: 650-900 lbs chicken manure per quarter-acre
- Late February top dressing: additional 200 lbs during barley heading stage
- From a natural farming standpoint, releasing ten ducklings per quarter-acre is preferable -- they weed, pick off insects, and turn soil

### Straw Mulching
All straw and chaff must be scattered uncut over the entire field surface. Barley straw must be returned immediately after threshing because once wet by rain it becomes five times heavier and potassium leaches out. Spreading fresh straw is described as "quite a bold and revolutionary step in rice farming."

### Key Insight
Seventy percent of nitrogenous components absorbed by rice and barley are supplied directly by the soil. If all straw and chaff were returned to fields, farmers would need to apply at most fifteen percent of nitrogenous requirements artificially.

## Fukuoka's Duck Integration System

One of the most [[metzner-three-distinctive-features-shamanic-entheogenic-ceremonies]] of Fukuoka's [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] method is the integration of ducks into the rice-barley rotation. Rather than applying [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]], Fukuoka released ten ducklings per quarter-acre into the fields after the rice seedlings had established. The ducks served multiple functions simultaneously: they consumed insects and pests (including the rice borers and leafhoppers that plague conventional paddies), they grazed on weeds (particularly the competitive grasses that would otherwise require hand weeding), and their paddling action in the shallow water oxygenated the soil and prevented the soil from compacting. Their manure provided a steady source of nitrogen and other nutrients throughout the growing season. The ducks were kept in the field until the rice plants began to flower, at which point they were removed to prevent them from eating the ripening grain. This duck-rice integration is an early example of integrated pest management and livestock-crop integration that has since been adopted and refined by farmers in Japan, Southeast Asia, and beyond. The system demonstrates Fukuoka's core principle that nature already provides all the functions that farmers attempt to replace with chemical inputs — the challenge is to design systems that allow natural processes to operate freely. Fukuoka's observation that soil improves under no-till rather than degrading has been confirmed by decades of subsequent research in conservation agriculture worldwide.

## Fukuoka's Philosophy of "Doing Nothing"

The barley winter grain method cannot be fully understood apart from Fukuoka's broader philosophical framework. His concept of "doing nothing" does not mean literal inaction but rather the elimination of unnecessary human interventions that disrupt natural processes. Fukuoka argued that modern agriculture's problems — soil degradation, pest outbreaks, dependence on chemical inputs, farmer indebtedness — are all consequences of trying to improve upon nature rather than working within its patterns. His method identifies and removes the specific human actions (plowing, weeding, applying fertilizer, spraying pesticides) that are unnecessary when natural processes are allowed to function. What remains — broadcasting seed, [[fukuoka-scattering-straw-technique]], occasional management of clover and ducks — is the minimum set of human actions needed to guide the system toward food production while maintaining ecological health. This philosophy is elaborated in his seminal work [[fukuoka-natural-diet-theory]], which extends the same principles to human nutrition and health. ## Weed Ecology in Natural Farming

Fukuoka's approach to weed management represents a fundamental departure from conventional agriculture. Rather than treating weeds as enemies to be eliminated, his method views them as indicators of soil conditions and competitors that can be managed through [[williams-pdc-ecological-design-principles]] rather than chemical or mechanical warfare. The key insight is that weeds are opportunistic — they colonize bare soil. By maintaining continuous ground cover through living mulches (clover), dead mulches (rice and barley straw), and dense crop plantings, the conditions that favor weed establishment are eliminated. Fukuoka's rice straw mulch serves as a physical barrier to weed seed germination while also improving soil structure and moisture retention as it decomposes. He also observed that certain "weeds" are actually beneficial — providing habitat for predatory insects, breaking up compacted soil with deep taproots, and cycling nutrients from subsoil layers. The few weeds that do emerge in his system are managed by occasional hand-pulling before they set seed, or by the trampling action of ducks released into the field. Over time, the weed seed bank in the soil is depleted through natural germination and decay, further reducing weed pressure. This philosophy aligns with the broader [[fukuoka-road-one-straw-revolution-continuation]] demonstrates the broad applicability of these methods.
