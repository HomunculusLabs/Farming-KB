# Fukuoka Barley and Winter Grain Method

updated: 2026-04-18

Masanobu Fukuoka's barley and wheat cultivation evolved through three stages: (1) tillage and ridge cultivation, (2) level-row, light-tillage or no-tillage cultivation, and (3) natural cropping based on no-tillage cultivation. The final method requires no plowing, no chemical fertilizers, and minimal labor.

## Historical Context

Barley and wheat were second only to rice in importance as Japanese food staples. As recently as twenty years before Fukuoka's writing, paddy fields were always double-cropped with a winter grain after the rice harvest. The traditional process was extremely laborious:

1. Plow the field
2. Break up clods of earth
3. Make seed furrows
4. Sow seed in furrows
5. Cover seed with dirt
6. Apply prepared compost
7. First weeding before year's end
8. Second and third weeding in the new year
9. Loosen soil with a hoe between rows
10. Gather soil around plant bases to prevent frost damage
11. Trample shoots to promote root growth
12. Spray pesticide twice
13. Harvest at end of May (often during rainy season, requiring grain drying)

This arduous process was driven not by money or labor efficiency but by pride -- the farmer was afraid of being called lazy for leaving fields fallow over winter.

## Fukuoka's Three Stages of Development

### Stage 1: Tillage, Ridging, and Drilling
Naked barley and wheat seed was normally drilled at 6-7 inch seeding width on ridges spaced 3 feet apart. Fukuoka experimented with wider seeding (25-40% increase) but found no yield improvement and reduced stability. He then tried multiple rows per ridge (2, 3, 4 rows) which eventually led to low ridges almost level with the ground.

### Stage 2: Light-Tillage, Low-Ridge Cultivation
Switched to light-tillage and drilled individual seeds in straight, narrow rows. Discovered that naked barley susceptible to moisture damage during light-tillage actually performed better with no-tillage. Began studying seeding techniques for unplowed fields.

Key discovery: sowing ladino clover as ground cover together with barley, and scattering rice straw over the planted field, showed great promise for weed control. At the time, agricultural experts strictly forbade leaving straw on paddy fields for fear of disease, but Fukuoka confirmed that rice straw left on the ground during autumn decomposes entirely by spring, leaving no trace of pathogenic microbes.

### Stage 3: No-Tillage, Direct-Seeding Cultivation
Built an experimental seeding device; tried dibbling, drilling, and individual seeding in furrows. Combined with full straw cover, became convinced of direct seeding without tillage. Went from sparse to dense seeding, then returned to sparse before settling on broadcast seeding.

Conclusions from experiments:
- No-tillage cultivation not only does not degrade the land, it improves and enriches it (demonstrated by 10+ years of continuous no-tillage rice/winter grain succession)
- The method is extremely simple, yet provides total germination and weed control with less labor and higher yields than other methods
- Full potential can only be tapped by combining with natural farming rotation and direct-seeded rice

## The Natural Method

The realization that rice and barley, both grass family members, should be grown the same way was key. Fukuoka found that mixed seeding and even simultaneous seeding were possible. Barley seed is broadcast directly over standing rice or immediately after rice harvest, covered by scattered rice straw, and left until the late spring harvest.

### Fertilization
- After rice harvest: 650-900 lbs chicken manure per quarter-acre
- Late February top dressing: additional 200 lbs during barley heading stage
- From a natural farming standpoint, releasing ten ducklings per quarter-acre is preferable -- they weed, pick off insects, and turn soil

### Straw Mulching
All straw and chaff must be scattered uncut over the entire field surface. Barley straw must be returned immediately after threshing because once wet by rain it becomes five times heavier and potassium leaches out. Spreading fresh straw is described as "quite a bold and revolutionary step in rice farming."

### Key Insight
Seventy percent of nitrogenous components absorbed by rice and barley are supplied directly by the soil. If all straw and chaff were returned to fields, farmers would need to apply at most fifteen percent of nitrogenous requirements artificially.

## Fukuoka's Duck Integration System

One of the most distinctive features of Fukuoka's natural farming method is the integration of ducks into the rice-barley rotation. Rather than applying chemical fertilizers, Fukuoka released ten ducklings per quarter-acre into the fields after the rice seedlings had established. The ducks served multiple functions simultaneously: they consumed insects and pests (including the rice borers and leafhoppers that plague conventional paddies), they grazed on weeds (particularly the competitive grasses that would otherwise require hand weeding), and their paddling action in the shallow water oxygenated the soil and prevented the soil from compacting. Their manure provided a steady source of nitrogen and other nutrients throughout the growing season. The ducks were kept in the field until the rice plants began to flower, at which point they were removed to prevent them from eating the ripening grain. This duck-rice integration is an early example of integrated pest management and livestock-crop integration that has since been adopted and refined by farmers in Japan, Southeast Asia, and beyond. The system demonstrates Fukuoka's core principle that nature already provides all the functions that farmers attempt to replace with chemical inputs — the challenge is to design systems that allow natural processes to operate freely. ## Soil Microbiology in No-Till Barley

The long-term success of Fukuoka's no-tillage barley method depends on a healthy and diverse soil microbial community. Conventional tillage disrupts fungal hyphae, kills earthworms, and releases stored soil carbon as CO2. By eliminating tillage entirely, Fukuoka's method preserves and builds soil biological networks. The permanent ground cover (clover living mulch, straw mulch, and crop residues) provides a continuous food source for soil microorganisms, which in turn cycle nutrients, suppress pathogens, and improve soil structure through the production of glomalin and other soil-binding compounds. Mycorrhizal fungi, which are severely damaged by tillage, form associations with both rice and barley roots in Fukuoka's system, extending the effective root zone and improving phosphorus uptake. The soil in his fields developed a characteristic crumbly, dark structure with high aggregate stability — the hallmark of biologically active, well-structured soil. This soil quality is directly related to the [[query-what-is-the-soil-food-web-and-why-does-it-matter]] complexity that develops under continuous no-till management with organic matter return. Fukuoka's observation that soil improves under no-till rather than degrading has been confirmed by decades of subsequent research in conservation agriculture worldwide.

## Fukuoka's Philosophy of "Doing Nothing"

The barley winter grain method cannot be fully understood apart from Fukuoka's broader philosophical framework. His concept of "doing nothing" does not mean literal inaction but rather the elimination of unnecessary human interventions that disrupt natural processes. Fukuoka argued that modern agriculture's problems — soil degradation, pest outbreaks, dependence on chemical inputs, farmer indebtedness — are all consequences of trying to improve upon nature rather than working within its patterns. His method identifies and removes the specific human actions (plowing, weeding, applying fertilizer, spraying pesticides) that are unnecessary when natural processes are allowed to function. What remains — broadcasting seed, scattering straw, occasional management of clover and ducks — is the minimum set of human actions needed to guide the system toward food production while maintaining ecological health. This philosophy is elaborated in his seminal work [[fukuoka-road-one-straw-revolution-continuation]] and in [[fukuoka-natural-diet-theory]], which extends the same principles to human nutrition and health. The [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] has influenced permaculture, regenerative agriculture, and conservation agriculture movements worldwide.

## Seed Ball Technique for Direct Seeding

Fukuoka developed the seed ball (tsuchi dango) technique as a method for direct-seeding grains and vegetables without tillage. Seeds are encased in balls of clay-rich soil mixed with compost and sometimes inoculated with beneficial microorganisms. The clay protects the seeds from birds, insects, and desiccation until rainfall softens the ball and allows germination. The compost within the ball provides initial nutrients for the seedling. Fukuoka used seed balls extensively for broadcasting vegetables and cover crops into his rice and barley fields, allowing him to grow dozens of vegetable species simultaneously without any tillage or bed preparation. The seed ball technique has been widely adopted in natural farming and guerrilla gardening communities around the world. It is particularly valuable for establishing plants in degraded or compacted soils where conventional seeding would fail. ## The Clover Living Mulch System

A critical component of Fukuoka's natural barley method is the use of white clover (Trifolium repens) as a permanent living mulch. The clover serves multiple functions simultaneously: it fixes atmospheric nitrogen through its symbiotic relationship with Rhizobium bacteria, provides a dense ground cover that suppresses weed germination by blocking light, retains soil moisture by reducing evaporation, and protects the soil surface from erosion and compaction. When barley is broadcast into the standing clover, the clover acts as a nurse crop — sheltering the germinating barley seeds and seedlings while continuing to provide nitrogen. As the barley grows taller, it shades the clover, naturally reducing its vigor. After barley harvest, the clover recovers and again dominates the field until the next rice crop is established. This year-round living mulch eliminates the need for both synthetic nitrogen fertilizer and herbicides. Fukuoka found that maintaining the clover required minimal management — occasional mowing if it grew too thick, and allowing it to reseed naturally. The clover-barley-rice rotation creates a self-sustaining system where the three species support each other without external inputs. This approach is closely related to modern [[comparison-crop-rotation-vs-cover-cropping]] strategies used in organic and conservation agriculture.

## Weed Ecology in Natural Farming

Fukuoka's approach to weed management represents a fundamental departure from conventional agriculture. Rather than treating weeds as enemies to be eliminated, his method views them as indicators of soil conditions and competitors that can be managed through ecological design rather than chemical or mechanical warfare. The key insight is that weeds are opportunistic — they colonize bare soil. By maintaining continuous ground cover through living mulches (clover), dead mulches (rice and barley straw), and dense crop plantings, the conditions that favor weed establishment are eliminated. Fukuoka's rice straw mulch serves as a physical barrier to weed seed germination while also improving soil structure and moisture retention as it decomposes. He also observed that certain "weeds" are actually beneficial — providing habitat for predatory insects, breaking up compacted soil with deep taproots, and cycling nutrients from subsoil layers. The few weeds that do emerge in his system are managed by occasional hand-pulling before they set seed, or by the trampling action of ducks released into the field. Over time, the weed seed bank in the soil is depleted through natural germination and decay, further reducing weed pressure. This philosophy aligns with the broader [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] of working with natural processes rather than against them.

## Yields and Long-Term Results

Fukuoka's natural barley and rice method consistently produced yields comparable to or exceeding conventional methods on his farm in Ehime Prefecture, Japan. He reported barley yields of approximately 400-500 kg per quarter-acre and rice yields of similar magnitude, achieved with no-tillage, no chemical fertilizers, no pesticides, and minimal labor input (primarily broadcasting seed and scattering straw). After more than three decades of continuous no-tillage cultivation, he observed that his soil had improved markedly — becoming darker, more crumbly, and more moisture-retentive. The soil's microbial activity increased, evidenced by the rapid decomposition of straw mulch and the presence of abundant earthworms. Importantly, the cost of production was dramatically lower than conventional methods because there was no expenditure on machinery fuel, chemical inputs, or hired labor. While some critics have noted that Fukuoka's results are specific to his climate and soil conditions, the underlying principles — permanent ground cover, living mulches, straw return, and minimal disturbance — have been successfully adapted to diverse climates around the world. The [[fukuoka-road-one-straw-revolution-continuation]] demonstrates the broad applicability of these methods.
