# Neurotropic Fungi Volatility and Potency Degradation

The active indole alkaloids in neurotropic fungi, particularly psilocybin and psilocin, are subject to degradation over time. This degradation has significant implications for both scientific chemical analysis and the traditional knowledge of indigenous peoples who have used these fungi for centuries. The volatility of these compounds means that storage conditions, specimen age, and handling methods all affect measured potency and psychoactive effects.






## Chemical Stability of Psilocybin and Psilocin

Psilocybin (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) and its dephosphorylated counterpart psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) are the primary psychoactive compounds found in bluing species of Psilocybe, Conocybe, Copelandia, and other neurotropic genera. These indole derivatives are not indefinitely stable in dried fungal tissue.

Repke, Leslie, and Guzmán (1977a) demonstrated that the presence of baeocystin, a related indole alkaloid, varies significantly according to the age of the studied materials. In specimens of Psilocybe baeocystis and Psilocybe cyanescens analyzed 20 to 60 days after collection, no trace of indoles could be detected. This finding suggests that even relatively short delays between collection and analysis can yield false-negative results, a critical issue for taxonomic and chemical studies of these fungi.

Ohenoja, Jokiranta, Mustola, Kaikkonen, and Airaksinen (1987) provided quantitative data on this degradation in herbarium specimens of Psilocybe semilanceata. They measured psilocybin content at three different collection dates: specimens from 1869 contained only 0.014 percent dry weight, those from 1954 contained 0.67 percent, and specimens from 1976 contained 0.84 percent. This clear inverse correlation between specimen age and alkaloid content confirms that psilocybin gradually degrades over decades of herbarium storage.






## Volatility and Airborne Exposure

A remarkable observation by Guzmán provides anecdotal but compelling evidence for the volatility of psilocybin and related compounds. While conducting fieldwork in Oaxaca, Mexico in 1958, Guzmán was trying to sleep in a small, closed-door room of an indigenous home. The room contained a large collection of fresh or semifermented neurotropic specimens that had been gathered with the help of local Indians for commercial purposes. The mushrooms were kept in sacks and included a mixture of Psilocybe caerulescens, P. cordispora, P. cubensis, P. mexicana, and P. zapotecorum.

Despite having consumed no fungi himself, Guzmán experienced colored hallucinations during the night. The air of the room was heavy with a strong acrid aroma from the fungal collection. When he stepped outside into fresh air, the hallucinations ceased immediately. This experience suggests that psilocybin, psilocin, or their volatile precursors or degradation products can become airborne in sufficient concentrations to produce psychoactive effects through inhalation in enclosed spaces with large quantities of fresh or fermenting material.






## Indigenous Knowledge of Potency Loss

The indigenous peoples of Mexico, particularly the Mazatec and Mixtec Indians of Oaxaca, have developed sophisticated practical knowledge about the potency of sacred mushrooms over centuries of use. They wisely advise that old dried specimens kept for more than one year are not good to use and should be discarded. This traditional knowledge aligns precisely with the scientific findings on alkaloid degradation.

Guzmán personally verified this indigenous wisdom through experimentation. He observed that normal doses of hallucinogenic fungi, specifically Psilocybe mexicana in one case and P. caerulescens in another, were only slightly neurotropic or entirely inactive when consumed, because the fungi had been kept dried for almost a year. This confirmation of traditional knowledge by a leading mycologist underscores the depth of empirical understanding possessed by these indigenous communities.






## Implications for Chemical Studies

The degradation of psilocybin and related compounds has important consequences for the reliability of chemical analyses reported in the mycological literature. Several studies that reported negative results for the presence of psilocybin in known neurotropic species may have been working with degraded material rather than genuinely non-active specimens.

This is particularly relevant for the many controversial or contradictory chemical analyses that have been published. For example, some researchers reported finding psilocybin in Panaeolina foenisecii while others did not, and similar discrepancies exist for numerous other species. While taxonomic misidentification accounts for some of these contradictions, the age and storage conditions of the analyzed material likely contributed to many false-negative results.

The case of Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides illustrates this point well. Hofmann, working with Heim and Wasson (1958), did not find any indole compounds in specimens that Wasson had gathered in a popular market in Mexico. These specimens had been preserved for a long time as sacred fungi. Given the known degradation pathways of indole alkaloids, it is entirely possible that these fungi originally contained detectable levels of psychoactive compounds that had degraded beyond detection by the time of analysis.






## Practical Considerations for Storage

The evidence from both scientific studies and indigenous practice suggests that neurotropic fungi should ideally be consumed or chemically analyzed as soon as possible after collection. For scientific purposes, immediate freeze-drying or chemical extraction in the field would yield the most accurate potency measurements. For traditional or recreational use, the indigenous practice of using fresh specimens or recently dried material within a year appears to be well-justified.

The finding that even herbarium specimens from 1869 still retained trace amounts of psilocybin (0.014 percent) indicates that complete degradation takes many decades, but the practical potency loss becomes significant within months to a few years. The rate of degradation is likely influenced by temperature, humidity, light exposure, and the specific chemical matrix of the fungal tissue, factors that vary considerably between species and preparation methods.
