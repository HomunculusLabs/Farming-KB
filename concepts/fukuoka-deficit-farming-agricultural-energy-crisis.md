### The Desert Beneath the Fields
From an airplane, a third of the American continent east and west of the Rockies
appears as a reddish-brown desert. The 80,000 center-pivot sprinkler systems are
"very much like oases in the middle of a desert." Irrigation brings salt up to
the ground surface where it accumulates, gradually poisoning the soil.

### Monoculture"s Toll
The land lacks fertility for continuous cropping, so one-third to one-half of fields
are left fallow each year, creating an alternating pattern of green and brown
stripes visible from the air. Modern farming stresses labor productivity and
efficiency, leading inevitably to continuous monocropping that depletes soil
fertility and sets in motion a negative cycle of economic failure.

## Economic Consequences

The economic implications are stark. Farmers working hundreds of acres on the
shores of the Mississippi — America's richest grain belt — are unable to live as
well as Japanese farmers working just three or four acres. Faced with rising debts
and deepening poverty, they are leaving the land.

The fundamental cause behind this trend is the destruction of the American continent
through erroneous farming practices. Fukuoka calls this "just and proper retribution
for those who thought they could put nature to work for them without fearing God
and nature."

## The Pine Blight Connection

Japanese pines, but a decade earlier. The identical symptoms — one tree succumbs,
