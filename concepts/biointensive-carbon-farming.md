# Biointensive Carbon Farming

Carbon farming is the practice of growing dedicated crops specifically for
producing biomass to feed the biointensive-composting|compost pile. In the
[[comparison-biointensive-vs-square-foot-gardening]] system, approximately
60% of growing area is devoted to carbon/compost crops -- making it the
largest single allocation and the foundation of soil
sustainability.
## Why Carbon Farming?

Conventional agriculture depletes soil organic matter at rates far exceeding
natural replacement. GB reverses this by growing all needed organic matter
on-site. The 60% figure comes from careful
calculations:
- Each 100 sq ft bed requires approximately 50-100 pounds of finished compost per crop cycle
- To produce this compost sustainably, enough biomass must be grown to maintain a positive carbon balance
- Carbon crops provide both the raw material for compost and (often) a food harvest

## Carbon Crop Categories

### High-Biomass Carbon Crops

These produce large amounts of biomass per unit area:

| Crop | Dry Biomass/100 sq ft | Notes |
|------|----------------------|-------|
| Rye (grain) | 15-25 lbs | Winter cover crop, also produces grain |
| Wheat | 10-20 lbs | Dual-purpose: grain + straw |
| Sorghum | 15-30 lbs | Heat-tolerant, very high biomass |
| Jerusalem artichoke | 20-40 lbs | Perennial, also produces tubers |
| Corn | 12-25 lbs | Stalks + leaves provide excellent carbon |
| Comfrey | 15-25 lbs | Perennial, deep-rooted dynamic accumulator |
| Oats | 10-18 lbs | Fast-growing, good winter crop |

### Dual-Purpose Crops (Carbon + Food)

Many carbon crops also produce food, improving the efficiency of the 60%
allocation:
- **Grains**: Wheat, rye, oats, barley (straw = carbon, grain = calories)
- **Legumes**: Fava beans, vetch, clover (fix nitrogen + provide biomass)
- **Jerusalem artichokes**: Tubers for food, stalks for carbon
- **Sunflowers**: Seeds for food/oil, stalks for carbon

## The Carbon Ratio

A sustainable GB system needs a carbon ratio of approximately 1.5:1 to 2:1 --
meaning for every unit of carbon removed in harvest, 1.5 to 2 units must be
returned to the soil as compost. This ensures net soil building over
time.
Jeavons' research shows that with proper management, GB can build soil at
approximately 1 inch of topsoil per year (compared to natural rates of
0.01-0.05 inches per year in temperate
climates).
## Cover Cropping for Carbon

Between main crop cycles, beds can be cover-cropped:

- **Winter covers**: Rye, vetch, clover, oats -- protect soil, add organic matter
- **Summer covers**: Buckwheat, cowpeas, sorghum -- quick biomass production
- **Living mulches**: Low-growing clovers between rows

## Integration with Calorie Farming

The 60% carbon crop allocation and 30%  
[[biointensive-calorie-crops]]
 allocation overlap significantly. Many carbon crops also produce
calories. The key insight is that any crop's above-ground biomass (even from
calorie crops) should be returned to the compost pile after harvest. Only the
edible portion is
removed.
## Building Humus

The goal of carbon farming is not just biomass quantity but humus quality:

- A mix of carbon materials (straw, leaves, green manure) produces more diverse compost
- Fungal-dominated compost (from woody materials) builds more stable humus
- Regular composting of carbon crops creates a self-reinforcing cycle: better soil -> more biomass -> better compost -> better soil

## Comfrey as a Carbon Crop
Comfrey ([[symphytum-officinale]]) deserves special attention as a carbon crop in
the GROW BIOINTENSIVE system. Its deep taproot (which can extend 6-10 feet
into the subsoil) mines minerals and nutrients unavailable to shallow-rooted
crops, bringing them to the surface in its leaves. Comfrey can be cut 4-6
times per growing season, producing substantial biomass each time. Its leaves
contain approximately 2.5-3.0 percent nitrogen on a dry weight basis, making
it one of the few non-leguminous plants with nitrogen levels comparable to
legumes. The leaves break down rapidly in compost piles, contributing both
carbon and
nitrogen.

Comfrey also serves as a dynamic accumulator, concentrating
potassium,
calcium, phosphorus, and trace minerals from deep subsoil layers. Russian
comfrey (Symphytum x uplandicum, the 'Bocking 14' cultivar) is sterile and
propagated only by root cuttings, preventing the invasive spread that can
occur with common comfrey. Its use as a mulch around fruit trees and
vegetables is well-documented in [[permaculture]]
practice.
## The Composting Process and Carbon Quality
Not all carbon biomass is equal in its composting behavior. The ratio of
carbon to nitrogen (C:N ratio) determines both the speed of decomposition and
the quality of the finished compost. Woody materials (high C:N, 100:1 or
higher) decompose slowly but produce more stable, long-lasting humus. Green
materials (low C:N, 15:1 to 25:1) decompose quickly and provide nitrogen but
produce less stable humus. A well-designed carbon farming system provides a
mix of both types, creating compost that is both nutrient-rich and
biologically
stable.

The GROW BIOINTENSIVE method emphasizes building compost piles that
reach
internal temperatures of 131-140°F (55-60°C) for at least 3 days to kill
weed seeds and pathogens, then maintaining temperatures above 90°F for
several weeks to complete decomposition. Turning the pile introduces oxygen
and redistributes moisture, ensuring aerobic decomposition. Under-ventilated
or overly wet piles produce anaerobic conditions that generate foul odors,
methane, and phytotoxic compounds. The goal is finished compost that is dark,
crumbly, and earthy-smelling, with no recognizable input
materials.
## Carbon Farming and Climate Change
Carbon farming has significant implications for climate change mitigation.
Agricultural soils have lost an estimated 50-70 percent of their original
organic carbon content globally through intensive tillage, monoculture, and
removal of crop residues. Rebuilding soil carbon through dedicated carbon crop
cultivation and compost application can sequester atmospheric CO2 at rates of
0.3-0.8 metric tons per acre per year, depending on climate, soil type, and
management practices. The Rodale Institute's long-term farming systems trial
has demonstrated that organic systems sequester approximately 1,000 pounds of
carbon per acre per year more than conventional
systems.

While these sequestration rates are modest compared to global
emissions,
the
aggregate potential is significant when multiplied across millions of acres of
agricultural land. Moreover, the co-benefits of carbon farming -- improved
soil health, increased water retention, reduced need for synthetic
fertilizers, and enhanced crop resilience -- make it a valuable strategy
regardless of its carbon accounting. The GROW BIOINTENSIVE approach of growing
carbon crops on 60 percent of cultivated area represents one of the most
deliberate and systematic frameworks for soil carbon sequestration available
