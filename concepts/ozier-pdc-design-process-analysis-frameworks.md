## The Base Map

Every design begins with a **base map**, the map of the entire site
within legal property limits. An existing base map can typically be
obtained from the local county or city office, or created using Google
Earth or surveying tools.

Best practices for base map work:

1. Keep an original copy as reference and work on duplicates.
2. Draw data directly onto copies or create data overlays using
   transparencies or tracing paper.
3. With tracing paper, put all analytical data on one sheet, since
   multiple layers make it difficult to see the base map underneath.
4. With transparencies, create multiple sheets with related data grouped
   on each (e.g., sectors and zones on one, water flow and soil types on
   another).
5. Digital tools can create layered [[holmgren-ecological-succession-and-pulsing]], health needs, dietary needs,
physical limitations, budget and timeframe, vision for the site, desired
level of hands-on involvement, number of people served, and social
habits.

## Observation and Interaction

The guide emphasises that many experienced designers recommend observing a
site for a full year before implementation, with the most rigorous
suggesting three years of observation. While most people cannot wait this
long, the designer should observe as often as possible under varied
conditions: day, afternoon, evening, morning, during rain, after rain,
dry conditions, and any other conditions available.

Observation should include camping on the site, doing small projects to
see their effects, and interacting with the site through hiking, fishing,
making noise, and being quiet.
