Endophytic fungi that colonize living plant tissue without causing immediate, overt negative effects occupy a fascinating position between mutualism and parasitism. When these cryptic colonizers do eventually express pathogenic behavior, they often produce distinctive symptoms including galls, cankers, and witches brooms. Understanding the spectrum from asymptomatic endophytism to visible deformity is essential for plant pathology and fungal ecology.

## Definition and Circumscription

The terms endophyte and endophytic fungi describe the internal mycota of living plants. In the broadest sense, endophytic fungi are those capable of symptomless occupation of apparently healthy plant tissue. This definition includes virtually the entire spectrum of symbiotic interactions: parasitism, commensalism, and mutualism. Contemporary applications of the terms are not always consistent, but generally refer to fungi colonizing living plant tissue without causing immediate harm.

## The Continuum from Endophyte to Pathogen

Many fungi commonly reported as endophytes are regarded as minor or secondary pathogens by forest pathologists. Their common occurrence in both healthy and diseased tissues underscores the uncertainty of boundaries separating endophytes, facultative pathogens, and latent pathogens. The behavioral differences between many "endophytic" fungi and those considered "latent pathogens" are slight and may simply reflect differences in the duration of the latent phase and the degree of injury sustained by the host.

## Episodic Growth as a Defining Feature

Fungi described as endophytic characteristically exhibit a prolonged, inconspicuous period in which growth and colonization temporarily cease, resuming after a physical or maturational change in the host. This episodic growth pattern is a defining feature of endophytes, whether they are ultimately considered commensal saprobes, latent pathogens, or protective mutualists. The transition from quiescence to active growth often triggers the visible symptoms that attract attention.

## Types of Plant Deformities

### Fungal Galls

Fungal galls represent one of the most recognizable symptom groupings caused by endophytic and pathogenic fungi. Galls are abnormal growths of plant tissue induced by fungal colonization. Various coelomycetous fungi have been associated with gall formation, and the gall structure provides a unique microhabitat that may support additional fungal species.

### Witches Brooms

Witches brooms are dense clusters of abnormally short branches that result from systemic fungal infections. These deformities are commonly associated with perennial infections by rust fungi and certain basidiomycetes. The broom structure alters the plant's architecture and may persist for years, serving as a long-term niche for the causative fungus.

### Cankers and Stem Deformities

Rust infections on woody stems can produce cankers that sporulate in contiguous areas more than a meter long. These perennial infections cause progressive stem deformation and may eventually girdle and kill the host branch or trunk. Systemic infections may cause plants to appear stunted, chlorotic, or otherwise discolored.

## Tissue Specificity and Distribution

The species composition of the internal mycobiota is distinct for various hosts, organs, and tissues. Endophytes colonize foliar surfaces (epiphytes), internal tissues of foliage (foliar endophytes), young and old bark (bark endophytes), wood (xylem endophytes), and roots. Distribution patterns vary with tissue age, spatial position, and temporal factors.

## Clavicipitaceous and Nonclavicipitaceous Endophytes

For grass hosts, endophytes often denote systemic clavicipitaceous infections that provide protective mutualism to the host plant, including defense against herbivores through alkaloid production. Nonclavicipitaceous endophytes, in contrast, colonize woody perennials and other hosts with more limited, non-systemic infections that may be commensal or latency-based.

## Screening and Isolation

Screening field populations for asymptomatic endophytes requires careful surface sterilization and culture protocols. Selective isolation agents, molecular sequence approaches, and histological methods each contribute to understanding endophyte diversity. Herbarium specimens can also be screened for endophytic fungi, extending the temporal range of diversity assessments.
