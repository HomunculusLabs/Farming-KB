## Overview

Managing a lawn through its [[soil-food-web]] represents a fundamentally different approach from chemical lawn care. Instead of feeding grass directly with soluble salts, the gardener feeds soil microbes, which in turn feed the grass roots. This section covers assessment, feeding, aeration, weeding, pH management, and application techniques specific to lawn [[soil-food-web-restoration-tools]].

## Assessing Lawn Soil Biology

### Biological Indicators

Before applying amendments, assess the current state of the lawn's soil food web:

- **Earthworms**: Their presence indicates healthy populations of bacteria, fungi, and protozoa (their food). Signs include birds hunting worms, worms visible after rain, and surface castings deposited at night
- **Microarthropods**: Visible with a hand lens, macroscope, or light microscope. These assist with nutrient cycling, open grass clippings, and aerate soil. A [[berlese-funnel]] can extract and count them
- **Mushrooms**: Usually a positive sign indicating healthy fungal activity beneath the grass

If worms and microarthropods are abundant, the lawn likely needs maintenance rather than full restoration.

## Feeding Soil Microbes (Not Plants)

### Rule #14

Avoid additives with NPK numbers greater than 10-10-10. A high phosphorus concentration (above 10) not only prevents [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] from growing but kills existing populations, locking up phosphorus and making it unavailable to grass.

### Recommended Organic Microbe Foods

| Product | NPK | Application Rate |
|---------|-----|-----------------|
| [[soybean-meal]] | 6-1-1 | 3–4 lbs/100 sq ft |
| [[alfalfa-meal]] | — | 4 lbs/100 sq ft |
| Blood meal | — | 4 lbs/100 sq ft |
| Cottonseed meal | — | 4 lbs/100 sq ft |
| Feather meal | — | 4 lbs/100 sq ft |
| Fish bone meal | — | 3 lbs/100 sq ft |

These feed soil biology — they are not directly absorbed by plant roots, hence "microbe food, not fertilizer." Fish bone meal produces a heavy fishy odor for several days. Rates should be adjusted after initial applications based on results.

### Grass Clippings as Bacterial Mulch

Lawns prefer slightly bacterially dominated soils (Rule #2). Leaving grass clippings on the lawn:

- Provides sugars that attract healthy bacterial populations
- Fosters protozoa for nutrient cycling
- Reduces mowing frequency (absent high nitrate uptake from [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]])

### Leaf and Twig Mulching

Instead of raking fallen leaves and twigs, mulch them in place with a lawn mower run over them once or twice. This:

- Opens material for fungal decomposition
- Supports the fungal component essential for soil structure and drainage
- Helps decompose grass stems that build up as thatch in the absence of fungi

## Plug Aeration

Lawns lacking a healthy soil food web (due to poor drainage, chemical fertilizers, or weed killers) benefit from plug aeration:

- 2-inch soil plugs are pulled throughout the lawn, creating channels
- Holes allow water, air, and organic food to reach the root zone
- Plugs should remain on the lawn to decompose naturally
- Schedule in early spring every 3–4 years to address compaction from snow, pets, children, and vehicles
- Fungi are the first organisms lost to compaction — aeration specifically helps maintain them
- Apply organic microbe food after aeration so it falls into plugholes

## Inoculation Methods

### Small Lawns

Apply a thin layer (up to ½ inch) of bacterially dominated compost using a fertilizer spreader.

### Large Lawns

Apply slightly bacterially dominated compost tea at 5 gallons per acre.

### New Lawns

Before broadcasting seed, mix it with vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungal spores. Roll wet grass seed in VAM 24 hours before planting and store in a dark, cool spot. VAM colonization helps grasses compete with weeds, blocks root-eating nematodes, and improves water and nutrient uptake.

## Weed Management Through Soil Biology

### Dandelions

Indicate calcium-poor soil surfaces. Their long taproots mine calcium from deeper layers, depositing it in the soil when they die. To accelerate their disappearance:
- Boost fungal activity (fungi tie up calcium more effectively than bacteria)
- Apply corn gluten (10-10-10 NPK) as a pre-emergent when weeds are going to seed — prevents new seeds from developing secondary roots

### Clover and Quack Grass

Indicate insufficient nitrogen cycling. Add nematodes and protozoa via compost, tea, or protozoa soup.

### Chickweed

Thrives with excess nitrate — the direct result of commercial lawn fertilizer. Stop chemical applications and increase fungal biomass to shift available nitrogen from nitrate toward ammonium.

### Moss

Indicates the lawn soil is overly fungally dominated rather than slightly bacterial (as grasses prefer). Apply very bacterial teas and thin topdressing of bacterially dominated compost. Remove existing moss with a thatching rake; iron may be needed to kill it first.

### Fairy Rings

Overcome by increasing fungal diversity through compost or compost tea applications. The diverse fungi outcompete the fairy ring monoculture.

## pH Management Shortcut

Instead of applying hundreds of pounds of lime directly (which acts slowly, taking a full season per pH point), mix lime into compost during production. Microbes tie up the lime and release it gradually through normal food web cycling. This requires approximately one-quarter the amount and less time for equivalent results.

## Chlorine Considerations

Chlorine in irrigation water generally does not harm lawn microbes when applied through sprinklers — the fine mist spray and air exposure dissipate most chlorine. For additional protection, install an inexpensive chlorine filter on the outdoor hose bib.

## Chemical Remediation (Rule #15)

When chemical intervention is unavoidable (herbicide for severe weeds, fast nitrogen green-up for events), always follow with compost tea application within a few days. The tea microbes will:

1. Detoxify the soil by breaking down residual chemicals
2. Repopulate the depleted microbiology
3. Repeat after one week and recheck soil food web status

Fungi are particularly important for pesticide degradation, attacking complex chlorinated carbon rings. Provide complex protein sources (kelp, fish hydrolysate, humic acids) to feed fungal populations during remediation.

## Long-Term Outcome

A lawn with a thriving soil food web requires significantly less maintenance:
- No thatching or raking of clippings and leaves
- Reduced watering frequency
- Less frequent mowing
- Freedom from dangerous chemical applications
