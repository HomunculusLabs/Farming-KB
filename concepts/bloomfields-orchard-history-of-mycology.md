# History of Mycology

A tour through the personalities and milestones that shaped mycology as a science, drawn from Nicholas P. Money's *Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard* (2002). The book frames fungal biology through the lives of eccentric, obsessive researchers who advanced the field — often in isolation, sometimes at great personal cost. Money, a professor of botany at Miami University (Ohio), writes with scientific precision leavened by dark humor and personal anecdote.

## A.H.R. Buller (1874-1944)

Arthur Henry Reginald Buller was the dominant figure in 20th-century mycology. Born in Birmingham, England, he earned a Ph.D. at the University of Leipzig under Wilhelm Pfeffer in 1899. He emigrated to Winnipeg, Canada, in 1904 to become the first professor of botany at the University of Manitoba, where he remained for four decades — living at the McLaren Hotel for all of those years, a bachelor devoted entirely to his science.

Buller's magnum opus was the seven-volume *Researches on Fungi* (1909-1950), a work of staggering breadth that remains essential reading. His lonely hours at the microscope produced some of the field's most piercing insights. He discovered that rust basidiospores are launched by the same fluid-drop mechanism (Buller's drop) that propels mushroom spores — a finding that established rusts as basidiomycete fungi, distant cousins of gilled mushrooms. This connection would not have been obvious from morphology alone; it required painstaking observation of the microscopic droplet that appears at the base of each rust spore before it is launched from its spike.

Buller also documented the fungal "flower" analogy: rust spermagonia exude sugary nectar and spermatia that insects carry between infections, functioning as flowers that deceive pollinators into serving as vectors for fungal sex. The rust *Puccinia monoica* takes this further still — it causes infected plants to produce yellow leaf clusters that perfectly mimic real flowers, diverting pollinators from surrounding species and altering the ecology of the entire habitat.

Buller was known for his physical demonstrations of spore discharge, puffing clouds of spores across lecture halls to the delight and discomfort of his audiences. His work on the mechanics of basidiospore liberation — the fluid drop on the hilar appendix that merges with a surface droplet to catapult the spore via a center-of-mass shift in roughly one microsecond — remains foundational. The mechanism is so precise that it operates within the constraints of fluid physics at a scale invisible to the naked eye.

## Curtis G. Lloyd (1859-1926)

Curtis Gates Lloyd was a self-taught mycologist from Cincinnati who made a fortune in the patent-medicine business and devoted his wealth to collecting and studying fungi. He was an inveterate traveler who corresponded with mycologists worldwide and built one of the largest private collections of fungal specimens in existence.

Lloyd was famously combative. He attacked what he called "scientific narcissism" — the vanity of researchers who named species after themselves or their patrons. He erected his own stone monument while still alive, embodying the grandiosity he criticized in others. Lloyd specialized in gasteroid fungi (puffballs, earthstars, stinkhorns) and published extensively through his own press, the Lloyd Library. His life illustrates the tension between amateur enthusiasm and professional authority that has characterized mycology from its earliest days.

Money relates the story of giant puffballs (*Calvatia gigantea*) growing in pastures, and how the largest specimens inevitably attract vandals who kick them to pieces. He speculates about why humans harbor this impulse to destroy mushrooms — perhaps an instinctive mistrust rooted in our evolutionary history of being poisoned by fruiting bodies, or perhaps simply the tactile pleasure of rupturing a water-saturated structure. Lloyd would have appreciated this observation.

## Anton de Bary and the Fathers of Modern Mycology

Anton de Bary (1831-1888), often called "the father of modern mycology," established the field as a rigorous discipline. Working in Strasbourg, he trained a generation of mycologists including Marcus Hartog, the irascible professor at Queen's College, Ireland. De Bary's work on the life cycles of rusts, smuts, and other fungal pathogens laid the groundwork for plant pathology as a science.

Hartog himself was a colorful figure. He staked his reputation on the claim that *Achlya* spores must possess flagella to leave their sporangia, inventing the terms "aerotaxy" and "pneumatotaxy" to describe the stimuli he believed drove their exit. A century later, electron microscopy revealed that *Achlya* spores bore only short flagellar stumps — "phantom limbs" that could wave but not swim. Money imagines Hartog "burning with rage, hurling the journal across his office, and immediately writing an article accusing the researchers of deception."

## C.T. Ingold (1905-2010)

Cecil Terence Ingold made landmark contributions to the study of fungal spore dispersal, particularly the "Ingoldian conidium" — the type of spore produced by aquatic hyphomycetes that colonizes submerged leaves in streams. These conidia are passive vehicles for transmission, lacking the motility and sensory sophistication of zoospores. If they don't hit a submerged leaf, they are eaten by predators, suffocate in mud, or wash downstream to oblivion. Ingold's careful documentation of spore types and their dispersal mechanisms provided a taxonomic framework that remains in use.

## John Raper and Fungal Sex

John Raper at Harvard was the first to demonstrate that sexual development in water molds is coordinated by chemical signals. Working with *Achlya ambisexualis* (a species he discovered himself), Raper showed that the female releases antheridiol to attract males, who respond with antheridial branches and release oogoniol in return. This back-and-forth chemical conversation culminates in fertilization.

Raper's techniques for collecting water molds were characteristically ingenious. Pond water and sediment samples are poured into glass dishes, sterilized seeds are sprinkled on the surface to bait zoospores from the sediment, and within a day each seed sprouts a fringe of water mold hyphae. Cannabis seeds, with their high fat content, proved to be "superb baits." Money notes dryly that these seeds are "grown in greenhouses for scientific purposes and shipped (to my students' dismay) presterilized and incapable of germination."

## Dennis Benjamin and Mushroom Toxicology

Dennis Benjamin, a pathologist at Cook Children's Medical Center in Fort Worth, Texas, serves as Money's expert source on mushroom poisoning. Benjamin estimates that 6-7 mg of pure amatoxin — the amount in a single *Amanita* cap — is sufficient to kill an adult. He has documented cases where children required liver transplantation after consuming misidentified mushrooms, including a family in Ohio who added slices of a pure white mushroom (a destroying angel) to their soup.

## The Amateur-Professional Partnership

Money observes that mycology is rare among the sciences in the continuing importance of amateur practitioners. Amateur mycologists are responsible for much of our knowledge of mushroom distribution and diversity — a contribution perhaps rivaled only by astronomy in its scale. Their field skills often exceed those of professional mycologists. However, Money expresses ambivalence about encouraging mushroom hunting as recreation, noting that over-collection can limit outcrossing, diminish genetic diversity, and impact forest ecosystems by removing mushrooms as food sources for animals and breeding grounds for insects.

## Fungi as Agricultural Enemies

The history of mycology is inseparable from the history of agriculture. Fungi cause more plant diseases than all other enemies combined. Since the beginning of agriculture, billions of livelihoods and lives have been lost to rusts, smuts, bunts, mildews, potato blight, and rice blast. The hawthorn rust (*Gymnosporangium globosum*) and black stem rust of wheat (*Puccinia graminis*) exemplify the complex life cycles that mycologists have labored to decode — each requiring multiple hosts and producing several distinct spore types. Annual losses to agriculture caused by fungi are incalculably high, and chemical fungicides represent a tremendous investment by farmers and consumers alike.

## Money's Perspective on the Field

Money frames the entire enterprise of mycology as an exercise in studying organisms that refuse to behave like textbook subjects. Fungi collapse every convenient category — they are not plants, not animals, not bacteria — yet they pervade every ecosystem and interact with virtually every other form of life. His narrative emphasizes that mycology has always attracted eccentrics: people willing to spend decades watching water molds swim, or puffing spore clouds across lecture halls, or building personal monuments to themselves in cemeteries. This institutional tolerance for obsessive behavior has been both the field's greatest strength and its most awkward inheritance.

## Women in Early Mycology

The history of mycology includes contributions from women who were often marginalized or denied formal recognition. Beatrix Potter, best known for her children's books, was a serious mycologist whose detailed illustrations of fungal spore germination and her theory of symbiotic lichenization anticipated findings confirmed decades later. Her paper on spore germination of *Agaricus velutipes* was presented to the Linnean Society in 1897 — women were not permitted to attend — and was ultimately rejected. Mary Banning, working independently in Maryland in the late nineteenth century, painted over a thousand watercolors of the fungi of the eastern United States, documenting species never before illustrated.

## The Lichen Controversy

One of the most protracted debates in mycological history concerned the nature of lichens. For most of the nineteenth century, lichens were believed to be single organisms. Simon Schwendener challenged this in 1869 by proposing that lichens were associations between a fungus and an alga — a hypothesis initially ridiculed as implausible. The debate persisted for decades until experimental work in the early twentieth century confirmed Schwendener's dual-organism theory beyond doubt. The controversy illustrates how slowly the scientific community can accept radical ideas about biological individuality.

## The Rise of Plant Pathology

The late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw mycology increasingly directed toward practical applications. The Irish potato famine of the 1840s, caused by the oomycete *Phytophthora infestans*, demonstrated with catastrophic clarity the vulnerability of food systems to fungal pathogens. In response, agricultural experiment stations were established across Europe and North America. The development of the Bordeaux mixture — copper sulfate and lime — as a fungicide in the 1880s was one of the first major successes of applied mycology, inaugurating the era of chemical crop protection.

## Molecular Mycology and Modern Revolutions

The advent of molecular biology in the late twentieth century transformed mycology as profoundly as the invention of the microscope. DNA sequencing revealed that fungi are more closely related to animals than to plants — a relationship suspected from biochemical similarities (both use chitin, share glycogen as storage compound) but definitively established through ribosomal RNA analysis in the 1980s. This reclassification elevated fungi to their own kingdom. Molecular phylogenetics has since reorganized fungal taxonomy at every level, and environmental DNA sampling has revealed entirely new phyla — fungal lineages never directly observed but whose genetic signatures indicate vast, previously unknown branches of the fungal tree of life.

The book's title itself — *Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard* — refers to an imagined figure whose fruit trees are silently colonized by fungi, a reminder that mycological discovery often begins not in the laboratory but in the unnoticed processes of decay and symbiosis unfolding in gardens, forests, and pastures. Money suggests that every orchard is a fungal ecosystem waiting to be understood.

## Legacy and Continuing Relevance

Many of the researchers profiled by Money established techniques and concepts that remain central to modern mycology. Buller's work on spore discharge mechanics informs current research on [[bloomfield-fungal-spore-discharge-and-dispersal]]. Raper's discovery of pheromone-mediated sexual communication in water molds anticipated the broader study of [[fungal-pheromones-and-sexual-signaling]]. De Bary's insistence on rigorous life-cycle documentation remains the standard for plant pathologists studying [[bloomfield-fungal-plant-pathogens]].

## Fungi in Folklore and Cultural Imagination

Throughout most of human history, fungi were regarded not as organisms amenable to scientific study but as mysterious, often menacing features of the landscape. European folklore is saturated with mushroom-related superstitions: toadstools were believed to spring up where toads sat, fairy rings were attributed to dancing elves, and luminous fungi feeding on decaying wood were interpreted as supernatural lights. This mycophobic tradition, documented by Wasson in his cross-cultural analysis of mycophagy, contrasts sharply with the mycophilic cultures of Eastern Europe, Asia, and Mesoamerica, where mushroom collecting and consumption have been celebrated rather than feared.

The persistence of these cultural attitudes has practical consequences. In mycophobic societies, knowledge of fungal identification tends to be poor, leading to accidental poisonings and a general failure to appreciate the ecological importance of fungi. Money notes that the history of mycology is partly the story of overcoming this cultural resistance — of learning to see mushrooms not as objects of fear or disgust but as organisms of extraordinary beauty and biological sophistication. The tension between folk understanding and scientific knowledge continues to shape public engagement with fungi, from foraging practices to conservation policy.

## The Future of Fungal Conservation

As human activities increasingly threaten biodiversity worldwide, fungal conservation has emerged as an urgent concern. Fungi are excluded from most biodiversity legislation and conservation frameworks, which were designed with animals and plants in mind. The IUCN Red List of Threatened Species includes relatively few fungal taxa, despite estimates that fungal extinction rates may parallel or exceed those of better-studied groups. Macrofungi with large, showy fruiting bodies — the very species most likely to be noticed and collected by mycologists — are often the most vulnerable to habitat destruction, air pollution, and over-collection. Money's work underscores the need for mycological knowledge to inform conservation decisions and for the public to recognize fungi as a vital component of the biosphere deserving of the same protection afforded to animals and plants.

## The Role of Microscopy in Fungal Discovery

The invention and refinement of the optical microscope was the technological foundation upon which mycology as a discipline was built. Without magnification, the vast majority of fungal diversity — the hyphae, spores, and microscopic fruiting structures that constitute most of the kingdom — remains invisible. Buller's insights into spore discharge depended entirely on high-magnification observation of microscopic fluid droplets on individual spores. De Bary's life cycle studies required tracking fungal development within host tissues at the cellular level. Raper's pheromone experiments demanded observation of hyphal growth responses to chemical gradients too subtle to detect with the naked eye.

The electron microscope, introduced to mycology in the mid-twentieth century, revealed cellular ultrastructure — the membranes, organelles, and wall layers that distinguish fungal cells. Transmission electron microscopy resolved the fine structure of septal pores (the dolipore septum unique to basidiomycetes), the organization of spore wall layers, and the details of host-pathogen interfaces at the nanometer scale. Scanning electron microscopy provided dramatic three-dimensional images of spore ornamentation, hyphal surface features, and fruiting body anatomy that transformed fungal taxonomy and identification. Each advance in imaging technology opened new dimensions of fungal biology to investigation, reinforcing Money's observation that mycology has always been a discipline defined by its tools.

## Mycology and the One Health Framework

Contemporary mycology increasingly operates within the One Health framework — the recognition that human, animal, and environmental health are interconnected. Fungi are central to this framework: they cause diseases in humans (candidiasis, aspergillosis, cryptococcosis), crops (rusts, smuts, blights), and wildlife (chytridiomycosis in amphibians, white-nose syndrome in bats). The emergence of drug-resistant fungal pathogens (particularly azole-resistant *Aspergillus fumigatus*, linked to agricultural fungicide use) illustrates how environmental management practices can directly impact human health. Climate change is expanding the geographic range of fungal pathogens and altering the balance between beneficial and harmful species in ecosystems worldwide. Money's broad perspective on fungal biology — encompassing pathogens, decomposers, mutualists, and extremophiles — anticipates this integrated view of mycology as essential to understanding and managing the health of the biosphere.

## Fungal Conservation and Biodiversity

The conservation of fungal diversity has only recently begun to receive the attention it deserves. Unlike animals and plants, fungi are rarely included in national biodiversity inventories, protected species lists, or environmental impact assessments. Yet fungal diversity underpins ecosystem function: mycorrhizal networks support forest productivity, saprobic fungi drive nutrient cycling, and fungal pathogens regulate population dynamics across all trophic levels. The loss of fungal species — through habitat destruction, pollution, climate change, and over-collection — represents an erosion of ecosystem services that is difficult to quantify but potentially catastrophic. Money's work, by making fungal biology accessible and compelling to a general audience, contributes to the cultural shift needed to place fungi at the center of conservation thinking alongside more visible organisms.
