# Melon and Pumpkin Growing Organic

Melons and pumpkins are members of the Cucurbitae family that require
warm conditions and plenty of organic matter in the soil. True pumpkins
need four months to mature and are best suited to warm climates, but
many related squashes thrive in cooler areas and provide excellent
winter storage crops. Melons need careful attention to training and
fruit support but reward the gardener with sweet, juicy fruit that
cannot be matched by shop-bought produce.

## Growing Melons Outdoors

Melons (Cucumis melo) need a well-manured soil with a pH of about
6.0. New, quick-maturing varieties like Ogen and Sweetheart ripen
quickly and are ideal for all areas, especially for cloche culture in
cool climates or growing outside in warmer regions. Sow inside at
65 degrees F (18 degrees C) in mid-spring, putting two seeds in each
3in (8cm) pot and thinning to one if necessary. Plant outside under
cloches in late spring with 3ft (90cm) between plants.

It is important to water melons regularly. When the plants have three
leaves, pinch off the growing point to encourage sideshoots. Stop the
sideshoots again after three leaves. When fruits form, pinch back to
two leaves beyond the fruit. This disciplined training concentrates
the plant's energy into producing fewer but larger, sweeter fruits.
Harvest by cutting the fruits as soon as they feel soft when you press
the ends. Melons do not store well and must be eaten fresh.

## Growing Melons in the Greenhouse

Modern cantaloupe and F1 hybrid varieties have replaced old-fashioned
musk or sweet melons. These newer varieties need only a little heat
at the propagation stage and can be grown without heat during summer.
In warm climates they thrive outside; in temperate areas, Lunabel is
recommended for greenhouse cultivation.

Sow individually in 3in (8cm) pots in early spring at 70 to 75
degrees F (21 to 24 degrees C). Plant about 12 to 18in (30 to 45cm)
apart in a well-manured border or, ideally, a hot bed. Alternatively,
grow in growing bags or pots with careful watering. In a cold house,
plant out in mid-spring. Pinch off the growing point to keep only two
true leaves. A week later, two sideshoots will grow; allow them to grow on the ground or train up wires. Stop these shoots after seven
to ten leaves. When embryo fruits appear, stop two leaves past the
fruit and allow only about five fruits to develop per plant.

Support maturing fruits with nets to prevent stems from snapping while
they ripen. Melons may bruise or split if they fall to the ground.
Allow insects access to the greenhouse for pollination. Spray over
the leaves and wet down the paths each morning, except when fruit is
ripening, when the atmosphere should be kept dry with free ventilation.
Feed with liquid fertilizer at each watering throughout growing season.

## Pumpkin Varieties and Cultivation

True pumpkins are best suited to warm climates as they take four
months to reach maturity. The largest varieties like Big Max, Atlantic
Giant, and Mammoth are impressive but impractical for eating unless
your family is very large or you want to make jam. The smaller Uchiki
Kuri has a wonderful sweet, nutty flavor and stores very well.
Vegetable Spaghetti is ideal and actually improves with keeping until
midwinter. Most seed catalogs now stock a wide range of different
shapes, sizes, and flavors.

All pumpkins and winter squashes need a well-manured soil, so dig in
plenty of compost or manure. They prefer a pH of about 6.0. In the
hamilton three year plan, grow them in Plot A. Sow inside at
about 65 degrees F (18 degrees C) in mid-spring, two seeds per 3in
(8cm) pot. Most winter squashes are trailing varieties requiring a
lot of room, so plant out at least 3ft (90cm) apart when all danger
of frost has passed in late spring. If sowing outside, do so about
two weeks before the last expected frost.

## Pumpkin Maintenance and Harvesting

Feed pumpkins with liquid animal-manure fertilizer at two-week
intervals from midsummer to early fall. There is no need to remove
male flowers from pumpkins, unlike some other cucurbits. Pinch back
the trailing stems regularly to keep the plants in check and prevent them from smothering neighboring crops. Ensure the plants receive
plenty of sunshine throughout the growing season.

Leave winter squashes on the plant as long as possible while there is
plenty of sunshine. At the end of the season, raise the fruits off
the ground by putting them on a piece of wood or brick to avoid
rotting. When they start to fade, cut the fruit but leave it in the
sun to continue ripening if possible. The harder the skins at the
end of the season, the better the fruits will keep. Store them in a
frost-free place and eat them in midwinter.

## Key Points

- Melons need pH 6.0 and plenty of organic matter; sow at 65 degrees
  F (18 degrees C) in mid-spring
- Train melons by pinching the growing point at three leaves, then
  stopping sideshoots at three leaves and again two leaves past fruit
- Greenhouse melons need only five fruits per plant for best results
- Support greenhouse melons with nets as they ripen to prevent stem
  breakage
- Ogen and Sweetheart are quick-maturing melon varieties ideal for
  cooler climates
- Uchiki Kuri and Vegetable Spaghetti are the best eating pumpkins
  for home gardens
- Raise ripening squashes off the ground on bricks to prevent
  bottom rot
- Harder skins mean better storage; keep in a frost-free place for
  winter use
