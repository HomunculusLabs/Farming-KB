# Sweet Flag (Acorus calamus) Ethnobotany

Sweet flag, also known as sweet calamus or Acorus calamus, is a perennial
marsh plant that has occupied a unique position in the ethnobotanical
traditions of cultures across the Northern Hemisphere. It grows in damp places
in both the north and south temperate regions and is one of two species in the
genus Acorus. A member of the arum family (Araceae), sweet flag has been used
as a medicine, stimulant, and potentially hallucinogenic plant for centuries,
though its psychoactive properties remain imperfectly understood.

## Botanical Description

Sweet flag is a semiaquatic perennial herb that typically grows 30 to 100
centimeters tall. It features sword-shaped leaves that resemble those of
irises, arising from a creeping, branching rhizome (rootstock). The plant
produces a cylindrical spadix covered with tiny greenish-yellow flowers.
The rhizome is the primary part used by humans, possessing a distinctive
sweet, aromatic fragrance that has made it valued for centuries as a
flavoring agent, medicine, and ceremonial plant.

The rhizome grows horizontally beneath the mud of marshes, stream banks, and
shallow water. It is thick, fleshy, and covered with fibrous root remnants.
When fresh, the interior is white and spongy; when dried, it becomes firm
and takes on a reddish-brown coloration. The characteristic aroma comes from
volatile essential oils contained within the rhizome tissue.

## Traditional and Indigenous Use

There is indirect evidence that indigenous peoples of northern Canada employed
sweet flag as both a medicine and a stimulant. According to the Golden Guide
to Hallucinogenic Plants, these peoples may chew the rootstock as a
hallucinogen. In excessive doses, the plant is known to induce strong visual
hallucinations. However, the line between traditional medicinal use and
deliberate psychoactive use is often blurred in ethnobotanical accounts.

Sweet flag has a long history of use in traditional medicine across many
cultures. In Ayurvedic medicine (India), it is known as vacha and has been
used for thousands of years as a brain tonic, memory enhancer, and remedy for
digestive complaints. In traditional Chinese medicine, the dried rhizome
(shi chang pu) is used to calm the spirit and promote mental clarity.

European herbalists valued sweet flag as a strengthening tonic and digestive
aid. The plant was commonly grown in monastery gardens during the Middle Ages
and was one of the most important medicinal herbs in the European herbal
tradition. [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] tribes used it variously as a tonic, stimulant, and
ceremonial plant, with different tribes employing different parts and
preparations.

## Chemistry and Psychoactive Constituents

The intoxicating properties of sweet flag are believed to be primarily due to
two related compounds: alpha-asarone and beta-asarone. These phenylpropanoid
compounds are found in the essential oil of the rhizome. However, the
[[psilocybin-psilocin-chemistry-and-pharmacology-emcdda]] of the plant remain poorly understood, and the
relationship between specific constituents and psychoactive effects has not
been fully elucidated.

The asarone content varies significantly depending on the botanical variety
and geographic origin of the plant. Asian varieties of Acorus calamus
typically contain higher levels of beta-asarone, which has raised safety
concerns because beta-asarone has shown carcinogenic potential in animal
studies. [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] varieties (sometimes classified as Acorus americanus)
are generally tetraploid and contain little or no beta-asarone, relying
instead on alpha-asarone and other essential oil constituents.

The essential oil of sweet flag contains numerous additional compounds
including eugenol, methyl eugenol, camphor, and various sesquiterpenes. The
complex chemistry of the plant may contribute to [[beta-caryophyllene-entourage-effects-cannabis]] similar to
those observed in other psychoactive plants where multiple compounds act
synergistically.
