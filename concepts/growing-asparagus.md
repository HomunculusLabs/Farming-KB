# Growing Asparagus

Starting an asparagus bed is an investment taking several years to pay off, and it demands good management if the bed is not to be ruined. Most people believe asparagus establishment requires considerable expense and that they must wait two years after planting. The method described here gives a much more productive result at a far lower cost and in far less time.

## The Seed-Growing Method vs. Crowns

The conventional method is to buy 2-year-old crowns from a nursery. However, the seed method is superior:

- Six-month-old seed-grown plants already have root systems **far larger** than so-called 2-year-old crowns for sale at nurseries
- Cost is far lower — one packet of seed provides all you'll ever need
- You can select for all-male plants (which produce more and larger spears)
- Total time to first harvest is about the same

## Climate Requirements

- Asparagus needs sufficient winter chilling — a few cool months will do (not necessarily freezing soil)
- In [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], generally north of southern Georgia
- In Australia, anywhere south of Sydney
- It does not do well in clay — requires well-drained soil or roots become diseased in heavy rain

### Dealing with Clay Soil

If clay describes your situation and asparagus is your passion, consider making a special [[comparison-chinampas-vs-raised-bed-gardening]] of sandy soil at least 1 foot (30 cm) above everything else. One gardener built a marine plywood box 4 feet (120 cm) wide and 8 feet (240 cm) long, filled with creek sand, and grew his asparagus in the raised bed during winter rains.

## Planting

### Bed Preparation

1. In [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]], prepare a 4-foot-wide (120 cm) bed as though for a high-demand crop
2. Asparagus plants can exceed 5 feet (150 cm) high and may shade emerging crops — choose the position carefully
3. Make an extra fertile strip down the center of the bed
4. Spread a layer of compost 2 inches (5 cm) thick and a foot (30 cm) wide down the center
5. Work in a full dose of COF or complete fertilizer
6. Sow asparagus seeds 2 inches (5 cm) apart down this strip, about the time apple trees are blooming
7. Keep seeds moist until they sprout

### First Season Care

1. A month after seedlings come up, thin to about 6 inches (15 cm) apart
2. Side-dress with [[chicken-manure]] compost or more COF
3. If you want a small harvest, allow them to grow fast in their first season — a few weeks of cutting during the first year is acceptable
4. Irrigate the seedlings — keep the nursery row moist
5. The bed should be thoroughly weeded — **always**

### Creating an All-Male Bed

The seedling plants will be roughly 50/50 male and female:

1. Toward the end of the first summer, some plants will prove to be males (forming little pollen sacks) and females (making seedballs that turn red when ripe)
2. **Before the seedballs ripen** and before any seed drops on the bed, dig up and destroy the root crown of all female plants
3. Make absolutely sure they are all dead
4. Now you have an all-male row spaced roughly 1 foot (30 cm) apart
5. Fill any gaps wider than 24 inches (60 cm) the next spring

**Why all-male?** Seed formation is a huge burden on the plant and reduces the size of its spears. Making pollen is not nearly so demanding. Males make more shoots and larger ones.

The second important reason: if no seed drops on the bed, you won't have to deal with overcrowding. The bed will continue producing large handsome spears as long as it is kept fertile and weed-free.

## Ongoing Maintenance

### Annual Spring Care

1. Each spring before asparagus starts growing, top-dress the bed with a 1/2-inch-thick (1.25 cm) layer of finished compost or aged manure
2. Add a hefty dose of COF or other complete fertilizer
3. Give it a dusting of lime (5 lbs per 100 sq ft or 2.25 kg per 10 sq m) once a year
4. Keep weeds out — thoroughly, always

### The Salt Tradition

Each spring before the crowns shoot, broadcast 1/2 lb of rock salt per square yard (250 g per sq m) of bed. All the 19th-century growers swore by salt on asparagus beds. Those living by the ocean might mulch their beds with seaweed, which naturally provides the salt.

## Pests and Diseases

### Asparagus Beetle

The worst [[aact-insect-pest-suppression-grubs-caterpillars-mites-ingham]] — it and its larvae defoliate the ferns, reducing harvest the coming year.

- Spray with pyrethrum/rotenone every few days when beetles are active
- **Most important prevention**: During early autumn, clip all ferns at ground level (in a manner that won't damage the crowns) and remove them from the bed
- Compost the ferns in a heap hot enough to kill beetles and pupae, or burn them

## Varieties

- Gardeners using all-male [[cervantes-hybrid-varieties-types]] have reported disease problems
- Hybrids that produce the usual 50/50 mix of sexes are fine
- **UC 157** is commonly recommended and has proved adaptable to most places
- Unless your local agricultural expert has a strong recommendation, start with UC 157

## Harvest

### When to Start

- First spears larger than 3/8 inch (1 cm) can be harvested as early as the second year
- First shoots in spring will usually be the largest ones
- As the season progresses, shoots in the roots will gradually become depleted

### When to Stop

- Stop when there are no more shoots exceeding 3/8 inch (1 cm) — typically 2 weeks in the second year, longer in later years
- Once you've quit for the year, do not cut any shoots that come up — give the plant a chance to recharge its food storage for next spring

### Seed Warning

Do not allow seed to happen — it creates a burden on the plant and leads to overcrowding. To grow all the asparagus you'll ever want for as long as you garden in a place, you'll need to buy only one big packet of seed.
