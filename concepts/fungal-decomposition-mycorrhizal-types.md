## Fungal Decomposition and Mycorrhizal Types

Fungi are central to ecosystem functioning, driving decomposition, nutrient cycling,
and plant-microbe symbioses across all major biomes. Their roles span from breaking
down complex organic matter to forming intimate associations with plant roots that
shape community structure and biogeochemical cycling. As [[john-dighton]] outlines in
*Fungi in Ecosystem Processes*, understanding these roles requires examining fungal
functional groups, [[fungal-enzymatic-capabilities]], and the diverse mycorrhizal strategies
that underpin terrestrial productivity.

## Decomposition Processes

Decomposition is the enzymatic breakdown of organic matter, primarily plant litter,
and fungi are among the most efficient decomposers in terrestrial ecosystems. The
process is governed by substrate quality—especially the C:N ratio—environmental
conditions, and the composition of fungal functional groups present. Litter with a
low C:N ratio decomposes rapidly because nitrogen is available to support microbial
growth, whereas recalcitrant litter with high C:N ratios limits decomposition rates
until external nitrogen is mineralized.

### Enzymatic Machinery

Fungi produce a suite of [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]] to degrade complex polymers:

- **Cellulase** breaks down cellulose, the most abundant polymer in plant cell walls.
  Activity is commonly assayed using carboxymethyl cellulose as a substrate.
- **Protease** releases nitrogen from protein-rich tissues, a critical step when
  litter has a high C:N ratio and nitrogen limits microbial activity.
- **Phosphatase and phytase** liberate phosphate from organic compounds, making
  phosphorus available to plants and microbes in phosphorus-limited soils.
- **N-acetylglucosaminase** degrades chitin, a major component of fungal cell walls
  and arthropod exoskeletons, recycling nitrogen in the process.

The carbon use efficiency of fungi—the fraction of assimilated carbon retained as
biomass versus respired as CO₂—strongly influences how much carbon is ultimately
stored in soils versus returned to the atmosphere. Climate change may alter these
efficiency patterns through warming and shifts in moisture regimes, with cascading
effects on global carbon stores.

### Succession and Functional Groups

Decomposition follows predictable successional patterns. Early colonizers like
*Cladosporium* and *Chaetomium* target labile sugars and starches. As decomposition
progresses, fungi that degrade recalcitrant compounds like lignin and cellulose
become dominant. Litter from C3/C4 grasses versus woody material supports distinct
fungal communities due to differences in chemistry and recalcitrance.

Fungal biomass is commonly estimated via ergosterol, a sterol unique to fungal
membranes. Biomass measurements are essential for quantifying the contribution of
fungi to carbon and nutrient fluxes in brown earth soils and other soil types.

### Food Web Connections

Decomposer fungi form the base of soil food webs. Collembola (springtails) and other
microarthropods graze on [[fungal-hyphae]], regulating fungal biomass and influencing
decomposition rates. This grazing can stimulate fungal activity through compensatory
growth, creating complex feedback loops in the detrital food web.

## Mycorrhizal Types

Mycorrhizal symbioses are mutually beneficial associations between fungi and plant
roots. Three major types dominate terrestrial ecosystems, each with distinct
ecological strategies and host ranges.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae (AM)

Arbuscular mycorrhizae, formed by Glomeromycota, are the most widespread type,
associating with the majority of herbaceous plants, grasses (including C3/C4 grasses),
and many tropical trees. They produce intracellular arbuscules—branched haustorial
structures—within root cortical cells where nutrient exchange occurs.

AM fungi enhance phosphorus uptake through extensive hyphal networks and phosphatase
activity in the rhizosphere. They are particularly important in grasslands and
agricultural systems, though they are generally less effective at accessing organic
nutrient sources compared to ectomycorrhizal or ericoid partners.

### Ectomycorrhizae (ECM)

Ectomycorrhizae form primarily with woody plants in temperate and boreal forests.
The fungal mantle surrounds root tips, and a Hartig net of hyphae penetrates between
root cortical cells without entering them. Many ECM fungi produce hydrolytic and
oxidative enzymes, allowing them to access nitrogen and phosphorus from organic
matter—a trait that makes them key players in nutrient mobilization from decomposing
litter.

ECM fungi contribute significantly to carbon stores in forest soils by channeling
large fractions of plant-derived carbon into stable mycelial biomass and
recalcitrant compounds. *Armillaria* species, well-known ECM associates, also
function as root pathogens, illustrating the continuum between mutualism and
parasitism. Forest decline events have been linked to disruptions in ECM networks,
highlighting their importance to tree health and stand resilience.

### Ericoid Mycorrhizae

Ericoid mycorrhizae associate with plants in the Ericaceae family, including
*[[calluna-vulgaris]]* (heather) and *Arctostaphylos uva-ursi* (bearberry). These
symbioses are critical in acidic, nutrient-poor habitats such as heathlands and
boreal peatlands. The thin hyphal coils within epidermal cells enable ericoid
fungi to access nitrogen from complex organic sources. They produce proteases and
other enzymes that break down polyphenol-protein complexes abundant in acidic
soils, giving ericoid plants a competitive advantage in harsh environments.

## Biogeochemical Cycling and Bioremediation

Fungi mediate biogeochemical cycling through both decomposition and mycorrhizal
pathways. They influence soil pH through [[staycare-fungal-organic-acid-production-metal-leaching]], form calcium
[[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]] that affect calcium cycling, and perform chelation of metals in
the rhizosphere. Mycorrhizal hyphal networks create physical connections between
plants, facilitating nutrient transfer and influencing plant community dynamics.

### Bioaccumulation and Bioremediation

Fungi have remarkable capacities for bioaccumulation and biosorption of heavy
metals and radionuclides. Studies around Chernobyl have demonstrated fungal uptake
of radioactive cesium, and arsenic bioaccumulation by certain fungal taxa shows
promise for environmental cleanup. These bioremediation capabilities arise from
fungal cell wall chemistry—chitin and melanin bind metals effectively—and from
active transport mechanisms that concentrate contaminants within fungal tissues.

### Biocontrol and Bacteria-Fungal Interactions

Fungi serve as biocontrol (biological control) agents against plant pathogens and
pests through balanced antagonism—suppressing competitors without causing harm to
the host plant or beneficial organisms. Interactions between fungi and bacteria are
especially important: *Bacillus* species on fungal hyphae can suppress pathogens,
while some fungi like *Aspergillus* produce antimicrobial compounds that shape
[[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]]. These cross-kingdom interactions regulate nutrient
availability and influence disease outcomes in natural and managed ecosystems.

## Pathogenic Impacts on Ecosystem Processes

Fungal pathogens reshape ecosystems at landscape scales. The chestnut blight
(*Ceratocystis* species) eliminated American chestnut from eastern North American
forests, fundamentally altering forest composition, decomposition dynamics, and
nutrient cycling. Lichen-forming fungi such as *Cladonia* species, while not
pathogens, serve as bioindicators of air quality and contribute to nitrogen fixation
in some nutrient-poor ecosystems.

Understanding [[fungal-decomposition]] and mycorrhizal strategies is essential for
predicting ecosystem responses to global change, managing forest health, and
developing sustainable approaches to bioremediation and agriculture. The interplay
between decomposition pathways, mycorrhizal nutrient exchange, and microbial
interactions defines the functional diversity that makes fungi irreplaceable
components of Earth's ecosystems.
