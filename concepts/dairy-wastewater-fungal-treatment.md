# Dairy Wastewater Fungal Treatment

## Overview

The dairy industry is one of the largest single sources of food-processing wastewaters globally. The cheese industry is particularly significant due to its by-product whey, with approximately 121 million tons released annually based on 13.5 million tons of cheese production (1993 figures). Only about 50% of whey is utilized globally, with the remainder improperly disposed of in water bodies or on land. [[protein-fat-wastewater-fungi]] using yeasts and filamentous fungi provides effective treatment with valuable byproduct recovery.

## Whey Composition and Characteristics

The principal components of whey are:

- **Lactose:** 44-52 g/L (responsible for 90% of COD and BOD)
- **Proteins:** 6-8 g/L
- **Mineral salts:** 4.3-9.5 g/L
- **COD:** 50,000-80,000 mg/L
- **BOD:** 40,000-60,000 mg/L

Discharge onto land can reduce soil alkalinity, decrease manganese availability, inhibit seed germination, and contaminate groundwater. The high BOD can disrupt biological processes in conventional wastewater treatment plants, making specialized fungal approaches valuable as part of broader mycoremediation-basics|mycoremediation strategies.

## Lactose-Utilizing Yeasts

At least 98 yeast species across 19 genera can utilize lactose. The most important species include:

- **Kluyveromyces fragilis:** The primary species for industrial biomass production from whey; provides good yields and acceptability as a safe microorganism
- **Kluyveromyces lactis:** Used in mixed yeast cultures for whey treatment
- **Kluyveromyces marxianus:** Metabolizes most lactose within 22 hours to produce 8.8 g/L biomass and 2.10 g/L ethanol
- **Candida pseudotropicalis:** Effective in free and alginate-entrapped form
- **Torulopsis cremoris:** Shows maximum specific growth rate 26% higher in batch vs. continuous culture
- **Candida utilis:** Effective in mixed yeast cultures with T. cremoris; widely used in [[protein-fat-wastewater-fungi]]

## Bioreactor Systems

### Immobilized Systems

Alginate-entrapped Candida pseudotropicalis cells demonstrate slightly better treatment than free cells, achieving Indian Standard Institution safe levels for COD and BOD. A beta-galactosidase-based biocatalyst was developed by immobilizing whole K. lactis cells in calcium-alginate beads, achieving 99.5% lactose hydrolysis in 30 hours at 30 degrees C without ethanol production.

### Airlift Bioreactors

Airlift bioreactors with repeated fed-batch operation using mixed yeast cultures (T. cremoris and C. utilis) achieved:
- 96% COD reduction
- 0.454 g biomass per g lactose yield (mixed culture)
- 92.68% COD removal efficiency

### Scale-Up Considerations

Scale-up of batch whey fermentation by K. fragilis to 1000-dm3 stirred fermentors has been successfully modeled by varying stirrer speed, airflow rate, and initial lactose concentration. The lactose-to-(NH4)2SO4 ratio of 12 is optimal for cell yield (0.421 g/g), 100% lactose consumption, and 84% COD removal.

## Byproduct Recovery

Whey treatment can simultaneously produce:

- **Yeast biomass:** 90-95% BOD reduction achievable; K. fragilis shows higher biomass yield at low lactose concentration
- **Ethanol:** More than 90% conversion efficiency via continuous fermentation with cell immobilization
- **Lactase enzyme:** Production integrated into the treatment process

## Advantages of Fungal Whey Treatment

Compared to conventional bacterial wastewater treatment, fungal systems offer several distinct benefits: the ability to operate at low pH (3.5-4.0) provides a natural sterilizing effect against bacterial competitors, eliminating the need for sterile operating conditions. The recoverable yeast biomass represents a value-added product, transforming a waste stream into an animal feed supplement. This approach demonstrates the broader principle of [[fungal-industrial-wastewater-treatment]] where treatment and resource recovery occur simultaneously.

## Challenges and Limitations

Despite promising laboratory results, fungal treatment of dairy wastewater faces several practical challenges at commercial scale. Whey composition varies significantly depending on cheese type, seasonal factors, and processing methods, requiring adaptable treatment systems that can handle fluctuating organic loads. The high concentration of salts in some whey streams (particularly from brined cheeses) can inhibit microbial activity and reduce treatment efficiency. Continuous operation of fungal bioreactors requires careful monitoring and control of pH, temperature, and dissolved oxygen to maintain optimal conditions. The economic viability of fungal treatment depends heavily on local markets for byproducts — yeast biomass as animal feed, ethanol as biofuel, and lactase enzymes for food processing — which may not exist in all dairy-producing regions.

## Comparison with Conventional Treatment Methods

Conventional dairy wastewater treatment typically relies on anaerobic digestion followed by aerobic polishing. While anaerobic digestion effectively reduces COD and produces biogas (methane), it performs poorly on lactose-rich streams like cheese whey due to rapid acidification and pH drops that inhibit methanogenic bacteria. Aerobic treatment systems (activated sludge) handle lactose well but are energy-intensive due to aeration requirements. Fungal treatment offers a middle ground: it operates effectively at low pH without the need for pH adjustment, produces valuable byproducts that conventional systems do not, and requires less energy than full aerobic treatment. However, fungal systems generally have lower throughput rates than anaerobic digestion and may require larger reactor volumes for equivalent daily processing capacity.

## Environmental Benefits

Fungal treatment of dairy wastewater provides several environmental advantages beyond simple pollution reduction. The conversion of whey organic matter into yeast biomass creates a protein-rich animal feed supplement, closing the nutrient loop between dairy processing and livestock production. Ethanol production from lactose fermentation offers a renewable fuel source. The ability to operate without chemical pH adjustment reduces the need for acid or base addition and the resulting salt burden on receiving waters. When integrated into a comprehensive waste management strategy, fungal treatment can significantly reduce the environmental footprint of dairy processing operations, particularly in regions where whey disposal remains a major pollution problem.

## Key Fungal Species Used in Dairy Waste Treatment

Several fungal species have demonstrated particular effectiveness in dairy wastewater treatment:
- *Kluyveromyces marxianus*: A thermotolerant yeast that rapidly ferments lactose to ethanol, with growth rates at elevated temperatures (up to 45 degrees C) that outcompete bacterial contaminants
- *Trichoderma reesei*: A filamentous fungus producing large quantities of cellulases and hemicellulases that break down plant-based contaminants in mixed dairy waste streams
- *Aspergillus niger*: Produces citric acid and gluconic acid that help maintain low pH while also generating extracellular enzymes for organic matter degradation
- *Rhizopus oryzae*: Effective at producing lactic acid from lactose and has been used commercially for feta cheese whey treatment
- *Candida pseudotropicalis*: Another lactose-fermenting yeast with high ethanol yields, commonly used in industrial whey fermentation
Each species offers distinct advantages depending on the specific composition of the waste stream and the desired end products. Mixed fungal cultures often outperform single-species inoculations by combining complementary metabolic capabilities.

## Integration with Anaerobic Digestion

The most promising commercial approach combines fungal pretreatment with anaerobic digestion in a two-stage process. In the first stage, fungi break down complex organic molecules (proteins, fats, and some carbohydrates) into simpler compounds at low pH. In the second stage, the partially treated effluent is fed to an anaerobic digester where methanogenic bacteria convert the simpler compounds to biogas. This combined approach addresses the weakness of each technology individually: fungal pretreatment prevents the rapid acidification that plagues direct anaerobic treatment of whey, while anaerobic digestion captures the energy value remaining in the fungal effluent. The two-stage process achieves higher overall COD removal and energy recovery than either technology alone, making it the most economically viable configuration for large dairy processors.

## Regulatory and Economic Barriers to Adoption

Despite the technical promise of fungal treatment systems, several regulatory and economic barriers slow their commercial adoption. In many jurisdictions, dairy wastewater treatment systems must meet strict discharge permits that specify effluent quality parameters (COD, BOD, nitrogen, phosphorus, pH) with defined compliance timelines — fungal systems, being relatively novel, may face additional permitting scrutiny compared to proven conventional technologies. The capital cost of fungal bioreactor systems can be significant for smaller dairy processors, though operating costs are generally lower than conventional alternatives. The lack of standardized design guidelines and performance benchmarks for fungal treatment systems makes engineering firms reluctant to specify them in facility designs. Insurance and financing institutions may be unfamiliar with the technology, creating additional hurdles for dairy processors seeking to invest in fungal treatment. Overcoming these barriers will require demonstrated performance data from full-scale installations and the development of industry-standard design protocols.

## Nutrient Recovery from Treated Effluent

Beyond organic matter removal, fungal treatment systems offer opportunities for nutrient recovery that conventional treatment methods cannot easily provide. The fungal biomass produced during wastewater treatment is rich in protein, essential amino acids, and B vitamins, making it a valuable single-cell protein (SCP) resource for animal feed supplementation. Phosphorus, which is a finite resource facing global supply constraints, can be recovered from fungal biomass through acid extraction or thermal processing, producing a slow-release phosphorus fertilizer. The treated effluent itself, after fungal processing, contains reduced organic loads but retains mineral nutrients (nitrogen, potassium, calcium, magnesium) that make it suitable for agricultural irrigation — a circular approach that returns dairy-derived nutrients to crop production. Life cycle assessments of fungal treatment systems with nutrient recovery show significantly lower environmental impacts than conventional treatment followed by synthetic fertilizer application, particularly in terms of energy consumption, greenhouse gas emissions, and phosphorus depletion.

## Pilot-Scale and Commercial Installations

Several pilot-scale fungal treatment systems have been demonstrated at dairy processing facilities worldwide, providing valuable performance data for scaling the technology. A notable installation at a medium-sized cheese plant in Europe processed approximately 200 cubic meters of whey per day using a *Kluyveromyces marxianus* fermentation system, achieving 85% COD removal while producing ethanol as a saleable byproduct. In New Zealand, a fungal-aerobic hybrid system at a dairy processing plant reduced total nitrogen in effluent by 70% while producing single-cell protein for cattle feed supplements. Research installations in India have demonstrated effective treatment of buffalo milk dairy waste using locally isolated fungal strains, producing fungal biomass that was successfully incorporated into poultry feed formulations. These installations have confirmed laboratory findings at commercial throughput rates while also revealing practical engineering challenges — including foam control in aerated bioreactors, temperature management in exothermic fermentation processes, and the need for robust feedstock pre-screening to remove particulate matter that can clog distribution systems.
