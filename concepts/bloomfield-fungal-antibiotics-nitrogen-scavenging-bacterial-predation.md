# [[fungal-antibiotics]] and Nitrogen Scavenging Strategies

## Overview

Fungi face a fundamental nutritional challenge when feeding on plant
tissues: plants provide abundant sugar but negligible protein. Without
proteins or alternative nitrogen-containing molecules, fungi cannot build
their own proteins or nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). To overcome this
nitrogen deficit, fungi have evolved a remarkable arsenal of strategies
including the secretion of antibiotics to kill bacteria, the trapping and
consumption of nematode worms, and other predatory behaviors. These
behaviors blur the line between decomposer and predator in the fungal
kingdom.

## Nematode-Trapping Fungi

Beyond antibacterial warfare, some fungi obtain nitrogen by trapping and
killing nematode worms. A single gram of soil can house as many as
1,900 microscopic nematodes and more than 1,000 meters of hyphae. At
this density, encounters between hyphae and worms are constant and
unavoidable.

Fungal nematode traps range from simple to elaborate:

- **Adhesive branches:** Short hyphal branches exude a powerful
  adhesive. Passing worms become cemented to the tips. In their
  attempts to escape, the worms become stuck on adjacent branches.
  Sometimes branches detach from the [[mycelium]], but the animal's fate
  remains the same. Within hours, the fungus penetrates the cuticle
  and dissolves internal tissues.

- **Adhesive nets and cages:** More complex three-dimensional networks
  of adhesive hyphae that ensnare nematodes as they pass through.

- **Constricting rings:** The most dramatic trap type. These
  explosive rings consist of three curved cells that inflate
  explosively when disturbed by a passing worm. The interior wall
  of the ring swells outward, gripping the animal with a pressure
  of up to 18 atmospheres. This is one of the fastest cellular
  movements in all of biology.

## Chemical Luring of Nematodes

Nematodes are attracted to fungal traps by chemical attractants,
described as "siren songs" that draw the worms toward their doom. The
chemical signals may mimic the compounds that nematodes normally use to
locate food sources such as bacteria or plant roots. Once the nematode
enters the trap, the mechanical stimulus triggers the capture
mechanism, whether adhesive or constricting.

This chemical deception strategy parallels other fungal chemical
communication systems, from pheromone-based mating to the volatile
organic compounds that attract insects to fruiting bodies.

## Nitrogen Acquisition and Ecological Competition

The ability to obtain nitrogen from bacterial and animal sources gives
fungi a significant competitive advantage in nitrogen-limited
environments. Wood-decay fungi that can supplement their diet with
bacterial cytoplasm or nematode proteins can grow faster, produce
larger fruiting bodies, and colonize more territory than fungi limited
to plant-derived nitrogen.

This predatory behavior also influences soil ecology more broadly.
Fungal predation on nematodes helps regulate nematode populations,
which can be significant plant parasites themselves. In this way,
nematode-trapping fungi may serve an indirect protective function for
plants by reducing populations of plant-parasitic nematodes in the
soil.

## Predatory Yeast: Metschnikowia

Among the most unusual fungal predators is the yeast Metschnikowia,
which produces barbed ascospores. These spores are armed with
bristle-like appendages that can hook into nematode cuticles or other
small organisms. The yeast represents a remarkable example of a
single-celled fungus that has evolved predatory capabilities, extending
the concept of fungal predation beyond the filamentous forms.
