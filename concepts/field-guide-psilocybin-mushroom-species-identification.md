# Field Guide to Psilocybin Mushroom Species Identification

This field guide, originally written by F.C. Ghouled and published
by Guidance Publications (New Orleans, 1972), provides identification
criteria for three psilocybin-containing mushroom species common to
the southern United States. The guide was transcribed and posted to
the Usenet newsgroup alt.drugs in 1994, making it one of the earliest
digital mycological identification resources. It covers Psilocybe
cubensis, Panaeolus subbalteatus, and Psilocybe caerulescens.

## General Identification Principles

All psilocybin mushrooms contain psilocybin and psilocin in varying
amounts. The most reliable field identification marker is the
bluing reaction: an enzyme occurring with psilocybin turns blue
through oxidation when the flesh is damaged. To test, crack the stem
and observe the reaction, which takes 20-120 minutes. The blue color
is similar to blue ink, appearing blue-green if the flesh is
yellowish. With one uncommon exception, all psilocybin mushrooms
turn blue in this manner.

A non-psychoactive genus also produces blue-staining, but it is
easily distinguished: those mushrooms are large, bulbous, smooth,
with yellow or yellow-brown coloration, pores instead of gills, and
solid fleshy stems. They do not grow on dung.

## Psilocybe Cubensis

**Habitat:** Cow, horse, pig, sheep, or goat manure (grass or grain
fed animals only), or enriched soil. Most commonly found on cow
manure in well-drained or sloped areas of pastures.

**Season:** 65-85 degrees Fahrenheit, following rain storms. Best
in late spring and early fall; hot summer months produce lower
quality specimens with reduced potency. Immature mycelium is damaged
by excess water but mature mycelium demands large quantities for
maximum production.

**Cap:** 0.5 to 4 inches wide. Cone-shaped when young, quickly
becoming convex, then flat, with edges uplifting to form a bowl in
maturity (24-48 hours after rain). Sticky protective film on fresh
specimens. Color varies from near-white with gold center-spot to
light brown with gold center. Becomes translucent and dark-olive
when water-soaked. Dries to yellow-rust with orange-gold center.

**Gills:** Closely spaced, light brown when young, deep purple to
black at maturity. Connected to stem when young, may separate with
age.

**Stem:** 1.5 to nearly 6 inches tall, up to 0.5 inch thick. Base
(volva) often thickened. Hollow and fibrous, white or lighter than
cap. Veil ring on upper stem usually turns blue with age. Inside
flesh gives fastest bluing reaction.

**Flesh:** White, little odor, tastes like fresh grain.

## Panaeolus Subbalteatus

**Habitat:** Cow dung, found alongside P. cubensis approximately
12% as frequently.

**Cap:** Bluntly cone-shaped with incurved margin when young,
expanding but usually retaining the incurved margin. Light-cinnamon
color, uniform, covered with small white flecks when young. A
distinctive lighter or darker ring forms at center with age.

**Gills:** Very closely spaced, light brown when young, becoming
black at maturity.

**Stem:** 1.5 to 4 inches tall, never over 0.5 inch thick. Very
uniform shape. Top vertically grooved, lower portion covered with
hairlike scales and fine white powder. Usually white, may show
sepia or light-cinnamon tint. Hollow. Bluing noted best in stem.

**Flesh:** White to yellowish. Taste and odor like fine table
mushrooms. Matures slowly, rarely seen in older stages.

## Psilocybe Caerulescens

**Habitat:** Banks of streams and rivers, throughout the southern
United States. Different substrate preference from the other two
species.

**Cap:** 1 to over 3 inches wide. Cone-shaped when young, expanding
to convex-flat. Margin curves downward. Smooth, sticky surface.
Deep green to black when young, fading to cinnamon or rust, sometimes
retaining faded olive-green.

**Gills:** Closely spaced, wide, light-cinnamon to light brown,
becoming dark brown to black. Gill edges lighter than faces.

**Stem:** 1.5 to 4 inches tall, up to 0.5 inch thick. Even, hollow,
smooth at top with thick fibrous hairs below. Veil falls away very
early. Fibrous and tough.

**Flesh:** Off-white to yellowish with occasional light brown tints
in cap. Strong grain-like odor. Turns blue after handling.

## Safety Considerations

The guide emphasizes that several non-psilocybin species are
dangerously poisonous. Beginners should never eat mushrooms in the
field. First compare specimens to descriptions, then consume only a
tiny test amount if confident in identification. The Amanita muscaria
is explicitly warned against due to its toxicity. The Galerina genus
is particularly dangerous, as some Galerina species closely resemble
Psilocybe species in appearance and grow in similar habitats but
contain lethal amatoxins.

## Distinguishing Psilocybin Mushrooms from Look-alikes

Several genera of non-psychoactive or toxic mushrooms share
superficial resemblance with psilocybin-containing species:

**Galerina species:** Contain deadly amatoxins (the same toxins
found in Amanita phalloides, the death cap). Galerina marginata
can grow alongside Psilocybe species on decaying wood and shares
a similar brown cap and stature. Key distinguishing features
include a brown spore print (rusty brown) versus the dark purple-
brown to black spore print of Psilocybe species.

**Panaeolina foenisecii (Haymaker's mushroom):** Common in lawns
and frequently confused with Panaeolus subbalteatus. Typically
smaller and lacks the distinctive zone pattern on the cap. Does
not blue when damaged and contains no psilocybin.

**Conocybe species:** Some contain psychoactive compounds but are
rarely consumed intentionally due to difficulty of identification
and the presence of toxic look-alike species. Identification
requires microscopic examination of spore morphology.
