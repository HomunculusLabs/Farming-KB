# Conocybe cyanopus

Conocybe cyanopus is a tiny, rarely encountered psychoactive mushroom species
notable for its bluish-green discoloration at the base of the stem. Despite its
small size and extreme rarity in Europe, it contains significant concentrations
of psilocybin comparable to [[psilocybe-semilanceata]].

## Discovery and Taxonomic History

Around 1930, J. Schaffer discovered numerous Conocybe species growing in
abundance on a fertilized grassy area near Potsdam, Germany. Intrigued by their
extraordinary variety of forms and colors, he undertook the taxonomic
classification essential to pursuing mushroom research. One species found in
Potsdam, Berlin, and Germany's Harz Mountains showed bluish discolorations at
the base of their stems.

This "Galera" species was included by Kuhner as Conocybe cyanopoda in his 1935
monograph on the genus Conocybe. Today, the species is known as Conocybe
cyanopus (Atk.) Kuhn.

The species with a blue base had been previously discovered in the United States
(Ithaca, NY) in 1918 and was judged by Kuhner to be identical with the European
mushrooms, suggesting a wide natural distribution across the Northern Hemisphere.

## Habitat and Distribution

Conocybe cyanopus grows on grassy areas or moss, fruiting from summer through
fall. The species is extraordinarily rare in Europe, hardly ever found among
lists of mushroom discoveries from European countries. Aside from Schaffer's
original discovery, the mushroom was reportedly found only twice within the
territory of the former East Germany over a 60-year period (both during the
1980s).

The second European discovery was made on July 2, 1989 near Potsdam, where
several fruiting bodies were found growing in a grassy area on sand.

The genus Conocybe is a member of the Bolbitiaceae family, which includes the
[[panaeolus-subbalteatus-profile]] species. The mushrooms are small
and fragile, decay quickly, and grow mostly in grassy and mossy areas where they
are easily overlooked. Very few mycologists specialize in the study of Conocybe
due to its lack of attractiveness and the difficulty of preserving specimens.

## Morphology

- **Cap:** 0.3–2.5 cm broad, nearly hemispheric to convex, striated, ocher to
  dark brown without grey-green stains. Surface smooth, hygrophanous, paler
  when dry
- **Stem:** 2–4 cm long, 1–1.4 cm thick, whitish at first, equal to slightly
  curved at the base, silvery later on. Stains bluish-green, particularly at
  the base, in reaction to injuries or with age
- **Gills:** Adnexed to narrowly adnate, close, pale at first then darkening
  to rusty brown as spores mature
- **Spores:** 7–10 × 4–5 micrometers, ellipsoid to slightly amygdaliform,
  smooth to faintly roughened, reddish-brown in deposit
- **Basidia:** 4-spored, pleurocystidia absent, cheilocystidia present,
  18–25 × 6.5–10 micrometers
- **Spore print:** Rusty brown to dark brown

The bluish discoloration at the stem base is the most reliable field
identification characteristic, as no other European Conocybe species display
this reaction. However, the blue staining can be faint and easily missed without
careful examination.

## Chemistry

Psilocybin was discovered for the first time in Conocybe cyanopus fruiting bodies
collected on September 4, 1961 in Seattle, WA. No psilocin was detected in those
American samples. Norwegian researchers later confirmed trace amounts of psilocin,
along with 0.33–0.55% psilocybin, in Finnish samples. Beug and Bigwood reported
0.93% psilocybin in samples from the Pacific Northwest.

Gartz's analysis of five European mushrooms collected in 1989 near Potsdam revealed:

| Mushroom Dry Weight (mg) | Psilocybin (%) | Baeocystin (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.84 | 0.15 |
| 6 | 0.73 | 0.12 |
| 7 | 1.01 | 0.20 |
| 10 | 0.91 | 0.16 |
| 12 | 0.89 | 0.14 |

Concentrations of psilocybin were strikingly similar to those found in
[[psilocybe-semilanceata]] samples, placing
C. cyanopus among the more potent European psychoactive species.

## Sclerotia Formation

After several days, spores from one of the fruiting bodies germinated on malt
agar and grew very slowly into their permanent forms or "sclerotia." The
sclerotia showed no blue discolorations and contained 0.25% psilocybin when
dry, with no additional alkaloids detected.

Sclerotia formation is also known from [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] and [[psilocybe-tampanensis-cultivation]]. The
ability to form these hardened resting structures may explain how the species
survives unfavorable conditions and persists in locations where it is rarely
observed fruiting.

## Significance

Due to its small size and extreme rarity, Conocybe cyanopus is not a significant
contributor to intoxications in Europe, nor is it likely to gain such prominence
in the future. However, its discovery as a psilocybin producer expanded the known
taxonomic range of psychoactive fungi.

Analyses of other non-bluing Conocybe species, such as Conocybe tenera and
Conocybe lactea, revealed only physiologically inactive ingredients. Samples of
Conocybe species from warm countries have not yet been analyzed and may yield
remarkable results in terms of chemical composition.
