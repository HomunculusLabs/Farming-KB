# Permaculture Ethics and Foundations

From "Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability" by David Holmgren.

## The Three Ethics

The three ethical principles of permaculture provide the foundation for the entire design system:

1. **Care of the Earth**: Provision for all life systems to continue and multiply. This is the first principle because without a healthy earth, human civilisation cannot function. It includes all living and non-living things: plants, animals, land, water, and air.

2. **Care of People**: Provision for people to access those resources necessary for their existence. This ethic recognises that human beings are part of nature and that our systems must provide for human needs while maintaining ecological integrity.

3. **Fair Share (or Setting Limits to Population and Consumption)**: By governing our own needs, we can set resources aside to further the above principles. This third ethic is often the most challenging because it requires self-discipline and restraint.

## Ethics as Design Criteria

The ethics are not just abstract moral statements -- they function as design criteria. Every design decision should be evaluated against these three ethics. If a design element does not contribute to earth care, people care, and fair share, it should be reconsidered.

Holmgren emphasises that the ethics provide a framework for thinking, rather than a set of rules. They help guide the application of the twelve design principles in specific contexts.

## From Sustainable to Sustainable-Through-Energy-Descent

The subtitle "Beyond Sustainability" reflects Holmgren's argument that the concept of sustainability has been co-opted by the very systems that are causing ecological destruction. True sustainability must acknowledge energy descent -- the inevitable decline in available energy from fossil fuels -- and design for a future with less, not more, energy throughput.

The transition from abundant cheap energy to energy descent will be the defining challenge of the 21st century. Permaculture provides a design framework for navigating this transition.

## Energy as the Foundation

Energy literacy is fundamental to permaculture thinking. Understanding the energy flows through systems -- from solar energy to fossil fuels to human labour -- is essential for effective design. The concept of EMERGY (embodied energy) developed by Howard Odum provides a quantitative framework for comparing different energy sources and uses.

## Practical Applications

- Conduct a thorough site analysis before design, mapping zones, sectors, soil, water, and existing vegetation.
- Prioritize elements that serve multiple functions — every component should fulfill at least three roles.
- Design for energy efficiency by placing frequently-used elements closest to the zone of daily activity.
- Use biological resources (plants, animals, fungi) instead of mechanical or chemical alternatives wherever possible.
- Plan for succession — design initial plantings that will evolve into mature, self-maintaining systems.
- Create redundancy in critical systems to increase resilience against failure or disruption.
- Incorporate small-scale, slow solutions that can be tested and adjusted before large investments.
- Map and maximize edge effects between different ecosystems or landscape elements.

## The Twelve Design Principles

Holmgren articulated twelve design principles that operationalize the three ethics in practical terms. These principles are not prescriptive rules but rather pattern-based design tools derived from observation of natural systems and traditional land-use practices:

1. **Observe and interact** — Good design depends on a free and harmonious relationship between nature and people, in which careful observation allows the designer to intervene appropriately and with minimal effort. Time spent in observation before action is never wasted.
2. **Catch and store energy** — By developing systems that collect resources at peak abundance, we can use them in times of need. This applies to solar energy, water, biomass, seeds, and financial resources.
3. **Obtain a yield** — Any system that does not produce a tangible yield will eventually be replaced by something that does. The yield should be defined broadly to include not just food and materials but also ecosystem services, social benefits, and personal fulfillment.
4. **Apply self-regulation and accept feedback** — Discouraging inappropriate activity ensures that systems can continue to function well. Negative feedback is essential for keeping systems within productive bounds.
5. **Use and value renewable resources and services** — Make the best use of nature's abundance to reduce our consumptive behavior and dependence on non-renewable resources. The service of natural systems (e.g., decomposition, pollination, water purification) is more valuable than any product they might yield.
6. **Produce no waste** — By valuing and making use of all the resources that are available to us, nothing goes to waste. The waste of one system becomes the input for another, as demonstrated in [[keyline-design]] and [[hugelkultur-bed-construction-detailed]] systems.
7. **Design from patterns to details** — By stepping back, we can observe patterns in nature and society. These can form the backbone of our designs, with the details filled in as we go. The [[permaculture-pattern-language]] of permaculture provides a vocabulary for reading and designing landscapes.
8. **Integrate rather than segregate** — By putting the right things in the right place, relationships develop between those things, and they work together to support each other. The [[mollison-guild-analysis-and-species-interaction]] approach identifies beneficial species combinations.
9. **Use small and slow solutions** — Small and slow systems are easier to maintain than big ones, make better use of local resources, and produce more sustainable outcomes. This principle is exemplified in [[mollison-permaculture-two-zone-planning-and-site-layout]] where the most intensively managed areas are kept small and close to the dwelling.
10. **Use and value diversity** — Diversity reduces vulnerability to a variety of threats and takes advantage of the unique nature of the environment in which it resides. The [[biodiversity-of-fungi-biomass-carbon-soil-structure]] of soil fungi, for example, underpins ecosystem resilience.
11. **Use edges and value the marginal** — The interface between things is where the most interesting events take place. These are often the most valuable, diverse, and productive elements in the system.
12. **Creatively use and respond to change** — We can have a positive impact on inevitable change by carefully observing, and then intervening at the right time.

## Energy Descent and Permaculture's Response to Peak Oil

Holmgren's framework explicitly addresses the challenge of energy descent — the historical period of declining net energy availability that follows the peak of fossil fuel extraction. While mainstream sustainability discourse assumes that technological innovation and renewable energy can maintain current levels of energy consumption, Holmgren argues that this assumption is fundamentally flawed. The [[energy-descent-action-planning]] methodology he developed provides communities with a structured process for adapting to lower energy futures.

The permaculture response to energy descent emphasizes building local resilience through diversified food production, reduced dependence on centralized infrastructure, and strengthened community networks. Holmgren's concept of "retrofitting" applies permaculture design principles to existing suburban developments, transforming them from energy-intensive, car-dependent environments into productive, walkable communities with integrated food, water, and energy systems. This [[permaculture-retrofit-suburbia]] represents a pragmatic alternative to both the "techno-optimist" vision of green capitalism and the "doomer" narrative of civilizational collapse.

## Permaculture as Design Science

Holmgren emphasizes that permaculture is best understood as a design science — a methodology for assembling conceptual, material, and strategic components into coherent patterns that meet human needs while regenerating natural systems. Unlike conventional agriculture, which applies standardized techniques across diverse contexts, permaculture design begins with site-specific observation and proceeds through a structured [[permaculture-design-process]] that accounts for climate, topography, soils, water, existing vegetation, and human social factors.

The [[holmgren-design-science-discipline]] that Holmgren articulates draws on systems ecology, landscape architecture, traditional ecological knowledge, and appropriate technology. It is inherently interdisciplinary, requiring the designer to integrate insights from natural science, engineering, economics, and social science. The [[pdc-design-project-methodology-and-case-study]] provides a 72-hour curriculum for teaching this design science, though Holmgren has emphasized that true competence in permaculture design requires years of practice and observation beyond formal training.

## Critiques and Evolving Perspectives

Holmgren's framework has not been without criticism. Some scholars argue that the three ethics are too vague to function as meaningful design criteria, and that different practitioners can invoke the same ethics to justify radically different — even contradictory — design decisions. The third ethic in particular has been subject to multiple interpretations: "fair share" has been read variously as redistributive economics, population control, voluntary simplicity, and return of surplus to the first two ethics.

Others have noted that permaculture, as traditionally practiced, has not adequately addressed issues of social justice, race, gender, and colonialism. The [[holmgren-community-design-and-intentional-communities]] dimension of permaculture has received less attention than its agricultural and ecological aspects, though this imbalance is gradually being corrected. Holmgren himself has acknowledged these gaps and has increasingly emphasized the social and economic dimensions of permaculture design in his later work, including his writing on [[community-finance-and-alternative-economies]] as essential components of a permaculture society. The [[williams-pdc-invisible-structures]] developed within the PDC framework represents one attempt to address these social dimensions systematically.
