# Fungal Symbiosis with Insects

Fungi form intimate and often obligate symbiotic associations with a remarkable diversity of insects, including leafcutter ants, termites, ambrosia beetles, wood wasps, and many others. These symbioses range from mutualistic fungus gardens to parasitic relationships and casual dispersal associations. Insect-fungal symbioses are responsible for approximately 20% of Neotropical photosynthate turnover.

## Leafcutter Ants and Their Fungal Cultivars

The attine ant-fungus association is one of the most complex and well-studied mutualisms in nature:

- **Higher attines** (Atta, Acromyrmex, Apterostigma): Cultivate Leucoagaricus gongylophorus, probably a single species in which all representatives may be clonal descendants of a common ancestor (Chapela et al. 1994; Fisher et al. 1994)
- **Lower attines** (Mycocepurus, Mycetarotes, Sericomyrmex): Cultivate fungi that eventually produce basidiocarps consistent with Leucoagaricus or Leucocoprinus species
- **Escovopsis**: A specialized parasite of attine fungus gardens that can devastate cultivated fungi
- **Culture collections**: A comprehensive collection of attine-associated fungi is maintained at Cornell University and the National Fungus Collection

The nomenclature of attine-associated fungi remains fluid. A conservative approach is to refer to each isolate by the name of the ant species that cultured it, together with location and date records.

## Termites and Fungal Gardens

Termite gardening symbioses are restricted to Old World higher termites, primarily of the tribe Macrotermitinae (family Termitidae):

- **Range**: Tropical Africa, extending eastward to southeastern Asia
- **Fungal associates**: Species of Termitomyces (Lepiotaceae), the same basidiomycete family associated with most attine ants
- **Fungus comb**: Termites construct "combs" of fecal pellets on which Termitomyces grows; the combs are referred to as fungus gardens
- **Basidiocarp production**: Most Termitomyces species form basidiocarps annually at the onset of the rainy season
- **Dispersal**: In some species, fungal inoculum is dispersed in the guts of alates (winged reproductives)
- **Edibility**: Termitomyces mushrooms are considered choice edibles and are actively collected by local people

**Xylaria** is regularly associated with Termitomyces in combs, but the relationship is not fully understood. When combs are removed from nests, Xylaria quickly overgrows Termitomyces, suggesting the termites and nest conditions (e.g., high CO2 levels) normally suppress saprobic competitors.

## Ambrosia Beetles and Bark Beetles

- Ambrosia beetles cultivate fungi in galleries bored into wood; the fungi serve as the primary food source for both adults and larvae
- Bark beetles carry stain and blue-stain fungi (Ophiostoma, Ceratocystis) that may help overcome tree defenses and serve as nutritional mutualists
- Some fungi sporulate deep within beetle galleries, requiring mites or beetles for dispersal since beetles may lose accumulated spores during their last molt in the outer bark
- Filamentous ascomycete genera such as Melanospora, Kathistes, Subbaromyces, and Sphaeronemella may depend completely on arthropod dispersers, as suggested by their long-necked ophiostomatoid perithecia

## Wood Wasps (Siricidae)

- Several genera of resupinate aphyllophoralean wood-decaying basidiomycetes have specific interactions with wood wasps
- The wasps require fungi for nutrition (carried as oidia in mycangia), but the fungi do not depend on wasps for dispersal
- Amylostereum areolatum with Sirex noctilio has become a serious pathogen of Pinus radiata in New Zealand and Australia
- Cerrena unicolor associates with Tremex species in hardwoods; all other wood wasp associates occur in conifers

## Other Associations

- **Laboulbeniales**: Ectoparasitic ascomycetes on insect exoskeletons, representing an obligate symbiotic relationship
- **Trichomycetes**: Gut-inhabiting fungi commensal with aquatic insect larvae
- **Septobasidiales**: Scale insect-associated basidiomycetes
- **Yeastlike endosymbionts**: Carried by leafhoppers and other insects; can be purified using isopycnic density centrifugation

## Molecular Methods

DNA extraction from minute, nonculturable symbiotic fungi (e.g., Laboulbeniales, Trichomycetes, endosymbionts) is challenging due to organism size and contamination from host DNA. PCR-based methods using primers that discriminate between insect and fungal DNA templates have been developed. In situ PCR techniques show great potential for molecular analysis of endosymbiotic fungi.

## Sampling Methodologies for Insect-Associated Fungi

Vast numbers of fungi are associated with a variety of insects and other arthropods to form symbioses of various types. The fungi of these associations include necrotrophic and biotrophic parasites, which may be dispersed by their hosts. In other interactions, insects use fungi directly as food or as sources of enzymes. Few protocols are available for quantitative sampling of insect fungi. Problems involve the unpredictable timing of fruiting structures, the microscopic nature of many species, and the patchy distribution of associated arthropods.

### Trapping Methods for Host Insects

- **Flight-interception traps**: Large-area nets (~3 m across) suspended vertically above trays of preservative. Effective for obtaining quantitative samples rich in beetles (Coleoptera) and flies (Diptera). In lowland tropical moist forest in Indonesia, average weekly catches of beetles ranged from 100 to 250 species.
- **Light traps**: UV and mercury vapor lamps attract a wide range of insects including Coleoptera and Diptera. Frequently attract beetles associated with water, which can be a rich source of Laboulbeniales.
- **Pitfall traps**: Containers sunk into the ground for trapping active ground-dwelling invertebrates such as carabid beetles. Catches are determined by both population size and activity.
- **Canopy fogging**: Insecticidal fogging provides enormous insight into the structure and richness of canopy fauna. Large numbers of beetles (Chrysomelidae, Corylophidae) — hosts of Laboulbeniales — can be obtained.
- **Litter samples**: Known volumes of litter sampled for ground- and litter-dwelling invertebrates, providing beetles, ants, millipedes, and mites.

## Necrotrophic Parasites of Arthropods

Fungi that kill insects represent a very diverse group. *Cordyceps*, with more than 200 described species, is the largest genus. Species of *Cordyceps* most frequently attack Lepidoptera, Hymenoptera, Coleoptera, and Orthoptera. Necrotrophs may be host-specific, and many are potential biocontrol agents. Most species do not sporulate until the host is dead or nearly so. In some cases the host actually moves around and disperses spores actively.

Major groups include:
- **Zygomycota**: *Entomophthorales* — *Entomophaga*, *Entomophthora*, *Erynia*, *Furia*, *Massospora*, *Pandora*, *Zoophthora*, *Neozygites*, *Conidiobolus*
- **Ascomycota**: *Hypocreales* — *Cordyceps*, *Torrubiella*, *Gibellula*, *Hirsutella*, and their anamorphs including *Beauveria*, *Metarhizium*, *Paecilomyces*, *Verticillium*

## Trichomycetes: Gut-Inhabiting Fungi

Trichomycetes exist throughout the world and are found attached to the gut lining of aquatic insect larvae (midges, blackflies, mayflies, stoneflies, and beetles). Currently about 225 species and 55 genera are recognized, but the true richness is far greater. Most species appear to be commensals, though some may be mutualistic by aiding in digestion.

Special adaptations evolved in Trichomycetes include holdfasts for attachment to the chitinous gut lining of the host and timing of spore release to coincide with the molting cycle of the host. They can be studied by dissecting the guts of aquatic insect larvae and examining the hindgut under a compound microscope. Most harpellid isolates grow and sporulate on standard mycological media.

## Laboulbeniales: Ectoparasitic Ascomycetes

The Laboulbeniales are arthropod-associated ascomycetes that form obligate ectoparasitic relationships with insect exoskeletons. They are among the most highly specialized and morphologically distinct of all fungal groups. Their precise placement on specific body parts of host insects and their host specificity make them valuable for both systematic and ecological studies.

Large-area flight-interception traps and light traps provide quantitative samples that have been used to assess infection patterns of Laboulbeniales across sites and elevations. Catches from water-associated beetles (Gyrinidae, Dytiscidae, Hydrophilidae) are particularly rich sources.

## Collection and Preservation

Once collected, fungi of interest or their insect hosts can be placed in various containers. Material should be maintained in as unchanged a condition as possible until study, especially when cultivation is important. Many fungi tolerate drying and can be cultivated from herbarium specimens many years old, but others die within hours of drying.

For molecular studies, it is best not to store specimens in liquid and not to dry them at high temperatures for long periods, as DNA can degrade when moist specimens lie about. Many groups of insect-associated fungi have never been cultured, yet they should still be collected and preserved as voucher specimens.
