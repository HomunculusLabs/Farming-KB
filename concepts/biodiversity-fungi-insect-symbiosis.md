## Trichomycetes: Gut-Inhabiting Fungi

Trichomycetes exist throughout the world and are found attached to the gut lining of aquatic insect larvae (midges, blackflies, mayflies, stoneflies, and beetles). Currently about 225 species and 55 genera are recognized, but the true richness is far greater. Most species appear to be commensals, though some may be mutualistic by aiding in digestion.

Special adaptations evolved in Trichomycetes include holdfasts for attachment to the chitinous gut lining of the host and timing of spore release to coincide with the molting cycle of the host. They can be studied by dissecting the guts of aquatic insect larvae and examining the hindgut under a compound microscope. Most harpellid isolates grow and sporulate on standard mycological media.

## Laboulbeniales: Ectoparasitic Ascomycetes

The Laboulbeniales are arthropod-associated ascomycetes that form obligate ectoparasitic relationships with insect exoskeletons. They are among the most highly specialized and morphologically distinct of all fungal groups. Their precise placement on specific body parts of host insects and their host specificity make them valuable for both systematic and ecological studies.

Large-area flight-interception traps and light traps provide quantitative samples that have been used to assess infection patterns of Laboulbeniales across sites and elevations. Catches from water-associated beetles (Gyrinidae, Dytiscidae, Hydrophilidae) are particularly rich sources.

## Collection and Preservation

Once collected, fungi of interest or their insect hosts can be placed in various containers. Material should be maintained in as unchanged a condition as possible until study, especially when cultivation is important. Many fungi tolerate drying and can be cultivated from herbarium specimens many years old, but others die within hours of drying.

For molecular studies, it is best not to store specimens in liquid and not to dry them at high temperatures for long periods, as DNA can degrade when moist specimens lie about. Many groups of insect-associated fungi have never been cultured, yet they should still be collected and preserved as voucher specimens.
