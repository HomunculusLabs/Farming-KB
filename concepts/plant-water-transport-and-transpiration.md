# Plant Water Transport and Transpiration

Water movement through plants is one of the most elegant processes in botany, driven by a combination of physical forces that require no energy expenditure from the plant itself. Understanding how water travels from soil into roots, up through stems, and into leaves is fundamental to understanding plant nutrition and health.

## How Water Enters Roots

Water enters plant roots through two primary mechanisms: **interception** and **mass flow**.

### Interception
Roots, and specifically [[root-hairs]], physically grow into areas of soil that contain water. A microscopic film of water typically coats soil particles, and roots come into direct contact with this moisture. Root hairs dramatically increase the surface area available for this contact — a single rye plant can have miles of root hairs in just 2 cubic feet of soil.

### Mass Flow
In most instances, mass flow is the dominant mechanism. When a root acts like a sponge in a puddle, water flows toward it. This occurs because of **hydrogen bonds** between water molecules — the same bonds that drive [[transpiration]] in the xylem. Water molecules are pulled by hydrogen-bonded neighbors out of the soil and into the plant, pulling those behind them closer and toward the root surface.

## The Apoplastic and Symplastic Pathways

Once water reaches the root surface, it can travel through the root via two distinct pathways:

### Apoplastic Pathway
Water moves through the **cell walls** and the spaces between cells without crossing any membranes. This pathway is relatively unregulated — anything dissolved in the water can travel along with it.

### Symplastic Pathway
Water crosses the **plasmalemma** (cell membrane), probably via aquaporin channels, and enters the cytoplasm of the cell. From there it moves from cell to cell through **plasmodesmata** — tiny channels connecting adjacent plant cells. This pathway is highly selective because cell membranes control what enters and exits.

## The Endodermis and Casparian Strip

The **endodermis** is the critical control point between the apoplastic and symplastic pathways. This layer of tightly packed cells contains the **[[casparian-strip]]** — a band of cells impregnated with **suberin**, a waxy substance. The Casparian strip blocks the [[apoplastic-pathway]], forcing all water and dissolved substances to cross a cell membrane and enter the symplastic pathway. Because cell membranes are far more selective than porous cell walls, this gives the plant precise control over what it absorbs.

## Root Pressure and Osmosis

Water enters root cells because of **root pressure**, driven by osmosis. Root cells typically maintain a higher concentration of nutrients (solutes) than the surrounding soil water. This concentration gradient causes water to flow across the root cell membranes into the cells to dilute the internal concentration. This osmotic pull is especially important at night when transpiration has ceased.

## The Xylem: Upward Water Transport

Once inside the root, water enters the **xylem** tissue, which runs up through the stem and into the leaves. Three forces drive water movement through the xylem:

### 1. Transpiration
The primary driver. When **stomata** (leaf pores) open during the day to allow CO₂ in for photosynthesis, water in the xylem at the base of these openings evaporates into the atmosphere. **Trichomes** — tiny hair-like structures on leaves — help draw evaporated water up and away, speeding the process. Approximately 90% of a plant's water loss occurs through transpiration.

### 2. Cohesion
Water molecules are **polar**, with one side carrying a partial negative charge and the other a partial positive charge. This polarity causes water molecules to stick to each other (cohesion). When one molecule evaporates from a stoma, it pulls the molecule behind it upward, creating a chain reaction all the way down the column. This generates **negative pressure** at the bottom of the water column, pulling new water molecules into the root system.

### 3. Adhesion
Water molecules also stick to the surfaces of their container — in this case, the xylem tubes. This creates a concave **meniscus** where water contacts the tube walls. The tension created by adhesion, combined with cohesion, can fill even a redwood-sized xylem system.

Together, these forces can move water and dissolved ions through xylem at speeds of up to **30 meters (100 feet) per hour** — all without the plant expending any metabolic energy.

## Nighttime: When Transpiration Stops

At night, stomata close and transpiration ceases. During this period, plants **respire** — utilizing the water and nutrients transported during the day. Although water is no longer moving upward, the forces of cohesion and adhesion maintain the water column in place within the xylem.

## The Phloem: Sugar Transport

The **phloem** is the other half of a plant's vascular tissue, responsible for transporting sugars produced during photosynthesis, along with amino acids, organic compounds, and even RNA, to where they are needed.

### Structure
Phloem consists of specialized, elongated cells called **sieve elements**, which lack lignin. These connect vertically to form **sieve tubes**, separated by perforated **sieve plates** lined with **callose** (a polysaccharide). At maturity, sieve elements lose their nuclei, vacuoles, and ribosomes but retain plastids, [[endoplasmic-reticulum]], and mitochondria — they remain alive, unlike xylem cells.

Each sieve element pairs with a **companion cell** that performs the functions of missing organelles. Sugar molecules are collected in companion cells and actively loaded into sieve tubes, requiring energy.

### Pressure Flow Mechanism
Unlike xylem, phloem transport is driven by **hydrostatic pressure**, not transpiration:

1. **Source**: Sugar is actively loaded into sieve tubes at a source (e.g., leaf chloroplasts during summer, or root storage cells in spring)
2. **Osmosis**: The high sugar concentration draws water from adjacent xylem vessels into the phloem
3. **Pressure**: The influx of water creates positive hydrostatic pressure inside the sieve tubes
4. **Sink**: At the receiving end (the sink), sugars are unloaded and ions flow out through plasmodesmata, lowering the concentration and relieving pressure

This bidirectional system (though not simultaneously in both directions) explains why trees are tapped for maple and birch syrup only in spring — that's when stored nutrients move from roots upward through the xylem.

## Stems: Protection and Support

Stems evolved primarily to protect vascular tissue and provide structural support for leaves, fruits, and flowers. Specialized dermal cells on the stem exterior provide waterproofing, while ground tissue cells act like packing material around the xylem and phloem.

## Key Takeaways

- Water moves through plants via transpiration, cohesion, and adhesion — requiring no metabolic energy
- The Casparian strip in the endodermis forces selective uptake by blocking the apoplastic pathway
- Phloem transport uses hydrostatic pressure from osmotic water influx to move sugars from source to sink
- Root hairs increase root surface area enormously, enabling efficient water and nutrient interception
- Up to 90% of plant water is lost through transpiration, making stomatal regulation critical for water balance
