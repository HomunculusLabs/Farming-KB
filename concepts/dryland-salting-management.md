# Dryland Salting and Water Table Management

## Overview

[[soil-collapse]] concentrating [[cyclic-salt]]**: Salt deposited by rainfall that is normally carried back to sea by stream flow, roughly balancing input and output in undisturbed landscapes.
- **[[connate-salt]]**: Ancient salt trapped in soil profiles since geological time. This salt is mobilized by groundwater changes, soil collapse, and deforestation, and represents the bulk of dryland salting problems.

### Stream Salt Amplification

In farmed areas, salt levels in streams can be 20 times higher than in undisturbed catchments, as connate salt is mobilized by groundwaters and soil collapse processes.

## Progression of Dryland Salting

### Warning Signs

- Raised water table or soil compaction
- Brownish patches of sea barley grass (*[[hordeum-maritimum]]*) and buckthorn plantain (*Plantago cornopus*) as surface indicators
- Loss of crop vigor
- Saturated, anaerobic subsoils

### Active Salting

At a groundwater level of approximately 1.8 m, capillary action takes over and surface evaporation creates salt crusts (efflorescences). Salted areas then spread uphill, rushes (*Juncus*) colonize, and bare soil areas increase. In Western Australia, the uphill spread is attributed to damming of groundwater flow by lower collapsed soil areas.

### Self-Reinforcing Cycle

- Wet years exacerbate the problem, which does not necessarily recede in dry years
- Collapsed soils create perched water tables that further waterlog surrounding areas
- Salt is washed downstream, spreading the problem to new areas
- Dying vegetation reduces transpiration, allowing water tables to rise further

## Extent of Deforestation and Its Consequences

Original tree cover remaining:
- Western Australia: 0.7%
- South Australia: 3%
- Victoria: 13%
- Ethiopia: 4%

All these regions suffer from increased stream and groundwater salting, raised water tables, and increased artesian pressures from deep salt leads, risking surface salt seepages. The correlation between deforestation and salting is clear: country under forest in the same areas is not salted. It is agricultural clearing, wood-chipping, and net forest loss that creates salt deserts.

## The Role of Trees in Salinity Control

Evergreen trees provide multiple benefits for salinity management:

- **Transpiration**: Trees transpire more water than crops, keeping water tables down
- **Infiltration**: Forest soils permit better rain infiltration and maintain soil pore space
- **Salt excretion**: Forests in drylands excrete salts that accumulate in surface soils, though this is concentrated further by soil collapse

The critical strategy is to forbid further clearing (South Australia has done so), trial [[interceptor-banks]], and revegetate upland or intake areas with trees.

## Cautious Approaches to Dryland Design

Given the multiple interactions between salts, evaporation, soils, slopes, drainage, and land use, and given that problems may not manifest for 100 years or more, designers should follow a sequence of cautious approaches:

1. **Small-scale trials**: Try a variety of small earthwork and land use systems on only one slope or soil type.
2. **Monitoring**: Track infiltration, evaporation, salinity changes, and crop health over several years.
3. **Cautious extension**: Extend only systems that show no salt increase over the short term.
4. **Avoid stagnant storage**: Never store runoff where tree roots cannot remove the water, unless using subsurface storage in sand basins or sand dams.
5. **Flood diversion**: Where groundwater tables are shallow, concentrate on lessening infiltration by diverting floodwater to streams and floodways.
6. **Swale infiltration**: Where soils are free-draining, increase local infiltration by swales to support tree cover.
7. **Efficient irrigation**: Use ridge trickle irrigation in dry periods; allow floodwaters to flush furrows of accumulated salts during rains.
8. **Prefer surface water**: Favor roof tanks and surface swales over wells or bores, which lower total aquifer resources and create local excesses.
9. **Local assessment**: Use back-hoe pits or deep pit samples to establish local drainage conditions before choosing strategies.
10. **Yield assessment**: Manage natural yields (e.g., native wildlife) before imposing exotic species on fragile environments.
11. **Observe natural productivity**: Patches where yields are naturally high indicate safe conditions for increased production.

## Summary of Evidence

- Soil collapse in clay-fraction soils causes waterlogging, gullying, and crop death from compaction and anaerobic conditions.
- In deforested areas with permeable intake soils, salty groundwaters rise by flood infiltration.
- Trees are the most effective means of removing excess soil water compared to crops or fallow.
- Interceptor banks enable collapsed soils to recover by eliminating overland flow.
- Local assessment (via back-hoe pits) is essential before recommending any strategy.
- Until interceptor banks are constructed in collapsed soils, trees will not survive.

## Practical Applications

This concept has several practical applications in cultivation and
management systems. Practitioners implement these techniques across
diverse climates and growing conditions, adapting principles to local
environmental constraints and available resources.

## Regional Variations

Approaches vary significantly across geographic regions due to
differences in climate, soil type, and cultural practices. Temperate
regions emphasize different strategies compared to tropical or arid
environments, with local adaptation being essential for success.

## Research Directions

Current research explores optimization of these methods through
controlled studies and long-term field trials. Areas of active
investigation include efficiency improvements, cost reduction,
and integration with complementary techniques and technologies.

## Environmental Considerations

Environmental factors play a significant role in the implementation
and success of these approaches. Soil conditions, water availability,
temperature ranges, and seasonal patterns all influence outcomes.
Sustainable practices prioritize working within ecological limits.

## Economic Aspects

Cost-benefit analysis reveals varying economic viability depending
on scale, market access, and resource availability. Small-scale
operations often achieve better per-unit economics through reduced
overhead and direct market channels compared to industrial approaches.
