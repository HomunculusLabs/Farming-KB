# Ectomycorrhizal Community Responses to Environmental Stress

## Overview

Ectomycorrhizal fungi (EMF) are highly sensitive to environmental perturbation, and their community structure, diversity, and function shift markedly under a range of anthropogenic stressors including atmospheric pollution, heavy metal contamination, acid deposition, nitrogen enrichment, and climate change. The research synthesized in Dighton's *Fungi in Ecosystem Processes* (Chapter 6) draws on a broad suite of experimental and observational studies to document how these stressors restructure EMF communities and cascade through forest ecosystems.

## Pollution-Driven Changes in EMF Community Structure

### Heavy Metal Contamination

Heavy metal pollution from industrial sources represents one of the most thoroughly documented stressors on EMF communities. Rühling, Söderström, and colleagues conducted landmark surveys of macromycete fruit body production in heavy-metal-polluted coniferous forests of northern Sweden (Rühling & Söderström 1990; Rühling et al. 1984). Their work near the Gusum brass mill demonstrated dramatic declines in both the number of fungal species and the total fruit body production of mycorrhizal and litter-decomposing macromycetes in contaminated soils. The pattern was consistent: as soil metal concentrations increased, EMF diversity and sporocarp abundance declined, with sensitive species disappearing first and more tolerant taxa persisting.

Nordgren et al. (1986) corroborated these findings with direct measurements of soil microbial activity and mycelial lengths in heavy metal-polluted areas, showing reduced fungal biomass alongside shifts in bacterial physiological groups. The mechanisms of metal toxicity include disruption of enzyme function, oxidative stress, and direct interference with hyphal membrane integrity. Thompson & Medve (1984) demonstrated that aluminium and manganese—mobilized under acidic conditions—directly inhibited growth of ectomycorrhizal fungi in culture, linking metal toxicity to acidification pathways.

Some EMF species exhibit considerable metal tolerance. Tam (1995) documented [[mycorrhizal-fungi-heavy-metal-tolerance]] by EMF species and demonstrated that *Pisolithus tinctorius* could ameliorate metal toxicity for its host plant, reducing metal uptake into plant tissues while maintaining mycorrhizal function. Turneau et al. (1993, 1994) provided ultrastructural evidence of element distribution in *Paxillus involutus*–*Pinus sylvestris* mycorrhizae from heavily polluted forests, and in *Pisolithus tinctorius* mycelium treated with cadmium dust. Their observations suggested that compartmentalization of metals within fungal vacuoles and cell walls is a key tolerance mechanism, effectively sequestering toxic elements away from sensitive cellular machinery.

The capacity for metal biosorption extends beyond mycorrhizal species to saprotrophic fungi as well. Tobin et al. (1984) demonstrated uptake of metal ions by *Rhizopus arrhizus* biomass, while Starling & Ross (1991) documented zinc uptake by *Penicillium notatum*. These findings collectively suggest that fungal cell walls—rich in chitin, glucans, and other polysaccharides with metal-binding functional groups—provide an effective biosorbent matrix for heavy metal sequestration. The implications for EMF communities are that metal-tolerant species may survive in contaminated soils by internalizing and compartmentalizing metals, while sensitive species are excluded, leading to the reduced diversity patterns observed by Rühling and colleagues.

### Sulphur Dioxide and Acid Deposition

Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) pollution and acid rain have profound effects on EMF communities through both direct phytotoxicity and indirect soil-mediated pathways. Prescott & Parkinson (1985) demonstrated that sulphur pollution significantly reduced rates of litter decomposition in pine forests, altering the substrate availability for fungal decomposers and potentially disrupting nutrient cycling feedbacks to mycorrhizal hosts. The reduction in decomposition rates under sulphur pollution implies slower nutrient mineralization, which in turn may increase host plant dependence on mycorrhizal fungi for nutrient acquisition—a potential compensatory mechanism that could be overwhelmed under severe or chronic pollution loads.

Newsham et al. (1992a, 1992b) conducted detailed experiments on the effects of dry-deposited SO₂ on fungal decomposition of angiosperm tree leaf litter, documenting changes in saprotrophic fungal community composition and litter chemistry. Their work showed that SO₂ exposure altered both the species composition of decomposer communities and the chemical trajectory of decomposition, with implications for nutrient release to mycorrhizal systems. The shift in decomposer communities under SO₂ stress may favor tolerant generalist species at the expense of sensitive specialists, reducing the functional diversity of the fungal community and its capacity to decompose diverse litter types.

The Liphook forest fumigation experiment provided field-scale evidence of pollution effects on mycorrhizas. Shaw, Dighton, and colleagues (Shaw et al. 1992, 1993) exposed Scots pine and Norway spruce to SO₂ and ozone in an open-air fumigation system, documenting alterations in mycorrhizal community composition and FDA (fluorescein diacetate) activity of leaf litter. Shaw (1996) further showed that acid mist and ozone reduced FDA activity, a proxy for overall microbial metabolic activity, in leaf litter environments. The Liphook results were particularly valuable because they integrated the effects of realistic pollutant exposures on mature forest trees rather than seedlings, providing more ecologically relevant evidence of community-level impacts.

Stroo & Alexander (1985) demonstrated that simulated acid rain reduced mycorrhizal infection of *Pinus strobus*, while Ruark et al. (1991) showed that combined exposure of loblolly pine seedlings to acid precipitation and ozone altered soil rhizosphere chemistry, creating conditions unfavorable for mycorrhizal establishment. Qui et al. (1993) examined interactive effects of ozone and acid precipitation on ectomycorrhizal formation on loblolly pine seedlings, finding significant reductions in mycorrhizal colonization under combined stress. The interaction between ozone and acid precipitation is noteworthy because these pollutants frequently co-occur in industrial regions, and their combined effects on EMF may be greater than the sum of individual stressors.

Acidification also mobilizes toxic aluminium and manganese ions in soil solution. Thompson & Medve (1984) showed that these metals directly inhibited EMF growth in culture, providing a mechanistic link between acid deposition and mycorrhizal decline. Schier (1985) further documented aluminium toxicity effects on red spruce and balsam fir seedlings, highlighting the aboveground consequences of soil acidification for tree species that depend heavily on ectomycorrhizal associations. Skeffington & Brown (1986) documented the long-term soil chemistry changes resulting from five years of acid treatment, including increased leaching of base cations and accumulation of exchangeable aluminium—conditions that progressively degrade the soil environment for mycorrhizal fungi.

### Nitrogen Deposition

Atmospheric nitrogen deposition has emerged as a major driver of EMF community change. Elevated nitrogen fundamentally alters the mutualistic balance between EMF and their hosts by reducing plant dependence on fungal nutrient acquisition. Rühling & Tyler (1991) experimentally added nitrogen to the forest floor of a beech forest and observed shifts in the macrofungal flora, with declines in mycorrhizal species and increases in litter saprotrophs reflecting the relaxed nitrogen limitation.

The NITREX (NITRogen saturation EXperiments) network provided ecosystem-scale evidence. Reynolds et al. (1998) evaluated critical loads of nutrient nitrogen and acidity for terrestrial systems using NITREX experiments, demonstrating that nitrogen additions beyond critical thresholds led to soil acidification, base cation depletion, and reorganization of belowground microbial communities including mycorrhizal fungi.

Nylund (1989) challenged the classical nitrogen-carbohydrate theory of ectomycorrhiza formation, arguing that the simple model—in which low plant nitrogen status promotes carbohydrate allocation to mycorrhizal fungi—was insufficient to explain observed patterns. This theoretical revision has important implications for understanding EMF responses to nitrogen deposition, as it suggests that nitrogen effects on community structure may operate through more complex physiological and ecological pathways than previously assumed.

Tietema et al. (1993) documented that nitrogen cycling in acid forest soils was fundamentally altered by increased atmospheric nitrogen input, with potential cascading effects on mycorrhizal function. Treseder & Allen (2000) proposed that mycorrhizal fungi play a significant role in soil carbon storage under elevated CO₂ and nitrogen deposition, suggesting that shifts in EMF communities under nitrogen enrichment may have feedback consequences for ecosystem carbon budgets. The interplay between nitrogen and carbon dynamics in mycorrhizal systems is particularly relevant given current projections of continued increases in both atmospheric CO₂ and nitrogen deposition across many forest regions.

## Forest Decline and Ectomycorrhizal Feedbacks

The phenomenon of widespread forest decline in Europe and North America during the late 20th century has been partially attributed to mycorrhizal dysfunction under combined environmental stresses. Oren et al. (1989) investigated the performance of Norway spruce (*Picea abies*) stands at different stages of decline, documenting altered carbon relations and reduced stand growth that were consistent with impaired mycorrhizal nutrient acquisition. Their work suggested that as pollution stress accumulates, the carbon economy of trees shifts away from mycorrhizal investment toward immediate stress response, creating a positive feedback loop: reduced mycorrhizal function leads to poorer nutrient status, which further weakens tree vigour and reduces carbon availability for maintaining the mycorrhizal symbiosis.

Termorshuizen & Schaffers (1987, 1989, 1991) conducted an extensive series of studies on the decline of ectomycorrhizal carpophores (fruit bodies) in stands of Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*) in The Netherlands, correlating reduced sporocarp production with stand vitality decline and regional air pollution gradients. Their work established that carpophore decline preceded visible canopy symptoms, suggesting that belowground mycorrhizal disruption may serve as an early warning indicator of forest health deterioration. Sobotka (1964) had earlier documented effects of industrial exhalations on soil biology of Norway spruce stands in the Ore Mountains, providing historical context for the progressive nature of pollution impacts on forest soil ecosystems.

## Carbon Transfer and Interspecific Linkages

A landmark finding in mycorrhizal ecology was the demonstration of reciprocal carbon transfer between ectomycorrhizal tree species. Simard et al. (1997a, 1997b) used carbon isotopes to trace bidirectional carbon movement between ectomycorrhizal *Betula papyrifera* (paper birch) and *Pseudotsuga menziesii* (Douglas-fir) through a shared mycorrhizal network. Their field experiments demonstrated net carbon transfer from birch to Douglas-fir, revealing that mycorrhizal hyphal connections can serve as conduits for resource sharing between competing tree species. This "wood-wide web" has profound implications for understanding forest community dynamics under stress: when one species is weakened by pollution or other stressors, the mycorrhizal network may partially compensate through inter-plant carbon transfer—provided the network itself remains intact. The Simard findings also imply that pollution-induced disruption of mycorrhizal networks could sever these inter-plant connections, amplifying the competitive disadvantages suffered by stressed trees.

## Fungal Food Webs and Collembola Interactions

EMF communities exist within complex soil food webs, and environmental stress that alters fungal communities cascades to higher trophic levels. Shaw (1988, 1992) documented consistent feeding hierarchies among collembola species on fungal food resources, demonstrating that the springtail *Onychiurus armatus* exhibited clear preferences among fungal taxa. These selective feeding patterns mean that shifts in EMF community composition under pollution can alter the availability of preferred food resources for fungivorous microarthropods, potentially destabilizing [[soil-food-web-structure]].

The implications of selective collembola grazing extend beyond simple trophic transfer. By preferentially consuming certain fungal species, collembola can exert top-down control on EMF community composition, potentially amplifying or dampening the effects of pollution-driven shifts. If pollution eliminates the preferred food species of a dominant collembola species, the resulting trophic mismatch may reduce collembola populations, with further cascading effects on predatory mites and other higher consumers. Shaw's work on fungal food webs emphasized that the structure and stability of these belowground communities depends on maintaining diverse fungal resources—a condition that pollution frequently disrupts.

Ruess et al. (1993, 1996) demonstrated that experimental acidification affected nematodes, bacteria, and fungi simultaneously, illustrating the integrated nature of soil community responses to pollution stress. Their combined microcosm and field experiments revealed that acidification reduced fungal biomass and mycorrhizal colonization while altering nematode community structure, providing direct evidence that pollution effects propagate through multiple trophic levels of [[soil-food-web]].

## Elevated CO₂ and Climate Change Interactions

Elevated atmospheric CO₂ influences EMF community structure through changes in host carbon allocation. O'Neill et al. (1987) documented increased mycorrhizal colonization and seedling growth in *Pinus echinata* and *Quercus alba* under enriched CO₂, suggesting that rising CO₂ may strengthen the mycorrhizal symbiosis by increasing the carbohydrate supply available to fungal partners. Rygiewicz et al. (2000) examined morphotype community structure of ectomycorrhizas on Douglas-fir seedlings grown under elevated CO₂ and temperature, finding that warming and CO₂ enrichment altered the relative abundance of different mycorrhizal morphotypes, potentially shifting the functional composition of EMF communities. Certain morphotypes that may be more efficient at nutrient foraging were favored under elevated CO₂, while others declined.

The interaction between CO₂ elevation and nitrogen deposition is particularly important. Nylund & Wallander (1989) examined the effects of ectomycorrhiza on host growth and carbon balance, providing baseline data for understanding how the symbiosis mediates plant carbon economies. Under elevated CO₂, plants allocate more carbon belowground, potentially increasing the resource pool available to mycorrhizal fungi. However, when combined with nitrogen deposition—which reduces plant dependence on mycorrhizal nutrient acquisition—the net effect on EMF communities becomes difficult to predict. Rillig & Allen (1999) examined the role of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] in plant-to-ecosystem responses to elevated CO₂, noting that the resource balance model predicts increased mycorrhizal allocation under CO₂ enrichment when other resources (particularly phosphorus and nitrogen) remain limiting.

Sanders et al. (1998) demonstrated increased allocation to external hyphae of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] under CO₂ enrichment, a finding that may extend to ectomycorrhizal systems with implications for soil carbon storage [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]]. Seegmüller & Rennenberg (1994) showed interactive effects of mycorrhization and elevated CO₂ on growth of young pedunculate oak, suggesting that the benefits of mycorrhizal colonization may be amplified under future atmospheric conditions. These climate change interactions add another layer of complexity to understanding EMF community responses to the multifactorial stress environments created by human activities.

## Landfill Reclamation and Ectomycorrhizal Applications

EMF have been investigated for their potential role in restoring degraded and contaminated sites. Senior et al. (1993) and Tosh et al. (1993) developed criteria for fungal selection in landfill site reclamation programmes, recognizing that ectomycorrhizal inoculation could improve tree establishment on stressful substrates. Successful reclamation requires selecting EMF strains tolerant of the specific combination of stressors present—often including heavy metals, extreme pH, low organic matter, and phytotoxic compounds such as methane and hydrogen sulphide.

The approach to landfill reclamation with EMF involves several key considerations: (1) screening candidate fungal isolates for tolerance to site-specific contaminants; (2) ensuring compatibility between selected EMF strains and target tree species; (3) developing inoculation methods that deliver viable propagules to the rooting zone; and (4) monitoring long-term mycorrhizal establishment and tree growth. The work by Senior and colleagues represented an early systematic effort to bring mycorrhizal technology into applied restoration ecology, bridging the gap between laboratory studies of fungal tolerance and field-scale remediation.

Read (1991) framed mycorrhizal function in terms of ecosystem response to the "law of the minimum," arguing that mycorrhizal fungi enable plants to overcome limitations in nutrient availability that would otherwise constrain productivity. This perspective is directly relevant to landfill reclamation, where multiple limiting factors simultaneously constrain plant growth and where mycorrhizal inoculation can address several of these limitations simultaneously.

## Fungal Bioremediation of Metals and Radionuclides

Beyond their ecological roles, EMF and other fungi have been explored for bioremediation of contaminated environments. Singleton & Tobin (1996) reviewed fungal interactions with metals and radionuclides for environmental bioremediation, documenting mechanisms including biosorption, bioaccumulation, precipitation, and enzymatic transformation of toxic elements. Their synthesis highlighted the potential for both living fungal biomass and dead fungal material (as biosorbent substrates) to remove metals from contaminated water and soil, offering low-cost remediation alternatives to conventional physicochemical treatments.

Rizzo et al. (1992) demonstrated biosorption of metals by *Armillaria* rhizomorphs, showing that these persistent soil structures could serve as passive metal scavengers. The rhizomorph architecture of *Armillaria*—dense aggregations of differentiated hyphae—provides high surface area for metal binding and can persist in soil for extended periods, making them potentially effective long-term biosorbent materials. Rommelt et al. (1990) investigated the influence of soil basidiomycetes on migration of radiocaesium (¹³⁴Cs + ¹³⁷Cs) and strontium-90 in coniferous forest soils, finding that fungal hyphae significantly retarded the downward migration of these radionuclides by adsorbing and retaining them within the organic horizon.

The Chernobyl nuclear accident provided a tragic but informative natural experiment on [[singh-radionuclide-accumulation-by-fungi]] fungi. Olsen et al. (1990) and Oolbekkink & Kuyper (1989) documented the role of fungi as significant sinks for radiocaesium, including substantial accumulation of Chernobyl-derived fallout in fruit bodies. Randa & Benada (1990) identified mushrooms as a significant source of internal contamination by radiocaesium in human populations, highlighting the dual nature of [[fungal-radionuclide-accumulation]]: while it can immobilize contaminants in soil, it also creates a pathway for radionuclide entry into human food chains. The mechanisms proposed for radiocaesium accumulation include active transport through potassium channels (exploiting the chemical similarity of Cs⁺ and K⁺), passive sorption to cell wall components, and intracellular sequestration.

Sinsabaugh et al. (1993) provided complementary evidence on wood decomposition enzyme activity in relation to nitrogen [[desert-soils-nutrition-and-phosphorus-dynamics]], demonstrating that pollution-induced changes in nutrient availability can alter the enzymatic capabilities of fungal communities, with implications for both decomposition processes and the functional resilience of fungal-mediated ecosystem services.

## Synthesis and Implications

The collective evidence from these studies reveals several consistent patterns in EMF community responses to environmental stress:

1. **Diversity declines** under most forms of pollution, with sensitive species lost first and tolerant taxa becoming dominant. This pattern has been documented for heavy metals (Rühling et al. 1984), nitrogen deposition (Rühling & Tyler 1991), SO₂ exposure (Shaw et al. 1992), and combined pollutant stresses.
2. **Functional shifts** accompany compositional changes, including reduced mycorrhizal colonization rates, altered nutrient cycling pathways, disrupted carbon allocation between host and fungus, and changes in enzymatic capabilities of the fungal community.
3. **Trophic cascades** propagate through fungal food webs, affecting collembola, nematodes, and other soil organisms that depend on fungal biomass as a food resource. The loss of preferred fungal taxa can destabilize multi-trophic interactions.
4. **Compensatory mechanisms** exist within the system—inter-plant carbon transfer through shared mycorrhizal networks may buffer some stress effects on weakened trees, and metal-tolerant EMF species can maintain mycorrhizal function in contaminated soils even as community diversity declines.
5. **Interacting stressors** produce complex, often synergistic effects that are difficult to predict from single-stressor experiments. Acid deposition, nitrogen enrichment, ozone exposure, and heavy metal contamination frequently co-occur in polluted landscapes, and their combined effects on EMF communities may exceed the sum of individual impacts.

The long-term consequences of EMF community restructuring under environmental stress remain uncertain but potentially profound. As ectomycorrhizal networks are dismantled by chronic pollution, forest ecosystems may lose access to the nutrient acquisition, carbon sequestration, and stress-buffering services that these symbioses provide. The resulting feedbacks to aboveground forest productivity, species composition, and resilience to additional stressors represent a critical knowledge gap.

The evidence reviewed here also points toward practical applications. Understanding which EMF species are tolerant of specific contaminants enables targeted inoculation for restoration of contaminated sites. The bioremediation potential of fungi for metals and radionuclides, while promising, requires careful evaluation of trade-offs between soil immobilization of contaminants and potential food-chain transfer. Future research should prioritize field-scale experiments that examine multi-stressor interactions, long-term community trajectories, and the functional consequences of EMF diversity loss for ecosystem-level processes.

These findings underscore the vulnerability of EMF-mediated ecosystem processes to anthropogenic environmental change and highlight the need for integrated management approaches that consider belowground microbial communities in pollution mitigation, forest health monitoring, and ecosystem restoration efforts.
