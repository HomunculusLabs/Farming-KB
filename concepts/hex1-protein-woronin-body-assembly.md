## HEX-1 Protein and Woronin Body Assembly

## Overview

**HEX-1** is the major structural protein of the Woronin body core in Euascomycete fungi. First identified through purification of [[woronin-bodies]] from *[[neurospora-crassa]]*, HEX-1 defines a family of Euascomycete-specific proteins that self-assemble into a crystalline lattice capable of withstanding the high turgor pressures inside fungal hyphae. The discovery of HEX-1 and its crystal structure resolved a century-old question about the composition and function of Woronin bodies, and established them as a specialised class of peroxisomes.

## Discovery and Identification

Woronin bodies were purified independently by Jedd & Chua (2000) and Tenney et al. (2000) from *Neurospora crassa*. Key findings from the purification work:

- Antibodies raised against HEX-1 specifically decorated the Woronin body matrix in *N. crassa*, *Aspergillus nidulans*, and *Magnaporthe grisea*.
- HEX-1 exists as an extremely large and stable protein complex within the Woronin body.
- The *hex-1* gene encodes a consensus **peroxisome-targeting signal 1 (PTS-1)** at its C-terminus.
- Expression of *hex-1* in yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) generates intraperoxisomal protein assemblies morphologically similar to native Woronin bodies.
- Recombinant HEX-1 **spontaneously crystallizes in vitro**, demonstrating it is a self-assembling structural protein.
- *Neurospora* *hex-1* mutants lack visible Woronin bodies and bleed protoplasm through septal pores after damage, confirming HEX-1 is essential for Woronin body function.

## Crystal Structure of HEX-1

The HEX-1 crystal structure was solved at **1.8 Å resolution** (Yuan et al., 2003), revealing the atomic basis of Woronin body assembly:

### Monomer Structure

Each HEX-1 monomer adopts a **two-domain structure** consisting of mutually perpendicular antiparallel β-barrels. This fold is shared with eIF-5a (eukaryotic initiation factor 5a), suggesting an evolutionary relationship through gene duplication (see Phylogenetic Origins below).

### Lattice Assembly

Monomers associate through **three intermolecular contacts** to produce a lattice structure with the overall form of **cross-linked helical filaments**. The crystal contact residues are conserved across HEX-1 orthologs, indicating that lattice formation is an evolutionarily conserved feature.

### Functional Validation

Mutations that disrupt crystal contact residues were used to test the lattice model:

- **In vitro**: Disrupted HEX-1 self-assembly completely.
- **In vivo**: Produced aberrant Woronin bodies with a **soluble, non-crystalline core**. The assembly-defective protein still accumulated in peroxisomes, forming spherical, osmophilic vesicles, indicating that protein import and concentration are independent of crystallization.
- **Functional assay**: These vesicles provided **no Woronin body function**—they could not prevent protoplasmic bleeding following hyphal damage.

The analogy is apt: like a sink drain plug, the Woronin body requires **sufficient structural integrity** to resist the turgor pressure exerted by the column of cytoplasm. Only a solid crystalline core provides this resistance.

## Peroxisomal Biogenesis

HEX-1 reaches the Woronin body through the **peroxisomal import pathway**:

1. **Cytosolic synthesis**: HEX-1 is translated on free ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
2. **PTS-1 recognition**: The C-terminal PTS-1 signal is recognised by the peroxisomal import receptor PEX5.
3. **Translocation**: HEX-1 is translocated into the peroxisomal lumen.
4. **Self-assembly**: Within the lumen, HEX-1 reaches sufficient concentration for spontaneous crystallisation.
5. **Maturation**: In most Euascomycetes, a hexagonal intermediate matures into an oval form (see Alternative Splicing below).

The Woronin body thus represents a **specialised peroxisome** in which the normal complement of peroxisomal enzymes is largely displaced by HEX-1 crystals.

## Alternative Splicing and Morphological Variation

Most Euascomycetes produce **two HEX-1 isoforms** through alternative splicing between the first and second exon. This results in two proteins differing at the amino-terminus by several kilodaltons.

### Correlation with Morphology

| Species | HEX-1 Forms | Woronin Body Shape |
|---|---|---|
| *Neurospora crassa* | Single isoform | Large, hexagonal |
| *Magnaporthe grisea* | Two isoforms | Small, oval |
| *Aspergillus nidulans* | Two isoforms | Small, oval |
| *Trichoderma reesei* | Two isoforms | Small, oval |
| *Penicillium chrysogenum* | Two isoforms | Small, oval |

### Co-Assembly Model

The presence of two isoforms may determine oval morphology through **co-complex formation**: the co-assembly of different isoforms may perturb monomer packing in the crystal lattice, altering the shape of the resultant assembly. This is consistent with observations that the oval form matures from an early hexagonal intermediate (*Fusarium oxysporum*, Wergin 1973).

### Regulatory Splicing in Pathogenesis

In *Magnaporthe grisea*, nitrogen starvation and growth in planta regulate the production of HEX-1 splice variants, favouring use of a particular 3' splice acceptor sequence. This suggests alternative splicing may serve as a regulatory mechanism linking Woronin body function to environmental conditions.

## Phylogenetic Origins: Relationship to eIF-5a

HEX-1 shares both **sequence** and **structural** homology with eIF-5a proteins:

- eIF-5a is an ancient protein found from Archaea through all eukaryotes.
- It is highly conserved and functions in mRNA transport and stability in the cytoplasm.
- eIF-5a does **not** form large protein complexes.

### Evolutionary Model

The evolutionary scenario is:

1. **Gene duplication**: *hex-1* arose from duplication of an ancestral *eIF-5a* gene.
2. **Fold conservation**: Residues determining the overall β-barrel protein fold were maintained.
3. **Surface divergence**: Surface residues evolved to acquire new functions—peroxisomal targeting (PTS-1 acquisition) and self-assembly (crystal contact residues).
4. **Functional innovation**: The new protein gained the ability to form large crystalline assemblies with mechanical properties suitable for septal pore plugging.

This model conforms to the hypothesis that gene duplication followed by acquisition of novel function can drive evolutionary success (Ohno, 1970).

## Taxonomic Distribution of hex-1

The *hex-1* gene has been identified in **17 Euascomycetes**, including all seven with sequenced genomes. It is present in the earliest-divging Euascomycetes (Pezizomycetes: *Tuber borchii*, *Ascobolus stercorarius*, *Morchella esculenta*). In contrast, *hex-1* homologues are **absent** from all nine fully sequenced fungal genomes outside the Euascomycetes, including:

- Hemiascomycetes (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Ashbya gossypii*)
- Archiascomycetes (*Schizosaccharomyces pombe*)
- Basidiomycota (*Coprinus cinereus*, *Ustilago maydis*, *Cryptococcus neoformans*)
- Zygomycota (*Rhizopus oryzae*)

This distribution strongly supports a **single evolutionary origin** of Woronin bodies in the ancestral Euascomycete.

## Functional Roles Beyond Septal Plugging

### Pathogenicity

In *Magnaporthe grisea*, *hex-1* mutants are defective in:

- **Appressorium morphogenesis**: The specialised infection structure fails to form properly.
- **Invasive growth**: Reduced ability to grow within plant host tissue.
- **Nitrogen starvation response**: Hyphal death occurs under nitrogen limitation, linking Woronin body function to stress responses.

These findings suggest Woronin bodies may serve broader roles in maintaining cellular integrity beyond emergency septal pore plugging, potentially acting in developmental contexts where cellular compartmentalisation is required.

### Potential as Fungicide Targets

Woronin bodies are likely present in **all Euascomycete pathogens** (including *Aspergillus fumigatus*, *Botrytis cinerea*, and *Coccidioides immitis*). The absence of *hex-1* from non-fungal organisms and the essential nature of Woronin body function for pathogenicity make the HEX-1 protein or its assembly pathway potentially attractive targets for the development of novel fungicides.
