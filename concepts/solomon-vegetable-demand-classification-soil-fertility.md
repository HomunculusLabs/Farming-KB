# Vegetable Demand Classification and Soil Fertility Gradient

## Overview

Steve Solomon's *Gardening When It Counts* introduces a practical
three-tier classification system for vegetables based on their soil
fertility requirements: low-demand, medium-demand, and high-demand
crops. This framework enables gardeners to match their available soil
resources to appropriate crops, maximizing food production regardless
of how much compost, manure, or fertilizer they can obtain. The system
is designed for "hard times" gardening where resources are limited.

## The Three Demand Tiers

### Low-Demand Vegetables

These crops produce adequately in relatively poor soil with minimal
amendment. They include many root crops, legumes, [[lettuce-and-leafy-greens]]
that have evolved to extract nutrients efficiently. Examples include
potatoes, Jerusalem artichokes, garlic, onions, leeks, shallots,
many bean varieties, and certain leafy greens. Low-demand crops can
be grown with as little as a quarter-inch layer of composted manure
or finished compost plus agricultural lime.

### Medium-Demand Vegetables

These require moderate soil improvement for good production. The
standard recommendation is a quarter-inch layer of sacked steer
manure or finished compost, plus a three-millimeter layer of
composted chicken manure, plus agricultural lime at approximately
50 pounds per 1,000 square feet. Many common garden vegetables
fall into this category, including tomatoes, peppers, eggplant,
summer squash, cucumbers, and most brassicas.

### High-Demand Vegetables

These are the most demanding crops, requiring substantial soil
fertility investment. Solomon specifically identifies celery and
cauliflower as examples requiring extremely high soil fertility.
The recommendation for these crops is a full inch of finished
compost or manure, plus chicken manure, plus lime, plus the
[[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof]] (COF) blend. Without this level of
investment, high-demand vegetables will be disappointing.

## The Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF)

Solomon developed a custom organic fertilizer blend called COF
(Complete Organic Fertilizer) designed to supplement modest amounts
of manure and compost. When COF is used alongside compost, the
resulting vegetables contain far more human nutrition than vegetables
grown by any other method, including commercial organic produce.

The COF concept is based on the principle that if soil in an area
contains a reasonably balanced mineral profile, plants grown in that
soil will be nutritious. Transporting manure or compost from
surrounding vegetation maintains that balance. However, if local
soils lack a perfect [[garden-soil-fertility-mineral-balance-solomon]], adding specific rock powders
and [[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]] can correct deficiencies.

## Soil Improving Gradient Approach

Solomon advocates a gradient approach to soil fertility rather than
an all-or-nothing system. Each step up the gradient gives better
results, allowing gardeners to work within their means:

**Step 1 (minimum):** No soil improvers — only low-demand crops
are feasible, spade the soil and keep weeds hoed.

**Step 2:** Quarter-inch layer of composted manure — enables both
low and medium-demand vegetables on the amended area. Unamended
areas can still grow low-demand crops.

**Step 3 (better):** One-inch layer of steer manure or half-inch
of finished compost, plus COF at approximately 4-6 quarts per 100
square feet. This approach works for low, medium, and high-demand
vegetables.

**Step 4 (best):** Generous compost/manure application plus COF.
Exceeding recommended manure/compost amounts by half or double is
always wise, but never apply more than double the COF recommendation
to avoid unbalancing soil minerals.
