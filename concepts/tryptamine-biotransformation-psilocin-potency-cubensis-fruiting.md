# Tryptamine Biotransformation to Psilocin in Psilocybe cubensis Fruiting Bodies

## Overview

[[jochen-gartz]]'s 1989 study demonstrated that mycelial cultures of *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]* can hydroxylate and methylate exogenously supplied tryptamine to produce exceptionally high levels of psilocin — up to 3.3% of the dry mass of fruiting bodies. This represents the highest psilocin concentrations ever measured in any mushroom species and reveals the remarkable biosynthetic capacity of the enzymatic systems in *P. cubensis*.

## The Biosynthetic Pathway

The biotransformation pathway involves two key enzymatic steps that convert simple tryptamine into the psychoactive indole alkaloid psilocin:

1. **Hydroxylation:** Addition of a hydroxyl group at the 4-position of the indole ring, converting tryptamine to 4-hydroxytryptamine
2. **Methylation:** Addition of a methyl group at the 4-hydroxy position via O-methylation, converting 4-hydroxytryptamine to psilocin (4-HO-DMT)

Normally, *P. cubensis* produces primarily psilocybin (the phosphorylated form) with only trace amounts of psilocin. When tryptamine is supplied exogenously, the enzyme systems preferentially channel the substrate through hydroxylation and methylation to produce psilocin, with minimal further phosphorylation to psilocybin.

## Experimental Conditions

Gartz used a dried cow dung and rice-grain substrate mixture (2:1 ratio) with twice the amount of water. A 25 mM concentration of tryptamine hydrochloride was added to this medium. Key parameters included:

- Substrate: Cow dung/rice-grain (2:1) with double water volume
- Tryptamine concentration: 25 mM (as hydrochloride salt)
- Strain: A previously characterized strain of *Psilocybe cubensis*
- Fruiting method: Direct fructification without casing layer
- Harvest: Mushrooms freeze-dried immediately, sealed in plastic, stored at -10°C
- Analysis: HPLC and TLC for indole alkaloid quantification
- Flushes: Five successive flushes harvested per culture

## Results: Psilocin Levels by Flush

The addition of tryptamine produced dramatically elevated psilocin levels across all five flushes, while psilocybin levels dropped compared to control cultures:

| Flush | Psilocin (with tryptamine) | Psilocin (control) | Psilocybin (with tryptamine) | Psilocybin (control) |
|-------|---------------------------|-------------------|------------------------------|---------------------|
| 1     | 2.1%                      | —                 | 0.01%                        | 0.55%               |
| 2     | 3.3%                      | 0.01%             | 0.02%                        | 0.48%               |
| 3     | 2.8%                      | 0.02%             | 0.20%                        | 0.51%               |
| 4     | 3.1%                      | 0.09%             | 0.07%                        | 0.46%               |
| 5     | 2.9%                      | 0.15%             | 0.13%                        | 0.61%               |

The psilocin values of 2.1-3.3% are unprecedented — values reported for psilocin in dried mushrooms are always below 1% in the absence of tryptamine supplementation. The second flush produced the highest psilocin concentration at 3.3%.

## The Phosphate Hypothesis

Gartz proposed that a reduced amount of phosphate in the culture media may have decreased the biosynthesis of psilocybin from psilocin. The normal pathway involves phosphorylation of psilocin to produce psilocybin. With limited phosphate available, the phosphorylation step may be inhibited, causing psilocin to accumulate rather than being converted to its phosphorylated form.

This hypothesis is supported by the consistently low psilocybin levels (0.01-0.20%) in tryptamine-supplemented cultures compared to controls (0.46-0.61%), despite the massive increase in total indole alkaloid production. The cow dung/rice substrate may naturally be phosphate-limited relative to rye-grain or pure rice substrates.

## Complete Substrate Utilization

A significant finding was that no residual tryptamine was detected in any of the fruiting bodies or in the vegetative mycelia extracted from the substrate. This indicates that the enzyme systems in *P. cubensis* have a high capacity to process all available tryptamine precursor into downstream products. The fungus does not accumulate tryptamine but rather converts it efficiently through the full enzymatic pathway.

## Absence of Baeocystin

Under the tryptamine-supplemented conditions, *P. cubensis* failed to produce detectable amounts of baeocystin (the mono-methylated intermediate). This contrasts with other studies that have reported traces of baeocystin in some *P. cubensis* strains. The complete methylation of the hydroxylated intermediate to psilocin, without accumulation of the partially methylated baeocystin, suggests that the O-methyltransferase enzyme has high catalytic efficiency under these conditions.

## Comparison with Previous Biosynthesis Studies

Earlier submerged culture studies had established that radioactive tryptamine functioned as a better biosynthetic precursor than tryptophan for psilocybin production, with at least 22.4% of the psilocybin formed derived from labeled tryptamine. However, those studies found psilocin levels at or near zero in mycelial tissue.

Gartz's study was the first to demonstrate this biotransformation in fruiting (rather than submerged) mycelia, and the first to achieve such dramatic psilocin accumulation. The key difference is that fruiting mycelia possess the full complement of developmental enzymes that may not be expressed in submerged culture.

## Practical Applications and Limitations

The [[pf-tek]] community ([[psylocybe-fanaticus]]) subsequently applied Gartz's technique using [[brown-rice-flour]] and vermiculite substrate in half-pint jars, but found that the 25 mM concentration was excessive for this lighter substrate. A reduced concentration of approximately 0.1 g tryptamine HCl per jar produced fruit bodies that were at least 3 times normal potency, though some grew dwarfed.

Gartz's heavier cow dung/rice substrate was more nutritious and buffered, supporting higher tryptamine concentrations without the growth inhibition observed in the lighter PF TEK substrate.

## Significance for Fungal Biochemistry

This study demonstrates that fungal enzymatic systems can serve as efficient biocatalysts for the transformation of simple precursors into complex alkaloids. The hydroxylation and methylation capacity of *P. cubensis* enzyme systems rivals that of dedicated industrial biocatalysts, achieving >99% substrate conversion with high regioselectivity.
