# Insectary Plantings

Insectary plantings are dedicated areas of flowering plants grown specifically to attract, feed, and shelter beneficial insects — predators, parasitoids, and pollinators. By providing nectar, pollen, and habitat, insectary plantings increase the population of insects that control pests and pollinate crops, reducing the need for chemical interventions.

## Why Insectary Plantings Matter

- **Supplemental food**: Many beneficial insects need nectar and pollen as adult food sources, especially parasitic wasps and hover flies that do not feed on pests as adults
- **Reproduction support**: Well-fed adult parasitoids produce more eggs, leading to more pest-killing offspring
- **Overwintering habitat**: Perennial insectary plants provide shelter for beneficials through winter
- **Landscape connectivity**: Insectary strips and hedgerows help beneficials move between crop areas
- **Pollination**: Flowering insectaries support pollinator populations that increase fruit and seed set in nearby crops

## Plant Families for Beneficial Insects

### Umbellifers (Apiaceae)

The most important family for attracting predatory and parasitic insects. Their flat, umbrella-shaped flower clusters (umbels) provide ideal landing platforms for small wasps and flies. Tiny flowers produce accessible nectar.

**Top Umbellifers for Insectaries:**

| Plant | Bloom Period | Height | Notes |
|-------|-------------|--------|-------|
| Dill ([[anethum-graveolens]]) | Jun-Aug | 3-4 ft | Annual, reseeds freely, excellent for parasitic wasps |
| Fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) | Jul-Sep | 4-6 ft | Perennial, bronze fennel is less invasive, attracts ladybugs |
| Cilantro/Coriander ([[coriandrum-sativum]]) | May-Jul | 1-2 ft | Annual, let it bolt and flower, attracts hover flies |
| Parsley ([[petroselinum-crispum]]) | Jun-Aug | 1-2 ft | Biennial, flowers in second year |
| Yarrow ([[achillea-millefolium]]) | Jun-Sep | 2-3 ft | Perennial, drought-tolerant, long bloom period |
| Queen Anne's Lace ([[daucus-carota]]) | Jun-Aug | 2-4 ft | Biennial, can be weedy in some areas |
| Ammi majus (Bishop's weed) | Jun-Aug | 3-4 ft | Annual, excellent cut flower, very attractive to wasps |
| Lovage (Levisticum officinale) | Jun-Aug | 4-6 ft | Perennial, edible leaves, long-lived |

### Composites (Asteraceae)

Daisy-like flowers attract a wide range of pollinators, ladybugs, and predatory bugs. Their composite flower heads provide many small florets in a single visit.

### Mints (Lamiaceae)

Aromatic plants in the mint family produce nectar-rich flowers that attract a wide range of beneficial insects. Many are perennial and drought-tolerant.

| Bee balm (Monarda didyma) | Jun-Aug | 2-4 ft | Perennial, attracts hummingbirds and bees, spreads by rhizomes |

| Oregano (Origanum vulgare) | Jul-Sep | 1-2 ft | Perennial, let it flower for beneficials, edible leaves |

### Legumes (Fabaceae)

Legumes provide nectar and pollen, fix nitrogen, and serve as host plants for some beneficial insects.

| Plant | Bloom Period | Height | Notes |
|-------|-------------|--------|-------|
| White clover (Trifolium repens) | May-Oct | 0.3 ft | Perennial, excellent ground cover, nitrogen fixer |
| Crimson clover (Trifolium incarnatum) | Apr-Jun | 1-2 ft | Winter annual, excellent spring nectar source |
| Sweet clover (Melilotus spp.) | Jun-Sep | 3-6 ft | Biennial, very attractive to bees, can be weedy |

## Insectary Design Strategies

### Insectary Strips

Dedicated strips of flowering plants within or alongside crop areas.

- **Width**: Minimum 3-6 feet for effective habitat; 10+ feet for maximum benefit
- **Placement**: Every 50-100 feet across the field or garden
- **Orientation**: North-south strips provide sun exposure on both sides
- **Species mix**: Combine early, mid, and late bloomers for continuous flowers
- **Mowing**: Mow only 1/3 of the strip at a time to maintain continuous bloom and habitat

### Hedgerows

Linear plantings of woody shrubs and perennial flowers along field edges, fence lines, or property boundaries.

- **Benefits**: Windbreak, privacy, wildlife corridor, and year-round [[beneficial-insect-habitat]]
- **Species**: Combine flowering shrubs (elderberry, chokeberry, serviceberry) with perennial flowers and native grasses
- **Maintenance**: Prune annually to maintain shape and flower production

### Interplanting (Within Crop Rows)

Mix insectary flowers directly within crop rows.

- **Intercrop ratios**: 1 insectary plant per 5-10 crop plants, or one row of insectaries for every 4-6 rows of crops
- **Examples**: Alyssum between broccoli rows, dill between tomato rows, buckwheat between pepper rows
- **Advantage**: Beneficials are immediately present where pests appear
- **See [[intercropping-systems]] for detailed interplanting strategies

### Cover Crop Insectaries

Use [[cover-cropping-guide]] that double as insectary plantings.

- **Buckwheat**: Attracts hover flies, parasitic wasps, and bees. Flowers in 6 weeks from seed. Extremely effective summer insectary.
- **Crimson clover**: Excellent spring nectar source for bees and parasitic wasps
- **Phacelia**: One of the best overall insectary plants; attracts hover flies, bees, and parasitic wasps. Flowers for 6-8 weeks.
- **Sweet alyssum**: Low-growing, long-blooming annual. Attracts hover flies and tiny parasitic wasps. Excellent for borders and between crop rows.

## Seasonal Bloom Planning

Continuous bloom from early spring through late fall is the goal. Plan insectary plantings to cover each period:

### Early Spring (March-May)

- Crocus, dandelion (leave some!), plum/cherry blossoms, clover, phacelia, alyssum, creeping thyme

### Late Spring (May-June)

- Yarrow, dill, cilantro, calendula, catnip, clover, chives, baptisia

### Summer (June-August)

- Fennel, cosmos, zinnia, sunflower, coneflower, agastache, bee balm, buckwheat, lavender, coreopsis

### Fall (September-November)

- Goldenrod, asters, Joe-Pye weed, sedum, marigold, late-blooming zinnias and cosmos

### Winter (December-February)

- Leave dried flower stalks and seed heads standing for overwintering habitat
- Evergreen shrubs provide shelter
- Some warm winters allow late kale and brassica flowers to support bees
