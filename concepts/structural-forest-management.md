## [[structural-forest-design|Structural]] Forest Management and Coppice Systems

## Overview

Structural forests provide essential [[gadd-fungal-biodegradation-concrete-building-materials]] — poles, posts, fencing, and timber — within [[permaculture]] systems. Rather than managing forests for sawlogs (the conventional forestry approach), Mollison advocates managing primarily for round pole timber, which is of far more immediate use to homesteaders and communities. Structural forests can be managed alongside food forests, with many species serving dual purposes as forage, food, and material producers.

## The Case for Round Pole Timber

The strongest [[singh-wood-rot-fungi-structural-timber-decay]] is growing round timber — uncut, whole poles. Round poles are more useful than sawn lumber for most homestead construction needs. Australians build entire house frameworks using about nine two-inch poles, then filling in with mud, brick, stone, wattling, or chicken wire and cement. The complete framework costs approximately $800. These pole-frame homes with chicken wire and cement infill can be beautiful structures.

The advantage of pole management over sawlog management is time and continuous yield. While sawlogs require 40 years or more, poles can be harvested in four to six years. Poles represent a continual-product forest, whereas sawlogs represent an eventual-product forest.

## Key Structural Tree Species

### Black Locust (*Robinia pseudoacacia*)

Black locust is the premier structural tree in American permaculture:
- **Pioneer species**: Naturally colonizes disturbed sites
- **Soil builder**: Improves soil through [[legume-nitrogen-fixation-and-green-manure]] leaf litter
- **Durability**: Posts have been documented lasting 90 to 100 years at near maximum strength
- **Impact resistance**: Has the highest impact loading strength of any timber known — the traditional material for mallet heads
- **Coppicing**: The more you cut, the more you get — ideal for sustained harvest
- **Productivity**: 4,000 to 5,000 stems per acre, harvestable in four to six years
- **Chicken forage**: Ideally suited to stocking with chickens, which increase the nutrient level of the forest
- **Fence post economics**: With prices heading toward $5 to $8 per six-foot post, black locust represents an excellent investment

### Bamboo

Bamboo offers numerous domestic uses:
- **Structural uses**: When used as reinforcement in concrete, it achieves two-thirds the strength of steel per diameter. An inch of bamboo replaces a quarter-inch of steel rod. Unlike steel, it doesn't rust in concrete and bonds better.
- **Construction guidelines**: Cut at two to four years of age, dry for about eight months before use
- **Additional uses**: Gutters, downpipes, cups, knives, plates
- **Food value**: Bamboo shoots are excellent food (note: small amounts of sweet shoots can be eaten raw, though there is some risk of cyanide poisoning from uncooked shoots)
- **Propagation**: Mostly vegetative; two to four heavy-seeding bamboos suit [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] climates
- **Timeline**: Generally 10 years before first harvest unless existing clumps can be divided
- **Garden utility**: Even small bamboo is useful for garden structures

### Eastern Red Cedar

Eastern red cedar is a good structural and pioneer tree that naturally begins to disappear as the succeeding forest grows up around it. The optimal time to harvest is just as it starts to become eclipsed by the next successional stage.

### Tea Tree (*Melaleuca*)

Tea tree is not American but has exceptional qualities:
- Grows very thickly — stems can be spaced hand-width apart
- Produces high-value oil from leaves, giving double value when cut
- Long-lasting in fences — 30 to 40 years
- Sells for approximately 30 cents per stick
- Weaves well for baskets and lobster traps
- Fast-growing — ready for another cut in five years
- Installation: Use sticks between black locust posts and rails, standing on rows of rocks

## Coppice Systems

Coppicing is the practice of cutting trees at ground level and allowing them to regrow from the stump. A whole set of plants are "cut-and-come-again" — black locust, willows, poplars, and ashes.

### Willow Coppice

Willow is the classical coppicing tree. Different coppice heights serve different purposes:
- **Forage coppice**: Start above cow level so cattle can browse without damaging the stool
- **Basketry coppice**: Start below ground level, using striped willow buried in wet sites as billets in rows

The Tasmanian basket makers — five generations in the craft — maintained just a half acre of willow coppice outside their door, manuring and tending it carefully. A quarter acre of forage coppice could carry cattle through droughts, either by cutting and throwing the forage or by controlled grazing.

### Poplar Uses

Poplar serves multiple functions:
- Good forage for livestock
- Good splitting wood
- Good for interior work (not durable for exterior use)
- Not suitable for bent-wood furniture

### Stopping Unwanted Coppice

If you want to prevent a stump from regrowing, the simplest method is to exclude light — throw a piece of carpet or any opaque material over the stump. Alternatively, putting road salt in the stump will kill it.

## Forest Management Strategies

### Product Analysis

Before managing any forest, determine the end uses of all products: bark, leaf, oil, and medicine. Conduct a crude economic analysis of end product values. The forester on a well-managed site should be a very busy person.

### Dry Distillation

Dry distillation is a low-technology system for extracting multiple valuable products from wood:

**The Process:**
1. Load a brick-lined container with wood
2. Close it off and light a fire underneath
3. The wood inside cannot combust — it is cooked to charcoal
4. Volumes of gas rise: methane, methanol, creosote

**Products Obtained:**
- **Methane**: Can be used for cooking gas (4% powers the pumping of the rest)
- **Methanol**: Can run tractors and machinery
- **Creosote**: For painting and proofing planks; can turn birch into non-rot products
- **Charcoal**: Excellent cooking fuel, readily saleable
- **Hot water**: Any amount can be drawn from the system
- **Heating mass**: The system supplies heat below the house

**The French Method:**
The French historically used a double brick chamber — one for the fire, the other for cooking the timber. This was superior to the old method of covering charcoal with mud, which wasted most of the biomass and released all gases to the atmosphere.

**Environmental Benefits:**
Flue gases can be passed through pond water to precipitate [[peat-calcium-carbonate-perlite-casing-psilocybe-cubensis-miniculture]], which is then thrown on fields or back into lakes. The system releases clean gas to the environment.

### Wildfire Prevention

Priority in any forest is cutting dead and downed material:
- Fallen logs lodge against standing trees and create fire scar risk
- Old chestnut logs in [[north-carolina-mycodiversity]] don't rot quickly because fire frequency is relatively high
- Ground fuels and standing dead fuels will eventually burn in wildfire
- Moving dead material out for fuel (or dry distillation) is better than leaving it for wildfire

### Nest Boxes and Wildlife Habitat

Hollow logs from harvested old trees should be:
- Left standing in the forest where they will be occupied by wildlife
- Sold as nest boxes — a potential income source
- Placed in the forest to increase hole-nesting sites for squirrels, birds, and other animals
- Sparrows nest in open areas, not deep in woods, so forest nest boxes favor woodland species

### Zone-Based Management

Different forest zones require different management approaches:
- **Inner zones**: Far more productive in human and animal forages; manage for high forage-drop species like oak, cherry, and apple
- **Reflective species**: White birch near gardens screens and creates microhabitats for food production
- **Secondary/tertiary zones**: Structural forests, bamboo clumps, shelter trees
- **Plank forest areas**: Identified for future sawlog production
- **Undisturbed reserves**: Left alone as controls and for natural processes — these areas prevent mistakes and may be too dangerous to manage

### Brush Piles and Firewood

Brush piles serve as excellent winter shelter for a wide range of animals. Cordwood left in the woods (rather than brought to the house) provides habitat for lizards and salamanders. However, new brush piles must be built each year, as animals occupy the current year's pile.

### Community Forest Management

A strategy emerging in North Carolina assembles people in touch with the forest into cooperative groups. Individuals who can't supply enough of a product (like beech) alone can meet orders collectively. Groups also share tools and equipment. This cooperative model is well-suited to permaculture's emphasis on community-scale production.
