# Ayahuasca Tourism: Medical Risks and Dangerous Prescription Drug Interactions

## Overview

R. Stuart's 2002 paper "Ayahuasca Tourism: A Cautionary Tale," published in the *MAPS Bulletin*, documents the medical risks faced by tourists who participate in ayahuasca ceremonies in South America. The paper focuses on the Amazonian cities of [[ayahuasca-commercial-vs-authentic-ceremony-dynamics|Iquitos]] and San Francisco, Peru, where ayahuasca tourism has become a significant industry. A central concern is the dangerous, potentially fatal interaction between ayahuasca's monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) and common prescription medications, particularly those used to treat AIDS, depression, and psychiatric disorders.

The paper serves as an urgent warning that the cultural and pharmacological context of ayahuasca use creates a serious responsibility gap when Western tourists bring pharmaceutical medications into ceremonial settings.

## The MAOI Mechanism

Ayahuasca is a brew typically made from [[ayahuasca-botany-and-preparation|Banisteriopsis caapi]] (which contains the beta-carboline alkaloids **harmine** and **harmaline**) combined with [[ayahuasca-pharmacology|Psychotria viridis]] or Diplopterys cabrerana (which contains N,N-dimethyltryptamine, or DMT). The harmine and harmaline act as **monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs)**, temporarily blocking the monoamine oxidase enzyme in the digestive tract. This inhibition is pharmacologically necessary: without it, DMT would be rapidly broken down by monoamine oxidase before reaching the bloodstream, rendering it orally inactive.

However, this same MAOI mechanism is responsible for the most serious medical risks associated with ayahuasca. Monoamine oxidase is responsible for metabolizing a wide range of endogenous and exogenous compounds. When the enzyme is inhibited, certain substances can accumulate to dangerous levels in the body, triggering hypertensive crises, serotonin syndrome, and other potentially fatal reactions.

The MAOI activity in ayahuasca is primarily attributed to the reversible inhibition of MAO-A (monoamine oxidase subtype A), which preferentially metabolizes serotonin, norepinephrine, and melatonin. This selectivity is relevant to understanding which drug interactions are most likely to be dangerous. Pharmaceutical MAOIs used in psychiatry carry similar interaction profiles, and decades of clinical experience with those drugs inform our understanding of ayahuasca's risks.

## Medication Categories at Risk

### Antiretroviral and AIDS Medications

Stuart's paper identifies AIDS medications as a category of particular concern. Antiretroviral drugs, protease inhibitors, and related compounds used in HIV/AIDS treatment regimens can interact unpredictably with MAOIs. The paper documents a specific fatality in Bolivia where an AIDS patient died during an ayahuasca ceremony. According to the report, the individual was healthy enough to dance at the beginning of the service, suggesting that the adverse reaction was acute rather than the result of general frailty. This case underscores the severity and rapid onset of MAOI-related drug interactions.

### Antidepressants

Antidepressant medications pose a significant risk when combined with ayahuasca, particularly:

- **Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs):** When combined with MAOIs, SSRIs can cause **serotonin syndrome**, a potentially life-threatening condition characterized by agitation, rapid heart rate, high blood pressure, dilated pupils, hyperthermia, and in severe cases, seizures, coma, and death.
- **Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs):** These carry the same serotonin syndrome risk as SSRIs.
- **Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs):** Combined with MAOIs, TCAs can amplify adrenergic effects, leading to hypertensive crisis.
- **Monoamine oxidase inhibitor antidepressants:** Combining pharmaceutical MAOIs with ayahuasca's natural MAOIs compounds the inhibition dangerously.

The typical recommendation is a **washout period of at least two weeks** (and in some cases up to five weeks for drugs with long half-lives like fluoxetine) between discontinuing an SSRI or other antidepressant and participating in an ayahuasca ceremony.

### Psychiatric Medications

Beyond antidepressants, other psychiatric medications carry interaction risks:

- **Antipsychotics:** May interact unpredictably with the serotonergic and dopaminergic effects of ayahuasca.
- **Benzodiazepines:** While not directly causing the same hypertensive risks, benzodiazepines can mask or alter the ayahuasca experience and may complicate the pharmacological picture.
- **Mood stabilizers (lithium, valproate):** These can have complex interactions with MAOIs and the psychoactive components of ayahuasca.
- **Stimulant medications** used for ADHD (amphetamines, methylphenidate): Combined with MAOIs, these can precipitate hypertensive crises.

## The Responsibility Gap: Shamans and Pharmaceuticals

A central theme of Stuart's paper is the **responsibility gap** that exists in ayahuasca tourism. In Peruvian and broader Amazonian culture, ayahuasca is regarded as an **herbal tonic or traditional medicine**, not as an illegal or dangerous drug. Most ayahuasca practitioners, including experienced and respected shamans, are thoroughly trained in the preparation and ceremonial use of the brew within their cultural framework but are **fundamentally unfamiliar with Western pharmaceuticals**.

This creates a dangerous asymmetry:

- Shamans typically do not ask about or screen for prescription medications prior to ceremonies.
- Even if they did, most lack the pharmacological knowledge to evaluate drug interaction risks.
- Tourists frequently do not disclose their medications, either because they do not understand the risk or because they believe the ceremonial setting is safe.
- The cultural framing of ayahuasca as beneficial and natural may lead tourists to underestimate medical risks.
- Language barriers further complicate communication about medical history.

The paper makes clear that the responsibility for understanding and managing these risks falls squarely on the **tourist**, not the shaman or ceremony organizer.

## The Bolivia Fatality Case

The most sobering evidence in Stuart's paper is the account of an AIDS patient who died during an ayahuasca ceremony in Bolivia. The individual was reportedly in sufficiently good health to **dance at the beginning of the service**, indicating that death resulted from an acute adverse reaction rather than general immunocompromised weakness. While the exact medication involved is not specified in the paper's summary, the case illustrates how rapidly and unpredictably MAOI-antiretroviral interactions can become fatal, even in individuals who appear physically robust.

This case is particularly relevant to the tourism context: it demonstrates that participants may not perceive themselves as medically vulnerable, and that ceremonial settings lack the medical infrastructure to respond to acute pharmacological emergencies.

## Cultural Context and Misconception

Stuart's paper highlights an important cultural dimension to the risk. In Peru, ayahuasca is widely regarded as a benign herbal tonic with a long history of safe traditional use. This perception is accurate within its traditional context, where indigenous practitioners have deep knowledge of which plants to combine, what dietary restrictions (the *dieta*) to observe, and how to screen participants based on traditional medical frameworks. However, this culturally embedded safety does not translate automatically to the tourism setting, where participants arrive with bodies conditioned by Western pharmaceuticals that have no analogue in the Amazonian pharmacopoeia.

The disconnect is compounded by the spiritual framing of ayahuasca tourism. Many tourists approach ceremonies with the belief that the medicine "knows" what the participant needs, or that the shaman's spiritual authority ensures safety. While these beliefs may hold meaning within a ceremonial context, they offer no protection against pharmacological mechanisms that operate regardless of spiritual intent.

## Tourism Infrastructure and Medical Preparedness

Ayahuasca tourism in Iquitos has developed largely without regulatory oversight. Retreat centers vary enormously in quality, from professionally run operations with basic medical screening to informal ceremonies held in private homes. Very few operations in the early 2000s (the period Stuart documents) had systematic procedures for:

- Collecting medical histories from participants
- Screening for contraindicated medications
- Having emergency medical plans or trained personnel on site
- Providing pre-ceremony education about drug interactions

While some improvement has occurred in the decades since Stuart's paper was published, the fundamental responsibility gap remains: there is no universal standard for medical screening in the ayahuasca tourism industry, and enforcement of any existing guidelines is minimal.

## Practical Safety Guidance

Based on the risks outlined in Stuart's paper, individuals considering ayahuasca ceremonies should observe the following precautions:

1. **Consult a physician** before participating in any ayahuasca ceremony if currently taking any prescription medication.
2. **Disclose all medications** to ceremony organizers, even if they do not ask.
3. **Observe appropriate washout periods** before ceremony participation, particularly for SSRIs (minimum two to five weeks depending on the specific drug).
4. **Never discontinue psychiatric medication** without medical supervision; abrupt withdrawal can itself be dangerous.
5. **Research the pharmacology** of ayahuasca and understand the MAOI mechanism before traveling.
6. **Be aware that herbal and over-the-counter supplements** can also interact dangerously with MAOIs (e.g., St. John's wort, dextromethorphan, pseudoephedrine, tyramine-rich foods).
7. **Recognize that shamanic screening** is no substitute for medical screening; cultural expertise in ayahuasca preparation does not extend to pharmaceutical drug interactions.
8. **Ensure emergency medical access** is available in the area where the ceremony takes place.

## Broader Context

Stuart's paper contributes to a larger body of literature on [[ayahuasca-pharmacology|ayahuasca pharmacology]] and the [[alan-shoemaker-ayahuasca-legal-peru|legal and regulatory landscape]] surrounding ayahuasca use in Peru. The tension between traditional Amazonian medicine and Western pharmacology is a recurring theme: what is considered a safe and beneficial practice within its indigenous cultural context can become dangerous when transplanted into the unscreened, unregulated context of spiritual tourism. The paper's cautionary tone reflects genuine concern that without greater awareness and responsibility, more preventable deaths will occur.
