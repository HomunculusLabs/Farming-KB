# Floodwater Harvesting and Braided Stream Systems

Floodwater harvesting and [[permaculture-design]]. Drawing on indigenous knowledge from Aboriginal
Australians, Papago (Sonora) peoples, and fluvial geomorphology, these
techniques transform ephemeral water flows into productive agricultural systems
and permanent water reserves.

## Slope Stabilization for Infiltration

Maximizing infiltration on slopes is the first step in any dryland water
strategy. **Spinifex** and **porcupine grasses** form circular tussocks that
break open over time, creating crescent shapes with horns pointing uphill.
Detritus accumulates behind these tunulate clumps, forming sandy rubble banks
that function as natural swales — trapping moisture, [[vetiver-grass]]** provide a living barrier that
enhances both water infiltration and structural slope stability, and can be
combined with any of the above earthworks.

## Floodwater Harvesting

Floodwater harvesting captures ephemeral runoff from large catchments and
directs it to smaller cultivated areas, concentrating scarce water where it is
most productive. This is the deliberate management of natural flood pulses,
not conventional irrigation.

### Indigenous Models

**Aboriginal Australians** constructed earthwall dams to impound water for
cultivating *Panicum* grasses on floodplains. The **Papago people of the
Sonoran Desert** developed a more elaborate system along the Colorado River,
including temporary basins, irrigation canals, and stone-and-earth field
boundaries. Crop complexes were typically 4 hectares or less.

### Rainfall and Runoff Relationships

- **Summer storms** deliver 40–60% of annual rainfall but only ~15% of runoff
  reaching the mainstream; most runoff stays in intermittent streams.
- Approximately **8 cm of rain** is needed to generate useful surface runoff.
- Typical catchment-to-field ratios range from **15:1 to 27:1** for reliable
  harvests.
- Hardy crops like sorghum can be produced with runoff ratios as low as **4:1**.

### Design Elements for Floodplain Agriculture

Braided rivers naturally deposit silt and [[organic-mulch]] at high-water marks.
Design elements that build on this process include:

- **Scoop fences**: Structures that trap seed, manure, twigs, molluscs, and
  organic debris from floodwaters, converting them into garden inputs.
- **Gridiron bank systems**: A network of low banks (0.5–1 m high) stabilized
  by stone, tussock, or brush that partitions the floodplain into cells,
  slowing water and promoting even silt deposition.
- **Broadcast sowing**: Millet, sorghum, and legumes broadcast directly into
  wet silt deposits after flood recession, exploiting moisture and nutrients.

## Braided Stream Systems

Braided streams feature multiple interconnected channels dividing and rejoining
across a broad, sediment-laden bed — one of the most productive fluvial
environments for dryland water harvesting.

### Principles of Braiding

A key inverse relationship governs braided systems: **multiple smaller streams
create a larger total wetted area than a single main channel** of equivalent
discharge. Braiding depends on shallow water flow over extensive deep sand
sheets. Each braid-form begins as a diamond-shaped pattern with plunge-pools at
its corners. In low flow, only channel flow persists; sheet flow across the
interfluve ceases.

### Design Interventions

- **Deflection structures**: Concreted, deeply-bedded deflections in the main
  channel split flood flows, widening and slowing water to wet more alluvium.
- **Notched weir walls**: Placed across a braided area, these initiate finer
  braiding patterns by redistributing flow at controlled points.
- The goal is to maximize wetted perimeter and minimize velocity, promoting
  infiltration and sediment deposition.

## Scour Holes and Permanent Desert Lagoons

Scour holes are deep, permanent water bodies in arid landscapes where
constrictions accelerate flood flow, causing hydraulic excavation of the
streambed.

### Formation and Construction

Near mountain ranges, natural ridges constrict flood flows, increasing velocity
and producing hydraulic digging that excavates deep basins. These permanent
lagoons persist because they recharge from floods and lose water slowly through
deep sand seepage rather than surface evaporation.

Scour holes can be deliberately constructed using **rock-cement walls** to
create a constriction and **bulldozed banks** to direct flow into the
excavation zone. **Sand dams upstream** further increase deep sand seepage
recharge, keeping pools full longer.

### Silt Dam Requirements

Open water evaporates rapidly in arid climates, so **silt dams with 9–10 times
the capacity of each pool** are needed to intercept sediment before it fills
the scour hole — reflecting the enormous sediment loads of desert floods.

## Sandy River Beds

Sandy river beds support distinctive ecological communities with microtopographic
niches:

- **Palms and large trees** establish in small embayments off the main channel,
  where deeper sand and occasional water retention support root development.
- **Bank differentiation**: The **slip-off (silty) slope** on the inside of a
  meander supports one tree assemblage, while the steeper **erosion bank** on
  the outside supports another.
- **Burrowing animals** preferentially use the silty slip-off slope for shelter,
  contributing to soil aeration and nutrient cycling.

## Design Synthesis

Effective dryland water design integrates all these elements: slope
stabilization feeds infiltration into the broader catchment; floodwater
harvesting captures and concentrates runoff; [[braided-stream]] management
distributes it across the widest area; scour holes provide permanent reserves;
and sandy river bed ecology ensures productive plant and animal communities.
Together, these strategies form a resilient approach to [[water-management]] in arid
and semi-arid environments.
