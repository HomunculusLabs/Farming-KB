### Leguminous Ground Covers

- **White Dutch clover** (Trifolium repens) -- the premier living mulch;
  fixes nitrogen, tolerates foot traffic, bee-attracting flowers. Can be
  maintained as a permanent, living mulch interplanted with other crops
- **Strawberry clover** (Trifolium fragiferum) -- similar to white clover,
  more tolerant of wet soils
- **Subterranean clover** (Trifolium subterraneum) -- self-seeding annual

### Edible Ground Covers

- **Creeping thyme** -- culinary herb, fills gaps between stepping stones
- **Wild strawberry** -- produces small but flavorful berries
- **Nasturtium** -- edible leaves and flowers, trails beautifully
- **Oregano** (creeping varieties) -- culinary, spreads to form mats
- **Mint** (in contained areas) -- culinary, aggressive spreader

## Integration with Polycultures

Living mulches play a critical role in
[[gaias-garden-food-forest-case-study-jerome-osentowski]], ground covers:

- Occupy ground that would otherwise succumb to weed invaders
- Prevent soil compaction from foot traffic and rain
- Create a humid microclimate at soil level that benefits soil organisms
- Provide a transition zone between taller plants and bare soil

Hemenway notes that ground-hugging plants should preferably be varieties
that offer food or habitat, snuggling into edges and the spaces between
shrubs and herbs. Sample species for forest garden ground layers include
strawberries, nasturtium, clover, creeping thyme, ajuga, and prostrate
varieties of flowers such as phlox and verbena.

## Living Mulch in Vegetable Gardens

In annual vegetable systems, white clover is the most commonly recommended
living mulch. It can be established between rows or as pathways in garden
beds. The clover fixes nitrogen for neighboring vegetables, suppresses
weeds, and attracts pollinators when in bloom. Key considerations:

- Clover may compete with vegetable seedlings for water and nutrients;
  establish clover first, then cut it back before planting
- In intensive vegetable systems, clover works best as a between-row or
  pathway cover rather than interplanted with crops
- Dutch white clover tolerates mowing and light foot traffic, making it
  suitable for garden paths that double as nitrogen-fixing corridors

## The Polyculture Living Mulch Effect

Hemenway describes how thick plantings in a polyculture create a living
mulch that protects the soil, curtails evaporation, and reduces the need
for water. The diversity of such plantings bewilders most pests, since a
monoculture of any single crop is a magnet for specialized pests but a
mixed polyculture with dozens of species presents no concentrated target.
