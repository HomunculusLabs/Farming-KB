## Sunlight Geometry and Day Length for Winter Greenhouse Production

## Latitude Comparisons: A Paradox of Climate

[[eliot-coleman]] opens Chapter 5 of *The Winter Harvest Handbook* with a
counterintuitive observation that reframes how northern growers should think
about their winter growing potential. Portland, Maine sits at roughly the same
latitude as St. Tropez on the French Riviera. New York City shares its latitude
with Naples, Italy. These geographic parallels reveal that the midwinter sun in
the northeastern United States is significantly stronger and available for
longer each day than in southern Europe, despite the dramatic temperature
differences that separate these paired cities.

The distinction matters enormously for [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]]. Winter cold in
Maine is a function of continental air masses and the absence of a moderating
ocean current, not of solar deprivation. The sun provides more than enough
energy for cold-hardy crops to survive and grow slowly during the depths of
winter. The problem is temperature, not light, and temperature can be managed
with simple, low-cost structures.

## The 10-Hour Day Threshold and the Persephone Months

Coleman identifies 10 hours of daylight as the critical threshold below which
plant growth effectively ceases for most vegetable species. He refers to this
period as the "Persephone months," after the Greek goddess who descends to the
underworld each winter, leaving the world above in suspended animation. During
the Persephone months, plants in a minimally heated greenhouse are not dead but
dormant, waiting for light to return in sufficient quantity to resume growth.

At Coleman's farm in Harborside, Maine (latitude 44°N), the 10-hour day begins
around November 5th and ends around February 5th — approximately three months
of negligible growth. The plants harvested during this period were planted and
reached harvestable size during the longer days of autumn. The greenhouse
grower's task is to maintain living plants in a state of suspended animation,
harvesting them progressively as needed.

## Growth Dynamics Below the 10-Hour Threshold

When day length drops below 10 hours, photosynthesis slows to the point where
plants can barely maintain their current biomass. The decline is gradual rather
than sudden, becoming practically negligible around the 10-hour mark. Leafy
greens such as spinach, lettuce, mâche, and claytonia are the primary crops
during this period because their harvestable portion is already present; the
grower is cutting a living storage crop rather than harvesting new growth.

This dynamic has profound implications for [[fall-winter-planting-schedules]]. Winter-harvested
crops must be planted early enough in the fall to reach near-maturity before
the Persephone months begin. Coleman typically seeds his winter greens in
September and early October, giving them six to eight weeks of active growth
before November 5th. Success or failure in the winter harvest is largely
determined by decisions made during these autumn planting weeks.

## Cross-Quarter Days and the Agricultural Calendar

Coleman draws on the ancient Celtic calendar of cross-quarter days, which mark
the midpoints between solstices and equinoxes. These dates align remarkably
well with practical winter growing realities:

- **Samhain (November 1)**: Near the onset of the Persephone months. Final
  plantings must be completed.
- **Imbolc (February 2)**: Near the end of the Persephone months. Overwintered
  crops begin responding to lengthening days.
- **Beltane (May 1)**: Midpoint between spring equinox and summer solstice.
  Transition to warm-season growing.
- **Lughnasadh (August 1)**: Midpoint between summer solstice and autumn
  equinox. Time to begin seeding fall and winter crops.

These cross-quarter days provide a framework more useful to the winter grower
than solstices and equinoxes because they align with biological transitions
rather than astronomical ones.

## British Latitude and the American Advantage

Coleman is unsparing in his assessment of Britain's suitability for winter
harvesting. London sits at approximately 51.5°N, a latitude he describes as
"polar-bear country." At this latitude, the winter sun is low, weak, and brief.
By contrast, most of the continental United States lies between 30°N and 48°N,
receiving significantly more midwinter solar energy. Even Minneapolis (45°N)
and Seattle (47.5°N) receive more usable winter sunlight than London.

This geographic reality explains why British [[historical-intensive-gardening-literature]] emphasizes
winter protection more heavily than American sources. The British challenge is
fundamentally different: less total solar energy means slower recovery after
cold snaps. American growers, particularly in the 35°N to 45°N band, have a
substantially easier task from a solar perspective.

## W.J.C. Lawrence and Scientific Validation

The 10-hour rule is not merely Coleman's empirical observation. He cites
W.J.C. Lawrence, a British horticultural researcher who confirmed the 10-hour
daylength threshold from research conducted in London during the mid-twentieth
century. Lawrence demonstrated that plants in unheated glasshouses showed
negligible growth below 10 hours of daylight regardless of temperature. This
finding has been corroborated by subsequent research and is widely accepted in
horticultural science.

## Greenhouse Orientation and the Myth of Supplementary Lighting

Coleman recommends orienting greenhouses on an east-west axis to maximize
interception of low-angle winter sunlight from the southern sky. The sun's
angle at midwinter noon in Maine is roughly 22° above the horizon, compared to
71° at the summer solstice. Low, wide structures with steeply sloped south-facing
glazing perform far better than tall structures designed for summer ventilation.

Perhaps the most liberating insight is Coleman's dismissal of supplementary
[[marijuana-horticulture-cervantes-artificial-lighting-and-electricity]]. During the Persephone months the limiting factor is
temperature, not light. Before and after the Persephone months, natural day
length is sufficient. Supplemental lighting adds cost and energy consumption
without meaningful benefit for cold-hardy vegetable production.

## Practical Implications for the Winter Grower

Understanding sunlight geometry allows working with nature rather than against
it. The key principles are: plant crops to maturity before the Persephone
months, maintain living plants through the dormant period using passive and
minimal heating, orient structures to capture maximum low-angle winter sun,
and trust that returning February and March light will restart growth without
artificial intervention. These principles require no expensive technology, only
knowledge of latitude, day length, and the biology of cold-hardy vegetables.
