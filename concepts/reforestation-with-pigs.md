# Reforestation with Pigs

Sepp Holzer has developed a distinctive method of using pigs as partners in
reforestation and land restoration. Pigs are "blessed by nature with a plough
in front and a compost spreader at the back," making them ideal workers for
preparing ground for tree planting in areas that are difficult to cultivate
with machinery. This method is a key element of [[holzer-livestock-integration]] approach.

## The Pig as a Land Management Tool

Pigs use their snouts to dig in the ground for food. They eat cockchafer
grubs, caterpillars, seeds, mice, snails, and all sorts of other bugs,
thereby regulating overpopulation of soil pests. Their manure adds fertility
to the soil. They can work any ground: dry or wet, hard or soft, sandy or
loamy. They are especially helpful in challenging areas like mountainsides
and wetlands that cannot be cultivated with machinery.

## How to Work with Pigs

The process involves four main steps:

**Step 1: Scattering food bait.** An electrified fence keeps pigs in the areas
where they are needed. Food is scattered on spots where ground needs to be
worked. Corn, peas, broad beans, or leftover kitchen waste mixed with beans
work well. The food is scattered among brambles, junipers, or raspberries
that need clearing.

**Step 2: The pigs dig the ground.** The earth takes on the smell of food,
and the pigs continue digging and churning even after eating. Pigs born
outdoors do this naturally, but pigs born in a pigsty need to be taught by
drilling a hole in the ground with a stick and burying food for them to find.

**Step 3: Move pigs and sow seeds.** After the first round of digging, mixed
tree seeds are scattered over the worked ground. The pigs can be moved to the
next area while the seeds germinate. Pigs eat some seeds but excrete others,
which often germinate more easily after passing through a pig's stomach.

**Step 4: The mixed fruit forest develops.** Step by step, pigs are led
through the whole area to be reforested. Pig manure mixed with existing
leaves and organic matter adds to overall fertility.

## Important Considerations

It is important not to overuse the ground. Keeping pigs too long in the same
area or having too many in a small space causes soil compaction and acidity.
The increase in nitrogen-loving plants like sorrel and stinging nettles
indicates overuse. A stone should only be turned once by a pig in order to
find a snail. Pigs must always have adequate drinking water.

Jerusalem artichokes are excellent pig food and also a valuable human
vegetable that grows in poor soil. Various vegetable seeds can be added to
the tree mix to provide food for game and distract them from young trees.

## Seed Mix for Reforestation

Holzer takes a bucket full of diverse seeds: oak, beech, chestnut, cherry,
pear, fir, ash, hazel, and various fruit and berry bushes, all mixed
together. Seeds are sown five or six together, just as nature does, rather
than in rows at regular distances. This creates natural polyculture
conditions where plants support each other.

The rain washes scattered seeds into the ground. They wait for the right
conditions of warmth and moisture to germinate. Strong and independent plants
develop this way, without needing additional watering or composting. They
must be protected from game, goats, and sheep until established.

## Advantages Over Conventional Reforestation

Conventional forestry often clears all existing vegetation before planting
uniform rows of a single species. Holzer's method with pigs:

- Leaves old trees standing, even dead ones, as they protect new growth
- Creates diverse, mixed forests rather than monocultures
- Costs much less than planting nursery-grown saplings
- Produces stronger plants that withstand frost, drought, and competition
- Adds fertility through manure rather than requiring artificial fertiliser
- Works on steep slopes, rocky ground, and wet areas inaccessible to
  machinery
- Creates rough ground with varied microclimates that increase
  [[biodiversity-fungi-soil-fungal-communities]]

## Historical Context

Keeping pigs outdoors was traditional in Alpine regions. Pigs were kept in
vast areas together with sheep, horses, goats, and cattle. Tree seeds were
sporadically sown, and once established the pigs were moved to other areas.
Farmers had less competition and were not as specialised as today. Many
Alpine hamlets got their names from the livestock they kept: Saudorf (sod
village) for pig farming, Huhnerbuhl for chicken keeping, Ganslberg (goose
hill) for geese.

This traditional way of life is mostly lost, as intensive animal husbandry
has seduced farmers to specialise and overstock, leading to overgrazing and
[[desertification-causes-reversal]].

## After Fire Reforestation

After forest fires, Holzer recommends planting mixed crops straight into the
ashes: nuts, chestnuts, cherries, oaks, apple trees, cedars, Siberian
yellow pines, beeches, and more. The burnt biomass is excellent natural
fertiliser. Steep slopes need protection from erosion through
[[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]] and hugelkulturs.
Areas should be planted quickly, before the next snowmelt carries nutrient-
rich ash into rivers.
