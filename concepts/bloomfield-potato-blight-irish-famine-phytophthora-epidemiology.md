# Potato Blight, Phytophthora infestans, and the Irish Famine

## Overview
Phytophthora infestans is the [[bloomfield-pythiosis-insidiosi-swamp-cancer-oomycete-pathogen]] responsible for potato late blight,
the most devastating disease in agricultural history. Linked forever with the Irish
famine of the 1840s, this pathogen caused the deaths of approximately one million
people and the emigration of a million more, halving Ireland's population within a
few years. Today, Phytophthora infestans causes $3 billion in annual losses
worldwide, making it the most costly living enemy of global agriculture.

## The Irish Famine of the 1840s
The introduction of the potato to Ireland allowed the population to swell to 8
million by the 1840s, the majority of whom—perhaps 6 million—were utterly
dependent on bumper crops of potatoes for their own sustenance and as the commodity
used to meet rent payments. This extreme dependency on a single crop created a
precarious situation. When the temperature plunged late in the summer of 1845,
weeks of fog and continuous rain followed, creating conditions ideal for the spread
of blight. Phytophthora got the better of the crop with devastating speed.

A contemporary account from the Kilkenny Journal (December 4, 1846) describes an
inquest following the discovery of a 30-year-old mother and her three children:
their bodies were partially covered with filthy rags saturated with mud and frozen.
The doctor was unable to detect in the mother's stomach or bowels a trace of food
having entered for more than twenty hours before death. The child's stomach
contained a very small quantity of half-digested potatoes.

## Causes and Complexity
While the British are often blamed for the catastrophe, and the fact that the
situation was maintained by British landowners is an appalling episode in history,
placing blame wholly on the British ignores the complexity of the tragedy. The
Irish were poised for annihilation by their dependence on a single crop. The pathogen
itself has a predilection for unusually wet climates, and the weather of 1845
provided perfect conditions for its spread. The combination of biological
vulnerability, ecological conditions, and socioeconomic structures created one of
the worst demographic disasters in modern European history.

## Reverend Miles Berkeley and Disease Identification
An English clergyman, the Reverend Miles Berkeley, was responsible for identifying
Phytophthora as the cause of the disease symptoms. At the time of the famine, the
cause of potato blight was unknown and was attributed to various causes including
the recent introduction of the steam engine, hidden volcanoes, and the devil. A
Belgian scientist had discovered that removing infected leaves and stems when
blotches appeared could prevent the spread from leaves to tubers, but nobody in
Ireland was aware of this simple preventive measure.

## Oomycete Biology and Zoospore Dispersal
Phytophthora is an oomycete (water mold), related to the microorganism that causes
human pythiosis. Like other water molds, it produces swimming zoospores that develop
in sporangia. These sporangia can be dispersed by wind. If a sporangium drifts onto
a wet leaf, the zoospores are expelled into the film of water and charge over the
waxy cuticle before forming a cyst. A germ tube grows from the cyst, forms an
appressorium, and pierces the epidermis with a penetration hypha.

## Infection Cycle and Sporangia Production
Early in its infection, Phytophthora makes haustoria and feeds from the potato
plant without widespread destruction of its tissues. But after a few days, tissue
damage becomes profound and the pathogen feeds on the increasingly soupy remains
of its dying host. Just five days after entering the leaf, Phytophthora reemerges
through open stomata and releases its airborne sporangia from the tips of aerial
hyphae. As many as 300,000 sporangia can be launched each day from a single blotch
on a potato leaf. Washed from the leaves, the sporangia release their spores into
the soil to infect the buried tubers.

## Historical Strain vs. Modern Strain
Most plant infections today are caused by a single strain of Phytophthora that was
long thought to be the same one that precipitated the Irish famine. By extracting
DNA from [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]] of potato leaves collected at the time of the famine,
researchers have determined that this is not the case—a different strain destroyed
potatoes in the 1840s. This finding has important implications for understanding
the evolution and epidemiology of the pathogen.

## Origin and Genetic Diversity
Plant breeders searching for resistant strains of potato are interested in locating
the pathogen's birthplace, reasoning that wild potatoes that evolved alongside
Phytophthora must have developed strong defenses. Areas of extraordinary genetic
diversity are particularly fruitful hunting grounds. A Mexican origin for
Phytophthora seems logical because the greatest variety of strains coexist in the
Toluca Valley north of Mexico City. However, some researchers are convinced that it
sprang from [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], which is also the ancestral home of the potato itself.

## Tuber Infection and Storage Loss
Because the fungus does not usually spread directly from leaves to tubers, part of
the crop can be salvaged by removing infected foliage when blotches appear.
However, potatoes that appear healthy at harvest time can still rot in storage, a
process accelerated by bacteria that melt the starchy tissue into a slimy mess.
This storage rot means that even successful harvests can be lost after the fact,
compounding the [[bloomfield-chytrid-olpidium-synchytrium-agricultural-impact]] of the disease. The interaction between
Phytophthora and secondary bacterial invaders creates a particularly destructive
synergy in stored potatoes.

## Modern Control Strategies
Contemporary management of potato blight relies on multiple approaches: fungicide
spraying programs, cultivation of resistant potato varieties, and improved weather
monitoring systems that predict blight risk conditions. Despite these efforts,
Phytophthora infestans remains a formidable adversary due to its rapid reproduction,
genetic adaptability, and ability to evolve new races that overcome host resistance.
The pathogen's oomycete biology makes it distinct from true fungi, which has
implications for the selection and effectiveness of chemical control agents.

## Sudden Oak Death: A Modern Phytophthora Threat
More than fifty species of Phytophthora are recognized, infecting almost every kind
of broad-leaved plant and causing tens of billions of dollars in crop damage each
year. A newly identified species is responsible for sudden oak death in California,
where infected tanoaks, coastal live oaks, and black oaks have been reported along
a 200-mile stretch of the northern Californian coastline from Monterey to Mendocino
the mycelium spreads underneath the bark, and red ooze laced with
infectious sporangia seeps from infected trees. Once the rot is established,
ascomycete fungi and wood-boring beetles take hold and the oaks disintegrate. The
loss of mature oak trees has cascading effects on forest ecosystems, affecting
[[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]], [[mycofiltration-watershed-protection]], and biodiversity along the California coast.
