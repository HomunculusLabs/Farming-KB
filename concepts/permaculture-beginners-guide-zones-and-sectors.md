# Permaculture Beginners Guide — Zones and Sectors

[[graham-burnett]]'s introduction to zones and sectors in "Permaculture: A Beginners Guide" (2001) presents zoning as a practical framework for correct placement — positioning elements in ways that are most appropriate based on frequency of human attention needed. Zones are numbered 0 through 5 as concentric rings moving outward from the center of human activity. See [[permaculture-zones-and-sectors]], [[holmgren-zones-and-sectors-design-framework]], and [[mollison-zone-and-sector-analysis]] for more comprehensive treatments from other sources.

## Zoning: Correct Placement

The fundamental principle: zoning is about correct placement. Elements are positioned based on how often they need human attention, care, or interaction. The system is numbered from 0 to 5, conceived as concentric rings moving outward from a central point where human activity and need for attention is most concentrated, to areas requiring no intervention at all.

## Zone 0 — The Home

Home is the center point — where the heart is, where we center ourselves and feel safe. Zone 0 encompasses wherever we live: a house, cottage, bedsit, or bender.

**Design principles for Zone 0:**
- Construct and function in sustainable, energy-efficient, ecologically harmonious ways
- Choose locally available or renewable materials (wood, [[rammed-earth]], straw bales)
- Avoid fired bricks or concrete due to high environmental impact
- Site or design buildings to utilize airflow, solar energy, and water efficiently
- Minimize non-renewable energy inputs

**Practical steps when major renovation isn't possible:**
- Analyze inputs and outputs of your house
- List electrical appliances and usage frequency
- Track daily water usage
- Measure weekly rubbish output
- Identify what is truly necessary and what can be reduced

Not everyone has resources for eco-villages or major retrofitting — most people, especially urban dwellers, have limited housing choices. But steps can still be taken to make homes more energy-efficient, less wasteful, and healthier environments.

## Zones 1 and 2 — The Garden

Food growing is the primary emphasis of Zones 1 and 2, reflecting the basic need to eat. Burnett advocates shifting from industrialized agriculture toward a gardening philosophy.

**Key concepts:**
- Move away from prairie farming monocultures toward market and home gardens
- Create edible landscapes, polycultures, and forest gardens
- Design plantings with multiple yields and functions
- Growing your own food reduces pressure on land resources
- Reconnects with skills and sensations largely lost in the late 20th century

**Scope:** Very few people can grow ALL their own food, but everyone can make a significant contribution from gardens, allotments, balconies, or even windowboxes. The emphasis is on what is possible rather than self-sufficiency as an absolute.

## Design Ideas for Small Gardens

Burnett emphasizes that with intelligent design and ingenuity, even the smallest garden area can have its growing potential maximized. The guide presents visual design layouts showing how elements can be stacked and arranged to produce multiple yields from limited space.

## Zones 3 and 4 — Broadscale Strategies

Zone 3 and 4 address broader land management and agricultural-scale strategies. Burnett uses UK farmland statistics to illustrate the scale:

- 43 million acres of farmland in the UK
- One-third rough grazing (moorland)
- One-third permanent pasture (hilly, water meadow, steep gradient, thin soils)
- One-third arable land suitable for conventional crop production

**Problems with industrial agriculture:**
- Continuous ploughing degrades soil
- Grubbing up hedgerows removes habitat
- Removal of trees eliminates ecosystem services
- Chemical fertilizers and pesticides cause erosion, compaction, and nitrate poisoning of watercourses

**Broadscale permaculture responses:**
- Reverse damage caused by industrial methods
- Utilize land considered unsuitable for conventional cultivation
- Apply sustainable farming techniques on a larger scale

## Zone 5 — Wilderness

Though not described in extensive detail in the beginners guide, Zone 5 represents the unmanaged wilderness — areas where no human intervention occurs. These areas serve as reservoirs of biodiversity and reference ecosystems for understanding natural patterns.

## Rolling Permaculture — Incremental Implementation

Burnett introduces "Rolling Permaculture" as a strategy for implementing sustainable design incrementally:

- Industrial agriculture erodes and poisons land but provides employment and food
- Modern towns depend on environmentally harmful infrastructure
- Immediate total replacement is impossible
- Start small: work on the margins first
- A farmer might use permaculture on "waste" land or Set-Aside fields
- Gradually "roll" the design across the rest of the site
- Sustainable incomes slowly replace destructive practices
- Inner city permaculture projects can serve as examples and inspiration

This approach acknowledges the reality that sustainable systems must replace destructive ones gradually, with each successful project demonstrating viability and spreading ideas.

## Connection to Broader [[permaculture-principles]]

Zoning connects to several core permaculture principles outlined in the guide:

- **Every element should serve many functions:** Zone placement maximizes each element's contributions
- **Every function should be supported by many elements:** Diverse plantings across zones ensure resilience
- **Work with nature, not against:** Zoning follows natural patterns of attention and energy flow
- **Stacking:** Multiple layers and functions occupy the same space, especially in Zone 1-2 gardens
- **Everything cycles:** Waste products from one zone become inputs for another

## What Goes in Each Zone — Practical Guide

Burnett's guide provides a practical framework for deciding which elements belong in which zone based on the frequency of human attention required:

**Zone 0 — Daily living:**
- Home, kitchen, indoor spaces
- Energy systems, water collection
- Seed starting, sprouting
- Herb gardens in windowsills

**Zone 1 — Several times daily:**
- Intensive vegetable beds
- Salad greens, herbs, soft fruit
- [[composting-systems]] close to kitchen
- Greenhouse or polytunnel
- Small livestock (chickens, rabbits)
- Elements needing frequent observation and harvesting

**Zone 2 — Daily to weekly:**
- Fruit trees, berry bushes
- Pond and water features
- Larger composting areas
- Main crop vegetables (potatoes, brassicas)
- Bees
- Elements needing regular but less frequent attention

**Zone 3 — Weekly to monthly:**
- Main crop orchards
- Broadacre vegetables
- Pastured livestock
- Windbreaks and shelterbelts
- Large-scale composting
- Elements managed on a seasonal cycle

**Zone 4 — Occasional:**
- Managed woodland, coppice
- Foraging areas
- Timber and firewood production
- Grazing animals with minimal management
- Wildlife corridors and habitat areas

**Zone 5 — Never:**
- Unmanaged wilderness, wildlife reserve
- Reference ecosystem for observation
