# Exploring the Rich [[history-of-plant-science]]

This article, published on the Biodiversity Heritage Library (BHL)
blog by Stephen Hales on October 10, profiles Dr. Mary Williams and
her use of historic botanical literature in educating plant
biologists. The piece illuminates how centuries-old scientific works
remain relevant to modern botanical education and research, and how
digital archives like the BHL are transforming access to these
foundational texts.

## Nehemiah Grew and the Birth of Plant Anatomy

The article opens with a landmark in botanical history: in 1682,
Nehemiah Grew published *The Anatomy of Plants*, containing the
first known microscopic depiction of pollen. Grew, now recognized
as the "Father of Plant Anatomy," revolutionized botanical science
by exploiting the microscope to outline key morphological differences
in plant stems and roots. He proposed the groundbreaking hypothesis
that stamens are a plant's male reproductive organs, a fundamental
insight that preceded the formal understanding of plant sexual
reproduction by over a century.

Grew's detailed line drawings of grapevine stems showing transport
vessels are highlighted as examples of observational quality that
remains instructive today. Dr. Williams notes that "because of
their simplicity in some ways these centuries-old line drawings are
clearer and easier to comprehend than an image taken today using a
powerful microscope," a testament to the clarity achieved by skilled
observation without technological mediation.

## Dr. Mary Williams and Teaching Tools in Plant Biology

Dr. Williams has studied and educated about plants for over 30
years, beginning with her PhD in Plant Molecular Biology. After
serving as a professor at Harvey Mudd College from 1995-2009, she
became a Features Editor for the American Society of Plant
Biologists, writing "Teaching Tools in Plant Biology," a series
connecting advanced undergraduate students to current research
through textbook-style review articles, diagrams, and images.

Williams emphasizes that education involves more than teaching
current models: students must learn to synthesize information and
develop their own models. Examining how scientific understanding
has changed over time is a powerful pedagogical tool for developing
critical thinking skills.

## Historical Works in Modern Education

The article highlights several historic texts that Williams
incorporates into her teaching:

**Stephen Hales' *Vegetable Staticks* (1727):** An account of
studies on water transport in plants, made with only simple tools
yet producing key discoveries and models still useful today.
Hales is considered a pioneer of plant physiology.

**Darwin and Darwin's *The Power of Movement in Plants* (1880):**
Charles Darwin and his son Francis investigated phototropic
movement of *Phalaris canariensis*, producing "brilliant studies
that set the stage for the discovery of the plant hormone auxin."
Reading this work reveals how exceptional scientists first observe
a phenomenon and then systematically investigate it.

**Marcello Malpighi's *Anatome Plantarum* (1675):** Alongside
Grew's work, one of the two 17th-century plant anatomy books that
Williams considers her favorites in the BHL collection of over
150,000 volumes.

## The Biodiversity Heritage Library as Research Resource

Williams discovered BHL through serendipitous Google searching in
2012 and now conducts monthly searches for materials. She values
the diversity, openness, and public domain status of the collection,
which allows easy sharing with students. The ability to see primary
sources in their original publication format, including historical
typefaces and drawings, creates a connection "across the centuries"
that transcribed documents cannot replicate.

BHL was then engaged in the Mining Biodiversity project to enhance
search functionality through text mining, semantic metadata, and
result visualizations, addressing Williams' primary critique that
in-book search could be improved.

## Significance for Modern Science

This article illustrates a broader principle: modern scientific
advances rest on foundations laid over centuries. The tools have
changed from simple microscopes to electron microscopes and
molecular biology techniques, but the fundamental approach of
careful observation, hypothesis formation, and systematic testing
remains constant. Historic literature provides not only technical
knowledge but also inspiration and methodological models that
remain relevant to contemporary research in plant science and
all biological disciplines.

The ability to trace ideas from their original formulation through
centuries of refinement helps students understand science as a
cumulative, collaborative human endeavor rather than a collection
of static facts. Williams' approach of teaching through historical
primary sources offers a powerful pedagogical tool that could be
applied across all scientific disciplines.

## The Role of Digital Archives in Education

The Biodiversity Heritage Library represents a new paradigm in
access to scientific literature. With over 150,000 volumes
digitized and available freely online, BHL provides unprecedented
access to the historical record of biological science. The ongoing
Mining Biodiversity project aims to enhance discoverability through
text mining, semantic metadata, and improved search interfaces.

For educators like Williams, digital archives eliminate the
practical barriers that previously made historical primary sources
inaccessible for classroom use. No longer requiring physical access
to rare book collections, instructors can assign primary readings
from the original publications and have students engage directly
with the scientific observations and reasoning of earlier eras.
