# Agar Plate Preparation and Culture Techniques

Agar plates are a foundational tool in mycology, providing a sterile, nutrient-rich surface for isolating, propagating, and studying fungal cultures. Mastering agar work is essential for anyone working with mushroom cultivation, strain selection, or mycoremediation research. The technique bridges the gap between wild spore collection and large-scale spawn production, giving the cultivator precise control over strain genetics and purity.

On agar, a single spore can germinate into a monokaryotic culture, or a tissue sample from a fresh mushroom can be cloned to preserve its exact genetic identity. Agar work also enables the cultivator to isolate clean mycelium away from contaminants, select the most vigorous sectors of growth, and build up enough inoculum to move on to grain spawn and eventually fruiting substrates.

## 1. Agar Plates Overview

An agar plate is simply a petri dish filled with a thin layer of agar — a gelatinous substance derived from seaweed that serves as a solid tissue culture substrate. The agar provides both a structural matrix and, when supplemented with nutrients, the food source that mycelium needs to grow. Standard plates are typically 90–100 mm in diameter, though smaller 60 mm plates are useful for conservation work where medium volume is limited.

You can purchase **sterile glass petri plates** (reusable after thorough cleaning and resterilizing) or **disposable plastic petri plates**. Glass plates offer long-term economy and can be autoclaved repeatedly, while plastic plates are convenient for single-use workflows and eliminate cross-contamination risk from incomplete cleaning. Small jars can also serve as acceptable substitutes, though their shape makes some inoculation techniques more awkward.

Agar powder is available from mycological supply companies and many Asian markets, where it is sold as a common food ingredient. For regular use, **lab-grade agar purchased in bulk** is the most economical choice — a single kilogram can produce hundreds of plates.

The choice between glass and plastic ultimately depends on your scale and workflow. Home growers doing occasional cloning may prefer the convenience of plastic, while commercial operations or research labs typically invest in glass for its reusability. Either way, always handle plates by their edges and avoid touching the inner surfaces.

## 2. Step 1: Preparing Agar Gel

The most common and beginner-friendly agar formulation is **potato agar** (PDA — Potato Dextrose Agar without the added dextrose). It is inexpensive, easy to prepare, and supports a wide range of fungal species. The basic principle is simple: extract water-soluble nutrients from a starch source, then add agar powder to create a gellable medium. To prepare it:

1. **Slice or shred a small potato** — no need to peel it. The skin contains additional nutrients that benefit mycelial growth.
2. **Boil** the potato pieces in **3–4 cups of water** for approximately **1 hour**. A gentle, sustained simmer is better than a rolling boil.
3. Monitor the pot carefully to **prevent foam from boiling over**. Starchy potato foam can make a real mess on the stove. If the water level drops significantly during the boil, add more to compensate for evaporation.
4. **Strain out the potato solids** using a fine mesh strainer or cheesecloth, and measure the hot broth into an Erlenmeyer flask. A 500 mL or 1 L flask is typical.
5. Add approximately **1 teaspoon of agar powder (~5 g) per 2 cups of broth**.
6. **Always add the agar to hot liquid** to prevent clumping — cold liquid will form stubborn lumps that are very difficult to dissolve even with reheating.
7. **Swirl the flask** to help the agar dissolve evenly. Professional laboratories use hot plate stirrers with magnetic stir bars for more consistent results, but manual swirling works well for small batches.

If the agar does not fully dissolve, you can briefly return the flask to a gentle heat source while continuing to swirl. Avoid vigorous boiling at this stage, as it can create foam and alter the concentration of the medium through excessive evaporation.

## 3. Step 2: Sterilizing the Agar

Sterilization is the most critical step in the entire process. Any surviving microorganisms will contaminate your plates and ruin your cultures — sometimes silently, making it hard to diagnose failures later. The goal is to kill all bacteria, yeasts, and mold spores present in the medium or on the flask surfaces.

- Use a **pressure cooker** operating at **12–15 PSI**; **15 PSI is preferred** because lower pressures require significantly longer cook times to achieve the same level of sterilization.
- **Metal-to-metal seal pressure cookers** are the best choice for mycological work because they are durable and do not rely on degradable gaskets. Gasketed models are acceptable for skill development, but the rubber gaskets wear out over time and can harbor contaminants in microscopic cracks.
- Plug the neck of the Erlenmeyer flask with **cotton or polyester fill** and cover the top with **aluminum foil**. This creates a breathable yet contaminant-proof seal that allows steam to penetrate while blocking airborne spores.
- **Process the agar at 15 PSI for 20 minutes.** This is sufficient to kill virtually all bacteria, mold spores, and their heat-resistant endospores.
- Allow the cooker to **cool to 2–3 PSI before moving it**. Jostling a fully pressurized cooker is dangerous.
- **Open only while the pressure is still above 0 PSI** — opening below atmospheric pressure creates a vacuum that can suck contaminated ambient air directly into the flask through the filter plug, defeating the entire sterilization process.
- **Do not open too early** or the agar will boil violently out of the flask. If the pressure gauge drops below 0 PSI before you open it, the agar must be **resterilized**.
- **Open the cooker only in a clean room or in front of a laminar flow hood** to minimize the chance of airborne contamination entering the flask.

Timing the sterilization cycle is important. Start the timer once the cooker has reached the target pressure, not from the moment you turn on the heat. Rushing the depressurization phase is one of the most common beginner mistakes and a leading cause of unexplained contamination.

## 4. Step 3: Pouring the Agar

Pouring requires careful temperature management and a practiced clean technique:

- Allow the sterilized agar to cool to approximately **100°F (38°C)** before pouring. An infrared temperature gauge (around $30) is invaluable for accurate readings.
- The **pouring window centers around 100°F**. If the agar has already gelled in the flask because it cooled too far, simply reheat it just enough to melt it again.
- If adding **antibiotics**, do so between **105–110°F (41–43°C)** — high enough that the agar is still liquid, but cool enough to preserve the antibiotic compounds.
- **Wipe the outside of the petri plate sleeve with alcohol** and trim the bottom edge open. Then **invert the entire stack** so the bottoms face upward and the lids are underneath — gravity keeps contaminants from settling onto sterile surfaces.
- **Lift the bottom plate off the stack**, pour agar until roughly **half full** (~1/8 to 1/4 inch deep), then lower the stack back down. The lid of the next plate acts as the cover for the one you just poured.
- Approximately **2 cups of prepared agar fills a standard sleeve of 20–25 plates**.
- Work smoothly to **prevent sloshing** — splashing agar onto the rim or lid creates contamination pathways.
- Let the poured plates **cool undisturbed for 1 hour**, then verify gelling by gently tilting one plate. The surface should remain level.
- **Label every plate or sleeve** with the date, recipe, and additives. Colored marker codes for different formulations simplify lab organization.

A common beginner error is pouring the agar too hot, which causes condensation to form on the plate lid. This condensation can drip back onto the agar surface and create pathways for contaminant spores to migrate. If excessive condensation forms, let the plates sit with their lids slightly cracked in a sterile environment until the moisture evaporates.

## 5. Step 4: Storing Agar Plates

Proper storage extends the usable life of your plates and prevents contamination:

- Once plates have been inoculated, **seal the edges with tape or Parafilm** to prevent contaminants from entering and to slow dehydration. Parafilm is preferred because it is breathable yet seals tightly.
- Store extra **blank plates in new ziplock bags**, grouping 3–4 plates per bag. Plastic ziplock bags are clean straight from heat-injection manufacturing and create an effective sealed storage environment.
- In the **refrigerator** (not the freezer), properly stored plates last **1–2 months and possibly longer**. Freezing is not recommended as it can damage the agar matrix.
- **Discard any bags immediately** that show green, yellow, or black powdery growth — these are mold contaminations that can sporulate aggressively and spread to other stored plates.

For long-term culture maintenance, mycelium can be transferred to fresh plates every 4–6 weeks to keep cultures active and healthy. This periodic subculturing also provides an opportunity to inspect for hidden contamination and select the most vigorous leading edge of growth for the next transfer.

## 6. Alternative Agar Bases

Potato agar is an excellent starting medium because it is relatively difficult for bacteria to colonize, giving fungal cultures a natural competitive advantage. However, many other agar bases are worth exploring:

- **Powdered brewer's malt** — rich in complex sugars and nutrients, favored by fast-growing gourmet species like oyster mushrooms.
- **Dog food or rabbit food pellets** — inexpensive and surprisingly effective; the extruded pellets break down easily in boiling water and provide a balanced nutrient profile including proteins and minerals.
- **Tomato juice** — provides acidity and unique micronutrients suited to certain species that prefer slightly acidic conditions.
- **Cattle feed, cornmeal, beet pulp** — agricultural byproducts adaptable into agar formulations with experimentation, often at very low cost.

Different **ecotypes** of the same fungal species can vary significantly in enzymatic capability, so a formulation that works for one wild collection may not work as well for another isolate. Keep a **detailed journal** of your agar formulas and species performance. Remember that **more supplementation = higher contamination risk** — the goal is finding the optimal nutrient range that supports robust mycelial growth without encouraging competitors.

When experimenting with new formulations, always prepare a control plate of your standard recipe alongside the test plates. This allows you to directly compare growth rates, morphology, and contamination resistance rather than relying on memory from previous batches.

## 7. Adding Fungal Growing Media (Substrate Agar)

For picky or hard-to-clone species, adding a small amount of the mushroom's **preferred fruiting substrate** to the agar can dramatically improve results. This technique is sometimes called "substrate-acclimated agar" and is especially useful for species that have co-evolved with specific wood types or plant materials.

- Add **dried powdered sawdust** (from the target wood type) or **screened wheat straw powder** to your agar recipe before sterilization. A tablespoon per cup of broth is usually sufficient.
- This helps cultures adapt to their eventual growing substrate and begin expressing the specific lignocellulose-degrading enzymes needed for successful fruiting.
- **Split gallery plates** are a powerful diagnostic method: prepare a plate where one half contains familiar agar and the other half contains a more challenging medium enriched with the substrate additive.
- To make a split plate, set the petri base at an angle on a wooden dowel or folded foil, pour the first medium into the lower half, allow it to gel, then flip flat and pour the second medium on the raised side.
- Transfer mycelium onto the **"safe" familiar side** and observe growth at the boundary with the new medium. This reveals whether the culture readily accepts the substrate or stalls at the interface.
- With **consecutive transfers** from the leading edge on the new medium, gradually increase the substrate additive concentration, step by step acclimating the mycelium to the full-strength target medium.

This gradual acclimation approach mimics the natural adaptation process and produces more robust cultures than transferring directly onto a novel substrate. It is particularly valuable for medicinal mushrooms like reishi (Ganoderma spp.) and lion's mane (Hericium spp.) that may have specific substrate preferences.

## 8. Adding Antibiotics

Antibiotic-supplemented agar is used when bacterial contamination is a persistent problem — particularly common with wild-collected specimens or environmental samples that carry heavy bacterial loads. Bacteria can outcompete fungi on nutrient-rich agar, so antibiotics level the playing field and give the slower-growing mycelium a chance to establish.

- **Ampicillin and streptomycin** together provide broad-spectrum bacterial control, the standard laboratory approach for suppressing gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria respectively.
- A more practical solution for small-scale growers is **sterile injectable penicillin G formulated for livestock**, available at farm supply stores.
- Antibiotics must be added **below 110°F (43°C)** — higher temperatures denature and destroy the active compounds, rendering them useless.
- Use a **sterile syringe** to inject the antibiotic into the cooled agar, then swirl gently to distribute evenly without introducing bubbles.
- Wipe both the antibiotic jar and syringe with alcohol before each use. Store the vial and a dedicated syringe together in a sealed ziplock bag in the refrigerator.
- Antibiotics can also be added to **liquid cultures** (grain spawn jars, liquid inoculum) to suppress bacterial growth during longer-term storage of mycelial inoculum, extending viability and reducing loss rates.

Note that antibiotics only suppress bacteria — they have no effect on fungal contaminants like molds (Trichoderma, Aspergillus, etc.). For comprehensive contamination control, antibiotics should be combined with proper sterile technique and clean workspace practices rather than relied upon as a sole defense.
