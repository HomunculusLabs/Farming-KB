# Jivaro Natem Shamanism

The Jivaro people -- comprising the Shuar, Achuar, Huambisa, and Aguaruna
groups of the upper Amazon basin in Ecuador and Peru -- maintain one of
the most thoroughly documented shamanic traditions involving the use of
Banisteriopsis hallucinogenic drinks. The Jivaro call their entheogenic
brew natem, and its use permeates every aspect of their spiritual and
social life. Natem makes it possible for almost anyone to achieve the
trance state essential for the practice of shamanism, resulting in
approximately one out of every four Jivaro men becoming a shaman.

## Preparation of Natem

The natem brew is prepared from segments of the Banisteriopsis caapi
vine, a forest liana belonging to the family Malpighiaceae. The Jivaro
boil the vine segments together with the leaves of a similar vine,
which is probably also a species of Banisteriopsis, to produce a tea.
The shaman blows away the froth on the bubbling natem brew as it
cooks, checking its appearance to assess readiness. After the natem
has boiled down and cooled, it is ready to drink.

## Shamanic Apprenticeship

Any adult Jivaro male who desires to become a shaman simply presents
a gift to an already practicing shaman, who administers the
Banisteriopsis drink and gives some of his own supernatural power in
the form of tsentsak to the apprentice. To give the novice some
tsentsak, the practicing shaman regurgitates what appears to those
who have taken natem to be a brilliant substance in which the spirit
helpers are contained. He cuts part of it off with a machete and gives
it to the novice to swallow.

The recipient experiences pain upon taking the tsentsak into his
stomach and stays on his bed for ten days, drinking natem every
evening. The Jivaro believe they can keep magical darts in their
stomachs indefinitely and regurgitate them at will. The shaman
donating the tsentsak periodically blows and rubs all over the body
of the novice, apparently to increase the power of the transfer.

## Sexual Abstinence and Discipline

The novice must remain inactive and not engage in sexual intercourse
for at least three months. If he fails in self-discipline, he will
not become a successful shaman. At the end of the first month, a
tsentsak emerges from his mouth. With this magical dart at his
disposal, the new shaman experiences a tremendous desire to bewitch.
If he casts his tsentsak to fulfill this desire, he will become a
bewitching shaman. If, on the other hand, the novice can control
his impulse and reswallow this first tsentsak, he will become a
curing shaman.
