# Permaculture Duck Integration for Orchards and Gardens

Ducks are among the most versatile and gentlest poultry species in a
permaculture system. They serve multiple functions simultaneously: pest
control (especially slug and snail management), soil fertilization, pond
aeration, weed management, egg and meat production, and feather harvesting.
Unlike chickens, ducks require less direct care and can thrive on forage
when their environment is properly designed. Their integration into orchards,
gardens, and aquaculture systems exemplifies the permaculture principle of
each element performing multiple functions.

## Species Selection

Several duck breeds are popular with permaculture growers. Khaki Campbell
is the most common domestic breed and a prolific egg layer, producing nearly
as many eggs as chickens. Indian Runners are similarly productive but not
good meat birds. Muscovy, Rouen, and Pekin serve primarily as meat breeds.
For dual-purpose production (both eggs and meat), heritage breeds such as
Ancona, Appleyard, Buff, Magpie, and Saxony are recommended. Saxony is
particularly valued for its foraging ability and reliable egg laying. Ducks
are social creatures and need a flock of at least two to remain healthy and
content.

## System Design

The ideal duck system centers around a small pond with an island for
nesting, located in or adjacent to Zone 2. The enclosure should include a
forage garden around the pond where ducks can graze on greens, bugs, and
weeds. Stocking rates are approximately 25 ducks per acre of pond surface.
If fish are present in the pond, duck manure fertilizes the water, feeding
algae-eating organisms and supporting pond plant growth that in turn feeds
the fish. When the duck area borders the Zone 1 garden, the flock can be
allowed periodic access to consume slugs and pests, but only after garden
plants are well established and taller than the ducks.

## Feeding and Nutrition

Ducks with adequate water, young grass, and a forage garden require minimal
supplemental feed. Their diet should include wheat as the primary grain,
supplemented with oats. Hard fruits and vegetables must be crushed before
feeding. Protein sources include liquid milk, hard-boiled eggs, and the
insects and snails they forage. All ducks require calcium from eggshells or
seashells, plus grit for digestion. Unlike other poultry, ducks need
additional niacin, which fresh greens and peas typically provide in
sufficient quantities.

Homemade poultry chick feed formula: 30% finely ground grain (wheat, corn,
oats); 20% protein (fish meal, meat meal, yogurt, cottage cheese, worms,
bugs, grubs); 50% greens (alfalfa meal or fresh chopped greens); extras
include wheat germ, sunflower seeds, linseed meal, sand for grit, and
ground shells for calcium.

## Housing and Protection

Each duck needs four square feet of housing. During the day, a three-sided
shelter near the pond provides adequate protection. At night, ducks must be
secured from predators in a barn room with straw bedding or a dedicated
shed. Most domestic ducks can fly and need wing clipping after each molt;
clip only the long flight feathers of one wing. During winter when forage
is scarce, additional mulch such as fallen leaves or hay should be spread
in their area. Ducks tolerate freezing temperatures if they can retreat to
their shelter from wind.

## Breeding and Egg Production

Distinguishing males from females can be done by voice: females have a loud,
raspy quack while males are quieter or nearly silent. Some males become
protective of females during breeding season. Ducks begin laying in spring
at six to seven months of age and continue for three years or longer. They
lay consistently in the morning, making a predictable schedule possible.
Let ducks out after 10:00 AM to allow egg collection first, then lure them
back in the evening with grain. A motherly duck will raise her own ducklings
more successfully than artificial methods. Separate mother and ducklings
from the flock for six to eight weeks after hatching.

## Orchard Integration

Ducks integrate beautifully into orchard systems. They forage for fallen
fruit (reducing pest habitat), consume insects and slugs around tree bases,
and deposit nutrient-rich manure. Their broad, flat feet cause less damage
to soil structure than chickens. In established orchards with well-mulched
areas and mature plants, ducks provide continuous pest control and
fertilization without the destructive scratching behavior that makes
chickens problematic near young trees. The key is ensuring the orchard is
well-established before introducing ducks, and maintaining adequate mulch
to prevent soil compaction from their feet.

## Seasonal Management

Duck-orchard integration requires seasonal adjustment of management
practices. During fruiting season, ducks may need temporary exclusion
from areas with low-hanging fruit to prevent damage. In autumn,
ducks effectively clean up fallen fruit, reducing pest overwintering
sites. Winter management focuses on protecting ducks from predators
while allowing continued manure deposition on dormant trees.
