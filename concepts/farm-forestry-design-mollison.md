# Farm Forestry Design (Mollison)

Source: Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Bill Mollison, Section 12.10
(pp. 425-427)

## Why Farm Forestry?

Since approximately 1972, farmers in mesothermal climates have increasingly
developed tree systems on their land. Several converging pressures drive this
transition. The loss of farm firewood and building materials due to overclearing
of forests has forced farmers to grow their own timber and fuel. Concern for
wildlife, especially birds important to crop pest control, has motivated the
retention and planting of habitat trees. Soil erosion on steep slopes exceeding
fifteen degrees has made tree planting an urgent practical necessity.

Farmers have come to realize that trees provide forage in hard times for both
livestock and wildlife. Variation of product from trees buffers economic changes
in prices for annual crops. Tree crops offer a more assured income coupled with
wider markets for exotic vine crops, fruits, and nuts. Shelter effects of trees
improve yields in all other farm production including crops, orchards, and
livestock. Most importantly, farm forestry represents a transition to
tree-oriented production less subject to drought or cold effects, and away from
mass market crop products controlled by large retail or processing firms.

## Support Services for Farm Forestry

For farm forestry to succeed at scale, support services are needed. These
include combined plant nurseries and new species collections for seed, bud, and
scion materials, vegetative propagules, and grafted, layered, or tissue-cultured
plants, as well as processing and market systems. Pioneer farmers are now often
providers of plant materials to others commencing commercial operations. If a
bioregion is integrated and cooperative, such enterprises can cope with very
different plant and animal groups.

## Types of Nursery Enterprises

Mollison identifies several essential nursery types for supporting farm forestry:

- **Bamboo nursery**, together with canegrasses and large clump grasses, a
  currently neglected forestry resource with enormous potential.
- **Aquatic and edge plant nursery**, including fish forages, insectary
  species, and marshland perennials for bee fodders and wildlife refuges.
- **Berry fruit and vine nursery**, since many species grow in mesothermal
  climates and offer diverse market opportunities.
- **Fish breeding**: a few reliable species can supply farm ponds with stock.
- **Poultry species**: providing breeds suited to local soils and range
  conditions.
- **Bee fodder plants**, also butterfly and insectivorous bird attractors,
  providing hedgerow, forage crop, fruits, and flowers.
- **Hedgerow tree species**: region-specific selections, pioneer tall grasses,
  and vines for fedges on coasts.
- **Conservation and reforestation trees** for extending natural forests.
- **Specialty nurseries** for herbs, salt-affected areas, smog tolerance,
  medicinals, food dyes, and stabilisers.

## Forest System Types

### Crop in Pasture (Silvopastoral)
Selected high-value trees are widely spaced in rows to permit good pasture
development between rows. Animals are introduced as trees harden, typically in
years three through six. Many trees compete with pasture for water or nutrients,
but less competitive species include Acacia melanoxylon, Eucalyptus camaldulensis,
Grevillea robusta, and honey locust. Trees such as poplar, fig, willow,
chestnut, oak, and pine may provide forage and other products.

### Woodlot Systems
Woodlots are planted primarily for forest yields but can be highly variable.
**Firewood Production** operates on a two to seven year rotation, with species
chosen for persistent coppice and good fuel value, such as tagasaste, Acacia,
Casuarina, and eucalypt species. **Polewood Production** is increasingly
important for fencing and construction, using durable timbers including chestnut,
osage orange, black locust, and rot-resistant eucalypts. **Long-Term Fine
Timbers** such as black walnut, oak, rosewood, and fine cedars are reserved for
steep land or rocky soils, planted as potential retirement trusts reaching
maximum value at forty to one hundred years.

### Hedgerow Forestry
Hedgerow and contour-bank forests, roadside forest, watershed forest, and
steep slope forests permit true polycultural forestry with six to thirty species
chosen for fruit and nut yields, forages, honey, wildlife foods, browse, mulch,
and stickwood production. Product harvest is on a gather-and-select basis with
constant replanting, unlike the cut-and-run approach of monoculture forestry.

## The Small Forest Farm

Large logging operations waste thirty to seventy percent of harvested timber.
In contrast, a small twelve to sixteen hectare forest site managed by a family
can yield fungi, seed, small fruit, furs, meat, wild fruits and nuts, honey,
poles, coppice, bark for craft and dyes, medicinals, and mulches on a
sustainable basis. The small forest farm generates diverse products from the
same land area, creating economic resilience through product diversity.

## Warnings Against Monoculture Forestry

There can be five or six types of forestry on any farm of fifty hectares or
more. A bioregional forestry devoted to one species of eucalypt or pine is the
antithesis of permaculture and sets up conditions for market glut, depressed
local economy, and land ownership concentration. Monoculture also creates
catastrophic fire risk, as eucalypts and pines provide fuels for firestorm in
settled districts. Displacement of bioregional needs by industrial feedstocks
leads to unemployment and social disruption.

## Design Principles for Integration

Effective farm forestry design maximizes edge effects and microclimate
modification. Riparian zones should be planted with appropriate species to
filter runoff and provide wildlife corridors. Windbreaks should be multi-species
polycultures rather than single-species rows. Integration of livestock with
tree crops requires careful timing, excluding animals during tree establishment
and introducing them only after root systems are well developed.
