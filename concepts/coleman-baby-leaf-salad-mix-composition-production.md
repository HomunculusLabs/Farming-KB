# Coleman Baby Leaf Salad Mix Composition and Production Methods

## Overview

Baby leaf salad mix (often called mesclun) is one of the most profitable and productive crops in Coleman's winter harvest system. Rather than growing individual greens as separate crops, the salad mix approach combines multiple species into a single planting, harvesting at the juvenile leaf stage for maximum tenderness, flavor diversity, and rapid turnover. At Four Season Farm, salad mix is a cornerstone product, featured in winter farmers' market displays and restaurant sales throughout the cold months. (see [[lettuce-and-leafy-greens]]).

## Defining Baby Leaf Stage

Baby leaf refers to greens harvested at 3 to 5 inches (8–13 cm) in height, before the plants reach maturity. At this stage:
- **Texture is tender**: Cell walls are thin and delicate, suitable for raw salads. (see [[coleman-chicory-family-winter-greens-endive-escarole-radicchio]]).
- **Flavor is mild to moderate**: Many greens become increasingly bitter or pungent as they mature. Baby leaves capture the sweet, mild phase. (see [[succession-planting]]).
- **Growth cycle is short**: Most baby leaf greens reach harvestable size in 21 to 35 days from sowing, depending on species and season. (see [[coleman-winter-harvest-cut-and-come-again-techniques]]).
- **Nutrient density is high**: Young leaves concentrate vitamins and minerals relative to their size.

## Salad Mix Species Palette

Coleman's salad [[coleman-salad-and-braising-mix-production]] mix draws from a diverse palette of species, each contributing distinct flavors, colors, and textures. The standard mix includes:

### Lettuces (Base and Sweetness)
Lettuces form the bulk of the mix (typically 40–60% by volume):
- **Oakleaf types**: Red and green oakleaf lettuces provide tender, lobed leaves with mild flavor.
- **Butterhead types**: Boston and Bibb lettuces contribute soft, rounded leaves.
- **Looseleaf types**: Red and green Salad Bowl, Black-Seeded Simpson, and similar varieties.
- **Cos/Romaine**: Baby romaine leaves add crunch and structure.

Coleman recommends selecting at least 4–6 lettuce varieties for genetic diversity and visual variety.

### Brassicas (Spice and Peppery Notes)
Brassica leaves contribute peppery, mustardy flavors:
- **Arugula** (*Eruca vesicaria*): Perhaps the most important non-lettuce component. Adds nutty-peppery flavor.
- **Mustard greens**: Red giant, green wave, and mizuna provide varying levels of heat.
- **Tatsoi**: Spoon-shaped leaves with mild, sweet flavor.
- **Pak choi**: Crisp stems and tender leaves with subtle cabbage flavor.

### Chicory Family (Bitter Balance)
Chicories provide bitter contrast that elevates the overall flavor profile:
- **Radicchio**: Baby radicchio leaves add deep red color and pleasant bitterness.
- **Frisée (curly endive)**: Lacy, finely cut leaves with bitter notes.
- **Escarole**: Broader leaves, milder than frisée, still contributing complexity.

### Other Components
- **Cress**: Upland cress or watercress adds peppery bite.
- **Sorrel**: Lemon-flavored leaves used sparingly as an accent.
- **Beet greens**: Bulls' Blood beet thinnings contribute deep red color and earthy flavor.

## Mix Composition Ratios

Coleman suggests a balanced mix that emphasizes lettuce (the mildest and most universally appealing component) while incorporating enough brassica and chicory for flavor complexity:

- **Lettuces**: 50–60% of total volume
- **Brassica greens**: 20–30%
- **Chicory family**: 10–15%
- **Accent herbs and specialty items**: 5–10%

The exact ratios shift seasonally. In winter, when customers crave fresh, vibrant flavors, Coleman leans toward slightly more brassica and slightly less chicory. In spring, when palates are more adventurous, the chicory proportion increases.

## Sowing Methods

### Broadcast Sowing
The traditional mesclun approach is to broadcast sow a pre-mixed blend of seeds at a rate of approximately 1 ounce (28 g) per 100 square feet (9.3 m²). Advantages include:
- **Speed**: Large areas can be sown quickly.
- **Natural mixing**: Different species intermingle naturally, creating an attractive blend.
- **Density management**: Some species germinate faster than others, creating a staggered natural harvest window.

Broadcast sowing requires careful seed distribution to avoid clumping. Coleman recommends sowing in two passes at right angles for even distribution, then lightly raking and pressing the bed.

### Row Sowing for Management
Alternatively, each component can be sown in separate rows within the same bed. This allows:
- **Species-specific management**: Different greens may need different irrigation or protection.
- **Selective harvest**: Individual rows can be harvested when ready rather than cutting the entire bed.
- **Better disease monitoring**: Problems are easier to identify and manage in row-sown crops.

For commercial production, many growers use a hybrid approach: broadcast the lettuces (which are relatively uniform) and row-sow the more management-intensive components.
