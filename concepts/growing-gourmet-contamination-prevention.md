# Contamination Prevention

Contamination is the single greatest threat to mushroom cultivation. [[mycopesticides-stamets]] identifies [[six-vectors-of-contamination]] and provides a systematic framework for preventing each one. The key insight is that all contamination is preventable by eliminating these vectors, and that time of exposure is the universal variable affecting all of them.

## The Six Vectors of Contamination

The principal vectors of contamination are:
1. The Cultivator
2. The Air
3. The Media
4. The Tools
5. The Inoculum
6. Mobile Contamination Units (MCUs)

The overriding coefficients affecting each vector are the number of contaminants and the exposure time. The more of each, the worse the infestation. If one vector is open, a multitude of contaminants pass through it, often confounding diagnoses for inexperienced cultivators.

## Vector 1: The Cultivator

The human body teems with populations of micro-organisms. Diverse species of fungi (including yeast), bacteria and viruses call the body home. When healthy, these populations achieve equilibrium. When ill, one or more groups proliferate out of control. Unhealthy people should not be in a sterile laboratory.

Most frequently, contaminants are spread via touch, breath, or flaking skin. Many cultivators wear gloves to minimize skin-borne contaminants. Stamets prefers to wash hands every 20-30 minutes with antibacterial soap, and disinfects hands with 80% isopropyl alcohol immediately before inoculations and every few minutes throughout the procedure.

**Prevention measures:**
- Shower, wash, and wear newly laundered clothes before entering the laboratory
- Wash hands with antibacterial soap every 20-30 minutes
- Disinfect hands with 80% isopropyl alcohol before and during inoculations
- Wear gloves as an option (Stamets finds them uncomfortable but some prefer them)
- Do not enter the laboratory when ill

## Vector 2: The Air

Air is a sea of microorganisms hosting invisible contaminants that directly contaminate sterilized media once exposed. When a person walks into the laboratory, he brings in contaminants and disturbs contaminant-laden floor particles, re-releasing them into the lab's atmosphere.

**Prevention measures:**

### Three-Door Lab Design
Always have at least three doors prior to entry into the sterile laboratory from the outside. Each room or chamber shall, by default, have fewer airborne particulates the nearer they are to the laboratory.

### Positive-Pressure HEPA Filtration
By positive-pressurizing the laboratory with an influx of air through micron filters, the airstream naturally directs against entering personnel. HEPA filters eliminate all contaminants down to 0.3 microns with 99.99% efficiency. Only 1 of every 10,000 particulates exceeding 0.3 microns passes through, effectively generating a sterile wind downstream.

### Still-Air Spray Method
For those not installing micron filters, the "still-air" method uses aerosol sprays of commercial disinfectants like Pinesol or a dilute solution of isopropanol or bleach. The cultivator enters the work area and sprays a mist high up in the laboratory, walking backwards as he retreats. As the disinfecting mist descends, airborne particulates are trapped, carrying contaminants to the floor. After a minute or two, the cultivator re-enters and begins work.

**Important warnings:**
- Do not mix dimyceliumnts, especially bleach and ammonia
- This method can potentially damage lungs or exposed mucous membranes
- UV lamps are effective only when contaminants are directly exposed; shadowed areas remain unaffected
- Lab doors should be electrically switched to UV lights so they turn off at entry

### CO2 and Air Exchange
Without fresh air exchange, carbon dioxide levels rise from out-gassing by mushroom mycelium. Elevated CO2 triggers contaminant growth. Heavily packed spawn rooms also see oxygen levels decrease, eventually asphyxiating personnel. Since the only way to exchange air without introducing contaminants is by filtering, the combination of fans and micron filters is the only recourse.

### Water Misting for Growing Rooms
The air in growing rooms does not require the degree of filtration needed for the laboratory. Cleaning the air by water misting is practical and effective (rain is nature's best method). Spray down each growing room twice a day, starting from the ceiling and broadcasting back and forth, eventually washing towards the center gutter. Each wash-down of a 1000 sq. ft. growing room takes about 15 minutes.

## Vector 3: The Media

Often the medium upon which a culture is grown becomes the source of contamination. Insufficient sterilization is usually the cause.

**Standard sterilization times:**
- Liquid media: 15-20 minutes at 15 psi (250F / 121C) -- Stamets considers this far too brief for many endospore-forming bacteria
- Malt extract or potato dextrose [[agar]]s: at least 40 minutes at 15 psi
- Soil extracts: soak 24 hours, then sterilize extracted water for minimum 1 hour
- Fresh hardwood sawdust: 2-3 hours at 15 psi
- Supplemented sawdust (with rice bran): 4-5 hours at 15 psi
- Autoclave with 240 tightly packed bags: 5 hours at 18 psi

**Key principle:** As the percentage of nitrogen-supplements increases relative to the base substrate, the greater the likelihood of contamination, and thus the greater the need for full and thorough sterilization.

**Over-sterilization danger:** Prolonged steam sterilization (more than 5 hours at 15-18 psi) causes complex chemical transformations. Sawdust takes on a dark brown color, has a distinctly different odor, and resists decomposition by mushroom mycelium. Turpentines, volatile oils, and toxic by-products are responsible.

**Media should be wetted just prior to filling.** If water is added and allowed to sit for more than 6 hours, legions of contaminants spur to life. The more contaminants at make-up, the more that survive the sterilization cycle.

## Vector 4: The Tools

All tools of the trade are included -- from the scalpel to the pressure cooker to media vessels. Insufficient sterilization of tools is a direct vector since contact with media is immediate.

**Prevention measures:**
- Flame-sterilizing scalpels is the preferred method over topical disinfection with alcohol or bleach
- Although the interior of a pressure cooker has been sterilized, the outside has not. Contaminants can be easily picked up by hands and re-distributed to the workstation. Disinfect the outside before beginning transfers.
- All items on the inoculation table should be prioritized by degree and recentness of sterility (freshest sterilized material kept upstream, mycelium directly downstream)
- Use sterilization indicator test strips placed into a few grain-filled jars to test effectiveness of sterilization cycles

## Vector 5: The Inoculum

The inoculum is the tissue being transferred -- whether from a living mushroom, mycelium from another petri dish, or spores. Bacteria and molds can infect the mushroom tissue and be carried with it every time a transfer is made. Isolation of the inoculum from mushroom mycelium can be frustrating because many contaminant organisms grow faster than newly emerging mushroom mycelium.

**Prevention measures:**
- Cultivators must constantly "run" or transfer their mycelium away from rapidly developing competitors
- Several techniques can purify contaminated mycelium
- Select petri dish cultures no more than two weeks old, with a margin of uncolonized media (~1/2 inch) to detect recently landed contaminants

## Vector 6: Mobile Contamination Units (MCUs)

MCUs are organisms that carry and spread contaminants within the laboratory. These living macro-organisms act as vehicles spreading contaminants from one site to another. They are especially damaging because they are difficult to isolate.

**Common MCU examples:**
- Ants
- Flies
- Mites (the most difficult to control)
- Small children ("small bipedal offspring")

Mites are the spawn manager's worst nightmare. Their minute size, preference for fungi as food, and penchant for travel make them extremely difficult to eradicate. Once mite contamination levels exceed 10%, the demise of the laboratory is only one generation away. Typically, a MCU carries not one contaminant, but several.

**Prevention measures:**

### Decontamination Sticky Mats
Place stacks of petri dishes on newly exposed sticky mats on a laboratory shelf with several inches of space separating them. These zones of isolation, with culture dishes incubating upon a highly adhesive surface, make the migration of mites and other insects extremely difficult. The upper sheet is removed every few weeks to expose a fresh, clean storage plane.

### Total Lab Shutdown Protocol
When mite contamination exceeds 10%, the only solution is to totally shut down the laboratory:
1. Remove all cultures including petri dishes, spawn jars, etc.
2. Thoroughly cleanse the laboratory several times using 10% household bleach solution
3. Wash floors, walls, and ceiling using two buckets -- the first being the primary reservoir, the second for rinsing debris
4. Lock the lab tight for each day after wash-down
5. Through three successive cleansings, the mite problem can be eliminated or subdued to manageable levels
6. Regenerate mycelia from carefully selected stock cultures

## Blank Controls

A critical diagnostic tool is leaving containers of media uninoculated at every step of the cultivation process. These "blanks" give the cultivator valuable insights into which vector of contamination is operating.

- Leave some culture dishes uninoculated and unopened
- Leave a few samples from each sterilization cycle uninoculated (petri dishes, grain jars, sawdust/bran bags)
- Observe for a period of two weeks

**Diagnostic interpretation:**
- If uninoculated units contaminate within 48-72 hours: media or containers were insufficiently sterilized
- If contamination occurs near the end of two weeks: contamination is probably endemic to the laboratory, particularly where these units are being stored
- Under ideal conditions, uninoculated media should remain contamination-free regardless of how long stored

## Universal Factor: Time of Exposure

All vectors are universally affected by one variable: time of exposure. The longer the exposure of any vector of contamination, the more significant its impact. Good laboratory technicians are characterized not only by their speed and care, but by their rhythm. Transfers are done in a systematically repetitive fashion. Controlling time of exposure can have a drastic impact on the quality of laboratory technique.
