# Wind Power Basics

Small-scale wind turbines convert kinetic energy from wind into electricity for homestead power systems. Wind complements [[holmgren-trees-solar-power-plants-detailed]] effectively because wind often blows when the sun is not shining (night, winter storms, overcast days). A properly sited and maintained small wind turbine can generate 200-10,000+ kWh per year for an off-grid or grid-tied homestead.

## Is Wind Right for Your Site?

### Minimum Requirements

- Average annual wind speed: Minimum 10 mph (4.5 m/s) at hub height
- Property size: Minimum 1 acre for tower setbacks
- Zoning: Check local ordinances for height restrictions and noise limits
- Neighbors: Consider visual impact and noise
- Grid access: Determine if you want grid-tied or off-grid

### Wind Resource Assessment

Before investing in wind power, assess your wind resource:

1. **Check wind [[maps]]**: NREL (National Renewable Energy Laboratory) wind resource maps for the US provide initial estimates
2. **Install an anemometer**: Monitor wind speed at hub height for 12+ months
3. **Observe vegetation**: Flagging (trees leaning in one direction) indicates consistent wind
4. **Consider local topography**: Ridges, gaps, and open areas accelerate wind; valleys and forests reduce it
5. **Compare seasonal patterns**: Wind should complement your solar production (windier in winter when solar is weakest)

### Realistic Expectations

- A 400W turbine in 12 mph average wind generates approximately 100 kWh/month
- A 1kW turbine in 12 mph average wind generates approximately 250 kWh/month
- A 3kW turbine in 12 mph average wind generates approximately 700 kWh/month
- Energy output scales with the cube of wind speed — doubling wind speed produces 8x the power

## Turbine Types

### Horizontal Axis Wind Turbines (HAWT)

The most common type — blades rotate around a horizontal axis, pointed into the wind by a tail vane or active yaw mechanism.

**Small HAWT sizes for homesteads:**
- 400W (6-8 foot diameter): Supplemental power, charging small battery banks
- 1kW (10-12 foot diameter): Powers a small off-grid cabin
- 3kW (12-16 foot diameter): Powers a medium off-grid home
- 10kW (22-25 foot diameter): Powers a large home or small farm

**Advantages of HAWT:**
- Higher efficiency than vertical axis turbines
- Well-understood technology with many manufacturers
- Proven reliability with decades of installations

**Disadvantages:**
- Must be pointed into the wind (requires tail or yaw mechanism)
- Higher noise levels
- Requires tall towers for good wind access
- More maintenance (moving parts exposed to weather)

### Vertical Axis Wind Turbines (VAWT)

Blades rotate around a vertical axis. Popular for urban installations but generally less efficient.

**Types:**
- Darrieus (eggbeater shape): Most efficient VAWT design
- Savonius (S-shaped scoops): Simple, low-speed, low efficiency

**Advantages of VAWT:**
- Omnidirectional — no need to point into the wind
- Lower noise
- Can be mounted closer to ground (though performance suffers)
- More bird-friendly
- Better in turbulent wind conditions

**Disadvantages:**
- Lower efficiency (30-40% vs. 40-50% for HAWT)
- Higher cost per watt
- Less proven technology at small scale
- Poor starting torque in low winds

## Tower Height

Tower height is the single most important factor in wind turbine performance. Wind speed increases with altitude due to reduced ground friction.

### Height Guidelines

- Minimum tower height: 30 feet above any obstacle within 300 feet
- Typical residential tower: 60-100 feet
- Rule of thumb: Higher is always better — every additional foot of height increases output
- Wind shear: Wind speed at 100 feet is typically 20-40% higher than at 30 feet

### Tower Types

**Tilt-up towers (Most Common for Small Systems)**
- Base-hinged, tilted up with a gin pole and winch
- Allows maintenance at ground level
- Most practical for homestead installations
- Cost: $3,000-8,000 installed

**Fixed towers**
- Lattice (open frame) or monopole (single tube)
- Require climbing or a bucket truck for maintenance
- More stable in high winds
- Cost: $5,000-15,000 installed

**Rooftop mounting (Not Recommended)**
- Turbulence from the roof reduces performance significantly
- Vibration and noise transmitted to the building
- Structural concerns — roof may not support the loads
- Not worth the risk for the small power gains

## Battery Bank and Charge Controller

### Charge Controller

- Diversion (dump) load controller: Routes excess power to a heating element
- MPPT controller: Maximizes power extraction from the turbine
- Must be designed for wind (handles wild AC from turbine, converts to DC for batteries)

### Battery Bank

Wind turbines produce variable power — a battery bank smooths the output.

- **LiFePO4**: Best choice for new installations — long life, high efficiency, no maintenance
- **Lead-acid (flooded)**: Lower cost, requires maintenance (watering, equalizing)
- **Lead-acid (AGM)**: Maintenance-free but shorter life and higher cost than flooded
- Size the bank for 2-3 days of autonomy (no wind or sun)

### Dump Load

When batteries are full and the wind is blowing, excess energy must be diverted to prevent battery damage. Common dump loads:
- Water heating element (most useful — pre-heats domestic hot water)
- Space heater
- Air heater in a greenhouse or workshop

## Grid-Tie vs. Off-Grid

### Grid-Tied

- Sell excess power to the utility (net metering)
- No battery bank required (grid acts as backup)
- Simpler system, lower cost
- Requires utility approval and interconnection agreement
- Utility may not allow wind turbines in some areas
