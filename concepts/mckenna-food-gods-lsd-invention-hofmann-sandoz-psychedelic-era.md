# LSD, Hofmann, and the Opening of Pandora's Box

## The Ergot Connection

The story of LSD begins with **ergot** (*[[lsd-d-lysergic-acid-diethylamide]]**, designated LSD-25,
first produced on November 16, 1938, by the Swiss chemist **Albert
Hofmann**.

## The Accidental Discovery: April 16, 1943

LSD-25 was initially set aside as a compound of insufficient interest.
For five years it gathered dust in the Sandoz archives. On April 16,
1943, Hofmann decided to resynthesize it for a closer look. During the
work, he accidentally absorbed a minute quantity — possibly through
his fingertips — and soon experienced what he later described as "an
uninterrupted stream of fantastic pictures, extraordinary shapes with
an intense, kaleidoscopic play of colors." Hofmann suspected that LSD
was responsible. Three days later, on **April 19, 1943**, he
deliberately ingested 250 micrograms of LSD-25 — what he believed to
be a threshold dose — and embarked on the most famous bicycle ride
in the history of pharmacology. As the compound took hold, Hofmann
asked his laboratory assistant to escort him home by bicycle. The
ride became a hallucinatory ordeal: the landscape distorted, his
neighborhood appeared threatening and alien, he feared he was dying
or losing his mind. This date — April 19 — has since been celebrated
as **"Bicycle Day"** by psychedelic enthusiasts worldwide.

## Delysid and the Research Era

Hofmann's experience convinced Sandoz that LSD-25 was a compound of
remarkable potency and interest. Marketed under the trade name
**Delysid** beginning in the late 1940s, Sandoz distributed it freely
to psychiatric researchers. Effective doses measured in micrograms,
making LSD the most potent psychoactive substance known. Throughout
the 1950s, LSD was investigated as a treatment for alcoholism, anxiety,
depression, and as an adjunct to psychotherapy. Humphry Osmond coined
the term **"psychedelic"** ("mind-manifesting") in 1957. The
scientific establishment regarded LSD as a powerful tool for
understanding the mind.

## MK-Ultra: The Dark Side of Government Research

The same properties that made LSD interesting to psychiatrists made it
irresistible to [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]]. Harvard dismissed both Leary and Alpert in
1963, but their notoriety only grew. Leary became the most prominent
public advocate for psychedelics in the world, a role that made him
both a countercultural hero and a government target.

## The Counterculture and the Mass Media

LSD escaped the laboratory with astonishing speed. By the mid-1960s,
the drug had become the central sacrament of a burgeoning
counterculture. Ken Kesey's **Merry Pranksters** conducted acid tests
across America. Rock musicians publicly embraced psychedelics. But
mass media coverage was overwhelmingly sensationalist — linking LSD to
psychosis, suicide, and moral collapse. The drug became a flashpoint in
generational conflict, and the gap between transcendent experiences and
horror stories created a climate of panic that made suppression
inevitable.

## Criminalization and Suppression

On October 6, 1966, California became the first state to criminalize
LSD. The federal government followed with the **[[ayahuasca-pharmacology-and-cultural-context]], ritual framework, and ecological grounding that
traditional plant use provided. Pandora's box had been opened, but the
instructions for safely navigating what lay inside had been lost with
the suppression of shamanic knowledge.
