# Mushroom Growing Situations and Cave Cultivation

## Overview

Mushrooms may be grown in a variety of situations, from purpose-built structures to natural underground caves. The fundamental requirement is the ability to regulate temperature and moisture within ranges suitable for mushroom growth. This section examines the diverse growing environments used historically and the specific conditions that make each suitable or unsuitable for commercial mushroom production.

## The Fundamental Environmental Requirements

The main factors that must be regulated for successful [[mushroom-cultivation]] are temperature and moisture. Any situation in which these conditions may be controlled to accord with the requirements of the mushroom should prove satisfactory. This simple principle explains the diversity of growing environments that have been employed successfully — from elaborate purpose-built houses to natural caves and even modified outdoor beds.

## Cave Cultivation in France

### The Parisian Limestone Quarries

The French mushroom industry owes its enormous scale to the subterranean limestone quarries beneath Paris and its suburbs. Parisian buildings were constructed primarily of limestone mined from beneath the city itself and the adjacent countryside. The mining operations left vast labyrinthine systems of caves — excluding the catacombs beneath the city proper, which are not used for mushroom cultivation.

The suburban caves are situated from a few feet to 50 feet beneath the surface. They vary from 5 to 25 feet in width (exceptionally 50 feet) and from 5 to 20 or more feet in height. In suburbs such as Montrouge, Vitry, Châtillon, Issy, Nanterre, Rosny-sous-Bois, and Passy, mushroom growing was one of the really important industries. An estimated 2,000 kilometers (about 1,200 miles) of mushroom beds existed in these cave systems.

### Cave Access and Ventilation

Entry to the champignonnières was either through an open quarry readily accessible, or through a hole only a few feet in diameter, accessed by ladder with a windlass for lifting and lowering materials. The cave systems were very well ventilated by means of chimneys or chutes. Air exchange was often facilitated by small charcoal fires beneath these air chutes and occasionally by special ventilating devices.

The term "champignonnière" (mushroom cave) became practically synonymous with "carrière" (quarry) in the regions around Paris, so ubiquitous was mushroom growing in these underground spaces.

## Growing Situations in the United States

### Special Mushroom Houses

Near Philadelphia, Chicago, Boston, and other production centers, mushrooms were grown wholly in special mushroom houses. Unquestionably, the major part of the American mushroom crop was produced this way. These purpose-built structures allowed precise control over the growing environment.

### Caves and Underground Quarries

Some commercial growers in Minnesota, Ohio, Indiana, New York, and Missouri secured underground quarries, caves, or storage cellars of considerable extent. Such places were considered excellent when readily accessible, provided there was no danger from flooding or seepage water. Natural caves in the limestone section of Missouri had been used successfully, though poor arrangements for removing old beds sometimes led to accumulation of spent bedding material and increasing mushroom pest populations, eventually forcing abandonment of old cave gardens for new ones.

### Beer Storage Cellars

Many antiquated beer storage cellars of limited capacity were in use in various cities. These structures, originally designed to maintain cool, stable temperatures for lagering beer, naturally provided conditions suitable for mushroom growing.

### Natural Caves

Natural caves occur in Kentucky, Indiana, Ohio, Virginia, and Arkansas, with potential for future use. Caves or deep cellars always have the advantage of permitting mushroom production during a long period — frequently throughout the entire year — but they must offer the possibility of adequate ventilation.

### Coal Mines

Abandoned coal mines were generally considered unsuitable as usually left, though hill coal mines in some sections were thought to possibly repay investigation for commercial mushroom production.

## Mushroom House Construction

### Simple Structures

For temporary use, a mushroom house could consist of a very simple boarded shed or unused barn, provided with a heater since winter is the important season for mushroom work. Many commercial mushroom houses that paid well were not particularly elaborate.

### Permanent Construction

For a permanent investment, the house should be built with due consideration for convenience, durability, and economy. Key construction principles include:

**Drainage**: Perfect drainage is a factor of the first importance. This may be complicated by the fact that for convenience in heating and more uniform temperature during warmer months, it is often convenient to construct the house over an excavation that may be several feet below the natural surface.

**Bed Tiers**: The greatest economy is attained by constructing houses high enough to accommodate beds in tiers of from two to five. About 2¼ feet of free space must be allowed between beds in tiers, since each bed is at least 8 to 10 inches deep, requiring somewhat more than 3 feet in height per bed level.

**Wall Construction**: The better class of mushroom houses traditionally featured double walls packed with fresh shavings for insulation. More expensive houses were made of hollow tile or concrete. Hollow tile was particularly recommended where the outlay was justified, because the interior of a mushroom house always has a moist atmosphere, and the contact of moisture-laden air with cold walls produces constant precipitation or "sweating."

**Roof Design**: A sufficiently sloping roof provided with air space corrects, to a large extent, the condensation problem. Ventilators should typically be placed in the roof, as this accomplishes the desired purpose with less inconvenience from direct drafts.

### Bed Supports

The upright supports for beds in tiers of four or five should be scantling not less than 2×6 inches, placed about 4 feet apart. Experienced commercial growers adopted large gas piping for the supports, which offered great permanency and were perfect from a sanitary standpoint. Wall beds should seldom be more than 3 feet across, but beds approachable from either side may be 6 feet across. Aisles of at least 3 feet should be left for convenient installation and removal of beds.

## Open-Air Culture

### English Practice

In Southern England, where winter temperature extremes are less severe, culture in modified hot beds or cold frames was practiced. Simple culture frames of the usual type were employed, except that no glass was used — they were covered with sheet iron or boards, with a layer of straw placed on top if necessary. Fresh manure beneath the compost provided bottom heat to force early growth of the spawn.

### Limitations of Open-Air Culture

Open-air culture for mushrooms cannot be regarded as having great possibilities of success in most regions. The difficulties are considerable:

- Commercial mushroom growing demands a more or less uniform temperature, which outdoor environments cannot provide
- Daily temperature variation is usually considerable
- Drying winds are not infrequent
- It is difficult to regulate the moisture factor
- Beds must be protected from drenching rains

### Potential in California

Certain sections of California were identified as potentially suitable for open-air culture. The mean monthly temperature of San Francisco and surrounding areas is normally 50° to 60°F, with a non-pronounced daily range. Both *[[agaricus-campestris]]* and *A. arvensis* are found in parts of California during winter months, suggesting natural suitability. The optimal mean temperature for outdoor beds was considered to be somewhat below the ideal growing temperature — 48° to 56°F — as this provides a buffer against warm spells.

### Field Spawning Experiments

Inquiries about spawning pastures and lawns to establish natural cultures for autumn harvest were common. Experiments along these lines had no permanent success across a variety of conditions. Results were wholly inconsistent and dependent on the season. English correspondents reported similar unreliable outcomes. Nevertheless, the labor involved in spreading spawn about lawns or pastures is so minimal that it may be worth experimenting whenever spawn is available — in favorable seasons, one may be well repaid.

## Key Takeaways

The choice of growing situation ultimately depends on the grower's ability to control the two critical variables: temperature and moisture. Natural caves offer year-round production potential but require accessibility, drainage, and ventilation. Purpose-built houses offer maximum control and convenience at higher capital cost. Simple structures and modified outdoor beds can succeed in favorable climates but carry significant risk from environmental variability.
