# Zone and Sector Analysis in Permaculture

Zone and sector analysis is the master pattern of permaculture design. It is the primary method for placing elements in a landscape based on energy conservation principles. See [[permaculture-design]] and [[permaculture-site-analysis]] for the broader design context.

## Zones: Frequency of Visit

Zones are concentric rings around the home (Zone 0), numbered by frequency of human visit and management intensity:

- **Zone 0**: The house, home center, or settlement. The most intensely managed area. See [[permaculture-zone-0-house-design]].
- **Zone 1**: The area immediately surrounding the home visited daily. Includes kitchen gardens, culinary herbs, small animals needing daily care, propagation nursery, rainwater tanks, fuelwood close to door. See [[zone-1-intensive-garden]].
- **Zone 2**: Less frequently visited -- orchard, main crop gardens, poultry houses, ponds, composting areas, small livestock.
- **Zone 3**: Farm scale -- main crop areas, pastures, larger livestock, orchards, woodlots for coppice. Visited weekly to monthly.
- **Zone 4**: Forage and forestry -- timber, large animal grazing, wildlife corridors. Visited seasonally.
- **Zone 5**: Unmanaged or wilderness zone. Used for conservation, observation, and as a reference system.

The golden rule: develop the nearest area first, get it under control, then expand the perimeter. A single perimeter should enclose all needs.

### Zone 0: The Home Center

Zone 0 centers on good house design with an attached [[permaculture-earthworks-guide]] or shadehouse. Living components integrate directly into the structure: sod roofs, vines on trellis, pot plants, roof gardens, and companion animals. In some climates, structures are formed from the natural environment -- bamboo, rattan, wattle and daub, thatch, or earth-covered construction. See [[permaculture-zone-0-house-design]].

### Zone 1: Intensive Garden

Within 6m (20 feet) of a home, householders can produce most food necessary to existence. This zone includes seedlings, young trees for outer zone placement, mother plants for cuttings, rare and delicate species, small domestic animals (fish, rabbits, pigeons, guinea pigs), culinary herbs, and rainwater catchment tanks connected to [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]] systems. Techniques: complete mulching, intensive pruning, annuals with fast replacement, full land use, and nutrient recycling of household wastes. "In this zone, we arrange nature to serve our needs."

### Zone 2: Orchard and Forage

Spot-mulched orchards, main-crop beds, ranging domestic animals, terraces, small ponds, hedges, and trellis. Forage ranges for milk cows, goats, and poultry. Home orchards with less intensive pruning than Zone 1. Water may be piped from Zone 3 storages.

### Zone 3: Farm Zone

The "farm zone" of commercial crop and animals for sale or barter. Managed by green manuring, spreading manure from Zone 2, and soil conditioning. Contains natural or little-pruned trees, broadscale farming systems, large water storages, feed-store or barns, and field shelters as hedgerow or windbreak. See [[permaculture-zones-in-practice]].

### Zone 4: Forage and Forestry

Area bordering on forest or wilderness, still managed for wild gathering, forest and fuel needs, pasture or range. Planted to hardy, unpruned, or volunteer trees. Water stored as dams only, with piped input to other zones.

### Zone 5: Wilderness

Natural, unmanaged environment for occasional foraging, recreation, or "just let be." "This is where we learn the rules that we try to apply elsewhere."

### What Decides Zoning?

Two factors determine zoning: (1) the number of times you need to visit the plant, animal, or structure, AND (2) the number of times the plant, animal, or structure needs you. For example, a poultry shed is visited ~460 times/year while an oak tree may be visited only twice. See [[permaculture-zones-system]] for the full framework.

### Dooryard Gardens: A Classic Example

Edgar Anderson's description of Central Honduras dooryard gardens illustrates the Zone 1 ideal: a compact garden-orchard of several hundred sq ft with neat plantations grouped together -- fruit trees (nance, citrus, melias, mango), coffee bushes in shade, tapioca, taro, corn, beans, climbing squashes, chayote, luffa gourd, flowers, and grain amaranth. "Where orchard, field, house and garden have no clear boundaries."

## Sectors: External Energy Flows

Sectors are directional energies that pass through the site -- sun, wind, fire, water flow, pollution, views, wildlife movement. While zones radiate from Zone 0, sectors are compass directions from which energies arrive. Sectors are more site-specific than conceptualised zones. "Whereas settlement or house is the ground zero for zones, it is the through point for sectors." Energies arrive as "arrows winging their way towards the home." We erect shields, deflectors, or collectors in response. See [[permaculture-sector-analysis]] and [[sectors-in-permaculture]].

Design decisions for sectors include:

- **Block or screen** destructive energies (cold wind, fire, unwanted views)
- **Channel** energies for special uses (funnel wind to turbines, direct water to gardens)
- **Open out** sectors to allow beneficial energies (sunlight, warm breezes)

Components rotate in zones until they work to govern sector energies. Combine both zone and sector diagrams to make "a spiderweb of placements." See [[permaculture-zones-and-sectors]] for combined mapping techniques.

### Pine Tree Placement Example

A pine tree belongs in Zone 4, away from the fire danger sector (it accumulates fuel), towards the cold wind sector (hardy windbreak), and should also bear edible nuts to serve multiple functions.

### Poultry Shed Placement Example

A poultry shed should BORDER Zone 1 (frequent visits), sit away from the fire sector, BORDER the annual garden (for manure collection), BACK ONTO a forage system, possibly ATTACH to a greenhouse, and form part of a windbreak.

## Slope, Aspect, and Orientation

No site is flat. Slope determines unpowered water flow from source to use point. The ideal humid landscape profile has:
- High plateaus/ridges for forest and water collection
- Steep upper slopes as protected watershed
- Midslope for settlement and intensive agriculture
- Foothills and plains for water storage dams and extensive cropping

See [[permaculture-slope-analysis-and-reading]] for detailed slope analysis methods and [[keyline-design]] for slope-based water management.

### Slope and Infrastructure

Slope analysis informs [[permaculture-access-and-circulation-design]]: high access roads serve heavy cargo and mulch delivery, while low access roads serve fire control. Slope permits placement of hot air and hot water collectors below storages (thermosiphon effect), attached glasshouses, hot air collectors, reflection ponds, solar ponds, and shadehouses. Where no slope exists, use towers for water tanks and hollows for heat collectors.

## Settlement Patterns

Successful and permanent [[permaculture-designers-manual-settlement-types]] are placed at critical junctions of two natural ecologies -- foothill forests and plains, plain and marsh, or land and estuary. "Successful and permanent settlements have always been able to draw from the resources of at least two environments."

## The Basic Energy-Conserving Rules

1. Every element (plant, animal, or structure) must be placed so that it serves at least two or more functions.
2. Every function (e.g. water collection, fire protection) is served in two or more ways.

These rules reflect the broader [[permaculture-pattern-language]] of functional interconnection.
