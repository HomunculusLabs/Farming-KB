# Rust Fungi — Biodiversity, Life Cycles, and Collection Rust fungi (Urediniomycetes) are among the most economically important plant pathogens, causing diseases on a vast range of host plants. Their complex life cycles and high host specificity make them both fascinating and challenging to study. ## Diversity - Approximately **168 genera and 7,000 species** currently accepted - More than half in the genus **Puccinia** (47% of North American records) - North America: 53 genera, ~925 host-fungus associations - Tropical America: significantly understudied; 20% of species collected in one 40-hectare neotropical tract were new to science - About one-third of plant species in that tract had their own rust species - Expected diversity in tropics exceeds temperate regions - Hosts: ferns, gymnosperms, monocots, and dicots (no rusts on mosses or liverworts) ## Life Cycle and Spore Stages A single species may produce up to **five distinct spore-producing structures**: 1. **Spermagonia:** Produce spermatia and receptive hyphae (sexual stage) 2. **Aecia:** Produce aeciospores (often on alternate host in heteroecious species) 3. **Uredinia:** Produce urediniospores (repeating stage; drives epidemics) 4. **Telia:** Produce teliospores (overwintering stage; dikaryotic) 5. **Basidia:** Produce basidiospores (meiotic; infect primary host) ### Heteroecious vs. Autoecious - **Heteroecious:** Require two unrelated host species (often different families) to complete life cycle - **Autoecious:** Complete life cycle on single host species - **Microcyclic:** Reduced life cycles (missing some spore stages); more common in cold climates where short growing season selects for perennial telial hosts ## Seasonal Patterns (Temperate Regions) - **Spring:** Aecial stages most prevalent - **Mid-season:** Uredinial states develop (orange pustules on foliage) - **Fall:** Telia develop, usually overwintering in dead host tissue - **Tropical regions:** Teliospores may develop and germinate at any time of year; thick spore walls and pigmentation (common temperate identification features) far less developed ## Symptoms and Identification ### Symptoms - Infected plants may appear stunted, chlorotic, or discolored - Heavy uredinial sporulation turns affected tissue orange - Perennial systemic infections: witches' brooms, cankers, galls - Small infections on vigorously growing plants easily overlooked ### Identification Approach 1. **Host identification** is the starting point — collect healthy foliage and reproductive structures 2. Examine spore morphology under microscope 3. Consult regional indices and taxonomic monographs 4. Note: microcyclic rusts can simulate the habit of macrocyclic parental forms ### Collection Notes - Collect both new and old infected tissues (spore stages of early and advanced infections may differ) - Perennial systemic infections may cause deformities useful for identification - uredinia typically 0.1-0.5 mm diameter on leaves; stem canker rusts may sporulate over areas >1 meter ## Notable Species - **Puccinia graminis:** Wheat stem rust — devastating crop disease - **Hemileia vastatrix:** Coffee rust — threatens coffee production worldwide - **Cronartium ribicola:** White pine blister rust — devastating forest disease - **Endocronartium harknessii:** Western gall rust ## Economic Significance Rusts are unable to grow saprobically — found only where hosts occur. They usually infect healthy, vigorously growing plants. Although rarely fatal, they severely limit growth and fruiting ability. Control requires resistant varieties, fungicides, and removal of alternate hosts. ## Collection and Preservation - Dry infected plant material as herbarium specimens - Collect from multiple hosts if heteroecious - Note host species, plant part affected, and geographic location - SEM may be needed for spore ornamentation details

## Life Cycle Stages in Detail

The five spore stages of macrocyclic rusts represent one of the
most complex life cycles among fungi. Pycniospores (also called
spermatia) are produced in flask-shaped spermagonia and serve
as the gametes. They are transferred between spermagonia by
insects or rain splash to effect plasmogamy. Aeciospores are
dikaryotic spores produced in aecia, often forming on an
alternate host in heteroecious species. These spores are
capable of infecting the telial host and initiating the
uredinial stage. Urediniospores are the repeating asexual spore
stage that drives epidemic spread during [[rust-fungi]] cause billions of dollars in crop losses annually
worldwide. Wheat stem rust (Puccinia graminis f. sp. tritici)
has been a persistent threat to global wheat production for
centuries, with major epidemics causing 10-30% yield losses
in susceptible varieties. The emergence of the Ug99 lineage in
East Africa, first identified in Uganda in 1999, threatens
the vast majority of global wheat cultivars and prompted an
international surveillance effort. Coffee leaf rust (Hemileia
vastatrix) devastated coffee production in Sri Lanka in the
19th century, famously shifting the country's plantation
economy from coffee to tea. Soybean rust (Phakopsora
pachyrhizi) has spread rapidly across the Americas since 2001,
requiring intensive fungicide applications. In forestry, white
pine blister rust (Cronartium ribicola) has killed billions of
five-needle pines across North America since its introduction
in the early 1900s.

## Host-Pathogen Interactions

Rust fungi are obligate biotrophs: they obtain nutrients only
from living host tissue through specialised feeding structures
called haustoria, which penetrate host [[bloomfield-plant-hypersensitive-response-rust-arms-race]] that limits infection. However, rust
populations evolve rapidly through mutation and genetic
recombination, overcoming deployed resistance genes. The gene-
for-gene relationship between rust avirulence genes and plant
R genes has been intensively studied in flax rust (Melampsora
lini) and serves as a model system in plant pathology. Rust
genomes are among the largest known for fungi (up to 2 Gbp
in some Puccinia species), largely due to repetitive elements
and transposable sequences that may facilitate rapid evolution.

## Collection and Identification Methods

Successful rust collection begins with recognising symptoms in
the field: look for orange uredinial pustules on leaf surfaces
and undersides, dark brown or black telial sori, and
hypertrophied or discoloured host tissue. Collect infected
leaves, stems, or branches with both old and new sori intact.
Press and dry specimens as standard herbarium vouchers, keeping
host tissue attached. Always identify and record the host plant
species to species level where possible, as host identity is
the primary taxonomic character for many rust genera. Under the
microscope, mount fresh spores in lactophenol or water and
examine wall thickness, ornamentation (echinulate, verrucose,
reticulate), germ pore number and position, and pedicel
structure. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is invaluable
for resolving fine spore wall details. [[biodiversity-fungal-molecular-identification-dna-barcoding]]
using ITS and 28S rDNA regions is increasingly important for
distinguishing morphologically similar species and confirming
host-range data.
