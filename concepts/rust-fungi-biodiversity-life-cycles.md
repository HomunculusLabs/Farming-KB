# Rust Fungi — Biodiversity, Life Cycles, and Collection

Rust fungi (Urediniomycetes) are among the most economically important plant pathogens, causing diseases on a vast range of host plants. Their complex life cycles and high host specificity make them both fascinating and challenging to study.

## Diversity

- Approximately **168 genera and 7,000 species** currently accepted
- More than half in the genus **Puccinia** (47% of North American records)
- North America: 53 genera, ~925 host-fungus associations
- Tropical America: significantly understudied; 20% of species collected in one 40-hectare neotropical tract were new to science
- About one-third of plant species in that tract had their own rust species
- Expected diversity in tropics exceeds temperate regions
- Hosts: ferns, gymnosperms, monocots, and dicots (no rusts on mosses or liverworts)

## Life Cycle and Spore Stages

A single species may produce up to **five distinct spore-producing structures**:

1. **Spermagonia:** Produce spermatia and receptive hyphae (sexual stage)
2. **Aecia:** Produce aeciospores (often on alternate host in heteroecious species)
3. **Uredinia:** Produce urediniospores (repeating stage; drives epidemics)
4. **Telia:** Produce teliospores (overwintering stage; dikaryotic)
5. **Basidia:** Produce basidiospores (meiotic; infect primary host)

### Heteroecious vs. Autoecious
- **Heteroecious:** Require two unrelated host species (often different families) to complete life cycle
- **Autoecious:** Complete life cycle on single host species
- **Microcyclic:** Reduced life cycles (missing some spore stages); more common in cold climates where short growing season selects for perennial telial hosts

## Seasonal Patterns (Temperate Regions)
- **Spring:** Aecial stages most prevalent
- **Mid-season:** Uredinial states develop (orange pustules on foliage)
- **Fall:** Telia develop, usually overwintering in dead host tissue
- **Tropical regions:** Teliospores may develop and germinate at any time of year; thick spore walls and pigmentation (common temperate identification features) far less developed

## Symptoms and Identification

### Symptoms
- Infected plants may appear stunted, chlorotic, or discolored
- Heavy uredinial sporulation turns affected tissue orange
- Perennial systemic infections: witches' brooms, cankers, galls
- Small infections on vigorously growing plants easily overlooked

### Identification Approach
1. **Host identification** is the starting point — collect healthy foliage and reproductive structures
2. Examine spore morphology under microscope
3. Consult regional indices and taxonomic monographs
4. Note: microcyclic rusts can simulate the habit of macrocyclic parental forms

### Collection Notes
- Collect both new and old infected tissues (spore stages of early and advanced infections may differ)
- Perennial systemic infections may cause deformities useful for identification
- uredinia typically 0.1-0.5 mm diameter on leaves; stem canker rusts may sporulate over areas >1 meter

## Notable Species
- **Puccinia graminis:** Wheat stem rust — devastating crop disease
- **Hemileia vastatrix:** Coffee rust — threatens coffee production worldwide
- **Cronartium ribicola:** White pine blister rust — devastating forest disease
- **Endocronartium harknessii:** Western gall rust

## Economic Significance
Rusts are unable to grow saprobically — found only where hosts occur. They usually infect healthy, vigorously growing plants. Although rarely fatal, they severely limit growth and fruiting ability. Control requires resistant varieties, fungicides, and removal of alternate hosts.

## Collection and Preservation
- Dry infected plant material as herbarium specimens
- Collect from multiple hosts if heteroecious
- Note host species, plant part affected, and geographic location
- SEM may be needed for spore ornamentation details
