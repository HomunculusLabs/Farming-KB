# Mycoremediation of PCBs and Dioxins

Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and dioxins are among the most persistent and toxic environmental contaminants. Their chemical stability — the same property that made them useful industrially — makes them extremely resistant to natural degradation. White-rot fungi are among the few organisms capable of significant PCB and dioxin transformation. See [[mycoremediation-basics]] for the general framework.

## PCBs (Polychlorinated Biphenyls)

### Background
- 209 congeners with varying degrees of chlorination (1-10 chlorine atoms)
- Used from 1929-1979 in transformers, capacitors, hydraulic fluids, paints, and sealants
- Banned by US Congress in 1979; Stockholm Convention (2001) banned globally
- Persist for decades to centuries in the environment
- Bioaccumulate and biomagnify through food chains
- Toxic: endocrine disruption, neurological damage, immune suppression, carcinogenic

### Fungal Degradation Mechanisms
PCBs are degraded by white-rot fungi primarily through ligninolytic enzyme systems:

- **Lignin peroxidase (LiP)**: Oxidizes biphenyl rings via one-electron abstraction, producing aryl cation radicals. This is particularly effective because LiP has a high redox potential (~1.5V) capable of attacking the electron-rich PCB rings.
- **Manganese peroxidase (MnP)**: Generates Mn(III) chelates that act as diffusible oxidants, attacking PCBs in solution
- **Laccase with mediators**: Laccase alone cannot directly attack non-phenolic PCBs, but with mediators like ABTS or HBT, the oxidation range extends to include chlorinated biphenyls

The degradation pathway:
1. Initial oxidative attack on biphenyl ring
2. Chlorine removal (dechlorination) — partially or completely
3. Hydroxylation to chlorinated dihydrodiols
4. Ring cleavage to chlorinated benzoic acids and catechols
5. Further breakdown via beta-oxidation and TCA cycle

### Key Research Findings

- **Trametes versicolor** achieved 95% degradation of chlorinated biphenyls (Zeddel et al., 1993)
- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium** degrades mono- and di-chlorinated biphenyls; mineralization of 14C-PCB confirmed
- **Bjerkandera adusta** degrades highly chlorinated PCBs (5-7 chlorines) via MnP system
- **Pleurotus ostreatus** shows PCB degradation with simultaneous fruiting body production

### Factors Affecting PCB Degradation
- **Degree of chlorination**: Higher chlorination = slower degradation; mono- and di-CBs degrade much faster than penta- and hexa-CBs
- **Congener structure**: Ortho-chlorinated congeners are more resistant due to steric hindrance
- **Bioavailability**: PCBs strongly adsorb to soil organic matter and sediment; bioavailability is often the limiting factor
- **Nutrient limitation**: White-rot fungi produce ligninolytic enzymes under nitrogen limitation
- **Surfactants**: Addition of Tween 80 or other surfactants increases PCB bioavailability

## Dioxins (Polychlorinated Dibenzo-p-Dioxins and Furans)

### Background
- 75 polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxin (PCDD) and 135 polychlorinated dibenzofuran (PCDF) congeners
- **2,3,7,8-TCDD** (Seveso dioxin) is the most toxic synthetic compound known
- Formed as unintended byproducts of combustion, chlorine bleaching, and chemical manufacturing
- Agent Orange contained dioxin contaminants
- Extremely persistent (half-life of 7-12 years in humans; decades in soil)
- Toxic: chloracne, immune suppression, endocrine disruption, cancer (IARC Group 1)

### Fungal Degradation Mechanisms
Dioxin degradation by fungi follows similar pathways to PCB degradation:

- **Lignin peroxidase**: The primary enzyme for dioxin attack due to its high redox potential
- **Manganese peroxidase**: Contributes through Mn(III)-mediated oxidation
- **Cytochrome P450**: Some fungi use intracellular P450 for initial ring oxidation

### Key Research Findings

- **Phanerochaete sordida** degrades nearly 60% of 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (the most toxic dioxin congener)
- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium** shows limited but detectable dioxin transformation
- **Cordyceps sinensis** demonstrates a novel dioxin degradation pathway producing catechol intermediates — unique among fungi
- **Trametes versicolor** degrades chlorinated dibenzofurans via laccase-mediator systems
- Degradation is slow (weeks to months) and rarely achieves complete mineralization

### Challenges
- Dioxins are extremely hydrophobic (water solubility: ~0.00002 mg/L for TCDD)
- Strong sorption to soil organic matter limits bioavailability
- The electron-withdrawing chlorine atoms make the rings resistant to oxidative attack
- Most studies show transformation (partial degradation) rather than complete mineralization

## Pentachlorophenol (PCP)

PCP is related to dioxins and PCBs and is one of the most studied chlorinated compounds for fungal degradation:

- Widely used as wood preservative until restricted in the 1980s
- Contaminates soil and groundwater at wood treatment facilities
- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium**: Model organism for PCP degradation; mineralizes PCP to CO2 under nitrogen limitation
- **Trametes versicolor**: Degrades PCP via laccase; forms pentachloroanisol as intermediate
- **Phanerochaete sordida**: Degrades PCP more efficiently than P. chrysosporium in some conditions

## Chlorophenol Degradation

Chlorophenols are intermediates in PCB/dioxin degradation and also industrial pollutants in their own right:

- Found in pulp and paper mill effluents, pesticide manufacturing
- White-rot fungi are the most effective organisms for chlorophenol degradation
- **Trametes versicolor**: One of the most effective; removes >99% of 2,4,6-trichlorophenol
- **Pleurotus sajor-caju**: Degrades pentachlorophenol and lower chlorophenols
- Degradation involves dechlorination (removal of chlorine atoms) followed by ring cleavage
- Laccase is the primary enzyme; LiP and MnP also contribute

## Comparative Performance

| Contaminant | Most Effective Fungi | Degradation Rate | Notes |
|-------------|---------------------|-----------------|-------|
| Mono/di-CBs | T. versicolor, P. chrysosporium | 80-95% in weeks | Lower chlorination degrades faster |
| Penta/hexa-CBs | B. adusta, T. versicolor | 20-50% in weeks | Requires longer treatment |
| 2,3,7,8-TCDD | P. sordida, C. sinensis | 40-60% in months | Slow; transformation more than mineralization |
| PCP | P. chrysosporium, T. versicolor | 80-99% in weeks | Well-studied; relatively fast |
| Chlorophenols | T. versicolor, P. sajor-caju | >99% in days | Fastest degradation of all chlorinated compounds |

## Field Applications

- **Hudson River**: PCB-contaminated sediments — fungal bioaugmentation studied as supplement to dredging
- **Wood treatment sites**: PCP-contaminated soil — white-rot fungal composting in biopiles
- **Agent Orange sites**: Vietnam — fungal remediation of dioxin-contaminated soil researched but not widely deployed
- **Creosote sites**: Combined PCB/PAH contamination — fungal treatment effective for both fractions

## Advantages and Limitations

### Advantages
- White-rot fungi can attack highly chlorinated compounds that resist bacterial degradation
- Extracellular enzymes don't require pollutant uptake into cells
- Can be deployed on-site with simple inoculation methods
- Low cost compared to incineration (the standard treatment for PCBs/dioxins)

### Limitations
- Complete mineralization is rare; most studies show partial transformation
- Bioavailability is often the limiting factor — contaminants must be accessible to enzymes
- Degradation rates are slow compared to chemical treatments
- Scale-up from lab to field is challenging
- Regulatory frameworks for fungal remediation of PCBs/dioxins are still developing

## Future Directions

- **Genetically engineered fungi**: Enhanced expression of ligninolytic enzymes for improved degradation
- **Enzyme immobilization**: Purified LiP/MnP/laccase immobilized on supports for bioreactor applications
- **Mediator optimization**: Improved mediator systems for laccase to extend substrate range
- **Combined treatment**: Fungal degradation followed by chemical or thermal polishing
- **Nanoparticle enhancement**: Carbon nanotubes or metal nanoparticles to increase enzyme stability and activity
