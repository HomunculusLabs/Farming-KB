Source: Paul Stamets, *The Mushroom Cultivator* (1983), Chapter IX

Mushroom formation follows four distinct stages, each requiring specific environmental conditions. Understanding and controlling these stages is the key to maximising yields.

## Stage I: Initiation (Casing Run)

After casing, environmental conditions remain the same as during spawn running:
- Substrate temperature maintained in optimum range for mycelial growth
- RH: 90-100%
- Fresh air kept to minimum (only to offset overheating)
- CO2 build-up beneficial to mycelial growth

Within 48 hours of casing, substrate temperatures surge upward due to hampered diffusion of metabolic gases. This surge is a positive sign of mycelial vitality.

## Stage II: Temperature Drop and CO2 Reduction

The change from vegetative to generative growth is triggered by:
1. **Lowering substrate and air temperature** to fruiting range
2. **Introducing fresh air** to lower CO2 concentration

### Temperature Drop
- Air temperature is lowered several degrees below the current substrate temperature
- The substrate temperature gradually falls over 48 hours
- This change signals the mycelium to fruit

### CO2 Reduction
- Agaricus brunnescens: does not pin properly above 2,000 ppm CO2
- The inhibitory effect of CO2 on mushroom formation gives growers high control over pinning
- Not until CO2 is removed will pinheads form
- If CO2 remains high: mycelium totally covers the casing surface (condition called "overlay")

### Overlay: Causes and Correction
- Overlay makes casing impervious to water and produces few pinheads
- Causes: casing too dry, humidity too low, air temperature too high, or CO2 too high
- Counteracted by "patching" (scratching the surface to break the mat)
- The cause must be diagnosed and corrected
- Few flushes from overlain casing will be as great as from properly managed casing

## Stage III: Primordia Formation (Knotting)

Once temperatures lowered and CO2 reduced, primordia begin to form. Maintain:
1. Constant fresh air supply (CO2 below 1,000 ppm)
2. Constant temperature within fruiting range
3. RH: 95%
4. 12-hour on/off light cycle

### The Pinning Process
- Temperature drop + high humidity + CO2 reduction = environment conducive to pinhead formation
- Parameters held constant until pins are "set"
- Any abrupt changes in temperature or humidity are harmful
- Pinhead initials form in humid valleys of the casing layer as small knots of mycelium
- Within 5 days: knots enlarge into small mounds/buttons, soon differentiating into mushrooms

### CO2 Management During Pinning
- Slowed mycelial growth = greatly reduced CO2 evolution
- Fresh air supply moderated to minimum maintaining 1,000 ppm
- Oversupply of fresh air leads to excessive evaporation and drying
- RH must never fall below 90%
- If dry air is a problem: light misting 2-5 times daily
- Some growers "knock down" mycelium with forceful watering on first day of initiation
- Once pinning begins, forceful watering kills developing pins

## Stage IV: Pinhead Development

After pinheads reach pea size (3-5mm), further development depends on:
1. **Air temperature**: held constant within fruiting range
2. **RH**: lowered to 85-92% (increases evaporation rate, essential for maturation)
3. **Fresh air**: CO2 below 2,000 ppm (1,500-2,000 ppm range desirable)
4. **Light**: 12-hour on/off cycle

### The Role of Evaporation
- If humidity remains too high: pinhead development retarded
- Raise air temperature 1-2F or increase air movement to lower humidity
- No stagnant air pockets allowed (breeding grounds for pathogens)

### Temperature and Growth Rate (Lambert, 1938)
For Agaricus brunnescens:
- 2mm pinhead to mature mushroom: 22 days at 50F, 10 days at 60F, 6 days at 70F
- Faster development at higher temperatures but overall yields diminish
- Optimum cropping temperature: 62-64F

## Relationship Between Primordia Formation and Yield

### Critical Relationships
1. During primordia formation, pinheads for first AND second flush are generated (second flush primordia present as thickened mycelial knots)
2. 60-75% of total yield normally from first two flushes -- the few days of pinhead initiation are the most critical
3. More pins set = higher yield (if sufficient nutrients), but smaller individual mushrooms
4. Fewer pins = larger mushrooms but lower total yields
5. The substrate only supports a certain number of primordia per flush; excess pins "abort"
6. Early-forming pins delay growth of neighboring primordia -- remove "volunteers" before they develop

### Volunteer Pin Removal
- Early-forming pinheads along borders or in shallow areas should be removed
- Their removal advantages remaining primordia constituting the first flush
- Be careful not to disturb the casing during removal

## Influence of Light on Pinhead Initiation

### Photosensitive Species
Species requiring light for primordia formation are "photosensitive." Not all mushrooms require light (e.g., Agaricus brunnescens), but certain spectra are stimulatory for many species.

### Psilocybe cubensis Light Research (Badham, 1979)
- More pinheads initiated by blue and UV light
- Distinct peaks at 370, 440, and 460 nanometers
- As little as 0.5 millisecond per day at these wavelengths caused primordia to form
- Red, infrared, and green light (wavelengths >510nm) were ineffective

### Cultivation Implications
- Incubate photosensitive species in total darkness
- Expose to light only after mycelium shows through casing layer
- Red light recommended for checking cultures without premature pinning
- Pleurotus ostreatus: most responsive to 2,000 lux/hour for 12 hours/day (grow-lux fluorescent)

## Common Abnormalities

- **Long stems, small caps**: high CO2 (Agaricus, Pleurotus) or low light (Pleurotus)
- **Scaling**: dry air on Psilocybe cubensis caps
- **Rosecomb**: contact with chemicals, especially petroleum-based
- **Border breaks**: mushrooms forming between casing and glass in jars (high humidity pockets, premature light stimulation)
