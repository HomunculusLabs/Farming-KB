# Extending Yields and Cultural Impediments

The concentration of yields into one short period is a fiscal strategy, not an environmental or subsistence one. It has resulted in a "feast and famine" regime in both markets and fields, with consequent high storage costs and periodic scarcity. Permaculture aims to disperse food yield over time so that many products are available at any season.

## Strategies for Extending Yields Over Time

### Temporal Diversity

- **Selection of early, mid, and late season varieties** to spread harvest across the growing period
- **Planting the same variety in early or late-ripening situations** using microclimate, aspect, or elevation differences to stagger maturity
- **Selection of long-season varieties** that yield over extended periods rather than in a single flush

### Structural Diversity

- **General increase in system diversity** so that leaf, fruit, seed, and root are all product yields simultaneously
- **Self-storing species** such as tubers, hard seeds, fuelwood, and rhizomes that can be cropped on demand rather than at peak harvest
- **Regional trade** between communities, or utilization of land at different altitudes and latitudes to extend seasonal availability

### Preservation Strategies

Food can be stored for days, weeks, or years through a series of preservation techniques:

- Preserving (canning, fermentation)
- Drying (solar, dehydrator, air)
- Pitting (underground storage in cool, stable conditions)
- Cool storage (root cellars, evaporative cooling)

## Yields and Storage

How yields endure is as important as how they are produced. There are unlimited opportunities to use durable yields in terms of season or lifetime.

Water not exposed to evaporation and pollution, or containing natural cleansing organisms, will keep indefinitely. Shelters may outlast the forests that build them, or can be constructed from living or durable materials such as ivy, concrete, or stone. Energy alone — like the food which is part of energy — is difficult to store. Batteries leak or decay, heat escapes, and insulation breaks down. Only living things, like forests, increase their energy store over time.

Because of seasonal and diurnal cycles, storage strategies demand close attention. Very little famine would occur if grains, fish, and fruit available in good times were stored for lean times. Mollison advocated that people should "mulch their recipe books" — which often specify out-of-season or non-garden foods — and replace them with books emphasizing low-energy preservation methods or how to live easily from the garden in season.

## The Principle of Enough in Practice

The principle of "enough" governs both the production and consumption sides of yield. Oversupply of resources — whether fertilizer, water, food, or information — is a form of chronic pollution that throws systems into disorder. Just as undersupply causes famine, oversupply causes obesity, waste, and systemic pathology. Ethical resource management requires balancing both extremes.

The critical yield strategy is ultimately about governing appetites — both personal and societal. Yield has no known limits because we cannot know all ways to conserve, store, and save energy, nor can we fail to improve any system we build and observe.

## Cultural Impediments to Yield

### Zoophobia in Affluent Societies

Mollison identified a significant barrier to yield in affluent societies: an almost total fear of anything that moves (**zoophobia**). This condition, most developed in affluent North American culture, manifests as resistance to keeping rabbits, quail, pigs, poultry, or cows in city farms or urban gardens. These animals are commonplace city farm animals in England, ordinary village animals in Asia, and the edible guinea pig lives comfortably in South American homes — but in the United States, they are effectively banned from urban environments.

### The Consequence of Excluding Useful Animals

When useful animals are abolished from cities, the ecological niche they occupied does not remain empty. Instead, it is filled by less desirable species:

- **Pigeons** forage the streets
- **Thousands of gulls** defecate in New York City reservoirs, fresh from garbage piles
- **Gigantic garbage bins** are tipped over by large, flea-ridden dogs in Los Angeles
- **Rats half the size of dogs**, also flea-ridden, tunnel under bus stops in Washington, D.C. by the millions — not far from the White House

The exclusion of productive animals from human settlements creates pest problems that are far worse than the imagined problems of keeping domestic animals. This represents a profound failure of understanding about ecological systems and the role of animals in nutrient cycling, pest control, and food production.

### Broader Cultural Barriers

Cultural barriers to yield extend beyond zoophobia:

- **Aesthetic restrictions** on what can be grown in gardens or parks
- **Regulatory impediments** to processing, preserving, or selling home-grown produce
- **Social taboos** against consuming certain edible species
- **Economic structures** that favor centralized production and distribution over local self-reliance
- **The "feast and famine" marketing regime** that concentrates rather than disperses yield

The perverse effect of these cultural impediments is that societies systematically reject available yields while simultaneously creating the conditions for pest outbreaks, waste, and resource degradation. People themselves become the main impediment to using their potential yields.

### Overcoming Cultural Barriers

Overcoming these impediments requires:

- **Education** about the productive roles of animals and diverse plants in human settlements
- **Demonstration** of integrated urban food systems that are clean, productive, and aesthetically pleasing
- **Regulatory reform** to allow appropriate animal keeping and food processing in urban areas
- **Cultural shift** toward accepting seasonal eating and diverse food sources
- **Financial recycling** within communities to support local production systems

The path to abundant, sustainable yield is blocked not by physical limitations but by attitudes, regulations, and cultural assumptions that can be changed through design, education, and example.
