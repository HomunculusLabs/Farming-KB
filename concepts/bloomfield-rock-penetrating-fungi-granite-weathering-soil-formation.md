# Rock-Penetrating Fungi, Granite Weathering, and the Origins of Soil

## Fungi That Eat Stone

Among the most astonishing feats of fungal biology is the ability of certain species to grow inside solid granite bedrock. These rock-penetrating fungi, primarily basidiomycetes that also form [[fungal-community-profiling-rock-mineral-surfaces-gadd]], displacing metal cations

### Geological Scale
Over millions of years, fungal-mediated weathering has influenced the global carbon cycle, the composition of river water, and the rate at which atmospheric CO₂ is sequestered through the formation of carbonate minerals.

### The Calcium Cycle Connection
The calcium released by fungal rock weathering enters the soil solution and eventually the groundwater, ultimately reaching rivers and oceans. There, it combines with bicarbonate to form calcium carbonate (limestone), sequestering carbon in sedimentary rock. This long-term carbon sequestration pathway, driven initially by fungal weathering of silicate rocks, has operated for hundreds of millions of years and represents one of the major geological mechanisms for removing CO₂ from the atmosphere.

## Experiments Measuring Fungal Force

Money describes how sophisticated instrumentation has allowed mycologists to measure the tiny forces exerted by individual hyphae. When the wall of a hypha yields, some of the internal turgor pressure is applied against the material at the apex, and the fungus pushes forward with a force of a few **micronewtons**—millionths of a Newton. One micronewton is produced when a mass of one ten-thousandth of a gram, equivalent to an eyelash, presses down under gravity.

This seemingly negligible force becomes powerful when multiplied across the millions of hyphal tips that comprise a single mycelial network. The collective force generated by a large mycelium can contribute to the physical disaggregation of rock, working in concert with chemical dissolution to break down even the toughest mineral matrices.

## Boletes and the Quarrying Activity

Money specifically identifies **boletes** (members of the Boletaceae, a family of ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes) as being responsible for much of the quarrying activity in forest soils. These are the pored mushrooms (as opposed to gilled mushrooms) that form associations with oaks, pines, spruces, and other forest trees. Their mycelia spread through the soil and into the underlying rock, mining minerals that their tree partners could never access alone.

## The Wisconsin Fossil Spores

The Wisconsin fossil spores mentioned by Money deserve special attention. These ancient spores, preserved in rocks far older than the earliest land plant fossils, provide direct evidence that mycorrhizal fungi—or their immediate ancestors—existed long before the plants they would eventually partner with. This raises the intriguing possibility that fungi were the first organisms to colonize land in a significant way, preparing the ground (literally) for the later arrival of plants.

The large size of these fossil spores is significant because large spores are characteristic of mycorrhizal fungi, which must store enough reserves to sustain the germinating hypha until it can establish a connection with a host plant. This adaptation suggests that the mycorrhizal lifestyle was already established by the time these spores were formed, implying an even earlier origin for the fungal-plant partnership.

## The Speed of Biological Weathering

Biological weathering through fungal activity is estimated to proceed at rates 10 to 1,000 times faster than purely abiotic (chemical) weathering of the same minerals. This acceleration is due to the combination of acid secretion, physical disruption, and the continuous renewal of the weathering front as hyphae extend deeper into the rock. In essence, fungi don't just speed up weathering—they fundamentally change its character, transforming what would be a slow, uniform dissolution into a focused, efficient mining operation.

The next time you walk through a forest and see boletes fruiting at the base of a tree, consider that beneath your feet, their mycelium is actively eating the stone—dissolving the bedrock that supports the entire ecosystem and slowly, imperceptibly, transforming solid granite into living soil.
