# Mollison: Keyline [[water-conservation]] System

Source: Bill Mollison, [[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 7, Section 9)

## Overview of [[keyline-design]]

Mollison identifies P.A. Yeomans' Water for Every Farm / The Keyline Plan (1981) as the pioneering modern text on landscape design for water conservation and gravity-fed irrigation. See also [[keyline-design]] for more detailed keyline design principles.

Originally written in 1954, it was the first book in English on total water design for foothill farms, encompassing access, tree belts, soil creation, low tillage, and creative water storage.

The Keyline system provides drought-proofing for farms with very low maintenance and operating costs.

## Two Basic Strategies

There are two fundamental strategies of water conservation in run-off areas.

The first is diversion of surface water to impoundments (dams, tanks) for later use.

The second is storage of water in soils.

Both result in recharge of groundwater.

## Main Productive Earthwork Features

The primary productive earthwork features in the Keyline and permaculture approach include dams and tanks for storage, swales as absorption beds, diversion systems or channels, and irrigation layouts especially for flood or sheet irrigation.

## The Keypoint Concept

The keypoint is located in the valleys of secondary streams in humid landscapes, at the highest practical construction point in the hill profile.

This is usually where the stream profile changes from convex to concave, which can be judged by eye.

A descending contour then picks up all other keypoints on the main valley.

Keypoint dams are primarily used to store irrigation water, and a second or third series can be run below the primary series.

The spillway of the last dam in a series can be returned upstream to meet the main valley, effectively spilling surplus to streams.

## Keyline Principles

The keyline itself links keypoints in primary valleys across a landscape.

The system patterns tree planting, soil treatment, and fencing alignment along these keyline contours to maximise water distribution across the landscape.

Yeomans (personal communication, 1978) stated that if 10-15% of a normal humid lowland or foothill landscape were fitted with small earth storages, floods, drought, and fire threat could be eliminated.

## Dam Types and Placement

Mollison describes several common dam types in extensive landscapes:

### Saddle Dams

The highest available storages on saddles or hollows in the skyline profile of hills.

Uses include wildlife, stock watering, and high storage.

They can be fully excavated below grade or walled on either or both sides.

### Ridgepoint Dams

Built on the sub-plateaus of flattened ridges, usually on a descending ridgeline and below saddle dams.

Their shape is typically that of a horse's hoof.

### Contour Dams

Walls built on contour wherever slope is 8% or less.

Uses include irrigation, aquaculture, and flood-flow basins in semi-arid areas.

### Barrier Dams

Constructed across flowing or intermittent stream beds, requiring ample spillways, fish ladders, and careful construction.

Made most frequently as energy systems but also for irrigation if constructed well above main valley floors.

### Turkey's Nest Dams

Above-grade tanks where water must be pumped in, often by windmill or solar pump.

Common in flatlands as stock water tanks or for low-head irrigation.

### Check Dams

Not intended to create water storages but to slow water flow and create silt fields and water-spreading across eroding valleys.

In drylands, permeable gabion barriers of rock-filled mesh baskets serve this purpose.

## [[dam-construction]] Principles

Earth dams with retaining walls 6 metres high or less, having a large oversize stable spillway, are no threat to life or property if well-made.

They need not displace populations, stop stream flow, create health problems, fill with silt, or block fish migrations.

Dams made anywhere but as barriers on streams effectively add to stream flow in the long term.

The key to efficiency is the length of the dam wall compared with the length of water dammed.

A short dam at a constricted site where the valley behind is flattish is very cost-effective.

## Sealing Leaky Dams

Mollison details several methods: gley (anaerobic bacterial slime from green plant material), bentonite (volcanic ash clay powder), explosives for shattering compacted subsoil, clay cores, and impermeable membranes.

## Slope Considerations

Almost every type of dam is cost-effective if located on an area of 5% or less slope.

Essential dams on higher slopes can be built of concrete, rock-walled, or excavated when water for a house or settlement is the limiting factor.

## Open Water Storage in Arid Areas

Open-water storages are most appropriate in humid climates where rainfall exceeds evaporation potential.

In arid to subhumid areas, open water storages concentrate dissolved salts through evaporation.

Seepage from earth dams in arid areas can create areas of salted or collapsed soils downhill.

## Integration with Landscape

Small dams are jewels in the landscape when fenced and planted to 30-60 metres of forest and fruit surround.

They provide biologically clean water, and if topsoil is returned, lime used, and edges planted, mud will decrease and eventually clear.
