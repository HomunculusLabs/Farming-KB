# Panaeolina foenisecii: Taxonomic Controversy and Psilocybin Content Debate

## Overview

Panaeolina foenisecii (Pers.: Fr.) Maire, commonly known as the haymaker's
mushroom or the brown mottlegill, is one of the most contentious species in the
neurotropic fungi literature. This small, brown dung-inhabiting mushroom has been
the subject of contradictory chemical analyses spanning several decades, with
some researchers reporting the presence of psilocybin and others finding no
trace of indole alkaloids. The species remains a textbook case of the
difficulties inherent in mycochemical research and the perils of
misidentification in the study of psychoactive fungi.

## Description and Distribution

Panaeolina foenisecii is a very common fungus found in the prairies and
grasslands of many parts of the world. It is a cosmopolitan species, yet its
true distribution is poorly documented. In Japan, where this species is very
common, it was not recorded by Imazeki and Hongo (1983, 1987) or Imazeki et al.
(1988), and was only reported by Hongo (1986). Surprisingly, there are no
reports of this species from [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]] and the Caribbean region, despite
its abundance elsewhere. The fungus typically fruits on grass, dung-enriched
soil, and in lawns and pastures during warm, wet conditions.

## Conflicting Chemical Analyses

### Studies Finding No Psilocybin

Several prominent researchers have failed to detect psilocybin or psilocin in
Panaeolina foenisecii:

- **Mantle and Waight (1969)**: No psilocybin or psilocin detected.
- **Ott and Guzmán (1976)**: No indole alkaloids found.
- **Beug and Bigwood (1982)**: No psychoactive compounds detected.
- **Stijve (1987)**: No psilocybin found.
- **Stijve et al. (1984)**: Negative results for psilocybin.
- **Stijve and Meijer (1993)**: Failed to find psilocybin and other
  psilocybian compounds, also testing Panaeolina specimens alongside
  Rickenella straminea with negative results.

### Studies Reporting Psilocybin

Contradicting the above, multiple studies have reported the presence of
psilocybin in P. foenisecii:

- **Robbers et al. (1969)**: Reported detection of psilocybin.
- **Ola'h (1969)**: Found psilocybin in specimens studied.
- **Fiussello and Ceruti-Scurti (1972)**: Reported psilocybin content.
- **Pollock (1976)**: Detected psilocybin in analyzed material.
- **Bresinsky and Besl (1990)**: Listed the species as containing psilocybin.

## Likely Explanation: Toxic Rather Than Neurotropic

Allen and Merlin (1992c) discussed doubts regarding the psychoactive properties
of P. foenisecii and concluded that the species is more likely toxic than
neurotropic. This assessment aligns with the broader pattern observed among
Panaeoloideous fungi, where the majority of species are considered toxic
rather than hallucinogenic. The gastrointestinal distress sometimes
associated with ingestion of P. foenisecii is more consistent with toxicity
from other compounds than with the effects of psilocybin.

## Role of Misidentification

The persistent confusion surrounding P. foenisecii likely stems from several
factors:

1. **Field misidentification**: P. foenisecii frequently grows alongside
   [[copelandia-cyanescens]] and other genuinely psychoactive Panaeolus species
   in tropical dung habitats. The white to pale brown fruiting bodies can
   appear similar to untrained observers.

2. **Mixed collections**: Chemical studies sometimes inadvertently analyzed
   mixtures of species rather than pure collections of P. foenisecii. Guzmán
   personally found a mixture of Panaeolus spp. and [[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]],
   together with P. coprophila, all identified as P. coprophila in the ENCB
   Herbarium at Mexico City, in material used by Leslie and Repke to
   isolate psilocybin.

3. **Lack of voucher specimens**: Some early chemical studies did not
   preserve voucher specimens, making retroactive verification of
   identification impossible.

4. **Specimen age and handling**: The age of studied specimens significantly
   affects alkaloid content, as demonstrated by Repke et al. (1977a) who
   showed variations in baeocystin presence according to specimen age in
   [[psilocybe-baeocystis]] and P. cyanescens.

## Broader Pattern in Panaeoloideous Fungi

The controversy surrounding P. foenisecii is representative of a broader
pattern in the Panaeoloideous fungi. The taxonomic boundaries between
Panaeolus, Copelandia, and Panaeolina have been debated extensively. Some
species formerly placed in Panaeolus have been transferred to Copelandia on
the basis of their bluing reaction and confirmed psilocybin content. The
absence of a bluing reaction in P. foenisecii further argues against its
classification as a genuinely neurotropic species.

## Hongo's Japanese Panaeolina Species

Hongo described several species of Panaeolina from Japan, including
endemic taxa. These species are considered endemic to their regions and
contribute to the taxonomic complexity of the genus. The Japanese species
described by Natarajan and Raman from India add further dimension to the
[[psilocybe-genus-global-distribution-and-biogeography]] taxonomic understanding of Panaeolina.

## Conclusion

The weight of evidence, particularly from the more rigorous modern analyses
using improved chromatographic techniques, suggests that Panaeolina foenisecii
does not contain psilocybin or psilocin in pharmacologically significant
quantities. The positive reports from earlier studies most likely resulted
from contaminated or misidentified specimens. The species should be regarded
as a common, widespread, and potentially toxic grassland fungus rather than
a neurotropic mushroom. Its inclusion in many historical lists of
psychoactive fungi serves as a cautionary tale about the importance of
meticulous taxonomic work in mycochemical research.
