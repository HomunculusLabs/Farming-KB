## Overview

Actively aerated compost tea (AACT) is the third tool in the soil food web gardener's toolkit, alongside compost and mulch. It puts concentrated microbiology directly into soils and onto leaf surfaces, overcoming the key limitations of compost and mulch: slow delivery to the rhizosphere and inability to adhere to leaf surfaces. AACT is a fast, inexpensive, and effective way to manage soil food web microbiology in gardens and landscapes.

## How AACT Differs from Other Preparations

It is critical to distinguish AACT from several related but inferior preparations:

- **Compost leachate**: Liquid that drains from compost under pressure or gravity. Contains some color and nutrients but imparts little microbial life because bacteria and fungi are attached to organic matter with biological glues and do not simply wash off.
- **Compost extract**: Compost soaked in water for weeks, producing an anaerobic soup. Loss of aerobic microbial diversity and risk of anaerobic pathogens and alcohols make it unsafe and inadvisable.
- **Manure tea**: Manure suspended in water for weeks. Also anaerobic, and virtually assures the presence of *E. coli* and other pathogens.

AACT, by contrast, is a fully aerobic mixture that concentrates beneficial, aerobic microbes.

## The Science of Microbial Extraction

Modern compost teas are aerobic concentrates. When properly brewed, the bacterial population grows from approximately 1 billion per teaspoon of compost to 4 billion per teaspoon of tea. The process involves adding compost plus microbial nutrients to dechlorinated water and aerating the mix for one to two days.

Extracting microbes from compost requires significant energy. The bacteria are held in place by biological slime — analogous to dental plaque on teeth, which requires daily mechanical removal. Fungal hyphae grow not only on the compost surface but inside its nooks and crannies. The brewer's action must be strong enough to tease microbes out but not so violent that it kills them once liberated.

### Minimum Organism Standards

Per milliliter of properly made compost tea:

- Active bacteria: 10–150 µg
- Total bacteria: 150–300 µg
- Active fungi: 5–20 µg
- Flagellates and beneficial nematodes should also be present

## Compost Tea Brewers

### Commercial Brewers

Commercial brewers range from small 5–20 gallon systems (sufficient for several acres) to units producing 1,000+ gallons per brew. When purchasing, insist on seeing biological test results demonstrating the machine can extract viable fungal populations, not just bacteria.

### DIY Brewer Construction

A simple, effective brewer can be built from readily available components:

1. **Container**: A standard 5-gallon plastic bucket
2. **Aeration source**: Aquarium air pump (largest affordable) with dual air outlets preferred; use at least two single-outlet pumps if a double is unavailable
3. **Bubble diffuser**: Air stone or a 2-foot section of ¼-inch drip irrigation soaker hose coiled and taped to the bucket bottom (soaker hose provides better bubble coverage)
4. **Tubing**: ~4 feet of plastic tubing connecting pump to diffuser

Bubbles smaller than 1 millimeter can physically cut up microbes, so standard aquarium air stones work well. Use a rubber grommet to thread tubing through the bucket wall for a clean setup.

### Compost Sock

Placing compost in a porous bag eliminates the need to strain tea before spraying. A pair of queen-size pantyhose works well — stretch the waist over the bucket rim and drop compost into the legs, or tie off the legs and fill the resulting bag. If using tea only as a soil drench, straining is unnecessary.

## Siting and Environmental Considerations

- **Temperature**: Room temperature is ideal. Cold slows microbial activity; excessive heat cooks microbes or forces dormancy. An aquarium heater with automatic thermostat can maintain warmth; ice may be needed in hot conditions.
- **Light**: Brew away from direct sunlight — UV rays kill microbes.
- **Foaming**: Compost proteins (mainly worm bodies) cause foaming, so site the brewer where spillage is tolerable.

## Cleaning and Maintenance

Bacterial bioslime accumulates in unexpected locations — bucket walls, bottom crevices, inside tubing and fittings. It must be cleaned immediately after use:

- While still wet, wipe or flush with hose water
- Dried slime can be removed with 3% hydrogen peroxide or 5% baking soda solution
- Disassemble hoses and fittings for thorough cleaning
- Keep air stones and tubing clean to prevent clogging

## The Phyllosphere Connection

Plants generate exudates not only from roots (the rhizosphere) but also from leaves. These leaf exudates attract bacteria and fungi to the **phyllosphere** — the area immediately surrounding leaf surfaces. As in the rhizosphere, these microbes compete with pathogens for space and food, and in some cases actively protect leaf surfaces from attack. Neither compost nor mulch can deliver microbiology to the phyllosphere, but AACT can be sprayed directly onto foliage, making it uniquely valuable for aboveground plant protection.

## Advantages Over Compost and Mulch

AACT addresses practical limitations of the other two soil food web tools:

- **Reduced physical labor**: No turning compost piles or carting heavy materials
- **Scalability**: A small 5-gallon brew can treat several acres
- **Speed**: Microbes are delivered directly where needed — rhizosphere and phyllosphere
- **Leaf coverage**: Tea adheres to leaf surfaces, which compost and mulch cannot reach
- **Concentration**: Microbial populations are 4× higher than in the source compost

## Nutrient Cycling in Mulch Systems

Even with optimal bacteria and fungi in mulch, nutrient cycling requires protozoa and nematodes. Protozoa can be cultivated by soaking fresh grass clippings, alfalfa, hay, or straw in dechlorinated water for 3–4 days with aquarium aeration. The resulting protozoa soup, visible with a hand lens, can be poured onto mulches to dramatically increase nutrient cycling capacity.

## Summary

Actively aerated compost tea is the most versatile and efficient tool for soil food web management. It overcomes the physical and biological limitations of compost and mulch by delivering concentrated, living microbiology directly to both soil and leaf surfaces. The key requirements are: quality compost as the microbial source, dechlorinated water, continuous aeration to maintain aerobic conditions, appropriate supplemental nutrients, and careful handling during application to protect living organisms.
