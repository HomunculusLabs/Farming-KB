# Organic Soil Management

Comprehensive approach to building and maintaining healthy soil without synthetic chemicals. Based on Geoff Hamilton's organic gardening methods. See [[geoff-hamilton-organic-techniques]] for the full organic system and [[no-till-gardening-method]] for minimum-disturbance approaches.

## Understanding Soil

Soil is not just dirt -- it is a living ecosystem. A healthy soil contains billions of microorganisms per teaspoon, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and arthropods. These organisms break down organic matter, fix nitrogen, make minerals available to plants, and create soil structure through their activity.

### Soil Types

| Type | Characteristics | Management |
|------|----------------|------------|
| Clay | Heavy, holds water, slow to warm, prone to compaction | Add organic matter, avoid working when wet, raise beds |
| Sandy | Free-draining, quick to warm, poor nutrient retention | Add organic matter frequently, mulch heavily, water regularly |
| Loam | Ideal balance of sand, silt, clay -- rare in gardens | Maintain with regular compost additions |
| Chalky | Alkaline, free-draining, nutrient-poor | Add organic matter, use acid-loving plants in containers |
| Peaty | Dark, moisture-retentive, acidic | Lime for alkaline-loving crops, add coarse material for drainage |

## The Four Phases of Soil Management

Hamilton identifies four continuous phases that form the cycle of organic soil care:

### Phase 1: Digging and Preparation

- Double-dig only once at establishment for deep beds (see [[geoff-hamilton-organic-techniques]])
- Thereafter, use minimum cultivation -- surface-forking only
- Never walk on beds; use permanent paths to prevent compaction
- Work soil only when conditions allow -- not too wet (creates clods) or too dry (creates dust)
- Cover bare soil immediately after preparation to prevent erosion and nutrient loss

### Phase 2: Organic Matter Addition

Sources of organic matter ranked by availability and value:

1. **Garden compost** -- the backbone. Mix green (nitrogen-rich) and brown (carbon-rich) materials. See [[composting-systems]]
2. **Well-rotted manure** -- farmyard manure aged 6+ months. Avoid fresh manure (can scorch plants, contains weed seeds, may harbor pathogens)
3. **Leaf mould** -- decomposed autumn leaves, excellent soil conditioner. Takes 1-2 years. Oak and beech leaves are slower; smaller leaves decompose faster
4. **Green manures** -- living plants grown specifically to be dug in. See [[cover-cropping-guide]]
5. **Worm-worked compost and vermicompost** -- exceptionally rich in plant-available nutrients and beneficial microorganisms. Worm populations indicate soil health (50+ per sq ft in healthy beds)
6. **Mushroom compost** -- spent substrate from mushroom cultivation. Good soil conditioner but can be alkaline
7. **Seaweed** -- rich in trace minerals. Rinse salt from beach-harvested seaweed before use

### Phase 3: Mulching

- Apply 2-4 inch layer of organic mulch after planting
- Materials: compost, well-rotted manure, leaf mould, straw, wood chips (for paths), shredded bark
- Benefits: moisture retention, weed suppression, soil temperature regulation, gradual nutrient release, habitat for soil organisms
- Keep mulch away from direct contact with plant stems to prevent rot
- Replenish annually as mulch breaks down

### Phase 4: Crop Rotation

- Never grow the same family of crops in the same spot two years running
- Minimum 3-year rotation; 4-year is better for brassicas (club root prevention)
- Standard rotation: Legumes (fix nitrogen) > Brassicas (heavy feeders) > Roots (light feeders) > Onions/Potatoes
- See [[companion-planting-reference]] for interplanting strategies

## Alternative Soil Conditioners

- **Rock dust** -- adds trace minerals, slowly releases nutrients. Apply every 2-3 years
- **Lime** -- raises pH. Apply in autumn/winter. Use dolomitic lime if magnesium is also needed. Never lime at the same time as manure (causes ammonia loss)
- **Bonemeal** -- slow-release phosphorus source. Good for root crops and flowering plants
- **Wood ash** -- source of potash and trace minerals. Use sparingly; raises pH
- **Comfrey tea** -- potassium-rich liquid feed. See [[geoff-hamilton-organic-techniques]] for preparation

## Soil Testing

- Test pH annually in autumn -- most vegetables prefer 6.0-7.0
- Test nutrient levels every 2-3 years using a laboratory soil test
- Home pH kits are adequate for routine monitoring
- Adjust pH based on test results -- lime to raise, sulfur/peat to lower
