# Entomopathogenic Fungi: Biocontrol and Mycopesticides

## Overview

Entomopathogenic fungi are fungi that infect and kill insects, serving as natural population regulators in virtually every terrestrial ecosystem. Paul Stamets' work on mycopesticides — using these fungi as targeted biological control agents — represents one of the most practical applications of mycological research for agriculture and structural pest management. Unlike [[comparison-parasitoid-wasps-vs-chemical-pesticides]] that poison non-target organisms and accumulate in food chains, entomopathogenic fungi are host-specific or narrow-spectrum, self-propagating, and biodegradable, offering a fundamentally different approach to pest control.

The concept of using fungi against insect pests is not new — the earliest recorded observation of fungal insect pathogenicity dates to the 1830s, when *[[beauveria-bassiana]]* was identified as the cause of silkworm disease. However, Stamets' contribution was in demonstrating practical, effective formulations and application methods that make fungal biocontrol viable at commercial scale, including a series of patents on fungal pest control technologies.

## Key Species

Two genera dominate entomopathogenic fungal biocontrol:

**[[metarhizium-anisopliae]]** (formerly *Entomophthora anisopliae*) is a soil-dwelling fungus with a remarkably broad host range among insects. Different strains of *M. anisopliae* specialize on different insect groups, including termites, beetles, flies, grasshoppers, and various agricultural pests. Stamets has focused particularly on its efficacy against termites (*Reticulitermes* spp. and *Coptotermes* spp.), demonstrating that treated wood and soil can remain protective for months after application. The fungus infects termites through contact — when a termite encounters *Metarhizium* spores on treated surfaces, the spores adhere to the insect's cuticle, germinate, and penetrate through the exoskeleton.

**Beauveria bassiana** is the most widely studied and commercially produced entomopathogenic fungus. It infects over 700 species of insects, though individual strains typically show preference for specific hosts. Commercial *B. bassiana* products are registered for use against whiteflies, thrips, aphids, Colorado potato beetles, and many other agricultural pests. The fungus produces a distinctive white halo of spores around dead infected insects, visible as fuzzy white growth on cadavers — a phenomenon called "white muscadine disease."

## Infection Mechanism

The infection process of entomopathogenic fungi follows a precisely orchestrated sequence of events:

**Stage 1 — Adhesion**: When an insect contacts fungal spores (conidia), the spores adhere to the insect cuticle using hydrophobic interactions and specialized adhesive proteins. The spores are remarkably persistent — they can remain viable on treated surfaces for weeks or months, waiting for contact with a susceptible host.

**Stage 2 — Germination**: Once attached to the insect, the spore senses chemical cues from the cuticle (particularly specific lipids and fatty acids) and germinates, producing a germ tube that grows across the insect's outer surface.

**Stage 3 — Penetration**: The germ tube differentiates into an appressorium — a specialized penetration structure that generates mechanical pressure and secretes a cocktail of cuticle-degrading enzymes (proteases, chitinases, lipases). These enzymes dissolve the insect's exoskeleton, allowing the fungal hyphae to enter the hemocoel (insect body cavity).

**Stage 4 — Internal colonization**: Once inside the insect, the fungus proliferates rapidly, producing blastospores (yeast-like cells) that circulate through the hemolymph (insect blood). The fungus consumes the insect's internal tissues and organs, typically killing the host within 3–10 days depending on the fungal species, spore dose, insect size, and environmental conditions.

**Stage 5 — External sporulation**: After killing the host, the fungus grows back out through the insect's body wall and produces fresh spores on the cadaver surface. These spores are then dispersed by wind, water, or contact to infect new hosts — creating a self-amplifying cycle that can spread through an insect population without additional human intervention.

## The Recruitment Effect

One of the most significant discoveries highlighted by Stamets is the "recruitment effect" — the ability of entomopathogenic fungi to attract additional insects to the vicinity of infected cadavers. Research has shown that infected insects, in the period between initial infection and death, often exhibit altered behavior that brings them into contact with nestmates or conspecifics. When the infected insect dies and sporulates, the resulting spore cloud can infect multiple additional insects.

For social insects like termites and ants, this effect is particularly devastating. Termites groom each other constantly as part of their social behavior, which efficiently transfers fungal spores from infected individuals to healthy nestmates. A single infected termite returning to the colony can trigger an epidemic that spreads through the entire colony, a phenomenon Stamets calls "auto-dissemination." This is fundamentally different from chemical pesticides, which kill individual insects on contact but do not self-propagate.

## Presporulating Mycelium

Stamets holds patents on the use of presporulating mycelium (mycelium that has not yet produced spores) as a biocontrol agent. The innovation lies in timing: by applying fungal mycelium rather than mature spores, the material is less detectable by insects (which have evolved grooming and avoidance behaviors in response to spores). The mycelium grows on the treated surface and produces spores in situ, creating a time-release biocontrol effect. Termites encountering presporulating *Metarhizium* mycelium do not recognize the threat, groom normally, and become infected.

## Applications

**Structural pest control**: *Metarhizium* formulations applied to wood, soil, or building foundations provide long-term protection against termites and carpenter ants. Unlike chemical termiticides that degrade and require reapplication, fungal treatments can remain effective for years as the mycelium persists in treated wood and soil.

**Agricultural pest management**: *Beauveria bassiana* is used commercially against a wide range of crop pests. Application methods include foliar sprays, soil drenches, and seed treatments. The fungus can be integrated into existing IPM ([[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]]) programs with minimal disruption to [[beneficial-insects-cannabis]], as it shows strong host specificity at the strain level.

**Public health**: Entomopathogenic fungi show promise against disease vectors including mosquitoes (vectors of malaria, dengue, Zika) and tsetse flies (vectors of African sleeping sickness). Field trials in Africa and South America have demonstrated significant reductions in mosquito populations using *Metarhizium* and *Beauveria* formulations applied to breeding sites and resting surfaces.

## Advantages Over Chemical Pesticides

Fungal biocontrol offers several fundamental advantages over chemical pesticides:

- **Selectivity**: Individual fungal strains target specific insect groups, leaving beneficial insects (pollinators, predators, parasitoids) unharmed.

- **No resistance development**: Unlike chemical pesticides, which select for resistant insect populations through repeated use, fungal biocontrol uses a living organism that co-evolves with its host. The multi-target infection mechanism (enzymatic penetration, internal colonization, immune evasion) makes resistance evolution extremely unlikely.

- **Self-propagation**: Once established, fungal treatments can spread through pest populations without additional application, reducing long-term costs.

- **Biodegradability**: Fungal spores and mycelium are organic materials that decompose naturally, leaving no persistent residues in soil, water, or food.

- **Synergy with ecosystem**: Fungal biocontrol works within existing ecological relationships, supporting rather than disrupting natural pest regulation.

## Major Entomopathogenic Fungal Genera
Several fungal genera are commercially important as biocontrol agents:

### *Metarhizium* Species
*Metarhizium anisopliae* and *M. brunneum* are broad-spectrum entomopathogens
effective against soil-dwelling insects, termites, beetles, and cockroaches.
Conidia attach to the insect cuticle, germinate, and penetrate through the
exoskeleton using mechanical pressure and cuticle-degrading enzymes
(proteases, chitinases, lipases). Once inside the hemocoel (body cavity),
the fungus proliferates rapidly, producing blastospores that circulate in
the hemolymph. Death typically occurs within 3-7 days of infection.

### *Beauveria* Species
*Beauveria bassiana* is the most widely commercialized entomopathogenic
fungus, used against whiteflies, aphids, thrips, and coffee berry borers.
It produces characteristic white colonies on infected insects, giving rise
to the common name "white muscardine disease." *Beauveria* products are
registered in dozens of countries and applied as foliar sprays, soil
drenches, and seed treatments.

### *Isaria* (formerly *Paecilomyces*) Species
*Isaria fumosorosea* is effective against whiteflies, thrips, and mites.
It is particularly valued in greenhouse horticulture where chemical
pesticide resistance has developed. The fungus produces pinkish sporulating
colonies on infected insects, facilitating field identification of
infection prevalence.

## Mode of Infection: From Conidia to Death
The infection process involves several precisely orchestrated steps:

1. **Attachment**: Conidia (spores) adhere to the insect cuticle through
   hydrophobic interactions and adhesion proteins on the spore surface.
2. **Germination**: Under favorable humidity (typically >90% RH), conidia
   germinate and produce a germ tube that grows along the cuticle surface.
3. **Penetration**: Appressoria (specialized [[fungal-appressorium-and-infection-structures]]) form at
   the germ tip and generate mechanical pressure plus secrete cuticle-
   degrading enzymes to breach the exoskeleton.
4. **Proliferation**: Once inside the hemocoel, the fungus produces
   blastospores that multiply in the insect hemolymph.
5. **Death and Sporulation**: The insect dies from a combination of
   nutrient depletion, toxin exposure, and physical disruption of internal
   organs. The fungus then emerges from the cadaver and produces new
   conidia for secondary infection cycles.

## Environmental Safety Profile
Entomopathogenic fungi exhibit high specificity for their insect hosts
and pose minimal risk to mammals, birds, fish, and most beneficial insects.
This selectivity arises from the requirement for specific cuticular
receptors and physiological conditions found only in susceptible insects.
