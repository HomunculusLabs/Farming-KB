    10|# Fungal Endophytes in Grassland Ecology
    11|
    12|Source: Symbiotic Fungi (Varma and Kharkwal, eds.), Chapters 1, 12, 16, 18
    13|
    14|## Introduction
    15|
    16|Fungal endophytes in grassland ecosystems participate in complex multipartite
    17|interactions involving [[mycorrhizal-fungi]], soil bacteria, and plant communities.
    18|The mycorrhizosphere -- the soil volume influenced by mycorrhizal fungal hyphae --
    19|supports distinct microbial communities that profoundly affect plant fitness, nutrient
    20|cycling, and ecosystem function.
    21|
    22|Roots release 10-20% of total net fixed carbon as low-molecular-weight compounds,
    23|forming the basis for the rhizosphere environment. Its specific conditions lead to
    24|selection of distinct microbial communities.
    25|
    26|## The Mycorrhizosphere Concept
    27|
    28|The fungal hyphae emanating from mycorrhizas release a substantial amount of acquired
    29|plant carbon to the soil, creating the mycorrhizosphere (Foster and Marks 1967). This
    30|energy-rich environment promotes bacterial growth and survival (Hobbie 1992).
    31|
    32|Mycorrhizosphere organisms influence plant development through multiple pathways:
    33|
    34|- Production of plant growth regulators
    35|- Increasing root branching rate or root permeability
    36|- Improving nitrogen or phosphate availability
    37|- Supporting survival in contaminated soils
    38|- Assisting plant resistance against pathogens through biological control
    39|- Direct effects on soil quality
    40|- Enrichment of microorganisms that improve plant fitness
    41|
    42|## Mycorrhiza Helper Bacteria
    43|
    44|Mycorrhiza helper bacteria (MHB, Garbaye 1994) directly promote mycorrhiza formation.
    45|A strong correlation exists between induction of fungal growth in dual cultures and
    46|mycorrhization helper effect.
    47|
    48|Types of mycorrhizosphere bacteria:
    49|
    50|- Intrahyphal bacteria in ectomycorrhizal fungi (Bertaux et al. 2003)
    51|- Intra-spore bacteria in [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (Bianciotto et al. 1996)
    52|- Bacterial species colonizing hyphal surfaces and mycorrhizal roots
    53|- Gram-negative species: Pseudomonas, etc. (Gryndler and Vosatka 1996)
    54|- Gram-positive species: Streptomyces, etc. (Ames 1989; Maier et al. 2004)
    55|
    56|## Streptomyces AcH 505: A Model Helper Bacterium
    57|
    58|Streptomyces sp. AcH 505, isolated from the rhizosphere of Norway spruce (Picea abies),
    59|demonstrates the dual action of helper bacteria:
    60|
    61|- Significantly promoted mycelial growth of Amanita muscaria
    62|- Promoted mycorrhization rate
    63|- Simultaneously suppressed plant pathogens:
    64|  - Armillariella obscura
    65|  - Heterobasidion annosum
    66|
    67|This dual action -- promoting beneficial [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]] while suppressing
    68|pathogenic fungi -- represents an important application potential for plant
    69|production systems.
    70|
    71|## Fungus-Bacterium Interaction Dynamics
    72|
    73|Three co-culture methods are commonly used:
    74|
    75|1. **Direct agar co-culture**: bacteria next to fungus; diffusible molecule interactions
    76|2. **Cellophane membrane method**: membrane below fungal material; 10 kDa cut-off
    77|   allows small soluble metabolites and volatiles but not macromolecules
    78|3. **Cross-walled Petri dishes**: confirms volatile-mediated effects
    79|
    80|Key factors influencing interaction outcomes:
    81|
    82|- **Media composition**: AcH 505/A. muscaria mutualistic on MMN but antagonistic
    83|  on ISP2 (AcH 505 sporulates more strongly on ISP2, antifungal metabolites dominate)
    84|- **Inoculum size**: fine balance between too small (no effect), adequate (promotion),
    85|  and excess (no influence or antagonism)
    86|- **Fungal growth rate**: bacteria compensate for poor growth but may not increase
    87|  maximum growth under optimal conditions
    88|- **Temperature**: 10 degrees C (normal for temperate/boreal forest soils) maximizes
    89|  reproducibility of [[laccaria-bicolor]]-Pseudomonas fluorescens dual cultures
    90|
    91|The slowest-growing Amanita muscaria strain was most responsive to helper
    92|streptomycetes AcH 505 and AcH 1003.
    93|
    94|## [[root-exudates]] and Synergistic Interactions
    95|
    96|Root exudates mediate interactions between AM fungi, bacteria, and pathogens.
    97|The interaction between rhizobia and AM fungi typically acts synergistically:
    98|
    99|- Both symbioses together produce greater N and P content than either alone
   100|- Legumes are coarse-rooted and inefficient at extracting soil phosphorus
   101|- AM fungi provide essential link for adequate phosphorus nutrition
   102|- Enhanced phosphorus leads to increased nitrogenase activity
   103|- This in turn promotes root and mycorrhizal growth
   104|
   105|## Carbon Flow in Grassland Soils
   106|
   107|AM fungi contribute significantly to carbon cycling in grassland ecosystems.
   108|Key findings from 13C-labelling studies with Plantago lanceolata:
   109|
   110|- Recently assimilated carbon appeared in extraradical AM hyphae within days
   111|- Carbon remained largely in hyphae for at least 30 days
   112|- Carbon allocation to rhizosphere bacteria occurs at much lower rate than
   113|  direct transfer to AM fungi
   114|- Retention of carbon in AM mycelium contributes significantly to soil organic carbon
   115|- AM fungi believed to contribute greatly to carbon cycling in all types of grassland
   116|
   117|Carbon flow through the mycorrhizosphere can be described as a conceptual model with
   118|carbon pools (plants, roots, AM fungi, saprophytic microorganisms, soil organic matter)
   119|connected by transfer arrows. The amount of recently assimilated carbon retained in each
   120|pool can be estimated using 13C-labelling techniques.
   121|
   122|## Interactions with Soil Pathogens
   123|
   124|Mycorrhizal fungi interact with root pathogens that parasitize feeder roots. In
   125|natural ecosystems with low phosphorus uptake, a major role may be protection from
   126|endemic pathogens such as Fusarium spp.
   127|
   128|Proposed protective mechanisms:
   129|
   130|1. **Mechanical barrier**: especially the ECM mantle to pathogen entry
   131|2. **Antibiotic production**: compounds that suppress pathogens
   132|3. **Nutrient competition**: including siderophore production
   133|4. **Induced host defense**: generalized defense mechanisms
   134|
   135|Mycorrhizae may stimulate root colonization by selected biocontrol agents, but
   136|understanding of these interactions remains limited.
   137|
   138|## Tropical Forest Restoration Applications
   139|
   140|AM fungi serve as important tools in ecological restoration:
   141|
   142|- Use as additional tool in restoration has proved effective
   143|- Maintaining native AM fungal communities contributes to soil property recovery
   144|- AM fungi form aggregates and retain organic material
   145|- Improves soil structure and reduces erosion
   146|- Light-demanding pioneer species and shade-tolerant late successional species
   147|  show different AM fungal dependencies during forest succession
   148|- Gigasporineae less affected by soil disturbance than Glomineae
