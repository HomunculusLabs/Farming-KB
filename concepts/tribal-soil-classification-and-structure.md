# Tribal Soil Classification and Structure

Indigenous and traditional societies have developed remarkably sophisticated systems for classifying soils, often far more practical for local agriculture than modern taxonomic approaches. [[soil-life]]
- **Plant indicators** — specific plants signal acidity, drainage conditions, or fertility levels
- **Catena** — the relationship of soils along a slope sequence, from ridge to valley
- **"Hot" and "cold" soils** — relative fertility ratings; hot soils are productive, cold soils are poor
- **Usage** — practical applications such as pigments, pottery clay, or salt extraction
- **Work needed for crop** — energy input required to prepare the soil for planting
- **Crop suitability** — soils matched to specific crops (yam soil, taro soil, etc.), with some traditions recognising up to 12 distinct crop-specific soil types
- **Organic content** — assessed visually and by feel

These classification systems are holistic: they integrate geology, hydrology, biology, and human use into a single framework that directly guides agricultural decisions.

A farmer who knows that termite mounds indicate well-structured, fertile subsoil, or that specific wild plants flag acidic conditions, has immediate practical information without need for laboratory analysis. The recognition of up to 12 distinct crop-specific soil types demonstrates a level of agronomic precision that rivals modern soil surveys.

## Modern vs. Traditional Classification

Modern soil science uses more complex nomenclature, relying on Munsell colour charts for precise colour description and standard sieves for particle-size analysis. While more technically precise, these systems can be less accessible to farmers working intuitively with their land. Tribal classifications are often equally predictive of crop performance because they encode generations of localised observation.

## Soil Physical Structure

Soil structure depends on the arrangement of particles into aggregates (nodules or clumps). These aggregates are held together by:

- **Root networks** — living and decaying roots bind soil particles
- **Clay minerals** — provide plasticity and electrochemical bonding
- **Chemical bonds** — calcium, iron, and aluminium oxides act as cements
- **Organic glues and fibres** — humus, polysaccharides, and [[fungal-hyphae]]

Uncompacted, healthy soils are open, crumbly, or soft. Compacted soils require either plasticity from clay content or glue and fibre from organic sources to maintain structure.

### Threats to Soil Structure

Mechanical tillage with ploughs and harrows can powder fragile soil assemblies, destroying the [[crumb-structure]] that took years to develop. Once broken down, soils become prone to erosion, compaction, and surface sealing.

## The Soil Mantle

The living soil mantle is remarkably thin — Mollison compares it to the shine on an orange skin. It extends as living mud below waters. Soils rarely extend below 1–2 metres, and the actively living system is often only 6–12 cm deep. Within this thin layer exist:

- **Minerals** — silica, iron and aluminium oxides, calcium compounds
- **Soil waters** — the film of water coating every particle
- **Gases** — air in pore spaces, essential for root respiration
- **Life forms** — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, insects, worms
- **Once-living remains** — humus, the stable residue of decomposed organic matter

## Soil Elements and Chemistry

Of the 103 known elements, soils are predominantly composed of **aluminium**, **silica**, **iron**, and **calcium**. The periodic table serves as a useful guide for understanding which elements are available in soil, water, and plants, and how they contribute to nutrition cycles.

Mollison raises an important caution regarding **radioactive elements** found in some clays, bricks, paints, and stone, as well as emissions from domestic appliances. Awareness of elemental composition is essential for safe building and land use.

Permaculture designers should test materials intended for earth building, especially clays and rock dusts, for elevated radioactivity. The periodic table provides a framework for understanding not just nutrient availability but also potential hazards in the soil [[artillery-fungus-and-built-environment-fungi]].

## Particle Size Classification

Soil particles are classified by diameter:

| Type | Size Range |
|------|-----------|
| **Coarse sand** | 0.05–2.0 mm |
| **Silt** | 0.02–0.05 mm |
| **Clay** | < 0.002 mm |

Coarse particles are relatively inert. The critical difference lies in surface area: 1 gram of clay has up to **1000 times** the surface area of 1 gram of sand. This enormous surface area gives clay its chemical reactivity, water-holding capacity, and plasticity.

The USDA classification system organises soils by these particle-size proportions into texture classes such as sandy loam, clay loam, and silty clay.

## Soil Crumb Structure and Pore Space

When lime aids the formation of soil crumbs, pore space can reach **20–60%** of total soil volume. Organic materials and gels further hold this structure open during rainfall events, preventing surface sealing and maintaining infiltration. This pore space is critical — it houses both air and water in balance, supporting the diverse soil life that drives nutrient cycling.

## The Jar Test Method

A simple field test for soil composition:

1. Collect a soil sample from the depth of interest
2. Fill a tall, clear jar approximately one-third full with the sample
3. Add water to nearly fill the jar
4. Add a pinch of salt or a drop of dish soap to help dispersion
5. Shake vigorously until all particles are suspended
6. Set the jar on a level surface and allow it to settle

Particles settle by size: sand falls first (within 1–2 minutes), then silt (within 1–2 hours), and clay remains in suspension for up to 24–48 hours. The resulting layers reveal the approximate proportions of sand, silt, and clay.

**Practical thresholds**: approximately **40% clay** is needed for stable dam walls; less clay is required for mud bricks, where additional fibre provides binding strength.

## Key Principles

- Traditional soil knowledge encodes practical, locally calibrated agricultural intelligence
- Soil structure is a living assembly held together by biological and mineral bonds
- Mechanical disturbance destroys the crumb structure that supports soil health
- The active soil layer is remarkably thin and must be protected
- Simple field tests like the jar method can reveal essential soil composition
- Surface area, not just particle size, determines soil chemical behaviour
