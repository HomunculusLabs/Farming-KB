updated: 2026-04-18 Polyaromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) [[query-how-does-lignin-degradation-by-fungi-work]] is one of the most promising applications of white-rot-fungi-effluent-decolorization to break down recalcitrant environmental pollutants that resist bacterial attack.

## PAHs as Environmental Pollutants

PAHs are carcinogenic chemicals formed during organic combustion. Key characteristics:
- Two or more fused benzene rings, insoluble and stable in the environment
- Concentrated at industrial sites, petroleum facilities, and wood-preserving operations
- Creosote contains 85% PAHs by weight
- Atmospheric fall-out has been steadily increasing through the 20th century

### PAH Biodegradability Hierarchy

| Ring Structure | Examples | Biodegradability |
|---------------|----------|-----------------|
| 2-3 rings (linear) | Naphthalene, anthracene | Highly biodegradable |
| 2-3 rings (angular) | Phenanthrene | Moderately biodegradable |
| 4 rings | Pyrene, benz[a]anthracene | Less biodegradable |
| 5-6 rings | Benzo[a]pyrene, dibenzo-p-dioxin | Least biodegradable |

PAH degradation depends on microbial ability to introduce oxygen into the rings, increasing both solubility and chemical reactivity.

## Why Fungi Over Bacteria for PAHs

Bacteria use intracellular dioxygenases and cytochrome P450 enzymes. PAH structures larger than 2-3 rings are too insoluble to cross the bacterial cell wall. White-rot fungi overcome this through extracellular oxidative systems that attack higher-molecular-weight PAHs directly.

## Key Fungal Species for PAH Degradation

### [[singh-cytochrome-p450-fungal-bioremediation]]
The model white-rot fungus, extensively studied for PAH degradation:
- Mineralizes phenanthrene, pyrene, and benzo[a]pyrene in lab cultures
- Degrades PAHs in soil supplemented with straw, hay, or wood
- [[fungal-lignin-peroxidase-veratryl-alcohol]] (LiP) and [[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]] (MnP) are primary enzymes

### [[staycare-trametes-versicolor-bioremediation-applications]]
- Colonized soil and degraded 3,4-dichloroaniline and benzo[a]pyrene
- Mycelium-coated pellets removed over 80% of pentachlorophenol in 4 weeks
- Produces all three major [[singh-ligninolytic-enzymes-fungal-bioremediation]] (LiP, MnP, laccase)

### [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]]
The [[cotter-oyster-mushroom-cultivation]], superior soil colonizer:
- Superior to P. chrysosporium and T. versicolor in soil colonization
- Effective against pyrene and benzo[a]pyrene in sterile sand microcosms
- Successfully used with straw in unsterile soils with PAHs and heavy metals

### Marasmiellus troyanus
Isolated from a toxic waste site:
- Completely degrades benzo[a]pyrene in liquid culture
- Alginate-encapsulated formulations removed ~90% of benzo[a]pyrene from soil microcosms in 6 weeks; does not sporulate in the laboratory

### Lentinula edodes (Shiitake)
- Spent sawdust cultures biotransformed pentachlorophenol in soil

## Enzymatic Mechanisms of PAH Degradation

White-rot fungi deploy three major extracellular enzyme systems working synergistically:
- **Lignin peroxidase (LiP)**: A heme-containing glycoprotein generating reactive aryl cation radicals through one-electron oxidation. Oxidizes PAHs with ionization potentials up to 7.55 eV, effective against high-molecular-weight compounds like benzo[a]pyrene. Requires hydrogen peroxide and is stabilized by veratryl alcohol as a redox mediator.
- **Manganese peroxidase (MnP)**: Oxidizes Mn(II) to Mn(III), forming a diffusible chelate with organic acids (oxalate, malonate) that penetrates soil micropores to attack adsorbed PAHs. Often the dominant peroxidase in soil environments where Mn(II) is abundant.
- **Laccase**: A multicopper oxidase using molecular oxygen as electron acceptor. Combined with redox mediators such as HBT or ABTS, laccase becomes highly effective through diffusible electron shuttle mechanisms.

The non-specific, radical-based nature of these systems is precisely what makes them effective against PAHs. Unlike bacterial enzymes requiring specific substrate binding, fungal extracellular enzymes generate free radicals attacking a broad spectrum of aromatic structures.

## Field Trials and Measured Outcomes

- **Creosote-contaminated soil**: P. ostreatus with straw achieved 60-70% total PAH removal over 12 weeks in mesocosm trials, with preferential degradation of 3-ring PAHs.
- **Former gasworks sites**: T. versicolor in compost biopiles reduced total PAH concentrations by 50-80% depending on contamination levels.
- **Wood treatment facilities**: Composting with P. chrysosporium reduced pentachlorophenol and PAHs simultaneously, though outcompeted without selective biocides in some trials.
- **Long-term persistence**: Monitoring over 1-2 years showed treated plots continued declining in PAH concentration even after fungal activity declined, suggesting fungal oxidation products become bioavailable to bacteria completing the mineralization pathway.

## Limitations and Challenges

Nutrient competition from indigenous microorganisms can suppress white-rot fungal growth, particularly in organically rich soils. Soil conditions such as pH, moisture, and temperature must be maintained within narrow ranges. Achieving uniform colonization of large soil volumes remains difficult. Scale-up has consistently produced lower degradation rates, partly because PAHs become sequestered in soil organic matter over time, becoming increasingly inaccessible even to extracellular enzymes.

## Soil Bioremediation Approaches

### Biostimulation
Adding nutrients (N, P) and [[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]] (straw, wood chips) to stimulate indigenous microbial activity. Often more effective than bioaugmentation alone.

### Bioaugmentation
Introducing specific fungal species via formulated inocula:
- **Substrate carrier**: Wheat straw most effective for P. ostreatus
- **Selective biocides**: Benomyl (115 ppm) selectively benefits white rot
- **Inoculum density**: Higher initial inoculum improves colonization
