## Wild Mushroom Markets in Europe

## Overview

Wild mushrooms are marketed extensively in many European countries, forming an important component of local food economies and culinary traditions. The scale of wild mushroom commerce varies dramatically by country and city, from modest local markets to the extraordinary spectacle of Munich, which was the largest wild mushroom market in the world. Market regulation, species diversity, and cultural attitudes toward wild fungi all influence the character and safety of these markets.

## Market Regulation and Safety

### The Prussian Law of 1812

An early and remarkably clear law restricted mushroom sale in Prussia to the following species only: *Morchella esculenta*, *M. conica*, *Psalliota campestris*, *Lactarius deliciosus*, *Agaricus cebaceus*, *Merulius cantharellus*, *Boletus edulis*, and *Clavaria flava*. All other species were to be discarded as either poisonous or easily confused with poisonous forms.

### The Milan Decree of 1820

The government of Milan declared certain fungi edible, naming about half a dozen common species, with each province authorized to post a list of species found harmless from local experience.

### The Principle of Regulation

Wherever wild mushrooms are offered for sale, municipal regulations should be strict and competent inspectors should examine all products. In European cities, regulations are not always adequate to prevent accidents through market sales, but in many cases a resident botanist acts as consulting officer or referee to whom doubtful mushrooms are brought. The fundamental principle: **it is senseless to eat any and all mushrooms just because they are not poisonous** — there is no more point to that than eating crows simply because they are not toxic.

## Economic Significance

The scale of European wild mushroom commerce is remarkable. The Munich market alone handled nearly 2 million pounds in a single season. Beyond direct market sales, wild mushrooms contribute to rural economies through:

- Forest keeper wages partially derived from mushroom harvesting privileges
- Seasonal employment for collectors and market vendors
- Preservation industries (drying, oil, and butter preservation)
- Hotel and restaurant demand driving local collection
- Export trade (particularly for dried boletes and truffles)

The contrast with American markets reflects not biological scarcity but cultural difference — the species are present in North American forests, but the knowledge, tradition, and market infrastructure for their exploitation had not developed.
