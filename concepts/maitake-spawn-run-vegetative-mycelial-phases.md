# Maitake Spawn Run Vegetative Mycelial Phases

## Overview

The spawn run of maitake (*Grifola frondosa*) encompasses the entire vegetative growth period — from initial substrate colonization through mycelial maturation and the metabolic transitions that prepare the fungus for primordia initiation. Compared to other cultivated mushrooms, maitake has a notably prolonged spawn run, typically spanning 40 to 42 days from inoculation to the first appearance of primordia. This extended timeline reflects the species' complex metabolic preparation requirements and is characterized by a sequence of visually distinct morphological stages that serve as reliable markers for cultivators monitoring crop progress.

This page focuses specifically on the observable, morphological stages of the maitake spawn run. It is distinct from general spawn-run-and-colonization topics by detailing the unique visual progression of *G. frondosa*, and it is distinct from [[maitake-strain-selection-and-spawn-types]] preparation topics by covering what happens after inoculation, during the incubation phase itself.

## Prolonged Spawn Run Duration

*Grifola frondosa* is distinguished among cultivated mushrooms by the length of its vegetative phase. While many commonly grown species (e.g., *Pleurotus ostreatus*, *Lentinula edodes*) complete spawn runs in 14 to 21 days, maitake requires approximately 40 to 42 days before primordia are ready to form. This prolonged timeline is intrinsic to the species and is not an indication of slow or problematic colonization.

Rather, the extended duration reflects the extensive metabolic restructuring the mycelium must undergo to transition from active substrate degradation to reproductive competence. The mycelium must not only fully colonize the substrate but also undergo a prolonged maturation period involving accumulation of secondary metabolites, reorganization of cellular resources, and physiological priming for the reproductive switch.

Cultivators must plan facilities and scheduling around this extended incubation. Substrate bags occupy incubation space for roughly six weeks, and environmental conditions must be maintained consistently throughout to avoid stalls, contamination, or premature initiation. The long occupancy time means that any interruption in temperature control, humidity management, or air circulation during the spawn run can have outsized consequences on final yield.

## The Two Metabolic Periods

The spawn run can be conceptually divided into two overarching metabolic periods:

1. **Growth period** — The phase of active vegetative colonization during which mycelium expands through the substrate, degrading organic matter and accumulating biomass and nutrient reserves. This is the period of most rapid mycelial extension and substrate penetration. The growth period dominates roughly the first three weeks of incubation.

2. **Maturation period** — A subsequent phase in which metabolic activity shifts away from pure growth and toward preparation for reproduction. During this period, the mycelium reorganizes its physiology, accumulates secondary metabolites, and begins expressing the visual markers — notably orange-brown exudates and surface coat formation — that signal readiness for primordia initiation. The maturation period typically spans from around day 17 through day 40.

The transition between these periods is gradual and marked by observable changes in the appearance of the substrate surface, as described in the staged progression below. Understanding this bipartite structure helps cultivators interpret what they are seeing and make informed management decisions.

## Sequential Morphological Stages

### Stage 1: Undifferentiated White Mycelia (Days 0–9)

Immediately following inoculation, the mycelium begins to expand outward from the spawn grain into the surrounding supplemented substrate. The earliest visible growth appears as undifferentiated white mycelia — a pure, bright white, cottony or appressed growth front that advances radially through the substrate. At this stage, the mycelium is engaged almost entirely in the growth period: aggressive colonization, enzymatic degradation of the substrate matrix, and biomass accumulation.

By approximately **day 9**, the mycelium has achieved **initial colonization** of the substrate mass. At this point, the visible mycelial front is well established, though the interior of the substrate block may not yet be fully colonized. The surface appears uniformly white with no discoloration or structural differentiation. This is the most visually simple stage and corresponds to the peak of the active growth period.

### Stage 2: Full Surface Penetration (Day 17)

By approximately **day 17**, the mycelium has achieved **full surface penetration** of the substrate block. The entire exposed surface of the substrate within the cultivation bag is covered by white mycelium, and the interior of the block is substantially colonized. The mycelial mat at this stage is still uniform in appearance — white, relatively flat, and undifferentiated.

Full surface penetration is an important milestone because it indicates that the mycelium has established dominance over the substrate. Competition from opportunistic contaminants is effectively eliminated once the substrate is fully occupied by the vigorous maitake mycelium. From this point forward, the metabolic emphasis begins to shift from outward colonization toward internal consolidation and preparation for the maturation period.

### Stage 3: White Mycelia with Orange-Brown Discoloration (Day 30)

Around **day 30**, a striking visual change occurs. The previously uniform white mycelial surface begins to show patches of **orange-brown discoloration**. This coloration results from the accumulation of **metabolic exudates** — secondary metabolites and oxidized phenolic compounds produced as the mycelium shifts into the maturation period. These exudates may appear as diffuse staining across the surface or as more concentrated droplets and patches.

This orange-brown discoloration is a normal and expected part of maitake spawn run physiology. It is not a sign of contamination or degradation. Rather, it signals that the mycelium is actively reorganizing its metabolic pathways in preparation for reproductive growth. The exudates are byproducts of the enzymatic and oxidative processes associated with this transition.

Cultivators should recognize this stage as a positive indicator. Substrate blocks that reach this discoloration phase on schedule are likely progressing normally toward primordia initiation. Blocks that fail to show any discoloration by day 30 may indicate issues with strain vigor, substrate composition, or environmental conditions.

### Stage 4: Mycelial Coat Formation

Following the appearance of orange-brown exudates, the mycelial surface begins to consolidate into a more structured form known as the **mycelial coat**. This coat is a densified, often slightly leathery or crust-like layer of mycelium that forms over the substrate surface. It represents further maturation of the mycelial network as hyphae become more densely intertwined and the surface layer differentiates from the underlying substrate.

The mycelial coat serves important functions in the transition to fruiting. It helps regulate moisture retention at the substrate surface, provides a structural matrix from which primordia can emerge, and may play a role in creating the microenvironmental conditions necessary for initiation. The coat formation is a clear visual indicator that the maturation period is well advanced.

### Stage 5: Uneven Topography with Grayish Amorphous Mass

As the mycelial coat matures, the substrate surface develops an **uneven topography**. Rather than remaining flat and uniform, the surface becomes irregular, with mounded areas and depressions. This unevenness is a precursor to primordia formation — the mycelium is beginning to organize into localized concentrations that will become the initiation sites.

Overlying this uneven topography, a **grayish amorphous mass** begins to appear. This diffuse, gray-colored material is the earliest visible expression of reproductive differentiation. It represents the mycelium redirecting growth into compacted masses that will soon consolidate into discrete primordia. The transition from the orange-brown discoloration of stage 3 to the grayish tones of this stage is one of the most reliable visual cues that the spawn run is nearing completion.

### Stage 6: Grayish Primordia Formation (Days 40–42)

The final stage of the spawn run is the formation of **grayish primordia** — the first discrete, organized reproductive structures. These primordia appear as dark gray, roughly spherical masses on the substrate surface. They are initially small but quickly enlarge to approximately **2.54–5.08 cm (1–2 inches)** in diameter within the sealed bag.

At this stage, primordia may also display small **exudate droplets** on their surface, which can appear clear or light yellow. These droplets are a normal metabolic byproduct and should not be removed, as doing so can damage the delicate forming structures.

The appearance of grayish primordia at the target size marks the end of the spawn run and the beginning of the primordia initiation phase proper. The crop is now ready for transition to the fruiting facility.

## Light Exposure During Spawn Run

Light plays a critical and somewhat counterintuitive role throughout the maitake spawn run. Unlike some species that require complete darkness during vegetative growth, *G. frondosa* benefits from **low-level light exposure** throughout the entire spawn run period.

Research by Wu et al. (1997) demonstrated that maintaining approximately **50 lux** of light during the spawn run facilitates subsequent primordia initiation. This low-level illumination does not trigger premature fruiting but instead appears to condition the mycelium at a physiological level, preparing it for the reproductive transition. The mechanism may involve light-regulated gene expression or photoreceptor-mediated signaling that primes the mycelium for initiation when environmental conditions shift.

Practically, this means that spawn run rooms should not be kept in total darkness. Low ambient light — such as that provided by indirect fluorescent or LED lighting on a standard day/night cycle — is beneficial and should be maintained throughout the 40- to 42-day incubation period. The 50 lux level is quite dim, roughly equivalent to a softly lit interior room, and is easily achieved without specialized equipment.

It is worth noting that the beneficial effect of low-level light during spawn run is distinct from the role of light during primordia initiation itself. During spawn run, the 50 lux level serves a preparatory, conditioning function. During the subsequent initiation phase, light intensity is increased to approximately 200 lux to actively drive the reproductive transition. The two lighting regimes serve different physiological purposes and should not be conflated.

## Transition to Primordia Initiation and Fruiting

When grayish primordia reach the target diameter of 2.54–5.08 cm within the sealed bag, the spawn run is complete and the crop enters the primordia initiation phase. The transition involves the following steps:

1. **Increase light to 200 lux** — Elevate light intensity from the spawn run level of 50 lux to approximately 200 lux. This fourfold increase, combined with ample oxygen availability and sustained high humidity, drives the final consolidation of primordia within the sealed bag environment.

2. **Maintain sealed bags** — Primordia initiation occurs while bags remain sealed. The bag environment provides the high humidity and elevated CO₂ conditions that support early primordial development. Opening bags prematurely risks dehydration of the delicate primordial structures.

3. **Hold for 2–3 days** — Allow the primordia to consolidate within the sealed bag for an additional 2 to 3 days after reaching target size. This consolidation period strengthens the primordial structures and ensures they are robust enough to survive exposure to the open fruiting environment.

4. **Transfer to fruiting facility** — Move the bags to the dedicated fruiting room where environmental controls (temperature, humidity, fresh air exchange, and lighting) are configured for [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]].

5. **Open bag tops** — After 2–3 days in the fruiting facility, open the tops of the bags to expose the developing primordia to the fruiting environment. This allows for gas exchange and marks the formal beginning of the fruiting cycle.
