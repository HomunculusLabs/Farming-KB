# Trichomycetes: Gut Fungi of Arthropods

Trichomycetes are obligate gut fungi that inhabit the digestive tracts
of mandibulate arthropods worldwide. Currently about 225 species and 55
genera are recognized, but six genera and more than 60 species have been
described since Lichtwardt's 1986 monograph, indicating that species
richness is far greater than formerly realized. These fungi are hidden
within host guts and are not discernible until animals are dissected
and examined microscopically, requiring specialized techniques for
collection, study, and culture.

## Taxonomic Classification and Orders

Trichomycetes comprise four orders of fungi associated with arthropod
guts, although molecular evidence suggests that not all are closely
related. The Harpellales is the largest order with 33 genera and 141
species, primarily infecting dipteran larvae in freshwater habitats.
The Eccrinales includes 14 genera and 52 species found in millipedes,
crabs, anomurids, isopods, and insects across freshwater, terrestrial,
and marine environments. The Asellariales contains three genera and 11
species infecting isopods and springtails. The Amoebidiales, which
traditionally have been studied alongside Trichomycetes, comprises two
genera and 12 species found on insect and crustacean larvae in
freshwater. Amoebidiales are not phylogenetically related to the other
orders but are included because collectors frequently encounter them.

## Host Associations and Habitat Distribution

All Trichomycetes species are associated with mandibulate arthropods
that are detritivores, algivores, or omnivores. They are apparently not
found in predators, carnivores, or organisms that consume tissues of
living vascular plants. Because they are obligate gut parasites, their
geographic and habitat distributions depend entirely on those of their
hosts. Harpellales and Amoebidiales are predominantly aquatic, as are
most Asellariales and some Eccrinales. Harpellales commonly inhabit
lotic (flowing water) environments including streams, waterfalls, and
seeping cliffs, infecting nymphs of mayflies and stoneflies and larvae
of midges, blackflies, mosquitoes, and craneflies.

Lentic (still water) habitats including ponds, pools, lakes, and
swamps may also host Harpellales in mosquito larvae, bloodworms, and
other arthropods. Several genera of Eccrinales and one species of
Asellariales are marine, living in intertidal crustaceans such as
isopods, amphipods, crabs, hermit crabs, and mud shrimps. Some
Eccrinales occur at abyssal depths around hydrothermal vents. Terrestrial
hosts of Asellariales and Eccrinales include millipedes, isopods, and
certain beetles.

## Biology and Ecological Interactions

Most Trichomycetes appear to be commensals, obtaining nutrients from
ingested substances passing through the gut. In some cases, infection
of individual hosts may approach 100 percent, a remarkable level given
that fungi are shed at each molting event along with gut linings and
that reinfection propagules per thallus are significantly fewer than
spores produced by most other fungi. Some evidence suggests that
certain Smittium species (Harpellales) may provide sterols and B
vitamins to mosquito larvae deprived of those essential nutrients,
though detecting such subtle nutritional relationships is hampered by
the inability to culture most species and the need for experimental
hosts free of other gut microorganisms.

At least one species, Smittium morbosum, kills mosquito larvae by
inhibiting ecdysis. Some Harpellales in blackfly larvae occasionally
grow from the gut into developing ovaries, resulting in sterile adult
females that disseminate the fungus by flying to new sites and
ovipositing ovarian fungal cysts in place of eggs. These pathogenic
interactions blur the line between commensalism and parasitism within
the group. Full development of gut fungi occurs primarily in sexually
mature arthropods, although Asellariales and Eccrinales can also infect
immature stages.
