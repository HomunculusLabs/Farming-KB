Fungal salt tolerance encompasses the physiological mechanisms that allow fungi to survive and function in environments with elevated salt concentrations, ranging from saline soils and salt marshes to marine habitats and hypersaline conditions such as the Dead Sea.

Ion homeostasis, the regulation of intracellular ion concentrations, is central to these adaptations.

## Osmotic Adaptation Mechanisms

All marine fungi show physiological adaptations that allow them to survive under the stress of a high saline environment, especially in their regulation of osmotic potential.

Fungi employ two principal strategies for osmotic adaptation: the accumulation of compatible solutes (organic osmolytes) and the regulation of ion transport across cell membranes.

Compatible solutes are organic compounds that can accumulate to high intracellular concentrations without interfering with normal metabolic functions.

Common fungal compatible solutes include glycerol, erythritol, arabitol, mannitol, trehalose, and various amino acids such as proline, glutamic acid, and alanine.

These compounds lower the intracellular water potential to match the external osmotic potential, preventing water loss and maintaining cell turgor.

Ion transport regulation involves both the active extrusion of sodium ions (Na+) from the cytoplasm and the selective uptake of potassium ions (K+).

The Na+/H+ antiporter and the plasma membrane H+-ATPase are key components of this system.

By maintaining a low Na+/K+ ratio in the cytoplasm, fungi preserve enzyme function and membrane integrity under saline conditions.

## Fungi in Marine Environments

Marine fungi are a diverse group dominated by the ascomycete order Halosphaeriales, which includes some 43 genera and 133 species.

A lower diversity of basidiomycete and mitosporic fungi is found in marine ecosystems, with species distributions related to environmental conditions.

Marine fungi are categorized by their habitat and function.

Arenicolous (sand-inhabiting) fungi are mainly decomposers utilizing algae, leaves, and detritus as resources.

Lichen fungi form associations with algae in marine environments.

Some ascomycetes are true parasites of seaweeds, producing effects ranging from minor discoloration to gall formation that limits photosynthetic function.

The majority of marine fungal species are saprotrophs, particularly involved in the decomposition of wood and mangrove-derived plant litter.

Wood is a major resource for fungi in marine ecosystems, with 107 species isolated from decomposing marine wood, including 73 Ascomycotina, 2 Basidiomycotina, and 29 mitosporic species.

## Hypersaline Environments

Fungi have been isolated from extremely hypersaline environments including the Dead Sea, with total salt concentrations of 340 grams per liter.

The number of fungal species isolated from the Dead Sea has been reported to be 55, demonstrating that fungi can tolerate salinity levels far exceeding those of normal marine environments.

Hortaea werneckii and Wallemia ichthyophaga are among the most halotolerant fungal species known, capable of growth across extremely broad salinity ranges.

These species are found in hypersaline environments worldwide, from solar salterns to salted foods, and represent some of the most eukaryotically halotolerant organisms known.

## Salt Marsh Fungi

In salt marsh ecosystems, fungal decomposer communities show distinct adaptations to fluctuating salinity.

The decomposition of salt marsh grass Spartina relies on a community of fungi to decompose leaves separately from those decomposing roots and rhizomes, which constitute more than half the plant biomass.

Live fungal biomass in salt marsh systems is typically low (less than 20 mg per gram of substrate), and the fungal strategy is to grow rapidly within the substrate and immobilize nutrients into fungal biomass.

Fungi in salt marsh Spartina leaves can contain 75-100% of the total nitrogen of the decaying material, demonstrating their importance in nutrient retention.

Fungal biomass, measured as ergosterol content, is negatively related to redox potential in Spartina salt marshes, showing a rapid decline of fungal activity with increasing sediment depth.

However, fungi demonstrate the ability to conduct oxygen from aerobic regions through hyphae to advancing mycelial fronts that are physiologically active in anaerobic zones.

## Mangrove Fungi

Mangrove swamps are the tropical equivalent of salt marsh habitats, with greater structural complexity and higher primary production.

Fungal biomass in decaying mangrove leaves is much lower than in decaying salt marsh leaves, but fungi still play an important role in decomposition.

The high rate of primary production in mangrove ecosystems provides abundant substrate for fungal decomposers.

## Ion Homeostasis Mechanisms

The maintenance of appropriate intracellular ion concentrations under saline stress requires coordinated activity of multiple transport systems.

The plasma membrane H+-ATPase generates the proton electrochemical gradient that drives secondary active transport of ions.

Na+/H+ antiporters use this gradient to extrude sodium from the cytoplasm.

K+ transporters selectively accumulate potassium to maintain the high K+/Na+ ratio required for enzyme function.

Vacuolar sequestration of sodium provides an additional mechanism for cytoplasmic ion homeostasis.

Sodium ions pumped into the vacuole by tonoplast Na+/H+ antiporters are effectively removed from the cytoplasm while contributing to vacuolar osmotic potential.

This compartmentalization strategy allows fungi to tolerate high external sodium concentrations while maintaining favorable cytoplasmic conditions.

## Ecological Implications

Fungal salt tolerance has implications for ecosystem functioning in saline environments.

The ability of fungi to decompose organic matter and cycle nutrients under saline conditions is essential for the productivity of salt marsh, mangrove, and coastal ecosystems.

Climate change-induced sea level rise is expected to increase the extent of saline soils globally, making the salt tolerance of fungal communities increasingly important for ecosystem resilience.

The fungal decomposer community in salt marshes is also important for sustaining invertebrate herbivore populations.

The amphipod Ulorchestia spartinophila has complex dietary requirements and grows best when fed on decaying leaves containing high fungal biomass, indicating that fungal activity in saline environments supports higher trophic levels.
