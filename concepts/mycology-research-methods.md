# Mycology Research Methods

## Overview

Mycology — the study of fungi — employs a diverse toolkit of field, laboratory, and computational methods. The discipline spans from traditional [[bloomfield-mushroom-foraging-immigrant-poisonings-paddy-straw-confusion]] and identification to cutting-edge genomic sequencing and metabolomics. This guide covers the core methods used in [[telluride-mushroom-festival-modern-mycological-culture]] research.

## Field Methods

### Survey and Collection

Systematic fungal surveys require careful planning and documentation:

- **Site selection**: Based on habitat type, substrate availability, season, and research questions. Transect-based sampling, plot-based surveys, and opportunistic collection each serve different purposes
- **Seasonal timing**: Most temperate fungi fruit in autumn (September-November in the Northern Hemisphere), but many species fruit in spring or other seasons. Multi-season surveys are necessary for comprehensive inventories
- **Collection protocol**:
  - Photograph specimens in situ before collection (cap, gills/pores, stipe, habitat, substrate)
  - Collect complete specimens including the base of the stipe (important for identification)
  - Wrap each specimen individually in wax paper or aluminum foil (never plastic bags, which promote decomposition)
  - Record collection data: date, location (GPS coordinates), habitat, substrate, associated trees/plants, collector name, field number
  - Collect in duplicate when possible: one for immediate study, one for drying/herbarium
  - Use a field notebook or digital collection app (iNaturalist, MycoMap)

### Environmental DNA (eDNA) Sampling

Non-invasive methods to detect fungal presence from environmental samples:

- **Soil cores**: Collect soil samples (5-10 cm depth) and extract total DNA. Metabarcoding reveals the complete [[air-pollution-fungal-community-responses]] (mycobiome) present, including non-fruiting species
- **Air sampling**: Spore traps (Burkard volumetric sampler, Rotorod, passive settling plates) capture airborne spores for both allergen monitoring and [[fungal-biodiversity-assessment-methods]]
- **Water sampling**: For aquatic fungi; filter known volumes of water through sterile filters and extract DNA
- **Substrate sampling**: Collect small pieces of wood, leaf litter, or dung for eDNA extraction

### Habitat Documentation

- **Vegetation survey**: Record dominant tree species, canopy cover, understory composition
- **Soil analysis**: pH, moisture content, organic matter, nutrient levels
- **Microclimate**: Temperature, humidity, light levels (using data loggers for long-term studies)
- **Photopoints**: Permanent photo locations for monitoring temporal changes

## Culturing Techniques

### Aseptic Technique

All fungal culture work requires strict aseptic (sterile) technique to prevent contamination:

- Work in a laminar flow hood (Class II biological safety cabinet) whenever possible
- Sterilize work surfaces with 70% ethanol or 10% bleach between operations
- Flame sterilize inoculating loops, scalpels, and forceps before each use
- Wear gloves; change between handling different cultures
- Minimize time that culture vessels are open

### Media Preparation

Common [[fungal-culture-media]]:

- **Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA)**: General-purpose medium; supports growth of most fungi. 200g potato, 20g dextrose, 15g agar per liter. Autoclave at 121°C for 15 minutes
- **[[malt-extract-agar]] (MEA)**: Similar to PDA; good for mushroom-forming fungi. 30g malt extract, 15g agar per liter
- **Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA)**: Standard clinical mycology medium; acidic pH (5.6) inhibits bacterial growth. 40g dextrose, 10g peptone, 15g agar per liter
- **Cornmeal Agar (CMA)**: Promotes sporulation in many fungi; useful for microscopic identification
- **Minimal media**: Defined synthetic media for studying nutritional requirements and metabolic capabilities
- **Selective media**: Media with antibiotics (chloramphenicol, gentamicin) to suppress bacterial contamination; cycloheximide to inhibit [[saprotrophic-fungi]] when isolating pathogens

### Isolation Methods

- **[[spore-print]] isolation**: Collect spores on sterile paper or glass, then streak onto agar using a sterile loop. Individual spores germinate into pure cultures
- **Tissue culture**: Cut small pieces (2-3 mm) from the interior of a fresh fruiting body using a flame-sterilized scalpel. Place on agar surface. The interior tissue is typically sterile
- **Single [[single-spore-isolation-and-monokaryon-culture]]**: Dilute spore suspension to achieve ~1 spore per plate, or use micromanipulation under a dissecting microscope to transfer individual spores
- **Soil [[particle-filtration-dilution-plating-microfungal-isolation]]**: Serial dilution of soil samples plated on selective media for quantitative assessment of soil fungal populations
- **Baiting**: Place sterile substrates (hair, snake skin, insect exoskeleton, cellulose) in soil or water samples to bait specific fungi. Transfer growing hyphae to agar

### Culture Maintenance

- **Slant cultures**: Inoculate agar slants for long-term storage at 4°C (6-12 months)
- **Mineral oil overlay**: Cover slant cultures with sterile mineral oil to prevent desiccation (1-2 years)
- **Cryopreservation**: Store cultures in 15-25% glycerol at -80°C (decades). Most reliable long-term method
- **Lyophilization (freeze-drying)**: Suitable for spore-forming species; vacuum-dried ampoules stored at -20°C
- **Periodic subculturing**: For actively used strains; risk of genetic drift with repeated subculturing

## Microscopy

### Light Microscopy

Standard mycological microscopy uses compound microscopes (400-1000x magnification) with the following preparations:

- **Wet mounts**: Place specimen in a drop of water, lactic acid, or Melzer's reagent on a microscope slide. Cover with coverslip
- **Melzer's reagent**: Contains iodine and potassium iodide. Tests for amyloidy (blue-black), dextrinoidy (reddish-brown), or inamyloidy (no reaction)
- **KOH mounts**: 5-10% KOH dissolves dark pigments and clarifies tissues
- **Congo red**: Stains cell walls and hyphae for contrast
- **Cotton blue**: Binds to chitin in cell walls; general fungal stain

### Key Microscopic Features

- **Spores**: Size, shape, color, wall thickness, ornamentation (smooth, spiny, reticulate, verrucose), number per basidium, germ pore presence
- **Basidia**: Shape, size, number of sterigmata, presence/absence of clamp connections
- **Hyphae**: Diameter, presence of clamp connections, septation pattern, specialized hyphae (skeletals, binding hyphae, cystidia)
- **Cystidia**: Sterile cells on gill/pore faces (pleurocystidia) or edges (cheilocystidia). Shape, size, wall thickness, contents
- **Pileipellis (cap cuticle)**: Type of hyphal arrangement (cutis, trichoderm, ixocutis, hymeniderm)

### Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Provides high-resolution surface detail:

- Spore ornamentation at nanometer scale
- Hyphal surface features
- Hymenial surface topology
- Requires critical point drying or freeze-drying of specimens
- Gold or platinum coating for conductivity

### Fluorescence Microscopy

- Calcofluor white stains chitin and cellulose, fluorescing bright blue under UV excitation. Used for rapid detection of fungi in clinical specimens
- DAPI stains fungal nuclei for cell biology studies
- GFP-tagged strains for live-cell imaging of hyphal growth and interactions

## DNA Analysis

### DNA Extraction

- **CTAB method**: Standard protocol for fungi. CTAB lyses cells; chloroform removes proteins; isopropanol precipitates DNA
- **Commercial kits**: Qiagen DNeasy, Zymo Fungal/Bacterial kits — faster and more consistent but expensive
- **Mechanical disruption**: Bead beating with glass beads to break tough fungal cell walls, especially melanized fungi
- **Herbarium specimens**: Require modified protocols; degradation may limit DNA quality

### Molecular Identification

**PCR Amplification**:
- **ITS region (Internal Transcribed Spacer)**: The universal fungal barcode. Primers ITS1/ITS4 or ITS1F/ITS2 amplify the ITS1, 5.8S rRNA, and ITS2 regions. Matches to curated databases (UNITE, GenBank) provide species-level or genus-level identification
- **LSU (28S rRNA)**: Large subunit ribosomal RNA. Useful for higher-level (order, family, genus) identification
- **SSU (18S rRNA)**: Small subunit ribosomal RNA. Used for deep phylogenetic studies
- **RPB1/RPB2**: RNA polymerase II subunits. Multi-gene phylogenetic analyses for species delimitation and phylogenetic studies
- **TEF1-α**: Translation elongation factor 1-alpha. Additional phylogenetic marker for species-level resolution

- **DNA Sequencing**:
  - **Sanger sequencing**: Standard for single-locus identification. PCR product purified and sequenced bidirectionally. Cost-effective for routine identification
  - **Next-generation sequencing (NGS)**: Illumina MiSeq for metabarcoding; sequences hundreds of samples simultaneously using barcode-tagged primers
