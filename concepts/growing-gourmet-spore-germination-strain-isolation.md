## Spores vs. Cloning: Genetic Implications

A culture arising from cloning is fundamentally different
from a culture originating from spores. When spores are
germinated, many different strains are created, some
incompatible with one another. A cultivator will not know
what features will be expressed until each and every strain
is grown out to the final stage of mushroom production.

This form of genetic roulette results in very diverse
strains, some more desirable than others. Spores offer the
most diverse source of genetic characteristics, far more
than the phenotypic clone. If you want the greatest number
of strains, collect the spores. If you want to capture the
characteristics of the mushroom you have found, then clone.

## The Contamination Challenge

When a concentrated mass of spores is germinated, the
likelihood of bacteria and weed fungi infesting the site
is greatly increased. Bacteria replicate faster than
mushroom spores can germinate. As a result, the germinating
spores become infected. However, if [[macrofungal-documentation-photography-spore-prints]] are made
correctly from clean specimens, contamination is usually
not a problem.

Once inoculated, petri dish cultures should be taped with
an elastic film such as Parafilm to protect the incubating
mycelium from intrusive airborne contaminants.

## Purifying Cultures After Spore Germination

Many cultures originating from spores are associated with
other micro-organisms. Several techniques are available for
cleaning up a culture:

### Antibiotic Sandwich Method
For bacterially infested cultures, sandwich the mycelium
between two layers of media containing an antibiotic such
as gentamycin sulfate. The hyphae push through the media
while bacteria are left behind.

### pH Adjustment Method
If the culture is mixed with contaminant molds, adjust the
pH of the media to favor the [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]]. Many
contaminant fungi are strong acidophiles, whereas Oyster
mushrooms grow well near neutral pH.

### Barrier Race Method
Glass tubes can be filled with finely chopped, moistened
straw or wood sawdust and sterilized. The contaminated
culture is introduced to one end. The mushroom mycelium
races through the tube and, with luck, reaches the opposite
end first, where a sample of emerging mycelium is
transferred to fresh media plates.

### Repeated Subculturing
The rule is to immediately subculture all points of visible
growth away from one another as soon as they become visible.
This disperses colonies so they can be dealt with individually.
Repeated subculturing and dispersal usually results in success.
