# Grasslands and Range Management Strategies

In Chapter 10 of Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Mollison presents a
comprehensive framework for grasslands and range management in tropical
and subtropical environments. He frames this not as conventional
agricultural pasture management, but as a design challenge requiring
attention to water storage, soil structure, forage species diversity,
and the integration of trees with grazing systems.

## Core Management Strategies

Mollison identifies four primary management strategies for maintaining
productive grasslands and rangelands:

### 1. Addition of Forage Species to Grasslands

Forage species can be grass legumes or trees; the latter providing
foliage, food sugars, seed carbohydrates, or fruits. The aim is to
increase the diversity and nutritional quality of the available forage,
moving beyond monoculture grass pastures toward complex polycultures
that support a wider range of livestock species.

### 2. Environmental Changes

Changes in the physical environment are often necessary to support
productive grazing. These include water storage and soil structure
improvement through [[mollison-swale-design-details]], irrigation
systems, windbreak and shelter establishment via
[[mollison-windbreak-design]], and the addition of key fertiliser or
trace elements. Plant species can be maintained by slashing or light
cultivation of pasture.

### 3. Conservation of Forage

Forage conservation is achieved through rotational or periodic rests from
grazing, the production and storage of hays and silages, the supply of
protein or urea supplements and molasses in drought, and by keeping
stocking rates below worst-case conditions. This strategy acknowledges
the inherent variability of tropical climates and plans for deficits
rather than reacting to them.

### 4. Stock Management

Well-planned buying and selling keeps animal numbers in tune with
seasons and longer-term fluctuations. For example, timing calving or
buying in animals in spring or early summer, and selling them or dry
cows in autumn to lessen winter feed demand. At the extreme, stock can
be penned and fed harvested fodders. A sequence of species or a species
polyculture can be planted to take best advantage of seasonal forages.

## The Forage Deficit Problem

Mollison emphasises that in any grassland system there are inherent
periods of deficit. The classic pasture yield curve for mesothermal
(climate) regions shows two deficit periods:

- The late summer deficit arises from grass seeding, droughting, and
  drying-off of grasslands.
- The winter deficit comes from slow or no growth, with snow cover or
  blizzards affecting herd grazing times.

For the summer period, green leaf from forage tree plantations largely
overcomes the shortage. However, evergreen forage trees may not
withstand a hard frost if lopped in the last month of autumn. Species
such as tagasaste need mature leaf to carry into frost periods and
should not be winter-lopped. Deciduous species like willow and poplar
are also summer leaf forage, and chips of their wood can be fermented
to high-value winter concentrates.

## Grassland and Orchard Integration

The leaf swards valued by graziers may also suit green-crop cover for
orchardists where regular slashing for easy fruit harvest is practiced.
In these cases it is essential that the orchard crop is well established
using manures before twining legumes are planted. Soils under slashed
pastures are of excellent structure, and erosion is effectively stopped.

This integration connects [[mollison-orchard-poultry-integration-and-woodlot-design]]
with broader [[mollison-animal-range-management]].

## Grassland Establishment Sequence

Mollison outlines a step-by-step process for establishing perennial
grass swards on weedy or eroded areas, a process that takes one to three
years:

1. Choose a land-forming system such as swaling, interception banks, or
   pitting. Establish dams for irrigation above good soil types.

2. Sow pioneer grasses such as molasses grass or Sorghum almum mixed
   with sawdust into slashed weeds, or drill selected grass and legumes
   after slashing.

3. Burn molasses grass or drill selected perennials and broadcast
   Sorghum almum as a companion pioneer.

4. Concurrently with earth-forming, plant leguminous trees along swales
   at 30-100 metre strip spacing through the area to be grassed. Allow
   2-3 years to grow with light grazing to year 3.

5. Commence managed rotational grazing, and drill or broadcast forage
   legumes into established grasses. About 15-18 fields are necessary
   for rotation.

## Sustainable Livestock Density

Mollison argues that good managers allow a 2-9 year rotation of herds,
using 15-18 fields or runs, and pay close attention to observed
regeneration of browse. The longer (9 year) period is used on subhumid
desert borders, the shorter period on humid lowlands. The best strategy
is to balance browsing species with plant species, to allow rest periods
on range, and to avoid fixed stocking of animals on any one part of the
range.

## Philosophy of Mixed Systems

Mollison repeatedly emphasises that all domestic species systems
relating to "livestock only" farming are far more simple and less
sustainable than the mixed farm situation of crop, forest, marsh, and a
variety of livestock balanced to forage wastes. Intensive broadscale
systems call for energy and government subsidy, and are political rather
than environmental systems.

The net product of a grassland in a cool humid climate has a bimodal
growth curve with two deficit periods, always a feature of open
grasslands. This contrasts with the stability of diverse natural systems
where browsers, grazers, and mixed feeders each have specific food
preferences and seasonal patterns.
