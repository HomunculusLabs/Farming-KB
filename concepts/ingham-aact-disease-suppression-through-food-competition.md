# Disease Suppression Through Food Competition and Niche Exclusion

Elaine Ingham's Field Guide for Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) presents a fundamentally different paradigm for managing plant disease: rather than killing pathogens with chemicals or fungicides, AACT works by establishing a robust community of beneficial microorganisms that **outcompete** disease-causing organisms for food and space. This approach leverages the soil food web's natural competitive dynamics to suppress disease without any direct killing action.

## The Core Principle: AACT Does Not Kill

Ingham's central thesis is that properly brewed AACT does not contain any organism that kills or attacks pathogens directly. Instead, the tea introduces massive populations of beneficial bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes that occupy available niches on leaf surfaces, in the root zone, and throughout the rhizosphere. Disease suppression is achieved entirely through **competitive exclusion**: beneficial organisms consume the food resources that pathogens need to survive and reproduce, leaving pathogens starved and unable to establish populations large enough to cause infection.

This distinction is critical. Chemical fungicides and bactericides work by poisoning the target organism, but they often harm beneficial organisms simultaneously, creating a biological vacuum that pathogens can re-colonize rapidly. AACT, by contrast, fills every available niche with beneficial life, making it extremely difficult for pathogenic organisms to gain a foothold.

## The Two Control Points for Disease Organisms

Ingham identifies two leverage points for controlling disease organisms:

1. **The disease organism itself**: Directly reducing pathogen populations (the approach taken by conventional pesticides).
2. **The food the disease organism needs**: Removing or pre-empting the food resources that pathogens require to grow, reproduce, and infect plant tissue.

AACT targets the second control point. By flooding the plant's surfaces and root zone with beneficial organisms that consume exudates, leaf surface sugars, decomposing organic matter, and other resources, AACT removes the nutritional foundation that pathogens depend on. A pathogen landing on a leaf treated with AACT finds no available food and cannot multiply to infectious levels.

## How Beneficial Organisms Outcompete Pathogens

Beneficial bacteria and fungi applied through AACT rapidly colonize plant surfaces and the soil around roots. They consume:

- **Plant exudates**: Sugars, amino acids, and carbohydrates exuded by roots and leaves.
- **Leaf surface resources**: Pollen, dust, honeydew from insects, and other organic deposits on foliar surfaces.
- **Decomposing organic matter**: Dead plant tissue and organic residues in the soil.

By consuming these resources faster than pathogens can access them, the beneficial community creates a state of resource deprivation for disease organisms. The pathogens may still be present in the environment, but they cannot reach population densities sufficient to cause disease symptoms.

## Food Web Succession: Bacteria First, Then Fungi

Ingham's succession model explains how different microbial communities dominate at different stages. In disturbed or immature systems, bacterial dominance is typical. As systems mature, fungal communities become more established. AACT can be tailored to shift this balance by adjusting the brewing recipe:

- **Bacterial-dominant teas**: High in simple sugars (see [[ingham-molasses-types-brewing-effects]]), these promote rapid bacterial colonization. Useful for annual crops, vegetables, and early-season applications where fast resource capture is needed.
- **Fungal-dominant teas**: Brewed with fungal food sources and longer extraction times, these favor beneficial saprophytic fungi. Essential for perennials, trees, and grass systems where fungal pathogens like fusarium and rhizoctonia are threats.

The succession model means that timing matters. Early-season applications often begin with bacterial teas to establish a baseline of resource competition, followed by fungal teas as the season progresses and disease pressure from fungal pathogens increases.

## Specific Disease Cases from the Field Guide

Ingham's Field Guide documents AACT's effectiveness against numerous diseases through competitive exclusion:

### Foliar Diseases

- **Black spot (Diplocarpon rosae)**: On roses, regular foliar applications of bacterial-dominant AACT consume the leaf surface exudates that black spot spores require for germination. Applications every 7 to 14 days during the growing season maintain sufficient beneficial coverage.
- **Powdery mildew**: AACT coats leaf surfaces with beneficial organisms that occupy the sites where powdery mildew would normally establish. Fungal-dominant teas are particularly effective, as beneficial fungi compete directly with the mildew organism for leaf surface resources.
- **Late blight (Phytophthora infestans)**: On tomatoes and potatoes, AACT applications to foliage create a biological barrier. The beneficial organisms consume leaf surface moisture and nutrients, reducing the conditions that late blight sporangia need to germinate and penetrate leaf tissue.
- **Anthracnose**: Foliar and fruit applications of AACT help beneficial organisms colonize fruit surfaces and leaf tissue, consuming exudates that anthracnose spores depend on for infection.
- **Sooty mold**: By supporting healthy plant metabolism and reducing the honeydew deposits that sooty mold grows on (indirectly, through overall plant health improvement), AACT reduces sooty mold establishment.

### Soil-Borne Diseases

- **Fusarium wilt**: AACT applied as a soil drench introduces beneficial fungi and bacteria that compete with Fusarium for root exudates and organic matter in the root zone. Fungal-dominant teas are particularly important here, as beneficial mycorrhizal and saprophytic fungi occupy root surfaces and prevent Fusarium colonization.
- **Rhizoctonia**: Similar competitive dynamics apply. Beneficial organisms in AACT consume the resources in the soil that Rhizoctonia needs for damping-off and root rot. Early-season soil drenches are critical for protecting seedlings.
- **Verticillium wilt**: AACT soil applications build a diverse root-zone community that pre-empts Verticillium. The key is maintaining aerobic conditions, as Verticillium and other wilt pathogens are often favored by anaerobic soil conditions.
- **Brown rot**: On stone fruits, AACT applied to the soil around the root zone supports overall tree health, while foliar applications to blossoms and developing fruit help beneficial organisms compete with brown rot spores on fruit surfaces.

## Foliar Application Techniques

Ingham provides specific guidance for foliar AACT application to maximize disease suppression through competitive exclusion:

- **Coverage is critical**: The tea must thoroughly coat all leaf surfaces, including undersides, stems, and fruit. Incomplete coverage leaves niches open for pathogen establishment.
- **Application timing**: Apply in the early morning or late evening to avoid UV damage to the microbial community. UV light can kill exposed organisms within hours.
- **Frequency**: For active disease pressure, apply every 7 to 10 days. For preventative programs, every 14 days is often sufficient.
- **Adjuvants**: A small amount of surfactant or humic acid can help the tea spread and adhere to leaf surfaces, improving coverage and persistence.
- **Application volume**: Typically 15 to 30 gallons per acre as a foliar spray, adjusted based on crop canopy density.

Proper brewing technique is essential. See [[ingham-aact-brewing-techniques]] and [[ingham-dissolved-oxygen-management]] for ensuring the tea contains live, active organisms at the time of application.

## Brix Levels and Disease Resistance

Ingham draws a direct connection between plant brix (sugar content as measured by a refractometer) and disease resistance. Plants with higher brix readings produce more complex carbohydrates and secondary metabolites that are less accessible to pathogens. The relationship works through the soil food web:

1. Healthy soil biology delivers balanced nutrition to plants.
2. Well-nourished plants produce abundant, complex sugars and phenolic compounds.
3. These compounds are difficult for many pathogens to metabolize.
4. Higher brix readings correlate with thicker cell walls, stronger cuticles, and more robust immune responses.

AACT supports this cycle by building the soil food web that feeds plants properly. Foliar AACT applications can also directly boost leaf brix by supplying beneficial organisms that stimulate plant metabolism.

## Healthy Soil Biology Reduces Pest Pressure

Ingham extends the competitive exclusion principle beyond disease to insect pests. Plants growing in biologically active soil produce different chemical profiles than stressed plants. Insect pests use chemical cues to locate suitable host plants. When soil biology is healthy and plants are well-nourished:

- Plants emit fewer stress volatiles that attract pests.
- Plant tissues contain higher levels of defensive compounds that deter feeding.
- The overall plant canopy supports a more diverse community of beneficial insects that prey on pests.

This does not mean AACT acts as an insecticide. Rather, by supporting complete plant health through balanced soil biology, AACT creates conditions where pest pressure naturally diminishes.

## AACT vs. Chemical and Fungicidal Approaches

The distinction between AACT and conventional disease management is foundational to Ingham's approach:

| Aspect | Chemical/Fungicidal | AACT |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Direct killing of target organisms | Competitive exclusion via resource consumption |
| Effect on beneficials | Often harmful; broad-spectrum products kill non-target organisms | Supports and amplifies beneficial communities |
| Resistance risk | Pathogens can develop resistance to chemicals | No resistance possible; competition is an ecological process |
| Residual effect | Breaks down over time; repeated applications needed | Establishes self-sustaining biological communities |
| Soil health impact | Often degrades soil biology over time | Builds soil biology with each application |
| Cost trajectory | Input costs tend to increase over time | Input costs decrease as biological systems mature |

## The Role of Aerobic Conditions

Ingham emphasizes that anaerobic organisms are the real problem in most disease situations. Pathogens and disease-causing organisms are predominantly anaerobes or facultative anaerobes. AACT, by maintaining dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L throughout the brewing process (see [[ingham-dissolved-oxygen-management]]), ensures that only aerobic and facultatively aerobic beneficial organisms are produced. When this aerobic community is applied to soil and foliage, it pushes the local environment away from anaerobic conditions, further disadvantaging pathogenic organisms.

## Summary

Ingham's disease suppression model through AACT is fundamentally ecological rather than pharmaceutical. By understanding that disease organisms require food and suitable conditions to cause infection, growers can shift their strategy from killing pathogens to starving them. AACT provides the beneficial microbial workforce that pre-empts pathogen access to plant resources, maintaining aerobic conditions and occupying every available niche with competitive, beneficial life. The result is disease suppression that strengthens with each application rather than degrading, building lasting plant health through the power of the soil food web.
