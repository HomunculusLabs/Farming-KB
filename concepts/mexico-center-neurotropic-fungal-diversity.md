## Mexico as Center of Neurotropic Fungal Diversity

## Unmatched Species Richness

Mexico is the single most important country in the world for neurotropic
fungal diversity, with 76 documented species representing approximately
35% of all known hallucinogenic fungi. This includes 44 species of
Psilocybe (38% of the world total), making Mexico the center of diversity
for the largest genus of neurotropic mushrooms. The significance of this
concentration is magnified by the fact that many Mexican species are
endemic, found nowhere else on Earth.

## Indigenous Cultural Heritage

The importance of Mexico in the world of neurotropic fungi extends far
beyond species counts. Mexico is the cradle of sacred mushroom use in the
Americas, with a continuous tradition spanning millennia. The Aztecs used
teonanacatl ("flesh of the gods") in religious ceremonies, as documented
by Bernardino de Sahagun in the 16th century. Contemporary indigenous
groups including the Mazatec, Zapotec, Mixtec, Chinantec, Mixe, Nahua, and
Otomie continue to use neurotropic fungi in divination and healing rituals.

## Species of Particular Cultural Importance

Several Mexican Psilocybe species are deeply intertwined with indigenous
practices. [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America, reaches
its southern limit in northeastern Mexico via mesophytic forests associated
with Fagus (beech). Conversely, P. yungensis and P. subyungensis, primarily
[[gymnopilus-spectabilis-entity]], G. liquiritiae, G.
sapineus, G. lateritius, and G. subpurpuratus are documented. Hypholoma
naematoliformis and H. rhombispora are tropical species described from
Mexico. Conocybe siligineoides, used by the Mazatec, is among the most
important non-Psilocybe species.

## Gasteromycetes and Sacred Fungi

Mexico is notable for the use of gasteromycetes (puffballs and related
fungi) as sacred fungi. Lycoperdon candidum, L. oblongisporum, Vascellum
intermedium, V. pratense, and V. qudenii have all been used by indigenous
peoples. Dictyophora indusiata, the "veiled lady" or "stinkhorn" mushroom,
has a documented history of use in Mexico and Asia. These fungi lack
confirmed neurotropic chemistry but are culturally significant.

## Church of the Sacred Mushroom

A unique cultural phenomenon documented in Mexico is the church dedicated
to the worship of sacred mushrooms, "Nuestro Senor del Honguito" (Our Lord
of the Little Mushroom), in Chignahuapan, Puebla. This represents one of
the few instances of formalized religious veneration of fungi, blending
indigenous [[sacred-mushroom-traditions-in-mexico]] with Catholic iconography.
