# Permaculture Guild Design Method

Guild design is the art and science of creating plant communities where species support each other, reducing the gardener"s work while increasing yields and ecological health. Toby Hemenway devotes two full chapters in Gaia's Garden to guild design, presenting it as the core technique for assembling productive, self-maintaining gardens.

## What Is a Guild?

A guild is a carefully designed group of plants (and sometimes animals) that work together like a natural ecosystem. Each member performs multiple functions, and the connections between members create a resilient, self-regulating community. Guilds are human-made assemblages that mimic natural plant communities.

## The Roles Within a Guild

Hemenway identifies seven essential roles that should be filled in any guild:

### 1. Food Producers (Central Element)
The primary crop -- usually a fruit or nut tree -- that the guild is designed to support. This is the "anchor" of the guild around which all other elements are arranged.

### 2. Nitrogen Fixers
Plants that convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms available to other plants, reducing or eliminating the need for imported fertilizers:
- Perennials preferred: Dutch or New Zealand white clover, alfalfa, lupine
- Edible options: fava beans, cowpeas, peas
- Shrubs: buffaloberry, ceanothus, indigo, Russian olive
- Trees: black locust, alder, mesquite, tagasaste, carob (see [[permaculture-nitrogen-fixing-trees]])

### 3. Nutrient Accumulators (Dynamic Accumulators)
Plants with deep taproots that mine minerals from subsoil and bring them to the surface in their leaves:
- Chicory, dandelion, yarrow, plantain, comfrey
- As leaves are slashed and decompose, these minerals become available to shallow-rooted plants (see [[permaculture-dynamic-accumulator-plants]])

### 4. Mulch Plants
Species that produce abundant biomass for in-place composting:
- Comfrey, artichokes, cardoon, rhubarb, clovers, nasturtiums
- Slashed periodically and left to decompose around the central tree
- A ring of comfrey around a fruit tree can be cut 4-5 times per summer

### 5. Insectary Plants
Species that attract pollinators and beneficial predatory insects:
- Umbellifers: fennel, dill, coriander, celery, parsley (flowers)
- Composite flowers: yarrow, calendula, chamomile, cosmos
- Members of the mint family: bee balm, lavender, rosemary
- Borage is especially valued for attracting bees and other pollinators

### 6. Pest Repellents
Plants that exude substances repelling harmful insects:
- Nasturtiums, certain marigolds, garlic, alliums
- These are the least well-understood guild members; use with caution as they may also repel beneficials

### 7. Weed Suppressors / Grass Barriers
Dense-rooted plants that prevent invasive species from invading the guild:
- Comfrey, Jerusalem artichoke, lemongrass, Maximilian sunflower
- These create a wall of thick growth above and below ground

### 8. Habitat Creators
Piled rocks, logs, brush, and small water features that shelter lizards, frogs, snakes, and birds -- the predators that keep pest populations in check.

## Two Methods for Designing Guilds

### Method 1: Observation of Natural Communities

This approach requires spending time in intact native plant communities:

1. Identify local plant communities (oak-hickory woodland, chaparral, etc.)
2. Observe which species grow together naturally
3. Note the functions each species performs
4. Identify the patterns of interaction between species
5. Substitute related edible or useful species for the native ones while maintaining the same structural roles and relationships

This method produces guilds that are deeply adapted to local conditions.

### Method 2: The Research-Based "Paint-by-Numbers" Approach

For those without time for extended field observation:

1. **Choose the central element**: Your primary food-producing tree or shrub
2. **List the functions needed**: Nitrogen fixation, pest control, pollination, mulch, etc.
3. **Research candidate plants** for each function that are compatible with your climate and the central plant
4. **Check compatibility**: Ensure plants don't compete excessively for light, water, or nutrients
5. **Cross-reference**: Check companion planting references and pest/predator relationships
6. **Design the spatial arrangement**: Place tall plants to the north (in Northern Hemisphere), shade-tolerant plants under the canopy, and spread roots to avoid direct competition

## Design Principles for Guilds

### Multiple Functions Per Element

Choose plants that serve more than one role. Comfrey is the archetype -- it produces mulch, attracts insects, accumulates potassium and minerals, provides medicine, and can be made into liquid fertilizer. Clever guild designers select as many multifunctional members as possible.

### Appropriate Numbers

The larger the plant, the fewer needed:
- 1 central tree
- 1-2 large supporting plants (artichokes, shrubs)
- Several medium plants (comfrey, fennel)
- Dozens of small plants (bulbs, insectary flowers)
- Hundreds of ground covers (clover)

### Spatial Arrangement

- Place the tallest elements on the north side (Southern Hemisphere: south side) to avoid shading other plants
- Underplant trees with shade-tolerant species
- Create vertical layers to use all available space
- Leave access paths for harvest and maintenance
- Consider mature sizes, not just planting sizes

### Evolve Over Time

Guilds are not static. As the central tree grows, light conditions change, and the guild composition should evolve accordingly. Some plants will naturally decline as conditions shift; the gardener can accelerate this by replacing them with more appropriate species.

## Example: Apple Tree Guild

A classic guild described by Hemenway:

- **Central**: Semi-dwarf apple tree
- **Nitrogen fixers**: Clover ground cover beneath, fava beans in spring
- **Accumulators**: Chicory, dandelion, yarrow, plantain
- **Mulch plants**: Comfrey ring around the drip line
- **Insectary plants**: Fennel, dill, coriander, yarrow flowers, borage
- **Pest repellents**: Nasturtiums, garlic chives
- **Bulbs**: Daffodils and garlic around the perimeter (deter deer and rodents)
- **Habitat**: Rock piles for lizards and snakes
