# Fire-Resistant Landscape Design
Bill Mollison addresses fire in [[permaculture-mapping-and-reading-the-landscape]] extensively in Chapter 5
(Climatic Factors) and Chapter 14 of Permaculture: A Designers' Manual.
He treats wildfire not as an external threat to be fought but as a
landscape process that must be understood, anticipated, and designed for.
Fire-resilient design is presented as an essential component of
permaculture planning in fire-prone regions.
## Understanding Fire Behaviour
Mollison begins by explaining the factors that determine fire behaviour:
- Fuel load: The quantity and arrangement of combustible material. Fine
  fuels (grass, leaves, twigs less than 6mm diameter) ignite easily and
  spread fire rapidly. Heavy fuels (logs, branches) burn longer and
  hotter but are harder to ignite.
- Fuel continuity: Fire spreads where fuels are continuous -- ground
  fuels connecting to shrub fuels connecting to canopy fuels create
  "fuel ladders" that allow ground fires to climb into tree canopies.
- Slope: Fire travels faster uphill. A fire on a 20-degree slope travels
  approximately twice as fast as on flat ground; on a 40-degree slope,
  approximately four times as fast.
- Wind: Wind-driven fires are the most dangerous and unpredictable.
  Wind increases oxygen supply, preheats fuels ahead of the flame front,
  and can carry burning embers kilometres ahead of the main fire.
- Moisture content: Green, living vegetation with high moisture content
  is significantly more resistant to ignition than dry dead material.
- Weather: Temperature, relative humidity, and atmospheric stability
  affect fire intensity and behaviour. Hot, dry, windy conditions create
  extreme fire danger.
## Defensible Space Zones
Mollison adapts the concept of defensible space around buildings into a
permaculture zoning framework:
### Zone 1: Immediate Building Perimeter (0 to 5 metres)
This is the most critical zone for protecting structures. Design
requirements:
- Remove all flammable vegetation within 3 to 5 metres of buildings
- Use non-combustible [[mollison-designers-living-mulches-ground-covers]]: gravel, stone, concrete, brick
  paving, or closely mown green lawns (maintained and irrigated)
- Store firewood, fuel, and combustible materials well away from
  buildings (minimum 10 metres, preferably 20+ metres)
- Install non-combustible fencing (metal, masonry) rather than wooden
  fences that can act as fire fuses leading flames directly to buildings
- Ensure roof and gutters are clear of leaves and debris
- Use fire-resistant [[mollison-designers-cool-climate-house-design]]:
  metal roofs, masonry walls, sealed eaves, and screened vents to
  prevent ember entry
### Zone 2: Inner Protection Zone (5 to 30 metres)
The goal is to reduce fire intensity and prevent flame contact with
buildings:
- Remove lower limbs of trees to eliminate fuel ladders (limbs should
  be pruned to at least 2 metres above ground)
- Space trees so that canopies do not touch (crown separation of at
  least 3 to 5 metres)
- Replace highly flammable shrubs with fire-resistant species
- Maintain lawns or low ground covers, mowed regularly
- Remove dead wood, [[leaf-litter-microfungal-diversity-tropical-temperate]], and accumulated debris
- Retain isolated specimen trees with cleared, low-fuel understories
### Zone 3: Outer Management Zone (30 to 100+ metres)
This zone provides strategic fuel reduction and firebreak function:
- Reduce overall fuel load through controlled grazing, selective clearing,
  or prescribed burning
- Maintain fuel breaks: grazed corridors, mowed strips, or cleared lines
  that slow fire spread and provide access for firefighting
- Retain some larger trees for wind reduction and shade, but thin
  dense stands to reduce canopy continuity
- Manage grazing animals (cattle, goats, sheep) to keep grass and
  understorey fuels reduced
## Fuel Reduction Strategies
Mollison describes multiple approaches to reducing fuel loads:
- Controlled burning: Prescribed fires conducted under safe conditions
  (cool, calm, moist weather) to reduce accumulated fuels. Requires
  skill, planning, and appropriate weather windows.
- [[permaculture-designers-manual-grazing-management]]: Livestock (particularly goats and cattle) consume
  grass and shrub fuels. Rotational grazing through fire-prone areas
  before the ficompostingis highly effective.
- Mechanical clearing: Slashing, mowing, or mulching to reduce fuel
  height and continuity. Best suited to Zone 1 and Zone 2.
- Strategic pruning: Removing lower branches, dead wood, and ladder
  fuels from retained trees.
- Composting and mulching: Converting cleared vegetation to compost or
  sheet mulch rather than burning it in piles.
## Species Selection
Mollison emphasises that plant [[mollison-designers-mixed-orchard-design-species-selection]] is one of the most
powerful tools for fire-resistant landscape design:
### Fire-Resistant Species (Low Flammability)
These species have high moisture content, low volatile oil content,
thick bark, or other fire-adapted characteristics:
- Succulents: Aloe species, Sedum, Carpobrotus (pigface) -- very high
  water content makes them virtually non-flammable
- Deciduous trees: Many deciduous species are relatively fire-resistant
  when in leaf due to high moisture content; bare winter branches are
  less of a concern in Mediterranean climates where fire season
  coincides with summer leaf-on period
- Broad-leaved evergreens with high moisture content: Laurus (bay
  laurel), Arbutus (strawberry tree), Quercus ilex (holm oak in some
  conditions)
- Ground covers: Some low-growing species maintain high moisture and
  resist ignition
### Fire-Susceptible Species (High Flammability)
These should be avoided near buildings or carefully managed:
- Resinous conifers: Pinus (pine), Cupressus (cypress), Juniperus
  (juniper) -- contain volatile oils and resins that burn intensely
- Eucalyptus: Many species have highly flammable bark, leaves, and
  volatile oils. Eucalypts drop copious bark and leaf litter that
  accumulates as deep, aerated fuel beds
- Heaths and heathers (Ericaceae family): contain flammable oils
- Dry grasses: Annual grasses that cure (dry) in summer provide fine
  fuel that ignites easily and spreads rapidly
- Gorse (Ulex) and broom (Cytisus): Dense, resinous shrubs that burn
  with extreme intensity
Mollison notes that native vegetation in fire-prone regions has evolved
with fire and may require fire for regeneration. The goal is not to
eliminate all flammable species from the landscape but to manage their
distribution and proximity to settlements.
## Ember Barriers and Building Protection
Embers (spotting) are a primary mechanism of house loss during wildfires.
Embers can travel kilometres ahead of the fire front and ignite buildings
through:
- Accumulation in gutters and on roofs
- Entry through unscreened vents, eaves, and gaps
- Ignition of combustible materials stored against or near buildings
- Ignition of wooden decks, furniture, and fences
