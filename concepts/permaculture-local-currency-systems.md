# Permaculture Local Currency Systems

updated: 2026-04-18

Local currency systems are community-issued money backed by real resources that circulates indefinitely within a community, providing a constantly available resource for trade. Unlike conventional money that derives from external agencies and accumulates in cities and multinational banks, community money cannot be used outside its community and thus stays in local circulation.

## Preconditions for Valid Currency

1. **Backed by real resources**: Timber, clean water, or other locally available assets
2. **Community confidence**: Transparent reserves that are publicly available and assessed
3. **Demand for use**: A real need for goods or services lacking locally

## Steps to Establish Local Currency

### 1. Find and Lease a Reserve Resource
The currency is based on something redeemable: "a cord of timber, cut and stacked in the woodyard" or "a gallon of water bottled and redeemable at a certain place." Notes can exceed reserves if used for procreative assets only (e.g., forests), which represent increasing wealth.

### 2. Print the Currency
Preferably in a solid local material difficult to duplicate. Numbered, dated, and counted. Value should not exceed reserve value by more than 3 times. Notes can be dated and reissued every 4-5 years to prevent hoarding and check forgeries.

### 3. Educate Local Businesses
Teach businesses and banks to accept and use the currency, which can be exchanged for national currency at published rates.

### 4. Start Satisfying Demand
Lend currency to builders, farmers, and small businesses. Borrowers repay in local currency, creating demand.

### 5. Local-Only Acceptance
Producers of local goods accept local currency only, creating external demand. Other regions must "buy" the currency to obtain local goods.

## Evolution of Money Systems

Mollison outlines a progression from informal barter through to sophisticated community financial institutions:

1. **Informal local work and goods exchange**
2. **Small regional exchange**
3. **Multiple LET Systems for bioregion**
4. **Nationwide exchange, publications, and services**
5. **Local currency mint**
6. **Ethical stock exchange**

## Historical Precedent

Many small towns in the 1930s funded their public works through local currency systems. The E.F. Schumacher Society in Great Barrington, Massachusetts has data on these systems and operates one.

## Biological Reserves as Wealth

If reserves are living things such as trees, wealth increases and can be created over time. Even if currency was originally based on bricks, it can be used to create biological reserves (forests) that grow in value. Forests represent firewood but reserves are never cut except in dire emergencies.

## Currency Diagram

A local currency system needs:
- Stored wealth (forest, water, stone, biological stocks)
- A small amount available for redemption or trade at specified times
- A "community mint" with money reserved for reprinting
- Local suppliers and exchange rates published by the bank
- Community financial institutions
- A mechanism for trade outside the region at published exchange rates

## LETS (Local Exchange Trading Systems)
LETS systems are a practical implementation of local currency principles
that have been widely adopted since the 1980s. In a LETS, members list
goods and services they can offer and goods and services they need.
Transactions are recorded in a central accounting system using a local
unit of account (not physical currency). Every transaction is a
two-sided entry: one member's credit is another's debit. Because the
system is mutual credit rather than commodity-backed, no reserves are
needed and the money supply automatically adjusts to meet demand. LETS
have been established in hundreds of communities worldwide, with varying
degrees of success. The most successful systems tend to be those
integrated with [[permaculture-community-scale-design]]
where face-to-face relationships provide the trust foundation that
formal currency requires.

## Examples of Successful Local Currencies
Several historical and contemporary examples demonstrate the viability
of community currencies. The Worgl experiment in Austria (1932) issued
stamped scrip that depreciated monthly, encouraging rapid circulation
and funding public works during the Great Depression. Unemployment
dropped 25% within a year before the Austrian central bank shut it
down. The Ithaca Hours system in Ithaca, New York (1991-present) issues
paper notes denominated in hours of labour. The Chiemgauer in Bavaria
(2003-present) loses 2% of its value quarterly if not re-stamped,
mimicking natural depreciation and preventing hoarding.

## Demurrage: The Key Innovation
Demurrage -- a deliberate loss of value over time -- is perhaps the
most important design feature of local currencies. Unlike national
currencies that reward hoarding (through interest), demurrage-bearing
currencies penalise holding and incentivise circulation. Silvio Gesell
developed this concept in the early 20th century, arguing that money
should function like a railway ticket -- useful when in circulation
but costly to hold idle. Mollison recommends dating and reissuing
notes every 4-5 years to achieve a similar effect.

## Connection to Bioregional Organisation
Local currency systems are a financial expression of [[mollison-bioregional-organisation-and-resource-index]]. Just as bioregionalism asks
people to identify with their natural watershed and ecosystem rather
than arbitrary political boundaries, local currencies ask them to invest
their economic energy locally. A bioregional currency backed by regional
products (timber, water, fibre, food) creates a direct economic link
between ecological health and community prosperity. When [[community-finance-and-alternative-economies]] is grounded in
real, local, biological resources, speculation becomes impossible by
design.

## Challenges and Limitations
Implementing local currency systems faces several practical challenges.
Critical mass is essential: a currency needs enough participants and
enough goods and services available to be genuinely useful. Tax
treatment is often unclear, as governments may not recognise local
currencies for tax purposes or may treat them as barter. Scalability
is limited by definition -- the system works best at the community or
bioregional scale. Counterfeiting and trust erosion are ongoing risks
that require community governance structures. Despite these challenges,
local currencies remain one of the most powerful tools in the
[[pdc-right-livelihood-and-permaculture-economics]] for building community resilience and reducing dependence on
external financial systems.

## Time Banking and Labour Exchange
Time banking represents another approach to local exchange that
complements commodity-backed currencies. In a time bank, one hour of
any person's time is valued equally with one hour of any other
person's time. This egalitarian principle means that an hour of
gardening, an hour of childcare, and an hour of plumbing are all
exchangeable at a 1:1 rate. In the [[permaculture-designers-manual-bioregional-planning]] context, time banks are
particularly effective because they recognise the inherent value of
all community contributions, including those (such as ecological
restoration) that are difficult to price in conventional markets.
