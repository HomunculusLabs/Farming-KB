# Drip Irrigation and Water Management

[[water-management]] in the [[coleman-claytonia-winter-harvest-cultivation]] system is fundamentally different
from summer growing. The cold, low-light environment of an unheated
greenhouse dramatically reduces evaporation, and the freeze-thaw cycles
of winter create unique challenges for irrigation. Coleman's approach
balances the need for consistent soil moisture with the risks of
overwatering in cold conditions.

## Winter Irrigation Practices

Coleman traditionally did not water in the greenhouses from about
mid-November to early March, since the soil seemed moist enough and
evaporation levels were low along with the low sun angle. However, a
[[fukuoka-textdoc-pesticide-creates-pest-problem-citrus-orchard]] led him to revise this practice. When aphids appeared on
spinach starting in early to mid-February, research showed that nitrogen
levels in the soil were the main factor in aphid multiplication. Nitrogen
can build up in the soil when there has been no rain or irrigation to
flush it through. Coleman's solution was to begin irrigating thoroughly
once a week on sunny mornings starting in mid-January. This flushed out
excess nitrogen and the aphid problem disappeared. The extra moisture
did not cause any new problems, and he continues the practice.

## Irrigation for Weed Management

Irrigation plays a strategic role in Coleman's [[fukuoka-textdoc-weed-control-with-weeds-rice-barley-clover-discovery]] program. When
practicing pre-emergence flaming, the seedbed may be irrigated during the
pre-sowing period to encourage weed-seed germination. The weeds are then
flamed off before the crop emerges. Coleman notes that irrigating after
sowing can wash slightly covered seeds back to the surface, so timing
matters. Water is also applied to transplants immediately after setting
them out, especially on dry or windy days, to reduce transplant stress
and improve resistance to pests like cucumber beetles.

## The French Irrigation Heritage

The historical [[parisian-market-gardening]] gardeners also understood efficient water
delivery. At [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]]'s garden in the 1970s, a third rail cart
carried the irrigation system. It had long arms sticking out to either
side, dispensing water through nozzles, and was self-propelled by water
pressure as it towed the hose behind itself down the lightweight rail
track. This elegant solution combined irrigation with the space-efficient
transport system that gave access to all parts of the intensively cropped
garden.

## Water Quality and Testing

All irrigation and wash water at [[coleman-four-season-farm]] comes from a drilled
well that is tested annually. Water quality is particularly important for
a farm selling fresh, raw foods. The [[coleman-winter-harvest-washing-and-packing]] system must be
prevented from freezing, which influences [[passive-solar-design]]. One-quarter
of the original washing and packing greenhouse had a concrete floor with
space for the washing and packing operation, requiring protection from
freezing temperatures.

## Evaporation and Freeze Protection

Evaporation is a cooling process, and in winter this matters
significantly. Water left on the soil surface can lower soil
temperatures, potentially inhibiting seed germination and plant growth.
Coleman notes that if evaporation is allowed to proceed unchecked, it can
freeze up the soil surface. Managing soil moisture carefully to prevent
excess surface water while maintaining adequate subsurface moisture is a
key skill for the winter grower.

The freeze-thaw cycle also affects irrigation infrastructure. Hose and
pipe connections must be drained or protected during the coldest periods.
The temporary nature of some winter watering needs means that portable or
easily drained systems may be preferable to permanently installed drip
lines [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]].
