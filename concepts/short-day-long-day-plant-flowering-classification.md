# Short-Day and Long-Day Plant Flowering Classification

Plants are classified by their [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] to photoperiod (day length) into
three major categories: short-day plants, long-day plants, and day-neutral plants.
This classification, arising from the work of Wightman W. Garner and Harry A.
Allard in 1918, is fundamental to both plant biology and [[chrysanthemum-night-interruption-flowering-commercial-horticulture]].

## Short-Day Plants (SDP)

Short-day plants flower when the duration of continuous darkness exceeds a critical
threshold — that is, when days are short and nights are long. These plants
typically flower in late summer or autumn as days naturally shorten. Examples
include chrysanthemums, soybeans, poinsettias, strawberries, and cannabis
(photoperiod-sensitive cultivars). The critical night length varies by species;
for soybeans it is approximately 10 hours of darkness, while for chrysanthemums it
can be 9-12 hours depending on the cultivar.

It is important to note that "short-day plant" is somewhat of a misnomer. The
plant is actually responding to the length of the dark period, not the light
period. A more accurate term would be "long-night plant," but the original
nomenclature based on day length has persisted in the botanical literature.

## Long-Day Plants (LDP)

Long-day plants flower when the duration of continuous darkness falls below a
critical threshold — when days are long and nights are short. These plants
typically flower in spring and early summer as days lengthen. Examples include
irises, barley, spinach, lettuce, radishes, and clover. For many long-day plants,
the critical night length is approximately 8-10 hours. Some long-day plants have
an additional vernalization requirement (exposure to prolonged cold) before they
will flower regardless of photoperiod.

## Day-Neutral Plants (DNP)

Day-neutral plants flower regardless of photoperiod. Their flowering is controlled
by other factors such as plant age, accumulated thermal time, or resource
availability rather than day length. Examples include tomatoes, cucumbers, corn,
and many tropical plants. Day-neutral behavior is thought to represent the
ancestral condition, with photoperiod sensitivity evolving later as plants
colonized higher latitudes where seasonal day-length variation is more pronounced.

## Quantitative vs. Qualitative Responses

Within each category, responses can be either qualitative (obligate) or
quantitative (facultative). A qualitative short-day plant will not flower at all
unless the night is longer than the critical length. A quantitative short-day
plant will flower faster or more profusely under short days but may eventually
flower under long days as well. This distinction has important practical
implications for growers managing crops across different seasons and latitudes.

## The Maryland Mammoth: Founding Example

The [[maryland-mammoth-tobacco-and-the-discovery-of-photoperiodism]] began with [[garner-allard-maryland-mammoth-tobacco-experiment]], a strain
that appeared in southern Maryland around 1906. This remarkable plant could reach
four and a half metres in height and produce almost a hundred leaves, but rarely
flowered — a serious problem for farmers who needed seed for the next crop.
Garner and Allard discovered in 1918 that Maryland Mammoth was an extreme short-
day plant: it would keep growing vegetatively under long summer days and only
flower when days shortened naturally in winter, or when artificially shortened by
moving plants to a dark shed each afternoon. This finding launched the entire
field of photoperiod research.

## Agricultural Applications

Understanding photoperiodic classification has enabled dramatic manipulation of
crop flowering schedules. Greenhouse chrysanthemum growers delay flowering from
autumn to spring using night-interruption lighting. Cannabis cultivators control
the vegetative-to-flowering transition with precise dark-period management. Breeders
select for photoperiod insensitivity to develop varieties adapted to different
latitudes. In soybean breeding, the manipulation of photoperiod sensitivity has
been crucial for expanding the crop's cultivation range from the tropics to high
latitudes.

## Molecular Basis

The molecular basis of photoperiodic flowering involves the interaction between
circadian clock genes (such as CONSTANS in Arabidopsis) and light-sensing pathways.
CONSTANS protein accumulates only under long-day conditions and activates the
floral integrator gene FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT). FT protein, also known as
florigen, travels from leaves to the shoot apex where it triggers the floral
transition. In short-day plants, the regulatory logic is inverted: FT expression
is promoted under short days and suppressed under long days.

## Latitude and Photoperiodic Adaptation

Photoperiodic responses are closely tied to geographic latitude. At the equator,
day length varies minimally throughout the year (approximately 12 hours), and
most tropical plants are day-neutral. As latitude increases, seasonal variation
in day length becomes more pronounced, and photoperiod sensitivity becomes
advantageous. Short-day plants are typically adapted to lower latitudes or
autumn flowering, while long-day plants are adapted to higher latitudes or spring
flowering. This latitudinal gradient explains why many photoperiod-sensitive crops
must be bred for local adaptation — a soybean variety optimized for 40°N latitude
may fail to flower properly if grown at 30°N.

## Interaction with Other Environmental Cues

Photoperiod does not act in isolation. Flowering time is influenced by
interactions between photoperiod, temperature (vernalization and thermal time),
light quality (red-to-far-red ratio), and plant age. Some plants require both a
specific photoperiod and a period of cold exposure (vernalization) before they
will flower — winter wheat is a classic example. Temperature can also modify the
photoperiodic response: high temperatures can shorten the effective critical day
length in some species, while low temperatures can lengthen it. These interactions
make flowering time prediction a complex but essential aspect of crop management.

## Photoperiod and Cannabis Cultivation

Cannabis is predominantly a short-day plant, with most photoperiod-sensitive
cultivars requiring 12 or more hours of continuous darkness to initiate and
maintain flowering. This characteristic has made photoperiod control central to
both indoor and outdoor cannabis cultivation. Indoor growers maintain 18/6 or
24/0 light cycles during the vegetative phase and switch to 12/12 to trigger
flowering. Outdoor growers must plan planting schedules around natural day-length
changes at their latitude. [[autoflowering-cannabis]] varieties, which carry
[[cannabis-ruderalis]] genetics, are day-neutral and flower based on age rather than
photoperiod, offering advantages in regions with short growing seasons.
