    10|# Mushroom Cultivation Pests and Contamination
    11|
    12|Contamination by competitor organisms and pests is the most common cause of failure in mushroom cultivation. The Mushroom Cultivator provides the most comprehensive identification guide, cataloging dozens of contaminants by genus. This page summarizes the major threats and their management.
    13|
    14|## Bacterial Contaminants
    15|
    16|### Bacillus (Wet Spot)
    17|- Most common bacterial contaminant in grain spawn
    18|- Causes sour, foul odor detectable through the dry vermiculite layer within 2 days of inoculation
    19|- Grain appears wet, slimy, or matted
    20|- Caused by insufficient sterilization or overly wet grain
    21|- Prevention: extend sterilization time; ensure proper grain moisture
    22|
    23|### Pseudomonas (Bacterial Blotch and Pit)
    24|- Causes dark brown, sunken lesions on mushroom caps
    25|- Thrives in wet conditions with poor air circulation
    26|- Prevention: improve FAE; reduce surface moisture; do not water mushrooms directly
    27|
    28|## Mold Contaminants (Fungal Competitors)
    29|
    30|### Trichoderma (Forest Green Mold)
    31|- The most aggressive and common contaminant in mushroom cultivation
    32|- Rapidly spreads as a thick green carpet, overwhelming mushroom mycelium
    33|- Spores are ubiquitous in soil and air
    34|- Prevention: rigorous [[mushroom-sterile-technique]]; pasteurize substrates properly
    35|
    36|### Aspergillus (Green Mold)
    37|- Common airborne contaminant
    38|- Green to yellow-green powdery colonies
    39|- Some species produce mycotoxins
    40|- Appears as colored growths in pastels to greens
    41|
    42|### Penicillium (Bluish Green Mold)
    43|- Very common contaminant on agar and grain
    44|- Distinctive blue-green color with white edges
    45|- Produces abundant spores that spread easily
    46|- Once sporulating, do not open the culture in a flow hood (spreads spores)
    47|
    48|### Neurospora (Pink Mold)
    49|- Bright orange-pink, very fast growing
    50|- Often appears on grain substrates
    51|- Can spread rapidly through a growing area
    52|- Immediate disposal required
    53|
    54|### Mucor and Rhizopus (Black Pin Mold)
    55|- Rapid growers with coarse, cottony mycelium
    56|- Produce dark sporangia on stalks ("pin mold" appearance)
    57|- Common on bread and grain
    58|
    59|### Dactylium (Cobweb Mold)
    60|- Fine, cobweb-like growth over mushroom surfaces
    61|- Can overgrow fruiting bodies
    62|- Often confused with mushroom mycelium but grows faster and more wispy

    86|## Non-Contaminant Organisms
    87|
    88|### Actinomyces and Streptomyces (Firefang)
    89|- These are actually beneficial in compost -- they produce the characteristic "sweet earthy" smell of good compost
    90|- Appear as white, gray, or tan powdery growth in compost
    91|- Indicator of proper composting, not a problem
    92|
    93|## Insect Pests (from Stamets)
    94|
    95|### Mushroom Flies
    96|- **Sciarid flies**: Small dark flies whose larvae feed on mycelium and damage crops
    97|- **Phorid flies**: Hump-backed flies; larvae damage mushroom tissue
    98|- **Cecid flies**: Gall midges
    99|
   100|**Control measures**: Screen vents and air intakes; yellow sticky traps; maintain clean growing areas; remove spent substrate promptly.
   101|
   102|### Mites
   103|- Microscopic arthropods that feed on mycelium
   104|- Can vector mold spores and bacteria between cultures
   105|- Often introduced via contaminated substrate or tools
   106|
   107|### Nematodes (Eelworms)
   108|- Microscopic worms that damage mycelium
   109|- More common in compost-based systems
   110|- Indicator of poor hygiene or contaminated water
   111|
   112|## Virus (Die-Back Disease)
   113|- Causes progressive loss of vigor and yield over successive flushes
   114|- No visible signs on mycelium or mushrooms
   115|- Spread via contaminated tools or infected spawn
   116|- Prevention: use only clean cultures; sterilize tools between uses
   117|
   118|## Contamination Prevention Summary
   119|
   120|1. **Sterilize properly**: Follow recommended pressure cooking times; never cut corners
   121|2. **Use control jars**: Test sterilization by leaving jars uninoculated
   122|3. **Work clean**: Laminar flow hood, clean surfaces, washed hands
   123|4. **Flame sterilize**: Between every tool use
   124|5. **Monitor closely**: Check cultures daily for early signs of contamination
   125|6. **Discard promptly**: Heavily contaminated cultures should be disposed of immediately
   126|7. **Isolate if possible**: Light contamination on agar can sometimes be isolated away from
   127|8. **Manage environment**: Proper FAE, humidity, and temperature reduce susceptibility
   128|9. **Clean growing areas**: Remove spent substrates; sanitize chambers between grows
   129|10. **Outdoor disposal**: Contaminated grain spawn can sometimes be salvaged by discarding in a garden (may produce mushrooms outdoors)
   130|
   131|## Troubleshooting Guide
   132|
   133|| Symptom | Likely Cause | Reference |
   134||---------|-------------|-----------|
   135|| Colored growths in jar (pastels to black) | Mold contamination | Sterilize longer; check technique |
   136|| Sour foul odor within 2 days of inoculation | Bacterial contamination (Bacillus) | Dry grain better; extend sterilization |
   137|| No growth, no contamination | Dead spores/mycelium | Cool jars before inoculation; fresh cultures |
   138|| Growth stops at certain point | Dried substrate | Increase water in formula |
   139|| Fuzzy white growth on caps | Low humidity (not contamination) | Increase humidity |
   140|| Tall spindly mushrooms, small caps | High CO2 | Increase fresh air exchange |
   141|| Green/blue spreading on agar or grain | Trichoderma/Aspergillus/Penicillium | Improve sterile technique |
   142|| Wet slimy spots on grain | Bacterial wet spot | Reduce grain moisture; extend sterilization |
   143|
