# Marine and Mangrove Fungal Decomposition

## Overview

Marine fungi are a diverse ecological group rather than a strict taxonomic classification, encompassing species from multiple fungal lineages that have adapted to life in saline environments. They play essential roles in decomposing wood, plant litter from mangrove swamps, and salt marsh vegetation, thereby driving nutrient cycling in coastal and marine ecosystems.

## Diversity and Adaptation

### Taxonomic Composition

Marine ecosystems harbor significant fungal diversity:

- The ascomycete order **Halosphaeriales** is particularly well-represented, with some 43 genera and 133 species.
- Lower diversity of basidiomycete and mitosporic (asexual) fungi are found.
- Distribution is related to environmental conditions: some species are restricted to tropical and subtropical marine systems, while others have cosmopolitan distributions.

### Physiological Adaptations to Salinity

All marine fungi show physiological adaptations for survival under high saline stress, particularly in their regulation of osmotic potential (Jennings, 1983; Clipson and Jennings, 1992). Remarkably, fungi even exist in the hypersaline conditions of the Dead Sea at total salt concentrations of 340 g/L, where 55 species have been isolated (Kis-Papo et al., 2001).

### Decomposer Strategies

Newell (1996) identified four basic strategies adopted by marine decomposers (organo-osmotrophs):

1. **Surface area maximization**: High substrate affinity allows enzyme diffusion into solid particles. Particularly characteristic of prokaryotes.
2. **Tunneling and surface erosion**: Penetration of solid substrates by chains or series of single cells, common in bacteria and labyrinthulids.
3. **Ectoplasmic nets and rhizoids**: Penetration via adsorptive networks, adopted by true chytrids, thraustochytrids, and hyphochytrids.
4. **Self-extending tubular reactors**: Networks of chitin-laminarin or cellulose-laminarin tubes, signifying mycelial eumycotic fungi and oomycete protoctists.

## Habitat Types and Functions

### Arenicolous (Sand-Inhabiting) Fungi

These fungi are primarily decomposers that utilize algae, leaves, and detritus as resources in sandy marine environments.

### Lichen Fungi in Marine Environments

Marine lichens form associations with algae and have ecological functions analogous to terrestrial lichens, including soil formation. Primitive lichen associations exist between fungi and microalgae, though some such as *[[leiophora-pelvetiae]]* are thought to be parasitic on the seaweed *Pelvetia canaliculata*.

### Parasitic Marine Fungi

A range of ascomycete fungi are true parasites of seaweeds, causing effects ranging from minor thallus discoloration to gall formation and malformations that limit photosynthetic function.

## Wood Decomposition in Marine Ecosystems

Wood is a major resource for fungi in marine ecosystems. Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer (1979) documented 107 fungal species from decomposing wood in marine habitats, including 73 ascomycetes, 2 basidiomycetes, and 29 mitosporic species. Wooden structures such as breakwaters, jetties, and piers provide abundant substrate, and [[fungal-decomposition]] causes substantial economic damage.

### Competitive Advantage Over Marine Borers

Fungi have a competitive edge over marine wood-boring animals due to their ability to survive in low-oxygen sediments. Fungi can conduct oxygen from aerobic zones through their hyphae to metabolically active fronts growing into anaerobic sediments.

### Fungus-Fauna Symbiosis in Wood Decomposition

As in terrestrial ecosystems, tight linkages exist between fungi and fauna in marine wood decomposition:

- Wood-boring marine mollusks preferentially settle and feed on fungus-colonized, partially decomposed wood rather than fresh wood.
- The wood-boring crustacean gribble (*[[limnoria-tripunctata]]*) has increased longevity on fungus-colonized wood and is incapable of reproduction without marine fungi in its diet, likely due to enhanced availability of proteins, essential amino acids, and vitamins.

## Salt Marsh Decomposition

### Spartina Decomposition

Salt marsh grass (*Spartina*) decomposition relies on distinct fungal communities for different plant parts:

- **Leaves**: Decomposed primarily by ascomycetes, with *Phaeosphaeria spartinicola* as the dominant species.
- **Roots and rhizomes**: Decomposed by a separate fungal community. Roots and rhizomes constitute more than half of total plant biomass.

### Fungal Biomass and Nutrient Immobilization

Fungal biomass in salt marsh systems is relatively low (< 20 mg/g substrate), but fungi grow rapidly within the substrate and efficiently immobilize nutrients. Fungi can contain 75–100% of the total nitrogen in decaying cordgrass leaves. The rapid immobilization strategy prevents nutrient loss during the early stages of decomposition.

### Anaerobic Tolerance

Salt marsh sediments transition rapidly from aerobic to anaerobic conditions with increasing depth. While fungal biomass declines with depth (negatively related to redox potential), fungi demonstrate remarkable ability to grow from aerobic into anaerobic zones:

- Balsa wood panels buried in anaerobic salt marsh sediment were colonized within 12 weeks.
- Many species cannot grow in entirely anaerobic conditions but can advance 5–10 mm in 15 days from aerobic to anaerobic zones, conducting oxygen through hyphae (Padgett and Celio, 1990).
- Mansfield and Bärlocher (1993) confirmed the negative relationship between fungal biomass (ergosterol) and redox potential.

### Food Web Support

Fungal biomass in decomposing salt marsh material sustains invertebrate herbivore populations. The amphipod *Ulorchestia spartinophila* grows best and produces the most offspring when feeding on decaying leaves with high fungal biomass.

## Mangrove Swamp Decomposition

Mangrove swamps are the tropical equivalent of temperate salt marsh habitats, occurring in more sheltered areas away from direct wave action.

### Fungal Community Composition

Mangrove fungal communities are almost exclusively saprotrophic and distinct from salt marsh communities (Newell, 1996):

- Approximately 23 ascomycete species, 17 mitosporic species, and 2 basidiomycete species.
- Includes ascomycetes, mitosporic fungi, chytrids, and chromistan oomycetes.

### Biomass Dynamics

Fungal biomass in decaying mangrove leaves is much lower than in salt marsh vegetation:

| Decomposition Stage | Mass Loss (%) | Ergosterol (μg/g organic mass) |
|--------------------|--------------|-------------------------------|
| Live/senescent     | 0            | < 1                           |
| Red-brown stage    | 30           | 19                            |
| Black, submerged   | 40–60        | 85                            |

Actual biomass may be greater than ergosterol measurements suggest, as many marine oomycetes (e.g., *Halophytophthora*) do not contain ergosterol. Mangrove leaves rapidly accumulate large populations of oomycete fungi, while ascomycetes such as *[[lulworthia-grandispora]]* may comprise up to 50% of the fungal community.

### Detritus Export and Carbon Cycling

Export of plant detritus from mangrove ecosystems is an important contribution to ocean nutrient loading. Outwellings from mangrove swamps transfer 60–260 tonnes/year of carbon, primarily as dissolved organic carbon (DOC). This process, in which fungi play a major role through decomposition and mineralization, is an important component of nutrient additions to near-shore waters.

### Knowledge Gaps

Hyde and Lee (1995) identified several important gaps in understanding:

- Rates of chemical transformation depend on mangrove stand age, flora diversity, and microhabitat proportions.
- The end product of fungal decomposition in mangroves is likely dissolved organic matter rather than particulate organic matter, but the origins and movement of this DOM in marine estuarine ecosystems are poorly understood.

### Fungal Dispersal via Mangrove Propagules

The mangrove tree (*[[rhizophora-mangle]]*) produces viviparous seedlings that develop on the tree, fall into water, and drift with currents. These seedlings serve as vehicles for fungal dispersal. Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer (1979) documented transport of *Keissleriella blepharospora* and *Lulworthia* species from the tropics to the coast of North Carolina via the Gulf Stream on drifting mangrove propagules.
