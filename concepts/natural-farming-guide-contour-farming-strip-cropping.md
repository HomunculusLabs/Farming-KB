# Contour Farming and Strip Cropping on Slopes

## Overview

Contour farming is a foundational practice in sustainable agriculture
that involves orienting all planting, roads, structures, and earthworks
along the natural contours of the land rather than in straight lines. The
*Natural Farming and Sustainable Living Guide* emphasises that following
contours is essential for water management, erosion prevention, and
maximising the productive potential of sloped terrain.

## Why Contour Matters

Farming with contours respects the natural movement of water across the
landscape. When rows are planted up and down a slope, water accelerates
downhill, carrying topsoil with it and creating gullies. When rows follow
the contour, each row acts as a miniature dam, slowing water movement and
allowing it to infiltrate into the soil. This principle applies not just
to crop rows but to roads, paths, orchard plantings, and any other
linear element on the land.

The guide stresses that roads should never be built with steep slopes but
should follow the contours of the land. The road leading to a house should
slope slightly uphill to drain water away, and the slope gives the road
better access to the sun in wet or snowy climates. Roads can even be
designed as part of a [[contour-swale-irrigation-design]] system.

## Zone Boundaries and Contour

When designing zone boundaries on a permaculture map, the edges should
follow the contours of the land and its various characteristics. The guide
recommends colour-coding everything on the map for easier visualisation.
The rigid lines representing zones on the map should be "very blurry in
real life," with edges curved or zigzagged rather than following strict
geometry.

Orchards planted for personal use need not be in rows at all, but if
planting for income generation, rows should be formed along contours. The
edge between zones should be curved or zigzagged, working with the
natural shape of the land.

## Strip Cropping

Strip cropping involves growing several types of plants in strips next to
each other for mutual benefit. The guide emphasises that this does not
mean straight lines but rather "curves, zigzags, keyholes, or following
the contours of the land." Strips can also follow a pattern.

The strip cropping approach relates to broader [[cover-crops-green-manures]]
strategies and can be combined with [[chop-and-drop-mulching]] for soil
building. Each strip serves multiple functions: nitrogen fixation, pest
deterrence, wind protection, or nutrient accumulation.

## Dam Types Along Contours

The guide describes several dam types that follow contour lines:

- **Keypoint dam**: Built in the valleys of small streams along the
  contours of the terrain. Streams always run downhill, so these are
  excavated at the highest point of whatever slope exists. Used to store
  water for irrigation.

- **Saddle dam**: Constructed at the saddle point between two ridges,
  following the contour to capture water flowing through the low point.

- **Horseshoe dam**: A curved structure that follows the contour of a
  small valley or re-entrant.

- **Barrier dam**: A larger dam constructed across a streambed at the
  bottom of a series, with a very large spillway that directs overflow
  into streams via the contour of the land.

- **Contour dam**: Follows a specific contour line across a slope,
  capturing sheet flow from the surface above.

## Water Management Principles

The contour approach to water management works on the principle that water
should be slowed, spread, and sunk into the land rather than allowed to
run off. Every contour-based structure, from a crop row to a dam,
contributes to this goal. The guide connects this to broader [[ozier-pdc-hydro-cycle-and-watershed-management]]
principles.

Houses in a community should be placed in the shelter of warmer sides of
hills and follow the contour of the land. This positioning provides
natural insulation, wind protection, and drainage.

## Windbreaks and Shelterbelts on Contour

Tree windbreaks and forests for animal forage should cover 20-30% of the
land and will increase production by providing shelter and microclimates
for plants and animals. These should be planted to fit the contours of the
land and allowed to mature enough that animals will not damage them, a
process that can take 30 years. Animals can be allowed in to graze before
the grass is harvested for hay or whatever [[cover-crops-green-manures]]
are being grown.

## Roads as Contour Features

The guide provides specific guidance on road placement along contours.
Small roads and trails should be worked into the design early rather than
trying to figure out access to remote areas later. The road from the
highway to the house should slope slightly uphill to drain water away.
Roads should never have steep slopes but should follow the contours.

A road can function as part of a swale or dam system, with rainwater
runoff benefiting either structure. Even if the road cannot drain water
directly into a swale or dam, it should drain into a ditch with a pipe
going under the road and diverting water to the right place.

## Practical Implementation

To implement contour farming effectively:

1. Map the contours of the land using an A-frame level, laser level, or
   professional survey.
2. Mark contour lines at regular intervals appropriate to the slope.
3. Design all linear features (rows, roads, fences, swales) to follow
   these lines.
4. Plant permanent features like trees and perennial crops on contour
   first, as they establish the long-term framework.
5. Add annual crops and green manures in strips between permanent
   plantings.
