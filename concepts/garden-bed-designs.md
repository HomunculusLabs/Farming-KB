# Garden Bed Designs

Garden beds concentrate soil, nutrients, and water in defined growing areas, improving yields and reducing maintenance compared to row cropping. Different bed designs suit different climates, spaces, and growing goals, from [[edible-landscaping]] to intensive [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]].

## Raised Beds

The most popular backyard garden bed type. A framed box filled with soil mix, raised above ground level.

### Advantages
- Improved drainage in heavy or compacted soils
- Soil warms faster in spring, extending [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]
- Reduced bending and kneeling for the gardener
- Clean separation between garden and lawn
- Easy to add amendments and manage soil biology

### Construction
- **Dimensions**: 4 feet wide (reachable from both sides), 8-12 feet long, 10-18 inches tall
- **Materials**: Untreated cedar, redwood, or hemlock (naturally rot-resistant); avoid pressure-treated lumber near food crops; galvanized metal, concrete blocks, or stone also work
- **Soil mix**: 50% topsoil, 30% compost, 20% aeration (perlite, vermiculite, or coarse sand)
- **Bottom**: Line with cardboard to suppress grass/weeds before filling (no plastic — blocks drainage and soil life)
- **Placement**: Orient long sides east-west for even sun exposure; level the frame

### Limitations
- Higher cost than in-ground beds
- Dry out faster than in-ground beds; need more frequent watering
- Not ideal for deep-rooted crops (carrots, parsnips) unless built tall (18+ inches)

## Keyhole Gardens

Originating in Africa, keyhole gardens are circular raised beds with a central compost basket and a notch for access.

### Design
- Circular bed, approximately 6 feet in diameter
- Central compost basket (wire cylinder) filled with kitchen scraps and green waste
- A wedge-shaped notch (keyhole) allows access to the basket
- Soil slopes upward from the outer edge toward the central basket

### How It Works
- Compost in the central basket leaches nutrients outward into the surrounding soil
- Moisture wicks from the compost basket to the bed
- Kitchen scraps decompose in place, feeding the garden continuously
- Excellent for [[water-management]] [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-arid-regions]]

### Construction
- Build circular stone, brick, or wood wall 3 feet tall
- Place wire mesh basket (1 foot diameter) in the center
- Fill with layers: cardboard, branches, green material, soil, compost
- Plant intensively around the basket

## Wicking Beds

Self-watering garden beds that draw moisture up from a reservoir below the soil via [[capillary-action-in-soils]].

### Design
- Waterproof container with a water reservoir in the bottom (6-12 inches)
- Permeable barrier (geotextile fabric) separates reservoir from soil
- Fill pipe allows water level monitoring and refilling
- Overflow drain prevents waterlogging
- Soil above the barrier draws water upward as plants transpire

### Advantages
- Reduced watering frequency — refill reservoir weekly in summer
- Consistent moisture reduces plant stress
- Ideal for hot climates and busy gardeners
- Can be built at waist height for accessibility

### Construction
- Use food-grade plastic tubs, lined timber beds, or IBC totes
- Install overflow outlet 2 inches below soil line
- Fill reservoir with coarse gravel or scoria
- Cover with geotextile fabric
- Add 8-12 inches of soil mix (lighter than standard — more compost, less sand)

### Limitations
- Salt buildup over time — flush periodically with heavy watering
- Not suitable for root vegetables in shallow reservoir designs
- Initial cost is higher than simple raised beds

## Hugelkultur Beds

Hugel beds (mound culture) bury wood and organic debris under a soil mound, creating a long-term, self-fertilizing garden bed. Central to [[permaculture-design]] and [[regenerative-agriculture-history]] growing.

### Design
- Mound of rotting wood, branches, leaves, [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]
- Covered with inverted sod, compost, and topsoil
- Can be freestanding mounds or built in trenches

### How It Works
- Decomposing wood acts as a sponge, absorbing and slowly releasing water
- Fungal decomposition provides a steady nutrient supply
- Heat from decomposition extends the growing season
- Improves soil structure over 5-10+ years as wood breaks down

### Construction
1. Dig a trench 6-12 inches deep (optional, but helps in dry climates)
2. Fill bottom with large logs and branches (avoid cedar, black walnut, and allelopathic species)
3. Add smaller branches, twigs, and leaves
4. Cover with nitrogen-rich material (grass clippings, manure, compost)
5. Top with 4-6 inches of topsoil and compost mix
6. Mulch heavily and water thoroughly

### Best Practices
- Use a mix of wood sizes for varied decomposition rates
- Hardwoods last longer than softwoods
- Let settle for a few weeks before planting; nitrogen may be temporarily tied up
- Plant potatoes, squash, beans, and other heavy feeders in the first year
- Mounds can be 3-6 feet wide and 2-5 feet tall

## Straw Bale Gardens

[[container-growing-vegetables-herbs]] directly in conditioned straw bales, no soil required.

### Conditioning Process (10-14 days)
- Days 1-3: Water bales thoroughly, add high-nitrogen fertilizer (fish emulsion, blood meal)
- Days 4-6: Water daily, half-rate nitrogen fertilizer
- Days 7-9: Water daily, reduced nitrogen, add phosphorus and potassium
- Days 10-12: Water only, add balanced fertilizer
- Day 14+: Plant — bales should feel warm inside (microbial activity)

### Advantages
- No soil needed — works on concrete, patios, contaminated sites
- Raised height reduces bending
- Excellent drainage
- Bales decompose into compost after one season

### Best Crops
- Tomatoes, peppers, squash (top of bale)
- Lettuce, spinach, herbs (side of bale — cut small pockets)
- Potatoes (stuff seed potatoes into holes in the bale)
- Avoid root vegetables (carrots, beets) — limited depth

### Limitations
- Single-season use (bales decompose significantly)
- Requires purchased straw (not hay — hay contains seeds)
- Conditioning requires daily attention
- May need staking for tall plants
