# Psilocybe Global Biodiversity Hotspots

The genus *Psilocybe* is the largest and most widely distributed group
of neurotropic fungi, containing approximately 116 known species as
documented by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz in their comprehensive 2000
survey. The distribution of these species is markedly uneven, with the
majority concentrated in or near the Austral hemisphere, particularly
in subtropical humid forests.

## Global Distribution Pattern

Psilocybe species show a strong preference for subtropical and tropical
humid forest environments. The genus reaches its greatest diversity in
Mesoamerica, with secondary centers of diversity in South America,
Southeast Asia, and Oceania. This distribution pattern correlates with
the availability of suitable substrates — particularly herbivore dung,
decaying wood, and rich forest litter — in warm, humid conditions.

The concentration in or near the Austral hemisphere is notable. While
Psilocybe species do occur in temperate regions of the Northern
Hemisphere (including Europe and North America), the genus achieves
far greater species richness in tropical and subtropical latitudes.
This pattern distinguishes Psilocybe from many other mushroom genera
that peak in diversity in temperate forests.

## Major Biodiversity Centers

### Mexico

Mexico is the global center of Psilocybe diversity, harboring 44
species — approximately 39% of the world's total. This extraordinary
concentration reflects Mexico's complex topography, varied climate zones,
and position at the intersection of Nearctic and Neotropical biogeographic
realms. Mexican Psilocybe species occupy habitats ranging from tropical
lowland forests to high-altitude subtropical cloud forests.

### South America

The Amazon Basin and adjacent montane forests support significant
Psilocybe diversity, though the full extent remains poorly documented
due to limited mycological exploration. Countries including Colombia,
Ecuador, Peru, Brazil, and Venezuela all harbor multiple species. The
Andean cloud forests are particularly rich habitats.

### Southeast Asia and Oceania

The humid tropical forests of Southeast Asia and the Pacific islands
support substantial Psilocybe diversity. New Guinea is especially
notable, both for its species richness and for the well-documented
traditional use of neurotropic fungi among indigenous peoples there,
as reported by Singer and others from the 1950s onward.

### Sub-Saharan Africa

African Psilocybe diversity is less thoroughly documented, but species
have been reported from West, Central, and East Africa. The tropical
forest belt stretching from Guinea to the Congo Basin likely harbors
undiscovered species.

## Habitat Preferences

Most Psilocybe species are saprotrophic, growing on decaying plant
material. The two primary substrate types are herbivore dung (coprophilous
species) and decaying wood or forest litter (lignicolous and humicolous
species). A smaller number of species grow in grasslands, on moss, or
in disturbed habitats.

Coprophilous species such as *Psilocybe cubensis* are among the most
widely distributed, found throughout the tropics and subtropics wherever
large herbivores deposit dung in open or semi-open environments.
Lignicolous species tend to be more habitat-specific and geographically
restricted.

## Correlation with Indigenous Use

There is a strong correlation between Psilocybe biodiversity hotspots
and regions where indigenous peoples have documented traditional use of
neurotropic fungi. The most extensive ethnomycological traditions are
found in Mexico (among Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Nahua, and other
indigenous groups) and New Guinea. Both regions are major biodiversity
centers for the genus.

This correlation suggests that the availability of diverse Psilocybe
species in these regions facilitated the development of sophisticated
mycological knowledge and ritual practices. Indigenous peoples in these
areas could observe the effects of multiple species and develop
preferences for particular taxa, a depth of knowledge that was impossible
in regions with few or no native Psilocybe species.

## Taxonomic Considerations

The number of recognized Psilocybe species has grown substantially
since the first taxonomic surveys of the 1950s and 1960s. Guzmán's work
spanning four decades was instrumental in documenting this diversity.
However, molecular phylogenetic studies have suggested that the genus as
traditionally defined may be polyphyletic, and some species may be
reassigned to other genera as taxonomic revisions continue.

## Conservation Concerns

Psilocybe biodiversity faces threats from habitat destruction, particularly
deforestation of tropical and subtropical forests. Many species have
narrow geographic ranges and specific habitat requirements, making them
vulnerable to land-use change. The destruction of cloud forests in
Mexico and the Amazon Basin could eliminate species before they are
even documented.
