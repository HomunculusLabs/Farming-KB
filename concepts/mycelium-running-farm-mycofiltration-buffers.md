# Farm Mycofiltration Buffers

Mycofiltration around farms addresses one of the most pressing
environmental challenges of modern agriculture: the contamination
of watersheds by animal waste from concentrated livestock operations.
Factory farms crowd livestock into tight quarters for efficient
feeding and slaughtering, causing overly focused and growing outflows
of waste products that threaten the health of all. Mycofiltration
membranes using mushroom mycelium can filter pathogens, silt, and
chemical toxins from agricultural runoff before they reach waterways.

## The Agricultural Waste Crisis

Manure ponds commonly amassing around cattle, hog, and chicken
factory farms are bursting with fecal-rich effluent leaching into the
watersheds. River ecosystems are imperiled by Pfiesteria, Listeria,
Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, amoebic parasites, and viruses.
These pose increasing human health risks as corporate farming policies
aim for profit at the expense of environmental health. Hog farms are
particularly worrisome to environmental scientists.

When hurricane Floyd hit North Carolina in 1999, monsoonlike rains
caused dikes to burst and manure ponds to overflow, flooding thousands
of acres with animal feces and causing incalculable health problems.
The collateral damage included contaminated wells, fisheries, and
crops. Many diseases spread, including ones pathologists are still
at a loss to identify. This ecological mess eroded the public trust
in farmers as good neighbors.

## Heavy Metal Remediation

Toxic levels of zinc and copper accumulate in livestock feedlots as
a by-product of manure production. A particular study in North
Carolina showed that the mycelium of a mold fungus, Aspergillus
niger, removed 91 percent of the copper and 70 percent of the zinc
from treated swine effluent. Other mushroom-forming fungal species
may be used to remediate sites contaminated with heavy metals and
other pollutants. This demonstrates that mycofiltration can address
not only biological contaminants but also chemical pollutants.

## Bunker Spawn Buffers

A variety of forms of mycelial mats can prevent downstream pollution.
Bunker spawn, consisting of mycelium in burlap sacks, can be used to
build mycelial buffers to capture microbes and nutrients. These
portable mycofiltration units can be deployed around farms, suburban
and urban areas, watersheds, and factories. The burlap sack provides
structural support for the mycelium while allowing water to flow
through the fungal membrane.

Installation of mycofiltration membranes can utilize debris from
forests, pulp and paper mills, city recycling centers, farms, and
breweries. This debris is then inoculated with toxin-specific
mushrooms, creating a self-sustaining filtration system that
improves over time as the mycelium matures and strengthens its
network throughout the substrate.

## Pathogen Filtration

Mushroom mycelium has an unquenchable appetite for organic debris.
The mycological landscaper can select mushroom species that target
and consume the bacteria and protozoa in a habitat. Mycofilters and
entire landscapes can be customized with mosaics of mycelial mats to
prevent infection from coliform or staph bacteria and protozoa. The
mats can even trap disease-carrying insects, thwarting disease vectors
and protecting public health.

A strain of zhu ling ([[polyporus-umbellatus]]), a polypore mushroom,
was found to be 100 percent effective in vitro in inhibiting the
malarial parasite Plasmodium falciparum. Mycomulching with this
species around a malaria-infected swamp could reduce the background
population of malaria, since the fungus likely consumes the parasite
and secretes antibiotics into the habitat.

## Factors Affecting Mycofiltration Efficiency

Several factors influence the efficiency of mycofiltration
installations. These include slope of the land, flow rate of water
through the filter, thickness of the mycelial mat, and the specific
mushroom species selected for the target contaminants. The type of
substrate used (wood chips, straw, cardboard) also affects
performance. Covering the site with shade-providing plants can
prevent mycofiltration beds from drying out. Inoculation rates
should be calibrated to the specific waste profile of each site.

## Key Principles

- Source control: Mycofiltration membranes placed between
  waste sources and waterways intercept contamination at
  the point of generation
- Species specificity: Different mushroom species target
  different contaminants, from bacteria to heavy metals
- Scalability: Systems range from small bunker spawn buffers
  to large landscape-scale mycelial mats
- Self-sustaining: Once established, mycelial networks grow
  stronger over time as they process organic debris

## Applications

- Deploying bunker spawn buffers around manure ponds and
  livestock feedlots to filter waste effluent
- Installing mycofiltration mats between agricultural fields
  and adjacent streams and watersheds
- Using [[aspergillus-niger]] and other fungal species to remove
  heavy metals from contaminated livestock waste streams
- Designing multi-species mycofiltration mosaics that target
  both biological and chemical contaminants simultaneously
