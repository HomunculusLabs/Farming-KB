# Composting Toilet Design
# Permaculture Designers Manual - Dry Toilet Systems

updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

The permaculture approach to human waste management views sewage not as a disposal problem but as a resource for nutrient cycling, energy production, and soil building. The Designers Manual addresses composting (dry) toilets as one component of an integrated waste management strategy, alongside [[permaculture-designers-manual-biogas-digester]], [[permaculture-designers-manual-greywater-systems]], and biological water treatment.

Mollison notes that all of the pollution from sewage arises from the frequent, wasteful, and unnecessary flushing of toilets by those living in the effluent society. In Sweden, it is compulsory to use dry toilets in remote, unsewered, or unsuitable areas.

## When to Use Dry Toilets

Dry toilets are quite specifically useful where:
- No methane system is used
- Sewage is not used in the production of plants
- Soils do not suit septic tanks
- Cities have critical water supply problems

Dry toilets are not always appropriate, except in cities and other water-critical areas. They are considered unnecessary on farms or in well-drained soils, or wherever sewage is used to produce methane by anaerobic digestion in tanks.

## Water Saving from Toilet Design

No clean water need be used to flush toilets if there is a diversion from a hand-basin to the toilet tank. In Australia, hand-basins moulded into toilet flush tanks are available. It is essential to use low-flush toilet bowls with such systems, as they otherwise flush incompletely and build up heavy pathogenic bacteria populations.

This simple solution addresses 40% of domestic water misuse and encourages hand cleanliness rather than the false cleanliness/tidiness of toilet flushing for its own sake.

## Design Considerations

### Handbasin-Flush Toilet Integration

Water use in handbasins about equals that for toilets, so in effect no extra, or clean, water is used in toilets. The holding tank can contain several flushes and excess drains to the toilet. This system:
- Eliminates the need for separate handbasin water supply
- Reduces total household water consumption by approximately 40%
- Can be retrofitted to existing toilet systems
- Requires low-flush toilet bowls for complete operation

### Earth Closet Systems

Much simpler but still quite hygienic systems use:
- Rope and pulley showers
- Basins for hands and laundry (often under a lean-to)
- Earth-closet toilets
- These systems work well and save a great deal of water, usually a fraction of that caught on roof areas and stored in house tanks

## Limitations of Dry Toilets

Mollison provides a balanced assessment of dry toilet limitations:

### Reduced Potential Uses

Dry toilets reduce the potential uses of sewage, just as compost is a reduction in the potential use of mulch. When sewage is routed to a biogas digester instead, it produces methane gas for cooking and lighting, and the digester effluent is a high-quality liquid fertilizer. Dry toilets sacrifice these additional yields.

### Regulatory Barriers

In the USA, UK, and Australia, one has to fight hard to get permission to use dry toilets, as it is the vested interest of industry and town clerks to supply and charge for sewerage systems. This regulatory barrier remains a significant obstacle to widespread adoption.

## Integrated Waste Management

The composting toilet should be viewed as part of an integrated waste management system rather than a standalone solution. The full system includes:

1. **Source separation**: Keep greywater separate from blackwater where possible
2. **Handbasin diversion**: Route handbasin water to toilet tanks
3. **Low-flush systems**: Use minimal water when flushing is necessary
4. **Greywater reuse**: Route all greywater to gardens, swales, or mulch pits
5. **Biogas option**: Where feasible, route sewage to anaerobic digesters for methane production
6. **Biological treatment**: Use pond systems, reed beds, and plant filters for any remaining waste
7. **Composting**: Where dry toilets are used, ensure proper composting temperatures and times for pathogen destruction

## Composting Process

While the Designers Manual does not provide extensive detail on composting toilet construction, it emphasizes that proper composting of human waste requires:
- Adequate carbon material (sawdust, straw, leaves)
- Proper moisture management
- Sufficient retention time for pathogen destruction
- Protection from rain and groundwater contamination
- Regular management and monitoring

## Pathogen Destruction in Anaerobic Systems

For comparison, in anaerobic digester systems, 90-94% of worm eggs are destroyed, as are many harmful bacteria. This pathogen destruction is a significant advantage of biogas systems over simple composting toilets. Properly managed composting toilets can achieve similar results through sustained thermophilic composting.

## Design for Different Contexts

### Urban Settings

In cities with critical water supply problems, dry toilets can dramatically reduce water demand. However, regulatory barriers and space limitations must be addressed.

### Rural Settings

On farms with adequate land and suitable soils, septic systems or biogas digesters are generally preferred, as they preserve the nutrient and energy value of sewage.

### Remote Areas

In remote, unsewered, or unsuitable areas, dry toilets are the most practical and environmentally sound option. The compost produced can be used on ornamental plants or fruit trees after proper processing.

### Arid Areas

In water-scarce regions, the water savings from dry toilets are particularly significant. Every litre of water not used for flushing is a litre available for food production.

## Relationship to Broader Water Strategy

The composting toilet decision is part of a broader water management strategy that includes:
- Roof water catchment for drinking
- Greywater reuse for irrigation
- Swale systems for infiltration
- Biological water treatment
- Community-scale water management
