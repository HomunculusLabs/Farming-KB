# Tidal and Marine Aquaculture

Tidal and marine aquaculture in [[permaculture-design]] leverages the natural energy of tides, salt marsh ecosystems, and estuarine environments to produce food, treat waste, and create multifunctional landscape elements. Bill Mollison presents these systems as among the most productive and self-maintaining aquatic strategies available to the permaculture designer.

## Polyculture Aquaculture Design

The foundation of productive aquaculture is **polyculture** — raising multiple species in integrated systems rather than monoculture ponds. A well-designed polyculture uses trickle systems connecting ponds at different pH levels, where water flows from one pond to the next, each supporting different organisms suited to its specific water chemistry.

### Forage Ponds and Trout Production

Trout production can operate at two scales:

- **Extensive systems** rely on natural forage and lower stocking densities.
- **Intensive systems** supplement with artificial feed but require more management.

Forage ponds are stocked with small fish such as **stickleback**, which breed rapidly and feed larger predatory fish like trout. Water trickles from the forage pond into the trout pond, carrying live food directly to the predators. This eliminates the need for purchased feed and creates a self-sustaining food chain.

### Insect Light Traps

Placing light traps over ponds attracts insects at night, which fall into the water and become additional fish food. This simple technique significantly boosts the natural food available in any aquaculture system without added cost or labor.

## Tidal Marsh Systems

Tidal marshes are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth. Mollison emphasizes that permaculture designers should work with tide ranges, which vary from as little as 2 feet to as much as 27 feet, depending on geography.

### Salt Flat Utilization

Salt flats — areas periodically flooded and exposed by tides — can be converted into productive systems. The key is understanding the local tide range and designing systems that function within those parameters. Even modest 2-foot tides can drive productive aquaculture if the design captures and holds water effectively.

## Ancient Fish Traps

Indigenous peoples worldwide developed sophisticated tidal fish capture systems:

- **Polynesian and Maori stone fish traps** use V-shaped or funnel-shaped stone walls built across tidal flats. As the tide rises, fish swim over the walls to feed; as the tide falls, they become trapped in enclosed pools. These structures last for centuries with minimal maintenance and represent some of the earliest forms of sustainable aquaculture.

These ancient designs inform modern permaculture approaches to tidal inlet construction and passive fish harvesting.

## Key Salt Marsh Plants

### Spartina (Cord Grass)

**Spartina** is the primary forage crop of temperate salt marshes. It serves multiple functions:

- Provides food and habitat for estuarine organisms.
- Acts as a **nurse ground** for cool water fish species.
- Stabilizes shorelines and traps sediment, building marshland over time.
- Can be harvested for forage or mulch.

### Mangroves

In tropical climates, **mangroves** replace Spartina as the dominant coastal plant community. Mangroves provide:

- Critical nursery habitat for tropical fish and shellfish.
- Coastal protection from storm surge and erosion.
- Wood for construction and fuel.
- A filtering system for water moving between land and sea.

### Sea Lavender

**Sea lavender** (*Limonium* species) is a salt marsh plant valued for producing **salt marsh honey**. Bees foraging on sea lavender yield a distinctive honey, demonstrating that salt marshes can support pollinator systems and apiculture alongside aquaculture.

### Goose Grazing

Salt marshes naturally support grazing animals. Geese grazing on salt marsh grasses represent an integrated animal-aquaculture system where terrestrial herbivores and aquatic organisms coexist in the same landscape, each benefiting from the productivity of the tidal zone.

## Oyster Cultivation

Oysters thrive at **60% air exposure** — they need to be out of water for more than half the tidal cycle. This requirement shapes where and how oyster beds are placed.

### Inland Oyster Ponds

Mollison describes **inland oyster ponds** where the tide is regulated artificially using boards across sluice gates. By adjusting the boards, the designer controls how much water enters and drains from the pond, mimicking the optimal tidal exposure for oyster growth far from the coast. This allows oyster cultivation in areas not directly connected to tidal waters.

## Lobster and Shellfish

- **Lobster cities**: Lobsters are highly territorial and need shelter. Piles of broken clay pots create ideal habitat — each pot fragment becomes a lobster dwelling, and dense aggregations form productive "cities" of shellfish.
- **Sponge cultivation**: Sponges can be grown on submerged structures in tidal zones, providing a harvestable crop and water filtration services.

## Tidal Inlet Design

Tidal inlets can be designed with **funnels** that channel fish into capture zones as tides [[ebb-and-flow]]. Combined with **longshore drift traps** — structures that intercept the natural movement of sediment along coastlines — these systems passively collect shell, limestone, and other valuable materials carried by currents.

## Self-Flushing Inland Tidal Ponds

By connecting inland ponds to the sea through designed channels, the tidal flow itself flushes and refreshes the water. These **self-flushing ponds** require no mechanical pumps or filtration — the tide does all the work. They are suitable for cultivating **flounder** and other species that migrate between fresh and salt water.

## Cormorant Management

Cormorants are efficient fish predators that can devastate aquaculture stocks. The permaculture solution is **submerged nets** — placed below the water surface, they prevent cormorants from diving and feeding on fish while allowing water flow and fish movement.

## Sewage Treatment Through Marsh Systems

One of the most powerful applications of aquaculture is **waste treatment**. Mollison outlines a multi-stage system:

1. **Holding pond**: Raw sewage collects and begins anaerobic breakdown.
2. **Marsh system**: Water flows through planted marshes where plants and microbes process nutrients.
3. **Tree filtration**: Water passes through zones of fast-growing trees (willows, poplars) that absorb remaining nutrients.
4. **Nutrient-rich pond**: Clean, nutrient-enriched water collects in a final pond suitable for aquaculture or irrigation.

### Swiss Rush (Scirpus)

**Swiss rush** (*Scirpus* species) is identified as the **most efficient water cleanser** among marsh plants. Incorporating Scirpus into treatment marshes dramatically improves water purification performance.

### Town-Scale Design

Mollison presents a case study of a town-scale sewage treatment marsh system that **saved $30,000 per year** compared to conventional mechanical treatment. The system required less energy, less maintenance, and produced useful outputs (fertile water, biomass, fish) instead of waste sludge.

## Pond Multifunctionality

Beyond fish and shellfish production, ponds serve many functions in a permaculture design:

- **Barrier**: Ponds act as physical barriers to livestock, fire, or unwanted access.
- **Fencing**: Water is an effective and low-maintenance boundary.
- **Reflection**: Ponds reflect light into adjacent growing areas, extending productive hours.
- **Fire protection**: Water bodies provide emergency fire suppression capacity.
- **Recreation**: Swimming, fishing, and aesthetic enjoyment.
- **Water cleaning**: Constructed wetlands and ponds purify water moving through the landscape.

## Case Study: The Grasshopper Farmer

Mollison recounts the story of a farmer who was growing grasshoppers as animal feed — a labor-intensive and low-value enterprise. After converting to **trout production** using polyculture aquaculture design, the farmer achieved far higher yields with less labor, demonstrating the transformative potential of well-designed aquatic systems over marginal terrestrial enterprises.

## Design Principles Summary

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|------------|
| Work with tides | Design capture and flushing systems around natural tidal cycles |
| Polyculture over monoculture | Multiple species in integrated food webs |
| Use biological filtration | Marsh plants and trees replace mechanical treatment |
| Mimic natural structures | Stone traps and lobster cities based on animal behavior |
| Multifunctional design | Every water element serves production, protection, and ecological roles |
| Minimal energy input | Gravity, tides, and biological processes do the work |
