# Fungal Nutrient Translocation and Element Redistribution

Fungi are nondiscrete organisms with extending hyphal networks capable
of translocating elements within their thalli. This ability to move
nutrients, carbon, and water in relation to gradients of supply and
demand, at scales from millimeters to tens of meters, profoundly affects
[[dighton-mycorrhizal-diversity-ecosystem-function]] and the modification of heterogeneously distributed
resources.

## Mechanisms of Translocation

Olsson and Jennings (1991) demonstrated that translocation of 14C and
32P through hyphal systems of Rhizopus, Trichoderma, and Stemphylium
occurs by diffusion. The rate of carbon translocation within the fungal
thallus reacts in real time to provide directional flow to building
phases of hyphae. In the face of high [[vegetable-nutrient-demand-levels]] at advancing
hyphal fronts, nutrients and carbon are translocated acropetally through
cytoplasmic flow and diffusion in the cytoplasm and apoplasm.

In contrast to diffusion of C and P, Gray et al. (1995) showed that
translocation of 137Cs through [[schizophyllum-commune]] hyphae was slower
than diffusion, suggesting incorporation into structural components of
the cytoplasm or hyphal wall, reducing movement rate. This presents a
mechanism for long-term accumulation of radiocesium in basidiomycete
fungi.

## Phosphorus Translocation in Mycelial Cords

Wells and Boddy (1990) measured translocation of solutes through fungal
tissues colonizing wood. [[phanerochaete-velutina]] translocated 75% and
[[bloomfield-stinkhorns-phallus-impudicus-osmotic-fruiting]] 13% of phosphorus from a decomposed wood resource to
newly colonized wood through mycelial cord systems. Maximum P
translocation rates reached 7225 nmol P per square centimeter per day
through cords.

Cords formed only in unsterile soil, suggesting the trigger for cord
formation derives from other organisms. Field experiments showed
translocation over distances up to 75 cm between decomposing resources
on the [[fungal-ecology-forest-floor]] into living wild strawberry and moss plants.
Translocation is temperature-dependent, with greater rates at higher
temperatures. Transition from wet to dry soil induces cord thickening in
Phanerochaete velutina and reduced phosphorus translocation, though
wetting has no effect on cord structure or P movement.

## Spatial Redistribution of Elements

If an element were always translocated away from dying regions,
translocation would increase the length of time of immobilization into
fungal components. This temporal regulation of [[solomon-cool-climate-compost-soil-temperature-nutrient-release]], combined
with spatial redistribution through translocation, allows fungi to
smooth out heterogeneity in resource distribution on the forest floor.

Fungi therefore are major contributors to soil fertility by decomposing
organic residues from dead plant and animal remains. Their exoenzyme
activity removes dead mass and mineralizes contained nutrients, providing
sources for further [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]]. During this process, fungi
perform the important function of regulating nutrient release in both
space (translocation) and time (immobilization).

## Leaf Litter Patch Dynamics

Patch accumulation of leaf litter on the forest floor of pine barrens
ecosystems has been related to the density and distribution of understory
herbaceous vegetation stems. The size of leaf litter patches depends on
the density of leaf-trapping ericaceous stems. [[fungal-decomposition-resource-quality-determinants]] within
patches differs among patch sizes because the proportion of litter
material varies. Interpolation from leaf litter patch scale to ecosystem
scale of resolution remains a challenge.
