# Rain Garden Design
Rain gardens are shallow, vegetated depressions designed to capture, absorb, and filter stormwater runoff from impervious surfaces like roofs, driveways, and sidewalks. They combine [[water-management]], [[fungal-ecology|ecology]], and [[native-plant-gardening]] to reduce flooding, filter pollutants, and recharge groundwater.
## Bioretention Principles
Rain gardens function as bioretention systems — engineered landscapes that use soil, plants, and microbes to treat stormwater:
- **Physical filtration**: Soil and root zones trap sediments and particulates
- **Biological treatment**: Soil microbes break down organic pollutants, petroleum hydrocarbons, and some nutrients
- **Chemical adsorption**: Soil particles bind heavy metals and phosphorus
- **Infiltration**: Water percolates into the ground rather than running off into storm drains
- **Evapotranspiration**: Plants absorb water and release it through leaves, reducing total volume
A mature rain garden can capture 90% of runoff from typical rain events and significantly reduce peak flow to storm drains.
## Siting and Sizing
### Location
- **Minimum distance from buildings**: 10 feet (prevents foundation water damage)
- **Minimum distance from septic systems**: 25 feet
- **Slope**: Ideal slope is 1-5%; avoid steep slopes (erosion risk)
- **Sun exposure**: Full sun to part shade preferred for maximum plant diversity
- **Soil type**: Sandy or loamy soils drain fastest; clay soils require amended rain garden soil or deeper construction
- **Utility lines**: Call before digging — avoid underground utilities, septic fields, and wells
### Sizing
The standard approach sizes the rain garden to handle runoff from a specific drainage area:
- **Simple method**: Rain garden = 5-10% of the total impervious drainage area (roof, driveway, sidewalk)
- **Example**: 1,000 sq ft roof draining to a rain garden = 50-100 sq ft rain garden
- **Clay soils**: Increase to 10-15% of drainage area (slower infiltration)
- **Sandy soils**: Reduce to 3-5% of drainage area (faster infiltration)
- **Depth**: 4-8 inches of ponding depth (water should infiltrate within 24-48 hours)
### Advanced Sizing
For precise design, calculate based on:
- Drainage area (square feet of impervious surface)
- Design storm (typically the 1-inch, 24-hour rainfall event)
- Soil infiltration rate (conduct a percolation test)
- Ponding depth
- See local extension office guidelines for regional design standards
## Construction
### Step-by-Step Process
1. **Mark the outline**: Use hose or spray paint to define the garden shape (oval, kidney, or crescent shapes work best)
2. **Excavate**: Dig to planned depth (4-8 inches below grade, plus 6 inches for amended soil layer). Create a flat bottom and gently sloped sides (3:1 maximum slope)
3. **Amend the soil** (if native soil is clay):
   - Mix 60% native soil + 20% sand + 20% compost to a depth of 12-18 inches
   - Or install a gravel layer (4-6 inches) at the bottom with geotextile fabric above
4. **Create the berm**: Use excavated soil to build a low berm (4-6 inches) on the downhill side to hold water
5. **Level the bottom**: Ensure the bottom is flat so water spreads evenly
6. **Add an overflow**: Create a low point (notch) in the berm or add a standpipe to direct overflow during extreme rain events
7. **Install edging** (optional): Stone, brick, or metal edging defines the garden and prevents lawn encroachment
8. **Plant**: Install native plants adapted to wet and dry conditions (see below)
9. **Mulch**: Apply 2-3 inches of double-shredded hardwood mulch (do not use floatable mulch like pine bark nuggets)
10. **Test**: After the first rain, observe where water flows, how quickly it infiltrates, and adjust berm height or overflow as needed
## Native Plant Selection
Rain gardens experience alternating wet (after rain) and dry (between rains) conditions. Plants must tolerate both extremes.
### Center Zone (Wettest — temporarily ponded)
- Blue flag iris (Iris versicolor)
- Swamp milkweed (Asclepias incarnata)
- Cardinal flower (Lobelia cardinalis)
- Blue vervain (Verbena hastata)
- Joe-Pye weed (Eutrochium purpureum)
- Great blue lobelia (Lobelia siphilitica)
- Boneset (Eupatorium perfoliatum)
- Soft rush (Juncus effusus)
### Middle Zone (Seasonally Moist)
- Purple coneflower (Echinacea purpurea)
- Black-eyed Susan (Rudbeckia hirta)
- Wild bergamot (Monarda fistulosa)
- New England aster (Symphyotrichum novae-angliae)
- Switchgrass (Panicum virgatum)
- Little bluestem (Schizachyrium scoparium)
- Butterfly weed (Asclepias tuberosa)
- Spiderwort (Tradescantia ohioensis)
### Outer Zone (Driest — upland conditions)
- Prairie dropseed (Sporobolus heterolepis)
- Yarrow (Achillea millefolium)
- Wild columbine (Aquilegia canadensis)
- Prairie clover (Dalea spp.)
- Blue sage (Salvia azurea)
- Prairie bluestar (Amsonia illustris)
### Trees and Shrubs (Rain Garden Edge)
- Red maple (Acer rubrum)
- River birch (Betula nigra)
- Winterberry (Ilex verticillata)
- Buttonbush (Cephalanthus occidentalis)
- Serviceberry (Amelanchier laevis)
- Elderberry (Sambucus canadensis)
- Spicebush (Lindera benzoin)
- Ninebark (Physocarpus opulifolius)
## Stormwater Management Benefits
- **Volume reduction**: Absorbs 30-40% more water than conventional lawn
- **Peak flow reduction**: Slows runoff, reducing flash flooding downstream
- **Pollutant removal**: Filters 80-90% of sediments, 50-80% of nutrients, and significant heavy metals
- **Groundwater recharge**: Increases infiltration compared to impervious surfaces
- **Combined sewer overflow reduction**: In urban areas, reduces burden on stormwater infrastructure
## Maintenance
### Establishment Period (Years 1-2)
- Water deeply during dry spells (1-2 times per week)
- Weed aggressively — bare soil is an invitation for invasive species
- Monitor drainage — if water ponds more than 48 hours, amend soil or add drainage
- Replace dead plants promptly to prevent weed gaps
### Established Rain Garden (Year 3+)
- Minimal watering needed (only during severe drought)
- Weed 2-4 times per year
- Cut back dead stems in early spring (leave 6 inches for overwintering insects)
- Replenish mulch every 2-3 years as it decomposes
- Inspect berm and overflow after major storms
- Divide overgrown perennials every 3-5 years
### Common Issues
- **Standing water**: Usually indicates compacted soil or inadequate depth; aerate or amend
- **Erosion**: Stabilize with additional plants or stone in the inflow area
- **Mosquitoes**: Not typically a problem if water infiltrates within 48 hours; add native fish (mosquitofish) if water persists
- **Invasive plants**: Monitor and remove promptly; Japanese knotweed, purple loosestrife, and reed canary grass can colonize rain gardens
