# Stable Isotopes in Saprotrophic Fungi

The natural abundance of stable isotopes, particularly nitrogen-15 (15N) and
carbon-13 (13C), has become an increasingly important tool for investigating
[[fungal-ecology]]. By measuring isotope ratios in fungal tissues, researchers can
trace nutrient pathways through ecosystems, distinguish between trophic groups
of fungi, infer substrate utilization patterns, and examine the role of fungi
in element cycling. This approach has proven especially valuable for studying
[[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs]] in their natural environments.

## Isotope Notation and Principles

Natural abundance of 15N is expressed in delta units, indicating parts per
thousand deviations from the ratio 15N:14N in atmospheric N2. Positive delta
15N values indicate greater abundance of 15N; negative values indicate lower
abundance relative to air. Variations in biological material are small, with
total ranges within ecosystems usually approximately -10 per mil to +20 per
mil.

The notation for 13C is similar, where the reference standard is the Vienna-
PeeDee Belemnite. Biological materials have negative values of delta 13C; the
more negative the value, the lower the 13C abundance. Delta 13C of plant
material varies considerably, from -40 to -9 per mil, with much variation
accounted for by differences between C3 and C4 plants.

## The Saprotrophic-Mycorrhizal Divide

One of the most significant findings from stable isotope studies is the
consistent difference between ectomycorrhizal (ECM) and [[saprotrophic-fungi]] in
both 15N and 13C signatures. ECM fungi are enriched in 15N compared with
both their host plants and saprotrophic fungi. Similarly, ECM fungi are more
depleted in 13C than saprotrophic fungi.

A joint plot of both delta 13C and delta 15N parameters usually results in
clear separation between the two trophic groups. This difference has been
called the saprotrophic-mycorrhizal divide and has been used to suggest the
trophic status of fungi where this was unknown. This divide reflects
fundamental differences in how these fungal groups acquire and process
nutrients within ecosystems.

## Mechanisms of Isotope Fractionation

The enrichment of ECM fungi in 15N relative to their host plants is
surprising given the high level of root colonization in most temperate and
boreal forests (typically greater than 95%). The most likely explanation is
discrimination against the heavier isotope 15N during transfer of nitrogen
from ECM fungi to the host plant, resulting in accumulation of 15N within
the ECM mycelium.

15N enrichment in ECM fungi may also reflect inherent differences among
species in their ability to utilize organic nutrient sources. Species of ECM
fungi with high delta 15N values in their sporocarps have greater ability
to degrade and utilize organic sources of nitrogen than those ECM species
with low delta 15N values.

## Substrate Relationships in Saprotrophic Fungi

The delta 15N of organic matter in forest soils characteristically increases
from the litter layer downwards into more decomposed material. Negative values
(from approximately -6 to +3 per mil) in the fresh litter layer reflect those
of the plant species present, but values quickly become positive in the F and
H layers and continue to increase in the mineral soil.

Studies have separated saprotrophic taxa into two groups: those considered to
grow on litter and woody debris, and those that can utilize organic material
at more advanced states of decomposition. Species in the first group had
lower delta 15N signatures than those in the second, correlating with the
delta 15N of their presumed substrates. The sporocarps of litter and wood
fungi also had higher nitrogen concentrations.

## Carbon Isotope Patterns

Kohzu et al. examined enrichment in 13C between substrate and fungi and found
a much greater enrichment of approximately 3.5 per mil between delta 13C of
substrate and delta 13C of fungus. In laboratory experiments with Trametes
versicolor, the wood substrate value remained remarkably constant despite
70% mass loss over 181 days, while the mycelium was consistently enriched in
13C compared with the wood.

The enrichment in fungal material in 13C compared with wood may relate to
differences in cellular components within fungal tissue. Chitin may be
enriched in 13C relative to woody substrates. Because much of the nitrogen
in fungi is contained within chitin, measurements of vacuolated mycelia could
result in delta 13C values higher than those of the wood.

## Intra-Tissue Isotope Variation

Different chemical components within plants and fungi may differ considerably
in both delta 15N and delta 13C. Proteins and amino acids in ECM fungi were
enriched by as much as 9 per mil compared with the cell wall chitin. Within
plants, cellulose is enriched in 13C compared with lignin. Structural plant
components (foliage, stems, roots) may also differ with respect to both 13C
and 15N isotopes.

These intra-tissue differences mean that bulk tissue measurements represent
averages that may mask important ecological information. Compound-specific
isotope analysis offers the potential for more refined understanding of
fungal resource utilization.

## Species-Level Patterns

Fungal species potentially differ in how they fractionate for or against
the heavier isotope within each isotope pair during uptake and subsequent
metabolic transformations. Abraham and Hesse examined fractionation by four
mould fungal species growing on a range of carbon and nitrogen sources.
Differences were found between the fungi both in fractionation on uptake and
in the 13C enrichment of many amino acids.

A discriminant analysis of isotope ratios in four amino acids (threonine,
isoleucine, phenylalanine, valine) divided the fungi into their respective
taxonomic groups. This finding could potentially be used to investigate
selective feeding on fungal species by soil fungivores. However, if fungi
differ markedly in fractionation patterns, the interpretation of delta 15N
and delta 13C values concerning original substrates may be complicated.

## Radiocarbon Dating

Bomb-carbon or radiocarbon (14C) has also been used to investigate fungal
trophic status. Careful measurements of 14C concentrations allow accurate
determination of the age of organic matter utilized by fungi. Sporocarps of
known saprotrophic species contained carbon that had been photosynthetically
fixed by plants more than 6 years previously, whereas the carbon in known
ECM species was only 0 to 2 years old. This method provides very strong
evidence for trophic status, but is prohibitively expensive for routine use.

## Applications to Mycorrhizal Research

In mycorrhizal research, the natural abundance of 15N in host plants has
been closely linked to the type of mycorrhizal association formed. It has
also been used to indicate the importance of ECM fungi in nitrogen cycling
and in supplying host plants with nitrogen derived from organic sources.

The abundance of 13C has been determined in single spores of the arbuscular
mycorrhizal fungus [[gigaspora-marginata]], using the widely different delta 13C
of C3 and C4 plants to show that spore delta 13C decreased linearly from the
C4 plant towards the proximity of the C3 plant in dual cultures.
