## Ultraviolet and Infrared: The Invisible Borders

Beyond the visible spectrum lie two biologically significant
regions. Ultraviolet light has wavelengths shorter than visible
blue light and carries enough energy to damage biological
molecules, which is why it causes sunburn in humans. Many
insects and some birds can see ultraviolet light, and plants
possess photoreceptors that detect UV wavelengths, using this
information for growth regulation and protective responses.

Infrared light has wavelengths longer than visible red light
and is perceived by humans as radiant heat rather than visible
light. Plants also respond to far-red light, a band just beyond
visible red, through the phytochrome system. The phytochrome
pigment switches between two forms depending on whether it
absorbs red or far-red light, enabling plants to detect the
ratio of these two wavelengths and thereby sense whether they
are in full sunlight or shaded by other vegetation.

## Light as Biological Energy

The electromagnetic energy carried by photons of visible light
is precisely matched to the energy requirements of biological
photoreceptors. Photoreceptive proteins contain chromophores,
light-absorbing chemical groups, whose electron energy levels
correspond to the energy of visible photons. When a photon of
the right wavelength strikes a chromophore, it is absorbed and
its energy excites the chromophore's electrons, triggering a
conformational change in the protein that initiates a signalling
cascade.

This mechanism operates identically in human rhodopsin and plant
phytochrome, despite the different downstream effects. In humans,
the signal travels via the optic nerve to the brain for visual
processing. In plants, the signal triggers hormonal responses
that alter growth patterns, flowering time, or stem elongation.
The underlying physics of photon absorption is the same.

## Photoreceptors as Biological Antennas

The human eye contains specialised proteins called photoreceptors
that absorb light energy in the same way that a radio antenna
absorbs radio waves. Rhodopsin in rods and photopsins in cones
have molecular structures tuned to absorb specific wavelengths.
Blue photopsin absorbs short-wavelength light, while red
photopsin absorbs long-wavelength light.

Plants employ an analogous but distinct set of photoreceptors.
Phytochromes absorb red and far-red light. Cryptochromes absorb
blue and ultraviolet light. Phototropins absorb blue light for
directional sensing. Each receptor type functions as a tuned
antenna, responding only to photons within its specific wavelength
band, and converting that absorbed energy into a biochemical
signal the organism can use.

## Implications for Plant Vision Research

The fact that both humans and plants detect the same narrow band
of electromagnetic radiation, despite having evolved entirely
independently, underscores the fundamental importance of visible
light as a biological information channel. Plants do not form
images as humans do, but they extract far more information from
light than most people realise. They detect direction, intensity,
colour, duration, and spectral quality, using multiple photoreceptor
systems operating simultaneously.

Understanding the electromagnetic properties of light is therefore
a prerequisite for understanding how plants sense their environment.
Each photoreceptor type responds to a specific band within the
spectrum, and the combination of signals from multiple receptors
gives the plant a surprisingly detailed picture of its light
environment, one that includes information about season, shading,
competition from neighbouring plants, and time of day.
