# Photosynthetic Efficiency and CO2 Enrichment

## Overview

[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-climatic-factors-in-design|design]]. In permaculture, understanding how plants capture and convert
light energy into biomass—and the [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]] that limit or enhance this
process—allows designers to create systems that dramatically outperform conventional
approaches. [[photosynthetic-efficiency-and-climate-design|design]] can modify the climatic and
atmospheric conditions governing plant productivity.

## The CO2 Limitation on Photosynthesis

The primary limiting factor for [[photosynthetic-efficiency]] in most plants is the leaf's
ability to obtain sufficient carbon dioxide (CO2) from the air. Atmospheric CO2 at
sea level is approximately 0.03–0.04%, well below the saturation point for most C3
crop plants. When CO2 levels are raised to approximately **0.13%** (roughly three to
four times ambient), photosynthetic rates increase by a factor of **two to three
times**. Most crops operate far below their theoretical potential simply because CO2
availability constrains the biochemical machinery within the leaf.

## Strategies for CO2 Enrichment

Permaculture offers elegant, low-energy methods for supplying additional CO2:

### Composting for CO2 Supply

Active composting generates substantial CO2 as organic material decomposes. By
integrating composting within or adjacent to growing areas—particularly in
greenhouses—designers create a continuous supply of enriched air. The warmth from
composting further extends the growing season, making this a dual-benefit strategy.

### Animal Integration in Greenhouses

Housing animals within greenhouse structures provides reliable CO2 through
respiration. Chickens, rabbits, and other small livestock produce CO2 continuously,
and their body heat adds thermal energy. This exemplifies the [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]]
of each element performing multiple functions: animals provide CO2, heat, manure, and
products while the greenhouse provides shelter and climate moderation.

## Latitude, Climate, and Biomass Production

### High Latitude Advantages

Contrary to popular assumption, high-latitude summers can be ideal for biomass
production. Extended day length above 45° latitude provides far more total
photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) per day than equatorial regions receive.
Although the growing season is shorter, the intensity and duration of light during
that period produce remarkable biomass accumulation.

### Equatorial and Tropical Constraints

Equatorial regions rely on year-round growth that is often photosynthetically less
efficient per unit of leaf area, compensating through perennial crop strategies
and continuous production rather than peak seasonal efficiency.

### Rice Yields: Temperate vs. Tropical

Rice yields in [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] can be **four to five times higher per crop** than
[[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]]. Longer, cooler temperate summers allow more photosynthate
accumulation during grain-filling without tropical respiratory losses. However,
tropical regions can harvest **up to three crops per year**, partially compensating
for lower per-crop yield and producing competitive annual totals.

## Assisting Tropical Crop Yields

### Permeable-Crowned Trees as Overstory

Strategically placing permeable-crowned trees above annual crops significantly
improves tropical understory performance. Partial shade reduces leaf temperature and
transpiration stress while allowing sufficient light for photosynthesis:

- **Prosopis with millet in India**: The canopy filters intense sunlight, reducing
  heat stress while contributing nitrogen. Millet yields are significantly higher.
- **Partially-shading taro in Hawaii**: Taro benefits from light filtration that
  prevents leaf scorching and reduces water demand.

### Leguminous Tree Integration

Crops grown under leguminous trees in the tropics consistently outperform open-field
crops. Nitrogen fixation, leaf litter mulch, [[tree-temperature-effects-and-microclimate-modification]] from
species such as Leucaena, Gliricidia, and Calliandra create favorable root
environments and reduce abiotic stress.

## True Tropical Plant Physiology

True tropical plants tolerate higher light levels and maintain effective
photosynthesis even at CO2 concentrations as low as **0.10%**. This low-CO2
efficiency gives tropical species an advantage where rapid decomposition and soil
respiration create fluctuating CO2 availability. However, cultivating temperate-
adapted crops in tropical climates remains a persistent problem—European vegetables
and temperate grains frequently underperform due to heat-induced respiration
losses, photoinhibition, and inadequate dormancy cues. Permaculture advocates
replacing these with locally adapted tropical species.

## Modifying Climatic Factors Through Design

Permaculture modifies local climatic factors through sensible placement of design
components. By positioning structures, vegetation, water bodies, and [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-water-harvesting-and-earthworks|earthworks]] to
interact with prevailing winds, solar angles, and cold air drainage, designers
create favorable microclimates the regional climate alone cannot provide.

### Creating Useful Energy Conversions

Wind and solar energy convert into productive outcomes beyond electricity.
Windbreaks convert wind energy into thermal moderation and reduced
evapotranspiration. Thermal mass absorbs solar energy during the day and releases
it at night—foundational passive conversions in permaculture site design.

### Active Climate Moderation Systems

- **Tree systems**: Windbreaks, shade trees, and forest gardens modify temperature,
  humidity, and wind speed. Tree belts can raise leeward temperatures by several
  degrees.
- **Pond and water systems**: Water bodies act as thermal buffers, absorbing heat
  during warm periods and releasing it slowly. They also increase local humidity.
- **Earth systems**: Berms, swales, and terraces modify surface temperature, water
  availability, and cold air drainage.
- **Hedgerow patterning**: Strategic hedgerow placement creates a mosaic of
  microclimates suited to different crops or production stages.

### Temperate Grass and Shelterbelt Response

Temperate grass growth increases measurably behind windbreaks. The **tagasaste**
(*Chamaecytisus proliferus*) shelterbelt demonstrates this: pasture productivity in
the lee of tagasaste hedges can increase by 15–30% due to reduced wind stress,
lower evapotranspiration, and warmer sheltered temperatures.

## Designer's Checklist

When assessing a site for photosynthetic optimization and climate modification:

- **Rainfall data**: Monthly and seasonal precipitation totals, variability, and
  extremes. Essential for predicting biomass potential and designing water
  harvesting systems.
- **Temperature data**: Monthly averages, extremes, frost-free days, and growing
  degree days. Drives photosynthetic rate and growing season length.
- **Wind data**: Prevailing direction and speed, seasonal variations, and extremes.
  Affects evapotranspiration, mechanical stress, [[windbreak-and-shelterbelt-design]].
- **Hardiness zones**: Identify the USDA or equivalent zone to determine perennial
  species survival ranges. Guides species selection and frost-sensitive placement.
