# America Is Drying Up

In *The Road Back to Nature*, [[desertification]] caused by [[fukuoka-critique-soil-cultivation]] consuming and exhausting organic matter
- Few [[fertilizer-pollution-environmental-impact]] through irrigation gradually renders the land less and less productive, creating a vicious cycle where more irrigation is needed to compensate for declining fertility.

## The Fallow System

Because the land lacks fertility for continuous cropping:

- One-third to one-half of fields are left fallow each year
- From the air, the land shows alternating green and brown stripes
- This creates a checkerboard pattern visible from great altitude

Farmers working many-thousand-acre farms in central California grow rice one year, leave the land fallow the next simply to get rid of unwanted millet, and raise summer wheat the third year — meaning they grow only one crop of rice every three years. Fukuoka saw this as an extraordinary waste of land and energy.

## The Cycle of Impoverishment

Fukuoka connects this to his broader analysis of [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]], creating a negative cycle
- The negative cycle becomes a basic cause for economic failure

## Declining Energy Returns

Fukuoka quantified the failure of modern scientific farming in terms of energy returns:

- The yield ratio — the ratio of energy yield to energy input — has been halving every ten years
- At the time of his writing, only one half of the energy poured into the land was recovered
- To harvest one calorie of food energy, farmers were putting two calories into the land
- American agriculture could be better characterized as "an industry striving to curtail declines in production" rather than raising productivity

## The Economic Consequence

The ecological destruction translated directly into economic hardship. Farmers who worked farms covering many hundreds of acres on the shores of the Mississippi — America's richest grain belt — were unable to live as well or earn as much as Japanese farmers working just three or four acres. Faced with rising debts and deepening poverty, they were leaving the land.

Fukuoka saw this as "just and proper retribution for those who thought they could put nature to work for them without fearing God and nature."

## Slash-and-Burn in Disguise

Fukuoka's most damning characterization was that "modern scientific farming amounts to no more than essentially slash-and-burn agriculture in total disregard of nature." Despite its technological sophistication — enormous machines, chemical inputs, center-pivot irrigation — the fundamental pattern was the same as primitive [[fukuoka-natural-fertility-cycle]] that makes food production possible. The drying of America is not merely an ecological phenomenon but a civilizational one — the visible evidence of a philosophy that seeks to dominate nature rather than work within its patterns.
