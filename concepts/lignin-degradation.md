### Laccase
Laccases (EC 1.10.3.2) are multicopper oxidases that reduce molecular
oxygen to water while oxidizing phenolic substrates. With appropriate
mediator molecules (both natural and synthetic), laccases can also oxidize
non-phenolic lignin. Laccases are found across fungi, bacteria, and plants.
Fungal laccases typically contain four copper atoms arranged in three
spectroscopically distinct sites: a type 1 (blue) copper that serves as the
primary electron acceptor from substrate, a type 2 (normal) copper, and a
type 3 (coupled binuclear) copper cluster where molecular oxygen is reduced
to water. White rot fungal laccases have redox potentials ranging from 0.5
to 0.8V. Natural mediators produced by white rot fungi include
3-hydroxyanthranilic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, and 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic
acid. Synthetic mediators such as 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HBT) and
N-hydroxyphthalimide (NHPI) dramatically extend the substrate range of
laccases but are expensive for industrial scale. Laccase gene families in
white rot fungi can contain over 17 members, each with distinct expression
patterns and substrate preferences. Unlike peroxidases, laccases do not
require H2O2, making them easier to deploy in biotechnological applications.

### Auxiliary Enzymes
Several supporting enzymes maintain the ligninolytic system:
- **Aryl alcohol oxidase** and **glyoxal oxidase** generate H2O2 needed by
  peroxidases. Glyoxal oxidase is secreted by P. chrysosporium and produces
  H2O2 through oxidation of glyoxal, methylglyoxal, and other alpha-hydroxy
  carbonyl compounds, linking it to carbohydrate metabolism.
- **Glucose oxidase** supplements H2O2 production by converting glucose to
  gluconic acid and H2O2, providing a steady supply for peroxidase activity.
- **Cellobiose dehydrogenase** links cellulose degradation to lignin
  oxidation through quinone reduction. It oxidizes cellobiose to cellobionic
  acid while reducing quinones and phenoxy radicals, thereby disrupting the
  antioxidant system that protects lignin from further degradation.

## Fenton Chemistry in Brown Rot

Brown rot fungi use a non-enzymatic approach to depolymerize cellulose.
They generate hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction (Fe2+ + H2O2
→ Fe3+ + OH- + OH·). Hydroxyl radicals are non-specific oxidants that
cleave cellulose chains, but brown rot fungi do not fully mineralize
lignin, instead modifying it through demethoxylation and depolymerization.
