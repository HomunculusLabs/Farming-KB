# Fukuoka One Straw Revolution Principles

The "One Straw Revolution" refers to Masanobu Fukuoka's philosophy and practice of natural farming, articulated across his major works including "The Natural Way of Farming." The name evokes the idea that profound transformation can begin with a single, simple action -- like scattering a handful of rice straw over a field. The principles underlying this revolution encompass agricultural technique, ecological philosophy, and a spiritual worldview rooted in the concept of [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]] (nothingness).

## The Five Principles of Natural Farming

Fukuoka identified five foundational principles that he "sought to demonstrate the validity of" over fifty years of farming:

1. **No tillage** -- The soil is never plowed or turned. [[fukuoka-no-cultivation-principle]]
2. **No fertilizer** -- No chemical fertilizers or prepared compost are applied. [[fukuoka-no-fertilizer-principle]]
3. **No pesticides** -- No chemical or biological pest control agents are used.
4. **No weeding** -- Weeds are not pulled; they are managed through ground cover. [[fukuoka-no-weeding-no-pesticides]]
5. **No pruning** -- Fruit trees are allowed to grow in their natural form. [[fukuoka-argument-against-pruning]]

Fukuoka stated: "During the many years that have elapsed since, I have never once doubted the possibilities of a natural way of farming that renounces all human knowledge and intervention. To the scientist convinced that nature can be understood and used through the human intellect and action, natural farming is a special case and has no universality. Yet these basic principles apply everywhere."

## The Philosophical Foundation: Mu

Fukuoka's agricultural revolution rests on a philosophical revolution in how humans perceive their relationship with nature. His [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]] holds that the human intellect creates an illusory world of distinctions -- good and bad, beneficial and harmful, crop and weed -- that does not exist in nature itself.

He wrote: "Properly speaking, nature is neither living nor dead. Nor is it small or large, weak or strong, feeble or thriving. It is those who believe only in science who call an insect either a pest or a predator and cry out that nature is a violent world of relativity and contradiction in which the strong feed on the weak. Notions of right and wrong, good and bad, are alien to nature."

The revolution begins when the farmer abandons the conceit that he "grows" crops and instead recognizes that nature grows them. This is not passive resignation but active trust -- what Fukuoka called [[fukuoka-road-do-nothing-farming]].

## The Scientific Farming Critique

Fukuoka argued that scientific agriculture creates the very problems it then claims to solve:

- Plowing hardens the soil, requiring more plowing to soften it
- Chemical fertilizers kill soil life, requiring more fertilizer to compensate
- Pesticides eliminate natural predators, creating pest outbreaks that require more pesticides
- Pruning destroys tree form, requiring more pruning to correct the damage
- Weeding opens soil for new weeds, requiring perpetual weeding

This creates what Fukuoka called a "never-ending toil of man" -- a self-perpetuating cycle of intervention. "Scientific farming attempts to correct and improve on what it perceives as the shortcomings of nature through human effort. In contrast, when a problem arises, natural farming relentlessly pursues the causes and strives to correct and restrain human action."

## Nature Is Perfect

A central tenet of the One Straw Revolution is that nature is already perfect and complete. Human knowledge can never create soils more perfect than those of nature. "No amount of research can teach man everything there is to know about the soil, and he will certainly never create soils more perfect than those of nature."

This does not mean nature is static. Nature "lives and it grows." The farmer's role is to "direct these vast hidden forces to the growth of crops" rather than replacing them with human effort. As Fukuoka summarized: "The best plan, then, is true non-action; it is no plan at all."

Fukuoka pointed to the grove of trees surrounding Japanese village shrines as evidence: "These trees were not grown with the aid of nutrition science, nor were they protected by plant ecology. Saved from the axe and saw by the shrine deity, they grew into large trees of their own accord."

## The Holistic Biosystem

Fukuoka insisted that nature cannot be understood by dissecting it: "The living and holistic biosystem that is nature cannot be dissected or resolved into its parts. Once broken down, it dies." This critique of [[fukuoka-critique-western-dietetics-nutritional-science]] extends to all agricultural research that isolates variables in controlled conditions.

He argued that scientists "collect data and findings piecemeal on a dead and fragmented nature and claim to 'know,' 'use,' or 'conquer' nature." But what they examine "is no longer what they think it to be." This [[fukuoka-fallacies-scientific-understanding]] is perhaps the most radical aspect of the One Straw Revolution.

## The Self-Perpetuating Cycle of Intervention

Fukuoka traced the logic of how each human intervention creates the need for further intervention:

- The farmer plows because he plowed last year, creating compacted soil that requires plowing
- He applies fertilizer because previous fertilizers killed the soil's natural fertility
- He sprays pesticides because previous sprays eliminated the pests' natural predators
- He prunes because previous pruning destroyed the tree's natural form

"Man, therefore, is compelled to act because he earlier created the very conditions that now require his action." Breaking this cycle requires not finding better techniques but recognizing that the original intervention was unnecessary. This insight connects to Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-philosophy-of-non-intervention]].

## The Method in Practice

Despite its philosophical depth, the One Straw Revolution produced concrete, replicable techniques:

- [[fukuoka-no-till-grain-production-method]] yielding 22+ bushels per quarter-acre of each grain
- [[fukuoka-seed-balls-and-direct-seeding]] for direct seeding without nursery beds
- [[fukuoka-white-clover-cover-crop-system]] replacing chemical nitrogen
- [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-philosophy]] replacing compost preparation
- [[fukuoka-natural-orchard-management]] without pruning or fertilizers
- [[fukuoka-duck-rice-fish-symbiosis]] for weed and pest control

Fukuoka reduced his labor to "essentially just sowing seed and spreading straw" -- the "one straw" of the revolution. The quarter-acre farmer, working one or two people, could grow all necessary crops.

## Energy Productivity and Self-Sufficiency

Fukuoka provided quantitative evidence for the efficiency of natural farming. Natural farming produces 130 pounds of rice -- or 200,000 kilocalories of energy -- per man-day of labor, without the input of any outside materials. This is about 100 times the daily calorie intake of a farmer on a natural diet. Cultivation with horses or oxen requires five to ten times as much energy input, and mechanized agriculture requires ten to fifty times as much. "Since the efficiency of rice production is inversely proportional to the energy input, scientific agriculture requires an energy expenditure per unit of food produced up to fifty times that of natural farming."

Fukuoka's young apprentices demonstrated that a person's minimum daily calorie requirement is about 1,000 calories for a "hermit's diet" of brown rice with sesame seeds and salt, and 1,500 calories on a diet of brown rice and vegetables -- enough to do a farmer's work. This confirmed the [[fukuoka-village-self-sufficient-community]] vision of the quarter-acre farmer.

## Restoration Through Renunciation

The paradox of the One Straw Revolution is that restoration comes through renunciation rather than addition. "Once we accept that nature has been harmed by human knowledge and action, and renounce these instruments of chaos and destruction, nature will recover its ability to nurture all forms of life." The farmer does not need to do more; he needs to do less -- but with understanding and intention.

## The Global Vision

Fukuoka saw the One Straw Revolution as having implications far beyond agriculture. He proposed using [[fukuoka-clay-pellet-desert-greening]] to revegetate the world's deserts. He envisioned [[fukuoka-decentralized-self-sufficient-communities]] based on quarter-acre farms. He argued that his approach was "a first step toward the restoration of nature" on a global scale.

He warned that the alternative -- continued expansion of scientific agriculture -- would lead to the destruction of arable lands and the loss of vegetation worldwide, driven by "human civilization and the misguided methods of crop cultivation that arose from man's arrogance."

## Progressive Simplification

Fukuoka described his method as one of progressive subtraction rather than addition. While scientists constantly say "Let's try this, let's try that," introducing new methods that require additional expenditures and effort, Fukuoka took "the opposite tack." He eliminated unnecessary practices one by one, telling himself, "I don't need to do this, I don't need to do that." The result was a farming system that becomes simpler and more productive the longer it is practiced -- the opposite of scientific agriculture, which becomes more complex and less efficient over time.

He illustrated the problem with the example of plowing: "Flood a field with water, stir it up with a plow, and the ground will set as hard as plaster. If the soil dies and hardens, then it must be plowed each year to soften it. All we are doing is creating the conditions that make a plow useful, then rejoicing at the utility of our tool. No plant on the face of the earth is so weak as to germinate only in plowed soil."

## Why Natural Farming Has Not Spread

Fukuoka acknowledged that natural farming "will never see general application in its true form" but argued it can still "serve as a brake to slow the mad onslaught of scientific agriculture." He attributed the resistance to natural farming to how deeply human civilization has become invested in the illusion of control:

> "That natural farming has yet to gain wide acceptance shows just how mortally nature has been afflicted by man's tampering and the extent to which the human spirit has been ravaged and ruined."

He also recognized institutional barriers. Researchers at agricultural testing stations confirmed that his no-tillage methods worked, yet the recommendations that emerged from these institutions still favored conventional practices with chemical inputs. The [[fukuoka-why-natural-farming-blocked-in-japan]] reflects his analysis of how agricultural bureaucracy, economic interests, and the inertia of scientific thinking prevent the adoption of simpler, more effective methods.
