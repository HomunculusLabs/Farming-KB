# Fukuoka One Straw Revolution Principles

The "One Straw Revolution" refers to Masanobu Fukuoka's philosophy and practice of [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]], articulated across his major works including "The Natural Way of Farming." The name evokes the idea that profound transformation can begin with a single, simple action -- like scattering a handful of rice straw over a field. The principles underlying this revolution encompass agricultural technique, ecological philosophy, and a spiritual worldview rooted in the concept of [[fukuoka-no-cultivation-principle]]

5. **No pruning** -- Fruit trees are allowed to grow in their natural form. [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]] holds that the human intellect creates an illusory world of distinctions -- good and bad, beneficial and harmful, crop and weed -- that does not exist in nature itself.

He wrote: "Properly speaking, nature is neither living nor dead. Nor is it small or large, weak or strong, feeble or thriving. It is those who believe only in science who call an insect either a pest or a predator and cry out that nature is a violent world of relativity and contradiction in which the strong feed on the weak. Notions of right and wrong, good and bad, are alien to nature."

The revolution begins when the farmer abandons the conceit that he "grows" crops and instead recognizes that nature grows them. This is not passive resignation but active trust -- what Fukuoka called [[fukuoka-critique-western-dietetics-nutritional-science]] extends to all agricultural research that isolates variables in controlled conditions.

He argued that scientists "collect data and findings piecemeal on a dead and fragmented nature and claim to 'know,' 'use,' or 'conquer' nature." But what they examine "is no longer what they think it to be." This [[fukuoka-philosophy-of-non-intervention]].

## Energy Productivity and Self-Sufficiency

Fukuoka provided quantitative evidence for the efficiency of natural farming. Natural farming produces 130 pounds of rice -- or 200,000 kilocalories of energy -- per man-day of labor, without the input of any outside materials. This is about 100 times the daily calorie intake of a farmer on a natural diet. Cultivation with horses or oxen requires five to ten times as much energy input, and mechanized agriculture requires ten to fifty times as much. "Since the efficiency of rice production is inversely proportional to the energy input, [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]] requires an energy expenditure per unit of food produced up to fifty times that of natural farming."

Fukuoka's young apprentices demonstrated that a person's minimum daily calorie requirement is about 1,000 calories for a "hermit's diet" of brown rice with sesame seeds and salt, and 1,500 calories on a diet of brown rice and vegetables -- enough to do a farmer's work. This confirmed the [[fukuoka-clay-pellet-desert-greening]] to revegetate the world's deserts.
