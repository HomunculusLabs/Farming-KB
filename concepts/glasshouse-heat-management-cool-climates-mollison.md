# Glasshouse Heat Management in Cool Climates

The cool temperate climates are those places where greenhouses are most
commonly used for speciality crop or aquatic species production, for house
heating, and for the winter production of vegetable and fruit crop. Mollison
treats the glasshouse as a thermally sophisticated structure where
70-90% of the cost of crop can be that of artificial heating, making heat
management the critical design factor.

## Insulation Strategy

### Ground Insulation

Insulation is essential not only for the ground below the greenhouse,
usually as a trench 1 m or so deep provided with a panel insulation, or with
straw or sawdust-filled plastic bags, but also for the endwalls, the
poleward walls, and the poleward slope of the roof (all can be solid
insulated walls). The sunward roof slope and wall can be air-insulated,
either by using trapped bubble insulation as standard plastic sheets, by
keeping air at pressure between double plastic walls, or by using insulated
shutters at night to cover the glazed areas.

### Interior Heat Mass

Inside the greenhouse, where the whole earth floor inside the walls is
insulated from the cold earth outside, several additional heat masses can
be provided:

- **Water tanks or drums** (plastic or metal): These can double as fish
  ponds, integrating aquaculture with plant production. Water has the highest
  volumetric heat capacity of any common material, making it ideal for thermal
  storage.

- **Domestic animals**: Areas below plant benches, if used to house rabbits,
  guinea pigs, poultry, or any small domestic animals at night, will provide
  considerable winter heat through body warmth.

- **Compost heaps**: Long compost boxes along the poleward wall, or external
  to but below the level of the greenhouse itself, provide sustained heat
  through microbial decomposition.

- **Hot water storage**: Tanks filled from panel or trough-concentrator solar
  heat collectors provide stored heat that can be released on demand.

### Dark Curtains as Heat Radiators

Dark curtains within the greenhouse act as heat (long wave) absorbers and
radiators. During the day they absorb solar radiation, and at night they
re-radiate this stored energy as infrared heat, moderating temperature drops.

## Ventilation and Humidity Management

Manual or automatic venting of the greenhouse, fans to create air circulation
inside, and a close watch on excessive humidity complete the design of
efficient greenhouse units. Excessive humidity is relieved by a heat
exchanger to the exterior, or by venting hot air. Condensation management
is critical because high humidity promotes fungal diseases that can rapidly
destroy crops in the confined greenhouse environment.

## Passive Solar Greenhouse Variants

Mollison notes that the principles of [[greenhouse-design]] extend beyond
purpose-built structures. Several low-cost alternatives provide frost-free
and warm plant environments:

- Glazing over an earth trench in a sunward slope
- A narrow street between houses or shops that acts as a solar collector
- The sunward side of a massive stone or brick wall

These passive structures use the same thermal mass and insulation principles
as formal greenhouses but require minimal construction investment.

## Entry Design

All entries to greenhouses and houses need to be porches or covered areas,
provided with two successive doors, preventing heat escape as air loss to the
exterior. This double-entry vestibule design is standard in cold-climate
architecture and applies with equal importance to greenhouse access.

## Crop Selection for Cool Climate Glasshouses

As greenhouse construction is relatively expensive, the crop selection in
early years should be oriented towards high-value crops such as bell pepper,
tomato, snow pea, ginger, kiwifruit, and so on (depending on local
preferences and demands). It is manifestly less profitable to grow low-value
or bulky crops, but a domestic greenhouse can profitably provide a wide range
of family food (animal and vegetable) rather than a commercial market crop.

The distinction between commercial and domestic greenhouse priorities is
important: a commercial grower must focus on high-value, high-turnover crops
to justify the capital investment, while a homesteader can use the
greenhouse for diversity and self-sufficiency, producing out-of-season
vegetables, seedling starts, and winter greens that would otherwise need to
be purchased.

## Integration with Broader Systems

The greenhouse in permaculture is never an isolated structure. Mollison
emphasises integration with:

- **House heating**: A greenhouse attached to the sunward wall of a dwelling
  provides passive solar heating to the house while the house provides
  backup warmth to the greenhouse at night

- **Animal systems**: Rabbits, poultry, or guinea pigs housed below benches
  provide CO2 enrichment (enhancing plant growth) and body heat

- **Composting**: Active compost bins provide heat and CO2, while plant
  waste from the greenhouse feeds the compost

- **Water systems**: Rainwater collection from the greenhouse roof feeds
  irrigation; greywater from the house can be treated through greenhouse
  plant beds
