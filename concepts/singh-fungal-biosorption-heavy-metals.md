# Singh Fungal Biosorption of Heavy Metals
#mycoremediation #heavy-metals #biosorption #bioaccumulation #wastewater-treatment

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: [[harbhajan-singh]], Mycoremediation: [[fungal-bioremediation]] (2006), Chapter 11

## Overview

Fungal biosorption is the process by which living or dead fungal biomass sequesters toxic metals from aqueous solutions. This technology offers a promising and economical alternative for treating metal-laden industrial effluents. Conservative estimates value new biosorbents in the North American environmental market at $27 million per year (Volesky, 2001). The fungal-specific form is termed mycosorption, and the biomass used is called mycosorbent. See also [[fungal-ecology-mycoremediation]], [[acid-mine-drainage-treatment]], [[factors-affecting-mycoremediation-efficiency]].

## Metal Contamination Sources

Industries discharging metal-laden wastewater include: electroplating, electronic circuit production, steel/nonferrous processes, chemical/pharmaceutical manufacturing, and coal-fired power plants. Acid mine drainage is a major source of metal contamination over large areas. In the US, 389 of 703 National Priority List sites contain toxic metal contaminants; 100,000+ such sites estimated in the EU.

## Biosorption vs. Bioaccumulation

### Three Categories of Metal Removal
1. **Biosorption**: metal ions bound to fungal surface (living or dead biomass)
2. **Intracellular uptake**: metal ions transported across membranes (requires living biomass)
3. **Chemical transformation**: metals reduced, methylated, or dealkylated by fungi (requires living biomass)

### Advantages of Dead Biomass
- No growth, metabolic energy, or transport requirements
- Strong affinity for metal ions (no competing protons from metabolism)
- Metal toxicity is not a concern
- Can be generated as waste by-product from industrial fermentation
- Pretreated (washed with acids/bases), dried, and granulated
- All factors reduce final process cost

## Mechanisms of Fungal Biosorption

### Metabolism-Independent Mechanisms
- Ion exchange
- Chelation
- Adsorption
- Crystallization
- Precipitation
- Ion entrapment in inter/intrafibrillar capillaries and polysaccharide spaces

### Metabolism-Dependent Mechanisms
- Intracellular accumulation
- Extracellular metabolite production (organic acids, phosphatases)
- Metal solubilization and complexing

### Cell Wall Functional Groups
Fungal cell walls contain chitin, chitosan, glucan, proteins, lipids, and other polysaccharides. Functional groups involved in metal binding include:
- Carboxyl (-COOH)
- Sulfate, phosphate, hydroxyl
- Amino, imino
- Sulfonate, imidazole, sulfydryl
- Carbonyl, thioether

Filamentous fungi generally possess higher adsorption capacities than other microorganisms. The biomass usually contains more functional groups than monofunctional ion-exchange resins.

### Specific Binding Mechanisms Documented
- Rhizopus nigricans: amino groups of cell walls bind Cr(VI); chemical modification increases active binding sites
- Mucor cell walls: amine functional groups remove chromium from tanning effluent
- Yeast (S. cerevisiae): -COOH groups involved in lead biosorption (FTIR spectroscopy)
- Mucor rouxii: -COOH groups are binding sites for metals
- Three-step Pb(II) accumulation in S. cerevisiae: metabolism-independent (3 min) -> metabolism-dependent (2 hours) -> mixed mechanism (24 hours)

## Fungal Metal Transformation

### Reduction
- [[aureobasidium-pullulans]], [[candida-albicans]], S. cerevisiae: Ag(I) -> Ag(0)
- S. cerevisiae: Fe(III) -> Fe(II)
- Fusarium spp.: Se(IV/VI) -> Se(0); Te(II/VI) -> Te(0)
- [[aspergillus-niger]] dead biomass: Cr(VI) -> Cr(III) via two mechanisms
- [[fusarium-oxysporum]]: Ag(I) -> Ag(0) forming 5-15 nm silver nanoparticles stabilized by proteins

### Methylation
- [[alternaria-alternata]]: Se(IV/VI) -> (CH3)2Se (dimethyl selenide, volatile)
- Penicillium spp.: As(V) -> (CH3)2HAs or (CH3)3As (volatile arsenic compounds)
- Penicillium spp.: Te(IV/VI) -> (CH3)2Te

### Dealkylation
- Penicillium MR-2: Organic Hg -> Hg(II) -> Hg(0)

### Metal Leaching
- Oxalic acid: leaching agent for Al, Fe, Li (forming soluble metal oxalate complexes)
- Bjerkandera fumosa, Phlebia radiata, [[trametes-versicolor]], Fomitopsis pinicola: produce oxalate crystals on metal-containing substrates
- Brown-rot fungi: maintain oxalic acid up to 600 uM/g; induction related to copper tolerance
- [[penicillium-simplicissimum]]: zinc adsorption accompanied by citric acid production
- Aspergillus niger cultural filtrate: solubilizes 18% Cu, 7% Ni, 4% Co

## Biosorption Reactor Types

### Batch Stirred-Tank Reactors
- Earliest used for metal-biosorbent effectiveness testing
- Plug-flow system: 2-5 min retention time, 87% copper reduction (Butter et al., 1998)
- [[rhizopus-arrhizus]] batch reactors in series for Cu(II) and Ni(II) removal

### Continuous-Flow Stirred-Tank Reactors (CFST)
- Two types: continuous fresh biosorbent feed, or retained batch in system
- Rhizopus arrhizus: heavy metal removal demonstrated

### Fixed Packed-Bed Reactors
- Pycnosporus sanguineus: >90% removal of Pb(II), Cu(II), Cd(II)
- 5 cm diameter, 1 m length column
- Lead uptake increases with bed height (4-15 cm)
- Efficiency maintained for first two cycles, slight drop in third/fourth

### Immobilized Reactors
- Most commercially oriented biosorption research
- Aspergillus niger in polysulfone matrix: >50% removal of Pb, Cd, Cu, Ni
- [[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]] in PVA gel: 97% Cu(II) removal
- Mucor rouxii in polysulfone: 4.0 mg/g Pb, 3.7 mg/g Cd
- Trametes versicolor: heat-killed mycelia showed HIGHER Cd(II) uptake (153 mg/g) than live (124 mg/g)
- Lentinus sajor-caju: dead mycelia (90% Cd removal) > live mycelia (78%)

## Evaluation Models

### Langmuir Model
Monolayer sorption on surface with finite identical sites:
q = qmax * bCf / (1 + bCf)

### Freundlich Model
Heterogeneous surface sorption:
q = K * Cf^(1/n)

Other models: Scatchard-Langmuir, Redlich-Peterson, BET, Radke-Prausnitz, Dubinin-Radushkevich

### Key Limitation
Most models focus on single-metal systems. Limited knowledge on multimetal systems makes predictive conclusions difficult. [[biosorption-isotherms]] do not always provide meaningful interpretation and are not applicable under variable environmental conditions.

## Factors Affecting Biosorption

- Type of metal and ionic form
- pH, temperature
- Biomass concentration and preparation type
- Initial metal ion concentration and characteristics
- Concentration of interfering ions
- Stirring (magnetic field can intensify biosorption and recovery)
- Multiple-site binding creates irregular isotherms
