# Blewit (Clitocybe) Cultivation

## Overview

The blewit (*[[clitocybe-nuda]]*) is a striking late-season mushroom recognized by its purplish-blue coloration that fades to tan or beige with exposure to light and expansion. Its arrival in fall and early winter signals that a heavy frost or freeze has occurred, as these mushrooms are triggered by cold weather. The blewit forms complex alliances—or battles—with [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] in the soil, making it both fascinating and challenging to cultivate. Spotting a lone blewit lifting recently fallen leaves to reveal a sliver of purple or blue is one of the most rewarding experiences in autumn foraging.

## Common Species

- *C. nuda* (blewit, wood blewit)
- *C. saeva* (blue legs)

## Difficulty Level

- Outdoor cultivation: **2** (intermediate)
- Indoor cultivation: **3** (advanced)

## Ecology and Natural Habitat

Blewits are cold-weather fungi that fruit in the fall and early winter after significant cold snaps. They grow on composted hardwood leaves, composted manure, and leaf litter in forests and gardens. The mushrooms create a carbon dioxide-rich microclimate under their bubble of leaves, which keeps the cap mostly hidden—very few make it into open air to sporulate, suggesting they may rely on alternative [[fungal-spore-biology]] such as insects.

Blewits can resemble blue species of *Cortinarius*, some of which are potentially toxic. However, [[spore-print]] colors are remarkably different: blewits produce **creamy pink spores** while *Cortinarius* produces **rusty orange spores**. There should be absolutely no mistake when using spore prints for identification.

## Mycelium and Spawn

Blewit mycelium is **bluish and linear**, and it does not exude many metabolites except in the presence of contamination or during colonization on pasteurized substrates. Some native strains exhibit **bioluminescence**—one isolated strain luminesces bright orange under black light.

## Fruitbody Development

The blewit is a stemmed mushroom with the cap positioned centrally and **attached gills** that generally lift and become wavy at maturity. The outer margin of the cap sometimes has a distinct color zone—a different shade of watermarked purple or cream compared to the center of the cap. This color zoning feature allows identification from yards away. The **spore print is creamy pink**.

## Fruiting Conditions

Most blewit strains require a **significant cold shock** to fruit. In outdoor beds, blewits typically do not appear until temperatures have dropped below freezing for at least a few days. After the cold shock, a brief warming period of just a few days above freezing can trigger a massive fruiting event. Local wild strains are always preferable, as they are adapted to regional microbial communities and climate patterns.

## Wild Spawn Expansion Techniques

### Cardboard Culture

Cardboard culture works well with blewits. Wrap up the purple stem bases into **"blewit burritos"** and place them in a plastic bag in the refrigerator for a few weeks.

### The Blewit Bomb

Blewits are remarkably resilient—they can freeze solid, thaw, and continue to mature. This quality enables a creative expansion technique:

1. Place stem bases, soil and all, in a blender with a little water
2. Pulverize the mixture
3. Pour into a ziplock bag and freeze for a few days
4. Place the frozen chunk on top of a compost pile
5. As it slowly melts, it coats the compost beneath with blewit mycelium and possibly the microbial triggers needed for fruiting

This technique has produced nice patches of blewits the following year just feet away from the compost pile where the "bomb" was deployed.

### Preferred Materials

Blewits prefer primarily hardwood tree bark and leaf compost. Massive natural fruitings have been observed on composted horse manure, producing some of the largest blewits encountered. An intuitive cultivator should study and mimic the conditions responsible for such prolific natural fruiting.

## Laboratory Isolation and Spawn Culture

Blewits are **easy to clone** from tissue located inside the stem base, which is usually fluffy and sometimes hollow. Using a sterile scalpel or dental pick, pull out a small chunk of this mycelium and flip it upside down onto an agar plate for best results.

Spore germinations are possible but prone to contamination. Because the mycelium grows slower than most [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]], very good sterile technique and **antibiotic agar media** are essential to give it enough time to generate sufficient mycelium on petri dishes for transfer to grain.

Blewit strains **store well on colonized grains submerged in water**.

## Preferred Fruiting Substrates

Blewits prefer **composted hardwood leaves** or composted mixtures of manure and cereal straws with agricultural by-products such as bean hulls, cornstalks, cotton waste, beet pulp, and sugarcane bagasse. These cold-weather fruiters enjoy a complex growing medium such as a composted mix of hardwood leaves, twigs, and bark debris.

## Casing Soil

A **microbial [[casing-soil]] is required**. When the entire surface of the substrate is a continuous white mat, dress the bed with microbial casing soil to stimulate primordia formation.

## Outdoor Cultivation

- **Media**: Composted leaves, spent [[oyster-mushroom]] substrate, composted hardwood sawdusts, or manure-rich composts of cereal straws with cotton hulls or other agricultural by-products. Blewits prefer a complex matrix over a basic one.
- **Bed preparation**: Spread compost in shallow rows or beds approximately 8–10 inches deep. Allow mycelium to colonize, then dress with microbial casing soil.
- **Spawn type**: [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] spawn
- **Inoculation to fruiting**: 6–12 months for colonization; fruiting depends on when cold weather arrives
- **Expected yields**: Good but sporadic; 4–5 pounds per 16 square feet of 8-inch-deep beds
- **Bed replacement**: Replace beds every two years, or at the end of each winter for more intensive production (allowing time for new beds to establish and fruit the following fall)

## Indoor Cultivation

Although typically cultivated outdoors, some success has been reported indoors on composted mixtures of sawdust and rice bran or composted manure and straw formulas.

- **Media**: Composted sawdust/rice bran or manure/straw. Fill trays 8–10 inches deep. Poke narrow holes in a diamond pattern every 6–8 inches, fill with spawn, and cover with wet compost. Cover substrate with perforated plastic or drilled plywood.
- **Cold shock**: Critical—blewits will not fruit without it. Options include refrigeration or flash freezing of colonized trays.
- **[[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]]**: Adding beneficial nematodes as a food source may prompt the mycelium to react as in the wild, possibly triggering a feeding or fruiting cycle.
- **Spawn type**: Grain or supplemented sawdust spawn
- **Inoculation to fruiting**: Approximately 6 weeks minimum (3–4 weeks colonization and casing, plus 3 weeks cold shock and maturation)
- **Expected yields**: Low and unpredictable; 2–3 pounds per square foot. More research is needed to improve indoor methods.

## Harvesting

Blewits form **tight buttons** that quickly enlarge—at which point they store poorly. The most desirable mushrooms are large and firm, with cap margins still rolled slightly inward toward the stem. Pick them at the button to tight-cap stage for best quality and storage.

## Storage

Firm, fresh mushrooms keep up to **two weeks at 38–42°F (3–6°C)**. Dried blewits do not rehydrate well but can be powdered. The best long-term storage method is **sautéing and then freezing**, which preserves texture and flavor effectively.

## Marketing

Blewits are well-described as **"sweet and silky shiitakes."** When sliced, the flesh is smooth, but sautéing can make the mushroom unappealing to some people as it tends to soften and become slightly slimy where the smooth layer cooks off. The solution is to add sautéed blewits to **soups and gumbos**, where they blend perfectly with soft cubes of potato, leeks, and a splash of sherry.

## Nutritional Value

Blewit mushrooms are approximately **90% water** with a protein content of **24–26% by dry weight**. They contain compounds that inhibit both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria and have shown high activity against tuberculosis. *Clitocybe* species also contain antifungal and antibacterial compounds that suppress the growth of *[[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]]* and *Serratia aureus*.

## Mycoremediation Applications

- **Foliar bactericide and insecticide**: Extracts from blewit fruitbodies can be used as foliar applications for pest and disease management.
- **Metal hyperaccumulation**: Blewits are well known for their ability to hyperaccumulate metals in their mycelium, making them well suited for **mycofiltration of contaminated soil and water**.
