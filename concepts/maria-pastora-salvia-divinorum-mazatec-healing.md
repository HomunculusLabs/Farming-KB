# María Pastora — Salvia Divinorum and Mazatec Healing Practices

## Overview

During his expeditions to the Mazatec region of Mexico, Albert Hofmann encountered another sacred psychoactive plant used by indigenous healers: *Salvia divinorum*, known locally as ska María Pastora ("the herb of Mary the Shepherdess"). This concept page describes Hofmann's encounter with Mazatec healing practices and the chemical investigation of this enigmatic plant.

## The Curandera Consuela García

### The Healing Session

Hofmann describes being admitted to the hut of "curandera Consuela García, a woman of some forty years, barefoot like all Indian women in this region." She "timidly admitted us to her hut and immediately closed up the doorway with a heavy bar."

The session took place on bast mats on a stamped mud floor. Since Consuela spoke only Mazatec, Herlinda (a bilingual Mazatec woman) translated her instructions into Spanish. The curandera lit a candle on a table "covered with some images of saints, along with a variety of rubbish."

### The Preparation

The curandera prepared a magic potion ceremoniously, after kindling copal incense that "soon filled the whole hut with its aroma." The combination of Catholic saints' images with indigenous ritual elements (copal incense, sacred plants) illustrates the syncretic nature of Mazatec spiritual practice—pre-Columbian traditions maintained under a Catholic surface.

### Gordon Wasson's Participation

When the curandera asked who wished to drink of the potion, "Gordon announced himself." Since Hofmann was suffering from a severe stomach upset, he could not participate; his wife substituted for him. The curandera then led the ritual, which involved specific preparations, prayers, and the ceremonial consumption of the potion.

## Salvia Divinorum

### Discovery and Naming

The plant used in the curandera's potion was identified as "a hitherto undescribed species of Salvia, which was named Salvia divinorum by these authors" (Wasson and Hofmann's botanical collaborators). The name means "divine sage," reflecting its sacred status in Mazatec culture.

### Ska María Pastora

The Mazatec name, ska María Pastora, means "the herb of Mary the Shepherdess"—again linking the plant's indigenous use to Christian iconography. The Virgin Mary serves as a bridge between indigenous and Catholic spiritual traditions.

### The Chemical Mystery

"The chemical investigation of the juice of the magic sage in the laboratory in Basel was unsuccessful. The psychoactive principle of this drug seems to be a rather unstable substance, since the juice prepared in Mexico and preserved with alcohol proved in self-experiments to be no longer active."

This failure to identify the active principle of *Salvia divinorum* was a significant frustration for Hofmann. The plant's psychoactive compound—later identified as salvinorin A, a neoclerodane diterpenoid—proved exceptionally difficult to isolate and characterize because of its instability and its presence in very low concentrations in the plant material.

### Salvinorin A — A Unique Pharmacology

Although Hofmann did not live to see the identification of salvinorin A (accomplished in the 1990s), the compound turned out to have a pharmacological profile unlike any other known psychedelic: it is a kappa-opioid receptor agonist, making it the first naturally occurring non-nitrogenous psychedelic compound and placing it in an entirely different pharmacological class from the tryptamines (psilocybin, DMT) and lysergamides (LSD).

## The Mazatec Healing Tradition

### Syncretism

The Mazatec healing session described by Hofmann illustrates the characteristic syncretism of indigenous Mexican spiritual practice:
- Catholic saints' images on the altar
- Copal incense (pre-Columbian purification practice)
- Psychoactive plant preparations (indigenous pharmacology)
- Prayers in Mazatec (indigenous language) and Spanish (colonial language)

This layered tradition is not a corruption of an original practice but a dynamic, adaptive system that has incorporated new elements while maintaining its core structure.

### The Role of the Curandera

The curandera functions as both healer and priest, combining botanical knowledge with spiritual authority. Her ability to prepare and administer the sacred plants, combined with her knowledge of prayers and ritual procedures, makes her the central figure in the community's health care system.

### Privacy and Secrecy

The closed door, the barred entrance, the timidity of admission—all reflect the Mazatec's protective attitude toward their sacred practices. The secrecy is not merely cultural conservatism but a genuine concern that the plants' power could be misused or profaned by outsiders who do not understand the ceremonial framework.

## The Unresolved Problem

"Where the chemical nature of the active principle is concerned, the problem of the magic plant ska María Pastora still awaits solution." Hofmann wrote these words in the 1970s or early 1980s; the solution (salvinorin A) would not come until the 1990s. The long delay in identifying the active principle reflects both the compound's unusual chemistry (a non-alkaloid diterpenoid, unlike most psychoactive plant constituents) and the limited resources available for studying a plant that was not part of the mainstream psychedelic research agenda.
