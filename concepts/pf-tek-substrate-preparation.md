# PF TEK Substrate Formula and Jar Preparation

## Overview

The **PF TEK** ([[psylocybe-fanaticus]] Technique), introduced in 1991 and fully
published in 1992, is a revolutionary home [[miniculture-rye-grain-cultivation-method-for-psilocybe-cubensis]] *Psilocybe
cubensis*. Its innovation lies in using **vermiculite as a structural base**
with **brown rice powder** as the nutrient source, eliminating the need for
[[comparison-pressure-canning-vs-water-bath-canning]] and enabling sterilization with a simple vegetable steamer.

## The Innovation: Vermiculite as Substrate Base

Traditional grain-based substrates produce dense mycelial mats with little air
space. The PF TEK's key insight was using **horticultural vermiculite** as a
structural medium:

- Mycelial threads **stretch across vermiculite particles**, creating an airy,
  oxygenated substrate
- The vermiculite provides excellent **water retention** while maintaining
  aeration
- The result is a "cake" rather than a dense block — more surface area for
  fruiting and easier handling

As PF stated: "The secret is in the vermiculite." The technique was copied
worldwide and became the foundation of home [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]].

## Standard Substrate Formula (per 1/2 Pint Jar)

### Ingredients
- **1/8 cup** brown rice powder (freshly ground preferred)
- **1/2 to 2/3 cup** horticultural vermiculite (medium grade)
- **40–45 cc** (≈3 tablespoons + 1 teaspoon) water

### Maximum Fruiting Formula
- **1/4 cup** brown rice powder
- **1/2 cup** vermiculite
- **60 cc** water

The maximum fruiting formula produces larger yields but requires loose lids
during incubation to prevent water accumulation.

## Vermiculite Selection

Vermiculite coarseness significantly affects water-holding capacity and thus
substrate performance:

| Type | Particle Size | Water Holding |
|------|--------------|---------------|
| Fine | ~1 mm average | Higher (preferred) |
| Coarse | ~4–5 mm, up to 8 mm | Lower |

The **fine type is recommended** because it holds more water, which translates
to better [[mycelial-growth-and-colonization]] fruiting. If the coarse type is the only option,
reduce water proportionally. Store availability varies regionally — PF
recommends shopping around to compare types.

## Brown Rice Powder

Freshly ground brown rice from a coffee bean grinder is recommended over
pre-packaged rice flour:
- Freshness "sometimes makes a big difference" in colonization vigor
- Any standard brown rice works — no special varieties needed
- Grind to a fine powder, similar to flour consistency

## Jar Selection

Jars must meet specific criteria:
- **1/2 pint (8 oz / 250 ml) capacity** — larger jars are unreliable
- **Tapered sides with no shoulder** — essential for cake removal
- Recommended jars: KERR wide mouth 1/2 pint, BALL regular mouth 1/2 pint,
  BALL 1/2 pint jelly jar
- Alternative: 250 ml tapered drinking glasses with tin foil covers

**Note**: Even though KERR and BALL 1/2 pint regular mouth jars look similar,
the KERR version is NOT tapered — the BALL is preferred.

## Lid Preparation

Standard canning jar lids are modified for gas exchange:

1. Place the lid rubber-sealing-edge **upwards** on a flat surface
2. Using a sharpened **3-penny nail** held in vise-grip pliers, punch **4 holes**
   inside the periphery of the rubber sealing edge
3. The holes allow gas exchange during colonization while the dry vermiculite
   barrier prevents contaminant entry

## Step-by-Step Jar Preparation

### Step 1: Mixing
Place 1/2 cup vermiculite in a mixing bowl. Add brown rice powder on top.
Slowly add water directly onto the rice powder, wetting it first. Mix
thoroughly. The mixture should feel **damp and cohesive**. Mix each jar's
substrate individually for accurate formula rendering.

### Step 2: Loading
Fill the jar **very loosely**. Leave **1/2 to 3/4 inch** of space at the top.
Level the substrate. Wipe the jar interior above the substrate clean with a
tissue. Fill the top space with **plain dry vermiculite** and level it off.

This dry vermiculite layer is PF's **original discovery** and critical innovation:
- Acts as a **contaminant barrier** against airborne spores and bacteria
- Absorbs and **regulates moisture transpiration** and condensation
- Protects the wet nutrient substrate below

### Step 3: Lidding
Place the modified lid on with rubberized edge up (needle holes facing down).
Screw the band on loosely at this stage. Cover needle holes with
professional-grade masking tape (must hold during steaming).

### Step 4: Steam Sterilization
- Use a **3-piece vegetable steamer** (pot, basket insert, lid)
- Ensure jar bottoms are elevated above direct pot bottom contact
- Heat water to boil, add jars (loosely banded)
- Turn heat to lowest possible boil and steam for **1 hour**
- A **tight-fitting pot lid** is essential for successful steaming
- **Do not overheat** — overheating dries the substrate

### Overheating Diagnosis
If jars show OK spore germination but **growth halts at a certain point** with
no contamination, the substrate is likely too dry. The remedy is to increase
water content in subsequent batches.

## Control Jar Technique

After sterilization, let jars cool and tighten lids. Store **uninoculated**
for several days as a control:
- Watch for colored growths or appearance changes
- Check for rancid bacterial odor by loosening the lid
- If contamination appears at this stage, extend sterilization time
- If jars remain clean, they are ready for inoculation
- If contamination occurs **after** inoculation, the syringe or technique
  (disturbing the dry barrier) is at fault
