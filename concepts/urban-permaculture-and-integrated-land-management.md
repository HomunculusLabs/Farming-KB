# Urban Permaculture and Integrated Land Management

## Overview

Urban areas and degraded landscapes represent both the greatest challenge and the greatest opportunity for [[permaculture]]. Mollison documents how vacant lots, rooftops, walls, and food waste streams can be transformed into productive systems, while simultaneously presenting the Hawaiian *ohana* and Māori *marae* as models of integrated land management that integrate human settlements with their ecological context from hilltop to shoreline.

## Urban Agriculture

### Vacant Land Transformation

The Green Guerillas of the Bronx, New York, transform vacant land in urban wastelands into productive, beautiful, and recreational spaces as city farms. This model demonstrates that urban decay can be reversed through community-driven agricultural action, turning neglected parcels into assets that produce food, build community, and improve neighborhood safety.

### Glide Farm: Urban Herb Production

Glide Farm in the South Bronx produces 8% of New York City's herbs while providing employment for many people in an area of urban decay. This demonstrates that even small urban agricultural operations can achieve significant market share when they focus on high-value crops suited to intensive urban production.

### Schreber Gardens (Germany)

The productive Schreber gardens adjoin almost every settlement in West Germany, providing food and much-needed recreation in cities. People are permitted to spend weekends on these mini-farms. This model represents a widespread cultural integration of urban agriculture into everyday life, where allotment gardening is recognized as essential infrastructure rather than a hobby.

### Urban Building Surfaces as Agricultural Space

Mollison argues that most urban buildings should be regarded as additional agricultural space:

- **Sun-facing walls** provide radiant heat for marginal crops — espaliered pears grow well on west or sun-facing walls, "softening" the hard surfaces of urban buildings
- **Trellis systems** over alleys, shopping complexes, and building walls create vertical growing space
- **Rooftops** with ice plants and herbs provide active cooling through transpiration and shade (documented at Village Homes, Davis, California)

This reframing transforms the urban environment from an agricultural wasteland into one of the most productive landscape types per unit area, because vertical surfaces, waste heat, and concentrated human populations create unique advantages.

### Composting Urban Food Waste

The Bronx Frontier group operates a small [[composting]] business — documented as "the only example of productive use of New York's food waste." The compost produced is sold off-site to create meadows over rubble-filled wastelands, completing a cycle from urban waste to urban greening.

This model is particularly significant because it addresses two urban problems simultaneously: food waste disposal and the rehabilitation of degraded land.

## Energy Conservation in Urban Areas

### Project WARM (Louisville, Kentucky)

Project WARM volunteers retrofit and insulate old folks' homes by the thousand, sharply reducing energy costs and deaths from cold. The cost amortizes in two winters. Mollison argues that only minimal funds are directed to such sound expenditures in Western society, and that it is always cheaper to conserve energy rather than generate it.

He further argues that such conservation projects make many dangerous or dirty power-generating systems obsolete (specifically mentioning nuclear power), because reduced demand eliminates the perceived need for high-risk energy sources.

## The Ohana and Marae Models

### Hawaiian Ohana System

The Hawaiian *ohana* was a geomorphic and sociological unit in which land and people were integrated for sustenance. Based on the radial valley of a volcanic island, the ohana system organized land use from the protected hilltop forests to the shore and beyond into the lagoon:

1. **Protected skyline and hill forests ("taboo" forests)**: Protect soils and waters, mine and release plant nutrients from upper slopes. Used for limited forage and mulch provision, but under strict protection. Clearing these forests brings compound catastrophes — landslide, loss of nutrients, desertification, and social disruption.

2. **Midslope or keypoint**: A diversion of stream water here leads water out to ridges for terrace crop and village use. This is the critical point where cropping commences downslope. The stable plateau and bench sites above flood reach, sheltered from hurricane and tsunami, are prime settlement sites.

3. **Low slopes (2°-15°)**: Well suited to earth-shaping as terrace and padi, with limited grazing and innovative forestry. These are the sustainable agricultural areas where attention to sub-contoured agriculture, windbreaks, and access directs runoff and water to crops.

4. **Coastal flats and valley floors**: Rich, deep humus soils accumulate here. Greywater and processed manures from settlement and livestock add to fertility, supporting extensive aquaculture or rich forest/orchard crops.

5. **Shorelines**: Preserved for approximately 100 meters (330 feet) inland for essential windbreak, shore stability, and cloud generation over land. Quiet harbors accept fishing settlements. Shores with 15-20 year tsunami history need sacrificial palm/Casuarina barriers to reduce storm damage. Lightly-built temporary shore housing is used only when working the coast, with permanent homes safely inland.

6. **Lagoon and estuary sea impoundments**: Ancient sea-walls of semi-permeable coral blocks with flood-tide gates and inner pens are an excellent use of shallows. Restricted mangrove plantings stabilize banks and provide sea mulch while catching silt. Warm-water lagoons are rich in algae and good for cultivated seaweed, and impound mullet, mangrove fish, oysters, eels, shellfish, and prawns. Mobile phosphates are fixed in plants and mud within days and become available for growth.

7. **Marine constructs**: Artificial reef systems of tires, coral blocks, and palm trunk boxes greatly enlarge habitat for sedentary fish and lobsters, consolidated by corals within a year. Weak electrolysis on metal mesh creates artificial coralline deposits more rapidly. Log barriers full of sand, stabilized with mangroves, provide shore barriers and lagoon walls.

### Māori Marae System

The Māori *marae* shared similar principles — the integration of land and people for sustenance within a geomorphic framework. Mollison suggests these systems may have evolved out of early errors of over-clearing, excessive burning, and extinction of useful animals before reaching equilibrium.

This observation is prescient: both the Hawaiian and Māori systems represent societies that learned from environmental mistakes and developed stable, productive land management practices that sustained large populations for centuries.

## Elements of a Village Complex in the Humid Tropics

Based on traditional and modern villages, Mollison provides a checklist for building integrated village complexes:

### Core Infrastructure
- Well-arranged housing grouped around a compound
- Compound with processing and storage areas, threshing floors, dancing areas, meeting house
- Cooperative or retail store, bulk fuel depots, firewood and mulch depots
- Well, piped water, tanks, or dam integrated for clean water supply
- Plant nursery to serve gardens and forest

### Agricultural Zones
- At each house: 0.25-1.0 hectares of home garden and orchard based on self-reliance
- At garden borders: ranges for domestic species (chickens, guinea pigs, ducks, rabbits, small pigs, pigeons) as manure resources
- Where water is plentiful: fish ponds beneath animal housing (manure feeds fish)
- Sector or zone of fuelwood plantation integrated with windbreak
- Stock yard and sheds for cattle and pigs at commercial level
- Careful zonation of tree polyculture

### Special Facilities
- Log-trimming or boat-building area
- Fish net drying racks
- Canoe or small boat landing, wharf
- Freezer, solar pond installation
- Power house
- Large community drying shed
- Craft work areas
- Vehicle or draught-animal park area

## Evolving a Polyculture on Degraded Land

When starting to evolve permaculture on grasslands or compacted soils, the process follows a specific sequence:

### Site Preparation
1. Thoroughly plan the site
2. Rip, swale, pit, or dam every area to be planted
3. Ensure maximum wet-season soil water storage for dry periods
4. Begin at the highest point of the property and around the house or village

### Initial Planting
On loosened soils and in mulched swales, co-plant a mix of:
- **Tree legumes**: Acacia at 3 × 3 meter spacing
- **Fruits**: At 5 × 8 meter spacing
- **Bananas**: At 2 × 2 meter spacing
- **Palms**: At 10 × 10 meter spacing
- **Gap fillers**: Arrowroot (*Canna*), cassava, sweet potato, comfrey at 1-1.5 meter spacing

All larger planting holes should be seeded with nasturtium, Dolichos, Haifa clover, broad bean (fava), buckwheat, Umbelliferae (dill, fennel), lupin, vetch, dun peas, chilies, pigeon pea, or any useful non-grass mix available.

### Succession Timeline
- **0-18 months**: Completely carpet and overshade the ground with dense plantings
- **18-20 months**: Begin mulching with grass, arrowroot, comfrey, banana, and Acacia tops
- **Ongoing**: Place shade-loving species (coffee, dry taro) in open spots; add paths, annual crop openings, bee plants, flowers, and fire-resistant ground covers (comfrey, Tradescantia, Impatiens, succulents)

### The Quarter-Hectare Principle

Mollison states: "It is far better to occupy a quarter hectare thoroughly than to scatter trees over 2 hectares, as production is higher and maintenance less, moisture is conserved, and frost excluded."

This principle is central to permaculture design: intensive management of a smaller area outperforms extensive management of a larger area in terms of total yield per unit of input.

## Key Principles

1. **Urban surfaces are agricultural space**: Walls, roofs, and alleys represent enormous underutilized growing area
2. **Energy conservation precedes energy generation**: Retrofitting insulation is always cheaper and safer than building new power plants
3. **Integrate from hilltop to ocean**: The ohana model provides a template for complete landscape integration
4. **Shorelines need buffers**: 100-meter coastal setbacks protect against storms and tsunamis while providing windbreaks and cloud generation
5. **Lagoon systems capture nutrients**: Sea impoundments retain leached nutrients and convert them to productive aquaculture
6. **Start intensive, not extensive**: A thoroughly managed quarter hectare outproduces a scattered 2 hectares
7. **Village design integrates all functions**: Housing, food production, water, fuel, processing, and community facilities form an interconnected system
