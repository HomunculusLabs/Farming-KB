# Integrated Land Management and Tropical Hill-to-Shore Profiles

Integrated land management draws on traditional geomorphic and sociological units—such as the Maori marae and Hawaiian ohana—where land and people were unified for sustenance. These systems may have evolved from early errors of over-clearing, excessive burning, and extinction of useful animals before reaching ecological equilibrium. The modern challenge is to apply these lessons before similar errors compound into irreversible damage.

## The Hill-to-Shore Profile

For a hill-to-shore profile (often a volcanic cone), a stable tropical landscape requires the following integrated features arranged from summit to sea:

### Protected Skyline and Hill Forests
The uppermost slopes serve as protected forest reserves that:
- Protect soils and waters from erosion and nutrient loss
- Mine and release plant nutrients from steep upper slopes
- Provide limited forage resources and mulch provision
- Act as water catchments for lower elevations

Clearing these forests brings compound catastrophes including landslide, loss of nutrient in water and crop, desertification, and consequent severe social disruption. These forests require iron-clad protection. At the base of these forests, as the slope eases to 15° or less, water can be diverted or harvested to replenish groundwater and irrigate terraces and crops.

### Midslope and Keypoint
The **keypoint** is the critical transition zone where stream water can be diverted out to ridges for terrace crop and village use. Cropping commences below this point. Human occupation and complex cultivated forests and gardens are established downslope.

The stable plateau, the hill rising above the valley, and bench sites above the reach of flood and sheltered from hurricane and tsunami are prime cluster settlement (village) sites, with some scattered housing on higher ridges and the forest edge.

### Low Slopes (2°-15°)
Low slopes are well suited to earth-shaping as terraces and padi fields, with limited grazing and innovative forestry. These are the sustainable agricultural areas where attention to:
- Sub-contoured agriculture directs runoff and water to crops
- Windbreak design reduces evapotranspiration
- Access planning minimizes soil disturbance

### Coastal Flats and Valley Floors
Rich and often deep humus soils accumulate on valley floors. Greywater and processed manures from settlement and livestock add to fertility, where extensive aquacultures or rich forest and orchard crops can be developed.

### Shorelines
Shorelines are preserved for approximately 100 meters (330 feet) inland for:
- Essential windbreak and shore stability
- Cloud generation over land
- Storm surge protection

Selected quiet harbors and refuges accept fishing settlements based on sea resources. Shores with 15-20 year tsunami history need sacrificial palm or Casuarina barriers to reduce storm damage ashore. Lightly-built temporary shore housing is used only when working the coast, with more substantial homes and sheltered gardens safely inland or behind earth embankments.

## Lagoon and Estuary Systems

### Sea Impoundments
Ancient and innovative sea-walls (usually of semi-permeable coral blocks with entries, flood-tide gates, and a series of inner pens) are an excellent use of shallows. Features include:

- **Restricted mangrove plantings** stabilize banks and provide sea mulch while catching silt washed down from above
- **Warm-water lagoons** are rich in algae and serve as sites for cultivated seaweed
- **Fish impoundment** fattens mullet, mangrove fish, oysters, eels, shellfish, and prawns
- **Nutrient cycling:** Mobile phosphates are fixed in plants and mud within a few days, becoming available for growth

These rich estuaries are invaluable as managed maricultures, concentrating nutrients that would otherwise be lost to the sea.

### Marine Constructs
Artificial reef systems greatly enlarge habitat for sedentary fish and lobsters:
- **Tyre reefs** on sand substrate consolidate within a year
- **Coral block and palm trunk structures** are cemented by coral growth
- **Electrolysis on metal mesh** creates artificial but permanent coralline deposits more rapidly
- **Log barriers** filled with sand and stabilized with mangroves provide shore barriers, lagoon walls, and marine "edge"

## The Hawaiian Ohana System

The Hawaiian ohana represents a refined model of integrated land management across a volcanic island's radial valley:

- **Zone A (Taboo Forest):** Protected upper slope forests preserving watersheds and biodiversity
- **Zone B (Stream Diversion):** Water directed from keypoint to ridge terraces and main village
- **Zone C (Sacrificial Forests):** Palm stands to modify hurricane damage
- **Zone D (Fish Ponds):** Coastal ponds retain leached nutrients, growing mullet and shellfish
- **Zone E (Reef Systems):** Coral-modified reefs shelter crayfish and fish

This system demonstrates how each zone's output becomes the next zone's input—forest protects water, water irrigates crops, crop waste feeds fish, fish waste fertilizes the system.

## Windbreaks for Tropical Systems

Primary tall windbreaks of Araucaria, Cupressus, Casuarina, Pinus, hardy Phoenix palms, and even mangroves may be needed in front-line coastal locations. These are followed in the lee by hardy quickset species such as:

- **[[coprosma-repens-entity]]** (mirror bush) — salt-tolerant coastal shrub
- **[[echium-fastuosum]]** (pride of Madeira) — drought-tolerant bee forage

It is always best to find local plant species that do well in the district rather than importing exotic species.

## Erosion Control

Contours planted at specific intervals provide effective erosion control:

- **Medium slopes (2°-7°):** Contour plantings at 10-meter intervals
- **Steep slopes:** Contour plantings at 5-meter intervals

Species used include root sets of Canna, [[vetiver-grass]], lemongrass, or pampas grass, set out at 0.3-0.6 meter spacing to form unbroken cross-slope hedges. These systems:
- Disperse water flow energy
- Create silt traps behind living walls
- Develop deeper soil where trees can be planted or crops grown
- Provide mulch material

In desert areas, yuccas, agaves, and aloes may provide similar structural effects.

## Integrated Fencing Systems

Cut-and-build fences using Euphorbia tirucalli and Acacia tortilis protect compounds from grazing animals. Euphorbia tirucalli can be propagated from cuttings even in arid conditions. Acacia tortilis is ideal for cut-and-build fences due to its thorny branches. When cutting Euphorbia, protective goggles should be worn and skin covered if allergic to the milky sap.

## Design Principles

The integrated land management approach rests on several key principles:

1. **Work with topography:** Design follows the natural hill-to-shore gradient
2. **Preserve upper catchments:** Forest protection prevents downstream catastrophe
3. **Capture water at the keypoint:** The transition zone is the critical leverage point
4. **Create nutrient cycles:** Every output should become an input to the next system
5. **Design for catastrophe:** Sacrificial elements absorb storm and tsunami energy
6. **Use local species:** Adapted plants outperform imported alternatives
7. **Integrate social and ecological systems:** Land management and community organization are inseparable
