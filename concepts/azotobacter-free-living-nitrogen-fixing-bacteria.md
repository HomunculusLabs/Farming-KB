# Azotobacter — Free-Living Nitrogen-Fixing Bacteria

Azotobacter is a genus of free-living, aerobic, nitrogen-fixing bacteria belonging to the family Azotobacteraceae within the Gammaproteobacteria class. First described by Martinus Beijerinck in 1901, these organisms are 
among the most studied free-living diazotrophs and play a significant role in soil [[azotobacter-chroococcum]]* is the most commonly isolated species and is widely

When subjected to nutrient limitation, particularly carbon or phosphorus starvation, 
Azotobacter cells undergo a differentiation process producing a spherical, thick-walled cyst surrounded by a protective coat composed of lipids, proteins, and polysaccharides. The cyst wall contains alkylresorcinols and 
unique lipids that confer extraordinary resistance to environmental stressors. These cysts can survive extreme desiccation, UV radiation, and temperatures up to 80°C for extended periods — in some documented cases, 
remaining viable for over 10 years under dry storage.

The process of encystment involves several morphological and biochemical changes. The rod-shaped vegetative cell rounds up, and the central body becomes surrounded by a thickened intine layer and an outer exine coat. 
During this process, the cell accumulates poly-β-hydroxybutyrate (PHB) granules as a carbon and energy reserve, and the RNA content of the cell increases significantly in preparation for the rapid metabolic activation 
required during germination. Protein synthesis shifts toward stress-response proteins including catalase, superoxide dismutase, and heat-shock proteins.

Cyst germination is triggered by the availability of fresh carbon substrates and favorable moisture conditions. The process begins with the swelling of the cyst, followed by the emergence of a vegetative cell through a 
rupture in the cyst coat.
