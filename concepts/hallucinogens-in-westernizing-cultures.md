# Hallucinogens in Westernizing Cultures

## Overview

The global spread of Western society has historically entailed the introduction of Christian
belief systems into indigenous contexts throughout the Americas, Africa, Oceania, and Asia.
In many regions, this cultural encounter has not simply eradicated native hallucinogenic
practices but has produced complex **syncretisms** — fusion systems in which traditional
psychoactive substances continue to be employed, now invoked under the auspices of Christian
supernatural figures. The resulting practices represent neither pure pre-Columbian tradition
nor orthodox Christianity, but rather dynamic cultural accommodations shaped by colonial
pressure, economic necessity, and persistent indigenous cosmologies.

The ethnographic record from North and South America reveals a consistent pattern:
hallucinogenic plant use persists at the margins of Westernizing societies precisely where
institutional religion and biomedicine fail to meet the psychological and social needs of
marginalized populations. Far from being mere survivals or degenerative remnants of
pre-contact culture, these syncretic practices represent active, adaptive responses to the
disruptions of colonization, urbanization, and cultural change. They deserve serious
attention both as ethnographic phenomena in their own right and as evidence for the
cross-cultural persistence of altered states of consciousness as a human therapeutic and
spiritual resource.

## Syncretism: Indigenous Hallucinogens and Christian Overlay

Throughout Latin America and Native North America, the collision of indigenous psychoactive
plant traditions with missionary Christianity produced hybrid religious forms of remarkable
complexity and resilience. Traditional hallucinogens — peyote (*[[lophophora-williamsii]]*),
[[psilocybin-mushrooms]] (*[[psilocybe-mexicana]]*), and ayahuasca (*[[banisteriopsis-caapi]]*) — were
reinterpreted within a Christian symbolic framework. Saints, the Virgin Mary, and Jesus
Christ replaced or were merged with pre-Christian spirit beings, while the ritual use of
visionary plants was recast as a means of communion with the Christian divine.

This syncretic process was rarely imposed from above by ecclesiastical authorities; rather,
it emerged organically from the practical needs of colonized and acculturating peoples who
sought to preserve culturally meaningful healing and divinatory practices under conditions
of rapid social change. The resulting systems typically retain indigenous ritual structures,
cosmological assumptions, and therapeutic logic while incorporating Christian personnel
(saints, Christ, God the Father), liturgical elements (prayer, hymnody, moral discourse),
and symbolic vocabulary. The depth and sincerity of Christian identification varies
considerably: some practitioners understand their work as genuinely Christian ministry,
while others employ Christian elements as protective camouflage for fundamentally indigenous
practices.

## The Native American Church

The **[[native-american-church]]** (NAC) constitutes the most well-documented and legally
recognized fusion of hallucinogenic drug use with Christianity in the Western Hemisphere.
Emerging in the late nineteenth century among Plains and southwestern American Indian
communities, the NAC centers on the ritual consumption of **peyote**, a mescaline-containing
cactus native to the Rio Grande basin and the Chihuahuan Desert of northern Mexico.
Peyote use among indigenous peoples of the region has antecedents extending back centuries
and possibly millennia, as evidenced by archaeological finds and early colonial chronicles.

Practitioners of the NAC — who are enrolled members of federally recognized tribes — employ
peyote in all-night ceremonial meetings structured around a distinctive liturgy that blends
indigenous and Christian elements. The ceremony, typically held in a tipi or dedicated
meeting house, involves prayer, the singing of peyote songs (accompanied by water drum
and gourd rattle), the ritual consumption of peyote buttons, and the sharing of communal
food and tobacco at dawn. Jesus Christ is addressed directly as a participant in the
ceremony, and peyote is understood as a sacrament — a visible sign conveying spiritual
grace — through which participants communicate with the divine, receive guidance, and
effect cures for physical and psychological illness.

The NAC represents a successful institutional adaptation of indigenous hallucinogen use to
the conditions of settler-colonial modernity. Its incorporation of Christian elements
enabled it to survive the systematic suppression of native religious practices that
characterized U.S. federal Indian policy from the 1880s through the 1930s. The church's
legal status was ultimately affirmed by the U.S. Congress through the American Indian
Religious Freedom Act of 1978 and subsequent judicial decisions, and it continues to serve
as a primary vehicle for spiritual and therapeutic practice among diverse tribal communities
across the United States and Canada.

## Mestizo Curanderismo in Mexico and Peru

Beyond the formally organized Native American Church, hallucinogen-based syncretic
practices persist throughout rural and urban Latin America under the broad rubric of
**curanderismo** — a heterogeneous system of folk healing practiced by *curanderos*
(healers) among Indian peasant and mestizo populations. In both Mexico and Peru,
curanderos employ native hallucinogenic substances within ritual frameworks that blend
pre-Columbian shamanistic cosmology with Catholic symbolism. These practitioners conduct
curing rites along lines established by ancient shamanism, invoking Christian saints and
indigenous spirits alike to diagnose and treat illness attributed to supernatural causation.

Ecclesiastical authorities generally regard curanderos with suspicion or outright hostility,
frequently categorizing them as *brujos* (witches) whose practices constitute heterodoxy or
superstition incompatible with orthodox Catholic doctrine. Despite this institutional
disapproval — and occasional active persecution — local populations make extensive use of
curandero services. The practical logic is straightforward: curanderos provide supernatural
diagnostic and therapeutic interventions — spirit contact, soul retrieval, counter-sorcery,
divinatory counsel, and the negotiation of relationships with powerful non-human beings —
that are unavailable through either the Catholic Church or the formal medical profession,
and at costs affordable to economically marginalized communities.

Curanderismo thus occupies a functional niche at the intersection of religion, medicine,
and social support. It is sustained not merely by cultural inertia but by the persistent
demand for services that address the experiential realities of illness and misfortune in
contexts of poverty, rapid social change, and inadequate institutional infrastructure. The
curandero's authority derives from demonstrated efficacy — the ability to produce
subjectively convincing diagnostic narratives and therapeutic outcomes — rather than from
formal credentialing or institutional sanction.

## The Mescalero Apache: Adoption and Rejection of Peyote

The Mescalero Apache case provides a critical counterexample to the pattern of successful
hallucinogen adoption and institutional integration. Peyote was introduced to the Mescalero
circa 1870 and was actively used within the community for approximately four decades before
being definitively rejected around 1910. Boyer, Boyer, and Basehart have offered a
psychocultural explanation for this rejection that underscores the importance of personality
structure and socialization dynamics in mediating the effects of psychoactive substances.

Mescalero socialization practices, they argue, produce adults whose hostilities are
repressed only with considerable difficulty — a characteristic shaped by cultural norms
governing emotional expression, interpersonal aggression, and the management of anger. The
physio-psychological effects of peyote — which include the intensification of affect,
emotional release, and the lowering of psychological defenses — made it impossible for many
Mescalero individuals to maintain their culturally conditioned repression of hostile impulses.
Under the influence of peyote, previously suppressed aggressions surfaced with disruptive
force, resulting in interpersonal conflict, bloodshed, and the exacerbation of existing
feuds. Peyote consequently came to be defined within Mescalero culture as an inherently
dangerous and morally evil substance, and its use was definitively abandoned.

This case is ethnographically significant for several reasons. First, it demonstrates that
the social consequences of hallucinogen use are not determined solely by pharmacological
properties but are profoundly shaped by the preexisting psychological and social structures
of the user community. Second, it highlights the instructive contrast between the Mescalero
experience and the harmonious communal use of peyote among other North American tribes
(such as the Kiowa, Comanche, and Navajo) and the similarly integrative use of ayahuasca
among Amazonian groups like the Cashinahua, Sharanahua, and Campa. In these societies,
cultural norms, socialization patterns, and ritual frameworks channel the psychoactive
experience toward socially constructive outcomes — healing, social cohesion, and spiritual
insight — rather than toward disruption and interpersonal violence.

## Ayahuasquero Curing in Iquitos, Peru

The work of Dobkin de Rios on **ayahuasquero** (mestizo ayahuasca shaman) practice in
Iquitos, Peru, provides a detailed account of hallucinogen-based healing in an urbanizing
Amazonian context. Iquitos is a city of mixed ancestry where slum-dwellers — many of
indigenous or mixed indigenous-European descent — face conditions of poverty, inadequate
healthcare infrastructure, and social marginalization. When Western biomedical treatment
fails to resolve illness — a frequent occurrence given the limitations of available medical
services — many residents turn to supernatural explanations, attributing their suffering to
sorcery, spirit attack, or divine punishment. In this context, the ayahuasquero serves as
a specialist in supernatural diagnosis and cure.
