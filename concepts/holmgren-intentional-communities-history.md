# Holmgren Intentional Communities History

Updated: 2026-04-19

David Holmgren provides a critical analysis of intentional communities in *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002), drawing on decades of observation within the permaculture movement and its precursors.

## A Century of Experimentation

Intentional communities have existed for over a century, with various waves of experimentation. Holmgren notes that permaculture itself emerged partly from the communitarian movements of the 1970s, and the Tagari community (founded by Bill Mollison) was an early attempt to embody permaculture principles in communal living.

The history of intentional community experiments provides a rich, if often sobering, record of attempts to create alternatives to conventional household and settlement patterns. These experiments have contributed valuable knowledge to permaculture design even when the communities themselves did not survive.

## The Tagari Experience

The Tagari community, one of the best-known permaculture communities, lasted only a few years as a close-knit shared-purse community. This experience and others like it revealed common failure patterns that Holmgren analyzes honestly.

The Tagari experience is significant because it showed that even communities founded with strong permaculture principles and skilled practitioners could fail due to social and organizational dynamics that overwhelmed the design intelligence of the members.

## Common Failure Patterns

Holmgren identifies several reasons intentional communities often fail:

- **Lack of diversity** in age, interests, and skills — communities tend to attract similar types of people

- **Idealism without practical skills** — strong ideological commitment without the pragmatic abilities needed for land management

- **Social dynamics overwhelm design** — interpersonal conflicts and governance problems consume energy

- **Economic unsustainability** — inability to generate sufficient income while maintaining values

- **Founder burnout** — key individuals exhaust themselves carrying the community

These failure patterns connect to [[holmgren-self-regulation-feedback-detailed]] and the argument that human social systems require functional feedback mechanisms just as ecosystems do. When feedback breaks down, communities fragment.

## Diversity as a Design Principle

Drawing on [[permaculture-introduction-and-ethics]], Holmgren argues that successful communities need diversity of age, interests, skills, and perspectives — echoing [[permaculture-principle-use-and-value-diversity]]. Communities that lack this diversity tend to become echo chambers that eventually fragment.

This lack of diversity is partly structural: intentional communities tend to attract people at similar life stages with similar values, creating homogeneity that undermines resilience. The [[holmgren-diversity-detailed]] principle applies as strongly to social systems as to biological ones.

## Rapid Growth Unlikely

Holmgren suggests that rapid growth of intentional communities is unlikely until external conditions force it. As long as economic growth and affluence provide better opportunities for income (from social welfare to consultancy), the incentive for communal living remains low. The real driver will be declining energy availability making conventional lifestyles increasingly difficult.

This connects to [[low-energy-futures-in-permaculture]] and the argument that external energy constraints, not ideological advocacy, will be the primary driver of settlement pattern change.

## The Household as Primary Unit

Despite critiquing communal failures, Holmgren emphasizes that the household — not the intentional community — is the primary unit of permaculture design. Permaculture strategies focus on facilitating individuals, households, and local communities in increasing self-reliance and self-regulation, building outward from the household rather than starting with the grand communal vision.

This household-first approach connects to [[holmgren-household-energy-storages]] and the understanding that resilient systems must be built from the smallest viable units upward.

## Cohousing as Alternative

Holmgren sees potential in cohousing models that balance private household space with shared common facilities, providing some economies of scale and social connection without the governance challenges of full communal living. This is elaborated in [[holmgren-cohousing-movement]].

## Connection to Energy Descent

Within the [[holmgren-tripartite-altruism]] framework, intentional communities represent bottom-up experiments in low-energy living. While their direct impact has been limited, their value lies in developing knowledge, skills, and models that will become increasingly relevant as energy descent progresses.

## The Affluence Insurance Factor

Holmgren observes that affluence has provided a safety net for intentional community experiments. Generational and family wealth, the welfare state and philanthropy have all served as insurance against the adverse consequences of failures. This insurance has been critical for allowing experimentation that would otherwise be too risky.

As this safety net erodes during energy descent, the conditions that made intentional community experimentation possible will change. Future experiments will need to be more pragmatic and self-sustaining from the outset.

## Competition Within Communities

In any rapidly developing area of knowledge or work, people are often surprised and dismayed that competition between like-minded people can be common. When the field is new and open, competition is a natural response. This competition is especially confusing when the field (such as permaculture) has a strong focus on co-operation.

Holmgren connects this to [[competition-cooperation-permaculture]]: systems that are immature and growing rapidly tend to be dominated by competitive relationships; mature systems show a high degree of mutualistic and symbiotic relationships.

## Lessons for Permaculture Design

The failures of intentional communities yield important lessons for permaculture design:

- Start small and build gradually rather than attempting total community redesign

- Prioritize economic sustainability alongside ecological goals

- Design for diversity in membership and function

- Create governance structures that distribute responsibility rather than concentrating it

- Build from the household outward rather than imposing communal structures top-down
