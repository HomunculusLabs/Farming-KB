# Design from Patterns to Details — Permaculture Principle 7

Design from Patterns to Details is the seventh of [[david-holmgren]]'s twelve
permaculture-design-principles. It advises designers to begin by observing
and understanding the large-scale [[patterns-in-nature]] and society before
attending to specific details. By working from the macro to the micro,
designers can create systems that are integrated with natural processes rather
than imposed upon them.

## The Nature of Patterns

Patterns in nature are the recurring forms, processes, and relationships that
emerge at every scale from molecular to planetary. They include branching
patterns in trees and rivers, spiral patterns in shells and galaxies, wave
patterns in dunes and water, and network patterns in mycelium and neural
systems. These patterns are not random; they represent energy-efficient
solutions to common design challenges that have been refined through millions
of years of evolution.

[[permaculture-design]] recognises that these natural patterns embody
principles of efficiency, resilience, and adaptability that can inform human
design. By understanding the patterns that shape a site, a community, or an
ecosystem, designers can create solutions that work with rather than against
natural forces.

## From Macro to Micro

The principle suggests a specific design methodology:
1. Observe the broadest patterns (climate, landscape, watershed, social
   context)
2. Identify the medium-scale patterns (landform, vegetation communities,
   economic flows)
3. Design specific elements and connections within the framework established
   by larger patterns

This approach ensures that detailed design decisions are informed by and
consistent with the larger systems within which they will function. Designing
details first and then trying to fit them into a larger context often leads to
contradictions and inefficiencies.

## [[zone-and-sector-analysis]]

The permaculture zone and sector concepts are among the most widely
applied design tools for translating this principle into practice. Zones
represent areas of differing intensity of management, from Zone 0 (the home
centre) through Zone 5 (unmanaged wildland). Sectors represent external
energetic forces and material flows (sun, wind, water, wildlife, fire) that
influence a site.

Holmgren extends these concepts to a meta-level where zones of influence
start with the personal and extend to the global. The zones are partly
physical and partly conceptual, working from a core of integration and
strength to a wider domain of uncertainty and flexibility. Strategies
effective in one zone will not necessarily work in another.

## Landscape Patterns

Understanding landscape patterns is essential for site design. Key patterns
include:
- Watershed boundaries and water flow patterns
- Slope and aspect effects on microclimate
- Vegetation succession patterns and stages
- Soil type distribution across the landscape
- Wildlife movement corridors and habitat patterns

These patterns determine where different elements should be placed and how
they should connect. Water harvesting structures should follow natural water
flow patterns. Tree planting should respect microclimate patterns created by
landform. Buildings should be positioned to maximise solar access and wind
protection.

## Social and Economic Patterns

The principle extends beyond physical design to social and economic systems.
Understanding patterns of trade, governance, information flow, and social
organisation allows designers to create interventions that work with existing
patterns rather than against them. The contrast between industrial and
sustainable cultural patterns — centralised versus distributed, large versus
small, fast versus slow, linear versus cyclical — provides a framework for
understanding the transition required for sustainable-culture.

## Biomass as Pattern Indicator

Plant and animal biomass serve as indicators of ecological pattern and
fertility. The ratio of above-ground to below-ground biomass, the proportion
of woody to herbaceous vegetation, and the total living biomass of an
ecosystem all reflect its successional stage, productivity, and health.
Monitoring these patterns over time provides feedback on the effectiveness of
management practices.

## The Danger of Detail-First Design

Designing from details upward is a common error that leads to systems that
are technically correct but functionally poor. A house may be beautifully
designed in detail but poorly sited for solar access. A farm may have
excellent individual components but lack integration between them. A
community project may be well-organised internally but disconnected from the
larger social and economic context.

The principle of [[observe-and-interact-permaculture-principle]] supports this
approach: careful observation of patterns must precede design intervention.
Without pattern understanding, even well-intentioned designs may create more
problems than they solve.

## Patterns in Time

Patterns are not only spatial but temporal. Seasonal patterns, ecological
succession, cultural cycles, and long-term climate trends all provide
essential context for design. Understanding the temporal pattern of energy
descent — the transition from fossil fuel abundance to renewable energy
constraint — is essential for designing systems that will be appropriate for
future conditions.

## Connection to Other Principles

This principle connects closely to several others. [[integrate-rather-than-segregate]] requires understanding the patterns of connection between system
elements. [[use-small-and-slow-solutions]] suggests starting with pattern
understanding before scaling up. [[use-edges-and-value-the-marginal]]
recognises that the most productive and dynamic patterns often occur at the
boundaries between systems.
