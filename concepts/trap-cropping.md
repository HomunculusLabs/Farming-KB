# Trap Cropping

Trap cropping is the practice of growing a sacrificial "trap" crop that attracts pests away from the main cash crop. The pests concentrate on the trap crop, which is then monitored, managed, or destroyed along with the pests. It is a key strategy in integrated pest management (IPM) and pairs well with [[companion-planting-reference]] and [[insectary-plantings]].

## How Trap Cropping Works

The principle is simple: pests preferentially feed on or lay eggs on the trap crop rather than the main crop. This preference may be due to earlier maturity, stronger attraction cues (color, volatile chemicals), or simply that the trap crop is more palatable.

### The Push-Pull Strategy

The most sophisticated trap cropping approach combines push-pull principles:

1. **Push**: Repel pests from the main crop using repellent plants, intercropping, or deterrent sprays (see [[natural-pest-deterrents]])
2. **Pull**: Attract pests to a trap crop planted nearby
3. The combined effect keeps pests concentrated on the trap crop where they can be managed

## Trap Crops for Major Pests

### Aphids

**Trap Crops:**
- **Nasturtiums**: One of the most effective aphid trap crops. Aphids swarm nasturtiums while leaving nearby crops relatively untouched. Plant 2-3 nasturtium plants per 10 square feet of garden.
- **Radishes**: Fast-growing, aphids prefer radish leaves over many vegetable crops. Interplant radishes with brassicas.
- **Mustard**: Attracts cabbage aphid and other brassica-feeding aphids. Plant as a perimeter trap crop around cabbage, broccoli, and kale.
- **Chervil**: Attracts aphids; useful near lettuce and other leafy greens.

**Management:**
- Monitor trap crops regularly for aphid colonies
- Knock aphids off with a strong water spray
- Apply insecticidal soap to trap crop only (see [[natural-pest-deterrents]])
- Destroy severely infested trap crop plants before aphids produce winged forms that migrate to the main crop

### Flea Beetles

Flea beetles are tiny jumping beetles that chew small holes in leaves. They are particularly damaging to young brassica seedlings.

**Trap Crops:**
- **Radishes**: The classic flea beetle trap crop. Radish leaves are highly preferred. Interplant radishes between or around brassica crops. Even if radish leaves are destroyed, the roots still develop.
- **Mustard**: Strong attractant for flea beetles. Plant as a border around cabbage, broccoli, and eggplant.
- **Napa cabbage**: Highly attractive to flea beetles. Plant at the perimeter of brassica beds.
- **Chinese giant mustard**: Extremely attractive; sometimes called a "dead-end" trap crop because flea beetles cannot complete their life cycle on it.

**Management:**
- Plant trap crops 2-3 weeks before the main crop to establish attraction before the main crop emerges
- Use floating row covers on the main crop while trap crops lure flea beetles
- Destroy trap crops once heavily infested, or apply targeted treatments
- See nematode-management and [[weed-management-strategies]] for complementary approaches

### Squash Bugs

Squash bugs (Anasa tristis) attack cucurbits, causing wilting and plant death. They are difficult to control once established.

**Trap Crops:**
- **Blue Hubbard squash**: The most effective squash bug trap crop. Research from Oklahoma State University showed Blue Hubbard attracted significantly more squash bugs than zucchini, yellow squash, or pumpkins. Plant 1 Blue Hubbard per 2-3 main crop plants.
- **Naked-seeded pumpkin**: Also attractive to squash bugs. Less commercially available but effective.
- **Turk's Turban squash**: Attracts squash bugs and vine borers.

**Management:**
- Plant Blue Hubbard 2-3 weeks before the main crop
- Monitor trap crop for squash bug eggs (bronze-colored clusters on leaf undersides)
- Crush eggs by hand or scrape them off
- Destroy trap crop plants once infested, or apply targeted treatments
- Combine with [[beneficial-insects-guide]] for natural control

### Tomato Hornworm

Tomato hornworms (Manduca quinquemaculata) and tobacco hornworms (Manduca sexta) can defoliate tomato plants rapidly.

**Trap Crops:**
- **Dill**: Hornworms prefer dill over tomatoes. Plant dill at the perimeter of tomato beds. Adult moths are also attracted to dill flowers for nectar.
- **Borage**: Attracts hornworms and also repels them through volatile compounds (conflicting research — may depend on density). Plant near tomatoes.
- **Moonflowers (Datura inoxia)**: Extremely attractive to hornworm adults for egg-laying. A "dead-end" trap crop — larvae that feed on datura die from the toxic alkaloids.

**Management:**
- Hand-pick hornworms from trap crops (they are large and easy to spot)
- Parasitic braconid wasps provide biological control — leave parasitized hornworms (those with white cocoons on their backs) alone
- See [[beneficial-insects-guide]] for braconid wasp support

### Cabbage Worms and Loopers

Imported cabbageworm, cabbage looper, and diamondback moth larvae damage brassica crops.

**Trap Crops:**
- **Mustard (especially Indian mustard)**: Strongly preferred by cabbage butterflies for egg-laying. Plant as a border crop around all brassicas.
- **Nasturtiums**: Attract cabbage white butterflies. Plant around cabbage and broccoli.
- **Radishes**: Secondary trap crop for cabbage worms.

**Management:**
- Monitor trap crops for eggs and small larvae
- Apply Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) to trap crops only, or to the entire brassica bed when larvae are small
- Use floating row covers on the main crop until the trap crop is established
- Destroy trap crop at end of season to reduce overwintering population

### Mexican Bean Beetles

Mexican bean beetles (Epilachna varivestis) are yellow-orange ladybug-like insects that skeletonize bean leaves.

**Trap Crops:**
- **Soybeans**: Preferred over snap beans and lima beans. Plant a border of soybeans around the bean patch.
- **Black-eyed peas (cowpeas)**: Also attractive; useful as a secondary trap crop.

**Management:**
- Monitor soybean trap crop for beetles and yellow egg clusters
- Hand-pick adults and crush eggs on the trap crop
- Apply insecticidal soap or neem oil to trap crop
- Destroy trap crop at end of season

### Corn Earworm

Corn earworm (Helicoverpa zea) larvae feed on corn silk, kernels, tomatoes, and beans.

**Trap Crops:**
- **Early sweet corn**: Plant a trap crop of corn 2-3 weeks earlier than the main crop. Earworm moths prefer to lay eggs on the earliest silking corn.
- **Pigeon peas**: In warmer climates, pigeon peas are a preferred host.

**Management:**
- Apply Bt or mineral oil to silks of the trap crop
- Destroy trap crop after earworm season
- For tomatoes, use Bt when caterpillars are small

### Slugs and Snails

Slugs and snails feed at night on a wide range of plants.

**Trap Crops:**
- **Chervil**: Attracts slugs away from lettuce and other leafy greens.
- **Marigolds**: Slugs are attracted to marigold flowers.
- **Beer trap boards**: Not a plant, but boards or melon rinds placed on the soil surface attract slugs as daytime shelters — lift and remove them in the morning.

**Management:**
- Hand-pick slugs at night with a flashlight
- Beer traps (shallow dishes of beer sunk into the soil) drown slugs
- Diatomaceous earth around the main crop (see [[natural-pest-deterrents]])
- Reduce mulch depth near susceptible crops (slugs hide in mulch)

## Trap Crop Design Principles

### Perimeter Trap Cropping

Plant the trap crop completely around the border of the main crop. Pests approaching from outside encounter the trap crop first and concentrate there.
