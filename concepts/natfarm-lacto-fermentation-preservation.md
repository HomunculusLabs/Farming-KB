## Lacto-Fermentation for Food Preservation

Lacto-fermentation has become more popular again in recent years because it
saves the [[bionutrients-lab-lactic-acid-bacteria-serum]]. This acid acts as a
natural preservative, creating an environment where harmful bacteria cannot
survive. The process also produces enzymes and beneficial probiotics that
aid digestion. Unlike canning, which destroys many nutrients through high
heat, fermentation preserves and even enhances the nutritional value of
foods. Vitamins, particularly vitamin C and B vitamins, are actually
increased during the fermentation process.

## Equipment and Containers

It is highly recommended that fermenting foods be put into jars with
rubber-sealed lids. The rubber seals release gases that build up during the
fermentation process, preventing an explosion. Traditionally, people used
crocks as well. You can sterilize jars by pouring boiling water in them, but
soap and water are sufficient. Grow the food yourself or source it from a
farmer who has clean produce and a good reputation.

## Sauerkraut

Sauerkraut is another classic lacto-fermented food. The process is similar
to kimchi but simpler: chop cabbage, add salt, and pack it tightly into a
container. The salt draws liquid out of the cabbage, creating a brine that
submerges the cabbage and protects it from harmful bacteria while the
beneficial lactobacillus bacteria do their work. The fermentation period
ranges from a few days to several weeks depending on temperature and taste
preference. Cooler temperatures produce slower fermentation and a crisper
result.

## Yogurt

Yogurt is technically fermented milk. Bacteria turn lactose into lactic
acid. While store-bought yogurt contains flavoring and sugar, homemade
yogurt is simply milk and starter. You can use any milk, and the starter can
be plain yogurt with active cultures. The milk is heated, cooled to a warm
temperature, mixed with the [[wild-yeast-bread-baking]].

## Safety Considerations

Many people worry about botulism when preserving food. Botulism is an
invisible, odorless, and tasteless bacteria that can paralyze or kill. The
[[cho-lactic-acid-bacteria-preparation]] produced during fermentation creates an acidic environment (pH
below 4.6) where botulism spores cannot grow. This makes properly
fermented foods inherently safe. Key safety practices include using clean
equipment, keeping food submerged in brine, maintaining proper
temperatures, and watching for signs of spoilage such as unusual colors or
odors. Mold on the surface can be scraped off; the ferment below is
usually still safe.
