# Trusts and Legal Strategies for Permaculture

Trusts in the public interest form the legal basis on which churches, universities, schools, research establishments, hospitals, public services, aid programmes, and charities operate. About 18 to 20% of businesses may also be non-profit trusts owned or operated by charitable trusts that benefit from them. In [[permaculture-design]], trusts are essential legal instruments for achieving earth repair, community development, and financial self-reliance.

## The Case for Trusts Over Bureaucracy

It is possible, even sensible, to completely replace the bureaucracy of public services with a series of locally administered trusts. Holland (in particular) largely supplants expensive paid public services — burdened with heavy salary and capital costs, and liable to inaction, self-interest, and executive inefficiency — with publicly formed trusts called *stichtings*. In any small country, such trusts can run all public operations, with the government simply conveying tax capital back to regions via local trusts.

Trusts can also self-fund via non-profit businesses to become foundations fully equipped with their own income sources.

## Legal Structure of a Trust

Legally, a trust body consists of two essential elements:

1. **A Trustee** — ideally a private company with 3 or 4 directors who can appoint others if one dies or resigns, and who can be anonymous. A company does not die, unlike its directors.
2. **A Trust Deed** — a document registered with the public company registrar, stating the purposes of the organisation.

### Advantages of a Corporate Trustee
- Directors need to be few in number (3 or 4 are enough)
- The small group can act quickly and decisively without reference to a cumbersome board
- Directorships should be restricted to those who are very active in trust affairs
- Directors should preferably live close to each other in one region

### Associations
Any trust can have an unregistered, no-cost association of volunteers, aides, or clients who can publish a newsletter and generally assist the trust in its affairs.

## The Two-Trust System: Charitable Trust Plus Trading Trust

The most effective structure combines two trusts:

### Trust A — Charitable Trust ("For the Public Good")
- Holds all real property which is not at risk
- Includes land, buildings, copyrights, leases, and important equipment clear of debt
- Takes no risks — it does not trade
- Accepts volunteer or contract labour

### Trust B — Non-Profit Trading Trust
- Takes normal business risk
- Can rent or lease real property from Trust A
- Can pay wages and costs to staff or volunteers
- Gifts all profits to Trust A (the main beneficiary)
- Can fund any other charity or trust

This structure ensures that the charitable assets are protected from business risk while generating income through the trading arm.

## Naming Conventions

Trusts often have names including: church, foundation, institute, communion, school, congregation, charity, bureau, trust, or even company. When trusts are formed to trade, they can take or own any business name that suits their work. Such businesses are administered by trustees.

## Setting Up a Trust

### Steps
1. **Closely define purposes** — specify the group to whom it will apply (e.g., "all the citizens of Australia" or "those suffering from spina bifida")
2. **Instruct a lawyer** to draw up the trust deed and register it along with the trustee company
3. **Reduce legal costs** by buying copies of trust deeds from other ethical organizations to use as models
4. **Find the right lawyer** — corporate lawyers are often knowledgeable about trusts; many general lawyers give bad advice on trust formation

### Duration
The trust deed should include an estimate of duration. If intended to be permanent, the legal phrasing is typically something like "until 21 years after the death of the last descendant of Ming emperors" — a legally indefinable period.

### Will and Asset Transfer
The trust deed states the "will" of the trust, leaving its assets to an allied trust if the trust completes operations, closes down, or fails from lack of interest or funds.

## Other Legal Structures

Additional simple legal structures include:
- **Companies** — for-profit entities with share structures
- **Cooperatives** — member-owned and democratically controlled
- **Credit unions** — member-owned financial cooperatives
- **Public investment trusts** — pooled investment vehicles

All are well-outlined in company law with excellent support services and routine arrangements. A good accountant for bookkeeping and an efficient office manager to communicate with the target population are also necessary.

## Scale and Network Approach

France is estimated to have 100,000 public interest groups, each with its own areas of interest and subscribers, with about 10,000 forming up annually. As so few dedicated people can operate a trust effectively, it is far better to set up many small local trusts than to risk the power-centered inefficiencies of a monstrous hierarchical system. Every dissenter or group of dissenters should set up trusts to promulgate their own views, or form an independent trust in a cooperative network of like trusts.
