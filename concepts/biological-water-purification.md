## Biological Water Purification

The only long-term insurance of good water supply to a settlement is by rigorous control of a forested catchment, including a total ban on biocides and metallic processing. Biological water purification treats common contaminants using natural living systems rather than chemical additives. The fundamental principle: water safe for humans is also safe for other living things.

## Common Water Contaminants

Problem contaminants most likely to affect drinking water include:

### Turbidity
Silt and fine particles suspended in water. Reduced by settling, filtration, and bank stabilization using grasses, clump plants, and aquatic vegetation.

### Bacterial and Organic Pollution
Sewage-derived decay products including *E. coli*, disease organisms, viral or protozoan pathogens, and parasitic worm eggs.

### Metallic Pollutants
Chromium, cadmium, lead, and mercury. These interact synergistically with other factors — acid rain dissolves poisonous forms from rocks, soil, and even drinking tanks.

### Biocides
Aldrin, Dieldrin, 2,4-D, 2,4,5-T, dioxin, PCB, organophosphates, and halogenated hydrocarbons. Combinations can be deadly; Aldrin prevents DDT being excreted, and DDT co-distills with water so distillation does not help.

### Excessive Fertiliser
Nitrogenous compounds, phosphates, sodium, and potassium salts that increase aquatic biological activity, resulting in further metal uptake.

### Acids
pH below 5.5 increases metallic pollution dramatically. Acid rain dissolves aluminium, mercury, lead, cadmium, and selenium from rocks and soils.

## Contaminant Interactions

These factors interact in dangerous ways:
- Acid rain dissolves poisonous metals from rocks, soil, and water infrastructure
- Organisms convert inorganic mercury to organic forms readily absorbed by the body (as occurred in Minamata, Japan)
- Sewage in water aids conversion to biologically active metals
- Excessive fertiliser increases aquatic biological activity, increasing metal uptake
- Cooking may increase water content of metals by a factor of 5 due to protein binding
- Aluminium from acidic rain leaching is a major cause of tree and lake death

## Water Treatment Methods

### Aeration
Oxygenation by wind, mechanical aeration, increasing turbulence in flow, trickle columns, vegetation, phytoplankton, or injected air.

### Settling
Spreading flow in still-water ponds or rush beds to allow particles to fall out, filter out, or flocculate.

### Skimming and Sieving
Removing large organic particles before further treatment.

### Filtration
Sand beds, charcoal-fibre columns, soils, and roots of aquatic plants.

### Coagulation/Flocculation
Using chemical additives (lime, salt, ferric sulphates) or organic (bacterial) gels to clump fine particles for settling.

### Biological Removal
By bacteria, phytoplankton, and higher plants working in sequence.

### pH Adjustment
Adding calcium (as lime) or sulphur compounds as needed.

## Sand Filtration

Sand filters are classical and widely used. Britain and many cities use sand filters followed by chlorination. Key specifications:

- **Depth**: 1.2 m (4 feet) minimum
- **Structure**: Top half fine sand, bottom half coarse
- **Maintenance**: Top 1 cm periodically swept, removed, dried or roasted to remove organic particles before returning

Activated charcoal from bones, willow, or coconut husks serves as a fine filter in homes. Fine dripstone supplies cool water in domestic water cleaners.

## Multi-Stage Biological Filtration System

A complete gravity-fed biological filtration system uses these stages in sequence:

1. **Trickle filters** of loose pebbles (2.5–10 cm) forming an active bacterial surface layer to absorb nutrients
2. **Sand filter** to absorb bacterial pollution
3. **Freshwater mussels** (or their shells substituted for pebbles) that individually filter up to 100 litres/day, digesting bacteria. They monitor acidity (dying at pH 5.5) and are sensitive to biocides like Dieldrin
4. **Watercress bed** to remove dyes and nitrates
5. **Active carbon column** (10%) and silicon dioxide (90%) — burnt rice, oat, or wheat husks

The result is clear, sparkling, safe water with no machinery required if laid out downslope for gravity flow.

## Lime Treatment

Freshly burnt lime removes phosphorus and sludges in primary settling lagoons. Crushed marble, limestone, burnt lime, crushed shells, or whole shells in water tanks and ponds flocculate particles, causing them to settle. Liming further reduces turbidity if pH is 6.0 or less.

## Ferric vs. Aluminium Sulphate

Ferric and aluminium sulphate are added to water to cause fine particles to flocculate and settle. However, as pH increases due to acid rain, aluminium goes into solution and may bind to protein in food. **Ferric sulphate is safer**, especially if water is initially or reasonably alkaline. Any inorganic or metallic salt deserves very cautious use.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

## Research Directions
Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.
Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.
