# Fungal Alkane Metabolism Pathways

Fungi utilize multiple pathways for the metabolism of aliphatic
hydrocarbons found in petroleum and other environmental contaminants.
These pathways include monoterminal oxidation, diterminal oxidation,
and subterminal oxidation, each involving distinct enzymatic mechanisms
and producing different metabolic intermediates.

## Monoterminal Oxidation Pathway

The most common major pathway of alkane metabolism involves monoterminal
oxidation to the corresponding alcohol, aldehyde, and fatty acid. This
process requires a mixed-function alkane monoxygenase system along with
NAD-dependent alcohol and aldehyde dehydrogenases. The initial attack
occurs at the terminal methyl group by alkane monoxygenase, involving
insertion of molecular oxygen and an electron transfer system that
combines [[singh-cytochrome-p450-fungal-bioremediation]]
as the terminal oxidase with NADPH-cytochrome P450-reductase.

## Fatty Acid Catabolism

Following initial oxidation, fatty acids generated from alkane
assimilation serve as building blocks (Brakemeier et al., 1995). Further
catabolism involves activation of fatty acids to acyl-CoA ester via
acyl-CoA synthetase, followed by beta-oxidation yielding acetyl-CoA.
The acetyl-CoA can act as an anabolic precursor or be further
catabolized to CO2 via the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Terminal oxidation
followed by beta-oxidation represents the pathway for alkane
mineralization in marine fungi (Singer and Finnerty, 1984).

## Diterminal Oxidation

Certain fungi possess alternative pathways for alkane degradation.
Some yeasts and species of Aspergillus, Botrytis, and Penicillium can
oxidize both terminal methyl groups of aliphatic hydrocarbons to produce
long-chain dicarboxylic acids through diterminal oxidation. This
oxidation may occur simultaneously or the omega-methyl group may be
oxidized after fatty acid formation. Several species of Candida
accumulate dioic acids on alkanes (Uchio and Shiio, 1972; Il'Chenko,
1984), and this pathway has been exploited for dicarboxylic acid
overproduction.

## Subterminal Oxidation

Subterminal oxidation is initiated by formation of a secondary alcohol,
followed by oxidation to the corresponding ketone and ester, which
cleaves to yield both a primary alcohol and acetate. This pathway is
noted in species of Aspergillus, Cladosporium, Penicillium,
Verticillium, Cunninghamella echinulata, Fusarium lini, Mortierella
isabellina, and Rhizopus nigricans (Hoffmann and Rehm, 1976a; Kremer
and Anke, 1997). Subterminal oxidation can occur at various positions,
forming mixtures of secondary alcohols.

## Alkane Chain Length Preferences

Both yeasts and bacteria show decreasing abilities to metabolize alkanes
with increasing chain length. Filamentous fungi do not exhibit
preferential degradation for a particular chain length. Degradation of
long-chain alkanes greater than C24 can occur in complex mixtures, but
major studies do not support fungal growth on these substrates. Alkanes
from C12 to C26 within the aliphatic fraction of crude oil are
metabolized by various fungal species. Kremer and Anke (1997) compiled
a comprehensive list of alkane-assimilating genera of fungi.

## Hydrocarbon Uptake Mechanisms

The uptake of hydrocarbons involves penetration of insoluble substrate
into cells through various mechanisms. The most acceptable mechanism is
via a transport process. Membrane-bound electron-dense inclusion bodies
(vesicles) in Cladosporium resinae have been detected during growth on
alkanes (Cooney et al., 1980). Dodecane uptake by C. resinae occurs in
two stages: passive adsorption to the outer cell surface followed by
Michaelis-Menten saturation kinetics.

## Ultrastructural Changes During Metabolism

In eukaryotic microorganisms, hydrocarbon catabolism is associated with
distinct and induced ultrastructural characteristics. The most important
changes include the appearance of crystalline peroxisomes in yeasts and
fungi. During alkane degradation by Candida species, peroxisomes are
induced in large proportions of the cell volume in high numbers, similar
to methylotrophic yeasts. The intracellular distribution of biochemical
pathways is located in diverse microbodies including peroxisomes,
microsomes, and mitochondria.

## Cooxidation of Hydrocarbons

In certain cases, fungi can initiate the breakdown of complex
hydrocarbons despite nonoccurrence of growth. Some degree of partial
conversion may occur with an alternative substrate as both carbon and
energy source. Few fungi can metabolize cycloalkanes, and their
metabolism appears to proceed via cooxidation. Cyclohexane assimilation
occurs through a mechanism similar to subterminal oxidation, forming
dicarboxylic acid subsequently metabolized by beta-oxidation.
