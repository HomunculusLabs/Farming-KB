## Intertidal Aquaculture and Marine Systems

Intertidal aquaculture encompasses the cultivation of marine organisms in the zone between high and low tide, leveraging natural tidal cycles for productive food systems. This approach extends [[permaculture-design-principles]] from land into the marine and estuarine environment, creating integrated systems that produce food, materials, and ecological benefits.

## Sea Grasses as Multi-Function Resources

Sea grasses, including eel grasses (*Zostera* species), are foundational organisms in intertidal zones. They serve multiple critical functions in permaculture-designed marine systems:

- **Nutrient absorption**: Sea grasses rapidly absorb dissolved nutrients. A bag of super-phosphate placed in a sea grass bed can be fully absorbed within three days, demonstrating their extraordinary capacity for nutrient cycling.
- **Insulation material**: After composting for approximately 10 days, shed sea grass material becomes an excellent insulation product. It is fibrous, chocolate-colored, and free of the health risks associated with mineral fiber insulation. Unlike synthetic alternatives, it is safe to handle and extremely durable.
- **Garden mulch**: Composted sea grass can be applied directly to gardens as mulch, particularly effective when laid on sopping wet soil. Salt content is not a concern except in areas receiving less than 20 inches of annual rainfall.
- **Beach building**: Unharvested sea grass contributes silica skeletons that help build up beach plants, while decomposed material returns as mud to channels, fertilizing offshore marine ecosystems.

Sea grasses typically shed their upper portions in large quantities during autumn or early summer, depending on variety. These wash ashore by the ton and can be easily caught on fences placed along the shoreline. Under many legal systems, individuals retain the right to harvest a single load of eelgrass regardless of who leases the intertidal zone.

## Intertidal Title and Access

Understanding property rights in intertidal zones is essential for marine [[permaculture-design]]:

- **Ancient grant titles** typically extend to the low tide mark
- **Freehold titles** (non-Crown charters) generally extend to high tide
- **Modern titles** may be set back 100 feet or more from the beach, which has been converted to beach reserve
- **Leased access** to the intertidal zone is typically inexpensive and not difficult to obtain

## Irish Stone Field Aquaculture

The Irish developed a sophisticated form of marine aquaculture using stone fields placed on hard sea bottoms. These "fields" consist of rows of stone laid across submerged areas, and are visible from the air as enormous underwater acreages. Key principles include:

- **Small fields produce more than large ones**: The Irish deliberately kept their fields small because smaller fields trapped more radiant heat from the stone walls, creating warmer microclimates that increased productivity.
- **Tidal harvesting**: Kelp is cut, tied with rope, and transported using the tide itself — sometimes 10-12 tons at a time — right into bog channels. When the tide recedes, the kelp is deposited on shore for collection.
- **Kelp processing**: Stems are stacked and dried like firewood, then fired with peat to extract potash for agricultural use. Fronds are laid down as mulch.
- **Stone handling**: Very large boulders can be moved in the sea by floating them on boats and rolling them off at the desired location.

## Octopus Culture

Octopus can be cultured in intertidal pond systems through a remarkably simple technique:

1. Create ponds in the intertidal zone that are exposed at low tide and slightly flooded at high tide
2. Place ordinary clay pots in the ponds — each pot becomes home to one octopus
3. At high tide, octopus emerge to feed on mollusks in the surrounding area
4. At low tide, they return to their pots

This system takes advantage of the octopus"s natural behavior — they apparently swim over intertidal browsing lands looking for suitable habitat. The same pond systems are also productive for growing sponges.

## Intertidal Fish Trap and Scour Hole Systems

By combining natural drift events with constructed barriers, complex self-maintaining systems can be designed:

- **Drift fences** and **barrier fences** direct water flow and accumulate material
- **Pond scour holes** create still-water environments that attract fish
- **Leaky rubble walls** on open coastlines create conditions for mussel settlement and shrimp habitat
- These systems operate on the principle that still-water periods (not excessive tidal flow) create productive conditions for species like prawns

## Estuarine Aquaculture

Estuaries offer unique opportunities because they contain multiple water types — cold fresh water, warm fresh water, and warm salt water — often within close proximity. By designing adjoining ponds with different salinity gradients, a remarkable diversity of species can be cultured simultaneously, from trout to grey mullet to eels.

## Salmon Raft Culture

When wild salmon runs declined (due in Ireland's case to river "improvement" that destroyed salmon weirs, and to efficient Japanese gill nets at sea), the Irish developed an alternative: inshore salmon culture in giant floating rafts. These rafts are moored in quiet tidal areas behind islands, with salmon grown in large flooded nets. Raft culture is applicable across all aquatic systems, from tiny ponds for insect attractants to large-scale net systems.

## Spira as a Multi-Purpose Algae

Spira (*Spirulina*) is an algae with extraordinary properties for aquaculture and human use:

- **Desalination**: A modest spira plant can desalinate 10,000 gallons of water per day
- **Nutrition**: Approximately 86% total protein, of which 68% is complete protein, with no cellulose, making it almost entirely digestible
- **Fuel value**: Higher BTU than coal when used as fuel
- **Productivity**: Can yield three crops per day in summer
- **Water treatment**: Cleans up sewage water and grey water
- **Animal feed**: Can be fed to ducks and pigs, or dried into powder for human consumption

## Seabird Phosphate Collection

A largely untapped resource is the phosphate deposited by seabirds at roosting sites. One documented example involved a concrete platform the size of a football field built off the West African coast. Multiple roosting sites attracted seabirds whose droppings were collected as both liquid (piped ashore during rain and evaporated) and solid (shoveled into bags). This operation annually generated millions of dollars in phosphate — effectively reconstituting a natural phosphate island.

The Dutch have implemented a similar concept with bat roosts built throughout agricultural fields. These slatted rack structures control mosquitoes while producing carefully collected and distributed manure. Approximately 200 terns consistently roosting in the right location can supply an entire village or small island with phosphate.

## Grey Water and Sewage Systems

Marine and coastal permaculture integrates with waste water management:

- **Half-pipe disposal systems**: Open-bottomed half-pipes placed below the frost line lead toilet water directly to tree crops. They feature small dams every four feet that slow water flow and create continuous small ponds behind them. Unlike round pipes, they resist root invasion and work indefinitely.
- **Grey water recycling**: Shower and sink water can be shunted directly to mulch systems or through glasshouses where it releases heat.
- **Tile drains**: Where sufficient domestic water flows downhill, standard plastic tile drains with integrated dams can irrigate tree crops while safely processing sewage.

## Flood Plain Forestry

River systems offer productive design opportunities:

- **Tree planting**: Willows, poplars, and similar species withstand flooding and can be planted at right angles to banks with a taper toward the river, creating scour hole lagoons that provide fish habitat.
- **Poplar propagation**: Poplars can be grown to 90 feet, sawn off at the butt, and planted directly in holes as instant 90-foot hedges. A single tree can yield two 45-foot or three 30-foot planting units.
- **Silt deposition**: Flood-deposited silt provides excellent growing conditions for crops like asparagus.

## Large Dams: A Critical Assessment

Mollison strongly criticizes large dam projects based on documented negative effects:

- **Reduced fertility**: Trapping silt deprives downstream river systems of nutrients
- **Disease increase**: Particularly in tropical regions, by preventing natural flood scouring
- **Fisheries destruction**: Cold water released from dam bases wipes out species for miles downstream
- **Low biological utility**: Despite massive investment, large dams provide minimal biological benefit
- **Centralized pollution**: They inevitably support centralized power systems and polluting industries

The permaculture preference is for reasonably small impoundments that serve local needs with minimal ecological disruption.
