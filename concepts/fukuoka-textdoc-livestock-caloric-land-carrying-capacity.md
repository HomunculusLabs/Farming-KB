# Livestock Caloric Efficiency and Land Carrying Capacity by Diet Type

Fukuoka presents a systematic caloric analysis of different livestock systems and human diets, demonstrating that meat-based diets are profoundly energy-inefficient and that the land required to support different diet types varies by orders of magnitude. His analysis challenges the assumption that livestock farming contributes to food security and argues that animal agriculture on a large scale fundamentally undermines the productivity of the land.

## The Fundamental Energy Inefficiency of Livestock

Fukuoka uses the example of yellowtail fish farming to illustrate the core problem of converting feed into animal protein. Raising one yellowtail to marketable size requires ten times its weight in sardines. This same pattern of energy loss applies across all livestock systems: a silver fox consumes ten times its weight in rabbit meat to produce a single pelt, and a rabbit consumes ten times its weight in grass to reach marketable size.

Applied to human diets, the pattern means people must work ten times as hard to eat beef as they would to eat grain directly, and five times as hard if they want to nourish themselves on milk and eggs rather than plant foods. Farming with the labor of animals therefore appears to benefit man but actually increases his labor many times over. The farmer becomes a servant to his livestock rather than its master.

## Quantified Feed Conversion Ratios

Fukuoka provides specific energy conversion efficiency figures for different animal products, showing the dramatic losses at each trophic level:

- **Broilers (chicken meat)**: 50% efficiency (half of feed energy is recovered as edible product)
- **Pork**: 20% efficiency (four-fifths of feed energy is lost)
- **Milk**: 15% efficiency
- **Beef**: 8% efficiency (ninety-two percent of feed energy is wasted)

These figures mean that raising beef cattle cuts the food energy recoverable from land tenfold. People who eat beef consume ten times as much land-based energy as people on a diet of rice. Fukuoka argues that few are aware of how livestock industries, which raise cattle in indoor stalls with feed grain shipped from the United States, have helped deplete American prairie soils. Such practices are not merely uneconomical — they amount essentially to a campaign to destroy vegetation on a global scale.

## Land Requirements by Diet Type

Fukuoka quantifies the land area required to support one human being under different dietary regimes, revealing a fifty-fold range:

| Diet Type | Land Required (sq. yards per person) |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|
| Grains    | 200                                  |
| Potatoes  | 600                                  |
| Milk      | 1,500                                |
| Pork      | 4,000                                |
| Beef      | 10,000                               |

These figures demonstrate that dietary choice has an enormous impact on how many people a given area of land can support. Moving from a grain-based to a beef-based diet multiplies land requirements by fifty times.

## Global Carrying Capacity Implications

Using these land requirement figures, Fukuoka calculates the theoretical carrying capacity of Earth under different dietary patterns:

- **Beef-only diet**: Current world population has already reached the limit; no further growth is possible
- **Pork diet**: World could support three times current population
- **Milk diet**: World could support eight times current population
- **Potato diet**: World could support twenty times current population
- **Grain diet**: World could support sixty times current population

Fukuoka points to the United States and Europe as clear evidence that beef production impoverishes the soil and denudes the earth. The massive scale of American beef production, requiring vast acreages of feed grain, has visibly degraded prairie soils and driven the conversion of forest to pasture across Latin America.

## The Japanese Bantam Versus White Leghorn Comparison

Fukuoka illustrates the difference between natural and artificial livestock systems using a detailed comparison of chicken breeds. The Japanese Bantam is a native breed that, when left to roam freely, lays just one small egg every other day. By conventional metrics, this is low productivity. However, a breeding pair of Bantams left to nest naturally will hatch a clutch of chicks, growing from two birds to a flock of ten or twenty within a year.

These birds collectively lay many times as many eggs each day as the best variety of White Leghorn. The Bantams are efficient calorie producers because they feed themselves and reproduce on their own, literally producing something from nothing. Moreover, as long as the number of birds remains appropriate for the space available, raising chickens in this way does not harm the land.

Genetically-upgraded White Leghorns raised in cages lay one large egg a day, but to produce this output, they must be given feed grain having twice the caloric value of the eggs produced. Such artificial methods are counterproductive — instead of increasing calories, they actually cut the number of calories in half. Restoration of the wastes to the land is not easy, and soil fertility is depleted to the extent of the caloric loss.

## The Illusion of Improved Breeds

People persist in believing that raising large numbers of genetically improved breeds with high feed conversion efficiency in enclosures is the only workable approach to mass production. Fukuoka argues the very opposite is true. Artificial livestock practices consisting essentially of the conversion of feed into eggs, milk, or meat are actually very energy-wasteful. The larger and more highly improved the breed of animal being raised, the greater the energy input required and the greater the effort and pains that must be taken by the farmer.

## The Ideal Livestock System

Fukuoka envisions a fundamentally different approach to animal husbandry based on natural integration rather than industrial confinement. In his idealized vision:

- Bees busily make the rounds of clover and vegetable blossoms beneath trees laden with fruit
- Semiwild chickens and rabbits frolic with dogs in fields of growing wheat
- Great numbers of ducks and mallards play in the rice paddy, controlling insects and weeds
- Black pigs and boars grow fat on worms and crayfish at the foot of hills and in valleys
- Goats peer out from thickets and trees, converting scrub into milk

This scene, he argues, should be viewed not as a picture of primitive, economically disadvantaged life but as an organic partnership between man, animal, and nature. An environment comfortable for small animals is also an ideal setting for man.

## Breed Selection Guidance

Fukuoka provides specific guidance on selecting appropriate breeds for natural livestock systems:

- **Cattle**: Select breeds that can graze mountain pastures. Native breeds such as Jersey cattle, thought to be of lower productivity, actually have higher feed efficiency and do not deplete the land
- **Pigs**: The wild boar and the black Berkshire pig, being closer to nature, are more economical than the supposedly superior white Yorkshire breed
- **Goats**: Rather than large dairy cattle which impoverish the soil, each family should keep a goat. Strong native varieties that live on roughage should be raised
- **Poultry**: Free-ranging native breeds that reproduce naturally are more efficient than caged commercial hybrids
- **Wild species**: Deer, boars, rabbits, wildfowl, and even edible rodents would be more economical and better protect nature than conventional livestock

## The Fishing Industry Parallel

Fukuoka extends his caloric analysis to modern fishing, arguing it follows the same destructive pattern. The industry raises expensive fish by feeding them several times their weight in smaller fish while rejoicing at how abundant fish have become. Research on breeding technology for shrimp, sea bream, and eels will not increase the number of fish in the sea. Such misguided efforts undermine both the fishing industry and the oceans.

He warns of the dangers of treating fish with large doses of chemicals to prevent pelagic diseases caused by pollution from fish farming centers, creating a chemical arms race in the oceans analogous to that on land. The rise in demand for sardines as feed for yellowtails recently resulted in an acute shortage of sardines that made the smaller fish a luxury item — a vivid illustration of the systemic absurdity.

## The Paradox of Modern Livestock Improvement

Fukuoka identified a central paradox in modern animal agriculture: the very
efforts to improve productivity through genetic selection and concentrated
feeding actually reduce overall system efficiency. Because it is so
energy-inefficient, modern livestock farming cannot be considered as
"production" in a basic sense. In fact, true efficiency has become so low
and man has been driven to such extremes of toil and labor that he is even
attempting to increase the efficiency of livestock production by raising
large, genetically improved breeds.

The larger and more highly improved the breed of animal being raised, the
greater the energy input required and the greater the effort and pains that
must be taken by the farmer. This is the opposite of what one would expect
from genuine agricultural progress, where efficiency improvements should
reduce labor and resource requirements rather than increase them.

If poultry and livestock are to truly benefit man, they must be capable of
feeding and fending for themselves under the open sky. Only then will food
become naturally plentiful and contribute to man's well-being.

## The Natural Diet as Foundation

Fukuoka concludes that the way to enrich human diet does not require mass production. It requires relinquishing human knowledge and action, and allowing nature to restore its natural bounty. Man ought to know that nature is fragile and easily harmed. It is far more difficult to protect than everyone seems to think. And once it has been destroyed, nature cannot be restored.
