# Mushroom Culture Slant Preservation and Cryogenic Storage

Every mushroom strain is certain to die out eventually, making the [[growing-gourmet-stock-culture-library|stock culture library]] the pivotal center of any mushroom cultivation enterprise. Once a strain is taken into culture from spores or tissue, the resultant strains can be preserved for decades under normal refrigeration, perhaps centuries under liquid nitrogen. Culture slants function as "back-ups" in the same way that computer operators maintain backup files.

## Test Tube Slant Preparation

Culture slants are prepared by filling test tubes with nutrified agar media, sterilizing them, and laying them at a 15 to 20 degree angle on a table to cool. The resulting slanted surface maximizes the area available for mycelial colonization. Standard sizes are 100 x 15 mm for working cultures and 100 x 20 mm for long-term storage.

One culture in a standard 100 x 15 mm petri dish can inoculate 50 to 100 test tube slants measuring 100 x 20 mm. After incubation for 1 to 4 weeks, or until a luxurious mycelium has been established, the test tube cultures are placed into cold storage.

## Sealing Culture Slants

The gap between the screw cap and the glass tube should be sealed with a commercially available elastic, wax-like film (such as Parafilm). Test tube slants not sealed with this film are prone to contamination with molds after several months of cold storage. This seemingly simple step is critical for long-term viability.

## Storage Organization

Stamets recommends sealing test-tube slants in plastic zip-lock bags, three to four bags per strain, each containing 4 slants. These are then stored in at least two locations remote from the main laboratory. This dual-location strategy prevents catastrophic loss from events like fires, electrical failure, or natural disasters.

Cedar boxes are used for organizing and protecting the slants during storage. Each box should be clearly labeled with species, strain codes, and dates.

## Refrigeration Parameters

Household refrigerators, especially modern ones, suffice for culture storage. The most important considerations are:

- **Temperature stability:** Limiting temperature variation to 2 to 3 degrees F (1 degree C) is crucial for long-term preservation
- **Mass:** Refrigerators with the greatest mass and thermostatic controls provide the most stable temperature environment
- **Temperature fluctuation danger:** With temperature variation, condensation occurs within the culture tubes, spreading a contaminant should one be present throughout the culture
- **Freeze-thaw cycles:** When mushroom cultures freeze and thaw repeatedly, they die. This is the primary danger of frost-free refrigerator cycles

Older refrigerators with more thermal mass are often preferred for culture storage. The modern frost-free cycle, while convenient for food storage, poses a genuine threat to mycelial viability by periodically raising temperatures above freezing and then dropping them back down.

## Storage Duration by Method

| Storage Method | Temperature | Maximum Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Room temperature | 68-75 degrees F (20-24 degrees C) | 6 to 12 months |
| Standard refrigeration | 35-39 degrees F (2-4 degrees C) | 5+ years |
| Cryogenic (liquid nitrogen) | -302 degrees F (-150 degrees C) | Decades to centuries |

## Annual Culture Rotation

With ten or more replicate slants per strain, stock cultures can be safely stored for 5 years by refrigeration alone. As a precaution, one or two representative culture slants should be retrieved every year, brought to room temperature for 48 hours, and subcultured to newly filled media dishes. Once revived and determined to be free of contamination, the mycelium can be subcultured back into test tube slants and returned to refrigeration.

This circular path of culture rotation ascertains viability and prolongs storage with a minimum number of cell divisions. The importance of maintaining cell lines closest to their genetic origins cannot be overemphasized.

## Assessing Retrieved Cultures

When retrieving cultures from prolonged storage, the appearance immediately indicates potential viability or clear inviability:

- **Viable indicator:** Aerial, cottony mycelium that is fluffy and rises above the agar surface
- **Non-viable indicator:** Mycelium that is not aerial, flat, with a highly reflective sheen over its surface — the culture has likely died
- **Contamination indicator:** If culture caps have not been sealed, contaminants (usually green molds) are often visible, giving the mycelium a speckled appearance. These cultures make re-isolation most difficult

Ultimately, cultivators cannot determine viability of stored cultures until they are subcultured into new media and incubated for one to three weeks.

## Cryogenic Storage (Liquid Nitrogen)

Cryogenic storage is the best method for preserving a strain long-term. Liquid nitrogen storage vessels are commonly held at -302 degrees F (-150 degrees C).

### Cryoprotectant Media

Test tube slants are filled with specially designed cryoprotectant media to help the mycelium survive the shock of sudden temperature change. The standard cryoprotectant involves the use of a 10% glycerol and dextrose media.

### Cooling Rate Protocol

Wang and Jong (1990) discovered that a slow, controlled cooling rate of -1 degree C per minute resulted in a higher survival rate than sudden immersion into liquid nitrogen. This slow reduction in temperature allowed the mycelium to discharge water extracellularly, protecting the cells from the severe damage that ice crystals would otherwise cause.

### Grain vs. Agar Media for Cryogenics

Strains were found to be better preserved on grain media than on agar media for cryogenic storage. However, for those with limited liquid nitrogen storage space and large numbers of strains, preservation in ampules or test tubes of liquid cryoprotectant media is more practical.

## Species-Specific Storage Exceptions

The **Paddy Straw Mushroom** (Volvariella volvacea) is the notable exception to standard cold storage protocols. This tropical mushroom demonstrates poor recovery from cold storage at both 34 degrees F (2 degrees C) and -300 degrees F in liquid nitrogen. When the mycelium is exposed to temperatures below 45 degrees F (7.2 degrees C), drastic die-back occurs.

**Special handling for Volvariella volvacea:**
- Store at no less than 50 degrees F (10 degrees C)
- Test frequently for viability
- For prolonged room temperature storage, many mycologists cover the mycelium with liquid paraffin
- Consult Jinxia and Chang (1992) for detailed protocols

## Asian Storage Methods

Culture banks in Asia commonly preserve cultures in straight test tubes whose ends are stuffed with hydrophobic cotton or gauze. The gauze is sometimes covered with plastic film and secured tightly with a rubber band. Other libraries offer cultures in test tubes fitted with a press-on plastic lid specially designed for gas exchange. The need for gas exchange is minimal provided that the culture's growth is slowed by timely placement into cold storage.

## Common Causes of Culture Loss

Understanding why cultures fail helps prevent catastrophic losses:

- **Improper sealing:** The single most common cause of contamination in stored slants. Unsealed caps allow microscopic mold spores and bacteria to enter over time.
- **Temperature fluctuations:** Frost-free refrigerator cycles are the leading cause of culture death even when seals are intact. The freeze-thaw cycle ruptures cell walls.
- **Flooding or water damage:** Low-lying storage areas are vulnerable to flooding. Always store cultures above ground level.
- **Power outages:** Extended power failures can cause temperature excursions. A backup generator or insulated cooler as a temporary measure can save valuable strains.
- **Inadequate backup quantity:** Cultivators who maintain fewer than 5 backup slants per strain have no margin for error when contamination or accidents occur.

## Record-Keeping for Stored Cultures

A comprehensive culture database should track:

- Species name, strain code, and collection number
- P-value at the time of slant preparation
- Date the slant was prepared and date placed into storage
- Media formula used
- Storage location (building, refrigerator, box number)
- Date of last viability check and result
- Notes on any observed changes in mycelial morphology upon retrieval

## Practical Recommendations Summary

1. Maintain at least 10 backup slants per strain across two geographically separate locations
2. Perform annual viability checks by retrieving and subculturing representative samples
3. Seal all slant caps with Parafilm or equivalent to prevent moisture and contaminant ingress
4. Track the [[growing-gourmet-stamets-p-value-strain-age-system|P-value]] of each slant to know its generational age
5. Use the oldest slants first for working cultures, preserving the youngest for archival purposes
6. For tropical species like Volvariella, avoid standard refrigeration and use specialized warm-storage protocols
7. Inspect stored slants visually every 3 to 6 months for signs of contamination or desiccation
8. Never return a retrieved slant to storage once it has been opened — subculture it into fresh slants instead
