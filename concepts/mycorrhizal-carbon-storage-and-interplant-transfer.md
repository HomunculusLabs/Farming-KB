# Mycorrhizal Carbon Storage and Interplant Carbon Transfer

## Overview

Roots and their mycorrhizal fungal associates constitute a massive and often overlooked carbon pool in terrestrial ecosystems. Below-ground carbon allocation, storage in fungal biomass, formation of stable soil aggregates, and interplant carbon transfer through mycorrhizal networks all play critical roles in ecosystem carbon dynamics. Understanding these processes is essential for predicting how forests and grasslands will respond to elevated atmospheric CO₂ and for developing strategies to enhance soil carbon sequestration.

## Below-Ground Biomass and Carbon Allocation

### Scale of Below-Ground Carbon

Vogt (1991) provided comparative biomass allocation data for 24 forest examples, of which only nine included both above- and below-ground standing stock carbon. The data revealed that below-ground biomass carbon accounts for a substantial proportion of total tree carbon:

| Tree Species | Below-Ground Carbon (% of Total) |
|-------------|----------------------------------|
| *Pinus menziesii* (Douglas fir) | 16% |
| *Pinus elliottii* (Slash pine) | 64% |

These figures demonstrate that ignoring below-ground allocation significantly underestimates forest carbon storage.

### Root Respiration and Carbon Flux

Bowden et al. (1993) estimated that nearly two-thirds of soil respiration in a temperate mixed hardwood forest comes from root activity, and root litter decomposition contributes 70–80% of total soil respiration across a range of forested ecosystems. Specific values include:

- Tulip tree (*Liriodendron tulipifera*): Root respiration estimated at 35% of soil respiration (Edwards and Harris, 1977)
- Slash pine (*Pinus elliottii*): Root respiration at 62% of soil respiration (Nakane et al., 1983)

### Conifer vs. Deciduous Patterns

Bonan (1993) proposed that coniferous trees allocate approximately twice as much carbon to roots as deciduous species. This is interpreted as a trade-off for nitrogen acquisition: conifer leaf litter has characteristically low quality, leading to slow N mineralization rates and lower N demand for growth, which is offset by greater below-ground carbon investment for soil exploitation and nutrient uptake.

In mor soils (distinct surface organic horizons), Bradley and Fyles (1996) found that carbon investment in roots primarily supported soil exploitation where nitrogen was in ready supply. In mull soils (organic matter incorporated into mineral horizons), photosynthates were used to stimulate N mineralization through rhizodeposition of easily assimilated carbon to prime microbial decomposition of organic nitrogen.

### Root Turnover in Heathlands

Aerts et al. (1992) demonstrated the importance of root turnover in heathland ecosystems:
- *Molinia* root turnover contributed 67% of total litter production, 87% of litter nitrogen loss, and 84% of total litter phosphorus loss
- *Calluna* and *Deschampsia* in the same ecosystem contributed 2–3× lower percentage values

## Interplant Carbon Transfer

### Mycorrhizal Bridges

Finlay and Read (1986a) demonstrated significant carbon transfer between plants interconnected by mycorrhizal bridges. This transfer is enhanced between:
- Source plants optimizing photosynthesis (sun-exposed)
- Sink plants growing in shade

### Field-Scale Transfer

Simard et al. (1997a, 1997b) documented carbon transfer both within and between tree species in field conditions. This finding challenges the traditional concept of each plant as an isolated competitive unit and supports the view that root-mycorrhizal networks function as resource-sharing systems for whole plant communities.

### Implications

The interplant transfer of carbon, along with potential interplant nutrient transfer (Finlay and Read, 1986b, 1986c), implies that forest root networks act as community-level resource conservation systems. This has implications for:
- Forest management and thinning practices
- Understanding competition vs. facilitation in plant communities
- Modeling ecosystem carbon cycling

## Carbon Longevity in Root Systems

Gaudinski et al. (2001) used radiocarbon dating to determine the age of fine roots in eastern US forests:
- Mean age of fine roots: 3–18 years
- Dead root age: 10–18 years
- Carbon in roots was fixed 3–8 years earlier in organic soil horizons
- Carbon in roots was fixed 11–18 years earlier in mineral soil horizons

These ages establish root biomass and necromass as midterm carbon stores in soils, with significant implications for carbon sequestration timelines.

## Mycorrhizal Responses to Elevated CO₂

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Hyphal Growth

Sanders et al. (1998) found a five-fold increase in arbuscular mycorrhizal extraradical mycelium under elevated CO₂. Rillig et al. (2000) studied New Zealand pastures along a CO₂ gradient near cold CO₂ springs and found linear increases:
- Mycorrhizal root colonization: r² = 0.47, P = 0.0016
- Soil hyphal length: r² = 0.76, P < 0.0001

### Species-Specific Responses

Klironomos et al. (1997, 1998) demonstrated differential responses among arbuscular mycorrhizal genera:
- *Glomus* species: Caused increased plant growth only under elevated CO₂
- *Acaulospora denticulata* and *Scutellospora calospora*: Caused no difference in plant growth but altered the form of the mycorrhizal association

### The Feedback Loop

Fitter et al. (2000) proposed a positive feedback mechanism:
1. Increased atmospheric CO₂ → enhanced plant carbon fixation
2. Enhanced fixation → increased mycorrhizal growth
3. Increased mycorrhizal growth → improved P (arbuscular) or N (ectomycorrhizal) capture
4. Improved nutrition → alleviated nutritional stress → further increased plant growth

However, they noted that direct effects of CO₂ on mycorrhizal hyphal growth would not be expected, since these fungi already grow in a high-CO₂ soil atmosphere. Indirect effects via enhanced host photosynthesis are the primary driver.

### Carbon Turnover Trade-Offs

For arbuscular mycorrhizae, high turnover of extraradical hyphae increases soil respiration and may counteract enhanced carbon fixation. Sanders et al. (1998) found no increased P uptake despite increased hyphal extension under elevated CO₂, suggesting the additional hyphal mass contributes to below-ground carbon stores and soil aggregation rather than nutrient acquisition.

Treseder and Allen (2000) argue differently for ectomycorrhizae, whose long-lived mycelium may resist microbial attack and enter the "slow pool" of soil carbon, contributing to long-term carbon storage.

## Glomalin as a Carbon Sink

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] produce the glycoprotein glomalin (Wright and Upadhyaya, 1996), which has significant implications for soil carbon storage:

- Treseder and Allen (2000) calculate glomalin could account for 30–60% of carbon in undisturbed soils
- Rillig et al. (2000) showed increased glomalin content in grassland soils under long-term elevated CO₂ exposure
- Glomalin contributes to [[soil-aggregate-formation]] and stability, physically protecting carbon from decomposition

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Hyphal Biomass Estimates

Rillig and Allen (1999) provided estimates of arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphal biomass across ecosystems:

| Ecosystem Type | Hyphal Length | Hyphal Dry Weight |
|---------------|--------------|-------------------|
| Poplar | 0.02 m g⁻¹ soil | — |
| Shrub steppe | 38 m ml⁻¹ soil | — |
| Prairie | 111 m ml⁻¹ soil | ~457 mg ml⁻¹ soil (~500 kg ha⁻¹) |

## Summary

Mycorrhizal fungi represent a critical but often neglected component of ecosystem carbon budgets. Below-ground carbon allocation through roots and mycorrhizae constitutes a major carbon flux, with interplant transfer through mycorrhizal networks adding a community-level dimension to carbon cycling. Under elevated CO₂, mycorrhizal responses are species-specific but generally lead to increased below-ground carbon investment, with potential for long-term carbon storage through glomalin production, soil aggregate stabilization, and the formation of slow-turnover fungal biomass. The trade-off between carbon cost to the host and carbon storage benefits to the ecosystem remains a key area of uncertainty.
