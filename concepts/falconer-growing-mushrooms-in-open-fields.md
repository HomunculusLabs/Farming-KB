# Growing Mushrooms in Open Fields

## Overview

William Falconer's Chapter VI addresses the cultivation of mushrooms
in open fields, a method that takes advantage of natural conditions to
produce crops with minimal artificial intervention. Under suitable
conditions, mushrooms can be grown easily and abundantly outdoors, with
the planting of spawn being the principal labor required.

## Natural Occurrence and Uncertainty

During the late summer and fall months, mushrooms often appear
spontaneously and in great quantity in open pastures. However, in their
natural condition they are an uncertain crop: in one year they may occur
in the greatest abundance, and in the next perhaps none can be found in
fields where they had been so numerous the previous year.

The popular opinion holds that after a dry summer mushrooms abound in
the fields, but after a wet summer they are a very scarce crop. The
inference is that excess moisture kills the spawn in the ground. This
may be true to a certain extent, but good spawn planted by hand in the
fields in early summer will produce mushrooms toward fall regardless
of whether the summer has been wet or dry. That said, a wet spell
immediately succeeding the planting of spawn will kill a great deal of
it.

## Ideal Field Conditions

As a rule, wild mushrooms abound most in rich, old, well-drained,
rolling pasture lands. They avoid dry, sandy, or wet places, and the
neighborhood of trees and bushes. In attempting to cultivate them in
the open fields, one should endeavor to provide similar conditions. The
chief requisite is good spawn, for without this mushrooms cannot be
raised.

## Planting Method

About the middle of June, take a sharp spade in the pasture and make
V-shaped or T-shaped cuts in the grass sod about four inches deep.
Raise one side enough to allow the insertion of a bit of spawn two to
three inches square, so that it shall be about two inches below the
surface. Then tamp the sod down firmly.

By cutting and raising the sod in this way, without breaking it off,
the sod is not as likely to die of drought in summer. Plant as much or
as little as may be desired, at distances of three, four, or more feet
apart. During the following August or September the mushrooms should
show themselves, and continue in bearing for several weeks.

## Mr. Henshaw's Method

Mr. Henshaw of Staten Island was very successful in growing mushrooms
in the fields as well as indoors. His plan was very simple. About the
end of June, or as soon as dry weather sets in, the old beds from the
mushroom house are removed. If there is any live spawn in the bottom
of the beds, it is put in a wheelbarrow and taken to the field, where
it is planted in open places, but never under trees.

In planting, a sod is lifted and a shovelful of the manure containing
the spawn is put in the hole, then the sod is replaced and beaten down
firm. This is done at distances of twelve feet apart. If no live spawn
is available from indoor beds, common brick spawn is used instead,
putting about a quarter of a brick into each hole, returning and
beating down the sod as already stated.

If a dry time comes after the spawn is put in the pasture, a good
supply of mushrooms is almost certain in the fall. This method
demonstrates that leftover spawn from indoor cultivation can be
productively repurposed for outdoor growing.

## The Carter and Company Method

Carter and Company, seedsmen of London, described another method of
growing mushrooms in meadows. In March (or May in American climates)
the grower begins to collect droppings from the stables. When enough
have been gathered, they are taken to the meadow, where holes dug
here and there about one foot or eighteen inches square are filled with
them. The soil removed is scattered over the surrounding grass.

When all the holes have been filled and made solid, two or three pieces
of spawn about one inch square are placed in each hole. The grower
treads all down firmly, replaces the turf, and beats it tightly down.
Under this system, in August and September mushrooms appear without
fail in abundance and without any further care. The method is simple
and the result certain.

Fresh holes were spawned every year under this system, ensuring
continuous production. This method is particularly noteworthy for its
use of enriched planting stations rather than simple spawn insertion
into existing sod.

## Key Principles

Several key principles emerge from Falconer's discussion of field
cultivation:

- Good spawn is the absolute prerequisite; without it, no mushrooms
  can be produced
- Well-drained, rolling pasture land provides the best natural
  conditions
- Avoidance of tree cover and excessively wet or dry locations is
  essential
- A dry period after planting favors mushroom production
- Brick spawn can substitute for natural spawn when fresh material
  is unavailable
- Spawning should be done in mid-June for fall harvests
- Enriched planting holes with stable manure provide superior results
  to simple sod insertion

## Relationship to Indoor Cultivation

Field growing complements indoor cultivation in several ways. Spawn
from exhausted indoor beds can be repurposed outdoors, extending the
productive life of the mycelium. The outdoor method requires far less
infrastructure and labor than cellar or house growing, making it
accessible to those without specialized mushroom-growing facilities.

However, outdoor crops are subject to the uncertainties of weather in
a way that protected indoor crops are not. The chief risk is excessive
rainfall shortly after spawning, which can kill the spawn before it
has established itself in the soil.
