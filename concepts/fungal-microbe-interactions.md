# Fungal-Microbe Interactions

Fungi [[fungal-adaptations-environmental-gradients]] [[fungal-biogeochemistry]] interact with a vast array of other microorganisms -- bacteria, archaea, viruses, other fungi, and protists -- through relationships ranging from mutualism to competition to parasitism. These interactions fundamentally shape microbial community structure and ecosystem function.

## Fungal-Bacteria Interactions

### In Mycorrhizal Symbiosis
Mycorrhizal fungi interact intimately with bacteria in the rhizosphere and mycorrhizosphere:
- **Dinitrogen-fixing bacteria** form consortia with cordgrass ascomycetes on decaying blades (Newell and Porter, 2000)
- **Helper bacteria** can stimulate mycorrhizal formation and function
- **Mycorrhization helper bacteria** (MHB) in the *Pseudomonas* and *Streptomyces* genera enhance EM colonization
- **Competition** occurs when bacteria and fungi compete for the same nutrients or space

### In Decomposition
Bacteria and fungi often show sequential patterns during decomposition. In saltmarsh cordgrass, bacterial and fungal communities show distinct dynamics on decaying grass blades (Buchan et al., 2003). Inside standing-dead shoots, ascomycete fungi carry out decomposition free from prokaryotic competition. Fungi tend to dominate later decomposition stages when more recalcitrant compounds remain.

### Antagonistic Interactions
- **Antibiotic production**: Many soil fungi produce antibiotics that inhibit bacterial growth
- **Bacterial antifungal compounds**: Bacteria produce compounds that inhibit fungal growth
- **Lysis**: Some bacteria lyse fungal hyphae (mycophagy)

## Fungal-Fungal Interactions

### Mycelial Combat
When fungal mycelia encounter each other, several outcomes are possible:
- **Deadlock**: Neither advances; a persistent confrontation zone forms
- **Replacement**: One species overgrows and replaces another
- **Partial replacement**: Mutual penetration with territory exchange

These interactions determine community composition and resource partitioning in wood decomposition and soil systems.

### Parasitic Fungi
Some fungi are parasites on other fungi (mycoparasites):
- **Nematode-trapping fungi**: Capture nematodes but interact with soil bacteria during decomposition
- **Hyperparasites**: Fungi that parasitize other fungal species

## Fungal-Virus Interactions

Fungal viruses (mycoviruses) are widespread but often cryptic:
- Many fungi harbour dsRNA viruses without visible symptoms
- Some mycoviruses reduce fungal virulence (hypovirulence), useful for biological control of plant pathogens (e.g., *Cryphonectria parasitica* and chestnut blight)
- Viruses can affect fungal metabolism, growth, and reproductive capacity

## Fungal-Insect Symbioses

Fungi form intimate associations with insects:
- **Beetle gut fungi**: Wood-ingesting beetles (*Odontotaenius disjunctus*, Passalidae) harbour xylose-fermenting yeasts (*Pichia stipitis*-like) in their hindguts. Fungus-feeding beetles (Ciidae, Erotylidae) harbour diverse, often undescribed yeasts in their midguts
- **Yeast endosymbionts**: *Symbiotaphrina* species in anobiid beetles provide sterol biosynthesis capability
- **Termite gut fungi**: Termites harbour diverse gut microbiota including yeasts with cellulolytic capabilities
- **Green lacewing yeasts**: *Metschnikowia* species associated with *Chrysoperla* lacewings

### Functional Roles of Gut Fungi
- **Cellulose and hemicellulose degradation**: Xylose-fermenting yeasts process hemicellulose backbones
- **Detoxification**: Some symbiotic yeasts detoxify plant defensive compounds
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Suggested but not conclusively demonstrated in all systems
- **Vitamin synthesis**: Microbes may provide essential vitamins to insect hosts
- **Pheromone production**: Some gut microbes influence insect chemical communication

## Fungal-Protist Interactions

Fungi interact with protists in soil and aquatic environments:
- **Predation**: Amoebae graze on fungal hyphae and spores in soil
- **Endosymbiosis**: Some protists harbour fungal endosymbionts
- **Competition**: Protists and fungi compete for bacterial prey in some environments

The hindgut of passalid beetles contains amoeboid protists alongside yeasts and bacteria, forming a complex, compartmentalized microbial community.

## Ecological Implications

Fungal-microbe interactions have profound ecosystem-level consequences:
- Determining rates and pathways of organic matter decomposition
- Controlling plant disease through biological interactions
- Regulating nutrient cycling through coupled fungal-bacterial processes
- Shaping microbial community assembly and succession
