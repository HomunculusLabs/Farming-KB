## The Cannabis Health Index Score Methodology

The Cannabis Health Index (CHI) score is an evidence-based rating system developed by [[blesching-cannabis-chi-scoring-evidence-based-medicine]] system assigns values to research studies based on the intrinsic strength of evidence associated with each study type, following the established hierarchy of evidence in medical research:

| Study Design | CHI Score Range | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Double-blind, placebo-controlled, clinical human trials and crossover trials | +/-5 | Gold standard; eliminates bias from both patients and researchers |
| Clinical human trials and cohort studies | +/-4 | Controlled human data without full blinding |
| Reviews of relevant literature and studies, and human case studies | +/-3 | Synthesizes multiple studies; case studies provide direct human data |
| Animal studies | +/-2 | Controlled experiments but limited cross-species applicability |
| Laboratory (in vitro) studies | +/-1 | Mechanistic data without whole-organism context |

This hierarchy reflects the scientific consensus that double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized controlled trials provide the strongest evidence, while laboratory studies, though valuable for mechanistic understanding, provide the weakest evidence for clinical efficacy.

The hierarchy also reflects practical considerations: human trials are more expensive and difficult to conduct than animal or laboratory studies, so conditions with higher CHI scores have attracted greater research investment, which itself signals scientific interest and preliminary promise.

## Interpreting CHI Scores

### Score of 3.5 to 5

A CHI score in this range indicates solid, actual evidence that cannabis may be a useful treatment. Studies achieving these scores typically include clinical human trials or comprehensive literature reviews with positive findings. Blesching recommends sharing these citations with healthcare practitioners for review and serious consideration of cannabis as a treatment option.

### Score of 1.5 to 3.4

This range suggests that while more research is needed, it is probable that cannabis could be a useful treatment. The evidence is not yet conclusive but promising enough to warrant discussion with healthcare providers, particularly when patients have concerns about the effectiveness or safety of conventional alternatives.

### Score of 0 to 1.5

A score in this range suggests that it is merely possible that cannabis is therapeutic, or that study results were nonexistent, mixed, or inconclusive. Evidence at this level should be considered preliminary and insufficient to guide treatment decisions on its own.

## Important Caveats

### High Scores Reflect Research Volume

Blesching emphasizes that a high total CHI score reflects the amount of available published research to a much larger extent than the degree of efficacy of cannabis for treating the particular disease. The disease CHI score is a simple sum of all individual study scores, so higher overall CHI numbers indicate that a large amount of research has been conducted rather than that cannabis is definitively more effective for that condition.

### Relative Comparisons

When comparing CHI values between diseases, the values are relative to the available academic literature. A lower value does not necessarily mean cannabis is a less effective treatment for one condition versus another. It may simply mean less research has been published, or that the research that exists used lower-tier study designs (animal studies rather than [[cannabis-terpenoid-profiles-detailed]] detailed profiles. sativa-dominant strains, indica-dominant strains, and their respective hybrids can produce different therapeutic outcomes for the same condition. The CHI methodology accounts for these differences by noting which specific strains, preparations, and cannabinoid profiles were used in each study.
