## The Three Core Ethics

### Care for the Earth

Care for the Earth encompasses multiple levels of meaning. At the most
grounded level, it means caring for living soil as the source of terrestrial
life and the foundation for human well-being. The state of soils has been
regarded as the best measure of future health and well-being of society. Beyond
soil, it extends to stewardship of place, country, and territory, as understood
by indigenous cultures and through the concept of bioregionalism.

The Gaia hypothesis of James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis provides a scientific
framework: the earth is a self-organised system. If humanity seriously affects
fundamental life-support systems, we risk being "neutralised" by co-evolutionary
mechanisms such as climate change or disease. Care for the Earth in this global
context is driven by both ethical restraint and fear of planetary consequences.

Care for the Earth also means accepting all lifeforms as intrinsically valuable,
regardless of their current usefulness to humans. The traditional saying "live
and let live" encapsulates this modest approach to avoiding harm. When killing
other living things is necessary, it should be done consciously and
respectfully; not using what is killed is considered the greatest disrespect.

## Common Challenges and Solutions

Several recurring challenges tend to arise in work related to this
topic. These include variability in environmental conditions, the
complexity of multi-variable interactions, and the difficulty of
predicting outcomes with certainty in dynamic systems. Anticipating
these challenges enables more proactive and effective management.

Building resilience into implementations through diversity, redundancy,
and adaptive capacity helps buffer against unpredictable events and
conditions. This approach recognizes that some degree of uncertainty is
inherent in working with natural systems and plans accordingly rather
than assuming perfect predictability or control over outcomes.

Documentation and record-keeping support continuous improvement by
creating a reference base of observations, interventions, and results.
This accumulated knowledge enables progressively better decision-making
and helps identify patterns that might otherwise be overlooked in the
complexity of day-to-day management and observation activities.
