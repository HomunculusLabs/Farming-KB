# Neurotropic Fungi Taxonomy and Classification

Neurotropic fungi — also called hallucinogenic, psychotropic, magic, sacred,
psychedelic, or entheogenic mushrooms — are a chemically diverse group capable
of altering mood, thought, and [[plant-sensory-perception]]. The term "hallucinogenic"
is somewhat misleading, as these substances more accurately produce perceptual
distortions rather than true hallucinations (sensory perceptions without
external stimuli). Changes in thought, mood, and self-awareness are equally
important effects. Users prefer "psychedelic"; scientists use "hallucinogens."
These terms are used interchangeably in practice.

## The Four Major Groups

Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (2000) classified 216 known species of neurotropic
fungi into four groups based on chemistry and ethnobotanical use. Over 250
species have been reported in the literature, but the authors curated the list
to 216 based on reliable evidence, excluding species with doubtful
identifications or unconfirmed chemistry.

### Group 1: Psilocybin-Containing Fungi (Indole Substances)

The largest group, containing psilocybin, psilocin, baeocystin, and
norbaeocystin. The genus **Psilocybe** dominates with 116 species (54%).
Other genera include: **Gymnopilus** (14), **Panaeolus** (13), **Copelandia**
(12), **Hypholoma** (6), **Pluteus** (6), **Inocybe** (6), **Conocybe** (4),
**Panaeolina** (4), **Gerronema** (2), and **Agrocybe**, **Galerina**,
**Mycena** (1 each). Many Panaeoloideous species lack reliable chemical
confirmation. Ott (1993) noted contradictions, particularly in Gerronema,
Hygrocybe, and Inocybe per Gartz (1986e).

### Group 2: Ibotenic Acid-Containing Fungi

**Amanita muscaria** (fly agaric), **A. pantherina**, and **A. regalis**.
These contain ibotenic acid, muscimol, and muscarine — chemically unrelated
to psilocybin. They carry substantial toxicity risks; some related Amanita
species are fatally poisonous. A. muscaria grows mycorrhizally with Pinus and
Betula in northern hemisphere forests and has been introduced to pine
plantations in tropical regions including Brazil, Colombia, Tanzania,
Australia, and New Zealand. Wasson and Wasson (1957) documented its sacred
use among the Chukchee and Koryak peoples of Siberia.

### Group 3: Ergot Fungi

Five species of **Claviceps** (C. purpurea, C. nigricans, C. paspali,
C. rolfesii, C. tripsicii) and two species of **Cordyceps** (C. capitata,
C. ophioglossoides). [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]] produces ergot alkaloids with a long
history of causing ergotism. Cordyceps species are used ceremonially by the
Trique Indians in Mexico's Nevado de Toluca region, called "hombrecitos"
(little men) and "mujercitas" (little women). An Elaphomyces specimen (the
Cordyceps host truffle) serves as a ceremony "director." The Trique also use
Elaphomyces to heal wounds and "rejuvenate the organism."

### Group 4: Sacred Fungi Without Chemical Confirmation

Fungi used as sacred mushrooms without reliable chemical studies: boletes
(Boletus and Heimiella, 8 species), **Russula** (6 species), and 6
gasteromycete species from Lycoperdales and Phallales. Heim and Wasson (1962)
reported [[lycoperdon-mixtecorum]] and L. marginatum as narcotic fungi among the
Mixtec Indians, but Ott et al. (1975) determined these were common edible
species confused with genuine neurotropic fungi. Some puffballs were called
"fallen stars" by North [[yage-common-themes-south-american-indians]] for their fairy ring growth pattern.

## The Bluing Reaction

Species of Psilocybe that turn blue when bruised are generally considered
neurotropic. This occurs in sections Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae,
Cordisporae, Cubensies, Mexicanae, Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae,
and Zapotecorum. However, absence of bluing does not exclude neurotropic
properties, and several reported bluing species have failed chemical
confirmation.

## Problems of Misidentification

Chemical studies frequently suffer from taxonomic errors. Guzmán found that
material used by Leslie and Repke to isolate psilocybin was a mixture of
Panaeolus spp., Psilocybe mexicana, and P. coprophila — all misidentified as
P. coprophila. P. pseudobullacea is non-bluing with no confirmed activity, yet
Marcano et al. (1994) isolated psilocybin from Venezuelan specimens, probably
an unidentified species. Høiland (1978) reported psilocybin in P.
atrobrunnea, likely confused with P. coprinifacies or P. maire.
