# Fungi and the Convention on Biological Diversity

The United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), signed by
153 nations at the 1992 Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro, established the
most comprehensive international framework for biodiversity
conservation. The implications of this convention for [[fungal-biology-fundamentals]]
and conservation are immense, yet fungi remain significantly
underrepresented in CBD implementation efforts compared to plants and
animals.

## Convention Objectives and Fungal Relevance

The CBD stands to preserve the ecological, genetic, social, economic,
scientific, cultural, recreational, and aesthetic values of biological
diversity and its components, which are considered important for
evolution and for maintaining life-sustaining systems of the biosphere.
Fungi are directly relevant to these objectives because:

- Fungi constitute one of the largest and least-known groups of
  organisms despite their ubiquity and importance to nature and humans
- As habitats are degraded and destroyed each year, hundreds or
  perhaps thousands of [[gadd-fungal-species-recognition]] are being lost even before they
  are known
- Fungi play pivotal roles in all ecosystems, including nutrient
  cycling, decomposition, plant mutualisms, and pathogenesis
- Fungal genetic resources have enormous potential for biotechnology,
  medicine, and industry
- Fungi serve as indicators of ecosystem health and environmental
  change

## The Biodiversity Knowledge Gap

Despite the CBD's broad objectives, there is little understanding of
how the fungi now known interact with the abiotic and biotic components
of their environments. This knowledge gap derives largely from:

- Lack of manpower and financial resources for systematic mycology
- Declining infrastructural and financial support for taxonomic
  mycology at a time of renewed interest in conservation and
  biodiversity
- The enormous diversity of fungi, with estimated total species
  numbers ranging from 1.5 million to over 5 million
- The technical difficulty of studying organisms with cryptic life
  cycles and often ephemeral fruiting structures
- Limited public awareness of fungal importance compared to plants
  and animals

## Systematics Agenda 2000 and Fungal Inventory

Systematics Agenda 2000, a major scientific initiative, recommended
that the systematics community survey the biota of the world within 25
years. Such an undertaking would place unprecedented demands on
herbaria and culture collections to house and maintain specimens. The
implications for fungal systematics are particularly acute because:

- Recent accelerated deforestation and general habitat destruction
  have created urgency for documenting fungal diversity before
  habitats disappear
- Many fungal groups have never been surveyed in most geographic
  regions
- The number of undescribed fungal species is estimated to far exceed
  the number already described
- Capacity for fungal taxonomy and identification is limited in
  biodiversity-rich tropical countries

## Access to Genetic Resources and Benefit Sharing

The CBD framework raises important questions about access to fungal
genetic resources and the sharing of benefits arising from their use:

- Fungi from biodiversity-rich countries may have commercial
  potential as sources of pharmaceuticals, enzymes, or other products
- International agreements are needed to ensure equitable sharing of
  benefits from fungal genetic resources
- Culture collections and herbaria serve as repositories of genetic
  diversity that may be accessed for commercial development
- Permit requirements for collecting and exporting fungal specimens
  reflect CBD principles
- Balancing conservation with scientific access requires careful
  policy development

## Collection Permits and Legal Frameworks

The CBD has influenced the legal frameworks governing fungal
collecting:

- Herbaria should not accept specimens unless accompanied by copies
  of appropriate authorizing permits (collection, export from host
  country, import into country of deposition)
- Collecting in some areas (national parks, private lands, other
  countries) may be restricted
- Acceptance of specimens without proper authorization can damage
  herbarium credibility, limit eligibility for federal funds, and
  lead to criminal prosecution
- Specific regulations and guidelines pertain to research involving
  potentially pathogenic fungi
- International transport of fungal cultures is governed by
  multiple regulatory agencies

## Herbaria and Culture Collections Under the CBD

Natural history collections are critical infrastructure for CBD
implementation:

- Herbaria and their associated data document species distributions,
  phenologies, host associations, and composition data for specific
  sites
- Collections provide materials for chemical and molecular analyses
- Historical collections contain information necessary to examine
  changes caused by natural events and human activities
- Collections must be fully integrated into planning of broad
  inventories and their execution
- Curators must keep abreast of developments in science and
  environmental policy while continuing to fulfill their primary
  mission as repositories

## Applications to Aquatic Fungal Diversity

The CBD framework is particularly relevant to understudied groups
such as aquatic fungi:

- Freshwater habitats have disappeared at a rapid rate, with many
  continuing to be altered by agrarian practices, land development,
  and pollution
- Documenting fungal species in existing aquatic habitats is
  important so that the effects of habitat alterations on species
  composition can be detected
- Databases of fungal pathogens from aquatic animals contribute to
  CBD objectives by documenting biodiversity components
