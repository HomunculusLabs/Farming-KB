# Protozoa in Compost Tea and Nutrient Cycling

Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic organisms that play a
critical role in the [[soil-food-web]] by cycling nutrients. In
Elaine Ingham's framework, protozoa are the primary mechanism by
which plant-available nitrogen is released in soil systems. Without
protozoa, nutrients remain locked in bacterial biomass and are not
accessible to plants.

## The Three Groups of Protozoa

Ingham's field guide identifies three groups relevant to compost
tea and soil biology:

### Flagellates
- The smallest protozoa, with flagella for movement
- Most tolerant of disturbed or compacted conditions
- 25,000 or more per gram in quality compost
- First to recover after disturbance
- Present in aerobic compost tea but absent from anaerobic brews
- Important indicator: their presence confirms aerobic conditions

### Amoebae
- Larger than flagellates, move by pseudopod extension
- 25,000 or more per gram in quality compost
- Require more oxygen and better soil structure than flagellates
- Critical for nutrient cycling in well-structured soils
- Also absent from anaerobic brews
- Present in good compost, especially worm compost

### Ciliates
- The largest protozoa, with cilia for movement
- Only 50-100 per gram in quality compost
- Higher numbers indicate anaerobic conditions, compaction, or
  water-logging
- They thrive in compacted, oxygen-limited environments
- An important warning signal in soil assessment
- If ciliates are above desired range, soil is anaerobic and needs
  fungal, protozoan, and nematode improvements

## Why Protozoa Matter for Nutrient Cycling

Protozoa consume bacteria, releasing plant-available nitrogen
through their waste products (ammonium). This process is the
dominant natural mechanism for nitrogen mineralization in soil. The
chain works as follows:

1. Bacteria and fungi immobilize nutrients in their biomass
2. Protozoa eat 10,000 bacteria per hour per individual
3. Protozoa excrete excess nitrogen as ammonium
4. Plant roots take up the ammonium (or it is converted to nitrate)
5. Without protozoa, nitrogen remains locked in bacterial bodies

When protozoa are absent or too low, nutrient cycling stops. This
is why plate-count-based compost teas (which only grow bacteria)
cannot cycle nutrients effectively. Anaerobic brews do not contain
flagellates or amoebae and thus cannot cycle nutrients. Nutrients
become immobilized in bacterial biomass and many nutrients are
lost as ammonia, nitrous oxide, or hydrogen sulfide gas.

## Protozoa in Compost Tea

For protozoa to be present in [[compost-tea-aact]], several
conditions must be met:

1. The compost must contain adequate protozoa numbers (50,000+ per
   gram)
2. The tea must remain aerobic throughout brewing
3. The tea maker must extract organisms from compost effectively
4. The brew must not exceed 24 hours to prevent oxygen depletion

Worm compost is the best source of protozoa for compost tea. Good
AACT machines that maintain aerobic conditions extract protozoa
well. Machines that go anaerobic (Soil Soup, some Growing Solutions
systems) do not extract or maintain protozoa.

## Protozoa and Algae Control

Protozoa consume algae, especially in aquatic systems. Ingham
cites University of Wisconsin work showing that algal blooms in
lakes were resolved by restoring protozoan populations. The food
chain is clear: protozoa eat algae. When pesticides and high
inorganic fertilizers eliminated protozoa from golf course ponds,
algal blooms followed.

## Restoring Low Protozoa Numbers

When protozoa numbers are too low in soil (per SFI testing), the
remedy is:
- Add an inoculum of protozoa from good compost, especially worm
  compost
- Commercial protozoa inocula are available
- Protozoa are also available from forest soil
- Apply compost tea with high protozoa content
- Reduce compaction and improve aeration to support protozoan
  survival

## Protozoa and Soil Assessment

In Ingham's soil foodweb assessment framework, protozoa are
evaluated at several levels:
- Are any protozoan groups too low? That group needs improvement.
- Are ciliates too high? This signals anaerobic, compacted
  conditions.
- What are the ratios between groups? Proper balance indicates
  healthy soil structure.
- Is nutrient cycling adequate? This depends directly on protozoan
  and nematode presence.

## Protozoa in Anaerobic vs. Aerobic Conditions

The distinction is critical for tea quality:
- Aerobic teas contain flagellates, amoebae, and some ciliates --
  full nutrient cycling capacity
- Anaerobic teas contain only ciliates (which tolerate low oxygen)
  and lack flagellates and amoebae
- Anaerobic teas cannot cycle nutrients effectively
- Going anaerobic and returning to aerobic conditions never
  restores the full protozoan diversity
