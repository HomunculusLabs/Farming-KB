# Fungal Roles in Agriculture

Paul Stamets describes fungi as essential but underappreciated partners in agricultural systems. From soil building to crop protection, fungal communities play roles that conventional agriculture often ignores or actively disrupts through chemical inputs.

## Soil as Ecological Currency

Stamets frames soil as "ecological currency" -- if overspent or depleted, the environment goes bankrupt. Fungi are the primary builders of agricultural soils through their decomposition of organic matter. The richness and depth of soil is a legacy from centuries of mycelial activity. With each harvest cycle, if organic matter is not returned, soils lose nutrients and gradually become overtaxed. A 21-year study in Germany found that no-till organic farming methods were superior to conventional methods in energy use and effects on wildlife, largely due to the health of resident mycoflora.

## Mycorrhizal Partnerships with Crops

Reforestation and agricultural efforts are greatly enhanced when [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are introduced to sprouting seeds or to the roots of young plants at planting time. [[mycorrhizal-inoculation]] extends the effective root zone of plants, improving access to water, phosphorus, and other minerals. Mycorrhizal hyphae act as extensions of the root system, reaching into soil micro-pores that roots cannot access, dramatically increasing the surface area for nutrient and water absorption.

Research cited by Stamets demonstrates that mycorrhizal seedlings survive at a rate of 80% of the time, compared to only 57% for seedlings without this fungal association (Varma et al. 1999). Root and shoot mass also doubled in inoculated plants. These fungi have shown growth-enhancing properties when paired with maize (Zea mays), tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), and parsley (Petroselinum crispum). Many researchers believe that secondary products from woodland ecosystems -- including the fungal contributions to [[soil-fertility-management]] -- provide strong economic incentives to maintain fungal communities.

### Endophytic Fungi in Agricultural Crops

[[endophytic-fungi-in-agriculture]] -- once thought to be pathogens -- are increasingly recognized as engaging plants in mutually beneficial relationships. In a 2003 experiment in Panama, researchers found that when endophyte-free leaves from the chocolate-producing cocoa tree (Theobroma cacao) were inoculated with endophytes, leaf necrosis and mortality declined threefold, suggesting a biodefensive effect against pathogens such as Phytophthora.

Joan Henson and other researchers (2004) filed a patent application using a Curvularia species isolated from grasses in the geothermal zones of Yellowstone and Lassen Volcanic national parks. This thermally tolerant endophyte confers drought and heat tolerance to its host plant. Grasses inoculated with this endophyte showed enhanced resilience, and Stamets concludes that pairing endophytes with agricultural crops can increase yield, decrease disease, and reduce the need for fertilizers and insecticides.

## Companion Planting with Fungi

Stamets advocates integrating mushrooms into agricultural systems through companion planting strategies. In the context of [[mycorestoration-ecology]], companion planting with fungi sets the stage for emerging oases in damaged landscapes. Each succeeding mycelial mat provides different components as the ecosystem steers toward recovery. Saprophytic mushroom species that have been found to promote the growth of several crops include the garden giant (Stropharia rugosoannulata) and the elm oyster (Hypsizygus ulmarius).

### The Fukuoka Connection

Stamets references Masanobu Fukuoka's "one-straw revolution" approach and suggests customizing it for mycological applications. Fukuoka invented "seedballs" -- clay-soil pellets rich in microbes and seeds -- designed to jump-start weakened habitats. Although Fukuoka did not specifically select the populations of fungi in his seedballs, he appreciated their contribution. See [[natural-farming-philosophy]] for more on his methods.

Stamets describes one of our tasks as improving upon Fukuoka's pioneering method and customizing it for mycorestoration projects: "Beginning with one straw, the saprophytes lead the charge, followed by mycorrhizae and endophytes." He also describes a manager of a chestnut orchard in Quebec who observed Psilocybe mushrooms fruiting prolifically where trees had been intentionally inoculated with fungal allies, suggesting that fungal companions can enhance orchard productivity.

## Agricultural Waste as Mushroom Substrate

Mushroom cultivation can transform agricultural waste into valuable products:

- Straw from wheat, rice, and other grains
- Corncobs and corn stalks
- Cotton waste
- Coffee grounds and processing waste
- Spent grains from breweries

These materials serve as [[mushroom-substrates]] for growing gourmet and medicinal mushrooms, and the spent substrate becomes enriched compost for crop fields. This closed-loop approach is central to [[composting-systems]] and regenerative agriculture history.

## Mycofiltration for Farm Runoff

Around cattle and pig feedlots, the flow of coliforms, nitrates, and phosphorus affects downstream watersheds, especially during rainy seasons. [[mycofiltration-of-water-stamets]] membranes using locally available agricultural waste as a carrier for mycelium can capture this microbial outflow. If wheat or cornfields are nearby, cornstalks and wheat straw can furnish substrate without importing materials.

Each mushroom species secretes unique mixes of extracellular enzymes and acids, and using a plurality of species can have a synergistic effect for more complete capture of pollutants than any single species alone. The art is in species selection and their timely introduction -- matching mycelia with compatible plants and conditions to create effective biological filtration barriers.

## Sheet Mulching with Mycelium

Creating a sheet mulch -- a shallow compost bed 6 inches to 2 feet thick -- allows mycofiltration properties of mycelium and surface areas of substrate particles to capture microbial and chemical outflow. The essential idea is to grow mats of mycelium matched to the cubic size of the contamination source. The mycelium acts as a living membrane, with its fine cellular filaments extending between plant fibers, secreting enzymes that break down organic matter into basic nutrients while synthesizing polysaccharides that sponge moisture and bind soil particles.

## Mushrooms as Pest Control

[[mycopesticides-stamets]] using entomopathogenic fungi can target specific agricultural pests without harming crops, beneficial insects, or mammals. Key genera include Metarhizium (especially M. anisopliae), Beauveria (especially B. bassiana), and some Paecilomyces species. When insects come into contact with spores of these fungi, the spores attach and germinate, boring hyphal pegs through the insects' exoskeletons using chitin-dissolving enzymes. Once inside, the mycelium forks throughout the insect's body, eventually killing it.

Since the 1990s, several patents have been awarded exploiting these fungi as natural insecticides. Stamets developed a method of culturing pre-sporulating mycelial phenotypes that lack the repellent smell of spores and instead emit attractants and feeding stimulants. In trials at Texas A&M University, Formosan termites, eastern subterranean termites, and fire ants were attracted to the mycelium, fed upon it, and carried fragments back to the nest, dying from fungal infection within 2 to 3 weeks.

Mycelial matrices can be projected via many carriers available on farms: wood chips, sawdust, paper, cardboard, biodegradable fabrics, and agricultural waste products. Customized mycelial landscapes could forestall pest outbreaks associated with livestock and farms while simultaneously preventing and cleaning up pollution. See [[cordyceps-and-entomopathogenic-fungi]] for more on the biology of insect-pathogenic fungi.

## Phosphorus Cycling

Mycelium absorbs phosphorus from its surroundings, moving these mineral salts over distances and later releasing them when mushrooms rot or the mycelium dies. Fungal-decomposing bacteria then absorb the phosphorus. As mushrooms rot, the ecosystem benefits from this cycling in which bacteria allow phosphorus, zinc, potassium, and other essential minerals to be redeposited back into the nutritional bank. Like salmon carcasses, mushroom carcasses fertilize the ecosystem.

The connection to [[fungal-role-in-phosphorus-cycling]] is central to agricultural fertility: nearly a mile of mycelium can entangle a gram of pasture soil, creating an extraordinarily dense nutrient-exchange network. Polysaccharides manufactured by the mycelium act as mucilaginous soil-binding agents (Caeser-TonThat 2002), simultaneously boosting soil structure and the human immune system when consumed. See also [[soil-food-web-structure]] for the broader context of fungal nutrient cycling.

## Recycled Organic Matter

Stamets draws a parallel between leaving wood chips in forests and leaving stubble on farmed land. In both cases, returning organic matter to the soil supports the fungal and bacterial communities that maintain soil health. The recycling of organic debris lessens reliance on fertilizers, herbicides, and pesticides. The resident mycoflora, particularly the higher fungi (sexual fungi that produce mushrooms), aggressively decompose stubble in no-till farms and increase water-stabilizing aggregates (glomalins) in soil structure.
