# Essential Gardening Tools and Their Care

Steve Solomon's "Gardening When It Counts" advocates a deliberately minimalist
approach to gardening tools. Unlike [[coleman-british-adoption-of-french-intensive-gardening]] methods that require
numerous specialized implements, Solomon argues that successful vegetable
production for self-sufficiency requires only a few well-maintained hand tools.
His philosophy is rooted in the experience of gardeners who produced substantial
food before the advent of modern [[solomon-garden-centers-seedling-traps]] and their extensive product
catalogs.

## The Minimalist Tool Philosophy

Solomon explicitly challenges the garden center industry's promotion of
appealing but unnecessary merchandise. He writes that most people look to
"Everybody Else" for guidance before making decisions, and that garden
magazines, garden centers, and seed catalogs all promote the fiction that
appealing merchandise is useful and essential. In reality, to garden
successfully you only need a few hand tools, used properly.

Solomon positions himself as "the gardening grandfather you never had" —
someone who would teach practical tool skills including sharpening shovels and
hoes, and working the earth. He emphasizes that most people alive today never
had a grandfather who knew how to raise vegetables, who sharpened shovels and
hoes and knew how to work the earth with them.

## The Essential Tools

### Spade or Shovel
The primary earth-moving tool. A quality spade with a sharp blade makes digging
far easier than struggling with a dull one. Solomon favors a standard
long-handled digging spade over short-handled models for most garden tasks.
The spade is the foundation tool for [[azurescens-outdoor-bed-preparation-woodchips]], turning compost, and
harvesting root crops.

### Garden Hoe
The hoe is the primary weed-control tool in a low-input garden. Rather than
relying on mulch-intensive methods that require large quantities of organic
material, Solomon advocates regular, shallow hoeing to control weeds when they
are small. A sharp hoe cuts through weeds cleanly at or just below the soil
surface, severing them from their root systems with minimal soil disturbance.

### Garden Fork
Essential for aerating compacted soil, turning compost piles, and harvesting
[[growing-root-vegetables-guide]] without damaging them. The fork's tines penetrate soil more
easily than a spade blade in heavy or rocky ground and are less likely to
slice through potatoes, carrots, or other root crops during harvest.

### Hand Trowel
For transplanting seedlings, planting small seeds, and working in tight spaces.
A sturdy trowel with a comfortable grip is sufficient; expensive ergonomic
models are unnecessary for the competent gardener.

### Rake
A garden rake (not a leaf rake) for leveling seedbeds, smoothing soil after
digging, and creating furrows for planting. The rigid tines of a garden rake
are essential for proper seedbed preparation.

## Sharpening Shovels and Hoes

Proper sharpening is perhaps the most neglected skill in modern gardening, yet
it dramatically reduces the physical effort required for soil work. A sharp
spade or hoe cuts through soil and roots rather than pushing through them.

### Technique
1. Secure the tool firmly, blade facing up, in a vise or by clamping it
2. Use a mill bastard file (10-inch, second-cut) for most sharpening tasks
3. Hold the file at the existing bevel angle of the blade edge
4. Push the file away from your body in long, smooth strokes along the edge
5. Maintain consistent angle and pressure throughout each stroke
6. Work from one end of the blade to the other, counting strokes to ensure
   even sharpening
7. Remove the wire edge (burr) by lightly drawing the file flat across the
   back side of the blade

### Frequency
Tools should be sharpened at the beginning of each [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] touched
up periodically during intensive use periods. A few minutes of sharpening can
save hours of physical effort over the course of a growing season.

### What Not to Do
- Do not use power grinders unless you are experienced — they remove metal too
  quickly and can ruin the temper of the tool steel
- Do not sharpen the inside (concave) edge of a hoe blade — only the outside
  cutting edge
- Do not attempt to create a razor edge; garden tools need a sturdy bevel, not
  a fine knife edge

## Tool Maintenance Beyond Sharpening

- **Cleaning**: Remove soil after each use to prevent rust and tool
  deterioration. A stiff brush or putty knife works well for caked-on soil.
- **Oiling**: Apply a light coat of oil (linseed oil, WD-40, or motor oil) to
  metal surfaces before storage to prevent rust, especially in humid climates.
- **Handle care**: Sand rough handles smooth and apply linseed oil annually.
  Replace cracked or split handles promptly — a broken handle during use can
  cause injury.
- **Storage**: Store tools in a dry location, hanging rather than resting on
  their blades. Proper storage extends tool life significantly.

## Why Quality Matters

Solomon emphasizes that inexpensive, poorly made tools are a false economy.
A quality tool, properly maintained, will last decades or even generations.
Cheap tools with thin metal, poorly attached handles, or inferior steel will
bend, break, or fail to hold an edge, costing more in replacement and
frustration than the initial savings justify. When purchasing tools, look for
solid forged heads, securely riveted or welded connections, and handles made
from quality hardwood (ash or hickory) or fiberglass.

## Contrast with Intensive Gardening Methods

Intensive raised-bed systems often promote specialized tools such as dibbers,
soil blockers, broadforks, and various implements for close-spacing
management. Solomon's approach deliberately avoids this tool proliferation,
arguing that wide-spaced row gardening with properly maintained basic tools
requires far less equipment investment and produces comparable or superior
nutritional results per unit of gardener effort.
