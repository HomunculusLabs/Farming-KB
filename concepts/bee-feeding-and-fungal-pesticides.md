# Bee Feeding and Fungal Pesticides

## Overview

Fungal pesticides (mycopesticides) use entomopathogenic fungi -- fungi that parasitize and kill insects -- as alternatives to [[metarhizium-anisopliae]] for carpenter ant control, he noticed white wedge-shaped sectors of growth that lacked or had delayed spore formation. After successive transfers, he isolated a nonsporulating mycelial phenotype that was still lethal to insects but emitted attractants and feeding stimulants.

This was a key discovery because:
- Insects can detect entomopathogenic spores and avoid them
- Social insects like ants and termites have guards that refuse entry to spore-carrying individuals
- The sporeless mycelium, lacking the repellent odor, is actively sought out by insects as food
- Once ingested or contacted, the mycelium germinates inside the insect and kills it

### Protecting Hives from Small Hive Beetles

The small hive beetle (Aethina tumida) is another significant pest. Entomopathogenic fungi applied to soil around hives can target beetle larvae and pupae that drop to the ground to complete their life cycle. This approach targets the beetle's weakest life stage -- the soil-dwelling pupal stage -- while leaving adult bees unaffected.

### Hive Biosecurity and Fungal Safety

The selectivity of entomopathogenic fungi is crucial for beekeeping. Metarhizium anisopliae does not harm mammals and does not cause human allergies. Strain-specific targeting means that biopesticides can be formulated to control specific threats to bee colonies without harming the bees themselves.

Key safety considerations:
- Bees are insects, so in theory they could be susceptible. However, bee body temperature (35°C inside the hive) and grooming behavior provide natural protection
- Most entomopathogenic fungi have optimal growth temperatures below 35°C, limiting their ability to infect healthy adult bees
- Bees' social grooming behavior removes spores from nestmates, further reducing infection risk
- Strain selection can further optimize safety profiles

### Concerns and Cautions
- Spore-based products risk infecting non-target insects if not carefully formulated
- The presporulating mycelium approach offers greater selectivity
- Field application must consider foraging patterns and exposure risks
