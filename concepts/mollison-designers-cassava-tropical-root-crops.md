# Cassava and Tropical Root Crops

Tropical root crops form the calorie foundation of permaculture systems in
humid tropics and subtropics. Cassava (Manihot esculenta), also known as manioc
or yuca, is the most important of these, providing a reliable carbohydrate
source on marginal soils where grain crops fail. Bill Mollison gave extensive
coverage to cassava and related tropical root crops in the Designer's Manual,
recognising their critical role in tropical food security and their compatibility
with permaculture design principles.

## Cassava as a Permaculture Staple

Cassava is one of the most productive calorie crops per unit area, capable of
producing 20 to 40 tonnes of fresh root per hectare on soils that would not
support maize or rice. It tolerates acid soils, low fertility, and periodic
drought. Its deep root system allows it to access water and nutrients unavailable
to shallow-rooted crops. These characteristics make cassava an ideal component
of [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-1-intensive-garden]] and broader [[mollison-designers-tropical-pasture-grass-species-and-establishment]].

Cassava is a perennial shrub that can be harvested over an extended period,
typically eight to twenty-four months after planting. This flexibility allows the
grower to leave roots in the ground as a living storage system, harvesting only
what is needed. This reduces post-harvest losses and provides food security
during lean periods.

## Varieties and Selection

Two main types of cassava are recognised: sweet cassava with low cyanogenic
glycoside content, which can be eaten after simple cooking, and bitter cassava
with high cyanide content, which requires processing to remove toxins. Mollison
noted that the bitter varieties are often more pest-resistant and higher-yielding,
making them preferred for staple production where processing traditions exist.

For permaculture design, selecting a mix of sweet and bitter varieties provides
flexibility. Sweet varieties can be planted near the house in Zone 1 for quick
harvest, while bitter varieties occupy Zone 2 and Zone 3 where their pest
resistance and yield are more valuable. The [[mollison-designers-seed-saving-variety-preservation]] principle applies to
cassava through the selection and propagation of stem cuttings from superior
plants.

## Planting and Propagation

Cassava is propagated from stem cuttings rather than seeds. Mature stems,
approximately one to two centimetres in diameter, are cut into sections of 15
to 20 centimetres and planted horizontally or at an angle in prepared soil.
The [[mollison-designers-plant-nursery-propagation-bed-design]] can be used to sprout cuttings before field planting, though
direct planting is common in the tropics.

Planting spacing varies with variety and soil fertility. In permaculture
systems, cassava is often interplanted with legume trees, [[mollison-designers-banana-circle-waste-recycling-system]], or
other crops rather than grown in monoculture. The tall cassava canopy provides
partial shade for understory crops, and the leaf fall contributes mulch to the
soil surface.

## Cyanide Detoxification

All cassava contains some level of cyanogenic glycosides, primarily linamarin.
Processing to remove these compounds is essential, especially for bitter
varieties. Traditional processing methods include peeling, grating, fermenting,
and cooking. The fermentation process, in which grated cassava is left for
several days, allows enzymes to break down the glycosides. The resulting product
can be dried as flour (farinha in Brazil, gari in West Africa) or cooked as a
porridge or flatbread.

Mollison emphasised that processing is not a problem but a design opportunity.
The [[mollison-designers-food-processing-preservation-storage]] infrastructure of the permaculture settlement should include
facilities for cassava processing, turning a potential hazard into a value-added
product. The byproducts of processing, including cassava peels and pulp, are
valuable [[mollison-designers-poultry-forage-design-pastured-systems]] or [[mollison-designers-pig-tractor-systems-and-land-preparation]].

## Companion Planting with Cassava
Cassava integrates well with many tropical permaculture species. Legume trees
such as [[mollison-designers-leucaena-multipurpose-nitrogen-tree]] and gliricidia fix nitrogen and provide mulch.
[[mollison-designers-moringa-and-multipurpose-tropical-trees]] provides nutritious leaf greens. Ground covers such as sweet potato
protect the soil surface. The [[mollison-designers-companion-planting-charts-and-principles]] approach creates productive
polycultures whose total caloric and nutritional output exceeds cassava
monoculture while maintaining soil health and reducing pest pressure.

## Soil Management for Cassava

While cassava tolerates poor soils, it responds well to fertility improvements.
The [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]] chapter discusses how organic matter management, mulching,
and green manure crops can build soil fertility for cassava production. Cassava
depletes soil potassium if harvested continuously without fertility inputs, so
the designer should plan for regular inputs of potassium-rich organic matter.

[[mollison-designers-mulch-pit-interplanting-technique]] and [[mollison-designers-banana-circle-waste-recycling-system]] provide concentrated fertility
that benefits cassava planted on their margins. The nutrient cycling from these
intensive Zone 1 elements supports cassava production in adjacent Zone 2 areas.

## Pest and Disease Management

Cassava is relatively pest-free compared to grain crops, but several pests and
diseases can cause significant losses. The cassava mealybug and cassava green
mite are major pests in Africa, while bacterial blight and mosaic virus affect
crops worldwide. The [[mollison-designers-integrated-pest-management-design]] approach uses biological
control agents, resistant varieties, and cultural practices to manage these
threats.

Genetic diversity is the best defence against cassava pests and diseases.
Maintaining many different varieties in the system ensures that no single pest
or disease can destroy the entire crop. This aligns with the [[mollison-designers-crop-multiplier-selection-breeding]]
strategy of maintaining diverse genetic resources.

## Other Tropical Root Crops

Several other root crops complement cassava in tropical permaculture systems:

- **Taro (Colocasia esculenta)**: A wetland or irrigated crop producing large
  corms. Taro is suited to the margins of [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]] and [[mollison-designers-aquatic-edge-planted-wetland-design]].
- **Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas)**: A versatile ground cover that produces
  edible tubers and edible leaves. Excellent for soil protection on slopes.
- **Yam (Dioscorea spp.)**: Climbing vines producing large underground tubers.
  Yams are often grown on trellises in association with [[mollison-designers-vine-and-fruit-tree-integration]].
- **Arrowroot (Maranta arundinacea)**: Produces easily digested starch from
  rhizomes. Tolerates partial shade and is suited to understory planting.
- **Tannia (Xanthosoma sagittifolium)**: Related to taro but more tolerant of
  drier conditions. Produces edible cormels and leaves.

## Nutritional Considerations

Cassava provides primarily carbohydrates with limited protein, fat, and
micronutrients. In a permaculture system, cassava is never the sole food source
but part of a diverse diet that includes legumes, leafy greens, fruits, and
animal products. The integration of cassava with [[mollison-designers-domestic-animal-integration-design]] provides the
protein complement that cassava alone lacks.

## Harvest and Storage

Cassava roots deteriorate within 48 hours of harvest if left at room
temperature, beginning a process of vascular streaking and cyanide release. For
this reason, cassava is best harvested as needed rather than stored fresh.
Processing into dried flour or fermented products extends storage life to
several months. The [[mollison-designers-cold-caves-root-cellar-storage]] concept applies differently in the tropics, where
below-ground storage may be too warm and humid for root crops.
