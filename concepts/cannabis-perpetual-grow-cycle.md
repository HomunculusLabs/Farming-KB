# Cannabis Perpetual Grow Cycle

Greg Green's "The Cannabis Grow Bible" describes the perpetual grow cycle as a method for maintaining continuous harvests by keeping separate vegetative and flowering zones operating simultaneously within the same grow space. This approach maximises productivity and ensures a regular supply of cured bud.

## The Perpetual Grow Concept

The objective of the perpetual grow is to keep most of the room flowering at all times. This means that while your plants are flowering you will have an equal amount of plants in the [[comparison-cannabis-vegetative-stage-vs-flowering-stage]] and the other half is in its [[query-best-light-schedule-for-cannabis]] vegetative growth, while a second room runs on a strict 12/12 light schedule for flowering. Plants are moved from the vegetative room to the flowering room as space becomes available from harvesting mature plants.

This setup allows the grower to have plants at various stages of flowering, providing staggered harvests throughout the year. The vegetative room can also serve as the mother plant and cloning area, ensuring a continuous supply of genetics.

## Integration with Training Methods

The perpetual grow cycle works well with high-yield training methods. Sea of Green (SOG) setups are particularly compatible, since small plants can be flowered quickly and replaced with new clones. Screen of Green (ScrOG) systems can also be run perpetually, though they require more space and longer turnover times between harvests.

Green notes that expert growers have adapted the perpetual concept to rooms as large as 14 ft by 24 ft with multiple HPS lights suspended vertically from the ceiling, with plants in large container buckets interspersed between the hanging lights.

## Timing and Strain Selection

Strain selection is critical for perpetual growing success. Green recommends choosing strains with known, consistent flowering times so that harvest schedules can be reliably predicted. Indica-dominant strains with shorter flowering periods of 7 to 9 weeks are ideal, as they allow faster turnover between cycles. The breeder's stated flowering time provides a baseline, but the grower should also learn to identify maturity through visual cues.

Green advises that with the right strains, a perpetual setup can deliver 4 ounces of dried bud every month from a small SOG or cabinet grow. Larger ScrOG operations can produce 30 to 50 ounces every two months when conditions are optimal.

## Managing the Perpetual Cycle

Key management considerations for a perpetual grow include maintaining separate cloning, vegetative, and flowering areas; [[cannabis-vegetative-stage]], cannabis plants require long daily light exposure to sustain vigorous growth. Two common schedules are used in perpetual systems:

- **18/6 schedule** (18 hours light, 6 hours dark): This is the most widely recommended vegetative schedule. The daily dark period allows plants to undergo essential metabolic processes including root respiration, starch translocation, and pigment regeneration. An 18/6 cycle also reduces electricity costs by 25% compared to continuous lighting and extends ballast and bulb life. Most growers find that vegetative growth rates under 18/6 are comparable to or only slightly slower than 24/0.

- **24/0 schedule** (continuous light): Some growers, including Green in certain contexts, note that continuous lighting can accelerate vegetative growth marginally. This approach is most practical for small cabinet setups where maximizing growth speed in a confined space is a priority. However, some indica-dominant strains may exhibit stress responses to continuous light, including leaf curling or chlorosis. Cannabis is a C3 plant and does not require a dark period for photosynthesis, but the dark period supports overall plant health.

For perpetual systems with separate rooms, 18/6 is generally preferred because the dark period provides a window for maintenance work (pruning, watering, transplanting) without disrupting the light cycle. Timer reliability is essential; a failed timer that extends the dark period in the vegetative room or shortens it in the flowering room can set the cycle back by weeks.

### Flowering Lighting: 12/12

The flowering zone must maintain a strict 12 hours of light followed by 12 hours of uninterrupted darkness. The dark period is the critical trigger for flowering -- cannabis produces the flowering hormone florigen (or its equivalents, FT and FD proteins) only during sustained dark periods. Any light interruption during the 12-hour dark period, even a brief flash from a door opening or a faulty timer, can delay flowering, reduce yield, or induce hermaphroditism.

In a perpetual system, the 12/12 flowering zone runs continuously, with individual plants entering and exiting at different times based on their age and development. The lighting system in the flowering room should be on its own dedicated timer and circuit, isolated from the vegetative room's electrical system to prevent any possibility of accidental interference.

Some advanced growers use a transition period when moving plants from [[query-how-to-prevent-transplant-shock-when-moving-cannabis-plants]] and ease the hormonal shift, though most commercial growers switch directly to 12/12 for simplicity and space management. The flowering room's light source (typically HPS or full-spectrum LED) should be optimized for the flowering spectrum, with higher red and far-red wavelengths to promote bud development.

## Managing Multiple Growth Stages

Running plants at different stages simultaneously is the defining logistical challenge of perpetual growing. Each stage -- cloning, vegetative growth, early flowering, mid-flowering, and late flowering/harvest -- has different environmental requirements, nutrient needs, and handling protocols. Managing these overlapping demands requires careful organization and planning.

### Zoning and Environmental Separation

The most effective approach is complete physical separation of growth stages into distinct chambers or rooms. Each zone should have independent control of temperature, humidity, light, and airflow. The cloning zone requires high humidity (80-90%), gentle lighting, and warm temperatures (75-80°F). The vegetative zone needs moderate humidity (50-70%), strong light, and active airflow. The flowering zone requires lower humidity (40-50%) to prevent bud rot, strong light, and excellent ventilation for odor control.

In smaller setups where separate rooms are impractical, light-proof dividers can create zones within a single room. However, this approach requires careful management of shared air and temperature, and light leaks between zones remain a significant risk. Automated environmental controllers can help maintain different setpoints in different zones, but they add cost and complexity.

### Scheduling and Record-Keeping

Accurate record-keeping is essential for coordinating a perpetual harvest. Each plant or batch should be tracked with its clone date, transplant date, flowering start date, expected harvest date, and strain information. Growers commonly use spreadsheets, wall calendars, or dedicated grow-management software to maintain this information. A typical perpetual schedule with 8-week flowering strains might look like this:

- **Week 1**: Harvest mature plants, move early-flowering plants to harvest positions, move vegetative plants into early-flowering positions, move rooted clones into vegetative positions, start new clones.
- **Week 2-8**: Monitor all zones, perform maintenance, and repeat the rotation each time a batch reaches maturity.

With this staggered approach, a portion of the flowering room is harvested every 1-2 weeks, depending on how many plants are in the system and how the rotation is structured. A common configuration is four batches of plants in the flowering room, staggered two weeks apart, ensuring that one batch reaches maturity every two weeks.
