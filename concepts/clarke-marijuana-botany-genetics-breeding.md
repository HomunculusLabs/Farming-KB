## True-Breeding Strains

When cross-pollination of two plants sharing a genetic trait results in
offspring that all exhibit the same trait, and all subsequent inbred
generations also exhibit it, the strain is said to be "true-breeding"
for that trait. Both gametes forming the offspring must carry an
identical complement of the genes influencing that trait. A strain may
breed true for one or more traits while varying in other
characteristics. For example, sweet aroma and early maturation may
breed true, while offspring vary in size and shape.

## Dominance and Recessiveness

When a cross produces offspring that do not breed true, the parents
have genes that segregate or are hybrid. Clarke illustrates this with
the example of webbed versus compound-pinnate leaf shape. When a
true-breeding webbed-leaf parent (ww) is crossed with a true-breeding
compound-pinnate parent (WW), 100% of the F1 offspring show
compound-pinnate leaves. The W gene masks the expression of w, making
compound-pinnate dominant and webbed recessive.

the heterozygous hybrid (Ww or wW) shows the dominant phenotype. When
