# Hamilton: Container and Greenhouse Growing

**Source:** Geoff Hamilton, *Organic Gardening* (Nick Hamilton edition)

**Updated:** 2026-04-21

Growing plants in containers is essential for the organic gardener who cannot guarantee that commercially raised plants have been cultivated without chemical sprays or synthetic fertilizers. Containers also extend the range of plants that can be grown—acid-loving plants on alkaline soils, tender plants in cold climates, and fruits and vegetables where ground space is limited.

[[geoff-hamilton-organic-techniques]] frequently demonstrated container growing on his television programmes, showing how even a small patio or balcony could be highly productive using organic methods.

## Pots and Containers

Wood and clay are traditional materials, but plastic and styrofoam containers are cheaper and in some respects give improved results. Any shallow container with adequate drainage holes can be used—from commercial seed trays to styrofoam meat trays, margarine tubs, yogurt cups, and cut-off plastic bottles. All must be thoroughly cleaned before use.

### Clay Pots

Two advantages over plastic: they are porous, allowing free air flow around the root ball and reducing overwatering risk; and they are heavier, important for tall, top-heavy plants. Disadvantages: porous nature harbors disease, difficult to clean and sterilize, more expensive, and compost dries out faster requiring more frequent watering. Best for alpines and plants needing excellent drainage.

### Plastic Pots

Require less frequent watering. Best for most plants when used with peat-free compost, which dries quicker than soil-based mixes.

### Seed Trays

Shallow trays 2-3in (5-7cm) deep, either 15 x 9in or 7 x 4in. Traditional wood trays are porous and harbor disease; plastic trays sterilize easily with boiling water and are cheaper.

### Module Trays

Divided into individual cells for sowing. Plastic versions are light and cheap; styrofoam has excellent heat retention. Roots are never disturbed during transplanting, eliminating growth check. Particularly suitable for [[hamilton-ornamental-trees]] techniques.

### Peat-Free Blocks

Compressed coir discs that reconstitute in water to fill propagation pots. Stand pots in a seed tray for stability. Sow directly and transplant before too much root shows.

## Growing in the Greenhouse

A greenhouse allows vegetables to be sown in midwinter and planted out under cloches in early spring, providing the first crops by late spring. It is easier to control the environment for optimal seed germination.

The [[hamilton-greenhouse-management-organic]] is the engine room of the organic garden, enabling the gardener to start crops early, extend the season, and raise plants from seed that would otherwise need to be bought as transplants.

### Sowing Under Glass

Fill trays with moist seed compost, firm lightly (peat-free requires minimal firming), and level the surface. Moisten compost thoroughly before sowing by working water in with hands. Sow seeds as directed—very small seeds (begonias, lobelias) need no covering; others are covered with their own depth of vermiculite. Cover trays with opaque plastic and place in warmth.

Check daily. As soon as the first seedling emerges, move the container to a light place—but never direct sunlight, which scorches seedlings. On a windowsill, make a "light box" from a cardboard box lined with kitchen foil to reflect light from all sides.

### Hardening Off

Seedlings grown in the greenhouse must be acclimatized to outside conditions before planting out. Place them first in a closed, unheated cold frame. Gradually open the frame during the day, then at night, until it is completely open.

A [[cold-frame-construction]] is one of the most useful structures in the organic garden. It bridges the gap between the warm greenhouse and the exposed garden, allowing plants to adjust gradually to lower temperatures and greater air movement.

### Citrus in the Greenhouse

In temperate climates, grow citrus in tubs with a minimum diameter of 18in (45cm). Use soil-based potting compost. Maintain minimum temperature of 45F (7C). Water well during the growing season; allow plants to dry between waterings in winter. Spray foliage with water every morning in summer (stop during flowering). Feed weekly with liquid kelp fertilizer. Prune only to remove dead, diseased, or crossing wood and fruited shoots after harvesting.

### Peaches and Nectarines Under Glass

Grown against a wall or post-and-wire support in the same way as outdoor fan-trained trees. Keep the greenhouse as cold as possible in winter for dormancy. Mulch and water well in spring; allow temperature to reach 50F (10C) before ventilating. Hand pollinate with a camel hair brush. Feed weekly with liquid kelp. Limit fruit to about 15 per square yard/meter. Spray daily with clear water (except at flowering).

### Tomatoes and Cucumbers

Tomatoes and cucumbers are among the most popular greenhouse crops. [[growing-tomatoes-organically]] thrive in grow bags or large pots, requiring regular feeding with liquid organic fertilizer once the first truss has set. Cucumbers need high humidity and consistent temperatures. Both benefit from careful ventilation management to prevent fungal diseases.

## Growing in Tubs Outdoors

Fruit trees can be grown in tubs on the patio, making productive use of small spaces. Use dwarfing rootstocks (M27 for apples). Container-grown citrus, olives, and other tender fruits can be moved indoors or to a greenhouse for winter protection.

### Key Principles

- Ensure adequate drainage holes in all containers.

- Use appropriate compost: soil-based for long-term plantings, peat-free for seasonal crops.

- Watering is the most critical aspect—lack of water is the main cause of failure. Container-grown plants need more frequent watering than those in the ground.

- Feed regularly: container plants cannot access nutrients from surrounding soil. Use [[hamilton-organic-fertilizers-and-plant-nutrition]] every one to two weeks during the growing season.

- Raise heavy-soil beds at least 18in (45cm) when growing citrus or other drainage-sensitive plants in the ground.

### Compost for Containers

Use a blend of loam-based compost with added [[mollison-designers-humus-building-and-soil-organic-matter]] such as well-rotted manure or leaf mould for long-term plantings. For seasonal bedding and vegetables, peat-free multipurpose compost enriched with a handful of [[mollison-aquaculture-harvest-and-fish-nutrition]] per container provides a good start.

## Protecting Plants

### Cloches

Place cloches in position two weeks before sowing to warm the soil for early outdoor sowings. Remove once seedlings are established and weather permits.

### Cold Frames

Essential for hardening off greenhouse-grown plants. A wood, metal, or brick frame with a glass top, sited close to the greenhouse.

### Shading

Use old onion nets or greenhouse shade netting for adjustable shading of cuttings and seedlings. Double shading on very sunny days, single on fair days, none on dull days. Too much sun causes wilting; too little prevents feeding through leaves.
