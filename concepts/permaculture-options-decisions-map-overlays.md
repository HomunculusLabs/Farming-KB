### Strengths of Map Overlays

Like the system of options, map overlays lead to certain inevitable ground placements. A good site map makes any landscape design much easier and far more visual. A good map indicates sensible options and hypotheses — dam sites, soil/crop suitability — which can later be checked against actual site conditions, available clay for dams, existing useful vegetation, and threatened habitat.

### Weaknesses of Map Overlays

The methodology may lead to uneasy compromises not necessarily inherent in other design methods. The danger is that map overlays omit minutiae and can never reveal evolutionary processes. Some factors cannot be mapped at all — ethical, financial, and cultural constraints. It is very difficult to include site-relevant details revealed by observation, or indicated by the analytic method of component inputs and outputs.

### The Analytical Trap

The purely analytic and overlay approach can lead to rigid systems where the very remoteness makes flexibility difficult, occasioning unforeseen work and expense not incurred by the more empirical and flexible observation and option systems. The latter both allow flexible response to fresh conditions.

## Integrating Design Methods

No single design method is sufficient. The most robust designs combine:
- **Analytical methods** for component relationships and energy flows
- **Observation** for site-specific processes and dynamic interactions
- **Options analysis** for economic, legal, and cultural constraints
- **Map overlays** for spatial visualization and hypothesis generation
