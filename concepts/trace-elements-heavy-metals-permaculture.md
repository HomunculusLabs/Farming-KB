# Trace Elements and Heavy Metals in Permaculture

Trace elements occupy a critical and often misunderstood niche in [[permaculture-design]]. Many are essential micronutrients at low concentrations yet become toxic poisons at slightly elevated levels. Understanding their behaviour in soils, water, and biological systems is fundamental to responsible land stewardship and human health. Bill Mollison emphasised that trace element management is not optional—it is central to creating productive, resilient systems. The window between deficiency and toxicity is often alarmingly narrow, making informed design decisions essential.

This page covers all seventeen trace elements and heavy metals discussed in the Permaculture Designer's Manual, organised into essential micronutrients, toxic contaminants, and radioactive gases.

## Essential Trace Elements

These elements are required by plants, animals, or humans in small amounts. Deficiency produces identifiable symptoms, but excess quickly becomes toxic. Permaculture designers must learn to recognise both states.

### Chromium (Cr)

A paradoxical element: poison to plants and animals at elevated levels, yet a trace element related to glucose tolerance in humans—insulin dependence may result from deficiency. Chromium occurs naturally in serpentine rock and is used in timber preservation. Guard against contamination from electrolytic and leather works, as chromium poisons the biological agents that treat sewage.

Some chromium is present in all organic matter, but firm knowledge of its metabolic function remains limited. It is relatively easy to remove at source before it enters broader ecosystems, making source control the preferred permaculture strategy.

### Manganese (Mn)

Readily available in acid soils except sandy types, where it may cause poisoning below pH 6.5. On alkaline soils (pH >7.5), grain crops and vegetables often show deficiency. Deficiency is typical on poor sands heavily dressed with lime.

Remedies include seed soakage, pelleting, or foliar sprays. Flooding mobilises manganese, and bacteria can fix insoluble manganese, creating problems in pipes and concrete water raceways at just 2–3 ppm. Aluminium sulphate from acid rain may mobilise manganese, mercury, and cadmium to lethal levels. Manganese leaches from acid soils and deposits in alkaline horizons as manganese-iron concretions, or on sea floors as larger nodules.

### Iron (Fe)

Normally plentiful in soils and critical for both plants and animals. Deficiency appears in alkaline drylands or heavily limed gardens, causing interveinal leaf yellowing in plants and anaemia (low blood iron) in people. Remedies include sulphur, iron chelates, or small amounts of ferric sulphate.

Ferrous iron spicules in soil assist ethylene formation, while ferric iron oxides remain "unavailable" to plants. Iron concretions and staining in sands are associated with microbial action along plant root traces. Iron-deficiency anaemia is common in desert populations and after blood losses, and deficiencies must be monitored in women of reproductive age.

Dietary sources include veal, liver, fish, soya beans, lettuce, parsley, and maize—meat and vitamin C enhance absorption. Excessive iron can result from brewing or cooking in iron pots; alcoholism and diabetes exacerbate excess.

### Cobalt (Co)

Deficient on poor coastal sands, often alongside copper. Essential for many legumes and livestock—deficient animals become "unthrifty." Cobalt is necessary for vitamin B12 synthesis in ruminants; only an ounce or two per acre is needed, traditionally supplied as a cobalt "bullet" placed in the rumen of sheep.

Deficiency causes anaemia and is often diagnosed via B12 blood tests. Excess cobalt is toxic, and radioactive cobalt is a serious poison requiring strict containment.

### Copper (Cu)

A necessary micronutrient that becomes a poison at higher concentrations. In plants, copper acts as an enzyme activator concentrated in chloroplasts. Deficiency occurs on shell-grit dunes, acid peaty soils, coastal heaths over poor quartzite sands, and deeply weathered basalts.

Black sheep are excellent indicator animals—they develop whitish or brown "steely wool" when deficient. Copper sulphate at 1 kg/ha every five years is the standard cure; old stockmen placed crystals directly in waterholes. Copper deficiency combined with iodine deficiency can lead to goitre and anaemia.

Levels above 12 micromoles/L are excessive in drinking water. High blood copper in desert populations often signals zinc deficiency. Copper toxicity causes liver cirrhosis, tremors, and personality changes—now alleviated by chelating agents. Common contamination sources include copper sulphate used for algae control and copper pipes or utensils.

### Zinc (Zn)

Deficient on leached sands, dunes, and alkaline sands—test for it in all desert gardens. Supplied by zinc sulphate spray or zinc salts in seed pellets, it is cheap and critical for tree establishment in arid zones. Zinc is essential to growth control hormones in plants.

In humans, deficiency causes metabolic imbalances, stunting, poor wound healing, and is linked to diabetes. Severe deficiency produces hair loss, moist eczema, impotence, apathy, and diarrhoea. Alcoholism, diabetes, feverish sweating, and stress all lower zinc levels. Coarse grains, unleavened bread, and low meat intake contribute to deficiency, especially on high-calcium soils. Prostate fluids are high in zinc, and zinc is necessary to several key enzymes.

### Selenium (Se)

Deficiency in lambs causes "white muscle" or "still lamb" disease, cured by selenium combined with vitamin E injection. Selenium deficiency causes "thrift" problems in animals across many countries. Just 5 mg/year is sufficient for a sheep—more causes toxicity.

Seaweeds may supply sufficient selenium to gardens through mulch or compost applications, making seaweed a valuable permaculture input for animal systems.

## Toxic Heavy Metals and Contaminants

These elements are primarily hazards in permaculture systems. They enter through industrial pollution, agricultural chemicals, or building materials, and must be managed through source control, soil buffering, and careful site selection.

### Arsenic (As)

Sometimes needed in trace amounts by horses (included in chicken pellets), but poisonous at only slightly elevated concentrations. Primary sources include gold processing wastes and chicken pellet foods. Permaculture systems should avoid introducing arsenic through imported feeds or contaminated materials.

### Nickel (Ni)

Not noted as a significant plant nutrient or poison on its own, but nickel combined with copper can intensify poisoning in people—relevant near mining areas or from office urns. Nickel also causes skin reactions (rashes) from jewellery and fixtures, indicating sensitivity even at low exposure levels.

### Strontium (Sr)

Radioactive Sr-90 is becoming a widespread danger from nuclear plants, polluting many areas and concentrating in milk at 4–8 times environmental levels. It causes cancer at absorption sites and leukaemia in children. There are no safe levels—milk must be monitored after atomic fallout. Ordinary non-radioactive strontium is a trace element without these hazards, but contamination from nuclear sources demands vigilance.

### Cadmium (Cd)

A dangerous poison concentrated by green leafy plants and shellfish. Sources include vehicle tyres and superphosphate fertilisers. High cadmium levels accumulate in acid soils of market gardens using artificial fertilisers. It causes itai-itai disease (Japan) and permanent deformations. Permaculture designers should avoid superphosphate use entirely and test soils near roadways before establishing food systems.

### Tin (Sn)

Ores and industrial wastes create plant establishment problems. Not toxic at low levels but can become toxic at high concentrations, particularly from canned foods. Permaculture systems favour fresh, local food over heavily processed alternatives to minimise tin exposure.

### Antimony (Sb)

Behaves similarly to arsenic—poisonous at elevated concentrations. Sources include industrial processes and ores. Permaculture sites near historical mining or smelting operations should be tested for antimony contamination.

### Mercury (Hg)

A common poison from mines, metal processing, and acid rain. Mercury is more active in organically polluted areas. It creates coordination and sanity problems, central nervous system dysfunction, bone deformity, and insanity. Permaculture systems should be designed to avoid mercury introduction entirely and to remediate contaminated sites through biological buffering and appropriate land use zoning.

### Thallium (Tl)

A toxin causing birth deformities. Sources include industrial waste and ore processing. Any site with suspected thallium contamination requires careful assessment before food production. Permaculture designers should research site history for industrial use.

### Lead (Pb)

A serious and widespread urban soil pollutant from petrol, old paints, and battery burning. Lead-concentrating vegetation should be removed, and food-growing areas need heavy organic soils to block plant uptake. Earthworms may concentrate lead to lethal levels, so contaminated sites require management strategies that account for soil biota behaviour. Urban permaculture sites demand lead testing before food production.

## Radioactive Gases

These elements straddle the line between essential nutrients and radiological hazards. Iodine is required by the thyroid, but its radioactive isotope is deadly. Radon is a gas with no biological role but significant health impacts in built environments.

### Iodine (I)

Deficiencies occur on weathered basalts, causing growth problems and goitre—remedied by fish, shellfish, and seaweed in the diet. Radioactive I-131 from nuclear plants and tests poisons milk and affects the thyroid, potentially causing cancer and death in children from hyperthyroidosis. Permaculture design must consider iodine both as an essential nutrient and as a radiological hazard requiring monitoring near nuclear facilities.

### Radon (Rn)

A gas from uranium decay that seeps upward through most soils, especially over volcanic areas and igneous rock. Radon pollutes super-insulated buildings if air is not exchanged—millions of homes in the UK and USA have dangerously high levels.

Avoid poorly ventilated homes in areas of radioactive ores, granites, and basalts. If super-insulating buildings, fit heat exchangers to ensure clean air exchange while maintaining energy efficiency.

## Permaculture Design Principles for Trace Elements

- **Test before acting:** Soil testing for trace elements is essential, especially on sands, alkaline soils, and near industrial or urban areas. Never assume adequacy without analysis.
- **Source control:** Prevent contamination at source—avoid superphosphates, monitor water quality, and reject contaminated materials before they enter your system.
- **Biological buffering:** Heavy organic matter in soils blocks uptake of many heavy metals including lead and cadmium. Build soil organic matter as a primary defence strategy.
- **Indicator species:** Use livestock and plants as living indicators—black sheep for copper deficiency, "unthrifty" animals for cobalt, interveinal yellowing for iron or manganese imbalance.
- **Dietary diversity:** A varied diet including seaweeds, fish, organ meats, and fresh vegetables provides most essential trace elements naturally, reducing the need for supplementation.
- **Narrow windows:** The margin between deficiency and toxicity is slim for many elements—apply supplements carefully and monitor responses in plants, animals, and people.
- **Remediation:** Many toxic elements can be immobilised through pH management, organic matter incorporation, and appropriate plant selection for phytostabilisation.
- **Site history:** Always research industrial, mining, and agricultural history of a site before establishing food production systems. Prevention is far easier than remediation.
