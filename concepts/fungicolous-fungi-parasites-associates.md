# Fungicolous Fungi — Parasites and Associates of Other Fungi
Fungicolous fungi are those that grow on, within, or in association with
other fungi. This diverse group includes biotrophic parasites, necrotrophic
pathogens, and commensals that exploit fungal sporocarps, hyphae, spores,
and sclerotia as substrata.

## Types of Fungicolous Relationships

### Biotrophic Mycoparasites
Obtain nutrients from living host fungi through specialized structures
(haustoria, contact cells) without killing them immediately. Often highly
host-specific.

### Necrotrophic Parasites
Kill host tissue and then exploit the dead fungal biomass. Generally less
host-specific than biotrophs.

### Mycosaprotrophs
Colonize dead or moribund fungal structures. A large portion of
Hypocreales fungi are mycoparasitic or mycosaprotrophic — a fact often
unrecognized.

## Taxonomic Groups Rich in Fungicolous Fungi

### Zygomycota
- **Piptocephalidaceae and Dimargaritaceae:** Biotrophic mycoparasites
  on Mucorales. Dispirales cannot utilize glucose; require glycerol,
  organic nitrogen, and vitamins.
- **Spinellus, Syzygites, Dicranophora:** Parasitize agarics. Spinellus
  requires organic nitrogen and temperatures near or below 20C.
- **Parasitella parricida:** Weak parasite of various Mucoraceae, using
  capturing hyphae.
- **Mortierella:** Highly chitinolytic; often appears indiscriminately on
  old fungal sporocarps. M. armillariicola known only from Armillaria.

### Ascomycota

#### Laboulbeniales — Pyxidiophoraceae
Heteroxenous biotrophic mycoparasites that can change hosts during life
cycles. Ascospores translocated by mites; the fungus can penetrate its
vector with haustoria.

#### Hypocreales
A large portion is mycoparasitic — extremely versatile in exploiting
fungal substrata:
- **Trichoderma:** Polyphagous mycoparasites; includes biocontrol agents
  (T. harzianum) that can hardly be distinguished microscopically from
  aggressive competitors of [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]]
- **Hypomyces:** Large genus of fungicolous species; H. lactifluorum
  transforms Russula and Lactarius sporocarps into distinctive orange
  lobster mushrooms; H. chrysospermus causes white mold on Boletus

## Mechanisms of Mycoparasitism

### Antibiosis
Many fungicolous fungi produce antifungal metabolites that kill or
inhibit the host fungus. Trichoderma species secrete peptaibols and
[[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] that disrupt host cell membranes. Gliocladium
roseum produces gliotoxin, a potent antifungal compound. These chemical
weapons allow the parasite to gain advantage before direct physical
contact with the host.

### Enzymatic Degradation
Chitinases and glucanases are primary weapons used by mycoparasitic fungi
to breach host cell walls. Trichoderma harzianum produces a complex
mixture of cell wall-degrading enzymes that are induced upon contact
with the host. The efficiency of enzymatic degradation determines how
quickly the parasite can colonize and consume the host tissue.

### Hyphal Coiling and Penetration
Direct physical interaction occurs through hyphal coiling around host
hyphae, followed by penetration via appressorium-like structures. This
contact triggers the production of lytic enzymes precisely at the
interaction site. Some biotrophic mycoparasites form haustoria within
host cells to extract nutrients while keeping the host alive.

## Ecological Significance
Fungicolous fungi play important regulatory roles in [[biodiversity-of-fungi-soil-fungal-communities-agriculture]],
preventing any single species from dominating. In forest ecosystems,
fungicolous fungi influencing ectomycorrhizal populations can indirectly
affect tree health and [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]]. In agricultural settings,
fungicolous interactions determine the outcome of biocontrol applications
and soilborne disease dynamics. The balance between mycoparasitic fungi
and their hosts contributes to decomposer community stability.

## Fungicolous Fungi in Mushroom Cultivation
Fungicolous fungi are among the most problematic contaminants in
commercial mushroom cultivation. Trichoderma species cause green mold
disease, one of the most widespread problems in [[growing-gourmet-button-mushroom-agaricus-cultivation]] and
specialty mushroom production. Verticillium species cause dry bubble
disease of [[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]]. Hypomyces species can ruin wild-harvested
mushroom crops. Understanding fungicolous interactions is essential for
effective contamination management strategies.

## Diversity and Conservation
The true diversity of fungicolous fungi remains poorly documented.
Many species are microscopic and easily overlooked during surveys.
They are often collected incidentally when host sporocarps are examined
closely. Tropical regions likely harbor vast undescribed fungicolous
species. Habitat destruction threatens not only host fungi but also the
specialized species that depend on them. Conservation of fungal
diversity must account for these intricate host-parasite relationships.
