# Cannabis Controlled Pollination and Seed Production

Controlled pollination is the foundation of serious Cannabis breeding, allowing the breeder to determine the genotype of future offspring rather than relying on random wind-borne pollinations.

## Biology of Pollination

Pollination begins with dehiscence (release of pollen) from staminate flowers. Millions of pollen grains float through the air on light breezes. If the pistil is ripe, the pollen grain germinates and sends out a long pollen tube containing a haploid generative nucleus. The pollen tube grows downward toward the ovule at the base of the pistils. Germination occurs 15 to 20 minutes after contact with the stigmatic surface; fertilization may take up to two days in cooler temperatures.

Pollen grain characteristics:
- Nearly spherical, slightly yellow
- 25 to 30 microns in diameter
- Smooth surface with 2 to 4 germ pores

## Controlled vs. Random Pollination

Seeds from imported shipments result from totally random pollinations among many genotypes. If only a few seeds appear in nearly sinsemilla Cannabis, they likely resulted from a late-flowering staminate plant or hermaphrodite, adversely affecting the offspring genotype. Controlled pollination techniques allow the breeder to determine the genotype of future offspring.

## Data Collection

Keeping accurate notes and records is key to successful breeding. A record book contains a numbered page for each plant, and each separate cross is tagged on the pistillate parent and recorded as "seed of pistillate parent X pollen or staminate parent." The date of pollination is included, with room for the date of seed harvest. Samples of parental plants are saved as voucher specimens.

## Pollen Collection Methods

### Direct Collection
A small vial, glass plate, or mirror is held beneath a recently-opened staminate flower releasing pollen, and pollen is dislodged by tapping the anthers. Early morning is the best time.

### Drying Collection
Whole limbs or clusters of staminate flowers are placed on paper or glass in a cool, still place. Pollen drops from anthers as they dry and may be scraped up. Pollen may be stored in a cool, dark, dry spot in a sealed container with desiccant (dry soda crackers or rice) in the refrigerator.

### Enclosure Collection
Paper or Gore-Tex bags are placed over selected limb tips before pollen release. The bags collect pollen as it sheds. Bags are collected early in the morning before solar heating. After drying, bags are shaken vigorously and pollen is screened into storage vials.

## Pollen Parent Selection

1. Healthy individuals with well-developed flower clusters are chosen.
2. As the first flowers begin to swell, they are removed and destroyed prior to pollen release.
3. When the plant enters full floral condition, the 20 most-flowered limb tips are selected.
4. All remaining flowering clusters are removed to prevent stray pollinations.
5. Large leaves are left on the remainder but removed at limb tips to minimize condensation.
6. Pollination enclosures are secured and the plant is checked for any shoots outside the enclosure.

## Seed Parent Pollination

1. A pistillate flower cluster is ripe for fertilization while pale, slender pistils emerge from the calyxes.
2. Healthy, well-flowered lower limbs on the shaded side of the plant are selected.
3. Two terminal clusters are chosen for each pollen bag.
4. A warm, windless evening is chosen for pollination.
5. Most shade leaves are removed from the limb tips.
6. The pollen bag is slipped over two inverted limb tips, tied securely, and shaken vigorously.
7. If only small quantities of pollen are available, it may be diluted 1:10 to 1:100 with flour or powdered fungicide.

## Post-Pollination Care

- Bags remain on the seed parent for three full sunny days (up to a week in cool/overcast conditions).
- After removal, calyxes begin to swell with seed, indicating successful fertilization.
- Seed parents need good irrigation for proper seed development.
- Seeds develop fastest in warm weather (2-4 weeks); in cold weather, up to two months.
- Seeds are removed when the calyx begins to dry and the dark shiny perianth is visible.
- Seeds are labeled and stored in a cool, dark, dry place.

## Pollination Enclosure Materials

- Paper and tightly woven cloth are most suitable
- Coarse cloth allows pollen to escape
- Plastic materials tend to collect transpired water and rot flowers
- Light-colored opaque or translucent reflective materials remain cooler
- Gore-Tex works with great success
- Long narrow bags (quart-bottle bags, giant popcorn bags) provide convenient shapes

## Sinsemilla Seed Production Strategy

The careful propagator produces seeds of pure types without pollinating the precious crop. Selected staminate parents are reproductively isolated while pollen is carefully collected and applied to only selected flowers of the pistillate parents. Early fertilization of a few basal flowers fixes the sex of the pistillate plant, helping prevent hermaphroditism. The remaining floral clusters are sinsemilla and continue producing THC-laden resins.

## Merged: Cannabis Pre-Flowering Sex Identification

Updated: 2026-04-18

Identifying the sex of cannabis plants during the pre-flowering stage is essential for removing males before they can pollinate females. The calyx is the ultimate pre-flowering indicator — learning to identify it is one of the most important skills for cannabis growers.

## When Sex Becomes Visible

Sex typically becomes identifiable 3-6 weeks after germination, depending on the strain and growing conditions. Photoperiod-triggered plants show sex after the light cycle is reduced to 12/12. Some indica-dominant strains show pre-flowers as early as 3 weeks, while sativa-dominant strains may take 6 weeks or longer. Autoflowering varieties typically show sex around week 3-4 regardless of light cycle.

## Female Identification (Desired)

Females produce the smokable, resinous buds. Look for:

1. **Two white pistils (hairs)** emerging from the stem nodes — these are the first visible sign of female flowers

2. **Calyx development** — small teardrop-shaped pods at the nodes where the pistils emerge

3. **White hairs (stigmas)** — initially white, turning orange/brown/red as the plant matures

The calyx is the key pre-flowering indicator. It is a small, V-shaped or teardrop-shaped structure at the branch junctions.

## Male Identification (Remove Immediately)

Males produce pollen sacs that will fertilize females, reducing bud quality. Look for:

1. **Small round balls** at the stem nodes — these are pollen sacs

2. **No pistils/hairs** — males do not produce the white hair pistils

3. **Clusters of balls** — as males mature, the pollen sacs appear in clusters

## The Calyx Test

If you see a calyx (teardrop shape) with two white pistils emerging from it, the plant is female. If you see round balls without pistils, it is male. This distinction can be subtle in early pre-flowering — use magnification if needed.

## Practical Tips for Sex Identification

Use a jeweler's loupe (30x) or hand magnifier for early identification. Check the nodes at the 3rd-5th branch junction from the bottom — these typically show sex first. Examine plants daily once they reach 3-4 weeks of age. Mark suspected males with a twist tie for identification before removing them. Some experienced growers can identify sex by examining leaf shape and growth patterns, but this is unreliable compared to visual inspection of pre-flowers.

## Action Required

- Remove and destroy male plants immediately upon identification

- Do not compost males near your grow area — pollen can survive

- A single male can pollinate an entire crop, causing seeded buds

## Hermaphrodites

Some plants may show both male and female parts. These should also be removed as they can self-pollinate. Environmental stress (light leaks, temperature extremes, nutrient stress) can trigger hermaphroditism in otherwise female plants. Some strains are genetically predisposed to hermaphroditism and should be avoided for sinsemilla production. If only a few pollen sacs appear on a mostly female plant, some growers carefully remove the sacs with tweezers and monitor closely.

## Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is confusing young female calyxes with male pollen sacs — both appear as small bumps at nodes in early stages. Patience is key: wait until distinguishing features (pistils vs. round balls) are clearly visible before acting. Another error is checking too early — plants under 3 weeks rarely show reliable sex indicators. Removing plants prematurely wastes genetics, while waiting too long risks pollination.

## Equipment Recommendations

A 30x loupe ($5-15) is the minimum investment for reliable sex identification. A digital pocket microscope ($20-40) provides clearer images and is useful for many other aspects of growing. Good lighting is essential — use a bright LED flashlight or headlamp during inspection.
