# Fruiting Chamber Design

A fruiting chamber is an enclosed space designed to provide the environmental
conditions that trigger and support mushroom fruiting. The two most critical
parameters are high humidity (typically 85 to 95 percent relative humidity) and
adequate fresh air exchange (FAE). Additional factors include temperature, light,
and CO2 concentration. The design of the fruiting chamber directly impacts yield,
quality, and consistency of the harvest.

## The Role of Humidity

Mushrooms are composed of roughly 90 percent water, and they lose moisture
rapidly to dry air. If the humidity in the fruiting chamber drops below 80
percent, the mushrooms will dry out, crack, and abort. The cap surface will
become rough and leathery instead of smooth and glossy. Maintaining high
humidity ensures that the mushrooms can grow to their full size and develop
proper texture and appearance.

Humidity is typically maintained through evaporation from the substrate itself
and from standing water or saturated materials in the chamber. Perlite, a
volcanic mineral that holds water in its porous structure, is a common humidity
medium. A layer of wet perlite several inches deep in the bottom of the chamber
provides a large evaporative surface area. Ultrasonic humidifiers can also be
used, either inside the chamber or plumbed in from outside, though they require
careful management to avoid over-saturation.

## Fresh Air Exchange

Fresh air exchange serves two purposes: it replenishes oxygen and it removes
carbon dioxide. As mycelium and growing mushrooms respire, they produce CO2. In
a sealed environment, CO2 levels rise quickly, which inhibits fruiting and
causes abnormal mushroom development. High CO2 levels produce long, stringy
stems and small, underdeveloped caps, a condition known as "leggy" growth.

Proper FAE means replacing the air in the chamber several times per hour. In
small-scale setups, this is often achieved passively through holes in the
chamber fitted with polyfill filters, which allow air movement while filtering
out contaminant spores. Active FAE uses a small fan to circulate air, with an
intake port and an exhaust port to create directional airflow.

The balance between humidity and FAE is the central challenge of fruiting
chamber design. More air exchange brings in drier ambient air, which lowers
humidity. More humidity retention limits air exchange and raises CO2 levels.
Every fruiting chamber design is, at its core, an attempt to find the optimal
balance between these two competing requirements.

## Monotub Design

The monotub is the most widely used fruiting chamber for small to medium-scale
cultivation. It consists of a large plastic storage tub, typically 50 to 80
quarts, modified with holes for air exchange. The standard monotub has two holes
near the substrate surface on each long side and one hole on each short side
just below the lid. The lower holes serve as intake ports, and the upper holes
serve as exhaust ports. All holes are stuffed with polyfill to filter incoming
air.

Monotubs are popular because they are inexpensive, simple to build, and require
minimal active management. The substrate itself provides most of the humidity
through evaporation, and the polyfill-stuffed holes provide passive FAE. A
monotub can often produce multiple flushes of mushrooms with little more than
occasional misting.

## Shotgun Fruiting Chamber

The shotgun fruiting chamber (SGFC) was popularized as an entry-level design.
It uses a clear plastic tub with numerous small holes drilled on all six sides,
including the bottom and lid. The bottom of the tub is filled with several
inches of wet perlite. Air moves through the holes by natural convection: warm
moist air rises from the perlite and exits through the upper holes, drawing
fresh air in through the lower holes. The SGFC works reasonably well but
requires frequent misting and fanning to maintain conditions.

## Martha Tent (Greenhouse)

A Martha tent is a freestanding greenhouse enclosure made of a plastic cover
over a metal frame, typically 4 to 6 feet tall. It provides a much larger
growing area than a monotub and is suitable for cultivators working with
multiple substrates or trays simultaneously. Humidity is provided by a cool mist
humidifier inside the tent, and a small circulating fan ensures even
distribution. Air exchange is handled by opening the front zipper periodically
or by installing an exhaust fan on a timer.

Martha tents require more equipment and monitoring than monotubs but offer
greater scalability. They are particularly well-suited for species that require
significant vertical space, such as oyster mushrooms growing in shelf-like
formations.

## Environmental Controllers

Advanced cultivators often incorporate environmental controllers that automate
temperature, humidity, and FAE management. These systems use sensors to monitor
conditions and relays to control humidifiers, fans, heaters, and air pumps.
While not necessary for basic cultivation, automation reduces the daily labor
of maintaining the fruiting environment and produces more consistent results,
especially for species with precise environmental requirements.

## Lighting Requirements

Mushrooms do not photosynthesize, but most species require light to trigger and
guide normal fruiting body development. Indirect ambient light or a simple LED
strip on a 12-hour on, 12-hour off cycle is sufficient. Direct sunlight should
be avoided as it can overheat the chamber. The light intensity needed is low;
even a dim room light is adequate for most species. Light quality in the 5000
to 6500 Kelvin range (daylight spectrum) produces the best results.
