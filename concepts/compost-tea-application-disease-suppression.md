# Compost Tea Application and Disease Suppression

Compost tea is a liquid extract produced by steeping high-quality compost in aerated water, concentrating the beneficial microorganisms—bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes—into a form that can be applied broadly across soils and plant surfaces. Proper application technique is just as critical as brewing quality tea; even the best compost tea will fail to deliver results if applied incorrectly. This page covers the full spectrum of application methods, timing considerations, equipment requirements, volume guidelines, and the disease-suppression and weed-management benefits that result from consistent compost tea use.

## Application Methods

There are two primary methods for applying compost tea: **soil drench** and **foliar spray**. Each targets different parts of the plant system and can be used together for comprehensive coverage.

### Soil Drench

A soil drench involves pouring or spraying compost tea directly onto the soil surface, where it percolates into the root zone. This method introduces beneficial microbes into the rhizosphere—the thin layer of soil immediately surrounding plant roots—where they can form symbiotic relationships with the plant, cycle nutrients, and outcompete pathogenic organisms. Soil drenches are particularly effective for establishing and maintaining a healthy soil food web, improving soil structure, and addressing root-zone diseases.

### Foliar Spray

Foliar application involves spraying compost tea directly onto the above-ground portions of plants—leaves, stems, and in some cases flowers or fruit. For foliar sprays to be effective, the tea must thoroughly coat the leaf surface. The target coverage is at least **70% of the leaf surface area**, and critically, **both the upper and lower sides** of each leaf must be treated. The undersides of leaves are especially important because stomata (the pores through which microbes can enter plant tissue) are predominantly located on the abaxial (lower) surface, and many foliar pathogens also colonize from below.

## Timing of Application

Timing is one of the most overlooked yet critical factors in compost tea application. The living organisms in the tea are vulnerable to environmental extremes, and applying at the wrong time can render an entire batch ineffective.

### Daily Time Windows

In southern latitudes where ultraviolet (UV) radiation is intense, compost tea should be applied **before 10:00 AM** or **after 3:00 PM**. UV light from direct sunlight rapidly kills the exposed microorganisms in the tea, destroying the beneficial populations before they have a chance to colonize. If midday application is unavoidable, the tea must be delivered in droplets with a **diameter of at least 1 millimeter**—larger droplets provide enough volume to shield the microbes inside from UV damage during descent to the leaf surface.

### Seasonal Timing

Two seasonal windows are particularly important for compost tea application:

- **After autumn leaf fall:** Applying compost tea after leaves have dropped ensures that beneficial microbes are present in the soil to decompose organic matter efficiently over winter and protect dormant roots and crowns from winter pathogens.
- **Before new spring growth:** An early spring application establishes microbial populations in the rhizosphere before plants begin their active growth phase, ensuring that nutrient cycling and disease protection are in place from the outset.

## Sprayer Equipment and Technique

The equipment used to apply compost tea must be chosen and configured carefully to avoid damaging the living organisms in the tea.

### Pressure Limits

Sprayer pressure **must not exceed 70 pounds per square inch (psi)**. Higher pressures create shear forces that physically destroy fungal hyphae, tear apart bacterial colonies, and kill protozoa and nematodes. The spray velocity should be slow and gentle—tea should essentially "parachute" down onto the plant surface rather than being blasted onto it. Concrete sprayers (the type used for applying textured coatings) are recommended because they are designed to operate at low pressure and move large volumes of liquid gently.

### Straining Requirements

Compost tea must be strained before it enters the sprayer to prevent clogging, but the straining mesh must be coarse enough to allow the beneficial organisms to pass through. The minimum mesh size is **400 micrometers**. Finer meshes will trap fungal hyphae, protozoa, and nematodes—the very organisms that provide the greatest disease-suppression benefits. A mesh of 400–800 micrometers is ideal, depending on the specific fungal species present in the tea.

## Application Volume

Standard application volumes for compost tea are:

- **Soil drench only:** 5 gallons per acre
- **Soil drench plus foliar spray:** 10 gallons per acre

These are minimum guidelines; increasing volume will not harm plants and may improve coverage and colonization. There is no upper limit—**you can never apply too much compost tea**. The soil food web will simply utilize whatever beneficial organisms are available, and excess will become part of the soil organic matter pool.

## Application Frequency

The frequency of compost tea applications should be adjusted based on the condition of the soil and the history of chemical inputs. On lawns or soils that have been heavily treated with synthetic chemicals (fertilizers, pesticides, fungicides), the native microbial populations have likely been severely depleted and must be rebuilt systematically:

1. **Every 2 weeks for the first 3 months** — This aggressive schedule rapidly reintroduces microbial diversity and begins the process of soil biological recovery.
2. **Monthly for the next several months** — Once initial populations are established, a monthly schedule maintains and builds diversity.
3. **3 times per year thereafter** — For long-term maintenance, three seasonal applications (spring, midsummer, autumn) are generally sufficient to sustain a healthy soil food web.

Repeated applications are important because each batch of compost tea introduces slightly different microbial species and strains. Over time, this **increases overall microbial diversity** in the soil and on plant surfaces, creating a more resilient and self-regulating ecosystem.

## Disease Suppression

One of the most powerful benefits of compost tea is its ability to suppress plant diseases without synthetic chemicals. The mechanism is primarily one of **competitive exclusion**—beneficial organisms occupy the same ecological niches as pathogens, consume the same food sources, and in many cases actively antagonize or consume pathogenic organisms. The type of tea (fungal-dominant vs. bacterial-dominant) determines which diseases are most effectively suppressed.

### Diseases Suppressed by Fungal-Dominant Tea

Fungal-dominated compost teas are particularly effective against fungal pathogens. The beneficial fungi in these teas compete with pathogenic fungi for space on root surfaces and leaf tissue, and many produce antifungal compounds:

- **Powdery mildew** — A common foliar disease across many crops
- **Downy mildew** — A serious pathogen of grapes, cucurbits, and other crops
- **Take-all** — A root disease of wheat and other cereals
- **Gray snow mold** and **pink snow mold** — Winter turfgrass diseases
- **Red thread** — A turfgrass disease indicating low nitrogen availability
- **Crown rots** and **root rots** — Destructive diseases of many ornamental and crop plants
- **Brown patch** — A major turfgrass disease favored by heat and humidity
- **Summer patch** — A root disease of turfgrass
- **Rusts** — Foliar diseases affecting a wide range of plants
- **Fairy rings** — Turfgrass condition caused by basidiomycete fungi in soil

### Diseases Suppressed by Bacterial-Dominant Tea

Bacterial-dominated compost teas excel at suppressing diseases caused by bacterial pathogens and some fungal pathogens where bacterial competition is the primary control mechanism:

- **Dollar spot** — One of the most common turfgrass diseases
- **Necrotic ring spot** — A serious disease of Kentucky bluegrass
- **Yellow patch** — A cool-season turfgrass disease
- **Leaf spots** — Various foliar diseases across many plant species
- **Pink patch** — A turfgrass disease caused by Limonomyces roseipellis
- **Stripe smut** — A systemic disease of turfgrass

### Choosing the Right Tea

Selecting between fungal-dominant and bacterial-dominant tea depends on the target disease and the plant type. In general, perennial plants, trees, and shrubs benefit from fungal-dominant teas (their natural root ecology is fungal-dominant), while annual plants, turfgrasses, and vegetables in early growth stages benefit from bacterial-dominant teas. Many practitioners alternate between the two types to provide broad-spectrum protection.

## Weed Management Through Compost Tea

An often-overlooked benefit of compost tea is its ability to shift soil biology in ways that favor desired plants over weeds. Different weed species are associated with specific soil biological imbalances, and compost tea can help correct those imbalances:

### Weeds Responding to Protozoa and Nematodes

- **Clover** — Indicates low nitrogen; protozoa and nematodes improve nitrogen cycling
- **Quack grass** — A persistent rhizomatous weed that responds to enhanced protozoan grazing and nematode predation in the root zone

### Weeds Responding to Fungal-Dominant Tea

- **Plantains** — Broadleaf weeds associated with compacted, bacterial-dominant soils
- **Chickweed** — An annual weed that thrives in disturbed soils with low fungal populations
- **Nut sedge** — A difficult perennial weed that can be suppressed by establishing robust fungal communities
- **Ivy** — Invasive ground covers that respond to fungal-dominated tea applications

The mechanism behind weed suppression is indirect: by restoring balanced microbial communities, compost tea improves growing conditions for desired plants, which then outcompete weeds for light, water, and nutrients. The weeds themselves are often "indicator species" that colonize disturbed or biologically depleted soils; as soil health improves, they naturally diminish.

## Key Principles Summary

| Principle | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Foliar coverage | 70% of leaf surface, both sides |
| Time of day | Before 10 AM or after 3 PM (southern latitudes) |
| Midday droplet size | At least 1 mm diameter |
| Maximum sprayer pressure | 70 psi |
| Minimum straining mesh | 400 micrometers |
| Soil drench volume | 5 gallons per acre |
| Drench + foliar volume | 10 gallons per acre |
| Can you apply too much? | No |
| Frequency (chemically treated soil) | Every 2 weeks for 3 months |
| Frequency (maintenance) | Monthly, then 3× per year |
| Repeated applications | Increase microbial diversity |
