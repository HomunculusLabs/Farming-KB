# Permaculture Settlement Siting for Fire Safety ## Overview Fire is one of the most common and destructive hazards in permaculture landscapes, and proper settlement siting is the first line of defense. [[permaculture-design]] Course, emphasizing that the real danger of fire is
not flame but radiation. Four hundred feet before a fire, hair catches
alight; at two hundred feet, bodies split and fat ignites. Effective
settlement siting for fire safety requires understanding fire periodicity,
fuel management, radiation shielding, and the relationship between
topography and fire behavior.

## Key Concepts

### Fire Periodicity and Site Assessment

Fire has a periodicity specific to each site, determined by two factors:
the rate of fuel accumulation and the amount of moisture retained on
site. In areas with 30 to 40 inches of rainfall, catastrophic fires
occur approximately every 25 to 30 years. Fire periodicity can be
assessed by examining cross-sections of old trees or researching
historical fire records. Critically, the more frequently an area burns,
the more likely it is to burn again soon, because fire removes moisture-
retaining humus and kills more than it consumes, creating fire-prone
litter buildup.

### Ridge and Valley Fire Behavior

Ridge tops are far more fire-prone than valley systems. Their vegetation
may even be fire-dependent, with species that germinate after fire. Sun-
facing slopes are more fire-prone than shaded slopes. The primary
strategy for settlement siting is to place dwellings in valleys and on
shaded slopes, using the topography as a natural fire barrier. Fire on
slopes always travels uphill rapidly, making downhill siting safer.

### Non-Fuel Buffer Zones

Settlements require at least 100 feet of non-fuel systems between
dwellings and forested areas. This buffer can be created through roads,
ponds, swales, pit mulching, and managed grazing. Plant species selected
for the buffer zone should have fire-resistant characteristics: very
high ash content, high water content, low volatile oils, and dense
growth habits. Ice plants and thick-leaf evergreens whose litter
decomposes quickly are excellent choices.

### Earth Banks and Water Systems

Earth banks serve as effective fire barriers and radiation shields. A
dam with an earth bank provides both water storage and fire protection.
The bank should rise toward the fire-danger side of the property. High
access roads also function as fire control lines. Sprinkler systems
placed downhill from the dwelling, activated before fire arrives, can
create wet ground that fire will not cross. Gravity-fed water systems
are essential because public water systems fail during fires.

### Radiation Shelter Design

When fire approaches, the primary survival strategy is to avoid direct
radiation. Mollison recommends digging T-shaped or L-shaped pits and
earthing them over, or using road conduits buried and covered. Clients
can shelter underground until the fire front passes. The safest houses
are wooden, with 13 to 15 percent higher survival than stone or brick,
because wood transmits heat slowly. White-painted or reflective
surfaces provide additional radiation protection.

### Dangerous Siting Patterns

Never place settlements at the head of converging valleys where fire
funnels are created. Avoid saddle points where ridges converge, as
these receive accelerated winds during fires. Mollison witnessed a
landscape architect placing a house with an acre of fire-promoting
eucalyptus and pampas grass converging toward it. While the aesthetics
were reasonable, the function could be fatal. See
