# Cannabis and Asthma

## Overview

Asthma is a chronic medical condition ranging from mild breathing difficulties similar to those associated with a head cold, to severe and life-threatening emergencies requiring rapid 911 intervention and transport to the nearest emergency room. Asthma does not discriminate — people from all walks of life suffer from it; however, children and senior citizens are the most vulnerable populations. Some children outgrow their asthma while others do not. The causes for asthma are poorly understood, and no pharmacological cure exists.

Orthodox medicine focuses on diminishing occurrence frequency and attempting to control acute symptoms. Physicians typically use oxygen and bronchodilators such as Albuterol, Azmacourt, Ventolin, or Theo-Dur to open constricted tubes leading to the lungs. These drugs stimulate beta-2 adrenergic receptor sites, which enlist responses including relaxation of the smooth muscles in the upper airways. However, there is evidence that regular use of inhalers contributes to making asthma worse over time. Other commonly used pharmaceuticals include steroids and additional bronchodilating agents.

### Common Triggers

Some of the main triggers of an asthma attack include emotional distress, dust, dust mites, molds, smoking, strong smells, pollution (both indoor and outdoor), industrial chemicals, food additives, colds, coughs, and strenuous exercise. Emotional and psychological triggers are well-documented in clinical settings.

### Common Signs and Symptoms

- Difficulty breathing and wheezing (higher pitch indicates tighter airways)
- Pale skin and diaphoresis (sweating)
- Fear and anxiety (both as triggers and as secondary symptoms)
- Tightness in the chest
- Coughing with clear sputum
- Spasms of the upper airways

## Cannabis Health Index Evidence

The Cannabis Health Index (CHI) rated the evidence for cannabis in asthma treatment with a total value of **24**, based on **7 reviewed studies** — representing a moderate-to-strong evidence base.

## Clinical Study Findings

### 1974 — Double-Blind Placebo-Controlled Crossover Study (CHI: 5)

Researchers developed a double-blind placebo-controlled crossover study enrolling ten patients with stable asthma. The study compared the efficacy of smoked cannabis (7 mg/kg of 2% natural marijuana), ingested THC (15 mg oral), placebo, and isoproterenol (a pharmaceutical bronchodilator commonly used in the 1970s to treat asthma). Results showed that while the initial effect of isoproterenol was more pronounced, the effects of smoked cannabis and oral THC lasted significantly longer. The researchers concluded: "These findings indicated that in the asthmatic subjects, both smoked marijuana and oral THC caused significant bronchodilation of at least 2 hours duration."

### 1975 — Exercise-Induced and Chemically-Induced Bronchospasm (CHI: 3)

Eight stable asthmatic patients were enrolled in a study at the UCLA Division of Pulmonary Disease to determine the effects of cannabis on both chemically and exercise-induced asthma. Smoked cannabis (500 mg with 2% THC) was compared against 500 mg of smoked placebo marijuana. Results showed that in both cases, smoked cannabis produced a prompt correction of the bronchospasms and associated hyperinflation, similar to isoproterenol. This was significant because exercise-induced bronchospasm is one of the most common and troublesome forms of asthma for active patients.

### 1976 — THC vs. Salbutamol (Ventolin) (CHI: 5)

Another double-blind placebo-controlled study at the Welsh National School of Medicine enlisted ten patients with asthma. THC 200 mcg in ethanol (inhaler form) was compared against salbutamol 100 mcg (Ventolin inhaler) and placebo ethanol. Results showed: "Salbutamol and THC significantly improved ventilatory function. Maximal bronchodilation was achieved more rapidly with salbutamol, but at 1 hour both drugs were equally effective."

The conducting scientist detected no variation in mood or heart rate during THC application and concluded: "The mode of action of THC differs from that of sympathomimetic drugs (drugs which stimulate the sympathetic nervous system and increase heart rate and blood pressure), and it or a derivative may make a suitable adjuvant in the treatment of selected asthmatics."

### 1977 — Aerosolized THC with Mixed Results (CHI: 3)

A random, double-blind trial was conducted on eleven healthy people and five patients with asthma. Researchers measured airway dynamics and heart rates while administering aerosolized THC, aerosolized placebo, isoproterenol, and 20 mg of oral and smoked THC. Aerosolized THC was less pronounced than isoproterenol in producing short-term bronchodilation (5 minutes) but significantly better in the longer time ranges of 1 to 3 hours.

However, the authors noted a critical caveat: "Aerosolized delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol caused significant bronchodilation in 3 of 5 asthmatic subjects, but caused moderate to severe bronchoconstriction associated with cough and chest discomfort in the other two." This local irritating effect on the airways may make aerosolized THC unsuitable for therapeutic use in some patients.

### 1978 — Dose-Response Bronchodilation (CHI: 3)

Researchers at the Sully Hospital Asthma Research Unit in Wales administered an aerosol containing THC to asthmatic patients in a steady state of dosage ranging from 50 to 200 mcg. Results showed that treatment increased the peak expiratory flow rate and forced expiratory volume in one second. Importantly, the onset, magnitude, and duration of the bronchodilator effect were all dose-related, establishing a clear dose-response relationship useful for clinical titration.

### 1983 — Nabilone vs. Terbutaline (CHI: 0)

The synthetic cannabinoid Nabilone (2 mg oral) was compared to the standard anti-asthmatic pharmaceutical Terbutaline sulfate in 6 healthy people and 6 asthmatic patients at UCLA. While Nabilone demonstrated moderate bronchodilator action in healthy subjects, it did not produce a therapeutic effect better than the placebo in asthmatic patients. The authors concluded: "Oral Nabilone (2 mg) does not result in significant acute bronchodilation in patients with asthma."

### 2006 — Endocannabinoid System and Inflammatory Lung Disease (CHI: 3)

Researchers from Boston tested numerous novel molecules that exert biological effects through the endocannabinoid system. The exploration suggested therapeutic potential of cannabinoids on inflammatory diseases such as asthma and allergic asthma, opening a new frontier for cannabinoid-based asthma research beyond THC alone.

## Strain-Specific Considerations

THC was the primary cannabinoid studied across all asthma trials, binding to both CB1 and CB2 receptors. Sativas and sativa-dominant strains tend to contain a cannabinoid profile with a slightly higher THC:CBD/CBN ratio than indica or indica-dominant strains. The mode of action of THC differs from sympathomimetic drugs, suggesting cannabinoids may offer bronchodilation through a unique pathway that could complement existing pharmaceutical approaches without the cardiovascular stimulation associated with drugs like isoproterenol.

## Mind-Body Medicine Perspective

Grace and Graham examined the psychosomatic underpinnings of 19 patients and discovered that in participating subjects, asthma occurred when a patient was faced with a life situation they wished would just go away. They wished someone else would take responsibility and make it disappear, or wished to avoid it altogether. Typical statements included: "I wanted them to go away," "I didn't want anything to do with it," "I wanted to blot it all out," "I wanted to build a wall between me and him," "I wanted to go to bed and pull the sheets over my head."

The authors concluded: "The reaction of the respiratory mucous membrane to a noxious agent is to exclude it by swelling of the membrane with consequent narrowing of the passageway, and to dilute it and wash it out by hypersecretion. When these changes are limited to the nose, the reaction is called vasomotor rhinitis; when they are sufficiently intense to include the bronchi, so that wheezing occurs, the name asthma is applied."

Wheezing appears akin to an internal smothering or suppression of something felt should not be released. Asthma is fundamentally a struggle to breathe in but especially to exhale. Non-physical triggers and alleviators are well recognized in emergency settings — most ER personnel know of cases where a patient's respiratory distress was diminished or completely resolved by "talking the patient down."

A study published by the American Journal of Psychiatry (1987) discovered that difficult or stressful dreams may contribute to asthma attacks during sleep, and an experiment by the American Psychological Association (1993) found that asthmatic patients experience up to three times the number of nightmares compared to healthy control groups.

Aggravating factors include facing situations one wishes would disappear and a belief that avoidance is the best course of inaction. Working toward deep relaxation, trust in self, and learning to relax in the presence of constricting emotions is recommended.

## Complementary Anti-Inflammatory Foods

Several foods with bronchodilatory and anti-inflammatory properties complement cannabinoid therapy:

- **Anise** — Iranian scientists discovered bronchodilatory qualities explaining traditional use for respiratory ailments
- **Bush tea (rooibos)** — Bronchodilator, antispasmodic, and blood pressure-lowering via potassium ATP channel activation
- **Cacao** — Theobromine is effective as an anti-tussive for persistent coughs
- **Garlic** — Used in Cuba for asthma treatment and phlegm reduction during colds
- **Nigella** — Extracts reduce all asthma symptoms and improve pulmonary function tests, reducing inhaler need
- **Turmeric** — Curcumin shows protective roles in COPD, acute lung injury, and allergic asthma through inflammation modulation

## Relationship to the Endocannabinoid System

The endocannabinoid system is present in lung tissue and plays a role in bronchial function and inflammatory responses. CB1 and CB2 receptors have been identified in the respiratory system, suggesting that cannabinoids may modulate airway reactivity and inflammation through direct receptor-mediated effects in the lungs. This anatomical basis supports the clinical observations of bronchodilation and provides a theoretical framework for understanding why cannabinoids affect asthma, despite the paradox of inhaling plant material for a respiratory condition.

The unique mechanism of THC — distinct from the sympathetic nervous system stimulation of pharmaceutical bronchodilators — may offer advantages for patients who experience cardiovascular side effects from standard medications. Since THC does not significantly increase heart rate at bronchodilating doses, it could be particularly suitable for patients with co-existing cardiovascular conditions.

## Current Treatment Limitations

Standard asthma management faces several limitations that cannabinoid therapy could potentially address:

- **Tolerance development** — Regular bronchodilator use may worsen asthma over time
- **Cardiovascular side effects** — Sympathomimetic drugs increase heart rate and blood pressure
- **Steroid side effects** — Long-term corticosteroid use carries significant risks including immune suppression and bone density loss
- **No cure** — All current treatments manage symptoms rather than addressing underlying causes

## Summary

Despite the counterintuitive nature of inhaling plant material for a respiratory condition, clinical evidence spanning 1974 to 2006 demonstrates that THC produces meaningful bronchodilation in asthma patients. While onset may be slower than pharmaceutical bronchodilators, effects can last longer. The unique mechanism of action — distinct from sympathomimetic drugs — suggests cannabinoids could serve as complementary adjuvants in selected asthmatics.
