# [[cannabis-and-asthma]]

Asthma is typically a chronic medical problem, ranging from mild breathing
difficulties to severe and life-threatening emergencies requiring rapid
intervention. It does not discriminate: people from all walks of life suffer
from asthma, but children and senior citizens are the most vulnerable. Some
children outgrow their asthma while others do not. The causes for asthma are
poorly understood, and no pharmacological cure exists.

## Conventional Treatment

Physicians typically use oxygen and bronchodilators (such as Albuterol,
Azmacourt, Ventolin, or Theo-Dur) to open constricted tubes leading to the
lungs. However, evidence exists that regular use of inhalers may contribute to
making asthma worse over time. Other commonly used pharmaceuticals include
steroids and drugs that stimulate beta-2 adrenergic receptor sites.

Main triggers of an asthma attack include emotional distress, dust, dust
mites, molds, smoking, strong smells, pollution, industrial chemicals, food
additives, colds, coughs, and strenuous exercise. Common signs and symptoms
include difficulty breathing, wheezing, pale skin, sweating, fear and anxiety,
tightness in the chest, coughing, or spasms of the upper airways.

## Cannabis and Asthma: The Evidence

The [[cannabis-health-index]] (CHI) assigns asthma a value of 24 based on 7
reviewed studies, indicating substantial therapeutic potential.

### Key Clinical Studies

Since 1974, studies have documented the effects of cannabinoids on human
patients with asthma. In the first such trial (1974), researchers enrolled ten
patients with stable asthma to compare smoked cannabis, ingested THC, placebo,
and isoproterenol (a pharmaceutical used to treat asthma). Results showed that
while the initial effect of isoproterenol was more pronounced, the effects of
smoked cannabis and oral THC lasted longer: "Both smoked marijuana and oral
THC caused significant bronchodilation of at least 2 hours duration."

In 1975, eight stable asthmatics were enrolled to determine the effects of
cannabis on both chemically and exercise-induced asthma. Smoked cannabis (THC
2%) produced a prompt correction of bronchospasms and associated
hyperinflation, similar to isoproterenol.

A 1976 double-blind placebo-controlled study of ten asthmatic patients found
that THC significantly improved ventilatory function. While maximal
bronchodilation was achieved more rapidly with salbutamol, at one hour both
drugs were equally effective. The conducting scientist concluded that "the
mode of action of THC differs from that of sympathomimetic drugs, and it or a
derivative may make a suitable adjuvant in the treatment of selected
asthmatics."

In 1978, researchers administered aerosolized THC (50 to 200 mcg) to
asthmatic patients. Results showed dose-related increases in peak expiratory
flow rate and forced expiratory volume, with onset, magnitude, and duration of
the bronchodilator effect all being dose-related.

By 2006, researchers from Boston had tested novel molecules acting through
the endocannabinoid system, suggesting therapeutic potential on inflammatory
diseases such as asthma and allergic asthma.

### Strain-Specific Considerations

The primary cannabinoid used in asthma studies was THC, which binds to both
CB1 and CB2 receptors. Sativas or sativa-dominant strains tend to contain a
slightly higher THC:CBD/CBN ratio than indica or indica-dominant strains.

## Mind-Body Medicine and Asthma

Grace and Graham discovered that in participating subjects, asthma occurred
when a patient was faced with a life situation they wished would just go away.
They wished someone else would take responsibility and make it disappear, or
wished to avoid it altogether. Typical statements included: "I wanted them to
go away" and "I wanted to go to bed and pull the sheets over my head."

They concluded: "When changes are limited to the nose, the reaction is called
vasomotor rhinitis; when they are sufficiently intense to include the bronchi,
so that wheezing occurs, the name asthma is applied."

Difficult or stressful dreams may contribute to asthma attacks during sleep.
Asthmatic patients have been shown to experience up to three times the number
of nightmares compared to healthy control groups.
