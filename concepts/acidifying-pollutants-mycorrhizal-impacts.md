# Acidifying Pollutants and Mycorrhizal Fungi

The effects of acidifying pollutants on mycorrhizal fungi represent one of the most thoroughly studied aspects of anthropogenic impacts on belowground fungal communities. Research emerging from the *Waldsterben* (forest dieback) phenomenon in central European forests during the 1970s and 1980s established that acidifying pollutants damage both root systems and their associated mycorrhizal symbionts through multiple interacting mechanisms. These findings have profound implications for forest health, nutrient cycling, and ecosystem resilience.

## The Dighton and Jansen Two-Directional Impact Model

Dighton and Jansen (1991) proposed an influential two-directional impact model describing how acidifying pollutants affect mycorrhizal development. This model identifies two principal pathways through which pollutants reduce mycorrhizal associations:

**Pathway 1: Aboveground Photosynthetic Reduction.** Pollutant-induced damage to tree canopies reduces photosynthetic capacity, which in turn decreases the allocation of photosynthate (carbohydrates) to roots and their mycorrhizae. Reduced energy supply has two consequences: it reduces overall mycorrhizal colonization of roots, and it selectively favors those fungal species that can survive on lower carbohydrate supplies. This pathway represents an indirect effect mediated through the host plant's carbon economy.

**Pathway 2: Soil Acidification and Metal Toxicity.** Acidifying pollutants lower soil pH, which increases the solubility of toxic metal ions — particularly aluminum, manganese, and magnesium (Van Breemen and Van Dijk, 1988; Skeffington and Brown, 1986; Tyler et al., 1987; Ruark et al., 1991). Increased metal availability causes direct root damage, root dieback, reduced mycorrhizal fungal growth, and diminished root colonization. This pathway represents a direct effect on the soil environment and root zone chemistry.

The recognition of these two pathways was significant because it explained how the same pollutant could simultaneously affect aboveground and belowground tree physiology, creating a compounding negative effect on tree health and mycorrhizal function.

## Evidence from Forest Decline Studies

Multiple lines of evidence support the negative effects of acidifying pollutants on mycorrhizal formation and function:

- **Root damage**: Sobotka (1964), Liss et al. (1984), Meyer (1987), and Blaschke (1988) all documented declines in mycorrhizal formation and reduced root vigor in declining forests.
- **Reduced fruiting**: Arnolds (1985, 1988), Jansen and Van Dobben (1987), and Fellner (1988) demonstrated reduced ectomycorrhizal fruit body production in polluted stands.
- **Community shifts**: In healthy forests, ectomycorrhizal fungal fruit bodies typically constitute 45–50% of all macrofungal fruit bodies. In polluted stands, this drops to approximately 10%, indicating a dramatic shift in fungal community structure.

## Stages of Forest Decline Assessed by Fungal Community Structure

Fellner and Pešková (1995) developed a classification system for stages of forest decline based on the ratio of saprotrophic to ectomycorrhizal fungal fruit bodies:

| Disturbance Level | Ectomycorrhizal Status | Lignicolous Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Latent** | Decreasing to 40% | Increasing to >30% |
| **Acute** | Decreasing to <40% | Increasing to >40% |
| **Lethal** | Decreasing to <20% | Increasing to >55% |

This staging system provides a practical tool for assessing forest health using macrofungal surveys, reflecting the sensitivity of ectomycorrhizal fungi to pollution-induced stress relative to wood-decomposing (lignicolous) saprotrophs.

## Changes in Ectomycorrhizal Community Structure

The effects of acidifying pollutants are not uniform across all ectomycorrhizal fungal species. Markkola and Ohtonen (1988) studied ectomycorrhizal communities in Finnish forests and found differential responses among species:

- **Species reduced**: *Piloderma*, *Dermocybe*, *Hebeloma*, and a "type 03" ectomycorrhizal morphotype were significantly reduced in the presence of acidifying pollutants.
- **Species increased**: *Cenococcum* was found to increase, suggesting some degree of pollution tolerance or competitive advantage under acidic conditions.

Dighton and Skeffington (1987) found that simulated acid rain applied to *Pinus sylvestris* trees in lysimeters altered ectomycorrhizal community structure by reducing multibranched morphotypes associated with large amounts of extraradical hyphae. This observation was significant because it suggested the effect of pollutants was greatest on fungal hyphal growth — the exploratory network essential for nutrient foraging — rather than on the mycorrhizal root structures themselves.

## Aluminum Toxicity and Fungal Tolerance

Aluminum toxicity is a key mechanism by which acidification damages mycorrhizal fungi. In addition to direct root damage (Schier, 1985), Thompson and Medve (1984) demonstrated differential aluminum sensitivity among ectomycorrhizal fungal species:

- **Sensitive species**: Growth of *Cenococcum*, *Pisolithus*, and *Thelephora* was suppressed at 146 mM aluminum concentration.
- **Tolerant species**: *Suillus* showed no growth reduction below 1000 mM aluminum concentration.

The ratios of Al:Ca, Al:Mg, and Al:PO4 were found to be important determinants of fungal growth (Jongbloed and Borst Pauwels, 1988, 1989; Kottke and Oberwinkler, 1990). Improved growth of various ectomycorrhizal species at low Al:PO4 ratios, even at high aluminum concentrations (Jansen et al., 1990), was explained by the absorption and immobilization of aluminum into polyphosphate droplets within fungal hyphae (Kottke and Oberwinkler, 1990). This mechanism represents a potential detoxification strategy that varies in effectiveness among species.

## Field Fumigation Experiments: SO2 and Ozone

McLeod et al. (1992) conducted a landmark field fumigation experiment in which SO2 and O3 were released over circular plots containing monospecific stands of Scots pine, Sitka spruce, and Norway spruce. Multivariate analysis of ectomycorrhizal community structure revealed that SO2 fumigation affected only Scots pine, where a reduction in *Paxillus involutus* mycorrhizae occurred (Shaw et al., 1992, 1993).

Paradoxically, fruit body surveys showed that *Paxillus involutus* fruited more abundantly in Scots pine plots receiving high SO2 loading — the converse of the root colonization pattern. This discrepancy highlights the complexity of interpreting fungal responses to pollutants and underscores the need to distinguish between belowground (root colonization) and aboveground (fruiting) responses.

## Ozone Effects on Arbuscular Mycorrhizae

Qui et al. (1993) studied the effects of acid rain and ozone on two provenances of loblolly pine (*Pinus taeda*) and found that the ozone-tolerant provenance had less reduction in root surface area, less change in ectomycorrhizal colonization, and less change in mycorrhizal species composition than the ozone-intolerant provenance. This demonstrated that host plant tolerance to pollutants can mediate the effects on mycorrhizal communities.

Duckmanton and Widden (1994) showed that ozone altered the nature (but not the degree) of arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization in sugar maple (*Acer saccharum*) by reducing the number of arbuscules while increasing the number of vesicles and hyphal coils within the root cortex. This shift was interpreted as a response to reduced photosynthesis: vesicles and hyphal coils are less energy-demanding to produce than arbuscules, and vesicles may serve as storage structures — a stress adaptation.

## Soil Chemistry Changes and Nutrient Interactions

The complex interplay between acidifying pollutants and soil nutrient availability adds further dimensions to mycorrhizal responses. Liming experiments, intended to offset acidification effects, have shown:

- Kuyper (1989) demonstrated that nitrogen addition and liming stimulate saprotrophic fungi and leaf litter decomposition where nitrogen levels in litter are low, but suppress saprotrophic activity where litter nitrogen content is already high.
- The effect of liming on mycoflora was found to be very similar to that of nitrogen fertilization.
- Antibus and Linkins (1992) showed that liming reduced acid phosphatase activity of the mycorrhizal community in the forest floor litter layer, likely through increased nitrogen and phosphorus availability reducing the demand for enzymatic nutrient acquisition.

Phosphorus availability changes were implicated in forest decline in the Vosges region of France (Estivalet et al., 1990), where seedling growth responses suggested reduced P availability in declining soils. Changes in the rhizospheric microbial community, including increases in *Penicillium*, *Trichoderma*, *Acremonium*, and *Cylindrocarpon*, were associated with declining forest health and may exert antagonistic effects on ectomycorrhizal fungi of Norway spruce.

## Antagonistic Soil Fungi in Declining Forests

Estivalet et al. (1990) identified several fungal genera — *Penicillium*, *Trichoderma*, *Acremonium*, and *Cylindrocarpon* — as more regularly isolated from declining forest soils compared to healthy forest soils. They proposed that these fungi may exert antagonistic, though not strictly pathogenic, effects on ectomycorrhizal fungi of Norway spruce. This antagonism, combined with the direct toxic effects of acidification, may create a compounding negative effect on tree mycorrhizal status and overall forest health.

## Conclusions

The effects of acidifying pollutants on mycorrhizal fungi are mediated through multiple pathways — aboveground photosynthetic reduction, belowground soil acidification and metal toxicity, changes in nutrient availability, and shifts in competitive interactions within soil fungal communities. The species-specific nature of these responses means that pollution does not simply reduce mycorrhizal abundance but fundamentally restructures the mycorrhizal community, with potentially significant consequences for tree nutrition, forest health, and ecosystem function. Understanding these complex interactions remains essential for predicting forest responses to ongoing and future atmospheric pollution.
