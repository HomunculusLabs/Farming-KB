## Decay Curves and Mass Loss Patterns

Fungal decomposition of organic substrates typically
follows one of several characteristic mass loss curves.

The single-exponential decay model describes a constant
proportional loss rate where the same fraction of
remaining material is decomposed per unit time,
producing a linear decline when plotted on a logarithmic
scale.

The double-exponential or asymptotic model describes an
initial rapid phase of decomposition of labile compounds
(sugars, amino acids, cellulose) followed by a slower
phase as recalcitrant materials (lignin, polyphenols,
cutin) dominate the remaining substrate.

Most real-world decomposition patterns fall between
these idealized models. In [[fungal-enzymatic-assays-identification-characterization]] activity,
and a humification phase (years to decades) where
resistant materials are transformed into stable soil
organic matter.

Wood decomposition follows a different trajectory, with
initial colonization by pioneer fungi followed by
successional replacement as substrate chemistry changes
through progressive decay.

## Substrate Quality Effects

The chemical composition of the substrate is the
strongest predictor of decomposition rate across diverse
ecosystems. The lignin:nitrogen ratio is a widely used
index of substrate quality: materials with high lignin
and low nitrogen decompose slowly, while materials with
low lignin and high nitrogen decompose rapidly.

Initial nitrogen concentration positively correlates
with decomposition rate because nitrogen is required
for fungal enzyme synthesis, particularly the
nitrogen-rich enzymes used in [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]] also supports larger fungal
biomass per unit of substrate.

The carbon quality index, which considers the
proportions of labile, cellulose, and lignin carbon
pools, provides more nuanced predictions than simple
lignin or nitrogen measures alone. Near-infrared
spectroscopy (NIRS) and pyrolysis-GC/MS characterize
substrate quality with greater precision for modern
kinetic models.

Polyphenolic compounds and condensed tannins, which
bind proteins and inhibit enzyme activity, are
increasingly recognized as important controllers of
decomposition kinetics, particularly in tropical
ecosystems where polyphenol-rich litter is common.
