# Growing Potatoes Organically

Potatoes are one of the most productive and rewarding vegetable crops. They require careful soil preparation and benefit enormously from organic [[query-which-cannabis-training-techniques-produce-highest-yields]], good for storage.

**Important:** Always buy certified seed potatoes. Home-saved tubers can carry virus diseases spread by aphids. Certified seed is grown in areas where aphid attack is rare.

## Chitting (Sprouting)

Place seed potatoes in trays or egg cartons in a cool, light, frost-free position in late winter. The shoots will develop; this gives them a head start. Choose the 3-4 strongest shoots and rub off the others. This produces fewer but larger tubers.

## Planting

Plant first earlies in early spring, second earlies in mid-spring, maincrop in late spring. Dig a trench 4-6in (10-15cm) deep and place tubers 12-15in (30-38cm) apart for earlies, 15in (38cm) for maincrop, with 24in (60cm) between rows.

**For deep beds:** Plant at 12in (30cm) spacing both ways.

## Maintenance

**Earthing up:** Draw soil up around the shoots when they are about 6in (15cm) tall, forming a ridge. This protects from late frosts, prevents tubers going green (exposure to light), and increases yield. Earth up again 2-3 weeks later.

**Watering:** Potatoes need plenty of water, especially when the tubers are swelling. Water thoroughly in dry periods.

**Weeding:** Regular hoeing between rows while plants are small. Once the foliage meets across the rows, weeds are suppressed naturally.

**Feeding:** If manure was dug in the previous fall, additional feeding is usually unnecessary. Otherwise, apply blood, fish, and bone meal at planting time.

## Harvesting

First earlies can be harvested when the flowers open. Second earlies a few weeks later. Maincrop should be left until the foliage dies down and turns yellow, then cut the haulms and leave the tubers in the ground for 10-14 days to set the skins before lifting.

## Storage

Store only sound, undamaged tubers in a cool, dark, frost-free place. Paper sacks or hessian bags allow some ventilation. Do not store near onions (gases cause sprouting). Check regularly and remove any rotting tubers.

## Pests and Diseases

**Potato blight:** The most serious disease. Dark brown patches on leaves that spread rapidly in warm, wet weather. Remove and burn all affected foliage immediately. Do not compost potato tops (blight spore risk). Spray with copper fungicide as a preventive in susceptible conditions.

**Wireworms:** Make small holes in tubers. More common on newly cultivated land. Trap with pieces of potato buried in soil.

**Slugs:** Particularly attack tubers near the surface. Earth up well and keep soil firm.

**Eelworm:** Serious soil pest. Choose resistant varieties. Rotate crops strictly.

**Common scab:** Raised rough patches on tuber skin. Worse in alkaline conditions. Do not lime before planting.

**Aphids:** Can spread virus diseases. Encourage ladybugs and hoverflies.

## Companion Planting and Intercropping

Potatoes benefit from several companion planting relationships. Horseradish planted at the corners of the potato bed is traditionally believed to improve disease resistance, though scientific evidence for this specific interaction is limited. Beans and other legumes fix nitrogen that potatoes can access, though the two crops compete for space if planted too closely. Marigolds and nasturtiums may help deter certain potato pests through their aromatic foliage. Potatoes should not be planted near tomatoes, eggplants, or peppers, as all are members of the Solanaceae family and share susceptibility to blight and other diseases. Crop rotation is far more important than companion planting for potato health: at minimum, potatoes should not follow other solanaceous crops, and ideally should not occupy the same ground more than once every three to four years.

## Harvesting Techniques

Proper harvesting technique preserves tuber quality and reduces damage. Use a digging fork rather than a spade, inserting it at an angle about 12 inches from the plant base and levering upward to lift the root zone. Work from the outside of the row inward to avoid spearing tubers. Allow lifted potatoes to dry on the soil surface for a few hours to toughen the skin before handling, which reduces bruising and storage rot. Harvest on a dry day when the soil is not waterlogged — wet soil sticks to tubers and increases handling damage. Sort potatoes immediately after harvest, setting aside any damaged or diseased tubers for immediate use (never store damaged potatoes, as they will rot and contaminate the entire batch).
