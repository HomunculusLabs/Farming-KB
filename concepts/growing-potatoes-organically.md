# Growing Potatoes Organically

Potatoes are one of the most productive and rewarding vegetable crops. They require careful soil preparation and benefit enormously from organic [[growing-alliums-organically]] methods. They are a key crop in rotation systems, helping to break up the soil for subsequent crops.

## Soil and Site

Potatoes need an open, sunny position with deep, well-drained soil. They are one of the most demanding crops in terms of organic matter. Dig the ground in fall and incorporate as much well-rotted manure or compost as possible. Potatoes are generally grown in Plot A of a three-year rotation (the plot that received manure or compost the previous fall).

Potatoes are not particularly pH-sensitive but do best around pH 5.0-6.0. Do not lime before planting as this can increase susceptibility to scab.

## Varieties

Choose varieties for purpose and season:

**First early:** Ready in about 10 weeks. Good for small gardens and new potatoes.

**Second early:** Ready in about 13 weeks.

**Maincrop:** Ready in about 15-20 weeks. Highest yields, good for storage.

**Important:** Always buy certified seed potatoes. Home-saved tubers can carry virus diseases spread by aphids. Certified seed is grown in areas where aphid attack is rare.

## Chitting (Sprouting)

Place seed potatoes in trays or egg cartons in a cool, light, frost-free position in late winter. The shoots will develop; this gives them a head start. Choose the 3-4 strongest shoots and rub off the others. This produces fewer but larger tubers.

## Planting

Plant first earlies in early spring, second earlies in mid-spring, maincrop in late spring. Dig a trench 4-6in (10-15cm) deep and place tubers 12-15in (30-38cm) apart for earlies, 15in (38cm) for maincrop, with 24in (60cm) between rows.

**For deep beds:** Plant at 12in (30cm) spacing both ways.

## Maintenance

**Earthing up:** Draw soil up around the shoots when they are about 6in (15cm) tall, forming a ridge. This protects from late frosts, prevents tubers going green (exposure to light), and increases yield. Earth up again 2-3 weeks later.

**Watering:** Potatoes need plenty of water, especially when the tubers are swelling. Water thoroughly in dry periods.

**Weeding:** Regular hoeing between rows while plants are small. Once the foliage meets across the rows, weeds are suppressed naturally.

**Feeding:** If manure was dug in the previous fall, additional feeding is usually unnecessary. Otherwise, apply blood, fish, and bone meal at planting time.

## Harvesting

First earlies can be harvested when the flowers open. Second earlies a few weeks later. Maincrop should be left until the foliage dies down and turns yellow, then cut the haulms and leave the tubers in the ground for 10-14 days to set the skins before lifting.

## Storage

Store only sound, undamaged tubers in a cool, dark, frost-free place. Paper sacks or hessian bags allow some ventilation. Do not store near onions (gases cause sprouting). Check regularly and remove any rotting tubers.

## Pests and Diseases

**Potato blight:** The most serious disease. Dark brown patches on leaves that spread rapidly in warm, wet weather. Remove and burn all affected foliage immediately. Do not compost potato tops (blight spore risk). Spray with copper fungicide as a preventive in susceptible conditions.

**Wireworms:** Make small holes in tubers. More common on newly cultivated land. Trap with pieces of potato buried in soil.

**Slugs:** Particularly attack tubers near the surface. Earth up well and keep soil firm.

**Eelworm:** Serious soil pest. Choose resistant varieties. Rotate crops strictly.

**Common scab:** Raised rough patches on tuber skin. Worse in alkaline conditions. Do not lime before planting.

**Aphids:** Can spread virus diseases. Encourage ladybugs and hoverflies.

## Green Manure Value

After harvesting, the remaining organic matter in the soil and the broken-up structure make potatoes excellent predecessors for other crops. The deep digging and earthing up process benefits soil structure.

## No-Dig Methods for Potatoes

No-dig potato cultivation has gained popularity among organic gardeners as an alternative to traditional trench planting. In this method, seed potatoes are placed on the soil surface and covered with a thick layer (6-12 inches) of organic mulch such as straw, hay, or partially decomposed leaves. As the potatoes grow, additional mulch is added to maintain coverage. The advantages include reduced soil disturbance (preserving soil structure and microbial communities), easier harvesting (potatoes are simply pulled from the loose mulch), and fewer weed problems. The disadvantages include potential slug damage in wet seasons, slower soil warming in spring compared to exposed soil, and the risk of mice nesting in the mulch. No-dig potatoes tend to produce slightly lower yields than trenched potatoes but with significantly less labor.

## Companion Planting and Intercropping

Potatoes benefit from several companion planting relationships. Horseradish planted at the corners of the potato bed is traditionally believed to improve disease resistance, though scientific evidence for this specific interaction is limited. Beans and other legumes fix nitrogen that potatoes can access, though the two crops compete for space if planted too closely. Marigolds and nasturtiums may help deter certain potato pests through their aromatic foliage. Potatoes should not be planted near tomatoes, eggplants, or peppers, as all are members of the Solanaceae family and share susceptibility to blight and other diseases. Crop rotation is far more important than companion planting for potato health: at minimum, potatoes should not follow other solanaceous crops, and ideally should not occupy the same ground more than once every three to four years.

## Harvesting Techniques

Proper harvesting technique preserves tuber quality and reduces damage. Use a digging fork rather than a spade, inserting it at an angle about 12 inches from the plant base and levering upward to lift the root zone. Work from the outside of the row inward to avoid spearing tubers. Allow lifted potatoes to dry on the soil surface for a few hours to toughen the skin before handling, which reduces bruising and storage rot. Harvest on a dry day when the soil is not waterlogged — wet soil sticks to tubers and increases handling damage. Sort potatoes immediately after harvest, setting aside any damaged or diseased tubers for immediate use (never store damaged potatoes, as they will rot and contaminate the entire batch).

## Nutrient Requirements and Feeding

Potatoes are heavy feeders, particularly requiring adequate potassium for good tuber development and disease resistance. In addition to the manure or compost dug in the previous fall, potatoes benefit from additional potassium sources such as wood ash (applied sparingly, as excess ash raises pH and may increase scab) or comfrey leaf mulch. Nitrogen is important for [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] but should not be over-supplied: excessive nitrogen produces lush foliage at the expense of tuber production and increases susceptibility to blight. Phosphorus supports root development and is best supplied through compost or bonemeal incorporated before planting. [[comparison-foliar-feeding-vs-compost-tea-nutrients]] with liquid [[comparison-seaweed-extract-vs-fish-hydrolysate]] during the growing season can provide trace minerals that improve tuber quality and disease resistance.

## Common Potato Pests and Organic Controls

Potatoes are susceptible to several significant pests that require organic management strategies. The Colorado potato beetle (*Leptinotarsa decemlineata*) is the most widely recognized potato pest; adults and larvae defoliate plants rapidly and can destroy entire crops. Organic controls include hand-picking (effective for small plantings), floating row covers to exclude adults during early growth, applications of [[cervantes-bacillus-thuringiensis-bt-control]] var. tenebrionis (Btt) which specifically targets beetle larvae, and spinosad-based sprays. The potato flea beetle creates small "shot-hole" patterns in leaves; [[comparison-neem-oil-cannabis-vs-diatomaceous-earth-cannabis]] and floating row covers provide effective control. Wireworms (click beetle larvae) bore into tubers, creating tunnels that render them unmarketable; crop rotation and avoiding planting potatoes after grass sod reduce wireworm pressure. Nematodes, particularly root-knot nematodes, cause galling on tubers; marigold intercropping and soil solarization provide partial control. The most effective organic pest management combines multiple strategies rather than relying on a single approach.

## Potato Diseases and Organic Prevention

Several diseases affect potato crops and can be managed organically through prevention and cultural practices. Late blight (*Phytophthora infestans*), the disease that caused the Irish potato famine, remains the most serious threat, particularly in wet seasons. Organic prevention includes spacing plants widely for air circulation, avoiding overhead watering, removing and destroying infected foliage immediately, and applying copper-based fungicides preventively (though copper use is controversial in organic circles). Early blight (*Alternaria solani*) causes lower leaf yellowing with concentric ring patterns; it can be managed with crop rotation, resistant varieties, and neem-based sprays. Common scab (*Streptomyces scabies*) causes rough, corky patches on tuber surfaces; it is prevented by maintaining slightly acidic soil pH (below 5.2), ensuring adequate moisture during tuber formation, and avoiding fresh manure applications at planting. Blackleg and soft rot bacterial diseases are prevented by planting certified disease-free seed potatoes and avoiding damage during harvesting.

## Storage Conditions and Post-Harvest Handling

Proper storage is essential to preserve the harvest. Potatoes should be cured at 55-65°F (13-18°C) in high humidity (85-95%) for 1-2 weeks after harvest to heal any cuts and thicken the skin for long-term storage. After curing, potatoes are best stored at 38-40°F (3-4°C) in complete darkness — exposure to light causes greening (solanine production), which is toxic and bitter. Storing potatoes below 38°F converts starches to sugars, producing an unpleasant sweet taste and causing darkening when fried. Potatoes store well alongside apples, which emit ethylene gas that suppresses sprouting. Onions should never be stored near potatoes, as onion gases accelerate potato sprouting and rot. Regular inspection during storage allows early removal of any tubers showing soft rot before the problem spreads.

## Seed Potato Selection and Preparation

Choosing the right seed potatoes is the foundation of a successful organic crop. Certified disease-free seed potatoes from reputable suppliers reduce the risk of introducing viral, bacterial, or [[bloomfield-fungal-pathogens-and-parasites]]. Save-your-own seed potato programs are possible for home gardeners but require careful selection of only the healthiest tubers from the best-performing plants — reusing market potatoes is not recommended due to disease risk and the likelihood that they were treated with sprout inhibitors. Seed potatoes should be "chitted" (pre-sprouted) before planting by placing them in a cool, bright location for 2-4 weeks before the intended planting date. Chitting produces strong, dark-green shoots that give the plants a head start at planting. Seed tubers with multiple strong sprouts can be cut into pieces, each bearing at least two eyes, and allowed to dry for 24-48 hours before planting to reduce the risk of soil-borne rot entering through the cut surface. Dusting cut surfaces with wood ash or sulfur provides additional protection against cut-seed decay.

## Potato Variety Selection for Organic Systems

Organic growers benefit from selecting varieties with inherent disease resistance, as the organic toolkit for managing potato diseases is more limited than the conventional one. Late blight resistance is the most critical trait for organic production in humid climates; varieties such as 'Sarpo Mira', 'Kennebec', and 'Defender' have demonstrated strong resistance in field conditions. Early-maturing varieties (completing their cycle in 70-80 days) can sometimes escape late blight outbreaks by maturing before conditions favor the disease. Scab-resistant varieties like 'Russet Burbank' and 'Superior' reduce reliance on pH management for scab prevention. For flavor and storage quality, heirloom varieties such as 'Bintje', 'Yellow Finn', and 'German Butterball' remain popular among organic gardeners despite their somewhat lower disease resistance. Cervantes recommends growing at least two or three varieties with different maturities and disease resistance profiles to spread risk across the growing season.
