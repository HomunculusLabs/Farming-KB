
# Mycorrhizal Networks (Common Mycelial Network)

The "[[wood-wide-web]]" — [[mycorrhizal-networks]] connect individual plants into an underground network through shared [[fungal-hyphae]]. Through these networks, plants can transfer carbon, nutrients, water, and chemical signals between individuals, including between different species. This is one of the most significant ecological discoveries of recent decades.

## How Networks Form

### Establishment
- AMF or ECM hyphae from one plant extend through soil and colonize nearby plants
- A single fungal individual can connect dozens to hundreds of plants
- Networks form naturally in vegetated areas — forests, grasslands, gardens
- Networks are especially dense in perennial plant communities

### Structure
- Extraradical hyphae form the physical connections
- Hyphal bridges link roots of different plants
- Networks can extend over meters to hundreds of meters in forests
- Individual fungal genets can cover hectares in old-growth forests

## What Flows Through Networks

### Carbon Transfer
- Carbon moves from larger, sun-exposed plants to shaded seedlings
- "Nurse trees" transfer carbon to understory seedlings
- Mother trees preferentially feed their own offspring (kin recognition demonstrated in [[douglas-fir]])
- Carbon can flow both directions depending on source-sink relationships

### Nutrient Transfer
- Nitrogen moves from nitrogen-fixing plants (legumes) to neighboring plants
- Phosphorus moves from phosphorus-rich to phosphorus-poor plants
- Transfer is driven by concentration gradients between plants

### Water Transfer
- Hydraulic lift: deep-rooted plants pull water up through the network
- Water redistributed to shallow-rooted neighbors during drought
- Demonstrated in many tree species

### Chemical Signals
- **Defense signals**: Plants under herbivore attack send chemical warnings through the network
- Neighboring plants receive signals and activate their own defenses before attack
- **Allelochemicals**: Some plants may send toxic compounds through networks to suppress competitors
- **Stress signals**: Drought, pathogen, and nutrient stress signals propagate through networks

## Ecological Significance

### Forest Dynamics
- Networks facilitate regeneration: seedlings connected to mature trees have higher survival
- "Mother trees" (large, old trees) are network hubs — connecting the most plants
- When mother trees die, their carbon is distributed through the network to neighbors
- Forest resilience depends on network integrity

### Plant Community Composition
- Networks can increase biodiversity by connecting species
- Networks can also favor dominant species by facilitating resource transfer
- Net effect depends on network structure and plant species involved

### Seedling Establishment
- Seedlings connected to mycorrhizal networks have:
  - Higher survival rates (10-50% improvement)
  - Faster growth
  - Better nutrient status
  - Greater drought tolerance
- This is why maintaining undisturbed soil under trees is so important for regeneration

## Threats to Networks

### Physical Disturbance
- Tilling severs hyphal connections (network destroyed in minutes)
- [[soil-compaction]] crushes hyphae
- Excavation removes network entirely
- Recovery takes months to years

### Chemical
- Fungicides directly kill [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]
- High P fertilizer reduces plant dependency on AMF (networks shrink)
- Herbicides may indirectly affect networks by removing host plants

### Climate Change
- Drought may stress networks (hyphae desiccate)
- Warming may shift fungal community composition
- CO2 enrichment may alter carbon flow patterns

## Conservation

### Forestry
- Retain living trees and root systems during harvest (avoid clear-cutting where possible)
- Minimize soil disturbance
- Maintain understory plants as network links
- Allow natural regeneration rather than planting bare-root seedlings

### Agriculture
- Reduce tillage (no-till or minimum till)
- Plant cover crops to maintain networks between cash crops
- Avoid fungicides where possible
- Rotate crops that support AMF (not brassicas)

## Practical Notes

Sterile technique is essential throughout all cultivation stages. Contamination from competing organisms is the primary cause of cultivation failure.

Environmental control during fruiting significantly impacts yield and quality outcomes.

Regular monitoring and documentation support continuous improvement in cultivation results.

Field observations and practical experience remain the most reliable guides for system optimization.
