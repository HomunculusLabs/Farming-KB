## The 19 Soil Food Web Gardening Rules

### I. Know Your Plant's Microbial Preference

**Rule 1 — Some plants prefer fungal soils; others prefer bacterial soils.**
Different plant types evolved alongside different microbial communities and
depend on specific fungi-to-bacteria ratios for optimal nutrition.

**Rule 2 — Most vegetables, annuals, and grasses prefer nitrate nitrogen and
bacterial soils.** Early-succession plants evolved in disturbed, bacteria-rich
environments where nitrogen is available as nitrate (NO₃⁻).

**Rule 3 — Most trees, shrubs, and perennials prefer ammonium nitrogen and
fungal soils.** Late-succession plants evolved in stable, fungal-dominated
ecosystems where nitrogen is held as ammonium (NH₄⁺) and released slowly.

### II. Inoculation with Compost

**Rule 4 — Compost inoculates beneficial microbes into soils.** Finished
compost is teeming with the full diversity of [[the-soil-food-web]] — bacteria,
fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and more.

**Rule 5 — Adding compost to the surface inoculates soil with the same food
web.** Surface application lets organisms migrate downward naturally, preserving
the existing food web structure rather than disrupting it.

### III. Mulch Strategy: Fungi vs. Bacteria

**Rule 6 — Aged/brown organic materials support fungi; fresh/green materials
support bacteria.** Browns (leaves, wood chips) have high C:N ratios favoring
fungi; greens (grass clippings, kitchen scraps) have lower ratios fueling
bacteria.

**Rule 7 — Surface mulch supports fungi; worked-in mulch supports bacteria.**
Fungi build networks from the surface down. Mixing mulch into soil breaks those
networks and favors fast-colonizing bacteria.

**Rule 8 — Wet and ground mulch speeds bacterial colonization.** Moisture and
smaller particle size increase surface area, accelerating bacterial breakdown.

**Rule 9 — Coarse, dry mulches support fungal activity.** Large woody pieces
create the slow-decomposition conditions where fungi thrive.

### IV. Targeted Supplements and Teas

**Rule 10 — Sugars help bacteria; kelp, humic/fulvic acids, and phosphate rock
dusts help fungi.** Simple carbohydrates fuel bacterial blooms; complex organic
compounds nourish slower-growing fungi.

**Rule 11 — Choose compost and nutrients to make teas that are fungal,
bacterial, or balanced.** Tailor teas by selecting source compost and foods:
[[fungal-compost]] + humic acids for fungal tea; [[bacterial-compost]] + molasses for
bacterial tea.

**Rule 12 — Compost teas are sensitive to chlorine and preservatives.** Always
use dechlorinated, non-chloraminated water to avoid killing the organisms.

**Rule 14 — Synthetic fertilizers kill most or all [[soil-food-web]] microbes.**
High-concentration salt-based fertilizers create osmotic shock, collapsing the
food web.

**Rule 15 — Stay away from high NPK numbers; follow chemical spraying with
compost tea.** If chemicals must be used, immediately re-inoculate with compost
tea. Prevention through biological health is always preferable.

### VI. Mycorrhizal Associations

**Rule 16 — Most conifers and hardwoods form mycorrhizae with ectomycorrhizal
fungi.** Ectomycorrhizal fungi form a sheath around root tips and extend hyphae
into surrounding soil — characteristic of many forest trees.

**Rule 17 — Most vegetables, annuals, grasses, shrubs, and perennials form
endomycorrhizae.** [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] penetrate root cells directly;
the vast majority of garden plants use this type.

**Rule 19 — Always mix endomycorrhizal fungi with seeds or apply to roots at
transplanting.** Direct contact at the earliest stage establishes the symbiosis
from day one.

### VII. Protect Soil Structure

**Rule 18 — Rototilling destroys or severely damages the soil food web.**
Mechanical tilling shatters fungal hyphae, kills worms and arthropods, and
releases stored carbon. No-till methods preserve the web built over years.

## The Forest Philosophy

> *"No One Ever Fertilized an Old Growth Forest"* (Chapter 22)

Old-growth forests have thrived for millennia without fertilizer, pesticide, or
irrigation — yet they are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth.

The mechanism is self-sustaining:

1. **Every plant produces exudates** — sugars, amino acids, and organic acids
   secreted through roots — that attract specific microorganisms to the
   rhizosphere.
2. **The attracted microbiology converts** unavailable minerals into
   plant-available forms exactly when and where the plant needs them.
3. **The complete soil food web operates** without man-made inputs: bacteria
   and fungi decompose matter; protozoa and nematodes eat microbes, releasing
   nitrogen; larger organisms aerate and structure soil.
4. **Mycorrhizal networks** extend root zones by orders of magnitude, mining
   distant minerals and sharing them between plants.

The lesson: the soil already knows how to grow plants. The gardener's job is to
**feed and protect the soil food web** that feeds the plant.

## Benefits of Restoring the Soil Food Web

- **Improved soil structure** — Fungal glomalin and bacterial exudates bind
  particles into aggregates, improving tilth and resisting compaction.
- **Mycorrhizal assistance** — Extended nutrient reach and inter-plant sharing.
- **Pathogen competition** — Diverse microbiology outcompetes disease organisms.
- **Correct nitrogen form** — Nitrate for annuals, ammonium for perennials.
- **Water management** — Aggregated soil both drains excess and retains
  moisture during drought.
- **Pollutant decomposition** — Soil organisms break down pesticides and
  contaminants over time.
- **Better taste and appearance** — Higher nutrient density and longer shelf
  life.
