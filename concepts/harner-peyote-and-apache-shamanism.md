# Peyote and Apache Shamanism

The chapter by L. Bryce Boyer, Ruth M. Boyer, and Harry W. Basehart in Harner's volume documents peyote use among the Mescalero Apache of New Mexico, providing a detailed case study of how a hallucinogen was adopted into shamanic practice and subsequently abandoned. This chapter offers a unique perspective on the social dynamics of hallucinogen use, showing that the introduction of a powerful psychoactive substance into a shamanic system can destabilize rather than reinforce the existing religious framework.




## Historical Background

The Apaches of the Mescalero Reservation include members of three tribes, in order of descending numbers: Mescaleros, Chiricahuas, and Lipans. The reservation was established in 1873 for the Mescaleros. The Chiricahuas were taken as prisoners of war in 1886 after Geronimo's capitulation; when freed in 1913, most chose to move to the reservation. The Lipans were largely destroyed as functioning groups during the latter half of the nineteenth century.

Nineteenth-century sources document peyote use among the Mescaleros from approximately 1867, the Chiricahuas from about 1875, and the Lipans from about 1885. Despite this documented history, the Apaches were excluded from early maps of peyote distribution and listed as non-users in official publications. The Mescaleros reportedly learned peyote rites from the Lipans before 1870, or from the Tonkawas, Yaquis, or other non-Apachean groups of northern Mexico. With the exception of slight experimentation with marijuana and perhaps LSD by younger people, the reservation Apaches used no other hallucinogenic drugs besides alcohol.




## Apache Religious Concepts

The Apaches conceive the world to be permeated by supernatural power (diy) that has no intrinsic attribute of good or evil; its virtue resides in its potency. Power approaches people through the agency of a plant, animal, or natural phenomenon by means of a dream or hallucinatory experience. Its acceptance is frequently accompanied by an ordeal. Ritual instruction may be received directly from the power or from other shamans. Any person is a possible power recipient. Morris Opler described the Mescaleros as "a tribe of shamans, active or potentially active."

An individual might own any number of powers. If he uses power for purposes not oriented toward the common good, he is accorded the status of witch. Those who use their powers for the group's benefit are called shamans, yet they are implicitly witches since a shaman who saves a life must then sacrifice his own or that of a loved one. Jealousies, enmities, and suspicion abound. Each shaman has private instructions and individually owned rites. Consistent with native concepts of leadership and authority, there has never been a chief shaman.




## Peyote Camp Ceremonies

Mescalero shamans acquired peyote power through personal contact with supernatural power invested in peyote flowers or "buttons." Various shamans became leaders of peyote camps where curing and other ceremonies were conducted. During these rites, multiple shamans and other participants used peyote, experiencing the usual perceptual and logical distortions, hallucinations, and physical effects. Whether the Lipans had a formal peyote camp is not known.

The peyote ceremonies were not accompanied by the acceptance of Christian beliefs and practices. The Mescaleros never became involved in the Peyote Religion (Native American Church). Instead, the use of peyote was intended to affirm the vitality of traditional religious practices at a time when the impact of reservation confinement contributed to an increased awareness of social and cultural deprivation.




## The Fundamental Incongruity

A critical finding of this chapter is what the authors call the "fundamental incongruity between the principles involved in ordinary Mescalero shamanistic ceremonies and the rules that applied to peyote rites."

In ordinary shamanistic practices, a single shaman is the principal figure. The experiences of attendants at ceremonies are subordinate. Religious ecstasy, visions, and communications with supernaturals are the shaman's prerogatives and validate his power and efficacy. The use of peyote by other people at ceremonies made its psychological and physiological effects common, and the uniqueness of the shaman's experiences disappeared. The peyote meetings became places where shamanistic rivalries and witchcraft flourished. Disruption resulted rather than cohesiveness through shared experience.

This is a crucial insight: shared hallucinogenic experience does not necessarily produce social cohesion. In the Mescalero case, it actively undermined the existing shamanic hierarchy by democratizing the visionary experience.




## The Abandonment of Peyote

Eventually, the antagonisms became so open and bloody that the peyote gatherings were abandoned. The hostilities which became overt during the meetings were ascribed to the peyote itself. Since its use involved witchcraft practices, its ingestion was equated with the potential for witchcraft. By 1958-71, the authors were unable to confirm any peyote use on the reservation. No one had knowledge of peyote use by the Chiricahuas.




## Peyote as Intrinsically Evil

Perhaps the most remarkable finding is that peyote power is unique among the Mescaleros in being uniformly considered bad. While Apache power generally has no intrinsic moral attribute, peyote is exceptional. Some Mescaleros also believe the owl is intrinsically evil (its hoot presages death), but even this is debated. Peyote's negative status is unequivocal.

During 1959-60 there were thirteen accredited shamans on the reservation, plus approximately fifteen "pseudoshamans" considered imposters. Of the shamans, only two were at times judged to be witches, and both were considered to still use peyote in illicit witchcraft and love magic ceremonies. The shamans, as legitimate possessors of peyote power, were not punished by the power. But the pseudoshamans had peyote "turn back" on them: one lost a leg in an accident, another had a close relative killed and another lose a limb.




## Socialization Patterns and Aggression

The authors link the dynamics of peyote use and abandonment to Apache socialization patterns. Child-rearing techniques tend to be uniform in emotional content. Gross inconsistency in maternal care is typical: the baby may be afforded tender, loving care but periodically the mother impulsively abandons the infant to the supervision of other children for hours or days while she engages in narcissistic pursuits. A husband's attention is no more constant. Under such conditions, the development of basic trust (Erikson) is stultified, producing marked ambivalence and suspiciousness.

With the birth of a new baby (usually when the previous child is 18-24 months old), the older child is abruptly and often brutally displaced, creating intense sibling rivalry that is strongly disapproved but insecurely repressed. In the aboriginal situation, other socialization practices directed hostilities outward toward outsiders, witches, and ghosts. During the long period when the Apaches were nomadic hunters, gatherers, and raiders, such externalization of aggression was functional. Reservation confinement removed these legitimate outlets, and peyote use, which made hallucinatory experiences common to all participants, intensified internal rivalries beyond what the social system could contain.




## Broader Implications

The Apache case demonstrates that the social consequences of hallucinogen use are not uniformly positive or negative but depend critically on the pre-existing social structure, personality patterns, and the relationship between individual and communal religious experience. The chapter challenges any simple assumption that the introduction of hallucinogens into a shamanic system will necessarily strengthen it. In the Mescalero case, peyote destabilized the shamanic hierarchy by democratizing visionary access and amplifying pre-existing patterns of suspicion and aggression.
