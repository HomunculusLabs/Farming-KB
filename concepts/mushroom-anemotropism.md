## Mushroom Anemotropism

Anemotropism is the growth response of an organism to wind or air currents. In mushrooms, anemotropism describes the phenomenon where the stipe (stem) of a basidiocarp grows into the direction of airflow, orienting the fruiting body toward moving air rather than away from it. This behavior was systematically documented by Edmond R. Badham in a 1982 study of *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]* published in *Mycologia* (Vol. 74, No. 2, pp. 275-279).

## Mechanism of Wind-Directed Growth

The proposed mechanism for anemotropic curvature involves the redistribution of the boundary layer around the stipe. In an airflow, the boundary layer is thicker on the leeward (downwind) side than on the windward (upwind) side of the mushroom. This asymmetric boundary layer creates differential evaporative demand across the stipe surface:

- **Windward side**: Exposed to greater drying effect due to thinner boundary layer and more rapid removal of water vapor from the immediate surface
- **Leeward side**: Sheltered within a thicker boundary layer, with reduced evaporative stress and higher local humidity

This gradient in evaporative demand may cause cells on the windward side to elongate at different rates compared to the leeward side, producing a bending curvature toward the wind. The process is influenced by at least four environmental factors that interact to determine the magnitude and direction of the response:

1. **Wind velocity** — drives boundary layer asymmetry and is the primary stimulus for the response; higher velocities increase the differential between windward and leeward sides
2. **Humidity** — modulates the magnitude of evaporative demand; lower humidity amplifies the windward-leeward gradient, while very high humidity may diminish the response
3. **Light** — heats the mushroom surface, increasing evaporation rates on the illuminated side; can either reinforce or compete with wind-driven asymmetry depending on light direction relative to wind
4. **Temperature** — affects the overall rate of water loss and the capacity of air to hold moisture; interacts with humidity to determine the absolute evaporative demand

The interaction of these factors means that anemotropism is not a simple directional response but rather a complex integration of multiple environmental signals that converge on the physical process of differential water loss across the stipe surface. Water vapor is likely the primary gas affected by boundary layer redistribution, but other gases including ethylene, CO₂, and O₂ may also be redistributed by airflow and contribute to the tropic response.

## Experimental Evidence in Psilocybe Cubensis

Badham's wind tunnel experiments used approximately 100 developing basidiocarps of *Psilocybe cubensis* under carefully controlled conditions:

- **Wind velocity**: 9 ± 3 cm/sec (measured with TSI hot wire anemometer Model 1650)
- **Humidity**: 94 ± 3% RH (measured with wet and dry copper constantan thermocouples, with automated humidifier control via reed switch relay)
- **Temperature**: 24.5 ± 1.5°C (controlled via Thermotrack 11 temperature proportioner with heat cable)
- **Light**: Cool white fluorescent at 60 cm, 12h photoperiod (2685 lux)
- **Energy flux**: 0.002 calories/cm²/min (Eppley radiometer Model PSI)

The wind tunnel featured a working area of 43 × 30 × 20 cm with a closed circuit design. Air passed through a polyester fiber filter to slow wind speed, a spin-type humidifier with heater for humidity control, and an eight-sided air baffle to ensure laminar flow in the working area. A water bath surrounding the working area served as both light filter and insulation.

Cultures were prepared by inoculating autoclaved brown rice (6 g per 20 ml deionized water) in 50 mm Petri plates. After hyphal colonization, the substrate was transferred to 13 cm pie tins with vermiculite and 25 ml water, covered with glass, and placed under fluorescent light until primordia reached 15-25 mm. A sterile mutant strain was also studied alongside the fertile strain.

Key findings from the experiments:

- Primordia placed in the wind tunnel with stipes between Stage 1 (slight pileus swelling) and Stage 2 (nearly spherical pileus) grew **into** the airflow over the first 24 hours
- This occurred regardless of whether the initial orientation was toward or away from the wind, indicating a true tropic response rather than passive bending
- When cultures were rotated perpendicular to the wind at 3 rpm, growth proceeded **upward** ([[coprophilous-habitat-negative-geotropism-spore-dispersal-adaptation]] |
| Stage 4 | More or less plane pileus (late maturity) | Negative geotropism |

The shift from anemotropism to negative geotropism at Stage 2 is one of the most significant findings. This second tropism masks the effect of the first and is linked to the onset of spore production. The recurvature (upward bending of the pileus away from the wind direction with lamellae facing downward) does not occur in sterile strains or in the absence of light, reinforcing the connection between geotropism, sporulation, and photic requirements.

## Comparison with Phycomyces

The genus *Phycomyces* (a zygomycete fungus, not a basidiomycete) demonstrates a well-characterized anemotropic response in addition to an [[adaptive-anemotropism-spore-dispersal-optimization-mushroom-evolution]]**: Growing into the airflow positions the pileus for efficient wind-assisted spore release and dispersal, maximizing the distance spores travel.

3. **Evaporative seeking**: Directed growth toward areas of greater evaporative demand may support transpiration-driven water and nutrient transport.
4. **Boundary layer management**: Growing into the wind may optimize gas exchange (CO₂ and O₂) critical for respiration during rapid stipe elongation.

## Relationship to Phototropism

Light plays a complex role in modulating anemotropic responses. At least four different responses to light have been described for mushrooms (Eger-Hummel, 1980), and *Psilocybe cubensis* is similar to *Coprinus congregatus* in this respect. In Badham's experiments:

- The degree to which anemotropism operates in field conditions versus controlled wind tunnel environments is unknown
- Most studies have focused on *Psilocybe cubensis*; the universality of anemotropic responses across agarics and other mushroom-forming fungi remains to be determined
- Decapitation experiments in other species have linked growth hormones to the lamellae, but the specific hormones involved in *P. cubensis* tropisms have not been identified
- Agar diffusates from gills can partially replace the influence of the pileus in other species, suggesting a diffusible tropic factor, but this has not been tested in *Psilocybe*
