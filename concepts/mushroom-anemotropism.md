# Mushroom Anemotropism (Wind Response)

Anemotropism is the growth response of an organism to wind or air flow. In
mushrooms, anemotropism causes stipe (stem) growth to be directed into the
wind during early development. This response was systematically studied in
[[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]] by Edmond Badham (1982) using controlled wind tunnel
experiments.

## Overview

Anemotropism in P. cubensis manifests as a pronounced bending of the stipe
toward the direction of air flow during the early stages of basidiocarp
development. When mushroom primordia between Stage 1 (slight pileus swelling)
and Stage 2 (nearly spherical pileus) are placed in a wind tunnel, stipe
growth is directed into the air flow. This response occurs regardless of
light direction and in both fertile and sterile basidiocarps.

## Badham's Wind Tunnel Experiments

### Experimental Setup

Badham studied approximately 100 developing basidiocarps in a closed-circuit
wind tunnel with controlled parameters:

- **Working area**: 43 × 30 × 20 cm
- **Wind velocity**: 9 ± 3 cm/sec (measured with TSI hot wire anemometer)
- **Humidity**: 94 ± 3% RH (wet and dry thermocouples)
- **Temperature**: 24.5 ± 1.5°C
- **Light**: "cool white" fluorescent, 12 h/day at 2685 lux (0.002
  cal/cm²/min)
- **Cultures**: grown on autoclaved brown rice (6g / 20ml water) in 50mm
  Petri plates, transferred to pie tins with vermiculite

### Key Observations

1. **Growth into wind** — when not rotated, stipes grew toward the air flow
2. **Upright growth when rotated** — when cultures were rotated perpendicular
   to the wind (3 rpm), growth was upward (negative geotropism dominated)
3. **No light dependence** — the anemotropic response occurred in both light
   and darkness
4. **Both fertile and sterile** — the response was observed in both normal
   and [[sporeless-mutant-geotropism-link-psilocybe-cubensis]] basidiocarps
5. **Reversed placement** — cultures placed with stipes directed away from
   the wind still bent toward the flow
6. **Stage-dependent** — the response was most pronounced in Stage 1–2
   development; bending was less pronounced at Stage 2 or later

## Proposed Mechanism: Evaporative Demand Gradient

Badham proposed that anemotropism results from a redistribution of the
boundary layer around the stipe:

1. **Boundary layer asymmetry** — wind creates a thicker boundary layer on
   the leeward (downwind) side than on the windward side
2. **Differential drying** — the windward side experiences greater
   evaporative demand (drying effect) due to thinner boundary layer and
   faster air movement
3. **Differential cell growth** — cells on the two sides of the stipe grow
   at different rates in response to the moisture gradient, causing the
   stipe to bend toward the wind (the side with greater evaporative demand)

### Evaporative Demand Factors

The drying effect is a function of at least four [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]]:

- **Wind velocity** — increases evaporative demand on windward side
- **Humidity** — lower humidity amplifies the differential
- **Light** — heats the mushroom surface, increasing water evaporation
- **Temperature** — affects both evaporation rate and cellular metabolism

### Comparison with Phycomyces

The zygomycete fungus Phycomyces blakesleeanus shows a similar anemotropism
in addition to an avoidance response. Johnson and Gamow (1971) demonstrated
the necessity of wind and the importance of water vapor to these responses.
The mechanism in P. cubensis may be analogous. Russo et al. (1977) suggested
that ethylene may be involved in the avoidance response in Phycomyces, and a
similar mechanism might operate in basidiomycetes.

## Alternative Mechanism: Mechanical Force

An alternative explanation is that the mushroom is responding to the physical
force of the wind rather than to evaporative effects. Studies on Phycomyces
sporangiophores (Dennison, 1961) showed that growth responds to both gravity
and centrifugal force, suggesting mechanical stimuli can influence fungal
growth direction. However, the evaporative [[evaporative-demand-hypothesis-mushroom-tropism]] is more widely
supported.

## Adaptive Significance

Anemotropism may serve two adaptive functions:

1. **Optimal spore dispersal position** — growing toward air flow could help
   mushrooms position their caps for the most effective spore release and
   dispersal, as wind is the primary agent of spore transport

2. **Spacing of clustered fruitbodies** — in cespitose (clustered) species,
   directed growth toward the evaporative demand could increase spacing
   between adjacent stipes, reducing competition and improving air flow
   around individual mushrooms

## Interaction with Other Tropisms

Anemotropism is the dominant directional stimulus during early stipe
elongation (Stage 1). At Stage 2, when spore production begins, negative
geotropism becomes dominant and masks the anemotropic response. The two
tropisms interact throughout development:

- **Stage 1**: anemotropism dominant (growth toward wind)
- **Stage 2 transition**: negative geotropism emerges (cap turns upward)
- **Stage 3–4**: negative geotropism fully expressed (cap opens horizontally)

The transition between tropisms is associated with the onset of sporulation
and appears to be hormonally regulated, with substances produced in the gills
modifying the growth response.

## Rotation Experiments

When basidiocarps were rotated into and parallel to the air flow at 3 rpm,
the result was continued stipe elongation with the pileus opening into the
wind. This demonstrates that when gravity effects are equalized by rotation,
the evaporative demand gradient (driven by wind) continues to influence growth
orientation, even at later developmental stages.

## Cultivation Implications

Understanding anemotropism has practical implications for [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]:

- **Uneven air flow** in [[fruiting-chambers-and-environmental-control]] can cause mushrooms to grow in
  irregular directions, reducing yield and quality
- **Air circulation design** should aim for gentle, uniform air movement to
  minimize directional stress on developing fruitbodies
- **Fans in fruiting chambers** should be positioned to avoid creating
  strong directional air currents that could trigger anemotropic bending
- **Rotating trays** or periodic repositioning can help ensure even growth
  if air flow patterns are unavoidably directional

## Veil as Protective Structure

Badham suggested that the partial veil (which covers the gills during early
development) may serve a dual tropic function: by protecting the uppermost
and unexpanded portion of the stipe from wind and light, the veil may
prevent premature anemotropic or phototropic bending before the mushroom is
ready for the geotropic readjustment that precedes spore release.
