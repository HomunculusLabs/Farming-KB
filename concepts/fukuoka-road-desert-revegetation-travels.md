# Fukuoka's Desert Revegetation Travels and Projects

In [[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]], [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] documents his travels to deserts around the world and his conviction that natural farming methods can revegetate even the most barren landscapes. This extends his [[fukuoka-natural-farming-method]] from individual fields to entire biomes.

## California: The Desert That Wasn't Always Desert

Fukuoka's first encounter with desert landscapes came during his 1979 visit to California. Flying over the American West, he was struck by the "bleak, yellow land" stretching endlessly below — a stark contrast to the rich mixture of trees and vegetation in Japan. The Californian plain was "an endless stretch of dry, yellow land" covered mainly with foxtail and wild barley, the grasses "amber yellow rather than green."

He asked why California had turned to desert and received unsatisfying answers. His own analysis was that "at some point, California became a desert" through human intervention — the simplification of vegetative cover, overgrazing, and monoculture farming. He observed that "the pattern of monoculture is all too clear" from the sky, with fields showing "an alternating pattern of green and brown stripes." This depletes soil fertility, "setting into motion a negative cycle."

## California Observations: Irrigation and Eucalyptus

Fukuoka's California observations went deeper than a first impression. Driving from the San Francisco airport to Berkeley, he noticed that mountains along the way were "the same drab, sandy color" with topsoil washed out and bare rock exposed. The grasses covering the plain were "amber yellow rather than green" — extremely poor land covered mainly with forage grasses such as foxtail and wild barley. Isolated desert-type trees were scattered over the yellow plains.

Wherever there was green, water was invariably being drawn for irrigation. Large fields of tomatoes or other crops measuring hundreds of acres would "emerge suddenly" from the yellow, but "without irrigation, the fields turn into parched grassland." Even the cattle were not allowed to graze leisurely in rolling green pasture; green grasses were grown in one special field while cattle "left to graze wearily in the middle of a scorching field of yellow grasses."

The cities of Berkeley and Los Angeles were "carpeted with a solid layer of green," but Fukuoka recognized this as "the artificial green of lawns and protected trees; it is not the green of nature." San Francisco, Berkeley, and Los Angeles were, in his view, "like artificial islands in the middle of a desert."

Traveling through the mountains on the outskirts of San Francisco, Fukuoka came upon large groves of eucalyptus trees. "All the large trees were eucalyptus. But this is not a tree native to California. It comes from Australia." The reason eucalyptus grew so hardily was that "there are no native American trees around to speak of." The crytomeria and cypress on university campuses had not originally grown there either. "As soon as one leaves town, the land becomes bleak and yellow again."

## California Was Not Originally Desert

Fukuoka's most important California deduction came from comparative observation. At a Zen Center on the San Francisco coast, the garden sat at the bottom of a valley encircled by eucalyptus trees, with "yellow, bare-topped mountains" surrounding the site on all sides. But continuing to the shore, he saw a mountain on one side covered with a green forest where Japanese pines had been planted fifty years earlier — now the site of a luxury housing development. The mountain on the other side was barren desert.

"Here, under identical climatic conditions, was an expanse of green on one side and a desert on the other. Why?"

His conclusion was decisive: "California was not originally desert at all. Something had probably caused it to dry out. If so, then it just might be possible to revive this land." He visited a redwood forest twenty minutes inland from the coast — a virgin forest with trees two or three hundred years old, some measuring twenty or twenty-five feet in circumference. During the last ice age, a few spots in California were spared from the glaciers, creating "glacier forests" where trees two thousand years old and 400 feet high still stand. About one-third of the undergrowth consisted of vegetation similar to that found in Japan. "Here, in the middle of an American desert we have a sacred forest where Japanese grasses grow among the underbrush."

## Monoculture and "Agriculture Run Amok"

Fukuoka extended his California observations to a broader critique of American agriculture. He identified a pattern he called "agriculture run amok" — the vast monoculture belts where "producing nothing but soybeans in the soybean belt and nothing but wheat in the wheat belt with large machinery on enormous farms" was considered "the height of rationality." He saw the same mentality in California's irrigated tomato fields stretching for hundreds of acres.

From the air, "the pattern of monoculture is all too clear," with fields showing "an alternating pattern of green and brown stripes." Each crop extracts specific nutrients while the soil receives no diversity of organic matter in return. This depletes soil fertility and "sets into motion a negative cycle." The American farmers who "stoutly defended monoculture" and the consumers who "can without the least concern eat the same food day after day" were, in Fukuoka's view, products of the same impoverished mindset.

## Mechanized Agriculture and Soil Destruction

Fukuoka's critique of mechanized agriculture focuses on what happens to the soil itself. He explains that when a farmer floods his rice paddy for six months and runs a tiller through it, "turning the soil into something akin to adobe mud," the constant activity "kills the microbes and drives the air from the earth." The soil breaks down into smaller and smaller particles until the farmer "must run his plow deep through the field to let air into the earth."

"All that is extra work. Man creates the conditions that require him to plow each year." In contrast, "if the land is not abandoned, but instead left in a natural state; if, as in the mountain forests, the earth is not plowed or tilled, but left to itself, in a few thousand years, a rich soil develops." Man "kills and destroys the soil," then takes samples to the laboratory, runs tests, and concludes that deeper plowing produces higher yields — a conclusion that is perfectly logical for dead soil under unnatural conditions but entirely wrong for living soil.

This connects to his [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] argument: the biological "tilling" performed by plant roots and small animals works the soil more deeply than any machine. "When left alone, the soil deepens and enriches naturally." Mechanical tilling "only kills it."

## The Dream of Sowing Seeds from the Sky

Seven years after his first flight, Fukuoka began "kicking up a row" about revegetation. News reached the United Nations, and he was invited by an officer in the Plan of Action to Combat Desertification, who asked if he could "come up with some ideas for greening the deserts." Fukuoka felt "almost embarrassed" by how earnest the officer was.

His core conviction: "the natural way of farming that I have practiced now for more than forty years could serve as well as a means for stopping and revegetating the deserts." This would "naturally transform a ravaged area into land where abundant grasses and trees grow in harmony with crops," and because no fertilizers or pesticides are used, "this might be useful also as a low-cost method of farming arid lands."

## Aerial Seed Sowing

Fukuoka's ultimate vision for desert revegetation was aerial seed sowing. He wanted to "run tests on methods of scattering drought-hardy clover seeds over arid lands by airplane." In his [[fukuoka-road-africa-greening-desert]] work, he found that this approach was not understood by the Somali government, but the dream persisted.

On his American travels, Fukuoka practiced roadside sowing. Riding through Oregon and California, he and companions "talked in the car of scattering daikon and green manure seeds directly onto the parched wasteland along the road." Young people took out bags filled with seed and scattered them. In Ashland, Oregon, after one of his lectures, a pilot stood up and offered his plane, "equipped with a gun for blasting the seeds into the soil," for sowing seed. An old woman offered her 750-acre piece of wasteland for the experiment.

## Rejection of Conventional Approaches

Fukuoka rejects the standard anti-desertification approaches of civil engineering — "drawing water, building dams, and constructing irrigation systems." He notes that in places like Somalia, large farms built with foreign aid featured "long irrigation canals on great levees" that were eventually abandoned. A 500-hectare farm built with U.S. aid had all its low bushes and shrubs cleared before bulldozing, and attempts to plant crops were unsuccessful. "When we visited the place, it looked like the empty remains of an abandoned airfield." He argues that irrigation results in "salt buildup" in the fields and is fundamentally unsustainable.

## The Plant Irrigation Method

Instead of canals, Fukuoka proposes what he calls "plant irrigation": "instead of making canals, what I would do is create a green belt consisting of various types of trees, using the roots of these trees to draw in river water underground and slowly penetrate dry ground away from the riverside." Or he would "employ special plants to draw up and store this ground water so as to make it available for use."

## The United Nations Connection

After his Africa trip, Fukuoka was contacted by Henri Lucy, a Frenchman living in Africa, who had learned of his work. Lucy said "he would propose in several countries that seed be sown by airplane and would try to have the U.N. do this." Lucy appeared to be "the person in charge of such matters at the U.N."

## The Spaniards Brought Bad Grasses

Fukuoka traced California's desertification to a specific historical event. He visited a redwood forest that had been preserved for two hundred years as a national park. "Outside the forest, just ten minutes away by car, the land is parched desert. But here, we have the complete reverse — a thick, lush forest." An Indian chief told him "that things had gotten screwed up when the Spaniards arrived and began raising cattle."

After investigation, Fukuoka concluded that "the pasture grasses the Spaniards had brought over with them contained foxtail seeds, and it was this that dominates the vegetation throughout California." The foxtail "grows to such a great density that other plants are unable to take well in the same ground. That is why the fields and hills in California have all turned yellow." The heat reflected from the yellow ground "raises it to forty degrees (104°F). Such a rise in temperature turns the place into a scorching desert."

## The Geological Connection to Japan

At the California state capitol in Sacramento, Fukuoka met a mineralogist who revealed that "the parent rock for Japan is the same as that for the region around San Francisco. Moreover, Hokkaido and the nearby islands have the same parent rock as southern Canada." The distribution of parent rock is "exactly the same on both sides of the Pacific." Japan has Mt. Fuji and California has Mt. Shasta — both large volcanos "similarly located." This geological kinship suggested to Fukuoka that Japanese plants could grow in California, and that the desert was not natural but human-made.

## The Zen Center Experiment

At the Zen Center on the San Francisco coast, founded by Shunryu Suzuki Roshi, Fukuoka found 400 members — "working people, students, and others who come and commute to work while undergoing training here." They meditated morning and evening and "tended a small, half-acre vegetable garden in which they grew the food they ate." The center practiced organic farming but concentrated "largely on spices, growing only a very limited selection of vegetables." Fukuoka was struck by how "yellow, bare-topped mountains surround the site on all sides" and showed them how to properly use tools like the hoe and scythe.

## The Global Desert Crisis

Fukuoka connected California's plight to a worldwide pattern. In Korea, China's Great Wall region, and the Silk Road, "trees are so far gone that no trees remain standing." In Brazil, "Japanese trading companies began to cut down the Amazonian rain forests" and "forests have given way to desert." The dense forests of Thailand and Southeast Asia "have been razed, leaving behind a barren land." The "global decline in vegetation has advanced to such a degree that tree-planting campaigns are clearly not enough."
