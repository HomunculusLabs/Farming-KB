# Desert Temperature Regimes and Soil Chemistry

Deserts present some of the most extreme environmental conditions on Earth, yet they support
remarkable ecosystems when water is available. Understanding the interplay between temperature
regimes, soil chemistry, and biological response is essential for any permaculture or land
management effort in arid regions.

## Desert Ecosystem Response After Rain

When rains finally arrive in arid landscapes, the transformation is sudden and dramatic. Life
becomes riotous and breeding proceeds unconfined. Fish breed and grow to maturity in just a
few months. Sea-birds — pelicans, flamingos, herons, terns, gulls, waders, and ducks — occupy
fresh waters for extended periods. Mammals including antelope and kangaroo flock to waterholes,
joined by desert quail, pigeons, finches, and parrots drawn to the brief abundance.

This boom, however, is followed by an inevitable collapse. Waters gradually turn salty, then
dry up entirely. Life scatters, dies, or returns to a state of dormancy and rest. Seeds are
blown across the landscape and buried, awaiting the next rainfall event.

The key strategy for permaculture in deserts is to capture the approximately 88% of water
that either evaporates or rushes away unused across the landscape. By storing this water below
ground, the growth period for plants can be significantly prolonged, and rehumidification of
the local environment occurs through plant transpiration. Naturally, only about 0.8% of
rainfall in arid regions recharges aquifers — the overwhelming majority is lost to evaporation
and runoff without intervention.

## Air Temperature Patterns

Air temperatures in deserts (up to about 60 metres in height) follow a pronounced daily cycle.
Temperatures typically rise by 10–25°C over the course of a day, peaking between 12pm and 3pm.
Pre-dawn temperatures at 6am range from 8°C to 30°C depending on the season and location.

Both air and soil temperatures follow a slower annual cycle that depends on latitude and the
amount of solar radiation received. This annual cycle affects soils to a depth of approximately
2 metres, where the annual fluctuation is reduced to about 5°C — a significant moderating
effect compared to the dramatic surface swings.

## Soil Temperature Patterns

Soil temperatures closely follow the air temperature curve but peak approximately one hour
earlier than air temperatures. The coldest soil temperatures occur just before dawn, around
5–6am. The heating effect diminishes rapidly with depth, and there is very little influence of
daily temperature changes at 30cm depth.

Peak surface temperature reduces by approximately 2°C for every 5cm of depth — a total
reduction of about 15°C at 30cm. Soils at this depth maintain fairly constant daily
temperatures, roughly 5°C higher than the lowest surface temperature and about 15°C lower than
the highest. In this way, deeper soil layers effectively even out the extreme heating of the
surface.

On favourable sun-facing slopes, temperatures at just 5cm depth can reach 60–70°C — a level
lethal to young plants. Surface air temperatures in deserts frequently reach 30°C, with
isolated records as high as 52°C (125°F).

## Convectional Currents

Layers of super-heated air that form just above the desert soil surface become unstable and
generate rapid convectional currents. This effect is especially pronounced on sun-facing
slopes where radiant heating is most intense. These thermals and updraughts can produce
cumuliform clouds, or in hot dry periods, clear-air turbulence that shapes local weather
patterns and influences seed dispersal and insect movement.

## Animal Heat Escape Strategies

Desert animals employ a range of strategies to survive extreme temperatures. Many species
burrow below 30cm where daily temperature fluctuations are minimal. Others seek high refuges
in low shrubs, on stumps, or on posts where airflow provides some cooling. Shade-seeking,
nocturnal activity, and the development of efficient cooling strategies and water economy are
all common adaptations.

Shade itself can be a limiting factor in desert environments. For example, the availability
of cave shelters limits the numbers of large desert marsupials that can survive in a given
area. Unsurprisingly, deserts support a far greater proportion of subterranean and nocturnal
fauna than any other biome.

## Soil Humidity and Depth

Soil humidity increases steeply with depth, particularly in dune sands. The average water
content is about 4% in the top metre, rising to 10–20% at depths of 2–6 metres, and reaching
near-saturation at 20–60 metres. For both plants and animals, it is far preferable to draw
water from these deeper, cooler, and more humid layers. This underscores the importance of
designing plant systems with deep root access in arid environments.

## Desert Soil Chemistry

Desert soils are dominantly alkaline, particularly in waterways where surface salts and
carbonates accumulate. Historically, human settlements were located to take advantage of water
runoff concentrating in these areas. A pH of 8.5–9.0 is not atypical, and drying waterholes
can reach pH 10–11.

Despite these extreme values, desert soils have high nutrient potential if the pH can be
adjusted and irrigation water is available. Acidic sandy soils do occur, typically forming
around deeply weathered granites. Both high and low pH areas suffer from low availability of
trace elements. In high-pH soils, deficiencies of zinc, iron, copper, and manganese are
common.

Citrus trees are particularly sensitive indicators: interveinal colour loss, leaf thinning,
and tip curl signal manganese deficiency. Zinc deficiency causes severe leaf yellowing, often
accompanied by manganese deficiency. Copper deficiency produces giant leaves, gum pockets, and
multiple budding or trunking. Remedies include foliar sprays, application of elemental
sulphur, and the use of appropriate oxides and sulphates.

## Non-Wetting Sands and Salt Problems

Non-wetting sands, caused by fungal colonisation of sand grains, prevent water from
penetrating the soil profile effectively. These can be ameliorated through the addition of
bentonite clay or humus, and by constructing swales and raised beds with high edges to retain
moisture.

Salt problems in desert soils can be addressed through flushing with fresh water, building
humus levels, ponding water with algae to encourage biological remediation, ionic or
distillation treatments, and selecting salt-tolerant crops. Notably, free-draining sands can
be irrigated with water containing salt levels up to 1500 ppm — much higher than the
tolerance levels for spray [[mycorrhizal-inoculation]] is essential in arid systems — acacias, eucalypts, legumes,
pines, Casuarinas, and garden crops all benefit from root-associated fungi that enable
phosphate accumulation from otherwise unavailable soil reserves. When applying phosphates, it
is best to use small quantities placed close to the crop to maximise uptake efficiency.
