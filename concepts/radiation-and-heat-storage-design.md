# Radiation and Heat Storage in Design

Radiation — the transfer of heat through electromagnetic waves — is one of the most powerful forces shaping site design in [[permaculture]]. It drives plant growth, determines frost patterns, affects human and animal comfort, and can be harnessed or mitigated through thoughtful placement of structures, vegetation, and materials. Understanding how radiation interacts with surfaces, air, and water is fundamental to creating productive and resilient landscapes.

## Radiation Effects on Living Systems

### Human Health Impacts

Areas of severe direct or diffuse radiation, and especially where the atmosphere is thin (on mountains) or albedo is high (in snow, granite, or white sand areas, or over hayfields in summer), can produce severe radiation burns, skin cancers (very common in Australia), and temporary or long-term blindness. Design must account for these hazards through shading, protective structures, and careful site selection.

### Plant Responses to Radiation

Plants must also be screened against sunburn by partial shade and by white paint on their stems in conditions of severe radiation, especially for young plants. Older plants may suffer bark damage but will generally survive. Both plants and animals have evolved sophisticated mechanisms to cope with excessive radiation:

- Many plants present **whitish undersides of leaves** to light as wind blows, carrying on a dynamic balance between light and wind factors
- Vines and trees may **alter leaf angles** to reflect light, trap air, or reduce the area of leaf exposed to light or wind
- Both **pigmentation and leaf movement** are used to balance the effects of variable incoming energy
- **Leaf pores (stomata) close down** to prevent moisture loss under combined high radiation and wind stress

## Thermosiphon and Automatic Heat Transfer

Automatic transfer of heat via thermosiphon effects is best achieved through several design strategies:

- **Placing heat sources below storage and use points** so that warm air naturally rises to where it is needed
- **Inducing cross-ventilation by building solar chimneys** to draw in cool air from shaded areas
- **Actively fanning heated air to underfloor gravel storages** where solar attics or trapped ceiling heat serve as the heat source
- **Eliminating heat-induced condensation** through the use of heat exchangers

These passive systems reduce or eliminate the need for mechanical heating and cooling, lowering energy consumption while maintaining comfortable temperatures.

## Heat Storage in Massive Materials

### Earth, Brick, Stone, and Concrete

Massive earth, brick, stone, or concrete heat storage masses must be properly insulated to retain heat that would otherwise be lost:

- **Conduction losses** (to the ground) are prevented by solid foam or air-trap insulation such as straw bales
- **Radiation losses** (to the exterior of houses) are prevented by reflection from double-glazed windows or reflective insulation hanging in air spaces

### Reflective Insulation

Reflective insulation is an important but often misunderstood tool in heat management:

- It **does not work** if it is dusty, dirty, or pressed against a conducting surface
- It is **most effective** as free-hanging sheets or ceiling sheets looped loosely across rafters
- It can be kept clean (and effective) only in situations such as solar attics
- **Plain white paint** is an excellent reflector for everyday use on walls or in concentrators

## Plant Selection for Heat Management

All plants with high biomass (especially trees) store heat in their mass, which is mainly water. This has significant design implications:

- Fairly small clearings may be **frost-free in cold climates** due to the thermal mass of surrounding trees
- **Dark evergreen trees** absorb and radiate heat effectively, making them suitable for frost-prone sites where warmth is desired
- **White-barked, shiny, or light-coloured trees** reflect heat, making them valuable in cool districts on forest edges and where light itself is a limiting factor

## Water and Stone as Thermal Mass

Water and stone are excellent heat storage materials due to their **high specific heat**:

- **Bodies of water** serve as exceptional thermal buffers, absorbing heat during the day and releasing it slowly at night
- **Stone walls and rock features** can capture and slowly release solar radiation
- **Air has a low specific heat** and is a very poor conductor of heat, making it a good insulator
- Many insulation systems work simply by **trapping air** or by using materials that are poor conductors (cork, sawdust, wood)

## Design Principles for Radiation

### Maximising Solar Gain

In subtropical to cool climates, the preliminary design choice is to choose house sites for the **maximisation of solar radiation**. This involves:

- Orienting buildings and growing areas toward the equator
- Using reflective surfaces to bounce additional light onto shaded areas
- Placing thermal mass where it will absorb direct sunlight
- Designing glazing to capture low-angle winter sun while excluding high-angle summer sun

### Sheltering from Excessive Radiation

Where excessive heat is a problem (arid and tropical areas), design must focus on **shelter from radiation**:

- Creating shade structures with deciduous plants that leaf out in summer
- Using light-coloured surfaces to reflect unwanted heat
- Channelling excess heat to fuel cross-ventilation systems
- Providing cool, dry air intake to homes through shaded corridors

### Converting and Redirecting Light

Designers should always be aware of opportunities to:

- **Convert light to heat** through absorption by dark surfaces and thermal mass
- **Reflect more heat onto cool areas** using white paint, polished metal, or water surfaces
- **Light dark areas** by reflection or by skylight placement
- **Store heat below insulated slab floors** for slow release through the night

## Forest Edge Microclimates

The interplay of sun and rain around forest edges creates distinct microhabitats (Figure 5.14 in the source):

- **Wet sides** of forest clearings typically face away from the sun (as most rain comes from the polar side of sites)
- **Dry sides** face the sun and experience rain-shadow effects
- These patterns create opportunities for **species-specific placement** based on moisture requirements
- The sunny edges of forests help protect seedlings from frost and are ideal for rearing small trees

Understanding these radiation-driven microclimate patterns allows designers to place plants and structures precisely where conditions suit them best, reducing the need for artificial irrigation, heating, or protection.

## Integrated Design Approach

The key insight from [[permaculture-design]] is that radiation is not a problem to be solved but a force to be directed. Every element on a site — buildings, trees, water bodies, rocks, and even the ground itself — either absorbs, reflects, stores, or transmits radiation. By arranging these elements thoughtfully, the designer can create self-regulating systems where:

- Excess heat from one area fuels ventilation in another
- Thermal mass buffers temperature extremes automatically
- Plant selection reinforces the desired thermal regime
- Reflective surfaces multiply the effective solar collection area
- Insulation preserves stored heat precisely where and when it is needed
