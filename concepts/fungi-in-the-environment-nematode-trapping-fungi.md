# Nematode-Trapping Fungi

Nematode-trapping fungi are a remarkable group of soil-dwelling fungi that capture and kill nematodes using specialized morphological structures called traps. They represent one of the most dramatic examples of predatory behaviour among fungi, with over 200 species distributed across all major taxonomic groups including deuteromycetes, basidiomycetes, chytridiomycetes, and zygomycetes. These fungi are found worldwide, from tropical to Antarctic soils, occurring in both agricultural and forest environments.

## Classification of Nematophagous Fungi

Nematophagous fungi are classified into three broad groups based on their infection mechanisms:

1. **Nematode-trapping fungi**: Attack free-living nematodes using specialized trap structures that develop from hyphal branches. This is the best-known group owing to their remarkable morphological adaptations and dramatic infection process.

2. **Endoparasitic fungi**: Infect nematodes by spore ingestion or adhesion to the nematode cuticle, then proliferate internally.

3. **Egg- and cyst-parasitic fungi**: Infect the sedentary stages of plant-parasitic nematodes using their hyphal tips to penetrate egg masses and cysts.

The majority of identified nematode-trapping species belong to a monophyletic clade among the apothecial ascomycetes, with the exception of a few species including the mushroom *Hohenbuehelia* (asexual state *Nematoctonus*).

## Trap Morphology

There is extensive variation in the morphology of trapping structures, even between closely related species. Four principal trap types are recognized:

- **Adhesive branches**: Erect hyphal branches covered with an adhesive material that snare passing nematodes upon contact.

- **Adhesive networks**: Complex three-dimensional nets, as seen in the well-studied *Arthrobotrys oligospora*. These nets are formed by repeated anastomosis of hyphal branches creating a mesh that physically ensnares nematodes.

- **Adhesive knobs**: Morphologically distinct spherical cells produced on the apex of slender hyphal stalks. A layer of adhesive polymers covers the knob surface. The knob of *Monacrosporium haptotylum* is a well-characterized example.

- **Constricting rings**: Mechanical traps consisting of three-celled rings that inflate rapidly upon nematode contact, constricting around the prey like a noose. *Arthrobotrys dactyloides* is the classic example.

The phylogenetic pattern within the clade of nematode-trapping fungi is concordant with the morphology of the traps, suggesting that trap type is evolutionarily conserved and informative for systematics.

## Infection Process

Following trap development, nematode-trapping fungi infect their hosts through a well-defined sequence of events:

1. **Attachment**: Trap cells adhere to the surface of the nematode via adhesive polymers on the trap surface.

2. **Penetration**: The fungus breaches the nematode cuticle using a combination of mechanical force and enzymatic degradation.

3. **Digestion**: Extracellular enzymes break down the internal tissues of the nematode.

4. **Assimilation**: Nutrients from the killed nematode are absorbed and distributed throughout the fungal mycelium.

Trap formation can occur spontaneously or be induced by environmental signals, including peptides and other compounds secreted by host nematodes.

## Molecular Mechanisms: The Serine Protease PII

The serine protease PII, a subtilisin, was one of the first pathogenicity factors characterized in nematode-trapping fungi. PII was isolated from culture filtrates of *Arthrobotrys oligospora*, and the corresponding gene was cloned. Functional analysis through transformation experiments demonstrated its importance:

- **Deletion mutants** (*DPII*) produced fewer infection structures and showed slightly lower virulence than wild type.
- **Over-expression mutants** with additional copies of the genomic PII gene developed increased numbers of traps and captured nematodes more rapidly.
- **Heterologous expression** of PII in *Aspergillus niger* confirmed its toxic activity when added to free-living nematodes.

## The Lectin AOL

AOL is a carbohydrate-binding lectin from *A. oligospora* belonging to a family of low-molecular-mass, saline-soluble lectins unique to filamentous fungi. Although AOL gene deletion did not affect the infection phenotype, expression studies revealed that AOL functions in nitrogen storage:

- Large amounts of AOL are synthesized during digestion of the nematode.
- The lectin is transported from the infected nematode to other parts of the mycelium, suggesting it shuttles nitrogen obtained from prey throughout the fungal colony.

## EST Analysis of Monacrosporium haptotylum

An expressed sequence tag database was developed for *Monacrosporium haptotylum*, which infects nematodes using adhesive knobs. Four directional cDNA libraries were constructed from:

- Mycelium growing in liquid culture
- Isolated knobs
- Knobs infecting *Caenorhabditis elegans* for 4 hours (early infection)
- Knobs infecting *C. elegans* for 24 hours (late infection)

From 8463 ESTs, 3121 contigs representing putative unique genes or transcripts were assembled. A striking finding was that 38-60% of assembled sequences showed no homology to any known protein sequences, with the highest proportion of orphans in the knob and 24-hour infection libraries. This suggests that trap cells and the infection process involve a large repertoire of novel, taxon-specific genes.

## Transcriptional Profiling: Traps versus Mycelium

Using a cDNA microarray of 3518 clones, global gene expression was compared between trap cells (knobs) and vegetative mycelium of *M. haptotylum*. Key findings:

- **23.3% of genes** (657 of 2822) were differentially expressed between knobs and mycelium, with 259 up-regulated and 398 down-regulated in knobs.
- Genes involved in transcription, cellular transport, signal transduction, cell rescue and defence, and cell growth were expressed at significantly lower levels in knobs compared to mycelium.

### Cell Polarity Changes

The transition from apical hyphal growth to the isotropic growth of a knob involves significant changes in cell polarity. Differentially expressed genes included homologues of:

- **Profilin**: An actin monomer-sequestering protein that inhibits actin filament growth and prevents polymerization, but can also promote polymerization under certain conditions.
- **Cofilin**: Binds actin monomers and is important for rapid turnover of actin filaments.
- **Rho and Ras family GTPases**: Small GTPases that act as molecular switches, regulating the actin cytoskeleton through GTP hydrolysis. In their GTP-bound active form, they signal proteins associated with polarized growth.

## Similarities to Plant Pathogenic Fungi

The infection structures of nematode-trapping fungi share striking similarities with appressoria formed by plant pathogenic fungi such as *Magnaporthe grisea* and *Blumeria graminis*. Both knob-type traps and appressoria:

- Develop as spherical cells at the tip of a hypha or germ tube
- Possess an adhesive layer that binds to the host surface
- Form a penetration hypha using both physical force and extracellular enzymatic activity
- Show similar patterns of gene regulation during development

These convergent features suggest that the genetic programs for forming specialized infection structures may have deep evolutionary roots shared across diverse fungal pathogens.

## The Caenorhabditis elegans Model System

The use of *C. elegans* as a host for studying nematode-trapping fungi offers significant advantages. Since the genome of *C. elegans* is fully sequenced, transcripts expressed by the fungus and the nematode can be easily separated bioinformatically, even when EST sequences are generated from cDNA libraries containing both organisms. Furthermore, analyses of the fungus-C. elegans system reveal information about the defence systems activated in nematodes in response to fungal infection, making this a valuable dual-organism pathogenesis model.

## Significance for Biological Control

Plant-parasitic nematodes cause enormous agricultural losses worldwide, and nematode-trapping fungi are considered promising agents for biological control. Understanding the molecular basis of trap formation, nematode infection, and nutrient assimilation through functional genomics approaches is essential for optimizing the deployment of these fungi as biocontrol agents. The discovery of extensive novel gene repertoires in trap cells suggests that there is still much to learn about the mechanisms underlying nematode predation by fungi. Future work combining transcriptomics with gene knockout approaches will be needed to functionally characterize the large proportion of orphan genes that appear to be specific to the nematode-trapping lifestyle.
