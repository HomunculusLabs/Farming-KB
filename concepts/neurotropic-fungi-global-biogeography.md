# Neurotropic Fungi Global Biogeography

## Overview

The geographic [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]]. Longitudinally, they extend from the western United States
to China and Japan in Asia.

Key distribution patterns by latitude:

**Tropical and subtropical species**: *Psilocybe cubensis*, *P. subcubensis*,
and *Copelandia* spp. are confined to tropical and subtropical regions. An
exception is *[[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]], including the Valley of Mexico at 2,220 meters altitude
and central Europe. In Maui, Hawaiian Archipelago, *C. cyanescens* grows at
3,000 meters elevation.

**Temperate species**: *Cordyceps capitata*, *C. ophioglossoides*, *Claviceps
purpurea* and allies, *Amanita* spp., and some *Gymnopilus* and *Inocybe*
species are confined to temperate regions.

**Mycorrhizal specialists**: *Amanita muscaria* grows in mycorrhizal association
with *Pinus* and *Betula* in forests of the Northern Hemisphere (including
Mexico), and has been introduced to pine plantations in tropical regions of
Brazil, Colombia, Tanzania, Australia, and New Zealand.

## Cosmopolitan Species

Some species cover a remarkably broad world distribution:
- *[[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]]* and allies — broadly distributed in temperate grasslands
- *Amanita muscaria* — circumboreal distribution, introduced to Southern Hemisphere

## Geographic Knowledge Gaps

The distribution map reveals significant blind spots where no neurotropic fungi
have been recorded, likely reflecting lack of mycological survey rather than
absence:

- **Southeastern Russia** — no records
- **Mongolia** — no records
- **Arabia** — no records
- **Turkey** — no records
- **Many regions of Africa** — substantial gaps
- **Korea** — no records of *Psilocybe*
- **Malaysia** — no records except Java and Sumatra
- **Hawaii** — no records of *Psilocybe*
- **Alaska** — very few records
- **Costa Rica** — few records

Within the United States, several states lack records of neurotropic *Psilocybe*
species: Arizona, Colorado, Illinois, Maryland, Vermont, Massachusetts, New
Hampshire, and Pennsylvania.

## Research Bias

Gartz (1996) aptly observed that "the mushrooms occur in abundance wherever
mycologists abound." This research bias is evident in the distribution data:
well-studied regions (Europe, North America, Mexico) show high species counts,
while under-surveyed regions show few or none. Mueller and Halling's (1995)
major analysis of Agaricales biodiversity in Neotropical forests notably failed
to consider any *Psilocybe* species despite their abundance in the study area.

The consequence is that whenever researchers study materials from under-surveyed
regions, new species are frequently discovered. Recent discoveries from the
1990s include new species from the USA, Mexico, Colombia, Puerto Rico, Spain,
Thailand, New Zealand, and South Africa.

## Pattern of Discovery

The history of discovery itself reveals the geographic bias. When neurotropic
fungi were first scientifically documented by Heim (1956) and Singer (1949),
scientists believed they occurred only in Mexico. Subsequent work by Wasson,
Singer, and others expanded the known range to the USA, South America, Europe,
Siberia, Southwest Asia, and Japan. Guzmán's 1983 world monograph of *Psilocybe*
demonstrated the broad distribution across all continents with the majority in
Latin America, and ongoing work continues to fill in the map.

## Illicit Trade and Introduced Distribution

The recreational drug trade has introduced tropical neurotropic fungi to regions
where they do not naturally occur. *Psilocybe cubensis*, a tropical species, is
frequently reported from Europe but almost exclusively as illegally cultivated
or dried specimens imported from tropical countries. This trade-driven
distribution should not be confused with natural occurrence.
