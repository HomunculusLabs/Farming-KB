# Mushroom Mycelial Morphology Types and Identification

## Overview

Mushroom mycelium displays a range of macroscopic morphological forms on agar media that can be used for preliminary strain identification, vigor assessment, and selection for further cultivation. Stamets classifies mycelial morphology into seven primary types based on the visual appearance of the mycelial mat on the surface of nutrified agar. Understanding these types is essential for evaluating mushroom strains and making informed transfer decisions.

## The Seven Mycelial Morphology Types

### 1. Linear Mycelium

Linear mycelium grows in relatively straight, parallel strands with minimal branching. It is typically seen on the reflective sheen of the agar surface. Linear growth often represents an early developmental stage that may transition into rhizomorphic or cottony forms as the culture matures. This type is common in young cultures and some slower-growing species.

### 2. Rhizomorphic Mycelium

Often similar to linear mycelium, rhizomorphic mycelium is commonly called "ropey." It is composed of braided, twisted strands of varying diameters. Rhizomorphic mycelium is considered the most desirable morphology for cultivation because it supports primordia formation.

**Key characteristics:**
- Braided, rope-like strands of varying diameters
- Indicates vigorous, actively growing mycelium
- Supports primordia (initial [[growing-gourmet-hypholoma-capnoides-cultivation]]*, *H. sublateritium*)

Some rhizomorphic mycelia take on a reflective quality, resembling the surface of silk. When a rhizomorphic mycelium degenerates with age, tomentose (cottony) formations typically take over — a sign of declining vigor.

### 3. Cottony (Tomentose) Mycelium

Cottony mycelium looks like tufts of cotton and is nearly aerial in its growth. This type is commonly called **tomentose** by mycologists.

**Species commonly showing cottony mycelium:**
- Oyster Mushrooms (*Pleurotus* species)
- Shaggy Manes (*[[grifola-frondosa]]*)

While cottony mycelium is normal for some species, in others it can indicate strain degeneration, particularly when it replaces previously rhizomorphic growth. Cultivators working with species that normally produce rhizomorphic mycelium should be alert to transitions toward cottony morphology.

### 4. Zonate Mycelium

Cottony mycelium often shows concentric circles of dense and light growth, or zones. Zonate patterns can result from two factors:

**Age-related zonation:** The newest mycelium on the periphery of the culture is usually light in color. The more-aged mycelium towards the center becomes strongly pigmented, creating visible concentric rings.

**Circadian zonation:** Zonations can also be a function of natural circadian cycles, even when cultures are incubated in laboratories where temperatures are kept constant. Growth occurs in spurts, creating rings of growth.

**Species commonly showing zonate mycelium:**
- *Ganoderma* species (Reishi)
- *Hypholoma* species
- *Hypsizygus* species

### 5. Matted or Appressed Mycelium

This dense mycelial type is typical of Reishi (*[[ganoderma-lucidum]]*) after two weeks of growth on 2% malt-extract agar media. So dense is this mycelial type that a factory-sharpened surgical blade cannot cut through it. The mycelium tears off in ragged sheaths as the scalpel blade is dragged across the agar surface.

**Warning sign:** Many species develop matted mycelia over time, especially wood rotters. Cultures that mysteriously die often have mycelium which appears matted but whose surface is flat and highly reflective — this atypical reflectivity can signal a dead or dying culture.

### 6. Powdered Mycelium

Powdered mycelium is best exemplified by [[chicken-of-the-woods]] (*[[marasmius-oreades]]*) and Shiitake (*Lentinula edodes*).
