## Lycanthropy and Solanaceous Hallucinogens

The European werewolf belief, or lycanthropy, may have pharmacological
roots in the tropane alkaloids produced by Solanaceae plants. Michael
Harner proposed that the classic symptoms of lycanthropy described in
medieval and early modern accounts closely match the known clinical
effects of atropine, hyoscyamine, and scopolamine. This connection
between solanaceous intoxication and animal transformation beliefs
provides a pharmacological explanation for one of the most persistent
folk beliefs in European history. Harner"s hypothesis was part of a
broader scholarly effort to identify the botanical and chemical
identities of the [[harner-european-witchcraft-hallucinogens]]
practices from the late medieval period through the early [[henbane-hyoscyamus-niger-ethnobotany]]), mandrake ([[atropa-belladonna]]).
Under its effects, subjects believed themselves transformed into
birds, fish, or geese, exhibiting behaviors appropriate to each
animal form. One subject "would seem sometimes to be changed into
a fish; and flinging out his arms, would swim on the Ground."
Another believed himself turned into a goose, eating grass and
beating the ground with his teeth.

## The Set and Setting Hypothesis

Harner argued that the different hallucinatory experiences produced
by the same solanaceous ointment (witches' flight versus werewolf
transformation) could be explained by modern understanding of set
and setting. The expectations and desires of the subject, combined
with environmental cues, strongly influenced the nature of the
hallucination. The use of a broomstick as a straddling device
suggested flight, while donning a wolf-skin girdle suggested animal
transformation. Boguet (1602) confirmed that the same ointment was
used both for attending the Sabbat and for becoming werewolves,
and he observed imprisoned subjects attempting to go on all fours
but stating they could not transform without their ointment.
