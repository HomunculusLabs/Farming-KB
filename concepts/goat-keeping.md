## Goat Keeping for the Small Homestead

Goats are versatile, intelligent animals suited to small-acreage homesteads. They provide milk, meat, fiber, and [[vermicomposting]] while clearing brush and browse. Goats are browsers (not grazers) — they prefer weeds, brush, leaves, and tree branches over [[pasture-management]] grass.

## Breed Selection

| Breed | Size | Milk/Day | Fat % | Best For |
|-------|------|----------|-------|----------|
| Nigerian Dwarf | 60-80 lb | 1-2 qt | 6-10% | Small homestead, high butterfat, pets |
| Pygmy | 40-60 lb | 0.5-1 qt | 4-5% | Meat, pets, brush clearing |
| Nubian | 130-170 lb | 1-2 gal | 4-5% | High butterfat milk, friendly |
| Alpine | 130-150 lb | 1-2 gal | 3-4% | Consistent high volume |
| Saanen | 130-160 lb | 1-3 gal | 2.5-3.5% | Highest volume milk producer |

**For a homestead:** Nigerian Dwarf for minimal feed/space with rich milk; Nubian for a family milk supply with good temperament; Alpine or Saanen for volume production.

## Housing and Fencing

- **Shelter:** Minimum 15-20 sq ft per goat indoors; draft-free, dry, well-ventilated; goats despise rain and cold wet conditions
- **Bedding:** Straw or wood shavings over packed dirt; clean weekly; deep litter system works (see [[homesteading-infrastructure]])
- **Fencing:** Minimum 4 ft high; woven wire field fence (4x4 in openings) with a single strand of hot wire at top and bottom; goats are escape artists — test every latch and gap
- **Electric fencing:** Multi-wire portable electric (5-7 strands) works well for rotational grazing; train goats to respect it first
- **Predator protection:** Secure shelter at night; livestock guardian dogs (LGDs) strongly recommended; guard donkeys or llamas are alternatives
- **Key principle:** If a goat can get its head through a gap, its body will follow — eliminate all openings wider than 4 in

## Feed, Forage, and Pasture Management

- **Browse is primary:** Goats thrive on brush, weeds, tree leaves, brambles, and forbs; rotational browsing prevents parasite load (see [[cover-cropping-guide]] for integrated systems)
- **Pasture:** Supplemental grass pasture is fine but not sufficient alone; provide 2-4% body weight in dry matter daily
- **Hay:** Good quality grass or grass-legume hay free-choice when pasture is insufficient; 2-4 lbs/day for a Nigerian Dwarf, 4-6 lbs/day for a full-size doe
- **Grain:** Only for lactating does, growing kids, or underweight animals; 1 lb grain per 3 lbs milk produced; avoid overfeeding grain (causes acidosis)
- **Minerals:** Loose goat-specific minerals free-choice (not sheep minerals — goats require copper); copper oxide wire particles (COWP) bolus 2-4x/year for copper deficiency; selenium may be needed in deficient regions
- **Water:** Clean, fresh water free-choice; goats drink 1-2 gallons/day; heated buckets in winter
- **Toxic plants:** Azaleas, rhododendrons, oleander, yew, cherry (wilted leaves), nightshade — know your property's plants

## Milk Production and Handling

- **Lactation length:** 285-305 days after kidding; dry off 2 months before next kidding
- **Milking:** Twice daily (12-hour intervals); sanitize teats before and after; strip first few squirts onto a strip cup to check for mastitis
- **Milk handling:** Strain immediately through a filter; cool rapidly to 38F within 1 hour; glass or stainless steel containers preferred
- **Milk storage:** 7-10 days refrigerated; freezes well for 6+ months (thaw slowly, shake to recombine)
- **Milk composition:** Nigerian Dwarf averages 6-10% butterfat (makes excellent cheese, butter, soap); standard breeds 3-4%
- **Equipment:** Stainless steel bucket milker, [[dairy-and-cheesemaking]] thermometer, milk filters, teat dip (iodine-based), glass mason jars for storage
- **Sanitation:** Wash all equipment with hot soapy water, rinse, then acid sanitize; dairy-grade cleanliness prevents off-flavors and bacterial contamination

## Breeding and Kidding

- **Breeding season:** Most dairy goats are seasonal breeders (August-January); Nigerians may breed year-round
- **Estrus cycle:** 18-22 days; heat lasts 12-36 hours; doe stands for mounting
- **Gestation:** 150 days (~5 months); breed so kidding aligns with favorable weather
- **Signs of kidding:** Udder fills 1-4 weeks prior; ligaments around tailhead soften 12-24 hours before; restless, pawing, nesting behavior
- **Kidding:** Labor lasts 1-6 hours; most does kid unassisted; intervene if active pushing exceeds 30 min with no progress
- **Kid care:** Clear airway, dip navel in iodine, ensure colostrum within first 2 hours (critical for immunity); bottle or dam-raise
- **Weaning:** 8-12 weeks; gradual separation over 1-2 weeks

## Castration and Disbudding

- **Castration (wethers):** Banding at 1-2 weeks with elastrator bands; or surgical castration with a Burdizzo by a vet/experienced handler; castrated males (wethers) are docile, odor-free companions
- **Disbudding:** Burn horn buds at 3-7 days using a disbudding iron (hot iron method); critical for safety — horned goats can injure other goats, handlers, and children; if horns grow, consult a vet for tipping

## Hoof Trimming

- Trim every 6-8 weeks; more frequently in wet conditions or on soft ground
- Use hoof shears; trim the wall flat with the sole, don't cut into the quick (pink tissue)
- Signs of overgrown hooves: limping, walking on pasterns, splayed toes
- Stand goat on a milking stand or have a helper; hooves soften after rain making trimming easier
- Pack hoof with copper sulfate or zinc sulfate paste if foot rot develops

## Common Health Issues

- **Internal parasites (worms):** The #1 health challenge; barber pole worm (Haemonchus contortus) is most dangerous; use FAMANCHA (eye membrane color scoring) to assess anemia; rotational grazing, multi-species grazing, and tannin-rich browse (sericea lespedeza, chicory) reduce parasite load; deworm strategically (fenbendazole, ivermectin, moxidectin) — always rotate drug classes to prevent resistance
- **CAE (Caprine Arthritis Encephalitis):** Viral, incurable; transmitted via colostrum/milk; test all new animals; prevention: pasteurize colostrum, raise kids on heat-treated milk or replacer, test-negative herd
- **CL (Caseous Lymphadenitis):** Bacterial abscesses at lymph nodes; spread via pus; test and cull; abscesses can rupture and contaminate environment for years; biosecurity is critical
- **Mastitis:** Inflammation of udder; check milk for clots/strings/blood; treat with intramammary antibiotics and anti-inflammatories; prevention via sanitation and proper milking technique
- **Enterotoxemia (overeating disease):** Caused by Clostridium perfringens from sudden diet change or overfeeding grain; prevent with CD&T vaccine (annual booster + pre-kidding booster)
- **Polioencephalalacia (thiamine deficiency):** Staggering, blindness, head pressing; treat immediately with injectable thiamine (B1); caused by excess sulfur in diet or abrupt feed changes
- **Mineral deficiencies:** Copper (rough coat, faded color, anemia — especially in Pygmies and Nigerians); Selenium (white muscle disease in kids); Zinc (crusty skin around nose/eyes)

## Goat Manure as Fertilizer

- **NPK:** Approximately 0.7-1.0% N, 0.3-0.5% P, 0.6-0.9% K (fresh); values vary by diet and bedding
- **C:N ratio:** ~12-20:1 (relatively balanced; can be composted with minimal additional carbon)
- **Composting:** Hot compost at 130-150F for 15+ days kills pathogens and weed seeds; turn every 3-5 days; ready in 2-4 months
- **Direct application:** Fresh goat manure is milder than chicken manure and can be side-dressed around established plants at 1-2 in depth; avoid direct contact with plant stems
- **Volume:** One adult goat produces ~1-2 lbs of manure per day; a small herd of 4 goats produces ~1,500-2,900 lbs/year
- **Pelleted manure:** Available commercially; can be used as a slow-release fertilizer at 2-3 lbs per 100 sq ft
