# Mycopesticides (Stamets)

Mycopesticides are fungi used to control insect pests, offering an environmentally friendly alternative to chemical pesticides. Paul Stamets' work on mycopesticides centers on [[singh-entomopathogenic-fungi-biocontrol-agents]] -- particularly Metarhizium anisopliae -- and represents one of the most commercially developed applications of mycorestoration.

## Discovery: Carpenter Ants and the Artist Conk

Stamets' interest in mycopesticides began when his house was damaged by an infestation of carpenter ants (Camponotus modoc). The spread of wood-digesting fungi such as the artist conk (Ganoderma applanatum) is well known to precede or coincide with termite and carpenter ant invasions; the fungi soften and moisten the wood, setting the table for these munching insects. Stamets discovered that the slumping house was under attack, with ants following the mycelial paths carved by the artist conk through the structure. Each day he awoke to find piles of sawdust on the floor near the walls; overnight more of his house had been milled into sawdust by ants. This observation -- that ant trails followed Ganoderma mycelial paths -- led him to investigate whether fungi could be turned against the insects rather than serving as their highways.

## Entomopathogenic Fungi: Mechanism of Action

When insects come into contact with spores of entomopathogenic fungi, the spores attach to the insects and germinate, boring hyphal pegs through the insects' exoskeletons using chitin-dissolving enzymes. Other portals of entry include the respiratory tract, anus, and mouth. Once inside, the mycelium forks and runs through the internal organs, interfering with the creature's metabolism and causing malaise, necrosis, and death in a few days. The insects, looking mummified with fuzzy mycelium, then become a launching platform for further sporulation. With some species, a tiny club-shaped mushroom classified in the genus Cordyceps can sprout from the dead insect carcass -- an example of fungal dimorphism, where the organism expresses itself in either a mold or a mushroom form.

## Metarhizium anisopliae

Metarhizium anisopliae is a green mold fungus highly prevalent in soils. It parasitizes insects by penetrating their exoskeletons, growing inside them, and eventually killing them while producing spores that spread to new hosts. It is widely used in agriculture against termites and other pests and does not harm mammals, cause human allergies, or lead to "sick house" syndrome. It is limited in colony size, producing spores highly pathogenic to ants, termites, locusts, mosquitoes, and mites.

## Key Innovation: Presporulating Mycelium

Stamets' critical insight was to isolate presporulating (white wedge) Metarhizium mycelium that had not yet produced spores. Unlike sporulated cultures, this presporulating mycelium was carried by carpenter ants back to their nests without triggering alarm behavior. Once inside the nest, the mycelium sporulated and infected the entire colony. This approach turned the ants' own social behavior against them.

Natural selection has given insects a decided advantage in detecting entomopathogenic spores and avoiding them -- insects can essentially "know" a mold plague when they sense or smell one. By using presporulating mycelium instead of spores, Stamets circumvented this detection mechanism.

## Patent and Development

Stamets obtained US Patent 6,660,290 (2003) for this method of using pre-sporulating fungal mycelium to control insect pests. The patent covers the use of fungal mycelium as a carrier that insects transport to their colonies, where the fungus then sporulates and kills the nest.

## Other Entomopathogenic Fungi

Besides Metarhizium, several other genera of entomopathogenic fungi have been explored:

- **Beauveria bassiana**: a white mold fungus first documented by Agustino Bassi in 1834, who noticed its spores causing muscardine disease in silkworms -- a discovery that prefigured germ theory. Beauveria also produces attractants prior to sporulation, similar to Metarhizium. It has been widely studied as a biopesticide for agricultural crop pests.
- **Paecilomyces species**: hosting many white mold fungi, Paecilomyces has earned significant research attention as an entomopathogenic agent.
- **Cordyceps species**: including C. myrmecophilia, which fruits from carpenter ant carcasses. Some Cordyceps species can express a mold state such as Metarhizium anisopliae, demonstrating fungal dimorphism.
- **Hirsutella, Mucor, Nomuraea, Aspergillus, and Entomophthorales**: additional genera likely to produce similar attractant agents for targeted insect species.

## Deployment Methods and Carriers

Entomopathogenic fungi can be delivered via many carriers, making deployment highly versatile:

- **Wood chips and sawdust**: natural substrates that blend into garden and forest environments
- **Paper and cardboard**: absorb mycelial extracts and can be placed along insect trails
- **Biodegradable fabrics**: for controlled-release applications
- **[[fungi-in-composting-and-agricultural-waste-conversion]] products**: cornstalks, [[non-sterilized-wheat-straw-oyster-cultivation]], and other roughage can serve as media for infusing mycelia
- **Sterilized rice**: a standard spawn medium used in laboratory and early-stage production

Follow-up research at Texas A&M demonstrated that termites seeking wood burrowed to the location of presporulating mycelium and stopped -- an "arrestant" behavior viewed as a unique benefit. The mycelium acts as both attractant and trap.

## Agricultural Applications

Mycopesticides have broad potential across agricultural settings:

- **Termite control in structures**: replacing toxic chemical treatments that temporarily succeed at best and are generally disappointing since the insects usually return. Chemical treatments can be toxic to humans, other animals, and the environment.
- **Crop [[cannabis-pest-management-guide]]**: forestalling or preventing pest outbreaks associated with farms, including beetles, locusts, and other crop-damaging insects.
- **Livestock pest management**: targeting flies and other insects associated with cattle and pig feedlots.

## Ecological Considerations

Stamets articulates several principles for responsible development:

- **Not to wage war against the insect kingdom** but to enlist fungal allies for intelligent, natural, and localized control of targeted insects when and where they threaten people, buildings, or the environment. The goal is balance, not extinction.
- **Targeted vs. broad-spectrum**: the EPA has encouraged the study of Metarhizium anisopliae specifically because it minimizes potential harm to nontargeted insects -- including bees, butterflies, and other [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] -- while precisely targeting unwanted pests.
- **Resistance management**: using the fungal genome as an indigenous source of new strains limits tolerance buildup in target insect populations, unlike chemical pesticides where resistance evolves predictably.
- **License patents** to those who will best promote nature-friendly alternatives to toxic chemical treatments.
- **Respect the sanctity of all species**, preserve biodiversity, and protect environmental health.
- **Avoid biopiracy** in impoverished countries and require informed consent.
- **Support nonprofit management** of mycopesticide technologies in developing nations.

## Vision: Customized Mycelial Landscapes

Stamets envisions landscapes customized with mycelial matrices populated by fungi that target specific insects and the diseases they carry. These matrices could:

- Forestall or prevent pest outbreaks associated with livestock and farms
- Stop beetle blights from spreading across forests
- Prevent and clean up pollution simultaneously
