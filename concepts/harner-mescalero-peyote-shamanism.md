# Mescalero Apache Peyote Shamanism

A unique case where experiences with a hallucinogenic cactus in shamanistic rituals resulted in serious conflict and, ultimately, proscription of its ceremonial use.

## Historical Use

The Apaches of the [[mescalero-indian-reservation]] include Mescaleros, Chiricahuas, and Lipans:
- Mescaleros used peyote in religious rites by **1867**
- Chiricahuas by **1875**
- Lipans by **1885** (obtained from [[carrizo-indians]])
- Mescaleros learned peyote rites from the Lipans around **1870**

The Apaches call peyote **hoos**. It was used only during Mescalero and Lipan shamanistic ceremonies, not for social purposes. No knowledge of Chiricahua peyote use was found.

## How Peyote Power Was Acquired

In the Apache framework, supernatural power has **no intrinsic attribute of good or evil**; its virtue resides in its potency. Power approaches people through plants, animals, or natural phenomena by means of dreams or hallucinatory experiences. Opler described the Mescaleros as "a tribe of shamans, active or potentially active."

Peyote use was acquired from personal contact with **power that approached people while it was invested in peyote flowers or "buttons."** Various Mescalero shamans acquired peyote power and became leaders of peyote camps where curing and other ceremonies were conducted.

## The Fundamental Incongruity

There was a fundamental conflict between ordinary Mescalero shamanistic practices and peyote rites:

- **Ordinary practice**: A single shaman is the principal figure; religious ecstasy, visions, and communications with supernaturals are the shaman's **privatives**
- **Peyote meetings**: Multiple shamans and participants all experienced peyote's effects simultaneously, making its psychological [[emcdda-psilocybin-mushroom-somatic-health-risks-and-physiological-effects]] **common**

The uniqueness of the shaman's experiences disappeared. The peyote meetings became places where **shamanistic rivalries and witchcraft flourished**. Disruption resulted rather than cohesiveness.

## Abandonment and Proscription

The peyote gatherings were not accompanied by Christian beliefs (the Mescaleros never joined the Peyote Religion). Hostilities became so open and bloody that eventually the peyote gatherings were abandoned. The hostilities were ascribed to the peyote itself.

Peyote power is **unique** among the Mescaleros in that it is uniformly considered **bad** (intrinsically evil) -- a basic deviation from the conceptualization of power without intrinsic properties of good or evil. Only **owl power** shares this classification.

The use of peyote was proscribed for shamans and thereafter employed only in **witchcraft rituals** and **love magic practices**.

## Psychological Analysis

Apache child-rearing practices engender much hostility, addressed institutionally toward outsiders, witches, and ghosts. Under the influence of hallucinogens (including alcohol), the unstable repression of hateful impulses became blatantly overt, threatening tribal unity.

The peyote camps introduced **simultaneous assumption of authority by more than one practitioner**, each vying for supremacy. The drug-induced regression resulted in releasing aggression in its earlier, childish form, directly toward parent and sibling surrogates. Bloodshed and feuds occurred.

The Mescaleros fear peyote for two reasons:
1. It has an **evil power** which will drive them to do evil (projection of intrapersonal asocial tendencies)
2. It causes **hallucinations** (displacement of fear of loss of control of destructive urges onto fear of loss of perceptual accuracy)

## Shaman Personality Findings

Apache shamans were found to have personality configurations typical for Apaches, differing only in greater creative potential and capacity for imposture. On Rorschach testing, shamans demonstrated:
- More hysterical signs than the general population
- Keener awareness of peculiarities and more selective theoretical interest
- Creative characteristics and high degree of reality testing potential
- Capacity to regress in the service of the ego

## The Dual Nature of Power and Witchcraft

Among the Mescalero, any individual might own any number of powers. If a person is thought to use power for purposes not oriented toward the common good, they are accorded the status of **witch**. Yet those who use their powers for the benefit of the group -- the shamans -- are implicitly witches as well, since a shaman who saves a life must then sacrifice either his own or that of a loved person. This built-in tension means that jealousies, enmities, and suspicion abound. Each shaman has private instructions concerning the use of power, and his rites are individually owned. Consistent with native concepts of leadership and authority, there has never been a chief shaman.

During 1959-60, there were thirteen accredited Mescalero, Chiricahua, and Lipan shamans on the reservation, plus approximately fifteen pseudoshamans who claimed supernatural power but were considered imposters. Only one living person (known as "Ancient One") was known to have participated in the peyote camp. Shamans considered legitimate possessors of peyote power were not punished by that power, but peyote had "turned back" on the pseudoshamans -- one lost a leg, another had close relatives killed.

## Contrast with Other Tribal Peyote Use

The Mescalero case stands in sharp contrast to the usual picture of amiable, harmonious group sessions of peyote use among other [[metzner-peyote-ceremonies-native-american-church-and-huichol-traditions]] were never accompanied by the acceptance of Christian beliefs and practices, and the Mescaleros never became involved in the formal Peyote Religion. The use of peyote was intended to affirm the vitality of traditional religious practices at a time when reservation confinement contributed to an increased awareness of social and cultural deprivation.
