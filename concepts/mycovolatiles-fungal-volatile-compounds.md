# Mycovolatiles Fungal Volatile Compounds

## Overview

Fungal volatile organic compounds (VOCs), sometimes called mycovolatiles,
are low-molecular-weight organic molecules emitted by fungi as metabolic
byproducts. These compounds include alcohols, aldehydes, ketones,
terpenes, and aromatic hydrocarbons. In [[paul-stamets]]' "Mycelium
Running," fungal volatiles are described as playing critical ecological
roles: they function as chemical signals between organisms, attractants
for insect spore dispersers, species identification cues for
mushroom foragers, and weapons in the fungal arsenal against
competing organisms and insect pests.

## Mycelial Outgassing and Species Signatures

Stamets describes how mycelium "breathes," emitting a fragrance that
is a valuable feature to the experienced mushroom gatherer. The
outgassing from mycelium carries scents that are often species
specific and recognizable. When walking through forests, Stamets has
detected the anise-rich fragrance of Clitocybe odora and the
pinelike fragrance of matsutake from the mycelium alone, before any
mushrooms have appeared.

As Stamets writes: "As mycelia's metabolisms surge, they emit
attractants, imparting sweet fragrances to the forest and
connecting ecosystems and their species with scent trails." This
concept positions mycovolatiles as part of a "biomolecular
superhighway" -- a chemical communication network through which
fungi maintain constant dialogue with their environment.

## Species-Specific Fragrance Profiles

Different fungal species produce characteristic volatile profiles
that serve as olfactory fingerprints:

- **Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus species):** Emit a unique
  fragrance described as mildly but pleasingly of anise
- **Garden giant (Stropharia rugoso-annulata):** Mycelium has
  a ropy quality and emits a rich, uniquely sweet fragrance
- **Clitocybe odora:** Produces an anise-rich fragrance detectable
  from the mycelial mat alone
- **Matsutake ([[tricholoma-matsutake]]):** Characterized by a
  distinctive pinelike fragrance
- **Blewit (Lepista nuda):** Identified in part by its brilliant
  purplish mycelium and associated scent

These scent signatures allow experienced foragers to locate hidden
mycelial mats and anticipate where mushrooms will fruit, even in
the complete absence of visible fruiting bodies.

## Volatile Compounds as Insect Attractants

One of the most commercially significant applications of fungal
VOCs is in biological pest control. Stamets' research demonstrated
that certain entomopathogenic fungi emit volatile attractants that
lure target insects to their mycelium. The green mold Metarhizium
anisopliae, a pathogen of termites and ants, produces both
attractants and feeding stimulants from its presporulating mycelium.

Key discoveries from this research include:

- Presporulating mycelium of Metarhizium anisopliae attracts
  Formosan termites, eastern subterranean termites, and fire ants
- Water and ethanol extracts of the mycelium on rice yielded
  powerful attractants and feeding stimulants
- These extracts remained effective even after serial dilution
  with water, reducing production costs
- Species-specific attractant production was observed: Metarhizium
  strains from one insect species' colony did not attract other
  insect species
- [[beauveria-bassiana]] also produces attractants prior to
  sporulation, as do many other genera of entomopathogenic fungi

## Chemical Ecology of Volatile Signaling

The ecological roles of fungal VOCs extend well beyond insect
attraction. In the forest ecosystem, volatile compounds serve as
connective tissue between organisms:

- **Spore dispersal:** Many mushrooms use insects to carry spores.
  The scent of the mushroom body signals to insects that food or
  habitat is available. Insects use mushroom scents as homes for
  their larvae, creating a mutualistic dispersal relationship.

- **Competitive signaling:** Fungi release gases including carbon
  dioxide and other volatile byproducts during metabolism. These
  emissions can inhibit the growth of competing microorganisms in
  the immediate vicinity, giving the fungus a competitive advantage
  in colonizing substrate.

- **Ecosystem connectivity:** Stamets proposes that mycelial
  volatile emissions create "scent trails" connecting different
  species and ecosystems, forming part of an invisible chemical
  web that governs nutrient cycling and species interactions.

## [[mycoforestry-applications]]

In mycoforestry, understanding fungal volatile profiles has
practical implications for habitat restoration. The scent of the
mycelium serves as a diagnostic tool: when establishing mycelial
colonies for erosion control or soil building, the emergence of
characteristic species-specific fragrances indicates successful
colonization and active growth.

The volatile emissions also play a role in the downstream ecological
effects of introduced mycelium. As mycelial mats establish and grow,
they modify the chemical environment of their habitat through
volatile production, influencing which other organisms can colonize
the area and shaping the trajectory of ecological succession.

## Future Directions: Mycovolatiles as Data

Stamets envisions a future where fungal networks are programmed to
collect environmental data. The volatile output of mycelial networks
could potentially be monitored as a real-time indicator of
ecosystem health, with changes in VOC profiles signaling shifts in
soil chemistry, the presence of pollutants, or the status of
decomposition processes. This concept aligns with the broader
vision of mycelium as "Nature's Internet" -- a sentient biological
network capable of sensing and responding to environmental changes.
