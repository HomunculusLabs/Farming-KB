## Overview

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are among the most important regulators of plant community structure and ecosystem function. By asymmetrically enhancing the growth and fitness of some plant species over others, mycorrhizae act as hidden architects of plant communities. Their influence extends from individual plant physiology to landscape-level patterns of succession and biodiversity, mediated through nutrient exchange networks, [[spore-dispersal]] by animals, and differential effects on plant reproduction.

## Mycorrhizae and Plant Succession

### Nutrient Availability as a Successional Driver

Pedersen and Sylvia (1996) identified nutrient availability as one of the major determinants of plant colonization success during seral succession. Mycorrhizal associations provide a critical advantage by enabling plants to sequester nutrients from limited soil resources more effectively than non-mycorrhizal competitors.

The dispersal of hypogeous fungal spores by small mammals creates a patchy but biologically meaningful distribution of mycorrhizal inoculum across the landscape. M.F. Allen (1991) observed that this patchiness allows both mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal plant species to establish in close proximity, increasing overall plant diversity during colonization.

### Contrasting Successional Patterns Across Ecosystems

Research across dramatically different disturbance regimes has revealed varied patterns of mycorrhizal involvement in succession:

- **Mount Saint Helens (volcanic disturbance)**: All colonizing vegetation consisted of mycorrhizal species (both arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal), reflecting the extremely nutrient-poor conditions of the volcanic substrate
- **Galapagos Islands**: A mixture of arbuscular mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal plants established, with non-mycorrhizal species dominating in fertile lowland soils and mycorrhizal species establishing in poorer rocky soils

Allen and Allen (1990) hypothesized that mycorrhizal dependence in developing ecosystems follows predictable patterns related to nutrient and water availability, though these models are also dependent on the specific plant species involved.

### The Lyman Glacier Model System

The forefront of the Lyman Glacier provides one of the best-studied examples of primary succession involving mycorrhizal fungi. Spores of mycorrhizal fungi (*Glomus macrocarpus*, *Hymenogaster*) survive passage through the guts of small mammals including voles, chipmunks, and squirrels (Trappe and Maser, 1976; Cazares and Trappe, 1994). These animals concentrate spore-laden feces at "safe sites" — concave surfaces of coarse rocky particles that trap tree seeds and protect them from desiccation.

The dark-septate endophyte *Phialocephala fortinii* significantly enhanced growth of lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*) seedlings in the glacier forefront, but with important nutrient-dependent variation (Jumpponen et al., 1998):

- **Without added nitrogen**: Mycorrhizal colonization significantly increased total plant phosphorus but did not increase biomass
- **With added nitrogen (100 kg/ha)**: Mycorrhizal colonization significantly increased both biomass and phosphorus content

This demonstrates that mycorrhizal benefits are context-dependent and modulated by environmental nutrient availability.

### Ectomycorrhizal Succession Dynamics

Jumpponen et al. (1999, 2002) documented a clear successional pattern of ectomycorrhizal fungi at the Lyman Glacier, identifying 68 species across 25 genera with no single species present at all successional stages. Ectomycorrhizal diversity peaked at canopy closure, a pattern also observed at other sites (Dighton et al., 1986; Last et al., 1987; Visser, 1995).

This diversity peak likely reflects both resource limitation (particularly phosphorus) and the increasing proportion of nutrients in organic forms at later successional stages. Greater mycorrhizal diversity enables the plant community to exploit a wider range of nutrient sources. Conn and Dighton (2000) confirmed that ectomycorrhizal communities adjust their enzyme profiles to match resource availability — when phosphorus is immobilized in decomposing litter, the community shifts toward acid phosphatase-producing types.

### Driver vs. Passenger Hypotheses

For arbuscular mycorrhizal communities, Hart et al. (2001) proposed two competing hypotheses to explain successional changes:

1. **Driver hypothesis**: Mycorrhizal fungi actively shape plant communities through interspecific differences in colonization efficiency and persistence, with some fungi preferentially promoting certain plant species
2. **Passenger hypothesis**: Mycorrhizal community composition passively follows changes in the plant community and environmental conditions, with no independent causal role in determining plant species composition

Resolving which hypothesis predominates in different ecosystems remains an active area of research with important implications for ecological restoration.

### The Mycelial Network and Ecosystem Stability

Amaranthus and Perry (1989) demonstrated the critical importance of maintaining intact mycorrhizal networks during forest management. When Douglas fir was planted into partially cleared sites where mycorrhizal roots of remaining trees were preserved, survival was approximately 90%. In totally cleared areas lacking the mycelial network, survival dropped to only 50% after two years.

The existing mycelial network provides stability through carbon and nutrient exchange between connected plants, enabling new recruits to access larger resource pools than they could independently. This synergistic activity between surviving mature plants and new recruits maintains ecosystem stability and preserves plant species composition following disturbance.

## Mycorrhizae and Plant Fitness

### Enhanced Reproductive Output

The effects of mycorrhizae extend well beyond vegetative growth into reproductive fitness. Sanders et al. (1995) demonstrated that arbuscular mycorrhizal plants produced significantly more flower buds and seeds, an effect linked to improved phosphate nutrition. Multiple studies have confirmed that mycorrhizal colonization increases:

- Seed production (Koide et al., 1988; Stanley et al., 1993)
- Offspring vigor, including seedling germination rates, leaf area, and root:shoot ratios (Lewis and Koide, 1990; Bryla and Koide, 1990; Koide and Lu, 1992)
- Root enzyme production in offspring

### Transgenerational Fitness Effects

Heppell et al. (1998) provided striking evidence that mycorrhizal benefits extend across generations. Offspring of mycorrhizal *Abutilon theophrasti* parents were significantly larger than those of non-mycorrhizal parents at every measured age:

| Age (days) | Parameter | Mycorrhizal Parent | Non-mycorrhizal Parent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | Shoot height (cm) | 12.5 | 9.4 |
| 20 | Shoot dry mass (g) | 61.2 | 30.9 |
| 47 | Shoot height (cm) | 30.6 | 19.8 |
| 47 | Shoot dry mass (g) | 521 | 154 |
| 94 | Survivors per box | 59.1 | 26.6 |
| 94 | Seeds per survivor | 17.9 | 10.6 |

Under high-density conditions, mycorrhizal offspring showed even greater improvement due to early self-thinning. This fitness advantage was transmitted to the next generation through increased total seed production, creating a compounding benefit over multiple generations.

### The Mutualism-Antagonism Continuum

Francis and Read (1994, 1995) proposed that plant responses to arbuscular mycorrhizal infection form a continuum from strongly positive (mutualism) to strongly negative (antagonism):

**Positive (Mutualistic) responses**:
- Asteraceae, Ericaceae, Fabaceae, Liliaceae, Pinaceae, Plantaginaceae, Ranunculaceae

**Neutral to commensal responses**:
- Burmanniaceae, Gentianaceae, Monotroceae, Orchidaceae, Triuridaceae, Gramineae, Caryophyllaceae, Resedaceae, Scrophulariaceae

**Negative (Antagonistic) responses**:
- Boraginaceae, Brassicaceae, Chenopodiaceae, Polygonaceae

This variation in plant response has profound implications for competitive fitness and plant community structure. Francis and Read (1995) noted that arbuscular mycorrhizae tend to be most beneficial to K-selected species and adverse to ruderals, suggesting that mycorrhizae can fundamentally shift the competitive balance between different plant life history strategies.

Janos (1980) highlighted that these asymmetric mycorrhizal effects can be a significant determinant of plant species composition in tropical forests, where the high diversity of both plants and mycorrhizal fungi creates complex interaction networks with cascading effects on community structure.

## Implications for Ecological Restoration

Understanding mycorrhizal roles in succession and fitness has direct applications for ecological restoration:

1. **Preserve mycelial networks** during disturbance to maintain plant establishment pathways
2. **Consider mycorrhizal dependence** when selecting plant species for restoration of different soil types
3. **Inoculate with appropriate mycorrhizal fungi** when restoring severely disturbed sites lacking natural inoculum
4. **Account for transgenerational fitness effects** when designing long-term restoration monitoring programs
5. **Recognize the driver vs. passenger distinction** to predict whether introducing mycorrhizae will actively shape or merely follow plant community development
