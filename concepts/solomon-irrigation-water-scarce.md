## Intensive Versus Extensive Spacing

Solomon argues that the single most important decision a gardener makes about
irrigation is plant spacing. He provides four columns of spacing
recommendations:

- **Column 1 (Intensive):** Close spacings on double-dug raised beds requiring
almost daily watering. Solomon does not recommend this approach.  - **Column 2
(Semi-intensive):** Wider spacings on raised beds or flat ground, suitable where
there is some irrigation or reliable rainfall.  - **Column 3 (Extensive, good
rainfall):** Even wider spacings on raised rows or flat ground, where the soil
moisture from winter rains and snowmelt can comfortably support crops through
the [[comparison-biofertilizers-and-soil-inoculants-vs-compost-tea-guide]] -- a barrel filled with water and a bucketful of fresh manure or
compost, brewed for a week. [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] containing trace elements as well as
NPK are effective when applied in highly dilute form and do not damage soil
life.

## Drought Response Strategies

When drought threatens, Solomon recommends this sequence:

1. Thoroughly hoe weeds to eliminate competition for soil moisture.  2. Begin
fertigation to help plants access deeper soil moisture.  3. Recognize that much
apparent drought damage is actually [[cannabis-grow-nutrient-deficiency-problem-solving]] from dry surface soil.
Fertigation addresses both problems simultaneously.

## Soil Assessment for Water Retention

Solomon provides a simple procedure to assess water-holding capacity: dig a hole
at least 36 inches (90 cm) deep and observe the soil profile. The key questions
are whether the soil is deep enough for roots to penetrate (at least 3 feet),
whether there are impermeable layers that would stop capillary rise of moisture,
and how much clay the soil contains. A jar test (shaking soil with water and
dish soap, then observing settlement rates) precisely reveals clay content,
which determines both water retention and fertigation rates.
