Soil fungi are consumed by a diverse array of fungivores, creating complex trophic cascades within soil food webs. The major consumers of soil fungi include:

- **Mycophagous nematodes**: Among the most important fungal grazers in soil, with many species specialized on particular fungal taxa
- **Protozoans**: Consume fungal hyphae and spores, particularly in the rhizosphere where they help cycle nutrients to plant roots
- **Collembolans** (springtails): Selective feeders that can distinguish between fungal species and prefer certain taxa
- **Oribatid mites**: Important fungal grazers in forest soils and litter layers
- **Enchytraeids**: Small oligochaete worms that feed on fungi in the litter-soil interface
- **Dipteran larvae**: Various fly larvae consume fungi in decaying organic matter
- **Earthworms**: While primarily detritivores, earthworms process fungal-rich organic material
- **Tardigrades**: Feed on fungal hyphae in water films around soil particles

Fungivore-fungus symbioses — wherein the fungivore facilitates growth of the fungus — have been reported for wood-boring beetles, attine ants, and certain termites. In many instances, soil animals feeding on fungi are either species-specific or species-selective, underscoring that from the consumer's perspective, all fungi are not equal, and biodiversity matters.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi in Soil

Mycorrhizal associations represent one of the most important functional groups of soil fungi. Ectomycorrhizal fungi form sheaths around root tips and extend hyphal networks into the soil, dramatically increasing the effective surface area for nutrient absorption. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) penetrate root cortical cells and form arbuscules, the sites of nutrient exchange.

Mycorrhizal sporocarp production can be increased through removal of leaf litter and debris, which acts as a moisture sink. In seasonally dry tropical forests, this technique helps maintain mycorrhizal fungi by preserving soil moisture. The diversity of ectomycorrhizal fungi in tropical forests is notable, though their distribution is patchy and linked to the presence of specific ectomycorrhizal host trees.

## Soil Disturbance and Human Impact

Human-caused disturbances — especially cultivation, erosion, and contamination — affect soil habitats and thus impact the diversity of the soil biota. Cultivation disrupts hyphal networks, compacts soil, and alters moisture regimes. Erosion removes the organic-rich surface layers where most fungal activity occurs. Chemical contamination from pesticides, heavy metals, and industrial pollutants can eliminate sensitive fungal species while selecting for tolerant ones.

The vast majority of soil fungi are unculturable with current techniques, meaning molecular methods are essential for understanding the true scope of soil fungal diversity. Standardized methods for comparing surveys across sites are still being developed, making it difficult to draw broad conclusions about global patterns of soil fungal diversity.

## Soil Fungi in Bioremediation

Soil fungi play important roles in bioremediation of contaminated sites. Their extensive hyphal networks allow them to access and decompose pollutants that bacteria cannot reach. Specific capabilities include:

- **Heavy metal tolerance**: Some soil fungi can accumulate or transform heavy metals, reducing their bioavailability
- **Organic pollutant degradation**: White-rot basidiomycetes can decompose lignin and similarly recalcitrant organic compounds, including some pesticides and industrial chemicals
- **Hydrocarbon decomposition**: Certain soil fungi can break down petroleum hydrocarbons, offering potential for cleanup of contaminated sites
