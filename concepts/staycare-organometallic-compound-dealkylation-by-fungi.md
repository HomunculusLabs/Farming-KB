# Organometallic Compound Dealkylation by Fungi

Organometallic compounds — molecules containing direct metal-carbon
bonds — represent a significant class of environmental pollutants.
These compounds include organotins used as antifouling agents and
biocides, organomercury compounds from agricultural and industrial
applications, and alkylleads formerly used as gasoline additives. Fungi
possess diverse mechanisms for transforming and detoxifying these
persistent contaminants, offering promising pathways for
bioremediation of organometallic pollution.

## Fungal Transformation Mechanisms

Fungal organometallic transformations may be applied to the removal of
alkylleads or organotins from water (Macaskie & Dean, 1987, 1990; Gadd,
2000b). Degradation of organometallic compounds by fungi can proceed
through two general pathways:

1. **Direct biotic action**: fungal-enzymes-degradation that cleave metal-carbon bonds.
2. **Indirect facilitation of abiotic degradation**: Fungi alter
   environmental conditions, such as pH, and excrete metabolites that
   promote the chemical breakdown of organometallic compounds.

Both mechanisms contribute to the overall detoxification capacity of
[[biodiversity-of-fungi-soil-fungal-communities-agriculture]] [[singh-fungal-biofilms-in-contaminated-environments]]. The ability of fungi
to modify their local microenvironment through [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]]
creates conditions favorable for both biological and chemical
transformation of organometallic pollutants.

## Organotin Degradation

Organotin compounds are among the most widely used and environmentally
problematic organometallic pollutants. Tributyltin oxide (TBTO) and
tributyltin naphthenate have been extensively employed as antifouling
agents on ship hulls, in marine paints, and as industrial biocides.
Their persistence and toxicity to marine organisms, particularly at
low concentrations, have made them a priority target for remediation.

Fungi can degrade organotin compounds through progressive dealkylation,
systematically removing butyl groups from the tin atom:

- **Step 1**: Tributyltin compounds are degraded to dibutyltin
  derivatives through removal of one butyl group.
- **Step 2**: Dibutyltins are further degraded to monobutyltin
  compounds through removal of a second butyl group.
- **Step 3**: The ultimate degradation product is inorganic tin(II),
  which is significantly less toxic than the parent organotin
  compound (Barug, 1981; Orsler & Holland, 1982).

This stepwise dealkylation process effectively converts highly toxic
organotin pollutants into relatively innocuous inorganic tin species.
The progressive removal of organic ligands from the tin atom
represents a complete detoxification pathway.

## Organomercury Detoxification

Organomercury compounds, including methylmercury and phenylmercury,
are among the most toxic environmental contaminants due to their
ability to bioaccumulate and biomagnify through food chains. Fungi
possess a two-enzyme system for organomercury detoxification that is
broadly analogous to the well-characterized mercury resistance system
found in mercury-resistant bacteria (Tezuka & Takasaki, 1988).

The fungal organomercury detoxification pathway involves two sequential
enzymatic reactions:

1. **Organomercury lyase**: This enzyme cleaves the carbon-mercury
   bond in organomercury compounds, converting them to mercury(II)
   ions (Hg2+) and the corresponding organic moiety. This is the
   critical dealkylation step that removes the organic component.

2. **Mercuric reductase**: The mercury(II) ions produced by
   organomercury lyase are subsequently reduced to elemental mercury
   (Hg0), which is volatile and can be removed from the system or
   sequestered.
