# Cordyceps and Claviceps as Sacred Neurotropic Fungi in Mexico

## Overview

While Psilocybe species dominate discussions of neurotropic fungi, the genus Cordyceps and its close
relative Claviceps have a significant but often overlooked ceremonial history in Mexico. Indigenous
communities in central Mexico, particularly in the Nevado de Toluca region, have used Cordyceps
species alongside Psilocybe mushrooms in traditional nocturnal ceremonies for centuries. These fungi
occupy a unique position as non-psilocybin [[ergot-fungi-claviceps-and-cordyceps-as-neurotropic-organisms]] whose cultural importance may stem
from their taxonomic relationship to [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]], the famous ergot fungus responsible for
ergotism epidemics throughout European history.

## Cordyceps Species in Mexican Ceremonial Use

Two Cordyceps species are documented as sacred fungi in Mexican [[sacred-mushroom-use-mexico-teonanacatl-indigenous-traditions]]. These species
are notable because they are parasitic fungi that grow on subterranean Elaphomyces (deer truffle)
hosts, creating a complex three-organism ecological and ceremonial relationship:

- **Cordyceps capitata** — Known locally among indigenous communities as "hombrecitos" (little men)
- **Cordyceps ophioglossoides** — Known locally as "mujercitas" (little women)

The gendered naming reflects the morphological characteristics that indigenous observers noted in the
fruiting bodies. These species parasitize Elaphomyces fungi, which serve as their nutrient source and
host. The Elaphomyces hosts themselves — including E. granulatus, E. muricatus, and E. reticulatus —
play a ceremonial role beyond their function as Cordyceps substrate.

### Medicinal Use of Elaphomyces

In the Trique Indian communities of Alta Mixteca, Oaxaca, Elaphomyces species are used independently
of the Cordyceps ceremonies for healing purposes. Specifically, they are applied to help heal wounds
and for a practice described as "rejuvenecer el organismo" (rejuvenating the organism). This medicinal
use of a fungal genus that is otherwise considered inedible demonstrates the depth of indigenous
mycological knowledge in Mexico.

### The Ceremony Director

During nocturnal ceremonies in the Nevado de Toluca region, a specimen of Elaphomyces is placed at
the center of the room to serve as the "director" of the ceremony. The Cordyceps species are consumed
either alongside Psilocybe muliercula or alone. This tripartite relationship — host fungus, parasitic
fungus, and Psilocybe mushroom — represents one of the most complex multi-species ceremonial practices
documented in ethnomycology. The symbolism is rich: the Elaphomyces represents the underground realm,
the Cordyceps represents transformation (parasitism as metamorphosis), and the Psilocybe represents
the visionary or divine realm.

## The Claviceps Connection

The genus Cordyceps is closely related taxonomically to Claviceps purpurea, the ergot fungus. This
taxonomic relationship is significant for several reasons:

1. **Chemical plausibility**: The shared biochemistry between Cordyceps and Claviceps raises the
   possibility that Mexican Cordyceps species could contain ergot-like alkaloids
2. **Cultural transfer**: Indigenous peoples may have recognized the shared psychoactive properties
   of these related fungi through empirical observation over generations
3. **Historical significance**: Claviceps purpurea is the source organism from which Albert Hofmann
   first isolated lysergic acid derivatives, leading to the synthesis of LSD-25

## Hofmann's Failed Analysis

Albert Hofmann, working with materials collected by Wasson's expeditions, analyzed specimens of
Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides but found no indole compounds present. However, these
specimens had been gathered from a popular market in Mexico and preserved for a long period before
analysis. Given what is now understood about the volatility of neurotropic compounds, Hofmann's
negative result is likely inconclusive rather than definitive.

Repke et al. (1977) demonstrated that baeocystin disappears from Psilocybe specimens within 20-60
days of collection. Ohenoja et al. (1987) detected decreasing psilocybin in [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]] of
[[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybe-semilanceata-temperate-cosmopolitan-distribution]]: 0.014% in specimens from 1869, compared to 0.67% from 1954 and 0.84% from
1976. The Mexican indigenous practice of discarding dried specimens older than one year aligns
precisely with these scientific observations.

## Claviceps Species as Neurotropic Fungi

The Guzmán, Allen & Gartz (1998) classification includes five Claviceps species as neurotropic
fungi, plus two Cordyceps species, placing them in Group 3 of their four-group classification system:

### Claviceps Species
- **Claviceps purpurea** — The classic ergot, widely distributed in temperate grasslands worldwide
- **Claviceps nigricans** — Less common, associated with specific grass hosts
- **[[claviceps-paspali]]** — Found on Paspalum grasses in tropical and subtropical regions
- **Claviceps rolfesii** — Tropical distribution, less well characterized chemically
- **Claviceps tripsicii** — Associated with Tripsacum grasses, New World distribution

### Ergot Alkaloid Chemistry

The ergot alkaloids produced by Claviceps species include a complex array of compounds:
- **Clavine alkaloids**: Simple ergoline derivatives including agroclavine and elymoclavine
