# Cool Temperate Establishment and Pioneer Systems

## Overview

Establishing productive permaculture systems in cool temperate climates requires specific strategies for dealing with frost, wind, poor soils, and aggressive pioneer weeds. Rather than fighting against succession, permaculture design harnesses natural pioneer species as nurse crops, using their vigor to prepare sites for productive tree crops. The examples documented here span New Zealand, Tasmania, the American West, and Germany, illustrating a coherent approach to cool climate establishment.

## Gorse as a Pioneer Nurse Crop

Gorse (*Ulex europaeus*) is widely regarded as an invasive weed, but in cool temperate permaculture it is valued as a powerful pioneer nurse crop. At Collingwood River, New Zealand, Dick Nichols demonstrates a systematic approach to converting gorse-covered land:

**Phase 1 — Site Preparation:** On 280 hectares of rampant gorse, lines are rolled down or slashed and eucalyptus, pines, and acacia are planted. "Zone 1" is fully cut and planted to pioneer trees. Tomato cuttings can be planted directly into the slash.

**Phase 2 — Understory Establishment (2-3 years):** The remaining gorse is slashed and mature rainforest species are slip-sown into the sward. After 5-6 years, the gorse has rotted down and rainforest establishes under acacia. The acacia is then felled, and native birds carry in rainforest seed.

**Phase 3 — Final Succession:** A self-sustaining rainforest system replaces the gorse monoculture, with native seed dispersal by birds completing the succession.

Dick Roberts in Nelson, New Zealand, uses gorse as "rough mulch" to protect fruit and forage trees in steep sheep pasture. Reduction of weed species by this method protects the succeeding crop. Tree crops will shade out gorse when thickly planted as a nurse crop.

### The Principle of Pioneer Succession

The key insight is that gorse performs essential ecological functions: nitrogen fixation, soil protection, wind shelter, and organic matter accumulation. By managing rather than eliminating gorse, these functions are preserved while transitioning the system toward the desired productive state. This approach is far more energy-efficient than clearing and planting on bare soil.

## Tagasaste: The Multipurpose Cool Climate Legume

Tagasaste (*Chamaecytisus proliferus*) is one of the most valuable multi-purpose plants for cool temperate systems:

**Shelter and Forage:** In Taheke, New Zealand, avocado trees thrive in frost-prone areas when protected within clumps of tagasaste. The tagasaste yields up to 7 meters of mulch trimmings per annum, providing both wind protection and a continuous mulch supply.

**Sheep Forage Systems:** At Banks Peninsula, New Zealand, DSIR trials by Doug Davies demonstrate tagasaste planted at 1 meter spacing within rows and 2 meters between rows, providing summer forage from coppice and short-period browsing by sheep. Grasses thrive between the rows, creating an integrated pastoral-agroforestry system.

**Establishment Through Nurse Crops:** On the Canterbury Plain, New Zealand, tagasaste is drilled with turnip for future sheep forage. Seedling trees establish through the turnip (brassica) crop at Matthew Carpenter's property, demonstrating how annual crops can serve as nurse crops for establishing perennial tree systems.

## Wind Protection Strategies

### Pampas Grass and Tagasaste Strips

Pampas grass (*Cortaderia selloana*) provides fast shelter for lambing ewes and serves as preferred forage for most livestock. It propagates readily by divisions and combines well with tagasaste strips, creating a multi-layered wind protection system at Banks Peninsula, New Zealand.

### Willow Coppice as Windbreak

Willow (*Salix* species) serves multiple functions in cool temperate windbreak systems:

**Strict Form Windbreaks:** Matsudana willow provides a narrow, strict windbreak for kiwifruit vine crops on the plains near Nelson, New Zealand. The columnar form minimizes space loss on the ground and requires little maintenance to control shape.

**Coppice Production:** Near Christchurch, New Zealand, willow coppice crops serve basketry, medicinals, forage, "stickwood" for radiant (mass) heaters, active charcoal filters, and artist's charcoal. "Willow water" — fresh chips soaked in water — provides gibberellic acid for promoting root growth in cuttings, making willow an essential nursery tool.

**Pollarding for Propagation:** At Perth, Tasmania, willows are pollarded for propagation cuttings at a nursery, demonstrating the integration of windbreak trees with nursery production.

## Small Livestock Integration

### Rabbit Tractors

At Forest, Tasmania, Michael Mangevelakis uses a rabbit cage "tractor" that cleans clover and grasses from mounded strawberry crops. Rabbits are part of the yield of the weed system and provide manure for the strawberries. The tractor system moves the rabbits across beds, simultaneously controlling weeds, fertilizing crops, and producing meat.

### Chicken Tractors

At Lake Chelan, Okanogan, USA, chickens "tractor" grasses for small fruit crops, weed small fruits over the autumn period, and provide manure at Michael Pilarski's farm. This system replaces mechanical weeding and synthetic fertilization with animal labor.

### Chicken-Heated Greenhouses

At Aspen, Colorado, chicken-heated greenhouses combined with solar-heated seedling cloches enable early planting in short-season high mountain areas. The body heat from chickens provides supplemental heating for frost protection.

### Turkey Forage Under Autumn Olive

At Wilton, New Hampshire, turkeys forage beneath autumn olive (*Elaeagnus umbellata*) which provides abundant berries. Siberian pea shrub (*Caragana arborescens*) and autumn olive are identified as the most important tree forage legumes for cool and cold climates, providing both poultry food and nitrogen fixation.

## Protective Planting Strategies

### Bramble-Apple Associations

Geoff Wallace at Kiewa, Victoria, Australia, observed free-seeded apples thriving in blackberries on his farm. His cattle demolished the brambles to reach the apples. He now plants apples in the brambles — a practice traditional in England — where they prosper in their protected hole, and soon the brambles are trodden down by cattle eager for sweet apples. This is a classic example of using thorny plants as protective nurse species for valuable fruit trees.

### Thorny Mulch Protection

At Mahalapye, Botswana, Dorothy Noaba plants fierce thorny mulch from *Acacia tortilis* to protect new trees from intense browsing pressure in compounds. J.M. Bassett at Calder, Tasmania, confines his rampant thorny berry vines in a "straightjacket" of roof combs, which also makes for easier fruit harvest.

### Fallen Tree Protection

Near desert inselbergs in Central Australia, fallen trees provide protection from feral animals for young trees and enable establishment of food trees within the branches. This mimics natural gap dynamics where fallen timber creates nurse sites for regeneration.

## Forest Integration

### Stump-Based Berry Production

At Whidbey Island, Washington, USA, stumps of firs grow many berry species. Slabs from fir stumps, fibers, and logs generate berry clumps on lawns or in clearings, as do fallen fir logs. Birds bring seed and manure to stumps, and species including salmonberry, black raspberry, huckleberry, and wild strawberry thrive — all protected from deer browsing.

At Cle Elum, Washington, USA, clearings in fir forest maintain high production of berry fruits, fungi, and seeds, all important crops to the Salish Indians. This demonstrates the principle that forest edges and gaps are often more productive than either open field or dense forest.

### Jean Pain Composting in Forest

At Tumbarumba, NSW, Australia, Neil Dauce and Jason Alexandra reduce forest fire fuels through a "Jean Pain" approach. Wood chips are mulched, composted, and fermented to methane. Mechanical removal of forest fuel reduces fire risk and enriches soils, as opposed to "cool burns" which impoverish soils and often lead to wildfire. This approach simultaneously addresses fire management, energy production, and soil building.

## Urban Applications

### The Green Guerillas

In the Bronx, New York, the Green Guerillas transform vacant urban wasteland into productive, beautiful, and recreational city farms. This demonstrates that cool temperate permaculture principles apply equally to urban environments.

### Schreber Gardens

In West Germany, productive Schreber gardens adjoin almost every settlement, providing food and recreation. People are permitted to spend weekends on these mini-farms, representing a model for greening cities and providing food security.

### Bronx Frontier Composting

A small composting business in the Bronx, operated by "The Bronx Frontier" group, represents the only example of productive use of New York's food waste at the time of documentation. The resulting compost mountains are sold off-site to create meadows over rubble-filled wastelands.

### Urban Building Surfaces as Agricultural Space

West or sun-facing walls in cities and towns provide radiant heat for marginal crops. Espaliered pears grow well on urban barn walls. Most urban buildings can be regarded as additional agricultural space, as can trellis systems over alleys, shopping complexes, and building walls.

### Energy Conservation: Project WARM

At Louisville, Kentucky, Project WARM volunteers retrofit and insulate old folks' homes by the thousand, sharply reducing energy costs and deaths from cold. The cost amortizes in two winters. This underscores the permaculture principle that it is always cheaper to conserve energy than to generate it, and that such conservation projects make dangerous or dirty power generating systems obsolete.

## Keyhole Beds and Herb Spirals

At Stanley, Tasmania, tomatoes are grown in keyhole beds sheltered by a windbreak of sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke) in old tires on a cold, wind-swept site. A herb spiral in the form of a ziggurat provides ample culinary herbs at the kitchen door, with drainage and aspect suited to most cool-area herb species (spiral 2 meters across, 1 meter high).

## Key Principles

1. **Work with succession, not against it** — Pioneer weeds like gorse are assets, not enemies
2. **Multi-purpose species selection** — Tagasaste, willow, and autumn olive each serve many functions
3. **Animal integration replaces machinery** — Tractor systems with rabbits, chickens, and turkeys
4. **Thorny plants as nurse crops** — Brambles, acacia, and lycium protect valuable species from browsing
5. **Forest gaps are productive zones** — Stumps, fallen trees, and clearings create micro-niches
6. **Urban surfaces are agricultural space** — Walls, roofs, and vacant lots all have productive potential
7. **Energy conservation before generation** — Retrofitting and insulation are more cost-effective than new power systems
