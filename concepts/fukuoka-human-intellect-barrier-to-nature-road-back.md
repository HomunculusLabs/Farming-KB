# Fukuoka on the Human Intellect as Barrier to Nature

[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s central philosophical argument from *The Road Back to Nature* (1987): that the human intellect and reason are the primary obstacles preventing humanity from returning to nature and reuniting with God. This is not a rejection of practical knowledge but a critique of the fragmented, analytical mode of thinking that dissects nature and, in doing so, destroys it.

## The Original Unity of God, Nature, and Man

Fukuoka begins with the premise that God, nature, and man were originally one indivisible whole. This unity was fractured by mankind through the exercise [[fukuoka-textdoc-errors-of-human-intellect]]. The "road back to nature" is therefore the effort to reunify what has been split apart — but this reunion is impossible through the same intellectual faculty that caused the separation.

He writes that "the fragmented and diffusive development [[fukuoka-pit-of-knowledge]] which expands outward without aim or direction has brought human thought to the extremes of confusion, recklessly splitting apart God, nature, and man — originally one indivisible — and leaving only a legacy of incoherent chaos."

The critical point is that the human intellect is not neutral — it is inherently fragmenting. Every act of analysis breaks wholes into parts, and the parts, once separated, are dead. Nature, as a living holistic biosystem, cannot survive this dissection.

## Reason and Understanding Are Mutually Antagonistic

Fukuoka draws a sharp distinction between reason (understanding, discriminative knowledge) and what he calls transcendentally perceived knowledge — the innate, [[non-discriminating-knowledge-and-critique-of-scientific-reductionism]] awareness that is "the basis of good sense."

Reason and understanding play opposing roles. The intellect attempts to open up nature, but it succeeds only in closing it down. [[fukuoka-nature-transcends-human-knowledge]] is a cumulative process of judgment by the human intellect. At first, reason appeared capable of becoming the means for conversing with God, but instead it turned out to be a dangerous weapon that strips man of wisdom and brutalizes God.

The flower perceived innocently is itself divine nature, but when examined with the intellect, it is transformed into the cold flower of reason, the heart of which nature shuts out.

This is not anti-intellectualism in the crude sense. Fukuoka acknowledges that the human intellect has practical utility within its limited domain. The problem arises when intellect is elevated to the sole means of understanding reality, when the discriminating mind is trusted above direct, non-conceptual perception of the living world.

## The Illusion of "Ravaged Nature" as Nature

Some people say that nature ravaged by man is still nature, that the wasted desert lands left in the wake of human civilization are nature nonetheless. Fukuoka vehemently disagrees. However far one may wander through such fields and mountains, however long one may live in a secluded glen, man can only gaze upon nature's outer shell; he will never have access to its true inner heart and soul.

The soul of nature is also the will of God who dwells in nature. This is not something that scientists can find by dissecting nature. Scientists are incapable even of knowing that they are in no position to understand the soul of a flower in the meadow.

This argument has practical agricultural implications: when agricultural scientists claim to understand soil by analyzing its chemical components, or to understand a plant by isolating its genetic material, they are studying fragments — dead pieces of a once-living whole. The knowledge gained, while internally consistent within its limited framework, tells us nothing about the living system from which the fragments were taken.

## The Perils of Named Knowledge

Fukuoka is particularly critical of the human tendency to name and classify. By naming things — calling one organism a "pest" and another a "beneficial insect," labeling one plant a "weed" and another a "crop" — humans impose categorical distinctions that do not exist in nature.

Notions of right and wrong, good and bad, are alien to nature. These are only distinctions invented by man. Nature maintained a great harmony without such notions, and brought forth the grasses and trees without the "helping" hand of man.

This extends to scientific taxonomy itself. Geneticists extract and synthesize genes, believing they are exploring the root of life. But nature's soul does not lie hidden within DNA. Nor is this where God resides. The map is not the territory, and the more precisely we map the components, the further we drift from the living whole.

## God Did Not Create — God Came to Dwell

Fukuoka offers a striking reformulation of the creation narrative. Rather than God creating heaven, earth, and the cosmos as a separate act of will, he proposes that "when the Earth was born and the meadow flowers bloomed, the butterflies fluttered about, and the birds sang, God came of his own choosing to dwell there."

This reframes the relationship between creator and creation. God is not a remote architect who built nature from outside but an immanent presence that emerges with and within the natural world. The implication for farming is profound: if God dwells within the natural processes of growth, decay, and regeneration, then agricultural practices that interfere with these processes are not merely inefficient — they are a form of spiritual blindness.

## The Futility of Scientific Approaches to Nature

Fukuoka argues that scientific methods, regardless of how rational or sophisticated, will always produce wrong results when applied to nature because they begin with misconceptions about the nature of reality itself.

"Because he starts off with misconceptions about nature and takes the wrong approach to understanding it, regardless of how rational his thinking, everything winds up all wrong. We must become aware of the insignificance of human knowledge and activity, and begin by grasping their uselessness and futility."

This is not nihilism but a form of radical humility. By accepting the limits of human knowledge, one becomes open to perceiving nature directly, without the distorting filter of preconceived categories. The farmer who stops trying to "improve" nature and instead observes and follows its patterns discovers that nature already provides everything needed for abundant harvests.

## The Paradox of Studying Nature

Fukuoka identifies a fundamental paradox: the more intensely one studies nature through scientific methods, the further one moves from actual nature. This is because:

1. **Analysis requires isolation**: To study any component of nature, it must be separated from its context — but the context is precisely what gives it life and meaning.
2. **Measurement requires reduction**: Quantification inevitably reduces complex, multi-dimensional phenomena to numbers, losing essential qualities in the process.
3. **Experimentation requires control**: Controlled experiments eliminate variables that are, in reality, integral parts of the system being studied.

"The living and holistic biosystem that is nature cannot be dissected or resolved into its parts. Once broken down, it dies. Or rather, those who break off a piece of nature lay hold of something that is dead, and, unaware that what they are examining is no longer what they think it to be, claim to understand nature."

## The Modern Condition: Flowers in Vases

Fukuoka uses the metaphor of cut flowers to illustrate the modern relationship with nature. People bring home flowers and display them in vases, vying with each other in the art of flower arrangement. Caught up as they are with the image of themselves represented therein, civilized man today is no longer able to see the flowers — or God — in the fields.

Modern man drops the flowers in the fields to embrace the wildly and falsely blooming flowers of vast civilizations. The photograph replaces direct experience. Nature remains only as a means for cleansing ignoble man.

Rather than indulging directly in the raw, unrefined beauty of nature, people return home with their rolls of exposed film and are content to enjoy the natural beauty captured in their photographs. They don't call upon their innate sense of beauty, finding instead a greater attraction in nature as an object for self-expression.

This extends to agriculture: modern farming practices are not about growing food in relationship with nature but about manufacturing food products using industrial processes. The farmer has become a hired hand of industrialized society, using oil energy to manufacture synthetic food products that are poor imitations of natural food.

## Toward Innocent Perception

The alternative Fukuoka offers is a return to innocent, non-conceptual perception — the way a child sees the world before language and categories intervene. The flower perceived innocently is itself divine nature. This is not regression to ignorance but the recovery of a mode of awareness that modern education systematically suppresses.

In practical terms, this means approaching farming and life with fewer preconceptions, fewer judgments about what is good or bad, fewer attempts to control outcomes. It means trusting the processes of nature and intervening only minimally — the essence of "do-nothing" farming.

## Connection to Agricultural Practice

This philosophy directly underpins Fukuoka's [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]]. Each "no" is a refusal to impose human intellectual judgments on natural processes:

- **No tillage**: Trusting that soil organisms and plant roots maintain soil structure without plowing.
- **No fertilizer**: Trusting that natural nutrient cycling provides everything plants need.
- **No pesticides**: Trusting that the balance of predator and prey prevents pest outbreaks.
- **No weeding**: Recognizing that the concept of "weed" is a human invention with no basis in natural ecology.

Each principle is not a negative prohibition but a positive statement of trust in nature's inherent wisdom. The farmer's role shifts from controller to observer, from manager to participant in a process that was perfect before human intervention and remains perfect when left alone.

## The Shortest Road

Fukuoka closes his preface with a poem and a proposition: "Instead of praying to God as a mighty power that reigns over the heavens, man should have frolicked innocently with this wonderful sprite, this angel inhabiting the fields. That was the shortest road back to nature and at once the Great Way back to the side of God."

The road back to nature is not a path of accumulating more knowledge or developing better technology. It is a path of unlearning — of stripping away the layers of intellectual construction that separate us from the living world we inhabit.

## The Unfinished Road

Fukuoka is frank about his own failures on this path. He recounts receiving a vision of God in his youth — a moment of total revelation — and then turning away from it out of fear and arrogance. The decades that followed were spent as a "dilettante farmer tilling a lost paradise," aware of what he had abandoned but unable to return.

This autobiographical confession serves to illustrate the power of the intellectual trap: even one who has directly perceived the unity of God, nature, and man can be drawn back into the world of discriminating thought, self-concern, and futile effort. The intellect is not merely an external obstacle but an internal one — woven into the fabric of human consciousness itself.

[[natural-farming]], for Fukuoka, is not a perfected system but a direction — "one of the spiritual lights that must be kept burning throughout the night." It exists as a brake against the "mad onslaught of scientific agriculture," a reminder that another way is possible even if it can never be fully realized through human effort alone.
