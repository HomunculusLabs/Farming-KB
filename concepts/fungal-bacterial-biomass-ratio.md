# Fungal to Bacterial Biomass Ratios in Soil

The ratio of fungal to bacterial biomass (F:B ratio) is one of the most important indicators of soil health and suitability for specific plants. Different plant types have evolved in ecosystems with characteristic [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]], and matching plants to their preferred F:B ratio is fundamental to successful [[soil-food-web]] gardening.

## The Two Nitrogen Forms

Plants require nitrogen for amino acid production, but two different forms are available through [[the-soil-food-web]]:

- **Nitrate (NO₃⁻)**: Preferred by annuals, vegetables, and grasses; prevalent in bacterially dominated soils
- **Ammonium (NH₄⁺)**: Preferred by trees, shrubs, and perennials; prevalent in fungally dominated soils

When nematodes and protozoa consume fungi and bacteria, nitrogen is released as ammonium in their waste. In bacterially dominated soils, bacterial slime creates a pH above 7, providing ideal conditions for nitrifying bacteria that rapidly convert ammonium to nitrate. In fungally dominated soils, organic acids produced by fungi lower the pH below 7, suppressing nitrifying bacteria and allowing ammonium to persist.

## Succession and Microbial Dominance

Plant succession drives the shift from [[bacterial-to-fungal-soil-succession]] dominance across ecosystems:

1. **Pioneer weeds and grasses** on bare soil → bacterially dominated
2. **Prairie plants** → transitional, balanced bacteria and fungi
3. **Shrubs** → increasing fungal dominance
4. **Soft hardwoods** and expanding saplings → fungal-dominated
5. **Mature hardwoods** → strongly fungal-dominated
6. **Old-growth conifer forests** → maximally fungal-dominated

Early succession plants are short-lived, making it difficult to establish mycorrhizal partnerships (there is little advantage to a partnership with a plant that dies quickly). Their litter is composed primarily of simple [[cellulose]], which bacteria readily digest. As ecosystems mature, plant litter increasingly contains [[lignin]] and complex carbohydrates—food sources that favor fungi. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] also benefit from longer-lived host roots, accumulating more biomass over time.

## Bacterial Populations Are Consistent

An important insight is that bacterial numbers remain remarkably similar across ecosystem types: approximately 100 million to 1 billion bacteria per teaspoon of soil in gardens, prairies, and forests alike. The shift in F:B ratio is driven almost entirely by changes in **fungal biomass**, not by decreases in bacterial populations.

## Specific F:B Ratio Targets

### Vegetables and Annuals

Most vegetables and annual flowers prefer bacterially dominated soils with F:B ratios of:

- **Carrots, lettuce, broccoli, and cole crops**: 0.3:1 to 0.8:1
- **Tomatoes, corn, wheat**: 0.8:1 to 1:1

### Lawns and Grasses

- **Lawns**: 0.5:1 to 1:1 (balanced, similar to prairie ecosystems)

### Perennials

Most perennials prefer fungally dominated soils, consistent with their longer lifespan and the [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-and-mycorrhizal-relationships]] they maintain over years.

### Shrubs

Shrubs generally require higher fungal dominance than perennials. Species native to conifer forests (e.g., rhododendrons) need very high F:B ratios, while those from deciduous forests (e.g., cotoneaster, lilac) require somewhat less.

### Trees

Trees require the highest fungal dominance, reflecting their forest origins:

- **Conifers**: 50:1 to 1000:1
- **Maples, oaks, poplars**: 10:1 to 100:1
- **Orchard trees**: 10:1 to 50:1
- **Riparian trees** (alder, beech, aspen, cottonwood): bacterially dominated when young (F:B < 1:1), shifting to 5:1 to 100:1 at maturity

### Prairies

Prairie ecosystems represent the transitional zone where bacteria and fungi exist in roughly equal proportions—neither clearly dominant. This balance supports the diverse mix of grasses and forbs characteristic of prairie plant communities.

## A Simple Rule of Thumb

Plant lifespan provides a reliable guide to nitrogen preference:

- **One season or less** (vegetables, annuals) → prefer nitrates → bacterially dominated soil
- **One year or more** (perennials, shrubs, trees) → prefer ammonium → fungally dominated soil

This pattern makes ecological sense: fungi are fragile organisms that require time to grow, and mycorrhizal fungi specifically need living roots to associate with. Longer-lived plants provide the stable conditions for fungal communities to develop.

## Measuring F:B Ratios

Agricultural testing laboratories can measure soil F:B ratios, providing essential baseline data for soil food web management. These tests quantify the fungal and bacterial biomass present, revealing which organisms dominate and in what proportion. This information, combined with knowledge of what you are growing, guides decisions about whether to add fungal foods or bacterial foods to shift the ratio in the desired direction.

## Practical Implications

Understanding F:B ratios transforms soil management from a guessing game into a science. Rather than applying generic fertilizers, gardeners can:

- Assess current soil biology through lab testing
- Identify the target F:B ratio for their specific plants
- Apply appropriate [[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]] (fungal foods like woody mulches, or bacterial foods like compost teas [[cover-crops-and-green-manures]]) to shift the ratio
- Monitor changes over time through follow-up testing

This targeted approach works with the soil food web rather than against it, supporting plant health through the natural nutrient cycling mechanisms that evolved alongside each plant type.
