# Banyan Tree Bacterial Endophytes

## Isolation, Cultivation, and In Planta Visualization

Endophytic bacteria reside within plant tissues without causing substantive harm
to their host. They may benefit the host through competition for habitat via
antibiotic production, induction of [[sterilization]].

## Cultivation of Endophytes

Surface-sterilized root segments are placed on Luria agar and potato dextrose
[[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]].

Maceration is an alternative approach for recovering slow-growing endophytes.
Surface-sterilized root tissue is homogenized in sterile buffer using a mortar
and pestle or blender. Polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) or EDTA is added to prevent
plant enzymes or toxic materials from killing microorganisms during
homogenization. Care is taken to minimize heat generation during maceration.

## In Planta Visualization of Endophytes

### Vital Staining with Tetrazolium Dyes

Isolation procedures give only rough population estimates and cannot localize
endophytes within tissue. Vital staining overcomes these limitations. Bacteria
inside surface-sterilized banyan root tissue utilize malic acid as a carbon
source and reduce 2,3,5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) to form water-
insoluble red-colored formazans. Actively respiring bacterial cells deposit
formazans as discrete dark red to purple granules, visualized by light
microscopy at 100x magnification.

Protocol: Surface-sterilized roots are dipped into sterile TTC solution (0.15 g
TTC in 100 ml of 50 mM malate potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7) and incubated
overnight at 30 degrees C. Longitudinal or cross sections are prepared by hand
or cryotome, mounted on slides with phosphate buffer, and examined. TTC-stained
sections can be counterstained with 1% aniline blue for enhanced contrast.

### Fluorescence Microscopy with Acridine Orange

Acridine orange is a fluorescent cationic dye that interacts with DNA and RNA
through intercalation or electrostatic attraction. It stains microorganisms in
unfixed specimens, enabling direct counting of endophytes in situ when combined
with epifluorescence microscopy. Root sections are stained with working
solution for 3-5 minutes at room temperature, rinsed with phosphate buffer, and
observed. Counterstaining TTC-treated sections with acridine orange allows
differentiation of bacteria from small particles.

By viewing under epifluorescence illumination (counting fluorescing viable
bacteria) and then switching to phase contrast (counting all TTC-stained cells)
without moving the stage, researchers can confirm the presence of living bacteria
in root tissue.

### Electron Microscopy

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
provide powerful tools for identifying and placing endophytic bacteria in plant
tissue. TEM reduces surface contamination risk because it requires fixation and
sectioning. However, plant tissue preparation for EM is time-intensive and
requires expertise. Access of fixatives to intercellular spaces can be limited
by suberized and lignified parenchyma cells, creating difficulties in detecting
bacteria.
