# Root Cellar Design and Build

A root cellar is an underground or partially underground storage structure
that uses the natural insulating properties of the earth to maintain cool,
humid conditions for food storage. Before refrigeration, root cellars were
the primary method of preserving fall harvests through winter. This guide
covers design principles, construction methods, temperature and humidity
control, ventilation, and best practices for stored crops.

## Why Build a Root Cellar

- **No electricity required** — passive cooling; ideal for off-grid homesteads
- **Extends harvest** — keeps crops fresh 3-6 months without processing
- **Nutrition** — stores whole foods without canning, freezing, or drying
- **Energy independence** — no reliance on refrigeration for bulk storage
- **Low cost** — construction costs are modest compared to walk-in coolers

## Ideal Conditions

Different crops require different storage conditions, but the general root
cellar target is:

| Parameter | Ideal Range | Critical For |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 32-40F (0-4C) | Most root vegetables, apples, cabbage |
| Humidity | 85-95% relative humidity | Root vegetables; prevents shriveling |
| Ventilation | Continuous gentle airflow | Removes ethylene gas, prevents mold |
| Darkness | Complete darkness | Prevents sprouting of potatoes and onions |

### Crop-Specific Requirements

| Crop | Temperature | Humidity | Storage Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potatoes | 38-40F | 85-90% | 4-6 months |
| Carrots | 32-35F | 90-95% | 4-6 months |
| Beets | 32-35F | 90-95% | 3-5 months |
| Turnips/Rutabagas | 32-35F | 90-95% | 3-4 months |
| Onions/Garlic | 32-35F | 60-70% (dry) | 5-8 months |
| Apples | 30-35F | 90-95% | 3-6 months |
| Cabbage | 32-35F | 90-95% | 3-4 months |
| Winter squash | 50-55F | 50-70% (dry) | 3-6 months |
| Sweet potatoes | 55-60F | 80-85% | 4-6 months |

Note: apples produce ethylene gas that causes potatoes to sprout — store
them in separate areas or well-ventilated spaces.

## Design Principles

### Underground Placement
The earth maintains a stable temperature roughly equal to the average
annual air temperature at your location. In most temperate regions, soil
temperature at 6-10 feet depth stays between 50-55F year-round. A root
cellar dug into the ground leverages this stability.

### Insulation
- **Earth cover** — 2-3 feet of soil above the cellar provides natural
  insulation equivalent to R-10 to R-15
- **Additional insulation** — rigid foam (XPS or EPS) on the ceiling and
  above-grade walls increases thermal performance
- **Floor** — concrete or compacted earth; no insulation needed on the floor
  (earth contact helps maintain cool temperature)
- **Door** — insulated exterior door; weather-stripped; consider a double
  door system (vestibule) for reduced air exchange when opened

### Ventilation
Proper ventilation is critical for removing ethylene gas (which causes
ripening and spoilage), excess moisture, and heat.

**Two-pipe system:**
1. **Intake pipe** — draws fresh air in from outside; positioned low in
   the cellar near the floor
2. **Exhaust pipe** — allows warm stale air to exit; positioned high near
   the ceiling (warm air rises)

**Sizing:** 4-inch PVC pipes are minimum; 6-inch is better for larger
cellars. Both pipes should have adjustable dampers for seasonal control.

**Winter operation:** open both pipes to maximize cold air intake
**Summer operation:** close both pipes to retain coolth

### Drainage
- The cellar floor should slope slightly toward a drain or sump
- French drains around the exterior perimeter prevent groundwater from
  seeping in
- Site the cellar on high ground if possible; avoid low-lying wet areas
- Install a sump pump if groundwater is a known issue

## Construction Methods

### Excavated Root Cellar (Traditional)

A hole dug into a hillside or into level ground, with walls and ceiling
of concrete block, poured concrete, or stone.

**Steps:**
1. **Excavate** — dig a hole 8x10x7 feet deep (adjust for desired size);
   allow 2-3 feet of soil above the ceiling
2. **Foundation** — pour a concrete footer below frost line
3. **Walls** — build concrete block or poured concrete walls; waterproof
   exterior with tar or membrane
4. **Ceiling** — reinforced concrete slab or heavy timber with rigid foam
   insulation above
5. **Waterproofing** — apply waterproof membrane to exterior walls; install
   French drain around perimeter
6. **Entrance** — build a stairway or sloped entrance; insulated door at
   the top
7. **Ventilation** — install intake and exhaust pipes through the ceiling
8. **Floor** — pour concrete floor with slight slope toward drain; or leave
   as compacted earth
9. **Finishing** — install shelving, bins, humidity monitoring, and
   thermometer

**Cost estimate:** $1,500-5,000 DIY; $5,000-15,000 contracted

### Earth-Bermed Structure

A structure built at or near grade level, then covered with earth on the
sides and top. Uses timber frame, straw bale, or concrete block walls.

**Advantages:**
- Easier excavation — less digging required
- Accessible — at grade or near grade entrance
- Versatile — can incorporate into a hillside or bank
- Natural insulation from earth berm

**Steps:**
1. Build a shed-like structure with sturdy walls (concrete block or timber)
2. Cover walls and roof with waterproof membrane
3. Backfill earth against the walls and over the roof (minimum 18-24 inches)
4. Install ventilation pipes through the roof
5. Add an insulated door on the exposed end

### Precast Concrete Shelter

Repurpose a precast septic tank or storm shelter as a root cellar.
Quick installation, waterproof, and structural. Cost: $2,000-5,000
plus installation. Ensure adequate ventilation ports.

### Earthbag Root Cellar

Polypropylene bags filled with dampened earth, stacked in a dome or
rectangular shape. Plastered with earth or cement stucco. Very low cost
($500-1,500), DIY-friendly, with high thermal mass from earth-filled walls.

## Management Best Practices

### Pre-Storage Preparation
- Harvest crops at peak maturity; handle carefully to avoid bruising
- Cure crops before storage: potatoes (2 weeks at 55-65F in darkness),
  onions/garlic (2-3 weeks warm and dry), winter squash (2 weeks at 75-85F)
- Do not wash root vegetables; brush off loose soil only
- Remove any damaged, diseased, or bruised specimens

### Storage Organization
- Use slatted wooden crates or mesh bags for root vegetables (airflow)
- Store potatoes and onions in separate areas
- Shelves should allow air circulation on all sides
- Check stored crops weekly; remove anything showing spoilage

### Pest Control
- Seal all entry points against rodents; hardware cloth on ventilation pipes
- Keep the cellar clean and free of rotting material

## Monitoring

Install a thermometer and hygrometer. Check weekly and adjust ventilation
dampers as needed.

- **Too warm** — open intake pipe wider
- **Too cold** — partially close intake pipe, add insulation
- **Too dry** — partially close ventilation; place water pans on the floor
