## Fungicolous Fungi — Mycoparasitism and Host Interactions

## Overview

[[fungicolous-fungi]] (also called mycophilic fungi) are species of fungi that are consistently associated with other fungi, including [[lichenicolous-fungi]] that grow on lichens. The term encompasses a spectrum of trophic relationships—parasitic, commensal, mutualistic, and antagonistic/competitive. Fungicolous fungi have been known for centuries and are represented in many ecological categories, playing important roles in ecosystems through detoxification of substrata not otherwise accessible to decomposition. Many are also economically important as biocontrol agents of plant-pathogenic fungi or as parasites that damage crops of edible fungi.

## Historical Context and Diversity

The study of fungicolous fungi has a long history:

- **Rudakov (1978)**: Surveyed approximately 1,700 nonlichenicolous fungicolous species
- **Hawksworth (1979a, 1981a)**: Reported 1,100 conidial fungicolous species on approximately 2,500 host species (including lichens)
- **Lichenicolous fungi**: About 550 species recognized in 1979; now probably exceeding 1,500

A subgroup, sporocarp-inhabiting fungi (SCIF), refers specifically to fungi growing on sporocarps (fruiting bodies) of other fungi or [[slime-molds]].

## Types of Fungicolous Associations

### Trophic Categories

Interspecific relationships between fungicolous fungi and their hosts can be broadly characterized as:

1. **Neutralistic**: No discernible effect on either organism
2. **Commensalism**: Regular association with little visible effect on the host (e.g., many lichenicolous fungi; Hawksworth 1988b)
3. **Mutualistic**: Both organisms benefit from the association
4. **Antagonistic/Competitive**: One organism adversely affects the other

### Mycoparasitism

The term mycoparasite (Butler 1957) applies strictly to relationships in which one living fungus serves as a nutrient source for another. Within mycoparasitism, two main strategies are recognized:

#### Biotrophic Mycoparasites

Biotrophs live in a balanced relationship with a living host, without causing immediate, overt harm (reviewed by Manocha 1987, 1991). Characteristics:
- Often form specialized infection structures (haustoria)
- Typically have a narrower host range than necrotrophs
- Some become necrotrophic in later stages when they begin to sporulate

#### Necrotrophic Mycoparasites

Necrotrophs kill the cells of the host. Characteristics:
- Relatively unspecialized
- Broader range of fungal hosts than biotrophs
- Often release toxins and lytic enzymes (particularly chitinase)

### Intermediate and Variable Strategies

The biotrophic–necrotrophic distinction is not absolute:
- Many intermediate forms exist
- Some species change behavior during development (Rudakov 1978, 1981)
- Trophic relationships can be host-dependent: many species grow as biotrophs on certain hosts but as necrotrophs on others
- Rudakov (1978) recognized six groups: biotrophs, facultative biotrophs, necrotrophs, facultative necrotrophs, "semisaprophytic" mycophiles, and "saprophytic associates"

### Evolutionary Considerations

A semi-biotrophic mode of parasitism (biotrophic organisms later becoming necrotrophic or saprotrophic) may be more primitive than holobiotrophy and saprotrophy. Obligate parasitism may be a fundamental attribute of primitive groups, from which saprotrophic subgroups have arisen repeatedly. The specific associations between parasites and hosts are products of long coevolutionary processes likely as old as the fungi involved (Hass et al. 1994).

## Hyphal Interference

A phenomenon termed **hyphal interference** (Ikediugwu and Webster 1970a; Dennis and Webster 1971c) occurs when the mycelium of one fungus, growing close to (within 50 μm) or in contact with that of another species, reduces the growth rate and causes cytoplasmic disruption of the second fungus.

### Ultrastructural Features

Electron microscopy studies have revealed:
- Vacuolation at the point of contact
- Abundance of lipid droplets
- Invaginations of the host plasmalemma at the parasite contact point

Examples include:
- *Coprinus heptemerus* touching *Ascobolus crenulatus* (Ikediugwu 1976a)
- *Phlebiopsis (Peniophora) gigantea* encountering *[[heterobasidion-annosum]]* (Ikediugwu 1976b)
- *Fusarium oxysporum*, *Trichoderma viride*, and *Penicillium expansum* causing vacuolation in *Aspergillus niger* hyphae (Park and Robinson 1964; Robinson and Park 1965)

## Mycoparasite–Host Interface Types

Jeffries and Young (1994) and Jeffries (1995) distinguished five principal types of mycoparasite–host interfaces:

### Necrotrophic Interfaces (Host Cytoplasm Degenerates)

**Type 1: Contact Necrotrophic**
- Neither hyphae nor haustoria penetrate the host mycelium
- Damage occurs via hyphal interference
- Examples: *Arthrobotrys superba*, *A. oligospora* (predaceous [[nematophagous-fungi]] functioning as contact mycoparasites); *Tilletiopsis* species killing cells of powdery mildew *[[sphaerotheca-fuliginea]]*

**Type 2: Invasive Necrotrophic**
- Hyphae penetrate the cell wall and enter the host cell with considerable growth within host hyphae
- Examples: *Talaromyces flavus*, *Schizophyllum commune*, *Trichoderma* species, and many others

### Biotrophic Interfaces (Host Cytoplasm Remains Healthy, At Least Initially)

**Type 3: Haustorial**
- A short haustorial branch from the parasite hypha penetrates the host hypha
- Examples: Mycoparasites among the Zygomycota and Tremellales; *[[sporidesmium-sclerotivorum]]* parasitizing *[[sclerotinia-sclerotiorum]]*

**Type 4: Fusion (Micropore)**
- Micropores develop in walls of host and parasite hyphae that are in contact, allowing cytoplasmic contact
- An unusual type of interface
- Examples: *[[gonatobotrys-simplex]]*, *Hansfordia parasitica*, *Melanospora zamiae*; *[[tetragoniomyces-uliginosus]]* on *Rhizoctonia solani*; *Syzygospora pallida* on *Phanerochaete*

**Type 5: Intracellular**
- The complete thallus of the mycoparasite enters a hypha of the host
- Examples: Many Chytrid species and some Oomycetes growing inside other fungi

### Specialized Structures

- **Callosities**: Wall thickenings forming around penetrating haustorial branches, analogous to lignitubers produced by plants as defense reactions (Young 1926; Swart 1975)
- **Colacosomes/Lenticular bodies**: Specific organelles of certain parasites that facilitate nutrient exchange between host and parasite
- **Anchoring cells**: Wide cytoplasmic connections with the host, as in *Parasitella parasitica* on *Absidia glauca*

## Determining the Mode of Interaction

### Field Observation

Mycoparasitism is difficult to observe in nature but can be inferred when growth abnormalities are seen in a host. Even unequivocal demonstration of parasitism in laboratory cultures does not prove such a relationship occurs in nature (Jeffries 1995).

### Laboratory Techniques

**Microscopic observation**:
- Water, lactic acid (preferred over lactophenol), or cotton blue mounts for standard observation
- Dilute phloxine staining differentiates living (unstained) from dead (immediately stained) cells (Griffith and Barnett 1967)
- Immunolabeled dyes for specific organism localization (Mendgen and Casper 1980)
- Fluorescence methods for detecting enzymatic activity or lectin locations

**Advanced techniques**:
- Infrared photomicrography for detecting enzymatic activity sites (e.g., *Trichoderma harzianum* vs. *Rhizoctonia solani*; Elad et al. 1983b)
- Transmission electron microscopy for ultrastructural studies of infected host structures
- Radiolabeled compounds for demonstrating nutrient flow (e.g., ³²P translocation from *Rhizoctonia solani* to *Arthrobotrys oligospora*; Olsson and Persson 1994)

**Dual culture assays**:
- Slide culture techniques (Lutchmeah and Cooke 1984) for observing parasitic action microscopically
- Cellophane disk method: growing potential antagonists on cellophane over agar, removing cellophane, then inoculating test fungus to assess inhibition
- Paired plate method: assessing volatile metabolite production by fastening inoculated plates opposite each other

## Physiological Bases of Mycoparasitism

### Nutrient Acquisition

- Foley and Deacon (1986) demonstrated that mycoparasitic *Pythium* species obtain carbon, nitrogen, thiamine, and sterols from hosts, with detrimental consequences for the host
- Many mycoparasites can grow axenically, but some contact biotrophs require at least host extracts
- Nutritional requirements in vitro may differ from those on a living host

### Antibiotic and Enzyme Production

- **Necrotrophs**: Release toxins and lytic enzymes (particularly chitinase) into the environment
