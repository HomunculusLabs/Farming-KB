# Plant Name Identification Problems

The identification of ancient plant names poses formidable difficulties that have plagued botanists since antiquity.

As Pliny observed some nineteen hundred years ago: "An added difficulty in botany is the variety of names given to the same plant in different districts."

This regional variation meant that a single species could accumulate dozens of local appellations, making cross-referencing and accurate identification nearly impossible without standardized systems.

The classical pharmacological tradition illustrates the scale of this problem vividly. Dioscorides, the first-century Greek physician whose *De Materia Medica* remained authoritative for over a millennium, records more than twenty separate names for the mandrake (*[[mandragora-officinarum]]*) alone.

Each name reflected local usage, linguistic variation, and sometimes deliberate obfuscation, rendering any attempt at a unified botanical taxonomy extremely difficult.

Compounding the problem was the absence of reliable classification methods. "Until comparatively recently, botanists lacked adequate methods of classification, so that plants tended to be grouped together on the basis of what we nowadays would consider secondary characteristics."

Plants sharing superficial visual traits — leaf shape, growth habit, or color — were placed in common categories regardless of their actual biological relationships.

This meant that ancient plant names often referred to groupings that bore little resemblance to modern taxonomic families.

Yet perhaps the most significant obstacle was deliberate secrecy. Pliny himself lamented: "The most disgraceful reason for this scanty knowledge is that even those who possess it refuse to teach it, just as though they would themselves lose what they have imparted to others."

Knowledge of potent plants — particularly those with psychoactive or [[amanita-muscaria-medicinal-properties-heart-anxiety-dreams]] — was closely guarded by specialist practitioners who treated it as proprietary and even dangerous to share.

This culture of concealment ensured that genuine botanical understanding remained fragmented and esoteric, passed only from master to apprentice within closed professional or cultic circles.

# Sumerian Philological Advantage

Despite these obstacles, John Allegro argued that Sumerian philology offers a unique pathway through the maze of ancient plant names.

"Despite the long gap in time between the Sumerian botanists and their Greek and Roman successors it now appears that many of the important names of plants remained virtually unchanged."

The Sumerians, writing in the third millennium BCE, composed plant names from meaningful word-elements that could be decoded millennia later through careful etymological analysis.

This linguistic durability provides modern researchers with a tool unavailable to classical philologists: the ability to reconstruct original botanical referents by analyzing the component meanings of Sumerian compound names.

However, this continuity is deceptive in some cases. Over the course of millennia, names originally designating one plant could migrate to entirely different species.

The result is widespread confusion in the botanical record, where a name's original referent has been lost and replaced by a later, often unrelated plant.

Yet Allegro contended that the original meaning remains recoverable through philological analysis of the Sumerian word-elements, even when the name has been transferred to a new botanical host.

The paeony provides a compelling example. The Sumerian name *BAR_IA_U_NA can be parsed as "capsule of fecundity" or "womb," which Allegro argues identifies the original referent not as the flower we now call paeony, but rather as *[[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]* — the fly-agaric mushroom.

Its distinctive umbel-like cap and potent pharmacological properties would have warranted such a designation. The name later attached itself to the familiar garden flower while the original fungal identification was lost.

Similarly, the name cotyledon derives from Sumerian *GU_TAL-U_DUN, meaning "ball-and-socket" or "penis-and-vulva."

This sexual imagery, Allegro argues, points directly to the mushroom's morphology — its stem (phallus) and cap (vulva) — and the hip-joint myths associated with this plant name ultimately originate in [[allegro-cross-and-mushroom-symbolism]], not in any property of the succulent plant that now bears the name.

# Vegetation Mythology

Allegro identified numerous biblical narratives as disguised mushroom mythology, their original botanical significance obscured by centuries of transmission and translation.

Among the most striking examples is Jotham's parable in Judges 9:7–15, in which the trees of the forest seek a king. After the olive, fig, and vine each decline the honor, the diminutive bramble — which Allegro reads as mushroom — accepts the crown and declares that all must take refuge under its canopy.

This inversion, where the smallest and most inconspicuous plant becomes king and protector, reflects the mushroom's paradoxical status in ancient cult practice: physically diminutive yet pharmacologically supreme.

The book of Jonah offers another case. The prophet is sheltered by a plant that "came into being in a night and perished in a night," a description that perfectly matches the rapid growth cycle of certain fungi.

The presence of worms, the plant's sudden appearance and disappearance, and even Jonah's own name — which Allegro connects to [[guzman-allen-gartz-indigenous-mexican-mushroom-nomenclature-sacred-names]] through Hebrew and Aramaic word-roots — all point to a narrative originally constructed around fungal symbolism.

A crucial insight concerns the transmission of these stories. Parables were not originally free-floating moral tales; they came with explanatory commentary that made their hidden botanical references explicit.

Over time, however, this instructional context was lost. The tales continued to circulate as "good yarns" — entertaining stories appreciated for their narrative surface while their deeper significance faded from collective memory.

"A vegetation myth could be adapted by a later writer, fully aware of its original significance, to serve as the medium for some new teaching," allowing successive generations to repurpose ancient botanical allegory for entirely different theological purposes.

# Mushroom Taboo and Name Proliferation

The mushroom's psychoactive properties placed it at the center of ancient religious practice — and simultaneously rendered its true name unspeakable.

"Since certain of the species contain drugs with marked hallucinatory properties, it is not surprising that the mushroom should have become the centre of a mystery cult."

The very potency that made the mushroom sacred also made its name taboo: to speak the true name was to risk revealing the cult's most guarded secret to the uninitiated.

This taboo produced a remarkable proliferation of folk-names and circumlocutions. The original designation was suppressed and replaced by an ever-expanding vocabulary of coded references, each culture and era generating its own euphemisms.

Over time, the true botanical identity became irrecoverable from the surviving literature alone, buried beneath layers of metaphor and misdirection.

Nowhere is this more apparent than in the Hebrew Bible. "In the Bible, where mushroom mythology plays a most important part, the word 'mushroom' has been nowhere noted."

The absence is itself evidence of the taboo: the sacred fungus could be described, alluded to, and celebrated in metaphor, but never named directly.

Instead, Hebrew uses the term *kotereth*, related to the Accadian *katarru*, meaning "mushroom-shaped capital of a pillar." This architectural metaphor appears many times in biblical descriptions of temple furnishings, encoding the mushroom's distinctive profile in plain sight.

Allegro offered a vivid analogy to illustrate the scale of the problem: "It is as if the only name by which we knew the mushroom was the folk-name 'toadstool,' and that some researcher of the future was faced with the problem of deciding what species of plant life served as the habitual perch of large frogs."

The folk-etymology would actively mislead investigators away from the true referent, just as biblical euphemisms have obscured the mushroom's central role in ancient Near Eastern religion.

# Mushroom Imagery in Mythology

The mushroom's distinctive morphology — a slender stem surmounted by a broad, domed cap — made it a natural model for a wide range of mythological and architectural forms.

Its silhouette resembles a sunshade or parasol, an image that appears in Greek mythology in the figure of Atlas bearing the celestial canopy and in the very name Olympus, which Allegro connects to the mushroom's umbrella-like shape.

The giants of Greek myth also reflect fungal morphology. The Sumerian term GIG-AN-TI, which entered Greek as "gigantes" (giants), encodes mushroom characteristics in its very etymology, connecting the primordial race of enormous beings to the fungus that loomed so large in ancient cosmological thinking.

Sexual imagery pervades mushroom nomenclature. The mushroom grows from a volva (a cup-like structure at its base), rises with remarkable speed in a manner resembling erection, and terminates in a glans-like cap.

These features naturally suggested phallic interpretations, and many ancient names for the mushroom are transparently sexual in origin.

The Hebrew *kotereth*, the Arabic *phutr*, and the Aramaic *piträ'* all encode this phallic dimension, and Allegro traces a direct line from these terms to the New Testament figure of Peter — whose name, he argues, preserves the mushroom's most characteristic designation.

# Mystery Cult Secrecy

The institutional framework that preserved mushroom knowledge was the mystery cult, and secrecy was its defining feature.

"The whole point of a mystery cult was that few people knew its secret doctrines." Initiation conferred privileged access to pharmacological and theological knowledge that was deliberately withheld from the general population.

The cult's power derived precisely from the gap between the exoteric rituals visible to all and the esoteric understanding available only to the initiated.

Within this framework, knowledge transmission followed strict protocols. "Normally the secrets of the sect were transmitted orally, novices being required to learn direct from their mentors by heart, and placed under the most violent oaths never to disclose the details even under torture."

Oral transmission minimized the risk of written exposure, while the severity of the oaths ensured that even captured initiates would resist interrogation.

This oral tradition also meant that the deepest secrets could be lost entirely if a chain of transmission was broken — a vulnerability that makes modern reconstruction extraordinarily difficult.

When secrets did need to be committed to writing, cult members employed elaborate concealment strategies. Special codes and ciphers were developed, as evidenced by the cryptic texts found among the [[allegro-dead-sea-scrolls]], which Allegro interprets as products of just such a secret tradition.

Alternatively, sacred knowledge could be embedded within seemingly innocuous documents — scriptural narratives, hymns, and legal texts whose surface meaning concealed a deeper, pharmacological message accessible only to those who possessed the interpretive key.

This strategy of concealment within plain sight reached its fullest expression, Allegro argues, in the New Testament itself. "Plant mythology, known for thousands of years... provided the New Testament cryptographers with their 'cover.'"

The gospel writers, inheritors of a mushroom-based mystery tradition, encoded their secret pharmacological theology within narratives of Jesus's life and ministry, using the ancient vegetation myths as a literary framework that would be recognized by initiates but remain opaque to outsiders.

"Thus was born the Gospel myth of the New Testament" — a text that functioned simultaneously as devotional scripture for the general faithful and as coded manual for those who understood its mycological referents.
