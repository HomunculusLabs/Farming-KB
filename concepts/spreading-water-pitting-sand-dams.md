# Spreading Water and Sand Dam Systems

Water spreading techniques and [[permaculture]] for rehabilitating arid, semi-arid, and degraded landscapes. These methods
focus on distributing runoff across broad areas, maximizing infiltration, and storing
water within geological substrates rather than in open surface reservoirs prone to
evaporation. Together, they form a continuum from small-scale surface treatments
(pitting) to medium-scale runoff management (spreader banks and spillways) to large-scale
subsurface water storage ([[sand-dams-and-clearwater-reservoirs]]).

## Pitting in Sands and Light Soils

Pitting is a surface roughening technique for sandy and light-textured soils where water
retention is minimal and runoff can be significant even from modest rainfall. By creating
a distributed network of small depressions, pitting intercepts overland flow, increases
water residence time on the surface, and promotes infiltration into fast-draining
substrates.

### Design and Construction

Pits are created by cutting a quarter-section slice off disc blades, producing long
shallow depressions separated by solid undisturbed ground. These form staggered
mini-swales analogous to contour swales at a much smaller scale. Unlike full contour
swales, pitting need not be strictly surveyed — the pattern can be drawn by eye, making
it practical for large areas with minimal equipment.

Standard dimensions: 1.0–1.5 m long, spaced 0.5–0.75 m apart, maximum depth 0.3 m.
No single pit is large enough to overflow catastrophically; excess water spills
gradually into adjacent pits through the staggered pattern. Many forms of pitting
machines exist, and hand-digging is feasible for small-scale applications.

### Establishment and Hydrological Effects

Seed is added directly to pits — broadcast, blown in, or placed manually. Pelleted
desert shrub seed with light fertiliser establishes permanent soil stability. Pits
provide microsites where seed is protected from wind and moisture concentrates for
germination. Pitting acts as immediate surface roughening and can eliminate dust storms
overnight.

Research by Cordon (1975) shows average annual arid-zone runoff is approximately 20%,
rising to 80% on eroded or compacted soils. Pitting captures a significant portion.
Vegetation in furrows remains green during drought. The technique works best in soils
with capacity to absorb moisture — primarily sands and loamy sands.

On slopes greater than 2%, contour furrows at 2 m intervals are recommended, with only
every 20th to 30th furrow surveyed for accuracy. Swales should be "broken" —
deliberately interrupted — to allow controlled flooding to the next downslope series.
Scale can expand to 2–4 m spacing for larger installations.

## Spilling Water Downslope in Fragile Soils

Concentrated flows from road culverts, animal tracks, or diversion drains pose serious
erosion risk in fragile soils. Discharging concentrated flow onto vulnerable slopes
initiates gully formation. Safe methods must dissipate both energy and concentration
before water reaches erodible ground.

### Method 1: Spreader Banks

Very long spreader banks are low embankments aligned roughly on contour that intercept
concentrated flow and redirect it laterally as a thin sheet across the slope below.
They require regular maintenance and inspection during rain events to ensure correct
function and prevent breaching.

### Method 2: Grassed Spillways with Steering Banks

Grassed spillways use established vegetation to slow and filter flowing water. Steering
banks — small guide embankments — direct concentrated flow onto the vegetated surface
where friction and root systems dissipate energy. These take several years to fully
evolve as grass matures and root networks develop, but once established they are highly
resilient and self-maintaining.

### Method 3: Sealed Spillways and Pipes with Splash-Pools

For sites requiring engineered solutions, downslope sealed spillways or buried pipes
carry concentrated water to splash-pools, which overflow into swales or dams. The
splash-pool converts high-velocity flow into lower-energy sheet flow before it enters
the broader earthwork system. Appropriate where flows are large or fragile soils cannot
tolerate direct concentrated discharge.

### Method 4: V-Shaped Boulder Channels

V-shaped channels filled with graded boulders down to smaller rocks and gravels provide
multiple stable flow paths imitating natural dryland breccia sorting below cliffs. Large
boulders anchor the channel and break up main flow, intermediate rocks further dissipate
energy, and gravel beds filter and spread remaining water. Highly stable and appropriate
for restoration projects replicating natural drainage patterns.

## Sand Dams and Clearwater Reservoirs

Sand dams are among the most effective water storage systems for arid and semi-arid
foothill environments. Rather than storing water in open evaporation-vulnerable
reservoirs, sand dams trap water within sand and gravel substrates behind a subsurface
wall, creating a buried reservoir protected from evaporation and contamination.

### Site Selection and Construction

Suited to gently-rolling foothill washes carrying broad floodwater surges during
episodic rainfall. The dam backs up water 10–20 times the dam width. The wall is keyed
into both side banks 4–6 m deep. Two-metre-deep bays are cut into upstream banks for
increased storage and planting areas. Coarse stone placed against the upstream face acts
as a filter, with seepage flowing to a clearwater reservoir downstream.

### Storage and Filtration

First floods fill storage with silt and coarse sands. Shingle and rock layers filter
naturally, producing clean seepage for the clearwater dam. The clearwater dam should
occupy less than one-ninth of the total backed-up area, keeping it compact and deep.
Clearwater dams should be 5–9 m deep with reduced surface area rather than shallow and
wide, minimizing evaporation losses.

The dam intentionally leaks through the river bed. This controlled seepage creates
elevated groundwater downstream, supporting forest growth extending 1–2 km from a 50 m
wide wall. Rubble and sand behind the wall function as a buried reservoir with
remarkable retention — one documented example dropped only 8 cm in seven years.

### Ecological Integration

Bays fill with sand developing flat silt beds suitable for large trees: pecan, palm,
avocado, and mango. Where the barrier dam becomes fully sand-filled, sub-aquatic and
wetland species establish: asparagus, nipa palms, water chestnut, and swamp cypress.
Melons grow prolifically as swampy conditions recede, with legume-melon sequences as
productive follow crops. Bird guano mixed with 30–40 parts damp sand serves as
excellent phosphate fertiliser for plantings within the dam's zone of influence.

### Applicability

Hundreds to thousands of suitable sites exist near foothills in desert regions
worldwide. Sand dams represent one of the most scalable and ecologically productive
water harvesting strategies for dryland restoration, combining water storage, erosion
control, agricultural production, and reforestation in a single integrated system.
