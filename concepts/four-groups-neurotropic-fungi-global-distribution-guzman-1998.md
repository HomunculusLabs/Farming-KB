# Four Groups of Neurotropic Fungi and Their Global Distribution

## Overview

The 1998 comprehensive survey by Guzman, Allen, and Gartz catalogued **216 known species of neurotropic fungi** worldwide and organized them into four distinct chemical and cultural groups. This classification remains the most authoritative global accounting of hallucinogenic mushrooms and related fungi, drawing on more than 450 bibliographic references. The work revealed striking patterns in the geographic distribution of these species, with the highest diversity concentrated in subtropical humid forests near the Austral hemisphere.

## Group 1: Psilocybin and Related Indole Alkaloids

This is by far the largest group, containing species that produce [[psilocybin-compound]], [[psilocin]], [[baeocystin]], and/or [[norbaeocystin]]. The genera and their species counts within this group are:

| Genus | Species Count | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Psilocybe | 116 | Dominant genus; 54% of all psilocybin-containing fungi |
| Gymnopilus | 14 | Mostly temperate, wood-inhabiting |
| Panaeolus | 13 | Coprophilous; some lack chemical confirmation |
| Copelandia | 12 | Tropical and subtropical, formerly in Panaeolus |
| Hypholoma | 6 | Wood-inhabiting, mostly temperate |
| Pluteus | 6 | Wood-inhabiting on dead hardwood |
| Inocybe | 6 | Mycorrhizal; some lack chemical confirmation |
| Conocybe | 4 | Small, fragile, easily overlooked |
| Panaeolina | 4 | Including [[panaeolina-foenisecii]], the "haymaker's mushroom" |
| Gerronema | 2 | Tropical, lignicolous |
| Agrocybe | 1 | Single confirmed species |
| Galerina | 1 | Note: most Galerina are deadly poisonous (amatoxins) |
| Mycena | 1 | Single confirmed species |

Several species in this group, particularly among the Panaeoloideous fungi, have been reported based on visual similarity or traditional use but lack reliable chemical studies confirming the presence of psilocybin or related compounds.

## Group 2: Ibotenic Acid and Muscimol

This group contains three species of [[amanita]] that produce **ibotenic acid** (a glutamate receptor agonist) and its decarboxylation product **muscimol** (a GABA_A receptor agonist). These compounds have an entirely different pharmacological profile from the psilocybin group:

- **[[amanita-muscaria]]**: the iconic red-and-white fly agaric; historically used as an entheogen by Siberian peoples including the Chukchee and Koryak
- **[[amanita-pantherina]]**: the panther cap; contains ibotenic acid based on analysis by Hatfield (1979)
- **[[amanita-regalis-entity]]**: the king fly agaric; confirmed ibotenic acid content

Unlike the psilocybin-containing fungi, which are typically consumed fresh or dried, traditional Siberian use of [[amanita-muscaria]] often involved the practice of **eurphorization** (drinking the urine of someone who had consumed the mushroom), as the kidneys excrete the active muscimol while filtering out much of the toxic ibotenic acid and other unpleasant compounds.

## Group 3: Ergot Fungi

This group includes species that produce **ergot alkaloids** (ergolines), a chemically distinct class of neuroactive compounds:

- **[[claviceps-purpurea]]**: the classic ergot fungus of cereal grains; source of ergotamine and lysergic acid, the precursor to LSD
- **4 additional Claviceps species**: various grass parasites with ergot alkaloid profiles
- **2 Cordyceps species**: insect-pathogenic fungi that produce ergot-related compounds

The ergot alkaloids have a long history of both toxic epidemics (ergotism, or "St. Anthony's Fire") and medicinal use (migraine treatment, obstetric applications). The chemical relationship between ergot alkaloids and LSD ([[lysergic-acid-diethylamide]]) places this group at the foundation of modern psychedelic research.

## Group 4: Sacred Fungi Without Confirmed Chemistry

This group encompasses fungi traditionally used in sacred or ceremonial contexts for which **no reliable chemical studies** have confirmed neurotropic constituents. The cultural importance of these fungi may stem from actual psychoactive properties yet to be characterized, from confusion with genuinely psychoactive species, or from symbolic rather than pharmacological significance:

### Boletes (8 species in 2 genera)
Several bolete species were reported by Heim (1963, 1966, 1978) as hallucinogenic among New Guinea aborigines. However, Ott (1993) stated that these boletes do not possess any confirmed neurotropic properties, and their ceremonial use may involve confusion with Psilocybe species that grow in the same habitats.

### Russula (6 species)
Various Russula species have been reported in ceremonial contexts, though chemical analysis has not confirmed psychoactive constituents. Like the boletes, their sacred status may reflect cultural associations rather than pharmacological effects.

### Gasteromycetes (6 species in 3 genera, Lycoperdales and Phallales)
Puffballs and related fungi used in religious or magical contexts:

- [[lycoperdon-mixtecorum]] and [[lycoperdon-marginatum]]: reported by Heim and Wasson (1962) as narcotic fungi among the Mixtec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico
- Ott et al. (1975) later determined these were common edible species, suggesting the Mixtec use may have involved confusion with genuinely psychoactive Psilocybe species from the same region
- [[lycoperdon-perlatum]]: used in Mexican traditional medicine; forms fairy rings in grasslands but is edible and non-psychoactive
- Several Ganoderma species have been used in religious contexts (a church in Chignahuapan, Puebla, Mexico was built specifically in honor of Ganoderma lobatum), though this likely relates to psilocybe use in the same region

## Geographic Distribution Patterns

### Mexico: The Global Center of Diversity
Mexico has the highest number of neurotropic fungal species of any country, with **76 species** total, of which **44 belong to Psilocybe** (39% of the world total). This diversity is concentrated in the subtropical humid forests of central and southern Mexico, particularly in Oaxaca, where multiple indigenous groups (Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Chinantec, and others) maintain traditional mushroom ceremonies.

### New Guinea: The Second Center
New Guinea harbors a significant diversity of neurotropic fungi and well-documented indigenous use among aboriginal peoples, as reported by Singer (1958, 1960a), Heim and Wasson (1965), and Heim (1965a, 1978). The subtropical humid forests of the Austral hemisphere appear to be particularly favorable for Psilocybe diversity.

### General Pattern
The majority of Psilocybe species are found in or near the **Austral hemisphere**, primarily in **subtropical humid forests**. This pattern contrasts with temperate genera like Gymnopilus and Inocybe, which show broader latitudinal distribution.

### Temperate and Boreal Species
Gymnopilus species are predominantly temperate, growing on dead wood. [[amanita-muscaria]] has a circumboreal distribution across the Northern Hemisphere, associated with birch and pine forests. [[claviceps-purpurea]] is cosmopolitan in temperate grain-growing regions.

## Taxonomic and Nomenclatural Challenges

The survey highlights ongoing taxonomic confusion:

- Several Psilocybe species originally classified in Stropharia (e.g., [[stropharia-cubensis]], now [[psilocybe-cubensis]])
- Copelandia was formerly included in Panaeolus
- Common names in indigenous languages often refer to multiple species (e.g., "teonanacatl" is used indiscriminately for any Mexican hallucinogenic fungus, not just the specific species originally described by Sahagun in 1569-1582)
- The Nahuatl word "teonanacatl" literally translates to "divine mushroom" and has been applied broadly

## Ethnobotanical Significance of Mexican Diversity

The extraordinary concentration of neurotropic fungal species in Mexico correlates with the richness of indigenous mushroom-using traditions. Guzman (1997) documented more than 200 common names for hallucinogenic fungi in Mexican Indian languages. Some notable examples include:

- **"Apipiltzin"** (Nahuatl): "kid or little boy"
- **"Atkad"**: "mayor or leader"
- **"Di nize taaya"**: "fungus of the genius"
- **"Shi tho"**: "that eruptions thing"
- **"Teotlaquilnanacatl"**: "divine fungus"
- **"San isidros"** (Spanish): after Saint Isidore, patron of agriculture; commonly applied to [[psilocybe-cubensis]]
- **"Pajaritos"** (Spanish): "little birds"; commonly applied to [[psilocybe-mexicana]]
- **"Derrumbes"** (Spanish): "landslides"; commonly applied to [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]]

These names reflect the deep cultural integration of psychoactive mushrooms into Mesoamerican cosmology, agriculture, and social hierarchy. The use of terms like "little boy" and "mayor or leader" suggests that different species or preparations were associated with different ceremonial roles or intensities of experience.

## Confirmed vs. Unconfirmed Reports

A significant finding of the survey is the number of species reported as neurotropic that lack chemical verification. This problem is particularly acute among:

- **Panaeoloideous fungi**: many species identified visually as psychoactive based on blueing reactions or ceremonial use, but never subjected to rigorous chemical analysis
- **Inocybe species**: several reported as psychoactive, but the genus also contains highly toxic species (muscarine-containing), making identification risky
- **Gasteromycetes**: ceremonial use documented, but chemical studies have consistently shown these to be non-psychoactive edible species, suggesting cultural confusion with Psilocybe

The survey serves as a caution against accepting ethnobotanical reports of psychoactivity without chemical confirmation. The case of [[schizophyllum-commune]], reported as hallucinogenic in Australia by Southcott (1974) yet a common edible mushroom sold in Guatemalan and Mexican markets, illustrates how easily species can be misidentified in ceremonial contexts.
