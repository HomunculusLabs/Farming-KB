# Fungal Biodiversity and Species Estimation

Fungal biodiversity remains one of the most poorly characterized frontiers in
biological science. Despite their ecological importance and economic
significance, only a small fraction of estimated fungal species have been
formally described by taxonomists. Advances in molecular detection methods have
dramatically expanded our understanding of fungal diversity and revealed that
the described species represent only the tip of an enormous and largely
unexplored iceberg of fungal life on Earth.

## Current Knowledge of Described Diversity

Approximately 148,000 fungal species have been formally described and accepted
as of the mid-2020s. The rate of description has accelerated significantly,
with roughly 2,000-2,500 new species described annually by mycologists
worldwide. However, this pace is far insufficient to document the full extent
of fungal diversity within any reasonable timeframe.

Described species are unevenly distributed across taxa and geographies:
temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere are disproportionately
well-studied, while tropical and Southern Hemisphere diversity remains largely
unknown and under-sampled.

## Estimates of Total Fungal Diversity

### Historical Estimates

Early estimates of global fungal diversity ranged from 500,000 to 1.5 million
species, based primarily on ratios of fungal to plant species in well-studied
temperate regions. These estimates assumed relatively constant ratios across
all ecosystems and were likely significant underestimates given the much higher
plant diversity in tropical regions where fungal sampling has been minimal.

### Molecular-Based Estimates

[[environmental-dna-metabarcoding-fungi]] studies have revolutionized fungal
diversity estimation. By extracting and sequencing fungal DNA directly from
soil, water, air, and plant tissue samples, researchers detect fungal species
without the need for culturing or observing fruiting bodies. These studies have
revealed enormous undescribed diversity, particularly among microfungi and
unculturable taxa that were previously invisible to traditional mycological
methods.

A landmark 2017 study using high-throughput sequencing of soil samples from
diverse ecosystems globally estimated total fungal diversity at 2.2 to 3.8
million species. Some researchers argue even this may be conservative, with
estimates as high as 5.1 million when considering [[cryptic-fungal-species]]
and under-sampled habitats. Regardless of the exact number, it is clear that
over 90% of fungal species remain undescribed by science.

## Taxonomic Distribution of Undescribed Diversity

The gap between described and estimated species varies enormously across fungal
groups:

- Ascomycota: approximately 64,000 described out of an estimated 1.0-1.5
  million species (only 4-6% described)
- Basidiomycota: approximately 35,000 described out of an estimated 600,000-
  1.5 million species (2-6% described)
- Glomeromycota: approximately 300 described species, but molecular studies
  suggest true diversity may be 10-100 times higher
- Chytridiomycota: approximately 1,000 described, but eDNA studies reveal
  vast undescribed diversity in aquatic environments

Tropical forests likely harbor the greatest fungal diversity, yet remain the
least sampled habitat on Earth.

## Methods for Assessing Fungal Diversity

### Traditional Morphological Methods

Fruiting body surveys, microscopic examination of cultures, and herbarium
specimen identification remain the foundation of fungal diversity assessment.
These methods are labor-intensive, require specialized taxonomic expertise, and
are biased toward macrofungi that produce visible sporocarps. Many fungi do not
fruit under laboratory conditions, and fruiting in the field is often seasonal
and sporadic, leading to significant underestimation of true diversity.

### Culture-Based Methods

Isolation and cultivation of fungi from environmental samples provides living
material for identification and further study. However, the vast majority of
environmental fungal diversity cannot be cultured with standard techniques.
Culture-based surveys typically recover less than 5% of the species detected
by molecular methods from the same samples.

## DNA-Based Methods for Species Estimation

### Environmental DNA Metabarcoding

High-throughput sequencing of fungal DNA extracted directly from environmental
samples has become the dominant approach for estimating fungal diversity.
Standard methods target the ITS (Internal Transcribed Spacer) region of
ribosomal DNA, which serves as the primary fungal DNA barcode. Samples can be
collected from soil, water, air, leaf surfaces, wood, and insect guts.

Key advantages of eDNA metabarcoding include the ability to detect
non-culturable and dormant species, standardized laboratory protocols, and
the capacity to process hundreds of samples simultaneously. However, the
approach has limitations: it cannot distinguish between viable and dead
organisms, PCR primer bias affects detection of certain taxa, and sequence
data alone cannot provide information about fungal morphology, ecology, or
function.

### High-Throughput Sequencing Platforms

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) platforms such as Illumina MiSeq have made
large-scale fungal diversity surveys feasible and cost-effective. A single
sequencing run can generate millions of DNA reads from hundreds of samples,
revealing thousands of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) or amplicon sequence
variants (ASVs). Long-read sequencing technologies (PacBio, Oxford Nanopore)
are increasingly used to obtain full-length ITS sequences, improving taxonomic
resolution.

### Metagenomics and Metatranscriptomics

Shotgun metagenomics sequences all DNA in a sample without PCR amplification,
avoiding primer bias and capturing the full diversity of fungal genomes
present. Metatranscriptomics goes further by sequencing RNA to reveal which
fungal species are actively transcribing genes, providing insight into
functional activity rather than mere presence. [[fungal-metagenomics]]
approaches are particularly valuable for understanding fungal roles in
[[decomposition]] and nutrient cycling processes.

### Single-Molecule Real-Time Sequencing

Emerging single-molecule sequencing technologies offer the potential to
sequence full-length fungal genomes from environmental samples without
cultivation. This could resolve many currently ambiguous species
identifications and reveal the true extent of [[fungal-biodiversity-estimates]]
at the species level. However, these methods remain expensive and
computationally demanding for large-scale diversity surveys.

## Conservation Implications

### The Undescribed Diversity Crisis

The gap between described and estimated fungal diversity represents a
conservation challenge of enormous proportions. Species cannot be protected
if they are not known to science. Many fungal species may be going extinct
