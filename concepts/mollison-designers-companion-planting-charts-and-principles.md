# Companion Planting Charts and Principles

Companion planting is the practice of placing plant species in proximity for
mutual benefit, drawing on observed and researched interactions between plants
regarding nutrient exchange, pest deterrence, pollination support, and
microclimate modification. Mollison treats companion planting as a simplified
subset of the broader [[mollison-guild-design]] framework: while
guilds are comprehensive multi-species assemblies designed from functional
analysis, companion planting focuses on specific pairwise or small-group
interactions that can be readily applied in garden and farm settings.

## Scientific Basis and Design Caution

Mollison offers an important caveat about companion planting: many traditional
companion planting claims are based on anecdotal observation rather than
controlled research. He distinguishes between three levels of evidence:

1. **Well-documented interactions**: Nitrogen fixation by legumes benefiting
   neighbouring plants; trap cropping for pest management; physical support
   relationships. These are scientifically verified and form the reliable core
   of companion planting practice.
2. **Partially documented interactions**: Allelopathic effects (chemical
   inhibition between species); root exudate effects on soil biology; volatile
   compound pest deterrence. Some research supports these, but results vary
   with soil, climate, and cultivar.
3. **Anecdotal claims**: Many traditional companion planting combinations
   (e.g., basil and tomatoes, marigolds and everything) lack consistent
   scientific verification. Mollison advises treating these as experimental
   rather than prescriptive.

Despite these cautions, Mollison values companion planting as a practical
design tool because it encourages diversity, reduces monoculture risks, and
often produces beneficial results even when the mechanisms are not fully
understood. The [[mollison-co-action-analysis-and-species-interaction-in-guild-design]] framework provides a structured method for evaluating interactions.

## Categories of Plant Interactions

### Nutrient Exchange Interactions
The most reliable category of companion planting involves direct nutrient
benefits between species:

- **Legumes with non-legumes**: Beans or peas interplanted with corn,
   cereals, or leafy greens provide nitrogen through root exudation and
   nodule decomposition. The [[mollison-green-manure-soil-fertility]] system exploits this relationship at larger scale.
- **Deep-rooted accumulators with shallow-rooted crops**: Comfrey, chicory,
   and dandelion bring minerals from subsoil to surface through leaf fall,
   benefiting nearby shallow-rooted vegetables. See
   [[mollison-comfrey-waste-recycling-soil-building]].
- **Dynamic accumulator mulch**: Cutting accumulator plants and applying
   their leaves as mulch around nutrient-demanding crops transfers minerals
   directly. This is more reliable than relying on root exudate transfer alone.

### Pest Management Interactions
Companion planting for pest control operates through several mechanisms:

- **Trap cropping**: Plants that attract pests away from the main crop.
  Nasturtiums attract aphids away from brassicas and tomatoes; radishes
  attract flea beetles away from eggplant; mustard attracts cabbage white
  butterflies away from cabbage family crops.
- **Repellent plants**: Species whose volatile oils or root exudates deter
  specific pests. Garlic and chives deter aphids and certain beetles;
  marigolds (Tagetes spp.) deter root-knot nematodes through thiophene
  compounds in their roots; rosemary and sage repel cabbage moth.
- **Insectary plants**: Species that attract predatory and parasitic insects.
  Umbelliferous flowers (dill, fennel, coriander, parsley, yarrow, Queen
  Anne's lace) attract ladybirds, lacewings, parasitic wasps, and syrphid
   flies. These predators control aphids, caterpillars, and other pests across
   the garden.
- **Masking scent**: Strongly aromatic plants may mask the chemical signals
   that pests use to locate host plants. Onions interplanted with carrots
   (the classic combination) may deter carrot fly by masking carrot scent,
   though research results are inconsistent.
- **Nurse plant protection**: Taller or more pest-resistant plants shelter
   vulnerable seedlings. Sunflowers shade lettuce; casuarinas shelter young
   fruit trees from wind and sun. See [[mollison-barrier-plants-in-garden-design]].

### Physical Support and Spatial Interactions
Plants can modify each other's growing environment physically:

- **Climbing support**: Corn stalks support climbing beans (the classic
   Three Sisters combination). Sunflowers, Jerusalem artichokes, or trellised
   crops provide similar support functions.
- **Shade provision**: Tall crops shade heat-sensitive species. Tomatoes
   shade lettuce; pole beans shade spinach. This extends the growing season
   for cool-season crops in warm climates. See
   [[mollison-microclimate-creation]].
- **Wind protection**: Taller or denser plants shelter wind-sensitive species.
   Perennial hedges protect garden beds. See
   [[mollison-windbreak-design]].
- **Living mulch**: Low-growing species suppress weeds, conserve soil
   moisture, and moderate soil temperature beneath taller crops. Clover
   beneath corn; sweet potato beneath fruit trees; nasturtium beneath squash.

### Allelopathic Interactions (Negative Companions)
Some plants produce chemicals that inhibit the growth of neighbouring species.
Mollison warns that allelopathy is often overlooked in companion planting
charts:

- **Black walnut (Juglans nigra)**: Produces juglone, which is toxic to
   tomatoes, potatoes, eggplant, and many other garden plants. Do not locate
   vegetable gardens near black walnut trees.
- **Fennel**: Inhibits the growth of many garden vegetables; best grown
   in isolation. A poor companion for tomatoes, beans, and kohlrabi.
- **Sunflower seed hulls**: Accumulated sunflower seed hulls beneath bird
   feeders inhibit the growth of many plants. The allelopathic compound is
   concentrated in the hulls.
- **Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium)**: Root exudates inhibit the growth of
   peas, beans, and fennel. Keep wormwood separate from vegetable gardens.
- **Rye and wheat**: Rye residues can inhibit weed seed germination
   (sometimes desirable) but also inhibit the germination of small-seeded
   vegetable crops. Allow rye residues to decompose before planting.

## The Three Sisters: A Classic Polyculture

Mollison frequently cites the Iroquois Three Sisters (corn, beans, squash)
as the archetypal companion planting system, demonstrating how multiple
interactions combine in a productive polyculture:

- **Corn (Zea mays)**: Provides vertical support for climbing beans.
  Heavy nitrogen feeder; benefits from bean nitrogen fixation. Tall structure
  creates a modified microclimate.
- **Beans (Phaseolus vulgaris)**: Fix atmospheric nitrogen through Rhizobium
  bacteria in root nodules, supplying nitrogen to corn and squash. Use the
  corn stalks as a climbing support. Leaves add nitrogen-rich mulch at
  season's end.
- **Squash (Cucurbita spp.)**: Large leaves shade the soil surface,
  suppressing weeds and reducing moisture loss. Spiny stems deter mammal
  pests. Large leaves create a humid microclimate beneath.
- **System interactions**: The three species occupy different root depths
  (corn deep, beans intermediate, squash shallow), different canopy layers
  (corn tall, beans climbing, squash sprawling), and different nutrient
  niches (beans fix nitrogen, corn uses nitrogen, squash uses potassium).
  Total system productivity exceeds any monoculture of the three species.
