# Fungi Sustainability - Building Materials and Mycotecture

Mycotecture — the use of fungal mycelium as a structural or building material — represents an emerging frontier in sustainable design. The Fungi Magazine article by Ron Spinosa documents several foundational concepts relevant to mycotecture, grounding them in the broader frameworks of [[paul-stamets]]' mycological research and Gunter Pauli's ZERI design philosophy.



## Myceliated Wood Chips as Structural Substrate

Stamets describes mushroom mycelium as "the grand demolecularizer of plant fibers (lignin and cellulose), creating soil as an end consequence." This decomposition process has direct implications for building materials: as mycelium colonizes and binds woody substrate, it transforms loose organic matter into a densely matted, structurally coherent material.

Stamets frames wood chips as "valuable ecological currency that should be reinvested into the forest's ecobank to enhance sustainability." The [lignin degradation process mediated by [white-rot fungi breaks down the most recalcitrant plant polymers, leaving behind a mycelium-bound matrix with structural integrity.

The practical application documented involves filling depressions and roadside ditches with myceliated wood chips, where the fungal network binds the substrate into a structure that resists [erosion and silt flow. This demonstrates mycelium's capacity to create physically robust, soil-stabilizing materials from waste wood.



## [[unknown-mycelium-mycofiltration]] Structures

In Stamets' framework of mycofiltration, myceliated substrates serve as living filtration barriers. These structures perform similarly to conventional retaining walls or filtration systems but are grown rather than manufactured.

The SCZ-NM forestry projects in New Mexico employed myceliated wood chips along roadways to stabilize soil and filter runoff, functioning as both structural elements and biological water treatment systems. This approach parallels [[mycoremediation]] principles where fungal networks process contaminants while simultaneously binding soil particles.



## Low-Tech Cultivation Infrastructure

The article emphasizes that mushroom cultivation infrastructure is inherently low-cost and low-tech, relevant to mycotecture principles. Growing houses can be "very simply made at low cost, using low tech methods, on small plots of land." This accessibility extends to the production of mycelium-based materials, which require minimal industrial processing compared to conventional building materials.

The [oyster mushroom cultivation model demonstrates that viable mycelium-bound products can be produced with basic equipment: simple growing structures, pasteurized substrate, and minimal energy inputs.



## ZERI Integrated Design Philosophy

Gunter Pauli's ZERI (Zero Emissions Research and Initiatives) framework provides a design philosophy relevant to mycotecture. ZERI models its approach on natural ecosystems where all five kingdoms of life work together to process organic matter, maximizing productivity, biodiversity, and systemic resilience.

In this model, fungal materials would be integrated into [circular systems where waste from one process feeds another. The [packaging and materials cascade demonstrates how mycelium-bound products can flow through multiple value-added stages before returning to the soil.



## Spore-Oil Inoculation Method

Stamets devised an ingenious method for large-scale mycelial inoculation: mixing fungal spores into the bar-and-chain oil used in chainsaws and wood chippers. As forestry equipment processes wood, it simultaneously inoculates the material with fungal propagules.

This method demonstrates a scalable approach to producing myceliated building materials directly at the source of raw material. The concept parallels [[mycoforestry-practices]] where fungal inoculation is integrated directly into forest management operations.



## Forest Restoration and Material Production

SCZ-NM's work under the U.S. Forest Service Collaborative Forest Restoration Program illustrates the dual function of mycoforestry: reducing wildfire hazard through thinning while simultaneously producing myceliated structural materials from the thinned woody debris.

Native fungal cultures are maintained in a long-term culture bank and tested for their ability to grow on various tree species including ponderosa, piñon, and juniper, as well as invasive species like salt cedar and Russian olive. Matching native [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] to native tree species is a core principle that extends to selecting the right fungi for material production.



## Ecological Foundation for Mycotecture

The article establishes a broader ecological rationale for fungal building materials: [[mycorrhizal-networks]] connect 90% of all plant species underground. The [rhizosphere — a vast network of fungal hyphae interacting with bacteria, nematodes, and arthropods — is described as "absolutely essential for the wellbeing and resiliency of the entire ecosystem."

Mycotecture draws on these same biological binding and networking capabilities. The [glomalin and other fungal exudates that bind soil particles in natural ecosystems perform a function analogous to binding agents in conventional composite materials.



## Sustainability Context

The article connects fungal building materials to the broader sustainability movement, tracing from the 1987 UN Brundtland Report through the Millennium Development Goals. Mycotecture aligns with Goal 7 (Ensure environmental sustainability) by transforming waste biomass into useful structural materials while requiring minimal energy input.



## Current Applications and Companies

The mycotecture concept has moved beyond theory into commercial application. Companies like Ecovative Design (founded by Eben Bayer and Gavin McIntyre) have pioneered the use of mycelium-bound composites as replacements for polystyrene packaging, building insulation, and structural core materials. Their process involves growing specific fungal strains on [[agricultural-waste-substrates]] (hemp hurds, corn stalks, wood chips) to produce rigid boards and shaped products that are fire-resistant, water-resistant, and fully biodegradable.

Mycelium-based insulation panels have demonstrated thermal performance comparable to conventional synthetic insulation, with the added benefits of being carbon-negative during production (the fungal growth process sequesters carbon from the substrate) and producing no toxic off-gassing. At end of life, mycelium building materials can be composted or returned to soil, completing a circular material lifecycle impossible with conventional petrochemical-based construction materials.



## Limitations and Challenges

Mycotecture faces several practical challenges before widespread adoption in construction. Mycelium composites generally have lower compressive and tensile strength than concrete or steel, limiting their use to non-structural applications like insulation, partition walls, and decorative elements. Moisture sensitivity is a concern — while dried mycelium products are water-resistant, prolonged exposure to liquid water can promote unwanted fungal growth or structural degradation. Standardization and building code approval remain significant hurdles, as the material properties of mycelium composites vary with substrate, fungal species, growing conditions, and processing methods. Scaling production from laboratory to industrial quantities while maintaining consistent quality is an ongoing engineering challenge.
