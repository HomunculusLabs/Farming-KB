# Lichen Growth Forms and Substrate Ecology
Lichens are symbiotic associations between a fungus (the
mycobiont) and a photosynthetic partner (the photobiont),
typically a green alga or cyanobacterium. The integrated
lichen thallus develops a characteristic morphology that is
used to classify lichens into growth forms, each adapted to
particular substrates and environmental conditions. Lichenized
fungi represent a major component of [[fungal-biodiversity-marine-ecosystems-coastal-deep-sea]], with an estimated 13,500 to 17,000 species.

## The [[lichen-symbiosis]]

An individual lichen thallus is usually composed of one
species of fungus and one species of either alga or
cyanobacterium. Many thousands of fungal species, but only
about 150 to 200 species of photobiont, have been identified
as participants in lichen symbioses. Only the fungal partner
reproduces sexually while in the association. The lichen
thallus is more than the sum of its parts -- it survives in
more habitats and is more abundant than either partner when
alone.
The roles of lichenized fungi in communities and ecosystems
are best understood by studying the lichen thallus as a
photosynthetic unit that in many ways is equivalent to plants.
For this reason, methods for assessing the diversity of
lichenized fungi can be more similar to those for plants than
for other fungi.

## Growth Form Categories

Most lichen thalli fall into one of five general morphological
categories, although intermediates and transitions exist:

### Leprose

Leprose lichens consist of mealy particles of intertwined
fungal hyphae and algal cells, forming an unstructured,
granular crust. These are often among the smallest and
least conspicuous lichens. Genera such as Lepraria are
representative of this growth form and are typically found
on bark or rock surfaces in sheltered locations.

### Crustose

Crustose lichens are embedded on or in the surface of the
substratum, forming a crust-like layer. They are the most
diverse growth form in terms of species numbers, particularly
in tropical regions. Crustose lichens typically have several
layers, with the medullary fungal hyphae attached directly
to the substratum. They cannot be removed from the substrate
without destroying the thallus. Many crustose species are
saxicolous (rock-dwelling), colonizing diverse rock types
from acidic siliceous rocks to basic calcareous substrates.

### Squamulose

Squamulose lichens are composed of small, scale-like flakes
of thallus (squamules) that are attached at one point to the
substratum but can be partially detached. This growth form is
intermediate between crustose and foliose. Cladonia species
often produce squamules as their primary thallus, with
podetia (stalk-like structures) arising from the squamules.

### Foliose

Foliose lichens are leaf-like, with distinct upper and lower
surfaces. They usually are attached to the substratum by
special groups of root-like fungal hyphae called rhizines.
The upper cortex provides protection, while the medulla below
contains the photobiont layer. Foliose lichens are common on
both bark and rock substrates. Notable genera include
Xanthoria (bright orange, often on nutrient-enriched bark),
Physcia (common on tree trunks), and Parmelia.

### Fruticose

Fruticose lichens are shrubby or string-like, with thalli
that are either upright, tufted, or pendulous (hanging).

Lichenized fungi colonize a range of substrates, and species
typically show preferences for particular substrate types:

### Saxicolous (Rock-Dwelling)

Saxicolous lichens grow on rock surfaces and are among the
most important agents of biological weathering. They colonize
diverse rock types including:
- Siliceous rocks (granite, sandstone, quartz)
- Calcareous rocks (limestone, marble)
- Volcanic rocks (basalt, lava flows)
- Artificial substrates (concrete, brick, tombstones)

Rock chemistry is a primary determinant of saxicolous lichen
community composition. Calcareous and siliceous rocks support
distinctly different lichen floras.

### Corticolous (Bark-Dwelling)

Corticolous lichens grow on the bark of trees and shrubs.
Factors affecting corticolous lichen communities include:
- Bark pH and chemistry (affects nutrient availability)
- Bark texture and moisture retention
- Tree species and age
- Canopy position and light availability
- Forest continuity and management history

Old-growth forests typically support more diverse corticolous
lichen communities than young or managed forests.

## Factors Affecting Lichen Distribution

Major factors affecting the presence and abundance of
lichenized fungi include:
1. **Substratum chemistry, stability, and longevity** --
   rock chemistry, bark pH, and substrate durability
2. **Light availability** -- effects are often mediated
   through competition with faster-growing, larger plants
   and other lichens
3. **Moisture availability** -- most lichens require
   periodic hydration for photosynthesis and growth
