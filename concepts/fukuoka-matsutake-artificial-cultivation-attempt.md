## The Discovery: A Natural Medium

While running pure cultures of matsutake spawn, Fukuoka was
flame-sterilizing materials in the hearth fire, which he found more
reliable than alcohol sterilization. Since he was trying to isolate
mycelia from the caps of matsutake mushrooms, he was essentially
cooking the matsutake instead of sterilizing them. He dipped the cooked
mushrooms in soy sauce and ate them -- they were delicious.

This gave him a crucial insight: what man thought tasty, the matsutake
might like as well. He tried culturing the mycelia using a culture
medium containing soy sauce. The first successful test tube cultivation
of the matsutake had been done more than forty years earlier, but this
fungus had yet to be successfully cultivated in glass bottles despite
advanced pure culture methods. To a man, matsutake researchers all
lament that no microorganism is as difficult to cultivate as matsutake
spawn.

## The Chawan-Mushi Breakthrough

Fukuoka tried just about everything he could think of and found that
what the matsutake likes most of all is chawan-mushi (a Japanese egg
custard). When a delicious chawan-mushi is prepared and a matsutake
cap placed on top, the spores that fall from the cap grow well. In a
half-year, the container becomes filled with white mycelia. If kept at
about 61 degrees Fahrenheit, little matsutake come popping up one after
another.

The first time he saw four little matsutake come up in a one-liter
bottle, he was surprised and delighted. He immediately took it outside
in the sun to get a good photo, but had forgotten that matsutake
mycelia are very sensitive to high temperatures and die in about an
hour at 86 degrees. After all his trouble, the mycelia were killed by
the heat.
