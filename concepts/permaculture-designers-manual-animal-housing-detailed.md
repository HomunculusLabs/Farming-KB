# Animal Housing (Detailed)

updated: 2026-04-18

From: [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]]



## Overview

Permaculture animal housing integrates shelters into the broader design system, serving multiple functions beyond mere containment. Every element must serve at least two or more functions, and animal shelters are placed according to zone and sector analysis to minimise work and maximise beneficial connections with gardens, greenhouses, and forage systems.



## Placement Principles

A poultry shed, for example, should:
- BORDER Zone 1 (for frequent visits -- 460 visits per year for a typical household)
- Be AWAY from the fire sector
- BORDER the annual garden (for easy manure collection)
- BACK ONTO the forage system
- Possibly ATTACH to a greenhouse
- Form part of a windbreak system

Zone planning changes with distance from centre: Zone I features barns and poultry sheds attached to house/greenhouse; Zone II has feed stores and field shelter; Zone III features field shelter grown as hedgerow and woodlot.



## Rabbit Hutches and Chicken Roosts

In the integrated garden design, the hatched areas are roofs of rabbit hutches and chicken roosts, pigeon lofts, quail houses, or guinea-pig rest areas (depending on culture and preferences). These structures are integrated into the hedge system, with forage-mulch species providing food for the animals. Each little roof catches water and leads it to a small tank, the garden, or both.



## Pig Pens and Forage Integration

Electric fencing restrains pigs which root out and eat kikuyu and grasses, leaving Prosopis trees unaffected. The pigs can then be moved on, and trees planted. 100 pigs in 2 ha pens will tractor 4 ha in 2 months (Hawaiian Homelands area). This demonstrates the principle of using animals as earth-moving and site-preparation tools within their housing/forage system.

Nigerian polyculture for humid tropics includes pig or goat pen and forage crops, appropriate ridges for cassava, strip crop, zero runoff, and contour layout.



## Greenhouse Integration

Housing animals in greenhouses provides carbon dioxide for enhanced plant growth where light is more than sufficient. Animals housed at night will provide considerable winter heat, as will long compost boxes along the poleward wall, or external to, but below, the level of the greenhouse itself. Dark curtains within the greenhouse also act as heat absorbers and radiators.

In areas of plentiful water, fish ponds can be constructed over which some animals are housed, creating further system integration.



## Poultry Strawyards

Branches of legumes and forage trees may be used in poultry strawyards and animal bedding, on their path to the garden. For fire control, it pays to rake under bamboo and clump canes, and re-route the leaf mulch through animal bedding or poultry strawyards.



## Fenced Animal Runs

In the integrated garden design, fenced areas are provided for chickens and other poultry. These are changed over to suit the animals, and while one is rested it can be cropped if space allows. Both are well-shaded animal runs. The fence can be of woven bamboo, palm rib, banana grass stalks, or wire netting.



## Design Checklist for Animal Housing

- Locate for minimum walking distance from house and garden
- Connect to greywater and composting systems
- Integrate with greenhouse for heat and CO2 exchange
- Provide forage access or adjoining forage paddock
- Ensure adequate ventilation and drainage
- Design for easy manure collection and transfer to gardens
- Include water catchment from roofs
- Use local, low-energy materials
- Design for predator protection appropriate to species
- Allow for multiple species rotation
