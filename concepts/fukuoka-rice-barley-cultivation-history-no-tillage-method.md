## Fukuoka Rice and Barley Cultivation History and No-Tillage Method

## Spiritual Significance of Rice

Japan was once known as "the Land of Ripening Grain" — the farmer did not grow
the rice; nature did. The people merely partook of the blessings of the land
with a grateful heart.

Once man began to think he grew rice, scientific discrimination arose. This
created a rift between rice and the land, leading to a profound loss of unity
with nature.

Rice was reduced from a mere foodstuff, and the farmer's work was reclassified
as economically inefficient and unscientific.

As Fukuoka wrote: "More than just the staff of life, the rice grown on Japanese
soil was the very soul of the Yamato people." The original purpose of rice
cultivation was corrupted, moving from cultivation of rice as a spiritual
practice to starch production and ultimately to the pursuit of profits.

## Barley and Wheat Cultivation History

Barley and wheat were second only to rice as food staples in Japan, yet today
they are vanishing from Japanese soil.

Traditional barley cultivation was extraordinarily labor-intensive: plow the
field, break clods, make seed furrows, sow seed, cover, apply [[compost]], three
weedings, loosen soil, gather around base, trample shoots, spray pesticides,
and mature.

"All this work was done during the cold months, but harvesting time came at
the end of May, which felt even more swelteringly hot than midsummer." Despite
this grueling effort, farmers persisted for generations.

About fifty years ago, domestic wheat was improved but US imports were pushed.
Wheat proved late-maturing for the Japanese climate, resulting in unstable
harvests. In 1945, the Ministry adopted a policy of increased food dependence
on imports, and farmers were effectively encouraged to abandon wheat
cultivation.

"It was neither money nor labor that supported the arduous practice of double
cropping... It was pride." That pride has now been eroded, and Japan's calorie
self-sufficiency has dropped below forty percent, raising serious questions
about the nation's food security.

"Few field crops yield as many calories as barley," Fukuoka argued, and it
should be double-cropped with rice to restore food sovereignty.

## Fukuoka's Three Stages to Natural Cultivation

### Stage 1: Tillage, Ridging, and Drilling

He tested broader seeding (twenty-five to forty percent increase) — no yield
improvement, actually reduced stability.

Narrower seeding with wider rows gave higher yields but introduced ridge and
furrow problems. He raised rows per ridge from two to three to four. Farmers
now drill in single file.

### Stage 2: Light-Tillage, Level-Row

Switched to light-tillage, individual seeds in straight narrow rows. Discovered
naked barley doesn't need high ridges; no-tillage works even better. By 1950,
began studying narrow-row drilling on unplowed fields.

For weed control, he sowed ladino clover as ground cover and scattered rice
straw over the field. Experts forbade leaving straw on a paddy, claiming it
would breed pathogenic microbes. Fukuoka confirmed rice straw decomposes
entirely by spring with no pathogenic microbes present.

### Stage 3: No-Tillage, Direct-Seeding

He built an experimental device; tried dibbling, drilling, individual seeding
in furrows. Moved from sparse to dense to sparse, then settled on broadcasting.

His key findings from over a decade of no-tillage practice were:

- **No-tillage improves and enriches the land**, demonstrated over more than
  ten consecutive years.
- **It provides total germination [[jadam-soil-management-no-till-and-weed-control]]**, with less labor and
  higher yields than [[fukuoka-spiritual-philosophical-foundation]] for everything that
followed. During wartime mobilization, he was sent to the Kochi Prefecture
agricultural [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] into practice.
## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
