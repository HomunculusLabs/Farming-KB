## The Maryland Mammoth Discovery

The story begins with an unusual tobacco strain called Maryland Mammoth,
found by farmers in southern Maryland around 1906. This plant could
reach four and a half metres in height and produce nearly a hundred
leaves, yet it rarely flowered — a serious problem for farmers who
relied on harvested seed.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department
of Agriculture solved the mystery. They planted Maryland Mammoth in
pots and moved one group to a dark shed every afternoon, artificially
shortening daylight hours. Only the plants experiencing shortened days
flowered. This proved that plants measure the length of continuous
darkness — not daylight — to decide when to reproduce.

## Short-Day and Long-Day Plants

Botanists classified flowering plants into photoperiodic categories.
Short-day plants flower when uninterrupted darkness exceeds a critical
threshold (chrysanthemums, soybeans, poinsettias). Long-day plants
flower when the dark period falls below a critical minimum (irises,
barley, spinach). Day-neutral plants like tomatoes flower regardless
of photoperiod.

## Where Plants See Photoperiodic Light

Unlike phototropism, where the "eye" is in the shoot tip (as Darwin
showed), photoperiodic sensing is distributed across leaves. Illuminating
a single leaf at midnight regulates flowering in the entire plant.
