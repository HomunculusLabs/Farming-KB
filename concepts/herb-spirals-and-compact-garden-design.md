## The Herb Spiral

### Design Concept

The herb spiral is a three-dimensional earth construct on a small domestic scale, evolved by Mollison in 1978 as a kitchen-door garden design. It consists of an ascending spiral of earth on a 2-metre wide base, rising to 1 to 1.3 metres high. All herbs planted on the spiral ramp are accessible from the perimeter, with the compact form coiling up what would otherwise be a linear bed of more than 9 metres into a single mound placed conveniently at the kitchen door.

### Microclimate Creation

The spiral form naturally creates a gradient of microclimates across its surface, allowing diverse species with different requirements to coexist in a very small footprint:

- **Sunny, dry sites** (upper, outer curve): Ideal for oil-rich Mediterranean herbs such as thyme, sage, and rosemary that require well-drained [[fukuoka-ideal-rice-plant-form-dimensions-high-yield]]

### Water Efficiency Compared to Row Crops

The irrigation efficiency comparison is striking:

- A **3.8 m straight row** of crops requires three low-pressure sprinklers to cover adequately, along with significantly more mulch and far less shelter from wind and evaporation.
- A **1.2 m circular pit** (equivalent perimeter) needs only a single sprinkler — reducing water infrastructure and consumption by approximately two-thirds.
- The central mulch pit provides deep organic matter and moisture retention, acting as a reservoir that feeds surrounding plants.
- Plants eventually grow to overshade the centre, preventing evaporation from the mulch pit and creating a self-maintaining moisture system.

## Pattern Efficiency Principles

Several recurring principles emerge from these compact design applications:

### Circular vs. Linear Efficiency

Circular and spiral forms consistently outperform linear arrangements in resource efficiency. The geometry of compact forms reduces edge effects, water loss through evaporation, and the number of irrigation points needed while maximizing usable growing area relative to ground footprint.

### Three-Dimensional Thinking

Adding vertical dimension (as in the herb spiral) multiplies available growing surface without increasing ground footprint. Stepped, terraced, and spiralled configurations all exploit this principle to create diverse microclimates in minimal horizontal space.

### Central Resource Concentration

Placing water, mulch, and nutrient resources at the centre of circular designs allows plants to access them efficiently and eventually self-shade the resource core, reducing evaporative loss and creating positive feedback loops that reduce maintenance over time.

### Multi-Function Integration

Each pattern element serves multiple functions simultaneously: the herb spiral provides growing space, microclimate variety, aesthetic interest, and water efficiency in a single construct. The flood-plain interceptor manages water flow, captures materials, and protects settlements — all from the same patterned structure.
