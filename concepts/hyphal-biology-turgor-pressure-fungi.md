## Nutrient Uptake: The Proton Pump

Once enzymes release small molecules from the substrate, fungi use
sophisticated transport systems to absorb them. A key mechanism involves the
active export of hydrogen ions (protons) across the plasma membrane. This
proton pumping charges the membrane and creates an electrochemical gradient.

Hyphae exploit this gradient to import nutrients. Carrier proteins in the
plasma membrane offer pathways for proton reentry that coincide with uptake of
sugars, amino acids, and other molecules. Each carrier opens and closes as it
transmits a hydrogen ion, simultaneously shunting a nutrient molecule into
the cytoplasm. This proton-coupled transport is one of the fundamental
processes that allows the fungal cell to accumulate food molecules needed for
energy and growth.

## Turgor Pressure: The Conventional View

The accumulation of chemicals inside the hypha draws water inward through
osmosis. Because the rigid cell wall resists expansion, this water influx
generates internal hydrostatic pressure — turgor. A healthy hypha lives far
from equilibrium, maintaining internal solute concentrations that keep it
pressurized at 1-10 atmospheres (car tires are typically pressurized at about
2 atmospheres).

For over a century, mycologists agreed that hyphae use this turgor pressure
to drive expansion of the cell wall at their pointed tips. The hypha was
viewed as a pressurized vessel, always close to bursting, with wall expansion
driven by the outward force of internal pressure against a softened region at
the apex.

## Challenging the Paradigm

faster when most internal pressure was eliminated — by cultivating colonies
in high concentrations of soluble carbohydrates to disturb osmotic balance
and limit water influx.

The conclusion was that turgor pressure is not a requirement for growth.
While cells without walls (animal cells) do not become pressurized, the
reason fungi have walls is not primarily to enable pressurized growth. At a
fundamental level, the enlargement of a walled fungal cell is no different
from that of an animal cell with a more fluid surface. In both cases, water
influx distends the cell, but the level of internal pressure is not the
determining factor that fixes the growth rate.

## The Real Function of Turgor

If turgor is not essential for growth, why are fungi pressurized? The answer
becomes apparent when considering where fungi actually grow — through leaf
surfaces, inside logs, between toes, and through solid rock. While pressure
is not needed for growth in the absence of barriers, it enables hyphae to
overcome the physical obstacles that persist after their food sources are
weakened by enzymes.
