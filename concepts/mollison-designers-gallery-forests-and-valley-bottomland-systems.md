# Gallery Forests and Valley Bottomland Systems

Gallery forests are the ribbons of tree cover that line
riverbanks and valley floors in otherwise open landscapes,
particularly in the [[mollison-wet-dry-tropical-design-strategies]] and savannah regions. They represent some of the
most productive and biodiverse ecosystems, serving as
critical wildlife corridors, water regulators, and sources of
hardwood timber and special forest products.

## What Are Gallery Forests?

Gallery forests are the tree islands found along watercourses
in open grassland and savannah landscapes. In the wet-dry
tropics, arboreal species occur only within tree islands and
the gallery forests of valleys. These linear forests form
natural corridors connecting upper watershed forests with
coastal systems, and they persist where groundwater is
available year-round even in seasonal climates.

The term "gallery" refers to the corridor-like appearance
of these forests when viewed from across an open valley.
They are the tropical equivalent of temperate
[[mollison-stream-orders-and-branching-magnitudes]] systems, but often contain a richer assemblage of
species due to the combination of reliable water and high
temperatures.

## Ecological Functions

### Wildlife Habitat and Corridors
Gallery forests serve as critical habitat for arboreal
species that cannot survive in the open grasslands. In
African savannahs, they provide refuge for primates, birds,
reptiles, and specialised forest plants. As wildlife
corridors, they connect isolated forest patches, enabling
gene flow and seasonal migration between otherwise separated
populations.

### Stream Bank Stabilisation
Tree roots along watercourses stabilise banks against
erosion during flood events. The root networks bind soil
particles and prevent the channel scour that would otherwise
widen streams and destroy valley-bottom agricultural land.

### Water Quality Regulation
Gallery forests filter runoff before it enters streams,
removing sediments, nutrients, and pollutants. The forest
floor acts as a biological filter, and the root zone
intercepts dissolved nutrients that might otherwise cause
eutrophication downstream.

### Microclimate Creation
The shade and wind reduction created by gallery forests
moderate temperatures and reduce evaporation from adjacent
water surfaces and croplands. This microclimate effect can
extend the growing season and reduce water requirements for
nearby crops.

## Products and Services

Gallery forests produce hardwoods that are among the most
valuable timber products in tropical regions. Because these
forests have reliable water supply, the trees grow faster
and produce denser wood than upland species. Other products
include:

- Fruits, nuts, and edible leaves from specialised
  understory species
- Medicinal plants that require the moist conditions of
  valley floors
- [[mollison-designers-mulberry-and-sericulture-in-permaculture]] for silk production
- Rattan, bamboo, and thatching materials
- Honey from forest-edge bee forage

## Gallery Forests in the Wet-Dry Tropics

The wet-dry tropics present the most dramatic gallery forest
systems. These regions contain vast savannah grasslands with
thorn-bush and flat-topped Acacia trees (Africa), evolving
to steppe grassland on plateaus, with baobabs and
dry-deciduous trees. Gallery forests are the exception in
this landscape, occurring only where permanent or
near-permanent water is available.

Soils in gallery forest zones are generally more fertile and
alkaline than the surrounding wet tropics, especially where
they are less leached by rain towards the deserts. This
fertility, combined with reliable water, makes gallery forest
zones the most productive areas for both natural systems and
human use.

## Threats and Conservation

Gallery forests are under severe pressure from:
- Agricultural expansion into valley bottoms
- Overgrazing by cattle and goats that prevents
  regeneration
- Fire that spreads from surrounding grasslands
- Diversion of water for irrigation that lowers water
  tables
- Logging for hardwood extraction without replanting

In many areas, the removal of gallery forests has led to
accelerated stream bank erosion, loss of wildlife habitat,
and reduced water quality in downstream systems.

## Design for Gallery Forest Restoration

Permaculture designers can integrate gallery forest
principles into farm and settlement design:

### Stream Bank Planting
Establish [[mollison-designers-pioneer-species-for-exhausted-tropical-soils]] on degraded stream banks, followed by canopy
trees as the site stabilises. Priority species include
fast-growing nitrogen fixers and water-tolerant hardwoods.

### Integrated Valley Design
The valley floor can be designed as an integrated system
combining:
- Gallery forest along the watercourse
- [[mollison-aquaculture-designers-checklist]]
  ponds fed by stream water
- [[mollison-tropical-home-garden-design]]
  using the rich alluvial soils
- [[mollison-grain-crops-and-no-tillage-systems]] on the most fertile soils

### Buffer Zones
Maintain buffer zones of natural or planted forest between
agricultural land and watercourses. These serve the same
functions as natural gallery forests while allowing
productive use of the valley floor.
