# Glomalin and Soil Carbon

Glomalin is one of the most significant discoveries in soil science in recent decades. This glycoprotein, produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, plays a central role in soil carbon storage, soil aggregation, and overall soil health. Its discovery in 1996 by USDA soil scientist Sarah Wright resolved a longstanding mystery about the nature of recalcitrant organic matter in soils and opened new avenues for understanding how mycorrhizal fungi shape terrestrial ecosystems. The implications extend from [[mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration]] to global climate change mitigation.

## Discovery of Glomalin

For decades, soil scientists recognized that a substantial portion of soil organic matter was highly resistant to decomposition, but its origin was unknown. Conventional extraction methods failed to identify this mysterious, long-lasting component. In 1996, Sarah Wright, a soil research scientist with the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA), identified the substance as a glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi belonging to the phylum Glomeromycota.

Wright named the compound glomalin, after the fungal order Glomales (now reclassified within Glomeromycota). The discovery was transformative because it demonstrated that living mycorrhizal fungi actively produce a compound that persists in soil long after the fungi themselves have died, fundamentally altering our understanding of soil organic matter dynamics.

More on the producing organisms is available at glomalin and soil carbon.

## Chemical Structure and Properties

Glomalin is a glycoprotein, meaning it consists of a protein backbone with carbohydrate (sugar) side chains. It is a large molecule containing two carbon sites along with significant amounts of nitrogen, making it both a carbon and nitrogen reservoir in soil ecosystems. The molecular structure of glomalin is inherently sticky, a property that is central to its role in soil aggregation.

Several properties make glomalin remarkable among soil organic compounds:

First, it is extremely stable and resistant to microbial decomposition, contributing to its great longevity in soil. Estimates of glomalin's turnover time range from years to decades, far exceeding the typical residence time of most soil organic matter fractions. This recalcitrance is attributed to its complex molecular structure and the way it becomes physically protected within soil aggregates.

Second, glomalin is hydrophobic, meaning it repels water. This property, combined with its sticky nature, allows it to coat soil particles and bind them together even under wet conditions.

Third, glomalin contains iron and other metals tightly bound within its structure, which may contribute to its stability and its characteristic deep reddish-brown color when extracted.

## Role in Soil Aggregation and Structure

One of glomalin's most important functions is its contribution to soil aggregation. The [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]] extends beyond glomalin to include physical hyphal entanglement and other binding mechanisms, but glomalin is the primary biochemical agent. As arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi grow through soil, they produce glomalin as a component of their cell walls and also secrete it into the surrounding soil environment. The sticky glycoprotein acts as a biological glue, binding individual mineral particles (sand, silt, and clay) together into larger aggregates.

Soil aggregates are the fundamental structural units of soil. Well-aggregated soil has improved water infiltration, better aeration, enhanced nutrient retention, and greater resistance to erosion. Glomalin contributes to all three categories of soil aggregates: macroaggregates (greater than 0.25 mm), microaggregates (0.053 to 0.25 mm), and the silt-plus-clay fraction within microaggregates.

The process begins as hyphae grow through soil, depositing glomalin along their length. When hyphae die and decompose, the glomalin-coated soil particles remain bound together. New hyphal growth then entangles these particles further, creating increasingly stable aggregates. This ongoing cycle of hyphal growth, glomalin deposition, and hyphal turnover continuously builds and maintains soil structure.

## Contribution to Soil Carbon Pools

Glomalin represents a major component of the soil carbon pool. Research has shown that glomalin contributes 27 to 30 percent of the total carbon in soils where arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are present. This is a remarkable figure for a single compound produced by a specific group of organisms, and it underscores the outsized role that mycorrhizal fungi play in terrestrial carbon cycling.

As arbuscular fungi grow and die, they are continually adding glomalin to soils. The cumulative effect is a substantial and relatively stable carbon pool that persists for years to decades. Unlike more labile forms of soil organic matter that cycle rapidly through microbial biomass, glomalin provides long-term carbon storage.

This has profound implications for understanding how soils function as carbon sinks and how management practices that promote mycorrhizal fungi can enhance soil carbon sequestration. ## Relationship to Carbon Sequestration and Climate Change

The discovery that mycorrhizal fungi contribute up to a third of soil carbon through glomalin production has significant implications for climate change mitigation. Soil is the largest terrestrial carbon reservoir, holding approximately twice as much carbon as the atmosphere and three times as much as living vegetation. Any process that substantially influences soil carbon storage is therefore relevant to global carbon budgets.

Glomalin's long residence time in soil makes it particularly valuable for carbon sequestration. Agricultural and land management practices that support healthy arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal populations, such as reduced tillage, cover cropping, and minimal synthetic fertilizer use, can increase glomalin production and thereby enhance soil carbon storage.

Conversely, practices that disrupt mycorrhizal networks, such as intensive tillage and excessive phosphorus fertilization, can reduce glomalin inputs and lead to soil carbon depletion. Research into glomalin-based carbon sequestration strategies is ongoing, but the potential is substantial. If managed effectively, mycorrhizal fungi and their glomalin production could represent an important nature-based solution for climate change mitigation.

## Hydrophobins and Fungal Cell Wall Integrity

Closely related to glomalin in their functional importance are hydrophobins, another class of proteins produced exclusively by fungi. Though distinct from glomalin in structure and origin, hydrophobins are equally critical to fungal survival and soil ecosystem function.

Hydrophobins are small, cysteine-rich proteins that are always embedded in fungal cell walls. They possess a unique amphiphilic structure, with both hydrophobic (water-repelling) and hydrophilic (water-attracting) sides. The hydrophilic side attaches to the fungal cell wall, keeping the cell from drying out. The hydrophobic side faces outward, preventing water from rushing into the cell, which could dilute the cytoplasm and damage or drown the hyphal filament.

Monomers, the individual hydrophobin protein molecules, are assembled within the fungus and released from the growing tip of the hypha. At the cell surface, they join together and form a self-assembling coating that ensures that whenever a fungal hypha is exposed to air, it is protected by molecules that prevent desiccation.

Beyond water regulation, the hydrophobic surfaces of these proteins enable fungi to attach to other hyphae, bacteria, soil particles, plant roots, and fungal spores. This adhesive quality is critical for hyphal network formation and for the physical connections between mycorrhizal fungi and their host plants.

A single fungus can contain ten or more different kinds of hydrophobins, each with specialized attributes. Some facilitate inter- and intracellular communication, some regulate membrane flow, and others strengthen the hypha. These diverse molecules are the subject of intense research due to their potential medical and agricultural applications.

## Measurement and Detection Methods

Glomalin is typically measured using extraction methods developed by Sara Wright and colleagues at the USDA. The most common approach involves extracting soil with citrate buffer at elevated temperature (121 degrees Celsius) for cycles of 30 to 60 minutes. The extracted glomalin is then quantified using a Bradford protein assay or an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with antibodies specific to glomalin.

It is important to note that what is measured as glomalin in soil extractions is operationally defined and may include a mixture of glomalin-related soil proteins rather than pure, intact glomalin. As the compound persists in soil for years to decades, it undergoes modifications that are not yet fully understood. Researchers distinguish between easily extractable glomalin, total glomalin, and immunoreactive glomalin, each providing different information about the glomalin pool.

Hydrophobins are more challenging to measure directly in soil environments due to their lower concentrations and tight association with fungal cell walls. They are typically studied through protein extraction from fungal cultures, genetic analysis of hydrophobin gene expression, and microscopic observation of hyphal surface properties.

## Implications for Soil Health and Sustainable Agriculture

Understanding glomalin and hydrophobins has transformed how soil scientists and agronomists think about soil health. The key insight is that living mycorrhizal fungi actively produce compounds that build and maintain soil structure and store carbon. Soil health is therefore not merely about chemical composition or physical properties in isolation but is deeply connected to the biological activity of the soil food web.

For sustainable agriculture, this means that practices supporting mycorrhizal fungal communities have cascading benefits:

Improved soil structure from glomalin-mediated aggregation.

Enhanced carbon storage through stable, long-lived glycoprotein deposition.

Better water retention and drought resilience in aggregated soils.

More efficient nutrient cycling through active mycorrhizal networks.

Conversely, practices that harm mycorrhizal fungi, such as excessive tillage, bare fallow periods, and high rates of soluble phosphorus fertilizer, undermine these benefits by reducing glomalin production and depleting soil carbon reserves. The [[mycorrhizal-inoculation]] approach can help reestablish glomalin-producing fungal communities in degraded soils, offering a pathway to restore soil health in agricultural and restoration contexts.
