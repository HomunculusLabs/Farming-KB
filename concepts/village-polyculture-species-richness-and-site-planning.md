# Village Polyculture, Species Richness, and Site Planning

## Overview

The design of productive polycultures requires balancing species richness against management capacity, economic viability, and nutritional completeness. Mollison presents a detailed analysis of how many species a village-level polyculture can support, what determines the optimal number, and how to plan an entire site from initial assessment through ongoing evolution. The central tension is between fiscal economics (which peaks at 6-8 species) and nutritional-total yield (which peaks at 50-100 species).

## Species Richness in Village Polycultures

### Species Inventory by Usage Class

Mollison provides a detailed breakdown of potential species in a complex village polyculture situation:

| Usage Class | Species Count |
|---|---|
| Basic food species | 70-90 |
| Mulch and fodder provision | 10-30 |
| Medicinals and biocides | 20-50 |
| Structural and craft | 5-20 |
| Culinary herbs | 20-30 |
| Beverages and export specialties | 10-15 |
| Fuelwood and coppice | 12-30 |
| Special/sacred uses | 8-15 |
| **Total cultivated species** | **155-280** |
| Plus wild-gathered species | 40-80 |
| **Total species utilized** | **195-360** |

Some very useful species fall into 3 or more usage classes, meaning the actual number of distinct species may be lower than the sum of categories.

### Seven Factors Determining Species Richness

The number of productive, managed, and effectively-harvested species in a polyculture is decided by a complex of factors:

1. **Labor availability**: The number of people responsible for managing each crop or animal species. More hands allow more species to be properly tended, harvested, and processed.

2. **Proximity to settlement (zoning)**: Species richness is greatest near the home or village and simplifies with increasing distance. The most complex polycultures occupy zones 1-2 (kitchen garden, home orchard).

3. **Cost-benefit economics**: The relative fiscal economics of increasing inputs to optimum levels. Adding more species eventually reaches diminishing financial returns per unit of management effort.

4. **[[site-planning-and-pest-management-in-tropics|tropics]] (Figure 10.29) provides a concrete example integrating:

- Pig or goat pen integrated with the cropping system
- Forage crops on appropriate ridges
- Cassava strip cropping with zero runoff
- Contour layout for erosion control

This model demonstrates how livestock, annual crops, and tree crops can be arranged on contour to maximize water retention and nutrient cycling within a compact area.

## Earthworks for Polyculture

For a 2-hectare sub-tropical site with slopes of approximately 7°, the recommended earthworks include:

- Heavy emphasis on diverse water capture systems
- Fire control measures integrated into the earthwork design
- Swales on contour for water infiltration
- Padi (rice terrace) crop areas
- Dense fuel and food forestry plantings
- Complex water paths that distribute rainfall throughout the system

The design places slope to the sun side, maximizing solar exposure for crops while using the earthworks to manage water flow from ridge to valley.

## The Depopulation Problem

Mollison makes a critical observation about modern agriculture: "The depopulated, dehumanised, and now almost deserted wasteland of modern agriculture is unable to cope with any but the most basic and simple intercrop systems, thus sacrificing yield, quality, stability, and inevitably people."

This connects species richness directly to social structure. Complex polycultures require human attention and management. The labor-saving rationalization of modern monoculture agriculture has reduced both the capacity for complex food systems and the human population that food systems can support in rural areas. Restoring species richness requires restoring human presence and skill on the land.

## Key Principles

1. **Species richness decreases with distance from settlement**: Design for maximum diversity close to home and increasing simplicity outward
2. **Fiscal and nutritional optima differ**: Plan separately for cash income and food self-reliance
3. **Gardens are research stations**: Test new species and techniques in the intensive zone before scaling outward
4. **[[soil-testing]] pays for itself quickly**: Modest investments in trace elements produce disproportionate early returns
5. **Earthworks before planting**: Water infrastructure and wind protection must precede crop establishment
6. **Market assessment comes first**: Economic viability determines what should be grown, not the other way around
7. **Seven factors limit species count**: Labor, zoning, economics, guilds, processing, area, and maturity together determine the workable species palette
