# Village Polyculture, Species Richness, and Site Planning

## Overview

The design of productive polycultures requires balancing species richness against management capacity, economic viability, and nutritional completeness. Mollison presents a detailed analysis of how many species a village-level polyculture can support, what determines the optimal number, and how to plan an entire site from initial assessment through ongoing evolution. The central tension is between fiscal economics (which peaks at 6-8 species) and nutritional-total yield (which peaks at 50-100 species).

## Species Richness in Village Polycultures

### Species Inventory by Usage Class

Mollison provides a detailed breakdown of potential species in a complex village polyculture situation:

| Usage Class | Species Count |
|---|---|
| Basic food species | 70-90 |
| Mulch and fodder provision | 10-30 |
| Medicinals and biocides | 20-50 |
| Structural and craft | 5-20 |
| Culinary herbs | 20-30 |
| Beverages and export specialties | 10-15 |
| Fuelwood and coppice | 12-30 |
| Special/sacred uses | 8-15 |
| **Total cultivated species** | **155-280** |
| Plus wild-gathered species | 40-80 |
| **Total species utilized** | **195-360** |

Some very useful species fall into 3 or more usage classes, meaning the actual number of distinct species may be lower than the sum of categories.

### Seven Factors Determining Species Richness

The number of productive, managed, and effectively-harvested species in a polyculture is decided by a complex of factors:

1. **Labor availability**: The number of people responsible for managing each crop or animal species. More hands allow more species to be properly tended, harvested, and processed.

2. **Proximity to settlement (zoning)**: Species richness is greatest near the home or village and simplifies with increasing distance. The most complex polycultures occupy zones 1-2 (kitchen garden, home orchard).

3. **Cost-benefit economics**: The relative fiscal economics of increasing inputs to optimum levels. Adding more species eventually reaches diminishing financial returns per unit of management effort.

4. **Plant guild compatibility**: The need for effective plant guilds — harmonious ecological assemblies where species support each other through nutrient cycling, pest control, and microclimate modification.

5. **Marketing and processing capacity**: Whether existing processing and marketing systems can cope with a complex product mix. Many diverse products require diverse markets or processing facilities.

6. **Total area controlled**: Larger areas demand increasing simplicity at the cost of guild complexity. A 0.25-hectare garden can be far more species-rich per unit area than a 100-hectare farm.

7. **System maturity**: Older, more established systems provide more niches as canopy layers develop, while younger systems produce more regular but simpler yields.

### The Fiscal vs. Nutritional Tension

Mollison identifies a fundamental design tension:

- **Fiscal return peaks at approximately 6-8 species** in a system, because cash crops need to be produced at scale with efficient processing and reliable markets.
- **Nutritional-total yield peaks at 50-100 species**, well-distributed over all seasons, because diverse diets require diverse food sources and continuous year-round production.

These two factors — extensive-intensive and fiscal-nutritional — must be defined by designers for their specific needs and will have a profound effect on design. The practical resolution is sensible zonation: species richness close to the settlement (where management intensity is high and nutritional diversity matters most), and concentration on fewer species of high cash value in outer zones.

### The Garden as Trial Ground

Mollison states: "It is in the garden, however, that we may learn the value of such successful extension without sacrificing large amounts of energy and capital. Thus, our gardens are trial areas for the outer zones."

This principle positions the home garden as the research and development laboratory for the entire farm or settlement. Species, guilds, and techniques proven in the intensive garden zone can then be scaled outward as confidence and capacity allow.

## Planning the Whole Site

### Re-Assessment of Established Systems

Even in established polycultures, particularly in plantations, Mollison recommends re-surveying the site with special attention to:

- **Main access and harvesting ways**: Efficient movement of people and products reduces labor costs
- **Earth-shaping for rainwater harvest and specific crop**: Redirecting water flow to where it's most needed
- **Sufficiency of mulch**: Mulch is the engine of tropical soil fertility; its shortage limits all production
- **Best water and irrigation strategies**: Matching water delivery to crop needs and landscape form
- **Better village planning**: Optimizing the relationship between settlement and production areas
- **Improved processing for market**: Adding value on-site increases returns

### New Site Planning Sequence

For a new site, Mollison provides a priority-ordered sequence:

1. **Assess market and social context**: Future prices, potential for processing to higher value, labor availability, shares, legal systems, social necessities, and local self-reliance needs. This economic and social assessment comes first because it determines what should be grown.

2. **Analyze soils and nutrients**: Get advice on soils and necessary nutrients and trace elements. A modest amount of trace elements gives early vigor and early yields — this is a high-leverage investment.

3. **Plan ground layout**: Windbreaks, access roads, and water systems. Detailed design follows later but the broad layout must be established first.

4. **Execute essential earthworks**: Swales, dams, terraces, and access roads. These are permanent features that are expensive to modify later.

5. **Establish nursery**: Use selected varietal forms for new or replacement crops. The nursery should be producing before planting begins.

6. **Commence broadscale plantings**: After windbreaks and nurse crops are established, begin the main planting program.

7. **Continuous assessment and evolution**: Constant assessment, consultation, feedback, and innovative trials. Fill niches as they evolve.

### Priority of Windbreaks and Earthworks

On a new site, windbreak establishment and earth-shaping take absolute priority, preceding all planting. Wind protection is the prerequisite for successful crop establishment, and earthworks determine the water distribution pattern that will sustain all subsequent production.

### Intercrop Selection

Intercrop selection is a priority consideration, serving multiple functions:

- **Shelter**: Protecting more delicate crops from wind, salt, or sun damage
- **Nurse crop function**: Providing nitrogen fixation, mulch material, and microclimate modification
- **Pest management**: Some intercrops attract beneficial insects or repel pests

## Nigerian Polyculture Model

The Nigerian polyculture for humid tropics (Figure 10.29) provides a concrete example integrating:

- Pig or goat pen integrated with the cropping system
- Forage crops on appropriate ridges
- Cassava strip cropping with zero runoff
- Contour layout for erosion control

This model demonstrates how livestock, annual crops, and tree crops can be arranged on contour to maximize water retention and nutrient cycling within a compact area.

## Earthworks for Polyculture

For a 2-hectare sub-tropical site with slopes of approximately 7°, the recommended earthworks include:

- Heavy emphasis on diverse water capture systems
- Fire control measures integrated into the earthwork design
- Swales on contour for water infiltration
- Padi (rice terrace) crop areas
- Dense fuel and food forestry plantings
- Complex water paths that distribute rainfall throughout the system

The design places slope to the sun side, maximizing solar exposure for crops while using the earthworks to manage water flow from ridge to valley.

## The Depopulation Problem

Mollison makes a critical observation about modern agriculture: "The depopulated, dehumanised, and now almost deserted wasteland of modern agriculture is unable to cope with any but the most basic and simple intercrop systems, thus sacrificing yield, quality, stability, and inevitably people."

This connects species richness directly to social structure. Complex polycultures require human attention and management. The labor-saving rationalization of modern monoculture agriculture has reduced both the capacity for complex food systems and the human population that food systems can support in rural areas. Restoring species richness requires restoring human presence and skill on the land.

## Key Principles

1. **Species richness decreases with distance from settlement**: Design for maximum diversity close to home and increasing simplicity outward
2. **Fiscal and nutritional optima differ**: Plan separately for cash income and food self-reliance
3. **Gardens are research stations**: Test new species and techniques in the intensive zone before scaling outward
4. **Soil testing pays for itself quickly**: Modest investments in trace elements produce disproportionate early returns
5. **Earthworks before planting**: Water infrastructure and wind protection must precede crop establishment
6. **Market assessment comes first**: Economic viability determines what should be grown, not the other way around
7. **Seven factors limit species count**: Labor, zoning, economics, guilds, processing, area, and maturity together determine the workable species palette
