# Planning Earthworks and Revegetation

## Overview

The planning of earthworks is as critical as their execution. Poorly planned earth-moving operations can lead to soil degradation, erosion, structural failure, and long-term landscape damage. In permaculture design, earthworks are always planned as an integrated sequence: survey, design, soil testing, pegging, topsoil management, excavation, and immediate revegetation.

## Pre-Excavation Planning Sequence

### 1. Initial Site Decisions

Before any machinery or labourers arrive, decisions must be made about the placement of roads, dams, house sites, drains, and other structures. Contour maps and site plans should be used to inform these decisions. The key principle is to position elements where they will function most effectively within the overall design.

### 2. Soil Testing

Soil must be tested by auger holes, soil samples, and soil pits to determine whether it is suitable for the intended purpose. For dam construction, a good clay soil is essential. Professional advice or additional research should be sought before conclusively deciding on placement. Testing reveals:

- Soil type and structure (clay content, sand proportion, organic matter)
- Depth to bedrock or impermeable layers
- Permeability and water-holding capacity
- Presence of problematic materials (acid sulfates, quick clays)

### 3. Pegging the Site

The site must be pegged out using a level (simple or complex), a measuring tape, and stakes with red or white cloth attached so that earth-moving machine operators can follow them. Accurate pegging prevents costly rework and ensures the earthworks conform to the design.

### 4. Topsoil Management

A designated storage area must be planned for all topsoil removed during excavation. **Topsoil must never be mixed with subsoil.** It should be carefully stripped and stored separately to be later returned to the site as a growing medium and to stabilise subsoil erosion. Topsoil contains the majority of soil biology, organic matter, seed bank, and nutrient cycling capacity — its preservation is essential for rapid site recovery.

## Soil Volume Changes After Moving

When soil is moved, it becomes loose, increasing total volume to approximately 145% of the original. Even when compacted by machine, fill may occupy a space 10% larger than the cut it came from. Key settlement figures:

- **Conscientiously compacted clay** in dam walls may settle as little as 1% over time
- **Loose fill** will eventually settle to 75% or less of its uncompacted volume

This has great relevance to house foundations and wall stability — designers must account for settlement when planning fill depths and structural loads.

## House Site Preparation Sequence

A systematic approach to preparing a house site with drainage:

1. **Careful survey** — place pegs outside the site, call in the bulldozer
2. **Strip topsoil** — carefully mound it above and at either end of the site
3. **Cut to level** — use subsoil fill for access roads only
4. **Foundation trenches** — call in ditcher or backhoe to cut foundation and drainage trenches; pour foundations and slab, place paths, drains, and pipes
5. **Replace topsoil** — call in small blade and bucket machine (Bobcat or wheeled tractor) to replace topsoil and neaten the site; mound some soil to the windward side for hedges
6. **Plant or seed** all topsoil areas immediately to prevent erosion
7. **Fine-tune** with barrow and rakes; check drainage

When topsoil is replaced over fill, the area should not be over-compacted to avoid waterlogging. When replaced over deep or solid subsoil in a cut, the base subsoil should first be ripped or loosened to allow root penetration — the same treatment needed for old roads, quarries, parking areas, or heavily-trafficked fill before planting trees and meadows.

## Revegetation After Earthworks

Every time soil is moved, planting or seeding must follow immediately. The planting and stabilisation of the area should be planned in advance, with plant materials on standby for implementation as soon as possible after disturbance.

### Two Critical Reasons for Immediate Planting

1. **Erosion prevention** — can be severe on bare slopes at only 2% slope, especially during rain
2. **Weed prevention** — to prevent invasion by unwanted volunteer plants that may become difficult to displace later

### Plant Materials for Rapid Stabilisation

A broadscale scatter of mixed seed, raked in, prepares the way for permanent plantings. Species mixes that work in most soils include:

- **Annual seeds**: sunflower, mixed parrot seed with millets, pulses, chard, parsley, lupin, and clovers
- **Root crops** (soil "spikers"): parsnip (fresh seed), salsify, daikon radish, radish, turnip
- **Bulbs**: lilies, grape hyacinth
- **Perennial roots**: sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke), comfrey, chicory, horseradish, ginger, sweet potato, turmeric
- **Grass divisions**: bamboo, banana grass, pampas grass, aloes, agave, New Zealand hemp
- **Seedlings or sets**: elephant garlic, asparagus, globe artichoke
- **Cuttings**: elderberry, willow, poplar, mulberry, Pride of Madeira (Echium fastuosum)
- **Tubed seedlings**: Acacia, Prosopis, tagasaste, Coprosma, pines, eucalypts, shrubs

### Important Considerations

- **Clover** can be late-sown to allow vegetable seed to establish
- **Grasses** are not sown deliberately but will invade if spaces appear, and can be grazed by geese in a few years
- **Trees are dangerous on dam walls** — if they fall, they can take part of the wall with them; bamboos, ginger, sweet potato, pepino, and clump grasses are preferred for bank stabilisation
- **Trees at the base of walls** are advantageous for shading, water removal, and weed reduction

### Erosion Control Techniques

- **Net and pan planting** — effective in overgrazed, eroded, mined, or bulldozed sites
- **Log and branch barriers** — pegged across erosion channels to build up silt and leaf deposits, beside which fibrous-rooted hardy species are planted
- **Mulch behind barriers** — quickly stabilises the seed bed for planting
- **Cross-swales** of lemongrass or Vetiver grass to trap silt and spread water
- **On very steep slopes** — plant pampas, bamboo, lemongrass, and root-mat pioneers, with upslope plantings of chestnut, Acacia, carob, or olive that cascade seed downslope
- **Chisel ploughing** — effective in erosion control where implements can be used

Smooth-finishing banks and surrounds allows mowing or scything until selected plants take over. Failed plants can be replaced with successful species later in the cycle.
