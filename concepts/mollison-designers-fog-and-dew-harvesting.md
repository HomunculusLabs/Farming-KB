Fog and dew represent significant but often overlooked

## Dew: Formation and Significance

Dew is a common result of clear nights, rapid radiation
loss, and a moist air mass over coasts and hills. It occurs
more frequently in clear-sky deserts than in cloudy areas.
A slight wind speed of 1-5 km/h assists the quantity
deposited, while both still air and strong winds reduce
dewfall.

Key characteristics of dew formation include:

- Intensity of deposition is greatest 3-100 cm above
  ground level
- Highest deposition occurs over dry ground surfaces
  which chill more quickly at night
- Lower deposition occurs over wet earth, which retains
  heat longer
- Only in deserts is the 4-5 cm (1-2 inches) of dew per
  year of any significance in precipitation

## Distinguishing Dew from Guttation

It is important not to confuse dew (a radiation heat loss
effect from earth with clear night skies) with the moisture
found on leaves above warm damp ground on cloudy nights.
The latter is either guttation (water exuded from the
leaves) or distillation from rising ground vapour, which
represents no net gain to total precipitation. The waters
of guttation cling to the tips of leaves, while dew
condenses on the whole leaf area.

## Dew Capture Methods

### Stone Condensers

Dew may be captured by building piles of loosely-stacked
stones, where low night winds cool rock surfaces and dew
can accumulate to dampen the ground below. In the Negev
desert and other dry areas, some plants are associated
only with these dew condensers. Each mound of stones may
suffice to water one tree.

Very large radiation traps, such as those on Lanzarote
in the Canary Islands, may grow one grape vine in each
hole. The cinder-covered pits work by allowing [[hemenway-cold-air-drainage-and-frost-management]]
to sink, which then condenses moisture on the cinder
surfaces.

### Plant-Based Dew Collection

The most efficient dew-collectors are free-standing
shrubs of about 1-2 m (3-6 feet) in height. Groups or
solid stands of plants and grasses do less well in
trapping dew, and this may help to explain the discrete
spacing of desert plants. Perhaps 40% more dew is
trapped on scattered shrubs than would be caught in
still air, or on closed vegetation canopies.
