Slugs and snails (gastropods) are among the more visible members of the
[[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]]. While gardeners often
view them primarily as pests, they play important ecological roles in
decomposition and nutrient cycling. Above them in the food web, reptiles,
mammals, and birds serve as top predators and nutrient redistributors.

## Gastropod Biology

### Classification and Evolution

Gastropods (Greek for "stomach-foot") belong to the phylum Mollusca,
which contains some 40,000 species -- the largest group in the order.
Land snails emerged from the sea approximately 350 million years ago,
complete with calcium-based shells evolved for protection. Garden slugs
subsequently evolved from snails, losing their shells either entirely or
mostly.

### Shell Trade-Offs

The snail's shell provides protection but carries costs:
- Requires access to calcium, limiting where shelled gastropods can live
- Reduces mobility and shape control
- Must be maintained and carried

Slugs, by contrast, have greater mobility and can squeeze into spaces
a shell would not permit. Their scavenging range is reported to be up
to a mile per night. However, slugs are extremely susceptible to
dehydration and must find cover in moist areas during dry times. Snails
can seal their shells with a leathery operculum and remain dormant for
up to four years.

### Feeding and Reproduction

Both slugs and snails are hermaphroditic, capable of self-fertilization
though most cross-fertilize. Both mates can lay 100 to 200 translucent
oval eggs up to six times per year. Eggs are deposited just under the
soil surface, where they can remain dormant for years until moisture
conditions are favorable. Juveniles hatch in as little as two weeks and
begin foraging within a day or two. Sexual maturity is reached at about
six months; full growth takes about two years.

Gastropods move by gliding on a single muscular foot, secreting
glycoproteins -- a sticky slime of sugars and proteins. They can stretch
up to 20 times their body length. The hardened slime creates recognizable
paths and contains chemicals obnoxious to predators.

### The Radula

Both slugs and snails possess a radula -- a series of chitinous teeth
resembling a wood rasp. This allows them to grind food into very tiny
particles. Many species are capable of digesting [[fungal-cellulose-decomposition-fungal-enzymes]].

## Ecological Role in the Soil Food Web

### Decomposition

Despite their reputation as plant eaters, slugs and snails also graze
on fungi, algae, lichens, and rotting organic matter. As shredders,
they break down organic material before consuming it, opening it up for
bacteria and fungi to attack -- similar to the role played by
[[teaming-with-microbes-earthworm-ecology-and-vermicomposting]]
and [[teaming-with-microbes-mites-and-soil-arthropod-predators]].

### Underground Activity

Most gastropod activity occurs below ground. Slugs spend only 5 to 10%
of their time above the soil surface. For every slug visible above
ground, three or four are foraging underground. Their subterranean
travels create pathways for air, water, and roots, while their slime
helps bind soil particles.

### Food Web Position

Gastropods serve as prey for:
- Ground and rove beetles (especially larvae)
- Spiders
- Garden snakes, salamanders, and lizards
- Birds
- Specialized nematodes (available commercially for slug control)

When a nematode parasitizes a slug, parts become a meal while the
remainder is left for bacterial and fungal colonization and decay.

### Population Balance

In a healthy [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]], gastropod
numbers are naturally controlled by their predators. They become serious
pests only when chemical use and damaging practices have thrown the food
web out of balance, eliminating the predators that would otherwise keep
their populations in check.

## Higher Animals: Reptiles, Mammals, and Birds

### Soil Engineering

Burrowing mammals -- squirrels, mice, groundhogs, rabbits, chipmunks,
voles, moles, prairie dogs, and gophers -- mix, move, and deposit
organic matter as they tunnel through soil. Their burrows provide
pathways and reservoirs for water and air. While gardeners may resent
their presence, their engineering activities benefit soil structure.

### Nutrient Redistribution

The dung of all reptiles, mammals, and birds serves as food for soil
food web members, recycling nutrients back into the soil. Birds are
particularly important distributors: their guano contains nutrients and
microorganisms, and their feet carry [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa]] that
are spread as birds hop from spot to spot.

### Microbial Transport

All larger animals carry microorganisms on their bodies, feet, and in
their digestive tracts, moving them between locations. At death, their
carcasses become food for the entire soil food web -- a massive pulse of
nutrients and organic matter that fuels decomposition communities.

### Context-Dependent Impact

The role of larger animals differs between a vegetable garden and other
parts of the yard. But wherever they roam, their ecological function is
entirely underpinned by microarthropods and microorganisms, which far
outnumber them in any soil food web. The larger animals are the visible
tip of a much larger, mostly invisible ecosystem.
