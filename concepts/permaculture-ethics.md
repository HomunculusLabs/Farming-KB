# Permaculture Ethics

Permaculture ethics form the philosophical foundation of the entire design system. They are the moral compass that guides all permaculture practice, distinguishing it from mere technical landscaping or farming. david holmgren and [[permaculture-design]], the co-originators of permaculture, both identified three core ethics, though Holmgren's articulation has become the more widely referenced formulation. See also [[permaculture-introduction-and-ethics]] for a broader overview of how these ethics sit within the permaculture framework.

## Mollison's Foundational Philosophy

Before articulating the ethics themselves, Mollison defined the permaculture mindset as one of "working with rather than against nature; of protracted and thoughtful observation rather than protracted and thoughtless action; of looking at systems and people in all their functions, rather than asking only one yield of them; and of allowing systems to demonstrate their own evolutions."

This philosophy rests on a fundamental shift in how we relate to natural systems. The key question becomes: "What can I get from this land, or person?" versus "What does this person, or land, have to give if I cooperate with them?" The former approach leads to war and waste; the latter leads to peace and plenty. This cooperative mindset extends to [[permaculture-social-design]] and [[permaculture-community-resilience]].

## The Three Core Ethics

### Earth Care (Care for the Earth)

The first ethic requires that we care for the living and non-living systems of the earth -- soils, forests, water, air, and all creatures. david holmgren describes this as the obligation to maintain the health and integrity of natural systems as the foundation of all life, including human life. [[permaculture-design]] framed this more precisely: "Provision for all life systems to continue and multiply."

Practically, this means designing systems that build soil rather than deplete it, conserve and purify water, increase biodiversity, and restore degraded landscapes. Every [[permaculture-design]] decision should ask: does this action heal or harm the earth? This connects directly to [[permaculture-soil-strategies]] and [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]].

### People Care (Care for People)

The second ethic extends the obligation of care to human communities and individuals. It recognizes that human beings are part of natural systems, not separate from them. david holmgren emphasizes that people care begins with self-care and extends through family, community, and the wider world. Mollison stated it as: "Provision for people to access those resources necessary to their existence."

This ethic demands that [[permaculture-design]] systems provide for human needs -- food, shelter, energy, community, and meaningful work -- in ways that are sustainable and equitable. Mollison stressed that permaculture systems should provide surplus for sharing after meeting the designer's own needs.

Mollison expanded people care further into the concept of species care: "we expand people care to species care, for all life has common origins. All are our family." This deep ecological perspective sees care as extending across all living beings, not limited to the human sphere. This ethic also underpins [[permaculture-currency-and-economics]] and cooperative economic models.

### Fair Share (Share the Surplus / Set Limits to Consumption)

david holmgren reframed the traditional third ethic from "share the surplus" to "fair share," adding the crucial insight that we must also set limits to consumption and population. This ethic recognizes that the earth has finite resources and that equitable distribution requires both generosity and restraint. Mollison formulated it as: "Setting limits to population and consumption: By governing our own needs, we can set resources aside to further the above principles."

This ethic challenges the growth economy directly. It asks us to consider what is enough, to redistribute surplus rather than accumulate it, and to recognize that overconsumption by some creates deprivation for others and degrades the natural systems that support all life.

## Rules of Resource Use

Mollison derived practical rules from these ethics to guide the use of natural resources:

**The Rule of Necessitous Use** states that we should "leave any natural system alone until we are, of strict necessity, forced to use it." This places the burden of justification on the user, not the resource.

**The Rules of Conservative Use** apply once we have found it necessary to use a natural resource:
- Reduce waste, hence pollution
- Thoroughly replace lost minerals
- Do careful energy accounting
- Assess long-term, negative, biosocial effects on society

These rules translate the ethics into concrete design constraints for any [[permaculture-design]] project.

## Cooperation as Model

Mollison drew on ecological examples to illustrate the ethics in practice. He pointed to mycorrhiza on tree roots as a model of mutual aid: "cooperative species and associations of self-supporting species (like mycorrhiza on tree roots) make healthy communities." This observation leads to the resolve to cooperate and foster interdependence rather than compete. The [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]] embody this cooperative ethic in their emphasis on guilds and polycultures.

## Self-Reliance and Community

Mollison expressed the social dimension of the ethics as a commitment "to be neither employers nor employees, landlords nor tenants, but to be self-reliant as individuals and to cooperate as groups." This vision of self-reliance within cooperative community structures bridges the personal and political dimensions of permaculture, informing [[permaculture-community-resilience]] and [[permaculture-social-design]].

Mollison also argued that unity among people comes from common adherence to ethical principles: "Those who agree on such ethics, philosophies, and goals form a global nation." The permaculture ethics thus serve as a basis for global community beyond national boundaries.

## Ethics as Self-Regulating Mechanisms

david holmgren argues that ethics function as internal self-regulating feedback mechanisms, analogous to the internal constraints that prevent species from destroying their own habitat. In traditional cultures, taboos and ethics prevented overexploitation of resources. The modern growth economy has systematically dismantled these constraints, leading to ecological overshoot.

The permaculture ethics represent an attempt to rebuild these internal regulatory mechanisms at both individual and cultural levels. They are not abstract ideals but practical design criteria that can be applied to every decision, from garden layout to economic systems.

## Ethics and Spirituality

david holmgren connects the permaculture ethics to broader spiritual and indigenous traditions. He notes that indigenous cultures worldwide maintained ethical frameworks that prevented environmental destruction despite having access to abundant resources. The ethics of care are not unique to permaculture but represent a recovery of wisdom that was widespread before industrialization. See regenerative agriculture history for more on these traditions.

The ethics also connect to the concept of [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] -- as energy availability declines, ethical behavior becomes not just morally preferable but practically essential for survival and community cohesion.
