# Black Wattle and Green Manure Trees in Orchard Soil Building

Masanobu Fukuoka regarded green manure trees as essential components of any natural orchard system. Among these, the black wattle (*Acacia mearnsii*), a fast-growing leguminous tree, occupied a
central role in his method for rebuilding depleted orchard soils without chemical fertilizers or compost. The interplanting of fertilizer trees with fruit trees represents one of the most practical
and far-reaching techniques in Fukuoka's approach to natural farming, demonstrating how biological processes can replace mechanical and chemical inputs entirely.

## The Problem of Depleted Orchard Soil

When Fukuoka returned to his father's citrus orchard after World War II, he found the land severely degraded. The topsoil had washed away, leaving only red clay. His father's orchard, once regarded as
one of the best in the region, had run at a net loss over more than forty years of operation. The first thirteen years were unprofitable, the next twenty made money, and the final ten were again in
the red. Even during the profitable middle years, while his father celebrated healthy citrus crops and sturdy trees, the orchard soil was quietly being depleted beneath the surface.

Fukuoka concluded that conventional approaches to restoring the old trees — heavy fertilizer application, root-grafting, and blossom-thinning — would only invite further decline. The soil had been
worked to death by years of plowing, weeding, and chemical inputs. Planting new saplings in the poor soil would fare no better since these would not thrive in the impoverished ground. He resolved
instead to raise fruit trees that grow as the soil enriches, making green manure trees and cover crops the foundation of his soil management strategy.

## The Role of Green Manure Trees in a Natural Orchard

Fukuoka advocated interplanting evergreen and deciduous fruit trees together, always including green manure trees among them. He observed that the principle of "the right crop for the right land" must
be followed: hillside land and valley land must be treated as such, and monoculture of fruit trees should be avoided at all costs. The principal green manure species he recommended included:

- **Acacias** (members of the pea family), which produce nitrogenous fertilizer through root-associated microbes - **Myrtle**, which generates nutrients such as phosphoric acid and potash - **Alder**,
another nitrogen-fixing tree suited to diverse conditions - **Podocarpus**, valued for its contribution to soil conditioning and long-term soil health

He also encouraged interplanting large trees, shrubs, and climbing fruit vines such as grapevine, akebia, and Chinese gooseberry to make full three-dimensional use of orchard space. Leguminous green
manure plants and herbs could be planted as orchard undergrowth, with forage crops, semiwild vegetables, and free-ranging poultry and livestock completing the integrated system. A natural orchard
employing full three-dimensional use of space is, in Fukuoka's view, entirely different from conventional orchards that rely on high-production techniques. For the individual wishing to live in
communion with nature, this represents a paradise on earth.

## Black Wattle: The Principal Fertilizer Tree

Fukuoka considered the black wattle the single most valuable green manure tree for orchard rehabilitation. A member of the pea family, it serves the orchard in multiple capacities simultaneously:

1. **Rapid improvement of deep soil layers** — Its root system penetrates hard, depleted subsoil, opening channels for air and water penetration far beyond the reach of conventional tillage 2.
**Shelterbelt formation** — When planted between fruit trees, it creates windbreaks in just three to four years and reaches the size of a telephone pole in seven to eight years 3. **Summer shade and
soil protection** — In warm regions, the black wattle protects the soil from depletion by providing shade during hot months 4. **Pest suppression** — It is particularly effective in preventing the
emergence of orchard pests, especially mites and scales

Beyond these agricultural functions, the bark of the black wattle is rich in tannin and can be sold at a good price. The wood is excellent for making desks and chairs, and the nectar from its flowers
serves as a source of honey. No other evergreen tree of the pea family grows as quickly as the black wattle — five feet or more per year. This combination of agricultural utility and economic value
makes it uniquely suited to the self-sufficient farming model Fukuoka advocated.

## Soil Transformation Through Black Wattle

Fukuoka's most striking observation concerned the soil-transforming power of the black wattle. Five or six years after planting acacias in hard, lean soil, an area of more than 100 square yards around
each tree had become soft and porous. He regarded this as far easier and more effective than blasting with dynamite and burying organic matter — a technique some orchardists resorted to when dealing
with compacted subsoil.

When the trees were felled after five to six years of growth, each provided as much as a half-ton of high-quality organic material for burying in trenches within the orchard. Fukuoka had initially
tried using a variety of organic materials — straw, hay, twigs, small branches, ferns, wood chips, and lumber — for trench filling. He found that materials he expected to be cheapest (hay, straw,
ferns) were actually quite costly to haul in, while wood chips were inexpensive but difficult to transport. The most practical solution was to produce the organic material on-site by growing black
wattle trees in the orchard itself.

The process of returning to the orchard what had been grown there embodied Fukuoka's principle of closed-system fertility. As he put it: "Figuring that the easiest and most beneficial way was to
return to the orchard what had been grown there, I tried planting various types of trees and found the black wattle to be the best for the purpose."

## Black Wattle as Protector of Beneficial Insects

One of the most important roles of the black wattle, in Fukuoka's view, was its function as a protective tree for beneficial insects. The black wattle grows constantly throughout the year, always
sending out new shoots. These shoots attract aphids and scales, which in turn support a growing population of ladybugs and other predatory insects.

Planting five or so black wattle trees per quarter-acre keeps scales and mites down to a minimum. Fukuoka observed that parasitic wasps and four or five different types of ladybugs emerged naturally
in his orchard where black wattles were interplanted with citrus. In areas where these natural enemies fed in large numbers on the scales, he did not spray and yet the trees escaped serious damage.

The mechanism operates partly through the tannin the black wattle produces, which excretes a substance that repels certain insects. But Fukuoka emphasized that the most direct cause of rapid changes
in pest populations was changes in the microclimate created by the tree's canopy. Mites are especially sensitive to microclimatic changes in the tree. When black wattle is used as a windbreak or shade
tree, depending on the amount of sunlight and breeze to which the fruit tree is exposed, the number of mites and scales may drop dramatically or almost entirely vanish. In addition to these acacias,
other trees that support populations of beneficial insects will certainly be developed in the future.

## Replanting Old Orchards with Black Wattle

Fukuoka recommended the black wattle even when replanting an old, rundown orchard. For a 40- to 50-year-old orchard, he suggested planting a large number of acacias among the existing fruit trees,
then five or six years later felling all the fruit trees and acacias at once and replanting the entire orchard with three- to four-year saplings. He regarded this as far superior to running a
bulldozer through the orchard, as it simultaneously replenished the soil and rejuvenated the land.

Saplings do not take well with the black wattle, so it is better to plant the seed directly. All one has to do is scatter seed here and there throughout the orchard and, in six years or so, it becomes
hard to tell from a distance whether one is looking at a citrus grove or a forest.

## Companion Cover Crops: Ladino Clover, Alfalfa, and Bur Clover

Alongside green manure trees, Fukuoka employed a carefully considered system of ground cover plants. He planted the seeds of thirty legumes, crucifers, and grasses throughout his orchard and concluded
that a weed cover using ladino clover as the primary crop was most effective, with alfalfa, lupine, and bur clover as secondary crops.

Ladino clover eliminates weeds within two to three years, improves soil to a depth of 16 to 18 inches, does not need to be reseeded for six to eight years, does not compete strongly with fruit trees
for fertilizer or moisture, grows back readily after being cut or trampled, and does not hinder farming operations. Its disadvantages are susceptibility to summer-killing and sclerotium disease during
hot, dry weather, and retarded growth in shade.

Clover does not choke out other vegetation, but gradually becomes dominant by growing so thickly as to prevent the germination and establishment of other weeds. When trampled and cut, most weeds
weaken but clover grows all the more vigorously. Failure to understand this and properly control the clover will lead to certain failure. After the clover takes well and flourishes, if left alone it
becomes excessively luxuriant, leaving it open to attack by diseases such as leaf spot and the reemergence and eventual dominance of weeds again in five to six years.

Alfalfa proved invaluable for warm regions where clover loses summer vigor, and for cold, dry areas. Sending roots down six feet or more, it improves deeper soil strata and is highly resistant to
drought, cold, and high temperatures. Bur clover, useful for controlling spring weeds, withers in summer but regrows in fall to suppress winter weeds.

## The Long-Term Soil Management Vision

Fukuoka insisted that soil management divorced from the field, garden, and hillside is meaningless — only a method that enriches all simultaneously makes sense. The purpose of soil management, as he
defined it, is to promote the conversion of weathered material from bedrock into living organic soil, and the enrichment of that soil. He contrasted this with conventional soil management, which he
said consisted basically of clean cultivation that turns the soil into mere mineral matter.

The beneficial effects of natural soil management ultimately show up in the longer lifetime of fruit trees — two to three times that of trees grown by scientific methods. Fukuoka acknowledged that
natural soil improvement takes five to ten years to build up six inches of topsoil through cover crop cultivation, which appears inferior in a world pressed for time. But if farmland were correctly
understood as a legacy to be preserved for future generations, the general opinion of natural farming would improve. Land that grows fertile over time without plowing, weeding, or chemical fertilizers
represents not only an accumulation of labor and capital, but an increase in intangibles as well.

One can understand the nature of the soil more quickly by examining the weeds growing in it than by examining the soil itself. A succession of different herbs takes place over the years as the soil
becomes richer. By sowing vegetable seeds of the same family as the weeds growing in the orchard, vegetable plants can eventually be made to replace the weeds entirely.
