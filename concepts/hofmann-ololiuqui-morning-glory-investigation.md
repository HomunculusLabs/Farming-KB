# Ololiuqui Investigation - Morning Glory Alkaloids

## Historical Background

Ololiuhqui is the Aztec name for the seeds of certain climbing plants
(Convolvulaceae) that, like the mescaline cactus peyotl and the
teonanacatl mushrooms, were used in pre-Columbian times by the Aztecs
and neighboring peoples in religious ceremonies and magical healing
practices. Ololiuhqui is still used today by certain Indian tribes such
as the Zapotec, Chinantec, Mazatec, and Mixtec, who until recently
still led a genuinely isolated existence in the remote mountains of
southern Mexico.

## Early Scholarly Documentation

Richard Evans Schultes, director of the Harvard Botanical Museum,
published an excellent study in 1941 entitled "A Contribution to Our
Knowledge of Rivea corymbosa, the Narcotic Ololiuqui of the Aztecs."
The earliest records about this drug were written by Spanish chroniclers
of the sixteenth century. Bernardino de Sahagun wrote about the
"wondrous effects of ololiuhqui" in his Historia General de las Cosas
de Nueva Espana, describing it as an herb called coatl xoxouhqui (green
snake) whose seeds "stupefy and deprive one of reason."

Francisco Hernandez, a physician sent by Philip II to study native
medicaments from 1570 to 1575, published the first illustration and
detailed description of ololiuhqui in Rome in 1651. He described how
the priests of the Indians ate of this plant to visit with the gods,
after which "thousands of fantastic images and demons then appeared to
them."

## Botanical Identification

The botanical identification of ololiuhqui as seeds of Rivea corymbosa
occasioned many discussions. Recently preference has been given to the
synonym Turbina corymbosa. The Zapotec also utilize badoh negro, the
seeds of Ipomoea violacea. While Turbina corymbosa thrives only in
tropical or subtropical climates, Ipomoea violacea grows as an
ornamental plant dispersed over the whole earth in temperate zones. It
is the familiar morning glory found in gardens with blue or blue-red
striped calyxes.

## Contradictory Early Reports

When Hofmann decided in 1959 to investigate ololiuhqui, only a single
chemical study existed: the work of pharmacologist C. G. Santesson of
Stockholm from 1937, who had not succeeded in isolating an active
substance in pure form. Contradictory findings had been published about
the seeds' activity. Psychiatrist H. Osmond conducted a self-experiment
with 60 to 100 seeds in 1955, experiencing apathy and emptiness
accompanied by enhanced visual sensitivity, followed by relaxation.
However, V. J. Kinross-Wright's 1958 study found that eight subjects
taking up to 125 seeds perceived no effects at all.

## Seeds from Wasson

Through R. Gordon Wasson's mediation, Hofmann obtained two samples of
ololiuhqui seeds. The round, light brown seeds from Huautla were
identified as Rivea (Turbina) corymbosa, while the black, angular seeds
from San Bartolo Yautepec were identified as Ipomoea violacea.

## The Astonishing Chemical Discovery

Hofmann's laboratory assistant Hans Tscherter, who had also
participated in isolating the mushroom active principles, joined the
investigation. They hypothesized that the active principles could be
indole compounds, the same chemical class as LSD, psilocybin, and
psilocin. Extracts gave the blue coloration characteristic of indole
compounds. The identification led to an astonishing result that "at
first seemed scarcely believable."

The active principles of the ancient Mexican magic drug ololiuhqui
proved identical with substances already present in Hofmann's
laboratory. They were alkaloids obtained in the course of decades-long
investigations of ergot, partly isolated from ergot itself, partly
obtained through chemical modification of ergot substances. Lysergic
acid amide, lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide, and closely related
alkaloids were established as the main active principles. Also present
was ergobasine, whose synthesis had been the starting point of
Hofmann's investigations on ergot alkaloids.

## Connection to LSD

Lysergic acid amide and lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide are chemically
very closely related to lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD). Lysergic acid
amide had been produced synthetically during the investigations in
which LSD originated. "Nobody at the time could have suspected that this
compound synthesized in the flask would be discovered twenty years later
as a naturally occurring active principle of an ancient Mexican magic
drug." After discovering LSD's psychic effects, Hofmann had tested
lysergic acid amide in a self-experiment, establishing that it evoked a
dreamlike condition but only with a tenfold to twenty-fold greater dose
than LSD.

## Skeptical Reception

When Hofmann presented these findings at the IUPAC congress in Sydney,
Australia, in fall 1960, colleagues received the talk with skepticism.
Some suspected the ololiuhqui extracts could have been contaminated with
traces of lysergic acid derivatives from Hofmann's laboratory. The
occurrence of ergot alkaloids in higher plants (morning glory family)
contradicted the experience that certain substances are restricted to
respective plant families. Results were subsequently confirmed by
laboratories in the United States, Germany, and Holland.

## The Magic Circle

Hofmann described the studies on ololiuhqui as reaching "a kind of
logical conclusion" in his hallucinogenic drug research, forming "a
magic circle": the starting point had been the synthesis of lysergic
acid amides including ergobasin. This led to the synthesis of LSD. The
hallucinogenic properties of LSD led to the investigation of the sacred
mushroom teonanacatl, from which psilocybin and psilocin were isolated.
This proceeded to ololiuhqui, in which hallucinogenic lysergic acid
amides were again encountered, including ergobasin, "with which the
magic circle closed."
