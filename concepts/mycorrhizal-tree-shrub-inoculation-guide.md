## Overview

Mycorrhizal inoculation of trees and shrubs is a well-established practice supported by thousands of scientific studies. Both ectomycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi can dramatically improve seedling survival, growth rates, nutrient uptake, and drought resistance during nursery production, transplanting, and field establishment. Proper matching of fungal species to host plants is critical for success.

## Matching Fungi to Tree Species

Some ectomycorrhizal tree hosts show specificity for particular fungal associations, while others are generalists that can associate with a wide range of fungal partners. A single mature tree can be simultaneously associated with ten or more different mycorrhizal fungal species. Modern DNA and RNA identification techniques have improved the ability to determine optimal host-fungal pairings, though some lab-confirmed associations do not replicate in field conditions and vice versa.

Trees and shrubs without proper mycorrhizal associations struggle and usually die because they cannot obtain sufficient nutrients or water. A famous example occurred in Puerto Rico in the 1950s, where pine tree plantings using native island soils completely failed — every seedling died within two years despite fertilization. In 1955, soils from North Carolina pine forests were used to inoculate new plantings, and the trees thrived due to the appropriate ectomycorrhizal fungi.

## General-Purpose Nursery Fungi

Several mycorrhizal fungi species perform well as general-purpose inoculants for tree nurseries:

- Thelephora terrestris — widely used for conifer seedlings
- Laccaria laccata — effective across multiple conifer species
- Inocybe species — found in natural forests, good nursery performance
- Pisolithus arhizus — used for oak inoculation
- Scleroderma citrinum — best fungus for European beech
- Hebeloma arenosum — effective on pine seedlings
- Leccinum scabrum — good inoculant for birch seedlings
- Rhizophagus intraradices — used for red cedar, redwood, and giant sequoia

## Application Methods

The appropriate mycorrhizal association should be established as early as possible and maintained for the plant's entire life. Commercial planting soils often contain propagules, and liquid formulations can be applied through drip or watering systems. Several application approaches are effective:

Seedling roots can be dipped in granular or liquid formulations at transplanting time. Granular formulas can be placed in holes around drip lines. Liquid inoculants can be applied at any time to existing stock. A broad mix of mycorrhizal fungi helps ensure colonization, as each species imparts its own particular benefits.

Growers must carefully monitor fertilizers and chemicals around inoculated plants. Overfertilization, particularly with phosphorus and nitrogen, can reduce or eliminate mycorrhizal associations. Pesticide and fungicide applications can also be detrimental.

## Ectomycorrhizae in Forest Systems

In managing forests, silviculturists focus on large mother trees that are heavily colonized by mycorrhizal fungi. These trees share their mycelial network with seedlings and young trees in the understory, literally feeding and protecting smaller trees through interconnected underground networks. When large trees die, their nutrients return to the fungal network for reuse.

Unlike arbuscular fungal spores, ectomycorrhizal spores can survive forest fires by forming spore banks in the soil. Many shrub species sprout rapidly after fire and sustain mycorrhizal populations until regenerating trees can reconnect to the mycelial network, enabling immediate post-fire mycorrhizal regeneration.

## Reforestation and Site Reclamation

Mycorrhizal fungi can speed growth when reestablishing trees in disturbed areas such as chemical spill sites, borrow pits, and eroded terrain. Commercial nurseries sometimes use sterile growing media and fumigation that can destroy existing mycorrhizae, making inoculation essential at outplanting.

Organic matter levels in forest soil significantly impact mycorrhizal health. One study showed mycorrhizal formation was reduced by up to 90 percent when Douglas fir seedlings were grown in soils where timber operations removed organic material. Retaining slash and debris after harvest helps maintain mycorrhizal populations.

Mycorrhizae can also take up and isolate toxins including heavy metals and radioactive elements. Studies following the 1986 Chernobyl disaster found fungi growing inside the ruined reactor used radioactivity as an energy source, and native trees inoculated with mycorrhizal fungi such as Gomphidius glutinosus and Laccaria amethystina absorbed radioactive cesium.

## Documented Tree and Shrub Responses

Scientific studies have documented mycorrhizal benefits across a wide range of tree and shrub species:

**Fruit and nut trees:** Inoculated almond and walnut seedlings showed consistent growth increases regardless of planting medium. Apple seedlings inoculated with Rhizophagus intraradices showed increased phosphorus uptake, stem length, and dry weight within five months. Pear trees responded positively but results varied with phosphorus levels and fungal species.

**Conifers:** Inoculation with Laccaria proxima improved growth of Japanese black pine, jack pine, mugo pine, black spruce, red spruce, and white spruce. Ponderosa pine seedlings inoculated with Rhizopogon roseolus achieved 93 percent field survival versus 37 percent for uninoculated controls. Douglas fir inoculated with Hebeloma longicaudum, Paxillus involutus, and Pisolithus arhizus outgrown controls even with increasing fertilizer.

**Hardwoods:** Oak inoculated with Pisolithus arhizus showed improved seedling growth, shoot diameter, and height. Chokecherry saplings responded to Funneliformis mosseae and Rhizophagus intraradices with greater above- and below-ground mass. European beech colonized by Lactarius subdulcis better withstood drought.

**Shade and ornamental trees:** Incense cedar inoculated with Rhizophagus intraradices showed significantly increased survival on disturbed sites. Yew cuttings inoculated with R. intraradices produced significantly more roots than controls, matching or exceeding rooting hormone results.

## Commercial Applications

Knowledge of ectomycorrhizal associations enables profitable cultivation of edible mushrooms and truffles. Oak and hazelnut trees inoculated with Tuber melanosporum produce black truffles, with commercial operations ranging from Oregon to Tasmania. The demand for these ectomycorrhizal fruiting bodies makes this an active area of applied research.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Inoculation

While ectomycorrhizal inoculation is critical for conifers and many hardwoods, arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi are essential for the majority of agricultural and horticultural plants. AM fungi colonize the roots of approximately 80% of terrestrial plant families, including most vegetables, grains, legumes, and fruit trees. The primary genera used in commercial inoculants include Rhizophagus (formerly Glomus intraradices), Funneliformis mosseae, and Gigaspora species. Unlike ectomycorrhizal fungi, AM fungi cannot be cultured independently of plant roots, which complicates commercial production. Most commercial AM inoculants are produced using pot culture methods, where host plants are grown in sterile media and inoculated with target AM species, then the colonized root segments and surrounding media containing spores and hyphae are harvested and packaged.

## Inoculation Timing and Techniques

The timing of mycorrhizal inoculation significantly affects colonization success. For bare-root seedlings, inoculation at the nursery stage is most effective because it allows the fungal association to develop during the critical early growth period. Bareroot dip methods, where seedling roots are coated with a slurry containing mycorrhizal propagules immediately before planting, can be effective but are less reliable than nursery inoculation. For container-grown stock, incorporating inoculant into the growing medium at the time of potting ensures early contact between fungal propagules and developing roots. Direct field inoculation of established trees is possible through soil injection around the root zone, but results are variable because existing soil microbial communities and soil chemistry can inhibit introduced fungi. The most reliable approach is to ensure inoculation occurs during the earliest possible stage of plant development.

## Soil Conditions Affecting Mycorrhizal Success

Mycorrhizal colonization is strongly influenced by soil conditions. High soil phosphorus levels, whether from natural fertility or fertilizer application, are the most significant inhibitor of mycorrhizal formation because plants reduce their investment in fungal symbiosis when phosphorus is readily available. Soil pH affects both fungal survival and host plant physiology; most mycorrhizal fungi prefer slightly acidic to neutral conditions (pH 5.5-7.0). Soil compaction physically restricts root growth and hyphal exploration, reducing colonization opportunities. Excessive soil disturbance through tillage severs existing hyphal networks, which are the primary means by which mycorrhizal fungi spread from established plants to new seedlings. The presence of certain pesticides, particularly fungicides, can be directly toxic to mycorrhizal fungi, and their use should be minimized or eliminated in inoculated systems.

## Ectomycorrhizal Edible Mushroom Cultivation

The commercial cultivation of ectomycorrhizal edible mushrooms represents a unique intersection of forestry and agriculture. Truffle cultivation, the most commercially significant ectomycorrhizal mushroom enterprise, requires inoculating host trees (typically oak or hazelnut) with the target Tuber species and then managing the orchard for 5-10 years before the first harvest. The global truffle market exceeds several billion dollars annually, with European black truffle (Tuber melanosporum) and Italian white truffle (Tuber magnatum) commanding premium prices. Other ectomycorrhizal species with commercial potential include chanterelles (Cantharellus cibarius), porcini (Boletus edulis), and matsutake (Tricholoma matsutake). While the artificial cultivation of these species remains challenging, advances in mycorrhizal inoculation techniques are steadily improving success rates.

## Mycorrhizae and Climate Resilience

Mycorrhizal fungi play an increasingly recognized role in plant climate resilience. Inoculated trees show greater drought tolerance because the extensive mycelial network accesses water from a much larger soil volume than roots alone can explore. During drought, mycorrhizal plants maintain higher stomatal conductance and photosynthetic rates compared to non-mycorrhizal controls. Mycorrhizal fungi also help plants cope with temperature stress by improving nutrient uptake when root function is impaired by heat or cold. As climate change increases the frequency and severity of drought events worldwide, mycorrhizal inoculation is emerging as a practical tool for climate-adapted forestry and agriculture. Research suggests that maintaining diverse mycorrhizal communities may provide greater resilience than single-species inoculation, as different fungal species contribute different stress-tolerance mechanisms.

## Mycorrhizal Networks and Plant Communication

One of the most significant discoveries in mycorrhizal research is the role of fungal networks in facilitating communication and resource sharing between plants. Through the common mycorrhizal network (CMN), plants can transfer carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, water, and chemical signals to neighboring plants. Research by Suzanne Simard and colleagues demonstrated that mother trees can transfer carbon to their seedlings through mycorrhizal networks, giving shaded seedlings a survival advantage. Plants under insect attack can send chemical warning signals through the network, prompting neighboring plants to activate their defensive chemistry before the pests arrive. These findings transform the understanding of forests from collections of competing individuals to interconnected networks of cooperating organisms, with implications for forest management practices including selective harvesting and retention of legacy trees.

## Mycorrhizae and Nutrient Cycling

Mycorrhizal fungi play a central role in nutrient cycling within ecosystems. The extraradical mycelium that extends into the soil from colonized roots actively mines the soil for nutrients, particularly phosphorus and nitrogen, using enzymatic processes that plants cannot perform independently. Ectomycorrhizal fungi produce enzymes that break down organic matter, releasing nutrients from complex organic molecules that would otherwise be unavailable to plants. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi access phosphorus from mineral particles through acidification and chelation processes at the hyphal tip. When fungal hyphae die, the nutrients they have accumulated are released back into the soil in plant-available forms, creating a biological nutrient cycling system that is far more efficient than abiotic processes alone. This nutrient cycling function makes mycorrhizal fungi essential components of any sustainable agricultural or ecological restoration project.

## Post-Inoculation Management

Successful mycorrhizal inoculation requires appropriate post-planting management. The most critical factor is minimizing root disturbance after inoculation, as the mycorrhizal association is established through root-hyphal contact that takes weeks to develop fully. Practices that damage roots -- transplanting, aggressive cultivation, root pruning -- disrupt the developing mycorrhizal network and set back the colonization process. Similarly, the application of high rates of phosphorus fertilizer suppresses mycorrhizal colonization because the plant reduces its investment in fungal partners when phosphorus is readily available. Maintaining a continuous soil cover with organic mulch supports mycorrhizal development by providing a favorable habitat for hyphal growth and protecting the soil surface from temperature extremes and desiccation.

## Mycorrhizae in Restoration Ecology

Mycorrhizal inoculation is increasingly used in ecological restoration projects where degraded sites lack healthy soil biological communities. Mining sites, construction areas, and land cleared for agriculture often have severely depleted mycorrhizal populations that limit the establishment of vegetation. Inoculation of nursery stock before outplanting, or direct soil inoculation at restoration sites, can dramatically improve plant survival and growth rates. The mycorrhizal fungi also contribute to soil structure development, aggregate formation, and organic matter accumulation, creating a positive feedback loop that accelerates ecosystem recovery. Restoration ecologists have found that combining mycorrhizal inoculation with native plant seeding and appropriate site preparation produces the most successful outcomes across a wide range of degraded ecosystems.

## Commercial Inoculant Products

The market for mycorrhizal inoculant products has expanded significantly in recent years, with products available in various forms including granular, powder, liquid, and tablet formulations. Products vary in the species and strains of mycorrhizal fungi they contain, the number of propagules per unit, and the inclusion of supplementary materials such as humic acids, seaweed extracts, and beneficial bacteria. When selecting commercial inoculants, tree planters should consider whether the product contains species appropriate for the target plants (endomycorrhizal for most crops and herbaceous plants, ectomycorrhizal for many conifers and hardwoods), whether the propagule count is sufficient for the planting area, and whether the product has been independently tested for viability. Stored inoculants have limited shelf life, and viability declines rapidly at high temperatures or under prolonged storage, so fresh product from reputable suppliers should be used.
