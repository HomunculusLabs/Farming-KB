# Albedo and Solar Radiation

## Overview

Albedo is the measure of how much incoming solar radiation a surface reflects versus absorbs. It is one of the most critical factors influencing microclimate, site design, and energy conservation in [[permaculture]] systems. Understanding albedo allows designers to manipulate heat gain, frost risk, and growing conditions through strategic material and plant selection.

## What is Albedo?

The term comes from the Latin word for "whiteness." Every surface on Earth reflects some portion of incoming sunlight and absorbs the rest. The percentage of reflected energy is the albedo value, ranging from 0% (perfect absorber, a "black body") to 100% (perfect reflector). These values have profound implications for temperature, plant growth, and building design.

## How Albedo Affects Microclimate

### Temperature and Heat Storage

Surfaces with low albedo (dark soils, forests) absorb the majority of incoming radiation and convert it to heat. This heat is stored in the mass of the material and slowly released, warming the surrounding air and soil. Surfaces with high albedo (snow, white gravel, light-colored paint) reflect most energy away, keeping surfaces cool.

This distinction is critical for:

- **Frost prevention**: Dark surfaces absorb daytime heat and release it at night, reducing frost risk. Light surfaces near sensitive plants can actually increase frost by reflecting away heat that might otherwise be absorbed.
- **Building efficiency**: Dark-roofed buildings absorb heat (useful in cold climates), while light-colored roofs reflect it (essential in hot climates).
- **Soil temperature management**: Dark mulches and soils warm faster in spring, accelerating seed germination. Light-colored mulches keep root zones cool in hot climates.

### The Radiation Balance

The sun delivers approximately 2 Langleys per minute (gram calories per square centimeter) to the outer atmosphere. However, the quantity reaching ground level varies enormously due to:

- **Latitude and season**: Polar areas at 5 deg sun elevation receive energy through 11 atmospheres of air, reducing transmission to as little as 1%. At the equator with overhead sun, about 22% penetrates.
- **Slope angle**: Slopes facing the poles receive dramatically less energy than equator-facing slopes, similar to the latitude effect.
- **Atmospheric conditions**: Dust, water vapor, clouds, and ice all scatter and absorb radiation before it reaches the ground.

### Temperature Lapse Rate

Temperature decreases approximately 1 deg C for every 100 meters of altitude gain (about 3 deg F per 1,000 feet). This is the normal lapse rate, but exceptions occur in valleys and basins where **temperature inversions** trap cool, dense air below warmer air. These inversions cause fog, smog, and pollution buildup in low-lying areas and increase frost risk for valley-floor gardens.

## Design Implications

### Siting Houses and Gardens

The single most important energy conservation strategy in temperate to subtropical climates is placing dwellings and gardens on **sun-facing slopes in the [[thermal-belt]]**. These mid-slope positions:

- Receive maximum solar radiation
- Drain cold air downhill at night
- Lie above valley frost pockets
- Sit below exposed, chilly hilltops

In hill country, thermal belts may be found at 1,000-5,000 meters. On lower hillslopes, they typically occur at 100-200 meters. In hot deserts, frost-free zones may extend only 10-15 meters up mesa slopes. Local observation of existing vegetation, farmer knowledge, and trial plantings of frost-susceptible species are the best ways to identify thermal belts on a specific site.

### Managing Albedo in Design

- **Use dark mulches** (dark compost, rock dust) to warm soils for early spring planting
- **Use white gravel or reflective surfaces** near buildings in hot climates to reduce cooling loads
- **Plant dark evergreen trees** on cold forest edges to absorb and radiate heat
- **Avoid light-colored bark trees** (white-barked species) in cold districts where heat absorption is desired
- **Use white paint** on plant stems to prevent sunburn in high-radiation environments
- **Choose dark building materials** for heat retention in cool climates

### Plants as Heat Managers

All plants with high biomass (especially trees) store heat in their mass, which is primarily water. This stored heat moderates nearby temperatures. A fairly small clearing in a dense forest may remain frost-free in cold climates because the surrounding tree mass absorbs heat by day and radiates it into the clearing at night.

Water bodies and stone masses similarly store heat effectively due to their high specific heat. Air, by contrast, has low specific heat and is a very poor conductor, making it an excellent insulator. Many insulation systems work simply by trapping air in pockets (cork, sawdust, wood fiber, straw).

## Advection: Wind-Carried Heat and Cold

Winds transport heat between regions. **Advected warmth** arrives with winds from warmer areas, while **advected cold** flows down mountain slopes to pool in hollows. Advected warm air meeting cool areas causes condensation, critical for forest precipitation but potentially damaging in enclosed buildings where moisture control is needed.

In building design, advected air should be managed carefully. Only dry warm air should be directed into wooden structures, with provisions to vent condensation moisture to the exterior.

## Radiation and Human Health

In areas of severe direct or diffuse radiation, especially where the atmosphere is thin (mountains) or albedo is high (snow fields, granite outcrops, white sand), designers must account for:

- Severe radiation burns and skin cancer risk
- Temporary or permanent blindness from reflected light
- The need to screen plants and people from excessive exposure

Partial shade structures, white-painted stems on young trees, and careful site selection away from highly reflective surfaces all help mitigate these risks.

## Key Principles

1. **Dark surfaces absorb, light surfaces reflect** — use this to warm or cool specific zones
2. **Thermal belt placement** is the most impactful single design decision for energy conservation
3. **Temperature inversions** in valleys and basins create frost-prone zones that should be avoided for sensitive plantings
4. **Mass stores heat** — water, stone, and tree biomass moderate temperature extremes
5. **Air is an insulator** — trapped air in mulches, building walls, and clothing reduces heat transfer
6. **Advected winds** carry warmth, cold, moisture, and pollution — understand prevailing wind patterns for every site
7. **Radiation crosses space** and heats solids and liquids efficiently; it is the mechanism by which the sun warms Earth
