# Compost Building Guide

Comprehensive guide to building high-quality compost, distilled from Steve Solomon's "Gardening When It Counts." Producing compost that will effectively grow vegetables is a highly skilled activity requiring hard work, close attention, and a desire to observe what is happening inside the heap.

## Why Compost

Nature recycles — everything that grows eventually dies, falls to earth, and rots. Vegetables, unlike wild plants, have come to require higher than natural levels of nutrients. Most soil a vegetable gardener uses these days may be too poor to grow most kinds of vegetables. Composting is the primary means of increasing soil fertility while maintaining soil health.

## Carbon to Nitrogen Ratios (C/N)

Every decomposable material has a carbon-nitrogen ratio that predicts how it will decompose and how well it will feed plants.

### Key Principle: Soil Humus C/N = 12:1

- Soil humus has a C/N of around 12:1 in every climate
- If the amendment C/N is higher than 12:1, soil microbes "burn" carbon for fuel while preserving nitrogen. Result: increased humus, healthier soil, but temporary nutrient lockup
- If the amendment C/N is lower than 12:1, surplus nitrogen converts to ammonia gas, then to nitrates. Result: plants grow fast temporarily, but humus is consumed and soil [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] decreases

### C/N Reference Table

| Material | Approximate C/N |
|---|---|
| Sawdust | 500:1 |
| Paper | 175:1 |
| Cornstalks (dry) | ~100:1 |
| Straw (cereal) | ~80:1 |
| Tree bark | ~100:1 |
| Tree needles (conifer) | ~80:1 |
| Tree leaves (deciduous) | ~60:1 |
| Autumn grass | ~50:1 |
| Summer grass | ~25:1 |
| Legume hulls | ~30:1 |
| Cardboard (corrugated) | ~350:1 |
| Seaweed | ~20:1 |

## Temperature and Decomposition

The speed of organic decomposition doubles for every 10F (5C) increase in temperature (geometric increase). However, organic enzymes are destroyed at around 120F (50C). A few thermophilic enzymes in a [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-hot-compost-and-cold-compost]] heap can handle temperatures as high as 150-160F (65-71C), but if material heats beyond 150F (65C), it loses the bulk of its nutrients as methane, ammonia, and other gases off-gas.

Practical implications:
- Mulched soil at 75F (24C) — decomposition is slow; a 6-inch layer of hay might take a year
- Properly composted material in warm soil — decomposition completes in a few months
- Manure passed through an animal's gut — inoculated with microorganisms, decomposes much faster

## Compost Quality Standards

### Low-Grade Compost (barely suitable for a garden)
- ~1.5% nitrogen content
- C/N around 20:1
- N+P+K under 3%
- Use: mulch for ornamentals, light amendment for low-demand vegetables

### Medium-Quality Compost
- ~1.5-2% nitrogen content
- C/N around 15-20:1
- N+P+K 3-6%
- Use: good for low and medium demand vegetables

### High-Quality Compost (grows high-demand vegetables as sole fertilizer)
- Nitrogen content exceeding 3%
- N+P+K exceeding 6% (with significant phosphorus)
- C/N no higher than 12:1
- Contains significant phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and trace minerals

Note: In cool/maritime climates, use larger quantities of more potent compost. In hot climates, organic matter rots quickly and lower-quality compost may suffice.

## Sheet Composting

Spreading a layer of nutrient-rich organic matter atop the soil and shallowly digging it in. Decomposition happens faster than surface mulching because material is mixed into well-oxygenated soil.

### Critical timing
- Takes about 6 weeks for decomposition to complete in [[stamets-cultivator-agaricus-bitorquis-warm-weather-cultivation]] (if C/N no higher than 30:1 and layer is under 1 inch thick)
- In cold-winter climates: avoid autumnal sheet composting on beds intended for spring crops — decomposition halts in winter
- Best done in spring/summer if you can afford land out of production

### Cautions
- Poultry or rabbit manure should NOT be sheet-composted alone — these strong, low C/N materials are best used as fertilizers
- You can lower the C/N of high-carbon sheet compost by including a thin layer of poultry manure
- Lime should be spread along with sheet composting: ~50 lbs of equal mixture agricultural lime and dolomitic lime per 1,000 sq ft per year
- Do NOT sheet-compost with late-summer grasses or high C/N woody material on a new garden — nitrogen lockup can last until midsummer

## The Once-a-Year Heap (Medium Quality)

For gardens under 3,000-4,000 sq ft without livestock:

1. Starting in late autumn, steadily accumulate all vegetative wastes and kitchen garbage into one great stack
2. Chop anything longer than 30 cm into smaller bits while still green and tender
3. Allow grass clippings to dry for 24 hours before adding (prevents anaerobic heating)
4. Build the heap in early autumn after summer crop cleanup and leaf drop
5. The heap needs to be at least 3 feet (90 cm) across and 4-5 feet (120-150 cm) high

## Tumblers and Grinders

- **Tumblers**: The more frequently a compost heap is turned, the lower its value as fertilizer. Tumblers produce compost quickly but it tends to be low-grade, similar to municipal compost. Not recommended as primary fertilizer.
- **Grinders/Shredders**: Smaller particles decompose faster and hotter, but the research is clear — heaps that exceed ~150F (65C) lose bulk nutrients as gases. Grinding is not worth the cost and maintenance.

## Key Takeaways

- Compost is an intentional, controlled microbial ferment — like making wine, precision matters
- The average C/N of your starting material determines the outcome
- Composting is safer and more certain than sheet composting — decompose first, then apply
- Most homemade compost is not potent enough alone for high-demand vegetables — supplement with [[soil-testing]]-based amendments or [[hamilton-concentrated-organic-fertilizer-products]] fertilizer (COF)
