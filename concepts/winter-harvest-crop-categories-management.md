## Winter Harvest Crop Categories and Cold Season Management

## Overview

The Winter Harvest Handbook organizes its crop production system around a carefully categorized selection of cold-hardy vegetables, each managed through specific planting schedules, greenhouse environments, and harvesting protocols. The book"s extensive indexing reveals the depth and interconnection of the system's components — from individual crop varieties to structural design, soil management, and pest control. Understanding these categories and their management is essential for anyone implementing a successful year-round harvest program.

## The Winter Crop Portfolio

### Leafy Greens and Salad Components

The backbone of any winter harvest operation is its selection of cold-hardy leafy greens, which can be grown and harvested throughout the coldest months when properly protected:

**Arugula** (*Eruca vesicaria*) — One of the most reliable winter greens, arugula thrives in cool temperatures and is included in crop rotations at multiple points. It serves as both a standalone crop and a salad mix component. Sowing dates span from late August through December in a staggered schedule across outdoor fields, early houses, late houses, and cool houses.

**Lettuce** (*[[lactuca-sativa]]*) — The primary salad crop, lettuce is grown in multiple successions from late August through November. Different planting sites (outdoor, early greenhouse, late greenhouse, [[cool-greenhouse]]) allow for continuous harvest. The book emphasizes crop debris management, [[flame-weeding]] for weed control, and careful temperature management (lettuce grows optimally at cool temperatures around 70°F).

**Spinach** (*[[spinacia-oleracea]]*) — A cornerstone winter crop with extensive coverage. Key management considerations include aphid control, fertilizing with nitrogen-rich amendments, strategic planting schedules, appropriate spacing, and integration into both salad mix and braising mix. Spinach is noted as a crop with strong customer demand.

**Mâche** (*Valerianella locusta*) — Also known as corn salad or lamb's lettuce, mâche is one of the most cold-tolerant salad greens available. It thrives in the lowest temperatures of the winter greenhouse and is a critical component of the salad mix during the coldest months.

**Claytonia** (*Claytonia perfoliata*) — Miner's lettuce is another extremely cold-hardy species that produces well through the depth of winter. It occupies a specific niche in crop rotations and is valued for its reliable production when other greens slow down.

### Asian Greens

The handbook gives significant attention to Asian greens, which have proven exceptionally well-suited to winter harvest systems:

- **Mizuna** (*Brassica juncea* var. *japonica*) — A feathery-leaved mustard green with excellent cold tolerance. Used in salad mix and as a standalone crop.
- **Tatsoi** (*Brassica rapa* var. *rosularis*) — A spoon-shaped Asian green that forms attractive rosettes. Particularly cold-hardy and productive.
- **Pak Choi** (*Brassica rapa* subsp. *chinensis*) — A versatile Asian green used in salad mix, Stir-Fry Paks, and as a standalone crop. Strong customer demand makes it commercially important.
- **Mustard greens** — Various mustard varieties add spice and color to salad mixes and braising mixes.

### Root Vegetables

**Carrots** — A key winter crop with special considerations:
- Specific varieties like Napoli and Nelson are recommended for winter production
- Flame weeding is employed for weed control
- Carrots are interplanted with other crops and included in multiple crop rotations
- Customer demand for "sweet winter" or "candy" carrots is noted as a significant market opportunity
- Quick hoops provide additional protection over carrot beds
- Harvest season extends through the coldest months

**Turnips** — Specific varieties like Hakurei are recommended. Turnips are included in Stir-Fry Paks and have good cold tolerance. Spacing and sowing dates are carefully managed.

**Radishes** — A reliable, quick-growing winter crop included in Stir-Fry Paks. Radishes serve as a good indicator crop and provide early cash flow.

**Beets** — Both beet roots and beet leaves (especially the variety Bull's Blood) are important winter crops. Beet leaves contribute anthocyanins to salad mixes, adding both nutrition and visual appeal.

### Alliums

**Scallions** — Green onions are a reliable winter crop, with overwintered varieties performing particularly well. They are included in quick hoop systems and have their own dedicated planting schedule.

**Leeks** — A slower-growing but extremely cold-hardy allium. Leeks are a significant winter crop with strong customer demand, particularly for inclusion in Stir-Fry Paks. They require specific dibbling techniques for transplanting and careful spacing.

### Cooking Greens and Braising Mix

The handbook distinguishes between salad mix (raw consumption) and braising mix (cooked):

**Braising mix components** include:
- Chard (*Beta vulgaris* subsp. *vulgaris*)
- Kale (multiple varieties including dinosaur/Tuscan kale)
- Collards
- Mustard greens
- Asian greens

These crops are harvested at a slightly larger size than salad greens and are marketed for cooking applications, commanding different price points and serving different customer needs.

### Endive and Chicory

**Endive** — Multiple varieties including Bianca Riccia and golden frisée are important winter salad components. They are noted for their nutritional quality (anthocyanins) and distinctive bitter flavor that adds complexity to salad mixes.

**Escarole** — Related to endive, escarole provides bulk and mild bitterness to winter salads.

**Radicchio** — While noted as a winter crop, radicchio can be challenging due to pest issues (particularly aphids).

## Greenhouse Environment Categories

The handbook defines a clear hierarchy of protected growing environments, each serving a specific role in the year-round production system:

### Cold Houses (Unheated Greenhouses)

The primary production environment for winter harvest, cold houses rely entirely on passive solar heating and the thermal mass of the earth. Key characteristics:
- Temperatures typically range from just above freezing to the mid-50s°F
- Floating row covers provide an additional layer of protection inside the cold house
- Harvest season extends from approximately November through April
- Minimal to no energy costs
- Requires careful ventilation management to prevent overheating on sunny days
- USDA zone considerations affect design and crop selection

### Cool Greenhouses (Minimally Heated)

Cool greenhouses provide a slight temperature advantage over cold houses through minimal heating:
- Energy costs are modest compared to fully heated greenhouses
- Productivity is higher than in cold houses, particularly for salad mix production
- Used for crops that need slightly warmer conditions
- Washing and packing operations can be conducted inside during cold weather
- Plastic covers are standard

### High Tunnels

A simpler, lower-cost structure compared to full greenhouses:
- Typically use polyethylene plastic covers
- No permanent heating system
- Rely on passive solar gain and thermal mass
- Quick and economical to construct
- Used extensively for winter crop production

### Quick Hoops

The most economical and flexible protected growing structure:
- Made from PVC conduit bent into hoops
- Covered with [[floating-row-cover]] material
- Secured with sand bags along the base
- Highly portable and can be moved between beds
- Used for crops like carrots, onions, scallions, radishes, and spinach
- Cost-effective season extension tool

## Crop Rotation and Succession Planning

### Four-Site Planting Strategy

The handbook describes planting across four distinct sites, each with different environmental conditions and planting windows:

1. **Outdoor fields** — Harvest until early November; the first site planted in late summer
2. **Early greenhouses** — Planted next, providing protection as outdoor conditions deteriorate
3. **Later greenhouses** — Additional succession plantings for extended harvest
4. **Cool greenhouse** — The final planting site, with sowings extending into December for the latest harvests

### Rotation Principles

- Crops are rotated across beds and structures to break pest and disease cycles
- Nitrogen-fixing cover crops (cowpeas, red clover) are integrated into rotations
- Root crops follow leaf crops in a logical fertility progression
- Intensive cropping (multiple crops per bed per year) requires careful fertility management
- Tomatoes serve as a summer rotation crop in greenhouses, followed by winter greens

## Pest and Disease Management

### Organic Pest Control Philosophy

The handbook emphasizes prevention over palliatives:
- Healthy biological soil is the first line of defense
- Crop rotation disrupts pest and disease cycles
- Physical barriers (floating row covers) exclude insect pests
- Proper sanitation (removing crop debris) reduces disease pressure
