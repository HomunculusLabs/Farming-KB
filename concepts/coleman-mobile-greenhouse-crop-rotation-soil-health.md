# Coleman Mobile Greenhouse System for Crop Rotation and Soil Health

## Overview

The mobile greenhouse is a central innovation in Eliot Coleman's winter harvest system at Four Season Farm. By mounting standard hoop houses on skids and rails so they can be moved between growing areas, Coleman solves several persistent problems of permanent greenhouse production simultaneously: soil degradation, pest and disease buildup, nutrient excess, and the conflict between summer and winter crop requirements. The mobile greenhouse concept, originally developed in England in 1898, has been adapted by Coleman into an economical, practical system for small-scale commercial farming. (see [[coleman-greenhouse-summer-crops]]).

## Historical Origins

### The First Mobile Greenhouse

According to Coleman's research, the first mobile greenhouse was built in 1898 in England. Despite being a large glasshouse, it could be moved safely because railroad wheels running on steel rails supported its iron framework. The concept was ahead of its time and did not gain widespread adoption, likely because:

- Plastic-covered hoop houses had not yet been invented
- The day-length factor in winter growing was not well understood
- The concept of unheated winter vegetable production seemed implausible to most growers

### Coleman's Adaptation

Coleman revived the mobile greenhouse concept on a far less expensive scale, replacing the iron-and-glass railroad-mounted structure with lightweight pipe-frame hoop houses on wooden skids running along ground-level rails. This adaptation makes the mobile greenhouse accessible to any small-scale grower with basic fabrication skills. (see [[coleman-double-cover-microclimate-protection]]).

## How the Mobile Greenhouse Works

### Mechanical System

The mobile greenhouse at Four Season Farm uses:

- Standard gothic-style hoop houses, typically 30 feet wide by 96 feet long
- The house mounted on wooden skids or runners that slide along ground-level rails
- A system of anchors that secure the house in position when stationary
- Manual or mechanical systems for moving the house between positions (typically moved 2-4 times per year)

### The Annual Cycle

The mobile greenhouse follows a predictable annual rotation at Four Season Farm:

1. **Winter position (October through late spring)**: The greenhouse covers cold-hardy winter crops that were sown outdoors during August and September
2. **Transition (late spring)**: The greenhouse is moved off the winter crop area, exposing the soil to sun, rain, wind, and snow
3. **Summer position (summer through early fall)**: The greenhouse covers heat-loving summer crops (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, eggplants, sweet potatoes) on an adjacent area
4. **Transition (October)**: The greenhouse moves back to cover the next cycle of winter crops

This means each growing area is covered by the greenhouse for roughly one year and uncovered for the following year.

## Advantages Over Permanent Greenhouses

### Avoiding Pest and Disease Buildup

Permanent greenhouses create a continuous, protected environment that allows pest and disease populations to build up year after year. The mobile greenhouse breaks this cycle because:

- Every other year, the growing beds are fully exposed to the elements
- Sun, rain, wind, snow, and freezing temperatures kill or suppress many pests and diseases
- Natural predators have access to the uncovered soil
- The "cleaning" effect of exposure eliminates the need for soil sterilization or fumigation

### Preventing Nutrient Excess

In permanent greenhouses, continuous fertilization without leaching can lead to excessive soil nutrient levels, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus. The mobile greenhouse addresses this because:

- During the uncovered year, rainfall leaches excess nutrients
- Green manure crops can absorb and hold nutrients during the fallow period
- The natural cycles of wetting and drying redistribute nutrients in the soil profile
- Nutrient management can be reset each time the greenhouse returns to a position

### Eliminating the Summer Cooling Problem

Starting winter crops in a permanent greenhouse in August creates a serious cooling challenge—the greenhouse interior can reach temperatures that are lethal to tender seedlings. The mobile greenhouse eliminates this problem entirely:

- Winter crops are sown outdoors in open field conditions during August and September
- They grow in natural late-summer and fall weather with no overheating risk
- The greenhouse moves over the established crops only when cold weather arrives in October
- No shade cloth, ventilation fans, or evaporative cooling systems are needed

### Enabling Green Manure Rotation

Perhaps the most significant long-term benefit is the ability to grow deep-rooting leguminous green manure crops on the uncovered area:

- During the uncovered year, the soil can host a green manure crop for 10-13 months
- Options include clovers, vetch, field peas, alfalfa, and other legumes
- These crops fix nitrogen, improve soil structure with their roots, and add organic matter when turned under
- Green manures should be turned under 3-4 weeks before the planting date of the following crops
- The benefit is especially important during the initial soil-building years of a new operation

## Soil Health Benefits

### The Cleansing Power of Exposure

When greenhouse soil is uncovered, it benefits from natural processes that are impossible under cover:

- **Solar radiation**: UV light from direct sun exposure has a sanitizing effect on the soil surface
- **Rainfall**: Natural precipitation leaches excess salts and redistributes nutrients
- **Freezing and thawing**: Freeze-thaw cycles break up compacted soil and improve aggregation
- **Wind**: Drying winds help prevent surface crusting and reduce fungal disease pressure
- **Snow cover**: Snow insulates soil biology while slowly releasing moisture and nitrogen

### Carbon Building

The alternating covered/uncovered cycle supports carbon sequestration:
- During the covered year, root exudates and crop residues feed soil biology
- During the uncovered year, green manures add significant biomass
- The undisturbed periods between crops allow soil organic matter to accumulate
- Fungal networks that develop during the covered year can continue growing in the uncovered soil

## Economic Considerations

### Cost-Benefit Analysis

The mobile greenhouse requires an upfront investment in rails, skids, and anchoring systems beyond the cost of the hoop house itself. However, the economic benefits are substantial:

- Elimination of soil replacement or sterilization costs
- Reduced pest and disease management expenses
- Lower fertility costs due to green manure nitrogen fixation
- No cooling system needed for fall crop establishment
- The ability to grow both summer and winter crops on the same land area effectively doubles the productive capacity

### Scalability

The system is inherently scalable:
- A single mobile greenhouse on two positions is the simplest version
- Multiple greenhouses can share rails for efficient use of materials
- The length of the rail system determines the number of available positions

## Design Considerations

### Site Requirements

- Level ground with good drainage for both positions
- Adequate space for the rail system plus turning room at the ends
- Wind protection at both positions (or the ability to provide it)
- Reasonably uniform soil between positions for consistent results

### Timing of Moves

The timing of greenhouse moves is critical and depends on:
- Local climate and first frost dates
- Crop maturity and harvest schedules
- Green manure planting and incorporation schedules
- Weather conditions at the time of the move

## Summary

The mobile greenhouse represents one of the most elegant solutions in sustainable agriculture: a simple mechanical innovation that simultaneously solves soil health, pest management, nutrient cycling, and season-extension challenges. By allowing each growing area a full year of exposure to natural elements between covered years, the system harnesses the cleansing and restorative power of nature rather than fighting against it with chemicals and technology. Coleman's adaptation proves that the best solutions are often the simplest ones.
