## Implications for Cultivar Selection and Breeding

The finding that cultivar affects abundance but not presence has important
practical implications for Cannabis agriculture. It means that cultivar
selection cannot introduce entirely new microbial taxa into a growing
environment — the soil determines what is available. However, cultivar
selection can significantly influence which of the available taxa become
dominant community members, potentially favoring beneficial organisms over
neutral or detrimental ones.

This insight suggests that optimal microbiome management in Cannabis
cultivation requires attention to both soil selection and cultivar selection,
as they operate through complementary mechanisms. A grower seeking to promote
specific beneficial microbes should first ensure that those organisms are
present in the soil (step one), and then select a cultivar whose exudate
profile and root architecture favor their proliferation (step two).

For breeding programs, the 71 OTUs that showed significant abundance

selection. If certain abundance profiles are associated with desirable traits
such as disease resistance, nutrient use efficiency, or yield, breeders could
select for cultivars that naturally promote those microbial communities.

## Broader Significance for Plant Microbiome Science

The presence-absence versus abundance distinction documented in Cannabis
mirrors patterns observed in other plant species, though the Cannabis study
provided an unusually clear demonstration of the principle. In maize, for
example, rhizosphere communities also show strong soil-driven composition
effects with comparatively subtle genotype effects on abundance. Similar
patterns have been reported in Arabidopsis, rice, and wheat.

The Cannabis study is particularly valuable because it examined multiple
cultivars across multiple soil types in a controlled experimental framework,
providing robust statistical support for the two-tier model. The zero
unweighted differences between strains is a remarkably clean result that
leaves little ambiguity about the mechanism: Cannabis strains tune microbial
abundance but do not determine microbial presence.

This finding also has implications for microbiome engineering approaches.
Inoculant products that introduce novel microbial taxa may face barriers in
Cannabis systems because the plant does not directly control which taxa enter
the community — that is determined by the soil environment. Inoculants may be
more effective when applied to soil in advance of planting, allowing the
target organisms to establish in the soil reservoir from which the root
microbiome is subsequently recruited.
