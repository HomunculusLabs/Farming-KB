## Water Storage in Scarp and Wadi Landscapes

The scarp and [[water-management]] begins on the top erosion surface (the peneplain). Stone and cement dams can be erected across the generally gentle valleys of the upper plateau to hold clean water. These dams should be fitted with plugs or base pipes to release flood water more gently after the initial surge.

Additional strategies on the upper surface include:

- **Gutters** cut across the scarp top above useful caves to prevent excess water from splashing over cliff-face settlement sites.
- **Scour-hole rehabilitation**: Silt can be cleared from, and holes deepened and sealed to create tank storages. Some of these can lie inside dam walls as deep spots.
- **Side-valley diversion**: Gutters can be cut to spill water to side valleys, lessening the torrent in any one wadi channel.
- **Concrete or stone-cut chutes** leading to excavated pools at the cliff base moderate the erosive effect of overflow.

All wells, pools, and dams gather silt and require periodic cleaning. Dams can be cleaned by leaving base pipes open during alternative flows. Cliff pools are self-cleaning by turbulence.

Tree growth on the upper surface is limited to the very hardiest species, except in crevices or deliberately constructed rock-walled containers. The real productive zone lies below, on the wadi floor.

## Wadi Floor Development

The wadi floor contains deep sands, gravels, and silts that spill out for several kilometres onto the lower erosion surface. The primary strategy here is to erect strong rock walls across the floor and let silt fields build up behind them. For minor wadis, the entire width may be dammed with spillways cut in the pediment rock. In wide wadis, a central channel is left open and fields built to the sides, fed by side valleys.

Once the flood spreads out on the lower plain, broad walls of earth and stone can be formed to contain and soak water into fields. These banks are best stabilised with unpalatable shrubs.

### The Nabatean Model

The most extensively studied wadi development is the ancient Nabatean system of the Negev desert, documented by Michael Evenari and D. Koller (1956, "Ancient Masters of the Desert," *Scientific American* 194(4): 39–45). Key features include:

- Retention of 80–90% of runoff behind dykes in the wadis.
- Spreading of water via highly coordinated flood and soak regimes to orchards.
- A ratio of approximately 20 hectares of runoff catchment for every 1 hectare of irrigated land.
- Close attention required from the moment rain begins until fields are soaked.

The ideal is to let a minimum of water escape as runoff, absorbing as much fresh rainwater as possible in fields or silt beds. The target is to infiltrate about 0.5–1 metre of water into each field per rain event, which is sufficient to grow a crop of grains or to maintain palms and fruit trees. Upper reservoirs can be released soon after torrents cease to top up the system.

## Settlement Design

Settlements in scarp and wadi landscapes benefit from several natural advantages:

- **Cliff-base caves** provide shade, flood protection, and thermal mass for housing.
- **Windmills** over wells or bores near the scarp can pump fresh water to tanks or rock cisterns on the upper peneplain, from which it runs by gravity to houses at the cliff base.
- **Box canyons** provide natural fencing — bounded by steep cliffs with often only a single entry, they protect both human settlements and animal compounds from feral animals and predators.
- **Peneplain crusts** are usually formed over softer rock, making channel and gutter construction fairly simple.
- **Plentiful rock supply** on the pediment slope provides material for field walls.

## Minimal Settlement Without Major Works

Even without extensive water infrastructure, a few people can establish productive tree crops in a wadi by:

1. Seeking shaded sites and deep natural silts.
2. Erecting a windmill over a well or bore close to the scarp.
3. Pumping fresh water to tanks or rock cisterns on the upper peneplain.
4. Using gravity flow to supply cliff-base houses (shaded, flood-safe, and partially excavated into the rock).

With modern machinery, fencing, windmills, and solar panels or photovoltaics, many wadi systems now merely grazed or neglected could be creatively occupied on a modest scale and planted with desert forestry.

## Vegetation and Overgrazing

Large trees already grow in most wadis, naturally shaded and protected by cliffs and immune from sand blast. Restricting hoofed animals would allow many more trees to establish. Overgrazing by camels and goats is identified as the most obvious plague of arid lands. Where grazing animals have been removed or controlled, such as parts of South Australia, dense Callitris pine forests have regenerated, and palm forests can be established.

The wadi system offers many special niches for utilisation, and a rich flora and fauna exists in most deserts that can be preserved and encouraged. Simply by increasing shade, crevices, and ramp-access water cisterns, populations of desert quail and pigeon can be stabilised and supported.
