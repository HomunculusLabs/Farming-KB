# Mycorrhizal Carbon Cycling — Ericoid and Ectomycorrhizal Carbon Sequestration

## Overview

Mycorrhizal fungi are among the most significant yet overlooked components of the terrestrial carbon (C) cycle. Over 80% of plant species invest substantial amounts of their below-ground carbon flow into mycorrhizal fungal symbionts (Smith & Read, 1997; Leake et al., 2004). At the global scale, the annual carbon flux through soil respiration is ten times greater than fossil fuel combustion and recycles approximately 10% of atmospheric CO₂ (Raich et al., 2002). Roots and their associated mycorrhizas constitute the single most important component of this flux.

Understanding the mycorrhizal contribution to the carbon cycle is of paramount importance because this component is likely among the most sensitive to ongoing anthropogenic disturbance of both carbon and nitrogen biogeochemical cycles (Staddon et al., 2002; Nilsson & Wallander, 2003). The mycorrhizal symbiosis is characterized by a reciprocal exchange of photosynthetically-fixed plant carbon in return for nitrogen or phosphorus — the main plant-growth-limiting nutrients. Studies of mycorrhizal functioning have traditionally focused on their roles in providing nutrients to plants, but their importance as a significant component of the terrestrial carbon cycle has generally been overlooked.

## Three Major Mycorrhizal Types and Their Biome Distribution

The major biomes are dominated by plants with one of three kinds of mycorrhiza, each adapted to particular vegetation and soil characteristics of the bioclimatic regions in which it is most important (Read et al., 2004). Central to understanding the contributions of mycorrhizas to the plant–soil–atmosphere continuum of the carbon cycle is appreciation of the properties and functions of these three major types and the ecosystems in which they are of greatest importance.

### 1. Ericoid Mycorrhizas (ERM)
Dominant in heathlands and northern tundra, associated with ericaceous plants such as *Cassiope*, *Vaccinium*, *Erica*, *Calluna*, and *Rhododendron*. These regions are characterized by nutrient-poor, acidic soils with slow decomposition rates. The ericaceous hair roots are extremely fine and contain dense fungal coils within cortical cells, with the fungal structures typically occupying 10%–80% of root volume.

### 2. Ectomycorrhizas (EM)
Dominant in the boreal coniferous forest (taiga) — the world's largest vegetation system, stretching as a continuous circumpolar belt 1,000–2,000 km wide around the Northern Hemisphere. Key tree genera include *Pinus*, *Picea*, *Fagus*, *Quercus*, *Betula*, *Larix*, and *Salix*. The tundra grades into taiga, where ericaceous plants remain major but sub-dominant components under coniferous cover. Some very hardy EM species exist in the deciduous genera *Larix*, *Betula*, and *Salix*.

### 3. Arbuscular Mycorrhizas (AM)
Most important throughout temperate grasslands, subtropical and tropical savannas, and rainforests, associated with herbs, grasses, and many tropical tree species. The temperate deciduous forest includes both EM and AM trees, with an understorey increasingly dominated by AM herbs and grasses to the south. Some locally dominant tree species [[mycorrhizal-fungi-soil-carbon-sequestration-by-type]] are the heathland and boreal forest regions, which together cover approximately 70% of the vegetated land mass of the Northern Hemisphere and contain the majority of the world's soil carbon stocks, mainly stored in peat and raw humus. Soil organic carbon in these regions ranges from 12 to over 120 kg m⁻².

## Carbon Quality and Persistence in Boreal Soils

It is not only the quantity of carbon passing into and through ectomycorrhizas that is important for the global carbon cycle; it is the quality and persistence of carbon inputs that determines the extent of sequestration in soil. Some EM fungi, particularly in boreal forests with a summer dry season, secrete copious amounts of nutrient-mobilizing low-molecular-mass organic acids such as oxalic acid and citric acid.
