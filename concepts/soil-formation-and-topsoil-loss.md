## Soil Formation and Topsoil Loss

## Introduction

Soils are extraordinarily complex systems whose structure, permeability, organic content, gaseous components, mineral composition, pH, and water status change from hour to hour with depth, treatment, and micro-elevation. Many soils originate as complex mixtures derived from a variety of rock types, shaped by long and varied geological histories.

Despite extensive soil science, soil analysis services, and the best efforts of land stewards, topsoil is being lost at an ever-increasing rate globally. Australia, for example, has perhaps only 30% of its original soils in fair condition. The rest have been washed or blown away, or are sadly depleted in structure and yield — a pattern true of most countries where extractive agriculture and forestry occur.

## Soil Formation Processes

### Rock Breakdown

Soil formation begins with rock [[decomposition]] driven by several agents:

- **Water**: Combines with common minerals such as felspars in rock, causing them to swell and fracture. Water then hydrates felspars to produce [[kaolin]], clay, and potassium carbonate. The carbonates released make soil water a stronger carbonic acid than rain itself.

- **Atmospheric oxygen**: Dissolves in rain to oxidise iron minerals (pyrites), forming both haematite and sulphuric acid. This acid further dissolves metals, making water an effective rock decomposer.

- **Ice**: The expansion of water as ice, and the grinding power of glaciers, reduce rocks to flour.

- **Biological activity**: Plants wedge open rocks and mineral particles, recreate acids, and transport minerals in their sap to other locations.

### Soil Types by Origin

Soils can be classified by their origin as:

- **Residual soils**: Resting in place over their parent rock.
- **Transported soils**: Moved by water, ice, gravity, or wind from their original location.

The key factors in soil formation are rock type, climate, and topography (landform).

## The Topsoil Crisis

### Rates of Formation vs. Loss

The natural rate of topsoil formation is estimated at approximately **2-4 tonnes per hectare per year** as uncompacted topsoil. However, current agricultural practices remove topsoil at rates of **40-500 tonnes per year** through cropping and soil tillage.

Even the most ideal tillage practices barely keep pace with the most ideal conditions of soil formation. In the worst cases, **2000 years of soil** can be lost in a single erosion season — one sequence of flood or strong wind over cultivated soils.

### Where Soils Are Conserved or Increased

Topsoil is only conserved or increased in the following situations:

1. **Uncut forests**: Mature forest ecosystems maintain and build soil through continuous leaf litter, root networks, and biological activity.
2. **Quiet waters of lakes and ponds**: Sedimentation in still water bodies accumulates organic matter and mineral particles.
3. **Prairies and meadows of permanent plants**: Perennial grassland systems with undisturbed root zones build soil steadily.
4. **Mulched or non-tillage growing systems**: Gardens and farms that minimise soil disturbance maintain soil structure.

These are the core subjects of sustainable societies — yet they are not the subjects most taught in agricultural colleges or forestry courses, nor do they occupy the minds of politicians, investment bankers, or media figures.

## Value Systems in Soil Management

### Health (Human and Plant)

A conservative and careful approach is essential, especially regarding biocides and high levels of artificial fertilisers. Previous generations lived to ripe old ages on home-grown produce without herbicides, pesticides, or artificial fertilisers. The goal should be to improve upon, not diminish, that factor of long-tested vitality integral to good gardening.

Additional health benefits of gardening include:
- **Mild regular exercise**: Gardening functions as a steady, non-stressing form of physical activity.
- **Meditation**: The practice of observation and quiet contemplation reduces stress.
- **Meaningful work**: Food gardening represents "right livelihood" — work of direct value to others.
- **Lifelong learning**: Every day and season brings change, new observations, and constant learning opportunities.

### Yield vs. Sustainability

A critical impasse exists in modern agriculture: there is no doubt that the once-off yield of a ploughed and fertilised monoculture, supported by chemicals and large energy inputs, can out-yield almost every other production system. But the critical questions are:

- At what public cost?
- For how long can it be maintained?
- With what improvement in nutrition?
- With what guarantee of sustainability?
- With what effect on world hunger, soils, and health?

Abundant proof now exists that forced yields are temporary, and that plough cultures destroy both soils and societies. Modern agricultural systems carry the seeds of our own destruction, receiving public financial support while destroying the countryside through forest clearing, hedgerow removal, species loss, long-term soil degradation, and poisoning.

### Ethics of Soil Use

It is not the purpose of people on earth to reduce all soils to perfectly balanced market gardens. The focus should be on the approximately 4% of the earth needed for food production, with wider implications for the 60% of agricultural soils already ruined by the plough or polluted by emissions.

The largest job is the **restoration of soils and forests** for the health of the earth itself — not clearing or ruining more land, but putting in order what has been destroyed while attending to the modest area needed for survival and full nutrition.

[[john-jeavons]] estimated, based on garden productivity, that perhaps 94% of land could be returned to its own purposes. While these targets may not be achieved in the near term, the process of restoring forests and wilderness can begin now.
