## Condensation Strategies for Water Conservation

Where no piped water is available and water is in seriously short supply, trees and gardens need **condensation strategies**. The aim is to condense water either from night air, from transpired water, or from weeds and trimmings, and return it to root level for reuse.

## Shields of Plastic, Screen Material, or Metal

Small trees planted with a **3 or 4-stake frame** over which a bottomless and topless bag is pulled, and around which loose weeds are placed, live in a protected environment where soil moisture condenses at night and runs down the inner bag surface to the roots. Alternatively, **coastal ridge areas** can condense water from night air using fine metal screens or fences placed crosswind.

Shields provide multiple functions simultaneously:

- **Wind reduction** — cutting down desiccating winds
- **Mulch retention** — holding organic material in place
- **Animal protection** — shielding plants from small animals
- **Water condensation** — capturing night moisture

Personal field experience suggests a difference of **80-90% in survival** using shields compared to unprotected plants. Growth (and light availability) may double in tall clear plastic bag tubes around small trees.

## Organic Mulches

For very small areas or restricted tree sites, a variety of organic mulches provide great help. In extreme conditions, these need to be **45 cm or deeper** to be effective, but in a drought summer they may mean the difference between survival and death of plants.

### Deep Mulch Boxes

Special deep mulch boxes are excellent in forests or gardens for root crops such as yams and potatoes. Multiple compound influences aid plant growth simultaneously:

- **Weed suppression** — preventing competition
- **Water condensation or release** — moderating moisture availability
- **Wind protection of soil** — reducing evaporation
- **[[decomposition]] products** — adding nutrients over time

### Integration with Other Systems

Combinations with plastic sheets, pits, and drip-line systems make mulches even more effective in home gardens. Small waterholes benefit from being thatched over, and many padi crops (taro, rice) can be grown in mulch with much less water use than open ponds.

## Pit Evaporation Systems

These function as **survival strategies in deserts** but can be adapted to grow useful plants. They work by using less useful or cropped plants to give up water, or by using salted water to provide fresh drip to plants.

The basic principle: green leafy material placed in a pit gives off water which condenses at night on the underside of a plastic cone, perforated at the centre to allow rain through. By adding fresh leaves, dry water, or urine, a nearby hardy tree can be sparsely watered, or enough freshwater obtained for a drink.

## Closed Recycling Systems

When it is feasible or economic to **totally enclose plants**—as in glasshouse and plastic tunnel systems—some water losses occur due to ventilation, but little else. Condensation on inside surfaces leads back to plants, or salt water flows can be evaporated to air before cooling the vapour to potable water fed to plants.

### Key Principles

1. **Enclosure reduces evaporation** — sealed environments retain far more moisture than open fields
2. **Condensation recovery** — moisture that does evaporate condenses on surfaces and returns to the growing medium
3. **Saline water processing** — even salt water can be evaporated and condensed into fresh water for irrigation
4. **Scalability** — from small pit systems to large glasshouse complexes

## Integration Strategy

The most effective approach combines multiple condensation strategies simultaneously. A well-designed desert garden might use:

- **Plastic shields** around individual trees for condensation and wind protection
- **Stone mulches** between plantings for thermal mass and moisture retention
- **Organic mulches** at depth (45+ cm) for long-term moisture availability
- **Sheet plastic** as sub-surface barriers to prevent lateral water loss
- **Pit systems** for emergency water recovery
- **Closed recycling** where resources allow for maximum conservation

Each strategy reinforces the others, creating a resilient system that can maintain plant life even under extreme arid conditions.
