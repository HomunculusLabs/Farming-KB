# Psilocybin Mushroom Poisoning and Treatment

While psilocybin-containing mushrooms are among the least toxic of all psychoactive substances, poisoning incidents do occur — primarily from psychological distress rather than physical toxicity. This page covers the clinical presentation of psilocybin intoxication, historical accidental poisonings, medical treatment protocols, and the phenomenon of flashbacks. See [[psilocybin-toxicity-and-safety-profile]] for detailed pharmacological safety data.

## Historical Accidental Ingestions

Ancient evidence of cerebral mycetisms induced by accidental ingestion of hallucinogenic mushrooms has been documented worldwide. Early reports come from China in the 3rd century AD, Japan in the eleventh century, Great Britain in 1799 and the early 1800s, the United States around the early 1900s, and France in the early 1960s.

In Japan, over 366 accidental ingestions of psilocybin mushrooms were reported in 1929 alone, involving people foraging for wild edible mushrooms. In Africa during the 1940s, numerous unintentional intoxications occurred when mind-altering mushrooms were inadvertently sold as food by children in public markets.

## Species Responsible for Historical Intoxications

Outside of a few intoxications caused by [[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]] in Africa and one caused by [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] in England in the late 1700s, the majority of all pre-recreational intoxications were caused by various species of Panaeolus, with the exceptions of Japan and the northeastern United States where some inebriations resulted from ingesting Gymnopilus species.

Subjective effects of [[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]] intoxication were typically described as: drowsiness, lightheadedness, an inability to walk, a staggering gait, giggling, much hilarity, inappropriate speech, uncontrollable laughter, euphoria, and acting as if on a bender. [[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]] produced more tranquil and less hallucinogenic effects compared to [[psilocybe-genus-overview]] species.

## The Real Danger: Misidentification

The greatest danger from mushroom foraging lies not in consuming hallucinogenic species but in accidentally picking toxic lookalikes. Deadly poisonous Galerina species and Conocybe filaris, which are extremely poisonous, commonly share habitats with psilocybe cyanescens detailed, [[psilocybe-baeocystis-profile]], and [[psilocybe-stuntzii-profile]] in mulched gardens.

Chlorophyllum molybdites ("green gills" or "Morgan's Lepiota"), considered toxic but not deadly, is often picked in Florida and mistaken for [[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]]. In Tampa, Florida, over 90% of all mushroom poisonings were attributed to C. molybdites ingestion. This species is also common in Australia and may be mistaken for psilocybin mushrooms.

A sixteen-year-old girl from Whidbey Island, Washington died in December 1981 after accidentally eating Galerina autumnalis, having mistaken it for a Psilocybe species. She and her companions did not report the consumption to medical authorities for two days due to fear of prosecution. See [[mushroom-poisoning-and-lookalikes]] and [[psilocybin-mushroom-deadly-lookalikes]] for detailed identification guidance.

## Lethal Dose and Safety Margin

The LD50 of psilocybin in mice has been determined at 280 mg/kg oral ingestion, suggesting a 70 kg person would need to ingest 19.6 grams of pure psilocybin to produce death. However, Dr. Karl L.R. Jansen of the University of Auckland cautioned that calculating human LD50 from rodent studies is invalid due to different metabolic rates and drug disposal mechanisms. See [[psilocybin-toxicity-and-safety-profile]] for comprehensive toxicity data.

## Treatment Protocols

The major dangers associated with psilocybin poisoning are primarily psychological: anxiety or panic states ("bad trips"), depressive or paranoid reactions, mood changes, disorientation, and an inability to distinguish reality from fantasy.

Recommended treatment should always be primarily supportive:

### Supportive Measures

The "talk-down" technique is the preferred method for handling difficult experiences. It involves non-moralizing, comforting personal support from an experienced individual. This is aided by limiting external stimulation such as intense light or loud sounds, letting the person lie down, and perhaps listening to soft music. See [[psychedelic-crisis-response-protocol]] for detailed guidance.

### Medical Interventions

Tranquilizers need only be used in extreme situations and are generally not considered necessary. Diazepam at 0.1 mg/kg in children (up to 10 mg in adults) may be used to control seizures.

According to Dr. Rick Strassman, anti-psychotics have gone out of favor for treating difficult psychedelic experiences. Medicines with anti-cholinergic side effects, such as chlorpromazine, should not be given as psilocybin mushrooms can have marked anti-cholinergic effects of their own.

### Emesis and Gastric Lavage

When psychological manifestations have properly commenced, emptying the stomach via emesis or lavage has no effect on the duration or intensity of the experience. Dr. Jansen maintains that unless a more toxic fungus has been ingested or the patient is a young child, induced emesis is unnecessary, unhelpful, and may worsen the situation if the patient is already agitated.

### Clinical Presentation Categories

Dr. Jansen identified five categories of medical presentation:

1. Adolescents who took the drug with little knowledge of hallucinogens and without sensible supervision. They may self-present or be brought by parents.

2. Those who fall due to impaired balance or muscle weakness and suffer injury.

3. Those experiencing acute anxiety, panic, depression, paranoid reactions, or disorientation.

4. Cases of idiosyncratic physical reactions such as cyanosis.

5. Those with recurring phenomena after effects have passed, including prolonged psychosis.

## Flashbacks

Dr. Hall reported in 1973 that several drug users experienced recurring flashbacks from mushrooms similar to those associated with LSD. However, Dr. Karl L.R. Jansen stated there is no firm evidence that mushroom flashbacks can occur.

In a 1983 study of 318 cases of Psilocybe intoxication in England between 1978-1981, 21 patients experienced "flashback phenomena of some form" for up to four months after ingestion, though some of these resulted from drug synergy and polydrug abuse. Jansen cautioned that with such a controversial phenomenon, it is necessary to specify precisely what form flashbacks take, to distinguish them from psychological stress reactions wrongly attributed to past drug use.

## Expert Assessment

Dr. Gastón Guzmán and colleagues concluded that "field and laboratory studies strongly indicate that psychoactive mushroom use as it normally occurs does not constitute a drug abuse problem or a public health hazard." A California college student survey suggested that "the low frequency and few negative effects of hallucinogenic mushroom use indicate that abuse does not present a social problem, nor is there evidence for predicting the development of a problem."
