## Xylem and Phloem Transport Systems

Plants possess two integrated vascular transport systems that work in parallel to distribute water, mineral nutrients, and organic compounds throughout the organism. The **[[xylem]]** primarily moves water and dissolved ions upward from roots to shoots, while the **[[phloem]]** distributes sugars, proteins, and signaling molecules bidirectionally between sources and sinks. Together, they form a remarkably efficient plumbing and distribution network that sustains all plant metabolic processes.

## The Xylem: Unidirectional Water and Nutrient Transport

### Structure and Function

The xylem is composed largely of two types of dead cells—**tracheids** and **vessel elements**—arranged end-to-end to form continuous tubes. Because these cells are dead, they contain no cytoplasm, no [[plasmalemma]], and no [[plasmodesmata]], offering virtually no resistance to water flow. Living **xylem parenchyma cells** are positioned alongside the dead conducting elements and serve as the interface between the symplastic pathway of living root tissue and the apoplastic space of the xylem.

Xylem parenchyma cell membranes are packed with aquaporins, ion carriers, ion channels, and proton pumps that load water and nutrient ions into the xylem system. The loading process involves moving the nutrient solution out of the symplastic pathway and into the [[apoplastic-pathway]]—since the dead xylem vessels have no symplastic connections.

### Driving Forces

Two forces drive water through the xylem:

**Transpiration pull** is the primary force. Water evaporating from stomata on leaf surfaces creates tension (negative pressure) in the water column. Because of the cohesive properties of water molecules (hydrogen bonding) and their adhesive properties with xylem walls, this tension pulls a continuous column of water upward from the roots. This passive mechanism can move water to the tops of the tallest trees.

**Root pressure** is the secondary force. When transpiration is minimal (stomata closed, low evaporation), solutes accumulate in the xylem, drawing water in by osmosis. This creates positive pressure that pushes the xylem sap upward. However, root pressure can only push a column of sap a few meters—its limit for tall plants. When root pressure is strong enough, it can push sap out of leaf tips, a phenomenon called **guttation**.

### Xylem Sap Composition

Xylem sap is not pure water. It contains dissolved mineral nutrients, and its composition varies with environmental conditions:

- During hot weather with high transpiration rates, ion concentrations tend to be lower due to the higher volume of water moving through the plant.
- In spring, mineral uptake is usually higher than during summer heat.

### Spring Sap Flow: Sugars in the Xylem

A remarkable exception to the "xylem carries only water" rule occurs in spring. Stored starches in roots are converted into transportable sugars, which travel up through the xylem to supply growing buds and apical meristems. This sugary xylem sap is the source of **maple and birch syrups**.

**Birch trees** rely on root pressure created by osmotic movement of water toward the concentrated sugar solution. **Maple trees** use a different mechanism involving freeze-thaw cycles. Gases in the xylem sap (from cellular respiration of sugars) dissolve when temperatures cool at night, lowering pressure and pulling water into the plant. When morning warmth freezes the water in xylem tubes, gases become trapped in the ice. As temperatures rise, the expanding gases create pressure that pushes the sap upward. Once leaves and buds form in later spring, the xylem stops carrying sugars and the phloem assumes the role.

### Hormones in the Xylem

Certain hormones also travel through the xylem, including chemical signals that regulate stomatal closure during drought and growth-related signals. Remarkably, just one or two hormone molecules in a cell can trigger a response—demonstrating the extraordinary sensitivity of plant signaling systems.

### Tree Rings as Environmental Records

As plants grow, the oldest xylem ceases to function and is replaced by new cells at the pericycle. These layers of dead xylem form the rings visible in tree cross-sections. Xylem cell size varies with environmental conditions: large cells form during wet spring periods, while thinner tubes develop as temperatures rise and transpiration increases. This makes tree rings valuable records of historical precipitation and temperature.

## The Phloem: Bidirectional Organic Compound Transport

### Structure of Sieve Tubes and Companion Cells

Phloem **sieve tubes** are specialized conducting cells that lack nuclei, vacuoles, and most plastids, retaining only a limited number of mitochondria. This minimal internal structure reduces obstructions and allows sap to flow more easily. Each sieve tube has a plasmalemma that covers even the **sieve plates**—the perforated structures that connect individual sieve tube elements end-to-end.

Every sieve tube is paired with a **companion cell** produced from the same mother cell during division. The companion cell contains all normal organelles, including a nucleus, and controls the activities of its associated sieve tube. Companion cells have abundant mitochondria to produce energy for both cells. Numerous plasmodesmata connect each sieve tube to its companion cell, serving as the primary conduit for large molecules entering the phloem.

**Sieve plates** function as safety valves: when a plant is damaged, the fine openings become clogged with **P-proteins** (phloem proteins), sealing off the damaged section and preventing sap loss. This clogging only occurs in response to injury.

### Storage Cells Along the Phloem

Storage cells associated with the phloem extend from leaf veins all the way to roots, forming a distributed energy storage network. Phloem flow is **bidirectional**:

- In **spring**, stored starches are mobilized and flow **upward** to support new growth.
- In **autumn**, perennials, trees, and shrubs store carbohydrates **downward** in roots for winter survival.

Phloem longevity varies by species. Some plants replace their phloem annually, while others retain it for multiple years. Palm tree phloem can survive for more than 50 years. When phloem dies, meristematic cells at the root apical meristem produce replacements.

### Phloem Loading

Loading sugars into the phloem requires energy and occurs through two distinct strategies:

**Apoplastic loaders** pump hydrogen ions out of the companion cell membrane into the cell wall space. The resulting electrochemical gradient drives sugar molecules into companion cells through specialized transport proteins. This pathway is widely used in **[[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]]** and by most **herbaceous plants**. Sucrose is the primary sugar transported via this route.

**Symplastic loaders** pass sugars and amino acids into companion cells through plasmodesmata, using specific transport proteins embedded in the companion cell membrane. Most **trees** and **tropical plants** use this pathway, which carries a wider variety of sugars beyond sucrose.

The choice of loading pathway depends on environmental factors, plant species, and sugar type. Both pathways ultimately deliver sugars to companion cells, where energy is used to move them across the plasmalemma into the sieve tube.

### Source-Sink Pressure Flow Mechanism

The phloem operates on a **pressure flow mechanism** driven by source-sink concentration gradients:

1. **Loading at the source**: Sugars are actively transported into sieve tubes at photosynthetic tissues (leaves), creating high solute concentration inside the tube.
2. **Water entry**: Water from the adjacent xylem moves into the sieve tube by osmosis to dilute the concentrated solution, creating **turgor pressure**.
3. **Flow to the sink**: The pressurized sap (approximately 80% sugar) flows through the sieve tubes toward sink tissues where sugar concentrations are lower.
4. **Unloading at the sink**: Active transport removes sugars from the phloem into sink cells. Some sugars move into storage vacuoles via specialized transport proteins.
5. **Water recycling**: The now-dilute water exits the phloem and returns to the xylem via transpiration, completing the cycle.

Sink cells near the phloem typically have plasmodesmata that allow larger sugar and organic molecules to move from cell to cell for further distribution.

### Phloem Sap Composition

Phloem sap is far more complex than xylem sap:

- **Sugars**: Approximately 90% of dissolved solids. Surprisingly, glucose (the direct product of photosynthesis) is often absent or in very low concentrations; instead, more complex sugars dominate. Some species transport sugar-alcohols (e.g., sorbitol) instead of sugars.
- **Amino acids**: Up to 12% of phloem sap solids. Nitrogen-based molecules can constitute up to half of solids in new seedlings.
- **Proteins and messenger RNA**: Modern analytical techniques have revealed mRNA in phloem sap, part of a long-distance signaling system that coordinates responses to virus attacks, sets up defenses, regulates development, and controls nutrient partitioning.
- **Hormones, organic acids, ATP, and potassium ions**: All transported through the phloem.
- **Viruses**: Can also travel through the phloem, exploiting the plant's own transport system.

The pH of phloem sap is typically **alkaline (7.5–8.5)**, in contrast to the acidic xylem and vacuolar saps. Exceptions include perennials in spring, whose phloem sap is acidic.

### Partitioning: Resource Allocation

**Partitioning** is the process by which plants divide resources among competing organs. All parts of the plant compete for sucrose, proteins, and hormones. Plants grow with remarkable precision: root growth is calibrated to support the number of leaves above ground. If leaves are lost, roots must adjust to the reduced demand. Flowers and seeds develop only after sufficient resources accumulate.

Sugar itself is one of the signals controlling partitioning. High sugar concentrations in leaves stimulate root growth (increasing the sink), but excessive sugar accumulation in leaves feeds back to slow or stop photosynthesis, reducing the signal for further root growth.

## Xylem-Phloem Interconnection

The xylem and phloem are functionally linked. The high sugar concentration loaded into phloem sieve tubes draws water from the adjacent xylem into the phloem by osmosis, creating the turgor that drives phloem flow. Conversely, water unloaded from the phloem at sinks returns to the xylem via transpiration. This hydraulic coupling ensures that the two systems operate as an integrated whole.

## Key Takeaways

- The xylem is a unidirectional, largely passive system of dead cells that moves water and dissolved nutrients upward, primarily driven by transpiration pull.
- The phloem is a bidirectional, energy-requiring system of living (though simplified) cells that distributes sugars, proteins, hormones, and signaling molecules between sources and sinks.
- Xylem sap carries mineral nutrients and, in spring, stored sugars (the source of maple and birch syrups).
- Phloem loading uses either apoplastic (temperate, herbaceous plants) or symplastic (trees, tropical plants) pathways.
- The pressure flow mechanism—driven by source loading, osmotic water entry, and sink unloading—is the primary driver of phloem transport.
- Phloem sap contains mRNA and proteins that serve long-distance signaling functions, coordinating defense, development, [[query-how-do-phytohormones-regulate-plant-growth-and-stress-responses]].
- Tree rings, formed by seasonal variation in xylem cell size, serve as historical records of climate conditions.
