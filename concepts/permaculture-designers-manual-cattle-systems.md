# Cattle Systems

**Source:** Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Bill Mollison (1988)
**Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Overview

Cattle serve as large-scale converters of grasses and forbs to meat, milk, and manure in permaculture systems. Mollison emphasises that cattle must be managed carefully within multi-species grazing systems to prevent range degradation, soil compaction, and erosion. The key principle is small, high-value herds on abundant range.

## Range Management Principles

### Browse Equivalents
Range values are expressed as "cow browse days" -- the number of days one cow can forage on a unit area. This varies seasonally depending on plant species balance and enables rough equivalents to other animals:

One European cow browse year = 6 sheep = 18 kangaroo = 266 jackrabbits

### Range Behaviour
- Cattle can be expected to range only 3-7 km from water
- Deer may utilise 92% of a range area, whereas cattle exploit only 52%
- Kangaroo, goats, and small antelope can exist without free surface water except in extreme drought
- Cattle prefer annual grasses; kangaroo eat very few grass species but more selectively

### Water Placement as a Design Tool
For species with clear preferences for fixed-site resources (water), range management consists of providing these critical resources to enable species to utilise the range. Strategic water placement distributes grazing pressure across the landscape.

## Shelter Effects on Cattle

Trees and shelterbelts provide significant benefits for cattle:
- **Increased carcass weight** due to increased food intake in sheltered conditions
- On hot days, cattle graze all day when on shaded pasture, instead of sheltering from the sun
- **Increased crop production** in sheltered areas
- Lack of shelter can reduce weight gains by 16-30%

## Cattle in Orchards

Cattle are the last large animal introduced into mature orchard systems (Section 12.9):
- Years 0-3: Only poultry on range
- Years 3-7: Pigs for windfall fruit control
- Years 7-20: First sheep, then controlled cattle grazing
- 10+ years: Trees sufficiently hardy to withstand browsing

In farm forestry, cattle are let into tree-pasture systems as trees harden (years 3-6), and pastures are cut for hay over this period. Early grazing is supervised, or the area used by "soft" animals like sheep or geese.

## Cattle in Arid and Semi-Arid Systems

### Rotational Management
Highly selected and controlled meat and milk flocks (sheep, goats, cattle, donkeys, camels) thrive in deserts if:
- Herded or penned in 15+ rotated runs
- Each run gets 2-8 years to recover and re-seed
- Successful Australian graziers allow 7-9 years rest per run

### Drought Management
In all arid and semi-arid areas, large livestock such as draft animals and milking cattle are at risk in drought. From 2-4 provisions for drought feeding must be made. Without planning, small farmers may lose basic draft and milk animals, or sell them cheaply for slaughter.

### Species Complementarity
Kangaroo and cattle use desert vegetation in non-competitive and complementary ways. In tree stands, cattle gather in large groups; in open forbes and grasses, they spread in smaller groups; in grassy depressions, they cluster. Almost certainly, the eating of coarse dry grasses by kangaroo enables cattle to browse more easily in wet periods.

## Soil Compaction from Cattle

After only 9 years of stocking on range, soil pore space was reduced:
- 44% in the top two inches of soil
- 60% in the lower 24-inch layer
- Infiltration reduced from 4.84%/hr (slight grazing) to 0.82%/hr (depleted)

Up to five times less water is absorbed by eroded and degraded rangelands. Runoff water from rangelands may carry 6-10% silt by volume. In a flow of 17,500 t water/hour, 500 t were organic matter -- a staggering loss directly due to poor range management.

## Cattle for Blackberry Control

Cattle serve as a practical tool for large-scale blackberry control in steep gullies:
- Feed cattle in winter with untied bales of fodder thrown into blackberry gullies
- Cattle trample and destroy the vines, and "sow" grass
- Gullies need reafforestation later
- Successfully used by Lance Jones of Tasmania over many acres

## Cattle Forage Trees

In tropical and subtropical systems, cattle forage is a primary design consideration. The palm polyculture model (Fig 10.31) includes cattle forage zones. Suitable forage trees include:
- Leucaena leucocephala (high protein forage)
- Acacia spp. (pod and leaf forage)
- Opuntia (cattle forage, especially in arid areas)
- Honey locust (Gleditsia triacanthos) -- pod forage
- Mesquite (Prosopis spp.)
- Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus proliferus)

Tree forage and tall grass can be hand-fed to domestic stock in dry periods.

## Design Principles

1. **Small herds on large range:** Always prefer quality over quantity
2. **Long rest rotations:** 2-9 years between grazing of any paddock
3. **Shade provision:** Plant shelterbelts for 16-30% weight gain improvement
4. **Strategic water:** Place water to distribute grazing across the landscape
5. **Multi-species grazing:** Combine cattle with sheep, goats, and poultry
6. **Soil protection:** Maintain 65-70% vegetative cover to prevent erosion
7. **Drought planning:** Always maintain fodder reserves for dry periods
