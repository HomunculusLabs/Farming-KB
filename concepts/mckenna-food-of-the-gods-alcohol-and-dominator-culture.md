## The Four Stages of Abandoning the Mystery

McKenna outlines a four-stage process by which societies abandoned their
sacred plant relationships:

1. **Substitution with other psychoactive plants** — the original entheogenic
   sacrament is gradually replaced by other active botanicals that are easier
   to control or domesticate.

2. **Substitution with inactive materials** — the original plant is replaced
   by inert substances that function merely as symbols of the lost mystery,
   retaining ritual form but losing ecstatic content.

3. **Complete abandonment of plants** — societies turn to esoteric teachings,
   dogma, and empty rituals that preserve the social architecture of religion
   while severing the connection to direct botanical experience.

4. **Complete forgetting** — even the pretense of remembering is dropped. The
   original sacraments are rediscovered not as sacred, but as "evil and
   threatening," and are suppressed by the very institutions that inherited
   the authority of the older cults.

## The Psychology of Alcohol

At moderate doses, alcohol is libidinally stimulating — the ego feels
empowered, verbal facility increases, social inhibitions dissolve. But
this is followed by a narrowing of awareness, loss of motor control,
and what McKenna describes as infantile regression. The drug does not
expand consciousness; it contracts it, producing a counterfeit sense
of mastery that masks growing debility.

McKenna sees alcoholism as symptomatic of a deeper disequilibrium
between men and women, the individual and society. He quotes the grim
proverb: "Wife beating without alcohol is like a circus without
lions" — a statement that captures the degree to which alcohol has
become entwined with patriarchal violence.

## The Loss of the Natural Soul

Distilled alcohol, for McKenna, lost its "natural soul." Fermented
beverages retained some connection to the earth's vitality — the
yeast, the fruit, the grain, the seasonal cycle of fermentation. But
distilled spirits stripped away this living context, leaving behind
something "raw, untamed, and ultimately set against the human grain."

The shift from mushroom to mead, from wine to brandy, from sacred
communion to compulsive consumption, mirrors the larger arc McKenna
traces throughout *Food of the Gods*: the displacement of partnership
societies by [[partnership-vs-dominator-societies]], of the Great Mother by the jealous
sky-god, of ecstatic participation by hierarchical control. Alcohol
is both symptom and instrument of that displacement.
