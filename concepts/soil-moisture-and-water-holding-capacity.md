# Soil Moisture and Water-Holding Capacity

Understanding how much water your soil can hold and make available to plants is the foundation of intelligent irrigation and drought management. Soil type, depth, and structure together determine your garden's water reserves. A gardener who knows these parameters can make informed decisions about plant spacing, irrigation frequency, and drought resilience.

## The Soil Profile

Every gardener should dig a hole and assess the soil profile before starting a garden. The profile is rarely uniform — it is more like a layer cake.

### Digging the Test Hole

1. Use a soil augur, posthole digger, or neatly excavate a ditch at least 36 inches (90 cm) deep
2. Observe the layers as you dig
3. Ask yourself: Is this soil loose enough, friable enough for roots to penetrate?
4. Are you finding dense, airless layers where almost no roots can exist?

### Typical Layers

- **Topsoil (0-12 inches / 0-30 cm)**: Usually darker in color due to organic matter
- **Subsoil (below topsoil)**: Often lighter in color, different texture
- **Clay layer**: In many climates, you will encounter a layer of clay at some depth
- **Hardpan**: In some soils, an impenetrable layer that is a complete barrier to moisture rising by capillarity — these areas will require considerable irrigation

### Important Finding

If the subsoil is open to root penetration (not compacted or hardpan), roots can access deep moisture reserves. A sandy topsoil with a clayey subsoil that is open to root penetration can make more water available than it might seem when you just consider the top few inches. If the subsoil is a deep, open clay, there will be a huge reserve of moisture.

## Soil Fractional Analysis (The Jar Test)

This simple test determines the proportions of sand, silt, and clay in your soil. The size of soil particles directly determines how much water the soil can hold.

### Procedure

1. **Collect soil**: Take about one pint (500 mL) of soil from each layer you encountered in your test hole
2. **Prepare**: Remove any roots or stones, break it up as finely as possible
3. **Jar**: Put it in a quart (liter) canning jar with a good lid
4. **Mark**: Use a felt pen to mark where the top of the soil comes to on the jar
5. **Fill**: Add water to within one inch (2.5 cm) of the top
6. **Detergent**: Add one teaspoon (5 mL) of dishwashing detergent (low-suds type is best)
7. **Shake**: Seal and shake extremely hard for 5-10 minutes until every particle is separated (this is why the soap — it breaks apart soil aggregates)
8. **Alternative**: Take the jar to a hardware store and put it in their paint shaker for a few minutes

### Reading the Results

- **Immediately after shaking**: Set the jar down in bright light. Note the exact time. Some pure clay will have already settled on the bottom.
- **After 2 minutes**: Sand has settled. Mark the top of the sand layer.
- **After 2 hours**: Silt has settled (finer than sand but still visible particles). Mark the top of the silt layer.
- **After 12+ hours**: Clay settles (extremely fine particles). The first mark you made (original soil level) will be close enough for the clay fraction.

### Calculating Percentages

Divide the height of each layer by the total height of the soil column. For example, if the total is 80mm, sand is 40mm, silt is 24mm, and clay is 16mm:
- Sand: 40/80 = 50%
- Silt: 24/80 = 30%
- Clay: 16/80 = 20%

## Soil Classification

| Type | Definition | Garden Suitability |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Sandy** | Less than 10% silt or clay | Drains fast, holds little moisture, easy to work |
| **Loam** (medium) | 20-35% clay, mix of sand and silt | Ideal — good drainage and moisture retention |
| **Clayey** | More than 35% clay | Can be excellent if ≤50% clay; difficult if >50% |
| **Heavy clay** | More than 50% clay | Very difficult to work but holds enormous moisture |

### Clay Quality Matters

If clay settles within 12 hours, that is a good sign you have a relatively coarse sort of clay. Any soil that contains much fine clay will be harder to work. Coarse clay (like kaolin-type clays) is easier to manage than fine, "gumbo" clays.

## Water-Holding Capacity by Soil Type

The clay and organic matter content of your soil directly determines how much water it can hold for growing plants:

| Soil Type | Water Available Before Plants Wilt |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|
| Pure sand | Very low (~0.5 inch per foot of depth) |
| Sandy loam | Low-moderate |
| Loam | Moderate (~1.5 inches per foot) |
| Clay loam | High (~2 inches per foot) |
| Clay | Very high (~2.5+ inches per foot) |

## Daily Water Loss Rates

The amount of water extracted from soil each day depends on air temperature, humidity, wind, and crop canopy density:

| Temperature Region | Daily Loss |
|-------------------|-----------|
| Cool (W. Washington, Scotland, W. UK) | ~0.05-0.08 inches |
| Moderate (W. Oregon, northern US, E. UK) | ~0.08-0.12 inches |
| Hot humid (US Midwest, E. Australia) | ~0.12-0.18 inches |
| Hot dry (Mediterranean, inland) | ~0.15-0.25 inches |
| Low desert | ~0.25-0.35+ inches |

## Practical Application

### Example Calculation

Suppose you have a loam soil with a water-holding capacity of 1.5 inches per foot, and you want to allow the soil to lose about half its available moisture before re-watering (0.75 inches). If your crop canopy is extracting about 0.1 inches per day during midsummer, you need to water approximately every 7-8 days.

### Sandy vs. Clay Irrigation Strategy

- **Sandy soil**: Water more frequently with smaller amounts (less water per application, more often) because it holds less moisture and drains faster
- **[[clay-soil]]**: Water less frequently with larger amounts (more water per application, less often) because it has far more ability to hold moisture

## Capillarity and Subsoil Moisture

Deep subsoil moisture rising through capillarity acts like money in a bank account earning substantial interest. When plants are widely spaced (Column 3), their root systems continue to discover large amounts of soil water held in storage as they expand deeper. This is why widely-spaced plants can go weeks without rain or irrigation while closely-spaced plants need water daily.

## Managing Moisture Stress

Wise gardeners do everything possible to help their plants avoid moisture stress and especially avoid wilting. Any wilting is a shock to a tender vegetable plant, even if the plant appears restored the next morning.

### Strategies

1. **Wider spacing** gives each root system a greater area of soil to mine for water
2. **Surface cultivation** (dust mulch) preserves subsoil moisture by breaking capillary channels at the surface
3. **Organic matter** in the surface inch of soil prevents crust formation and helps form stable crumbs
4. **Fertigation** delivers water and nutrients directly to the subsoil root zone
