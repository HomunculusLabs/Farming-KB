# Wind Patterns and the Beaufort Scale

Wind is one of the most powerful and least controllable forces affecting [[permaculture]] sites. While designers have limited ability to generate or store wind, understanding its patterns, measuring its force, and designing appropriate shelter systems are critical skills for productive site design — particularly on coastal, island, subtropical, and exposed hill sites.

## Wind Speed Categories and Effects

### Crop and Livestock Impact by Wind Speed

| Wind Speed | Effect |
|-----------|--------|
| 8 km/h | Harmless to crops |
| 24 km/h | Reduced crop production, weight loss in animals |
| 32-40 km/h | Sheer mechanical damage to plants exceeds all other effects |

At severe wind speeds, plants can be uprooted and blown along like tumbleweeds. Trees suffer from a combination of mechanical damage, salt burn near coasts, sandblast in deserts and coastal dunes, and iceblast in cold climates. Wind transport of sand in deserts buries fences, buildings, trees, and crops.

## The Beaufort Scale

The [[beaufort-scale]] is the standard system for reporting and categorising wind force. It combines wind speed measurements with observable effects.

| 5 | 17-21 | 29-38 | 18-24 | Fresh breeze |

### Beaufort Scale — Effects on Land

| Beaufort No. | Effect |
|:---:|--------|
| 0-2 | Slight, no damage to crops or structures |
| 3-4 | Damage to very susceptible species; useful wind energy produced |
| 5-6 | Mechanical damage to crops, some damage to structures |
| 7-12 | Severe structural and crop damage; damage to windmills |

## Global and Regional Wind Patterns

### Trade Winds and Westerlies

Wind patterns follow predictable latitudinal bands:

- **Latitudes 0-35 degrees (both hemispheres):** Bi-modal and seasonal winds. Southeast or northwest in the southern hemisphere; southwest or northeast in the northern hemisphere. Modified locally by landscape, but windward and leeward coast effects are almost universal.

- **Latitudes 35 to occupied coasts:** Westerlies prevail in winter; easterlies occur more sporadically as high or low pressure systems pass and remain stationary to east or west. Cold winds blow from continental interiors in winter; warmer but chilling winds come from the seas.

### Tropical Islands and Peninsulas (0-28 degrees)

Two main wind modes characterise these regions:
- **Winter/trade winds:** Southeast in southern hemisphere, northeast in northern hemisphere
- **Monsoon winds:** Cross-equatorial monsoon winds deflect to blow from the northwest in the southern hemisphere summer, and as southwest monsoons in the northern hemisphere summer

Southeast Asia and Pacific/Indian Ocean islands are most predictably affected by these bi-modal systems. Between the two main wind seasons are two short periods of relative calm or shifting wind systems.

## Wind Harmonics — The Rhythmic Nature of Wind

Wind does not blow steadily. Ground winds vary as gusts and calms in a predictable, locality-specific way with regular pulses.

### Frequency and Amplitude

Research by [[frank-bethwaite]] (New Zealand-born Australian boat designer) established that:

- **Typical mid-latitude gusting frequency:** 49-60 minutes
- **Gust strength:** Approximately 40% stronger than lulls
- **Lull characteristics:** Wind direction shifts to light crosswinds about 15 degrees off the main wind direction

These variations are systematic and regular. Frequencies, durations, and amplitudes can be measured using combinations of stopwatches, anemometers, and wind vanes, or recorded on automatic equipment.

### Visible Wind Patterns

Wind "waves" are made visible in:
- **Grasslands:** Wave fronts passing every 14 seconds, with gusts at longer intervals
- **Water surfaces:** Viewed from cliffs, wave patterns are clearly visible
- **Cloud formations:** "Rank and file" cloud systems reflect gusts, with spaces between ranks showing lulls and differently-aligned light clouds representing direction changes during lulls

### Period Examples by Location

| Location | Gust Period |
|----------|-----------|
| Bahrain | 5.25 minutes |
| Sydney | 6-12 minutes |
| Toronto | 10 minutes |
| Sea waves (general) | 5-12 per minute, lengthening in storms |

### Prevalent Winds vs. Energy Winds

In the westerly wind belts, two distinct wind types can be distinguished:

- **Prevalent winds:** 8-24 km/h, blowing on five out of seven windy days
- **Energy winds:** 16-40 km/h, blowing on the other two days, from 15-20 degrees off the direction of the prevalent winds

## Windborne Pollutants

Beyond mechanical damage, wind carries minute sulphur and nitrogen particles from industrial sources. Dry sulphur deposited on leaf and soil converts to acid in misty rains. In regions downwind of heavy industry (northeast coast of America and Canada, near acid production factories), acid rain can:
- Burn gardens and forests
- Make holes in garments and tents within days
- Pit and hole paints and roofing materials

Nuclear contamination — plutonium and other radioactive particles — can be added to the wind factor anywhere downwind of nuclear waste stores, test sites, or accident locations (Colorado, Virginia, Utah, the Urals, and elsewhere).

## The Chill Factor

The **chill factor** — removal of heat from surfaces and evaporation of fluids — is the mechanism that creates cool to cold climates in the tropics at lower altitudes than adiabatic or altitude factors alone would predict. The chill factor:
- Retards plant growth
- Lowers efficiency of solar devices and insulation
- Creates wind chill that removes up to 60% of heat from glazed areas and flat-plate collectors alone

## Transpiration and Wind

Flow of air over leaf surfaces promotes rapid transpiration, compounding the effect of high light intensity. When both wind and intense light occur together, shrubs and trees may lose water faster than they can replace it. Trees guard against this through:
- Presenting whitish leaf undersides to reflect light while wind blows
- Altering leaf angles to reflect light, trap air, or reduce exposed leaf area
- Closing leaf pores (stomata) to prevent moisture loss

Both pigmentation and leaf movement serve as dynamic balancing mechanisms between variable incoming energy and moisture conservation.

## Design Implications

### Reading Wind History on Site

For landscape designers, **wind-flagging** of older trees and wind-pruning patterns tell the story of cumulative wind effects over time. The site itself has summed total wind effects — reading these patterns is more reliable than short-term wind records.

### Shelter Strategy Selection

- **Predictable wind patterns:** Plan directional, patterned windbreaks of earthbank and trees oriented to the prevailing wind direction
- **Unpredictable multi-directional winds:** Impose a close rectangular or network pattern of windbreaks (common in some deserts)

Regardless of windbreak arrangement, buildings, gardens, and animal shelters should be positioned to face the sun and benefit from solar impact.

### Cyclonic and Hurricane Areas

In cyclonic or hurricane-prone regions, catastrophic winds become the **overriding design modification**, around which all other factors must be arranged. Standard windbreak strategies are insufficient; structural engineering and evacuation planning become primary concerns.
