# Permaculture Landscape Energy Storage: Water, Soil, Trees, and Seed

This page covers how nature catches and stores energy in landscapes, and how [[permaculture]] applies these principles to rebuild natural capital in four key storages: water, living soil, trees, and seed. Understanding these processes is fundamental to creating sustainable human support systems.

## How Nature Catches and Stores Energy

### The Terrestrial Evolution

For billions of years life was confined to shallow seas. About half a billion years ago, marine life colonized the land, creating [[soil-life]] capable of taming barren wasteland. Terrestrial ecosystems evolved to maximize the power of nature to use both climatic and geophysical energies.

**Photosynthesis:** Carbon Dioxide + Water + Sunlight → Carbohydrates + Oxygen

**Respiration:** Carbohydrates + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide + Water + Metabolic Energy

### Two Energy Sources

1. **Solar energy:** Visible light for photosynthesis, plus driving weather and climate (rain, wind, lightning, fire). Rainfall is embodied solar energy — solar heat evaporates water creating atmospheric moisture.
2. **Geothermal/nuclear energy:** Earth's internal nuclear power drives tectonic plates, building mountains, delivering rock minerals, creating fossil fuels and ore bodies.

### Stability vs. Productivity

Long periods of climatic and geophysical stability actually result in a rundown of available energy. Geologically young regions with recent mountain building tend to be much more biologically productive (e.g., New Zealand vs. Australia).

### The Inexorable Forces

All terrestrial ecosystems can be seen as design systems to overcome or limit:
- Gravity (leaching nutrients and water downward)
- Atmospheric oxidation (breaking down organic matter and locking up minerals)
- Fire (periodic destruction of stored carbon)

## Water Storage in Landscapes

### The Water Hierarchy

Water storages progress from ephemeral to permanent as water moves down under gravity:

1. **Vegetation canopies** — substantial quantities in tissues and humid air
2. **Forest litter and mulch** — open sponge absorbing and holding water
3. **Topsoil with [[humus]]** — stable moisture supply for plants
4. **Deep subsoils** (especially clay) — very stable moisture, harder to access
5. **Springs and soaks** — slow release of infiltrated water
6. **Streams and rivers** — base flows between run-off events
7. **Groundwater** — deep storage beyond plant recycling

### Wetlands as Kidneys

Swamps and wetlands are the kidneys of catchment landscapes — filtering and purifying water. They ameliorate destructive flood forces and capture energy as new alluvial soils from slow-moving floodwater.

### Three River Management Models (Tane 1996)

1. **Plumbing drainage model** — engineering approach, drain water away fast (discredited as destructive)
2. **Hydrographic model** — natural sinuous floodplains slow and divert flow optimally
3. **Ecographic model** — rivers and floodplains as highly productive ecosystems constantly renewing themselves

## Nutrient Storage in Landscapes

### The Challenge of Nutrient Leaching

Mineral nutrients are largely invisible but control productivity. Plants absorb nutrients in water-soluble forms, but solubility causes leaching beyond plant reach. Soil ecosystems evolved to store nutrients in non-soluble but available forms.

### Australia's Nutrient Challenge

Much of Australia's exquisite biodiversity results from adaptations to low and unbalanced mineral nutrients. Humans require high, balanced mineral fertility in food. Without it, civilizations crumble.

### Humus: Nature's Greatest Invention

Humus increases soil capacity to store mineral nutrients, water, and carbon simultaneously. Venerated in organic, biodynamic, and permaculture movements. In wet tropics, natural systems rely more on living plants for nutrient storage; [[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]], soil is the primary storage.

## Carbon Storage in Landscapes

### Trees as Carbon Stores

Trees are especially significant because they keep accumulating carbon as wood for hundreds to thousands of years. This long-lived woody biomass storage is one of the best measures of ecosystem ability to resist seasonal variability and disturbance.

### Carbon Beyond Greenhouse

For humankind, carbon storage is much more than greenhouse gas disposal:
- **Food crops** — body fuel, "the staff of life"
- **Fodder plants** — sustain grazing animals for food, fiber, horsepower
- **Fiber plants** — fabrics, paper, ropes
- **Timber** — every imaginable use
- **Fuel** — renewable energy for cooking, heating, smelting

### Soil Humus as Carbon Storage

Plant materials rotting into soil fuel microorganisms that cycle nutrients. Some carbon converts to stable [[teaming-with-microbes-humic-and-fulvic-acids]] lasting hundreds to thousands of years. Wes Jackson called loss of prairie soil humus "the mining of young coal."

We have burnt nearly half the world's fossil carbon AND over half the soil carbon in farmlands — invisible burning caused by ploughing and artificial fertilizers.

## Tree and Biomass Energy Storage

- **Woody perennials** are the most effective long-term carbon and energy stores in temperate climates
- A mature tree stores 2-6 tons of carbon; food-producing trees combine storage with yield
- Agroforestry systems store 30-50% more carbon than monoculture cropland
- Seed saving preserves genetic information — the **biological energy** captured through millennia of selection
- Key species for energy-dense yields: chestnut, oak (acorn), hazelnut, walnut — all store well

## Practical Design Strategies

- Swales and keyline plowing capture rainfall and direct it into soil storage along contour lines
- Mulch layers reduce evaporation by 25-50%, keeping soil moisture available for plant uptake
- [[cover-crops]] store nutrients in living biomass — **green manure** cycles nitrogen, carbon, and minerals
- Root crops (potato, sweet potato, cassava) store solar energy as starch accessible year-round
- Design principle: **every element should serve multiple energy storage functions** (e.g., a pond stores water, grows fish, reflects light, moderates temperature)

## Soil as Battery

- Healthy soil stores **3-8% organic matter** by weight — each 1% increase holds ~20,000 gallons of water per acre
- Mycorrhizal networks extend root access 100-1000x, increasing nutrient and water uptake efficiency
- Biochar amendment creates persistent carbon storage (500+ year half-life) while improving [[cation-exchange-capacity]]

## Key Design Principles

- Pattern understanding: match energy storage strategy to local climate, topography, and available materials
- Scale matters: small-scale water storage (ponds, tanks) is more manageable than large dams
