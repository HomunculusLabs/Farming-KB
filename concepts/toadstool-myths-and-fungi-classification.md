## Toadstool Myths and Fungi Classification

## Mushroom vs. Toadstool: There Is No Difference

One of Palmer's most fundamental points was the popular conflation of "mushroom" and "toadstool" as distinct categories. He recounts an incident on Boston Common where he was detaching a large fungus from the famous Liberty Tree. A bystander warned him it was not a mushroom but a toadstool. Palmer's response, which he urged readers to remember: "Every mushroom is a toadstool, and every toadstool is a mushroom."

The analogy was clear: calling one fungus a "mushroom" and all others "toadstools" was as imprecise as calling a beet a "vegetable" and every other garden plant merely a "plant." Both terms refer to the same group of organisms — the fleshy, macroscopic fungi. The distinction implied by common language (mushroom = edible, toadstool = poisonous) has no scientific basis.

## The Difficulty of Amateur Mycology in 1870s America

Palmer painted a vivid picture of the obstacles facing the amateur mycologist. Excepting the writings of Dr. Curtis of South Carolina, Palmer had "yet to meet with an original contribution to American literature on this obscure topic." Information was primarily derived from British authorities whose works described European, not American, species.

Even among British works, "discrepancies and obscurities will confront the student." Descriptions were imprecise, classifications were not harmonious, and generic names varied between leading authorities from the time of Sowerby onward. Most critically, "when you have placed a specimen in the proper family, you have not in any way defined its quality, since one mushroom may be esculent, and its twin brother poisonous."

Palmer emphasized that a unique skill was essential: "No one should attempt to gather mushrooms who has not the power to fix in his mind the exact characteristics of any specimen, so as not to be in the least danger of confounding it with its nearest neighbor."

## Debunking Common Identification Myths

Palmer systematically dismantled the identification rules commonly found in newspapers and popular guides. He presented a particularly egregious example from a "widely-circulated weekly" and refuted each claim:

### Myth: Mushrooms should peel like a potato
**Reality**: "Agreed, but nearly all the fungi peel off readily and handsomely." Peeling is not a reliable indicator of edibility.

### Myth: The stem ought to come out like an umbrella-handle
**Reality**: "Whole classes (many of them noxious) have free gills, which is the only reason why the stem leaves the cap so easily." Free gills are found in both [[classification-of-edible-and-poisonous-mushroom-families]] species.

### Myth: Mushrooms found in damp, dark places are poisonous
**Reality**: "Damp, dark places produce the very best mushrooms." Many prized edible species, including the champignon (*[[marasmius-oreades]]*), thrive in such environments.

### Myth: Mushrooms with very thin caps should be avoided
**Reality**: "The edible champignon (*Agaricus oreades*) has a very thin cap." Cap thickness is not correlated with toxicity.

### Myth: Stems growing from the side instead of the middle indicate danger
**Reality**: "The elm-tree mushroom (*[[agaricus-ulmarius]]*) has an eccentric stem" and is perfectly edible.

### Myth: All milky mushrooms are extremely dangerous (except the orange one)
**Reality**: "The brown milk-mushroom (*Lactarius vollemus*) is unquestioned, and several others are doubtless esculent." Many lactarii are edible despite producing latex.

### Myth: Mushrooms with gills of equal length are poisonous
**Reality**: "A whole family (Russulae) have equal gills." The entire [[russula-genus]] features equal-length gills, and many species are edible.

### Myth: Mushrooms that run into a dark, watery liquid are dangerous
**Reality**: This is a characteristic of certain melzer's-reaction-positive species and is not a toxicity indicator.

### Myth: Mushrooms that taste bitter or burn the mouth are poisonous
**Reality**: "The honey-colored mushroom (*[[agaricus-melleus]]*) is very acrid raw, as are also others, equally harmless when cooked." Acridity in some species is destroyed by heat.

### Myth: Mushrooms with a fetid, sickly smell are always dangerous
**Reality**: While odor can be informative, it is not a definitive toxicity indicator across all species.

### Myth: Mushrooms that turn various colors when bruised are dangerous
**Reality**: "Several esculent Agarics turn red when bruised, and many edible Boleti change color." Color change on bruising is common in both [[edible-and-inedible-toadstool-families]] species.

### Myth: Mushrooms marked by the trail of a snail should never be eaten
**Reality**: "I never saw a snail on a mushroom." If referring to slugs, Palmer reported often cutting them out of mushrooms, rejecting only the damaged portion.

### Myth: Salt or silver spoon tests can detect poison
**Reality**: Palmer dismissed these folk tests entirely: "no test of any kind, in form, shape, color, basis of growth, or experimental action, will distinguish healthful from harmful fungi."

## The Art Connoisseurship Analogy

Palmer drew a powerful analogy to art appreciation. How does an expert like Mr. Jarves distinguish a painting by Leonardo da Vinci from one by Guido Reni? The knowledge cannot be fully communicated in words — it requires direct experience and trained perception. Similarly, "from essays on the early painters, you draw conclusions which enable you to distinguish at sight the works of two artists."

In kitchen-lore, children learn to distinguish foods "with its earliest lessons at the mother's apron-string." Palmer argued that [[dna-barcoding-fungal-identification]] could only be advanced through two means: "first, exact [[fukuoka-textdoc-nature-must-not-be-dissected-limits-scientific-analysis]]; second, the circulation of arbitrary, traditional information" — essentially, [[palmer-mushroom-cooking-and-personal-experience]] guided by expert knowledge.

## The Four Grand Divisions of Fungi

Palmer proposed a simplified four-division system for identifying fleshy fungi, designed for the complete beginner:

### Division 1: Gilled Fungi (Agaricini)
The largest family. The undersurface of the cap bears thin plates (gills) set on edge, radiating from a central stem like spokes of a wheel. This division includes both the common meadow mushroom and the deadly *Amanita* species. Because of this range, Palmer regarded the Agaricini as "the least safe for the amateur."

### Division 2: Pore-Bearing Fungi (Boleti and Polypori)
Instead of gills, the undersurface has a spongy substance resembling fine sponge. If the stem is distinct and the tubes are easily separable, it is a *Boletus*. If it grows mostly on wood with an abortive stem, it is a *Polyporus*. Palmer noted that "if the plant is brilliant-colored, red or yellow, or turns blue when bruised, it is best to reject it."

### Division 3: Tooth-Bearing Fungi (Hydnei)
The undersurface bears small points or spines instead of gills or pores. Palmer considered these "also safe fungi for the experiments of the amateur." The spreading hydnum (*[[hydnum-repandum]]*) was the primary example — usually yellow, sometimes reddish, always firm-fleshed, with a deformed or eccentric stem.

### Division 4: Puffballs (Lycoperdaceae)
Globular fungi with enclosed spores. When young, the interior is filled with white, yellowish, purple, or black substance. When old, the contents discharge as dust. Palmer declared puffballs "the safest mushrooms for the beginner." If the skin is smooth and the interior perfectly white, "you may eat it without fear." He believed all white puffballs could be safely used if cooked at once, noting that "there is no poisonous fungus resembling them."
