# Homestead Labor and Volunteers

As a homestead grows beyond what one or two people can manage, labor becomes a critical consideration. Options range from informal volunteer exchanges to formal employees. Each approach has legal, financial, and interpersonal implications. This guide covers WWOOF, Workaway, apprenticeships, hiring, and labor law compliance.

## Labor Needs Assessment

### When Do You Need Help?
- Weekly farm work exceeds 40 hours for available people
- Seasonal peaks (planting, harvest, preservation) create bottlenecks
- You're turning down market days or orders due to lack of production capacity
- You want to scale (see [[homestead-scale-assessment]]) but lack labor bandwidth

### Types of Tasks Suitable for Helpers
- Planting, weeding, harvesting
- Animal feeding and chores
- Market setup and sales
- Food preservation (canning, drying, packing)
- Fence building and repair
- Building and infrastructure projects
- Social media and marketing (skilled volunteers)

## WWOOF (World Wide Opportunities on Organic Farms)

### How It Works
- WWOOF is a cultural exchange: volunteers work on organic farms in exchange for food, housing, and education
- No money changes hands between host and volunteer
- Volunteers (WWOOFers) register on WWOOF platform ($20-40/year)
- Hosts register separately ($20-40/year)
- Volunteers contact hosts and arrange stays (typically 1-2 weeks to 3 months)

### Expectations
- Work: 4-6 hours/day, 5-6 days/week
- Provide: meals, accommodation (private room, shared space, camping), farming education
- Communication: clear expectations about work hours, tasks, house rules, time off
- Orientation: show farm systems, safety procedures, expectations on day one

### Legal Status
- WWOOFers are generally classified as volunteers, not employees
- No wages, no payroll, no workers comp required (in most states)
- Must not displace paid employees
- Some states have specific volunteer labor laws -- check your state
- Liability: your farm liability insurance should cover volunteers (verify with your agent)
- Written agreement recommended: outline expectations, stay duration, terms

### Pros and Cons
- Pros: free labor, cultural exchange, enthusiastic helpers, minimal paperwork
- Cons: variable skill levels, training time investment, housing/feeding costs ($10-15/day per person), short-term (constant training cycle)

## Workaway

### How It Works
- Similar to WWOOF but broader (includes non-farm work: building, childcare, language exchange)
- Volunteers work ~5 hours/day, 5 days/week
- Provide food and accommodation
- Platform fee: hosts $50/year, volunteers $49/year
- Often attracts travelers with diverse skills (carpentry, photography, IT)

### Differences from WWOOF
- Less focus on [[coleman-deep-organic-farming]] specifically
- More diverse volunteer pool (international travelers)
- Some volunteers have specialized skills (web design, construction)
- Less structured than WWOOF

## Apprenticeships

### Informal Apprenticeships
- Longer-term arrangement (3-12 months)
- Deeper learning experience than WWOOF
- Small stipend or housing + food + education
- Structured curriculum or learning plan
- Apprentice takes on increasing responsibility over time

### Formal Apprenticeship Programs
- Organizations like:
  - Rogue Farm Corps ([[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]])
  - Quivira Coalition (New Market Farmer program)
  - MOFGA (Maine Organic Farmers and Gardeners Association) journeyperson program
  - ATTRA (National [[phosphate-accumulators-sustainable-agriculture]] Information Service) directory
- Often include formal education, mentorship, and networking
- Some offer stipends ($500-1,500/month)
- Application process competitive

### Creating Your Own Apprenticeship
- Define learning objectives and skill progression
- Set clear expectations: work hours, responsibilities, time off
- Provide housing, food, and a modest stipend ($300-800/month)
- Commit to teaching: regular check-ins, skill demonstrations, reading lists
- Duration: 6-12 months for meaningful skill transfer
- Written agreement covering all terms

## Hiring Employees

### When to Hire
- Steady, year-round work exceeds available volunteer hours
- Tasks require specific skills or reliability
- You're generating enough revenue to support payroll ($15-25/hour including taxes)
- You want accountability and consistency

### Types of Employees
- Part-time seasonal: harvest help, market sales (April-October)
- Part-time year-round: animal chores, maintenance
- Full-time: farm manager, production manager
- Independent contractors: specific tasks (fencing, equipment repair, bookkeeping)

### Employee vs. Independent Contractor
- Employee: you control what, when, and how they work
- Contractor: they control how to complete the work, provide own tools, set own schedule
- Misclassification penalties are severe (IRS and state agencies)
- When in doubt, classify as employee
- Common contractors on farms: accountants, mechanics, electricians, specific-project workers

### Payroll Setup
- Obtain EIN (Employer Identification Number) from IRS
- Register with state for unemployment insurance and withholding
- Workers compensation insurance (required in most states -- see [[homestead-insurance-guide]])
- Pay frequency: at least monthly; biweekly is standard
- Federal withholding: income tax + Social Security + Medicare (7.65% employer match)
- State withholding: varies by state
- File quarterly payroll tax returns (Form 941)

### Compensation Benchmarks (2024-2026)
- Farm laborer: $12-18/hour
- Experienced farm worker: $16-22/hour
- Farm manager: $40,000-55,000/year
- Market sales: $15-20/hour
- Include benefits discussion: flexibility, [[selling-farm-products-and-business]], housing (if applicable)

## Labor Laws

### Federal Requirements
- Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA): minimum wage, overtime (1.5x after 40 hours/week)
- Small farm exemption: farms with fewer than 500 man-days in any calendar quarter are exempt from FLSA minimum wage and overtime (but not from all labor laws)
- Child labor: workers under 16 restricted from hazardous tasks (operating machinery, working with certain animals, working at heights)
- OSHA: Occupational Safety and Health Administration applies to farms with 11+ employees (smaller farms largely exempt)
- H-2A visa program: for hiring seasonal foreign agricultural workers (complex, expensive, but legal pathway for labor shortage)

### State Requirements
- State minimum wage may exceed federal ($7.25/hour) -- most states are $12-16/hour
- State workers comp requirements vary
- State unemployment insurance: required for most employers
- State-mandated breaks and meal periods
