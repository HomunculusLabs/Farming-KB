# Chicken Systems

Source: Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Bill Mollison, 1988), Sections 2.10, 10.8, 12

Updated: 2026-04-18

The chicken is the archetype of multi-functional elements in permaculture design. As Mollison notes, if we consider the number of possible connections to and from a chicken, we see that these potential connections depend on the information we have about the chicken. In permaculture we always suppose that the chicken is busy making connections itself, about which we could not design. We must simply trust the chicken.

## Design Philosophy

Placed correctly, a chicken experiences no stress not common to all chickens about their daily business. If we place the other elements needed close by, the chicken has less stress than normal. It is the design approach itself that permits components to provide many functions without forcing functions upon that element. The chicken may be busy, but not overworked.

The orchard needs weeding, [[permaculture-designers-manual-pest-management]], some manure, and perhaps clean-up of fallen fruit. The chicken needs food, dust, shelter, water, and security. These two sets of needs can be matched to mutual benefit.

## Chicken Tractor Systems

The chicken tractor is a portable pen system where flocks are moved regularly over garden or orchard areas:

- Flocks are moved every 6 weeks, followed by a vegetable or small fruit crop
- Vines on fences add yield
- Bared areas are limed, raked, and sown to crop
- A full year rotation of chicken tractor systems can cover 12-16 pens on one hectare

At densities of 120-180/ha, chickens clean up windfalls and supply fertiliser to [[permaculture-designers-manual-cover-crops-detailed]]. A flock of this size rotated every 18 months removes all weeds, surface bulbs, weed seeds, and grasses, and can be used as a chicken tractor to clean the ground of nutgrass, Kikuyu, onion weed, Oxalis, or indeed almost every persistent weed.

As pens are cleared, chickens are moved on, the ground limed, and vegetable or orchard crops placed. Before they return, forage crops of buckwheat, wheat, sunflower, millet, black nightshade, cleavers, chard, and mustard can be sown, given 2-3 months to mature. The chickens thus plough and clear for their own forage.

A separate pen of comfrey, arrowroot, chard, and sunroot can be kept as drought forage, or "throw-over-the-fence" crop.

## Population Density

- Chickens are unstressed at up to 800-900/ha, provided these are housed as 20 or so small flocks in sheds about 2 m per side
- More usually, households keep one flock of 20-30 hens as a food supplement
- At 800/ha, chickens forage so well that other livestock cannot find sufficient browse
- At 350-400/ha, sheep and cattle can also use the range
- About 13 ha is plenty for commercial egg production, given a careful range plan

## Range Nutrition

On range, full nutrition can be obtained by providing seed from abundant trees and shrubs:

- Coprosma repens, Caragana, tagasaste, boxthorn (Lycium spp.)
- Pigeon pea, most Solanum species with berries, mulberry of 5-6 varieties
- Normal fruits, Acacias with edible seed, Leucaena, clovers, grasses
- "Weeds" such as cleavers, chicory, comfrey, Oxalis, and dandelion
- Insects and their larvae

Insect foods can be deliberately bred:
- Manure piles for fly larvae (maggots)
- Mulch piles for cockroaches
- Termites bred in sunken pits
- Loose compost piles to breed insects
- Mealworms in bran and root mixtures

Table scraps supplement diets. Large acorns, chestnuts, or starchy seeds can be sprouted or rolled to crack them for chicken food. In hot periods, fed-out grains can be sprouted for 2-3 days; in winter, unsprouted grains and grain legumes are preferred.

## Health and Disease Prevention

Given a free range of such richness, diseases are rare or absent. The only needs are:

- Dry dust baths with a little diatomaceous earth or dried bracken and neem tree leaves for ectoparasite control
- A few wormwoods for intestinal worm control
- Abundant shell grit or calcium (limestone) gravel for eggshells
- Hard silica grit to grind food in the crop
- Adequate predator protection
- Clean water
- Good shade and night shelter

## Breeding

People who breed their own chickens in the field keep a small flock of Wyandottes or Silky Bantams as mothers or broody hens, and provide secure pens for hen and chicks. Most hardy bantams breed their own chicks on range, as do older breeds such as Hamburghs, Australorps, or Dorkings.

By field selection, tender chickens of up to 3.5-4 kg weight can be produced. The contrast between these and the soft, fatty, bitter, diseased products of caged flocks fed on commercial pellet foods must be experienced to be believed.

## Integration with Other Systems

- Chickens in the orchard clean up fallen fruit and deposit manure
- Poultry manure is a nitrogen resource for [[permaculture-designers-manual-mulch-systems]] production
- Chickens provide [[permaculture-designers-manual-pest-management]] by consuming insect larvae
- The house and fence form essential trellis for climbing food plants
- Poultry are compatible with bees, orchards, pastures, wetlands, forests, herbs, and flowers
- Running a 13 ha flock of 3000 birds combines well with chicken tractor systems and orchard fruit production
