# Soil Life and Underground Ecology

The living soil is far more complex and dynamic than is commonly appreciated. Beneath the surface exists an entire ecology of burrowers, decomposers, nutrient cyclers, and root systems whose activities are as essential to landscape health as the more visible processes of photosynthesis and growth above ground. Understanding and working with underground life is fundamental to [[permaculture-design]].

## Burrowing Animals as Soil Builders

Before humans learned to cut open the soil, it was thoroughly dug, aerated, and overturned by multitudes of industrious burrowers. The unploughed meadows of Europe and America are as soft as a great mattress, well aerated due to the moles, gophers, worms, prairie dogs, rodents, and larvae eternally at work below ground, even under snow. Termites, ants, and crustaceans all contribute their part. The results are evident in the good soils and great productivity of unploughed ground that has not been compacted by hooves or machines.

### Termites and Ants

Termites and ants function as the earthworms of deserts and drylands, carrying tons of organic material to underground compost piles. In some of these piles they cultivate fungi to feed their colonies. The upthrown earth from ants or moles forms a specific niche for annuals to seed on and for wind-blown pioneer trees to occupy. If birds are the seed-scatterers of the forest, burrowers are its gardeners.

### Mammalian Burrowers

Underground fungal spores eaten by squirrels or wallabies are activated by digestive enzymes, breaking hibernation to occupy new ground and help new roots of acorns and eucalypts convert soil minerals and liquids to food. Gophers and moles industriously carry roots and bulbs to secret stores and sometimes forget their hoards, so that sunroot, gladioli, daffodils, and hyacinths spring up in unexpected places. Comfrey and sunroot spread in this way despite their lack of viable seed — they depend not on bees but on moles and gophers for their increase. Foxes eat fruits and defecate on gopher mounds, which are the dug-over areas where new trees establish.

Wombats may tunnel, overturn, and even topple many hectares of trees, leaving a richly-manured, open, and fertile bed for new forest succession. Rabbits industriously cultivate thistles, and their tunnels provide shelter for possums, squirrels, bandicoots, snakes, and frogs.

### Earthworms and Crustaceans

Worms and crustaceans, in their damp and sometimes semi-liquid burrows, move up and down like billions of pump plungers. They suck in and expel air — and thus nitrogen — to roots, in effect giving the soil its daily breath. Many creatures mix special mudbrick soils with body secretions, and from swallows to termites, create homes from stabilised soils.

Seeds and spores are buried, excavated, hidden, activated, and forgotten by burrowers, and recycled to life or humus as chance and nature dictate.

## Root Ecology

Roots are not simply passive absorbers of water and nutrients. They have their own complex ecology of penetrations, patterns, schedules, seasonal migrations, and equivalents of deciduous drop or bark decortication. Roots die seasonally, invade and retreat, and leave minute or massive tunnels for animals, fungi, and new roots to follow.

### Root Characteristics

Roots have several key attributes that parallel the above-ground characteristics of plants:

- **Penetration depths**: While agricultural crops exploit from 0.6 to 4 metres below the earth, some trees may penetrate to 50 metres in deep desert sands.
- **Spread patterns**: Roots extend far beyond the drip line of the canopy, creating extensive networks for resource acquisition.
- **Seasonal schedules**: Root growth and activity follow seasonal patterns, with periods of active growth and dormancy.
- **Seasonal migrations**: Roots move toward or away from the surface in response to moisture and temperature.

### Root Space Sharing

Root space sharing is scheduled so that different species access resources at different times. Spring bulbs have fed, flowered, and died before tree roots begin their upward thrust for nutrients and water. Tap-rooted late starters such as thistles and comfrey reach deep for late summer moisture, while a few plants and fungi take advantage of autumn rains for flowering and dispersal.

### Root Interactions

Some species secrete phenols or creosotes to inhibit other plants — bracken, tamarisk, members of the Juglandaceae, and Brassicas employ this competitive strategy. Others encapsulate or surround competitors, as Eucalyptus, willows, and tamarisks do. Some plants trap nematodes and other would-be predators, or poison them out — marigolds, certain fungi, and Crotalaria are examples.

Around the roots of dune trees, calcium and other minerals are deposited as stone-like secretions by root-associated fungi and bacteria. These mineral deposits represent significant long-term nutrient storage.

### Underground Storage Organs

Special storage organs are common underground: ligno-tubers, tubers, bulbs, and rhizomes. These fire-resistant and drought-resistant structures allow plants to survive adverse conditions and resprout when conditions improve. In low latitudes where there is no season of cold death, aerial roots may develop, or strangler figs send down roots from high in the crotches of other trees to the earth, building great buttresses as they gradually strangle their host.

### The Scale of Root Systems

A simple bluegum ([[eucalyptus-globulus]]) can easily embrace 1.5 hectares underground. A forest is so complex and even intergrafted below ground that the canopy seems simple by comparison. Many desert plants lead long and sturdy underground lives while appearing thin, straggly, and ephemeral above ground. Some insects, like swift moths (Hepialidae), spend 7 to 8 years underground as large bardi grubs, with only a few days of nocturnal, foodless life in air — mating and laying eggs before disappearing again to the root sheaths and soil. The grubs open thousands of shafts to the air and cycle tons of nutrient underground.

## Design Implications of Underground Ecology

For [[permaculture]] designers, the diversity of life and roots in soil can be used as effectively as the diversity of crowns and canopies above ground. Several specific applications are noteworthy:

### Slope Stabilisation

Unstable slopes are pegged with the great root "tree nails" of chestnut, pine, oak, and walnut. Even after a hundred years, the steep slopes of Tasmania are only just starting to collapse as the roots of cleared forests rot. These slopes could still be saved by planting pines, Acacias, or chestnuts. Bamboo not only holds landslides but, for light structures, provides an earthquake-proof mattress of roots. The root mats of swamp vegetation save bulldozers from watery graves, and the fibrous web of prairie grasses defeats the wind.

### Companion Planting Underground

It is as valid to plant an Acacia for the considerable by-product of swift moth or ghost moth larvae as it is to plant a mulberry for silkworms. Moles can serve as mole ploughs, gophers as daffodil gardeners. Even on shores and the bottoms of lakes and seas, burrowers work to carry nutrients below to roots and to bring up fresh minerals for decomposition, while assisting the flux of liquids and gases across the surfaces of mud media.

### The Prairie Dog Lesson

The consequences of ignoring underground ecology are starkly illustrated by the experience in the Arizona desert near Chilchinbito. Amused scientists, believing there was no conceivable relationship between prairie dogs and rain, recommended the extermination of all burrowing animals in desert areas planted to rangelands in the 1950s, ostensibly to protect the roots of sparse desert grasses. Today, that area has become a virtual wasteland. Fierce runoff, soil compaction, and lack of fresh seedbed have carried the grasses away entirely.

The opposite approach was taken at the Page ranch, where prairie dog burrows were used as water sinks and runoff was directed to flood down them, germinating stored underground seed. This created a productive dryland pasture. The contrast demonstrates that burrowing animals are not pests to be eliminated but essential components of healthy soil systems.

## Trees as Long-Term Nutrient Pumps

Trees act as long-term, inbuilt nutrient pumps, drawing minerals from deep subsoil layers and depositing them on the soil surface as leaves and bark. Fungi and soil crustaceans then convert this leaf litter into humus, making the nutrients available to shallower-rooted plants. This nutrient-pumping function is one of the strongest arguments for integrating trees into all agricultural and pastoral systems.

## Geology and Life Forms

Many rocks and strata on Earth arise from the actions of living organisms. Whether the nodules of manganese in oceanic depths, deposits of diatomaceous earth, coal, limestone, opals, or amber — all were once products of living organisms. Much of the strata we see, except much-changed granitic and volcanic deposits, were formed from or modified by life. All soils are life-created, as are the corals and coral sands of many oceanic islands.

Life is also busy transporting and overturning the soils, stones, and minerals of the Earth. Miles-long drifts of sea kelp may carry hundreds of tons of volcanic boulders held in their root systems. These streams of life have been followed over 300 kilometres, stranding on granite beaches and throwing boulders onto 9,000-year-old basalt piles, all carried there by kelp. Before flying to Japan and Alaska, millions of petrels annually fill their crops with Tasmanian pebbles, seeds, and charcoal, which will be voided somewhere in the Pacific.

Life moderates every erosion process, every river basin, every cliff and rock fall. It shapes and reshapes Earth in a thousand ways. The great forests that once covered the Americas would have had hydraulic weight exceeding any water catchment we can now afford to build, dispersed over a far greater area. This greatly moderates climate and, with it, geological processes. The removal of these forests has brought the hard bones of the earth to the surface across vast areas — a process that, if continued, will create a moon-landscape of the Earth.
