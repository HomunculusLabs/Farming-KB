## Key Points

1. This principle from David Holmgren's permaculture framework highlights how thoughtful design can create self-sustaining systems.
2. The principle connects directly to broader permaculture ethics: earth care, people care, and fair share.
3. Applying this principle requires careful observation of natural patterns before implementing design changes.
4. Energy cycling and resource conservation are core themes embedded in this design principle.
5. The principle encourages designers to think in terms of systems and relationships rather than isolated elements.
6. Small, slow solutions are preferred over large-scale interventions — this principle embodies that approach.
7. Integration rather than segregation of elements increases system efficiency and resilience.
8. Edge effects and marginal spaces often hold the greatest potential when this principle is properly applied.
9. The principle supports the broader goal of reducing dependence on non-renewable resources and external inputs.
10. Real-world application of this principle has been demonstrated across diverse climates and contexts worldwide.

## Practical Applications

- Conduct a thorough site analysis before design, mapping zones, sectors, soil, water, and existing vegetation.
- Prioritize elements that serve multiple functions — every component should fulfill at least three roles.
- Design for energy efficiency by placing frequently-used elements closest to the zone of daily activity.
- Use biological resources (plants, animals, fungi) instead of mechanical or chemical alternatives wherever possible.
- Plan for succession — design initial plantings that will evolve into mature, self-maintaining systems.
- Create redundancy in critical systems to increase resilience against failure or disruption.
- Incorporate small-scale, slow solutions that can be tested and adjusted before large investments.
- Map and maximize edge effects between different ecosystems or landscape elements.

# Holmgren: Seed Saving, Heirloom Varieties, and Genetic Diversity

updated: 2026-04-18

Holmgren's discussion of genetic diversity and seed saving connects [[permaculture-retrofit-suburbia]].

## Diversity as Systemic Principle

Holmgren argues that diversity in permaculture systems is not simply about having many species present but about the functional relationships between them. [[biointensive-seed-saving]].

## Seed Saving Networks

Holmgren references the work of activist entrepreneurs at the forefront of the seed-saving movement. The Seed Savers Handbook by M. and J. Fanton provides a valuable and practical guide to seed saving. These networks represent an important form of [[holmgren-genetic-engineering-and-evolution]] addresses the risks of genetic engineering within the broader context of permaculture ethics. The enclosure of the genetic commons through patenting of seeds and life forms parallels the historical enclosure of land commons. When corporations can patent seeds, farmers lose the right to save and exchange seed, breaking a cycle of adaptation and improvement that has sustained agriculture for millennia.

The reductionist thinking behind genetic engineering -- the belief that individual genes can be manipulated in isolation from their ecological context -- mirrors the same reductionism that has created so many problems in industrial agriculture. See also holmgren design science discipline for the contrast between reductionist and holistic approaches.

## Biological Capital

[[holmgren-ecosynthesis-and-weedscape-ecology]] explores how so-called weeds often carry valuable genetic material and ecological functions. The distinction between "useful" and "weed" species is often a cultural rather than ecological one. Many plants dismissed as weeds are nitrogen fixers, dynamic nutrient accumulators, or providers of habitat for beneficial insects. See [[beneficial-insects-pollinators]].

## Hybrid Vigour at the System Level

The concept of hybrid vigour applies not just to individual plants but to agricultural systems. The mixing of indigenous and exotic species, of traditional and modern varieties, creates systems with greater adaptive capacity than monocultures of any single type. This mirrors the social hybrid vigour that Holmgren identifies in sustainable communities -- the mixing of cultures and knowledge systems producing outcomes more robust than any pure tradition.
