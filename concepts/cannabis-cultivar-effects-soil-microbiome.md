# Cannabis Cultivar Effects on Soil Microbiome

## Overview

Different Cannabis cultivars (strains) maintain significantly different
microbial communities within their root interiors (endorhiza), even when
grown in identical soil conditions. This cultivar-specificity is a key
prediction of the two-tier [[two-tier-selection-model-plant-microbiome]] and has been demonstrated across
multiple experiments. While soil type remains the dominant factor shaping the
overall available microbial pool, host genotype fine-tunes which subset of
soil-derived microbes colonizes root tissues.

## Evidence from Controlled Experiments

### First Experiment: Three Cultivars, One Soil Type

Three Cannabis cultivars — Burmese (balanced hybrid), Bookoo Kush
(sativa-dominant hybrid), and Sour Diesel (sativa) — were grown in
composted soil with minimal edaphic variation. Key findings:

- **Endorhiza communities clustered significantly by strain** for both
  weighted (ADONIS: R² = 0.59, p = 0.004) and unweighted (R² = 0.39,
  p = 0.003) UniFrac analyses.
- **Rhizosphere and bulk soil did not show significant strain-level
  differences**, confirming that cultivar effects emerge primarily within
  root tissues.
- **Methylophilus was the key differentiator**: This genus comprised 13%
  of the endorhiza community in Bookoo Kush, 0.13% in Burmese, and was
  entirely absent from Sour Diesel (FDR: p = 0.012).

### Second Experiment: Two Cultivars, Two Soil Types

White Widow and Maui Wowie were grown across two soil types with significant
edaphic variation (Vista and Orange County, CA soils). This experiment
tested whether cultivar effects persist even when soil differences are large:

- **Soil type had the strongest effect** on overall [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]]
  (unweighted R² = 0.32; weighted R² = 0.21), confirming tier 1 dominance.
- **Cultivar effects remained significant** for both unweighted (R² = 0.10,
  p = 0.008) and weighted (R² = 0.27, p = 0.001) analyses.
- **Sample type (bulk soil vs rhizosphere vs endorhiza) was also
  significant**, with endorhiza showing the strongest differentiation.

## Strain Effects on OTU Abundance vs Presence

An important finding was that [[blesching-cannabis-strain-selection-receptor-targeting]] showed a larger effect on
OTU abundances than on OTU presence/absence. In statistical terms:

- **Weighted analysis (abundance-sensitive)**: 71 OTUs showed significant
  differences between strains (ANOVA with FDR correction).
- **Unweighted analysis (presence/absence only)**: Zero OTUs showed
  significant strain-level differences.

This means that different cultivars share largely the same species of
endophytes, but the relative abundance of those shared species varies
dramatically by host genotype. The plant is not so much choosing different
partners as it is adjusting the population sizes of a common set.

## Shared OTU Evidence for Soil-Derived Communities

White Widow was grown in two different soils, allowing a direct test of
whether endorhiza communities are soil-derived. Endorhiza samples shared
significantly more OTUs with the soil they were grown in (mean = 2934)
than with the other soil where a different White Widow plant was grown
(mean = 2162, t = -10.05, p = 1.2e-15). This confirms that the local
soil microbiome is the primary source pool for root-colonizing bacteria.

## Cannabinoid-Microbiome Correlations

Plants in the second experiment were tested for cannabinoid content,
including delta-9-THC. Mantel tests revealed significant correlations
between cannabinoid profiles and endorhiza community structure (r-stat:
0.863, p = 0.001). However, THC concentration also varied by soil type,
making it difficult to separate cannabinoid effects from edaphic effects.
This confounding means the causal relationship between cannabinoids and
[[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]] structure remains unresolved.
