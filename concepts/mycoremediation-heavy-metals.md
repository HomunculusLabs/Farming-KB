# Mycoremediation of Heavy Metals

Heavy metals are non-biodegradable, persist indefinitely in the environment, and bioaccumulate through food chains. Fungal biosorption offers a cost-effective alternative to conventional remediation methods (precipitation, ion exchange, membrane filtration). See [[mycoremediation-basics]] for the general framework.

## Biosorption Mechanisms

Unlike organic pollutants, heavy metals cannot be degraded enzymatically. Instead, fungi sequester them through biosorption — binding to cell wall components:

- **Ion exchange** — metal ions replace lighter ions (Ca2+, Mg2+, K+) on cell wall binding sites
- **Complexation** — metals form coordination complexes with nitrogen, oxygen, or sulfur atoms in cell wall polymers
- **Adsorption** — physical binding to cell surface via van der Waals forces
- **Precipitation** — metals precipitate as hydroxides or other insoluble compounds on the fungal surface
- **Intracellular accumulation** — active transport into cells via metallothioneins and phytochelatins

### Key Cell Wall Components for Metal Binding

| Component | Functional Groups | Metals Bound |
|-----------|------------------|--------------|
| Chitin/chitosan | Amino, hydroxyl | Cu2+, Zn2+, Cd2+, Pb2+ |
| Glucans | Hydroxyl, carboxyl | Cr3+, Ni2+, Co2+ |
| Proteins | Amino, carboxyl, sulfhydryl | Hg2+, Ag+, Cu2+ |
| Melanin | Carboxyl, phenolic | Cs-137, Sr-90, U, Th |
| Lipids | Phosphate, carboxyl | Diverse metals |

## Biosorption vs Bioaccumulation

- **Biosorption** uses dead or inactive biomass — faster, cheaper, not affected by toxicity; works via passive binding to cell wall
- **Bioaccumulation** uses living cells — slower but can actively concentrate metals via intracellular uptake; affected by metal toxicity at high concentrations
- **Both** approaches have applications; dead biomass is preferred for industrial wastewater treatment, living fungi for soil remediation

## High-Performance Fungal Species

### Aspergillus niger
- One of the most studied biosorbent fungi
- Removed 91% Cu and 70% Zn from swine effluent (Price et al. 2001)
- Cell wall contains chitin, glucans, and proteins with high metal affinity
- Can be grown on cheap waste substrates

### Rhizopus arrhizus
- Exceptional biosorbent capacity: 200 mg/g for Pb2+, 80 mg/g for Cu2+
- Chitin-chitosan content gives high binding capacity
- Effective in acidic conditions where bacterial systems fail

### Mucor rouxii
- Effective for Cr(VI) removal — reduces toxic Cr(VI) to less toxic Cr(III)
- Biosorption capacity: 45-60 mg/g depending on pH
- ph-dependent binding; optimal at pH 4-6

### Saccharomyces cerevisiae
- Baker's yeast is widely available and inexpensive
- Biosorption capacity: 10-100 mg/g depending on metal and conditions
- Can be immobilized on alginate beads for column reactors

### White-Rot Fungi (Pleurotus, Trametes, Phanerochaete)
- While primarily known for organic pollutant degradation, also effective for metals
- Mycelial mats provide large surface area for biosorption
- Can be used in constructed wetlands and soil applications

### Mycorrhizal Fungi
- Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and ectomycorrhizal fungi protect host plants from metal toxicity
- Glomus intraradices reduces heavy metal uptake by host plants while accumulating metals in fungal tissues
- Ectomycorrhizal Pisolithus tinctorius accumulates Cu, Zn, and Cd
- Suillus luteus shows tolerance to Zn and Cd, enabling plant growth on contaminated soils
- See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for the plant-fungal symbiosis

## Factors Affecting Biosorption

### pH
- Most metals bind optimally at pH 4-6
- At low pH, H+ competes with metal ions for binding sites
- At high pH, metals precipitate as hydroxides (not true biosorption)
- Cr(VI) is an exception — binds better at low pH as an oxyanion

### Temperature
- Biosorption is generally exothermic — slightly better at room temperature
- Higher temperatures can denature cell wall binding sites

### Initial Metal Concentration
- Higher concentrations drive more binding (mass action)
- But at very high concentrations, sites become saturated

### Biomass Concentration
- More biomass = more binding sites, but also more competition between cells
- Optimal ratio depends on metal concentration

### Contact Time
- Most biosorption is rapid — 80-90% within 30 minutes
- Equilibrium typically reached within 2-4 hours

## Radionuclide Biosorption

Fungi can also concentrate radioactive isotopes:

- **Cs-137 and Sr-90** — biosorbed by melanin-containing fungi (Cladosporium, Aureobasidium)
- **Uranium and thorium** — biosorbed by Rhizopus, Penicillium, and Mucor species
- **Melanin** — the dark pigment in many fungal cell walls has high affinity for radionuclides
- Applications: cleanup of nuclear waste sites, Chernobyl/Fukushima remediation

## Immobilized Fungal Biomass

For industrial wastewater treatment, fungal biomass can be immobilized:

- **Alginate beads** — fungal cells entrapped in calcium alginate gel
- **Polyacrylamide** — fungal biomass cross-linked with polyacrylamide
- **Silica gel** — fungal cells immobilized in silica matrix
- **Activated carbon** — fungal biomass combined with activated carbon

Immobilization advantages: reusable across multiple cycles, easy separation from treated water, mechanical stability for column reactors.

## Soil Applications

For in-situ soil remediation:

- **Mycorrhizal inoculation** — introduce metal-tolerant mycorrhizal fungi to protect plants and stabilize metals
- **Mycelial mats** — spread inoculated substrate over contaminated soil; mycelium grows through soil binding metals
- **Composting** — fungal-dominated compost can reduce metal bioavailability through complexation with humic substances
- **Phytostabilization** — combine metal-tolerant plants with mycorrhizal fungi to stabilize contaminated sites

## Advantages of Fungal Biosorption

- Low cost — can use waste fungal biomass from fermentation industries
- High capacity — cell wall polymers have numerous binding sites
- Fast kinetics — most binding occurs within minutes
- Works in extreme conditions — tolerates acidic pH where bacteria fail
- Regenerable — bound metals can be desorbed with acid for metal recovery
- Non-toxic — dead biomass poses no environmental risk

## Limitations

- Metals are not destroyed — only transferred from one medium to another
- Disposal of metal-loaded biomass is required (often treated as hazardous waste)
- Competitive binding between metals in mixed waste streams
- Biomass degrades over time in field conditions
- Performance varies significantly between species and strains
