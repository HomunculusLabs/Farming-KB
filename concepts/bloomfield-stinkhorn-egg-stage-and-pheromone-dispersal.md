# Stinkhorn Egg Stage Development and Insect-Mediated Spore Dispersal

The [[bloomfields-orchard-gasteromycetes-and-stinkhorn-fungi]] represent one of the most extraordinary reproductive
strategies in the [[bloomfield-stramenopila-oomycete-fungal-kingdom-distinction]]. Nicholas P. Money opens *Mr. Bloomfield's
Orchard* with a vivid account of a field expedition to Dartmoor in Devon,
England, led by the mycologist [[bloomfield-john-webster-ballistospore-discharge-discovery]] and his Spanish assistant Henry
Descals. The chapter, titled "Offensive Phalli and Frigid Caps," introduces
the reader to *[[bloomfield-stinkhorns-phallus-impudicus-osmotic-fruiting]]*—the shameless penis—a fungus whose life
cycle and mode of spore dispersal stand in stark contrast to the wind-driven
mechanisms of typical mushroom-forming fungi.

## The Devon Expedition

Money describes tramping up a steep, mist-shrouded slope on the edge of the
moors, where three men searched for "eggs." A pungent smell of dead deer hung
in the damp air, mingled with a suggestion of sweetness and even semen. The
odor was so intense that steamed glasses had to be wiped every few minutes.
Webster, the oldest of the party and wearing hunting pants that ended at the
knees, stopped and squatted, his blue eyes bulging as he scanned the forest
floor. Parting the pine needles, he uncovered five pure white eggs, somewhat
larger than golf balls. Each was attached to the soil by a branched umbilical
cord that snapped when tugged away from its siblings. The jelly-filled spheres
had cold skins. Money poses the question: what monsters will hatch from such
spawn? Nearby, a fully erect [[bloomfield-fungal-fruiting-body-morphology-ecological-function]] protruded six inches or more from
the pine needles, its head glistening with green-black syrup—the source of
the overwhelming odor. Hundreds of these apparitions dotted the slope higher
up, leading to the absurdist question of whether the collectors had wandered
into a colony of sexual deviants fixated upon live burial.

## The Egg Stage and Its Internal Anatomy

The transformation from egg to stinking horn is central to the stinkhorn's
reproductive success. When an unhatched egg is cut in half, the tissues of the
expanded structure are displayed in prefabricated form. A hollow shaft of
white spongy material called the **receptacle** runs pole-to-pole through the
egg's center. The receptacle is surrounded by the green-black cushion of
spores called the **gleba**, cased in a clear jelly veiled with white skin.
All of the architecture of the mature fruiting body is compressed within this
subterranean egg, awaiting the signal to deploy. Money notes that this
prefabrication is a remarkably conservative developmental strategy, with the
entire adult form encoded in the embryonic structure.

## Hatching and Rapid Emergence

The erection often begins in the cool of the night and is not complete until
sunrise. When the egg hatches, the receptacle expands by absorbing water and
ruptures the outer skin, carrying the spores on its tip into the air. The
jelly lubricates the extending shaft and helps keep the mass of spores in
place. Mechanically, the stinkhorn is comparable with the mammalian penis
because both erections are maintained by pressurized fluid rather than a solid
column of tissue. However, the origin of the pressurized fluid is fundamentally
different: penile blood pressure is generated by muscular activity, while
stinkhorn pressure is osmotic in origin, akin to the way water is soaked up
into a dry sponge. The receptacles are built from masses of corrugated hyphae
that are stretched into a weft of filaments when the egg hatches. Most of the
volume of the erect fruiting body is air.

## The Gleba and Its Chemical Arsenal

The spores are embedded in slime that contains a cocktail of volatile
chemicals, including hydrogen sulfide, formaldehyde, methylmercaptan, and
unique compounds called **phallic acids**. This chemical blend impersonates
the smell of rotting flesh, making the stinkhorn irresistible to flies, which
swarm on the head, and to slugs, which glide twenty or more feet for the
reward of the cadaverous confection. Within a few hours, the head is cleaned
down to the dimpled white surface of receptacle tissue, and the shaft begins
to wilt. Flies and slugs carry and defecate its spores, whose stinkhorn genes
contain the information needed to produce more stinkhorns. In common with
humans, stinkhorns are here because they are very good at making copies of
themselves.

## A Fundamentally Different Dispersal Strategy

Stinkhorns belong to the gasteromycete section of the Basidiomycota, fungi
whose spores form inside the fruiting body rather than on exposed gills.
Unlike gilled mushrooms that eject spores ballistically into the air and rely
on wind currents for dispersal, stinkhorns have evolved an animal-mediated
strategy analogous to insect-pollination in flowers. Money draws a direct
parallel with the Sumatran corpse flower (*Amorphophallus titanum*), which
emits similar putrid odors to attract carrion insects. Biologists already
recognize the significance of analogous characteristics in the origins of
flowers among insect-pollinated plants. Stinkhorns, [[bloomfield-cage-fungi-clathrus-anthurus-ileodictyon-receptacle-evolution]], and putrid
flowers have all evolved parallel features that attract insects that ordinarily
congregate around carrion.

## Ecological Significance and the Hidden Mycelium

Money emphasizes that stinkhorns and other mushrooms are merely the tips of
mycological icebergs. The umbilical cord at the bottom of the egg connects
with the larger organism that pulses unseen through [[biodiversity-of-fungi-leaf-litter-microfungi-survey-methods]], crawls under
the bark of dying trees, and connects with the roots of healthier ones. The
[[fungal-mycelium-hyphae-and-the-feeding-phase]] of the organism grows as masses of filamentous cells called
hyphae. Only when these hyphae have gathered sufficient food, and when the
[[bloomfield-what-lies-beneath-mycelial-architecture-hyphal-biology-subterranean-fungus]] is fattened and pumped full of water, can it surface to
disturb our composure. The apparently ornamental figure of the phallic mushroom
is really a very conservative structure, its form dictated entirely by the
functional demands of producing and dispersing spores.

## Related Gasteromycete Innovations

The stinkhorn's egg-based development is shared across related gasteromycetes.
Cage fungi like *Clathrus* and *Anthurus* package preformed receptacles into
eggs that unfold into open geometric structures upon hatching. Money notes
that small changes in receptacle development—such as weakening of tissue along
four or five tracks—could transform a Phallus-type fruiting body into an
Anthurus, suggesting that natural selection may have sculpted the existing
species in a relatively short period of time. The related bird's [[bloomfield-birds-nest-fungi-peridiole-splash-herbivore-dispersal]]
use raindrops to splash spore packets onto surrounding grass, while
*Sphaerobolus* operates as a tiny trampoline that launches spore balls with
enough force to ruin car paintwork. All of these innovations underscore the
evolutionary creativity of fungi in solving the fundamental challenge of spore
dispersal, each mechanism reflecting a different solution shaped by natural
selection and the specific ecological niches these organisms inhabit.
