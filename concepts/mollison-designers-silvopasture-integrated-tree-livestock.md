# Silvopasture Integrated Tree and Livestock Systems

Silvopasture is the intentional integration of trees with pasture and livestock
on the same land management unit. Bill Mollison recognised silvopasture as one
of the most productive and ecologically beneficial land use systems, combining
the yield of timber and tree crops with the income from livestock production
while providing ecosystem services including shade, soil protection, and carbon
sequestration. In the Designer's Manual, silvopasture is presented as a
fundamental strategy for [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-3-farm-forestry]] and tropical land management.

## The Biological Basis of Silvopasture

In natural ecosystems, herbivores and trees coexist in dynamic balance. Grazing
animals maintain open grasslands while browsing animals shape woodland structure.
Trees provide shade, shelter, and fodder for animals while benefiting from the
nutrient cycling that livestock manure provides. Silvopasture replicates these
natural interactions in a managed system that optimises both tree and animal
production.

The key insight is that trees and pasture together can produce more total yield
than either alone. Research consistently shows that well-managed silvopasture
systems produce 10 to 30 percent more total output than separate tree and
pasture systems on the same total land area. This synergy arises from the
complementary use of light, water, and nutrient resources by trees and pasture
species.

## Tree Species for Silvopasture
In temperate climates, deciduous trees such as oak, ash, walnut, honey locust,
and poplar provide summer shade and leaf litter for soil enrichment. The
[[mollison-designers-chestnut-as-multipurpose-tree-crop]] produces both timber and nut crops. [[mollison-designers-willow-water-and-natural-rooting-propagation]] provides fodder
and shade in moist sites. Tree spacing of 10 to 15 metres maintains pasture
productivity while allowing timber development; closer spacing of 5 to 8 metres
produces more tree biomass but requires shade-tolerant grasses.

### Tropical Silvopasture

In tropical systems, [[mollison-designers-leucaena-multipurpose-nitrogen-tree]] is the most widely used silvopasture tree. Planted
in rows four to eight metres apart, leucaena provides high-protein browse,
nitrogen fixation, and mulch for the inter-row pasture. Other tropical
silvopasture trees include [[mollison-designers-moringa-and-multipurpose-tropical-trees]], calliandra, gliricidia, and [[mollison-designers-casuarina-windbreak-erosion-control]].

The [[mollison-designers-shade-grown-coffee-and-cacao-systems]] systems are a form of silvopasture where the
understory crop is a perennial rather than pasture grass. These systems
demonstrate the principle that trees and understory crops can be mutually
beneficial when the shade level is appropriate.

## Pasture Management Under Trees
Pasture under trees differs from open pasture: light levels are reduced, soil
moisture varies with tree root competition, and nutrient cycling accelerates
with leaf fall and manure. The [[mollison-designers-tropical-pasture-grass-species-and-establishment]] selection should include
shade-tolerant species like signal grass and guinea grass in humid tropics, or
buffel grass in drier areas. Legume pasture species fix nitrogen for both
pasture and trees; the [[mollison-designers-legume-shade-tree-pasture-systems]] approach combines
nitrogen-fixing trees with nitrogen-fixing pasture legumes for maximum input.

## Livestock Integration

### Cattle in Silvopasture

Cattle are the most common livestock in silvopasture systems worldwide. They
benefit from shade that reduces heat stress, improving feed intake and weight
gain during hot periods. In tropical areas, shade can improve daily weight gain
by 10 to 20 percent compared to open pasture. The [[mollison-designers-free-range-forage-design-principles]]
principles apply to silvopasture grazing management.

Rotational grazing is essential in silvopasture to prevent overgrazing of
preferred species and to allow tree seedlings to establish. The paddock system
should be designed so that livestock are excluded from newly planted tree areas
until trees are tall enough to withstand browsing. [[mollison-designers-electric-fencing-in-permaculture-systems]] is the most
cost-effective way to create temporary paddocks in silvopasture systems.

### Goats and Sheep

Goats are natural browsers that can thrive in silvopasture by eating tree
foliage, weeds, and shrubs that cattle ignore. The [[mollison-designers-goat-management-and-browsing-systems]] chapter
discusses how goats can be used to control understory vegetation in tree
plantations, reducing competition for young timber trees while producing meat
and milk. Sheep prefer grasses and forbs and can be grazed alongside cattle in
a multi-species system that utilises a wider range of pasture plants.

### Poultry

Poultry in silvopasture systems control insects, distribute manure, and reduce
weed seed banks. The [[mollison-designers-chicken-house-over-swale-integration]] design can be adapted for silvopasture by
providing mobile coops that are moved through the tree-pasture system. Chickens
in silvopasture also reduce parasite loads by breaking dung pats and consuming
larvae. [[mollison-designers-geese-and-duck-weed-control]] can be used to graze grasses under fruit trees without damaging
the trees.

## Design Layout

The [[mollison-designers-slope-analysis-keyline-design]] is critical for silvopasture layout. On sloping land, tree
rows should follow the contour to minimise erosion and maximise water
infiltration. The [[mollison-designers-keyline-pattern-plow-technique]] provides the framework for tree and pasture
placement on undulating terrain.

Water infrastructure should be planned to serve both trees and livestock. [[mollison-designers-swale-spacing-and-contour-interval]] between tree rows capture runoff and increase soil moisture for both
components. [[mollison-designers-pond-construction-liner-sealing]] located within the silvopasture provide drinking water for
livestock and irrigation for dry-season pasture production.

## Carbon Sequestration

Silvopasture is one of the most effective land use systems for carbon
sequestration. Trees store carbon above and below ground, while the improved
soil organic matter under silvopasture adds additional carbon storage. The
combination of tree biomass and enhanced soil carbon can sequester two to ten
tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent per hectare per year, making silvopasture
a significant climate mitigation strategy.

## Shelter and Microclimate

Trees in silvopasture provide wind shelter for livestock, reducing cold stress
in winter and heat stress in summer. The [[mollison-designers-windbreak-configurations-for-crops-and-orchards]] principles
apply to silvopasture design, with tree rows oriented perpendicular to
prevailing winds for maximum shelter effect. The [[mollison-designers-microclimate-creation-frost-protection]] provided
by trees extends the growing season for pasture species in marginal climates.

## Economic and Establishment Considerations
Silvopasture requires initial investment in tree planting and fencing, with
timber returns typically after 15 to 30 years. Ongoing livestock income
provides cash flow during tree establishment. The [[mollison-designers-shelterbelt-economics-for-livestock-and-croplands]] analysis
applies here: the combined value of shade, shelter, and fodder often exceeds
the opportunity cost of land dedicated to trees. Trees should be planted before
or simultaneously with pasture, using [[mollison-designers-tree-planting-techniques-staking-guys]] that protect
seedlings. Ongoing management includes pruning for light penetration,
[[mollison-designers-coppicing-management-of-legume-trees]] fodder trees, and rotational grazing to maintain pasture quality.

## Establishment and Management

Establishing silvopasture requires careful planning. Trees should be planted
before or simultaneously with pasture establishment. In existing pasture, trees
can be established using [[mollison-designers-tree-planting-techniques-staking-guys]] that protect seedlings from
livestock. Tree guards, fencing, or temporary livestock exclusion are necessary
until trees are above browsing height.

Ongoing management includes periodic tree pruning to maintain light penetration
to the pasture, [[mollison-designers-coppicing-management-of-legume-trees]] of fodder trees, and rotational grazing management
to maintain pasture quality and prevent soil compaction.
