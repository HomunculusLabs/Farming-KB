# Nematode Management in Potato Crops Using Cover Crop Rotation

## Summary

Nematode pests in potato production, particularly root-feeding species
like root-knot and lesion nematodes, can be suppressed through an
integrated biological approach combining non-host cover crops,
mycorrhizal colonization, beneficial nematode inocula, and AACT
applications. Ingham documents cases where root-feeder populations
were reduced "from near a hundred per gram to less than detectable
within 6 months" using these methods, allowing potato production to
resume the following spring. (see [[mycorrhizal-biocontrol]]).

## Identifying the Pest Nematode

The first critical step is accurate identification of which root-
feeding nematode is present in the soil. Ingham emphasizes: "Your
friends need to know for certain WHICH root-feeder is present. Please
have them get their soil tested so they know." She recommends the Plant
Clinic at Oregon State University as a good resource for analysis of
parasitic nematodes. (see [[nematodes-in-soil-ecology]]).

Once nematodes have moved out of the potato and into the soil, removing
the seed potato eliminates further infection from that source, but "the
nematode is now present in that soil." This soil reservoir means that
management must focus on suppressing the existing soil population and
preventing resurgence, not just cleaning the seed stock. (see [[arthrobotrys-oligospora]]).

## Non-Host Cover Crop Strategy

The cover crop used in rotation "HAS TO BE A NON-HOST plant for the
lesion nematode" (or whichever root-feeding species is present). This
is not a generic recommendation -- the specific cover crop species must
be matched to the specific nematode pest. Ingham refers growers to her
husband Russ Ingham or nematologists at institutions like Washington
State University for species-specific cover crop recommendations. (see [[fungal-allelopathy-mechanisms-and-ecological-effects]]).

The principle is straightforward: if the nematode cannot feed on the
cover crop roots, the root-feeding nematode population will decline
during the cover crop phase because it is deprived of its food source. (see [[compost-tea-brewing-methods]]).
Meanwhile, beneficial soil organisms that compete with or prey on the
pest nematodes can build up their populations. The cover crop period
effectively starves the pest while building the biological defense
system of the soil.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi as Protection

Ingham states emphatically: "You MUST get the root system for whatever
crop you are growing colonized with mycorrhizal fungi." Mycorrhizal
inocula are available from commercial sources including EPM and
Mycorrhizal Applications. For annual crops like potato, the relevant
mycorrhizal type is VAM (vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizae), also
called AM (arbuscular mycorrhizae).

Mycorrhizal fungi provide physical protection to plant roots by forming
a fungal sheath around root surfaces that root-feeding nematodes must
penetrate before reaching the root. Additionally, mycorrhizal networks
improve plant vigor and nutrient uptake, making the plant more
resilient to any nematode damage that does occur.

## Beneficial Fungi for Nematode Suppression

The "general diversity of fungi in AEROBIC compost includes a herd of
nematode trapping and parasitic fungi," according to Ingham. These
specialized fungi have evolved mechanisms to capture, trap, or directly
parasitize nematodes. Some form adhesive networks or constricting rings
that ensnare nematodes as they move through soil. Others produce
spores that germinate inside the nematode body.

Ingham notes that "I am not aware of any inocula that you can buy on
the market, yet" specifically for nematode-trapping fungi, but directs
readers to stay tuned to the SFI e-zine as several companies are
working on developing them. In the meantime, using high-quality aerobic
compost with verified fungal diversity is the primary method for
introducing these beneficial fungi.

## Beneficial Nematodes

Bacterial-feeding, fungal-feeding, and predatory nematodes all
"interfere with the root-feeders" through competition, spatial
displacement, and direct predation. Predatory nematodes actively hunt
and consume root-feeding species, while bacterial and fungal feeders
compete for resources and space in the rhizosphere.

The challenge is getting these beneficial nematodes into the soil.
"Compost with good numbers of the good guys is needed." Ingham notes
that SFI is "working with other companies to produce nematode inocula"
and that "forest soil is the best place for the nematodes." For DIY
inoculum, she suggests finding undisturbed forest soil and using a
Baermann funnel extraction to recover nematodes, but cautions that you
need a DIC microscope to identify whether extracted nematodes are
beneficial or harmful species.

The SFI website lists composts containing good nematode diversity.
Desired levels include 20-100 beneficial nematodes per gram of compost,
comprising 10-15 bacterial-feeders, 5-10 fungal-feeders, and 1-5
predatory species, with zero root-feeding nematodes.

## How AACT Suppresses Pest Nematodes

AACT contributes to nematode suppression through multiple mechanisms.
First, it delivers the full suite of beneficial organisms -- bacteria,
fungi, protozoa, and nematodes -- into the soil if the compost used
for brewing contains adequate diversity. Second, the fungi in AACT
include nematode-trapping and parasitic species that directly attack
pest nematodes. Third, AACT improves overall soil health, which
supports the plant's own defense mechanisms.

For root-knot nematode specifically, Ingham notes that "CT supplies
fungi that, if they are in your compost, trap the nematodes." The key
requirement is that these trapping fungi must be present in the source
compost. This underscores the importance of starting with well-tested,
biologically diverse compost for AACT production.

## Integrated Management Timeline

Ingham documents that with all mechanisms working together -- non-host
cover crop, mycorrhizal colonization, beneficial nematode inoculation,
trapping fungi, and AACT applications -- root-feeder populations can
be reduced to below detectable levels within 6 months. This allows
potato production to resume the following spring. However, she stresses
that "testing is required to document that reduction." Soil foodweb
analysis should be performed before, during, and after the management
program to verify progress and guide adjustments.
