# Sector Analysis for Site Design

Permaculture sectors are an analytical tool to help designers understand
the flow of forces across a site. A sector analysis is presented as a
circle overlaid on the site plan, centered on Zone 0 or the center of
the lowest zone. Pie slices radiating from that circle represent forces
that flow across the site. For example, if easterly winds bring cold air,
you would draw a wedge pointing east and mark it as the cold easterly
winds sector.

There is no exhaustive list of sectors to include; the designer must
determine which forces affect the design. The sector analysis is not a
static diagram but a dynamic tool that informs every subsequent placement
decision.

## Typical Sectors

### Fire Hazard Sector
Note on every design which direction a wildfire or other fire would most
likely come from. This sector informs placement of firebreaks, water
storage, building materials, and vegetation management zones.

### Summer Wind Sector
In most ecologies, winds blow from a predominant direction during summer.
Simple climactic research reveals this pattern. The wedge should be about
80 degrees wide on the analysis circle. Summer winds may carry cooling
breezes desirable to channel into living spaces or hot desiccating air
to block.

### Winter Wind Sector
Winter winds typically blow from a different predominant direction than
summer winds. The wedge should also be about 80 degrees wide. Winter wind
sectors are critical for placing windbreaks, locating animal shelters,
and designing buildings for thermal comfort.

### Noise Sectors
If there are large noise generators near the site such as roads,
neighbors, or industrial areas, create a noise sector for each. The
angular width is at the designer's discretion. Noise sectors inform
placement of berms, dense plantings, water features, and buildings.

### Summer Sun Sector
This wedge denotes the solar azimuth during the summer solstice. Since
the analysis is graphically presented as a circle, make the actual
angles match the real solar azimuth angles. This sector reveals where
shade is needed and where solar access should be preserved.

### Winter Sun Sector
This wedge denotes the solar azimuth during the winter solstice, with
angles matching the actual solar azimuth. Winter sun sectors are critical
for passive solar building design, locating frost pockets, and placing
cold-sensitive plants.

### View Sectors
Both undesirable views (things to screen) and desirable views (sights to
preserve) should be mapped. Creating a sector for a desirable view
ensures the design does not accidentally block it with new elements.

## Creating the Sector Diagram

Sector diagrams can be frustrating because information visually overlaps.
A recommended approach is to start with a large circle centered on Zone
0. Create concentric rings equal to the total number of sectors planned.
For example, if you have four sectors (summer sun, winter sun, fire
hazard, noise), create four concentric rings.

Draw angle lines from the center out to the appropriate concentric ring
and label each sector. It is typical to put summer sun on the outermost
ring, winter sun second, prevailing winds third, and the rest in
whatever order suits the design. Color in the outermost arc of each
sector first, then fill in the wedges to avoid overlapping colors
incorrectly.

## Integration with Zones

Once the sector analysis is complete, you can use it to determine where
various elements should go to either block a force, allow it access, or
catch and harness it. Sectors and zones work together: zones tell you
how often you visit an element, sectors tell you what external forces act
on it.

For example, a greenhouse needs frequent visits (Zone 1) and maximum
winter sun exposure. A windbreak needs to be on the winter wind sector
boundary while serving as part of an animal system in Zone 2 or 3.

## Supporting Analysis Tools

### Soil Sampling
Take soil samples and test infiltration rates from around the site.
Where you observe differences in vegetation, ground coloration, or slope,
test soil to understand variation. Place sample data on the working copy
of the base map and color-code soil types.

### Surface Water Flow
Chart the flow of water across the site using topographical information
from the base map. This is instrumental in determining what earthworks
are needed to slow, spread, and sink water flow. The goal is to ensure
all rain falling on the site stays on the site and infiltrates into the
ground, or that runoff is cleansed by bioswales before reaching natural
waterways.

## Zones and Sectors Combined

Permaculture zones are ranked from 0 to 5, each rank denoting the amount
of human impact on an area. Zones are usually used in planning gardens
and crops but have much broader implications. When described for
beginners, zones are often shown as concentric circles, but in reality
they will almost never be concentric circles. They can be any shape but
will usually be large oblong or globular areas.

Zones are a rough estimate, not literal truth. They will not have sharp
lines of delineation in reality. In dense urban environments, a typical
home site may only have zones 0, 1, and 2. Not every zone analysis needs
all five zones.
