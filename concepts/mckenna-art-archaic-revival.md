# Art and the Archaic Revival

## Overview

In the final chapter of *Food of the Gods*, "Anticipating the Archaic Paradise,"
Terence McKenna articulates a vision of art as the primary shamanic response to
dominator culture. He frames the Archaic Revival as a clarion call to recover
humanity's birthright through creative expression synergized by psychedelic
experience. Art, in McKenna's formulation, is not merely an aesthetic practice
but a technology of consciousness, a means of reconnecting with the Gaian mind
and restoring the broken symbiosis between humans and the vegetable kingdom.

## The Shamanic Function of Art

McKenna identifies art as one of the primary functions of shamanism, a function
that is tremendously synergized by psychedelics. If psychedelics are
exopheromones that dissolve the dominant ego, then they are also enzymes that
synergize the human imagination and empower language. They cause us to connect
and reconnect the contents of the collective mind in ever more implausible,
beautiful, and self-fulfilling ways. Art becomes the medium through which the
psychedelic experience is integrated into culture and transmitted across
generations.

In McKenna's archaic framework, this creative function was continuous and
natural, not separated from daily life into a specialized professional activity.
The divine, inspiring power of the mushroom spoke through the bards and singers.
The indwelling spirit of the mushroom moved the hand that carved bone and
painted stone. Such creative expression was a commonplace of the Edenic world of
the Goddess. Life was lived close to a dimension of spontaneous magical and
linguistic expression that now shines only briefly in each person at the
pinnacle of experimental intoxication but that then was the empowered and
enveloping reality: the presence of the Great Goddess.

## The Ecstatic Origins of Human Creativity

McKenna traces the origins of human artistic creativity to the psychedelic
mushroom experience. The commonality of feeling generated by the mushroom held
the community together. The divine, inspiring power of the mushroom spoke
through the bards and singers. The indwelling spirit of the mushroom moved the
hand that carved bone and painted stone. This vision of art as a continuous flow
from the vegetable mind into human consciousness stands in stark contrast to the
modern Western conception of art as individual genius separated from nature and
community.

John Pfeiffer's observations about Upper Paleolithic cave art support this
connection. Pfeiffer noted that the placement of art within caves in often
nearly inaccessible spots was related to the use of the sites for initiation
ceremonies involving complex theatrical effects. He described "twilight-state
thinking" as a precondition for great culturally sanctioned truths to be
revealed. Twilight-state thinking, characterized by loss of objectivity,
temporal distortion, and a tendency to experience mild hallucinations, is
nothing more than a gloss for egoless and unbounded psychedelic arousal.
Pfeiffer neglected to discuss psychoactive plants, but McKenna fills this gap by
arguing that the twilight state was maintained and catalyzed by the ritual
ingestion of psilocybin mushrooms.

## The Technological Imperative

McKenna situates his call for an artistic Archaic Revival within a broader
technological context. New developments in many areas, including mind-machine
interfacing, synthetic pharmacology, and data storage, imaging, and retrieval
techniques, are coalescing into the potential for either a demonic or an angelic
self-imaging of culture. Those on the demonic side are fully aware of this
potential and are hurrying forward with plans to capture the technological high
ground, from which they hope to turn nearly everyone into a believing consumer
in a beige fascism from whose image factory none will escape.

The shamanic response to this situation, McKenna argues, should be to "locate
the art pedal and push it to the floor." Art is the counter-flow directed
against the tendency to leave people in designer states of consciousness not
their own, but the designs of Madison Avenue, the Pentagon, and the Fortune 500
corporations. This is not metaphor; it is, in McKenna's view, really happening
to us. The psychedelic option is off in a tiny corner, never mentioned; yet it
represents the only counterflow against the tendency to leave people in designer
states of consciousness.

## Psychedelics as Enzymes of Imagination

McKenna develops the concept of psychedelics as exopheromones, chemical signals
from the plant kingdom that regulate the symbiotic relationship between humans
and the vegetable mind. Beyond this ecological function, they act as enzymes of
the imagination. In the state of hallucinogenic intoxication, creativity is not
something that one expresses; it is something that one observes. This is a
crucial distinction: the psychedelic experience reveals a dimension of knowable
meaning that appears to be without connection to one's personal past or
aspirations, suggesting either a thinking Other or the deep structures of the
psyche made suddenly visible.

The existence of this dimension of knowable meaning that appears to be without
connection to one's personal past or aspirations seems to argue that we are
facing either a thinking Other or the deep structures of the psyche made
suddenly visible. Perhaps both. The profundity of this state and its potential
for positive feedback into the process of reorganizing the personality should
have long ago made psychedelics an indispensable tool for psychotherapy. After
all, dreams have made a major claim on the attention of theoreticians of psychic
process, as have free association and hypnotic regression; yet these are but
peepholes compared with the expansive view that psychedelics provide.

## The Octopus as Paradigmatic Image

McKenna proposes the octopus as the totemic animal image for the future human to
model in the shamanic task of re-sacralization and self-transformation. The
cephalopods, the squids and octopi, have perfected a form of communication that
is both psychedelic and telepathic, an inspiring model for the human
communications of the future.

An octopus does not communicate with small mouth noises; rather, it becomes its
own linguistic intent. Octopi have a large repertoire of color changes, dots,
blushes, and traveling bars that move across their surfaces, combined with their
soft-bodied physique, allowing them to obscure and reveal their linguistic
intent simply by rapidly folding and unfolding parts of their body. The mind and
body of the octopus are the same and hence equally visible; the octopus wears
its language like a kind of second skin. Their use of "ink" clouds to conceal
themselves may indicate the only way they can have anything like a private
thought; the ink cloud may be a kind of correction fluid for voluble octopi who
have misstated themselves.
