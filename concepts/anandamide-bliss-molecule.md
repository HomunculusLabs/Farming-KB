## Anandamide - The Bliss Molecule

Anandamide (N-arachidonoylethanolamine, abbreviated AEA) is an endogenous cannabinoid — a lipid-based signaling molecule produced naturally by the human body. Discovered in the 1990s, anandamide was the first endocannabinoid identified and is often called the "bliss molecule" for its role in promoting feelings of happiness, well-being, and contentment. The name derives from the Sanskrit word *ananda*, meaning "joy" or "bliss," reflecting its profound influence on mood and emotional states.

## Regulation: Synthesis and Degradation

Anandamide is not stored in vesicles like classical neurotransmitters. Instead, it is synthesized on demand from membrane phospholipid precursors and acts locally before being rapidly degraded:

1. **Synthesis**: Catalyzed by NAPE-PLD, which cleaves N-arachidonoyl-phosphatidylethanolamine (NAPE) to produce anandamide
2. **Degradation**: Primarily by FAAH (fatty acid amide hydrolase), which hydrolyzes anandamide to arachidonic acid and ethanolamine
3. **Transport**: Anandamide may be transported across cell membranes by a putative anandamide membrane transporter (AMT), though the existence and identity of this transporter remain debated

The rapid degradation of anandamide by FAAH is the primary reason for its short duration of action. Inhibiting FAAH (using FAAH inhibitors) prolongs anandamide signaling and has been explored as a therapeutic strategy for pain, anxiety, and mood disorders — an approach that would enhance the body's own endocannabinoid signaling without introducing exogenous cannabinoids.

## Factors Affecting Anandamide Levels

Several factors influence anandamide levels in the body:

- **Exercise**: Aerobic exercise ("runner's high") is associated with elevated anandamide levels, which may contribute to the mood-enhancing effects of physical activity
- **Diet**: Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acid intake influences the availability of arachidonic acid precursors for anandamide synthesis
- **Stress**: Acute stress can alter anandamide levels, with the direction of change depending on the type, duration, and intensity of the stressor
- **Genetics**: Genetic variations in FAAH (particularly the FAAH C385A polymorphism) affect anandamide degradation rates and have been linked to differences in pain sensitivity, anxiety, and risk for substance use disorders
- **Circadian rhythm**: Anandamide levels fluctuate with the sleep-wake cycle, with evidence suggesting higher levels during periods of rest and lower levels during active wakefulness
- **Social interaction**: Positive social experiences, particularly physical touch and bonding behaviors, may increase anandamide signaling, contributing to the feelings of well-being associated with social connection

## Anandamide and the Entourage Effect

Anandamide does not act in isolation within the endocannabinoid system. Its effects are modulated by other signaling molecules that together produce what has been termed an "entourage effect" at the endogenous level:

- **2-AG**: The other major endocannabinoid often acts in concert with anandamide, and the two can have complementary or competing effects at the same receptors
- **Palmitoylethanolamide (PEA)**: A related lipid that does not directly activate cannabinoid receptors but enhances anandamide's effects by inhibiting its degradation, functioning as an "entourage molecule"
- **Oleoylethanolamide (OEA)**: Another related lipid that may modulate anandamide signaling and contributes to satiety and lipid metabolism

These entourage interactions mean that anandamide's physiological effects depend not only on its own concentration but also on the balance of related signaling molecules in the local environment.

## Therapeutic Potential

Anandamide-based therapeutic strategies are an active area of pharmaceutical research, with several approaches under investigation:

1. **FAAH inhibitors**: Drugs that block the degradation of anandamide, prolonging its natural signaling. Examples include URB597 and PF-04457845, which have shown promise in preclinical studies for anxiety, depression, and pain.
2. **Anandamide transport inhibitors**: Compounds that block the putative anandamide membrane transporter, preventing cellular reuptake and increasing extracellular anandamide levels.
3. **CB1 allosteric modulators**: Molecules that enhance anandamide's activity at CB1 receptors without directly activating the receptor, providing a more subtle modulation of endocannabinoid signaling.
4. **TRPV1-targeted approaches**: Given anandamide's dual activity at CB1 and TRPV1 receptors, strategies that selectively target one pathway while avoiding the other could fine-tune therapeutic effects.

## Relationship to Cannabis

The discovery of anandamide provides a biological explanation for the effects of cannabis. THC, the primary psychoactive cannabinoid in cannabis, mimics anandamide by binding to the same CB1 receptors. However, THC differs from anandamide in several important ways:

- **Duration**: THC is degraded much more slowly than anandamide (hours vs. minutes), producing longer-lasting effects
- **Potency**: THC is a more potent CB1 agonist than anandamide
- **Receptor coverage**: THC activates CB1 receptors more broadly and intensely than anandamide's targeted, localized signaling
- **Side effects**: The prolonged, intense CB1 activation by THC produces effects (memory impairment, motor coordination changes, psychoactivity) that the body's natural anandamide signaling does not produce to the same degree

CBD (cannabidiol) indirectly influences anandamide levels by inhibiting FAAH, the enzyme that degrades anandamide. This FAAH inhibition may contribute to CBD's anxiolytic (anti-anxiety) and mood-stabilizing effects, as more anandamide remains available to activate CB1 receptors.
