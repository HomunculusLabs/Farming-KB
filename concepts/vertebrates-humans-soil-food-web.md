# Vertebrates and Humans in the Soil Food Web

While microorganisms and invertebrates form the foundation of soil food webs, larger animals—reptiles, mammals, and birds—also play significant roles. Humans, as the most influential actors in managed landscapes, have an outsized impact that can either support or undermine [[soil-food-web]] function.

## Larger Soil Animals

Reptiles, mammals, and birds that inhabit or traverse soil environments include squirrels, mice, groundhogs, rabbits, chipmunks, voles, moles, prairie dogs, gophers, snakes, and lizards. Though gardeners often view these animals as pests, their ecological contributions to soil health are substantial and entirely underpinned by the microarthropods and microorganisms that vastly outnumber them in any soil food web.

### Burrowing and Soil Mixing

All of these animals burrow and travel through soil, performing several important functions:

- **Mixing organic matter**: Animals incorporate surface litter into deeper soil layers through their burrowing activities
- **Moving and depositing organic material**: Organic matter is redistributed both vertically and laterally across the soil profile
- **Creating pathways**: Burrows serve as conduits for water infiltration, air exchange, and root penetration, improving overall soil structure
- **Forming water reservoirs**: Tunnel systems can store water that becomes available to plants during dry periods

### Nutrient Cycling Through Dung and Carcasses

The dung of all reptiles, mammals, and birds serves as a food source for other members of [[the-soil-food-web]] community. Bacteria, fungi, and detritivores break down animal waste, recycling it into plant-available nutrients. At death, animal carcasses are decomposed by soil organisms, returning their accumulated nutrients and organic matter to the soil.

### Microbial Transport

Larger animals function as vectors for microbial dispersal. They carry microorganisms on and in their bodies, feet, and fur as they move between locations. Birds are particularly effective at this—robins have been called "microbial taxicabs" because their feet carry protozoa that are spread from spot to spot as they hop across lawns and gardens.

### Birds as Bioindicators

The presence of birds in a yard or garden is a positive indicator of soil food web health. Birds congregating on lawns or in gardens signal that larger arthropods, worms, and larvae are present in sufficient numbers to support avian predators. Similarly, mole activity—while frustrating for lawn owners—indicates that food sources like Japanese beetle larvae exist in the soil, confirming that at least some links in the food chain are intact.

### Reframing "Pest" Interactions

Understanding soil food web dynamics changes how gardeners perceive animal pests. A mole tunneling through a lawn is evidence of an active food web with prey organisms present. Rather than resorting to chemicals and poisons, gardeners who understand these relationships can choose [[forest-management-strategies]] that maintain food web integrity while addressing specific conflicts.

## Benefits of a Healthy Soil Food Web for Gardening

A fully functional soil food web provides multiple benefits that directly improve growing conditions:

### Nutrient Retention

All members of the soil food web immobilize nutrients in their bodies. When fungi and bacteria are consumed by [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]], nutrients are released in plant-available form. Because plants attract specific bacteria and fungi to their rhizospheres through [[root-exudates]], the nutrients produced through this grazing are delivered precisely where roots can absorb them.

### Improved Soil Structure

Bacteria produce slime that binds tiny individual soil particles into larger aggregates, improving soil tilth. [[fungal-hyphae]] further stabilize these aggregates and bind them together over larger distances. Worms, insects, larvae, and even small mammals create tunnels of varying sizes that provide porosity for water retention, drainage, and aeration—all essential for healthy plant growth.

### Disease Defense

Soil food web organisms provide multiple layers of protection against plant pathogens:

- **Predatory organisms** hunt down and capture pathogenic bacteria, fungi, and nematodes
- **Beneficial fungi and bacteria** produce vitamins, hormones, and antibiotics that support plant health
- **Competitive exclusion**: Fungi and bacteria form barriers around plant roots, occupying the space and consuming the nutrients that pathogens would need to establish themselves
- **[[gadd-mycelial-resource-competition-and-combat-fungi]]**: Beneficial organisms compete with pathogens for oxygen, nutrients, and physical space

### pH Influence in the Rhizosphere

Soil food web organisms influence [[soil-ph]] at the rhizosphere level, which directly determines the predominant form of nitrogen available to plants. Bacterial dominance tends to raise pH (favoring nitrate production), while fungal dominance tends to lower it (favoring ammonium retention). Plants that receive their preferred nitrogen form perform optimally.

### Pollutant Degradation

Healthy soil food webs have remarkable capacity to break down pollutants. Lawn and garden chemicals, airborne contaminants, and various deposited substances can all be metabolized by specialized soil organisms. In a diverse, functioning food web, there is typically an organism capable of degrading almost any compound found in the soil.

## Human Impact on Soil Food Webs

Humans have a profound and often negative impact on soil food webs. Most gardeners have never been taught about soil food web systems and are unaware of the critical roles played by microbes, arthropods, and other soil organisms. Common gardening practices that damage soil food webs include:

- **Rototilling**: Destroys fungal hyphae networks, worm burrows, and soil aggregates; physically cuts apart larger organisms
- **Herbicides**: Eliminate weed species that contribute to root exudate diversity and organic matter inputs
- **Pesticides**: Kill both target and non-target arthropods, disrupting predator-prey relationships
- **Fungicides**: Reduce fungal populations essential for [[ectomycorrhizal-nutrient-cycling-and-forest-dynamics]] [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]]
- **Miticides**: Eliminate mites that serve important roles in decomposition and fungal population control
- **[[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]]**: Salt-based fertilizers irritate worms and other sensitive organisms; soluble nitrogen feeds plants directly, reducing the plant's need to support mycorrhizal partnerships
- **Soil compaction**: Eliminates the pore spaces that soil organisms depend on for air and water
- **Organic material removal**: Deprives the food web of the carbon inputs needed to sustain microbial and detritivore populations

When a niche is destroyed or a member of a food web niche is eliminated, the system begins to function imperfectly. Missing links must be filled by the gardener through amendments and management, or the system progressively fails.

## The Cooperative Approach

The fundamental principle of soil food web gardening is cooperation rather than domination. Rather than working against natural systems and then compensating with synthetic inputs, the gardener who understands and supports the soil food web lets its members perform the work they evolved to do—24 hours a day, 7 days a week. This approach requires less labor, fewer inputs, and produces more sustainable results than conventional gardening practices that inadvertently destroy the very organisms that make soil productive.
