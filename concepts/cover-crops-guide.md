# Cover Crops Guide

Cover crops are non-cash crops grown primarily to benefit the soil and subsequent cash crops. They protect bare soil, suppress weeds, cycle nutrients, improve soil structure, and support beneficial biology. Selecting the right cover crop depends on goals, climate, and rotation constraints.

## Cover Crop Families

### Legumes (Nitrogen Fixers)

Legumes form symbiotic relationships with Rhizobium bacteria, fixing atmospheric nitrogen into plant-available forms. Inoculate seed with the correct Rhizobium strain for maximum nodulation.

- **Crimson Clover** — hardy to USDA Zone 6, excellent spring N contribution, terminated by mowing at early bloom. Fixes 70-130 lb N/acre.
- **Hairy Vetch** — winter-hardy, vigorous vine, fixes 90-200 lb N/acre. Best combined with cereal rye. Hard to terminate without crimping or herbicide.
- **Field Peas** (Austrian Winter) — good cold tolerance, fixes 70-150 lb N/acre. Easy to terminate by mowing. Combines well with oats.
- **Cowpeas** — warm-season annual, drought-tolerant, fixes 100-200 lb N/acre. Excellent summer cover in the South.
- **Red Clover** — perennial or biennial, slow to establish but provides multi-season N contribution. Fixes 50-150 lb N/acre over a full season.

### Grasses (Biomass Builders and Scavengers)

Grasses produce extensive root systems and high carbon residue, improving soil aggregation and scavenging residual nitrogen.

- **Cereal Rye** — the most cold-tolerant winter annual grass. Produces massive biomass (4,000-8,000 lb dry matter/acre). Allelopathic properties suppress weeds. Excellent for [[no-till-methods]] systems.
- **Oats** — fast-growing, winter-kills in Zone 6 and colder (no termination needed). Good nurse crop for legumes. Produces 2,000-4,000 lb DM/acre.
- **Annual Ryegrass** — deep-rooted, breaks compaction layers, excellent for erosion control on slopes. Can become weedy if allowed to set seed.
- **Sorghum-Sudangrass** — warm-season, produces enormous biomass (6,000-12,000 lb DM/acre). Allelopathic root exudates suppress nematodes. Mow at 3-4 ft to encourage tillering.

### Brassicas (Biofumigants and Taproot Crops)

- **Daikon Radish** (Tillage Radish) — thick taproot penetrates compacted soil layers, creates channels for water infiltration. Winter-kills, leaving holes filled with organic matter. See [[green-manure-crops]] for biofumigant details.
- **Mustard** — contains glucosinolate compounds that release biofumigant gases upon incorporation. Effective against soil-borne pathogens and nematodes. See [[insect-allies]].
- **Forage Turnip** — deep taproot, good grazing option, winter-kills in northern zones.

### Broadleaves (Diversification)

- **Buckwheat** — fast maturity (6-8 weeks), attracts pollinators, mines phosphorus from soil. Thrives in poor soils. Winter-kills.
- **Sunflowers** — deep taproot, attracts beneficial insects, good for phytoremediation of contaminated soils.
- **Phacelia** — excellent pollinator attractant, quick growth, good winter annual in mild climates.

## Planting Timing

Timing is critical. Plant cover crops early enough for establishment before winter or heat stress.

### Winter Annuals

- **Cereal Rye**: Plant 4-6 weeks before average first fall frost. Can be planted as late as November in mild climates.
- **Hairy Vetch**: Plant 4-6 weeks before first frost. Requires early planting for good winter survival.
- **Crimson Clover**: Plant 6-8 weeks before first frost for best establishment.

### Summer Annuals

- **Cowpeas**: Plant after soil reaches 65°F. Direct seed 1-2 inches deep.
- **Buckwheat**: Plant after last frost. Succession plant every 3-4 weeks through summer.
- **Sorghum-Sudangrass**: Plant after soil reaches 65°F. Requires 60-70 days to maturity.

### Winter-Kill Crops

- **Oats**: Plant 6-8 weeks before first frost. Will die at temperatures below 20°F.
- **Daikon Radish**: Plant 4-10 weeks before first frost. Dies at temperatures below 25°F.

## Termination Methods

Choosing the right termination method depends on the cover crop species, timing, and subsequent crop. See [[no-till-methods]] for no-till termination approaches.

### Mechanical Termination

- **Roller-Crimping**: Most effective on cereal rye at anthesis (pollen shed). Requires a straight-stemmed, mature crop. The crimper bends and breaks the stem, killing the plant without incorporation. Essential for true no-till systems.
- **Mowing**: Effective on legumes at mid-bloom. May require multiple passes for aggressive species like hairy vetch. Leave residue on the surface.
- **Tillage**: Disking or rototilling incorporates residue and speeds decomposition but destroys soil structure. Last resort in regenerative systems.

### Winter-Kill

Oats, buckwheat, and sorghum-sudangrass die naturally with hard frosts. No active termination needed. Residue remains as mulch.

### Herbicide Termination

Glyphosate or other herbicides effectively kill cover crops but conflict with organic and regenerative principles. Use only as a last resort or during transition to no-till.

### Crimp-and-Crop Timing

For roller-crimper success, wait until the cover crop is at the correct growth stage:

- **Cereal Rye**: Pollen shed (anthesis), approximately 50-60% of heads shedding pollen. Too early and it regrows; too late and seed set creates a weed problem.
- **Hairy Vetch**: At least 50% in bloom. Often terminated simultaneously with rye in mixed stands.
- **Crimson Clover**: Mid-bloom stage.

## Multi-Species Mixes

Mixing cover crop families provides complementary benefits:

- **Soil Builder Mix**: Cereal rye + hairy vetch + daikon radish. Combines N fixation, biomass production, and compaction relief.
- **Summer Soil Builder**: Cowpeas + sorghum-sudangrass + buckwheat. Warm-season productivity with N fixation and pollinator support.
- **Pollinator Mix**: Crimson clover + buckwheat + phacelia. Sequential blooms support beneficial insects from spring through fall.
- **Winter Hardiness Mix**: Cereal rye + crimson clover + annual ryegrass. Maximum ground cover and N fixation through winter.

## Integration with Crop Rotation

Cover crops fill gaps in [[crop-rotation-principles]] and prevent fallow periods. Plan cover crops as dedicated rotation entries, not afterthoughts. A typical rotation might include:

1. Spring cash crop (e.g., tomatoes)
2. Summer cover crop (cowpeas + buckwheat)
3. Fall cash crop (e.g., garlic)
4. Winter cover crop (cereal rye + crimson clover)
5. Spring cash crop benefits from terminated cover crop residue

## Common Mistakes

- Planting too late in fall for adequate establishment before frost
- Failing to inoculate legume seed with correct Rhizobium strain
- Terminating cereal rye too early when roller-crimping (regrowth occurs)
- Allowing cover crops to set seed (creates weed problems in subsequent crops)
- Using a single species repeatedly (reduces diversity benefits)
- Ignoring moisture availability — cover crops can deplete soil moisture in dry regions before cash crop planting
