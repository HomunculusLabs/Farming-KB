# Mulberry and Sericulture in Permaculture

The mulberry (Morus spp.) is presented in the Permaculture Designers
Manual as a dual-purpose tree, valued both for its fruit and as the sole
food source for silkworms (Bombyx mori). Mollison uses the mulberry-silk
relationship as a prime example of how permaculture designers can
appreciate and utilise the underground and above-ground connections
between species.

## The Designer's Perspective on Underground Connections

Mollison's discussion of root systems and underground ecology provides
the philosophical foundation for understanding mulberry's role. He states
that it is as valid to plant a mulberry for silkworms as it is to plant an
Acacia for the considerable by-product of swift moth or ghost moth
larvae. The implication is that designers should think in terms of the
whole food web supported by each plant, not just the obvious primary
product.

The mulberry tree supports a complex of organisms: silkworms that produce
silk, birds that eat the fruit, leaf-feeding insects that feed insectivores,
and soil organisms that process the leaf litter. This web of interactions
is the essence of permaculture design thinking.

## Guild Analysis and Companion Planting

In the co-action and guild analysis sections, mulberry plays a
significant role. Mollison provides specific scoring for mulberry
interactions:

- Apple next to mulberry produces (+0): a good association
- Mulberry next to walnut produces (00): mutual benefit or at least
  no harm
- Apple then mulberry then walnut gives a neutral total, with
  mulberry as the intervenor resolving the primary conflict between
  apple and walnut
- Acacia next to mulberry gives (0+): a slightly positive
  association
- Apple-mulberry-Acacia-walnut gives an overall positive score

This guild analysis demonstrates how mulberry serves as a buffer species
that allows otherwise incompatible species to coexist in the same system.

## Silkworm Integration

Mollison references the traditional Chinese carp-pond complex, where
mulberries, ducks, and silkworms were integrated in a classic example of
permaculture thinking long before the term was coined. In this system:

- Mulberry trees grow on pond edges
- Silkworms fed on mulberry leaves produce silk
- Silkworm waste and mulberry leaf fall feed the pond
- Fish in the pond produce manure that fertilises the mulberries
- Ducks feed on pond organisms and control pests

This closed-loop system exemplifies the principle of every element
performing multiple functions.

## Edge Planting and Forage

Mulberry is listed as an edge species in several contexts. In the
discussion of [[mollison-goats-and-browsing-systems-in-permaculture]],
mulberry would be a valuable browse species.

## Fruit Production

Mulberry fruit is sweet, nutritious, and produced abundantly. The berries
are eaten fresh, dried, or made into preserves and wine. For poultry
keepers, mulberries provide summer feed that reduces grain costs. The
fruit also attracts birds that contribute to insect control in the wider
system.

## Species Selection

The Manual lists Morus spp. (mulberry) in the species appendix. Key
species include:
- Morus alba (white mulberry) -- preferred for silkworm production
- Morus nigra (black mulberry) -- excellent fruit quality
- Morus rubra (red mulberry) -- native to North America

White mulberry is the traditional food for silkworms, while black
mulberry produces the finest fruit for human consumption.
