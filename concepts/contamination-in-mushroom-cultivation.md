# Contamination in Mushroom Cultivation

Contamination is the single greatest cause of crop loss in mushroom
cultivation. Competing molds, bacteria, and yeasts exploit the same
nutrient-rich, humid environments that favor mushroom growth. Understanding
common contaminants, their sources, and prevention strategies is essential
for consistent production at any scale.

## Categories of Contaminants

### Mold Contaminants

**Trichoderma** (green mold) is the most common and destructive contaminant
in mushroom cultivation. It spreads rapidly as green spores, outcompetes
mushroom mycelium for substrate, and can produce volatile organic compounds
that inhibit mushroom growth. *[[trichoderma-harzianum]]* is particularly
problematic in commercial operations, capable of destroying entire crops
within days.

**Aspergillus** species appear as powdery colonies in green, yellow, or
black. They thrive on grain substrates and can produce mycotoxins. Spores
are ubiquitous in indoor environments and very difficult to eliminate
completely.

**Penicillium** presents as blue-green colonies with a characteristic
musty odor. Commonly found in poorly sterilized grain spawn and
insufficiently pasteurized substrates.

**Neurospora** (red bread mold) is especially prevalent in warm, humid
tropical cultivation. It spreads explosively — entire rooms can be
contaminated within hours. Instantly recognizable by its bright orange-red
color.

**Coprinus** (inky cap) and **Scopulariopsis** are less common but can
appear as secondary contaminants on spent substrate.

### Bacterial Contaminants

**Pseudomonas tolaasii** causes bacterial blotch on mushroom caps — dark,
sunken, slimy lesions that render mushrooms unmarketable. Favored by high
humidity, poor air circulation, and water sitting on cap surfaces.

**Bacillus subtilis** causes "stringy" or "ropy" grain spawn. The grain
appears slimy and fails to colonize properly with mushroom mycelium. Caused
by incomplete sterilization of grain.

### Yeast Contaminants

Yeast contamination appears as wet, slimy patches on substrate surfaces.
Common in grain spawn preparation. Typically caused by insufficient
sterilization or poor aseptic technique during inoculation.

## Sources of Contamination

### Airborne Contamination

Airborne spores are the primary vector. Conidia of *Trichoderma*,
*Aspergillus*, and *Penicillium* are present in virtually all indoor air
at some concentration. HEPA filtration (99.97% efficient at 0.3 µm) is the
standard for clean rooms and laminar flow hoods used during inoculation.

### Substrate-Borne Contamination

Raw substrate materials (straw, sawdust, grain) carry their own microbial
communities. Pasteurization and sterilization are designed to eliminate or
reduce these organisms, but failures in temperature, duration, or
penetration allow survivors to proliferate.

### Equipment and Surface Contamination

Tools, containers, work surfaces, and operator clothing can transfer
contaminants. Proper cleaning with 70% isopropyl alcohol or 10% bleach
solution is standard practice.

### Water Contamination

Standing water, reservoirs, and humidification systems can harbor bacteria.
Water used in [[substrate-preparation]] should be clean; potable water is
generally sufficient.

## Prevention Strategies

### Aseptic Technique

The most cost-effective contamination control is rigorous aseptic
technique: working in a clean area (still air box or laminar flow hood),
flaming inoculation tools, wearing gloves and masks, and minimizing the
time containers are open during transfers.

### Substrate Preparation

- **Pasteurization:** 60–80°C for 1–2 hours eliminates most competitors
  while preserving thermophilic beneficials.
- **Sterilization:** 121°C for 60–90 minutes (15 psi) eliminates all
  organisms. Required for supplemented substrates.

### Environmental Control

- Maintain positive pressure in grow rooms (filtered air in, unfiltered
  air out).
- Keep humidity high enough for mushrooms but avoid condensation.
- Ensure adequate fresh air exchange to prevent CO₂ buildup and stagnant
  conditions that favor contaminants.
- Clean grow rooms between crops with appropriate disinfectants.

### Spawn Quality

Using clean, vigorous spawn is fundamental. Spawn that is slow-growing,
discolored, or has off-odors should be discarded. Grain-to-grain transfers
amplify any contamination present in the parent culture.

## Salvaging Contaminated Substrates

Mold-contaminated substrates should generally be discarded — buried or
composted, not kept in the grow space. Some growers successfully manage
minor *Trichoderma* contamination on the substrate surface by removing the
affected area and increasing FAE, but this is risky.

Bacterial blotch on fruiting bodies can sometimes be managed by reducing
humidity, improving air circulation, and avoiding surface wetting.
