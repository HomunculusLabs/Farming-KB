# Resource Classification in Permaculture

In [[permaculture]], resources are the useful reserves created when living components and technological units translate incoming natural energies (sun, wind, rain) into storable forms. Understanding how different categories of resources behave under use is fundamental to sustainable design. Not all resources can be used in the same way or to the same ends, and ethical resource management requires knowing the consequences of exploitation for each category.

## Defining Resources and Yield

The energies coming into a permaculture system — sun, wind, rain — are captured by living components and some technological or non-living units built into the system, which translate these incoming energies into useful reserves called **resources**. Some of these resources must be used by the system for its own purposes: stocks of fish must be maintained to produce more fish. An ideal technology should at the very least fuel itself.

The **surplus**, over and above system needs, is the **yield**. Yield is any useful resource surplus to the needs of the local system, and thus available for use, export, or trade. The way to obtain yield is to be conservative in resource use, for energy, like money, is much more easily saved than generated. Resource saving involves recycling waste, insulating against heat loss, and routing resources to their next "use point."

## The Five Categories of Resources

Resources can be classified by what happens when they are used — their response to exploitation. This use-and-results approach reveals five distinct categories with profoundly different management implications:

### Category 1: Resources That Increase by Modest Use

These resources benefit from moderate consumption and decline or degrade if left unused. Green browse is a prime example: if deer do not browse shrubs, the latter may become woody and unpalatable. A browsed biennial, unable to flower, may tiller out and become perennial (as with fireweed, *Erechthites*, nibbled by wallaby in Tasmania). Seedling trees can be maintained at browse height, but if ungrazed, they "escape" to unbrowsable height and shade out other palatable plants.

**Information** is another resource that increases with use. It withers or becomes outdated if not used. Too little information impoverishes a system, but when freely used and exchanged, it flourishes and increases. This category is the most productive basis for sustainable society.

### Category 2: Resources Unaffected by Use

These resources remain unchanged regardless of how they are used. In impalpable terms, a view or a good climate is unaffected by use. In palpable terms, the diversion of part of a river to hydroelectric generation or irrigation (with water returned to the stream after use) is also unaffected, as is a stone pile used as mulch, heat store, or water run-off collector. A well-managed ecosystem is itself an example of resources unaffected by use — the system maintains its productive capacity despite harvesting.

### Category 3: Resources That Disappear or Degrade If Not Used

These resources are lost if not harvested or captured in time. An unharvested crop of an annual, grass that could be stored for winter, irruptions of oceanic fish, swarms of bees or grasshoppers, ripe fruit, and water run-off during rains all fall into this category. The key management strategy is timely capture and storage.

### Category 4: Resources Reduced by Use

These resources diminish with exploitation and must be carefully managed to avoid depletion. Examples include fish or game stocks unwisely harvested, clay deposits, mature forests, and coal and oil. These can still be used sustainably if the rate of use does not exceed the rate of regeneration (for biological resources) or if they are used strategically to create permanent beneficial changes before they are exhausted.

### Category 5: Resources That Pollute or Destroy Other Resources If Used

These are the most dangerous category. They cause damage to other resources when exploited. Examples include residual poisons in an ecosystem, radioactives, super-highways, large buildings or areas of concrete, and sewers running pollutants to the sea. A responsible society bans the use of this category entirely.

## Sustainability Implications

Categories 1 through 3 are those most commonly produced in natural systems and rural living situations, and are the **only sustainable basis of society**. Categories 4 and 5 result from urban and industrial development. If not used to produce permanent beneficial changes to the ecosystem, Category 4 resources become pollutants, and some are permanent pollutants in terms of human lifetimes.

It follows that a sane society manages Categories 1 through 4 wisely, bans the use of Category 5, and regulates all uses to produce sustainable yield. This is called **resource management**, and has been successfully applied to some fish and animal populations, but seldom to human societies' own resource consumption patterns.

## Ethics of Resource Use

Ethics of resource use are evolved by knowing about the results of resource exploitation. Forests, soils, air, water, sunlight, and seeds are resources that all regard as part of a common heritage. A second category belongs to groups, families, or persons: those fabricated, ordered, or otherwise developed resources that people create by their work. What people create, however, is always made from the common resource, so it is impossible to draw a firm line between private and common resources.

## The Principle of Enough

The key principle to wise resource use is the principle of **"enough."** This is basic to understanding societies in chaos or systems in disorder. Both over- and undersupply of resources have much the same destructive effect, except that oversupply has more grotesque results in life systems than undersupply. To a degree, undersupply can be coped with by reduced growth and wider spacing or dispersal of organisms, but oversupply can cause inflated growth, crowding, and sociopathy in social organisms. In people, both gross over- and under-nutrition are common. Ethical resource management is needed to balance out the pathologies of famine and obesity.

## Consequences of Mismanagement

Failure to manage resources wisely has dramatic consequences at the societal level. Superhighways and overpasses in Massachusetts alone need approximately 400 billion dollars to repair; the collapsing sewer systems of London and New York need some 80 billion. Neither can raise this money, demonstrating that an unthinking historical development strategy can cripple a future society. Today's luxuries — concreted highways, sprawling cities, and the release of persistent biocides — become tomorrow's disasters.

Immoral governments tolerate [[desertification]] and land salting, concreted highways and city sprawl, which take more good land permanently out of life production than the loss of territory to a conqueror. Programs of highway building and city expansion, the release of persistent biocides, and loss of soil will bring any society down more surely and permanently than war itself. Immorality of this nature is termed "progress" and "growth" to confuse the ignorant and to supplant local self-reliance for the temporary ends of centralized power.

Investment priorities can be decided on the same resource criteria at both national and household levels. The principle remains: invest in resources that increase with use, protect those unaffected by proper use, capture those that degrade if unused, conserve those reduced by use, and eliminate those that pollute.
