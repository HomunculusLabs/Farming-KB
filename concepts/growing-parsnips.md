## Growing Parsnips

Parsnips are like carrots with important differences. Most varieties are longer than carrots (meaning they need deeper loose topsoil), they have a slightly lower need for fertility, they grow more slowly, and they do not crack when quite big as some carrot varieties do. The biggest difficulty is getting short-lived parsnip seeds to germinate.

## The Seed Longevity Problem

Most seed sold in stores is faltering only two years after harvest. Growing your own parsnip seed is a good idea for several reasons:

1. **Fresh seed germinates reliably** — your neighbors will buy it eagerly because they know it will come up
2. **Uniform crop** — seed from hand-selected roots produces a uniform crop
3. **Income opportunity** — selling extra seed at the local [[garden-center-transplant-pitfalls]] can earn enough to pay for all the seed you wish to buy

## Growing Details

### Germination Tricks

Fresh strong parsnip seeds sprout slowly — rarely more than 25 percent emerge. To improve germination:

- **Sow thickly** — expect poor germination
- **Cover seed with [[compost]]** instead of soil — helps retain moisture
- **Presprouting (chitting) also helps**
- **Thin every few days** — progressively thin seedlings as they emerge

### Timing: The Critical Decision

**The problem with spring planting**: If parsnips are started in spring, when naturally moist conditions would make germination easy, they'll become so enormous by autumn that they're almost inedible and may show damage from insects and soil diseases.

**The solution**: Start them on or shortly after the **summer solstice** so they size up when weather gets cool. Those living closer to the equator might start their parsnips closer to the autumn equinox and consider them a crop that grows during the cool portion of the year.

### Getting Seed to Sprout in Heat

This is the main challenge with solstice planting:
- Sow thickly
- Cover with compost instead of soil
- Presprout if possible
- Keep soil consistently moist
- Thin progressively as seedlings emerge

### Flavor Improvement

Parsnip flavor improves after they go through some stiff frosts. This is why the solstice-timing strategy works so well — the roots size up during cool autumn weather and are sweetened by early frosts.

## Harvest and Storage

- Parsnips usually must be dug (tops don't lift easily)
- When soil is close to freezing, the whole patch may be dug and the roots cellared in moist sand (as carrots are)
- Some gardeners leave a part of their crop in the earth over winter — when the soil thaws in spring, they find that the parsnips have become sweeter and are still in good eating condition
- **Spring-harvested parsnips are often the sweetest**

## Saving Seed

Save seed as you would for carrots:
- Parsnips are biennial and insect-pollinated
- To avoid [[cervantes-hybrid-vigor-inbreeding-depression]], use at least 25 roots in the seed crop
- Grow seed from carefully hand-selected roots that show exactly the traits you desire
- Select the strongest and largest seed from the main central flowers
- The seed falls from the flowers when fully dry, much as dill seed does
- Cut off individual flowers one by one when one or two seeds have detached
- Let the flowers finish drying in a big bucket or spread on a tarp under cover

## Culinary Uses

- Roasted parsnips are a classic preparation
- They can be mashed like potatoes, often with butter and [[maple-syrup-production]]
- Parsnip soup is rich and creamy
- Grated raw parsnip can be used in slaws
- The flavor is sweet, nutty, and complex — especially after frost
