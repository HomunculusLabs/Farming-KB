# Hallucinogenic Plants Growing in the United States

A wide variety of psychoactive plants and fungi grow naturally, in gardens, or as ornamentals throughout the United States. Many of these contain powerful hallucinogenic or dissociative compounds that can be located and prepared by a determined individual. This overview covers the major categories of naturally occurring psychoactive botanicals found within US borders.

## DMT-Containing Plants

N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) occurs in several plants that grow commonly across the United States. Phalaris arundinacea, Phalaris tuberosa, and Phalaris aquatica, collectively known as reed canary grass, are found in fallow fields, sidewalk cracks, and lawns across the entire country. DMT content varies significantly between strains and individual plants, but high-percentage strains can be obtained through specialty suppliers.

Desmanthus illinoensis, the prairie bundleflower, also has a wide American distribution. The root bark of this prairie legume has been found to contain approximately 0.34% DMT by dry weight. The plant is a perennial that grows in prairies and open areas throughout the central and eastern United States.

DMT itself is not orally active because monoamine oxidase enzymes in the gut and liver rapidly break it down before it reaches the bloodstream. To achieve oral activity, DMT must be combined with a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI). In South America, the Banisteriopsis caapi vine provides the MAOIs (harmine, harmaline, and tetrahydroharmine) for the traditional ayahuasca brew.

In the United States, Peganum harmala (Syrian rue) has been introduced and now grows wild in the western states. It contains 2-4% MAOI by dry weight. Passiflora incarnata (passionflower) grows throughout the Southeast and much of the Midwest, with approximately 1% MAOI content. Non-traditional brews combining these American plants can produce ayahuasca-like effects, and recipes are available online.

## Psilocybin-Containing Mushrooms

Psilocybe cubensis and other psilocybin-containing mushroom species grow in many regions of the United States. These mushrooms contain psilocybin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) and its active metabolite psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine).

In the US, psilocybin mushrooms are primarily coprophagous, growing on cow and horse manure. Their range extends from Florida through the southern Gulf States to Texas, in pasture and grain fields, and along the coastline of the Pacific Northwest from California into Canada. They fruit most abundantly in warm, humid conditions following rainfall.

Psilocybe cubensis, the most common species, typically contains 10-12 mg psilocybin per gram of dried mushroom. Intoxication peaks within the first 2 hours and diminishes over 3-4 hours. A threshold dose is approximately 40 micrograms per kilogram of body weight, with typical recreational doses ranging from 1-2 grams of dried mushroom material.

A serious risk with wild mushroom foraging is misidentification. Several mushroom species growing in similar habitats are genuinely poisonous and can cause liver failure or death. While psilocybin mushrooms themselves are not lethal and do not cause organ damage, confusing them with toxic species such as Galerina species can be fatal.

## Peyote and Mescaline-Containing Cacti

Peyote (Lophophora williamsii) contains over 60 alkaloids, with mescaline as its principal hallucinogenic constituent at approximately 1.5% by dry weight. Peyote grows within a narrow strip of desert along the Texas-Mexico border, extending into the Chihuahuan Desert. It is a spineless, slow-growing cactus with a small crown visible above the earth and a tuberous root extending below.

When properly harvested, only the crown (button) is removed, leaving the root intact to sprout new crowns for future harvest. Peyote is typically consumed as fresh peeled buttons, dried whole buttons, dried powder, or as a tea. The taste is bitter and acrid, and nausea and vomiting commonly follow ingestion, especially in inexperienced users.

Other mescaline-containing cacti are widely available as ornamental plants. Trichocereus pachanoi (San Pedro) and Trichocereus peruvianus (Peruvian torch) are common in garden shops across the country. Trichocereus pachanoi has been found to contain anywhere from 0.33% to 2.375% mescaline by dry weight depending on the specimen. These cacti are typically prepared by boiling into a soup-like consistency for ingestion.

## Salvia Divinorum

Salvia divinorum, from the mint family (Lamiaceae), contains the psychoactive neoclerodane diterpene salvinorin-A. Unlike most classical hallucinogens that act on serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, salvinorin-A is the first known naturally occurring non-nitrogenous kappa-opioid receptor agonist.

Traditionally consumed by Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico, by chewing fresh leaves or drinking leaf juice, Salvia divinorum is now widely grown and sold in the United States. When smoked, salvinorin-A is psychoactive for approximately 15 minutes at doses of 200-500 micrograms. Oral absorption through the mucous membranes produces a less intense but longer-lasting experience of up to one hour.

The DEA has not scheduled Salvia divinorum at the federal level, though several states have enacted their own restrictions. The plant has no known reinforcing properties, and harm primarily occurs from inadequate preparation or from consuming it in dangerous settings such as while driving.

## LSA-Containing Plants

Lysergic acid amide (LSA, also called ergine) is found in the seeds of several plants. Argyreia nervosa (Hawaiian baby woodrose) contains approximately 0.14% LSA by dry seed weight. Ipomoea violacea (morning glory) contains approximately 0.02% LSA by dry seed weight. Both are popular ornamental plants, and commercial morning glory seeds are often coated with emetic poisons to discourage ingestion.

Hallucinogenic activity occurs at 2-5 mg of LSA, providing a 4-8 hour intoxication reportedly different in both quality and quantity from LSD. Seeds are crushed, germinated, eaten whole, or soaked in water for extraction. Five to ten Argyreia nervosa seeds or 150-200 Ipomoea violacea seeds yield average doses.

Historically, Ipomoea violacea and related species were used in Central America for shamanic and ceremonial purposes. The highest LSA concentrations have been found in Stipa robusta (sleepygrass) growing in the American southwest, though this species has no history of traditional use.

## Anticholinergic Plants

Several plants containing atropine and scopolamine grow throughout much of the United States. Datura stramonium (Jimson weed) grows wild across the entire country except Wyoming and Alaska. Atropa belladonna (deadly nightshade), Hyoscyamus niger (henbane), and Mandragora officinarum (mandrake) were introduced as ornamentals or medicinal plants.

Atropine and scopolamine are competitive antagonists of muscarinic cholinergic receptors and central nervous system depressants. In overdose, they induce a toxic delirium marked by anterograde amnesia, confusion, dissociation, hallucinations, delusions, and a giddy, excited affect. Coordination impairment, blurred vision, dry mucous membranes, and dangerously prolonged effects are characteristic.

These are among the most dangerous of the psychoactive plants discussed here. Bizarre self-injury is not uncommon because the delirium can last for days, creating a dramatic disconnection from reality. Symptoms including headache and pupillary dilation may persist for weeks. The frighteningly adverse overdose profile generally precludes common recreational abuse, though accidental poisonings from foraging occur regularly.

## Amanita Muscaria and Pantherina

Amanita muscaria (fly agaric) and Amanita pantherina (panther cap) contain the psychoactive compounds ibotenic acid and muscimol. Muscimol is a GABA receptor agonist with mild sedative effects. Ibotenic acid acts as an excitatory amino acid at glutamate receptors, inducing an agitated toxic delirium.

These mushrooms grow throughout much of the United States. Amanita muscaria is instantly recognizable with its bright red cap dotted with white spots. However, misidentification is a serious risk because deadly poisonous species such as Amanita phalloides (death cap) grow in close proximity.

Intoxication begins within 30 minutes and peaks over 2-3 hours. Effects have been described as similar to alcohol intoxication but can also include powerful hallucinations, slurred incoherent speech, seizures, severe nausea, transient deep sleep or coma, and headache persisting for weeks. Ibotenic acid decarboxylates to muscimol when mushrooms are dried, making dried preparations somewhat different in effect from fresh ones.

## Legal and Safety Considerations

Many of the plants described here are legal to possess, grow, or forage, while the extracted psychoactive compounds they contain are Schedule I controlled substances. Psilocybin mushrooms and their spores are Schedule I federally, though mushroom spore prints remain legal in most states (except California).

Peyote is a Schedule I substance, but members of the Native American Church with recognized tribal membership are exempt for traditional ceremonial use. The Santo Daime and Uniao do Vegetal (UDV), Brazilian religions using ayahuasca as a sacrament, have been engaged in federal litigation seeking religious exemption for their members in the United States.

The primary safety concern across all these botanicals is accurate identification. Misidentifying mushrooms is the most dangerous error, with potentially fatal consequences. Even correctly identified plants can cause serious harm through overdose, improper preparation, or consumption in inappropriate settings. Drug education should include awareness of these indigenous botanicals alongside their synthetic counterparts.
