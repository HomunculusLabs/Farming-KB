# [[query-how-to-fix-phosphorus-deficiency-in-cannabis|[[query-how-to-fix-sulfur-deficiency-in-cannabis|[[query-what-causes-nutrient-lockout-in-cannabis|Cannabis]]]]]]: Botany, History, and Chemistry

Cannabis (genus *Cannabis*), also known as marihuana, hasheesh, hemp, kif, bhang, or charas, is one of the oldest cultivated plants and arguably the most widespread hallucinogen in human history. It serves as a source of hemp fiber, edible fruit, industrial oil, medicine, and narcotic. Despite its great age and economic importance, the plant remains poorly understood in many respects.

## Botanical Description

*Cannabis* is a rank, weedy annual that is extremely variable and may attain a height of 18 feet. It flourishes best in disturbed, nitrogen-rich soils near human habitations, earning it the designation "camp follower" for its tendency to spread with human migration.

### Sexual Dimorphism

The plant is normally **dioecious** — male and female flowers occur on separate plants:

- **Male (staminate) plant:** Usually weaker and less potent
- **Female (pistillate) plant:** More robust; pistillate flowers grow in leaf axils

The intoxicating constituents are normally concentrated in a **resin** produced in the developing female flowers and adjacent leaves and stems.

### Leaf Morphology

Cannabis leaves are **palmately divided**, normally into 3–7 leaflets, occasionally into 11–13. Leaflets vary in length from 2 to 6 inches.

## Taxonomic Classification

The classification of cannabis is disputed among botanists, with disagreements about both family placement and species count:

### Family Placement

- Historically placed in the fig/mulberry family (**Moraceae**) or nettle family (**Urticaceae**)
- Now usually separated, together with the hop plant (*Humulus*), into its own family: **Cannabaceae**

### Species Debate

The traditional view holds there is one species, *Cannabis sativa*, which has developed many "races" or "varieties" partly through human selection:

- **Fiber varieties:** Selected for superior hemp fiber
- **Oil varieties:** Selected for higher seed oil content
- **Narcotic varieties:** Selected for stronger psychoactive properties (especially notable in India, where intoxicating properties had religious significance)

Current research indicates there may be additional species:

- ***C. sativa*** — the type species
- ***C. indica*** — shorter, more conical, often more potent
- ***C. ruderalis*** — a smaller, weedy form

All *Cannabis* is native to **central Asia**.

## Historical Use

### Ancient Origins

The history of cannabis use extends deep into antiquity:

- **China (~4800 years ago):** Chinese tradition records use of the plant. The oldest known name is TA MA (大麻), literally "the tall fiber plant." The Chinese called cannabis "liberator of sin" and "delight giver."
- **India (before 1000 B.C.):** Indian medical compilations report therapeutic uses. Early Hindus called it "heavenly guide" and "soother of grief."
- **Scythia (~3000 years ago):** The Scythians grew cannabis along the Volga and threw seeds and leaves on hot stones in steam baths to produce intoxicating smoke. This was documented by Herodotus around 500 B.C.
- **Egypt:** Specimens nearly 4,000 years old have been found at Egyptian sites. In ancient Thebes, the plant was made into a drink with opium-like effects.
- **Turkey:** Hemp fabrics from the late 8th century B.C. have been discovered.
- **Greece:** The physician Galen (c. A.D. 160) wrote that general use of hemp in cakes produced narcotic effects.
- **Asia Minor (13th century):** Organized murderers rewarded with hasheesh were known as *hashishins* — possibly the origin of the word "assassin" in European languages.

### New World Introduction

Hemp as a source of fiber was introduced to New England by the Pilgrims and to Latin American colonies by the Spanish and Portuguese.

### Archaeological Evidence

Frozen Scythian tombs in the Altai Mountains (Russia/Mongolia border) have yielded:

- Small tepee-like structures covered with felt or leather mats
- Copper censers (four-legged stool-like objects)
- Carbonized hemp seeds
- Two-handled pots containing cannabis fruits

## Chemistry of Cannabis

The chemistry of cannabis is notably complex. Many organic compounds have been isolated, some with narcotic properties and others without.

### Major Constituents

A fresh plant yields mainly **cannabidiolic acids**, which are precursors to the active compounds:

- **Cannabidiolic acid** (precursor)
- **Cannabinol (CBN)** — relatively inactive
- **Cannabidiol (CBD)** — non-intoxicating but biologically active
- **Tetrahydrocannabinol-carboxylic acid** (precursor)
- **Δ¹-Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)** — the primary psychoactive compound
- **Stereoisomers of tetrahydrocannabinol**
- **Cannabichromene (CBC)**

### The Primary Active Compound

Δ¹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) has been demonstrated to be the main psychoactive compound. The tetrahydrocannabinols form an oily mixture of several isomers. They are **non-nitrogenous** organic compounds derived from terpenes — they are not alkaloids, although traces of alkaloids have been reported in the plant.

### Chemical Instability

The narcotic effect of cannabis is highly variable due to the unstable character of some constituents. Over time:

1. Inactive cannabidiolic acid converts to **active tetrahydrocannabinols**
2. THC eventually converts to **inactive cannabinol**

These changes typically occur more rapidly in tropical than in cooler climates. Material from plants of different ages may thus vary greatly in narcotic effect.

## Methods of Use

Cannabis preparations and methods of consumption vary enormously across cultures:

### New World Methods

- **Marihuana (maconha in Brazil):** Dried, crushed flowering tips or leaves, smoked — often mixed with tobacco in cigarettes ("reefers")

### Old World Methods

- **Hasheesh:** Resin from the female plant, eaten or smoked (often in water pipes) across Moslem countries of northern Africa and western Asia. In Afghanistan and Pakistan, commonly smoked.
- **Bhang:** Plants gathered green, dried, made into a drink with water or milk, or into a candy (majun) with sugar and spices
- **Charas:** Pure resin, normally smoked or eaten with spices
- **Ganjah:** Resin-rich dried tops from the female plant, usually smoked with tobacco

Many of the more potent preparations may be derived from *C. indica*.

## Effects on the Human Body

Cannabis effects are **highly variable** from person to person and between plant strains — more so than most other hallucinogens.

### Psychological Effects

- **Principal effect:** Euphoria
- **Mild effects:** Mild sense of ease and well-being
- **Intense effects:** Fantastic dreams, visual and auditory hallucinations
- **Sensory alterations:** Beautiful sights, wonderful music, sound aberrations, bizarre adventures in compressed time perception
- **Cognitive changes:** Dreamy state of altered consciousness, interrupted normal thought, plentiful but confused ideas
- **Emotional swings:** Exaltation and inner joy may alternate with depression, moodiness, uncontrollable fear of death, and panic
- **Time distortion:** Perception of time is almost invariably altered
- **Auditory effects:** Exaggerated sound perception, hypnotic sense of rhythm

The drug is sometimes not classified as strictly hallucinogenic, as its characteristics are not typically psychotomimetic. Reported aphrodisiac properties have not been substantiated.

### Physiological Effects

Whether cannabis should be classified as a stimulant, depressant, or both has never been definitively determined. Physical effects include:

- Rise in pulse rate and blood pressure
- Tremor and vertigo
- Difficulty in muscular coordination
- Increased tactile sensitivity
