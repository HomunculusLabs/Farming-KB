# Nature Is Neither Living Nor Dead — The Holistic Biosystem

## Summary

Fukuoka articulates a fundamental philosophical principle: nature is neither living nor dead,
small or large, weak or strong. Notions of right and wrong, good and bad, are alien to nature
and are merely human inventions. The living holistic biosystem cannot be dissected or resolved
into its parts — once broken down, it dies. Fukuoka argues that man commits a grave error when
he collects data on dead, fragmented nature and claims to "know," "use," or "conquer" it,
because what he is examining is no longer nature at all.

## Key Concepts

Fukuoka's statement that "properly speaking, nature is neither living nor dead" is one of the

is actually studying is a dead fragment of nature, stripped of the relationships and context
that gave it life and meaning. The conclusions he draws from this study may be internally
consistent and technically precise, but they do not describe nature. They describe something
that exists only in the artificial conditions of the laboratory.

"Man commits a grave error when he collects data and findings piecemeal on a dead and
fragmented nature and claims to 'know,' 'use,' or 'conquer' nature." The verbs Fukuoka
chooses — "know," "use," "conquer" — reveal the attitude he is critiquing. Science does not
approach nature with humility or curiosity; it approaches nature as a resource to be exploited
and a territory to be dominated. The data collected on fragmented nature becomes the basis for
technologies that further fragment and dominate nature, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of
destruction dressed up as progress and knowledge.

The reason everything "winds up all wrong" despite the apparent rationality of scientific
thinking is that "he starts off with misconceptions about nature and takes the wrong approach
to understanding it." If your fundamental premise — that nature can be understood by breaking
it into parts — is false, then no amount of rational analysis built on that premise will lead
to correct conclusions. The error is not in the reasoning but in the starting point. "We must
become aware of the insignificance of human knowledge and activity, and begin by grasping
their uselessness and futility." This is a hard teaching, especially for a civilization that
has invested centuries of effort and billions of resources in the scientific enterprise. But
Fukuoka's point is not that all knowledge is worthless; it is that knowledge gained through
the dissection of living systems is not knowledge of those systems at all. It is knowledge
of something else — something dead and artificial — that has been mistaken for the living
whole.
