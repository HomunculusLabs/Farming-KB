# Drip Irrigation and Microirrigation

[[drip-irrigation]] and microirrigation are alternatives to overhead sprinklers for garden watering. While they have their advocates, both systems have significant limitations for [[dryland-vegetable-gardening]] that are often overlooked in garden books and product catalogs. Understanding these limitations — as well as the situations where these systems shine — helps gardeners make informed choices.

## Drip Irrigation: Not Recommended for Vegetable Gardens

The author used drip tubes on his trials grounds from 1982 through the early 1990s, not because drip was ideal, but because it was the only way to water extensive areas during daylight hours (his property had a well supplying less than 3 GPM / 11 liters per minute).

### Problems with Drip for Vegetables

1. **Expense**: Drip tubes are expensive, even when purchased in 1,000-yard (900 m) rolls
2. **Durability**: They are short-lived and troublesome
3. **Physical damage**: Easily cut by sharp hoes or shovels during cultivation and weeding
4. **Clogging**: Emitter holes tend to plug up, even with water filters. You must carefully inspect the entire system each and every time you turn it on
5. **Shifting**: Drip lines shift many inches from side to side as they expand and contract with temperature changes, so they won't dependably water a line of new seedlings
6. **Germination**: Even less suitable for germinating seeds than for established plants — drip delivers water to a point, not an area
7. **Sandy soils**: Drip systems are absolutely unsuitable on sandy soils because water goes straight down through the soil profile without spreading horizontally

### When Drip Might Be Useful

High-quality long-lasting drip lines might be useful for permanent situations such as:
- Raspberries in heavier soils
- Perennial plantings where the lines won't be disturbed by cultivation
- Situations where water supply is extremely limited

### The Verdict

Given a choice between drip lines and sprinklers for vegetable gardens, sprinklers are always preferred. Drip was used out of necessity (limited well output), not preference.

## Microirrigation: A Better Alternative

New advances in plastics manufacturing have created a hybrid between drip and sprinkling called microirrigation. These systems are a significant improvement over traditional drip for some applications.

### Components

- **Low-pressure plastic tubing** to carry water
- **Quick-disconnect fittings** for corners, plugs, connectors, and tees
- **Plastic spikes** to hold sprinklers
- **Miniature short-radius sprinkler heads** with emission rates measured in gallons (or liters) per hour, not per minute

### Where Microirrigation Works Well

- Under orchard trees
- In vineyards
- Around ornamental beds near houses
- Inside tunnel cloches to keep plants watered for a few weeks until the cloche is removed

### Cost Considerations

Microirrigation bits come bubble-packed in garden centres but are expensive that way. The much broader assortment in agricultural suppliers' shelf bins is significantly cheaper. If considering a microirrigation system, buy from agricultural suppliers.

### Warnings

- **Uniformity**: Be wary about getting uniform water application — distribution is often uneven
- **Filtration**: Use very effective filters — the nozzles are extremely fine and clog easily
- **Fragility**: The small nozzles are easily damaged or dislodged

## Why Sprinklers Are Generally Preferred

### Advantages Over Drip/Micro

1. **Coverage**: Water is distributed over an area, not a point — essential for [[seed-germination-and-stratification]] seedling establishment
2. **Durability**: Sprinkler heads are robust and long-lasting
3. **Flexibility**: Can be moved around the garden as needed
4. **Cost**: Lower initial investment and longer lifespan
5. **Simplicity**: Fewer components to maintain and fewer failure points
6. **Soil benefits**: Proper rainfall-type irrigation helps maintain good soil structure
7. **No clogging**: Open nozzles don't clog like drip emitters

### When to Choose Drip/Micro Instead

- **Extremely limited water supply** (less than 2-3 GPM)
- **Permanent plantings** where lines won't be disturbed
- **Orchards and vineyards** (not vegetables)
- **Protected environments** (greenhouses, cloches) where overhead watering causes disease
- **Steep slopes** where runoff is a problem with overhead irrigation

## Night Watering Considerations

### The Myth

Watering at night is widely believed to be harmful to plants because wet foliage overnight supposedly promotes fungal diseases.

### The Reality

Plants are naturally dampened by dew for much of the night during summer. Watering plants just before dark is usually fine. Night watering is actually the best time if:

- You have a heavy clay soil that can accept water all night without leaching
- You have a limited-output well (no competition from household use)
- Wind is minimal (typical of nighttime), reducing evaporation losses
- You can water from bedtime to breakfast without risk of leaching

### When Night Watering Is Problematic

- In humid climates where fungal diseases are already a serious concern
- With overhead sprinklers on disease-prone crops (tomatoes, beans)
- When temperatures are so mild that the foliage stays wet until mid-morning

## The Ideal Compromise

For most home vegetable gardeners, the best approach is:

1. Use **agricultural-grade low-angle sprinklers** (like Naan 501U) for general garden irrigation
2. Supplement with **fertigation buckets** for large, thirsty plants
3. Consider **microirrigation** only for permanent plantings or protected environments
4. **Skip drip** for vegetable gardens entirely
