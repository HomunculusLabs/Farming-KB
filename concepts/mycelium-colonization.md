# Mycelium Colonization

Mycelium colonization is the phase where fungal mycelium spreads through the
substrate, consuming nutrients and establishing the network that will
eventually produce mushrooms. This stage requires careful environmental
control and patience.

## The Biology of Colonization

Mycelium is the vegetative body of a fungus, consisting of a network of
thread-like hyphae. Each hypha is a single cell-wide tube that grows at its
tip, secreting enzymes to break down organic matter. As hyphae branch and
interconnect, they form a dense white mat called the mycelium. This network
is capable of extraordinary biological activity: decomposing lignin and
cellulose in wood, forming symbiotic relationships with plant roots, and
transmitting chemical signals across meters of substrate.

## Spawn Types

Mushroom cultivation begins with inoculating a substrate with spawn, which
is mycelium grown on a nutritious carrier material:

- **Grain spawn**: Mycelium grown on sterilized grains (rye, millet, wheat).
  Each grain kernel becomes a colonization point, making grain spawn fast
  and aggressive. Standard inoculation rate is 5-10% by weight.
- **Sawdust spawn**: Mycelium on hardwood sawdust. Preferred for outdoor log
  inoculation and some commercial operations. Slower to colonize than grain
  but less prone to contamination.
- **Plug spawn**: Wooden dowels colonized with mycelium, inserted into drilled
  holes in logs. Convenient for outdoor cultivation.
- **Liquid culture**: Mycelium grown in a nutrient broth, often with magnetic
  stirring. Can be used to inoculate grain jars or directly inject into
  substrate bags. Allows very fast expansion from small tissue samples.

## Inoculation Technique

Proper inoculation technique is critical to avoid contamination:

1. Work in a clean area. A still-air box or laminar flow hood is ideal.
2. Flame-sterilize inoculation tools (scalpels, syringes, needles) before
   and between uses.
3. Open containers only briefly and keep them close to the sterilized
   substrate surface.
4. Distribute spawn evenly throughout the substrate for uniform colonization.
5. Seal containers immediately after inoculation.

## Ideal Colonization Conditions

Different species have different temperature preferences, but general
guidelines apply to most cultivated mushrooms:

| Factor | Ideal Range | Notes |
|--------|------------|-------|
| Temperature | 20-26°C (68-79°F) | Species-specific |
| Humidity | High (sealed container) | No need for external humidity |
| Air exchange | Minimal (CO2 buildup) | Filter patches allow gas exchange |
| Light | Darkness or low ambient | Light is not needed; some species prefer dark |
| Duration | 2-6 weeks | Varies by species and substrate volume |

## Stages of Colonization

1. **Lag phase (1-5 days)**: Mycelium adapts to the new substrate. Visible
   growth may be minimal.
2. **Linear growth phase**: Mycelium spreads rapidly outward from inoculation
   points. White, thread-like growth becomes visible on substrate surfaces.
3. **Maturation phase**: Growth slows as the mycelium network densifies.
   The substrate becomes fully colonized, appearing uniformly white.
4. **Consolidation**: After full colonization, waiting an additional 1-2
   weeks allows the mycelium to strengthen its network. This improves
   fruiting performance and resistance to contamination.

## Signs of Healthy Colonization

- Bright white, rope-like mycelium spreading evenly from inoculation points
- Mushroom-like or earthy smell (not sour, ammoniacal, or sweet)
- Substrate firming up as mycelium binds it together
- Occasional aerial hyphae reaching toward the container walls

## Contamination During Colonization

The most common contaminants during colonization:

- **Trichoderma (green mold)**: Fast-growing green patches. Most frequent
  contaminant. Often indicates insufficient [[sterilization]].
- **Aspergillus/Penicillium**: Colored (green, blue, yellow) powdery growth.
- **Bacterial contamination**: Wet, slimy patches with foul odor. Often from
  contaminated grain or insufficient moisture control.
- **Cobweb mold (Dactylium)**: Gray, spider-web-like growth. Thrives in
  high humidity and poor air exchange.

Contaminated jars or bags should be discarded or buried outdoors. Opening
contaminated containers indoors releases millions of spores that compromise
future grows.

## Speed Optimization

To maximize colonization speed:

- Use fresh, vigorous spawn (not old or weakened cultures)
- Inoculate at 10% rather than 5% for faster colonization
- Shake grain jars at 20-30% colonization to redistribute colonized grains
- Maintain optimal temperature for the specific species
- Ensure proper gas exchange through filter patches or loose lids
- Use smaller substrate containers for faster turnaround
