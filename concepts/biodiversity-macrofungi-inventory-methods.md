# Macrofungi Inventory Methods

Inventories of macrofungi aim to document species diversity, habitat
preferences, and geographic distributions of conspicuous fungi at study
sites. Quantifying populations of macrofungi is difficult because
individual mycelia may produce from one to many sporocarps, and
sporocarps represent an unknown fraction of total biomass, which also
includes vegetative [[mycelium]] and sclerotia. Well-designed inventory
methods are essential for meaningful comparisons across sites and through
time.

## Opportunistic Sampling

Opportunistic sampling involves carefully walking through a study site
and collecting conspicuous sporocarps from as many habitats as possible.
This traditional approach, commonly used by taxonomists, provides an
efficient snapshot of the diversity present and is useful for rapid
assessment of potential sites for reserves. However, it does not yield
quantitative data and precludes rigorous comparisons between sites because
sampling effort is not standardized. Collector bias during opportunistic
sampling may cause some habitats or taxa to be overlooked. Despite these
limitations, opportunistic sampling is an important adjunct to plot-based
quantitative methods because additional species will be seen off-plot due
to the patchy distribution of sporocarps.

## Fixed-Size Plot Sampling

### Plot Establishment

Each permanent plot consists of 10 transects that are 100 m long, laid
out parallel to one another at 10-m intervals. Transects are marked every
5 m with a flag or stake, each assigned a unique letter and sequential
number (e.g., A1-A20; B1-B20). A plastic pipe or wooden pole with a rope
1.262 m long circumscribes 5-m2 circular subplots around each flag, giving
20 subplots per transect and 200 subplots total, covering a sampling area
of 1000 m2 (0.1 ha) per plot. Care should be taken not to walk in or
unnecessarily disturb the subplots.

### Sampling Protocol

All macrofungi occurring in a subplot are collected, labeled with the
transect letter and subplot number, and placed in appropriate containers.
At the end of the collecting day, specimens are transported to the field
station for sorting, describing, photographing, and drying. The
substratum (soil, leaf litter, wood) is noted for each specimen. Subplots
should be sampled every 2 weeks during the fruiting season, though
sampling intensity may be reduced with limited resources. Permanent plots
provide good estimates of diversity for a defined area and information on
annual variation in fruiting phenology.

### Plot Frequency as Abundance Measure

Currently, the best measure of species abundance is plot frequency, the
number of subplots in which a taxon occurs, because it reflects the
minimum area occupied by that species. However, because sporocarps of a
species distributed over several subplots may have originated from one
large or several small mycelia, plot frequency provides only a rough
estimate of abundance and importance.

## Sampling Small Ascomycetes Using Microplots

Microplots for microfungi are established adjacent to macrofungal
subplots along transects. Because substrata are collected and destroyed
during examination, different microplots are laid out for each sampling
event. Each person carries a plastic pipe to circumscribe 1-m2 circular
subplots. All microfungi on twigs, branches, and leaves within a plot
are collected, labeled, and examined under a stereomicroscope. Samples
are described, divided, and dried. Microplots should be sampled at
minimum twice per year (middle and end of fruiting season). The careful
examination required for all small substrata makes this approach
time-consuming and labor-intensive.

## Sampling Downed Logs

Large substrata occur at low frequency within small subplots, so a
separate protocol captures fungal diversity on downed logs. Logs more
than 20 cm diameter and 2 m long are selected: 30 logs in each of three
decay classes (Class 1: newly fallen with bark; Class 2: medium rotten;
Class 3: thoroughly rotten). Each log is marked, numbered, and
position-mapped; the tree is identified and dimensions measured. Two to
three sporocarps of common species are collected; infrequent species
are collected more extensively. Logs are sampled four times per year
(dry and rainy seasons) to capture temporal changes.

## Sampling Vegetative Mycelia

Most inventory work focuses on sporocarps, but other structures including
sclerotia, [[mycorrhiza]], mycelia, mycelial strands, cords, and
rhizomorphs can also be sampled. These vegetative structures reveal taxa
present but not fruiting. Molecular analyses of ectomycorrhizae have
shown that substantial numbers of species present on host roots at some
sites rarely fruit. Encouraging fruiting of some ascomycetes and small
[[agar]]ics by placing substrata in humid chambers may increase detection of
litter-inhabiting taxa.

## Extrapolation to Richness

Several methods for estimating total diversity from a limited number of
samples have been developed. The first-order jackknife estimator uses the
formula S* = Sobs + L[(n-1)/n], where S* is estimated total richness,
Sobs is total observed species, L is species found in only one sample,
and n is the number of samples. Chao's second estimator (Chao 2) uses
S* = Sobs + (L2/2M), where M is species found in exactly two samples.
This algorithm performs particularly well with data having a preponderance
of rare taxa, a situation frequently encountered with fungi.

## Recommended Integrated Approach

An integrated set of sampling protocols maximizes the number of
macrofungal species recorded at a site. This includes opportunistic
sampling to capture conspicuous fungi outside plots, fixed-size plot
sampling for quantitative data on soil and litter fungi, microplots for
small ascomycetes, and log sampling for lignicolous species. Repeated
sampling over time ensures that inconspicuous fungi and fungi on scarce
substrata are included. These protocols are being used in national fungal
inventories and yield standardized data comparable across sites and
studies.

## Documentation and Archiving

Specimen processing involves written descriptions, photographs, [[spore]]
prints, cultures, and macrochemical tests. Voucher specimens must be
preserved and archived in recognized herbaria. The greatest constraints on
macrofungal diversity studies are the paucity of fungal taxonomists and
identification resources. No region of the world yet has a complete
mycota equivalent to a vascular-plant flora. Project planning should
include background literature research on the vegetation, geology, and
likely fungal taxa of the study area, as well as pilot studies to
determine the sampling intensity required.
