# Mycorrhizae and Plant Succession

## Overview

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] play a fundamental role in plant succession — the directional process of community change following disturbance or colonization of new substrate. By enhancing plant nutrient acquisition, mycorrhizae influence which species successfully establish, persist, and ultimately dominate during seral succession. The availability of mycorrhizal inoculum, its spatial distribution, and the functional diversity of mycorrhizal fungal communities all shape the trajectory and composition of successional plant communities.

## Mycorrhizal Inoculum as a Determinant of Succession

One of the major components determining the success of early colonizing plants during seral succession is nutrient availability. The ability of plants to associate with mycorrhizal fungi and enhance their capacity to sequester nutrients from limited resources provides a competitive advantage. The dispersal of spores of hypogeous fungi by rodents is an important determinant of mycorrhizal inoculum for plants establishing on bare ground.

The distribution of mycorrhizal fungal spores by animals is rarely random. Small mammals defecate in middens and deposit more spores in areas of active feeding sites than in other localities. This patchy distribution of mycorrhizal inoculum potential influences the type of plant that can be successful in each microhabitat. During primary colonization, mycorrhizal inoculum potential can vary from none to abundant in locations only centimeters apart.

## Patterns of Mycorrhizal Dependence in Succession

M.F. Allen (1991) compared the importance of mycorrhizae in re-establishment of vegetation following disturbance across various ecosystems. Key observations include:

- **Mount Saint Helens**: Vegetation consisted entirely of mycorrhizal species, both arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) and ectomycorrhizal (ECM) forms.
- **Galapagos Islands**: A mixture of AM and nonmycorrhizal plants established. The distribution of mycorrhizal associations was related to soil nutrient content, with nonmycorrhizal plants developing in more fertile lowland soils and mycorrhizal plants establishing in poorer rocky soils.

Allen and Allen (1990) hypothesized patterns of mycorrhizal dependence in developing ecosystems relative to nutrient and water availability, proposing that mycorrhizae regulate plant competition during seral succession through these resource-mediated pathways.

## Case Studies in Primary Succession

### Mount Koma, Japan

On volcanic substrates of Mt. Koma, Titus and Tsuyuzaki (2002) found no effect of microsite on the AM colonization of *Agrostis scabra*. *Campanula lasiocarpa*, however, showed higher root colonization by AM fungi near rock than on flat sites or those occupied by *Polygonum*. Willow (*Salix reinii*) was heavily ectomycorrhizal across all sites. These data suggest that successional mycorrhizal models are not only dependent on environmental factors but are also plant species-dependent.

### Lyman Glacier Forefront

The Lyman Glacier forefront provides a well-studied example of mycorrhizal involvement in primary succession. Spores of a variety of fungal genera appear in the feces of pika, voles, chipmunks, marmots, mountain goats, and mule deer on the glacier forefront, forming an inoculum source for colonization of newly developing soils by early successional and slow-growing tree species including *[[abies-lasiocarpa]]*, *Larix lyallii*, *[[tsuga-mertensiana]]*, and *Salix* spp.

#### Safe Sites for Colonization

Jumpponen et al. (1999) identified "safe sites" on the glacier outwash where plant colonization was most likely. These sites consisted of concave surfaces of coarse rocky particles, ideal for trapping tree seeds and protecting them from desiccation. These microsites also formed foci for foraging small mammals (attracted by seeds), where deposition of mycorrhizal spore-laden feces further enhanced survival of germinating tree seedlings.

#### Dark-Septate Mycorrhizal Fungi in Early Succession

The dark-septate mycorrhizal fungus *[[phialocephala-fortinii]]* significantly enhanced growth of lodgepole pine (*[[pinus-contorta]]*), an early colonizer of the glacier forefront, but only in the presence of added nitrogen. Total plant phosphorus, however, was significantly enhanced by the mycorrhiza even without added nitrogen. This demonstrates how mycorrhizal benefits vary depending on which nutrient is limiting in the successional environment.

#### Microbial Community Succession

During plant succession on recent glacial till, microbial communities shift from bacterial domination to fungal-dominated communities. During this transition, carbon-use efficiency changes from high carbon respiration to an accumulating phase, indicating that fungi are a stabilizing force in developing ecosystems and facilitate net carbon fixation into biomass.

## Ectomycorrhizal Succession

### Species Turnover Through Succession

The existence of successions of ectomycorrhizal species during primary succession is supported by findings from the Lyman Glacier forefront. Across different plant successional stages, 68 ectomycorrhizal species belonging to 25 genera were identified, with no single ectomycorrhizal species occurring on all three successional sites.

### Peak Diversity at Canopy Closure

Ectomycorrhizal species diversity increases to a maximum where tree canopies start to overlap. This corresponds to observations from other studies where increased diversity of ectomycorrhizal fungi at canopy closure may relate to:

- The relative paucity of available nutrients (particularly phosphorus)
- An increasing proportion of nutrients locked up in organic forms

The increased diversity of mycorrhizal fungi at this stage may allow greater expression of mycorrhizal function to utilize mixed available resources of both inorganic and organic nutrients. The diversity of ectomycorrhizae growing into different tree litters reflects appropriate enzyme functions in relation to the relative availability of inorganic nutrients. Where phosphorus is immobilized during early stages of leaf litter decomposition, the ectomycorrhizal community contains a greater proportion of acid phosphatase-producing mycorrhizal types.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Succession

### Driver vs. Passenger Hypotheses

The succession of arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi on herbaceous plant roots is less obvious than that of ectomycorrhizal fungi, yet different AM species have contrasting effects on host plant performance. Hart et al. (2001) proposed two hypotheses to explain successional changes in AM fungal communities:

1. **Driver Hypothesis**: Mycorrhizal fungi are the driving force — they determine which plant species establish through interspecific differences in colonization and persistence potential.
2. **Passenger Hypothesis**: Changes in mycorrhizal species are dependent on plant community composition and environmental conditions; the mycorrhizae are passive followers of plant community dynamics.

These two hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, and the reality likely involves elements of both mechanisms operating at different stages or under different environmental conditions.

## The Importance of Mycelial Networks

Maintaining a continuous mycelial mat of mycorrhizal fungi is critical for rapid mycorrhizal development during colonization. Amaranthus and Perry (1989) demonstrated that when Douglas fir was planted into partially cleared sites where mycorrhizal roots were maintained on remaining trees, survival of newly planted trees was approximately 90%. In contrast, trees planted into totally cleared areas showed only 50% survival after 2 years.

The existing mycelial network likely provides greater ecosystem stability by allowing carbon and nutrient exchange between connected plants. New recruits can access a larger pool of nutrients and carbon than they could independently. This synergistic activity between surviving mature plants and recruits allows greater ecosystem stability and survival of the same plant species composition following disturbance.

## Saprotrophic Fungi and Plant Establishment

Even saprotrophic fungi can influence plant establishment during succession. Inoculation of the pulpwood tree *Gmelina arborea* seed with the fungus *Chaetomium bostrychodes* has been shown to improve seed germination. The probable mechanism involves enzyme production by fungal hyphae assisting in seed stratification or replacement of the scarification process, thereby breaking seed dormancy more effectively.

## Spore Dispersal Mechanisms in Succession

### Vertebrate Dispersal

Small mammals including the Oregon vole (*Microtus oregoni*) and the chickaree (*Tamiasciurus douglasi*) disperse viable spores of both AM fungi (*Glomus macrocarpus*) and hypogeous ECM fungi (*Hymenogaster*). Spores survive gut passage and assist in colonizing bare ground. The tassel-eared squirrel (*Sciurus aberti*) similarly disperses a variety of ectomycorrhizal fungal spores that survive gut passage and develop mycorrhizal associations with ponderosa pine.

### Midden Effects

Both hypogeous and epigeous mycorrhizal fungi are consumed by mycophagous mammals, resulting in deposition of viable spores in feces. The local concentration of feces in middens by small mammals accounts for the patchy distribution of mycorrhizal spores in the environment. This patchiness creates a mosaic of inoculum potential that differentially favors establishment of various plant species depending on their mycorrhizal dependence.

## Synthesis

Mycorrhizal fungi are central to plant succession through multiple mechanisms: enhancing nutrient acquisition of colonizing plants, creating patchy inoculum landscapes through animal-mediated spore dispersal, maintaining mycelial networks that stabilize plant communities, and shifting in diversity and function as succession progresses. The interplay between driver and passenger dynamics, combined with the physical and biological creation of "safe sites" for plant establishment, makes mycorrhizae indispensable architects of successional plant communities.
