# Mushroom Stump Cultivation

## Overview

Stumps are especially suitable for [[growing-gourmet-mushrooms-civilization-history]]. There are few better, or more massive platforms, than the stump. Millions of stumps are all that remain of many forests of the world. In most cases, stumps are seen as having little or no economic potential. These lone tombstones of biodegradable wood fiber offer a unique, new opportunity for the mycologically astute. With selective logging being increasingly practiced, cultivating gourmet and medicinal mushrooms on stumps will be the wave of the future.

## Advantages of Stump Cultivation

The advantage of the stump is not only its sheer mass, but with roots intact, water is continuously being drawn via capillary action through the dead wood cells from the underlying soil base. Once mycelium has permeated through wood fiber, the stump's water carrying capacity is increased, thus further supporting mycelial growth. The advantages of growing on stumps can be summarized as:

1. **Developing a new, environmentally friendly wood products-based industry** that adds value to forest management practices
2. **Recycling wood debris of little or no economic value** into valuable food and medicine
3. **Prevention of disease vectors from parasitic fungi** by pre-emptively inoculating stumps with beneficial species before harmful ones colonize
4. **Rapidly returning organic nutrients into the food chain**, benefitting other citizens of the forest community and invigorating the ecosystem

## Selecting Candidate Stumps

Candidates for stump culture must be carefully selected and matched with the appropriate species. Key selection criteria include:

- **Shade**: A stump partially or fully shaded is obviously better than one in full sunlight. Stumps in ravines are better candidates than those located in the center of a clear-cut
- **Moisture**: The presence of mosses, lichens, and/or ferns is a good indicator that the microclimate is conducive to mushroom growth
- **Freshness**: Stumps should be inoculated before the first season of wild mushrooms. With each mushroom season, the air becomes laden with spores, seeking new habitats. The open face of a stump, essentially a wound, is highly susceptible to colonization by wild mushrooms
- **Competitor absence**: The presence of competitor fungi generally disqualifies a stump as a good candidate
- **Species**: An uprooted stump is not as good a candidate as a well-rooted one

### The Critical Importance of Timing

If stumps are not inoculated within several months of being cut, the probability of success decreases. Therefore, old stumps are poor candidates. Even so, years may pass after inoculation before mushrooms form on a stump. The fastest recorded production from inoculation is 8 weeks, when an oak stump was inoculated with plug spawn of Chicken-of-the-Woods, Laetiporus (Polyporus) sulphureus. Notably, the stump face was checkered, with multiple fissures running vertically through the innermost regions of the wood. These fissures trapped water from rainfall and promoted fast mycelial growth.

## Stump Size and Longevity

Small-diameter stumps rot faster and produce crops of mushrooms sooner than bigger stumps. However, the smaller stump has a shorter mushroom-producing life span than the older stump. Often times with large diameter stumps, mushroom formation is triggered when competitors are encountered and/or coupled with wet weather conditions.

In a Colonial graveyard in New York state, a four-foot diameter oak has consistently produced clusters of Maitake mushrooms (Grifola frondosa), sometimes weighing up to 100 lbs. apiece, for more than 20 years. This remarkable longevity demonstrates that stump cultivation can be a multi-decade investment with sustained returns.

## Species Suitable for Stump Cultivation

### Primary Gourmet Species

- **Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)**: The classic log and stump mushroom; prefers dense hardwoods like oak
- **Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.)**: Broad adaptability; multiple species suitable including P. ostreatus, P. pulmonarius, P. eryngii
- **Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus)**: Medicinal and gourmet; produces distinctive cascading spines
- **Nameko (Pholiota nameko)**: Popular in Japanese cuisine; amber-colored with gelatinous caps
- **Chicken-of-the-Woods ([[laetiporus-sulphureus]])**: Fast colonizer; fruits within 8 weeks on appropriate stumps

### Medicinal Species

- **Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum)**: Premier medicinal mushroom; produces distinctive lacquered conks
- **Maitake (Grifola frondosa)**: Both gourmet and medicinal; can produce enormous clusters on large oak stumps
- **Turkey Tail (Trametes versicolor)**: Widespread medicinal polypore; source of PSK and PSP

### Clustered Wood Lovers

- **[[growing-gourmet-hypholoma-capnoides-cultivation]]** and **H. sublateritium**: Naturally colonize stumps and wood debris; chestnut-flavored caps

## Polyculture on Stumps

Large diameter stumps can harbor many communities of mushrooms. On old-growth or second-growth Douglas fir stumps common to the forests of Washington state, finding several species of mushrooms is not unusual. This natural example of "polyculture," the simultaneous concurrence of more than one species in a single habitat, should encourage experimentally inclined cultivators. Mushroom landscapes of great complexity could be designed.

## Ecological Considerations

### Disease Vector Prevention

Several polypores are especially good candidates for stump cultivation, particularly Maitake (Grifola frondosa) and Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum). As the anti-cancer properties of these mushrooms become better understood, new strategies for their cultivation will be developed. Stamets envisions the establishment of Maitake and Reishi mushroom tree farms wherein stumps are purposely created and selectively inoculated for maximum mushroom growth, interspersed amongst shade trees.

A number of Honey mushrooms, belonging to the genus Armillaria, can operate as both saprophytes or parasites. Should clear-cuts become colonized with these deadly, root-rotting species, satellite colonies can be spread to adjacent, living trees. Now that burning is increasingly restricted because of air pollution concerns, disease vectors coming from stump-age could present a new, as yet unmeasured, threat to the forest ecosystem. Purposeful inoculation of stumps with beneficial species can forestall invasion by parasitic fungi like Honey Mushrooms.

### Old-Growth Conservation

On a well-travelled trail in the Snoqualmie Forest of Washington State, hikers have been stepping upon the largest and oldest Polypore: Oxyporus nobilissimus, a conk that grows up to several feet in diameter and which can weigh hundreds of pounds. This species grows only on old-growth Abies procera (California red fir) or on their stumps. Less than a dozen specimens have ever been collected. Known only from the old-growth forests of the Pacific Northwest, the Noble Polypore's ability to produce a conk that lives for more than 25 years suggests unique anti-rotting properties from antibiotics or other compounds useful medicinally. With the loss of old-growth forests, cultivator-mycologists can play an all-important role in saving the fungal genome from the old-growth forest, a potential treasure trove of new medicines.

## Inoculation Methods

### Plug Spawn Method

Plug spawn can be inserted into the open face of each stump. If the stumps are checkered through with cracks, the plugs are best inserted directly into the fissures. This method is simple and requires minimal tools: a drill, plug spawn dowels, and wax for sealing.

### Wedge or Disc Inoculation Technique

Using a chain saw, a wedge is cut or a shallow disc is sliced from the open face of the stump. The newly cut faces are packed with sawdust spawn. The cut disc is then replaced. By hammering a few nails into the stump, firm contact between the cut faces is assured. This method allows for higher inoculation rates and faster colonization compared to plug spawn.

### Wood Species Matching

The broad-leaf hardwoods are easier to saprophytize with the gourmet and medicinal mushroom species described in Stamets' book than the softwood pines. Within the hardwood group, rapidly growing species such as alders and poplars decompose more rapidly and hence give an earlier crop than the denser hardwoods such as oaks. However, the denser and more massive stumps sustain colonies of mushrooms for many more years than the quick-to-rot, smaller diameter tree species. Spring inoculations give the mycelium the longest possible growing season.

Among the most suitable hardwood species for stump cultivation are:

- **Oak (Quercus spp.)**: Dense, long-lasting; excellent for Shiitake, Maitake, and Reishi. Oak stumps can produce for 20+ years
- **Alder (Alnus spp.)**: Fast-growing, soft hardwood; colonizes quickly, good for Oyster and Nameko. Shorter production span but faster returns
- **Poplar/Cottonwood (Populus spp.)**: Very fast decomposition; ideal for Oyster mushrooms. Production may peak in 2-3 years
- **Maple (Acer spp.)**: Moderate density; versatile substrate for many species
- **Birch (Betula spp.)**: Good for Oyster and Lion's Mane; decomposes at moderate rate
- **Willow (Salix spp.)**: Fast-growing; suitable for Oyster cultivation

Conifers are generally less suitable for most [[gourmet-mushroom-species-guide]], though some fungi adapted to coniferous forests (such as certain Larch-loving species) may be exceptions. The Noble Polypore (Oxyporus nobilissimus) is restricted to California red fir (Abies procera), demonstrating that specialized stump-fungus relationships exist even among conifer species.

## Published Research on Stump Cultivation

Pagony (1973) described the cultivation of Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus) on large diameter poplars with a 100% success rate. Inoculations occurred in the spring for fruitings which began in the ensuing fall, and continued for several years hence. An average of four pounds of Oyster mushrooms were harvested over four years (approximately 1 lb./year/stump).

Hilber (1982) reported on the utility of using natural wood (logs and stumps) for growing Oyster mushrooms, finding that per cubic meter of elm wood, the yield from one season averaged 17-22 kilograms.

Anselmi and Deandrea (1979) studied poplar and willow stumps inoculated with [[oyster-mushroom-spawn-preparation]], showing that this mushroom favored wood from newly felled trees, in zones which received speckled sunlight. Their study confirmed that Pleurotus ostreatus only attacked dead wood and never became parasitic, supporting the view that purposeful stump inoculation can prevent invasion by harmful parasitic species.
