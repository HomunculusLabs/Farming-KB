# Critique of Mechanization in Agriculture

Fukuoka's analysis of agricultural mechanization goes beyond the typical
environmental critique to expose a deeper structural paradox: mechanization
does not free the farmer but enslaves him, does not increase productivity
but destroys it, and does not feed the world but accelerates the depletion
of the land that sustains it.

## Machinery as the Largest Domestic Animal

Fukuoka characterizes the tractor as "the largest domestic animal of all —
a great guzzler of oil, a consumer good rather than a capital good." At
first glance, mechanized agriculture appears to increase productivity per
worker and thus raise income. However, examining the efficiency of land
utilization and energy consumption reveals it to be "an extremely
destructive method of farming."

The reasoning that seduces farmers is deceptively simple: if a horse can
do the plowing better than a man, and a ten-horsepower tractor is cheaper
than ten horses, then mechanization is progress. This logic appears
reasonable within a currency-based economic system but ignores the
progressive inorganic character and lowered productivity of the land that
results from large-volume production operations.

## The Surplus Labor Trap

A two-acre farmer who purchases a 30-horsepower tractor does not magically
become a 50-acre farmer unless the amount of land under cultivation
increases. If land is limited, mechanization only reduces the number of
laborers required. This surplus manpower begets leisure, which supposedly
can be applied to other work to increase income. The problem is that this
extra income cannot come from the land. In fact, crop yields will probably
decrease while energy requirements skyrocket.

The end result: the farmer is driven from his fields by his own machinery.
The use of machinery may make working the fields easier, but revenue from
crop production has shrunk. Yet taxes do not decrease, and the costs of
mechanization continue to climb.

## The Vicious Cycle of Chemical Dependency

Machinery, chemical fertilizers, and pesticides have drawn the farmer away
from nature. Although these products do not actually raise land yields,
because they are promoted as tools for boosting profits, farmers labor under
the illusion that they need them. Their use has wrought destruction on
nature, robbing it of its powers and leaving man no choice but to tend vast
fields by hand. This in turn makes large machinery, high-grade compound
fertilizers, and powerful poisons indispensable — and the same vicious
cycle continues without end.

## Scale Does Not Bring Stability

Larger and larger agricultural operations have not given farmers the
stability they seek. Farms in Europe are ten times larger, and in the
United States one hundred times larger, than the 6- to 7-acre farms common
to Japan. Yet farmers in Europe and the U.S. are, if anything, even more
insecure than Japanese farmers. The trend toward large-scale mechanized
agriculture has not solved the fundamental problems of farming — it has
amplified them.

## The Destruction of Soil Through Heavy Machinery

Fukuoka identifies specific ways that heavy machinery degrades farmland:

1. **Uneven topsoil**: Crude land-leveling leaves topsoil thickness that
  varies with bedrock depth, resulting in uneven crop growth.
2. **Soil compaction**: Heavy machinery causes excessive settling, leading
  to stagnant groundwater, root rotting, and suppression of initial crop
  growth.
3. **Microbial destruction**: Concrete levees and walkways destroy soil
  microbial communities, gradually turning living soil into dead mineral
  matter.

## The Spiritual Cost

Beyond the economic and ecological damage, Fukuoka laments the spiritual
loss. Farmers have "exchanged the joy of working outdoors in the open
fields for dreary hours of labor shut up inside a factory." The reduction
in labor brought about by scientific farming has succeeded only in forcing
farmers off the land. For every tractor operator, dozens of farmers are
driven from their fields and forced into factory work producing the very
implements and chemicals that natural farming does not need.

## The Illusion of Progress

Fukuoka frames mechanization within a broader critique of the illusion of
human progress through technology. "Today's technology will become the
dated technology of tomorrow, and tomorrow's reforms will become the
reforms of the day after." Each technological advance requires further
advances to address the problems it creates. The only exit from this
escalating spiral is to relinquish human knowledge and action entirely,
allowing nature to exercise its full powers.

The path forward is not better technology but less technology — returning
to the "do-nothing" approach where nature produces food through its own
processes, and the farmer's role is to assist rather than to dominate.

## The Farmer as Factory Worker

Fukuoka describes how the farmer's role has been transformed from
steward of the land to a cog in an industrial system. "The farmer
today has become a hired hand of industrialized society. He tries
without success to make money at farming with synthetic chemicals, a
feat that would tax even the powers of the Thousand-Handed Goddess
of Mercy. It is no surprise then that he is spinning around like a
top."

This transformation is not merely economic but spiritual. The farmer
who once derived satisfaction from direct communion with the land —
observing seasons, feeling soil, celebrating harvests — now operates
machinery and applies chemicals according to schedules determined by
external authorities. The knowledge of the land that once accumulated
across generations is rendered obsolete by standardized protocols
designed for maximum short-term output rather than long-term health.

The recovery of farming, for Fukuoka, requires not better technology
but a fundamentally different relationship between farmer and land —
one based on observation and partnership rather than domination and
control.
