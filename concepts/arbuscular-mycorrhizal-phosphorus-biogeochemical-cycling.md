# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Phosphorus Uptake and Biogeochemical Cycling

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi are the dominant mycorrhizal type in temperate grasslands, tropical forests, and agricultural systems. In these environments, rapid decomposition means most soil nutrients exist in inorganic form, with phosphorus typically being the primary limiting nutrient. AM fungi enhance plant [[dighton-mycorrhizal-phosphatase-phosphorus-acquisition]] through three complementary mechanisms: exploitation of a larger soil volume, accelerated phosphate transport via hyphal networks, and the ability to solubilize complex phosphate compounds.

## The Three Mechanisms of Mycorrhizal Phosphorus Enhancement

Bolan (1991) identified three rate-limiting steps in plant phosphorus uptake that AM associations help overcome:

1. **Release from soil particles**: AM fungi produce enzymes and organic acids that chemically modify the phosphate release mechanism
2. **Diffusion to the root surface**: Extraradical hyphae decrease the effective diffusion distance by exploring soil far beyond the root depletion zone
3. **Uptake across cell membranes**: AM fungi reduce the threshold concentration of P required for transfer across plant cell membranes

Together, these mechanisms allow mycorrhizal plants to access phosphorus that would otherwise be unavailable or only slowly available to non-mycorrhizal roots.

## Organic Phosphorus Utilization

While AM fungi are generally considered less enzyme-competent than ericoid or [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]], they are nonetheless capable of accessing organic phosphorus sources:

### Phosphatase Enzyme Production

AM fungi can produce phosphatase enzymes to solubilize poorly available phosphates (Azcón et al., 1976; Singh and Kapoor, 1998). Jayachandran et al. (1992) demonstrated this dramatically in big bluestem grass (*[[andropogon-gerardii]]*):

- **Non-mycorrhizal plants**: Could access phosphorus from glycerophosphate and adenosine monophosphate, but NOT from phytic acid, RNA, ATP, or CMP
- **Mycorrhizal plants (with *Glomus etunicatum*)**: Could access ALL forms of organic phosphorus, with uptake 500- to 600-fold higher than non-mycorrhizal plants

This demonstrates that AM fungi dramatically expand the range of organic phosphorus compounds accessible to their hosts.

## Phosphorus and Zinc Uptake in Calcareous Soils

### The Challenge of Calcareous Soils

In calcareous soils, phosphate is limited because of complexing with heavy metal ions (particularly iron and calcium). Tyler (1994) showed that calcifuge plant species (*Carex pilulifera*, *[[deschampsia-flexuosa]]*, *Holcus mollis*, *Luzula pilosa*, *Nardus stricta*, *Veronica officinalis*) cannot grow on limestone because they cannot decouple iron-phosphate complexes.

### Calcicole Adaptation

Calcicole species have developed mechanisms for acquiring both P and Fe from calcareous soils through production of organic acids in the rhizosphere. Ström (1997) documented dramatic differences in [[staycare-fungal-organic-acid-production-metal-leaching]]:

| Strategy | Species | Total Organic Acid (mmol m⁻³) |
|----------|---------|------------------------------|
| Calcifuge | *Deschampsia* | 17.3 |
| Calcifuge | *Viscaria* | 11.7 |
| Calcicole | *Gypsophila* | 40.8 |
| Calcicole | *Sanguisorba* | 32.6 |

Calcicoles produced substantially more dicarboxylic (malic, succinic, tartaric) and tricarboxylic (oxalic, citric) acids, which solubilize phosphate and essential heavy metals. While mycorrhizal involvement was not explicitly tested, evidence suggests fungi can produce organic acids that enhance this process (Azcón et al., 1976; Bolan, 1991; Singh and Kapoor, 1998).

### Mycorrhizal Enhancement in Wheat

Goh et al. (1997) showed that AM colonization of wheat roots in calcareous soil significantly increased the availability of both phosphorus and zinc, although this effect was not reflected in plant growth. Clark and Zeto (2000) further noted that AM fungi enhance nitrogen uptake as well, though this may be an indirect effect of greater plant size from overcoming phosphate limitations.

## Interaction with Nitrogen-Fixing Legumes

AM associations are particularly important for nitrogen-fixing leguminous plants. Phosphorus is required in substantial quantities for nitrogen fixation in root nodules, and AM fungi assist phosphate delivery to maximize nitrogen fixation efficiency (Azcón-Aguilar et al., 1979; Peoples and Craswell, 1992; Herrera et al., 1993). There is also evidence that AM fungi can utilize organic forms of nitrogen (Ames et al., 1983).

## Soil Structure and Fertility Maintenance

Beyond nutrient uptake, AM fungi contribute to soil health through:

1. **Aggregate formation**: Mycelia bind soil particles together, creating stable aggregates
2. **Organic matter contribution**: Glomalin and other fungal compounds help develop and maintain soil aggregates
3. **Aggregate stability**: These processes help retain soil fertility over time (Wright and Upadhyaya, 1998)

Jeffries and Barea (1994) reviewed the role of AM fungi in biogeochemical cycling and sustainability, emphasizing contributions to plant nutrition, root pathogen prevention, and soil structure maintenance.

## Species-Level Differences in AM Function

While the AM fungal community is less taxonomically diverse than ECM communities, there are functional differences among species. Dodd (1994) cited work by Jakobsen et al. (1992a, 1992b) showing that *Acaulospora laevis* exploits a greater soil volume than *Glomus* spp., obtaining phosphorus from greater distances from the root surface.

### Host Plant Control of Mycorrhizal Function

Koide et al. (2000) demonstrated that the AM effect varies among host plant species:
- **Lactuca**: AM colonization increased phosphorus uptake rate by 23% but reduced phosphorus use efficiency
- **Abutilon**: AM colonization increased phosphorus uptake rate by 32% with no change in use efficiency, resulting in significant growth increase
- **Beta**: No effect of AM colonization on either uptake or efficiency

This indicates that the outcome of AM colonization influences plant community fitness beyond simple growth enhancement, potentially affecting competitive interactions among species.

## Ecological Significance in Natural Ecosystems

### The Fitter Paradox

Fitter (1985) noted that AM associations often show minimal nutritional benefit in natural ecosystems compared to laboratory conditions. Possible explanations include:

1. **Plant interconnectedness**: [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]] may redistribute nutrients among plants
2. **Faunal grazing**: Collembola and nematodes reduce mycorrhizal function
3. **Root longevity differences**: Natural root systems differ from artificial growing conditions

Rangeley et al. (1982) found no effect of AM inoculation on clover growth in acid grassland without added phosphorus, but positive responses in higher-fertility brown earth soil in the second year.

### Seasonal Benefits

Sanders and Fitter (1992a, 1992b) found minimal mycorrhizal influence on phosphorus and heavy metal uptake by natural grasses, but suggested benefits may occur seasonally during periods of low [[phosphorus-availability]] and high plant demand.

### The Bluebell Case Study

Merryweather and Fitter (1995a, 1995b, 1996) provided compelling evidence for the ecological importance of AM associations in bluebell (*Hyacinthoides non-scripta*):

1. Bluebell is obligately mycorrhizal
2. As bulbs age and descend deeper into soil where phosphorus is depleted, AM colonization increases
3. Enhanced phosphorus uptake maintains fecundity (bulb diameter)
4. When benomyl was applied to reduce AM colonization, phosphorus concentrations in vegetative parts decreased, but preferential allocation to flowers and seeds was maintained — suggesting plant control over fitness-related nutrient allocation

### Faunal Grazing Effects

Collembolan grazing on AM extraradical hyphae reduces nutrient acquisition efficiency, particularly for phosphorus (Warnock et al., 1982). This effect is more pronounced in agroecosystems where reduced faunal diversity allows high collembola densities. Nematode feeding on mycorrhizal hyphae can also reduce effectiveness and alter plant competitive fitness (Brussard et al., 2001).
