# [[agaricus-campestris]] and related species are cultivated, fermented horse manure remains the nutrient substratum employed.

The best results are obtained only when the manure is hauled fresh from the stables and permitted to undergo a definite type of fermentation.

This principle held absolute sway in the mushroom industry from the 19th century through the early 20th century, and many of its essential tenets remain relevant to modern cultivation.

The transformation of raw stable manure into proper mushroom compost is arguably the most critical step in the entire cultivation process.

Failures in mushroom growing can most often be traced to deficiencies in compost preparation rather than any other single factor.

## Selecting Quality Manure

In selecting manure for mushroom work, only that which is recognized as of the highest quality should be taken.

It is the general experience that considerable bedding straw should be present with the manure, and if good straw, it is not likely to contain too much.

The straw of the various grains possesses distinct advantages over that of other grasses, perhaps on account of a certain resistance to complete fermentation or decay.

This resistance yields a highly porous substratum that maintains an excellent physical condition in the beds.

Unfortunately, Duggar noted, it was becoming increasingly difficult in some cities to obtain straw manure.

Animals were bedded with straw less and less, sawdust and shavings being the cheaper substitutes commonly used.

These bedding substitutes do not render the manure unfit for mushroom work, and compost containing these substances was extensively used even in Duggar's time.

However, in order to exclude poor material, growers were advised to avoid manure from veterinary hospitals or from stables that used disinfectants freely.

Any short, trashy compost does not make the best substratum for mushrooms.

It is probably less retentive of moisture and provides inferior structure for [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]].

## The Ideal Manure Profile

The French ideal was manure from grain-fed animals bedded with [[coleman-rye-straw-mat-cold-frame-insulation]].

When the French government decreed that other cereal straws should be substituted for rye in the cavalry stables to save expense, there was a strong but futile outcry on the part of French mushroom growers.

This demonstrates the traditional conservatism of the craft and the empirical knowledge that specific manure types produced superior results, even if the underlying reasons were not fully understood at the time.

Manure from animals fed largely upon grass had not proved satisfactory in tests.

French experience also ruled out manure obtained from stables employing chiefly leguminous fodders.

It had been demonstrated satisfactorily that manure from grain-fed animals yielded a more vigorous bed of spawn than any other.

Duggar was convinced that many growers insisted on a type of stable manure containing too little straw for the best results.

The balance between nitrogen-rich droppings and carbon-rich straw bedding was critical to achieving the proper carbon-to-nitrogen ratio in the finished compost.

## The Fermentation Process

In the fermentation of manure, the same end may be obtained by a variety of methods.

It is necessary to understand the principles involved rather than following a rigid recipe.

The essentials are adequate moisture at all times and sufficient forking over, or turning, to effect equal fermentation action and uniformity throughout.

Sometimes turning is also needed to force a temperature decline when the pile becomes too hot.

The manure contains many soluble organic substances which invite a vigorous development of mould fungi if it is not properly fermented.

During the fermentation process, the common mould fungi do not, as a rule, develop profusely.

Instead, bacterial and direct chemical action is facilitated.
