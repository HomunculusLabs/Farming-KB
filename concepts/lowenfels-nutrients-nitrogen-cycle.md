## Nitrogen Cycle and Organic Nitrogen Sources

## Overview

Nitrogen (N) is crucial to plant growth and is often the limiting nutrient in gardens. It is the
backbone of amino acids, the structural building blocks of proteins. No nitrogen means no
proteins, and no proteins means no enzymes. Nitrogen is also the base element for nucleotide
molecules, the building blocks of DNA and RNA, and is an essential part of chlorophyll
(C55H72MgN4O5). Most plants in a garden are about 3 to 4 percent nitrogen by weight. The Earth's
atmosphere consists of 78 percent nitrogen, but atmospheric nitrogen (N2) is off limits to plants
because nitrogen atoms form extremely strong triple covalent bonds.

## The Nitrogen Cycle

Atmospheric nitrogen (N2) is so tightly bound that it is unavailable for use in most biological
systems. A tiny fraction is made available by electrical and snow storms, but it was the job of
specialized microorganisms to break these bonds and make nitrogen available to plants. Some
nitrogen-fixing bacteria live in [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]], and only about 50 to 60
percent of [[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria-and-archaea-in-soil]] into NO3-. Some nitrate is absorbed by plants and assimilated, some remains
in the soil, and the rest washes out of the soil.

When soil or water containing nitrogen becomes anaerobic, nitrates are converted back to
atmospheric nitrogen by anaerobic microbes that use nitrogen compounds as their energy source.
This denitrification is a big loss for the gardener but completes the nitrogen cycle.

## Biological Nitrogen Fixation

Soil microbes responsible for making nitrogen usable by plants are known as diazotrophs. Up to
50 percent of the nitrogen in a garden can come from nitrogen-fixing organisms.

### Rhizobia

The most familiar diazotrophs are Rhizobia, soil bacteria that form symbiotic relationships with
legumes. Rhizobia provide the enzymes necessary to break apart triple-bonded atmospheric nitrogen
molecules, and the plant provides the housing for this activity as well as carbon-based exudates
that the bacteria consume. The amount of oxygen has to be limited for the enzymes to work, and
the root nodules provide such an environment. There is enough nitrogen for the legume and the
bacteria to share. Up to 20 percent excess nitrogen is produced as well, and it moves into the
soil and soil food web, where much of it is brought to plants by [[comparison-aronia-vs-goji-vs-sea-buckthorn]]. Most of these are pioneer plants, alder in
particular, and their symbiotic relationships with Frankia bring needed plant-available nitrogen
into poor soils.

### Free-Living Fixers

Azotobacter and Azospirillum are free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria that produce nitrogen
without entering into a symbiotic relationship with plants. They are frequently used when growing
cereal crops and are being developed for gardening use.

## Organic Nitrogen Sources

Good natural sources of nitrogen include alfalfa meal, bat guano, blood meal, cottonseed meal,
corn gluten meal, feather meal, [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]]. Alfalfa meal feeds bacteria and
fungi and is usually covered with protozoa, each of which can cycle 10,000 bacteria a day into
plant-usable ammonium. It contains [[ingham-natural-growth-regulators-soil-microbes]] hormones and is available for cycling into
nutrients for about 1 to 4 months.
