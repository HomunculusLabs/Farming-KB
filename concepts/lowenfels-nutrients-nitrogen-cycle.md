# Nitrogen Cycle and Organic Nitrogen Sources

## Overview

Nitrogen (N) is crucial to plant growth and is often the limiting nutrient in gardens. It is the
backbone of amino acids, the structural building blocks of proteins. No nitrogen means no
proteins, and no proteins means no enzymes. Nitrogen is also the base element for nucleotide
molecules, the building blocks of DNA and RNA, and is an essential part of chlorophyll
(C55H72MgN4O5). Most plants in a garden are about 3 to 4 percent nitrogen by weight. The Earth's
atmosphere consists of 78 percent nitrogen, but atmospheric nitrogen (N2) is off limits to plants
because nitrogen atoms form extremely strong triple covalent bonds.

## The Nitrogen Cycle

Atmospheric nitrogen (N2) is so tightly bound that it is unavailable for use in most biological
systems. A tiny fraction is made available by electrical and snow storms, but it was the job of
specialized microorganisms to break these bonds and make nitrogen available to plants. Some
nitrogen-fixing bacteria live in symbiotic relationships with plants in root nodules, such as
Rhizobia nodules on legumes. Others, such as Azotobacter, are free-living soil organisms.

Today, the Haber-Bosch process allows for artificial nitrogen fixation, and only about 50 to 60
percent of available nitrogen is produced by biological systems. The Haber-Bosch process provides
over half a billion tons of artificial manures each year, requiring a staggering 5 percent of the
world's natural gas production.

Fixed nitrogen in the form of ammonium (NH4+) and nitrate (NO3-) is taken up and assimilated into
various compounds within the plant. When plants die, they decompose. The decay by bacteria and
fungi results in ammonification, the production of NH4+. Some of this is taken up by plants, and
the rest is converted by nitrifying bacteria to nitrite (NO2-), which is then converted by
bacteria and Archaea into NO3-. Some nitrate is absorbed by plants and assimilated, some remains
in the soil, and the rest washes out of the soil.

When soil or water containing nitrogen becomes anaerobic, nitrates are converted back to
atmospheric nitrogen by anaerobic microbes that use nitrogen compounds as their energy source.
This denitrification is a big loss for the gardener but completes the nitrogen cycle.

## Biological Nitrogen Fixation

Soil microbes responsible for making nitrogen usable by plants are known as diazotrophs. Up to
50 percent of the nitrogen in a garden can come from nitrogen-fixing organisms.

### Rhizobia

The most familiar diazotrophs are Rhizobia, soil bacteria that form symbiotic relationships with
legumes. Rhizobia provide the enzymes necessary to break apart triple-bonded atmospheric nitrogen
molecules, and the plant provides the housing for this activity as well as carbon-based exudates
that the bacteria consume. The amount of oxygen has to be limited for the enzymes to work, and
the root nodules provide such an environment. There is enough nitrogen for the legume and the
bacteria to share. Up to 20 percent excess nitrogen is produced as well, and it moves into the
soil and soil food web, where much of it is brought to plants by mycorrhizal fungi. Many
commercial mixtures of Rhizobia are available, and there has to be a specific match between the
right species of Rhizobia with the right kind of plant.

### Frankia

Less is known about Frankia, the filamentous bacteria, or more properly actinomycetes. These
nitrogen-fixing bacteria associate with actinorhizal plants that include alders, several trees
and shrubs found in the South Pacific, and some familiar landscape plants such as oleaster,
bayberry, dryas, and the edible sea buckthorn. Most of these are pioneer plants, alder in
particular, and their symbiotic relationships with Frankia bring needed plant-available nitrogen
into poor soils.

### Free-Living Fixers

Azotobacter and Azospirillum are free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria that produce nitrogen
without entering into a symbiotic relationship with plants. They are frequently used when growing
cereal crops and are being developed for gardening use.

## Organic Nitrogen Sources

Good natural sources of nitrogen include alfalfa meal, bat guano, blood meal, cottonseed meal,
corn gluten meal, feather meal, fish emulsion, fish meal, fish powder, hydrolyzed fish, human
hair, human urine, soybean meal, and Chilean nitrate.

### Alfalfa Meal (N-P-K 2-1-3)

A good all-purpose source of nitrogen containing trace elements. Alfalfa meal feeds bacteria and
fungi and is usually covered with protozoa, each of which can cycle 10,000 bacteria a day into
plant-usable ammonium. It contains natural growth hormones and is available for cycling into
nutrients for about 1 to 4 months.

### Bat Guano (N-P-K 10-3-1)

Rich in soluble and readily available nitrogen. It can be used at the start of the season or when
a quick boost is needed. It is great for the first-time natural garden when there hasn't been
enough time for fertilizers and composts to decay. It will last from 4 to 6 months. If too much
is used, it can burn plants by pulling water out of cells via osmosis.

### Blood Meal (N-P-K 12-0-0)

Made from dried livestock blood, very high in readily available nitrogen. As a fast-acting
natural fertilizer, blood meal can counter the argument that natural fertilizers are released too
slowly. Nutrients are released for about 1 to 4 months. With such high nitrogen content, it can
burn plants. It also attracts dogs and cats but repels deer.

### Fish Emulsion (N-P-K 5-2-2)

Heat- and acid-processed fish parts resulting in a soluble, concentrated nitrogen fertilizer
with a more balanced profile of essential nutrients. It is full of micronutrients since fish
consume many things containing trace elements. Soluble and quick acting, it needs to be diluted.
It lasts from 1 to 4 months.

### Feather Meal (N-P-K 7 to 12-0-0)

A by-product of the poultry industry, feather meal is a high-nitrogen plant food that releases
nitrogen much more slowly than bat guano or blood meal. It is full of keratin, a complex protein
requiring more microbial digestion. Feather meal can continue to be a source of nitrogen for 6+
months.

### Human Urine (N-P-K 15-2-2)

Contains urea, which when pure has an N-P-K value of 46-0-0. Soil bacteria quickly convert urea
into ammonium. If the soil pH is acidic, this is the form mostly taken up by plants. If the pH
is basic, ammonium is converted into nitrates. Urine can burn tender plants if not diluted with at
least 8 parts water to 1 part urine.

## Nitrogen Mobility

Nitrogen remains mobile once inside a plant, meaning it can be transported to where it is needed.
This mobility is why the first signs of yellowing from nitrogen deficiency occur in older leaves.
Nitrogen is so critical to new growth that the plant will rob nitrogen from older cells to grow
new ones. Outside the plant, nitrogen also has a great influence on the pH of the soil. The pH in
the rhizosphere goes up when NO3- is added because hydroxyl ions (OH-) are released, increasing
the solubility of iron and aluminum phosphates.

Ammonium competes with calcium and magnesium ions for uptake sites. Apply too much ammonium, and
you may end up with not enough of the other cations. The release of hydrogen ions to get ammonium
through the plasmalemma results in lowering the pH around the root zone, whereas taking up nitrate
results in a higher pH.
