
# Rocket Mass Heater Design

A rocket mass heater (RMH) is a highly efficient wood-burning heating system that uses a J-shaped combustion chamber to achieve near-complete combustion of wood, followed by a thermal mass bench that absorbs and slowly radiates heat for many hours. RMHs typically use 75-90% less wood than conventional wood stoves while producing far less smoke and pollution.

## How It Works

The rocket mass heater operates on two key principles:

1. **Complete combustion:** The J-tube design creates a super-heated horizontal burn tunnel followed by a vertical heat riser. Wood is fed into the short end of the J. As gases rise through the heat riser, they reach temperatures of 1000-1500°F, achieving near-complete combustion of particulates and creosote.
2. **Thermal mass:** The hot exhaust gases travel through a sealed masonry bench (or cob bench) before exiting through a vertical chimney. The bench absorbs heat during the active burn (1-2 hours) and radiates it into the room for 12-24 hours afterward.

### The J-Tube Combustion Unit

The J-tube consists of:

- **Feed tube:** Vertical or near-vertical, where wood is loaded. Short enough that the flame cannot climb out (typically 6-8 inches diameter, 15-20 inches tall)
- **Burn tunnel:** Horizontal connection from the bottom of the feed tube to the base of the heat riser (same diameter as feed tube, 12-18 inches long)
- **Heat riser:** Vertical chimney above the burn tunnel where secondary combustion occurs. This is the hottest part of the system (typically 6-8 inches diameter, 30-48 inches tall)

The heat riser must be insulated on the outside to maintain extreme internal temperatures. Common insulation: perlite-clay mix, [[vermiculite]], or ceramic fiber blanket.

### Materials for the J-Tube

- **Firebrick (refractory brick):** Withstands extreme temperatures, available at masonry suppliers. Standard choice for the burn tunnel and feed tube.
- **Ceramic fiber blanket:** Used to insulate the heat riser. Rated to 2300°F.
- **Steel barrel:** A 55-gallon steel drum placed over the heat riser. The barrel acts as a heat radiator, warming the room immediately during the burn, and provides a transition from the square heat riser to the round exhaust system.
- **Cob or masonry:** The thermal mass bench downstream of the barrel.

## Thermal Mass Bench Design

The bench is where the rocket heater stores and slowly releases heat. It is the defining feature that distinguishes a rocket mass heater from a simple [[rocket-stove]].

### Cob Bench

A cob bench is the most common and accessible approach:

- **Construction:** Built from cob (clay-sand-straw mix) around a steel exhaust duct that carries hot gases from the barrel to the chimney
- **Duct material:** 6-inch diameter steel or cast iron pipe (single-wall is fine since gases have cooled significantly by the time they reach the bench)
- **Duct layout:** Typically runs horizontally under the bench with a vertical drop at the chimney end. The duct path should be as smooth as possible — avoid sharp bends.
- **Bench dimensions:** 4-8 feet long, 18-24 inches high, 18-24 inches deep
- **Cob thickness:** Minimum 4 inches around the duct on all sides

### Duct Layout Options

- **Straight run:** Simplest, best draft, least resistance. Duct runs straight from barrel to chimney.
- **L-shaped or U-shaped:** Allows the bench to turn a corner or wrap along a wall. Use gentle 45-degree bends — never 90-degree elbows.
- **Bell (stratification chamber):** Instead of a horizontal duct, the exhaust enters a large masonry chamber where hot gases rise to the top and cool gases exit at the bottom. This eliminates the duct clogging concern.

### Bench Surface

- Smooth cob or earthen plaster finish (see [[earth-plaster-and-natural-finishes]])
- Stone or tile inlay for durable seating surfaces
- Wood bench top (with an air gap above the cob) for a cooler sitting surface

## Efficiency and Performance

### Combustion Efficiency

- Conventional wood stove: 40-60% efficient, significant smoke and creosote
- Rocket mass heater: 80-90%+ efficient, near-smokeless exhaust
- The key is the insulated heat riser and the J-tube geometry which preheats combustion air

### Fuel Requirements

- Small-diameter wood: branches, twigs, scrap lumber cut to 4-6 inch lengths
- Fuel moisture content: Below 20% (ideally 10-15%) for cleanest burn
- Burn duration: A hot, efficient burn lasts 1-2 hours to fully charge the thermal mass
- Wood consumption: 1/4 to 1/2 cord per heating season in a well-insulated small home (vs 2-4 cords for a conventional stove)

### Heat Output

- Immediate radiant heat from the barrel during the burn: 10,000-30,000 BTU/hr
- Delayed radiant heat from the bench: 5,000-15,000 BTU/hr for 12-24 hours
- The combination provides comfortable, even heat without the overheating and cooling cycles of a conventional wood stove

## Sizing

### By Square Footage

- Small space (200-400 sq ft): 6-inch J-tube system, 4-5 foot bench
- Medium space (400-800 sq ft): 6-inch J-tube system, 6-8 foot bench
- Large space (800-1200 sq ft): 8-inch J-tube system, 8-12 foot bench or multiple heaters

### Important Sizing Principles

- Do not oversize — a too-large system overheats the space and wastes wood
- The bench length determines heat storage capacity, not the J-tube size
- In very cold climates, two smaller RMHs may be better than one large one

## Safety Considerations

### Clearances

- Barrel to combustibles: Minimum 36 inches (it gets very hot during a burn)
- Cob bench to combustibles: Minimum 4 inches of cob over the exhaust duct, then bench surface
- Feed tube opening: 36 inches from anything combustible above (in case flame shoots up)
- Floor protection: Non-combustible pad under the entire unit

### Chimney

- Must terminate above the roofline per local code
- Minimum height: 10-15 feet total from the base of the J-tube to the chimney top
- Chimney cap recommended to prevent rain entry
- Cleanout access: Provide a cleanout at the base of the chimney or at the lowest point of the exhaust run

### Carbon Monoxide

- Any wood-burning appliance produces carbon monoxide — install CO detectors
- Ensure the system drafts properly before regular use
- Never operate with doors and windows sealed tight — provide some fresh air makeup
- Backdrafting risk increases with short chimneys or blocked exhaust paths

### Building Codes

- Rocket mass heaters are not code-approved in most jurisdictions
- Work with your building department; they may accept it as a masonry heater under certain code sections
- The Masonry Heater Association of North America provides resources for code-compliant masonry heating

## Common Problems and Solutions

- **Smoke back from the feed tube:** System not drafting properly — check chimney height, duct obstructions, cold start procedures
- **Not heating enough:** Increase bench mass or burn duration; check for gaps in the barrel connection
- **Gases too cool in the bench (condensation):** Shorten the duct run, insulate the first portion of the duct, or use a bell system
- **Bench cracks:** Normal with cob — patch with earthen plaster. Large cracks may indicate foundation settling or duct expansion issues
- **Creosote buildup in duct:** Reduce fuel moisture, ensure complete combustion (hotter burns), increase draft
