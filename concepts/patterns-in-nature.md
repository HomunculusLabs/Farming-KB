# Patterns in Nature

Natural patterns are the templates for permaculture design. Mollison's Designer's Manual devotes Chapter 4 entirely to pattern understanding; Holmgren's Principle 7 ("Design from Patterns to Details") provides the practical framework. See [[permaculture-design]] for how patterns integrate into the design process.

## The General Pattern Model

Mollison derives all natural patterns from a single master model -- a stylised tree form. From this, six fundamental pattern types emerge:

## Branching (Tree Form)

The most universal pattern in nature: trees, rivers, blood vessels, lightning, root systems, erosion patterns.

- **Gathering flows**: Tributaries converging (streams to rivers, capillaries to veins)
- **Dispersal flows**: Distributaries diverging (tree canopy, river deltas, explosion patterns)
- **Design application**: Garden layout radiating from a central point (herb spiral), water distribution from a tank, access paths branching from a central hub

## Spirals

Galaxies, sunflowers, snail shells, global air circulation, whirlpools, chains of islands.

- Spirals arise whenever growth combines with rotation or expansion over time
- The Fibonacci sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...) governs spiral packing in seeds, leaves, and petals
- **Design application**: Herb spirals create microclimates (dry/sunny top, moist/shady bottom), water flow in ponds, garden bed access paths, chicken tractor rotation spirals

## Waves

Ocean waves, sand dunes, sound, light, ripples in sandstone, cloud streets.

- Waves are energy moving through a medium
- Interference patterns create complex structures (coastlines, dune fields)
- **Design application**: Swales on contour are wave forms that slow and spread water; terracing follows wave patterns on hillsides; plant spacing in windbreaks uses wave-like staggering for effectiveness

## Nets (Matrices)

Cracks in mud, honeycomb, basalt columns, bird bones, leaf veins, thermal wind cells.

- Nets arise from tension (drying, cooling, structural efficiency)
- Hexagonal packing is the most material-efficient tessellation in 2D
- **Design application**: Garden bed layouts, chicken wire fencing, plant spacing grids, intercrop patterns; the web of connections between elements in a design is itself a net

## Scatters

Algae on ponds, tree clumps in swamps, islands, lichens on rocks.

- Clusters of organisms distributed across a landscape following resource availability
- Size and spacing of clusters follows order-of-magnitude scaling (Odum's hierarchy)
- **Design application**: Zone placement (clustered by use frequency), tree planting in paddocks (shade clumps for livestock), habitat islands for wildlife corridors

## Tessellations

Pineapple skin, bee honeycomb, crystal lattices, brickwork, plant cell walls.

- Tessellation is the arrangement of shapes that fill a plane without gaps
- Nature tessellates for structural efficiency and resource packing
- **Design application**: Bed layouts (hexagonal close-packing maximises growing area), intercrop patterns, paving, greenhouse bench layout

## Scales in Space and Time (Holmgren)

Holmgren emphasises that systems operate at different scales. Functional scale is often more important than physical size: a predator may be smaller than its prey but occupies 10-100x more territory. Key design implications:

- **Small, fast systems** (garden beds, compost) nest within **large, slow systems** (orchards, watersheds)
- Predators typically need 1-2 orders of magnitude more territory than prey
- Design at the appropriate scale: "Use Small and Slow Solutions" (Principle 9)

## Landscape Patterns (Holmgren)

- **Energy clustering**: Fertility and harvestable resources concentrate at nodes (riparian corridors, oasis points). Design these concentration points deliberately
- **Soil mosaic**: Even at 1-square-metre scale, soils vary between bare sheds and humus-rich accumulation zones behind debris
- **Succession as pattern**: Ecosystems evolve from simple to complex; design for the expected pattern, not just the initial state

## Applying Patterns to Design

1. Start with the **largest pattern** (watershed, sun path, wind flow) -- see [[permaculture-design]]
2. Use **branching** for access and water distribution
3. Use **waves** for earthworks (swales, terraces)
4. Use **nets** for plant spacing and element connections
5. Use **spirals** for intensive growing areas
6. Use **scatters** for wildlife habitat and tree placement
7. Always consider **scale** -- small systems within large systems
