# Phototropism and Darwin's Light Experiments

## Overview

Phototropism is the directional growth response of a plant toward (positive
phototropism) or away from (negative phototropism) a light source. The
phenomenon was first systematically investigated by Charles Darwin and his
son Francis in the late 19th century, whose elegant experiments revealed
that plants possess a rudimentary form of sight. Their work, published in
*The [[darwin-power-of-movement-in-plants]] in Plants* (1880), established foundational
principles that continue to guide plant [[plant-sensory-biology]].

## Darwin's Seminal Experiments

### Background

Following the publication of *On the Origin of Species* (1859), Darwin
spent twenty years investigating plant movement and sensitivity. He was
fascinated by phototropism, noting that "there are extremely few [plants],
of which some part... does not bend towards lateral light." The question
was: how does a plant detect the direction of light, and which part of the
plant does the "seeing"?

### The Canary Grass Experiment

Darwin and his son grew canary grass (*Phalaris canariensis*) seedlings in
total darkness for several days, then placed a gas lamp so dim that "they
could not see the seedlings themselves, nor see a pencil line on paper."
After only three hours, the seedlings had curved toward the light. The
bending consistently occurred about two centimetres below the tip of the
seedling.

### The Five-Condition Experiment

To identify the light-sensing organ, the Darwins designed a classic
five-condition experiment using seedlings:

1. **Untreated control**: Seedling bends toward light (baseline)
2. **Tip removed**: Seedling does NOT bend — goes "blind"
3. **Tip covered with opaque cap**: Seedling does NOT bend — light blocked
4. **Tip covered with clear glass cap**: Seedling bends normally — light
   passes through glass to reach tip
5. **Middle covered with opaque tube**: Seedling bends normally — middle
   section doesn't need to see light

### Conclusions

From this elegant experiment, the Darwins concluded:

- The "eyes" of the plant are located at the **tip** (coleoptile apex)
- The **bending response** occurs in the **middle section** (elongation
  zone)
- A signal must travel from the tip to the bending region, telling it to
  grow asymmetrically

This was the first demonstration that plants can detect and respond to the
direction of light — a form of rudimentary sight.

## The Blue Light Receptor

### Julius von Sachs and Colour Sensitivity

In 1864, Darwin's contemporary Julius von Sachs discovered that **blue
light** is the primary colour inducing phototropism, while other colours
have little effect. This was later confirmed and refined:

- **Blue light** (~450 nm): Primary trigger for phototropic bending
- **[[far-red-light-phytochrome-switch-mechanism]]**: Does not induce phototropism (but serves other functions
  via phytochrome)
- **Green light**: Generally ineffective for phototropic response

This colour specificity implied a dedicated blue-light photoreceptor,
distinct from the red/far-red sensing phytochrome system.

### Modern Understanding: Phototropins

The blue-light photoreceptors responsible for phototropism are now known as
**phototropins**. These are flavoprotein photoreceptors that:

- Contain two chromophores called LOV (Light-Oxygen-Voltage) domains
- Undergo a conformational change upon absorbing blue light photons
- Are primarily located in the coleoptile tip (confirming Darwin's
  observation)
- Activate a signaling cascade that redistributes auxin

## The Auxin Connection

### Darwin's Unknown Signal

Darwin hypothesized that some signal travels from the tip to the bending
zone. In the 1920s, this signal was identified as **auxin**
(indole-3-acetic acid, IAA) by Fritz Went:

- Light causes auxin to redistribute to the **shaded side** of the stem
- Higher auxin concentration on the shaded side promotes [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]]
- The shaded side grows longer, bending the plant toward the light

### Molecular Mechanism

The modern understanding involves several steps:

1. Phototropins in the coleoptile tip absorb blue photons
2. A signaling cascade activates auxin transporters (PIN proteins)
3. Auxin is redistributed laterally to the shaded side
4. Auxin promotes [[chitin-chitosan-fungal-cell-wall]] loosening (via expansin proteins) and cell
   elongation on the shaded side
5. Differential elongation produces the bending response

## Phototropism vs. Photoperiodism

Chamovitz highlights that plants use different photoreceptor systems for
different light-sensing tasks:

| Function | Photoreceptor | Light Colour | Sensing Location |
|----------|--------------|--------------|-----------------|
| Directional bending (phototropism) | Phototropin | Blue | Coleoptile/shoot tip |
| Day/night measurement (photoperiodism) | Phytochrome | Red/Far-red | Leaves |
| General development | Cryptochromes | Blue/UV-A | Throughout plant |

This division of labour allows plants to simultaneously monitor multiple
aspects of their light environment using specialized molecular machinery.

## Ecological Significance

Phototropism is ecologically critical:

- It maximizes light capture for photosynthesis in competitive
  environments
- It allows seedlings emerging from soil to orient toward light
- [[shade-avoidance-syndrome]] enables plants to detect competitors and invest in
  stem elongation to overtop neighbours
- Differential use of blue vs. red receptors lets plants distinguish
  "open sky" (balanced spectrum) from "shaded" (red-depleted) conditions
