# Soil Pollution and Remediation

## Overview

Soil pollution from agricultural chemicals, industrial waste, and urban contamination represents one of the most persistent and difficult environmental challenges of the modern era. [[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual* draws on real-world cases from Australian agriculture to illustrate both the severity of contamination and the limited strategies available for remediation.

## Sources of Agricultural Soil Pollution

### Chronic Chemical Application

Orchards, sugarcane areas, pineapple, cotton, tobacco, and banana plantations have historically received extreme applications of mineral additives, arsenicals, [[aldrin]], DDT, copper salts, and dioxins. The cumulative effect of decades of chemical use creates contamination that persists long after application ceases.

### A Case Study: West Australian Apple Orchards

Even after 18-20 years of no chemical use, apple orchards in Western Australia continued to produce unsafe levels of Aldrin and [[dieldrin]] in the eggs of free-range chickens foraging on the land. Attempts to grow prawns in ponds on such lands failed due to residual Dieldrin levels in soils. This demonstrates the extraordinary persistence of organochlorine pesticides in the environment.

### Lead Contamination from Roadways

Soils within 100 meters of roadways where leaded petrol is used (and where 800-1,000 cars pass daily) may be contaminated to levels requiring non-food production use. Lead accumulates in surface soils and on plant foliage through dust deposition.

### Radioactive Contamination

Lock-up periods of tens or at times thousands of years apply for radioactive materials blowing off or leaking from waste dumps and strategic stockpiles of yellowcake uranium — affecting sites in Iowa, Kentucky, Russia, the UK, and France — or from accidents at atomic power plants and their waste products.

## Heavy Metal Contamination

### Copper Accumulation

Large quantities of copper are applied on most apple orchards. Copper stays at or near the soil surface in high concentrations. In pasture plants under such orchards, copper can reach 50 ppm — poisonous to sheep. Excess copper in the diet causes toxaemic jaundice (liver poisoning) and blood in the urine. People in Australian deserts often show high copper blood levels and blood in urine.

Copper uptake by plants increases with temperature and acidity. Molybdenum, zinc, and sulphur may buffer copper uptake in sheep.

### Arsenic

Arsenic may stay near the surface in clay soils or wash down to subsoil (50-60 cm deep) in acid sands. Leaching from clays or organic profiles (10-20 cm) is unlikely, although phosphate application may dislodge arsenic to deeper levels. Silver beet (Swiss chard) fed with nitrogen can have high arsenic concentrations.

### Cadmium and Industrial Metals

Cadmium and uranium-polluted soils are found in chemically based and heavily-fertilized market gardens and near industrial waste dumps. Long-term effects of nitrate-polluted soil water compound the problem.

## Groundwater Contamination

Groundwater beneath farms can contain 80-90 biocidal substances derived from fertilizers, sprays, and fuels. Near industrial waste dumps, dioxins, radioactives, and heavy metal wastes (cadmium, chromium, mercury) add to the contamination burden. It is estimated that several decades may be needed to clear most aquifers of pollutants. Almost every state of the USA has serious groundwater contamination problems.

### Water Supply Chemicals

Several substances are added to town water supplies, potentially including chlorine, fluorine, alum (aluminium sulphate), and other metabolic poisons. The recommendation is to drink tank water and harvest surface runoff for gardens.

## The Economic Reality

The costs of soil rehabilitation already far outstrip the profits of degradation, and may in fact be prohibitive for areas developed for farming from 1950 to the present — the period Mollison identifies as "the age of agricultural pollution." Even now, applied health standards would close down many farms and factories, and as awareness rises, this will be driven by public demand.

## Remediation Strategies

While there are no easy remedies for polluted soils, Mollison outlines several practical strategies:

### Lead Remediation

For lead-contaminated urban areas (where lead at 1,100 ppm can be present):

- Use **cracked bricks or gravel as a base** to prevent plant roots from contacting contaminated subsoil
- **Build up beds to 30 cm deep** with a rich composted soil of over 40% organic content
- **Grow vegetables and have leaf analyses done** to monitor uptake
- **Wash produce in dilute vinegar** if leaded petrol is still used locally

### Persistent Biocide Contamination

For old orchards, cane fields, or plantations with persistent biocide residues (DDT, Aldrin, Dieldrin, BHC):

- It is unlikely that any animal product (milk, eggs, meat) will be free of high biocide levels
- The only option is to convert to **forestry and non-food crop production** until remediation methods are developed
- Test vegetables for residual toxic materials

### General Principles

- **Do not use untested wells or bore waters** for any purpose
- **Harvest rainwater** as the safest water source
- **Avoid all biocides**, high levels of nitrates, and watch on-farm disposal of oils and fuels
- Some contaminated farms may need to be put into non-food production (as fuel forests or structural timber) for decades

## Soil and Human Health

### The Soil-Plant-Animal Health Chain

Soils harbor and transfer both diseases and antibiotics. Plants take up many modern antibiotics (penicillin, sulfa drugs) from soil, which may then be ingested at concentrated levels. Animals retain residual antibiotics (promoting new and resistant strains of disease organisms) and contain residual hormones and biocides. Most environmental pollutants are concentrated by both plants and animals, though the degree varies between species.

### Benefits and Risks from Food

Natural levels of soil antibiotics may sustain human health, and natural resistance to disease is partly transmitted through food. Vitamins and trace elements maintain many metabolic processes in minute but necessary amounts.

However, the global food system creates risks: global food distributed to global markets enables the global spread of "agricologenic" (farm-caused) diseases. The home water tank and house garden represent a limited and localized risk under personal control, while public water supplies and commercial foods carry distributed risk throughout modern societies.

### Human Nutrition Principles

Mollison offers four broad statements on human nutrition:

1. **Mixed diets** — a normally mixed (omnivorous) diet of local foods, plus an active life, has usually proved healthy
2. **Processed foods** — refined and processed foods, excessive animal fats, and food additives have resulted in malnutrition and degenerative diseases in developed countries
3. **Famine areas** — where critical vitamins or minerals are deficient, great care is needed with dietary changes; traditional diets guide local food tolerances
4. **Soil-food interaction** — there is a complex interaction between food, soil, trace elements, pH, biocides, and fertilizers; heavy use of macronutrient fertilizers can cause micronutrient deficiencies

## Prevention as the Primary Strategy

The overarching principle is prevention. We must not add to existing contamination. The emphasis is on avoiding biocides, minimizing synthetic inputs, managing on-farm waste responsibly, and building soils through biological rather than chemical means.
