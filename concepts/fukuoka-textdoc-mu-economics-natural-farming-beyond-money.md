## Overview

Fukuoka presents a radical economic philosophy centered on the concept of Mu, or
"cosmic nothingness," arguing that agriculture has nothing fundamentally to do with
profitability and that a new economics founded on Mu must replace the current monetary
system. He envisions a society of quarter-acre farmers operating outside the currency
economy, where crop prices become meaningless because production costs are zero.

## Agriculture Has Nothing to Do with Profitability

Fukuoka states unequivocally that agriculture has nothing fundamentally to do with
profitability. The overriding concern should be how to make use of the land. The goal
of farming is to produce a plentiful harvest by bringing out the full forces of nature,
because this is the shortest road to knowing and approaching nature. Farming is not
centered on income or on man; at its core are natural fields that transcend man. The
fields of nature are representatives of nature; they are God. The farmer is in the
service of God, so immediate gain is a secondary concern.

## The Japanese Farmer's True Productivity

Fukuoka mounts a defense of Japanese small-scale farming against the dominant
economic narrative. Japan has the world's highest land productivity and output per
unit of farmland, but labor productivity and income per worker are low. Economists
argue that high yields per acre mean nothing if remuneration per worker is low, and
have pursued larger-scale operations and labor-saving mechanization. Fukuoka counters
that low labor productivity despite high yields is cause for pride, not shame. Low
income merely indicates that produce prices are unreasonably low or farming materials
are unfairly high. The farmer never calculated wages because farming was done
entirely apart from monetary considerations.

## Zero Production Costs and the End of Crop Prices

The natural farmer has no use for chemical farming materials and does not reckon
household labor into expenses, meaning production costs are zero. Fukuoka envisions
a world where, if all farmers thought along these lines, crop prices everywhere would
settle to the same level and prices would no longer be needed. Prices are a man-made
device that do not exist in nature. Nature was free, non-discriminating, and fair to
begin with. Nothing has less to do with the crops of nature than money.

He poses a rhetorical question: what sense is there in importing oranges from the
United States and exporting mandarins back? People need only eat food grown close
at hand and be contented. A money-crazed economy has bred senseless competition in
food production and thrown dietary habits into chaos.

## The Farmland Price Crisis and Legal Obstruction

Fukuoka provides a detailed critique of Japan's land laws, arguing they obstruct
rather than enable people who wish to farm. Japan has about 15 million acres of
farming land, roughly a quarter-acre per adult. Divided among 20 million households,
each would receive three quarters of an acre of farmland plus two and a half acres of
mountain and meadow. Yet the Agricultural Land Law prevents city people from buying
farmland. Legally, a farmer is someone who owns at least one and a quarter acres,
meaning non-farmers can neither buy land nor lease and work it as tenant farmers.

Fukuoka traces how land prices were artificially inflated: the City Planning Law
divided land into zones, prohibiting farmland-to-housing conversion and pushing up
housing land prices. Farmland came to be assessed on the same scale as housing land
by local government, burdening farmers with taxes too high to afford. He argues this
was designed to drive farmers off the land. The Agricultural Land Law, established to
protect tenant farmers, now serves only to thwart the hopes of those wishing to
become farmers.

## The One Law That Would Suffice

Fukuoka proposes that when people can do without a law, it is best not to have it.
Only the barest minimum of laws is needed to create a world that can get along
without laws. Were it necessary to have a single law, it should be: "One shall build
one's house at least sixty feet from one's neighbor." If people scattered out and built
small houses on a quarter-acre wherever they pleased, the food problem, water and
sewage lines, and pollution would all resolve themselves. This would be the quickest
path to making the lands a paradise on earth.

## Historical Farming Self-Sufficiency

Fukuoka recalls that up until a few decades ago, seventy to eighty percent of
Japanese were small farmers. The poorest were called "one-acre farmers," yet they
were totally self-supporting with the richest and safest possible diet. They grew rice,
barley, sweet potato, and many vegetables in paddy fields and gardens. Fruits ripened
on trees next to the farmhouse, surrounded by a shelterbelt. A cow was kept under
the same roof, chickens ran loose, and a beehive hung from the eaves. Their poverty
was not of their own making but resulted from oppressive social systems and political
and economic mechanisms beyond their control.

## Mu Economics as the Only Choice

Since the end of the war, Japanese agriculture was regarded as an economic activity
and turned into a business, setting a course of destruction from within. Economic
relief measures such as raising rice prices, lowering material costs, or improving
labor productivity are not radical measures. Everything depends on whether people
can return to the viewpoint that "all is unnecessary," that one must "act without
acting." Turning back to the source of Mu and dedicating ourselves to a Mu economy
will not be easy, but it is the only choice we have.

## The Loophole: Miscellaneous Land Conversion

Fukuoka reveals how Japan's land laws contain loopholes that the clever exploit.
If earth is carried onto a piece of farmland, or the land is gradually turned into
a lumberyard with flowers and trees planted, it can be converted into a category
called "miscellaneous land." Once this is done, the land can easily be sold or a
house built on it. Even in sparsely populated areas, unused land is left abandoned
because it cannot be transferred or leased, since the use category cannot be
changed. Eighty percent of Japan's land area is mountains, forests, and wilderness
tied up by titles and laws that prevent practical use.

## The Historical Price of Farmland

Fukuoka provides historical context for farmland pricing. Until recently, farmland
prices were always stable. For prime farmland, the best price was 110 bushels of
rice per quarter-acre. Assuming a bushel of rice costs twenty dollars, this comes
to twenty-two hundred dollars per quarter-acre. Farmers used this rate as a
yardstick when buying and selling land to each other. Prices and taxes on farmland
became unjustly high when farmland began to be assessed on the same scale as
housing land by local government, which Fukuoka describes as clearly designed to
drive farmers off the land by burdening them with taxes too high to afford.

## The International Division of Labor Trap

Economists and agricultural scientists argue that because farming in Japan is
economically unjustifiable, Japan should move toward an international division of
labor in food production, perhaps having the United States produce Japan's food.
Fukuoka rejects this entirely. The low labor productivity of Japan's farmers in
spite of their high yields is, he insists, cause for pride. Farmers never
calculated wages for their labor because farming was done aside from any
considerations over money. The consumer determines produce prices, and the farmer
has never had control over the price of farm products or the costs of materials.
