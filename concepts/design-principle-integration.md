# Design Principle Integration in Permaculture

Holmgren's eighth design principle -- "Integrate Rather Than Segregate" -- is one of the most conceptually rich principles in the permaculture framework. It encompasses the analysis of ecological relationships, functional design strategies, industrial redesign, and the rebuilding of human community. The proverb "many hands make light work" reminds us of the intangible benefits from collective rather than solitary action as well as the synergistic nature of integrated systems in which the whole is greater than the sum of the parts. This principle connects directly to [[permaculture-design]], [[permaculture-design-principles]], and [[competition-cooperation-permaculture]].

## Integration in Nature

Perhaps the most startling example of integration is the complementary character of the two most important biochemical processes on earth, photosynthesis and respiration. Photosynthesis uses solar energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into carbohydrate and oxygen. Respiration releases the chemical energy in carbohydrates to support metabolic activity, but its by-products -- carbon dioxide and water -- are the raw materials for photosynthesis. "The perfect balance between these essential life processes could be taken as the ideal model of an integrated system."

In a similar way, plants provide food for animals, which in turn provide fertiliser needed by plants. Even plagues of leaf-eating insects in forests are now recognised as integrated elements of the ecosystem: they cull sick trees and recycle foliage as fertiliser. The flows of materials in these processes are cyclical rather than linear; they circulate in closed loops.

## Types of Ecological Relationships

Holmgren characterises ecological relationships on a scale from destructive to constructive:

### Predatory Relationships
One organism lives by the death of another, but a strong interdependence develops. The predator depends on the reproductive health of the prey population, and the prey depends on the predator to maintain health by weeding out sick and weak individuals. Animals or people harvesting bulbs and tuberous plants can benefit the plant by thinning the clump and loosening the soil -- a most benign example of predation.

### Parasitic Relationships
Parasites feed off larger, more powerful host organisms, generally reducing health without killing. This delicate balance tends to favour mildly debilitating parasites and suppresses the most lethal ones. Parasites often change the behaviour of their host to facilitate transfer between species.

### Competitive Relationships
Organisms with the same needs struggle by growth or behaviour to gain those needs before competitors do. Competition is more common between individuals of the same species, such as a regenerating stand of forest trees, but also occurs between species occupying the same ecological niche.

### Avoidance Relationships
Where plants and animals appear to compete for identical resources, diversity and specialisation tend to allow more efficient use by avoiding competition. In a forest of mixed species, some with shallow surface roots and some with deep taproots, trees can grow closer together. Different bird species work different parts of the same tree canopy for insects, each occupying a distinct niche. Avoidance relationships are neutral, with needs, timing or spacing providing a form of segregation.

### Mutualism
Where organisms have differing needs and in meeting those needs provide benefits to each other, the relationship is mutual or co-operative. Flocking behaviour provides mutual benefits in predator avoidance, navigation and assistance. In rainforests, trees provide shaded understorey for ferns, which in turn enhance the moist decomposing environment to more rapidly return litter to compost and nutrients for the tree. Mutualisms often link several species in a web of interdependence.

### Symbiosis
Symbiotic relationships go beyond mutualism to the point where organisms become so interdependent they cannot live without the other. Lichen (alga + fungus) is the classic example. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria on legume roots are critical for organic agriculture. Symbiotic integration is now thought to drive sudden evolutionary jumps -- the nucleated cell likely originated from a symbiosis between two previously free-living life forms.

### Polar Oppites or Emergent Union
Holmgren observes that the differences between symbiosis and predation may seem great, but both involve closer integration. Indigenous cultures regarded killing and consuming animals as one of integration -- "we are what we eat." Symbiotic relationships may appear constructive but represent complete loss of autonomy for both organisms. "These apparently opposite tendencies in nature can be seen as different paths to integration." This top-down overview of ecological relationships allows us to make sense of diverse and apparently contradictory evidence about how ecosystems work.

## Each Element Performs Many Functions

Every element in a system, especially living plants and animals, is itself a complex system with many characteristics, requirements, outputs, and potential uses. In nature, multi-functionality is the norm. A tree trunk holds up leaves for solar collection, conveys water and nutrients, and provides habitat for insects, birds, mammals and other lifeforms that benefit the wider ecosystem.

The "maximum yield fallacy" arises when a "monoculture of mind" simplifies thinking to focus on a single output. In modern agriculture, the problems of extra work and pollution from ignoring secondary yields were covered by fossil energy subsidies. Without increasing energy availability, this approach is highly dysfunctional.

Functional analysis requires working knowledge of the characteristics, needs, and outputs of potential elements. The classic example is the fowl: by locating it so it can behave naturally, gain access to its needs, and provide outputs directly used by other elements, we minimise our own work and minimise pollution. The neglected outputs (manure, heat, feathers) or characteristics (foraging, scratching) offer special design opportunities.

## Each Important Function Supported by Many Elements

The corollary of "each element performs many functions" introduces back-up system design. Back-up elements provide important functions when the usual source fails. In nature, a myriad of back-up systems exists for every important function.

The word "redundant" has come to mean unnecessary, but older meanings include "characterised by superabundance." In electronics, redundancy refers to elements not normally used but that come into operation if an active element fails. This design strategy derives from systems theory of complex self-regulating natural systems.

### Complementary Contribution
Different elements contribute in varying ways to an important function. The human eye integrates rod and cone cells responding to different light conditions. In soil ecology, a vast diversity of organisms contribute to recycling organic matter -- earthworms digest organic matter, while fungi process tough carbon-rich plant cellulose and bacteria process soft tissue and proteins. Each is more efficient at different tasks.

### Fodder Shrubs
Fodder shrubs and trees are an excellent example of complementarity. By accumulating edible feed without drying off like herbaceous pastures, they function as living haystacks available when pasture fodder is scarce. The high protein content of leguminous shrubs complements bulky dry grass fodder.

## Water Supply as Integrated Design

Holmgren provides a detailed case study of integrated water supply design. The conventional approach -- an electric pressure pump drawing from a ground-level rainwater tank plumbed as if connected to mains water -- creates multiple problems: the convenience of high pressure encourages waste, leaking taps cause pump cycling, and in a power failure no water is available.

A more integrated system includes a header tank on a hill providing moderate pressure, fed by an electric pump that only activates when the tank is half full. In a power failure, at least half the header tank remains available. A small additional tank on a low stand, catching roof water directly, can gravity-feed to the kitchen sink without any mechanical aids.

For irrigation, complementary systems work together: a slow windmill provides adequate water for part of the year, while a fast petrol pump handles peak demand. Because the petrol pump is in regular use for irrigation during peak bushfire season, it is more likely to be reliable in emergencies. These examples illustrate "a fine balance between the complementary and back-up aspects of design redundancy, which reflect the robust and reliable character of natural systems."

## Simplification and Segregation

Simplification is perhaps the human default response to systemic problems. By eliminating apparently less important elements, we reduce management complexity. When elements are essential or too powerful to eliminate, we resort to segregation. Simplification and segregation tend to go hand in hand -- use of one increases the opportunities for the other.

### Segregation in the Garden
Wide vegetable spacing prevents competition but requires more land and more work in weed control. Eliminating weeds makes systems biologically simpler but breaks down free environmental services of maintaining soil fertility and controlling pests.

### Segregated Homestead Design
On traditional farms, space is not limited; homesteads spread over large flat sites with little connection between elements. From a permaculture perspective, these segregated systems are highly inefficient and wasteful.

### Zoning Controls
Land use planning and zoning controls sought to segregate noxious industries from residential areas and eventually to separate all land uses under the logic of avoiding conflict. It is only more recently that segregation by zoning has been questioned, partly because of sustainability concepts such as permaculture.

### Ethnic Segregation
Under similar logic of avoiding conflict, segregation of social and ethnic groups has been tried throughout history. Social and ethnic segregation has been almost completely discredited as ethical or workable social policy.

## Cooperative and Industrial Integration

The redesign of manufacturing processes to achieve greater energy efficiency and reduced waste is taking hold, and with it comes the imperative to integrate previously segregated elements. Sometimes the natural response to industrial good news stories is to ask why the system wasn't designed that way in the first place. The answer is often that the integrated way is more complex, either physically or in its organisation. This complexity provides the rationale for segregated systems when resources are abundant.

Amory Lovins argues that large-scale redesign of industrial processes will lead to integration more akin to ecosystems. His promotion of the hypercar concept and the hydrogen economy rest more on fitting things together to create complementary and integrated systems than on the invention of new technologies. Whether low-tech permaculture models or high-tech corporate ways prevail, "integration of previously segregated systems appears to be a fundamental principle driving post-industrial design."

## Companion Planting and Permaculture Guilds

The emphasis on building more mutual and cooperative relationships while reducing predatory and competitive ones is a key permaculture strategy. Companion planting and [[guild-design-methodology]] are practical expressions of this principle -- placing plants and animals together so they support each other's needs and reduce competition.

## Implications for Design

The principle of integration demands that designers:
1. Analyse the full range of functions each element can perform
2. Ensure every important function is supported by multiple elements
3. Design for complementary and back-up relationships
4. Recognise that segregation is a valid but overused strategy
5. Build cooperative relationships that become more valuable as energy declines
6. Use functional analysis to identify connections between elements
7. Create systems where outputs of one element become inputs for another

## Integration in Modern Design Practice

Contemporary permaculture design builds on Holmgren's integration principle through several practical tools. Sector analysis maps the energies (sun, wind, water, wildlife, fire, noise) entering a site, allowing designers to place elements where they can intercept and use those flows productively. Zone planning arranges elements by frequency of use, ensuring that daily-needs elements like herbs and vegetables are close at hand while less-frequently-managed elements like timber trees are farther away. The integration matrix or element-connection diagram systematically identifies inputs, outputs, and characteristics of each element, then matches outputs of one to inputs of another — turning "waste" into resource. This analytical framework directly operationalizes the principle that integrated systems are more productive, resilient, and self-maintaining than segregated ones.
