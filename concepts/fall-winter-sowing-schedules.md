## Fall and Winter Sowing Schedules

Successful winter harvesting begins with precise succession sowing across multiple protected environments. [[eliot-coleman]]'s system at [[four-season-farm]] uses four distinct planting sites, each providing progressively more protection, to extend the harvest season from fall through the depths of winter.

## The Four Planting Sites

The scheduling system divides planting into four environments, each with its own microclimate characteristics and corresponding planting windows:

1. **Outdoor fields** — The least protected environment, used for crops harvested until early November. These are direct-sown in open ground and represent the earliest succession dates.
2. **Early greenhouses** — Heated or minimally heated structures that provide the first layer of cold protection. Crops are transplanted or direct-sown slightly later than outdoor sowings.
3. **Later greenhouses** — Similar structures but planted at later dates, providing harvest continuity after the early houses have been fully cut.
4. **[[cool-greenhouse]]** — The most protected unheated environment, receiving the latest sowings. These plantings mature during the coldest months when daylight is shortest.

## Key Crop Sowing Windows

### Arugula

Arugula receives one of the most extensive [[succession-planting]] schedules, reflecting its popularity as a [[cut-and-come-again]] salad green:

- **Outdoor**: August 26 through September 16 (multiple sowings)
- **Early greenhouse**: September 2–6 and again on September 22–30
- **Late greenhouse**: September 10
- **Cool greenhouse**: October 20–30, November 4–15, and December 10

This staggering ensures continuous availability of arugula for salad mix from October through February. The cool house plantings, sown as late as December 10, demonstrate how even midwinter sowings can produce under protection.

### Lettuce

Lettuce follows a similar multi-site strategy with dense succession planting:

- **Outdoor**: August 29 through September 6
- **Early greenhouse**: September 13–17
- **Late greenhouse**: September 19 through October 12 (the longest succession window, with sowings every 2–3 days)
- **Cool greenhouse**: October 24–30 and November 5–11

The late greenhouse receives the most intensive lettuce succession, with plantings scheduled every 2–3 days from mid-September through mid-October. This density accounts for the variable growth rates as daylight decreases through autumn.

### Mizuna

Mizuna, a staple Asian green for salad mix and braising mixes, is sown on a compressed timeline:

- **Outdoor**: August 10–16
- **Early greenhouse**: August 20
- **Late greenhouse**: (not specified in this schedule)
- **Cool greenhouse**: October 6–16 and November 5

The cool house sowings allow mizuna production well into winter. Mizuna's cold tolerance makes it one of the more reliable winter crops.

### Mâche (Corn Salad)

Mâche receives some of the latest sowings of any crop in the schedule:

- **Outdoor**: July 25–August 1
- **Early greenhouse**: August 3–7
- **Cool greenhouse**: November 30

The exceptionally late cool house sowing of November 30 reflects mâche's remarkable cold tolerance and its natural growth cycle — it thrives in short, cold days and actually performs poorly in warm conditions.

## Scheduling Principles

### Progressive Protection

Crops are sown later in more protected environments. The outdoor sowings happen first, followed by early house, late house, and finally cool house sowings. This prevents all sites from reaching peak production simultaneously and spreads the harvest across many weeks.

### Multiple Harvest Strategy

Dates shown in bold in the original schedule indicate sowings planned for multiple harvests. Rather than harvesting a planting all at once, these crops are cut repeatedly (cut-and-come-again), yielding multiple harvests from a single sowing.

### Site-Specific Calibration

Coleman notes that he is "not entirely satisfied with the precision of these dates yet" and continues adding more dates as production is fine-tuned. The best advice he offers is simple: **sow often and keep notes**.

### Environmental Matching

Each crop is matched to the site where it performs best. Cold-tolerant crops like mâche and claytonia thrive in the cool greenhouse, while faster-growing crops like arugula and lettuce benefit from the early and late greenhouse environments where slightly warmer temperatures promote more rapid growth.

## The Historical Foundation: French Intensive Gardening

Coleman's scheduling system draws on a rich tradition of intensive vegetable production, particularly the French *culture maraîchère* system. The annotated bibliography accompanying the sowing schedules provides a reading list spanning from the late 19th century to the present:

### Foundational Historical Texts

- **P. Aquatias, *Intensive Culture of Vegetables on the French System* (1913)** — Identifies "manure, water, and labor" as the backbone of intensive culture, detailing their application in year-round vegetable production.
- **L.H. Bailey, *The Forcing Book* (1897)** — A comprehensive classic on greenhouse vegetable forcing, with information still relevant today.
- **Henry A. Dreer, *Dreer's Vegetables Under Glass* (1896)** — A practical gem from an experienced grower who traveled widely to study techniques.
- **Peter Henderson, *Gardening for Profit* (1907)** — First published in 1867, the foundational American text on market gardening.
- **Peter Kropotkin, *Fields, Factories and Workshops* (1901)** — A perceptive ecological analysis described by *The Ecologist* as "one of the canonical texts of the ecological tradition."
- **A.J. Macself, *French Intensive Gardening* (1932)** — Hard data from a competent practitioner, with excellent illustrations.
- **C.D. McKay, *The French Garden in England* (1909)** — An early attempt to popularize French growing practices.
- **Thomas Smith, *French Gardening* (1909) and *The Profitable Culture of Vegetables* (1919)** — Smith was farm manager for Joseph Fels, an American soap manufacturer who funded small-scale intensive vegetable production in England.
- **John Weathers, *French Market-Gardening* (1909)** — Drawing on extensive experience, introduced by William Robinson who first brought French techniques to English attention.

### Winter-Harvest Specific References

- **Jean-Jacques Gerst, *Légumes sous Baches* (1993)** — A professional French technical manual covering every vegetable and every combination of high tunnels, low tunnels, reflective covers, floating covers, and heated/unheated greenhouses. Coleman rates it so highly he suggests learning French just to read it.
- **William J.C. Lawrence, *Science and the Glasshouse* (1948)** — Coleman's favorite historical greenhouse writer, noted for tireless experimentation and practical conclusions.
- **Percy Artiss, *Market Gardening* (1948)** — An almost-organic grower who insisted that "the soil must be biologically healthy."

## Practical Application

For growers adopting this system, the key takeaways are:

1. **Start with multiple protected environments** — Even two levels of protection (outdoor + one greenhouse or high tunnel) dramatically extend the season.
2. **Sow in succession** — Single sowings create feast-or-famine harvest patterns. Succession planting every 2–7 days, depending on the crop, ensures continuous production.
3. **Keep meticulous records** — Sowing dates must be calibrated to your specific latitude, climate, and protected growing structures.
4. **Match crops to environments** — Cold-tolerant crops go in the least-protected spaces; tender crops get the warmest spots.
5. **Plan for the Persephone period** — The sowing schedule is designed so that crops reach harvestable size before the days become too short for active growth (roughly November 10 – February 10 at Coleman's latitude in Maine).
