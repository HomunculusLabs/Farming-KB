# Fungal Biomass in Soil: Measurement and Significance

Fungal biomass in soil represents a major pool of organic carbon and nutrients that plays central roles in fungal-decomposition|decomposition, nutrient cycling, and soil food web dynamics. The quantification of fungal biomass is essential for understanding the contribution of fungi to ecosystem processes, yet measuring fungal biomass in complex soil matrices presents significant methodological challenges. Different measurement approaches yield different estimates, and the choice of method influences the interpretation of fungal contributions to ecosystem function.



## Methods of Measurement

Several methods are used to estimate fungal biomass in soil, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Direct microscopy involves extracting fungal hyphae from soil and measuring hyphal length under a microscope. This method provides direct visualization of fungal biomass but is labor-intensive and may underestimate biomass due to incomplete extraction of hyphae from soil aggregates.

Ergosterol analysis measures the concentration of ergosterol, a sterol found in the membranes of most fungi but absent from plants and most bacteria. See [[ergosterol-fungal-drug-target]] for more on this compound. Ergosterol concentration is converted to fungal biomass using a conversion factor. This method is relatively rapid and specific but may underestimate the biomass of fungi with low ergosterol content, such as some zygomycetes.

Phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis quantifies signature fatty acids associated with fungal membranes, particularly 18:2omega6,9. PLFA analysis provides information on both the biomass and community composition of soil fungi but requires careful sample handling and sophisticated analytical equipment.

Fluorescein diacetate (FDA) hydrolysis is a biochemical assay that measures total microbial activity, including fungal enzymatic activity. While not specific to fungi, FDA assay values correlate with fungal biomass in some soil types and can be used as a rapid index of overall microbial activity.



## Hyphal Length Versus Biomass

Kjøller and Struwe (1982) measured the abundance of fungal hyphae in different ecosystems and found that the values vary depending upon the metric used. Hyphal length appears to be a more important fungal investment of energy than biomass in cooler environments and where the available resources for decomposition are more recalcitrant. Biomass, with less hyphal extension, appears to be more important in warmer environments and where resources are of a higher quality.

Schmidt (1999) showed the importance of fungal biomass in the high-organic-matter-content soils of the tundra ecosystems of Siberia. Hyphal lengths of 393 and 27 meters per gram of dry weight soil were found in the Levinson-Lessing Valley, supporting typical tundra plant communities of dwarf willow and polygon soils, whereas low values of 9 meters per gram of dry weight soil can be found in the more fertile, lower-organic-content brown earth soils at Labaz.



## Fungal Biomass in Tropical Forests

Lodge (1993) discussed the role of fungi in nutrient cycling in tropical forest ecosystems. These systems have large nutrient capital in plant biomass but are frequently limited by nutrient supply from soil. Much of this limitation is caused by phosphorus binding to aluminum and iron oxides, making it less plant-available. Due to high rainfall, other essential nutrients, nitrogen and potassium, are likely to be leached from the rooting zone.

Fungal biomass in tropical forest soils ranges from 5 to 5 milligrams per gram of litter and 2.5 to 3 milligrams per gram of soil, with reports of 8 to 333 grams per square meter. These values represent a large reservoir of nutrients that can be slowly released upon fungal death and decomposition.



## Fungal Biomass as a Nutrient Reservoir

The nutrient content of fungal biomass can be substantial. Lodge (1993) showed that fungal biomass contains significant proportions of ecosystem nutrient pools. Fungal biomass contains approximately 22.2 percent of the phosphorus in leaf litter and 10.5 percent of soil-extractable phosphorus. For calcium, fungal biomass contains approximately 23.6 percent of soil-extractable calcium.

The phosphorus concentration in fungal tissues can reach 5 to 36 milligrams per gram, and the phosphorus content of leaf litter can increase tenfold due to immobilization by fungi. This nutrient immobilization capacity makes fungi important controls on nutrient retention, preventing the loss of nutrients through leaching during periods of high rainfall.



## Temporal Dynamics of Fungal Biomass

Fungal biomass in soil is not static but fluctuates in response to seasonal changes, resource availability, and environmental conditions. Lodge (1993) showed that the biomass of fungi in wet tropical forest soils is significantly and positively correlated with soil moisture and the amount of rainfall in the preceding week. This moisture dependence means that fungal biomass, and consequently the nutrient retention capacity of the fungal community, varies on short timescales in response to weather patterns.

In temperate ecosystems, fungal biomass typically peaks in autumn when litter fall provides fresh substrates for colonization, and declines during winter when low temperatures limit fungal growth. Seasonal patterns of fungal biomass influence the timing of nutrient mineralization and immobilization, creating feedbacks between fungal dynamics and plant nutrient uptake.



## Turnover Rates

Fungi generally have longer turnover times than bacteria in soil. Studies using carbon-14 and nitrogen-15 labeling techniques showed that the rate of carbon loss as CO2 was similar between bacterial and fungal cells, but the rate of mineralization of nitrogen as both ammonium and nitrate was slower in the decomposition of fungal cells. This slower nitrogen turnover means that fungal biomass represents a more persistent nutrient reservoir than bacterial biomass.

The slower turnover of fungal biomass has implications for nutrient cycling. When fungal biomass accumulates, nutrients are immobilized and unavailable for plant uptake. When fungal biomass turns over, either through grazing by soil fauna or through natural senescence, the immobilized nutrients are released in plant-available forms. The balance between fungal biomass accumulation and turnover determines the temporal pattern of nutrient availability.



## Fungal Biomass and Decomposition

In coniferous forests, plant biomass is high and litter fall is low, but accumulation of organic matter occurs mainly because of the recalcitrance of the litter to decomposition. These systems tend to be dominated by fungi as the main saprotrophic microorganism. Fungal biomass in these systems can represent a significant proportion of the total microbial biomass, with estimates suggesting that fungi may account for 50 to 90 percent of total microbial biomass in acidic forest soils. This dominance has important implications for [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] patterns in forest ecosystems.

Live fungal biomass in decomposing litter is typically low, at less than 20 milligrams per gram of substrate, but fungi can contain 75 to 100 percent of the total nitrogen in the litter. This high nitrogen content reflects the capacity of fungi to concentrate nitrogen from the surrounding environment through both active uptake and translocation from adjacent substrates. The [[fungal-nutrient-immobilization-and-translocation]] capacity of fungi is a critical mechanism for nutrient retention in nutrient-poor forest ecosystems.



## Effects of Environmental Stress on Fungal Biomass

Heavy metal contamination reduces both fungal biomass and enzyme activity in soil. Studies have shown that fluorescence diacetate fungal biomass and enzyme activity were reduced in heavy metal-contaminated soils compared to uncontaminated controls. The suppression of fungal biomass by metals has consequences for decomposition rates, bioremediation, and soil food web structure.

Other environmental stresses, including acidification, drought, and temperature extremes, can also reduce fungal biomass. The sensitivity of fungal biomass to environmental stress varies among fungal groups, with some species being more tolerant than others. This differential sensitivity can shift the composition of the fungal community and alter ecosystem function.



## Importance for Ecosystem Modeling

Accurate estimates of fungal biomass are needed for ecosystem models that predict carbon and nutrient cycling. Most ecosystem models currently treat the soil microbial biomass as a single pool, without distinguishing between fungal and bacterial contributions. However, the different turnover rates, enzymatic capabilities, and nutrient dynamics of fungi and bacteria mean that this simplification may introduce significant errors into model predictions.

The development of methods that can routinely quantify fungal biomass in soil, combined with advances in molecular methods for characterizing fungal community composition, is improving our ability to represent fungal processes in ecosystem models. These improvements are particularly important for predicting the response of ecosystems to environmental changes such as warming, elevated CO2, and nitrogen deposition.
