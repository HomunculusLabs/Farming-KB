# Mushroom Growing Situations and Cave Culture

## Overview

The fundamental requirements for [[mushroom-cultivation]] are the regulation of temperature and moisture. Any situation in which these conditions may be controlled to accord with mushroom requirements should prove satisfactory. This concept examines the diverse environments historically and currently employed for commercial mushroom production, with particular emphasis on the extraordinary cave systems of France.

## Core Environmental Requirements

The main factors requiring regulation in any mushroom growing situation are:

- **Temperature**: Must be maintained within a suitable range (typically 48°–56°F optimal)
- **Moisture**: The growing atmosphere must remain consistently humid without waterlogging
- **Ventilation**: Adequate air exchange is essential to prevent stagnation and CO₂ buildup
- **Darkness or subdued light**: Mushrooms do not require light for growth and typically prefer dark conditions

Any environment — whether natural or constructed — that permits control over these factors can serve for mushroom cultivation.

## French Cave Culture (Champignonières)

### Origin and Scale

France is the historic home of mushroom cultivation, and the industry there is inseparable from the extensive subterranean quarry systems surrounding Paris. Parisian buildings were constructed primarily of limestone mined beneath the city and its adjacent suburbs. Mining operations left vast labyrinthian cave systems. The caves directly under the city proper (the catacombs) are not used for mushroom propagation, but the suburban caves have become the famous mushroom gardens of the world.

### Physical Characteristics

The suburban caves exhibit the following features:

- **Depth**: From a few feet to 50 feet beneath the surface
- **Width**: Usually 5 to 25 feet, exceptionally up to 50 feet
- **Height**: 5 to 20 or more feet
- **Extent**: Estimated at upwards of 2,000 kilometers (about 1,200 miles) of mushroom beds across suburbs including Montrouge, Vitry, Châtillon, Issy, Nanterre, Rosny-sous-Bois, Passy, and others

The term **champignonière** (mushroom cave) became practically synonymous with **carrière** (quarry) in the suburban areas, so pervasive was mushroom growing in these underground spaces.

### Ventilation Systems

The cave systems are ventilated by means of chimneys or chutes. Air exchange is facilitated by:

- Small charcoal fires beneath air chutes, which create convective airflow
- Occasionally, special ventilating devices for more controlled circulation

### Access

Entry to champignonières varies considerably:

- Some are accessible through open quarry faces
- Others require descent through holes only a few feet in diameter, using ladders
- Windlasses are employed for lifting or lowering materials through narrow access points

## English Mushroom Growing

In England, mushrooms are grown in:

- Specially constructed mushroom houses
- Cellars
- Caves
- Occasionally in the open air (usually with protection such as cold frames)

The diversity of English growing situations reflects the country's relatively moderate winter climate compared to continental extremes.

## United States Production Environments

### Special Mushroom Houses

Near Philadelphia, Chicago, Boston, and other production centers, mushrooms are grown wholly in special mushroom houses. Unquestionably, the major part of the American market product is grown this way. These structures range from simple boarded sheds or unused barns to purpose-built commercial facilities.

### Caves and Underground Quarries

Some commercial growers in Minnesota, Ohio, Indiana, New York, and Missouri have secured underground quarries, caves, or storage cellars of considerable extent. Such locations are excellent when:

- Readily accessible
- No danger of flooding exists
- Seepage water does not make conditions too wet
- Adequate ventilation is possible

Natural caves in the limestone sections of Missouri have been used successfully, though poor management of old beds and accumulation of spent compost have sometimes led to abandonment. Natural caves also occur in Kentucky, Indiana, Ohio, Virginia, and Arkansas, with potential for future use.

### Beer Storage Cellars

Many antiquated beer storage cellars of limited capacity remain in use in various cities. These converted spaces benefit from existing insulation and underground temperature stability.

### Coal Mines

Abandoned coal mines are generally unsuitable as typically left, but hill coal mines in some sections might repay investigation for commercial mushroom production.

### Advantages of Underground Growing

Caves and deep cellars offer the significant advantage of permitting mushroom production during extended periods — frequently throughout the entire year — due to natural temperature stability. The trade-off is that they must offer adequate ventilation, and the cost of delivering manure from a distance may be prohibitive for remote locations.

## Open Air Culture

### English Experience

In Southern England, where winter temperature extremes are less severe, culture in modified hot beds or cold frames has proven successful. These are simple culture frames of the usual type, but without glass, covered instead with sheet iron or boards and finally a layer of straw if necessary. Fresh manure beneath the compost can provide bottom heat to force early spawn growth.

### Limitations of Open Air Culture

Open air culture cannot be regarded as having great possibilities of success in most regions due to:

- Considerable daily temperature variation
- Drying winds
- Difficulty regulating moisture
- Vulnerability to drenching rains

Deeper beds, fresh manure at the bottom for bottom heat, mulching with clean straw, and cloth covers can partially compensate for these challenges.

### Potential in California

Sections of California with mean monthly temperatures of 50°–60°F and limited daily range (such as the San Francisco area) were identified as potentially suitable for open-air culture. Both *[[agaricus-campestris]]* and *A. arvensis* are found naturally in parts of California during winter months, suggesting the climate is compatible.

### Spawning Pastures and Lawns

Experiments in spawning pastures and lawns to establish natural cultures for increased autumn supply have generally proven inconsistent and unreliable. Results depend heavily on seasonal conditions. While occasional success in favorable seasons may repay the minimal labor involved, this approach cannot be depended upon for consistent production.

## Key Principles

The choice of growing situation ultimately depends on:

1. **Temperature control**: The ability to maintain optimal growing temperatures
2. **Moisture management**: Consistent humidity without waterlogging
3. **Ventilation**: Adequate air exchange to prevent CO₂ accumulation
4. **Access and logistics**: Convenient delivery of compost and removal of spent beds
5. **Economic factors**: Construction costs, operating expenses, and proximity to markets
