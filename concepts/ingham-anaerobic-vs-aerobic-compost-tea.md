# Ingham — Anaerobic vs Aerobic Compost Tea

Updated: 2026-04-18

One of the most critical distinctions in Dr. Elaine Ingham's work is the difference between actively aerated compost tea (AACT) and anaerobic or poorly aerated brews. The biological outcomes of these two approaches are fundamentally different, and misunderstanding this distinction leads to crop damage, failed disease suppression, and contamination of soil and water systems. This page details Ingham's position on why [[cervantes-compost-tea-organic-brewing]] works and why anaerobic alternatives do not.

## The Core Distinction

Ingham states the difference plainly: "Aerobic compost teas will add the organisms that will break down and remove toxic materials. Anaerobic brews ADD toxins to the soil."

This is not a minor difference in technique -- it is a fundamental difference in biological outcome. Aerobic brews support beneficial organisms that improve soil health. Anaerobic conditions select for organisms that produce toxins harmful to plants, humans, and animals.

## How Anaerobic Toxins Are Produced

When oxygen levels drop in a compost tea brew, facultative and obligate anaerobic bacteria begin to dominate. These organisms produce metabolic byproducts that are toxic to aerobic life:

- **Alcohols**: Methanol, ethanol, and butanol produced through anaerobic fermentation
- **Organic acids**: Butyric acid (smell of vomit), propionic acid, valeric acid
- **Hydrogen sulfide**: Rotten egg smell, toxic to plant roots and soil organisms
- **Ammonia**: From anaerobic protein decomposition
- **Phenolic compounds**: From incomplete decomposition of lignins and tannins
- **Plant-toxic metabolites**: Various compounds that can damage or kill plant tissue

These materials accumulate in the brew and are then applied to soil or leaf surfaces, where they continue to suppress beneficial biology.

## Why Anaerobic Brews Can Appear to Work

The confusion arises because anaerobic brews CAN show disease inhibition in laboratory tests. The toxins produced by anaerobic organisms kill or suppress both pathogens and beneficial organisms indiscriminately. When applied to a petri dish, this looks like disease control.

Ingham is explicit: "While anaerobic brews can show inhibition of disease organisms (pathogen inhibition tests as performed by plate count labs, for example), these are not controls that are desirable in the soil, on leaf surfaces or on blossoms."

### The Illusion of Disease Suppression

Anaerobic brews function like chemical pesticides or antibiotics -- they kill broadly. The toxic materials "can be harmful to the plant, to humans, and to animals. While the toxins may kill some disease organisms on plate cultures in the lab, there is no evidence that they operate in the real world."

This is fundamentally different from the [[ingham-aact-disease-suppression]], which works through competitive exclusion -- beneficial organisms occupying space and consuming resources that pathogens would otherwise use.

## AACT Does Not Kill Anything

Ingham repeatedly emphasizes that properly made aerobic compost tea contains no toxins: "These organisms DO NOT act as pesticides, they are not toxic, they do not kill anything through toxic interactions."

The beneficial organisms in AACT work through:
1. **Competitive exclusion**: Occupying infection sites so pathogens cannot establish
2. **Resource consumption**: Eating the food that disease organisms need
3. **Predation**: Protozoa and nematodes consuming bacteria, including pathogenic species
4. **Habitat modification**: Building soil structure and maintaining aerobic conditions that favor beneficials

These are "non-toxic prevention methods." The EPA classifies predator-prey interactions as "mechanical control methods" -- fundamentally different from chemical or antibiotic approaches.

## Types of Compost Tea Defined

Ingham recognizes several distinct categories of brewed biological materials:

### Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT)

The gold standard. Maintained at dissolved oxygen levels above 6 ppm throughout the brewing process through active aeration. Produces tea dominated by aerobic beneficial organisms. See [[aact-definitions-and-types-ingham]] for brewing parameters.

### Fermentative Compost Tea (FCT)

Sometimes called "non-aerated" or "Korean Natural Farming style" teas. These select for facultative anaerobic organisms and can suppress certain diseases through antibiotic interactions. However, they also produce some toxins and do not build the full soil food web.

### Long-Brewing Compost Tea (LBCT)

Teas brewed for extended periods (days to weeks) without adequate aeration. These inevitably go anaerobic and should not be used on crops.

### Compost Extracts

Liquid made by steeping compost in water without brewing. These contain the soluble nutrients and organisms extracted from the compost but without the multiplication that occurs during active brewing. Not the same as tea.

## Anaerobic Conditions and Disease

Ingham makes a critical point about the relationship between oxygen and disease: "Almost all (please read that, ALMOST all) disease organisms do much better when oxygen is reduced, because the good guys that outcompete the bad guys require aerobic conditions."

The mechanism is straightforward:
- Reduced oxygen favors facultative anaerobes (many of which are pathogens)
- Reduced oxygen suppresses aerobic competitors (the beneficial organisms)
- Anaerobic metabolites directly damage plant tissue
- Anaerobic conditions prevent beneficial fungi from functioning, removing a major disease defense

### Oxygen Ranges and Fungal Pathogens

Fungi that cause necrosis on roots need to function in aerobic conditions, but they are "generally outcompeted in the root zone by beneficial aerobic fungi." The "worst" fungal pathogens do better in slightly oxygen-reduced conditions because their competitors are gone. Once oxygen drops to true anaerobic levels, even the pathogens cannot grow -- but neither can anything beneficial.

This is why maintaining aerobic conditions throughout the brew is essential: it keeps both pathogens AND their anaerobic competitors suppressed, while allowing the full range of beneficial organisms to thrive.

## The Smell Test

Ingham includes a "Smell Tea Test" as a practical field indicator of brew quality:

- **Good tea**: Earthy, sweet, or neutral smell -- like good forest soil
- **Starting to go anaerobic**: Slightly sour, beginning to smell off
- **Anaerobic**: Putrid, rotten egg, vomit, or septic smells

If the tea smells bad at any point during brewing, it has gone anaerobic and should be discarded or applied only to non-crop areas.

## Timing of Anaerobic Conditions in Brews

Ingham's research shows that a critical transition occurs around the 14th to 20th hour of brewing:

- Hours 0-12: Aerobic organisms dominate if properly aerated
- Hours 12-14: Bacterial populations peak, oxygen demand is highest
- Hours 14-20: Risk period -- if aeration is insufficient, the brew can go anaerobic as bacterial respiration exceeds oxygen supply
- Beyond 20 hours: Without adequate aeration, anaerobic conditions are firmly established

This is why [[aact-aeration-oxygen-management-ingham]] throughout the brew is non-negotiable. The aeration system must be capable of maintaining dissolved oxygen above 6 ppm even at peak bacterial activity.

## Transport and Storage Failures

Even properly brewed tea can become toxic if mishandled after brewing:

- Transporting tea for 10-15 hours without aeration allows it to go anaerobic
- Applying anaerobic tea to crops "clearly demonstrates not knowing what you are doing"
- Tea should be applied within hours of brewing, or kept actively aerated until application
- Temperature during transport matters -- warm conditions accelerate oxygen depletion

## Research Design Failures

Ingham is sharply critical of research that purports to show compost tea ineffectiveness:

- Researchers who apply tea to fields with less than 0.5% organic matter, then plow and wait three weeks before planting, are "doing everything possible to make certain they will see no benefit"
- Bacteria and fungi require organic matter to eat -- without food, they cannot perform their functions
- Plowing destroys the fungal networks that tea is trying to establish
- Three weeks without food kills the introduced organisms

These design failures, not limitations of compost tea, explain many negative research results.

## Practical Guidelines

1. Always maintain active aeration throughout the brewing process
2. Monitor dissolved oxygen levels -- target 6+ ppm
3. Use the smell test: if it smells bad, discard it
4. Apply tea within hours of brewing completion
5. Never transport tea without aeration for extended periods
6. Ensure adequate organic matter in soil before applying tea
7. Minimize tillage after tea application to preserve introduced fungi
8. Distinguish between AACT (aerobic, beneficial) and FCT (anaerobic, toxic) in your practice and communication
