# Faires Design Patterns for Climate Resilience

Climate resilience in permaculture design means planning for the full range of conditions a site will experience — not just average conditions but extremes, disasters, and gradual changes. The Faires guide provides design patterns for every major climate zone and type of natural disaster, emphasizing that the only way to prepare for nature's disruptions is to plan ahead.

## Desert Climate Design

Desert gardens face extreme heat, water scarcity, and temperature swings. The guide provides specific strategies:

**Water collection:** Dew is most easily collected in the desert where clear skies and gentle breezes prevail. Stack stones to collect moisture for plants below. Several stacks surrounding a grape vine or small tree can keep it alive.

**Species selection:** Olives, palms, citrus, avocado, apricot, banana, and papaya are desert staples. Choose trees that don't need much water or can withstand drought.

**Spacing:** Trees must be spaced farther apart than in temperate zones to prevent water competition. Plant during the rainy season.

**Rock mulch:** In deserts, rocks protect roots from heat and damage and act as [[faires-thermal-mass-applications-in-building]] that keeps roots warm at night.

**Sapling protection:** Palm leaves or brush propped over tree saplings protect them from intense sun. Fencing or dogs keep nibbling animals away.

**Interplanting:** Leguminous species between trees fix nitrogen and provide shade.

**Microclimate creation:** The graywater marsh and rainwater runoff feed into [[faires-swales-and-water-conservation]] directly to trees. Well-mulched raised beds of mud with shade from wood trellises, vines, or trees extend the growing season.

## Cold Climate Design

Cold climates demand strategies for heat retention and season extension:

**Reflective surfaces:** Trees with light-colored leaves (like poplar) can be planted in an arc around the house or orchard, facing the sun. They reflect heat and light back onto the garden. If planted on a slope, this warms cool air pooling at the bottom.

**Earth mounds:** An earth mound on the west side of the house protects from the hot evening sun and provides insulation. Earth slows down temperature changes.

**Spring soil warming:** Remove mulch from intensive growing areas in spring so the soil can warm up. Mulch has no heat-conducting properties. Replace mulch at almost every other time.

**Greenhouses:** The most obvious way to create a warm microclimate, particularly valuable in winter.

## Wind Protection Design

Wind is one of the most underestimated forces affecting garden productivity:

**Trellis windbreaks:** The quickest shelter for a Zone 1 garden is a trellis extending from house corners, planted with climbing vines. These grow fast enough to provide shelter while trees establish.

**Tree windbreak danger:** In rural, forested regions, clearing trees for a house site can create a serious hazard. Previously sheltered trees have weak root systems from growing protected by each other. New exposure can knock them onto the house.

**Tire walls:** Stack tires filled with earth, compost, or hay for instant wind protection while hedges establish.

**Bamboo windbreaks:** Particularly valuable in hurricane zones. Plant on the side of the house receiving the most wind.

**Hedge design:** A very thick hedge on the west side blocks the hottest sun and wind simultaneously.

## Temperature Modification

Multiple strategies for cooling and warming specific areas:

**Evaporative cooling:** Plants release water vapor, cooling the air. Filling a porous earthenware pot with water and covering with heavy, wet fabric creates a similar effect. Place these around areas you want to cool.

**Forest cooling:** Forests absorb heat during the day, shade the floor, and draw in cool air. At night, warm air flows out. This effect should be used by planting [[faires-hugelkultur-and-forest-garden-strategy]] and placing animals within them.

**Water bodies:** Even a small body of water or fountain cools the surrounding area through evaporation.

**Light redirection:** Any plants with high light and warmth needs can be placed on sunny sides of banks or small hills behind ponds. Light-colored leaves and sun-facing walls reflect light and heat onto plants.

**Temporary shade:** Fabric or other material shelters prevent sunburn during the hottest parts of summer.

## Disaster Preparedness

### Hurricane and Tornado

- Buildings should be flexible (as for earthquakes)
- Roof should have a sharp 45° angle — wind force pushes building down rather than picking it up
- Bamboo windbreaks prevent major damage
- Plant a small backup garden in a sheltered place away from the Zone 1 garden
- Build a storm cellar or safe room for escape and food storage

### Storm Cellars and Safe Rooms

**Storm cellar:** Underground room, either in the basement with an inside door or built separate from the house with an outside door. Often doubles as a [[faires-low-energy-cooking-and-food-preservation]].

**Safe room:** For people who can't build underground. A small room built of stone or concrete in the center of the house with a solid door and drop-down bar lock.

### Root Cellar as Storm Shelter

The root cellar naturally doubles as a storm shelter — an underground room with food storage serves dual purpose. This is an example of stacking functions: every element should serve multiple purposes.

## Annual Planning Calendar

The guide provides a seasonal calendar for temperate climate homesteads:

**Late Winter:** Plant earliest garden, harvest winter garden, tap maple trees for syrup

**Late Spring:** Harvest rye or barley, check on bees and split if needed, harvest early garden

**Summer:** Trim orchard trees, flood rice paddy, plant more transplants, harvest summer garden

**Early Fall:** Let ducks into Zone 1 gardens, geese into Zones 2-3 fields, sow rice seed, harvest last gardens, plant fall transplants

**Late Fall:** Harvest rice and dry, plant next year's rice one month after harvest, harvest honey and begin feeding bees, breed sheep, harvest fall garden

**Winter:** Check bees for food supply, dry up pregnant cows, order new bees and seeds, watch ewes for lambing

### Flood Preparedness

Flooding requires specific design considerations that complement other disaster strategies:
- Elevate critical infrastructure (wells, power systems, food storage) above historical flood levels
- Design swales and drainage to redirect floodwater away from structures and gardens
- Use raised beds and hugelkultur mounds to protect annual crops from inundation
- Select flood-tolerant species for low-lying areas (willow, elderberry, bamboo)
- Store emergency food and seed banks in upper stories or elevated locations

### Drought Resilience Strategies

Extended drought is among the most challenging climate disruptions for food production:
- Design multiple redundancy in water supply (well, rainwater, greywater, spring)
- Select drought-adapted varieties and perennial staples that survive without irrigation
- Build soil organic matter to maximum capacity — each 1% increase holds approximately 20,000 additional gallons of water per acre
- Use deep-rooted perennial systems rather than annual vegetables as the backbone of food production
- Maintain seed reserves of drought-tolerant landrace varieties adapted to local conditions
- Design [[faires-swales-and-water-conservation]] and earthworks to capture and infiltrate every drop of rainfall

## Principle: Plan for the Unexpected

Nature has its own way of disrupting plans. The only preparation is to plan ahead for extreme climate changes and natural disasters. This means designing redundancy into every system: multiple water sources, backup food production areas, diversified species selection, and structures that can withstand extreme events.
