# Mollison on Professional Permaculture Design Practice

**Source:** Bill Mollison, Permaculture Design Course, Pamphlet VIII (Designing for Permaculture), 1981
**Last Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Overview

Mollison lays out the professional ethics, business practices, design methodology, and common errors for permaculture designers. This pamphlet is the practical handbook for anyone pursuing permaculture design as a career.

## Professional Designers' Ethics

1. Cooperate; do not compete
2. Do not duplicate each other's work; create individual designs
3. Care for clients and be responsible to them
4. Work toward reforestation of Earth and restoration of soils
5. Care for environment and wildlife; side with "super client" Gaia
6. Seek most appropriate energy paths, utilizing appropriate energy
7. Recycle at highest level possible; everything is a resource
8. Divest of everything surplus to needs (Tasmania community practice)

## Designer's Role

- Know where to place things and **why**
- Not to tell people how to garden, build, or make dams -- but to **place things** for multiple functions, low energy input, high yield and stability
- Function is "knowing where to place things" -- creative observer role
- Build implementation groups; regional teams for carrying out designs

## Business Practices

- **Work 1-2 years** before applying for design diploma
- **Half of course participants must be women** (shortage of women designers)
- **Scholarships**: 1 per 6 paid tuitions; prioritize women and disadvantaged minorities
- **Rates**: tie to local landscape architects; ~$35/hour (1981); 1.5-2% of total investment for design
- **Standard job**: 1 day on site + typed/illustrated report (15-20 pages) for up to 40 acres
- **Large jobs (>100 acres)**: quote on time basis; start with pre-design brief
- **Finding land**: 0.5% of purchase price to find; additional 1.5% if they buy; ~$400/week plus expenses
- **Land management**: $2,000-5,000/year; but "as fast as we train designers, we lose designers" to management

## Six Criteria for Finding Client Property

1. Client must have own water supply potential on site
2. Site suits what client wants to do
3. Site is **undervalued** in present usage
4. Multi-titled or without structural limitations
5. Designer can improve property to high level
6. Not valued for sub-division (highest price)

## Report Methodology

1. Collect all data: client requirements, maps, regulations
2. Analyze client problems
3. Recognize applicable permaculture principles
4. Apply principles to specific problems
5. Design solutions as edges, patterning, functional relationships
6. Verify solution meets all client needs

## Report Structure

- General site description (orientation, vegetation, water, soils)
- Main map with numbered areas
- Themes affecting whole site (fire protection, trusts, community)
- Detailed treatment of each numbered area
- Priority staging / cost analysis
- Bibliography
- Documented plant list
- Resource list (people, hardware, tools)
- Offer unlimited follow-up questions at no charge

## Ten Type-One Errors

1. Designing for environmentally destructive clients
2. Not explaining reasons for recommendations
3. Recommending technology beyond client's ability
4. Not supplying adequate management data
5. Depersonalized reporting style
6. Vague expressions ("fairly large")
7. Poor patterning and edge linkages
8. Failing to recommend essential pre-treatments (e.g., soil conditioning)
9. Recommending illegal plant species
10. Failing to fully define on-site resources

## Resource Identification

- Client's skill, experience, material, and capital resources
- On-site resources (often unseen by client): rampant plants as crops, peat, clay, seed, herbs, insects, energy potential
- Social resources: teaching, recreation potential
- Example: 18-acre city farm covered in fennel --> licorice fraction worth $3,000/gallon via simple distillation
- Example: swamp with peat over blue clay --> $6/bag clay mine for potter
