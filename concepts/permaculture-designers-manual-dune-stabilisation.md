Sand dunes threaten settlements, farmland, and infrastructure through wind erosion and sand drift. The permaculture approach uses vegetation, earthworks, and strategic design to stabilise dunes and convert them from hazards to productive landscape elements.

## Dune Formation and Dynamics

### How Dunes Form
Sand is moved by wind in a series of interactions: wind moves along the dune back for a distance, creating crescent sands and transverse dunes. Wind-borne sand accumulates around obstacles such as tussock grass, creating small nebkas (sand hummocks) that grow as more sand is trapped. Over time, these nebkas coalesce into larger dune forms.

### Dune Types
The manual references several dune forms:
- Nebkas: small sand hummocks that form around tussock grass or shrubs
- Transverse dunes: crescent-shaped dunes formed by consistent wind direction
- Parabolic dunes: U-shaped dunes with the open end facing downwind
- Longitudinal dunes: elongated ridges parallel to prevailing wind
- Cliff dunes: sand accumulated against cliffs or scarps

### Sand Properties
All sands infiltrate water readily, which is both an advantage (no runoff problems) and a challenge (rapid drainage means low water retention for plants). Sand movement is driven by wind energy; the wavelength and amplitude of sand waves relate directly to wind speed and sand grain size.

## Stabilisation Strategies

### Plant-Based Stabilisation

#### Pioneer Grasses
Coarse tussock grasses are the primary pioneers for dune stabilisation:
- They trap wind-borne sand around their bases, initiating nebkas
- Their root systems bind sand particles and prevent wind erosion
- They provide organic matter as they die back, building soil over time
- Pennisetum species are recommended as tall grasses for dune work
- Vetiver grass provides deep root binding on dune faces

#### Tree Plantings
Trees provide long-term stabilisation once grasses have established initial ground cover:
- Casuarina species are among the most effective dune-stabilising trees, providing both wind reduction and deep root binding
- Acacia species pioneer on sandy soils and fix nitrogen, improving soil for subsequent species
- Tamarisk is specifically listed as a dune and desert shelter species
- Date palms can be established on stabilised dunes as productive crops

#### The Sequence
Stabilisation follows a successional sequence:
1. Pioneer grasses and hardy shrubs establish on windward dune faces
2. Sand accumulates around plant bases, building nebkas
3. As nebkas grow and merge, more protected sites allow tree seedlings to establish
4. Trees eventually overshadow and replace grasses, creating permanent stabilisation
5. Productive species can be interplanted as the system matures

### Earthworks for Dune Management

#### Nebkas by Design
The manual shows that nebkas (sand hummocks) can be deliberately created by planting tussock grass at specific intervals. Low rows of planted material across wind direction create nebkas that grow as sand accumulates, eventually forming stable dune ridges that protect downwind areas.

#### Wind Deflection
Strategic placement of fences, earth banks, or plantings deflects wind and reduces sand movement. The manual references a series of dune forms and interactions that can be designed to capture sand in desired locations rather than allowing uncontrolled drift.

#### Water Harvesting on Sand
Dune systems can be designed for water harvesting. Because all sands infiltrate water, diversion drains and swales can be constructed on dune surfaces to direct rainfall and runoff to planted areas. Sand-filled waterways can retain moisture for plant establishment.

## Plants for Dune Stabilisation

### Grasses
- Pennisetum species (tall clumping grasses for initial sand trapping)
- Vetiver grass (deep-rooted binding grass for dune faces)
- Banna grass (structural material and windbreak)
- Pampas grass (Cortaderia) for large-scale sand trapping
- Rhodon and other local tussock grasses

### Trees and Shrubs
- Casuarina species (primary dune-stabilising trees)
- Acacia species (nitrogen-fixing pioneers on sand)
- Tamarisk (salt-tolerant dune species)
- Prosopis (deep-rooted nitrogen fixer for arid sands)
- Date palms (productive species for stabilised dunes)
- Chinese tallow tree (Sapium sebiferum) for wax and shelter
- Albizia species for nitrogen fixation and shade

### Ground Covers
- Hardv ground covers suppress sand movement at the surface
- Vetches, nasturtium, runner legumes, annual lupins, and daikon radish are recommended for arid areas
- These cool the roots of establishing trees and vines

## Dune Systems in Settlement Design

### Desert Housing
The desert house should be sited near a run-off area, where water can be soaked into sand dams or swales to keep trees alive. Settlements in dune areas benefit from:
- Planting dune-stabilising species on the windward side
- Creating protected courtyards between dune ridges
- Using stabilised dune sand as a building material
- Establishing windbreaks of Casuarina, Acacia, and bamboo on poleward sides

### Agricultural Integration
Stabilised dune systems can support productive land use:
- Date palm orchards on stabilised dune ridges
- Olive and citrus plantings in swales between dunes
- Forage grasses on stabilised dune faces
- Fuelwood plantations using fast-growing Acacia and Casuarina

### Water Conservation
Sand dune areas offer unique water conservation opportunities:
- All rain infiltrates, with no surface runoff loss
- Underground water storage in sand is protected from evaporation
- Sand dams can be constructed in dune valleys to retain water
- Swale systems on dune surfaces direct water to planted zones

## Long-Term Evolution

The manual emphasises that dune stabilisation is not a one-time treatment but an ongoing design process. Initial pioneer plantings create conditions for more demanding species. Over time, what was a mobile sand dune can evolve into a productive, stable landscape supporting trees, gardens, and wildlife habitat. The key is to start with hardy pioneers, build soil and organic matter, and progressively introduce more productive species as conditions improve.

The design approach recognises that "a series of dune forms, and uses of sand" can be planned rather than merely endured, converting a hazard into a resource through thoughtful design and patient succession management.
