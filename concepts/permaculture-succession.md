# Ecological Succession and Garden Assembly

Ecological succession is the process by which landscapes evolve from bare ground through pioneer species to mature ecosystems. In nearly every climate, the end result of succession — when not interrupted by fire or other disturbance — is forest. Understanding and accelerating this process is central to permaculture gardening. From Gaia's Garden's final chapters, succession drives the transformation from a sterile yard to a self-sustaining ecosystem.

## The Succession Sequence

### Natural Succession
1. **Pioneer phase**: Fast-growing annuals and grasses colonize bare ground
2. **Shrub phase**: Perennial shrubs and short-lived trees establish
3. **Young forest**: Faster-growing trees form a canopy, shade-tolerant species establish beneath
4. **Mature forest**: Complex, multi-layered ecosystem with high biodiversity and closed nutrient loops

### In the Garden
Hemenway reframes succession as the journey from a conventional garden to a [[food-forest-design]]:
- **Year 1-2**: Open and sunny. Lots of space between young trees. Fill with annual vegetables, [[cover-cropping]], and fast-growing nurse plants. The garden looks sparse — "a twig farm."
- **Year 3-5**: Trees and shrubs begin to fill in. Shade increases. Shift from sun-loving annuals to shade-tolerant perennials. Berry bushes begin to fruit. Begin chop-and-drop mulching from shrub prunings.
- **Year 5-10**: Canopy starts to close. Forest-like structure emerges. Fruit production increases. Shade-tolerant species become dominant in lower layers. The system becomes more self-maintaining.
- **Year 10+**: Mature food forest. Maximum production. Self-maintaining. Prune to maintain light gaps and airflow.

## Accelerating Succession

Most permaculture techniques are designed to accelerate natural succession and make connections tight, many-layered, and efficient:

### Building Soil First
"The title of an excellent book by Grace Gershuny gives the answer: Start with the Soil." The soil is the base of the ecological pyramid. Deep [[sheet-mulching]] and dense planting create an expanding circle of vigorous life and fertility. This ramps up decomposer activity, which pumps out enormous nutrient surplus, throwing off food and energy in wild abundance.

### Nurse Plants
Nurse, scaffold, and chaperone plants help young or tender species establish:
- **Nitrogen fixers** (New Mexico locust, Russian olive, Siberian pea shrub, tagasaste): Build soil and shelter young fruit trees
- **Pioneer species**: Fast-growing plants that create shade and wind protection
- At Jerome Osentowski's Central Rocky Mountain Permaculture Institute, nitrogen-fixing nurse plants were planted first, then fruit trees were placed in their shelter
- At Roxanne Swentzell's garden in New Mexico desert, Russian olives and Siberian pea shrubs created the initial shade that allowed fruit trees to survive

### Growing by Chunking
Rather than imposing a large, untested pattern on a landscape all at once, start small and expand from successful nuclei:
1. Begin with a small, intensely managed bed near the house (Zone 1)
2. Get one area growing successfully
3. Repeat the pattern with appropriate variations for new sites
4. These small patches of fertility link up into a resilient whole

Joel Glanzberg described this at Roxanne Swentzell's garden: "We'd work on creating a favorable spot, concentrating our resources there, and then grow out from those nuclei. And those nuclei started to link up."

### The "Pop" Phenomenon
After 3-5 years of establishment, ecological gardens often undergo a dramatic transformation — the system suddenly "pops" into vigorous life. Hemenway observed this at multiple sites:
- Roxanne Swentzell's New Mexico desert garden popped in year 5
- The Bullock brothers' Orcas Island food forest popped after several years
- Hemenway's own Oregon garden popped after 5 years of sheet mulching and dense planting

**What happens during the pop:**
- Soil organisms reach critical mass, cycling nutrients rapidly
- Shade from maturing trees retains moisture and creates benign microclimates
- Leaf litter builds humus that stores minerals and water
- Beneficial insects and birds arrive and establish predator-prey balance
- Plants that struggled for years suddenly grow several feet in a season
- The garden shifts from needing imports to generating its own fertility

## The Producer-Consumer-Decomposer Cycle

Understanding succession means understanding this fundamental cycle:

1. **Producers** (plants) harvest sunlight and create biomass
2. **Consumers** (animals, insects) eat plant material, convert to manure
3. **Decomposers** (worms, bacteria, fungi) break down organic matter into available nutrients
4. Nutrients cycle back to producers

In most conventional gardens, decomposers are few and weak, which constricts the vigor of producers and consumers. [[sheet-mulching]] and [[permaculture-animal-integration]] ramp up all three components simultaneously.

## The Garden as Organism

Think of the whole garden as an organism. The densely interconnected soil, plant, and animal life surge with nutrients, water, pollen, chemical messages, and other "information flows." As the gardener expands this intensely planted area, the area of thick interconnections expands, nutrient and energy flows link up, and the system begins to function as a single being.

### Why Diverse Gardens Are Resilient
- **Redundancy**: If one species fails, many others playing similar roles plug the gap
- **Multiple food webs**: Every inhabitant has several places to feed
- **Varied habitats**: Every species can find its preferred microclimate
- **Self-regulation**: Pest outbreaks trigger predator population surges
- **Nothing wasted**: Every resource entering the system is seized upon by some organism
- **Invasion resistance**: With so many species present, new invaders rarely find empty niches

Scientific support comes from Drake and Pimm (University of Tennessee), who found that randomly assembled communities of 15-40 species frequently self-organized into stable, invasion-resistant ecosystems. The more species available, the more likely the community "clicks."

## Starting Your Ecological Garden

### Where to Begin
1. **Start with the soil** — create a small bed of rich soil near the front or back door
2. **Find or create benign microclimates** — sheltered from wind, moderated temperature
3. **Use nurse plants** — nitrogen fixers and fast-growing pioneers
4. **Plant densely** — bare soil is wasted soil
5. **Observe and adjust** — watch what thrives and what doesn't

### Microclimate Strategy
- **Desert**: Find moist, shady sites near swales or rocks
- **Cold climate**: Find sunny, well-drained spots with wind protection
- **Windy site**: Create windbreaks before planting
- **General rule**: Look for sites with no wild swings of temperature, moisture, or sunlight

### Ecological Compromises
Hemenway advises pragmatism: "Using nonrenewable resources to create a landscape is justified if in the long run, that landscape will conserve or provide more resources than it took to build it." A one-time power-tilling or use of non-ideal amendments during establishment is preferable to giving up in exhaustion. "Doing an imperfect something is better than doing a perfect nothing."
