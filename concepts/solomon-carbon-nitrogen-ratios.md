# Carbon-Nitrogen Ratios and Soil Organic Matter

Understanding carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratios is fundamental to managing
soil organic matter effectively. Solomon provides one of the clearest
explanations of this critical topic in "[[gardening-when-it-counts]]," showing
how C/N ratios determine whether adding organic material builds or
destroys soil humus.

## What Is the Carbon-Nitrogen Ratio?

Every decomposable organic material can be characterized by how much
carbon (carbohydrate) it contains in relation to the amount of nitrogen
(protein) it contains. This is called its carbon-nitrogen ratio, written
C/N or C:N.

The absolute standard of comparison is the C/N of soil itself. Except in
the driest, hottest deserts, all soil contains humus -- the stable
residue of decomposed organic matter. Humus is a complex substance highly
resistant to further [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]]
completely and vanish from the soil, this happens very slowly.

Soil humus has a carbon-nitrogen ratio of around 12:1 in every climate.

## Adding High-Carbon Materials (C/N Above 12:1)

When you amend soil with organic material having a C/N higher than 12:1
(most plant residues, straw, leaves, sawdust), soil microbes "burn" the
excess carbon for fuel while preserving nitrogen until the C/N reaches
12:1. The result is higher soil humus, better tilth, and eventually,
nutrients become available to plants. But during decomposition, microbes
temporarily tie up nutrients -- the soil seems less fertile temporarily.

### C/N Ratios of Common Materials

- Sawdust: ~500:1
- Straw (cereal): ~100:1
- Paper: ~175:1
- Tree bark/needles: ~60-200:1
- Autumn tree leaves: ~50-60:1
- Grass hay (poor quality): ~40-50:1

## Adding Low-Carbon Materials (C/N Below 12:1)

When soil is amended with material having a C/N below 12:1 (fresh manure,
seed meals, green vegetation), bacteria convert surplus nitrogen to
ammonia, then to water-soluble nitrates -- a first-class fertilizer. But
the resulting higher nitrate level encourages microbes to multiply and
attack existing humus. The net result: slightly lower soil organic matter
than before. This is also what happens with [[fukuoka-textdoc-animal-manure-human-waste-natural-cycle-heretic]]: ~10-15:1
- Legume hulls: ~15-20:1
- Seaweed: ~15-20:1
- Summer grass clippings: ~20-25:1

## The Key Insight

- **Low C/N materials** (below 12:1) produce immediate nitrates and rapid
  plant growth, but slightly reduce soil humus and long-term health.
