# Psilocybin Psychological Mechanisms

The neuroscience of how psilocybin produces its psychological effects, including Default Mode Network suppression, serotonin receptor activity, and neuroplasticity.

## Overview

Psilocybin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) is a prodrug -- it is converted in the body to its active metabolite, psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine), which is structurally similar to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT). Psilocin acts primarily as a serotonin receptor agonist, producing its profound psychological effects through interactions with specific brain networks and receptor subtypes.

## Serotonin 5-HT2A Receptor Agonism

The primary mechanism of action is agonism (activation) of the **serotonin 5-HT2A receptor**, predominantly found in the cerebral cortex:

- Psilocin binds to 5-HT2A receptors with high affinity, mimicking serotonin's action but producing far more pronounced effects.
- 5-HT2A receptors are densely concentrated in layer V of the prefrontal cortex, an area critical for executive function, self-awareness, and the integration of sensory information.
- The receptor activation triggers a cascade of downstream effects including changes in gene expression, neuronal excitability, and the release of glutamate (the brain's primary excitatory neurotransmitter).
- This receptor is the same target as LSD, DMT, and mescaline, which is why these substances produce qualitatively similar effects despite different chemical structures.

## Default Mode Network Suppression

The most significant finding of modern psychedelic neuroscience is the effect of psilocybin on the **Default Mode Network (DMN)**:

- The DMN is a network of interconnected brain regions (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, angular gyrus) that is most active during rest, self-referential thinking, mind-wandering, and the maintenance of one's sense of ego or self.
- Under psilocybin, activity in the DMN is **dramatically decreased**, particularly in the medial prefrontal cortex and the anterior cingulate cortex.
- This suppression correlates strongly with subjective experiences of ego dissolution, the sense of oneness with the universe, and the feeling that ordinary categories of thought have broken down.
- The degree of DMN suppression predicts the intensity of the mystical-type experience and, in therapeutic settings, the degree of clinical improvement.
- In depressed individuals, the DMN is often hyperactive and associated with excessive rumination (repetitive negative self-focus). Psilocybin's suppression of this network is thought to "reset" these rigid patterns.

## Increased Brain Connectivity

Psilocybin does not simply reduce brain activity -- it reorganizes it:

- Functional MRI studies show that psilocybin **increases connectivity between brain regions that do not normally communicate**, particularly between areas of the default mode network and other networks.
- This increased global connectivity may explain the synesthetic experiences, novel associations, and creative insights often reported during psychedelic experiences.
- The brain under psilocybin shows a pattern of greater entropy (disorder or randomness) in its activity, which some researchers interpret as a return to a more flexible, less constrained state of consciousness.

## Neuroplasticity

Emerging evidence suggests psilocybin promotes structural changes in the brain:

- **Dendritic growth**: Animal studies have shown that psychedelics including psilocybin promote rapid growth of dendritic spines (the connections between neurons) in the prefrontal cortex.
- **BDNF release**: Psilocybin may increase the release of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a protein that supports the survival and growth of neurons.
- **Glutamate signaling**: Activation of 5-HT2A receptors triggers increased glutamate signaling, which is associated with neuroplasticity and learning.
- These neuroplastic effects are thought to underlie the long-lasting therapeutic benefits observed after just one or a few psilocybin sessions -- the drug creates a "window of plasticity" during which entrenched patterns of thought and behavior can be reorganized.

## The Entropic Brain Hypothesis

Robin Carhart-Harris and colleagues have proposed that the brain under psychedelics moves from a state of constrained, predictable activity (associated with normal waking consciousness) toward a state of greater entropy or disorder (associated with psychedelic states). In this model:

- Normal waking consciousness is a "secondary consciousness" built on top of a more primary, fluid state.
- Psychedelics temporarily dissolve the constraints that maintain normal consciousness, allowing a more "primary" state to emerge.
- This model helps explain why psychedelic experiences can feel both infantile and profoundly insightful -- they return the brain to a more flexible, less conditioned mode of operation.

## Therapeutic Implications

The combination of DMN suppression, increased connectivity, and neuroplasticity explains why psilocybin can produce rapid and lasting therapeutic effects:

1. Ego dissolution allows patients to step outside their rigid self-narrative
2. Increased connectivity allows novel perspectives and emotional processing
3. Neuroplasticity creates a window for lasting change in thought patterns
