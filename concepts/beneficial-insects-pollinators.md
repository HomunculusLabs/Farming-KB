# Beneficial Insects and Pollinators in the Garden

**Updated:** 2026-04-18

Attracting beneficial insects and pollinators is one of the most effective strategies for natural pest control and garden productivity. A garden that supports a diverse population of insects, birds, and other helpful animals requires far less intervention than one that relies on chemical controls. This approach is central to [[organic-pest-and-disease-management]].

## Why Attract Beneficials?

In a healthy ecosystem, pests are kept in check by their natural enemies. Chemical pesticides kill beneficial insects along with pests, creating a cycle of dependence. By designing habitat for beneficials, the garden achieves biological pest control that is self-sustaining, free, and increasingly effective over time. This is a key principle of [[permaculture-insectary-plants]] and [[permaculture-companion-planting]].

## Categories of Beneficial Insects

### Pollinators
Essential for fruit, seed, and vegetable production:
- **Honeybees:** Most widely managed pollinator; excellent for orchards and large plantings
- **Native bees:** 4,000+ species in North America; often more efficient pollinators than honeybees. Includes mason bees, leafcutter bees, bumblebees, sweat bees, and many others
- **Butterflies:** Pollinate while nectaring; also indicators of ecosystem health
- **Moths:** Night-flying pollinators for night-blooming plants
- **Hover flies:** Excellent pollinators; larvae also eat aphids
- **Beetles:** Many species pollinate; some are important for specific crops

### Predators
Insects that eat other insects:
- **Ladybugs (ladybird beetles):** Both adults and larvae consume aphids, mealybugs, scale insects. A single ladybug can eat 5,000 aphids in its lifetime
- **Lacewings:** Larvae are voracious predators of aphids, thrips, mites, and small caterpillars
- **Praying mantises:** Generalist predators of many pest insects
- **Ground beetles:** Predatory beetles that hunt at night; eat slugs, caterpillars, and soil-dwelling pests
- **Assassin bugs:** Large predatory bugs that prey on caterpillars, beetles, and other insects
- **Spiders:** Not insects, but essential predators in every garden ecosystem

### Parasitoids
Insects that lay eggs in or on other insects; the larvae consume the host:
- **Parasitic wasps:** Tiny wasps that lay eggs in aphids, caterpillars, and other pests. Attracted by umbelliferous flowers (dill, fennel, yarrow, Queen Anne's lace)
- **Tachinid flies:** Parasitize caterpillars, beetles, and bugs

## Plants for Beneficial Insects

### Insectary Plants (Best)
The following plants are magnets for beneficial insects:

**Umbellifers (Apiaceae family):**
- Dill, fennel, coriander/cilantro, parsley, Queen Anne's lace, yarrow, caraway, anise
- Flat flower heads provide landing platforms for tiny wasps and flies
- Produce abundant nectar and pollen

**Aster family (Asteraceae):**
- Sunflowers, cosmos, zinnias, marigolds, calendula, chamomile, coneflowers, asters, goldenrod
- Long bloom season provides continuous food
- Diverse flower shapes attract diverse insects

**Mint family (Lamiaceae):**
- Lavender, rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage, basil, bee balm, mint
- Aromatic flowers attract bees and other pollinators
- Many are culinary herbs -- double function

**Other excellent insectary plants:**
- Buckwheat (excellent bee forage, fast-growing cover crop)
- Clover and alfalfa (nitrogen fixation + bee forage)
- Sweet alyssum (tiny flowers attract tiny parasitic wasps)
- Phacelia (one of the best bee plants; also a cover crop)
- Vetch (winter cover crop that blooms early for bees)

### Native Plants
Native plants are often the best choice for supporting native pollinators, as they co-evolved together. Include a diversity of native flowering plants that bloom in succession from early spring through late fall. This diversity of planting supports the [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]] approach to garden design.

## Designing for Beneficial Insects

### Provide Continuous Bloom
Plant species that flower in succession:
- **Early spring:** Crocus, snowdrops, hellebore, fruit tree blossoms, clover
- **Late spring:** Phacelia, clover, chives, catmint, yarrow
- **Summer:** Dill, fennel, sunflowers, zinnias, coneflowers, basil, lavender
- **Fall:** Asters, goldenrod, sedum, Japanese anemone, witch hazel

### Create Habitat Structure
- **Bare ground:** Ground-nesting bees (70% of native bees) need patches of bare, undisturbed soil
- **Dead wood and logs:** Habitat for beetles, solitary bees, and other insects
- **Rock piles:** Shelter for predatory beetles, spiders, and reptiles
- **Brush piles:** Overwintering habitat for many beneficial insects
- **Water sources:** Shallow dishes with pebbles for landing (change water regularly)
- **Mud sources:** Needed by mason bees for nest construction

### Avoid Pesticides
Even organic pesticides can harm beneficial insects. If pest control is necessary:
- Use targeted methods (hand-picking, row covers, insecticidal soap for specific pests)
- Spray in the evening when bees are less active
- Accept some pest damage -- a healthy ecosystem tolerates low pest levels
- Focus on building soil health and plant diversity

## Birds as Pest Controllers

Birds are among the most effective pest controllers in the garden:
- **Insectivorous birds:** Chickadees, nuthatches, warblers, wrens consume enormous quantities of insects
- **Berry-eating birds:** Also eat insects during nesting season
- **Hawks and owls:** Control rodent populations

To attract birds:
- Provide water (birdbaths, shallow dishes)
- Plant berry-producing shrubs (elderberry, serviceberry, dogwood, juniper)
- Provide nesting sites (birdhouses, dead trees, dense shrubs)
- Avoid pesticides (birds eat insects -- pesticides poison birds indirectly)
- Leave some seed heads for winter food

## Specific Pest-Predator Relationships

| Pest | Natural Enemy | Attract With |
|------|--------------|-------------|
| Aphids | Ladybugs, lacewings, parasitic wasps, hover flies | Dill, fennel, yarrow, sweet alyssum |
| Codling moth | Trichogramma wasps | Umbelliferous flowers |
| Cabbage worm | Parasitic wasps | Dill, mint family plants |
| Tomato hornworm | Braconid wasps | Small-flowered plants |
| Slugs | Ground beetles, frogs | Ground cover, rock piles |
| Spider mites | Ladybugs, lacewings | Drought-stressed plants attract them; avoid over-fertilizing |
| Whiteflies | Encarsia wasps | Marigolds, thyme |
| Japanese beetle | Tiphia wasps, nematodes | White clover (attracts Tiphia) |
