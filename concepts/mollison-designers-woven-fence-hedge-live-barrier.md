# Woven Fence Hedge Live Barrier

Living fences, woven wattle hedges, and hedgerow barriers are multifunctional
boundary and partition elements extensively discussed by Bill Mollison in the
[[permaculture-designers-manual-bamboo]]. These structures serve simultaneously as
livestock barriers, windbreaks, wildlife corridors, browse sources, privacy
screens, and microclimate moderators. Mollison advocates replacing dead fences
with living systems wherever possible, as they produce yields, require less
maintenance over time, and integrate with the biological systems of the farm.

## Living Fences

A living fence is a barrier formed by planting closely spaced trees, shrubs,
or succulents that grow together to form an impenetrable hedge. Living fences
are established by planting cuttings, seedlings, or rooted poles directly in
the ground along the desired fence line. Depending on species, a functional
barrier can develop within one to three years.

### Species Selection for Living Fences

Species selection depends on climate, function, and available plant material.
Mollison emphasises using locally adapted species that provide additional
yields beyond their barrier function:

- **Tropical and subtropical:** Gliricidia sepium (madre de cacao),
  Jatropha curcas, Euphorbia tirucalli, Hibiscus tiliaceus, Moringa
  oleifera, Leucaena leucocephala, Cajanus cajan (pigeon pea), and
  various cactus species (Opuntia, Cereus).

- **Temperate:** Osage orange (Maclura pomifera), hawthorn (Crataegus
  spp.), blackthorn (Prunus spinosa), willow (Salix spp.), hazel (Corylus
  avellana), honey locust (Gleditsia triacanthos), and multiflora rose.

- **Arid zone:** Various Acacia spp., Prosopis spp., Ziziphus spp., and
  cactus species that form dense spiny barriers with minimal water.

### Establishment Methods

Living fences can be established by several methods:

1. **Direct planting of cuttings:** Many species (Gliricidia, Jatropha,
  willow, poplar, fig) root readily from large cuttings (1 to 3 metres
  long) planted directly in the ground at 20 to 50 cm spacing. Cuttings
  should be placed during the rainy season or with supplemental irrigation.

2. **Seedling planting:** Nursery-raised seedlings are planted at close
   spacing (30 to 100 cm) and allowed to grow together. This method works
   for species that do not root easily from cuttings.

3. **Pollen and rooted poles:** Large living poles are driven into the
   ground, rooted in place, and then interwoven with smaller branches or
   wire. This produces an instant barrier that becomes more solid as the
   poles sprout and grow.

## Woven Wattle Hurdles

Wattle hurdles are traditional woven fence panels made from flexible
branches, typically willow, hazel, or ash. While not living structures
themselves, wattle hurdles are often used in combination with living fence
rows:

- As temporary barriers while living fence plants establish
- As infill between established living fence posts
- As portable fencing for rotational grazing systems

Wattle weaving involves driving vertical stakes at regular intervals and
weaving flexible horizontal branches between them in a basket-weave pattern.
The resulting panel is strong, attractive, and biodegradable. Willow
wattle hurdles are particularly valuable because willow cuttings used in
the hurdle can sprout and root, gradually converting the dead fence into a
living one.

## Hedgerow Systems

Traditional European hedgerows are complex linear ecosystems that combine
trees, shrubs, and herbaceous plants in dense boundary strips. Mollison
describes hedgerows as among the most ecologically rich landscape elements
in agricultural regions. A well-developed hedgerow may contain 5 to 15 tree
and shrub species in a layered structure that provides:

- **Wind protection:** Hedgerows reduce wind speed on the leeward side for
  distances of 10 to 15 times the hedge height. This improves crop yields,
  reduces soil erosion, and protects livestock from wind chill.

- **Wildlife habitat:** Hedgerows serve as corridors connecting habitat
  patches, supporting birds, mammals, insects, and amphibians. They are
  critical for maintaining [[fungal-biodiversity]] in agricultural landscapes.

- **Livestock exclusion:** Dense hedgerows form effective barriers against
  livestock without the cost and maintenance of wire fencing.

- **Browse and fodder:** Hedgerow leaves and shoots provide supplementary
  browse for livestock, particularly during dry seasons when pasture
  quality declines.

- **Wood and craft products:** Hedgerow management through periodic
  laying or coppicing produces poles, firewood, and craft materials.
  [[permaculture-designers-manual-coppice-systems]] integrate naturally with hedgerow management.

## Windbreak Function

Windbreak effectiveness depends on density and height. The most effective
windbreaks have a porosity of 40 to 60 percent, allowing some wind to pass
through while deflecting the majority upward. Solid barriers (walls, dense
hedges) create strong turbulence on the leeward side, while porous barriers
create a more extensive calm zone. Windbreak design is a critical component
of [[mollison-windbreak-design]] in [[pdc-integrated-farm-design]].

## Livestock Exclusion and Management

Living fences and hedges serve as permanent livestock barriers in
[[pasture-management]] systems. They eliminate the ongoing cost of replacing
wire, posts, and hardware. Some species, particularly spiny ones, are
effectively stock-proof once established. Others may require a single strand
of wire or electric fencing at the top or bottom to prevent livestock pushing
through gaps.

In rotational grazing systems, living fences define permanent paddock
boundaries while portable electric fencing provides temporary internal
subdivisions.

## Maintenance

Living fences require maintenance during establishment (watering, weeding,
protection from browsing) but become increasingly self-sustaining as they
mature. Ongoing management includes periodic pruning to maintain barrier
density, harvesting of useful products, and replacement of any gaps caused
by disease or damage. Traditional hedgerow laying (partially cutting through
stems and bending them horizontal) rejuvenates aging hedges and maintains
density at the base.
