## Cecids (Mycophila sp.)

Cecid adults are very small (less than 1mm), making them extremely difficult
to detect inside growing rooms. Larvae are 1-3mm in length and feed by
sucking nutrients directly from hyphae. They also attack mushroom stipes and
caps. The most alarming characteristic of cecids is their ability to
reproduce by paedogenesis: each larva produces 14-20 daughter larvae every 6
days without maturing into adults. This allows populations to explode rapidly.

Heavy cecid infestations turn mushroom bags or beds orange from the sheer
number of orange-colored larvae. Larvae are well-known vectors of various
bacteria that cause mushroom breakdown and deterioration. Their tiny size
makes exclusion through physical barriers the most effective control strategy.

## Phorids (Megaselia tamiladuensis)

Phorid adults are 2-4mm and are characterized by quick, hopping movement on
substrate surfaces. Larvae are 4-6mm long with white, transparent bodies and
no distinct black head capsule, distinguishing them from sciarid larvae.
Phorid larvae feed on mycelia and create cavities in [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]].
Phorids usually occur during summer cultivation but normally cause less damage
than other fly species.

## Mycetophils (Mycetophila sp.)

Mycetophil adults are large and yellowish in appearance. Larvae are 15-20mm
long, grayish-brown, and construct cocoons with threads on substrates or
mushrooms. Infection by mycetophil larvae causes young fruiting bodies to
turn brown and stop growing entirely. Larvae also create large cavities in
the stipes of developing mushrooms. Their relatively large size makes them
easier to detect than other pest species.

## Mites (Tarsonemus sp. and Histiostoma sp.)

Mites belong to the class Arachnida, not Insecta. Two major mushroom-damaging
genera are recognized: Tarsonemus and Histiostoma. Mites are small and
invisible to the naked eye, making early detection difficult. They feed on
mycelia and fruiting bodies, causing yield loss and decreased mushroom
quality. Beyond direct feeding damage, mites carry pathogens and nematodes.
Some species cause itchy rashes among growers handling infested substrate.
