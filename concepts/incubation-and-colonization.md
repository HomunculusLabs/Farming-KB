# Incubation and Colonization

## Overview

After inoculation, the jars enter the incubation phase, during which the spores
germinate and the mycelium colonizes the substrate. This stage requires
patience and attention to environmental conditions, particularly temperature
and light. Understanding the colonization timeline and the visible signs of
progress is essential for successful cultivation.

## Immediate Post-Inoculation Steps

After inoculation is complete, tighten the lid bands on all jars and retape
the needle holes with fresh masking tape. Place the jars in a safe location
out of direct sunlight. Only indirect light is required during incubation.

## Temperature Requirements

The ideal incubation temperature is around 70 degrees Fahrenheit (21 degrees
Celsius). At this temperature, spore germination will begin within 3 to 5
days. Any room temperature is generally acceptable. The guiding principle is
that cool temperatures are never a problem, but overheating must be avoided.

If indoor temperatures drop, overhead light shining down on the tops of the
jars provides a perfect heating technique for this culturing stage. A
clamping-type light with a reflector works well. Monitor temperature with a
thermometer to ensure jars are not overheated.

## Spore Germination

Germinating spores appear as small white fuzzy spots on the substrate. These
quickly grow and spread, developing cottony white growth and strandy
structures called "rhizomorphs." Rhizomorphs are rope-like bundles of
mycelial threads that indicate healthy, vigorous growth.

## Colonization Timeline

The complete colonization process follows this timeline:

1. **Spore inoculation to spore germination**: Within one week at 70 degrees
   Fahrenheit.

2. **Spore germination to complete colonization**: Approximately 2 to 3 weeks.
   The mycelium spreads throughout the substrate until the entire cake turns
   white.

3. **Colonization to fruiting cycle start**: Within 2 weeks after the
   substrate surface is fully colonized.

4. **Total time**: 4 to 6 weeks from spore inoculation to fruiting.

## The Canning Jar Lid: Loose or Tight

There are two choices for lid tightness during incubation, and the correct
choice depends on the substrate moisture content:

**Tight lid**: Preserves moisture content. Best when the substrate is on the
dry side. However, with very high moisture content, a tight lid can cause
water to collect in the bottom of the jar, which must be avoided.

**Loose lid**: Allows excess moisture to escape. Best when using the maximum
fruiting formula or when the substrate has high water content.

The correct approach is a balance between the water needs of the mycelium,
the jar size, available air space, and the type of vermiculite used. Only
through direct experimentation and comparison can the right balance be found
for a given set of conditions. The author recommends taking notes and going
with what fruits the best.

## The Pinning Process

After the substrate turns completely white with mycelium (2 to 3 weeks after
inoculation), the jars are left to sit in indirect light. The mycelium
continues to infiltrate the substrate, consuming nutrients until it has enough
stored energy to trigger the fruiting cycle.

In less than a week to a few weeks after surface colonization, tiny white
"pin" like structures begin to appear on the substrate surface. This stage is
called "pinning" and marks the beginning of the fruiting cycle.

### Primordia Formation

Soon after pinning, small round fungus growths appear that begin to turn
yellow. These develop into "primordia," which are tiny worm-like structures
with tiny reddish heads. The primordia are the first actual mushrooms and
signal that the cake is ready to be removed from the jar for fruiting.

## Removing the Fungus Cake from the Jar

The optimal time to remove the fungus cake is when primordia appear while
still in the jar. Handle with care to avoid damaging these delicate
structures.

### Removal Procedure

1. Remove the lid from the jar.
2. With a clean fork, scrape away the majority of the dry top vermiculite
   layer. Some wispy mycelium may be visible in this layer.
3. Place an old jar lid over the jar mouth and turn the jar upside down.
4. Lightly slam the jar down on a table cushioned with a magazine. The
   fungus cake will slide out onto the old jar cap.
5. The jar cap functions as a base for the cake during handling and transfer.

### Handling Precautions

When handling the fungus cake, be careful not to squeeze or bruise it.
Bruising results in a bluish discoloration on the mycelium. While the fungus
is resilient and can tolerate a certain amount of handling, minimize contact.
The aroma of a healthy cake is distinctly mushroomy and pleasant.

### Moisture Removal

As soon as the cake comes out of the jar, daub it with a piece of loose tissue
paper to soak up any water droplets that may have deposited on the cake during
removal. Immediately place the cake into the dual chambered terrarium for the
fruiting cycle.

## Aborts and Mutants

Some of the first mushrooms to form are "abhorts," characterized by convoluted
caps, gnarly stems, and stunted growth. Despite their appearance, abhorts are
described as primo in magic alkaloids, even more potent than the stately
normal specimens. Another form of mutant, blobs of fungus with little or no
cap, also appears and is good for harvesting.

The fruiting cycle lasts approximately 2 weeks. After the initial flush, the
mycelium cake begins to turn blue and no more mushrooms form. Secondary
fruiting may occur if the cake is thoroughly cleaned, but yields are usually
sparse and small.
