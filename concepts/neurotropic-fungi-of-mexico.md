# [[southern-hemisphere-origin-of-neurotropic-fungi]] of Mexico

Mexico possesses the highest diversity of [[neurotropic-fungi]] of any
country on Earth, with 76 known species — 44 of which belong to the genus
*Psilocybe*, representing 39% of all known *Psilocybe* species worldwide.
The indigenous peoples of Mexico have the longest and best-documented
traditions of [[sacred-mushroom-hypothesis]]-mayan-civilisation-and-psilocybin-mushroom-use]], dating back to [[pre-columbian-mushroom-use]]
civilizations. See also [[psilocybe-genus-diversity-and-taxonomy]].

## Teonanácatl and Indigenous Names

The Nahuatl word "teonanácatl" (literally "[[soma-controversy-fly-agaric-as-divine-mushroom]]" or "flesh of
the gods") was first reported by the Franciscan friar Bernardino de
Sahagún (1569-1582) and later by [[richard-evans-schultes]] (1939). The term
is now used indiscriminately to name any Mexican hallucinogenic fungi,
though originally it referred specifically to sacred mushrooms used in
indigenous ceremonies.

Guzmán (1997) reported more than 200 common names for neurotropic fungi
in Mexico, many in indigenous languages. These include:

- "Apipiltzin" — meaning "kid" or "little boy"
- "Atkad" — meaning "mayor" or "leader"
- "Di nizé taaya" — meaning "fungus of the genius"
- "Shi thó" — meaning "that eruptions thing"
- "Teotlaquilnanácatl" — meaning "divine fungus"

Spanish names commonly used by indigenous peoples include "san isidros"
(referring to San Isidro, patron saint of agriculture), "pajaritos"
(little birds), and "derrumbes" (landslides), referring respectively to
*Psilocybe cubensis*, *P. mexicana*, and *P. zapotecorum*.

## Indigenous Groups and Mushroom Use

The most well-documented ethnic groups that use neurotropic fungi in
Mexico include the Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Chinantec, Nahuatl, and
Otomí peoples of the central and southern regions of the country. These
groups use neurotropic mushrooms primarily in healing ceremonies and
divination rituals conducted by curanderos (healers) or shamans.

The Mazatec of Oaxaca are perhaps the best known internationally due to
the work of R. Gordon Wasson, who participated in a velada (night
ceremony) with the Mazatec curandera María Sabina in 1955. This event
was pivotal in bringing the existence of sacred mushrooms to Western
attention.

## Rediscovery and Scientific Documentation

The modern scientific study of Mexican neurotropic fungi began with the
rediscovery of their traditional use among several groups of indigenous
peoples. The key researchers in this period were:

- [[richard-evans-schultes-updated]] (1939) — first to study the mushrooms in
  detail, initially attributing all Mexican neurotropic fungi to
  *[[panaeolus-campanulatus]]* var. *sphinctrinus*
- R. Gordon Wasson (1953-1958) — amateur mycologist and banker whose
  New Yorker article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" (1957) brought
  international attention
- Roger Heim (1956-1958) — French mycologist who cultivated the
  mushrooms and confirmed their taxonomic identity
- Rolf Singer (1949-1960) — provided more accurate taxonomic
  classifications and expanded the known species list
- Gastón Guzmán (1959-present) — Mexican mycologist who became the
  world's foremost authority on *Psilocybe* taxonomy

Guzmán (1990a, 1990b) divided Mexican mycology into two periods: before
Wasson, Heim, and Singer's studies (before 1954-1958), and after. The
studies on neurotropic fungi were so important that they generated
interest in studying all fungi in Mexico.

## Species Diversity by Region

Mexico's neurotropic fungi are concentrated in the subtropical and
temperate humid forests of the central and southern states:

- **Oaxaca:** The richest state for neurotropic fungi diversity, home to
  the Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, and Chinantec peoples. Species include
  *P. mexicana*, *P. caerulescens*, *P. zapotecorum*, *P. muliercula*,
  and *P. hoogshagenii*.

- **Puebla and Tlaxcala:** Important for species such as *P.
  aztecorum* found at high elevations near Popocatépetl.

- **Veracruz:** Home to diverse species in the tropical forests of the
  Gulf coast region.

- **Chiapas:** Southernmost state with neurotropic fungi in both
  lowland tropical and highland temperate environments.

## The Mixtecan Narcotic Puffballs

Among the most unusual aspects of Mexican ethnomycology is the use of
certain puffball species (Lycoperdales) as "narcotic fungi" by the Mixtec
Indians of Oaxaca. Heim and Wasson (1962) reported the use of
*Lycoperdon mixtecorum* and *L. marginatum* as sacred fungi.

However, Ott et al. (1975) later found that the Mixtec "narcotic
puffballs" were actually a mixture of at least nine species of fungi,
most of which had no neurotropic properties. The conclusion was that the
Mixtec used these puffballs interchangeably with true neurotropic
species, creating confusion between edible puffballs and hallucinogenic
mushrooms. Some of the puffballs were experimentally proven to be
poisonous, including *Scleroderma verrucosum*.

## Dictyophora Indusiata — The Veiled Mushroom

*Dictyophora indusiata* and its three forms are used as special "narcotic"
fungi for divination purposes among the Chinantec Indians in Oaxaca.
Despite its traditional sacred status, no reliable chemical studies have
confirmed neurotropic properties. This represents another case where
indigenous classification of sacred fungi does not necessarily correspond
to the presence of known psychoactive compounds.

## The Cordyceps of Nevado de Toluca

*Elaphomyces* species — the truffle-like hosts of *Cordyceps capitata*
and *C. ophioglossoides* — are placed at the center of the room during
special nocturnal ceremonies performed by indigenous peoples of the
Nevado de Toluca region. These serve as a "director" of the ceremony.
The *Cordyceps* species themselves are used together with *Psilocybe
muliercula*, called "hombrecitos" (little men) and "mujercitas" (little
women), respectively.

## Modern Cultural Impact

The use of neurotropic mushrooms as a recreational drug, which began in
the 1960s, has led to the degradation of traditional use in Mexico.
Governments of many countries have forbidden the use and commerce of
these fungi, creating an underground market. The tropical species
*Psilocybe cubensis* is frequently reported from Europe, but nearly
always in the form of illegally cultivated or dried specimens imported
from tropical countries.
