## Mycorrhizal Fungi and the Whole Soil Food Web

## Terminology: Getting the Words Right

The terminology around [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] is frequently misused, even among professionals. [[elaine-ingham]] provides clear distinctions:

- **Mycorrhizal fungus** — Refers to the fungus itself, when discussed independently of any plant association. This is the organism in isolation.
- **Mycorrhiza** — A single association between one root and one mycorrhizal fungus. The word describes the *relationship*, not either partner alone. For example, "a mycorrhiza" is one such root-fungus partnership.
- **Mycorrhizae** — The plural form, meaning "fungus-root." This term describes multiple root-fungus associations. It does not refer to multiple fungi on their own.

Using these terms precisely matters in scientific communication and in understanding what is actually being measured, applied, or discussed in soil biology.

## Application Methods: Getting Spores Next to Roots

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are obligate symbionts — they must physically contact living roots to establish their beneficial relationship. This simple [[duggar-mushroom-nutrition-science-fermentation-biological-requirement]] creates the central challenge of application: **how do you get the spores next to the root?**

There is no single answer. Application methods vary enormously depending on:

- **The plant species** — Different plants form associations with different mycorrhizal types and have different root architectures.
- **The growing system** — Field crops, orchards, container plants, and restored landscapes each present different physical constraints.
- **Soil conditions** — Soil texture, moisture, temperature, and chemistry all affect spore movement and survival.

In some situations, applying spores to the soil surface works perfectly well. The spores are carried down by water movement, root growth, or soil fauna, and colonization can be rapid and extensive. In other soils — heavy clays, compacted layers, arid environments — surface application would be futile because the spores would never reach the root zone.

The critical considerations when designing an application strategy:

1. **Avoid harming the roots** — Physical damage or chemical injury during application defeats the purpose.
2. **Avoid killing the spores** — Spores are vulnerable to UV light, desiccation, extreme temperatures, and certain chemicals.
3. **Provide fungal foods** — Just getting spores to the root zone is not enough. Fungal foods (complex carbohydrates, humic acids, specific amino acids) should be placed near the root to support germination and early hyphal growth.

## The Whole Food Web: Why Bacteria Alone Are Not Enough

A recurring theme in Ingham"s work is that **the entire [[soil-food-web]] is required for genuine, lasting disease suppression**. Bacteria contribute important disease-prevention mechanisms, but they account for only a fraction of the full defensive capacity that a healthy soil ecosystem provides.

### The Stall Effect

Adding almost any organism to a severely degraded soil will produce a noticeable improvement. This initial improvement is real but misleading. Over time, the benefits **stall** because a partial food web cannot sustain itself or provide comprehensive protection. The missing functional groups create gaps that pathogens can exploit.

### Disease Prevention Spectrum

Diseases vary in how difficult they are to prevent through biological means:

- **Easy-to-prevent diseases** — Often controlled by bacteria alone, particularly through competitive exclusion on leaf surfaces or in the rhizosphere.
- **Harder-to-prevent diseases** — Require the full complement of fungal, protozoan, nematode, and microarthropod interactions to suppress. These organisms create the complex [[keystone-species-fungal]] that maintain disease suppression over time.

### Why Compost Teas Fail

Tea applications frequently disappoint when they lack the full set of organisms. A tea dominated by bacteria may show short-term benefits but will fail against the more challenging disease organisms. The tea must contain adequate fungal biomass, protozoa, nematodes, and the diversity needed to re-establish functional food web pathways. Without the complete organism set, the biological leverage is simply insufficient.

## Fungal Biomass Minimums and Targets

Ingham's research has established practical fungal biomass thresholds for compost teas used in disease prevention:

| Metric | Value | Notes |
|--------|-------|-------|
| **Minimum fungal biomass** | 2 micrograms per gram | Below this, tea reliably fails to prevent disease |
| **Maximum observed** | 800 micrograms per gram | Not a typo — extremely fungal-dominated teas can reach this level |
| **Practical range** | 2–800 micrograms | Higher values may be diluted while retaining effectiveness |

The 2 microgram minimum is an empirical threshold derived from field observations of where tea applications consistently succeed in disease suppression. Below this level, the fungal component is too weak to contribute meaningfully to the protective food web.

At the upper extreme, 800 micrograms represents an extraordinarily fungal-dominated tea. Such teas are typically brewed with strong fungal food sources and extended brew times. These can be diluted and still exceed the minimum threshold, making them efficient for large-scale application.

## Dormancy and Organism Preservation

A significant challenge in commercial tea production and distribution is keeping organisms viable during storage and transport. To extend shelf life, organisms in the tea must be put into a dormant state.

### Methods and Trade-offs

Dormancy is typically induced through osmotic or desiccation stress — changing the solute concentration or moisture level to trigger the organisms' natural survival responses. However, this process is not without cost:

- **Approximately 50% of species are lost** during the dormancy induction process. Many organisms simply cannot survive the transition to a dormant state under the methods currently available.
- The surviving species are typically the more resilient generalists, meaning that delicate specialists — often the most functionally important organisms — are disproportionately lost.

This species loss has profound implications for stored or shipped tea products. A tea that was fully diverse at the time of brewing may arrive at the application site with significantly reduced functional capacity.

## The Importance of Diversity

Diversity is not an abstract ecological ideal — it is a practical requirement for soil health. Ingham's data from SFI (Soil Foodweb Inc.) testing provides a striking benchmark:

> **Approximately 25,000 unique DNA strands per gram** are found in good, healthy soil and in high-quality compost.

This extraordinary diversity represents the full [[functional-redundancy-fungal-communities]] that allows a soil ecosystem to:

- Resist disturbance without collapsing
- Suppress a wide range of pathogens simultaneously
- Cycle nutrients efficiently across varying conditions
- Maintain productivity under environmental stress

When diversity drops significantly below this benchmark, the soil loses functional resilience. Gaps appear [[comparison-soil-protozoa-vs-nematodes-in-nutrient-cycling]] pathways, disease suppression weakens, and the system becomes increasingly vulnerable to disruption.

## DNA Testing: Approaches and Limitations

Modern DNA-based testing (such as metabarcoding and qPCR) offers powerful tools for characterizing [[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]]. However, these approaches have important limitations:

- **DNA persistence** — DNA from dead organisms persists in soil, so DNA-based counts may overestimate living, active populations.
- **Extraction bias** — Not all organisms lyse equally well during DNA extraction. Fungal spores with tough walls, for example, may be underrepresented.
- **Reference database gaps** — Many soil organisms have no close match in genetic databases, making identification to species level impossible.
- **Functional vs. taxonomic diversity** — DNA tests reveal *who* is present but not *what they are doing*. An organism detected by DNA may be dormant, inactive, or dead.

For practical soil management decisions, direct microscopy and functional assessments complement DNA data by revealing organism activity, biomass, and community structure in real time.

## Glomalin: The Sticky Foundation

Glomalin is a glycoprotein produced by vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi and certain bacteria. It is a major component of the sticky, aggregate-binding materials that give healthy soil its crumb structure and water-holding capacity.

Ingham describes glomalin as "the tip of the iceberg" — it is the most studied of the soil sticky materials, but many other biologically produced compounds contribute to soil aggregation and stability. The recognition of glomalin's importance represents a broader shift toward understanding that **soil physical structure is largely a biological product**.

Functions of glomalin and related compounds include:

- Binding soil particles into stable aggregates
- Sequestering carbon in recalcitrant forms
- Improving water infiltration and retention
- Protecting hyphae and organic matter from rapid decomposition

## SFI Testing and Morphotyping

Soil Foodweb Inc. (SFI) developed a practical assessment methodology based on **direct microscopy and morphotyping** — identifying and counting organisms by their visible physical characteristics rather than by DNA analysis.

Morphotyping advantages:

- Assesses **living, active** organisms rather than DNA remnants
- Provides **biomass estimates** that correlate with functional capacity
- Relatively rapid and cost-effective compared to DNA sequencing
- Directly informs management decisions with actionable thresholds

SFI testing focuses on the key functional groups of [[soil-food-web]]: total and active bacteria, total and active fungi, protozoa, nematodes (by feeding group), and microarthropods. By tracking these groups over time, practitioners can monitor the progress of soil food web restoration and diagnose specific deficiencies that need to be addressed.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Precision in terminology** reflects precision in understanding — mycorrhizal fungus, mycorrhiza, and mycorrhizae are not interchangeable.
2. **Spore placement** is the critical logistical challenge in mycorrhizal application; match the method to the site conditions.
3. **The whole food web** is required for lasting disease suppression — bacterial additions alone will stall.
4. **Fungal biomass of at least 2 micrograms per gram** is the minimum threshold for effective disease prevention via compost tea.
5. **Dormancy induction costs approximately half the species diversity** — fresh application is always preferable to stored product.
6. **25,000 unique DNA strands per gram** is the benchmark for healthy soil diversity.
7. **Glomalin and related biological glues** are fundamental to soil structure, not merely a curiosity.
8. **SFI morphotyping** provides practical, functional assessments that complement but do not replace DNA-based methods.
