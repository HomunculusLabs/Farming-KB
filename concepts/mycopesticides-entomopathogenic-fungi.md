## Overview

Mycopesticides are fungi that parasitize and kill insect pests, offering a biological alternative to chemical pesticides. Paul Stamets has been a leading advocate for developing entomopathogenic fungi as targeted pest control agents, documenting their use against termites, carpenter ants, and agricultural pests in "Mycelium Running."

Source: [[mycelium-running-techniques]]

## Entomopathogenic Fungi: How They Work

### Infection Mechanism

Entomopathogenic fungi infect insects through a multi-stage process:

1. **Spore attachment** — fungal spores land on the insect's exoskeleton and attach using adhesive properties
2. **Germination** — under appropriate conditions (moisture, temperature), spores germinate on the insect surface
3. **Penetration** — specialized hyphae called appressoria form, applying pressure and secreting enzymes to penetrate the insect cuticle
4. **Internal colonization** — once inside, fungal hyphae spread through the insect's body cavity (hemocoel)
5. **Death** — the fungus produces toxins and consumes the insect's internal tissues
6. **Sporulation** — fungal fruiting bodies emerge from the dead insect, releasing spores to infect new hosts

### Key Enzymes

The fungi produce cuticle-degrading enzymes including:
- **Proteases** — break down protein in the insect exoskeleton
- **Chitinases** — dissolve chitin, the main structural component of insect cuticle
- **Lipases** — break down lipid components of the cuticle

## Major Entomopathogenic Fungi

### Metarhizium Species

Metarhizium anisopliae and related species are among the most widely studied mycopesticides:
- Broad host range across insect orders
- Commercial formulations available in many countries
- Used against termites, grasshoppers, beetles, and other pests
- Can be applied as spore sprays or incorporated into bait stations

### Beauveria Species

[[beauveria-bassiana]] is the most commercially developed entomopathogenic fungus:
- Attacks a wide range of insect pests including whiteflies, thrips, aphids, and beetles
- Multiple commercial products available (e.g., BotaniGard, Mycotrol)
- Used in both greenhouse and field applications
- Compatible with integrated pest management (IPM) programs

### Cordyceps and Ophiocordyceps

The Cordyceps group includes species famous for their dramatic parasitic effects:
- **Cordyceps militaris** — attacks lepidopteran larvae
- **Ophiocordyceps unilateralis** — the "zombie ant fungus" that alters ant behavior to spread spores
- Some Cordyceps species have been used in traditional Chinese medicine for centuries
- The behavioral manipulation capabilities of some species offer intriguing possibilities for pest management

## Termite Control with Fungi

### The Termite Problem

Termites cause billions of dollars in damage annually. Traditional chemical treatments (arsenic, borates, fipronil) have significant environmental and health concerns.

### Fungal Approach

Stamets documented the use of Metarhizium anisopliae against termites:
- Termites that encounter the fungus become infected and carry spores back to the colony
- The social grooming behavior of termites actually facilitates fungal spread within the colony
- Unlike chemical baits, fungal spores can be self-propagating — infected termites spread the fungus to nestmates
- The fungus can persist in the environment, providing long-term suppression

### Challenges

- Termites have grooming behaviors that can remove spores before infection
- Environmental conditions (moisture, temperature) affect spore viability and germination
- Entomopathogenic nematodes also attack termites, creating competition
- Some termite species show behavioral avoidance of fungal spores

## Advantages of Mycopesticides

### Environmental Benefits

- **Target specificity** — many entomopathogenic fungi attack only insects or only specific insect groups
- **No residue** — fungi decompose after killing the host, leaving no harmful chemical residues
- **Self-propagating** — infected insects can spread the fungus to other pests
- **Resistance management** — harder for insects to develop resistance to a living organism
- **Organic certification** — many fungal pesticides are approved for organic agriculture

### Safety Profile

- Generally non-toxic to mammals, birds, fish, and plants
- No known carcinogenic or mutagenic effects
- Does not bioaccumulate in food chains
- Safe for beneficial insects at appropriate application rates

***

## Limitations and Challenges

### Environmental Sensitivity

- Spore viability depends on moisture, temperature, and UV exposure
- Most effective in humid environments
- Field persistence can be limited by weather conditions
- May require repeated applications for sustained control

### Production and Delivery

- Mass production of high-quality spores is technically challenging
- Formulations must protect spores during storage and application
- Application methods (spraying, bait stations, injection) must be optimized for each pest-target combination

### Regulatory Hurdles

- Registration of biological pesticides can be as complex and expensive as chemical pesticide registration
- Each new strain-target combination may require separate registration
- Quality control standards for biological products are still evolving

***

## Future Directions

- **Genetic improvement** — selecting or engineering strains with increased virulence and environmental tolerance
- **Combination products** — using multiple entomopathogenic fungi or combining fungi with other biological control agents
- **Smart delivery systems** — bait stations, attractants, and targeted application methods
- **Precision agriculture** — drone-based application for large-scale pest control
- **Climate adaptation** — developing strains effective under changing environmental conditions
