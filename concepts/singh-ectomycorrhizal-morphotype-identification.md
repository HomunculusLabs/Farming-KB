# Ectomycorrhizal Morphotype Identification

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) morphotyping is the process of identifying
[[ectomycorrhizal-associations]] based on their macroscopic and microscopic
morphological features. Unlike [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], which are
identified primarily from spores (see
[[singh-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-spore-identification]]), ECM fungi are most
commonly identified from the distinctive structures they form on fine
root tips. Morphotyping remains a fundamental skill in mycorrhizal
research, forestry, and ecological assessment, even as molecular methods
increasingly complement traditional approaches.

## Overview of Ectomycorrhizal Structures

ECM associations involve mutualistic symbioses between predominantly
Basidiomycota (and some Ascomycota) and the roots of woody plants,
especially trees in families such as Pinaceae, Fagaceae, Betulaceae,
Dipterocarpaceae, and Myrtaceae. The ECM root tip consists of several
distinct elements: the mantle (a fungal sheath surrounding the root), the
Hartig net (intercellular hyphal penetration into the root cortex), and
the extraradical mycelium (hyphae extending into the soil). Each of these
structures provides diagnostic characters for morphotyping.

## The Mantle: Macroscopic and Microscopic Features

### Macroscopic Characters
The mantle is the most visible feature for field identification. Key
macroscopic characters include:

- Color: mantles range from white, cream, and yellow to orange, red,
  brown, gray, and black. Some change color when bruised or exposed
  to air (oxidation reactions), providing useful diagnostic characters.
- Surface texture: mantles may be smooth, cottony, felty, woolly,
  hairy, or rough. Surface features such as warts, scales, or
  crystalline deposits (encrustations) are genus-specific.
- Shape and branching: ECM root tips may be monopodial-pinnate (like
  a feather), pyramidal, coralloid (branched), tuberculate (forming
  large sclerotium-like aggregates), or irregular.
- Size: tip dimensions vary from less than 1 mm to several centimeters
  in the case of tuberculate morphotypes.
- Ramification pattern: the overall branching architecture of the
  mycorrhizal root system is characteristic for certain fungal taxa.

### Microscopic Mantle Anatomy
Under the microscope, the mantle reveals its internal organization.
Mantle layers are described from the outermost (pseudoparenchymatous or
prosenchymatous) to the innermost layers. Pseudoparenchymatous mantles
consist of isodiametric cells resembling plant parenchyma, while
prosenchymatous mantles are composed of elongated hyphae arranged
parallel or loosely interwoven. The number and arrangement of mantle
layers, cell dimensions, septation patterns, and presence of specialized
cells (cystidia, lactifers) are important taxonomic characters.

## Hartig Net Structure

The Hartig net is the interface for nutrient exchange between the fungus
and the host plant. In most ECM associations, Hartig net hyphae grow
between cortical cells (intercellular Hartig net), while in some genera
(e.g., Laccaria) hyphae penetrate the cortical cells (intracellular
Hartig net). Hartig net morphology is described by the branching pattern
of the hyphae: palmetti (palmate, like fingers), in arbuscular-like
clusters, or as simple unbranched hyphae. The depth of Hartig net
penetration into the cortex (number of cell layers) varies among
species and can be observed in thin sections of fresh or fixed root
tips.

## Rhizomorph Types

Rhizomorphs are differentiated mycelial cords that extend from the ECM
mantle into the surrounding soil, functioning in long-distance nutrient
and water transport. Several rhizomorph types are recognized:

- Undifferentiated rhizomorphs: simple bundles of parallel hyphae
  with little structural differentiation.
- Differentiated rhizomorphs: complex structures with a central
  vessel hypha surrounded by specialized supporting cells. Found in
  genera such as Laccaria, Leccinum, and Suillus.
- Simple mycelial strands: loose aggregations of hyphae without
  true differentiation, common in many ECM fungi.
- Cystidium-covered strands: bearing projecting sterile cells on
  the surface, characteristic of certain genera.

Rhizomorph presence, type, and branching pattern are useful morphological
characters for field identification.

## Extraradical Mycelium

The extraradical mycelium extending into the soil provides additional
diagnostic features. Hyphal characteristics include diameter, wall
thickness, color, branching angle, presence of clamp connections
(characteristic of many basidiomycetes), and formation of specialized
structures such as hyphal strands, anastomoses (hyphal fusions), and
sclerotia. Some ECM fungi produce distinctive hyphal mats on the soil
surface, particularly in coniferous forests, that can cover large areas
and strongly influence soil chemistry.

## Field Identification Techniques

### In Situ Observation
Field morphotyping begins with careful excavation of fine root systems
using forceps and a hand lens (10 to 20x magnification). ECM root tips
are sorted by macroscopic features and photographed in situ with a
scale reference. Fresh color, texture, and branching pattern should be
recorded immediately, as colors may change upon drying. Field guides
and reference collections of known morphotypes from the study area are
invaluable resources.

### Sample Collection and Preservation
Root tips are collected into labeled vials, either placed in FAA
(formalin-acetic acid-alcohol) fixative for long-term storage or kept
moist in plastic bags for immediate processing. For molecular analysis,
tips are placed in CTAB buffer or silica gel. Proper labeling with
host tree species, soil type, depth, and collection date is essential.

## Microscopy Techniques

### Light Microscopy
Detailed morphotyping requires a compound microscope. Fresh or
fixed root tips are sectioned by hand (using razor blades) or with
a microtome after embedding in paraffin or resin. Sections are stained
with reagents such as cotton blue (for fungal cell walls), Melzer's
reagent (for amyloid reactions), or toluidine blue (for general
structure). Squash preparations of mantle tissue reveal cell
arrangement and dimensions.

### Molecular Identification
Molecular methods have revolutionized ECM identification. DNA is
extracted from individual root tips, and the ITS region of ribosomal
DNA is amplified with fungal-specific primers (e.g., ITS1F/ITS4) and
sequenced. Sequences are compared against reference databases (UNITE,
GenBank) for species-level identification.
