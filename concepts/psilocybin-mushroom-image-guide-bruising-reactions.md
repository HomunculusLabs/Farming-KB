# Psilocybin Mushroom Bruising Reactions

Blue bruising is one of the most widely known field indicators of
psilocybin content in mushrooms. The reaction occurs when psilocin
oxidizes upon exposure to air after tissue damage. The Psilocybin
Mushroom Image Guide, while primarily photographic, documents
species known for this characteristic reaction.

## The Chemistry of Blue Bruising

Blue bruising in psilocybin mushrooms is caused by the oxidation
of psilocin (not psilocybin itself). When mushroom tissue is
damaged -- bruised, cut, or crushed -- cellular structures break
and psilocin is exposed to oxygen. An oxidative enzyme converts
psilocin into blue-colored compounds. The intensity of bruising
correlates roughly with psilocin concentration, though this varies
significantly by species, age, and growing conditions.

## Blue Bruising by Species

### Strong Bruising Reactors

Species documented in the guide known for prominent blue bruising:

- [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] (Wavy Caps): among the strongest bruising
  reactors; stems and caps develop intense blue-black discoloration
  from even minor handling. This is well-correlated with its high
  potency
- [[psilocybe-azurescens]]: extremely strong blue bruising reaction;
  considered one of the most potent psilocybin mushrooms, and the
  bruising reflects this. Young specimens on alder wood chips
  develop blueing rapidly
- [[psilocybe-baeocystis]] (potent psilocybe): as the common name
  suggests, strong bruising consistent with high potency

### Moderate Bruising Reactors

- [[psilocybe-cubensis]]: moderate blue bruising, particularly on the
  stem base and where damaged. The guide's photos of fresh and
  dried specimens show typical bruising patterns. Thai "Giant"
  strains from Koh Samui exhibit standard cubensis bruising
- [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] (Liberty Cap): moderate bruising,
  though less immediately obvious than cyanescens. Stems may
  develop blue-green tones at the base

### Variable Bruising Reactors

- [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]] (=cinctulus): bruising can be subtle
  or absent, making spore print identification more critical
  for this species
- [[gymnopilus-spectabilis-entity]]: may show greenish bruising rather than
  the typical blue; this is a different enzymatic reaction and
  should not be confused with psilocin oxidation

### Copelandia Species

- Copelandia bispora, C. cambodgeniensis, C. tropicalis: these
  species blue-bruise prominently. C. cambodgeniensis is noted
  in the guide as the most potent Copelandia species, consistent
  with strong bruising

## Bruising as a Field Test vs. Confirmation

Blue bruising is a useful preliminary indicator but has important
limitations:

### False Positives Are Possible

Some non-psychoactive mushrooms can blue-bruise due to other
compounds. Notably, some Boletus species develop blue coloration
from different chemical reactions entirely.

### False Negatives Are Possible

- Very old or degraded specimens may have lost psilocin content
  and fail to bruise
- Some psilocybin-containing species bruise only weakly or not
  at all
- Improper handling may not produce sufficient tissue damage to
  trigger the reaction

### Environmental Factors

- Bruising intensity varies with growing conditions, temperature,
  humidity, and specimen age
- Dried specimens (extensively photographed in the guide) may show
  residual blue staining from original bruising but cannot
  demonstrate fresh bruising reactions

## Visual Documentation of Bruising

The image guide's approach of photographing specimens at multiple
stages (fresh, handled, dried) implicitly captures bruising
information. Key areas to observe for bruising in field photos:

- Stem base (most common bruising site)
- Cap margins
- Areas where the partial veil has torn
- Gills (may show blue-black discoloration in heavy spore
  deposits mixed with bruising)

## Integrating Bruising with Other Identification Methods

Blue bruising should never be used as the sole identification
criterion. It is most reliable when combined with spore print
color, habitat analysis, and morphological features. The
photographic guide provides visual reference for morphology and
habitat, while spore prints and bruising tests should be
performed in the field on fresh specimens.

## Bruising Intensity vs. Potency Correlation

While the guide does not quantify potency, the documented species
follow a general trend: species known for stronger bruising
reactions tend to be more potent. P. azurescens and P. cyanescens
(both very strong bruisers) are among the most potent psilocybin
mushrooms known. However, this correlation is imperfect and
should not be used to estimate dosage. Environmental conditions,
genetics, and specimen age all influence both potency and
bruising intensity independently.
