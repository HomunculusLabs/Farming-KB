## Amanita verna — Fools' Mushroom

Amanita verna is a lethal species closely allied to *A. phalloides* (the
Death Cap). It is every bit as poisonous as its notorious relative, and
arguably more dangerous: its entirely white colouration makes it all too
easy to mistake for common edible field mushrooms. The Swiss botanist
J. Bauhin aptly named it *Fungus stultorum* — the "fungus of fools."

- **Cap:** Up to 3 inches across, usually pure white though the centre may develop an ochraceous tint with age.
- **Stem:** Up to 5 inches tall, roughly 3/8 inch wide; long and slender, bearing a conspicuous sheathing volva at the base.
- **Gills:** White, free, and persistently white — a key character distinguishing this species from true mushrooms (*Agaricus campestris*), whose gills mature to pink then dark brown.
- **Volva:** Membranous, sheathing the stem base and persisting as a distinct sac-like structure.

Found in woods, particularly under beech (*Fagus*), fruiting in summer and early autumn. Despite the specific epithet *verna* (suggesting a spring appearance), it is only rarely encountered in spring. The persistently white gills and membranous volva at the stem base together distinguish *A. verna* from ordinary edible mushrooms. Any all-white mushroom found in woodland with a volva should be treated with extreme suspicion.

## Amanita virosa — Destroying Angel

*Amanita virosa* earns its common name "Destroying Angel" with good reason: it is one of the most deadly fungi in the world, containing the same amatoxins as *A. phalloides*. It is rare in Britain.

- **Cap:** Up to 5 inches across. Conical at first, often noticeably asymmetrical, and always umbonate when expanded. Pure white.
- **Stem:** Up to 7.5 inches tall, roughly 5/8 inch wide; slender and covered with floccose scales.
- **Ring:** An incomplete silky ring, frequently positioned lower on one side and sometimes remaining attached to the cap edge rather than the stem.
- **Volva:** Lax and wide, forming a loose sac at the base.

Found in damp woods during summer and autumn. The combination of a large pure-white cap that is always umbonate when expanded, an incomplete ring, and a lax wide volva together separate *A. virosa* from *A. verna*, which has a more regular shape and a tighter sheathing volva.

## Amanita mappa — False Death Cap

*Amanita mappa* is a cautionary tale in mycology: long regarded as a deadly species closely related to the Death Cap, it is now known to be harmless. Its offensive flavour renders it worthless as an edible regardless.

- **Cap:** Up to 3 inches across, lemon-yellow or white — never green, and lacking the dark radiating fibrils characteristic of *A. phalloides*. The shiny cap surface retains irregular patches of volva that resemble a map (hence the specific epithet *mappa*); patches may become ochre or brownish with age.
- **Stem:** Up to 3.75 inches tall, roughly 1/2 inch wide.
- **Base:** Truncate and bulbous, with a short thick margin. A distinct groove separates the volva from the stem tissue — an important diagnostic character.
- **Smell and Taste:** Odour of raw potatoes. Taste is initially insipid, becoming disagreeable.

Found in woods, especially beech, during summer and autumn. The absence of green colouration, the map-like volva patches on the cap, and the distinctive grooved separation between volva and stem base all help distinguish *A. mappa* from the true Death Cap.

## Amanita muscaria — Fly Agaric

The Fly Agaric is perhaps the most iconic mushroom in the world — the scarlet-capped, white-spotted toadstool of fairy tales. Despite its sinister appearance, it is far less dangerous than the white *Amanita* species and never causes death in healthy individuals.

- **Cap:** Up to 7 inches across. Initially roundish, becoming flat with a distinctly striate edge. Surface is scarlet to orange-red and viscid, dotted with white or yellowish warts arranged in concentric zones. In damp conditions, the cap may slip through the volva without tearing, resulting in specimens with no spots at all.
- **Gills:** White or yellow-tinted, thick, with a minutely toothed edge.
- **Stem:** Up to 10 inches tall, roughly 1 inch wide. Bulbous base encircled by zones of squamules. Bears a white ring with a yellow edge.
- **Flesh:** White, orange-yellow just beneath the cap skin. Pleasant taste with no appreciable smell.

Found mostly under birches (*Betula*), also under pines (*Pinus*). The name *muscaria* was given by Albertus Magnus in the 13th century, who observed that the mushroom, when broken and steeped in milk, attracts and kills flies. This practice was widespread: in Poland and Czechoslovakia the mushroom was used with sugar as a flytrap; in Romania it was placed on window-sills; and formerly in Britain and Sweden it was employed against household bugs (hence "Bug Agaric").

The Koryak tribes of north-eastern Siberia consumed *A. muscaria* for its capacity to induce states of excessive emotion and euphoria. It has been suggested that Viking berserkers ate it before battle. In homeopathy it appears under the name *Agaricus*. Symptoms appear 1–3 hours after ingestion, progressing through three phases: **delirium and hallucinations**, then **stupor**, then **complete forgetfulness**. Active constituents include **muscarine** (small amounts), **mycetoatropine/muscaridine** (principal psychoactive, atropine-like), and **choline** (responsible for GI symptoms). Among the Koryak, a single specimen could command a price of 3–4 reindeer.

## Amanita pantherina — False Blusher

*Amanita pantherina* is frequently confused with the edible Blusher, *A. rubescens*, but is poisonous and produces symptoms similar to — though more serious than — those of *A. muscaria*.

- **Cap:** Up to 4 inches across. Colour varies from brownish grey to brown or yellowish brown, with a darker centre. Viscid when damp, shiny when dry, with a striate edge. Dotted with small white warts.
- **Stem:** Up to 5 inches tall, roughly 1/2 inch wide, with a membranous border and scaly "bracelets" encircling the base where circular remnants of the volva persist.
- **Gills:** White and crowded.
- **Flesh:** White, with a slight radish-like smell and mild taste.

Found in woods, heaths, and pastures during summer and autumn. Unlike *A. rubescens*, the flesh and gills of *A. pantherina* do not redden or discolour when cut or bruised. The small white warts on the brown cap and the characteristic circular volva remnants at the base are additional distinguishing features.

## Lepiota helveola — Poisonous Lepiota

*Lepiota helveola* is a small but dangerous species, rare in Britain, that produces symptoms of the same type as those caused by *A. phalloides* — albeit generally milder in severity.

- **Cap:** Up to 1.25 inches across. Rounded at first, then flattened. The ochre-brown cuticle becomes pink or lilac with age and tears into scales.
- **Stem:** Up to 1.5 inches tall, roughly 1/4 inch wide.
- **Gills:** White at first, becoming cream-coloured, crowded, with a fimbriate (fringed) edge.
- **Smell and Taste:** Pleasant smell; sourish taste.

Grows in grass during autumn. Rare in Britain.

---

## Lepiota cristata — Crested Lepiota

*Lepiota cristata* is a common small mushroom that, despite long being regarded with suspicion, is not actually poisonous.

- **Cap:** Up to 1.5 inches across.
- **Stem:** Up to 5.5 inches tall, roughly 3/16 inch wide — notably tall and slender relative to the cap.
- **Cap Surface:** Brownish to reddish brown cuticle that breaks into concentric circles of small scales, revealing a white satiny surface beneath.
- **Smell and Taste:** Strong, disagreeable smell; unpleasant taste.

Despite its inclusion in discussions of poisonous fungi, *L. cristata* has not been demonstrated to cause poisoning. Its strong odour alone renders it unattractive as food.
