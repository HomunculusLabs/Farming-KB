# Slug, Wood Louse, and Mite Pest Control for Mushrooms

Beyond the major threats of maggots and fungal diseases, Falconer
identified several other pests that plagued Victorian mushroom growers.
Slugs, wood lice, mites, mice, and rats each required specific
detection and control strategies. His detailed observations on trapping
techniques and the limitations of various remedies provide valuable
insights into integrated pest management in historical mushroom
cultivation.

## Slugs as Mushroom Pests

Slugs are serious pests in the mushroom house, especially in
above-ground structures, and they also occur in annoying numbers in
cellars. Wherever hay or straw is used in covering the beds, or there
is much woodwork about the house, slugs appear to be most numerous.
They are very fond of mushrooms and attack them in all stages, from
the tiny button just emerging from the ground to the fully developed
plant.

In the case of buttons or small mushrooms, slugs usually eat out a
piece on the top or side of the cap, and as the mushroom advances in
growth these wounds spread open and display an ugly scar or
disfigurement. They also bite into the stems. With fresh, full-grown
mushrooms, slugs seem to have a particular liking for the gills and
eat patches out of them here and there.

## The Bullet Hole Mystery

Mr. A. H. Withington of New Jersey first called Falconer's attention
to "bullet" or "shot" holes cut clear through mushroom caps. Prof. C.
V. Riley suggested the damage might be done by some myriapod. Falconer
marked off part of a bed, left it uncared for, and soon found the holes
were the work of slugs, which he watched eating through the caps at
night by lantern light. As he observed, to find plant parasites one
must let them alone and watch; had they kept up their unsparing hunt,
no slug would have survived long enough to reveal the cause.

## Slug Trapping and Control

Slugs must be caught and killed. They can be found at night by hunting
with lamp-light, as their slimy track glistens and reveals their
presence. Several trapping methods are effective:

- Slate or half-rotten boards with a pinch of bran laid here and there
  about the beds. The slugs gather to eat the bran and hide beneath
  the rotten wood, where they can be caught and killed.
- Fresh lettuce leaves make a capital trap, though lettuces in January
  or February are about as scarce as mushrooms themselves.
- A dressing of salt is distasteful to slugs and not injurious to
  mushrooms.
- Strong, fresh lime water may be freely sprinkled over woodwork,
  pathways, walls, or elsewhere where slugs might gather and hide, but
  this solution should not be used upon the mushroom beds.
- Rigid cleanliness about the mushroom house and an ever-alert eye for
  slugs should keep them under control.

## Wood Lice

Wood lice are sure to be more or less abundant in every mushroom house,
even in the cellars. They crawl in through doors, ventilators, or other
interstices, and are brought in with the manure. They find shelter
about the woodwork, manure, or any bits of dry litter that may be
around.

They attack the pinhead and small button mushrooms by biting out little
patches in their tops and sides. Although these patches are small to
begin with, the blemish spreads as the mushroom grows and becomes an
objectionable feature.

## The Potato and Moss Trap for Wood Lice

Falconer described an ingenious trapping method: a half-boiled potato
(no salt) is put into a little pasteboard box, covered with very dry
swamp moss, and laid on its side open at the end on the bed. Wood lice
gather to eat the potato and remain because the dry moss affords a cozy
hiding place. Several boxes can be deployed, lifted quickly in the
morning, and any occupants shaken into a tin pail with water and
kerosene. The potato is changed periodically to keep it appetizing.

Additional control methods include hot water or kerosene emulsion on
woodwork and pathways (never on beds), and six-inch squares of
half-rotten dry boards laid in pairs as hiding traps. Falconer
discouraged poisoned baits in the mushroom house.

## Mites

Two kinds of mites are very common about mushrooms in spring and
summer. One is whitish and smaller than a "red spider" (one of the
commonest insect pests among garden plants), and the other is yellowish
and as large as or larger than a "red spider."

Falconer did not consider either of these mites worth considering as a
mushroom pest. The yellow mite, probably Lyroglyphus infestans, is
extremely common in strawy litter on the surface of hotbeds. He
believed they find their way into the mushroom house as manure vermin
rather than as mushroom parasites, being the effect and not the cause
of injury to the crop.

When mushrooms are wounded or cracked, particularly about the stem, the
crevices often become abundantly inhabited with these mites, but they do
no material damage. This is an important distinction: the presence of
mites indicates pre-existing damage rather than being the cause of it.

## Mice, Rats, and the Toad Question

Both the common house mouse and the white-bellied fence mouse are
mushroom destroyers, biting pieces from young caps that spread into
unsightly scars as the mushroom grows. With open mushrooms, they prefer
the gills, like slugs. Rats are far more destructive. Trapping is the
only remedy Falconer used; he refused poison in mushroom houses for
obvious reasons, preferring to make houses secure against their inroads.

Toads have been recommended as insect control, but Falconer emphatically
rejected them: the mushroom bed is a paradise for toads, which burrow
cozy holes and care nothing about heaving out the finest clumps of
young mushrooms.

## Buhach Powder for Manure Flies

Manure flies (a species of Sciara) appear in large numbers in spring and
summer, hopping about suspiciously. Though essentially harmless to the
crop, they soil mushrooms with their muddy feet. Lachaume recommended
pans of water with turpentine or a floating light to trap them, but
Falconer found pure buhach powder, a natural insecticide derived from
the pyrethrum family, dusted into the air or burned on a hot shovel,
was more effective than either method.
