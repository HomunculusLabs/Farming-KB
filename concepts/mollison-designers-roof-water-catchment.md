# Roof Water Catchment

Roof water catchment is the collection and storage of rainwater falling on
building roofs for domestic, agricultural, and landscape use. In the
Permaculture Designers Manual, Mollison identifies roof catchment as the
primary water supply strategy for settlements, especially in areas without
reliable surface water or groundwater. Roofs provide large, clean collection
surfaces that concentrate rainfall into manageable flows, making them ideal
for directing water into tanks, cisterns, and [[mollison-designers-keyline-land-shaping]] earthworks. A well-designed roof catchment system can supply
all domestic water needs in regions receiving as little as 400 millimeters of
annual rainfall.

## Principles of Roof Catchment Design

The fundamental equation for roof catchment is straightforward: the volume of
water collected equals the roof area multiplied by the rainfall depth, minus
losses from evaporation, first flush diversion, and system inefficiency.
Mollison provides a practical rule that 1 millimeter of rain on 1 square
meter of roof yields approximately 1 liter of water, accounting for minor
losses. This simple calculation allows designers to size both the catchment
area and storage volume to meet demand through the dry season.

The design process involves matching three variables: rainfall pattern
(annual total and seasonal distribution), roof area (collection surface), and
storage capacity (tank volume). If rainfall is evenly distributed, smaller
tanks suffice. If rainfall is highly seasonal with long dry periods, storage
must be sized to bridge the entire dry season. Mollison recommends designing
for the worst historical drought year on record to ensure system reliability.

## Collection Surfaces

### Roof Materials

The choice of roofing material significantly affects water quality and yield.
Metal roofs (galvanized iron, zincalume, or colorbond steel) are the most
efficient catchment surfaces, shedding water rapidly with minimal absorption.
Tiled roofs (concrete or clay) also work well but have higher initial
absorption losses. Thatched, tar-covered, or asphalt shingle roofs are less
suitable for drinking water collection due to leaching of organic compounds
and reduced runoff efficiency. Mollison recommends metal roofing for all
new construction intended for water catchment, both for efficiency and water
quality.

### Roof Area Sizing

To calculate the required roof area, divide annual water demand by annual
rainfall. For a household consuming 150 liters per day (approximately 55,000
liters per year) in a region receiving 800 millimeters of rainfall, the
minimum roof area is approximately 69 square meters. However, seasonal
distribution requires additional consideration. If 80 percent of rain falls
in 4 months and the dry season is 8 months, storage must hold approximately
36,500 liters, requiring tanks of at least this capacity to bridge the dry
period. Mollison advises adding a 20 to 30 percent safety margin to all
calculations.

## Storage Systems

### Tank Types and Materials

Water storage options range from small polyethylene tanks (500 to 5,000
liters) to large ferro-cement cisterns (20,000 to 100,000 liters) and
earthen dams. For domestic supply, Mollison favors ferro-cement tanks for
their durability, low cost, and suitability for owner construction. Concrete
tanks and commercial poly tanks are also common. The tank should be sited
below the roof overflow level to allow gravity filling, and above the point
of use to allow gravity-fed distribution, eliminating the need for pumps.

### Storage Volume Calculations

Storage sizing depends on the maximum dry period the system must bridge.
Mollison provides the formula: storage volume equals daily demand multiplied
by the number of days in the longest expected dry period, plus a safety
margin. For the household example above (150 liters per day) with a 120-day
dry season, minimum storage is 18,000 liters. Adding a 30 percent safety
margin gives a target of 23,400 liters. Where multiple tanks are used, they
should be connected in series so that water flows through all tanks before
overflowing, ensuring maximum utilization of storage capacity.

### Siting and Placement

Tanks should be placed on stable, level ground close to the collection roof
to minimize pipe runs and costs. In frost-prone areas, tanks should be
partially buried or insulated to prevent freezing. Shading the tank reduces
algal growth and evaporation. The tank overflow should be directed to useful
purposes such as garden irrigation, [[mollison-designers-sewage-aquaculture-systems]] ponds, or infiltration swales rather than wasted to storm drains.

## Filtration and Water Quality

### First Flush Diverters

The first several millimeters of each rainfall event carry the majority of
dust, debris, bird droppings, and pollutants accumulated on the roof surface
since the last rain. A first flush diverter is a simple device that captures
and discards this initial contaminated runoff, directing only cleaner water
to the storage tank. Mollison considers first flush diversion essential for
any system supplying drinking water. The diverter volume should be sized at
0.5 to 1 liter per square meter of roof area.

### Filtration Systems

After first flush diversion, additional filtration may include leaf screens
at gutter inlets, coarse gravel filters, sand filters, or commercial cartridge
filters depending on the intended water use. For drinking water, Mollison
recommends a combination of coarse pre-filtration followed by fine filtration
or treatment with UV light, boiling, or chemical treatment as a final step.
Water for irrigation and livestock requires less treatment, with leaf screens
and first flush diversion typically sufficient.

### Gutter and Downpipe Design

Gutters must be sized to handle peak rainfall intensity without overflow.
Mollison recommends oversized gutters and downpipes for reliability during
heavy storms. Regular maintenance of gutters is essential to prevent blockages
from leaves and debris. Leaf guards and screens reduce maintenance frequency.
All piping should be UV-stabilized and protected from physical damage.

## Integration with Site Design

Roof catchment integrates with many other permaculture elements. Overflow
from full tanks directs to [[mollison-designers-keyline-land-shaping]] swales
for landscape hydration. Stored water supplies [[mollison-designers-house-garden-integration]] through gravity-fed drip irrigation. In arid climates,
roof catchment can supplement [[mollison-designers-inclined-solar-desalination]]
during rare rainfall events. The system design should consider the entire
water budget of the site, connecting roof supply to landscape demand and
wastewater reuse in a closed-loop pattern.

## Summary

Roof water catchment provides a clean, reliable, and locally controlled water
supply by collecting rainfall from building roofs. Proper design requires
matching roof area, rainfall pattern, and storage volume to household or farm
demand. First flush diversion, filtration, and appropriate tank selection
ensure water quality. When integrated with broader site [[water-management]],
roof catchment becomes the foundation of a resilient and self-sufficient water
supply system in [[permaculture-design]].
