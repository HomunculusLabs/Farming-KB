# Component Analysis: The Chicken as Design Model

The chicken is the primary example Mollison uses to teach
component analysis in permaculture design. By systematically
listing the needs (inputs), products (outputs), behaviours,
and intrinsic characteristics of a single well-known
component, designers learn to connect elements so that the
outputs of one become the inputs of another.

## The Purpose of Component Analysis

We must be able to connect our component to other components.
The essentials are:

- That the inputs needed by the chicken are supplied by
  other components in the system

- That the outputs of the chicken are used by other
  components (including people)

The goal is a self-regulated system achieved by relative
placement: putting components where they can serve each other.

## Defining Outputs and Inputs

Mollison establishes two broad categories for any component:

**Outputs, Yields, or Products** are RESOURCES if they are
used productively, or can become POLLUTANTS if not used in a
constructive way by some other part of the system.

**Inputs, Needs, or Demands** have to be supplied, and if not
supplied by other parts of the system, then EXTERNAL ENERGY or
EXTRA WORK must be found to satisfy these demands.

This leads to two foundational definitions:

- A POLLUTANT is an output of any system component that is
  not being used productively by any other component of the
  system.

- EXTRA WORK is the result of an input not automatically
  provided by another component of the system.

As pollution and extra work are both unnecessary results of an
incompletely designed or unnatural system, the designer must
eliminate both by connecting components.

## Primary Needs of the Chicken

Primary needs are food, warmth, shelter, water, grit, calcium,
dust baths, and other chickens.

Secondary needs are for a tolerable social and physical
environment, giving a healthy life of moderate stress.

## Primary Products of the Chicken

Primary products include eggs, feathers, feather dust, manure,
various exhaled or excreted gases, sound, and heat.

## Derived Products

From eggs, a variety of foods can be made, and albumen can
be derived. From feathers, dusters, insulation, bedding,
rope, and special manures can be produced. Manure is used
directly in the garden or combined with leaf and stem materials
(carbon) to supply compost heat. Composted anaerobically, it
supplies methane for a house.

Heat and gases both have a use in enclosed glasshouses. The
list of derived products is limited only by lack of specific
information and by local needs for the products.

## Behaviours of the Chicken

Chickens walk, fly, perch, scratch, preen, mate, hatch eggs,
care for young, form flocks of 20-30 individuals, and forage.
They also process food to form primary products and to
maintain growth and body weight.

These behaviours are critical design data: scratching can
prepare garden beds; foraging can control pests; flocking
determines shed design; perching determines roost design.

## Breed Characteristics

Different breeds have different characteristics. Whether a
Rhode Island Red, Leghorn, Hamburg, or other breed, each has
specific traits that affect its suitability for different
roles in the system: egg production, meat, brooding, foraging
ability, climate tolerance, and size.

## The Design Implication

Placed correctly, a tree or chicken experiences no stress not
common to all trees or chickens about their basic needs. If
all essential connections are made, the tree or chicken has
less external work required of it and less effect (which may
be harmful or inherent) upon that element.

The chicken may be busy, but its needs are provided for and
its products are used. If the ultimate products (eggs, meat,
manure) are satisfactory, the chicken is acting as a
self-regulating component of the system.

Mollison advises: we must simply trust the chicken to make
connections itself, about which we have no specific
information. We supply what we believe to be essentials, and
let the chicken attend to all the connections we are unaware
of.

## Application to Other Components

The same analytical method applies to any component: a tree,
a house, a pond, a windbreak, or a person. By listing needs,
products, behaviours, and breed characteristics, the designer
can find connections that eliminate pollutants and extra work
from the system.

This methodology is the foundation of permaculture design
and the basis for [[mollison-guild-analysis-and-species-interaction]].
