## Core Design Principles

Desert house design integrates several foundational elements:

- **Massive walls** for thermal inertia — absorb heat during the day and release it
  slowly at night
- **Edge-insulated floors** to prevent ground heat transfer into living spaces
- **Draught-proofing** and **insulated ceilings** to minimise unwanted heat gain
- **Cross-ventilation** as an essential, non-negotiable design feature
- **White-painted exterior walls** to reflect solar radiation
- **Shade trees, palms, and vine trellises** surrounding the structure
- **Courtyard ponds and fountains** providing evaporative microclimate cooling

## Six Sources of Cool Air

Mollison identifies six principal methods for providing cool air to a desert house.
These can be combined for greater effect.

### 1. Internal Courtyards

Courtyards serve as thermal buffer zones and cool-air reservoirs. Effective courtyard
design features:

- Latticed or shaded overhead covering to block direct sun
- Height of two or more storeys so that walls naturally shade the courtyard floor
- Vegetation and water features to increase evaporative cooling

### 2. Fully Enclosed Vine Arbors

Enclosed vine arbors act as living air-conditioning systems:

- Mulched floors retain soil moisture
- Trickle irrigation keeps root zones consistently damp
- Arbors should occupy approximately 30% of the total house floor area
- Hanging ferns and house plants inside the arbor significantly aid cooling
- An integrated water tank provides thermal mass and irrigation supply

### 3. Earth Tunnels

Earth tunnels use the stable underground temperature to pre-cool incoming air:

- Minimum dimensions: **1 metre deep**, **20 metres long**
- Tunnels slope downhill with the **air intake positioned up-slope**
- Construction uses pipes, half-round culverts, or open ditches
- Cool humid air, being denser, naturally falls through tunnels into house rooms
- In swampy areas, tunnels should be **mounded above surface soil** to avoid
  waterlogging

### 4. Down-Draughts (Wind-Scoops)

Roof-mounted structures capture and channel prevailing winds downward:

- Sails, slats, or wind-scoop forms mounted on the roof
- Outlets fitted with damp hessian, coke evaporation beds, or unglazed clay pots
  to add evaporative cooling as air enters the building

### 5. Induced Cross-Ventilation via Solar Chimneys

A black-painted sheet-metal solar chimney mounted on ceilings or roof ridges creates
a powerful convective draw:

- Solar heating of the chimney causes hot air to rise and exhaust at the top
- This draws cool air from courtyards, arbors, and tunnels through the house
- The resulting cross-draught can be strong enough to blow out a candle

### 6. Earth Tunnels with Evaporative Cooling

Combining earth tunnels with evaporative cooling materials maximises cooling:

- **Unglazed clay pots** placed inside the tunnel absorb water and cool passing air
- **Pans of wet coke** (carbon aggregate) provide large evaporative surface area
- **Coarse fibreglass weave curtains** dampened with water act as evaporative wicks
- **Damp bark beds** line tunnel floors, releasing moisture into the airflow

## Integrated Building Systems

### The Shadehouse–Glasshouse Pairing

A fully enclosed vined shadehouse on the **shade side** of the house combined with a
sunward-facing **attached glasshouse** creates a continuous cool-humid cross-flow:

- Shadehouse: serves as the summer living area, cooling air drawn through vines
- Glasshouse: provides winter heating and vents hot air for cross-ventilation on
  hot days
- Together they generate air cooled to **10–15°C below ambient** through shade,
  vine transpiration, and induced airflow

### The Shadehouse as Dual-Purpose Space

- Functions as the primary summer living area
- The winter kitchen opens directly onto the shadehouse, which then serves as the
  summer kitchen
- Desert houses must have **integral trellis and garden systems** connected to the
  structure

### The Attached Glasshouse

The glasshouse serves multiple roles throughout the year:

- **Winter:** heats the house during cold periods
- **Summer:** vents hot air upward, drawing cool air through the house
- **Spring/Autumn:** extends the growing season for early and late crops
- **Year-round:** produces winter greens and provides a space for drying surplus
  produce

## Passive Heating Strategies

Desert houses also require heating for cold nights and winter months:

- **Solar gain windows** positioned to allow sunlight onto edge-insulated concrete
  slabs, which store and slowly release heat
- **Rigid foam insulation** placed in trenches 0.5–1 m deep at foundations to
  prevent heat loss to the ground
- **Trombe wall effect:** a thick mud-brick wall behind a glass window absorbs solar
  energy during the day and radiates heat into the interior after dark

## Performance and Economics

These integrated design strategies deliver exceptional results:

- Air temperatures reduced by **10–15°C below ambient** through passive means alone
- Cross-ventilation flows powerful enough to **blow out a candle**
- Fuel energy savings of **up to 80%** compared to conventional desert housing
- Installation and modification costs **amortize in 1–3 years** through energy savings
