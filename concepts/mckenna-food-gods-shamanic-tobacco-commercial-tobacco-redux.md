# Shamanic Tobacco vs. Commercial Tobacco: The Forgotten Visionary Plant

## Summary

McKenna draws a sharp distinction between shamanic tobacco—*Nicotiana rustica*, a
chemically complex, potentially hallucinogenic plant central to Maya shamanism—and
the debased commercial *Nicotiana tabacum* that conquered Europe. He documents how
shamanic tobacco contained beta-carboline MAO inhibitors that synergized with
nicotine to produce trancelike visionary states, how the Maya employed enema
rituals to administer tobacco and other psychoactives, and how European adoption
stripped tobacco of its sacred context while introducing the revolutionary
technology of smoking—a drug delivery method that would reshape world history and
pave the way for opium, hashish, cocaine, and DMT smoking.

## The Smoking Revolution: From Maya Cigars to Global Pandemic

Four years after Columbus's first voyage, the hermit Romano Pane, whom Columbus
had left in Haiti, described in his journal the native habit of inhaling tobacco
fumes through a bird-bone instrument inserted into the nose and held over tobacco
strewn on a bed of coals. McKenna calls the consequences of this simple
ethnographic observation incalculable. It introduced into Europe an extremely
efficient method for conveying drugs into the human body—one that made possible
the worldwide pandemic of tobacco smoking, provided a fast-acting route for
administering opium and hashish, and became the distant ancestor of the crack
cocaine and PCP smoker. "It also, it must be said, makes possible the most
profound of the indole-hallucinogen-induced ecstasies, the rarely encountered but
incomparable practice of smoking dimethyltryptamine."

This technological innovation—the inhalation of combusted plant material—was
fundamentally a New World contribution to global drug culture. While tobacco
smoking was widespread in North America at European contact, and hallucinogenic
DMT-containing snuffs were prevalent in the Caribbean, there are no confirmed
reports of materials other than tobacco being smoked in the pre-Columbian Americas.
The act of smoking itself, as distinct from eating, drinking, or snuffing
psychoactive substances, was an indigenous American invention with consequences
that continue to reverberate.

## Nicotiana Rustica: The Visionary Tobacco of the Maya

The high culture of the Maya that flourished until the mid-800s had an old and
complex relationship with tobacco. The tobacco of the Classical Maya was
*Nicotiana rustica*, still used among aboriginal populations in South America.
This species is "much more potent, chemically complex, and potentially
hallucinogenic than the commercial grades of *Nicotiana tabacum* available today."
The difference between this tobacco and cigarette tobacco is, in McKenna's
assessment, profound.

Wild tobacco was cured and rolled into cigars which were smoked. The trancelike
state that followed was central to Maya shamanism, partially synergized by
compounds that included MAO inhibitors. Recently introduced antidepressants of the
MAO inhibitor type are, McKenna notes, "distant synthetic relatives of these
natural compounds." The visionary quality of rustica tobacco thus has a
pharmacological basis that commercial tobacco entirely lacks.

## Beta-Carbolines and the Chemistry of Shamanic Tobacco

Francis Robicsek's research on Mayan tobacco's chemical complexity revealed that
nicotine is by no means the only bioactive substance in the tobacco leaf.
Alkaloids of the harmala group—harman and norharman—have been isolated from cured
commercial tobaccos and their smoke. They constitute a chemical group of
beta-carbolines including harmine, harmaline, tetrahydroharmine, and
6-methoxyharmine, all with hallucinogenic properties.

While no native tobacco varieties had been analyzed for these substances at the
time of McKenna's writing, it was "a reasonable supposition that their
composition may vary widely, depending upon the variety and growth, and that some
of the native-grown tobaccos may contain a relatively high concentration of them."
This is significant because beta-carboline MAO inhibitors are the same class of
compounds found in *Banisteriopsis caapi*, the ayahuasca vine. Their presence in
shamanic tobacco would explain the visionary, trancelike states reported by
indigenous users—states impossible to achieve with modern commercial tobacco,
which has been selectively bred for nicotine content while the accompanying
alkaloids have been diluted or eliminated.

## Tobacco as Shamanic Adjunct: The Universal Companion Plant

Tobacco was and is "the ever-present adjunct of the more powerful and visionary
hallucinogenic plants wherever in the Americas they are used in a traditional and
shamanic way." This observation places tobacco not as a primary visionary plant
but as a crucial companion plant—one that modulates, intensifies, and structures
the experience of more powerful hallucinogens. In Amazonian shamanism, tobacco
smoke is blown over patients to cleanse and protect them; mapacho (rustica)
cigars are smoked continuously during ayahuasca ceremonies; tobacco juice is
used as an emetic in purgative rituals. This pattern of tobacco as the constant
shamanic companion spans the entire hemisphere.

## Maya Enema Rituals: The First Drug Enemas

One of the traditional uses of tobacco involved the New World's invention of
enemas. Peter Furst's research revealed that the ancient Maya, like the ancient
Peruvians, employed enemas. Enema syringes and enema rituals were discovered in
Maya art—an outstanding example being a large painted vase dating A.D. 600-800
depicting a man carrying an enema syringe, applying it to himself, and having a
woman apply it to him.

If the enemas were, like those of the Peruvian Indians, intoxicating or
hallucinogenic, they might have consisted of fermented balche (honey mead), a
very sacred beverage possibly fortified with tobacco or morning-glory seed
infusions. Datura infusions and even hallucinogenic mushrooms may have been
administered in this way. Tobacco infusion alone could also have been used. This
method of administration bypasses the liver's first-pass metabolism, delivering
psychoactive compounds directly to the bloodstream—a pharmacological insight the
Maya apparently discovered independently thousands of years ago.

## Tobacco as Quack Medicine: The European Debasement

Any drug introduced into use inevitably winds up associated with quack medical
theories. Cocaine abuse was preceded by Vin de Mariani; heroin was touted as a
cure for morphine addiction. In 1661, the Danish physician Thomas Bartholin
recommended tobacco-juice enemas and tobacco-smoke enemas: "Smoke from two pipes
is blown into the intestines. A suitable instrument for this was devised by the
ingenious English." Not to be outdone, an eighteenth-century French physician
named Buc'hoz advocated "intra-vaginal insufflation of tobacco smoke to cure
hysteria."

Every drug, during introduction into a new culture, is hailed as a "love drug"—
the most effective advertising ploy. Tobacco was no different. Sailors told of
Nicaraguan women who smoked and "displayed an ardor undreamed of." This rumor
clinched tobacco's popularity among European women and helped the ex-Franciscan
monk Andre Thevet introduce tobacco to the French court in 1579. What had been a
sacred shamanic plant, chemically complex and visionary, was reduced through
commercial breeding and mass marketing to a delivery vehicle for nicotine
addiction—its companion alkaloids stripped away, its ritual context destroyed,
its enema rituals replaced by the cigarette.
