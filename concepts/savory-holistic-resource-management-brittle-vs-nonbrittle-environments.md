# Brittle vs Nonbrittle Environments

The distinction between brittle and nonbrittle environments is one of Allan
Savory's most important contributions to ecological understanding. This
classification, which departs from traditional rainfall-based or vegetative
classifications, fundamentally changes how land managers should approach
restoration, grazing, and [[desertification]] prevention.

## The Core Discovery

Savory observed that environments with similar rainfall could respond
completely differently to the same management practices. Areas in northern
Europe receiving only fifteen to twenty inches of rain per year were not
desertifying despite centuries of overgrazing, while areas in Africa receiving
forty to fifty inches annually had desertified rapidly under the same
practices. This led to the recognition that the standard classifications of
environments by rainfall or vegetation type were inadequate for understanding
why some environments desertification|desertify and others do not.

## The Brittleness Scale

Environments are classified on a continuum from one to ten:
- **1 = Nonbrittle extreme** (tropical forests): Rapid biological decay,
  close plant spacing, fast successional advance
- **10 = Brittle extreme** (true deserts): Slow oxidation-based decay, wide
  plant spacing, slow successional advance

No clear break exists between extremes. Grasslands, for instance, may lie
anywhere from one to nine or ten on the scale. A thirty-to-fifty-inch
rainfall area with very dry periods in the growing season and a long dry
season may be extremely brittle, while areas with less total rainfall but
reliable year-round humidity may be quite nonbrittle.

## What Determines Brittleness

The key factor is not total precipitation but the distribution of
precipitation and atmospheric humidity throughout the year. The features
that distinguish the two environment types derive from humidity patterns
rather than raw rainfall totals:

**Brittle environments** characteristically experience erratic distribution
of moisture during the year. They commonly have a long period of nongrowth,
which can be very arid. The seasonal pattern of humidity determines how
quickly successional communities establish on bare surfaces, and whether
decay of old plant material will be biological and rapid (occurring near
ground level, as in nonbrittle environments) or chemical (oxidation) and
slow (starting from upper parts first, as in brittle environments).

**Nonbrittle environments** characteristically experience reliable moisture
in the growing season. Even though total precipitation may seldom top
twenty inches, atmospheric humidity does not drop severely during the
remaining months.

## The Critical Difference: Response to Rest

The most important practical implication of the brittle/nonbrittle
distinction concerns the tool of [[mollison-designers-forest-condensation-and-micro-climate-rainfall]]. In nonbrittle environments,
prolonged rest allows the land to recover from disturbance. Biological
decay proceeds rapidly, plant material falls and breaks down near the
ground surface, new communities establish quickly, and soil cover is
maintained.

In brittle environments, however, prolonged nondisturbance leads to further
deterioration and instability. Old plant material accumulates because
breakdown occurs mainly through slow oxidation and weathering rather than
biological decay. Perennial grasses die as accumulated old growth blocks
sunlight from reaching growing points. Soil surfaces become capped and
exposed. This is why the old belief that all damaged land should be left
undisturbed has proven wrong for over half the earth's land surface.

## Brittleness Is Not Fragility

An important distinction: brittleness is not the same as fragility. A
fragile area may be nonbrittle (e.g., a delicate fern-dominated glade in
a forest), and a brittle area may be nonfragile (e.g., African savannahs
and American prairies). Brittleness specifically refers to the decay and
successional processes present and how the environment responds to certain
management forces.

## Implications for Land Management

The brittle/nonbrittle distinction has profound management implications:

- **Over half of the earth's land surface** leans toward the brittle end
  of the scale, meaning that practices developed in the nonbrittle
  environments of northern Europe and eastern North America are often
  inappropriate for most of the world's land.
- In brittle environments, some form of periodic soil surface disturbance
  is essential for maintaining healthy [[symbiotic-fungi-fungal-endophytes-grassland-ecology]] and productive
  watersheds.
- The role of [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] becomes critical in brittle
  environments, as herding animals and their predators historically provided
  the necessary disturbance.
- [[savory-fire-as-management-tool]] has very different effects in brittle versus
  nonbrittle environments, with fire generally being more damaging in
  brittle areas due to soil exposure.
- The [[savory-water-cycle-management]] and [[savory-mineral-cycle-management]] function very differently in the
  two environment types, with both being more vulnerable to disruption in
  brittle areas.

## Historical Context

The Tuli Circle in Zimbabwe provided a key early observation: after
thousands of animals starved during a population crash, the area continued
to deteriorate despite dramatically reduced animal numbers. This was
puzzling until the brittle/nonbrittle distinction explained that the area
required soil surface disturbance (which the formerly large herds had
provided) for new plants to establish. Without it, communities declined
and soil became unstable even without any animals present.

## Determining Brittleness in the Field

The easiest way to determine where an environment falls on the scale is
to look at various sites and assess the decay process on old vegetation.
At the nonbrittle extreme, decay is 100 percent biological, rapid, and
occurs near ground level. At the brittle extreme, decay is primarily
through oxidation and weathering, slow, and begins from the upper parts
of plants first. This assessment, combined with understanding of humidity
patterns and plant spacing, provides a practical field method for
determining brittleness.
