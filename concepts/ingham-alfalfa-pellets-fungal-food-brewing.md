     8|# Alfalfa Pellets as Fungal Food in AACT Brewing
     9|
    10|## Summary
    11|
    12|Alfalfa pellets and flaked alfalfa meal serve as versatile food resources
    13|in actively aerated compost tea (AACT) brewing, supporting both bacterial
    14|and fungal growth. While not the strongest fungal food available, alfalfa
    15|provides nitrogen, growth regulators such as triacontanol, and complex
    16|carbohydrates that certain fungi can metabolize. When combined with
    17|molasses and other fungal foods, alfalfa contributes to a balanced
    18|microbial community in the brew. (see [[compost-tea-ingredients-and-recipes]]).
    19|
    20|## Nutrient Profile and Characteristics
    21|
    22|Alfalfa is characterized as a high-nitrogen food resource in the AACT
    23|ingredient spectrum. Ingham specifically identifies "flaked alfalfa meal"
    24|as a material where you should "add if your plants need the N." Alfalfa
    25|contains approximately 16-17% protein and has a relatively moderate
    26|carbon-to-nitrogen ratio compared to purely fungal foods like wood chips
    27|or humic acids. (see [[ingham-molasses-types-brewing-effects]]).
    28|
    29|The C:N ratio of alfalfa pellets is approximately 12:1 to 20:1 depending
    30|on the source and processing method. This places it in a range that
    31|supports robust bacterial activity while also providing substrates that
    32|select for certain fungal species. This moderate C:N ratio makes it a
    33|useful intermediate food resource when you want both bacterial and fungal
    34|growth in a brew, unlike the strong fungal selectors which have much
    35|wider C:N ratios. (see [[compost-tea-brewing-methods]]).
    36|
    37|The key distinction Ingham makes is that alfalfa hay (not straw) is the
    38|correct material. Hay retains the leafy, nutrient-rich portions of the
    39|plant while straw is primarily structural cellulose with far less
    40|nutritional value. The nutrients are in the hay, as she emphasizes: "Air,
    41|no nutrients. The nutrients are in the hay. Remember, hay, not straw."
    42|
    43|## Role as Fungal Food in Brewing
    44|
    45|Ingham confirms that alfalfa pellets combined with molasses produce "very
    46|normal" bacterial activity along with "some fungal" activity. She
    47|describes this combination as "yummy food for certain species of
    48|bacteria, and some fungi as well!" However, she provides an important
    49|caveat about the relative fungal benefit: in some work her team
    50|conducted, cottonseed meal helped fungi more than alfalfa hay and meal. (see [[ingham-carbon-nitrogen-ratios-compost-tea]]).
    51|
    52|In different conditions, the relative selection pressure may differ. (see [[soil-foodweb-succession-bacteria-fungi-cycle-ingham]]).
    53|This means alfalfa functions as a moderate fungal food rather than a
    54|strong one. It will not overwhelmingly select for fungi the way humic
    55|acids, fish hydrolysates, or materials with wide C:N ratios such as
    56|wood chips, shredded paper, and dried leaves will.
    57|
    58|The strongest fungal foods are those with the widest C:N ratios and the
    59|most recalcitrant carbon, which bacteria cannot easily metabolize but
    60|fungi can break down enzymatically. Alfalfa's position as a moderate
    61|fungal food means it is best used when you want a balanced brew that
    62|supports both bacterial and fungal communities.
    63|
    64|If your goal is to maximize fungal biomass for a perennial crop or a
    65|disease-susceptible plant, you would want to supplement alfalfa with
    66|stronger fungal foods like humic acids or fish hydrolysate. Ingham notes
    67|that protein meals including corn, oat, soy, and barley "work well" as
    68|fungal foods in the brew, and that these can be combined with alfalfa
    69|for complementary effects.
    70|
    71|## Triacontanol and Plant Growth Regulators
    72|
    73|Alfalfa contains naturally occurring growth regulators, most notably
    74|triacontanol, a 30-carbon long-chain alcohol that functions as a natural
    75|plant growth stimulant. Triacontanol has been documented in agricultural
    76|research to enhance photosynthesis, increase nutrient uptake, and improve
    77|overall plant vigor when applied in trace amounts.
    78|
    79|When alfalfa pellets are included in AACT brewing, these growth-
    80|regulating compounds can become available in the finished tea. This
    81|provides a secondary benefit beyond the purely microbial effects, as the
    82|growth regulators may directly stimulate plant physiological processes
    83|when the tea is applied as a foliar spray or soil drench.
    84|
    85|The presence of triacontanol is one distinguishing feature of alfalfa
    86|compared to other protein meals such as blood meal and feather meal.
    87|These alternative protein meals provide nitrogen for microbial growth but
    88|lack the secondary metabolite profile found in alfalfa. Cottonseed meal,
    89|while sometimes more effective for fungal selection, also does not
    90|contain the same growth-regulating compounds.
    91|
    92|This makes alfalfa particularly attractive when the tea will be used as
    93|a foliar application where the growth regulators can directly contact
    94|plant surfaces and be absorbed. The growth-regulating effects complement
    95|the microbial benefits of the tea, providing a dual mechanism of action.
    96|
    97|## Interaction with Molasses in the Brew
    98|
    99|Alfalfa pellets are frequently used in combination with molasses in AACT
   100|recipes. Ingham confirms this is a standard and effective approach,
   101|noting that the combination produces normal and healthy microbial
   102|activity in the brew.
   103|
   104|Molasses serves primarily as a bacterial food at low concentrations,
   105|but Ingham's research revealed an important nuance: "molasses at low
   106|amounts helps bacteria, but molasses at 5% helps the fungi significantly
   107|more than bacteria." However, she emphasizes that "you still get
   108|enormous bacterial biomass when using those high levels of molasses. You
   109|just start giving the fungi a real edge."
   110|
   111|When combining alfalfa with molasses, practitioners must be mindful of
   112|oxygen consumption. Both the nitrogen in alfalfa and the sugars in
   113|molasses stimulate rapid microbial growth, which creates high oxygen
   114|demand. In well-aerated tea machines this is manageable, but in poorly
   115|designed machines the combination can drive dissolved oxygen below the
   116|critical 5.5 ppm threshold.
   117|
   118|Ingham advises that the molasses amount "relates more to the machine --
   119|how good is the machine at keeping things aerated?" In the best-designed
   120|machines, no molasses may be needed at all. In others, careful
   121|titration is required to avoid anaerobic conditions.
   122|
   123|## Complementary Fungal Foods
   124|
   125|Other fungal foods that pair well with alfalfa in AACT brewing include
   126|the following materials, as documented by Ingham:
   127|
   128|- **Humic acids**: 1-4 ounces (50-200 ml) per 50 gallons; among the
   129|  strongest fungal food selections available for brewing
   130|- **Fish hydrolysate**: 2-10 ounces (100-500 ml) per 50 gallons;
   131|  provides both fungal food and plant-available nutrients
   132|- **Kelp**: provides surface area for fungal hyphae and micronutrients
   133|  for plant health; an ounce in 5 gallons is sufficient for surface
   134|  area needs
   135|- **Protein meals**: corn meal, oat meal, soy meal, barley meal, and
   136|  feather meal all function as fungal foods in the brew
   137|- **Woody materials**: wood chips, shredded paper, shredded cardboard,
   138|  dried leaves -- anything with a wide C:N ratio
   139|
   140|The key principle from Ingham is to "add the right food to feed the
   141|function you want." You must also "make sure you have the microbes that
   142|will use that food and perform the function you want." Match the food
   143|resources to the needs of the target plant and the organisms required.
