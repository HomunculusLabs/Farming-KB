# Permaculture Sheet Mulch Detailed Guide

Sheet mulching (also called lasagna gardening or sheet composting) is the primary soil-building technique in permaculture. Toby Hemenway provides detailed instructions in Gaia's Garden, calling it "the backbone technique for converting lawn and waste land into fertile garden." It builds rich soil without tilling, suppresses weeds, conserves moisture, and sequesters carbon.

## What Is Sheet Mulching?

Sheet mulching is a layered composting system applied directly on top of the ground. Alternating layers of organic materials are stacked like a lasagna, with each layer performing a specific function. Over several months, the materials break down into rich, dark, loamy soil teeming with earthworms and microorganisms.

## Benefits Over Tilling

- **Preserves soil structure**: Does not destroy the existing soil food web
- **Suppresses weeds**: Smothering existing vegetation without herbicides
- **Builds soil**: Adds organic matter and feeds soil life
- **Conserves moisture**: Mulch layer reduces evaporation dramatically
- **Sequesters carbon**: Organic matter is converted to stable humus
- **Requires no machinery**: Can be done entirely by hand
- **Improves over time**: Soil gets better each year with additional mulching

## Materials Needed

### Base Layer (Weed Barrier)
- Cardboard (remove all tape, staples, and glossy labels)
- Unprinted newspaper (5-10 sheets thick)
- Avoid using cardboard with heavy wax coatings

### Nitrogen Layer (Green)
- Fresh grass clippings
- Kitchen scraps (no meat, dairy, or oils)
- Fresh manure (cow, horse, chicken -- aged at least 2 weeks)
- [[comfrey-plant-guide|Comfrey]] leaves
- Green garden waste
- Coffee grounds
- Seaweed

### Carbon Layer (Brown)
- Straw (not hay, which contains seeds)
- Fallen leaves
- Wood chips (for pathways; slower to break down)
- Shredded paper
- Aged sawdust
- Dried plant material

### Top Layer (Finished)
- Finished [[compost-building-guide|compost]]
- Well-rotted manure
- Rich topsoil (if available)
- Worm castings

## Step-by-Step Construction

### Step 1: Preparation

1. **Mow or cut** the existing vegetation as short as possible
2. **Water the area** thoroughly -- the soil and existing vegetation should be moist
3. **Mark the shape** of your bed with a hose or string

### Step 2: Lay the Weed Barrier

1. Overlap cardboard or newspaper pieces by at least 15 cm (6 inches) to prevent weeds from pushing through gaps
2. Extend the barrier 30 cm (12 inches) beyond the planned bed edge
3. Wet the cardboard thoroughly after laying it down -- it should be sodden
4. The barrier blocks light and smothers existing vegetation

### Step 3: Apply Nitrogen Layer

1. Spread a 5-10 cm (2-4 inch) layer of green/nitrogen-rich material
2. This feeds the decomposer organisms (bacteria, fungi, worms)
3. Fresh manure at this stage accelerates decomposition
4. Water lightly

### Step 4: Apply Carbon Layer

1. Add a 10-15 cm (4-6 inch) layer of brown/carbon-rich material
2. Straw, leaves, or shredded paper work well
3. This layer provides food for fungi and creates air spaces
4. Water lightly

### Step 5: Repeat Layers

1. Continue alternating thin nitrogen and thicker carbon layers
2. Aim for a total finished height of 15-30 cm (6-12 inches) above the original ground level
3. The pile will shrink significantly as materials decompose

### Step 6: Top With Finished Compost

1. Finish with a 5-10 cm (2-4 inch) layer of finished compost, composted manure, or topsoil
2. This provides an immediate planting medium while lower layers decompose
3. The finished layer is where you will initially plant

### Step 7: Mulch the Surface

1. Cover with a final 5-8 cm (2-3 inch) layer of straw, leaves, or wood chips
2. This protects the soil, conserves moisture, and prevents weed seeds from germinating
3. Pull back the mulch to create planting holes

## Timing

- **Best started in autumn**: Winter decomposition prepares beds for spring planting
- **Spring application**: Can plant directly into the top compost layer; lower layers decompose through the season
- **Minimum wait before planting**: 2-4 weeks if using finished compost on top; longer if using raw materials
- **Full decomposition**: 3-12 months for complete breakdown of all layers

## Planting Into Sheet Mulch

- Pull back the surface mulch to expose the compost layer
- Dig small holes for transplants; fill with additional compost if needed
- For seeds, create a seed bed of finer compost in the top layer
- Avoid disturbing the cardboard barrier below
- As the bed matures, roots will penetrate through the decomposing layers

## Common Mistakes

- **Too thin**: Insufficient depth leads to poor weed suppression and inadequate soil building
- **Cardboard gaps**: Weeds will push through any gaps in the barrier layer
- **Using hay instead of straw**: Hay contains seed heads that will sprout
- **Not watering enough**: Decomposition requires moisture
- **Using glossy paper or waxed cardboard**: These do not break down and may contain toxins
- **Piling against tree trunks**: Keep mulch 15 cm away from tree bark to prevent rot

## Variations

- **Instant bed method**: Use thicker finished compost layer for immediate planting
