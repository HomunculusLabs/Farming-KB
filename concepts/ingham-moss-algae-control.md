# Moss and Algae Control - Protozoa-Based Approaches

Extracted from Elaine Ingham's AACT Field Guide, covering biological
control of moss and algae through [[aact-nematode-protozoa-management-ingham]] restoration.

## The Fundamental Principle

Protozoa consume algae, especially in aquatic systems. The mechanism of
controlling algal blooms is restoring the predator-prey relationships
that keep algae in check -- not applying toxic chemicals.

## Lake Mendota Case Study

University of Wisconsin researchers documented a dramatic case. The algal
mess on Lake Mendota 20 years ago was so thick you could practically
walk on the algal bloom. The solution required getting the protozoa back
in the lake in high enough numbers.

**The trophic cascade:**

1. Sport fish (alien invaders) were added to the lake
2. Sport fish ate the native fish that ate smaller fish
3. The smaller fish ate protozoa (zooplankton)
4. Protozoa ate the algae (phytoplankton)
5. Without protozoa, algae bloomed uncontrollably

**The fix:** Native fish had to be brought back, because they ate fish
that ate the protozoa. It is a fish-eat-fish world, and you have to get
the links right, or the wrong critters dominate.

## Golf Course Ponds

In ponds on golf courses, the same pattern applies. Long ago, pesticides
and high levels of inorganic fertilizers took out not only the fish, but
the protozoa that eat the algae. The protozoa need to be re-established.

### The Algae Oxygen Paradox

Algae should be adding oxygen to the water, but only the green, living
layer on top. If the layer gets too thick:

1. Algae on the bottom die from lack of light
2. Dead algae become rich bacterial food
3. Bacteria eat the dead algae
4. Bacteria use up oxygen faster than the algae can replenish it
5. The water goes anoxic
6. Fish and other aerobic organisms die

This is why thick algal blooms actually deplete oxygen and create
anaerobic conditions -- the opposite of what thin, healthy algal growth
does.

## Soil Algae and Moss

While the Lake Mendota and golf course examples focus on aquatic systems,
the same principles apply to soil surfaces:

- Pesticides and high inorganic fertilizers kill protozoa in soil
- Without protozoa, algae and moss proliferate on soil surfaces
- Restoring protozoa through compost and AACT brings the system back
  into balance

## The Deeper Lesson

Ingham emphasizes: "Have to look beyond the obvious in so many of these
things. The mechanism of shutting down the pathogen is not through a
toxic chemical interaction. The organisms in the tea use the food
resources better than the pathogen does, and thus the 'good guys' win."

This applies equally to algae control -- it is not about killing algae
with chemicals but restoring the food web that keeps algae populations
in balance naturally.

## Hydroponics Applications

SFI has done some work in hydroponics dealing with algal problems and
some root disease. The trick is to add enough tea to the water to restore
the predator populations so that normal nutrient cycling is returned to
the water column. Some water "mold" problems are still being worked on.

## AACT Application for Algae Control

For aquatic systems:

- Apply compost tea containing protozoa to restore the grazing pressure
  on algae
- Address the root cause: stop applying pesticides and excessive
  inorganic fertilizers that kill protozoa
- In ponds, consider fish population management to restore the trophic
  cascade

For soil surfaces:

- Apply AACT with good protozoa numbers
- Reduce inorganic fertilizer inputs
- Allow the soil food web to re-establish naturally

## Key Takeaways

- Protozoa are the natural predators of algae in aquatic and soil systems
- Algal blooms result from broken food webs, not from algae being
  inherently problematic
- The trophic cascade matters -- removing one link (like fish that eat
  protozoa-eating fish) can collapse the whole system
- Thick algal blooms deplete oxygen (bottom layers die, bacteria consume
  dead algae, bacteria use up O2)
- Golf course ponds are classic examples of pesticide-induced protozoa loss
- Restoration requires re-establishing the full food web, not just adding
  one organism
- The same principle applies to soil moss and algae -- restore protozoa
