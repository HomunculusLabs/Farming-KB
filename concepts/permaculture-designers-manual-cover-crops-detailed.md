## Functions of Cover Crops

### Erosion Prevention

A cover crop is immediately sown to prevent erosion on exposed soils. After earthworks or land clearing, the soil surface is vulnerable to wind and water erosion. A quick-growing cover crop stabilizes the soil surface, intercepts rainfall energy, and reduces runoff velocity.

### Soil Building

Cover crops contribute to soil organic matter when incorporated as green manure. Their root systems:
- Break up compacted subsoils
- Create channels for water infiltration
- Exude compounds that feed soil organisms
- Cycle nutrients from deep soil layers to the surface
- Add humus when roots decompose

### Nitrogen Fixation

Leguminous cover crops or nitrogenous trees serve as cover crops for orchards and gardens, fixing atmospheric nitrogen through symbiotic relationships with Rhizobium bacteria. The Designers Manual emphasizes that legumes or nitrogenous trees are essential components of sustainable food production systems.

### Weed Suppression

Dense cover crop stands suppress weed germination and growth through competition for light, water, and nutrients. Some cover crops also exhibit allelopathic properties that inhibit weed seed germination.

## Cover Crop Species in the Designers Manual

### Leguminous Cover Crops

The manual references numerous legume species used as cover crops:

**Tree Legumes**
- Leucaena (Leucaena leucocephala) - fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing, mulch producer
- Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus palmensis) - suited to cool temperate areas, excellent fodder and mulch
- Gliricidia (Gliricidia sepium) - used in African alley cropping systems
- Sesbania (Sesbania spp.) - quick-growing, suited to tropical areas
- Erythrina (Erythrina spp.) - seasonal leaf drop, non-coppiced tall legumes
- Tipuana tipu - non-coppiced tall legume with seasonal leaf drop
- Pigeon pea (Cajanus cajan) - food and ground cover, used extensively in reclamation projects
- Lablab (Lablab purpureus) - quick-growing vine legume for tropical gardens

**Ground Legumes**
- Clover species (Trifolium spp.) - temperate climate cover
- Fava beans - cool season nitrogen fixer
- Lentils and peas - intercrop and nitrogen-fixing grain legumes
- Grams and pulses - intercrop and nitrogen-fixing

### Non-Leguminous Cover Crops

The manual also references non-legume species for continuous mulch production:
- Comfrey (Symphytum spp.) - deep-rooting, nutrient accumulator
- Vetiver grass (Vetiveria zizanioides) - erosion control, hedge plant
- Pennisetum species - fodder and mulch grasses
- Nicotiana - quick-growing annual
- Echium fastuosum - bee forage and mulch
- Cinnamon - aromatic mulch producer
- Wild ginger - ground cover in humid tropics
- Lemongrass - aromatic mulch and pest deterrent
- Lantana - vigorous ground cover (use with caution as it can become weedy)

### Cover Crops in Specific Contexts

**Pioneer Legumes for Reclamation**
At Assomada (Cape Verde) in the Sahel, pioneer legumes helped improve soil and halt erosion while providing forage and food locally. Pigeon pea supplied food and ground cover.

**Grassland Cover Crop Legumes**
Some vigorous grassland cover crop legumes are referenced for their ability to establish in degraded grasslands.

**Excellent Cover Crop for Acid Sandy Soils**
Several species are noted as excellent cover crops and seed in acid sandy or good soils, providing both soil protection and fertility building.

## Green Manure Cropping Systems

### Rotation with Green Manure

The Designers Manual references the Fukuoka approach, where green manure crops are scattered as part of a natural farming system. The logic that rejects grassed fields and green manure is based on a misunderstanding; the method includes sod and green manure cultivation as beneficial practices.

### Integration with Perennial Systems

In evolved polyculture systems, avenue cropping or mulch provision can be sustained by a carefully-planned system of:
- Mixed non-coppiced tall legumes giving seasonal leaf drop
- Row series of non-legumes for ground mulch
- Fast-growing, short-lived legumes on a 2-5 year cycle
- Perennial thin-crowned leguminous trees spaced throughout the system

## Cover Crops on Terraces

Cover crops are essential for terrace stability and productivity:
- About one-half to one-third of total terraced area should be devoted to mulch tree crop
- The upper one-third of hills, very steep slopes, terrace side-borders, and outer faces and crowns of bunds should be planted to productive and mulch-producing tree and ground crop
- This not only adds to terrace stability but provides local manurial-mulch crop for terrace cultures

## Mulch from Cover Crops

Cover crops provide constant mulch material for gardens and main crops. The strategy is to have dense planting of nucleus areas plus mulch, with a pioneer crop of quick-growing tree acacias, bananas, legumes such as lablab, deep-rooting comfrey, and a grove of bamboo and palms providing continuous mulch.

## Management Considerations

### Timing

Cover crops should be sown just before rain or irrigation to beat the growth of weeds. A possible two-crop sequence, with mulch also returned from crop wastes, maximizes productivity.

### Coppicing Constraints

Constant coppice weakens trees over 5-8 years, and replanting is necessary. Very few legume trees will sustain constant coppicing. Better strategies include:
- Confining coppice to warm wet periods
- Using non-coppiced species with natural leaf drop
- Mixing coppiced and non-coppiced species

### Integration with Food Production

Cover crops can also be food producers:
- Pigeon pea provides both food and ground cover
- Fava beans and lentils fix nitrogen while producing grain
- Sweet potato provides ground cover and edible tubers
- Many clovers provide both bee forage and nitrogen fixation
