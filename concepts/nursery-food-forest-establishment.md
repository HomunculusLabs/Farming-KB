# Nursery Techniques for Food Forest Establishment

## Overview

The establishment of productive food forests depends heavily on modern nursery techniques that accelerate tree growth and prepare plants for field planting. Bill Mollison drew heavily on the work of Slowgrove and other nursery innovators to develop approaches that allow families and communities to establish high-intensity food production systems with minimal capital and land. The nursery serves as a controlled environment where trees can reach advanced growth stages before being moved into field conditions where they would take many more years to mature.

## The Role of Nurseries in Permaculture

A nursery is fundamentally a sheltered, ideal environment for getting plants started. While trees are growing in the nursery, the land they will eventually occupy can be cropped and used for other purposes, potentially even being manured in preparation. Modern nurserymen using adjusted shade and nutrient regimes can achieve four to five times the growth rates of old open-bed nursery conditions. Revolutionary techniques now include cloning by root tip and single-cell generation of plants.

The permaculture approach predicates reduced fuel consumption for cooking, since most food forest crops are non-cook foods. In Mollison's view, there is no reason for using fuels for much of anything if food forests are properly established. This has profound implications for tropical island nations where cooking rice requires cutting trees for fuel, creating a destructive cycle of deforestation.

## Slowgrove's Approach and Nursery Kits

Slowgrove developed a systematic approach to establishing large-scale food forests. For a 4,000-acre community planting, the first step is to start the nursery running while ground preparation proceeds. Slowgrove designed a portable nursery kit — a complete, self-contained system that could be set up anywhere.

Mollison applied this concept in central Australia with Aboriginal communities. The nursery kit fit entirely on the back of a truck and included everything needed: drip lines, sprinklers, and shade houses. When a group of Aboriginal people moved to an outstation, they took their own trained nurseryman — someone who had been through a two-month training course and knew the nursery business.

The system worked through simple vegetative propagation:
- **Tamarisk**: Bundles of sticks placed in water, then stuck in sand to root
- **Grapes**: Twenty varieties of cuttings suited to the local area
- The nurseryman only needed a couple of months of training

## Tropical Food Forests

### Integration with Livestock

Mollison described an Indian government pig-killing station that had 68 acres but no system for utilizing pig manure. Nearby breadfruit trees dropped more fruit than all the pigs could eat. The solution was to integrate breadfruit and papaya into the pig operation, with workers carrying bananas to the pigs (pigs couldn't be run through banana stands). This demonstrated how existing resources could be dramatically better utilized.

### Non-Cook Food Forests

The best butter in the tropics is avocado — by a long margin superior to dairy butter. Formerly never produced or offered for sale in many regions, avocados, nuts, and other fruits are now rapidly being adopted. Mollison found that gardeners everywhere eagerly adopt new plant varieties when given the opportunity.

### Cold-Climate Application

The principles are not confined to tropical areas. Using modern nursery techniques, cold-temperate fruit and nut trees can be brought to a stage in one to two years in the nursery that would take eight years in the field alone. Container specimens can be shipped the year before they yield.

## Tree Combinations

### Fig-Grape Systems

One of the most elegant combinations in food forests is fig with grape. As soon as figs are up and bearing, a grape is established on a trellis. When the fig grows large enough, the grape is led into the fig's crown and pruning stops entirely. The grape reaches the crown and is naturally wind-pruned. The same principle works with elm, black walnut, and Tasmanian blackwood — all can carry grapes that bear as heavily as conventionally pruned vines.

The key requirement is a tree of limited height. The grapes are effectively pruned by the tree crown itself — no grapes can escape past it.

### Food Forest Structure

When designing a food forest, attention must be paid to edge and species placement:
- Most trees bear on the crown, but some must remain on the edge
- Crown bearers that are also edge species are differentiated from crown bearers that will stand within the clump
- Large nut trees belong in the interior
- It is sensible to set the forest off with bark yielders and close planting, then thin for crown yields, then for edge yields
- With diameters over 100 feet, the center can be treated as structural timber forest

In temperate forests, oaks bear well within the canopy and can serve as both forage and structural timber. The ideal approach is to plant forests at very small intervals — some species as close as three by three feet, nearly any species at nine by nine — which forces fast upright growth with good trunk development.

### Buffer Species

Large nut trees (hickories, pecans, walnuts — the *Juglans* genus) produce root exudates containing creosols that kill pioneering species. This is how they naturally increase against edges. A buffer forest of 20 to 30 feet of other trees must separate these from fruit trees. Excellent buffer species include:
- **Mulberry**: Stands right against nut trees with no crop loss, and against fruit trees without impairing their crop
- **Elderberry**: Snuggles up to both groups
- **Black locust**: Another effective buffering tree

## Black Walnut as a Case Study

About a decade before Mollison's lecture, investors bought an overcrowded island and planted it with black walnut as a veneer trade investment. The walnuts began producing, and some proved excellent. The investors began freeing the best selections and found themselves making substantial income from crushed nuts — they never even bothered with the veneer trade.

This illustrates the principle that a graded approach to food forest establishment doesn't take as long as one might expect: plant densely, select for early bearing and desirable characteristics, gradually thin to leave the best individuals, then allow crown development.

## Planting for Selection

The ideal sequence for establishing a food forest through selection:
1. Plant at very small intervals to force upright growth
2. Begin modest trunk trimming
3. Wait for bearing to start (it starts with small trees)
4. Select for early bearing, easy nut crackability, low tannin, and other desirable traits
5. Cut out trees that don't meet expectations but already have good trunk length
6. Continue thinning until left with excellent trees on multiple characteristics
7. Allow crown development

## Key Principles

- **Start with the nursery**: Accelerated growth in controlled conditions gives trees a years-long head start
- **Portable nursery kits**: Self-contained systems can be deployed anywhere, requiring only a trained operator
- **Integrate rather than segregate**: Food forests combine livestock, tree crops, and ground crops in complementary systems
- **Dense planting forces quality**: Close spacing produces straight trunks and allows rigorous selection
- **Tree combinations reduce labor**: Fig-grape, walnut-grape, and other pairings eliminate pruning needs
- **Buffer species enable diversity**: Juglone-producing nut trees can coexist with fruits when properly buffered
- **Non-cook foods reduce fuel dependency**: Avocados, nuts, and fruits eliminate the need for cooking fuel
