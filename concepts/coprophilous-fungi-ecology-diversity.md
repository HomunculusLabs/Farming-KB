# Coprophilous Fungi — Ecology and Diversity of Dung-Inhabiting Fungi

Coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungi are a diverse and ecologically important group that play crucial roles in breaking down animal waste and recycling nutrients. They have evolved remarkable adaptations for dispersal, germination, and survival on ephemeral substrata.

## Dispersal and Germination

### The Spore-Ingestion Cycle
Many coprophilous fungi have spores that germinate only after passage through the mammalian digestive tract. This remarkable adaptation involves:
- Spores discharged forcefully into the air, adhering to nearby vegetation
- Animals ingest vegetation (and spores) while feeding
- Digestive enzymes scarify or activate the spores
- Fresh dung provides a nutrient-rich, initially competition-free substratum

### Gelatinous Sheaths
Spores of many coprophilous Ascomycetes are surrounded by gelatinous sheaths or possess gelatinous attachments. These structures:
- Aid in adhesion to vegetation
- May facilitate passage through the digestive tract
- Help spores stick to new dung deposits

### Other Dispersal Mechanisms
- **Rain splash:** Disperses some species between dung deposits
- **Insects:** Important but often overlooked dispersal vectors
- **Mites:** Disseminate myxomycete spores and various fungi
- **Mammals:** Some cleistothecial ascomycetes have modified appendages for fur attachment (especially important for rodent dung fungi)

## Fungal Succession on Dung

A predictable succession of fungal groups colonizes dung over time:

1. **Zygomycetes (5-14 days):** Early colonizers; Mucorales dominate initially
2. **Ascomycetes:** Mid-succession; pyrenomycetes and discomycetes
3. **Basidiomycetes:** Late succession; some appear with Ascomycetes when lignin is available
4. **Myxomycetes:** Very late succession; relatively rare on dung

The sequence is based on types of substrata (cellulose, lignin) used by each group. However, timing overlaps considerably, and competition, nutrient availability, and environmental factors modify the strict linear progression.

## Factors Influencing Mycobiota

### Temperature
- Thelebolus and Preussia species dominate on leporid dung in early spring (cold)
- Other species dominate during warmer summer months
- Temperature directly affects fungal succession patterns

### Light
- Some taxa are positively phototropic (Ascobolus, some Podospora species)
- Light influences fruiting body development

### Moisture
- Self-evident influence; too dry prevents colonization, too wet favors bacteria

### Competition and Antagonism
- Hyphal interference is widespread among coprophilous fungi
- Coprinus heptemerus produces lethal hyphal interference against Ascobolus and Pilobolus
- Some fungi produce diffusible antibiotics to restrict faster-growing competitors
- Arthropod and fly larvae prey on fungi, paradoxically allowing rarer species to sporulate by reducing competition

### Defense Mechanisms
- Ornamented perithecial hairs in Chaetomium species
- Stiff bristle-like hairs in Coniochaeta species
- Diffusible compounds in Chaetomiaceae and Sporormiaceae
- Dark melaninized spore walls protect against UV radiation

## Geographic Patterns

### Latitudinal Gradient
Species richness decreases with increasing latitude:
- Tropical sites (0-30°): 153 taxa in 50 samples
- Gotland, Sweden: 112 taxa (locally rich)
- Other Swedish sites: 74-95 taxa
- New Zealand: 66 taxa of pyrenomycetes

### Regional Distinctness
- North-temperate, tropical-subtropical, and south-temperate groups appear distinct
- Within each group, distribution is fairly cosmopolitan
- Tropical savanna habitats (East Africa) show higher diversity per collection than temperate forests

### Animal Source Effects
- Different herbivores produce dung supporting different mycobiota
- Cattle dung is best-studied and richest for Myxomycetes
- Cow, hare, horse, rabbit, elk, moose, roe deer, goat, and sheep are best sources (in that order)
- Bird dung is generally poor for Myxomycetes
- Small animal dung (rats, mice, bats) is richest for coprophilous Zygomycetes

## Ecological Importance

Coprophilous fungi are critical for:
- Breaking down complex plant materials in dung
- Recycling micronutrients back into soil
- Returning carbon to the ecosystem
- Supporting dung-based food webs (insects, mites, other organisms)
- Source of novel antifungal natural products (many slow-growing coprophilous taxa)
