# Coprophilous Fungi — Ecology and Diversity of Dung-Inhabiting Fungi Coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungi are a diverse and ecologically important group that play crucial roles in breaking down animal waste and recycling nutrients. They have evolved remarkable adaptations for dispersal, germination, and survival on ephemeral substrata. ## Dispersal and Germination ### The Spore-Ingestion Cycle Many coprophilous fungi have spores that germinate only after passage through the mammalian digestive tract. This remarkable adaptation involves: - Spores discharged forcefully into the air, adhering to nearby vegetation - Animals ingest vegetation (and spores) while feeding - Digestive enzymes scarify or activate the spores - Fresh dung provides a nutrient-rich, initially competition-free substratum ### Gelatinous Sheaths Spores of many coprophilous Ascomycetes are surrounded by gelatinous sheaths or possess gelatinous attachments. These structures: - Aid in adhesion to vegetation - May facilitate passage through the digestive tract - Help spores stick to new dung deposits ### Other Dispersal Mechanisms - **Rain splash:** Disperses some species between dung deposits - **Insects:** Important but often overlooked dispersal vectors - **Mites:** Disseminate myxomycete spores and various fungi - **Mammals:** Some cleistothecial ascomycetes have modified appendages for fur attachment (especially important for rodent dung fungi) ## Fungal Succession on Dung A predictable succession of fungal groups colonizes dung over time: 1. **Zygomycetes (5-14 days):** Early colonizers; Mucorales dominate initially 2. **Ascomycetes:** Mid-succession; pyrenomycetes and discomycetes 3. **Basidiomycetes:** Late succession; some appear with Ascomycetes when lignin is available 4. **Myxomycetes:** Very late succession; relatively rare on dung The sequence is based on types of substrata (cellulose, lignin) used by each group. However, timing overlaps considerably, and competition, nutrient availability, and environmental factors modify the strict linear progression. ## Factors Influencing Mycobiota ### Temperature - Thelebolus and Preussia species dominate on leporid dung in early spring (cold) - Other species dominate during warmer summer months - Temperature directly affects fungal succession patterns ### Light - Some taxa are positively phototropic (Ascobolus, some Podospora species) - Light influences fruiting body development ### Moisture - Self-evident influence; too dry prevents colonization, too wet favors bacteria ### Competition and Antagonism - Hyphal interference is widespread among coprophilous fungi - Coprinus heptemerus produces lethal hyphal interference against Ascobolus and Pilobolus - Some fungi produce diffusible antibiotics to restrict faster-growing competitors - Arthropod and fly larvae prey on fungi, paradoxically allowing rarer species to sporulate by reducing competition ### Defense Mechanisms - Ornamented perithecial hairs in Chaetomium species - Stiff bristle-like hairs in Coniochaeta species - Diffusible compounds in Chaetomiaceae and Sporormiaceae - Dark melaninized spore walls protect against UV radiation ## Geographic Patterns ### Latitudinal Gradient Species richness decreases with increasing latitude: - Tropical sites (0-30°): 153 taxa in 50 samples - Gotland, Sweden: 112 taxa (locally rich) - Other Swedish sites: 74-95 taxa - New Zealand: 66 taxa of pyrenomycetes ### Regional Distinctness - North-temperate, tropical-subtropical, and south-temperate groups appear distinct - Within each group, distribution is fairly cosmopolitan - Tropical savanna habitats (East Africa) show higher diversity per collection than temperate forests ### Animal Source Effects - Different herbivores produce dung supporting different mycobiota - Cattle dung is best-studied and richest for Myxomycetes - Cow, hare, horse, rabbit, elk, moose, roe deer, goat, and sheep are best sources (in that order) - Bird dung is generally poor for Myxomycetes - Small animal dung (rats, mice, bats) is richest for coprophilous Zygomycetes ## Ecological Importance Coprophilous fungi are critical for: - Breaking down complex plant materials in dung - Recycling micronutrients back into soil - Returning carbon to the ecosystem - Supporting dung-based food webs (insects, mites, other organisms) - Source of novel antifungal natural products (many slow-growing coprophilous taxa) 
## Successional Stages on Dung

Coprophilous fungi colonize herbivore dung in a predictable
successional sequence. The primary colonizers appear within hours
of deposition and include zygomycete genera such as Pilobolus,
Mucor, and Rhizopus. These rapid colonizers exploit the nutrient-
rich, moist substrate before competitors can establish. Within
2-5 days, ascomycete genera including Saccobolus, Ascobolus, and
Podospora begin fruiting on the drying dung surface.

The secondary succession (1-4 weeks) is dominated by coprophilous
ascomycetes and basidiomycetes. Coprinus species (ink caps) are
characteristic secondary colonizers that rapidly decompose remaining
organic matter. The tertiary phase involves generalist saprobes
that persist on the residual substrate as it integrates with the
soil. This successional framework provides a model system for
studying fungal community dynamics and competitive interactions.

## Major Taxonomic Groups

The coprophilous mycobiota spans all major fungal phyla.
Zygomycota are represented primarily by Pilobolus (the "hat-
thrower" fungus, which launches sporangia toward light sources),
Mucor, and Rhizopus. Ascomycota dominate the secondary succession
with genera including Saccobolus, Ascobolus, Sordaria, Podospora,
Sporormiella, and Delitschia. Basidiomycota are primarily
represented by Coprinus, Psathyrella, and Bolbitius species.

## Spore Dispersal Mechanisms

Coprophilous fungi have evolved specialized spore dispersal
strategies adapted to their unique ecological niche. Many produce
spores that remain viable after passing through the herbivore
digestive tract, ensuring deposition on fresh substrate. Pilobolus
uses a phototropic turgor-powered mechanism to launch sporangia
up to 2 meters toward light, increasing the probability of landing
on vegetation that will be consumed by herbivores.

## Collection and Cultivation Methods

Standard collection methods involve returning to dung pats at
regular intervals (daily for the first week, then weekly) to
capture different successional stages. Fresh dung is placed in
moist chambers — sealed containers with damp filter paper — to
encourage sporulation of fungi that may not have fruited in the
field. Cultivation on sterile dung agar or modified PDA allows
isolation of individual species for taxonomic study.

## Ecological Role in Nutrient Cycling

Coprophilous fungi play a critical role in returning nutrients
from herbivore dung to the soil food web. By decomposing the
cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin components of undigested
plant material, they release nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium
in forms available to plants and soil microorganisms. This
decomposition process also reduces dung bulk and eliminates
pathogens, contributing to pasture hygiene and soil health.

## Notable Species

Pilobolus crystallinus is renowned for its precise spore
discharge mechanism. Coprinus comatus (shaggy mane) is a common
and edible coprophilous species. Sporormiella is widely used in
paleoecological studies as a spore preserved in lake sediments
that serves as a proxy for herbivore population density.
Podospora species are important model organisms for studying
fungal sexual reproduction and genetics.
