# Piule (Rhynchosia spp.)

Piule is the name given to several species of Rhynchosia that have
beautiful red and black seeds which may have been valued as a narcotic by
ancient Mexicans. What appear to be these seeds have been pictured, together
with mushrooms, falling from the hand of the Aztec rain god Tlaloc in the
Tepantitla fresco dating to approximately A.D. 300-400. This archaeological
evidence provides one of the earliest known depictions of psychoactive seed
use in Mesoamerica. Modern Indians in southern Mexico still refer to these
seeds as piule, one of the names also applied to the hallucinogenic morning
glory seeds.

## The Tepantitla Fresco: Ancient Evidence

The Tepantitla fresco is one of the most important archaeological artifacts
documenting pre-Columbian psychoactive plant use in Mesoamerica. Located at
the archaeological site of Teotihuacan near modern Mexico City, the fresco
dates to approximately A.D. 300-400 and depicts the Aztec rain god Tlaloc
with what appear to be psychoactive seeds and mushrooms falling from his
hand. The seeds depicted in the fresco closely resemble the red and black
seeds of Rhynchosia species, suggesting that the use of these seeds as a
narcotic or hallucinogen may extend back at least 1,600 years.

The association of the seeds with the rain god Tlaloc is significant, as
Tlaloc was one of the most important deities in the Mesoamerican
pantheon. His association with rain, fertility, and agricultural abundance
made him a central figure in religious ceremonies, and the depiction of
psychoactive seeds in his hand suggests that these plants played a role in
rituals aimed at ensuring rainfall and agricultural productivity.

## The Name Piule: A Shared Designation

The name "piule" is shared between Rhynchosia seeds and hallucinogenic
morning glory seeds (Turbina corymbosa and Ipomoea species), both of which
are used by indigenous peoples in southern Mexico. This shared nomenclature
reflects the visual similarity between the seeds of these different plants
and may also indicate a functional similarity in their ceremonial or
medicinal applications. The morning glories contain ergoline alkaloids
closely related to LSD, while the chemistry of Rhynchosia seeds is less
well characterized.

The practice of sharing names between pharmacologically distinct but
visually similar seeds is a recurring pattern in Mesoamerican ethnobotany,
also seen in the overlap between Erythrina colorines and Sophora
secundiflora mescal beans. This naming convergence can create significant
challenges for ethnobotanical researchers attempting to identify which
specific plant is being referenced in historical or ethnographic accounts.

## Botanical Description of Rhynchosia

The genus Rhynchosia comprises approximately 300 species belonging to the
bean family, Leguminosae (Fabaceae). These plants are distributed throughout
the tropics and subtropics of both hemispheres and include both herbaceous
and woody species. The seeds of some Rhynchosia species are visually
distinctive, with contrasting red and black coloration that makes them
easily recognizable and potentially attractive as decorative or ceremonial
objects in addition to any psychoactive use.

The genus is closely related to other important legume genera, and like
many legumes, Rhynchosia species are capable of nitrogen fixation through
symbiotic relationships with soil bacteria. Several species have documented
uses in folk medicine in various tropical countries, though the specific
medicinal applications vary widely across different cultural traditions.

## Chemistry: Positive Alkaloid Tests

Seeds of some species of Rhynchosia have given positive alkaloid tests,
confirming the presence of nitrogen-containing secondary metabolites in the
seeds. However, the specific toxic principles have still not been
characterized according to Schultes' account. This means that the active
compounds responsible for any psychoactive effects, their mechanism of
action, and their toxicity profile all remain unknown. The positive alkaloid
tests are suggestive but not conclusive evidence of psychoactivity, as many
plant alkaloids have pharmacological effects unrelated to hallucination or
intoxication.

The alkaloid chemistry of Rhynchosia is of particular interest because the
related legume genera include several well-known psychoactive plants. The
morning glories (Convolvulaceae, not Leguminosae) contain ergoline
alkaloids, while Sophora secundiflora contains the toxic alkaloid cytisine.
If Rhynchosia seeds do contain psychoactive alkaloids, they may belong to
novel chemical classes or may share structural features with alkaloids found
in other leguminous psychoactive plants.

## The Question of Hallucinogenic Use

While the Tepantitla fresco and modern indigenous terminology suggest that
Rhynchosia seeds may have been used as a psychoactive substance, the
evidence is not conclusive. The visual identification of seeds in ancient
art is inherently uncertain, and the shared name "piule" with morning glory
seeds could reflect visual similarity rather than equivalent psychoactive
properties. The positive alkaloid tests confirm bioactive chemistry but do
not specifically identify hallucinogenic compounds.

It is possible that Rhynchosia seeds were used in ancient ceremonies for
their psychoactive effects, which might range from mild stimulation or
sedation to more dramatic hallucinatory experiences. It is also possible
that the seeds served primarily symbolic or decorative purposes in ritual
contexts, with their red and black coloration carrying cultural or religious
significance independent of any pharmacological activity.

## Folk Medicine Applications

The seeds of some Rhynchosia species are important in folk medicine in
several countries across the tropics. Traditional medicinal uses likely
include treatment of digestive complaints, skin conditions, and various
other ailments, though specific documentation of Rhynchosia use in folk
medicine is less extensive than for many better-known medicinal plants. The
folk medicinal applications may be related to the same alkaloid compounds
that are responsible for any psychoactive effects.

## Archaeological Significance

If the identification of Rhynchosia seeds in the Tepantitla fresco is
correct, it would place the use of these seeds among the earliest documented
psychoactive plant practices in Mesoamerica, alongside the use of mushrooms
depicted in the same artwork. The fresco dates to the Teotihuacan culture,
which predated the Aztecs by many centuries, suggesting that the knowledge
and use of psychoactive seeds in central Mexico has a very deep history
extending well beyond the Aztec period.
