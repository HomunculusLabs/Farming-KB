# Freshwater Aquatic Fungal Decomposition

## Overview

Fungi are dominant decomposers [[fungi-in-freshwater-aquatic-ecosystems]] ecosystems, where they colonize decaying plant litter, wood, and suspended organic matter in streams, ponds, lakes, and deep aquifers. With more than 600 known species of aquatic fungi (Wong et al., 1998), these organisms possess specific morphological and physiological adaptations that allow them to thrive in aquatic habitats and play critical roles in [[ectomycorrhizal-nutrient-cycling-and-forest-dynamics]] carbon processing.

## Adaptations to Aquatic Life

The success of [[aquatic-hyphomycetes]] in freshwater systems stems from key adaptations that give them competitive advantages over terrestrial fungi entering the system via plant material:

- **Spore morphology**: Aquatic fungi produce tetraradiate (four-armed) and sigmoid (curved) spores that are more efficient at colonizing submerged substrates than the rounded, wind-dispersed spores of terrestrial fungi. These shapes increase the probability of attachment to submerged surfaces.
- **Submerged growth capacity**: Terrestrial fungi are unable to effectively macerate (soften and break apart) resources when submerged, whereas aquatic fungi are adapted to decompose material under water.
- **Fungal biomass dominance**: On decomposing leaf litter [[fungal-decomposition-in-aquatic-ecosystems-concluding-perspectives]], fungal biomass typically exceeds bacterial biomass.

## Plant Litter Inputs to Streams

The magnitude of plant litter entering freshwater systems is substantial, particularly in forested catchments:

- Headwater streams in forested catchments receive approximately 500 g dry mass/m² of plant litter input, with peaks exceeding 100 g/m² (Weigelhofer and Waringer, 1994).
- Litter enters streams via three pathways: aerial input (direct fall), and transport from right and left stream banks.
- In beech-dominated Austrian forests, leaf litter comprised 68–93% of inputs, wood 4–27%, and miscellaneous material 1–18%.

## Wood Decomposition in Streams

### Importance of Woody Debris

Wood represents up to 20% of total plant litter input in some streams and has particular ecological significance:

- Shearer (1992) estimated 250–800 tons/ha of woody debris enters stream systems of old-growth temperate coniferous forests, and 40–130 tons/ha in mixed hardwood forests.
- Wood has a much longer residence time than leaf litter, providing a stable substrate for fungal community development.
- The main woody material input occurs in winter (windthrow and weather damage), becoming available for fungal colonization in spring.

### Lignolytic Aquatic Fungi

Despite the absence of the classic white-rot and [[brown-rot]] basidiomycetes found in terrestrial ecosystems, lignolytic aquatic hyphomycetes exist:

- Dominant genera: **Tricladium**, **Anguillospora**, and **Dendrospora**.
- Approximately one-third (86 species) of aquatic hyphomycetes have been isolated from wood.
- Of 20 species examined, 7 produced enzymes capable of degrading carboxymethyl cellulose, cellobiose, amylose, xylan, xylose, lignin, and pectin, while 4 additional species could utilize five or more of these resources.

### Community Change Over Space and Time

Fungal communities on woody resources vary along:

- **Stream length** (habitat selection): Different species dominate at different positions along a watercourse.
- **Time at the same location** (resource succession): Species composition changes as decomposition proceeds.

## Leaf Litter Decomposition in Streams

### Fungal-Bacterial Interactions

Fungi are not the sole decomposers of leaf litter. Gaur et al. (1992) identified 13 bacteria and 24 fungal species on decomposing water hyacinth leaves and demonstrated a division of labor:

- **Bacteria**: Primarily involved in breakdown of polysaccharides and proteins; access dissolved organic matter; dominate early decomposition.
- **Fungi**: Responsible for decomposition of cellulose and lignocellulose; become important as dissolved organic matter declines and they can penetrate intact structural components.
- **Faunal comminution**: Non-microbial processing (physical breakdown by invertebrates) accounts for approximately 30% of early litter mass loss.

### Succession of Aquatic Fungi

Gessner et al. (1993) demonstrated temporal successions of aquatic fungi on alder leaf litter analogous to terrestrial successions:

1. **Early colonization (2 weeks)**: Community dominated by 5–6 species including *[[flagellospora-curvula]]*, *Tetracladium elegans*, *Lemonniera centrospharea*, *L. aquatica*, and *L. terrestris*.
2. **Mid-succession (4 weeks)**: More equitable community with approximately 11 species.
3. **Late succession (8 weeks)**: Continued community with some shifts in dominance.

**Fungal biomass (ergosterol) and conidial production both peak during mid-succession**, indicating maximum [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] during the transition from early to late colonization.

In the Florida Everglades, bacteria account for approximately 30% of respiration from decomposing leaf litter, with the remaining 70% from fungi (Hackney et al., 2000).

## Water Chemistry and Decomposition Rates

### Effects of Nutrients

Suberkropp (1995) demonstrated that fungal biomass was higher in streams with higher nitrogen content. Additional nitrogen had a more pronounced effect on spore production than on mycelial biomass, indicating resource allocation flexibility between growth and reproduction.

### Hardwater vs. Softwater Streams

Suberkropp and Chauvet (1995) performed reciprocal leaf exchange experiments between streams of contrasting chemistry, finding that decomposition rates varied by a factor of 9, microbial activity by a factor of 8, and fungal sporulation by a factor of 80 between streams.

**Hardwater streams** (higher pH, more nitrate-N and phosphorus, higher temperatures) supported faster litter decomposition, more abundant and diverse fungal biomass and activity, and approximately 6 common species across all hardwater streams.

**Softwater streams** had only 2 species in common with hardwater streams, different dominant species (*[[anguillospora-filiformis]]* and *Flagellospora curvula*), and slower decomposition rates.

Correlation analysis showed strong positive relationships between decomposition and both NO₃-N (r = 0.97) and PO₄-P (r = 0.83) across all streams.

### Temperature as a Primary Driver

Griffiths et al. (1995) compared decomposition of yellow poplar, red maple, and white oak leaves across three streams of differing pH and temperature. When data were temperature-corrected, between-site differences became less obvious, suggesting temperature is the primary driving variable. Physical changes during decomposition also varied: white oak and red maple exhibited skeletonization (vein structure remaining), while yellow poplar leaves simply became softer.

### Seasonal Variation

Thomas et al. (1989) documented seasonal patterns in aquatic fungal communities:

- **Greatest conidial abundance**: Early autumn (500–600 conidia/L).
- **Lowest abundance**: Winter (300–500 conidia/L).
- **Spore abundance** was significantly related to temperature (R² = 0.62).
- **Species composition** was related to rainfall and conductivity rather than temperature.

## Fungal Role in Aquatic Food Webs

### Support of Invertebrate Consumers

Aquatic fungi form an important food source for invertebrate shredders:

- Some shredders prefer leaves already colonized by fungi (Graca et al., 1993).
- Others selectively consume fungal mycelia directly.
- Fungal colonization enhances palatability by increasing the nitrogen content of leaf litter during initial stages of decomposition.

### Carbon Budgets

Gessner et al. (1997) developed field carbon budgets comparing sycamore in freshwater streams with cordgrass in salt marshes. Fungal biomass can form up to 15% of total detrital mass during decomposition. A higher percentage of carbon is released as dissolved organic matter (DOM) and fine particulate organic matter (FPOM) from faster-decomposing Spartina compared to sycamore.

## Competitive Interactions Among Aquatic Fungi

Bergbauer et al. (1992) found that decomposition rates of plant litter were reduced in mixed-species fungal assemblages compared to single-species colonization. This reduction was attributed to the production of antimicrobial compounds resulting in non-nutritional competition between fungal species — a finding analogous to combative interactions observed in terrestrial wood-decomposing fungal communities.

## Knowledge Gaps

Wong et al. (1998) emphasized that despite the dominance of fungi as decomposers in freshwater systems, significant knowledge gaps remain:

- Mechanisms of decomposition at the biochemical level are poorly understood.
- Fungal-fungal and fungal-bacterial interactions need further investigation.
- The relationship between fungal biodiversity and ecological function is largely unknown.
- Scaling from microbial-scale processes to ecosystem-level nutrient budgets remains challenging.

Gulis (2001) performed multivariate analysis of 146 samples from 92 aquatic systems, beginning to address some of these questions through quantitative community ecology approaches.
