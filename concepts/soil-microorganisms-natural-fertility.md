# Soil Microorganisms and Natural Fertility

Soil microorganisms are the foundation of natural soil fertility. Masanobu Fukuoka's farming system depends entirely on the activity of billions of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and other microorganisms per gram of soil to maintain and build fertility without any external inputs. Understanding the role of these organisms is essential to understanding why natural farming can produce high yields without fertilizers, tillage, or pesticides, and why conventional farming practices that destroy microbial communities create the very problems they then attempt to solve with chemical inputs.

## The Living Soil

### A Gram of Soil

One gram of ordinary topsoil contains approximately one hundred million bacteria, yeasts, molds, diatoms, and other microbes. Far from being dead and inanimate mineral matter, soil is teeming with life. These microorganisms do not exist without reason; each lives for a purpose, struggling, cooperating, and carrying on the cycles of nature. They decompose organic matter, fix atmospheric nitrogen, solubilize minerals, suppress pathogens, form associations with plant roots, and create the soil structure that retains water and air.

### The Soil as an Organism

Fukuoka viewed soil not as a substrate for growing plants but as a living organism that grows, enriches itself, and maintains its own fertility. He wrote: "It is in the nature of soil to swell and grow more porous with each passing year. This is absolutely essential for microorganisms to multiply in the earth, for the soil to grow more fertile, and for the roots of large trees to penetrate deep into the ground."

The soil works itself. Microorganisms and small animals act as nature's tillers, far more effectively than any mechanical implement. Grass roots work the soil to depths of 12 to 15 inches or more. When roots die, microorganisms proliferate, earthworms appear where humus accumulates, and moles begin burrowing through the improved soil.

## How Conventional Farming Kills Soil

### The Cascade of Destruction

Conventional farming practices initiate a cascade of soil destruction:

1. **Plowing** breaks up natural soil aggregates, reducing porosity and killing sensitive microorganisms
2. **Weeding** removes the plants whose roots maintain soil structure and whose decomposition feeds microbial communities
3. **Chemical fertilizers** acidify the soil and suppress microbial activity. More than 70% of common fertilizers (ammonium sulfate, super-phosphate, potassium sulfate) is concentrated sulfuric acid
4. **Pesticides** directly kill soil microorganisms. Fukuoka estimated that one gram of his farm soil contained 100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria, while the same volume of conventionally farmed soil would contain vastly fewer due to chemical applications
5. **Summer flooding** of rice paddies rots roots and creates anaerobic conditions that favor harmful organisms over beneficial ones

The result is what Fukuoka called the transformation of "rich, living, organic material into a dead, inanimate, yellowish-white mineral matter the only function of which is to physically support the crops."

### Why Fertilizer Becomes Necessary

Fukuoka demonstrated that fertilizer only appears necessary because conventional methods have first destroyed the soil's natural fertility. The farmer clears a forest, fells and removes all trees, branches, and leaves, digs up and burns roots, then turns the soil over and over until it becomes lifeless mineral matter. Only then does he plant fruit saplings and apply fertilizer, noting with satisfaction that the trees respond to the nutrients. The poor farmer has taken "the long way around," creating the conditions that make fertilizer seem indispensable.

## Nitrogen Fixation

### Biological Nitrogen Fixation

Nitrogen-fixing bacteria are among the most important soil microorganisms for agriculture. These bacteria, including species of Rhizobium, Azotobacter, and Clostridium, convert atmospheric nitrogen gas (N2) into ammonia (NH3) and other forms that plants can absorb. This process supplies the majority of nitrogen available to plants in natural ecosystems, entirely without human intervention.

### Legume-Rhizobium Symbiosis

Leguminous plants such as clover, alfalfa, beans, peas, and acacias form symbiotic relationships with Rhizobium bacteria. The bacteria colonize root nodules, where they receive carbohydrates from the plant and in return supply fixed nitrogen. This is why ladino clover, alfalfa, and black wattle are central to Fukuoka's farming system. A healthy clover cover can fix enough nitrogen to sustain both the cover crop and the companion grain or fruit trees.

### Free-Living Nitrogen Fixers

Beyond the well-known legume symbiosis, free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the soil and in association with plant root surfaces contribute significant quantities of nitrogen. Fukuoka's observation of 100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria per gram in his farm soil suggests that these organisms, combined with the legume symbiosis, can supply all the nitrogen that crops require under natural conditions.

## Mycorrhizal Associations

### The Matsutake Pine Connection

Fukuoka's investigation of the red pine dieback epidemic across Japan revealed the critical importance of mycorrhizal fungi. The matsutake mushroom (Tricholoma matsutake) lives symbiotically on the roots of red pines, forming a mycorrhizal association that benefits both organisms. When the matsutake died due to increasing soil acidity and proliferation of harmful molds, the red pines lost their symbiotic partner and became vulnerable to nematode infestation and pathogenic fungi.

### How Mycorrhizae Function

Mycorrhizal fungi form extensive networks of hyphae that connect to plant roots, extending the effective root surface area by hundreds of times. These fungal networks:

- Absorb water and minerals (especially phosphorus) from soil that roots alone cannot reach
- Transfer nutrients between plants through shared fungal networks
- Protect roots from pathogenic fungi and nematodes
- Produce glomalin, a sticky protein that improves soil aggregation and water retention
- Serve as a carbon sink, storing significant quantities of carbon in the soil

The death of matsutake mushrooms was Fukuoka's "solemn warning that something is amiss in the world of soil microbes." He predicted that the first telltale signs of global environmental change would appear in microorganisms, particularly in highly developed biological communities like mycorrhizal associations.

## The Role of Decomposition

### The Soil Food Web

Decomposition of organic matter in soil follows a complex food web. Bacteria and fungi are the primary decomposers, breaking down plant residues, animal remains, and other organic materials. Protozoa feed on bacteria, nematodes feed on fungi and protozoa, and larger organisms such as earthworms and arthropods feed on all of the above. At each trophic level, nutrients are mineralized and become available for plant uptake.

### Why Straw Mulch Works

Fukuoka's practice of scattering uncut rice and barley straw over his fields feeds this decomposition cycle directly. The straw provides carbon that supports bacterial and fungal growth. As these microorganisms decompose the straw, they temporarily immobilize nitrogen (creating a slight nitrogen deficiency), but as they die and are consumed by predators, nitrogen is released in plant-available forms. This is why straw applied half a year before crops are sown is more effective than composted material: the decomposition cycle has time to complete and release nutrients in synchrony with plant growth.

### Why Composting Is Unnecessary

Fukuoka argued that the composting of organic materials is fundamentally unnecessary because the soil's microbial community is perfectly capable of decomposing raw organic matter in situ. All the labor of preparing compost (turning piles, adding water, nitrogenous fertilizers, lime, super-phosphate, rice bran, and manure) accelerates decomposition by at most 10 to 20%, hardly justifying the effort. Applying straw directly and letting soil organisms do the work achieves outstanding results.

## Soil Acidity and Microbial Health

### The Acidification Problem

Chemical fertilizers, particularly ammonium sulfate, are a major source of soil acidification. Japan alone dumped approximately 1.8 million tons of sulfuric acid onto farmlands annually in fertilizer form during Fukuoka's era. Acidic conditions suppress and kill beneficial soil microorganisms, disrupt the soil ecosystem, and create conditions favorable to harmful organisms.

The red pine dieback epidemic illustrated this cascade: increased soil acidity killed matsutake mushrooms, which allowed harmful Botrytis fungi to proliferate, which weakened the pines, which made them susceptible to nematode infestation carried by long-horned beetles. The chain of causation traced back to soil microbial health.

### Natural pH Regulation

In natural systems, soil pH is regulated by the buffering capacity of humus and the activity of soil microorganisms. Continuous addition of organic matter through plant growth and decomposition, combined with acid-tolerant and base-producing microorganisms, maintains soil pH within a range suitable for most organisms and plants without any amendments.

## The Ecological Interconnectedness of Soil Life

### The Pine Rot Case Study

Fukuoka's detailed investigation of the pine rot epidemic demonstrated the extraordinary interconnectedness of soil organisms and plants:

1. Atmospheric pollution increases soil acidity
2. Increased acidity kills the matsutake mycorrhizal fungus
3. Without the mycorrhizal partner, pine roots decay and blacken
4. Harmful mold fungi (kurohen-kin) proliferate in the deteriorating conditions
5. New, undocumented pathogenic fungi colonize weakened trees
6. Nematodes arrive to feed on the pathogenic fungi (not the tree directly)
7. Long-horned beetles carry nematodes between trees
8. Weakened pines wither and die
9. Clear-cut and replanted with cedar, the changed tree composition affects birds, insects, and soil
10. Bamboo grass replaces the fallen forest, feeding mice that multiply and attack cedar saplings
11. Rodenticides kill mice, causing decline in weasels and snakes that eat mice
12. Humans begin raising mice to restore the rodent population for the weasels

This chain, which Fukuoka called "the stuff of crazed dreams," illustrates why targeting any single organism with a pesticide cannot solve ecological problems. The true cause is systemic degradation of the entire soil ecosystem.

### Implications for Farming

The lesson for agriculture is that healthy crops require healthy soil ecosystems. When soil microorganisms are thriving, plants receive balanced nutrition, are protected from pathogens, and develop strong natural resistance to pests. When the soil ecosystem is damaged, plants become dependent on chemical inputs that further damage the soil, creating a spiral of declining health and increasing chemical dependence.

## Restoring Soil Microbial Life

### The Natural Approach

Fukuoka demonstrated that damaged soil can be restored by simply stopping the practices that damage it and allowing natural succession to occur:

1. **Stop plowing**: Let roots and soil organisms work the soil
2. **Stop applying chemicals**: Let microbial communities recover
3. **Add organic matter**: Apply straw, leaves, and other plant residues as surface mulch
4. **Establish living ground cover**: Clover, alfalfa, and other plants feed soil organisms through root exudates and decomposition
5. **Introduce nitrogen-fixing plants**: Leguminous trees and cover crops restore nitrogen cycling
6. **Be patient**: Five to ten years are needed to build six inches of topsoil through natural processes

### Measuring Success

The success of soil restoration can be observed directly: earthworms return, soil darkens with humus, water infiltration improves, weeds shift from opportunistic species to those characteristic of fertile soils, and crop health and yields improve without any chemical inputs. Fukuoka noted that the beneficial effects ultimately show up in the longer lifetime of fruit trees, which is perhaps two to three times that of trees grown by scientific methods.
