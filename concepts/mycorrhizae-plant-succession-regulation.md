# Mycorrhizae in Plant Succession and Community Regulation

## Overview

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] play pivotal roles in regulating plant populations and structuring plant communities during [[ecological-succession]]. By enhancing nutrient acquisition, improving plant fitness, and facilitating seedling establishment, mycorrhizae influence which plant species succeed in colonizing disturbed or newly formed habitats. The availability and distribution of mycorrhizal inoculum can determine the trajectory of plant succession and the resulting community composition. Population and community regulation by fungi occurs through both promotion and reduction of growth, fitness, or reproductive potential of organisms, with asymmetric effects among species driving changes in community dominance.

## Mycorrhizae and Primary Succession

### Nutrient Availability as a Successional Determinant

Pedersen and Sylvia (1996) identified nutrient availability as a major component determining the success of early colonizing plants during seral succession. The ability of plants to associate with mycorrhizal fungi enhances their capacity to sequester nutrients from limited resources, providing a competitive advantage in nutrient-poor successional habitats.

### Animal-Mediated Spore Dispersal

Small mammals serve as critical vectors for mycorrhizal [[fungal-spore-dispersal]] during succession. Trappe and Maser (1976) demonstrated that spores of arbuscular mycorrhizal (*Glomus macrocarpus*) and hypogeous ectomycorrhizal (*Hymenogaster*) fungi survive passage through the guts of Oregon voles (*Microtus oregoni*) and chickarees (*Tamiasciurus douglasi*), providing viable inoculum for colonizing bare ground. Similarly, Kotter and Farentinos (1984) showed that ectomycorrhizal spores survive gut passage through tassel-eared squirrels (*Sciurus aberti*) and develop functional mycorrhizal associations with ponderosa pine.

Cazares and Trappe (1994) confirmed that mycophagy of both hypogeous and epigeous mycorrhizal fungi results in deposition of viable spores in feces. The patchy distribution of feces in middens by small mammals accounts for the heterogeneous distribution of mycorrhizal inoculum potential in the environment.

### Case Study: Mount Saint Helens

M.F. Allen (1991) documented that vegetation colonizing Mount Saint Helens after the 1980 eruption consisted entirely of mycorrhizal species, both arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal forms. In contrast, studies in the Galápagos Islands found a mixture of arbuscular mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal plants, with distribution related to soil nutrient content—nonmycorrhizal plants in more fertile lowland soils and mycorrhizal plants establishing in poorer rocky soils.

### Case Study: Lyman Glacier Forefront

On the forefront of Lyman Glacier, spores of various fungal genera appear in feces of pika, voles, chipmunks, marmots, mountain goats, and mule deer, forming an inoculum source for plant colonization of newly developing soils. Jumpponen et al. (1999) identified "safe sites" on glacier outwash—concave surfaces of coarse rocky particles ideal for trapping tree seeds and protecting them from desiccation. These sites also served as foraging foci for small mammals, creating positive feedback loops where seed deposition and mycorrhizal spore deposition co-occurred.

Jumpponen et al. (1998) demonstrated that the dark-septate mycorrhizal fungus *Phialocephala fortinii* significantly enhanced growth of lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*) seedlings on glacier forefront soil, but only with added nitrogen. Total plant phosphorus was significantly enhanced by mycorrhizal colonization even without added nitrogen.

### Microbial Community Succession

During plant succession on glacial till, microbial communities shift from bacterial domination to fungal-dominated communities. This transition changes carbon-use efficiency from high respiration rates to net carbon accumulation, indicating that fungi act as stabilizing forces in developing ecosystems and facilitate net carbon fixation into biomass (Ohtonen et al., 1999).

## Mycorrhizal Species Succession

### Ectomycorrhizal Succession

Studies on the Lyman Glacier forefront identified 68 ectomycorrhizal species belonging to 25 genera across successional stages, with no single species occurring on all three sites. Ectomycorrhizal species diversity increased to maximum where tree canopies began to overlap. This pattern corresponds with findings from other studies (Dighton et al., 1986; Last et al., 1987; Visser, 1995) where increased diversity at canopy closure relates to nutrient paucity (particularly phosphorus) and increasing proportions of organic-bound nutrients.

Dighton and Mason (1985) hypothesized that increased mycorrhizal diversity allows greater expression of mycorrhizal function to utilize mixed available resources. Conn and Dighton (2000) provided partial validation, showing that ectomycorrhizal community composition on different tree litters reflects appropriate enzyme functions relative to nutrient availability. Where phosphorus is immobilized during early litter decomposition, the ectomycorrhizal community contains a greater proportion of acid phosphatase-producing types.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Succession

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal succession on herbaceous plants is less obvious than ectomycorrhizal succession, but different species can have contrasting effects on host performance. Hart et al. (2001) proposed two competing hypotheses:

1. **Driver hypothesis**: Mycorrhizal fungi are the driving force, determining which plant species succeed through interspecific differences in colonization and persistence potential
2. **Passenger hypothesis**: Changes in mycorrhizal communities are dependent on plant community composition and environmental conditions, with mycorrhizae passively responding to these changes

## The Mycelial Network and Ecosystem Stability

### Importance of Continuous Mycelial Mats

Amaranthus and Perry (1989) demonstrated the critical importance of maintaining continuous mycorrhizal mycelial networks for plant establishment after disturbance. When Douglas fir was planted into partially cleared sites where mycorrhizal roots were maintained on remaining trees, approximately 90% of newly planted trees survived. On totally cleared areas, survival after 2 years was only 50%. The existing mycelial network likely provided:

- Greater system stability through carbon and nutrient exchange between connected plants
- Access to larger nutrient and carbon pools for new recruits
- Synergistic activity supporting ecosystem stability and species composition maintenance

### Saprotrophic Influences on Establishment

Even saprotrophic fungi influence plant establishment. Inoculation of *Gmelina arborea* seeds with *Chaetomium bostrychodes* improved germination, likely through enzyme production that assists in seed stratification or scarification processes (Osonubi et al., 1990).

## Allen and Allen's Successional Model

Allen and Allen (1990) hypothesized patterns of mycorrhizal dependence in developing ecosystems in relation to nutrient and water availability. Their model proposes that:

- In nutrient-poor, water-limited early successional habitats, mycorrhizal dependence is high
- As nutrient availability increases through soil development, the advantage of mycorrhizal association may shift
- The pattern of mycorrhizal dependence regulates plant competition throughout succession

However, studies on Mount Koma, Japan (Titus and Tsuyuzaki, 2002) showed that these patterns are also plant species-dependent, not solely determined by environmental factors. *Agrostis scabra* showed no microsite effect on arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization, while *Campanula lasiocarpa* had higher colonization near rock surfaces, and willow (*Salix reinii*) was heavily ectomycorrhizal across all sites.
