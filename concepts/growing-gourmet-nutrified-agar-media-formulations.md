# Nutrified Agar Media Formulations

Nutrified agar media form the foundation of every mushroom cultivation laboratory. Agar, a seaweed-derived compound, gelatinizes water and provides a semi-solid surface on which mushroom mycelium grows. By adding nutrients, cultivators create a medium that supports vigorous, healthy mycelial expansion from spores, tissue clones, and stock cultures.

## Primary Agar Media Types

Four agar formulations are most commonly recommended for gourmet and medicinal mushroom cultivation:

### Malt Extract Agar (MEA / MYA)

The workhorse of mycology laboratories. Fortified with yeast and optionally peptone:

- 1000 ml water
- 20 g agar agar
- 20 g barley malt sugar
- 2 g nutritional yeast
- 1 g peptone (optional, soybean-derived)

With peptone added, this medium is designated MYPA. This formulation yields the greatest success across the widest range of species described in the literature.

### Potato Dextrose Yeast Agar (PDYA)

Uses potato broth as the carbohydrate base:

- 1000 ml water (from boiling 300 g potatoes in 2-3 liters water for 1 hour)
- 20 g agar agar
- 10 g dextrose
- 2 g yeast
- 1 g peptone (optional)

Only the broth from boiling is retained; the potatoes themselves are discarded. Total volume should equal 1 liter after concentration.

### Oatmeal Malt Yeast Agar (OMYA)

A richer medium well-suited for demanding species:

- 1000 ml water
- 80 g instant oatmeal
- 20 g agar agar
- 10 g malt sugar
- 2 g yeast

The oatmeal does not require filtering, though some cultivators prefer to do so for a cleaner appearance.

### Dog Food Agar (DFA)

Pioneered by the late Dr. Steven Pollock:

- 1000 ml water
- 20 g dry dog food
- 20 g agar agar

This unconventional medium can support species that struggle on standard formulations. The protein and fat content of commercial dog food provides a broad spectrum of nutrients.

### Corn Meal Yeast Glucose Agar (CMYA)

Widely used by mycological laboratories for long-term culture storage:

- 1000 ml water
- 20 g agar agar
- 10 g cornmeal
- 5 g malt or glucose
- 1 g yeast

CMYA is not as nutritious as the other formulations described above, making it suitable for maintaining slow-growing stock cultures without encouraging excessive vegetative expansion.

## pH Considerations

After sterilization, these media formulations naturally settle to a pH of 5.5-6.8. Saprophytic mushroom mycelium generally prefers slightly acidic conditions, so near-neutral or slightly basic formulations work well since the mycelium will acidify the medium as it grows. Media designed for most saprophytes should be adjusted to pH 5.5-6.5. Many contaminant molds are strong acidophiles, so maintaining a near-neutral pH can favor mushroom mycelium over competitors. If the culture is mixed with mold contaminants, adjusting the pH toward neutral can selectively favor mushroom mycelium over acid-loving molds.

## Media Supplements

Strains grown repeatedly on mono-specific media risk limiting their repertoire of digestive enzymes. To prevent media-specific adaptation, the following supplements can be added to 1 liter of MEA or PDA:

**Nitrogen and carbohydrate supplements:**
- 2 g yeast or 1-2 g peptone
- 2 g oatmeal or oat bran
- 2 g rye or wheat flour
- 1 g soybean meal
- 1 g spirulina
- 2 g high-quality dry dog food

**End-substrate supplements (3-5 g per liter):**
- Sawdust, powdered straw, or sugar cane bagasse

Adding end-substrate components significantly reduces the "lag period" when mycelium encounters unfamiliar substrate materials later in cultivation. This simple step accelerates colonization and shortens the time to fruiting. Only 1-3 g of substrate per liter is recommended.

## Sugar Selection

Complex carbohydrate sources are strongly preferred over simple sugars. Cornsteep fermentative broth, cooked potatoes, wood extracts, and barley malt provide not only sugars but also minerals, vitamins, and salts that promote healthy growth. Simple sugars, while supporting initial growth, promote mutation factors and senescence over time. Strains cannot be maintained long-term on simple sugar media without losing vitality.

## Sterilization Procedure

1. Mix dry components together in a flask
2. Add 1 liter of water (well water, spring water, or mineral water preferred; chlorinated water is not recommended)
3. Stop the flask with non-absorbent cotton or loosely tighten the screw cap
4. Wrap in aluminum foil
5. Sterilize for 45 minutes at 15 psi (250°F)
6. Pressure cookers that do not release pressure during the cycle are ideal

**Critical precaution:** As the media cools, outside air is drawn into the pressure cooker. If this air carries contaminant spores, the media can contaminate before the cultivator handles it. Drape an alcohol-saturated paper towel over the air intake point. Wait 20 minutes after heat is off before handling.

## Pouring Technique

One liter of agar medium pours 20-40 petri dishes (100 x 15 mm), depending on pour depth. Before pouring, the work surface should be thoroughly wiped with 80% isopropanol. Plastic petri dishes usually come pre-sterilized. Glass dishes should be sterilized simultaneously in a petri dish rack.

**Avoid pre-pouring media into glass dishes before sterilization** — movement during the cycle causes media separation and spills. If cooling overnight in the pressure cooker, ensure either a vacuum forms or the cooker is placed in a HEPA-filtered airstream.

## Water Quality

Well water, spring water, and mineral water all produce good results. Chlorinated tap water is not recommended. Distilled water is unnecessary. The minerals naturally present in spring water may actually benefit mycelial growth.

## Agar Quality and Substitutes

Only high-grade agar should be used. Various agars differ substantially in their gelatinizing ability (minimum 10 g per 1000 ml water), mineral and salt content, and endemic populations of microorganisms including bacteria. Bacteria surviving in the agar can de-gelatinize the media, ruining entire batches. Pollution has increasingly affected the refinement of tissue-grade agar, driving prices upward.

Agar substitutes such as Gelrite are widely used by the plant tissue culture industry. However, these substitutes do not produce media firm enough for most mushroom cultivators. Plant tissue culturists seek a softer, gelatinous form for three-dimensional root growth, whereas mushroom cultivators need a semi-solid, firm surface upon which mycelium can spread in a two-dimensional plane.

## Cloning on Agar

Agar media serve as the platform for capturing mushroom phenotypes through tissue cloning. The best clone candidates are young mushrooms in "button" form, where cell division is most active. Two locations yield the highest success rates: the area directly above the gills (rich in basidia and genetic material), and the interior tissue at the base of the stem. The stem base, being in contact with the ground, is often the entry point for larvae carrying contaminants, making the gill area preferable.

By cloning, cultivators capture the exact genetic expression (phenotype) of a wild or cultivated mushroom. Under proper cultural conditions and barring mutation, the same features are expressed in subsequently grown mushrooms. This is in contrast to spore germination, which produces novel genetic combinations and unpredictable results.

## Purifying Cultures on Agar

Several techniques exist for cleaning contaminated cultures on agar media:

- **Antibiotic sandwich method:** For bacterially infested cultures, the mycelium is sandwiched between two layers of media containing gentamycin sulfate. Filamentous hyphae push through while bacteria are left behind.
- **pH adjustment:** If mold contaminants are present, adjusting the pH toward neutral can favor mushroom mycelium since many contaminant molds are acidophiles.
- **Organic barrier race:** Glass tubes filled with sterilized chopped straw, sawdust, or crushed corncobs create a selective medium. The contaminated culture is introduced at one end, and the fastest-growing mycelium (ideally the mushroom) reaches the opposite end first for transfer to fresh media.
- **Repeated subculturing:** Immediate subculture of all visible growth points disperses colonies so they can be dealt with individually.

The easiest approach is prevention: implementing proper sterile technique when initially making cultures so that "running away" from contamination is unnecessary.
