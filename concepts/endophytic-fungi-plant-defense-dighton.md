## Grass Endophytes (Clavicipitaceous)

### Neotyphodium/Epichloë Associations
The most studied endophyte-plant associations are those between clavicipitaceous fungi (*Neotyphodium* and *Epichloë* species) and cool-season grasses. These systemic endophytes:
- Are transmitted vertically through grass seeds
- Occupy the intercellular spaces of leaf sheaths, stems, and inflorescences
- Produce a range of bioactive alkaloids that deter herbivores

### Alkaloid Production and Defense
Endophytic grass fungi produce several classes of defensive alkaloids:

**Ergot alkaloids** — primarily ergovaline, cause vasoconstriction and reduced blood flow in grazing mammals, leading to conditions like fescue toxicosis in cattle grazing endophyte-infected tall fescue.

**Lolitrems** — cause tremors and stagger syndrome (ryegrass staggers) in sheep and cattle grazing perennial ryegrass infected with *Neotyphodium lolii*.

**Pyrrolizidines** — loline alkaloids (N-formylloline, N-acetylloline, N-acetylnorloline) that act as feeding deterrents and insecticidal compounds against a range of invertebrate herbivores.

**Peramine** — a pyrrolopyrazine alkaloid that specifically deters aphid feeding without affecting mammalian herbivores.

### Drought Tolerance
Belesky and Malinowski (2000) demonstrated that grass endophytes confer drought tolerance through loline alkaloid production, which acts as an osmoregulator. However, Cheplick et al. (2000) found no benefit of endophytes in *Lolium perenne* for drought tolerance — in fact, growth under both droughty and normal conditions was lower with the endophyte present. This suggests the effect of fungal endophytes is dependent upon a variety of environmental conditions and is not universally beneficial.

### Economic Significance
The tension between endophyte benefits (insect resistance, drought tolerance) and costs (mammalian toxicity) has led to the development of "novel endophyte" strains that produce peramine and lolines for [[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]] functions

## Saprotrophic Fungi as Plant Growth Promoters

Some saprotrophic fungi can functionally act as endophytes when applied to seeds or seedlings:

### Chaetomium bostrychoides
Osonubi et al. (1990) demonstrated that inoculation of *Gmelina arborea* (a Nigerian pulpwood tree) seeds or seedlings with the saprotrophic fungus *Chaetomium bostrychoides*:
- Increased seed germination
- Dramatically increased drought tolerance

After a drought event, inoculated seedlings showed:
- Shoot biomass: 9.36 g (seed inoculation) vs 5.89 g (uninoculated) after drought
- Root biomass: 4.47 g (seed inoculation) vs 2.65 g (uninoculated) after drought
- Root:shoot ratios shifted to favor greater root investment

This demonstrates that the boundary between endophyte, mycorrhizal partner, and saprotrophic growth promoter is not always clear-cut, and that saprotrophic fungi can provide significant benefits to plant hosts.
