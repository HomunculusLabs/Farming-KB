## Endorhiza Microbiome

The endorhiza microbiome refers to the community of microorganisms—primarily bacteria—that colonize the internal tissues of plant roots. Unlike rhizosphere microbes, which inhabit the soil zone immediately surrounding the root surface, endorhiza organisms live within the root cortex and vascular tissues themselves. These endophytic communities play critical roles in plant health, nutrient acquisition, and defense against pathogens.

## Definition and Scope

The term "endorhiza" specifically denotes the microbial community found inside root tissue, as distinguished from the rhizosphere (soil adhering to roots) and the rhizoplane (root surface). Bacteria that have colonized root tissue are known as endophytes when they do not cause disease. The endorhiza represents the most intimate plant-microbe association in the root zone, with direct physical contact between microbial cells and plant cells.

In the Cannabis microbiome literature, endorhiza samples are obtained by removing roots from soil, rinsing thoroughly with alcohol and sterile water to eliminate surface contaminants, and then extracting DNA from the internal root tissue. This rigorous surface sterilization ensures that only truly endophytic organisms are detected.

## Composition and Structure

### Core Endorhiza Community

Across Cannabis cultivars, a consistent core microbiome has been identified in the endorhiza. This core includes several well-known endophytic bacterial groups:

- **Pseudomonas**: Among the most extensively studied plant-associated bacteria. Species in this genus produce siderophores, antibiotics, and growth-promoting hormones. Many Pseudomonas strains are also biocontrol agents against soil-borne pathogens.
- **Cellvibrio**: An aerobic cellulolytic bacterium, notable for its ability to degrade plant cell wall components. Its presence in the endorhiza may relate to nutrient acquisition from root cell wall turnover.
- **Oxalobacteraceae**: A family within Betaproteobacteria that includes several plant-associated genera. Members are commonly isolated from the rhizosphere of various crop plants.
- **Xanthomonadaceae**: A family of Gram-negative bacteria that includes both plant pathogens and non-pathogenic endophytes. Their consistent presence in the Cannabis endorhiza suggests a non-pathogenic role.
- **Actinomycetales**: An order that includes Streptomyces and related genera, known for producing antibiotics and other bioactive compounds. These filamentous bacteria can colonize root tissues extensively.
- **Sphingobacteriales**: An order of environmental bacteria commonly found in soil and water. Their role in the endorhiza is less well characterized but their consistent presence suggests functional importance.

With the exception of Cellvibrio, all prevalent members of the core endorhiza community are well-known endophytic bacteria primarily within the orders Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria, consistent with observations from other plant systems.

### Taxonomic Shifts from Soil to Endorhiza

Several notable taxonomic changes occur as microbial communities transition from bulk soil through the rhizosphere and into the endorhiza:

- **Acidobacteria** show dramatic reduction in the endorhiza compared to bulk soil. These oligotrophic organisms are adapted to nutrient-poor conditions and are outcompeted in the carbon-rich root interior.
- **Proteobacteria** generally increase in relative abundance in the endorhiza, particularly Alpha- and Gammaproteobacteria, which include many known endophytic genera.
- **Firmicutes** may show variable patterns depending on the host species and growing conditions.

## Cultivar-Specificity

One of the most significant findings about the endorhiza microbiome is its strong cultivar-specificity. When soil type is controlled, different plant cultivars harbor significantly different endorhiza communities. In Cannabis studies, this effect was statistically significant using both [[weighted-vs-unweighted-unifrac-cannabis-strain-microbiome]] (ADONIS R² = 0.39, p = 0.003) distance metrics.

This cultivar-specificity is thought to arise from differences in root exudate profiles, immune recognition systems, and root architecture between cultivars. Each cultivar creates a unique internal environment that selects for a specific subset of the available rhizosphere bacteria.

### The Methylophilus Example

A striking illustration of cultivar-specificity comes from the genus Methylophilus, which showed dramatic differences between Cannabis cultivars:

- **Bookoo Kush**: Methylophilus comprised 13% of the endorhiza community
- **Burmese**: Methylophilus comprised only 0.13% of the endorhiza community
- **[[sour-diesel]]**: Methylophilus was completely absent from the endorhiza

This 100-fold difference between Bookoo Kush and Burmese suggests a highly specific interaction between this methylotrophic bacterium and the root environment of certain cultivars, possibly related to methanol production from pectin demethylation in root cell walls.

## Functional Roles

Endorhiza bacteria provide numerous benefits to their host plants:

### Plant Growth Promotion
Endorhiza bacteria support plant growth through several mechanisms including the production of phytohormones such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), cytokinins, and gibberellins that directly stimulate root and shoot development. They also produce low molecular weight compounds and enzymes involved in regulating plant growth and metabolism.

### Nutrient Acquisition
Endophytic bacteria enhance nutrient availability through nitrogen fixation, phosphate solubilization, and siderophore production. By converting atmospheric nitrogen to plant-available forms or solubilizing mineral phosphates, these organisms improve plant nutrition beyond what the soil alone can provide.

### Disease Suppression
Endorhiza communities suppress plant diseases through multiple mechanisms including competition for niches and nutrients, production of [[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]] in the host plant, and direct parasitism of pathogenic organisms. The presence of Pseudomonas and Actinomycetales in the core endorhiza is particularly relevant for disease suppression.

### Stress Tolerance
Endorhiza bacteria assist their host plants in tolerating abiotic stresses including drought, salinity, and heavy metal toxicity. They also help plants tolerate phytotoxic effects of environmental contaminants such as pesticides and organic pollutants.

### Terroir and Quality
Both endophytes and epiphytes may play a role in localized flavor or terroir for crop plants, as has been demonstrated for wine grapes. In Cannabis, the endorhiza community could potentially influence the terpene and cannabinoid profiles through metabolic interactions with the host plant, though this remains an active area of investigation.

## Ecological Dynamics

The endorhiza microbiome is not static but changes over time and in response to environmental conditions. Factors that influence endorhiza community dynamics include plant developmental stage, seasonal and diel temperature changes, water content fluctuations, soil pH, CO₂ concentration, and O₂ levels in the root zone.

Understanding these dynamics is important for developing strategies to manipulate the endorhiza for agricultural benefit, as the optimal community at one growth stage may differ from that at another. Longitudinal studies tracking the same plants over their full life cycle are needed to fully characterize these temporal patterns and identify windows of opportunity for microbiome intervention.

## Sampling and Methodology

Studying the endorhiza requires careful methodology to avoid contamination from rhizosphere or surface organisms. Standard protocols include:

1. Careful root excavation to minimize soil disturbance during sampling
2. Removal of loosely adhering soil by gentle shaking into sterile collection bags
3. Surface sterilization with ethanol (typically 70%) followed by multiple sterile water rinses
4. DNA extraction from internal root tissue (typically 0.25 g per sample) using commercial soil DNA kits
5. PCR amplification of marker genes (typically 16S rRNA V4 region) with barcoded primers
6. Sequencing and bioinformatic analysis with appropriate quality filtering using pipelines such as QIIME

The heating modification (65°C for 10 minutes prior to initial vortex) during DNA extraction has been shown to improve lysis of tough bacterial cell walls, yielding more complete community representation from root tissue samples.

## Comparison with Rhizosphere Microbiome

The endorhiza and rhizosphere microbiomes differ in several fundamental ways that reflect their distinct ecological niches:

| Feature | Rhizosphere | Endorhiza |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| Location | Soil adhering to root surface | Inside root tissue |
| Nutrient availability | Moderate (root exudates diluted in soil) | High (direct access to plant metabolites) |
| Community drivers | Primarily soil type ([[cannabis-microbiome-cultivar-specificity]] | Generally weak | Often strong |
| Sampling complexity | Lower (soil collection) | Higher (surface sterilization required) |

The rhizosphere serves as a transitional zone between bulk soil and the endorhiza, with community characteristics that are intermediate between these two compartments. In statistical analyses, rhizosphere communities often fail to cluster significantly by either soil type or cultivar, reflecting their dual influence.

## Future Directions

Research on the endorhiza microbiome is rapidly expanding, with several key areas of active investigation:

- **Functional metagenomics**: Moving beyond taxonomic surveys to understand what genes and metabolic pathways are present and active in the endorhiza.
- **Synthetic communities**: Constructing defined microbial consortia that can be inoculated to achieve specific functional outcomes such as disease suppression or growth promotion.
- **Host genetic control**: Identifying specific plant genes and pathways that control endorhiza community assembly, enabling breeding for beneficial microbiomes.
- **Cross-kingdom interactions**: Understanding how bacterial endorhiza communities interact with fungal endophytes and mycorrhizal partners within the same root system.
