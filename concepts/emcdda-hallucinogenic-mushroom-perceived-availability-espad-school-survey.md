# ESPAD School Survey: Perceived Availability of Magic Mushrooms

The European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs (ESPAD) provides standardized data on substance use and perceived availability among 15 to 16 year old school students across EU Member States and candidate countries. The 2003 ESPAD survey, conducted in 22 EU Member States plus Norway and four candidate countries (Croatia, Bulgaria, Turkey, and Romania), included questions about perceived ease of obtaining hallucinogenic mushrooms.

## Perceived Availability Rates

The ESPAD surveys reported that rates of students who perceived hallucinogenic mushrooms as "very" or "fairly easy" to obtain ranged from 4% to 28% across participating countries. This wide variation reflects differences in actual market availability, legal frameworks, cultural attitudes, and geographic proximity to natural growing habitats.

### Low Availability Countries (Under 10%)

Several countries reported perceived availability below 10% among school students:

- **Cyprus**: Among the lowest rates, consistent with its zero reported lifetime prevalence of mushroom use.
- **Finland**: Low perceived availability, also reporting zero lifetime prevalence.
- **Greece**: Limited retail infrastructure for hallucinogenic mushrooms.
- **Hungary**: Despite having legal controls, market penetration among adolescents remained low.
- **Latvia**: Low availability reflecting limited distribution networks in the Baltic region.
- **Lithuania**: Among the lowest prevalence and availability estimates in the EU.
- **Romania**: Zero reported lifetime prevalence and correspondingly low perceived availability.
- **Turkey**: Minimal exposure to hallucinogenic mushroom markets.

### High Availability Countries (Over 20%)

Five countries reported perceived availability above 20% among 15 to 16 year old students:

- **Czech Republic**: Consistently one of the highest prevalence and availability countries in the EU, reflecting a well-established market and cultural acceptance of hallucinogenic mushrooms.
- **Ireland**: Despite later prohibition, perceived availability was high during the survey period, consistent with reports of seasonal wild mushroom consumption in regions like Kilkenny.
- **Italy**: Substantial perceived access among adolescents, though actual prevalence data suggested more moderate use.
- **Poland**: High availability consistent with reports of young people actively hunting wild Psilocybe semilanceata in southern Poland.
- **United Kingdom**: High perceived availability during the pre-prohibition period when approximately 300 shops and market stalls sold mushroom products.

### The Netherlands Anomaly

The Netherlands presented a notable anomaly. Despite the absence of legal sanctions controlling the sale of fresh hallucinogenic mushrooms (prior to the 2002 Supreme Court ruling on prepared products), only 16% of Dutch school students reported easy access to hallucinogenic mushrooms. This relatively low figure may reflect the concentration of mushroom sales in smartshops and coffee shops that cater primarily to adult rather than adolescent populations, the existence of age restrictions at point of sale, or cultural norms that discourage adolescent experimentation relative to other countries with similar legal environments.

## Relationship Between Perceived Availability and Prevalence

Levels of perceived availability generally mirror estimates for prevalence, but at consistently higher levels. This pattern is expected and consistent with substance use research more broadly: the proportion of young people who believe they could obtain a substance if they wanted to exceeds the proportion who have actually used it. The gap between perceived availability and actual use reflects individual choice, social norms, risk perception, and access to alternative substances.

The correlation between perceived availability and prevalence was particularly strong for hallucinogenic mushrooms, suggesting that market access was a primary driver of use patterns in this period. Countries with established retail markets (Netherlands, UK, Czech Republic) showed both higher availability and higher prevalence, while countries with limited retail infrastructure showed correspondingly lower figures on both measures.

## Comparison with Other Substances

While specific comparative data for perceived availability of other substances from the same ESPAD survey were not highlighted in the EMCDDA case study, the general pattern of hallucinogenic mushroom availability tracking below that of cannabis and broadly similar to or below that of ecstasy is consistent with their respective market positions during this period. Cannabis, with its established and widespread distribution networks, was consistently perceived as the most easily available illegal substance across all participating countries.

## Impact of Legal Changes on Perceived Availability

The UK Mixmag survey conducted in 2005, shortly after the July 2005 prohibition, found that 67% of respondents reported mushrooms were less available than previously. While this data comes from a clubbing population rather than school students, it demonstrates that legislative changes can produce rapid and significant shifts in perceived market access. Similar effects would be expected in school populations, though the ESPAD survey cycle (conducted every four years) meant that the impact of the 2005 prohibition on adolescent perceived availability would not be captured until the 2007 survey.

## Gender Differences in Perceived Availability

ESPAD data on hallucinogenic mushroom use revealed consistent gender differences across the 11 EU Member States for which gender-disaggregated data were available (Finland, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Lithuania, Malta, Slovakia, Slovenia, Sweden, and the United Kingdom, together with Norway and Croatia). Following the common gender distribution patterns observed for illegal drug use, male students generally had higher prevalence rates than female students.

This gender gap was particularly pronounced among students who had used other hallucinogens or ecstasy. Among male students who had used LSD or other hallucinogens, lifetime prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use exceeded 40%, while the corresponding figure for females was somewhat lower. The gender disparity was less pronounced among students who had only used cannabis, suggesting that the factors driving male-female differences in hallucinogenic mushroom use were related to the broader pattern of gendered drug experimentation rather than to mushroom-specific factors.

## Foraging as an Availability Channel

Perceived availability was not solely a function of retail market presence. In countries where hallucinogenic mushrooms grew wild, foraging represented an independent availability channel that could inflate perceived access even in the absence of formal retail markets. Evidence from Poland, Ireland, and the Czech Republic indicated that users actively hunted for wild-growing magic mushrooms, particularly Psilocybe semilanceata.

In Poland, residents in local communities and shepherds in the south of the country observed young people aged 15 to 19 seeking magic mushrooms in wild habitats. In Ireland, representatives of local communities in the Kilkenny region reported that mushrooms were "popular when in season," suggesting seasonal patterns of wild consumption. In the Czech Republic, consumption of locally grown hallucinogenic mushrooms was also reported.

These foraging activities represent a distinct supply channel that operates independently of both retail and online markets. They also carry additional risks, as hallucinogenic mushrooms can be easily mistaken for non-psychoactive, inedible, or poisonous species. The primary distinguishable feature of most psilocybin-containing mushrooms is that they bruise blue when handled, but this identification feature may not be sufficient to prevent misidentification by inexperienced foragers.

## ESPAD Lifetime Prevalence by Country

The 2003 ESPAD survey found that lifetime prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use among 15 to 16 year old students ranged from 0% to 8% across 27 participating countries. The Czech Republic, Netherlands, France, and Belgium had the highest prevalence estimates. Three countries, Cyprus, Finland, and Romania, reported zero lifetime prevalence.

A notable finding was that ever-in-lifetime use of hallucinogenic mushrooms was equal to or higher than lifetime use of ecstasy in nine countries. This parity was significant because ecstasy was at that time a well-established recreational drug with mature distribution networks, while hallucinogenic mushrooms were considered an emerging trend. The equivalence suggests that mushrooms had achieved substantial market penetration among adolescent populations in several EU countries by 2003.

## Polydrug Use and Availability

Analysis of ESPAD data from 13 European countries demonstrated that school students aged 15 to 16 were much more likely to have used hallucinogenic mushrooms if they had used LSD or other hallucinogens, ecstasy, amphetamines, or cocaine than if they had used only cannabis or legal substances. This pattern indicates that perceived and actual availability of hallucinogenic mushrooms was closely linked to participation in broader illicit drug markets.

Students who had progressed to stimulant or hallucinogen use beyond cannabis existed within social networks where access to multiple substances was available. In this context, the availability of hallucinogenic mushrooms was a function of the overall richness of the local illicit drug market rather than of mushroom-specific retail infrastructure.

## Relationship to Actual Use Patterns

The gap between perceived availability and actual use provides insight into the factors that mediate between market access and consumption. Even in countries where perceived availability was high, actual lifetime prevalence among school students rarely exceeded 8%. This suggests that the majority of students who perceived easy access to hallucinogenic mushrooms chose not to use them, reflecting the role of personal attitudes, risk perceptions, social norms, and the availability of alternative substances in mediating drug use decisions.

The relatively low conversion rate from perceived availability to actual use may also reflect the specific characteristics of hallucinogenic mushrooms as a drug class. Their unpredictable potency, the risk of adverse psychological reactions, and the absence of socially reinforcing effects all serve as deterrents even among populations with ready access to the product.

## Czech Republic Loophole Exploitation

The Czech Republic presented an interesting case of retailer creativity in exploiting legal ambiguities. A newspaper reported that a shop in Prague was attempting to sell hallucinogenic mushrooms under the label of "aromatic and decorative goods." The mushrooms were sold with a leaflet warning customers not to ingest them, thus technically evading national legislation that prohibited the sale of mushrooms for human consumption. This strategy mirrored approaches used in other jurisdictions where retailers leveraged the gap between the legal status of a substance and the practical difficulty of proving intent to consume.

## General Population Prevalence Context

The ESPAD perceived availability data should be understood within the broader context of general population prevalence data from 12 EU Member States. Among young people aged 15 to 24, lifetime prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use ranged from less than 1% to 8%. The Netherlands, Czech Republic, UK, Germany, and Ireland had the highest prevalence estimates, while the lowest were reported in Lithuania, Hungary, and France.

The proportion of current users (those who had used during the last month) among those who had ever used was lower for hallucinogenic mushrooms than for cannabis and ecstasy. This pattern reflects the experimental nature of most hallucinogenic mushroom use and the self-limiting character of the experience. The effects of hallucinogenic mushrooms, including unpredictable potency and negative experiences such as nausea and anxiety, limit the appeal of regular use and contribute to natural attrition in user populations over time.

## Impact of Internet on Perceived Availability

The internet played a significant role in shaping perceived availability of hallucinogenic mushrooms among young people. Since the late 1990s, extensive internet repositories of information on hallucinogenic mushrooms emerged, contributing to the accessibility of information on mushroom identification, cultivation, effects, and user experiences. Websites such as Erowid, the Shroomery, and numerous country-specific forums provided young people with detailed knowledge about how to obtain and use hallucinogenic mushrooms, even in countries where formal retail markets were absent.

The internet also facilitated direct access to mushroom products through online shops, with the vast majority (82%) of identified online retailers based in the Netherlands. These shops offered international shipping and multiple language versions (primarily English, French, and German) to attract foreign customers. The Free Spore Ring Europe, a non-profit organization, offered spore prints for approximately 1 euro per print, making home cultivation accessible even to young people with limited financial resources.

The combination of readily available information and low-cost access to cultivation materials meant that perceived availability could be high even in countries with no domestic retail market for hallucinogenic mushrooms. This internet-driven availability channel operated independently of both physical retail outlets and wild foraging, creating a third supply pathway that was difficult to regulate through traditional drug control measures.

## Methodological Considerations

Perceived availability data from school surveys are subject to several limitations. The measure captures subjective perception rather than objective market conditions, and adolescents may overestimate or underestimate their access depending on social desirability, peer influence, or limited understanding of actual market dynamics. The school-based sampling frame excludes adolescents who are not enrolled in school, who may have different substance use patterns and access. Cross-country comparisons are complicated by potential differences in question interpretation and cultural norms regarding disclosure of sensitive information.

Despite these limitations, perceived availability data provide a valuable complement to prevalence data, offering insight into the market environment that shapes adolescent substance use decisions.
