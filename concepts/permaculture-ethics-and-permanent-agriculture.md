# Permaculture Ethics and Permanent Agriculture

## Overview

[[permaculture-ethics]] arise from enlightened self-interest: the recognition that human survival depends on the health of all natural systems. By expanding the concept of survival beyond the individual to include species and ecosystem health, [[permaculture]] establishes a philosophical foundation for sustainable land use and community design.

## The Evolution of an Earthcare Ethic

The ethical framework of permaculture develops through several stages of reasoning:

1. **Observation of dependence**: Humans recognize that their survival depends on the health of forests, soils, water systems, and biological diversity. When plant and animal species are lost due to human actions, survival opportunities diminish.

2. **Cooperation as a natural model**: Nature demonstrates that cooperative species and self-supporting associations (such as [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] on tree roots) create healthy, resilient communities. This observation leads to a resolve to cooperate and take support roles within society.

3. **Expansion of people-care to species-care**: Initially, helping family and friends is understood as aiding one's own survival. Over time, this ethic matures to encompass all humankind and, ultimately, all life — recognizing common origins.

4. **Ethical action**: Having evolved these ethics, the practitioner then devises ways to apply them to gardens, land, economies, and community structures.

## Three Historical Models of Permanent Agriculture

Mollison identifies three historical approaches to permanent agriculture, each with distinct social and ecological characteristics:

### 1. Feudal Permanence (Peasant Agriculture)

Practiced historically in old China as described by F.H. King, this approach involved hauling nutrients from canals, cesspits, pathways, and forests to sustain annual grain cultures. People were bound to the landscape through unremitting toil, serving a state or landlord. This model inevitably leads to famine and revolution due to its extractive nature and the social pressures it creates.

### 2. Baronial Permanence (Large-Scale Grazing)

Found on permanent pastures of prairie, pampas, and modern western farms, this model features large holdings with few people managing vast grazing leases, typically for a single species of animal. Once mechanized, these systems destroy whole landscapes and soil complexes. They operate at the lowest possible level of land use — pasture or cropland — which Mollison identifies as the least productive use of land conceivable. These are effectively "agricultural deserts."

### 3. Communal Permanence (Forest-Based Systems)

Forests represent a permanent agriculture requiring generations of care and knowledge, and thus a tribal or communal reverence found only in stable communities. This is the model Mollison advocates: the ability to plant a pecan or citrus tree when old, knowing it will not be cut down by one's children's children. The further a society departs from communal permanence, the greater the risk of tyranny, feudalism, and revolution — and the more work required for less yield.

## The Energy Dependency Problem

The defining characteristic that separates all permanent agricultures from modern conventional agriculture is energy self-sufficiency. In permanent systems, the energy needs of the system are provided by that system itself. Modern crop agriculture is totally dependent on external energy inputs — hence the vulnerability to oil supply disruptions and associated pollution.

Mollison's Figure 1.1 illustrates a transition from conventional agriculture to permaculture over a period of 3–8 years, tracking multiple accounting dimensions:

- **Financial**: Farm income versus production costs (excess cost over income represents subsidies; farm profits in conventional systems are achieved only through subsidy)
- **Energy**: Oil/calories as machinery, fuels, fertilizers, biocides — starting at a 10:1 loss ratio in conventional farming, potentially reaching a 1:120 gain in permaculture with firewood and fuels
- **Environmental**: Soil loss, water efficiency, pollution levels
- **Social**: Employment, food quality, human health, life quality

## The Transition Pathway

Basic changes in the transition from conventional agriculture to permaculture include:

- Replacing animal forage grains with tree crops
- Increasing forest cover on the property
- Adopting low-tillage or no-tillage methods on remaining croplands
- Retrofitting the house for energy conservation
- Producing some or all fuel on the farm

The real risk Mollison identifies is that the needs of people working "on the ground" — the inhabitants — are overthrown by the needs (or greeds) of commerce and centralized power. The forest is cut for warships or newspaper, and people are reduced to serfs in a barren landscape. This has been the fate of peasant Europe, Ireland, and much of the third world.

## Key Principle: Self-Reliance as Political Act

The assertion that people should "go home and garden" rather than participate in broadscale mechanized agriculture is presented not as a retreat from the world but as a solution to systemic problems. By meeting needs from existing settlements — producing food, catching water, generating fuel locally — vast areas of the globe can be freed for the rehabilitation of natural systems.

Without permanent agriculture, there is no possibility of a stable social order. The move from productive permanent systems (where land is held in common) to annual commercial agricultures (where land is a commodity) involves departure from a low-energy to a high-energy society, exploitative land use, and dependence on external energy resources largely provided by the third world.
