# Madeira and the Origin of Commercial Sugar Slavery

The commercial sugar plantation — the institution that would eventually
drive the transatlantic slave trade and reshape the economies of three
continents — began not in the Caribbean or Brazil but on a small
volcanic island in the eastern Atlantic. Madeira, colonized by Portugal
in the early fifteenth century, was the laboratory where the lethal
combination of sugar, slavery, and colonial extraction was first
assembled into a self-sustaining system.

## Prince Henry the Navigator

In 1432, Prince Henry the Navigator of Portugal established the first
commercial cane sugar plantation on Madeira. Henry was more manager and
entrepreneur than explorer — he organized and financed voyages of
discovery from his base at Sagres on the Portuguese coast, but rarely
sailed himself. His genius lay in recognizing the economic potential of
the Atlantic islands and in creating the institutional framework for
their exploitation.

Plantings of sugar were made in Portugal's other eastern Atlantic
holdings — the Azores, the Cape Verde islands, São Tomé — more than
sixty years before there was any European contact with the New World.
The model developed on these islands would be exported, with horrific
efficiency, to the Americas.

## The First Plantation Workers

The initial labor force on the Madeiran plantations was drawn not from
Africa but from Europe itself. More than a thousand men — including
debtors, convicts, and unconverted Jews — were taken from Europe to
work in the sugar operations. Their condition was one of quasi-servitude,
somewhat akin to the penal colonists and indentured servants who later
populated Australia and some Middle Atlantic American colonies.

This European labor force proved insufficient for the demands of sugar
cultivation. The work was brutal — cutting, crushing, and boiling cane
in tropical heat — and the death rate was staggering. The Portuguese
would soon look elsewhere for a more "renewable" source of labor.

## The Beginning of African Enslavement

In 1443, one of Prince Henry's returning captains brought news of a
capture at sea of a crew of black Arabs and Muslims. These men, who
were of mixed Arab-Negro parentage, claimed that they were of a proud
race and unfit to be bondsmen. They argued that there were in the
hinterland of Africa many "heathen blacks, the children of Ham," who
made excellent slaves, and who could be enslaved in exchange for their
freedom.

This exchange, as recounted by Henry Hobhouse in *Seeds of Change*, marks
the beginning of the modern slave trade — not the transatlantic trade,
which was yet to come, but its precursor: the trade between Africa and
southern Europe. The irony is sharp: captured people bargaining for
their own freedom by directing their captors toward other, more
vulnerable populations.

## A New Kind of Slavery

McKenna emphasizes that sugar slavery was qualitatively different from
anything that had preceded it. It was the first time since the Roman
latifundia that mass slavery had been used to grow a crop for trade
rather than subsistence. This distinction is crucial: the purpose of
sugar cultivation was not to feed people but to generate profit, and
the scale of the operation demanded a correspondingly massive and
disposable labor force.

Even more significantly, it was the first time in history that one race
had been uniquely selected for a servile role. Spain and Portugal
voluntarily abjured the enslavement of East Indian, Chinese, Japanese,
or European slaves to work in the Americas. The racial dimension of
New World slavery was not incidental but foundational — it was an
economic choice dressed in the language of racial hierarchy.

## Sugar Reaches the New World

Sugarcane was the first crop to be introduced into commercial
cultivation in the New World. By 1530, less than forty years after the
initial European contact, there were more than a dozen sugar
plantations operating in the West Indies. The speed of this expansion
testifies to the profitability of sugar and the ruthlessness with which
the colonial powers pursued it.

The slave trade was itself a kind of addiction, McKenna argues. The
early importation of African slave labor into the New World was for one
purpose only: to support an agricultural economy based on sugar. The
craze for sugar was so overwhelming that a thousand years of Christian
ethical conditioning meant nothing. An outbreak of human cruelty and
bestiality of incredible proportions was blandly accepted by the
institutions of polite society.

## The Scale of Suffering

McKenna draws a direct comparison between the modern [[cocaine]] trade and
the historical sugar trade — and finds the modern trade wanting in
scale of destruction. The distortion and dehumanizing of human
institutions caused by crack cocaine today is nothing compared with
what the European desire for sugar did in the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries.

The difference was not merely one of degree but of kind. Slavery in
the sugar colonies was sanctioned by mendacious popes and openly
pursued by corrupt but legitimate governments. The modern drug trade,
however brutal, is not involved in the wholesale kidnapping,
transporting, and mass murder of entire populations that was done to
further sugar production.

## Historical Context: Slavery Before Sugar

McKenna is careful to note that the roots of slavery in Europe reach
far back. During the golden age of Periclean Athens, fully two-thirds
of the city's residents were slaves. In Italy at the time of Julius
Caesar, perhaps one-half of the population were slaves. Under the Roman
Empire, slaves had no civil rights, and their testimony in court was
acceptable only if obtained by torture.

Slavery diminished with the dissolution of the Roman Empire, as all
social institutions dissolved into the chaos of the early Dark Ages.
Feudalism replaced slavery with serfdom — somewhat better, since a
serf could maintain a home, marry, till the land, and could not be
transported off the land. The return of mass slavery, driven by sugar,
represented a regression from even this modest improvement.

## Legacy

The plantation model developed on Madeira became the template for
colonial agriculture across the tropical world. Its basic elements — a
monoculture cash crop, a racially defined slave labor force, a distant
European market, and enormous profits concentrated in the hands of a
few — would be replicated from Brazil to the Caribbean to the American
South. The institution that began with a thousand European debtors on a
volcanic island would, within a century, reshape the demographic,
economic, and political landscape of the entire Atlantic world.
