The rhizosphere concept was first introduced by Hiltner in 1904. The rhizosphere is
recognized as one of the most complex microbial habitats on Earth, with microbial densities typically 10 to 1000 times higher than in
surrounding bulk soil. For cannabis, understanding the rhizosphere microbiome has become
increasingly relevant as the industry moves toward sustainable cultivation.

## Rhizosphere Composition and Diversity

The cannabis rhizosphere occupies an intermediate position in the diversity gradient from
bulk soil to endorhiza (root interior). Alpha diversity peaks in bulk soil and declines
progressively through the rhizosphere to the endorhiza. This pattern is consistent with
the well-established "rhizosphere effect," where plant root exudates selectively enrich
certain bacterial taxa while depleting others relative to the surrounding bulk soil.

In the Winston et al. study, rhizosphere samples from five cannabis cultivars (Burmese,
Bookoo Kush, Sour Diesel, White Widow, and Maui Wowie) were characterized using Illumina
16S rRNA V4 region sequencing and analyzed with the [[mycoparasite-host-interface-types-interaction-mechanisms]].

## Diversity Gradient: Bulk Soil to Endorhiza

The diversity gradient documented by Winston et al. follows a consistent pattern:
bulk soil > rhizosphere > endorhiza. This gradient is driven by increasingly selective
filtering at each compartment boundary. Bulk soil represents the broadest microbial
reservoir. As roots release exudates (sugars, amino acids, organic acids, secondary
metabolites), they create carbon-rich hotspots that favor fast-growing copiotrophs,
reducing overall diversity. The transition from rhizosphere to endorhiza adds another
layer of selection, as only bacteria capable of surviving host immune responses and
colonizing root tissues can establish inside the root.

This gradient has been observed across numerous plant species and is considered a
fundamental organizing principle of the root microbiome.
