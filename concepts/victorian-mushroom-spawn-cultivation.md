# Victorian Mushroom Spawn and Cultivation

## Overview

The [[companion-cultivation-of-mushrooms-with-garden-vegetables]] in the Victorian era was a blend of practical
horticulture, folk wisdom, and genuine scientific mystery. Julius A. Palmer's 1894
*About Mushrooms* provides one of the most detailed American accounts of the period,
drawing on years of personal experimentation with mushroom beds, [[azurescens-spawn-preparation-grain-to-wood]],
and the unresolved question of [[bloomfield-fungal-reproduction-and-chemical-communication]].

Palmer was unusual among Victorian mycologists in focusing on practical cultivation
and edibility rather than the taxonomic classification of microscopic fungi, which
he considered "the useless phantom" distracting from useful knowledge.

## The Horse Mushroom: Only Cultivable Species

In the 1890s, only one species of fungus was known to be susceptible to artificial
cultivation: the horse mushroom (*[[agaricus-arvensis]]*), known in its wild state as
the pasture mushroom (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*). These are so closely related that
"cooks and gardeners do not recognize any distinction between the two."

The horse mushroom's unique cultivability was attributed to its intimate
association with horses. It follows the horse through every climate, grows from
[[coleman-french-maraicher-horse-manure-hotbed-system]], and probably depends on passage through the equine intestinal tract
for spore activation. Palmer proposed that "whatever change is necessary to
continue the reproductive work of these bodies takes place either in the intestinal
canal of the horse or in his manure."

## Understanding Spawn

The reproductive body of the mushroom is the spore, analogous to seeds in higher
plants. But between the spore and the visible mushroom lies the intermediate
structure called **spawn**—a mass of white, thread-like cellular material that
permeates the substrate like fine roots.

Palmer observed spawn directly during construction work opposite the Brunswick
Hotel in Boston. As workmen broke up the soil, "a white substance ran through
every piece. Starting with large branches, it divided and sub-divided like the
veins on the back of the hand. The smell was very strong, quickly noticed on the
opposite side of the way." This subterranean network was the spawn of *Coprinus
comatus* (Shaggy Mane), which Palmer compared to "a white cellular vine."

The spawn corresponds to the vegetative body of the fungus; the mushroom itself
is merely the fruit. Just as a cutting of grape-vine will continue to grow and
bear fruit when transplanted, a piece of mushroom spawn transplanted to favorable
conditions "will continue to ramify, and in time show the result in the form of
mushrooms."

## Bed Preparation

Palmer described the standard Victorian mushroom bed:

1. The [[cervantes-green-algae-growing-medium]] should be about two-thirds horse manure and one-third
   manure from other grazing animals or rich loam.
2. The material must be broken and turned until a thermometer shows 60° to 80°
   Fahrenheit—hot enough for biological activity but not lethally so.
3. Small blocks of spawn are inserted into the prepared bed.
4. Within three weeks, the entire mass should be permeated by white threads
   visible as streaks through the spawn.
5. About ten days later, mushrooms appear at the surface.

## Common Causes of Failure

Palmer identified three primary reasons for cultivation failure, based on "many
successive experiments":

**Poor spawn quality.** Much commercial spawn contained "not a trace of the
fungus." Gardeners were better off collecting spawn from the edge of an old
mushroom bed—once started, they would "never need want for the means of
propagation."

**Simultaneous spawning.** Planting the entire bed at once rather than in
sections. Palmer recommended staging the bed "like a Pullman car, so that when
one end is in bearing, the other will be just preparing." This extended the
harvest period and reduced the risk of total crop failure.

**Atmospheric neglect.** Mushrooms are "very susceptible to atmospheric change."
The critical need is moisture in the air rather than in the soil. "Watering the
ground is worse than useless; the moisture must come from beneath, like the dew."
Palmer's solution was to place beds under greenhouse benches with vents in steam
pipes, so that condensed steam would "collect over the earth and keep it moist
and warm." This recreated the conditions of "dog-days" humidity essential for
fruiting.

## Street Sweepings: An Alternative Method

Palmer noted that street sweepings collected over time and spread loosely over
soil would, through gradual accumulation and disintegration, spontaneously
produce fine crops of [[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]]. This occurred because horse traffic on
city streets deposited both manure and spores. The method required patience—years
of accumulation—but demanded no specialized knowledge or purchased materials.

## The Unsolved Mystery of Spore-to-Spawn

Despite centuries [[history-of-mushroom-cultivation]], the fundamental biological question
remained unanswered in Palmer's time: how does the spore give rise to the spawn?

"The most powerful microscope fails to show any germ of the future plant in
these reproductive bodies. The spawn is apparently a collection of oblong cells
united at the ends, and the stalk as well as the whole plant seems to be but a
mass of these cells, extended into chains interlaced and united."

Palmer had thrown the refuse of hundreds of fungi onto a grass plot outside his
office for years. The soil must have received millions of spores, "yet not a
toadstool of any kind has ever grown there." The mystery was not in spore
production but in the conditions under which spores germinate to form spawn.
Microscopic observation showed "in some of the spores of fungi a conjugation
indicating a sexual difference," suggesting reproduction "must be analogous to
that of animal rather than vegetable nature."

## Palmer's Philosophy of Cultivation

Palmer compared [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] to [[holzer-natural-animal-husbandry]] rather than plant
agriculture. "It is certain that they require the care we give to animals, in
order to insure proper conditions for reproduction." The Victorians who treated
mushroom beds like [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]]—planting them "as they would peas or
nasturtiums"—invariably failed because they did not understand that the visible
mushroom was only the fruit of a living underground organism requiring specific
conditions of temperature, humidity, and substrate composition.
