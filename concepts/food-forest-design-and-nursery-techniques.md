## Food Forest Design and Nursery Techniques

Food forests are multi-layered perennial food production systems that mimic natural forest ecosystems while providing abundant yields of fruits, nuts, and other edible products. The permaculture approach to food forests integrates intensive nursery techniques with strategic planting design to establish productive systems rapidly, even in tropical and temperate climates.

## The Philosophy of Food Forests

The establishment of non-cook food forests is central to reducing fuel consumption in the third world, where cooking is the major fuel use. Recommending rice plots to island communities without considering the tree-cutting required for cooking fuel would be counterproductive — cutting trees to cook rice extends the rice crop but destroys the forest that sustains the island. Food forests eliminate the need to choose between fuel and food.

People adapt readily to new food plants. Even in the most conservative eating cultures — Australia once had the highest per capita meat consumption in the world — avocados, nuts, and tropical fruits have rapidly gained market presence. A gardener presented with a new seed will almost invariably plant it. The permaculture approach predicates reduced fuel consumption for cooking and maximum food production from perennial systems.

## Nursery Techniques for Rapid Establishment

Modern nursery techniques have revolutionized the speed at which food forests can be established. By adjusting shade and nutrients, nurserymen can achieve four to five times the growth rates possible under old open-bed nursery conditions. Key developments include:

- **Cloning by root tip**: A single rare seed can generate a whole population of plants through root tip propagation
- **Single cell generation of plants**: Revolutionary techniques for mass propagation
- **Controlled environment growth**: Sheltered, ideal conditions while ground preparation proceeds outside

The approach involves raising trees in optimal nursery conditions while simultaneously preparing and cropping the planting site. The site may be used for other purposes — including manuring crops — during the preparation period. When trees are sufficiently established, they are moved into the field situation.

### Slowgrove's Nursery Kit Approach

Slowgrove developed a portable nursery kit system designed for community-scale [[food-forest-establishment]]. For a 4,000-acre community project, the approach is to start the nursery running first while ground preparation proceeds. The entire nursery kit fits on the back of a truck and includes drip lines, sprinklers, and shade house infrastructure.

This system was adapted for use with Aboriginal communities in central Australia. A trained tribal nurseryman travels with the group to outstations, setting up the complete nursery at camp. Propagation begins with simple techniques:

- **Tamarisk**: Bundles of sticks placed in water, then rooted in sand
- **Grapes**: Twenty or more varieties of cuttings suited to the local area
- **Initial food crops**: Quick-establishing plants that provide food while trees mature

Only a couple of months of training are needed for a tribal nurseryman to become proficient. The system enables rapid revegetation of even remote areas.

### The Indian Nursery Deficit

India presents a striking case study in the relationship between nurseries and revegetation. Despite Gandhi's famous instruction that every Indian should plant a tree each year — displayed on signs across the country in both Hindi and English — there is a critical shortage of tree seedlings and nurseries. Bombay's Yellow Pages contain no nursery listings whatsoever. Anyone who established a nursery in India would rapidly become wealthy, because the demand for trees far exceeds supply. The problem is not willingness but availability.

## Food Forest Structure and Species Selection

### Crown Bearers vs. Edge Species

A well-designed food forest differentiates between species based on their position requirements:

- **Crown bearers that require edge position**: Certain fruit and nut trees must remain on the forest edge to receive adequate light
- **Crown bearers for interior planting**: Large nut trees like oaks can bear well within the canopy
- **Edge species**: Species that thrive at the boundary between forest and open land

The design process involves establishing structural timber as a core, then thinning progressively — first for crown yields, then for edge yields. As the forest diameter exceeds 100 feet, the center can be treated as structural. Designers may break crowns to create lakes or open areas, then restart the pattern.

### Tropical vs. Temperate Considerations

Tropical forests offer advantages for [[food-forest-design]] because stem-bearing species like cocoa and palms are naturally crown-bearing within the canopy. Temperate forests require more careful positioning, though oaks bear quite well within the canopy and can serve dual roles as forage and structural timber.

### The Fig-Grape Combination

One of the most elegant food forest combinations pairs fig trees with grape vines. The process works as follows:

1. Plant grapes on trellises near young fig trees
2. As the fig grows tall enough, lead the grape vine into the fig's crown
3. Stop pruning the grape — the fig tree provides natural height limitation
4. Wind pruning at the fig's crown edge keeps the grape productive without maintenance

The same principle applies to elm, black walnut, and Tasmanian blackwood as support trees for grapes. Because the grape cannot extend beyond the crown, it is effectively pruned by the tree itself and bears as heavily as conventionally pruned vines. The key requirement is a support tree of limited height.

## Integration with Animal Systems

Food forests integrate naturally with animal husbandry. In India, the government pig-killing station at 68 acres had an untapped resource — breadfruit trees growing a quarter mile away, dropping more fruit than all the pigs could consume. Combining breadfruit, papaya, and banana (carried to the pigs by workers) transformed a simple pig operation into a comprehensive food production system on the same acreage.

Successful village-scale change often comes from individual farmers rather than large-scale interventions. A Bombay-area farmer who forbade workers from washing clothes in the farm's spring water eliminated disease, then started a lime nursery that grew into a profitable business — the most successful village improvement achieved with minimal outside intervention.

## Planting Density for Quality Timber

The ideal method for establishing a mixed forest involves planting at very small intervals:

- Some species as close as 3 × 3 feet
- Nearly any species at 9 × 9 feet
- Seedlings are planted, not saplings

This close spacing forces fast upright growth with good trunk development. Modest trunk trimming follows, and bearing starts while trees are still small. Selection then proceeds for:

- Early bearing
- Easy nut crackability
- Low tannin content
- Overall bearing quality

Trees that don't meet expectations are culled once they have developed adequate trunk length. The process is graded and surprisingly rapid.

## Key Species for Food Forests

### Structural and Food Trees

- **Black locust**: Highest impact loading strength of any timber, coppices freely, provides chicken forage
- **Bamboo**: Structural uses, edible shoots, propagates vegetatively; 10 years to first harvest from clump
- **Avocado**: The best butter substitute in the tropics, far superior to dairy butter
- **Leucaena**: Fast-growing nitrogen fixer that spreads readily across fields
- **Coconut**: Superb for tropical food forest borders, providing shelter and food

### Buffer Trees

The large nut trees (hickories, pecans, walnuts — *Juglans*, meaning "balls of Jove") produce root exudates containing creosols that kill pioneering species. A buffer forest of 20-30 feet is needed between these and other fruit trees. Excellent buffer species include:

- **Mulberry**: Stands directly against nut trees with no crop loss, and against fruit trees without impairment
- **Elderberry**: Snuggles up to both nut and fruit tree groups
- **Black locust**: Another effective buffering species
