# Landform and Topography Reading

So much of the ecology is affected by the shape of the land. Just as soup is contained within a bowl, the ecology is contained with geological features such as hills, valleys, grottos, soil composition, and so on. The shape of the ground affects the local hydro-cycle, creates micro-climates, determines what plants can grow where, and through these effects the animal populations. A [[faires-chicken-coops-tractors-and-breeding-guide]], greenhouses, gazebos, garages, and yurts can fill the same role as hills with good design planning.

## Erosion and Slope Management

The greatest natural danger to topsoil is erosion, caused mainly by water and wind, with water being the greater threat. Unchecked water flows can literally wash topsoil away. The runoff drives the force of erosion and carves channels into the ground, creating larger and larger streams. To protect topsoil you need to slow the flow of water across it.

Techniques for controlling water flow on slopes include:
- **Ground covers** -- grasses, shrubs, ground vines. Root systems physically hold soil in place; leaves protect from wind and provide shade preventing the ground from drying out and blowing away.
- **Berms and swales** -- keylining across the slope of a hill catches and slows water.
- **Wind breaks** -- reduce wind load, thereby reducing erosion from dried soil blowing away.
- **Evaporation control** -- evaporation is caused by heat and wind, so reducing evaporation also reduces erosion.

## Topography in the Design Process

The topographical information of your site should be one of the first layers of data on your base map. It informs every subsequent analysis -- where water flows, where fire travels, where to place structures, where to create gardens, and where earthworks should be installed.

The energy of the slope of a hill is one of the fundamental energies a Permaculture Designer must learn to read and work with. Ridge lines determine watershed boundaries and can serve as natural fire breaks. Valley bottoms collect water and cold air, often creating frost pockets that affect planting decisions. Reading the land is the foundation upon which all other analysis is built.

## Contour Reading Techniques

Contour lines are the fundamental tool for reading landform on a topographic map. Each contour line connects points of equal elevation above a reference datum (usually sea level). The spacing between contour lines reveals the slope: closely spaced lines indicate steep terrain, widely spaced lines indicate gentle slopes or flat ground. When contour lines touch or merge, they represent cliffs or vertical faces.

To read contours effectively in the field, learn to see the land as a three-dimensional surface from the two-dimensional map:
- **Contour interval**: The vertical distance between consecutive contour lines (e.g., 5m, 10m, 20m). Always check the map legend for the contour interval before reading slope.
- **Index contours**: Every fifth contour line is typically drawn heavier (bold) and labelled with its elevation. Use these to quickly orient yourself on the map.
- **V-shaped contours pointing uphill**: Indicate valleys or drainage channels. The point of the V points toward higher ground.
- **U-shaped contours pointing downhill**: Indicate ridges or spurs. The open end of the U points toward lower ground.
- **Closed contour loops**: Represent hilltops (increasing elevation toward centre) or depressions (decreasing elevation toward centre, marked with hachures).
- **Parallel contour lines**: Represent uniform slopes. Evenly spaced parallels indicate a planar slope surface.

## Slope Analysis and Classification

Slopes are classified by gradient and each class has distinct design implications:
- **0-3% (flat to nearly flat)**: Prone to waterlogging and poor drainage. Suitable for buildings, roads, and [[mollison-rabbits-in-permaculture-design-and-soil-preparation]].

## Ridge and Valley Identification

Ridges and valleys are the primary structural elements of any landscape:
- **Ridgetops**: The highest lines of elevation, separating one watershed from another. Ridgetops are typically well-drained, windy, and exposed. They serve as natural fire breaks (fire moves uphill but must cross the ridgetop before descending the other side). Ridgetops are ideal locations for wind generators, communication equipment, fire watch towers, and hardy timber species.
- **Valley bottoms**: The lowest points, where water and cold air collect. Valley bottoms are typically the most fertile and moist areas but may suffer from frost pockets (cold air drainage) and waterlogging. They are ideal for water storage (dams, ponds), high-water-demand crops, and access roads.
- **Spurs**: Ridge-like projections extending from the main ridgetop into a valley. Spurs provide well-drained, elevated sites with views of the surrounding landscape -- ideal for house sites, lookouts, and frost-sensitive plantings.
- **Hollows**: Small valley-like depressions in the hillside. Hollows concentrate water flow and are natural locations for swales, mini-dams, or intensive planting.
- **Noses**: Convex protrusions on a slope where the ground surface curves outward. Water sheds off noses, making them drier than surrounding terrain. They are well-drained sites suitable for buildings and access tracks.

## Aspect and Microclimate

Aspect (the direction a slope faces) is one of the most important topographic factors affecting microclimate:
- **South-facing slopes** (in the Northern Hemisphere): Receive maximum solar exposure. Warmer, drier microclimate. Earlier spring growth, higher evapotranspiration. Suitable for heat-loving crops (tomatoes, peppers, grapes, [[bloomfield-amphibian-decline-fungal-disease-climate-interactions]] pressure for some crops.
- **West-facing slopes**: Shaded in the morning, exposed to hot afternoon sun. Higher temperature fluctuation between morning and afternoon. Can be challenging for frost-sensitive crops due to rapid thawing.

Aspect interacts with slope angle to determine total solar radiation received. A steep south-facing slope receives more solar energy per unit area than a steep north-facing slope at the same latitude. This effect is most pronounced at higher latitudes.
