# Fukuoka: Natural Farming as Spiritual Light

## The Moment of Realization

Fukuoka lays bare everything about his past because he dearly wishes to atone for his errors and make the fullest possible use of the few remaining years he has left on this earth. At the same time he wants to search for a way to preserve the light of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] that has managed somehow to remain burning to this day. This is because he has come to sense the danger that natural farming may vanish as just one transient farming technique.

## Not an Individual Achievement

Natural farming is not limited to Fukuoka's own humble experiences; it is not something that an individual can establish or that can be perfected by man. He sensed the will of God, but this task was beyond his powers.

## The True Nature of Natural Farming

Natural farming is nothing less than a true way of human life that revealed itself suddenly to Fukuoka when he learned that nature is always a total perfectionist, that it is the image of a God who faithfully practices absolute truth, and that man cannot survive away from nature's embrace. Of course, the actual methods and general principles were revealed to him during that brief instant.

## Always Started by God

Natural farming must always be started and developed according to God's instructions. All man ever had to do was to listen humbly to the word of God and lend just a little bit of a hand. Unfortunately, Fukuoka was off in a trance and, caught unawares, failed to hear the proper response. He was not able to become a faithful servant of God.

## Perfect but Appears Incomplete

Natural farming always appears to be incomplete, but it is at all times perfect and complete at the place of God. Acutely aware that, powerless as he and others are to do any better than merely imitate, he may have neglected the work of establishing natural farming because he did not call upon others and made no effort to create followers and students. However, he never once lost confidence in the fact that natural farming was possible.

## The Discontinuous Continuum

God in all ages appears as a discontinuous continuum. Natural farming too, since antiquity, may have arisen and vanished, and risen again to flourish.

## One of the Spiritual Lights

Natural farming is one of the spiritual lights that must be kept burning throughout the night. In this age in which we live, it is possible that if this light dies out, it may never burn again.

Natural farming is more than just a way of farming. It is without question the one and only path that remains for humanity to continue to survive on this earth. It must not be allowed to come to an end.

## God Has Abandoned Man

God has left man to his own devices; he has abandoned man. If man does not save himself, no one will do this for him.

Even if the going is steep, we must lay out the route by which our children and grandchildren can continue to live on this beautiful planet of ours.

## The Betrayal of Paradise

What happened, very simply, was that, taken aback by the vision of an ideal paradise before him, Fukuoka turned around and bolted from reality. Although he had noticed that our world was a garden of Eden, he turned his back on this knowledge and, without fully realizing it, chose instead the road of a dilettante farmer tilling a lost paradise.

When he realized that he had lost his original naivete and inspiration, he even begged to be readmitted to God's presence. But this was no longer allowed of him.

## Philosophical Significance

This preface to [[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]] is one of Fukuoka's most personal and spiritually charged passages. Written late in life, it carries the weight of a man looking back on decades of work and wondering whether it was enough. The tone is simultaneously prophetic and confessional -- a rare combination in agricultural literature.

Fukuoka's description of natural farming as a "spiritual light" elevates it from a technique to a calling. It is not one option among many for growing food; it is the essential connection between humanity and the divine order of nature. When this connection is broken -- through the arrogance of [[fukuoka-science-destroyer-of-nature]] or the complacency of consumer society -- humanity loses not just a farming method but its very reason for being.

The concept of the "discontinuous continuum" is particularly rich. It suggests that natural farming is not a linear progression but a recurring revelation -- something that appears, disappears, and reappears across history. Fukuoka sees himself as one node in this pattern, not its originator or culmination. This humility distinguishes his work from the typical reformer's conviction that he has discovered the final answer.

## Connection to Natural Farming Principles

The tension between the perfection of natural farming "at the place of God" and its apparent incompleteness in human practice is central to understanding Fukuoka's work. Natural farming is always perfect in principle but imperfect in execution -- not because the principle is flawed, but because human beings are unable to fully surrender their desire to control and improve.

This is why [[fukuoka-mu-economics-and-value-of-nothing-essay]] is so important. Mu is not a state that can be achieved and then maintained; it is a continuous practice of letting go. Each time the farmer reaches for a tool, each time the intellect suggests a "better" way, there is an opportunity to practice non-attachment. Natural farming is, in this sense, a spiritual discipline disguised as an agricultural method.

Fukuoka's confession that he "turned around and bolted from reality" upon seeing paradise is a deeply human moment. He is admitting that even he -- the man to whom natural farming was revealed -- was not equal to the task of living in full harmony with that revelation. This honesty makes his work more credible, not less. If even the originator of natural farming struggles with it, then the rest of us can be forgiven our difficulties.

## The Urgency of Preservation

Fukuoka's warning that natural farming "may vanish as just one transient farming technique" has proven prescient. In the decades since he wrote these words, natural farming has indeed been absorbed into the broader organic and sustainable agriculture movements, often stripped of its philosophical and spiritual dimensions. The techniques survive; the light dims.

This is why [[natural-farming-and-spirituality]] cannot be treated as optional or supplementary. It is the essence of the practice. Without it, natural farming becomes just another set of techniques -- "clothed in the robes of chemical agriculture," as Fukuoka puts it elsewhere.
