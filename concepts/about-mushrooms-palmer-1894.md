## About Mushrooms — Julius Auboineau Palmer (1894)

## Overview

*About Mushrooms: A Guide to the Study of Esculent and Poisonous Fungi* is a foundational American mycological text published in 1894 by Julius Auboineau Palmer, Jr. in Boston (Lee & Shepard, Publishers). The book represents the culmination of Palmer's twenty years of independent study and fieldwork on edible and poisonous mushrooms, compiled from a series of articles he published in various scientific and medical journals throughout the 1870s and 1880s.

## Historical Context

Palmer's work emerged during a period of growing interest in [[mycology]] in the United States, when few reliable guides existed for distinguishing edible from poisonous fungi. The book was dedicated to the medical profession of the United States, reflecting the practical public health importance of the subject — mushroom poisoning was a genuine and sometimes fatal concern in an era before modern toxicology.

Palmer described himself as an amateur naturalist with "no pretence to be of their number" (i.e., not formally trained as a physician), yet he corresponded extensively with medical professionals and chemists. He acknowledged receiving "consideration and courtesy" from the medical establishment, which made his work possible.

## Content and Structure

The book is composed of reprinted articles Palmer published across multiple venues:

- **Popular Science Monthly** (1877)
- **Boston Daily Globe** (1877)
- **Evening Traveler** (1877)
- **Forest and Stream** (1877)
- **Evening Transcript** (1879, 1882)
- **Moniteur Scientifique** (1879)
- **Medical and Surgical Reporter** (1879, 1883)
- **Essex and Newton Societies** (1881)
- **Philadelphia Medical and Surgical Reporter** (1885)
- **Boston Journal** (1889)
- **Mushroom Charts** (1885)

This collection format inevitably led to some repetition, which Palmer acknowledged frankly in his introduction. The articles span roughly two decades of mycological investigation.

## Key Contributions

### Amanita Poison Research

One of Palmer's most significant contributions was his analysis of the poisons of the **[[amanita]]** family of mushrooms. He published his findings in the *Moniteur Scientifique* and, six years later, **Charles McIlvaine** independently confirmed every statement Palmer had made. This confirmation lent considerable credibility to Palmer's analytical work.

The case reported by Dr. Shadle was highlighted by Palmer as a definitive example of both the action of Amanita poison and its successful treatment, and Palmer gave McIlvaine's account preference over similar cases from his own correspondence.

### The Paul Casamajor Connection

Early in his studies, Palmer received a letter from the late **[[paul-casamajor]]**, then head chemist, which Palmer identified as the one exception to his entirely self-directed education in natural history. Casamajor's guidance provided Palmer with a chemical foundation that informed his analytical approach to fungal toxins.

### Esculent vs. Poisonous Classification

Palmer's organizing principle was the practical distinction between **esculent** (edible) and **poisonous** fungi — a binary framework typical of 19th-century mycological guides intended for public safety. The book served as a field guide and reference for foragers, physicians, and anyone interested in the practical mycology of the northeastern United States.

## Publication History and Significance

The 1894 edition was scanned by Google as part of its library digitization project and has since entered the **public domain**. The scanned copy (149 pages, OCR-processed via Tesseract) preserves the original's marginalia, notations, and the physical artifacts of its long journey through libraries — marks that Palmer himself would have appreciated as evidence of the book's endurance.

The work is part of the **Reference Library of Cryptogamic Botany**, a collection gifted by **Elio Schaechter**, a prominent modern mycologist, indicating the book's continued relevance to the history of mycological science.

## Place in Mycological Literature

Palmer's *About Mushrooms* occupies an important niche in the history of American mycology:

- It bridges the gap between European mycological traditions and the emerging American school
- It exemplifies the **citizen scientist** tradition in natural history, with Palmer working outside formal academic institutions
- Its republication of articles across popular and medical journals demonstrates the broad public interest in mycology during the late 19th century
- The confirmation of Palmer's Amanita research by McIlvaine (who would go on to write the influential *One Thousand American Fungi*) establishes a chain of intellectual influence in American mycological toxicology
