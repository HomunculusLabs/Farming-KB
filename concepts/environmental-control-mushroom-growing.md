# Environmental Control for Mushroom Growing

Environmental control systems are critical infrastructure for successful [[outdoor-mushroom-cultivation]], integrating temperature, humidity, air circulation, fresh air exchange, and lighting into a balanced growing environment. paul stamets provides detailed guidance in The Mushroom Cultivator.

## The Growing Room System

A growing room must maintain temperature range at high humidity via insulation and an integrated control system incorporating heating, cooling, humidification, and air handling. Modern systems integrate all these functions into a single air handling system for balanced conditions.

## Temperature Control

Different mushroom species have precise temperature requirements at each stage:
- agaricus-bitorquis: Spawn run at 84-86°F, cropping at 75-77°F
- agaricus-brunnescens: Prefers cooler conditions than bitorquis
- [[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]]: Tolerant of warmer temperatures
- Thermal death limits for most species begin around 93-95°F over prolonged exposure

Temperature stratification and CO2 stratification are ongoing problems that the air circulation system must counteract, especially during spawn running when full recirculation is used.

## Humidity Management

- **Spawn running**: 90-100% relative humidity
- **Pinhead initiation (primordia formation)**: 95-100% relative humidity
- **Cropping**: 85-92% relative humidity, held constant

Excessive humidity without adequate air movement retards mushroom development and creates breeding areas for contaminants like Trichoderma and Pseudomonas. Saturated stagnant air pockets are particularly problematic. The balance between air circulation, fresh air, and humidification is described as "but one aspect of the Art of mushroom culture."

## Air Circulation and Fresh Air Exchange

The air circulation system should be designed to recirculate room air, accomplished by a mixing box with an adjustable damper that proportions fresh and recirculated air.

### During Spawn Running
Full recirculation (damper closed) maintains even conditions and counteracts temperature/CO2 stratification throughout the substrate mass.

### During Cropping
Fresh air requirements vary by species:
- agaricus-brunnescens: 4-6 air changes per hour
- agaricus-bitorquis: Half the fresh air of brunnescens
- [[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]]: 2-3 changes per hour (more CO2 tolerant)

### CO2 Monitoring
The most accurate method employs a multiple gas detector measuring CO2 in parts per million (ppm), from 300 ppm (natural level) up to 20,000 ppm. Physical development of mushrooms serves as a visual guide: high CO2 produces long stems and small underdeveloped caps in Agaricus and Pleurotus.

### Key Principle
Fresh air displaces existing room air which is exhausted. Unless fresh air is preconditioned to species requirements, the growing environment is constantly disrupted, overworking heating and humidification systems. CO2-tolerant species give growers a distinct advantage.

## Lighting

Many mushroom species require light for pinhead initiation and proper development. Mushrooms are phototropic — they will twist toward dim, distant light sources. Light intensity matters: 550 lux produced highest yields in [[shiitake-mushroom-cultivation]] ([[shiitake-mushroom-cultivation]]) cultivation. agaricus-bitorquis requires no light for fruiting.

Light stimulation must be carefully timed. "Border breaks" — mushrooms forming between casing and glass in jars — are caused by high humidity pockets and premature light stimulation.

## Evaporative Cooling

An important effect of air circulation and fresh air supply is evaporation of moisture from the cropping surface. This creates a cool microclimate surface and aids transport of nutrients in solution from substrate to growing mushrooms. If evaporation rate is too high and humidity falls below 85%, excessive drying occurs, causing small stunted mushrooms and cracked scaly caps. A dry cropping surface is difficult to recondition.

## Watering Protocol

- No substantial waterings before button stage (can damage pins)
- Once mushrooms reach button size, build casing moisture back up via daily light-to-moderate waterings with fine misting nozzle
- Water 2-4 times/day rather than once to allow slow absorption without surface damage
- After first flush, keep casing moist with light mistings until next flush reaches button stage
- Never allow casing to dry out completely
- Stop watering once mushroom caps reach adolescence (wet caps breed Bacterial Blotch)
