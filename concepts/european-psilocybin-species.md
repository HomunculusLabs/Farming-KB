# European Psilocybin Species

Beyond the well-known Psilocybe semilanceata, Europe hosts several additional psilocybin-containing genera that are less recognized but scientifically significant. Data primarily from Jochen Gartz's field research and chemical analyses (1980s-1990s). See also [[mushroom-species-guide]] and [[fungal-biodiversity]].

## Inocybe aeruginascens

Originally described by M. Babos in 1968. First found near Potsdam, Eastern Germany, growing in parks, gardens, and grassy areas. Unlike thin-fleshed Psilocybe species that fruit in fall, I. aeruginascens fruits earlier and frequently suffers from fly larvae infestations in older colonies.

**WARNING**: Inocybe is the same genus as potentially lethal muscarine-producing species. I. aeruginascens is the species most easily misidentified by laypersons.

### Alkaloid Content

| Location | Year Collected | Psilocybin (% dry wt) |
|----------|---------------|----------------------|
| Potsdam | 1984 | 0.38 |
| Potsdam | 1983 | 0.34 |
| Potsdam | 1982 | 0.33 |
| Berlin | 1975 | 0.11 |
| Hungary | 1967 | 0.22 |

Psilocybin levels are comparable to Psilocybe mexicana. Contains baeocystin at levels comparable to psilocybin. Only trace amounts of psilocin. Average alkaloid content is remarkably constant across samples and does not vary much with prolonged storage.

**Aeruginascin**: Gartz identified a previously unknown alkaloid unique to this species, named aeruginascin. Soluble only in polar solvents (water, methanol, acetic acid). Concentration comparable to psilocybin and baeocystin. The compound may contribute to the consistently euphoric experiences reported in involuntary intoxications, even under unfavorable set/setting conditions.

## Gymnopilus purpuratus

Originally described as Flammula purpurata by Cooke & Massee (1887) from Kew Gardens, England, on a tree fern trunk. Native to Australia and South America (Chile), fruits on dead tree trunks in May. Rediscovered in Europe in 1983 near Ribnitz-Damgarten on the Eastern German seaboard, growing on discarded bark and wood chips mixed with pig manure compost.

### How It Arrived in Europe

Likely introduced via imported feed grain from Argentina in the late 1970s. Spores survived passage through pigs' digestive systems and colonized compost heaps (wood chips + liquid pig manure, reaching ~176F internally). First confirmed psilocybin-containing European Gymnopilus species.

### Description

- **Cap**: 15-42 mm (occasionally up to 20 cm), convex without umbo, covered with pointy purplish-to-ruby scales on yellow background, dry
- **Gills**: Close, golden yellow becoming rusty yellow
- **Stem**: Not hollow, 6-10mm x 30-80mm (rarely 15cm), bruising grayish-blue to greenish at base
- **Cortina**: Sulphur yellow, does not form true annulus
- **Habitat**: Wood chips, bark, compost heaps; clusters of up to 22 mushrooms
- **Key feature**: Unlike most European Gymnopilus species, this one is NOT bitter

### Alkaloid Content

| Collection | Psilocybin (%) | Psilocin (%) | Baeocystin (%) |
|-----------|---------------|-------------|----------------|
| 1988, sample 1 | 0.29 | 0.28 | 0.05 |
| 1988, sample 2 | 0.31 | 0.29 | 0.04 |
| 1987, sample 1 | 0.32 | 0.03 | 0.03 |
| 1987, sample 2 | 0.21 | 0.02 | 0.02 |

Note: Psilocin is unstable — significant decay observed during one year of storage. Smaller mushrooms had higher alkaloid concentrations. This species contains more psilocin than any other European species.

### Yurimagua Indians Connection

Jesuit missionaries (17th-18th century) reported that the Yurimagua Indians of the western Amazon (Peru) prepared a potently intoxicating potion from a "reddish tree-dwelling mushroom with spicy taste." Gartz proposes this was likely a Gymnopilus species rather than the previously assumed Psilocybe yungensis, since Gymnopilus species are reddish and colonize dense tree trunks.

## Conocybe cyanopus

Originally discovered near Potsdam, Berlin, and Germany's Harz Mountains by J. Schaffer around 1930. Included by Kuhner as C. cyanopoda (1935). Also found in Ithaca, NY (1918). Extremely rare in Europe — reported only twice in 60 years within former East Germany.

### Description

- **Cap**: 0.3-2.5 cm, hemispheric to convex, striated, ocher to dark brown without grey-green stains
- **Stem**: 2-4 cm x 1-1.4 cm, whitish becoming silvery, stains bluish-green at base
- **Spores**: 7-10 x 4-5 um
- **Habitat**: Grassy areas or moss, summer through fall

### Alkaloid Content

| Sample | Dry Weight (mg) | Psilocybin (%) | Baeocystin (%) |
|--------|----------------|---------------|----------------|
| 1 | 5 | 0.84 | 0.15 |
| 2 | 6 | 0.73 | 0.12 |
| 3 | 7 | 1.01 | 0.20 |
| 4 | 10 | 0.91 | 0.16 |
| 5 | 12 | 0.89 | 0.14 |

Concentrations comparable to Psilocybe semilanceata. Sclerotia grown on malt agar contained 0.25% psilocybin with no additional alkaloids. First psilocybin detection: Seattle, WA (1961). Beug and Bigwood reported 0.93% in Pacific Northwest samples.

## Pluteus salicinus

Light-spored mushroom (rose-colored spore dust) in the Pluteaceae family. First described ~200 years ago in Europe but rarely mentioned. Psilocybin confirmed in 1981 (Illinois) and 1984 (Norway). Found in Holland, Finland, Sweden, France, Switzerland, and Germany.

### Description

- **Cap**: Up to 8 cm, lighter at margin, silver grey, hairy/felt-like, often scaly
- **Stem**: Up to 10 cm, grey-green or grey-blue discolorations at base
- **Spore print**: Rose/pink (unlike all other psychoactive genera)
- **Habitat**: Stumps of willows, alders, lindens, beeches, poplars, maples; May-October
- **Note**: Albino forms exist with grey-green stem bases and cap apex areas

### Alkaloid Content (German samples, non-bluing)

| Sample | Cap Psilocybin (%) | Stem Psilocybin (%) | Cap Urea (%) |
|--------|-------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| 1 | 1.38 | 0.48 | 2.50 |
| 2 | 1.57 | 0.71 | 2.60 |
| 3 | 1.57 | 0.71 | 2.60 |
| 4 | 1.22 | 1.14 | 1.40 |
| 5 | 1.39 | 0.64 | 2.40 |

Swiss samples (Stijve): average 0.25% psilocybin. Bluing Dubener marshlands samples: 0.2-0.7% psilocybin + trace baeocystin. European mushrooms produce only traces of psilocin. North American and European populations may not be identical species.

### Other Pluteus Species

- **Pluteus nigroviridis Babos**: Extremely rare (Hungary), only 0.035% psilocybin
- **Pluteus cyanopus**: Reported from Europe; bluish/violet discolorations

## Mushroom Identification Dangers

### Galerina Fatalities

On December 16, 1981, three teenagers on Whidbey Island, WA collected Galerina species mistaking them for Psilocybe. Two recovered after medical treatment; a 16-year-old girl died on December 24. Galerina species contain the same deadly amatoxins as Amanita phalloides — symptoms are insidious with a 12-hour latency period during which irreversible internal damage occurs.

### Key Identification Risks

- Psilocybin-producing species share characteristics with many culinary and toxic species
- "Little Brown Mushrooms" (LBMs) are especially problematic — habitats and variations are poorly studied
- Wood-debris Psilocybe species are much harder to identify than pasture-dwelling P. semilanceata
- North America has a much broader spectrum of mushroom species than Europe, increasing confusion risk
- Red light is NOT a safe "safe light" for mushroom work — it may interact with blue-light photoreceptor systems
