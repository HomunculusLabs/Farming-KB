# Mushroom Flies in Cultivation

Mushroom flies and midges are among the most damaging [[jadam-insect-pest-management]]s in
commercial and home [[oyster-mushroom-cultivation-economics]]. Attracted by the odor of
decomposing manure, vegetable matter, and growing mycelium, these
insects lay eggs on or near the mycelium and fruitbodies. Their larvae
cause extensive damage by directly feeding on mycelial cells or
tunneling through mushroom fruitbodies. Strict hygiene remains the
most effective long-term control measure.

## The Four Major Mushroom Fly Species

Four families of flies pose the greatest threat to mushroom crops:
Sciarid flies (Lycoriidae), Phorid flies (Phoridae), Cecid flies
(Cecidomyiidae), and Leptocera flies (Sphaeroceridae). Each has
distinct life cycles, behaviors, and damage patterns that cultivators
must learn to identify and manage.

Sciarid flies (Lycoriella solani, L. mali, L. auripila) are small
gnat-like insects with two long segmented antennae, large compound
eyes, a black head and thorax, and a yellow segmented abdomen. Females
are about 3 mm long with a swollen abdomen ending in an ovipositor,
while males measure about 2 mm with a narrow abdomen ending in a
clasper. Larvae are 6-12 mm long with twelve abdominal sections and a
distinct black shiny head, passing through four instars before
pupating. Pupation takes 2-3 days in a cocoon of fine silky threads
and compost fragments. At 75 degrees F, the life cycle runs: egg 2
days, larva 16 days, pupa 3 days, adult 5-7 days. At 61 degrees F: egg
7 days, larva 23 days, pupa 8 days. A single female can lay 150-170
eggs at a time. Populations peak in September and October.

## Phorid and Cecid Fly Identification and Biology

Phorid flies (Megaselia nigra, M. halterata) are distinguished by a
humped back, rapid jerky run, rounded third antennal segment, and
yellowish to reddish brown coloring. Adults measure 2-5 mm, with
females living 16 days and males 10 days. Larvae are 6-10 mm long,
white and semi-transparent, with mouth hooks bearing seven teeth. At 75
degrees F: eggs 2 days, larvae 5 days, pupae 8 days. At 61 degrees F:
eggs 4 days, larvae 14 days, pupae 28 days. Each female lays up to 50
eggs placed near mycelium. Phorids are considered the principal
mushroom pest in western Europe. Critically, females will not lay eggs
in total darkness, giving growers an effective prevention method during
spawn running. Larvae tunnel into mushrooms through the base of the
stem, causing secondary bacterial infections and brownish
discolorations.

Cecid flies (Heteropeza pygmaea, Mycophila speyeri) are among the
smallest mushroom pests at less than 1 mm long, making them nearly
invisible to the naked eye. H. pygmaea are orange with a long
segmented abdomen. Larvae are 1-3 mm, white to cream (H. pygmaea) or
bright orange (M. speyeri), and can jump up to 2 cm in dry conditions.
They reproduce through paedogenesis: mother larvae give birth to
daughter larvae, producing 14-20 daughters in six days. Resting mother
larvae can survive up to 18 months. The life cycle runs: egg 2 days,
mother larva 5-6 days, daughter larva 2 days, then 5-6 more days.
Cecid larvae pierce growing hyphae and suck out the contents. H.
pygmaea also carries a bacterium producing longitudinal brown stripes
on stems and black spots on gills.

## Leptocera Fly and Fly Control Measures

Leptocera heteroneura has large red compound eyes and a yellow and
black striped abdomen, resembling phorid flies but smaller with
distinctive wing venation. Larvae resemble house fly maggots with blunt
posterior ends tapering to slender heads equipped with mouth hooks.
Pupae are golden brown and barrel shaped. Life cycle: egg 3 days,
larva 14-28 days, pupa 10-14 days. Leptocera acts as a disease vector
and is a known carrier of mites, frequently associated with bacterial
infections.

The primary fly control measures, as outlined by Stamets and Chilton,
emphasize hygiene over chemical intervention. Pasteurization must reach
140 degrees F for 2 hours in composts or bulk substrates to kill all
insect life stages. All growing rooms must be airtight with weather-
stripped doors, positive pressure, and air locks. Fresh air intakes
and exhaust vents require fine mosquito netting screening. Tools and
implements should be cleaned with 2% chlorine solution before use on
new crops. All spent substrates, used grains, and trimmings must be
removed from premises to eliminate breeding areas. Wood surfaces
should be treated with cuprinol or copper sulfate, avoiding petroleum
products. Insect monitors using black lights (long wave UV) with
sticky paper should be installed to alert growers to fly emergence or
entry.

## Damage Patterns and Symptoms

Sciarid larvae cause damage by feeding on running mycelium and
compost, breaking it down into foul-smelling soggy mass. Symptoms of
sciarid attack include: dead pinheads; pins loosely connected to casing
from severed mycelial connections; brown or black spots on pinheads or
stems; "salt shaker pins" perforated by larval tunnels; and browning
of the stem where cut. Secondary damage comes from their role as
carriers of mites and diseases including Verticillium and Trichoderma.
A single sciarid fly can carry up to 20 mites.

Phorid larvae feed on mycelium and tunnel into mushrooms through the
stem base. Secondary bacterial infections cause brownish
discolorations. Cecid larvae directly consume hyphal contents, and H.
pygmaea produces brown stripes and black gill spots through associated
bacteria. Contamination of harvested mushrooms by tiny larvae is the
main commercial loss from cecid infestation.

## Key Points

- Four main fly families threaten mushroom crops: Sciaridae, Phoridae,
  Cecidomyiidae, and Sphaeroceridae
- Sciarid females lay 150-170 eggs; complete life cycle at 75F is 26-28
  days
- Phorid flies will not lay eggs in total darkness, enabling prevention
  during spawn running
- Cecid flies reproduce by paedogenesis (mother larvae birthing daughter
  larvae) enabling rapid population explosions
- A single sciarid fly can carry up to 20 mites, serving as a disease
  vector
- Pasteurization at 140F for 2 hours is essential to kill all insect
  stages
- Airtight rooms with screened vents and 2% chlorine disinfection are
  primary defenses
- Insect monitors with UV black lights and sticky paper provide early
  detection
