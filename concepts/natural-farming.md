# Natural Farming

Natural farming is an agricultural philosophy developed by Japanese farmer and
philosopher Masanobu Fukuoka (1913-2008). It is founded on the principle of
non-intervention — allowing nature to follow its own course without human
meddling. Fukuoka described it as the "methodless method of nature" and the
"unmoving way of Bodhidharma." Unlike organic farming, which substitutes
natural inputs for synthetic ones but still follows a framework of human
management, natural farming seeks to eliminate all deliberate agricultural
intervention.

## The Five Principles

Fukuoka distilled natural farming into five foundational principles:

1. **No tillage**: The soil is never plowed or turned. Microorganisms,
   earthworms, and plant roots naturally aerate and structure the earth.
   Fukuoka observed that plowing kills soil life and creates hardpan that
   then requires further plowing to break up — a self-perpetuating cycle
   of intervention.

2. **No fertilizer**: Chemical and organic fertilizers are both rejected.
   Soil enriches itself through the natural decomposition of plant residues
   and the activity of soil organisms. The application of compost, while
   seeming beneficial, actually depletes the land from which the organic
   material was originally taken.

3. **No pesticides**: Insects and diseases exist in natural balance.
   Fukuoka observed that when leafhoppers multiplied in his rice paddies,
   spider populations rose correspondingly to control them. Pesticides
   destroy this balance by killing both pests and their natural predators,
   creating conditions that require further chemical intervention.

4. **No weeding**: Weeds are not treated as enemies. Fukuoka used ground
   covers such as clover and allowed certain "weeds" to grow alongside
   crops, finding that they actually contribute to soil health and pest
   regulation. Nature does not arbitrarily designate one plant a weed.

5. **No pruning**: Fruit trees grow best in their natural form. Pruning
  distorts tree shape, creates dense foliage that blocks sunlight, and
   encourages disease. Unpruned trees in their natural habitat produce
   full harvests annually rather than in alternate bearing cycles.

## Philosophical Foundation

Natural farming is rooted in the Zen Buddhist concept of "Mu" (nothingness).
Fukuoka argued that human knowledge and action are the primary causes of
environmental destruction. The more humans attempt to "improve" upon nature
through scientific methods, the further they drift from the natural order.

Fukuoka distinguished sharply between natural farming and what he called
"scientific farming." Scientific agriculture is centrifugal and divergent —
it expands outward through increasing human wants and desires, growing ever
more complex and expensive. Natural farming is convergent — it moves toward
simplicity and non-action. Fukuoka viewed scientific farming as ultimately
doomed to extinction because its outward expansion cannot be stopped.

## Energy Efficiency

Natural farming is remarkably productive when measured by energy return on
labor. Fukuoka reported yields of approximately 130 pounds of rice (200,000
kilocalories) per man-day of labor, without any external inputs. This is
roughly 100 times a farmer's daily caloric intake. By comparison, traditional
farming with draft animals required ten times as much energy input, small-scale
mechanization doubled that again, and large-scale mechanization doubled it
yet again — a geometric progression of diminishing returns.

## Critique of Modern Agriculture

Fukuoka argued that modern agriculture does not truly increase yields above
what nature is capable of producing. Instead, high-yield practices are
attempts to artificially restore productivity after human intervention has
hamstrung nature. Farmers create adverse conditions through tillage,
chemicals, and monoculture, then celebrate their "conquest" of the very
problems they caused.

He also criticized organic farming as merely another form of scientific
agriculture — one that substitutes natural inputs for synthetic ones while
still relying on the same interventionist framework. The gains from organic
farming, he argued, are local and temporary, and when viewed from a broader
perspective, many efforts to "protect" nature through organic methods are
actually destructive.
