# Microscope Identification of Soil Biology

Microscopic identification of soil organisms is the foundation of the Soil Foodweb assessment approach. Direct microscopy allows practitioners to observe, measure, and categorize the living organisms in soil, compost, and compost tea.

## Equipment Required

The primary tool is a compound microscope with differential interference contrast (DIC) optics. A DIC microscope is needed to properly identify nematodes (distinguishing beneficial from root-feeding species). A dissecting scope can show nematode movement (via Baermann funnel extraction) but cannot differentiate species.

SFI has been developing a smaller, more affordable microscope for field use, though intensity of light remains a challenge in the design.

## Identification Methods

### Morphological Direct Counts

The standard SFI approach:
1. Dilute the soil or tea sample
2. Stain sub-samples with activity stains
3. Measure length, width, and number of individuals per organism group
4. Categorize by morphology to identify morpho-species
5. Distinguish active (living, functioning) from total organisms

Activity stains are critical: they differentiate organisms that are alive and performing their functions from the total set that merely has the correct morphology. This distinction between active and total biomass is central to SFI's approach and is something plate counts cannot provide.

### What Can Be Identified

**Bacteria**: Morphological types can be categorized, though molecular methods are needed for species-level identification. The morphological approach underestimates species diversity but is far more useful and less expensive than plate counts.

**Fungi**: Hyphal diameter is a key metric. The most beneficial fungi tend to have the widest diameter hyphae. Pathogens tend to have narrow diameter hyphae. Average hyphal diameter of 2.5 micrometers or greater is desired in compost. Fungi in sick soils generally lack wide-diameter hyphae.

**Protozoa**: Three groups identified by morphology:
- **Flagellates** (smallest, most numerous): First to appear when soil conditions improve
- **Amoebae**: Important bacterial grazers that release plant-available nitrogen
- **Ciliates** (largest): Presence in high numbers indicates anaerobic conditions; generally a warning sign

**Nematodes**: Four functional groups identified:
- **Bacterial-feeding** (beneficial): Consume bacteria, release nitrogen; should be most numerous (10-15/g)
- **Fungal-feeding** (beneficial): Consume fungi; moderate numbers (5-10/g)
- **Predatory** (beneficial): Eat other nematodes including root-feeders; critical but few (1-5/g)
- **Root-feeding** (harmful): Target should be zero; presence indicates soil health problems

Morphological ID of nematodes requires DIC microscopy and training. SFI offers training and accepts samples for identification.

**[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]]**: Assessed on root samples. VAM (vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizae) is differentiated from ericoid and ectomycorrhizal associations. The percentage of root system with mycorrhizal colonization is measured, and disease fungi or necrotic conditions in roots are identified where possible.

## Hyphal Diameter as Health Indicator

Literally the most beneficial species of fungi tend to have the widest diameter hyphae. A few pathogenic species have wide hyphae, but those conditions typically also select for narrow-diameter fungi. A sick soil's fungal community generally lacks wide-diameter hyphae.

The average hyphal diameter of 2.5 micrometers or greater is the target for compost. Below this threshold, the fungal community may not provide adequate [[aact-disease-suppression-mechanisms-ingham]], nutrient retention, or soil structure benefits.

## Nematode Extraction

Active nematodes are extracted from soil using a Baermann funnel. Soil samples are placed in a funnel lined with tissue paper, submerged in water. Nematodes migrate through the tissue into the water over 24-48 hours and collect at the funnel stem for observation.

Samples can also be sent to SFI or to Oregon State Plant Clinic for professional identification.

## Mycorrhizal Assessment Thresholds

- **Below 10-15% VAM colonization**: Inoculum of mycorrhizal fungi will significantly improve colonization. Sources include EPM (http://www.epm-inc.com) and Mycorrhizal Applications.
- **15-40% colonization**: Adding compost, compost tea, or humic acids helps improve levels
- **Above 30-40% colonization**: Root disease problems and root-feeding nematodes begin to disappear. This is the target range for healthy soils.

## Molecular Methods (Future)

[[staycare-molecular-approaches-fungal-bioremediation]] using probe replication and fluorescent excitation can identify beneficial [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] species. The equipment currently costs about $190,000 and there are repeatability issues. PCR isolation and probe development with specificity testing are required.

Molecular methods are already used by SFI for nitrogen-fixation assessment and beneficial organism assays. Most pathogenic bacteria have multiple names depending on which plant they infect, making [[biodiversity-fungal-molecular-identification-dna-barcoding]] essential for accurate disease diagnosis.

## Pleomorphism

Some microorganisms can assume different forms throughout their life cycles. Killing and fixing organisms for observation destroys this dynamic. This is a consideration when interpreting static microscope images versus living system behavior.

## Practical Assessment Protocol

1. Extract active nematodes from soil using a Baermann funnel
2. Observe nematode movement under dissecting scope
3. Transfer to DIC microscope for species identification
4. Prepare soil dilutions for bacteria, fungi, and protozoa counts
5. Apply activity stain to distinguish living from dead organisms
6. Measure hyphal diameters for fungal community assessment
7. Assess root samples for mycorrhizal colonization
8. Compare against desired organism levels for the specific soil type and plant community

## Root-Feeding Nematode Management

If root-feeding nematodes (particularly lesion nematode) are detected, use non-host cover crops to reduce populations. With proper tea applications and non-host crop rotation, root-feeder populations can drop from approximately 100 per gram to undetectable levels in about 6 months.
