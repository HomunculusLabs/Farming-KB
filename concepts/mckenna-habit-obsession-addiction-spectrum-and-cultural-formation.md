# The Habit-Obsession-Addiction Spectrum and Cultural Formation

## The Spectrum of Diminishing Free Will

Terence McKenna opens *Food of the Gods* with a framework that reframes the entire
drug debate. He identifies a spectrum running from **habit** through **obsession**
to **addiction** — a path of "ever-decreasing free will." These three terms are not
rigid categories but signposts along a continuum of behavioral commitment that
underpins all of human culture.

**Habit** is the baseline: "a settled tendency or practice." Habits are familiar,
repetitious, and largely unexamined — simply the things that we do. They form the
substrate of culture itself. As McKenna notes, citing an old adage, "People are
creatures of habit." Culture is largely a matter of habit, learned from parents
and those around us, then slowly modified by shifting conditions and inspired
innovations.

**Obsession** emerges when habits consume us, when devotion to them exceeds
culturally defined norms. The uniquely human dimension of free will feels violated.
People can become obsessed with almost anything: behavior patterns (reading the
morning paper), material objects (the collector), land and property (the empire
builder), or power over others (the politician). The obsessions of ordinary people
tend to focus on immediate gratification through sex, food, and drugs.

**Addiction** represents the far end of the spectrum — an obsession with the
chemical constituents of foods and drugs (metabolites). McKenna emphasizes that
addictions and obsessions are unique to human beings. While animals may show
preferences for intoxicated states, these behaviors are "enormously different"
from the human capacity for self-destructive chemical dependence.

This shift is significant for McKenna's argument because it reveals how the
culture systematically externalizes responsibility for drug use, removing it from

## Culture as Habit and the Narcotized West

McKenna's most radical move is to extend the habit-obsession-addiction spectrum
to culture itself. Culture is largely a matter of habit — learned behaviors,
assumptions, and values transmitted across generations. Yet when contrasted with
the "slower-than-glaciers" modification of species and ecosystems, culture
presents "a spectacle of wild and continuous novelty." If nature represents a
principle of economy, culture "surely must exemplify the principle of innovation
through excess."
