# Soil Foodweb Microbiology

The soil foodweb [[ingham-soil-foodweb-trophic-levels-protozoa-nematodes]] is the complex community of organisms living in soil [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]
that drive nutrient [[ingham-soil-foodweb-trophic-levels-protozoa-nematodes]] cycling, [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]], and plant health. First formalized by
Dr. Elaine Ingham, the soil foodweb concept describes the interactions between bacteria,
fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms that collectively determine
soil fertility and plant vigor.

## The Four Major Groups

### Bacteria

Bacteria are the smallest and most numerous organisms in the soil foodweb. A single gram
of healthy soil can contain between 100 million and 1 billion bacteria. They are the
primary decomposers of simple organic compounds — sugars, simple proteins, and
carbohydrates. Bacterial-dominated soils are characteristic of annual crop systems,
disturbed soils, and early-succession ecosystems. They excel at making nutrients
available from fresh organic matter and are particularly important for brassicas,
lettuce, and other leafy green vegetables that prefer bacterial-dominated soils.

Bacterial biomass in healthy agricultural soil ranges from 100 to 1,000 micrograms per
gram of soil. The ideal ratio of total bacterial to total fungal biomass depends on the
crop: most vegetables prefer a ratio of roughly 10:1 ([[bacterial-to-fungal-soil-succession]]), while
perennials, shrubs, and trees prefer ratios closer to 1:1 or even 0.1:1 (fungal to
bacterial). This distinction is critical for understanding why different crops thrive
in different soil conditions.

Beneficial bacterial species include Pseudomonas fluorescens (a well-known biocontrol
agent), [[bacillus-subtilis]] (which produces antibiotics that suppress pathogenic fungi),
and various species of Azotobacter and Rhizobium that fix atmospheric nitrogen. In
compost tea systems, the goal is to extract and multiply these beneficial populations
from high-quality compost and deliver them to plant surfaces and soil.

### Fungi

Fungi are the second major group and are the dominant decomposers in undisturbed,
late-succession ecosystems such as forests and prairies. Fungal hyphae — the thread-like
filaments that make up the fungal body — can extend for meters through soil, creating
a vast network that transports nutrients, physically binds soil particles into
aggregates, and connects plants in shared [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]].

There are several categories of soil fungi relevant to the soil foodweb. Saprophytic
fungi decompose complex organic materials including lignin, cellulose, and cutin —
compounds that bacteria cannot break down efficiently. These fungi are essential for
decomposing woody materials, leaves, and other recalcitrant organic matter. Beneficial
saprophytic fungi include Trichoderma species, which are aggressive competitors against
pathogenic fungi and are commonly found in high-quality compost.

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots. In exchange for carbon
(sugars) from the plant, mycorrhizal fungi extend the plant's effective root system,
increasing access to water and nutrients — particularly phosphorus, which is relatively
immobile in soil. There are two main types: endomycorrhizae (also called vesicular-
[[glomeromycota-arbuscular-mycorrhizae]] or VAM), which associate with most vegetable crops, grasses, and
annuals; and ectomycorrhizae, which associate with many trees and woody plants. VAM
fungi cannot be grown in compost tea because they require a living root to complete
their life cycle, but their presence in soil is strongly encouraged by fungal-dominated
compost tea applications.

Fungal biomass in healthy agricultural soil should be measured alongside bacterial
biomass. For most [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]], fungal biomass of 100 to 500 micrograms per
gram is desirable. In orchards and vineyards, fungal biomass should exceed bacterial
biomass.

### Protozoa

Protozoa are single-celled organisms that feed on bacteria and are the primary mechanism
by which nutrients mineralized by bacteria become available to plants. When protozoa
consume bacteria, they excrete excess nitrogen in the form of ammonium (NH4+), which
plant roots can immediately absorb. This process — bacterial consumption followed by
nutrient release — is one of the most important pathways for nitrogen cycling in
healthy soil.

The three major groups of soil protozoa are flagellates (the smallest and most numerous),
amoebae (which move by extending pseudopodia), and ciliates (the largest, which are
indicators of anaerobic or disturbed soil conditions). In healthy, well-aerated
agricultural soil, flagellates and amoebae should dominate. High ciliate populations
often indicate compacted, anaerobic, or otherwise stressed soil conditions.

Protozoa require thin water films on soil particles or leaf surfaces to move and feed.
This is one reason why maintaining proper soil moisture and applying compost tea as a
foliar spray during appropriate weather conditions is important — the organisms need
moisture to be active. Protozoan populations of 10,000 to 100,000 per gram of soil are
typical in healthy agricultural systems.
