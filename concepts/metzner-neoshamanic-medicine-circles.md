# Neoshamanic Medicine Circles — Hybrid Therapeutic Rituals

## Overview

Neoshamanic medicine circles are contemporary ritual structures that blend
elements of indigenous shamanic ceremony with Western psychotherapeutic
practice.

Ralph Metzner, who observed and participated in over 100 such circles
across Europe and North America, described them as "hybrid therapeutic-
shamanic rituals" representing a synthesis of the psychotherapeutic and
traditional shamanic approaches.

These circles typically involve plant entheogens such as psilocybin
mushrooms, ayahuasca, San Pedro cactus, or iboga, combined with
meditative, somatic, and therapeutic insight processes.

## Core Structural Elements

Metzner identified six consistent structural elements present across most
neoshamanic medicine circles, drawn from traditional shamanic ceremonies:
the circle formation (participants sitting or lying in a ring); an altar
or fire in the center; an experienced elder or guide, sometimes with
assistants; low light or semidarkness (eye shades sometimes used); music
(drumming, rattling, singing, or recorded music); and dedication of
ritual space through invocation of spirits of the four directions and
elements. A respectful, spiritual attitude is cultivated throughout.

## The Council Practice

A distinctive feature of many neoshamanic circles is the use of a
"talking staff" or "singing staff" — a practice originating among the
Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest, also known as "council."
Only the person holding the circulating staff may sing or speak, with no
discussion, questioning, or interpretation of the kind found in
conventional group psychotherapy. This format creates a container of
respectful listening and prevents the analytical mind from colonizing the
psychedelic experience with premature interpretation. Some sessions
involve minimal or no interaction during the expanded state.

## Participant Profile

Metzner noted that most participants in neoshamanic medicine circles
bring prior experience with one or more psychospiritual practices.

These commonly include shamanic drum journeying, Buddhist vipassana
meditation, tantra yoga, and holotropic breathwork.

Most participants have also engaged in various forms of psychotherapy and
body-oriented therapy, including Reichian, bioenergetic, and somatic
approaches.

The insights from these diverse practices are woven by participants into
their work with the entheogenic medicines.

Participants consistently report that the plant medicines, when combined
with meditative or therapeutic insight processes, function to amplify
awareness and sensitize perception, particularly amplifying somatic,
emotional, and instinctual awareness.

## Preparation and Integration

Neoshamanic circles typically include structured preparation and
integration phases.

Before the circle ritual, participants share their intentions and
purposes, and may engage in meditation, solo time in nature, or
expressive arts modalities such as drawing, painting, or journal work.

After the circle, usually the following morning, an integration practice
helps participants process the lessons learned and consider how to apply
them in their daily lives.

This emphasis on intention-setting before and integration after
distinguishes neoshamanic circles from purely recreational psychedelic
use.

It also reflects the influence of the set-and-setting model, which
Metzner and his Harvard colleagues helped establish in the 1960s.

## Relationship to Indigenous Practice

Metzner was careful to distinguish neoshamanic medicine circles from
authentic indigenous ceremonies.

The neoshamanic form borrows structural elements from indigenous
traditions — the circle, the altar, the fire, the darkness, the music,
the invocation of directions — but adapts them for a Western,
psychotherapeutically informed context.

Participants in these circles tend to have expanded their belief systems
beyond the conventional materialistic paradigm of Western science and
psychology.

While accepting the validity of Western psychological insights, including
those of Freud, Jung, and Reich, they have also come to accept the
reality of nonmaterial spirit beings and to recognize that humans live
in multiple worlds of consciousness.

## Significance for Modern Psychedelic Therapy

Neoshamanic medicine circles represent one of the most direct bridges
between the indigenous shamanic tradition and contemporary psychedelic
therapy.

They demonstrate that elements of traditional ceremonial form — darkness,
music, ritual structure, community container — can be effectively adapted
for therapeutic purposes without appropriating specific indigenous
practices.

The format anticipates many features of modern clinical psychedelic
therapy protocols, including the importance of set and setting, the role
of experienced guides, and the integration of preparatory and follow-up
work.

Metzner's documentation of these circles provides ethnographic evidence
for the natural convergence of shamanic and therapeutic approaches when
both are practiced with integrity and respect for the psychedelic
experience.
