# Client Interview

> From: Bill Mollison, *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (1988), Chapters 3, 14

The client interview and design consultation process is central to permaculture practice. Mollison emphasises that a design is a marriage of landscape, people, and skills in the context of a regional society. The designer must assess not only the physical site but the human resources, financial capacity, and aspirations of the occupants.

## Initial Assessment

Except for the complex subject of village design, a property design from one-quarter to 50 hectares needs firstly a clear assessment of "client or occupier needs", and stated aims or ideas from all potential occupiers (including children). A clear idea of the financial and skill resources of occupants is necessary so that the plan can be financially viable.

The process of building up real friendship between residents, designers, officials, and neighbours should be a conscious part of new initiatives. Small local seminars help a lot, as district skills and resources can be assessed.

## Information to Gather

### Client Resources
- Financial resources available for implementation.
- Physical skills of occupants (building, gardening, animal husbandry, crafts).
- Time available for management and maintenance.
- Special skills or professional knowledge (e.g., a good chemist can process plant oils easily).
- Number of people in the household and ages of children.
- Dietary preferences and food self-sufficiency goals.
- Long-term vision for the property.

### Site Information
- Property size, boundaries, legal status.
- Existing structures and their condition.
- Water sources and quality.
- Soil types and prior land use history.
- Access roads and easements.
- Existing vegetation and any established orchards or gardens.
- Climate data and microclimate observations.
- Any legal restrictions (zoning, covenants, heritage).

### Regional Context
- Local market opportunities for products.
- Nearby towns, gardens, and parks as plant resources.
- District successes and recommended species.
- Native species and exotics that usually accompany recommended plants.
- Community resources and cooperative opportunities.
- Existing networks for trade and barter.

## The Design Report

If a design ended at the physical and human aspects, it would still be incomplete. Careful financial and legal advice, plus an introduction to resources in these areas, and a clear idea for marketing or income from services and products (with an eye to future trends) is also essential.

As every situation is unique, the skill of design (and often of market success) is to select a few unique aspects for every design. These can vary from unique combinations of energy systems, sometimes with surplus for sale, to social income from recreational or accommodation uses of the property. Wherever occupants have special skills, a good design can use these to good effect.

## Site Observation with the Client

With a base map, aerial photograph, or a person as a guide, the designer can proceed to observe the site, making notes and selecting places for:

- Access ways and other earthworks.
- Housing and buildings.
- Water supply and purification, irrigation.
- Energy systems.
- Specific forest, crop, and animal system placement.

All the above are in relation to slope, soil suitability, and existing landforms. By inspection, some priorities may be obvious (fire control, access, erosion prevention). Other factors need to be tackled in stages as time, money, and species permit.

## Design Evolution and Review

At the end of each stage, trial, or project, both past performance and future stage evolution should be assessed, so that a guide to future adjustments, additions, or extension is assembled as a process. It is far better to train an owner-designer who can apply long-term residential management than to rely on a roving designer, except as an aide to initial placements, procedures, and resource listings.

## Effective Working Groups

For community designs, Mollison stresses the importance of effective working groups. There is no better guide to plant selection than to note district successes, or native species and exotics that usually accompany a recommended plant. Nearby towns, in gardens and parks, often reveal a rich plant resource.
