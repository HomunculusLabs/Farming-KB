# Fukuoka: Nature Must Not Be Dissected

In "The Natural Way of Farming," Masanobu Fukuoka presents a
fundamental epistemological critique of modern scientific agriculture:
the belief that nature can be understood by dissecting and analysing its
parts. This argument forms a cornerstone of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy]] and directly challenges the reductionist methodology that
underpins conventional agricultural science.

## The Illusion of Understanding

Scientific farming arose when man, observing plants as they grew, came
to know these and later grew convinced he could raise them himself. But
has man really known nature? Man looks at a stalk of wheat and says he
knows what wheat is. But does he really know wheat, and is he really
capable of growing it?

Man believes that to know something he must take it in his hand,
dissect it, and analyse it. He thinks the best way to learn about
nature is to collect and assemble as much data as possible. In his
efforts to learn about nature, man has cut it up into little pieces.
He has certainly learned many things this way, but what he has examined
has not been nature itself.

## The Living Whole

Nature is a living, organic whole that cannot be divided and subdivided.
When it is separated into two complementary halves and these divided
again into four, when research becomes fragmented and specialised, the
unity of nature is lost. The diagram of factors determining rice
cultivation yields illustrates this: originally, all determining
elements were joined in perfect order under a single conductor's baton
and resonated together in exquisite harmony. Yet, when science inserted
its scalpel, a complex and horrendously chaotic array of elements
appeared. All science has succeeded in doing is to peel the skin off a
beautiful woman and reveal a bloody mass of tissue.

## The Infinite Complexity of a Single Leaf

Even a single leaf presents infinite opportunities for study: the
collection of cells forming the leaf; the nucleus harbouring the mystery
of life; chromosomes holding the key to heredity; how chlorophyll
synthesises starch from sunlight and carbon dioxide; the unseen activity
of roots; the uptake of nutrients; the relationships between soil
components and microorganisms. These are but a few of the inexhaustible
array of topics scientific research has pursued. Yet this very
proliferation of specialised knowledge obscures the essential wholeness
of the living system.

## Limits of Human Perception

When man says he is capable of knowing nature, to "know" does not mean
to grasp and understand the true essence of nature. It means only that
man knows that nature which he is able to know. Just as the world known
to a frog in a well is not the entire world but only the world within
that well, so the nature that man can perceive and know is only that
nature which he has been able to grasp with his own hands and his own
subjectivity. But of course, this is not true nature.

## The Maze of Relative Subjectivity

Fukuoka illustrates this through the metaphor of Okuninushi no Mikoto,
the Shinto deity of agriculture, who carries a sack. People thrust their
hands into the sack, examine the contents, and draw conclusions about
what each object is and its purpose. Yet the truth, known only to
Okuninushi, may be entirely different from any interpretation. Each and
every interpretation carries the risk of grievous error because man
cannot know nature any more than he can know the intentions of a deity.

## Consequences for Agriculture

This epistemological critique has profound implications:

- Scientific research isolates variables but loses the system
- Fragmented knowledge cannot be reassembled into true understanding
- Agricultural practices based on partial knowledge create new problems
- The pursuit of data replaces the cultivation of wisdom
- Specialisation prevents holistic farm management

This perspective directly supports the [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]]. If nature cannot be improved through understanding its parts,
then human interventions based on partial knowledge (tillage, fertilizer,
pesticides, weeding) are inherently misguided. Instead,
[[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy]] argues for trusting the whole
system that science can never fully comprehend.

## The Paradox of Data Collection

Man's awareness is intrinsically imperfect, giving rise to errors in
understanding. Modern research presses forward on morphology, physiology,
ecology, and every conceivable aspect of plants, yet the more data
accumulates, the further we drift from the essential nature of the
organism as a living whole. This connects to [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture]] and [[fukuoka-limits-scientific-method-agriculture]].

## Connection to Mahayana Philosophy

This argument reinforces the [[fukuoka-mahayana-and-hinayana-natural-farming]] framework. Mahayana natural farming accepts that nature's
wholeness transcends human comprehension, while scientific farming's
fundamental error is believing that dissected knowledge can be reassembled
into effective action. [[fukuoka-nature-transcends-human-knowledge]]
extends this argument further.
