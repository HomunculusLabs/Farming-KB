    22|# Brazilian Ayahuasca Churches and Syncretic Entheogenic Ceremony
    23|
    24|In Brazil, the use of [[ayahuasca-brewing-guide]] has been organized into at least three legally
    25|recognized churches that represent a unique form of [[syncretic entheogenic folk-26|ceremony]] blending indigenous Amazonian shamanic practices with Catholic, African, and
    27|esoteric spiritual traditions. The Santo Daime, the Uniao de Vegetal (UDV), and the
    28|Barquinia each emerged in the mid-twentieth century from contact between rubber tappers
    29|working in the Amazon region and indigenous or mestizo [[ayahuasca-brewing-guide]] practitioners.
    30|Ralph Metzner provided a comparative overview of these churches in his 1998 analysis of
    31|[[hallucinogenic drugs in psychotherapy and shamanism]], identifying them as the third
    32|of his [[metzner four paradigms of entheogenic healing]].
    33|
    34|## Historical Origins
    35|
    36|All three churches were founded by rubber tappers working in the Amazon during the
    37|1950s who came into contact with the ayahuasca vine through mestizo or indigenous
    38|ayahuasceros. Each founder reported receiving a significant vision that instructed them
    39|to organize a church using the brew, known variously as "hoasca," "daime," or
    40|"vegetal," as the principal sacrament. The visionary origins of these churches mirror
    41|the foundational role of [[plant teachers]] in indigenous shamanic traditions, where
    42|specific [[psychoactive plants]] are understood to communicate directly with the
    43|practitioner and provide guidance for the formation of healing practices.
    44|
    45|The emergence of these churches represents a significant historical development in the
    46|global history of entheogenic practice: the transition of [[ayahuasca-brewing-guide]] use from the
    47|context of small-group [[shamanic healing and divination]] rituals, where only a
    48|limited number of people came into contact with the medicines, to organized religious
    49|institutions accessible to large numbers of people from all walks of life. This
    50|transition from shamanic to syncretic religious form is one of the key distinctions in
    51|Metzner's four-paradigm framework.
    52|
    53|## The Uniao do Vegetal (UDV)
    54|
    55|The UDV is the largest and most formally structured of the three churches. Its
    56|ceremonies feature participants sitting in rows in straight-backed chairs while
    57|maestres seated around a central table deliver sermons and lead songs. The ceremonies
    58|include a question and answer period, and longer-term members sometimes offer
    59|testimonials of life transformations, a practice reminiscent of Alcoholics Anonymous
    60|confessions. The overall atmosphere is one of orderly, contemplative worship.
    61|
    62|The UDV emphasizes intellectual understanding and doctrinal clarity. The highly
    63|structured ceremonial format reflects a commitment to creating a predictable, orderly
    64|setting for the entheogenic experience, consistent with the [[set and setting]]
    65|principles that govern effective psychedelic work. The formal structure also serves a
    66|protective function, providing a container for the intense experiences that ayahuasca
    67|can produce.
    68|
    69|The research of Charles Grob, Dennis McKenna, and others confirmed that long-term
    70|membership in the UDV and regular consumption of hoasca tea is associated with no
    71|adverse health effects and with a reduction in addictions and other psychopathologies.
    72|This finding is significant because it demonstrates that regular, structured use of a
    73|powerful psychedelic substance within a religious context can be not only safe but
    74|therapeutically beneficial, challenging the assumption that [[hallucinogenic-plants-of-the-world]] use is
    75|inherently risky.
    76|
    77|## The Santo Daime
    78|
    79|The Santo Daime was founded by Raimundo Irineu Serra, a Brazilian of African descent,
    80|and its ceremonies reflect this Afro-Brazilian heritage. The central practice involves
    81|the singing of hymns by the entire congregation, led by a small group of women singers.
    82|Some Santo Daime ceremonies also incorporate dancing in simple rhythmic, swaying steps,
    83|creating an atmosphere reminiscent of African-American gospel services.
    84|
    85|The Santo Daime's ceremonial aesthetics emphasize communal participation and embodied
    86|worship. The hymns, called "hinos," are considered to be received directly from the
    87|spiritual realm and function as vehicles for both individual and collective healing. The
    88|dancing and singing create a rhythmic structure that supports the flow of the
    89|entheogenic experience, similar to the role of [[shamanic singing and icaros in-90|ayahuasca ceremony]] in indigenous Amazonian practice. The kinesthetic element of the
    91|dancing adds a somatic dimension to the experience that is absent from the more
    92|sedentary UDV format.
    93|
    94|The Santo Daime has established significant satellite centers in North America and
    95|Europe, extending the syncretic ayahuasca tradition beyond its Brazilian origins. This
    96|international expansion has brought profound spiritual experiences with entheogenic
    97|plant medicines to a wide spectrum of people in Western industrial societies, many of
    98|whom have no prior exposure to either Amazonian shamanism or Brazilian folk Catholicism.
    99|
   100|## The Barquinia
   101|
   102|The Barquinia church is less well known outside of Brazil but represents a distinct
   103|ceremonial tradition within the Brazilian ayahuasca landscape. It has incorporated
   104|elements of Umbanda, an Afro-Brazilian religious movement that involves contact with
   105|deceased ancestors and deities known as orixas. Barquinia ceremonies involve both
   106|singing and dancing, blending Amazonian ayahuasca practice with African diasporic
   107|spiritual traditions.
   108|
   109|The incorporation of Umbanda elements into the Barquinia represents a further layer of
   110|religious syncretism, adding African spiritual technology to the already complex
   111|mixture of indigenous Amazonian and Catholic elements found in all three churches. The
   112|Barquinia's engagement with Umbanda cosmology, including its complex hierarchy of
   113|spiritual entities and its emphasis on mediumship and spirit possession, adds
   114|dimensions that are less prominent in the more intellectually oriented UDV and the
   115|more hymn-focused Santo Daime.
   116|
   117|## Social and Therapeutic Functions
   118|
   119|Metzner identified several important social functions served by these syncretic
   120|religious movements. The churches provide their members with social cohesion and a
   121|sense of participation and belonging that may be lacking in the increasingly atomized
   122|social landscape of modern Brazil. This community function is particularly important
   123|for working-class and rural populations who may have limited access to other forms of
   124|social support.
   125|
   126|Participation in both the Native American Church in the United States and the
   127|Brazilian hoasca churches has been shown to reduce the incidence of alcoholism and drug
   128|addiction. This anti-addiction effect is consistent with Metzner's observation that
   129|hallucinogens can serve to counteract the "consciousness-contracting and fixating
   130|effect of the addictive narcotics or stimulants." The consciousness-expanding quality
   131|of the ayahuasca experience appears to provide a genuine alternative to the
   132|escape-oriented patterns of substance abuse.
   133|
   134|Anthropologists including Michael Taussig and James Fernandez have noted that these
   135|churches also function as a protective shield of traditional lore against the
   136|encroachments of Christian missionaries and the seductions of Western consumer culture.
   137|This protective function is particularly important for indigenous and mestizo
   138|communities whose cultural traditions have been under sustained pressure since the
   139|colonial period.
   140|
   141|## Legal Status and Cultural Impact
   142|
   143|The Brazilian ayahuasca churches are officially recognized by the Brazilian government,
   144|and the use of ayahuasca is legal within their framework. This legal recognition,
   145|achieved through decades of advocacy and research demonstrating the safety and social
   146|benefits of these practices, represents a remarkable achievement in the history of
   147|entheogenic practice. Most countries prohibit the use of [[psychedelic-plant-sources]] substances
   148|regardless of religious or cultural context, making Brazil's approach uniquely
   149|progressive.
