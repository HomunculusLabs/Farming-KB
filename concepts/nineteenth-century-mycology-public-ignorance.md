### Universal Fear

An audience member at one of Palmer's lectures summed up the common attitude: "Toadstools are nasty things; nobody knows anything about 'em. Let 'em alone, and they'll never hurt you." This reflected what Palmer called "the majority sentiment" — one of complete ignorance masquerading as practical wisdom.

### Magazine Literature

Popular magazines offered little beyond "the rapidity of growth story, of the fungus that grew from iron in one night; the Emperor Claudius story; the Czar Nicholas story; the Russian intoxicant-fungus story, and other traditional lore displayed by facile writers and uninformed editors year by year."

## The International Contribution

### French Mycology

French mycology was significantly ahead of American work. The Moniteur Scientifique of Paris published Palmer's first major article in February 1879, when no American periodical would accept it. The works of Cordier (1875) and Sicard (1883) represented the most comprehensive practical texts available.

### Cross-Atlantic Confirmation

Palmer's theory that the Solanaceae provided antidotes for Amanita poisoning was confirmed independently by multiple researchers:

- Dr. Isaac Ott of the University of Pennsylvania demonstrated atropine's antagonism to muscarine in frog experiments
- A New Jersey physician suggested atropine from belladonna after observing a toadstool-poisoned patient
- Dr. T.F. Wood of North Carolina successfully used daturine and hyoscyamine
- Professor Schiff of Italy advocated stramonium treatment
- Dr. Shadle of Pennsylvania independently arrived at atropine treatment through clinical observation alone

This cross-national convergence of opinion, "between men who have given the subject attention," Palmer called "quite remarkable."
