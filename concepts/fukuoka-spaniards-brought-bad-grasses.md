# The Spaniards Brought Bad Grasses

In *The Road Back to Nature*, [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] traces the [[fukuoka-pine-blight-matsutake-portent]] not to natural climate forces but to a specific historical event: the introduction of invasive pasture grasses by Spanish colonizers. This analysis exemplifies Fukuoka's method of [[fukuoka-observation-based-farming]] leading to ecological insight.

## The Californian Landscape

Upon arriving in California, Fukuoka was struck by the endless expanse of dry, yellow land stretching in every direction. Hour after hour, no matter how far he drove, the scenery remained the same — amber-yellow grasses rather than green.

The land was extremely poor, covered mainly with forage grasses such as [[fukuoka-rice-and-barley-natural-farming]]. Isolated trees of several types, the kind one might expect in a desert, were scattered over the yellow plains. Occasionally, large fields of tomatoes or other crops measuring hundreds of acres would appear, but these were always irrigated. Wherever there was green, water was invariably being drawn for irrigation. Without it, the fields turned into parched grassland.

Even the cattle did not graze in green pastures as one might expect. The green grasses were grown in one special field, while the cattle were left to graze wearily in the middle of a scorching field of yellow grasses.

## Foxtail Invasion and Ecological Collapse

Fukuoka's key discovery came during a visit to a redwood forest about twenty minutes inland from the California coast. This was a virgin forest with trees two or three hundred years old, some measuring twenty or twenty-five feet in circumference. During the last ice age, a few spots in California were spared from the glaciers, creating "glacier forests" where gargantuan trees, some two thousand years old and 400 feet high, still stand today.

An elderly tribal Indian chief served as guardian of the forest. When Fukuoka asked what California had been like long ago, the chief replied that things had "gotten screwed up when the Spaniards arrived and began raising cattle."

After investigating and consulting experts, Fukuoka concluded that the pasture grasses the Spaniards had brought contained [[invasive-species-management]], which came to dominate California's vegetation. The mechanism he identified:

- Foxtail seeds set and mature around June, growing at such density that other plants cannot establish
- Normally, after one plant matures and withers, other plants grow in ongoing seasonal succession
- Foxtail's dominance breaks this succession cycle, creating monoculture yellow grasslands
- The seeds have irritating bristles that catch on clothing and even require surgical removal from dogs and cats
- Birds and beasts spread the seeds, turning entire regions into parched, yellow grassland

## Temperature Amplification

Fukuoka observed that when ground temperature reaches 30 degrees Celsius (86 Fahrenheit), the heat reflected from foxtail-dominated ground raises it to 40 degrees (104 F). This temperature amplification turns the landscape into what he calls a "scorching desert." The vegetation change altered the regional temperature and triggered [[desertification]].

From this, Fukuoka surmised that the vegetation in California underwent a critical change when the Spaniards brought over new grasses. These new grasses chased out the existing mix of plants, altering the temperature of the region and triggering the desertification process.

## Geological Evidence

During a conversation with a mineralogist at the California state environmental agency in Sacramento, Fukuoka learned that:

- The parent rock for Japan is the same as for the region around San Francisco
- Hokkaido shares parent rock with southern Canada; Siberia with Alaska; Southeast Asia with Mexico
- The distribution of parent rock is symmetrical across the Pacific — some specialists believe the Pacific was once a continent
- Japan has Mt. Fuji and California has Mt. Shasta, both large volcanos at similar latitudes
- About one-third of the undergrowth in the redwood forests consists of vegetation similar to that found in Japan

Since the parent rock and grasses are identical, Fukuoka reasoned that California and Japan should have similar climates including similar rainfall. The greatest difference is that Japan has four seasons while California has only winter and summer, with very little rainfall. California became a desert not by natural forces but because "men and machines" altered the land at some point.

## Evidence from Sacramento's Flood History

At his talk before thirty officials at the state capitol, Fukuoka pressed the point further. Old houses still standing in Sacramento had stairways entering directly onto the second floor — designed for access when floodwaters didn't recede. This remained as evidence that two or three hundred years ago, Sacramento, which today lies in the middle of a desert, saw some mighty big floods.

## The Eucalyptus Factor

Traveling through the mountains on the outskirts of San Francisco, Fukuoka encountered large groves of eucalyptus trees — all imported from Australia. The reason they grew so hardily was that there were no native American trees around to speak of. Even the cryptomeria and cypress trees on university campuses did not originally grow there. San Francisco, Berkeley, and Los Angeles were, in Fukuoka's view, "artificial islands in the middle of a desert."

## The Rain Falls from Below

Fukuoka connected the loss of vegetation to a loss of rainfall through his principle that rain "falls from below." If there is vegetation on the ground, water vapor rises, condenses into clouds, and falls back as rain. When the land was transformed into endless yellow stretches of foxtail, clouds no longer formed and rain no longer wet the ground. This created the conditions for the [[fukuoka-deficit-farming]] cycle to take hold.

## Proof from Green and Desert Side by Side

Fukuoka found particularly compelling evidence during a drive to the California coast. Continuing down to the shore, he saw an area on the mountain to his right covered with a green forest. Fifty years earlier, trees similar to Japanese pines had been planted there. Today, this was the site of a luxury housing development. The mountain on his left, however, was a barren desert.

Here, under identical climatic conditions, was an expanse of green on one side and a desert on the other. This stark contrast — same climate, same parent rock, yet vastly different outcomes — convinced Fukuoka that California was not originally desert at all. Something had caused it to dry out, and if so, it might be possible to revive the land.

## The Zen Center Confirmation

Fukuoka visited the Zen Center on the San Francisco coast, started by Shunryu Suzuki Roshi and later continued by Americans. The center had 400 members, with forty priests living there who meditated morning and evening and tended a half-acre vegetable garden during the day. Their pursuit of thought and enlightenment was intricately combined with the farming life.

But the garden, located at the bottom of a valley encircled by eucalyptus trees, was surrounded by yellow, bare-topped mountains. The land was desolate and almost entirely given over to foxtail. A little greenery could be seen — small bushes no more than five or six feet in height — but these looked more like desert vegetation than anything else. There were no useful trees about. This confirmed Fukuoka's sense that even places where people were earnestly trying to live close to nature were embedded in a landscape that had already been fundamentally degraded.

## Broader Implications

This analysis supports Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-view-of-nature-and-human-hubris]] that what appears to be "natural" in America is often a human-created falsehood. His conclusion that "nature as it exists today in California is not true nature at all" extends to his broader argument that modern civilization has everywhere replaced true nature with degraded substitutes.

The Spaniards were also the first to raise cattle in America. The land, which one would normally have expected to become richer as a result of livestock, grew poorer instead — a pattern Fukuoka observed worldwide, concluding that animal husbandry as practiced in modern fashion destroys the [[fukuoka-natural-soil-works-itself]].

## Fukuoka's View of Modern Restoration Efforts

Fukuoka was skeptical of conventional environmental restoration approaches, which he saw as treating symptoms rather than causes. Planting trees without first restoring the soil microflora, he argued, is like building a house on sand. The reason trees planted in California often fail is not that the wrong species were chosen, but that the soil ecosystem has been so degraded by chemical agriculture, overgrazing, and fire suppression that it can no longer support the diverse microbial communities necessary for tree health. His prescription for restoring California would begin not with planting but with allowing nature to recover: ceasing all agricultural inputs, letting weeds grow, and gradually reintroducing the microbial and plant diversity that industrial land management has eliminated.

## The Buffalo Connection

Fukuoka extended his analysis of grassland degradation to include the relationship between native herbivores and grassland health. The American bison, which once maintained the prairie ecosystem through grazing, wallowing, and manure deposition, was replaced by cattle that were managed very differently. Bison moved in large herds across vast distances, grazing intensively for short periods and then moving on, which allowed grasses to recover. Cattle, confined to fixed pastures, graze the same ground continuously, preventing the rest-and-recovery cycle that maintains healthy grasslands. This difference in grazing management, Fukuoka suggested, compounds the damage done by introducing non-native grasses. The combination of inappropriate grass species and inappropriate grazing management created the conditions for the widespread desertification he observed.

## Counterarguments and Scientific Context

While Fukuoka's observations about California's degraded landscape are compelling, mainstream ecologists offer additional explanations for California's Mediterranean climate. The state's summer-dry, winter-wet precipitation pattern is a product of global atmospheric circulation and the rain shadow of the Coast Ranges, not solely of vegetation change. Paleobotanical evidence shows that California has experienced periodic droughts and grassland expansion for thousands of years, long before Spanish arrival. However, Fukuoka's core insight — that human land management decisions have dramatically worsened the natural aridity — is supported by contemporary research on soil carbon depletion, watershed degradation, and the role of soil organic matter in water retention. His observation that vegetation type significantly influences local temperature and rainfall patterns aligns with modern understanding of the soil-plant-atmosphere continuum and the role of evapotranspiration in regional hydrology.

## Fukuoka's Proposed Solution

Fukuoka's prescription for restoring California's landscape was characteristically radical: stop all human intervention and let nature heal itself. He proposed aerial broadcasting of clay pellets containing a diverse mixture of seeds — including native grasses, trees, shrubs, and legumes — along with beneficial microorganisms. The clay pellets would protect seeds from birds and rodents while retaining moisture for germination. Once established, these diverse plant communities would begin rebuilding soil, moderating temperature, and restoring the water cycle. Fukuoka acknowledged that this approach would take decades to show results but argued that any faster solution would merely create another artificial system requiring continued human maintenance — the very problem that caused the degradation in the first place.

## Modern Scientific Validation

Contemporary research in ecohydrology and earth system science provides substantial support for Fukuoka's observations about the relationship between vegetation and climate. Studies using satellite remote sensing have documented the "vegetation-precipitation feedback" — regions with dense vegetation generate more atmospheric moisture through transpiration, which contributes to local and regional rainfall formation. Research on the Sahel region of Africa demonstrated that vegetation loss during drought periods created a positive feedback loop: less vegetation meant less transpiration, which meant less rainfall, which meant further vegetation loss. Conversely, vegetation restoration projects in the Sahel have been associated with increased rainfall. Similarly, research on California's chaparral ecosystems has shown that the replacement of native perennial grasslands with annual Mediterranean grasses reduced soil water infiltration and altered seasonal hydrology, consistent with Fukuoka's analysis.

## Global Implications of Grassland Degradation

The pattern Fukuoka described in California is not unique — similar processes of vegetation change and climatic degradation have occurred worldwide. The introduction of buffelgrass (*Cenchrus ciliaris*) into the Sonoran Desert for cattle forage has dramatically increased fire frequency in an ecosystem that historically burned very rarely, converting diverse desert scrub into buffelgrass monoculture. The replacement of native prairie with introduced grasses in the North American Great Plains reduced soil organic matter by 50-80% in the most intensively farmed areas, with corresponding reductions in water-holding capacity. In Australia, the introduction of gamba grass (*Andropogon gayanus*) increased fire intensity by an order of magnitude, threatening rainforest edges and monsoon forest remnants. These global examples confirm Fukuoka's central insight: introducing inappropriate plant species to new environments can trigger cascading ecological degradation that transforms landscapes fundamentally.

## Historical Ecology of California Grasslands

The transformation of California's grasslands began with Spanish colonization in 1769 and accelerated during the Mexican and American periods that followed. The native perennial grassland, dominated by species such as purple needlegrass (*Nassella pulchra*), wild rye (*Elymus glaucus*), and California oatgrass (*Danthonia californica*), had evolved under grazing pressure from tule elk, pronghorn, and other native herbivores but was adapted to the region's Mediterranean climate with its summer drought period. These native perennials maintained deep root systems that stored carbon, prevented erosion, and facilitated water infiltration into the soil profile. The introduction of annual Mediterranean grasses — including wild oats (*Avena fatua*), ripgut brome (*Bromus diandrus*), soft chess (*Bromus hordeaceus*), and foxtail barley (*Hordeum murinum*) — created a fundamentally different grassland type. These annuals complete their life cycle during the winter-spring rainy season and die by early summer, leaving the soil bare and vulnerable during the hot, dry months. The shift from perennial to annual grassland reduced root biomass by an estimated 70-90%, dramatically reducing soil carbon storage and water-holding capacity.

## Soil Microbiome Disruption and Cascading Effects

Fukuoka's emphasis on soil microflora as the foundation of ecosystem health has gained strong support from modern soil microbiome research. The introduction of exotic grasses disrupted not only the aboveground plant community but also the belowground fungal and bacterial communities that had co-evolved with native perennial grasses. Native perennial grasses support distinct mycorrhizal fungal communities and host endophytic fungi that contribute to plant stress tolerance and nutrient cycling. When exotic annual grasses replaced perennials, the associated microbial communities shifted as well, losing specialist species that depended on native plant hosts. This microbial simplification reduced the functional diversity of the soil food web, impairing nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and soil aggregate formation. The loss of mycorrhizal networks that connected individual plants belowground further weakened ecosystem resilience, as these networks facilitate resource sharing and chemical signaling between plants. Research using high-throughput sequencing has confirmed that restored native grasslands develop more diverse and functionally complex microbial communities than adjacent exotic annual grasslands.

## Fire Regime Alteration and Ecological Feedbacks

The introduction of Mediterranean annual grasses fundamentally altered California's fire regime, creating ecological feedback loops that further degraded the landscape. Native California grasslands and oak savannas historically burned infrequently — typically at intervals of 30-100 years — because the sparse, patchy distribution of fine fuels from native perennial grasses limited fire spread. Mediterranean annual grasses, by contrast, produce abundant, continuous fine fuel that dries to a crisp by early summer, creating conditions that support fire spread every 3-10 years. This increased fire frequency has several cascading effects: frequent fire kills fire-sensitive native perennial grass seedlings before they can establish, while fire-tolerant annual grasses resprout readily from seed after burning. Frequent fire also damages oak woodlands by killing saplings and girdling mature trees, converting oak savanna to annual grassland. Fire-promoted grassland then burns even more readily, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of grassland expansion and fire frequency increase. This fire-grass feedback loop is one of the most dramatic examples of how introduced species can fundamentally alter ecosystem processes, and it aligns precisely with Fukuoka's observation that human-introduced vegetation can create cascading ecological degradation.
