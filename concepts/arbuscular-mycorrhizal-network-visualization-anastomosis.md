# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Network Visualization and Anastomosis

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi develop extensive extraradical mycelium (ERM) that spreads from colonized roots into the surrounding soil, forming networks capable of interconnecting different plants. In vivo model systems have been developed to visualize, quantify, and experimentally study these intact [[mycorrhizal-networks]].

A key feature of these networks is anastomosis, the fusion of hyphae from compatible individuals, which creates functional connections between plants in a community.

## Extraradical Mycelium Structure and Growth

The extraradical mycelium of AM fungi is fundamental to [[plant-nutrient-uptake-mechanisms]] from soil and transfer to host plants. Two-dimensional experimental systems have shown that AM [[dighton-mycorrhizal-networks-carbon-transfer]] spread from colonized roots at growth rates ranging from 738 to 1,067 mm per day, depending on the host plant species. The hyphal extent can reach 10-40 mm of mycelium per mm of root length. This extensive network effectively increases the absorptive surface area of the root system far beyond the physical root-soil interface.

## Hyphal Anastomosis

Anastomosis is the fusion of hyphae belonging to the same or different individuals of an AM fungal species. Successful anastomosis depends on a highly regulated mechanism of self-recognition between compatible hyphae. Hyphae belonging to the same individual and to different individuals of the same isolate undergo successful fusion during both pre-symbiotic growth and the symbiotic phase. The formation of anastomoses between mycelia originating from different root systems colonized by a single AM fungal isolate demonstrates that extraradical networks maintain their self-recognition capacity even after establishment.

## Rejection of Nonself Hyphae

AM fungal hyphae can discriminate against nonself individuals. Hyphae belonging to different genera and species, and even to isolates of the same species originating from geographically different areas, are unable to fuse. These incompatible hyphae show rejection responses, either before or after contact, revealing the hyphal ability to recognize and reject nonself. This self-nonself recognition system has important implications for understanding genetic structure and population biology of AM fungi in natural ecosystems.

## Detection Methods

Anastomosis is detected using a double staining method designed to confirm both hyphal viability and protoplasmic continuity between fusing hyphae. First, germlings are stained for succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) activity, revealed by the deposition of formazan salts in viable hyphae. Second, membranes bearing SDH-stained germlings are mounted on microscope slides and stained with Trypan blue to visualize hyphal walls. All hyphal contacts are observed under light microscopy at magnifications of 125x to 500x and verified at 1,250x. Frequency of anastomoses is calculated by dividing the number of successful fusions by the total number of hyphal contacts.

## Pre-symbiotic Anastomosis

Anastomosis can be studied during the pre-symbiotic phase by pairing individually germinated spores. Spores are surface-sterilized, germinated in sterile distilled water in microtiter plates, and transferred onto membranes placed on moist sterile quartz sand. Pairings of different germlings are made by placing them approximately 1 cm apart. This approach has revealed that AM fungal identity is maintained during the pre-symbiotic growth phase through vegetative compatibility recognition systems.
