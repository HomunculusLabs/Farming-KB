# Biological Pest Control Organisms

Biological pest control relies on living organisms — including fungi, bacteria, protozoa, and predatory arthropods — to suppress pest populations below economically damaging thresholds. Dr. Elaine Ingham's field guide on [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]] Tea (AACT) provides practical case studies and guidance for leveraging these organisms in agricultural and horticultural systems. The central principle is that healthy soil and foliar biology, established through compost and compost tea applications, can replace or dramatically reduce chemical pesticide inputs.

## Mite Biocontrol Using Indigenous Parasites and Pathogens

Many effective biocontrol strategies for mite pests involve parasitic organisms and diseases specific to the target mite species. Just as bacteria can infect and eliminate human pathogens, mites are susceptible to their own suite of parasites and disease-causing organisms. The critical consideration is sourcing these control organisms locally.

Using **local, indigenous parasites and diseases** of mites is essential because endemic strains are already adapted to regional climate conditions, soil types, and the specific pest genotypes present. Non-local biocontrol agents may fail to establish or perform inconsistently. Engaging a local microbiologist, Soil Foodweb Inc. (SFI) advisor, or qualified lab is recommended to identify and culture the appropriate organisms. SFI emphasizes field-level microbiology — working with real-world agricultural and horticultural situations rather than controlled lab conditions. Small research plots (e.g., 10 ft × 10 ft) are generally not meaningful for validating biocontrol efficacy at production scale.

## Bulb Mites: Fungal Feeders, Not Plant Consumers

Bulb mites (*Rhizoglyphus* species) are frequently assumed to be direct plant consumers, but closer examination of their feeding behavior reveals a different story. In many observed cases, insects that people believe eat plants are actually **fungal feeders**. They consume fungi growing on or near plant tissues rather than the plant material itself.

The apparent damage to plants occurs through an indirect mechanism: when the fungal food source is depleted, the mites shift to attacking seedlings and young roots as an alternative food source. Once plants grow larger and more vigorous, mite damage ceases to be a significant problem. The vulnerable window is during germination and early seedling establishment. This understanding changes the management approach — rather than targeting the mites directly with pesticides, building healthy fungal communities and ensuring robust seedling vigor addresses the root cause.

## Eastern Rots: AACT Control Data

Eastern rots represent a challenging disease complex that has been addressed using AACT applications. Documented successes come from practitioners working with SFI labs, including Paul Wagner at the SFI New York lab and James Sottilo. Their results demonstrate that AACT can suppress these difficult rots, though the approach requires attention to tea formulation.

The key to controlling eastern rots with AACT is ensuring that **beneficial fungi are adequately grown in the tea**. Recipes must be specifically designed to promote fungal biomass. Jeff Lowenfels has been among the practitioners actively developing and refining these fungal-dominant tea recipes, with documented positive outcomes. Each disease situation may require recipe adjustments to achieve the right fungal-to-bacterial balance for the specific pathogen pressure.

## Banana Cultivation with AACT

A documented case study from an organic banana grower in Australia (April 2003) demonstrates AACT's effectiveness across multiple pest and weed categories in tropical production:

- **Root-borer control**: Compost and AACT applications effectively suppressed root-borer populations, protecting the root zone of banana plants.
- **Root rot suppression**: The soil food web established through AACT applications outcompeted root rot pathogens, reducing disease incidence.
- **Weed management**: One of the most striking outcomes was a shift in the weed spectrum. The grower reported that ryegrass replaced couch grass in the banana plantation — a transition from a problematic, competitive weed to a more manageable cover crop. This shift reflects improved soil biology and reduced ecological niches for opportunistic weed species.

The grower continued refining the program with the goal of achieving 2.5 boxes of bananas per tree, indicating that AACT is part of an ongoing optimization process rather than a one-time treatment.

## Moss and Algae Control Through Protozoa

Protozoa play a critical role in aquatic systems by consuming algae (phytoplankton). A well-documented example comes from Lake Mendota at the University of Wisconsin, where a severe algal bloom approximately 20 years ago was so dense that you could practically walk on it. Research demonstrated that the solution was straightforward in concept: **restore protozoa populations to sufficient levels**.

However, achieving this required understanding a trophic cascade involving fish species. The chain of relationships worked as follows:

1. Native fish → eat smaller fish → that eat protozoa (zooplankton) → that eat algae (phytoplankton)
2. "Sport fish" (alien invaders) had been introduced and ate the fish that ate the fish that ate the protozoa
3. This disruption removed the protozoa's predators' predators, allowing excessive protozoa predation on their own predators — ultimately removing protozoa and releasing algae from [[gadd-mycelial-network-resilience-grazing-pressure]]

Restoring the native fish species was necessary to re-establish the proper trophic balance and bring the algal bloom under control. The lesson is that biological control in aquatic systems requires getting the **entire food web links correct**, or the wrong organisms dominate.

### Golf Course Ponds

In managed pond systems on golf courses, moss and algae problems often stem from historical pesticide use and high levels of [[ingham-inorganic-fertilizers-soil-biology-damage]]. These inputs eliminated not only fish but also the protozoa that graze on algae. Re-establishing protozoa in these ponds is essential for long-term algae management.

The oxygen dynamics are particularly important: living green algae on the surface add oxygen to the water, but when the algal layer grows too thick, bottom-layer algae die and become food for bacteria. The bacteria rapidly consume oxygen during decomposition, depleting dissolved oxygen faster than the surviving algae can replenish it — creating anoxic conditions harmful to fish and other aquatic life.

## Vineyard Success with SARE Grant

A Sustainable Agriculture Research and Education (SARE) grant project provided rigorous data on AACT effectiveness in vineyard disease management. The key findings include:

- **Fungal biomass is critical**: Adequate fungal biomass on leaf surfaces was the determining factor for disease protection. When fungal biomass in the tea was sufficient, leaf surfaces were protected. When it was limited, protection failed — even though [[fungal-bacterial-biomass-ratio]] was consistently high.
- **Late-season vulnerability**: Late in the growing season, when fungal biomass was limited, the team had trouble maintaining leaf surface protection. This highlights the importance of monitoring fungal biomass throughout the season, not just early on.
- **Chemical spray reduction**: The program reduced sulfur and other chemical sprays from **10–14 applications per season down to just one** — a remarkable reduction for a technology still being optimized for application frequency and concentration.

Roger Hanson at Paso Robles Vineyard achieved two consecutive successful seasons controlling mildew and other pathogens. The essential requirement is **adequate bacterial AND fungal coverage** on the leaf surface.

## Beauveria Fungus for Fruit Fly Larvae Control

*[[beauveria-bassiana]]* is an entomopathogenic fungus that attacks fruit fly larvae in soil. The application protocol involves incorporating Beauveria into compost tea and applying it as a soil drench:

- **Fall application**: 150 liters per hectare as a soil drench around trees, allowing the fungus to establish over winter
- **Spring application**: Another 150 liters per hectare to maintain populations
- **Foliar spray**: Compost tea containing beneficial bacteria that repel adult flies should also be applied to foliage

### Seasonal Timing

Timing is critical because Beauveria cannot reach the maggot inside the fruit. The strategy targets the larval stages in soil — applying the fungus in fall gives it the entire winter to attack overwintering insect larvae. Spring applications reinforce protection before the next generation emerges.

## Beauveria and Humic Acid Relationship

Beauveria demonstrates strong competitive ability in diverse soil environments, and research suggests that **certain humic acids play an important role in supporting its growth and activity**. Humic acids appear to create favorable conditions that help Beauveria establish and persist in the soil food web. When incorporating Beauveria into AACT programs, including humic acid inputs may improve establishment and efficacy.

### Specificity Considerations

There is **specificity between insect-attacking fungi and the target insect species**. Bruce Elliott has worked on matching different fungal species to different insect and fungal diseases and pests. Not every biocontrol fungus works against every pest — proper identification of both the pest and the appropriate control organism is essential.

## Importance of Collecting Organism Survival Data

Dr. Ingham strongly emphasizes the need for empirical validation before investing in commercial biological inocula. The critical questions to answer through data collection are:

1. Do the organisms **survive and grow in the compost tea** during brewing?
2. Do they **survive and grow in the compost** after application?
3. Do they **survive and establish in the soil** under field conditions?

Without this data, practitioners risk spending significant resources on products that may not perform as advertised. Survival through the brewing process, through application, and through establishment in the target environment must all be verified. Biological products are living organisms — their viability depends on temperature, pH, oxygen availability, competition from resident microbiology, and compatibility with the tea [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-and-application]] method. Collecting this data is not merely academic; it is essential for making informed purchasing and application decisions.

## Integrating Biological Control with AACT Programs

Successful biological pest control is most effective when integrated into a comprehensive AACT program. The approach involves three interconnected layers:

1. **Soil drench applications** establish beneficial organisms in the root zone, where they outcompete pathogens, cycle nutrients, and create conditions unfavorable to soil-dwelling pests like root-borers and fruit fly larvae.
2. **Foliar sprays** coat leaf surfaces with bacteria and fungi that occupy infection sites, produce competitive exudates, and repel or suppress leaf-feeding insects.
3. **Compost amendments** provide the foundational biology that supports both soil and foliar applications, ensuring that the organisms introduced through tea have a viable community to join.

The vineyard case study illustrates this integration: bacterial biomass alone was insufficient for late-season protection — fungal biomass was the limiting factor. This underscores that biological control is not about any single organism but about achieving the right balance of the full soil food web.

## Key Principles Summary

- Source biocontrol organisms locally for adaptation to regional conditions
- Understand the actual feeding ecology of pests (many "plant-eating" insects are actually fungal feeders)
- Fungal biomass in compost tea is critical for disease protection on foliage
- [[keystone-species-fungal]] in aquatic systems require restoring complete food webs, not single organisms
- Humic acids support entomopathogenic fungi like Beauveria
- Bacterial inocula can repel leaf-feeding insects but require specificity matching
- Always validate that commercial inocula survive the brewing, application, and establishment process with field data
- Integrate soil drench, foliar spray, and compost amendment for comprehensive protection
- Monitor organism biomass throughout the season — late-season protection requires sustained fungal populations
