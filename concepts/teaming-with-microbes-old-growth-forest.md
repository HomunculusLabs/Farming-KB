# No One Ever Fertilized an Old Growth Forest

The final chapter of *Teaming with Microbes* (Chapter 22) takes its title from one of the book's most powerful observations: no one ever fertilized an old growth forest, yet these ecosystems maintain extraordinary productivity and diversity for centuries. This fact encapsulates the book's entire argument about the self-sustaining power of the [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]].

## The Forest Model

Old growth forests demonstrate that:

- Complex, productive ecosystems can maintain themselves indefinitely without external inputs
- The [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] is entirely capable of cycling nutrients
- Plants, through [[solomon-root-exudates-and-allelopathy]], control their own nutrition
- No fertilizer, pesticide, or herbicide is needed when the soil food web is intact
- Every single plant in a forest produces exudates and attracts microbiology to its rhizosphere, which in turn attracts micro- and macroarthropods, worms, mollusks, and the rest of a complete soil food web

## What Forests Do Differently

Old growth forests operate on principles that gardeners can emulate:

- **Closed nutrient loops** — nutrients are captured and recycled within the system
- **Undisturbed soil** — no tilling, no compaction, no chemical disruption
- **Fungal dominance** — the F:B ratio in forest soils is 10:1 or higher, as detailed in [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]]
- **Species diversity** — multiple plant species support diverse microbial communities
- **Layered organic matter** — leaf litter, woody debris, and humus provide continuous food for decomposers

## Succession and the Fungal Shift

The book describes how plant succession from bare soil to old growth forest is paralleled by a shift in the soil microbial community. Pioneer grasses and weeds support bacterial populations. As shrubs, soft hardwoods, and eventually mature trees establish, more organic matter is produced and fungal biomass grows in proportion to bacteria. Bacteria are limited to digesting simpler compounds, while fungi decompose increasingly complex organic materials including lignin. This natural progression moves from bacterially dominated early-succession soils toward the highly fungally dominated soils of old growth forests, a pattern central to [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]].

## The Gardener's Lesson

The forest model suggests that gardeners should:

- Focus on building and maintaining the soil food web rather than feeding plants directly
- Minimize soil disturbance (reduce or eliminate tilling)
- Maintain organic matter inputs through [[query-how-do-different-composting-methods-compare]] and mulching-guide|mulching
- Support fungal communities for perennial plantings with [[mycorrhizal-fungi-basics-lowenfels]]
- Emulate natural succession patterns

## The Comparison

The book implicitly compares modern gardening and agriculture to forestry:

- Modern gardening with chemical inputs is like intensive agriculture — productive in the short term but degrading over time
- Forest ecosystems represent the ultimate sustainable model
- The goal of soil food web gardening is to move the garden ecosystem closer to the forest model
- This doesn't mean converting gardens to forests, but adopting the principles that make forests self-sustaining

## Restoring the Soil Food Web

Lowenfels and Lewis emphasize that before construction, traffic, rototilling, and chemical applications, a healthy soil food web existed in every yard. It can be returned and even improved. With microbiology restored, soil structure improves, [[mycorrhizal-fungi-basics-lowenfels]] help all plants get the nutrients they need, pathogens face fierce competition, plants receive the kind of nitrogen they prefer, water drainage and retention improve, pollutants are decayed, and food tastes better. The gardener has many helpers and no need for chemicals that may harm family, pets, or the environment.

## A Philosophical Statement

The title is more than an observation — it's a philosophical statement:

- Nature has been managing soil fertility for hundreds of millions of years
- Human intervention (fertilizers, pesticides) is a recent experiment with mixed results
- The smartest thing gardeners can do is to support natural systems rather than replace them
- Trusting the soil food web is both more effective and less work than the chemical approach

## Key Points

- Soil microorganisms form the foundation of terrestrial ecosystem health
- The soil food web includes bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and arthropods
- Healthy soils require a balanced ratio of different microbial communities
- [[cervantes-compost-tea-organic-brewing]] and biological amendments can boost beneficial microbial populations
- Chemical inputs often disrupt the delicate balance of soil ecosystems
