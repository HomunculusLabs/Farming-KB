# Bokashi Composting

Bokashi is a Japanese composting method that uses [[jadam-anaerobic-fermentation-principles]] with [[query-how-do-you-make-effective-microorganisms-em-for-agriculture]] (EM) to pre-digest organic waste. Unlike traditional aerobic composting, bokashi works through lactic acid fermentation, similar to making sauerkraut or kimchi. The process is fast, low-odor, and can handle all food waste including meat, dairy, and cooked foods that would cause problems in conventional compost.

## What is Bokashi?

"Bokashi" is a Japanese term meaning "fermented organic matter." The method was developed by Dr. Teruo Higa in the 1980s and is rooted in Korean Natural [[natural-farming-principles-fukuoka]] of microbial management. The bokashi process does not produce finished compost -- it produces pre-fermented material that must be buried in soil to complete decomposition.

## Effective Microorganisms (EM)

The active ingredient in bokashi is a consortium of beneficial microorganisms, most commonly:

- Lactobacillus species -- produce lactic acid, lower pH, suppress pathogens
- Yeast (Saccharomyces spp.) -- produce vitamins, enzymes, and growth factors
- Phototrophic bacteria (Rhodopseudomonas spp.) -- synthesize amino acids and bioactive substances
- Bacillus subtilis -- produces antimicrobial compounds, aids decomposition
- Filamentous fungi (Aspergillus, Penicillium) -- break down complex organic compounds

These organisms work synergistically to ferment organic matter under anaerobic conditions, producing a pre-digested material that decomposes rapidly once exposed to soil biology.

## EM Bran Inoculation

The EM culture is typically inoculated into a carrier substrate, most commonly wheat bran:

Ingredients:
- Wheat bran -- 10 lbs
- EM-1 concentrated [[jadam-microbial-solution-jms-preparation-and-use]] -- 2 tablespoons
- Molasses -- 2 tablespoons (food source for the microbes)
- Chlorine-free water -- 2-3 cups (enough to moisten bran to ~35% moisture)

Process:
1. Dissolve molasses in warm water (110F)
2. Add EM-1 solution and mix
3. Let the EM-molasses solution sit for 30 minutes to activate
4. Combine with wheat bran in a large container
5. Mix thoroughly, ensuring even moisture distribution
6. Moisture target: squeeze a handful -- it should hold shape without dripping
7. Pack tightly into a plastic bag, removing as much air as possible
8. Ferment at room temperature (70-80F) for 2 weeks
9. The bran is ready when it has a sweet-sour fermented smell (like pickled vegetables)
10. Store in airtight container out of direct sunlight; shelf life is 6-12 months

## The Bokashi Fermentation Process

### Equipment
- Bokashi bin with airtight lid and drainage spigot (commercial or DIY from two 5-gallon buckets)
- EM bran (purchased or homemade)
- Kitchen scraps of all types

### Step-by-Step Process

1. Add food waste to the bokashi bin in layers no more than 1-2 inches thick
2. Sprinkle a thin, even layer of EM bran over each layer of food waste
3. Press down firmly to remove air pockets (use a potato masher or similar tool)
4. Seal the lid tightly to maintain anaerobic conditions
5. Drain the bokashi tea (liquid leachate) every 2-3 days through the spigot
6. Repeat until the bin is full (typically 2-4 weeks for a household)
7. Seal the full bin and let it ferment for an additional 2 weeks at room temperature
8. Bury the fermented material in soil to complete decomposition

### Bokashi Tea (Leachate)
The liquid that drains from the bokashi bin is a nutrient-rich byproduct. It contains metabolites from the fermentation process and some nutrients from the food waste.

Uses:
- Dilute 1:100 with water as a soil drench (fertilizer)
- Add to septic systems (helps break down waste)
- Pour down drains to reduce odor
- Do NOT use undiluted on plants (too acidic, can burn)

Note: Bokashi tea is anaerobic and can become foul-smelling if stored. Use within 24 hours of collection.

## Soil Burial

After fermentation, the bokashi material must be buried in soil where aerobic microbes complete the decomposition:

1. Dig a trench or hole 8-12 inches deep
2. Add the fermented bokashi material
3. Mix lightly with surrounding soil
4. Cover with at least 4-6 inches of soil
5. The material will decompose into rich, dark soil within 2-4 weeks
6. Do not plant directly into fresh bokashi burial for 2-3 weeks (too acidic)
7. After decomposition, the area will be exceptionally fertile

Alternative: add fermented material to a traditional compost pile as an accelerator.

## Pros and Cons

### Advantages
- Accepts ALL food waste: meat, dairy, bones, cooked food, citrus, onions
- Fast processing: 2 weeks fermentation + 2-4 weeks soil burial
- Compact: works in a small bucket under the kitchen sink
- Low odor: sealed system produces minimal smell (slightly sour/fermented)
- No turning required: no physical labor beyond pressing layers
- Nutrient retention: anaerobic process retains more nitrogen than hot composting
- Works year-round: indoor system unaffected by freezing temperatures
- Scalable: works for single person or large family

### Disadvantages
- Not finished compost: requires soil burial for final decomposition
- Requires purchased or prepared EM bran (ongoing cost)
- Needs soil for burial: apartment dwellers may not have garden space
- Strict anaerobic requirements: opening the lid frequently introduces oxygen
- Learning curve: correct moisture and bran application takes practice
- Potential for mold: white mold is normal (beneficial), but green/black mold indicates failure

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Strong putrid smell | Oxygen contamination or too much moisture | Add more EM bran, press tighter, ensure lid is sealed |
| White fuzzy mold on surface | Normal -- beneficial fungi | No action needed |
| Green or black mold | Failure of fermentation | Add more EM bran, bury immediately in soil |
| Fruit flies | Lid not sealed properly | Check lid gasket, ensure tight seal |
| Liquid not draining | Spigot clogged or insufficient food waste | Clear spigot, add more high-moisture scraps |
| Material looks unchanged after burial | Soil too cold or dry | Wait longer (up to 6 weeks in cold conditions), keep moist |
