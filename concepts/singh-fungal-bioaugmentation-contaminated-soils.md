# Bioaugmentation of Contaminated Soils with Fungi

Bioaugmentation is the deliberate introduction of selected microorganisms
to contaminated environments to enhance the degradation of pollutants.
In the context of mycoremediation, fungal bioaugmentation involves
introducing specific fungal strains or consortia to contaminated soils
to accelerate the removal of organic and inorganic pollutants. Singh's
review identifies bioaugmentation as one approach to soil bioremediation
that has been used when insufficient native microorganisms are present
at contaminated sites.

## Principles of Fungal Bioaugmentation

Fungal bioaugmentation targets sites where the indigenous microbial
community lacks the metabolic capacity or sufficient population to
degrade target contaminants efficiently. The approach involves
introducing high-quality fungal inoculum with high degradation potential
to contaminated soils. Bioaugmentation has been used on contaminated
sites when not enough of the appropriate microorganisms are present.
In recent years, fungal-bacterial co-cultures have demonstrated enhanced
detoxification of pollutants, opening new horizons in biodegradation
processes. These co-cultures and bioaugmentation approaches are employed
to overcome limited metabolic capabilities, though they also suffer from
certain limitations.

## Fungal Inoculum Production

Preliminary methods have been developed for the production of fungal
inocula for bioaugmentation of contaminated soils. These inocula can be
in the form of pelleted solid substrates coated with fungal mycelium,
prepared from cheap agricultural and industrial by-products. Pelleted
inocula have advantages over earlier fungal carriers such as corncobs,
wood chips, or straw, including better handling, improved survival
during transport and application, and enhanced competitiveness against
native soil microbes. Fungal inocula can be optimized in substrate
composition to enhance fungal growth, degradation abilities, and
competitiveness against native soil microbes.

## Biomass Assessment for Bioaugmentation

To optimize fungal growth and degradation ability, methods to measure
biomass are needed. The difficulty of separating fungal mycelia from
solid substrates leads to indirect methods for estimating biomass and
activity. These include methods measuring cell constituents, biological
activity, and nutrient consumption. The fluorescein diacetate (FDA)
hydrolyzing activity assay is employed for microbial biomass growth,
measuring the activity of multiple enzymes simultaneously. The FDA assay
evaluates biological potential of solid pellet inoculums for
bioaugmentation of contaminated soils with white-rot fungi.

## Phanerochaete chrysosporium Bioaugmentation

Phanerochaete chrysosporium has been extensively studied for
bioaugmentation applications. The FDA activity of P. chrysosporium
grown in pellets correlates with mycelia dry weight and ergosterol
content. In addition to optimum substrate composition, optimum
temperature and pellet structure influence high metabolic activity,
proliferation, and pollutant tolerance. P. chrysosporium and Trametes
versicolor introduced on contaminated soils using pellets with high
nitrogen content (C:N ratio of 50:1) show different removal patterns
compared to pellets with lower biological potential (C:N ratio of 309:1).
Engineered soil cells have led to successful mycoremediation of aged
pentachlorophenol-contaminated soil through bioaugmentation.

## PCB-Contaminated Soil Bioaugmentation

Bioaugmentation has been applied to PCB-contaminated soils using
fungal formulations. The bioaugmentation of PCB-contaminated soil
through fungal inoculation with white-rot fungi has been studied in
solid culture systems. During bioaugmentation of artificially
contaminated soil, specific fungal strains demonstrated the ability
to transform chlorinated biphenyls. The success of bioaugmentation
depends on the survival of introduced fungi in competition with native
microorganisms, the bioavailability of target pollutants, and
environmental conditions including moisture, temperature, pH, and
nutrient availability.

## Compost Bioaugmentation for PAH Removal

The influence of compost bioaugmentation on benzo[a]pyrene removal in
contaminated soil has been evaluated using P. chrysosporium. Composting
is one of the most promising reactor systems for hazardous soil
treatment, applicable to soils contaminated by PAHs, explosives, and
pesticides. The removal efficiencies were 62.8% and 65.6% for fungal
amended and unamended compost systems, respectively. In these cases,
bound residue formation is the primary mechanism for benzo[a]pyrene
removal in active compost systems. The compost matrix provides both
nutrients and a supportive environment for fungal growth and enzyme
production.

## Growth Promotion in Soils

A practical method has been developed to promote the growth of white-rot
fungi in soil without using large quantities of amendments or inoculum.
In Trametes versicolor, manganese peroxidase and laccase activities
increase with addition of either a carbon or nitrogen source and are
enhanced by addition of both supplements. Alfalfa is a more effective
nutrient source than other amendments. Fungi colonize alfalfa-amended
nonsterile soil despite competition from other microorganisms.
Mineralization of pentachlorophenol is more rapid in soil amended with
alfalfa and benomyl and inoculated with T. versicolor, achieving about
1.25% of 30 ppm PCP degraded to CO2 per day during a 4-day period.

## Limitations and Challenges

Despite its promise, fungal bioaugmentation faces significant
challenges. To date, studies on the role of fungal bioaugmentation at
PAH-contaminated sites remain limited. No doubt, fungal-bacterial
co-cultures and bioaugmentation are employed to overcome limited
metabolic capabilities, but these also suffer from certain limitations.
Key challenges include the survival of introduced fungi in competitive
soil environments, the bioavailability of pollutants in aged
contaminated soils, scale-up from laboratory to field conditions, and
the formation of bound residues rather than complete mineralization.
Little is known about the biodegradation of complex mixtures of
pollutants by introduced fungi in field conditions.

## Future Perspectives

The preferential oxidation of one pollutant over another in complex
mixtures will be a topic of great interest. Extensive research on the
enzymes involved in metabolic pathways is necessary to optimize the
biodegradation process by introduced fungi. Genes encoding enzymes
involved in the degradation of high-molecular-weight pollutants must be
cloned, sequenced, and characterized. The development of selective
bioengineered fungi could open a new era in bioaugmentation for soil
remediation. An understanding of metabolic pathways and metabolites is
crucial to the development of successful strategies for mycoremediation
in field soils.
