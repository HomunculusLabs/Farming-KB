## Overview

Buffering is the ability of the soil to absorb acids without changing pH. Carbon dioxide forms a
weak acid that breaks apart some water molecules in soil. In organic soils and soils with lots
of clay, the hydrogen cation is tied up, leaving the hydroxyl anion in solution. This causes the
percentage of hydrogen ions, the measure of acidity, to go down. Applications of organic matter
generally increase the pH.

In a natural system, the plant controls its rhizosphere pH. Plants synthesize and release exudates
of various components that adjust the pH. Acidic exudates attract fungi and alkaline ones attract
bacteria. The plant has a lot of control over the pH of the soil in which it grows, because pH
is important to how and whether nutrients are available.

## Correcting pH

The usual recommendation for acidic soils is to apply finely ground calcitic limestone (CaCO3) or
dolomitic limestone (CaMg[CO3]2). Calcitic lime is used for soils with high magnesium levels and
dolomitic lime for soils low in magnesium. The rate of application is directly related to the
buffering pH of the soil, so a soil test is very important. The limestone binds with hydrogen ions,
and the end product is carbonic acid (H2CO3), which further breaks down into carbon dioxide (CO2)
and water (H2O).

Correcting alkaline soils usually involves applications of sulfur and water, which becomes sulfuric
acid. The acid reacts with the soil chemicals and becomes gypsum (CaSO4).
