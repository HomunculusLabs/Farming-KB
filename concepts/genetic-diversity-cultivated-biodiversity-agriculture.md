## Genetic Diversity and Cultivated Biodiversity in Agriculture

Genetic diversity within species and cultivated biodiversity in agricultural systems are critical yet often overlooked dimensions of the broader diversity principle in [[permaculture]]. While species-level biodiversity receives most public attention, the variation within species — genetic, varietal, and age-related — and the diversity of human-managed agricultural systems are equally important for resilience, adaptation, and long-term sustainability.

## Genetic Diversity Within Species

Focusing solely on species count misses a crucial factor: the role of **diversity within dominant species**. Many widespread and dominant plants show great varietal diversity that is essential for ecosystem function and evolutionary potential.

### Provenance Variation

Widespread eucalypts such as manna gum (*[[eucalyptus-viminalis]]*) and red gum (*E. camaldulensis*) in southern Australia show great variation between provenances and even subspecies. This variation reflects:

- **Local adaptation** to specific environmental conditions — soil type, rainfall patterns, temperature extremes, frost frequency
- **Geographic isolation** from other populations of the same species, allowing genetic drift and local specialization

These natural variations between regional types are of critical importance as a **foundation for evolution of new species** and for selection and breeding of plants and animals for human use. Without this provenance variation, both natural adaptation and human-directed breeding would lack raw material.

### Individual Variation Within Populations

Within any local population of common and dominant plants, great individual variety is common. This genetic diversity serves essential functions:

- **Adaptation to changing conditions** — more vigorous individuals reproduce more successfully as conditions shift, allowing population-level adaptation without requiring new mutations
- **Resistance to catastrophic loss** — environmental conditions rarely remain stable long enough for one genotype to completely dominate; positive feedback reinforcing a particular characteristic gets periodically reversed
- **Maintenance of latent potential** — traits that appear "undesirable" under current conditions may become advantageous when conditions change, so the population retains genetic reservoirs

### The Paradox of Natural Selection

It is perhaps surprising that natural selection does not produce monocultures of identical individuals. Plant and animal breeders understand how difficult it is to completely breed out so-called undesirable traits, and how quickly they reappear once selection pressure is withdrawn. This occurs because:

- Environmental conditions fluctuate, periodically reversing which traits are advantageous
- Mutation and other "creative" forces continually generate novel diversity
- Heterozygosity (genetic diversity within individuals) often confers advantages through hybrid vigor

Nature rarely places all her eggs in one basket — genetic diversity at every level is a proven survival strategy.

## Pre-Industrial Cultivated Diversity

In pre-industrial agriculture, **polyculture was the norm**. Where monoculture existed, it was on a small scale. The reasons for this polyculture were themselves diverse and deeply practical.

### Self-Reliance Demands Diversity

Where agriculture provides for household subsistence needs rather than serving markets, crop diversity is essential for three reasons:

1. **Nutritional completeness** — no single crop provides all necessary nutrients. In many regions of India, legume crops such as lentils provide protein nutritionally complementary to grains, while simultaneously fixing nitrogen and providing a disease break for staple rice crops.

2. **Variety and satisfaction** — diverse diets satisfy human needs for sensory variety, cultural food traditions, and culinary interest.

3. **Regular supply** — different crops mature at different times, providing continuous harvests rather than seasonal glut and scarcity.

When central markets redistribute food, they provide for diverse human needs through marketplace variety rather than on-farm diversity. This makes monocultural specialization possible but damages the agricultural ecosystem"s ability to provide for people's needs independently of global supply chains.

### Security from Diversity

Crop diversity provides **insurance against failure**:

- **Seasonal variation** — different varieties and species respond differently to weather patterns, ensuring that a bad season for one crop may be a good season for another
- **Pest and disease resistance** — monocultures are vulnerable to catastrophic pest outbreaks; polycultures limit pest spread through physical separation, varied phenology, and natural predator habitat
- **Trade-offs in plant selection** — there is typically a trade-off between high yield and easy harvesting on one hand, and drought tolerance, pest resistance, and general hardiness on the other. Peasant farmers persist in cultivating lower-yielding varieties alongside higher-yielding ones precisely because the hardy varieties provide security.

### Cultural Maintenance

A third reason for crop diversity transcends practicality: people grow varieties for **aesthetic, sentimental, cultural, and spiritual reasons**. Growing Grandpa's special bean, or maintaining a variety that represents cultural heritage, celebrates lineage and acknowledges that not everything of value must be "productive." This commitment to diversity functions as homage or a symbolic tithe to nature"s abundance, acknowledging the importance of things beyond immediate material needs.

## The Green Revolution and Lost Diversity

### Trade-Offs of "Improved" Varieties

The "improved" strains of rice and wheat constituting the Green Revolution were selected for performance under a novel set of conditions provided by non-renewable resources and technology — specifically, synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and irrigation. The developers appeared unaware of the trade-off between monocultural productivity and resilience.

The "deeper wisdom" behind the Green Revolution assumed that inputs of fertilizer, pesticides, and water would compensate for lost resilience. These inputs were to be purchased from multinational corporations (or, for water, supplied by large government dam and irrigation projects funded by international aid and built by corporations).

### Crop Breeding Blindness

The concept of "improving" already highly bred traditional crops through modern breeding is often erroneous. What passes for improvement is typically:

- An **unconscious shift in selection criteria** — breeding for machine harvestability, uniform ripening, transport durability, or visual appearance rather than flavor, nutrition, or resilience
- **Blindness to lost attributes** — the infamous machine-harvestable tomato with no flavor exemplifies how changing selection criteria destroys other values
- **Failure under organic conditions** — some trials have shown that "improved" market hybrids consistently fail to yield as much as traditional varieties even with optimum organic fertilization and watering

### The Australian CSIRO Example

The early history of [[csiro]]"s scientific crop improvement gave priority to hardiness and natural disease resistance, establishing low-input farming as the norm in Australia long before current environmental concerns. However, diminishing returns from traditional breeding, combined with a shift from public to corporate funding, drove CSIRO toward genetic engineering and corporate objectives.

## Animal Breeding and Diversity

### Rare Breeds as Genetic Resources

Maintenance of rare livestock breeds is an important dimension of agricultural biodiversity conservation. Like [[heirloom-vegetable-varieties]], rare breeds of poultry, cattle, horses, and dogs represent careful selection over many generations for characteristics adapted to specific uses and environments — characteristics frequently appropriate to permaculture-designed systems.

Historically, breeding selection balanced **conformation** (physical appearance) with **performance criteria** including:
- Ability in the chosen task (herding, hunting, pulling, retrieving)
- Fecundity and reproductive health
- Growth rate on available forage
- Disease resistance
- General hardiness in local conditions

### Industrial Animal Husbandry

Modern production livestock breeding has separated and intensified selection. Elaborate performance criteria for factory farming of poultry, pigs, and beef and dairy cattle are turning animals into biological machines barely able to exist outside intensive rearing facilities. Vandana Shiva describes this as "the monoculture of the mind" — the most limited yardsticks measuring productivity without reference to wider context or adverse side-effects.

Over-bred European and North American cattle breeds continue to be shipped to Third World aid projects despite well-documented evidence that these breeds are unsustainable and inappropriate in tropical and subsistence conditions.

### The Breed Fancier Problem

Breeders of poultry and pet animals, especially dogs, have ruined many breeds they sought to conserve through **breeding by conformation without performance and hardiness criteria**. These criteria prevailed when animals were useful rather than ornamental:

- A Collie with the desired long narrow nose might also be small-brained and skittish — characteristics that would have eliminated it from breeding when dogs worked for a living
- [[black-australorp]] poultry have been maintained by fanciers with little interest in foraging ability or free-range egg production
- Working dog breeds like Blue Heelers and Kelpies face threats from popularity as pets, which selects for appearance over the intelligence and energy that make them valuable

It is relatively easy to restore wild hardiness in poultry and livestock through free-range breeding, but in the process, productivity and domestic characteristics are lost. Within a few generations, domestic birds can revert toward wild jungle fowl — impressive and hardy, but nearly impossible to find eggs from or contain in a run.

### The Purity Paradox

Diversity in animal breeding presents a paradox: traditional breed diversity is maintained by **purity of breeding** to exclude wild diversity and hybridization that would destroy the breed. But without an appropriate environmental context that tests functional performance, purity maintenance destroys the breed through inbreeding depression.

This paradox parallels debates over functionalism and aesthetics in horticulture and architecture. The balance between productivity and aesthetics, purity of line and hybrid vigor, fixed specialized form and diversity, is delicate and must be reconsidered in every situation and context.

## Food Fermentation Cultures

A subtle, invisible example of lost cultural food diversity is **microbiological diversity in food fermentation cultures**. The continuously maintained sourdough culture of a traditional European bakery was a co-evolved ecosystem of many different yeasts and microorganisms, reflecting local grain varieties, baking methods, and the microbial environment of often-ancient bakery buildings. Each village"s bread was unique.

In the 1920s, Japanese food scientists began "purifying" the culture for miso production in the interests of efficiency. Today, virtually all the world's fermented foods are produced with pure (monocultural) starter cultures. The diversity and complexity of what has been lost is incalculable, and recreating such co-evolved microbial ecosystems may take centuries of stable conditions.

## Key Principles for Design

1. **Maintain genetic diversity** — avoid genetic monocultures even within single species; use multiple varieties and provenances
2. **Select for resilience, not just yield** — hardiness, disease resistance, and adaptability to local conditions are as important as productivity
3. **Preserve traditional varieties** — they represent accumulated genetic wisdom adapted to specific conditions over generations
4. **Balance purity with hybrid vigor** — both pure-line breeding and planned hybridization have roles; neither should dominate exclusively
5. **Test in context** — breeding criteria must include performance in the actual environmental and management conditions where the organism will live
6. **Value non-productive diversity** — aesthetic, cultural, and spiritual dimensions of diversity have functional value in maintaining human commitment to diverse systems
