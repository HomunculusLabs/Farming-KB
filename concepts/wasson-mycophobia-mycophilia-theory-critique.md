# Critique of Wasson's Mycophobia and Mycophilia Theory

[[gordon-wasson-comparative-method-ethnomycology]]'s theory that the world's peoples could be divided into
mycophilic (mushroom-loving) and mycophobic (mushroom-fearing) cultures
became one of the most influential ideas in ethnomycology. However,
subsequent scholarship has revealed serious problems with both the
[[holmgren-permaculture-flower-conceptual-framework]] and the evidence Wasson marshalled in its support.
The theory, while historically important, has been substantially
undermined by anthropological, empirical, and logical objections.

## The Frazerian Comparative Method

Wasson's approach was rooted in the comparative method pioneered by
Victorian anthropologist James Frazer, whose Golden Bough attempted to
identify universal patterns in human culture by cherry-picking evidence
from sources widely separated in distance and time. This method strips
cultural practices of their essential context, producing a distorted
picture. Later anthropologists, particularly those influenced by Marxist,
feminist, and postmodernist thought, challenged Frazer's approach
emphatically. The discipline increasingly favoured localised ethnographic
fieldwork as the proper basis of anthropological inquiry.

Wasson's particular deployment of the comparative method was especially
problematic. He selected evidence that supported his thesis while
ignoring counterexamples, and drew connections between cultures whose
mushroom practices had entirely different meanings and functions. His
theoretical framework was imposed upon the data rather than derived
from it.

## The Problem with "Phobia"

The very premise that peoples are either mycophilic or mycophobic is
itself flawed. The term "phobia" implies an overwhelming and irrational
panic reaction, a force that bears down upon the sufferer and originates
deep within the unconscious. Yet anxieties about mushrooms are wholly
rational. In the absence of reliable methods for distinguishing edible
from poisonous species, and given the high price of getting the decision
wrong (a slow and painful death in the case of [[query-how-to-identify-and-avoid-the-death-cap-amanita-phalloides]], Amanita
phalloides), blanket avoidance of all mushrooms is the most sensible
and reasonable option. Fear of mushrooms is actually the fear of being
poisoned, no more irrational than aversion to other life-threatening
dangers.

## Empirical Evidence Contradicts the Thesis

A long-term study conducted during the 1970s in Colorado, where there
was no widespread custom of gathering [[edible-wild-mushrooms-by-region]], found an average
of only six cases of accidental poisoning per year requiring hospital
treatment. Fatalities were so rare as to be virtually non-existent. By
contrast, in the Polish province of Poznan, which has a similar
population size but a long-standing folk tradition of mushroom-picking,
an average of fifty incidents per year required hospital treatment, of
which around ten per cent proved fatal. The risk of being killed by
mushrooms was substantially higher in the nominally mycophilic culture,
directly contradicting Wasson's thesis.

This suggests that folk wisdom regarding [[psilocybin-mushroom-identification-guide]] is
worryingly unreliable: without a decent [[field-guide-psilocybin-bluing-reaction-identification]], the safest option
remains not to go mushroom-picking at all. The data undermines the
notion that mycophilic cultures possess some special, inherited
relationship with fungi.
