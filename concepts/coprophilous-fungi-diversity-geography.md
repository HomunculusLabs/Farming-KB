# Coprophilous Fungi Diversity and Geography

Source: Biodiversity of Fungi (Hyde et al., Ch. 21)

Coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungi represent one of the most speciose and ecologically important guilds of [[comparison-mycorrhizal-vs-saprotrophic-fungi]], playing a critical role in nutrient cycling by decomposing animal dung and recycling micronutrients back into ecosystems. Their diversity encompasses zygomycetes, ascomycetes, basidiomycetes, myxomycetes, and even myxobacteria.

## Global Diversity Patterns

Coprophilous fungal diversity exhibits a strong latitudinal gradient, with the greatest richness in tropical and subtropical regions. Cain (1934) recorded 112 taxa of coprophilous Sphaeriales from Ontario, Canada, while comparable surveys in East Africa and Venezuela yielded 118-159 taxa when all pyrenomycetes were included.

Richardson (2001) found 153 taxa from the zone 0-30 degrees north and south of the equator, with numbers dropping substantially beyond 40 degrees latitude. This pattern is consistent with broader latitudinal diversity gradients observed across many fungal groups.

The maximum diversity was recorded on Gotland Island in southern Sweden (153 taxa), while numbers from other parts of Sweden ranged from 74 to 95. In tropical savanna habitats such as those around Lake Amboseli in East Africa, approximately 100 taxa of coprophilous Ascomycetes were recorded in about 20 collections, with up to 30% overlap between collections.

Forested and alpine areas of Mt. Kenya yielded similar total numbers (~100 taxa) but lower taxa per collection (never more than 20 compared to 30-35 at Lake Amboseli), suggesting that open habitats support more diverse individual dung communities.

## Latitudinal and Geographic Groupings

Distinct geographic groupings of coprophilous fungi exist:

- **North-temperate group**: Characteristic of temperate latitudes

- **Tropical-subtropical group**: Found in equatorial regions

- **South-temperate group**: Distinct from both northern groups

Within each group, distribution of taxa is fairly cosmopolitan. Lundqvist (1972) pointed out that north-temperate and tropical-subtropical fungi appear distinct from corresponding south-temperate fungi. Approximately 65-70% of fungi from the more temperate Mt. Kenya differed from those at tropical Lake Amboseli. These patterns parallel broader [[alpha-beta-gamma-diversity-fungi]] observed across latitudes.

## Substratum and Host Specificity

A relationship exists between the type of substratum (dung source) and the composition of the coprophilous mycobiota. Ruminant dung (cattle, sheep) has a different mycobiota from lagomorph dung (rabbits, hares). Lundqvist (1972) suggested fungi can be grouped with a series of hosts, but the habitat of the host is likely more important than the specific substratum.

Parker (1979) similarly suggested that microclimate influences species composition, and feeding habits of the host affect species diversity (Ebersohn and Eicker 1992).

This host-habitat interaction means that a given fungal species may be found on diverse dung types as long as the environmental conditions at the deposition site are suitable. The physical characteristics of the dung — moisture content, fiber composition, and rate of desiccation — further modulate which species can successfully establish.

## Dispersal Mechanisms

Spores of many coprophilous taxa germinate only after passage through the mammalian digestive tract (Janczewski 1871; Massee and Salmon 1902). These spores, especially among Ascomycetes, frequently possess gelatinous sheaths or other gelatinous attachments that facilitate adhesion to vegetation. Spores are discharged forcefully into the air and readily adhere to nearby vegetation, where they are subsequently ingested by herbivores.

Additional dispersal mechanisms include rain splash, insects (especially important, per Stevenson and Dindal 1987), other [[insect-arthropod-associated-fungi]], and mammals. Some cleistothecial Ascomycetes dispersed by carnivores possess modified appendages for attachment to fur, particularly advantageous for fungi inhabiting rodent dung deposited at burrow entrances. Mites also play a role in dispersal of a wide range of coprophilous fungi (Malloch and Blackwell 1992).

## Seasonal and Environmental Influences

Richardson (2001) found greater diversity of coprophilous fungi in winter than in summer at northern latitudes, meaning large samples taken through different seasons are required for realistic diversity estimates. Certain species of Thelebolus and Preussia dominate on leporid dung under colder temperatures of early spring, while other species dominate during warmer summer months.

Light also influences fungal succession (Morinaga et al. 1980), with some taxa positively phototropic. These seasonal shifts mean that comprehensive surveys must span multiple seasons to capture the full complement of species at any given location. The interaction of temperature, moisture, and light creates a dynamic temporal mosaic that any single collection can only partially sample.

## Taxonomic Groups

### Zygomycetes

The most extensive survey was in southern California and adjacent parts of Arizona and Mexico, recording 88 species in 42 genera over 35 years. Pakistan yielded 78 species in 26 genera, while temperate Switzerland had only 20 species in 10 genera. This distribution mirrors broader patterns of zygomycete diversity, which tend to be richest in warm, semi-arid climates.

### Myxomycetes

Myxomycetes are not particularly common on dung in temperate forests, and truly coprophilous species are rare. Only five species were recorded from southwestern Virginia, none considered truly coprophilous. The most productive dung sources for [[bloomfield-slime-molds-myxomycetes-plasmodial-protists]] include, in descending order: cow, hares, horse, rabbit, elk, moose, roe deer, goat, and sheep. Bird dung is generally a poor substratum for Myxomycetes.

### Myxobacteria

Seven genera and 22 species of Myxobacteria are coprophilous, classified in four families of the order Myxococcales. These include Archangium (Archangiaceae); Cystobacter, Melittangium, Stigmatella (Cystobacteraceae); Myxococcus (Myxococcaceae); and Polyangium, Chondromyces (Polyangiaceae). These predatory bacteria contribute to the complex microbial food web on dung substrates.

### Ascomycetes

Ascomycetes are by far the most diverse and abundant group of coprophilous fungi. The Sordariaceae, Sporormiaceae, and Chaetomiaceae are particularly well represented. Pyrenomycetes alone account for over 150 taxa in some surveys. Their success is attributed to efficient spore discharge mechanisms, rapid germination, and adaptations for gut passage.

Genera commonly encountered include Podospora, Sordaria, Sporormiella, Ascobolus, Saccobolus, and Thelebolus. Many of these genera contain dozens of species, reflecting extensive adaptive radiation onto the dung substrate.

## Herbaria and Reference Collections

Important herbaria containing coprophilous fungi include: BPI, E, FH, FLAS, GB, L, LPS, NY, PC, S, SANK, TRTC, and UPS. Culture collections at ATCC, CBS, IMI, and NRRL maintain living reference material. These collections are essential for taxonomic studies and for preserving the biodiversity record of this ecologically important fungal guild.

## Conservation and Research Challenges

Coprophilous fungal diversity is threatened by declines in wild herbivore populations and the homogenization of grazing landscapes. As livestock replace diverse wild herbivore communities, the range of dung types available to fungi narrows, potentially reducing overall guild diversity. Modern agricultural practices including anti-helminthic drug treatments may also affect the mycobiota of treated livestock dung. Continued systematic survey work across under-sampled regions, particularly in the tropics and southern hemisphere, is needed to fully document global coprophilous fungal diversity.
