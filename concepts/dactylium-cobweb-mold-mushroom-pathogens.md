# Dactylium Cobweb Mold and Mushroom Pathogens

Chapter XIII of The Mushroom Cultivator profiles the most significant
mold pathogens of cultivated mushrooms. Three genera stand out as the
most troublesome: Dactylium (Cobweb Mold), Trichoderma (Green Mold
Blotch), and Verticillium (Dry Bubble). Proper identification is
essential because control measures differ for each.

## Dactylium dendroides — Cobweb Mold

### Macroscopic Appearance

A fast-growing greyish, cobweb-like mold that rapidly colonizes casing
surfaces. Under high humidity, Dactylium can completely envelop
mushrooms within 48 hours. It is one of the most visually distinctive
contaminants in mushroom culture.

### Microscopic Characteristics

-   **Spores**: Two-celled, approximately 20 microns in length.
-   **Mycelium**: Fine, aerial, greyish-white, spreading rapidly across
    the casing layer.

### Medium of Spread

Primarily airborne spores and contaminated equipment. Dactylium is
especially aggressive when casing materials are not properly prepared or
when relative humidity is excessive (>95%). It spreads rapidly through
contact — workers' hands, tools, or clothing easily transfer spores
between trays or beds.

### Measures of Control

-   Reduce humidity and increase air circulation to promote evaporation.
-   Cover affected areas with **salt (NaCl)** — Dactylium is highly
    sensitive to sodium chloride.
-   Spray with a 5% solution of formaldehyde or a 2% solution of
    sodium hypochlorite (bleach).
-   Lower room temperature to slow its growth rate.
-   Improve [[stamets-casing-layer-design]]. Affected mushrooms may be deformed or
discolored. On malt agar, colonies appear bright green with a woolly
texture.

### Microscopic Characteristics

-   **Spores**: Single-celled, approximately 4 x 5 microns.
-   **Mycelium**: Initially white, rapidly turning green as sporulation
    begins.

### Medium of Spread

Primarily through casing materials and airborne spores. Trichoderma
thrives in slightly acidic conditions and can persist in growing rooms
for extended periods. It is one of the most common contaminants in both
agar and grain culture.

### Measures of Control

-   Pasteurize or sterilize casing materials thoroughly.
-   Maintain proper pH (7.0-7.5) in casing to discourage Trichoderma
    growth.
-   Remove and destroy affected mushrooms immediately.
-   Clean and disinfect growing room surfaces between crops.

## Verticillium — Dry Bubble

### Macroscopic Appearance

Brown spot disease or wilt of the mushroom fruitbody. Unlike Dactylium,
Verticillium primarily attacks the fruitbody rather than colonizing the
casing layer. Affected mushrooms develop brown spots, become distorted,
and may wilt or become leathery.

### Microscopic Characteristics

-   **Spores**: Single-celled, small (2-3 microns).
-   **Mycelium**: White, compact, not prominently aerial.

### Medium of Spread

Verticillium is primarily vectored by **sciarid flies** (fungus gnats).
The flies carry spores on their bodies and deposit them on mushroom
surfaces as they feed. This is a critical distinction from Dactylium —
controlling the fly vector is essential for controlling Verticillium.

### Measures of Control

-   Implement fly control programs (screening, traps, biological
    controls).
-   Remove and destroy affected fruitbodies.
-   Maintain proper sanitation and air filtration.
-   *Agaricus bitorquis* shows natural resistance to Verticillium,
    making it a useful genetic resource.

## Differential Diagnosis Protocol

When a grey/white mold appears on mushrooms or casing:

1.  **Culture on malt agar.**
2.  **Green colonies?** = *Trichoderma*.
3.  **Not green.** Is the contaminant primarily on the casing surface
    or on the fruitbody?
    -   **Casing surface, fast, aerial, cobweb-like**: *Dactylium*.
    -   **Fruitbody, spots/wilt, not casing-dominant**: *Verticillium*.
4.  **Confirm microscopically**:
    -   Two-celled, 20-micron spores = *Dactylium*.
    -   Single-celled, 4-5 micron spores = *Trichoderma*.
    -   Single-celled, 2-3 micron spores = *Verticillium*.

## Species Resistance Notes

-   *Agaricus bitorquis* exhibits resistance to both Verticillium and
    Trichoderma, making it valuable for breeding programs.
-   Some *Pleurotus* species show natural tolerance to Dactylium.
