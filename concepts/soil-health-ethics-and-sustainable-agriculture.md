# Soil Health Ethics and Sustainable Agriculture

## Overview

Bill Mollison frames soil management within a broader ethical context, arguing that modern extractive agriculture has placed short-term yields above long-term soil health, human nutrition, and ecological integrity. This concept explores the philosophical and practical tensions between industrial agriculture and sustainable soil stewardship.

## The Soil Formation Crisis

Topsoil is being created at a rate of approximately **2–4 tonnes per hectare per year**, yet conventional cropping and tillage remove it at rates of **40–500 tonnes per year**. In extreme erosion events — floods or strong winds over cultivated soils — up to **2,000 years of soil accumulation** can be lost in a single season.

Even under the most ideal tillage conditions, agriculture barely keeps pace with natural soil formation. The only environments where soils are genuinely conserved or increased are:

1. **Uncut forests** — the original soil-building ecosystems
2. **Lakes and ponds** — where quiet water deposits sediment
3. **Prairies and meadows** of permanent plants — deep-rooted grasslands stabilize soil
4. **Mulched or non-tillage systems** — where soil structure is protected

These systems, Mollison notes, are "the core subjects of sustainable societies of any conceivable future" — yet they are not what is predominantly taught in agricultural colleges or forestry courses.

## The Knowledge Gap: Science vs. Field Practice

Mollison identifies a fundamental disconnect between soil science and practical soil management:

- **Soil science** concentrates on classification — what is there — but not on how to evolve soil
- **Farmers and gardeners** are often the practical innovators who create good soil through water control, modest aeration, and plant and animal management
- Scientists often collect results generated by others rather than generating them by example
- Science excels at explaining *why* things work (making skills teachable) but is less effective at initiating field work

Farmers' field days advance knowledge more effectively than scientific papers, and local educational sessions more than either. Mollison advocates for a coalition between scientists and farmers, driven by joint concern rather than institutional competition.

## Four Value Frameworks for Soil

Mollison identifies four broad value frameworks through which soils should be considered:

### 1. Health (Human and Plant)

A conservative approach is essential, especially regarding biocides and artificial fertilizers. Historical populations lived to old ages on home-grown produce without herbicides, pesticides, or artificial fertilizers. Additional health factors associated with gardening include:

- **Mild but regular exercise** — gardening as steady, non-stressing physical activity
- **Meditation** — the contemplative aspect of sitting and observing the garden
- **Meaning and purpose** — a conscious sense of "right livelihood" and direct value to others
- **Lifelong interest** — every day and season brings change, new observations, and constant learning

Permaculture adds dimensions of a life-oriented chess game involving elements, energy, life-forms, building structures, and their political, social, financial, and global implications.

### 2. Yield

The core impasse: a ploughed and fertilized monoculture supported by chemicals and large energy inputs can out-yield almost any other production system in the short term. But at what cost?

Critical questions include:

- **How long can it be maintained?** — forced yields are temporary
- **What improvement in nutrition?** — yield does not equal nutritional quality
- **What guarantee of sustainability?** — plough cultures destroy soils and societies
- **What effect on world hunger?** — industrial agriculture has not solved hunger
- **What effect on soils?** — long-term degradation is documented
- **What effect on health?** — biocide residues and nutritional decline

There is abundant proof that forced yields are temporary and that plough cultures destroy soils and societies. Public financial support is often directed toward systems that destroy the countryside through deforestation, hedgerow removal, species loss, soil degradation, and pollution.

### 3. Life in Soil

Soil organisms — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, earthworms, insects, and larger animals — are a major soil factor with profound effects on:

- **pH regulation** — microbial activity influences soil acidity/alkalinity
- **Mineral content and availability** — organisms make locked minerals accessible to plants
- **Soil structure** — fungal hyphae and root networks create and maintain crumb structure
- **Erosion prevention** — biological binding reduces wind and water erosion

### 4. Ethics

It is not humanity's purpose to reduce all soils to perfectly balanced market gardens, though this is achievable and necessary on the approximately **4% of the earth** needed for food production. The larger ethical obligation is:

- **Restoration** of soils and forests for a healthy earth itself
- **Cessation** of further clearing, deforestation, or ruination of land
- **Prioritization** of putting in order what has already been destroyed
- **Attention** to the modest area needed for survival and full nutrition

Without poorly drained, naturally deficient, leached, acidic, or alkaline sites, many plant species would disappear. They have evolved specifically to occupy less-than-perfect soil conditions.

## Land Recovery Potential

Colin Tudge (New Scientist, 1986) estimated that perhaps **60% of the British Isles** could be returned to nature — a conservative figure. John Jeavons, based on garden productivity data, estimated that perhaps **94% of land** could be returned to its own purposes if food were grown intensively on small areas.

Mollison acknowledges that this transformation will not happen in a single decade, but argues that starting the process is essential so that children can continue it, developing new forests and wilderness to explore.

## Soil Rehabilitation Timelines

Practical timelines for soil recovery:

- **3–4 years** to build a productive garden from average soil
- **5–15 years** to restore a devastated soil landscape (physical restoration and appropriate plant systems)
- **Decades or more** where past chemical pollution has occurred

The physical restoration of soils and development of appropriate plant systems takes 3–15 years. Chemical pollution remediation can take far longer — persistent biocides like Aldrin and Dieldrin can remain active in soils for 18–20 years after cessation of use.

## The Soil-Plant-Animal Health Connection

Soils harbour and transfer both diseases and antibiotics. Plants take up many modern antibiotics (penicillin, sulfa drugs) from soil, and these can be ingested at concentrated levels. Animals retain residual antibiotics (leading to resistant disease strains) and residual hormones and biocides.

Both dangers and benefits arise from food:

- Natural levels of soil antibiotics may sustain human health
- Natural resistance to disease is transmitted through food
- Vitamins and trace elements maintain metabolic processes in minute but essential amounts
- Pollutants are concentrated by both plants and animals, though the degree varies by species

When people lived as regional inhabitants, they adapted to local soils, plants, and nutritional levels — or they died out. Global food markets risk the global spread of "agricologenic" (farm-caused) diseases. The home garden represents a limited, localized risk under personal control, unlike commercial food distribution systems.

## Practical Approach to Soil Health

The sane procedure for health, as Mollison summarizes:

1. Maintain basic hygiene
2. Grow and eat healthy plants and animals
3. Avoid biocides and pollutants
4. Take easy exercise
5. Drink clean water or beverages
6. Stay as cheerful as this world permits (adopt a positivistic lifestyle)

The rest is up to chance, traffic accidents, megalomaniacs, and wars — which demand commonsense changes to social systems, plus a little good luck.
