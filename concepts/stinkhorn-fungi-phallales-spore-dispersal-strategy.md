## Overview

[[stinkhorn-fungi]], belonging to the order Phallales, employ one of the most dramatic and
olfactorily offensive [[spore-dispersal]] strategies in the fungal kingdom. Rather than
releasing spores passively into air currents like gilled mushrooms, stinkhorns produce
a sticky, foul-smelling spore mass designed to attract insects — primarily flies and
slugs — which then serve as unwilling vectors for [[solomon-root-exudates-and-allelopathy]], animal behavior exploitation,
and biomechanical engineering packed into a brief, spectacular display.

## The Common Stinkhorn: Phallus Impudicus

The species *Phallus impudicus*, whose Latin name translates literally to "the shameless
penis," is the archetypal stinkhorn and the most widely recognized member of the order.

### Anatomy of the Egg

The stinkhorn egg is far from a simple mass of undifferentiated tissue. It contains
fully preformed structures, compressed and folded, waiting for the moment of emergence.
Running from pole to pole through the center of the egg is a hollow white spongy
receptacle — the column that will become the visible stalk. Surrounding this receptacle
is the gleba, a green-black spore mass encased in a gelatinous matrix. The entire
assembly is veiled with a thin white outer skin, creating a sealed, pressurized capsule.

### Hatching and Expansion

When conditions are right — typically after sustained rainfall and warm temperatures —
the egg hatches. The receptacle begins to absorb water rapidly through osmotic
processes, expanding as it hydrates. This expansion generates sufficient internal
pressure to rupture the outer skin of the egg. As the receptacle extends upward, it
carries the gleba — still coated in its jelly — into the air. The gelatinous coating
serves as a lubricant, allowing the shaft to extend smoothly and rapidly without
friction against the rupturing egg membrane. The entire process from rupture to full
extension can occur in a matter of hours, producing a [[comparison-fruiting-body-vs-mycelium-extracts-medicinal-mushrooms]] that may stand
fifteen to twenty-five centimeters tall.

## The Chemistry of Attraction

The spore-laden gleba is embedded in a slime whose chemical composition has been
fine-tuned by evolution to mimic the scent of rotting flesh. Analysis has revealed the
presence of hydrogen sulfide (the characteristic odor of rotten eggs), formaldehyde,
methylmercaptan, and a suite of unique compounds called phallic acids that are found
almost exclusively in stinkhorn fungi. This chemical cocktail is extraordinarily
effective at attracting its target audience.

### Fly Attraction and Spore Transport

Flies are the primary vectors for stinkhorn spore dispersal. When the fruiting body
emerges, flies swarm onto the gleba-coated head, drawn from considerable distances by
the odor plume. They feed on the spore slime and become thoroughly coated in the sticky
mass. When they subsequently land on other substrates — decaying wood, leaf litter, or
fresh dung — they inadvertently deposit spores in new locations, complete with a small
nutrient packet from the residual slime.

### Slug Attraction

Slugs are secondary but enthusiastic participants in stinkhorn dispersal. Experiments
have documented slugs gliding more than twenty feet toward an emerging stinkhorn,
following the scent trail with remarkable determination. Upon reaching the fruiting
body, they consume quantities of the gleba and later defecate viable spores at distant
locations. The slime coating, it seems, offers a nutritional reward sufficient to
motivate this considerable expenditure of energy.

### A Fleeting Display

The stinkhorn's display is emphatically temporary. Within hours of emergence, the
combined feeding activity of flies and slugs strips the head of its gleba, leaving
behind only the white, dimpled surface of the receptacle. With its spore mass depleted
and its purpose fulfilled, the shaft wilts and collapses, often dissolving into an
unrecognizable mess within a day or two of emergence.

## Diversity Within the Phallales

Approximately thirty species with conspicuously phallic fruiting bodies have been
described, exhibiting considerable variation on the basic stinkhorn theme.
