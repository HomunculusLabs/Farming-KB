# Livestock Feed Management

Managing feed for a homestead livestock operation requires balancing nutritional needs, seasonal availability, and cost-effectiveness. Proper feed management is the single largest ongoing expense for most livestock keepers, and getting it right determines animal health, productivity, and profitability.

## Feed Types and Categories

Livestock feeds fall into several categories:

- **Roughages** — Pasture, hay, silage, and other high-fiber feeds. These form the bulk of ruminant diets and are essential for proper rumen function.
- **Concentrates** — Grains (corn, oats, barley), protein meals (soybean meal, cottonseed meal), and commercial pellets. Used to supplement roughage when energy or protein demands are high.
- **Supplements** — Minerals, vitamins, and specialized additives (kelp meal, apple cider vinegar, diatomaceous earth). Address specific nutritional gaps.
- **Fodder crops** — Grown specifically for livestock: [[fodder-growing-guide]], root crops (mangel beets, turnips), and annual forages.

## Ruminant Feed Management

Ruminants (cattle, sheep, goats) can digest cellulose through microbial fermentation in the rumen. Their diet should be:

- **70-100% roughage** for maintenance — pasture when available, good-quality hay when not
- **Supplemented with concentrates** during late gestation, early lactation, or rapid growth phases
- **Free-choice minerals** at all times — especially important for breeding animals
- **Gradual diet transitions** — never abruptly switch feed types; allow 2-3 weeks for rumen microbes to adapt

## Poultry Feed Management

Poultry require higher protein and more precise nutrient ratios than ruminants:

- **Starter feed (0-8 weeks):** 20-24% protein for chicks and poults
- **Grower feed (8-18 weeks):** 16-18% protein for developing birds
- **Layer feed:** 16% protein with ~3.5% calcium for egg production
- **Broiler feed:** 20-23% protein for rapid meat growth
- **Scratch grains** as a treat only — not nutritionally complete

## Swine Feed Management

Pigs are monogastric and require more concentrated feeds:

- **Grower/finisher diets** of 14-18% protein depending on age
- **Breeder sows** need higher protein (16%) during gestation and lactation
- **Kitchen scraps and garden waste** can supplement but should not exceed 25% of diet
- **Root crops** (pumpkins, sweet potatoes, turnips) are excellent fall feed supplements

## Seasonal Feed Planning

### Spring/Summer
- Maximize pasture utilization through rotational [[pasture-management]]
- Cut and store excess hay for winter
- Plant [[fodder-growing-guide]] crops for fall/winter feeding

### Fall
- Harvest and cure hay
- Stockpile root crops for winter feed
- Assess winter feed needs and purchase gaps

### Winter
- Feed stored hay and silage
- Supplement with concentrates as needed
- Monitor body condition scores and adjust rations

## Feed Storage

Proper storage prevents spoilage and nutrient loss:

- **Hay** — Store under cover, off the ground. Baleage (wrapped haylage) offers better nutrient retention than dry hay.
- **Grain** — Keep dry, cool, and rodent-proof. Use metal bins or sealed containers. Moldy grain can contain aflatoxins lethal to livestock.
- **Silage** — Requires anaerobic fermentation in sealed storage (silos, wrapped bales, bags).
- **Minerals** — Store dry; moisture causes caking and reduces palatability.

## Reducing Feed Costs

- Improve pasture quality and utilization (see [[pasture-management]])
- Grow your own [[fodder-growing-guide]] and hay
- Use [[composting-systems]] to turn manure back into fertility for feed crops
- Integrate livestock with [[silvopasture-systems]] for diversified forage
- Practice [[small-scale-dairy-guide]] efficient milking schedules that align with natural forage cycles
- Source bulk feed cooperatively with neighbors
