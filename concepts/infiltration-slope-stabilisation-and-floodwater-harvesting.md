# Infiltration, Slope Stabilisation, and Floodwater Harvesting

Effective [[permaculture-design]] in dryland regions.

## Infiltration Rates by Soil Type

The time required to infiltrate 25 mm of rain varies dramatically by soil type:

| Soil Type | Minimum Rate | Time for 25mm |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Sands and dust (loess) | 11–7 mm/hr | 2–3 hours |
| Clay-loams | 4–1 mm/hr | 6–20 hours |
| Alkaline swelling clays | 1 mm/hr | 24 hours |

These rates have direct design implications. Water must be held for a day or more to soak into clay soils, meaning earthworks in clay areas need larger storage capacity. In sands, rapid infiltration means water can be processed quickly but retention requires deeper storage or impervious layers beneath.

## The Sand-over-Clay Technique

A powerful strategy involves spreading neutral or acidic sands approximately 0.5 m deep or more over alkaline clay pans. This creates an excellent surface that first infiltrates rain rapidly, then holds it for extended periods — producing an ideal garden or tree site capable of storing up to 1 m of water long-term.

This effect occurs naturally at the discharge end of some gullies or canyons where sandy material accumulates over clay substrates. It can also be created artificially by opening deep swales in alkaline soils in front of advancing sands and gravels, allowing them to fill naturally.

People have also buried layers of clay, plastic, thick colloidal green matter (gley), tar, or latex under deep sands of gardens to achieve the same water-retention effect, with drainage slits every 100–200 m.

## Slope Stabilisation for Infiltration

### Natural Slope Stabilisers

**Spinifex and porcupine-type grasses** play a critical role in slope stabilisation and infiltration enhancement. Their normally circular tussock form breaks open to create an open crescent with horns pointing uphill. Detritus accumulates behind these crescent-shaped clumps, forming deeper sandy rubble banks that function as natural swales.

**Burning destroys this effect entirely**, allowing sudden and severe slope erosion to occur.

In cold deserts, tussock grasses and mosses perform the same stabilisation function for unstable rock slopes and soil flows.

### Vetiver Grass Contour Lines

Cross-slope [[vetiver-grass]] lines planted on contour greatly assist both water infiltration and slope stability. These living barriers slow overland flow, trap sediment, and create micro-terraces that increase the time water spends on slopes.

### Constructed Infiltration Aids

Where natural stabilisation is insufficient, designers can create:

- **Swales**: Most easily created on large scale; the primary earthwork for infiltration
- **Wadi dams**: Effective for local food forests where basic machinery is available
- **Levelled gardens**: Flat areas that capture and hold sheet flow
- **Soakage pits**: Point-specific infiltration points for concentrated runoff

## Floodwater Harvesting

### Traditional Systems

**Aboriginal Australians** (Stevenson, 1978) constructed earthwall floodwater dams to increase Panicum grass and seed production. **Papago and neighbouring groups** in the Sonora region of the USA (Nabham, 1979) developed sophisticated floodwater farming systems on river floodplains and deltaic deposits at wadi mouths.

### Papago Floodwater Farming

Key parameters from the Papago systems:

- **Rainfall threshold**: Approximately 8 cm needed for useful runoff
- **Catchment ratio**: 15–27 times the area of fields for general crops
- **Hardy crops** (sorghum): Can be produced with runoff only 4 times the field area
- **Seasonal distribution**: Summer storms bring 40–60% of water but only 15% of runoff to mainstream reservoirs; most runoff is localised in intermittent streams
- **Field size**: 4 hectares or less per field
- **Crop system**: Planted and wild/weed species grown together as a crop complex

### Floodplain Techniques

Along the Colorado River and similar systems, floodplain fields were constructed using:

- Temporary basins with irrigation canals
- Field boundaries of stone and earth reinforced with posts, brush, and vegetation
- "Gridiron" systems of low banks (0.5–1 m high) stabilised by stone on upstream sides, tussock grasses, tough low shrubs, live-set sticks, or heavy rock walls

These systems maintained high levels of major plant nutrients and were the basis of the rich Nilotic agriculture of ancient Egypt. Crops of millet, sorghum, and legumes were broadcast-sown into wet silt during floods and later harvested as stored crop.

## Braided Stream Dynamics

### The Inverse Area-Width Relationship

A close study of braiding patterns reveals a critical inverse relationship: small multiple streams produce much larger wetted areas than single main channels. The darker "green" areas of vegetation visible on aerial photographs of braided streams demonstrate this principle clearly.

### Creating Artificial Braiding

In dry periods, concrete deeply-bedded deflections in main channels can split flood flow into multiple small channels. The goal is to widen and slow floodwaters, wetting as much alluvium as possible before water is lost to saltpans.

Each braid form is initially diamond-shaped with plunge pools at each corner, caused by confluence of side streams. **Notched weirs** — level walls with notched openings placed across braided areas — can initiate and spread braiding patterns by imitating these natural plunge pools.

The braiding process depends on shallow water flow over extensive deep sand sheets; braiding does not occur underwater.

## Scour Hole Lagoons

Within 10–50 km of ranges, permanent desert lagoons may form through **hydraulic digging**: strong natural ridges approaching the river from one or both banks constrict flood flow, forcing increased velocity that excavates deep scour holes.

### Construction

These can be constructed or enhanced using:
- Rock-cement walls combined with bulldozed banks
- Stronger and deeper construction near the riverbed itself

### Design Considerations

- Deep sands in the river contain millions of litres of slow seepage (increased by sand dams higher in the system)
- Open-water systems should not be created too frequently unless silt dams of **9–10 times** the pool capacity are provided, as open water evaporates over time
- Below roost trees around such dams, bird guano can be gathered as phosphate fertiliser sheets (used as dilute solution or mixed with 30–40 parts damp sand)

## Integration: A Five-Step Design Sequence

1. **Assess infiltration rates** — test pit to determine soil-specific absorption timing
2. **Stabilise slopes** — establish spinifex/Vetiver grass, prevent burning, create swales
3. **Harvest floodwater** — design gridiron banks and field systems on floodplains
4. **Spread flow** — use notched weirs and channel deflections to maximise wetted area
5. **Create permanent water** — construct scour holes with adequate silt dam capacity
