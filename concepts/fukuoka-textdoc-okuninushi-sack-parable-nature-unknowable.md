# The Okuninushi Sack Parable and the Limits of Human Knowledge

In *The Natural Way of Farming*, Masanobu Fukuoka employs a parable
from Shinto mythology to illustrate the fundamental impossibility of
understanding nature through scientific inquiry. The sack of Okuninushi
no Mikoto, the deity of agriculture, anchors his broader epistemological
argument that human knowledge of nature is inherently limited,
subjective, and prone to catastrophic error.

## The Parable of the Sack

Fukuoka asks the reader to imagine that people wish to know what
Okuninushi no Mikoto carries around in the huge sack on his shoulder.
Their immediate impulse is to open the sack and thrust their hands
inside. They believe that to understand the interior of the sack, they
must know its contents.

Supposing they find the sack filled with all sorts of strange objects
made of wood and bamboo. Most people would immediately begin making
pronouncements about what these objects are: a tool used by travelers,
a decorative carving, a weapon. Yet the truth, known only to Okuninushi
himself, is that the object is an instrument fashioned for his
amusement. Moreover, because it is broken, he carries it merely for
use as kindling.

The parable establishes that observation of parts does not yield
understanding of the whole, and that the observer's interpretations,
no matter how careful, may be entirely wrong because they lack the
context of the creator's intent.

## Nature as the Sack

Fukuoka extends the parable directly to humanity's relationship with
nature. Man jumps into the great sack called nature, grabs whatever he
can, turns it over and examines it, asking himself what it is and how
it works, and drawing conclusions about what purpose nature serves.

But no matter how careful his observations and reasoning, each and every
interpretation carries the risk of grievous error because man cannot
know nature any more than he can know the uses for the objects in
Okuninushi's sack. The scientist studying nature is like someone
rummaging through the sack, making increasingly elaborate guesses about
each object. Each new observation seems to confirm the hypothesis,
leading to greater confidence in conclusions that may be completely
wrong.

## The Spiral of Confident Error

Fukuoka describes how scientific inquiry compounds its errors through
a spiral of increasing confidence:

1. A scientist sees shells attached to a piece of bamboo and mistakes
   it for a weapon.
2. Further investigation reveals that rapping the shells against the
   bamboo makes an interesting sound.
3. The scientist concludes this is a musical instrument.
4. From the curvature of the bamboo, the scientist infers it must be
   worn dangling from the waist while dancing.
5. With each step, the scientist believes himself closer to the truth.

Yet the truth, known only to Okuninushi himself, is that the object is
merely a broken instrument he carries as kindling. The parallel to
agricultural science is direct: a scientist analyzes soil, identifies
nutrient deficiencies, applies targeted fertilizers, observes increased
growth, and concludes the fertilizer is effective. But the apparent
success of each step masks the possibility that the entire framework
of understanding is wrong.

Just as he believes that he can know Okuninushi's mind by studying
the contents of his sack, so man believes that, by observing nature,
he can learn the story of its creation and can in turn become privy
to its very designs and purpose. But this is a hopeless illusion.

## The Frog in the Well

Fukuoka reinforces the parable with the classical image of the frog
in the well. Just as the world known to a frog in a well is not the
entire world but only the world within that well, the nature that
humans perceive and know is only that nature which they have been able
to grasp with their own hands and their own subjectivity. This is not
true nature.

He challenges the common assumption that flying to outer space will
teach about space, or traveling to the moon will reveal the moon. In
the same way, dissecting a stalk of wheat and analyzing its components
does not mean one knows wheat or is capable of growing it. He may
laugh at the folly of the proverbial frog in the well, but is unable
to laugh off his own ignorance before the vastness of the universe.

## The Flea That Cannot See Outside

Fukuoka extends the metaphor with that of a flea. A flea born and
raised in the sack without ever having seen the world outside will
never be able to guess that the object in the sack is an instrument
hung from Okuninushi's belt, no matter how much it studies the object.

Similarly, man, who is born within nature and will never be able to
step outside the natural world, can never understand all of nature
merely by examining that part of nature around him. Man's answer to
this is that, although he may not be able to view the world from
without, if he has the knowledge and ability to explore the furthest
reaches of the vast, seemingly boundless universe, is not this enough?

## The Problem of Subjectivity

Fukuoka argues that when man observes and judges, there is only the
thing called man and the thing being observed. It is man who verifies
and believes in the reality of an object, and it is man who verifies
and believes in the existence of man himself. Everything in this world
derives from man, and he draws all conclusions.

In which case, he need not worry about being God's puppet. But he does
run the risk of acting out a drunken role on the stage supported by the
crazed subjectivity of his own despotic existence. The scientist's
claim to objectivity through repeated inductive experimentation and
reasoning is itself subjective.
