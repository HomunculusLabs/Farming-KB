# Meadow Garden Polyculture

Toby Hemenway's example of a self-sustaining polyculture garden that works like a meadow — providing its own fertilization, weed suppression, pest control, and moisture management. From the foreword of Gaia's Garden.

## The Meadow Garden Concept

The meadow garden "works on totally different principles" from conventional gardening. Instead of tilling, sowing, weeding, and chemically controlling, it:

- Provides its own fertilization
- Has internal weed suppression and pest-control mechanisms
- Manages its internal moisture levels through dry times and wet
- Functions as a self-organizing ecology

## The Planting Sequence

### Preparation (One Month Before Last Frost)

Prepare the garden bed with [[gaias-garden-sheet-mulching]] or deep mulching. This builds soil and suppresses existing weeds without tilling.

### Initial Sowing (After Last Frost)

Broadcast seeds over the garden:
- **Radish** — fast-growing root crop; harvested first
- **Dill** — edible flowers and leaves; attracts beneficial insects
- **Parsnip** — long-season root crop; harvested last
- **Calendula** — edible flowers; attracts beneficial insects; self-seeds
- **Many varieties of lettuce** — staggered harvest over months

Spread one-quarter inch of compost over the seeds. "That's it."

### The Succession Harvest

| Time | Event | What Follows |
|------|-------|-------------|
| ~3 weeks | Radishes ready for harvesting | Cabbage seedlings fill the holes |
| ~6 weeks | Dense lettuce crop begins yielding | Other lettuce varieties grow to full size |
| Late spring/early summer | Lettuce space opens up | Possum beans and buckwheat take the space |
| Summer | Dill and calendula harvested | — |
| Extended period | Cabbages mature at different times | — |
| Fall | Parsnips ready to harvest | Garlic cloves and fava beans poked into openings |

### Year Two and Beyond

- Garlic and fava beans harvested the following year
- Self-seeding from calendula, dill, and other plants fills gaps
- The polyculture becomes increasingly self-sustaining

## How It Works

### Pest and Disease Control

"Enough botanical diversity to control pests and disease as well as to protect the plants from excess rain and drought." Many different plant species in close proximity:

- Make it harder for pests to find their host plants
- Attract predatory insects that keep pest populations in check
- Create microclimates that buffer against weather extremes
- Reduce disease transmission (different species aren't susceptible to the same diseases)

### Self-Fertilization

- Root systems of diverse plants explore different soil depths and nutrient profiles
- Nitrogen-fixers (fava beans) feed companion plants
- Decomposing roots and leaves build humus
- No external fertilizer needed

### Weed Suppression

- Dense planting leaves no space for weeds
- Living mulch from ground-covering plants shades soil
- Self-seeding plants fill any gaps that open
- [[gaias-garden-cover-crops-soil-building]] like buckwheat choke weeds

### Moisture Management

- Diverse canopy layers reduce evaporation
- Deep-rooted plants (parsnips) bring moisture up from below
- Mulch from decomposing plant material retains soil moisture
- Different root architectures access water at different depths

## Design Principles Illustrated

### Gardening in the Image of a Meadow

The meadow garden embodies the core insight from [[gaias-garden-ecology-for-gardeners]]: imitate natural systems rather than fighting them. A meadow is self-organizing, self-fertilizing, and self-regulating.

### Succession

The garden follows [[gaias-garden-ecology-for-gardeners]] — early fast crops give way to longer-season plants, which in turn create space for the next generation. This is succession designed by the gardener.

### Stacking Functions

Every plant in the polyculture performs multiple functions — food production, insect attraction, soil building, weed suppression, moisture retention — embodying the [[gaias-garden-stacking-functions-detailed]] principle.

## Scaling Up

Variations on the polyculture theme "expand the meaning of gardening from the traditional battle to control Nature to a conscious and conscientious attempt to imitate and re-create natural systems in the backyard." Ideas and patterns from nature can be blended and integrated to create larger systems that connect with each other to create "a self-tending and co-evolving garden landscape."
