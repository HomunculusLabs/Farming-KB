# International Mycology Research Network

The international mycology research network comprises a distributed community of universities, research institutes, and individual scientists collaborating across borders to advance the understanding of fungi in the environment. This network spans at least ten countries across four continents, connected through professional societies, joint publications, and shared research programs.

## Core Research Centers

### United Kingdom

The UK has historically been a stronghold of environmental mycology research, with several universities hosting major fungal research groups:

- **University of Dundee** — The Division of Environmental and Applied Biology, led by Geoffrey M. Gadd, has been a center for geomycology (fungal-mineral interactions), biogeochemical cycling, and fungal bioremediation. Key researchers include Euan P. Burford, Marina Fomina, and Karrie Melville. The Division of Mathematics (Fordyce A. Davidson) contributes mathematical modeling of mycelial form and function.

- **University of Oxford** — The Department of Plant Sciences hosts leading research on mycelial imaging and nutrient dynamics. Sarah C. Watkinson, Daniel P. Bebber, Monika Tlalka, Peter R. Darrah, and Mark D. Fricker have developed novel techniques for visualizing and quantifying nutrient transport in fungal networks using confocal microscopy and fluorescent probes.

- **Cardiff University** — The Cardiff School of Biosciences, with Lynne Boddy, Juliet Hynes, and T. Hefin Jones, specializes in the functional ecology of saprotrophic fungi and mycelial responses to heterogeneous environments.

- **University of Exeter** — The Washington Singer Laboratories house a major group studying fungal pathogenesis and genetics. Nicholas J. Talbot, Joanna M. Jenkinson, and colleagues have made significant contributions to understanding plant infection by pathogenic fungi, particularly rice blast disease. The group includes researchers studying functional genomics, cell biology, and fungal development.

- **University of Nottingham** — Paul S. Dyer's group focuses on fungal genetics and population biology. Nottingham hosted the landmark 2004 British Mycological Society Symposium on Fungi in the Environment.

- **Imperial College London** — Matthew C. Fisher's group in the Department of Infectious Diseases and Epidemiology specializes in fungal population genetics, molecular epidemiology, and the evolution of fungal pathogens.

### North America

- **United States** — Major mycology research centers include Louisiana State University (Meredith Blackwell, Sung-Oui Suh — fungal systematics and beetle gut mycoflora), the University of Georgia Marine Institute on Sapelo Island (Steven Y. Newell, Justine I. Lyons, Mary Ann Moran — saltmarsh fungal decomposition and laccase genes), Boyce Thompson Institute for Plant Research at Cornell (Maria J. Harrison, Jinyuan Liu, Melina Lopez-Meyer, Ignacio Maldonado-Mendoza — arbuscular mycorrhizal genomics), Miami University (Nicholas P. Money — fungal biology and built environment contamination), University of California Berkeley (John W. Taylor, Anne Pringle, Elizabeth Turner — fungal species concepts and population genetics), University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (James B. Nardi — insect-fungal interactions), and the Public Health Research Institute in Newark (David S. Perlin — antifungal drug resistance).

- **Canada** — The University of Toronto Mississauga (Jeremy Dettman) contributes to fungal evolutionary biology and species recognition.

### Europe (outside UK)

- **Sweden** — Three major centers: the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences in Uppsala (Andy F. S. Taylor, Petra M. A. Fransson — stable isotope ecology of saprotrophic fungi; Hanna Johannesson at Uppsala University — fungal evolutionary genetics), and Lund University (Anders Tunlid — fungal-nematode interactions and molecular ecology).

- **France** — The Institut Pasteur Aspergillus Unit in Paris (Anne Beauvais, Jean Paul Latgé) is a world center for research on *Aspergillus fumigatus* and other human fungal pathogens, particularly the role of cell wall components like α(1-3)-glucan in virulence.

- **Switzerland** — The Institute of Plant Biology at the University of Zurich (Rosmarie Honegger) specializes in lichen biology, particularly water relations in lichen symbioses.

### Asia-Pacific

- **Australia** — The University of New South Wales School of Biological, Earth and Environmental Sciences (Anne Ashford) contributes research on vacuolar systems and long-distance transport in fungal hyphae.

- **Singapore** — The Temasek Life Sciences Laboratory and National University of Singapore (Gregory Jedd) studies peroxisomal biology and organelle function in filamentous fungi, including the role of Woronin bodies.

## Collaboration Patterns

These research centers are connected through several mechanisms: the British Mycological Society (international membership), co-authored publications, shared model organisms, joint grant applications, and student exchanges. The 2004 BMS Symposium volume demonstrates the depth of cross-institutional collaboration, with most chapters authored by teams spanning multiple institutions and countries.

A notable pattern is the connection between UK fungal ecology groups and international partners studying complementary aspects — for example, the Oxford-Cardiff collaboration on mycelial imaging, the Exeter-Pasteur collaboration on fungal pathogenesis, and the Dundee-Sweden collaboration on fungal biogeochemistry.

## Research Infrastructure

Key shared resources include advanced microscopy facilities (confocal laser scanning microscopy at Oxford), molecular biology core facilities (genomics and transcriptomics at Exeter and Boyce Thompson), field stations (Sapelo Island Marine Institute for coastal fungal ecology), and culture collections maintained across multiple institutions.
