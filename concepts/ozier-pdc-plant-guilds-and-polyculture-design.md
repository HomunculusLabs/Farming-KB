# Plant Guilds and Polyculture Design In nature plants do not grow in isolated rows; they grow in clusters called plant communities. In a plant community various plants grow together in symbiotic and mutually beneficial relationships. In permaculture, designed plant communities appropriate for a specific site are called guilds. Creating plant guilds is also referred to as [[permaculture-principles]]
simultaneously: [[graywater-systems]], shade
screens, windbreaks, and engineered microclimates may all be part of a
guild. Like all permaculture elements, [[apple-guild-design-guide]] is a whole systems
[[hemenway-garden-design-process-observation-and-visioning]] limited only by the imagination and knowledge of the
designer.

## The Design Process

Plants need to be environmentally appropriate. The less appropriate the
plant, the more resources it will require and the less renewable it will
be. To choose plants, you should not choose the plant first. Instead,
think of the function you want, then research plants indigenous to your
area to find appropriate species that perform the needed function.

Look at all the candidate plants to determine if they can work together
in guilds. If not, research potential guild partners that can support
your primary plant. Analyze the effects of the microclimate created by
the plants and determine how that microclimate interacts with your overall
design.

## Species Categories

The most prized type of plant in a permaculture system is a native plant:
one that performs the desired function and grows naturally in the area.
Pioneer species tend to be small, hardy perennials with rapid propagation
rates. They move into relatively barren areas and, over generations,
build ecological activity that prepares the area for secondary succession
and more complex plant species.

Invasive species, also known as weeds, tend to have high propagation
rates. They enter pre-existing ecologies and destabilize them by
outcompeting existing plants. Niche species are complex plants that
thrive within narrow microclimates. Appropriate species are not native
but grow well within local conditions without destabilizing the ecology.

## The Three-Species Rule

As a rule of thumb, for every function a permaculture designer wants from
a plant, they should find three different species of plants to fulfill
that function and create guilds to support those plants. This redundancy
ensures resilience: if one species fails, others continue providing the
function.

## Maximizing Growing Space

Plants do not have to be grown only in the ground. They can be grown in
containers, on roofs, or in more complex food production systems such as
hydroponics, aeroponics, or aquaponics. All of these strategies should be
taken full advantage of when appropriate in every design.

[[permaculture-design]] will support a wide variety of volunteer
species alongside intentionally planted guilds.

## Practical Considerations

Successful implementation of Plant Guilds and Polyculture Design requires attention to
several practical factors including environmental conditions,
resource availability, and timing. Careful monitoring and
adaptive management help optimize outcomes across varying
conditions. Integration with other system elements enhances
overall effectiveness and creates beneficial synergies that
improve resilience and productivity over time.
