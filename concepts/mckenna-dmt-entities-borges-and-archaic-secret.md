# McKenna on DMT Entities, Borges, and the Archaic Secret

Terence McKenna's 1983 Esalen lecture "Tryptamine [[tryptamine-hallucinogens-and-consciousness]]" presents his most detailed first-person account of DMT entity encounters, connects the tryptamine experience to literary and philosophical traditions, and proposes that these compounds grant access to a translinguistic domain that has been preserved through secret lineages across human history. The lecture weaves together phenomenological description, speculative philosophy, and cultural criticism in McKenna's characteristic style.

## Context: The Esalen Conference

The lecture was delivered at the Lilly/Goswami Conference on Consciousness and Quantum Physics at Esalen Institute on the Big Sur coast of California in December 1983. McKenna describes himself as "an explorer rather than a scientist," noting that the area he investigates "contains insufficient data to support even the dream of being a science." He positions the tryptamine hallucinogens, specifically DMT, psilocybin, and ayahuasca, as uniquely relevant to discussions of both consciousness and quantum physics because of the intensity of the hallucinations and their concentration in the visual cortex.

## The DMT Onset and Hyperspace

McKenna describes the DMT experience as beginning approximately fifteen seconds after inhalation, with a characteristic sound "like [[mckenna-dmt-onset-phenomenology-ripping-cellophane]]" and an ascending tone. Within thirty seconds, all available receptor sites are occupied and a complete mode shift occurs. The experiencer arrives in what McKenna calls a "merry go raum" (from the German raum, meaning space), a domed, underground-feeling space where one feels like a child "come out somewhere in eternity."

This phenomenology references the twenty-fourth fragment of Heraclitus: "The Aeon is a child at play with colored balls." McKenna interprets this presocratic fragment not metaphorically but literally, as an accurate description of the tryptamine state. The information in these experiences, he argues, is presented "three-dimensionally and deployed fourth-dimensionally, coded as light and as evolving surfaces."

## Self-Transforming Machine Elves

McKenna's most famous contribution to psychedelic phenomenology is his description of the entities encountered in the DMT state, which he and his brother Dennis characterized as "self-transforming machine elves" in The [[tryptamine-hypercontinuum-and-invisible-landscape]]. These entities are described as:

- Dynamically contorting topological modules distinct from a continuously transforming background
- Hyperdimensional beings that "bathe one in love" in a non-erotic but open-hearted way
- Beings that produce sounds like music and language simultaneously, conveying information that "cannot be Englished"
- Fractal reflections of previously hidden, autonomous aspects of the psyche

The entities deliver instructions: "Don't be alarmed. Remember, and do what we are doing." They warn against falling into a "loop of wonder" that would quench the ability to understand, encouraging instead focused attention on what they are demonstrating. McKenna stresses the importance of maintaining an I/Thou relationship with these beings rather than one of passive worship, noting that "there is no dignity in the universe unless we meet these things on our feet."

McKenna argues that the touch of terror these encounters inspire is actually a mark of their authenticity. The willingness of experiencers to repeat the DMT experience, despite its intensity, validates it as "existentially authentic." He suggests that the rules of pharmacology do not seem to apply in this space, where control of existence is "really a matter of focus of will and good luck."

## Translinguistic Objects and Glossolalia

A central theme of the lecture is that tryptamines work directly on the language centers of the brain, producing an experience McKenna calls "the Logos." Under the influence of DMT and psilocybin, one encounters what he describes as "translinguistic objects" or "feeling-toned, meaning-toned, three-dimensional rotating complexes of transforming light and color" that carry semantic content impossible to translate into ordinary language.

This produces a state of glossolalia distinct from the classical speaking-in-tongues phenomenon. Rather than producing random vocalizations, the tryptamine-induced glossolalia expresses what McKenna calls "the assembly language that lies behind language" or a primal "Ursprache" (original language) that emerges from within. He connects this to Robert Graves's concept in The White Goddess and to the Kabbalistic language described in the Zohar.

McKenna proposes that this translinguistic capacity is a latent human ability that could, with ten thousand years of cultural engagement, become a "cultural convenience" comparable to mathematics or language itself. The [[mckenna-cognitive-dissonance-translinguistic-object]] produced by encountering a "translinguistic object" that cannot be mapped onto existing language generates the states of wonder, astonishment, and awe at the brink of terror that characterize the tryptamine experience.

## The Black Hole Effect

McKenna describes a "black hole effect" surrounding DMT: the more precisely one articulates the experience, the less others are able to understand it. He draws an explicit analogy to astrophysical black holes, where no signal can escape. This communicative impossibility, he suggests, explains why enlightened beings are traditionally silent, and why the tryptamine experience has not been more widely investigated despite its intensity and accessibility. He notes that "the tragedy of our cultural situation is that we have no shamanic tradition."

## Borges, the Aleph, and the Sect of the Phoenix

McKenna introduces Jorge Luis Borges's short story "The Sect of the Phoenix" as a literary parallel to the tryptamine experience. Borges describes a secret sect present in every human group, whose members have transmitted a trivial, momentary act across all generations. The Secret is "sacred, but always somewhat ridiculous; its performance is furtive." McKenna connects this to Borges's "The Aleph," a point in space that contains all other points, suggesting that the Aleph represents the experience of the Secret.

When asked in the Amazon why they had been chosen as ambassadors, the mushroom reportedly answered: "Because you did not believe in anything. Because you have never given over your belief to anyone." McKenna suggests the sect is "perhaps millennia old, but it has not yet been brought to light where the historical threads may run."

## Philip K. Dick and Valis

McKenna connects the [[psilocybin-mushroom-consciousness]] to Philip K. Dick's novel Valis, in which a creature of pure information (the Logos) was buried at Nag Hammadi with the Gnostic texts circa 370 AD, existing as static information until 1947 when translation reactivated it. Both Borges and Dick, for McKenna, are describing the same phenomenon from different angles: an ancient, transmissible gnosis that operates through information rather than matter. The mushroom consciousness, like Dick's Valis, is "a thing of pure information" that exists "in dream and in the psilocybin trance, at the quantum foundation of being, in the human future, and after death."

## The Mushroom as Symbiote

McKenna recounts the hypothesis from Psilocybin: The Magic Mushroom Grower's Guide, in which [[mckenna-stropharia-cubensis-extraterrestrial-hypothesis-spore-dispersal]] is proposed as an extraterrestrial species that achieved symbiosis with humanity through association with domesticated cattle. He balances this with a second interpretation: that the human soul is so alienated from modern culture that it is experienced as extraterrestrial. The DMT trance "would remain more weird" than any Hollywood-style alien contact, and what we encounter may actually be the alienated human Self.

## Eschatology and the Standing Now

The lecture concludes with McKenna's vision of the tryptamine state as "transtemporal," an anticipation of the future described by Plato's metaphor of time as "the moving image of eternity." He describes the [[mckenna-tryptamine-ecstasy-and-platonic-eternity]] as "a stepping out of the moving image and into eternity," the nunc stans (standing now) of Thomas Aquinas, from which all of human history is seen as leading toward a culminating moment.

McKenna identifies acceleration as visible in all processes around us: fire discovered millions of years ago, language perhaps 35,000 years ago, measurement 5,000 years ago, Galileo 400 years ago, then Watson and Crick and DNA. "What is obviously happening is that everything is being drawn together." He contrasts this accelerationist vision with the "dualistic conception" of physics that projects billions of years into both past and future, calling it "very unsophisticated when applied to the nature of [[mckenna-catalyzing-consciousness-and-language]]."

His ultimate vision is of the "exteriorization of the soul and internalization of the body," producing a "superconducting lens of translinguistic matter generated out of the body of each of us at a critical juncture." One's body image would exist as a "holographic wave transform while one is at play in the fields of the Lord and living in Elysium." History, he suggests, is "the [[mckenna-history-shockwave-of-eschatology]]."

## Shamanism and the Phenomenological Tradition

McKenna reflects extensively on the nature of shamanism as practiced in indigenous societies. He distinguishes it sharply from the popular image of shamans as "embedded in a tradition," arguing that in practice shamans are "the true phenomenologists of this world" who "know plant chemistry" yet call these energy fields "spirits." He insists that "spirit" as used by shamans is a technical gloss for a very complicated concept, comparable to how a quantum physicist might use the term "charm."

McKenna describes the signature qualities that identify potential shamans in traditional societies: oddness or uniqueness, epilepsy, survival of unusual ordeals such as lightning strikes, recovery from near-fatal illnesses after extended indeterminate states, and hypersensitivity to trance states. The distinguishing characteristic he observed from his world travels was "an extraordinary centeredness" combined with intellectual sophistication.
