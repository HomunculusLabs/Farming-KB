# McKenna on DMT Entities, Borges, and the Archaic Secret

Terence McKenna's 1983 Esalen lecture "Tryptamine Hallucinogens and Consciousness" presents his most detailed first-person account of DMT entity encounters, connects the tryptamine experience to literary and philosophical traditions, and proposes that these compounds grant access to a translinguistic domain that has been preserved through secret lineages across human history. The lecture weaves together phenomenological description, speculative philosophy, and cultural criticism in McKenna's characteristic style.

## Context: The Esalen Conference

The lecture was delivered at the Lilly/Goswami Conference on Consciousness and Quantum Physics at Esalen Institute on the Big Sur coast of California in December 1983. McKenna describes himself as "an explorer rather than a scientist," noting that the area he investigates "contains insufficient data to support even the dream of being a science." He positions the tryptamine hallucinogens, specifically DMT, psilocybin, and ayahuasca, as uniquely relevant to discussions of both consciousness and quantum physics because of the intensity of the hallucinations and their concentration in the visual cortex.

## The DMT Onset and Hyperspace

McKenna describes the DMT experience as beginning approximately fifteen seconds after inhalation, with a characteristic sound "like ripping cellophane" and an ascending tone. Within thirty seconds, all available receptor sites are occupied and a complete mode shift occurs. The experiencer arrives in what McKenna calls a "merry go raum" (from the German raum, meaning space), a domed, underground-feeling space where one feels like a child "come out somewhere in eternity."

This phenomenology references the twenty-fourth fragment of Heraclitus: "The Aeon is a child at play with colored balls." McKenna interprets this presocratic fragment not metaphorically but literally, as an accurate description of the tryptamine state. The information in these experiences, he argues, is presented "three-dimensionally and deployed fourth-dimensionally, coded as light and as evolving surfaces."

## Self-Transforming Machine Elves

McKenna's most famous contribution to psychedelic phenomenology is his description of the entities encountered in the DMT state, which he and his brother Dennis characterized as "self-transforming machine elves" in The Invisible Landscape. These entities are described as:

- Dynamically contorting topological modules distinct from a continuously transforming background
- Hyperdimensional beings that "bathe one in love" in a non-erotic but open-hearted way
- Beings that produce sounds like music and language simultaneously, conveying information that "cannot be Englished"
- Fractal reflections of previously hidden, autonomous aspects of the psyche

The entities deliver instructions: "Don't be alarmed. Remember, and do what we are doing." They warn against falling into a "loop of wonder" that would quench the ability to understand, encouraging instead focused attention on what they are demonstrating. McKenna stresses the importance of maintaining an I/Thou relationship with these beings rather than one of passive worship, noting that "there is no dignity in the universe unless we meet these things on our feet."

McKenna argues that the touch of terror these encounters inspire is actually a mark of their authenticity. The willingness of experiencers to repeat the DMT experience, despite its intensity, validates it as "existentially authentic." He suggests that the rules of pharmacology do not seem to apply in this space, where control of existence is "really a matter of focus of will and good luck."

## Translinguistic Objects and Glossolalia

A central theme of the lecture is that tryptamines work directly on the language centers of the brain, producing an experience McKenna calls "the Logos." Under the influence of DMT and psilocybin, one encounters what he describes as "translinguistic objects" or "feeling-toned, meaning-toned, three-dimensional rotating complexes of transforming light and color" that carry semantic content impossible to translate into ordinary language.

This produces a state of glossolalia distinct from the classical speaking-in-tongues phenomenon. Rather than producing random vocalizations, the tryptamine-induced glossolalia expresses what McKenna calls "the assembly language that lies behind language" or a primal "Ursprache" (original language) that emerges from within. He connects this to Robert Graves's concept in The White Goddess and to the Kabbalistic language described in the Zohar.

McKenna proposes that this translinguistic capacity is a latent human ability that could, with ten thousand years of cultural engagement, become a "cultural convenience" comparable to mathematics or language itself. The cognitive dissonance produced by encountering a "translinguistic object" that cannot be mapped onto existing language generates the states of wonder, astonishment, and awe at the brink of terror that characterize the tryptamine experience.

## The Black Hole Effect

McKenna describes a "black hole effect" surrounding DMT: the more precisely one articulates the experience, the less others are able to understand it. He draws an explicit analogy to astrophysical black holes, where no signal can escape. This communicative impossibility, he suggests, explains why enlightened beings are traditionally silent, and why the tryptamine experience has not been more widely investigated despite its intensity and accessibility. He notes that "the tragedy of our cultural situation is that we have no shamanic tradition."

## Borges, the Aleph, and the Sect of the Phoenix

McKenna introduces Jorge Luis Borges's short story "The Sect of the Phoenix" as a literary parallel to the tryptamine experience. Borges describes a secret sect present in every human group, whose members have transmitted a trivial, momentary act across all generations. The Secret is "sacred, but always somewhat ridiculous; its performance is furtive." McKenna connects this to Borges's "The Aleph," a point in space that contains all other points, suggesting that the Aleph represents the experience of the Secret.

When asked in the Amazon why they had been chosen as ambassadors, the mushroom reportedly answered: "Because you did not believe in anything. Because you have never given over your belief to anyone." McKenna suggests the sect is "perhaps millennia old, but it has not yet been brought to light where the historical threads may run."

## Philip K. Dick and Valis

McKenna connects the mushroom consciousness to Philip K. Dick's novel Valis, in which a creature of pure information (the Logos) was buried at Nag Hammadi with the Gnostic texts circa 370 AD, existing as static information until 1947 when translation reactivated it. Both Borges and Dick, for McKenna, are describing the same phenomenon from different angles: an ancient, transmissible gnosis that operates through information rather than matter. The mushroom consciousness, like Dick's Valis, is "a thing of pure information" that exists "in dream and in the psilocybin trance, at the quantum foundation of being, in the human future, and after death."

## The Mushroom as Symbiote

McKenna recounts the hypothesis from Psilocybin: The Magic Mushroom Grower's Guide, in which Stropharia cubensis is proposed as an extraterrestrial species that achieved symbiosis with humanity through association with domesticated cattle. He balances this with a second interpretation: that the human soul is so alienated from modern culture that it is experienced as extraterrestrial. The DMT trance "would remain more weird" than any Hollywood-style alien contact, and what we encounter may actually be the alienated human Self.

## Eschatology and the Standing Now

The lecture concludes with McKenna's vision of the tryptamine state as "transtemporal," an anticipation of the future described by Plato's metaphor of time as "the moving image of eternity." He describes the tryptamine ecstasy as "a stepping out of the moving image and into eternity," the nunc stans (standing now) of Thomas Aquinas, from which all of human history is seen as leading toward a culminating moment.

McKenna identifies acceleration as visible in all processes around us: fire discovered millions of years ago, language perhaps 35,000 years ago, measurement 5,000 years ago, Galileo 400 years ago, then Watson and Crick and DNA. "What is obviously happening is that everything is being drawn together." He contrasts this accelerationist vision with the "dualistic conception" of physics that projects billions of years into both past and future, calling it "very unsophisticated when applied to the nature of consciousness and language."

His ultimate vision is of the "exteriorization of the soul and internalization of the body," producing a "superconducting lens of translinguistic matter generated out of the body of each of us at a critical juncture." One's body image would exist as a "holographic wave transform while one is at play in the fields of the Lord and living in Elysium." History, he suggests, is "the shockwave of eschatology."

## Shamanism and the Phenomenological Tradition

McKenna reflects extensively on the nature of shamanism as practiced in indigenous societies. He distinguishes it sharply from the popular image of shamans as "embedded in a tradition," arguing that in practice shamans are "the true phenomenologists of this world" who "know plant chemistry" yet call these energy fields "spirits." He insists that "spirit" as used by shamans is a technical gloss for a very complicated concept, comparable to how a quantum physicist might use the term "charm."

McKenna describes the signature qualities that identify potential shamans in traditional societies: oddness or uniqueness, epilepsy, survival of unusual ordeals such as lightning strikes, recovery from near-fatal illnesses after extended indeterminate states, and hypersensitivity to trance states. The distinguishing characteristic he observed from his world travels was "an extraordinary centeredness" combined with intellectual sophistication.

## DMT as Endogenous Compound

McKenna notes the structural and pharmacological significance of the fact that DMT is an endogenous hallucinogen present in small amounts in the human brain. He connects this to the structural similarity between psilocybin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) and serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine), the major neurotransmitter found in all life and most concentrated in humans. The rapid onset of DMT (forty-five seconds) and brief duration (five minutes) demonstrate that "the brain is absolutely at home with this compound," in contrast to the prolonged retention of LSD in the body.

## Cautionary Notes

Despite his enthusiasm, McKenna includes explicit cautions. He notes that "all compounds are potentially dangerous, and all compounds, at sufficient doses or repeated over time, involve risks." He advises that "the library is the first place to go when looking into taking a new compound." He stresses the importance of building up to the experience gradually, reflecting deeply, and mapping experiences back onto "the history of the race and the philosophical and religious accomplishments of the species."

He also draws a distinction between plant-derived compounds and synthetic ones: "There is some certainty that one is dealing with a creature of integrity if one deals with a plant, but the creatures born in the demonic artifice of laboratories have to be dealt with very, very carefully."

## Absence in Art Historical Traditions

A puzzling observation McKenna raises is the near-total absence of DMT-like motifs in world art traditions. While he found similarities between his LSD experiences and Mahayana Buddhist iconography (which actually drove him to collect Mahayana art), he notes that "what amazed me was the total absence of the motifs of DMT. It is not there; it is not there in any tradition familiar to me." This absence, he suggests, may relate to the black hole effect that makes the experience so difficult to communicate and preserve culturally.

## The Stropharia Cubensis as Teaching Plant

A recurring theme in the lecture is McKenna's emphasis on Stropharia cubensis (now Psilocybe cubensis) as the primary vehicle for accessing the tryptamine dimension. He describes it as "the teaching plant par excellence" and contrasts it with other psychedelic substances, noting its unique capacity to produce experiences of genuine otherness rather than merely distorted perception. His specific focus on this species reflects his belief that it occupies a special position among entheogenic organisms, one connected to the ancient relationship between hominids and mushrooms that he develops more fully in his book Food of the Gods.

McKenna also speculates about the evolutionary implications of the human-psilocybin relationship, suggesting that the integration of psilocybin into early hominid diets may have influenced the development of language, visual acuity, and social bonding, themes he elaborates in his Stoned Ape hypothesis presented elsewhere in his work.

## Language and the Translinguistic

A thread running throughout the lecture is McKenna's fascination with the relationship between DMT experiences and language. He describes encountering "translinguistic matter" or "visible language" in the tryptamine space, suggesting that the DMT experience reveals a level of reality where language and matter are not separate but are aspects of the same phenomenon. This idea connects to his broader theory that language itself may have emerged from early hominid encounters with psychoactive mushrooms, which he argues would have increased synesthesia and the capacity to form complex vocalizations and conceptual associations.

He notes that in the DMT state, "the language is actually beheld," meaning it becomes a visible, tactile phenomenon rather than an abstract system of signs. This reverses the ordinary relationship between language and perception, raising profound questions about the nature of linguistic cognition and its relationship to consciousness.
