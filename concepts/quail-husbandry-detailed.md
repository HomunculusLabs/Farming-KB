# Quail Husbandry (Detailed)

Coturnix quail (Coturnix japonica) are the most practical micro-livestock for
the homestead. They reach maturity in just 6 weeks, begin laying eggs at 7-8
weeks, and require minimal space. A single breeder cage (24x18 inches) can
house a trio (1 male, 2 females). They are quiet, efficient, and ideal for
urban or space-constrained homesteads where chickens or ducks are not feasible.

## Why Coturnix Quail

- Fastest turnaround of any meat animal: egg to egg in 7 weeks, egg to
  harvest in 8-10 weeks.
- Minimal space requirements: 1 sq ft per bird in cages.
- Quiet: males produce a soft crow but are not loud enough to bother neighbors.
- No regulations in most areas (check local codes — often classified as
  "game birds" not poultry).
- Feed efficiency: 2.5-3 lbs of feed per lb of gain.
- High egg production: 250-320 eggs per hen per year.

## Breed Varieties

All Coturnix quail are the same species, but selective breeding has produced
several color and production varieties:

### Standard Varieties
- **Pharaoh (Wild Type)** — Brown, camouflaged. Hardy, good dual-purpose.
  The original Japanese quail.
- **Texas A&M** — White, bred for meat production. Larger, calmer, 10-14 oz
  at maturity. Good meat yield.
- **Jumbo Brown** — Larger than Pharaoh, 10-14 oz. Good meat and egg
  production. Most common homestead variety.
- **Golden** — Light tan/gold. Good layers, attractive.

### Color Varieties (Primarily Ornamental)
- **Tibetan (Rosetta)** — Dark brown/black with light belly. Hardy.
- **Tuxedo** — Brown with white chest. Attractive, good layers.
- **Scarlet (Red Range)** — Rust/red colored.
- **Silver** — Light gray/silver.
- **Cinnamon** — Light brown, calm.

### Production Considerations
- Jumbo Brown and Texas A&M are the best choices for meat production.
- Pharaoh and Golden are reliable layers.
- All varieties interbreed freely.

## Cage Systems

### Individual Breeder Cages
- Wire cage: 24x18x12 inches (length x width x height) for one trio.
- 1/2 inch wire floor with droppings tray beneath.
- Nipple waterers and tube feeders attach to cage front.
- Advantages: controlled breeding, individual records, clean eggs.
- Disadvantages: more labor, less natural behavior.

### Colony Cages
- Larger cages housing 4-6 females with 1-2 males.
- Minimum 1 sq ft per bird.
- More social interaction but risk of aggression and cannibalism.
- Remove aggressive birds immediately.

### Aviary / Floor System
- Birds kept on the ground with deep litter (pine shavings).
- More natural, allows dust bathing and foraging.
- Higher disease risk (contact with droppings).
- Eggs must be collected from floor (may be soiled).
- Less common for commercial production but suitable for homesteads.

### Cage Design Principles
- Wire mesh: 1/2 inch for floors, 1 inch for sides. Smaller mesh for
  quail (they can squeeze through gaps).
- Roof: solid or wire with cover to prevent escape and predator access.
- Droppings tray: slide-out tray beneath wire floor. Clean weekly.
- Ventilation: essential but no drafts. Quail produce ammonia quickly.
- Lighting: 14-16 hours of light per day for maximum egg production.
  A simple LED light on a timer works.

## Feeding

### Commercial Feed
- Game bird or turkey starter: 24-30% protein for first 6 weeks.
- Layer feed: 18-22% protein for mature birds.
- Feed consumption: approximately 15-20 grams per bird per day.
- Feed free-choice in tube feeders or small troughs.

### Water
- Nipple waterers are cleanest (use quail-sized nipples).
- Bell waterers or small crocks work but require daily cleaning.
- Quail need water at all times. They dehydrate quickly.
- Add poultry vitamins/electrolytes to water during stress periods.

### Supplements
- Grit (fine) free-choice if feeding anything other than commercial pellets.
- Oyster shell free-choice for layers.
- Mealworms as protein supplement (10% of diet max).
- Dark leafy greens (chopped fine), cooked egg, and seeds as treats.

### Feed Formulation
- See [[livestock-feed-formulation]] for making custom rations.
- Protein is the most critical nutrient — quail need higher protein than
  chickens at all life stages.
- Avoid medicated feeds unless specifically needed.

## Egg Production

- Coturnix hens begin laying at 6-8 weeks of age.
- Production: 250-320 eggs per year (nearly an egg per day).
- Eggs: approximately 10 grams (1/5 the size of a chicken egg).
- 5-6 quail eggs equal one chicken egg in volume.
- Quail eggs have a higher yolk-to-white ratio than chicken eggs — richer,
  creamier.
- Excellent for pickling, appetizers, and children's lunches.
- Egg color: white to cream with brown speckles.
- Collect eggs daily. Store at 40-45°F for up to 6 weeks.
- Hatching rate: highest for eggs 1-7 days old, declining after that.
- Do not wash hatching eggs — wipe with dry cloth only.

## Processing

### Harvest Age
- Ideal harvest: 7-10 weeks of age. After 10 weeks, meat becomes tougher
  and feed efficiency drops.
- Weight at harvest: 6-8 oz (Pharaoh), 10-14 oz (Jumbo/Texas A&M).

### Processing Steps
- Restrict feed 6-8 hours before processing (water OK).
- Dispatch: cervical dislocation is simplest for small birds.
- Scald at 140°F for 15-20 seconds (brief — quail skin tears easily).
- Pluck by hand or with a small plucker.
- Cool carcass in ice water for 15-30 minutes.
- Eviscerate through vent, remove crop and entrails.
- Rinse and pat dry.
- Yield: approximately 70% dress-out (live to edible weight).
- Quail are typically cooked whole (roasted, grilled, fried).
- See [[animal-harvest-and-processing]] for broader processing principles.

## Breeding and Incubation

### Breeding Ratio
- 1 male per 2-3 females in breeder cages.
- Males become aggressive in crowded conditions. Monitor and separate.
- Breeding season: year-round with 14+ hours of light.
