# Coprophilous Zygomycetes on Dung

Coprophilous Zygomycetes, particularly members of the order
[[biodiversity-zygomycota-diversity]], represent one of the most distinctive fungal guilds
associated with animal dung. These fungi are encountered on nearly all
collections of dung from wild animals, yet they remain poorly documented
in many regions. Their study requires specialized techniques for
sampling, isolation, culturing, and microscopic examination that differ
markedly from those used for other fungal groups.

## Sampling Dung for Zygomycetes

Zygomycetes are not cellulosic and therefore grow only on the
water-soluble nutrients contained in dung. Suitable dung should be dark,
shiny, and as fresh as possible. Dung that appears rough or whitish has
had nutrients leeched away and is not productive; spores may have been
washed away or may be too old to germinate. At least one species of
Mucor can be found on almost any dung collected from wild animals.

The best source of coprophilous Zygomycetes is the dung of small
animals, especially rats and mice, although good results have been
obtained from the feces of bats, small lizards, and frogs. The
relatively large feces of mammalian herbivores facilitate collection but
are not the best source for Zygomycetes because relatively few genera
are encountered and the possibility of introducing mites into the
laboratory increases. Some species are known from highly specific
sources: Dichotomocladium floriidanum, for example, is known only
from pocket gopher dung collected near Gainesville, Florida.

In desert areas, kangaroo rats dig shallow holes and deposit fecal
pellets nearby. Ants may also collect pieces of small mammal dung and
deposit them near nest entrances. In the Mojave Desert, dung at the base
of Joshua trees at approximately 1,220 m elevation has proven to be the
best source of Spirodactylon aureum. Other productive collecting
sites include storage areas, wood piles, and any locations where small
animals feed or congregate.

Dung should be collected using forceps and rubber gloves. Wet dung
should be placed in paper bags, never in airtight containers, because
fungal growth will commence before the dung can be processed. When
completely dry, dung pellets can be stored in airtight containers in a
refrigerator for long periods.

## Moist Chamber and Isolation Techniques

Moist chambers of sizes appropriate for the dung pellets are used to
isolate Zygomycetes. Species in the Mucorales usually begin to appear
after four or five days when incubated at room temperature (22-25
degrees C). It is best to begin incubating on a Wednesday or Thursday so
that the succession of fungi can be observed during the following week.

Several taxa grow only at temperatures below 20 degrees C, so incubating
some samples at 18 degrees C is beneficial. Coprophilous Zygomycetes
are isolated most easily using stainless-steel minuten insect pins
mounted in wooden handles. Forceps are sterilized in an open flame,
cooled in 95% ethyl alcohol, and used to transfer spores to sterile
media.

After each examination, tall sporophores of Mucor and other taxa should
be pushed down with a steel needle or inoculating loop, continued for
several days. This procedure facilitates isolation of zygomycetous
[[fungal-parasites]] such as Piptocephalis and Syncephalis, which
usually do not grow as high as the host fungi.

## Culture Media and Conditions

Zygomycetes, especially members of Mucorales, grow on many culture
media. Nutrient-rich media most useful for their culture include
malt-extract yeast-extract [[agar]] (MEYE), Emerson's YpSs agar, V8-juice
agar, and potato-dextrose agar. Media effective for zygospore formation
include Pablum agar, Pablum agar plus dextrose, Leonian's agar plus
yeast extract, acidic potato-dextrose agar, and whey agar.

Nutrient-poor media useful for Zygomycetes include modified synthetic
Mucor agar (MSMA), wheat germ agar, cornmeal agar, potato-carrot agar,
Choanephora agar, and 0.2-strength YpSs agar. These media simulate
natural growth conditions and are optimal for observing branching
patterns, stolon formation, and vegetative structures.

Most Mucorales grow well at 25 degrees C, though some taxa may do much
better at 30 degrees C. No coprophilous thermophilic Mucorales are
known, but Rhizomucor and Thermomucor are thermophilic and can be
isolated from compost piles that may contain dung. Many species are
psychrotolerant or psychrophilic, sporulating only at temperatures
below 20 degrees C. Zygospore formation in some species proceeds best
at 15 degrees C, whereas in others sexual spores are produced only after
30-60 days at 7-10 degrees C.

Pilobolus species are obligate coprophiles and must be grown on
media containing dung extract or hemin. Their sporangia are transferred
with forceps directly to medium, incubated at 37 degrees C overnight,
then placed in a lighted incubator. Sporangia should form in less than
a week.

## Microscopic Examination and Identification

Cultures from nutrient-rich media may enhance zygospore formation but
are not ideal for study because of increased aerial hyphae. Cultures
from nutrient-poor media enhance asexual reproduction and reveal
branching patterns, stolons, and rhizoids.

The best mounting agent for identification is KOH-phloxine. A small
amount of culture is transferred to a drop of 95% ethyl alcohol on a
slide; excess alcohol is drained and one drop of 2% KOH is added. The
specimen is manipulated to suit, covered with a cover slip, and sealed
with melted paraffin coated with clear fingernail polish. These mounts
are good for two to three days. More permanent preparations lasting one
year or more are made by mounting fungi in lactophenol cotton blue.

## Storage and Preservation

Culturable Zygomycetes can be stored in test tubes in a refrigerator
for six to twelve months. V8-juice agar promotes sporulation, as do
many nutrient-poor agars, making them ideal for storage. The cap should
be loose and wrapped with Parafilm. Zygomycetes can be stored for much
longer periods through lyophilization in double-strength skim milk or by
freezing at minus 85 degrees C in cryovials with 50% glycerol.
