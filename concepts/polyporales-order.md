# Order Polyporales

## Overview

Polyporales is an order of basidiomycete fungi within the class Agaricomycetes, comprising the polypores (bracket fungi, shelf fungi, and pore-bearing crusts). These fungi are among the most important decomposers of woody material in terrestrial ecosystems, playing critical roles in carbon cycling and forest dynamics. The order is characterized by a poroid (pored), merulioid (wrinkled), or smooth hymenophore, though some members have gills (notably Lentinula and Panus). Polyporales includes cultivated species (shiitake, oyster), medicinal fungi (reishi, turkey tail), and devastating forest pathogens (heart rot fungi).

## Taxonomic History and Molecular Redefinition

The concept of Polyporales has undergone radical revision with molecular phylogenetics. Traditional systems defined polypores as any basidiomycete with a poroid hymenophore, grouping them primarily by morphology. The multi-gene phylogenies of Hibbett et al. (2007), Binder et al. (2005, 2013), and Floudas et al. (2012) demonstrated that poroid fruiting bodies evolved independently at least 15 times across Agaricomycetes.

The current monophyletic Polyporales is defined by molecular data and includes many non-pored genera. The order now contains approximately 1,800 described species organized into approximately 20 families. Key families were redefined: Ganodermataceae, Polyporaceae, Fomitopsidaceae, Meruliaceae, Meruliaceae, and Phanerochaetaceae were reshuffled based on phylogenetic relationships rather than pore morphology.

## Morphological Diversity

### Bracket and Shelf Fungi
The classic polypore form consists of a hoof-shaped, semicircular, or fan-shaped fruiting body projecting horizontally from wood surfaces. These brackets (also called conks) may be:
- **Annual**: soft, fleshy, decomposing within one season (e.g., Trametes versicolor)
- **Perennial**: tough, woody, adding new growth layers annually (e.g., Ganoderma, Fomes)
- **Single**: solitary brackets (e.g., Ganoderma lucidum)
- **Overlapping**: imbricate clusters (e.g., Trametes, Bjerkandera)

### Pore Surface
The hymenophore consists of tubes opening as pores. Pore characteristics are taxonomically important:
- **Pore shape**: round, angular, elongated, daedaleoid (maze-like), lamellate (gill-like)
- **Pore size**: fine (<0.5 mm per pore) to large (2+ mm)
- **Color**: white, cream, yellow, pink, orange, brown, gray
- **Reaction**: some species change color when bruised or with chemical reagents

### Crust (Corticioid) Forms
Many Polyporales produce resupinate (crust-like) fruiting bodies that adhere tightly to the substrate surface. The hymenophore may be smooth, tuberculate, odontoid (toothed), or merulioid (wrinkled). Examples include Phanerochaete chrysosporium (a white-rot model organism) and various Stereum species.

### Effused-Reflexed Forms
These begin as crusts but develop raised, bracket-like margins. This intermediate morphology is common in genera like Stereum, Trametes, and Hymenochaete.

### Gilled Forms
Several genera within Polyporales have evolved true gills (lamellae), demonstrating that gill morphology does not reliably indicate relationship to Agaricales. Key gilled polyporale genera include:
- **Lentinula**: includes L. edodes (shiitake), commercially the second most cultivated mushroom worldwide
- **Panus**: oyster-like mushrooms with tough texture
- **Neolentinus**: tough, wood-inhabiting gilled fungi
- **Lentinus**: gilled polypores now mostly reclassified

### Toothed Forms
Some Polyporales have an odontoid (toothed) hymenophore resembling that of the order Hericiales. Examples include certain Irpex species with tooth-like projections.

## Major Families and Genera

### Polyporaceae
The core polypore family, though extensively revised. It includes Trametes (turkey tails and allies), Coriolopsis, and related genera. *Trametes versicolor* (turkey tail) is one of the most widespread wood-decay fungi globally and is extensively studied for its medicinal properties, particularly the protein-bound polysaccharides PSK and PSP used as cancer adjuvant therapies in Asia.

### Ganodermataceae
Ganodermataceae is defined by double-walled basidiospores with a distinctive inner wall that is ornamented or truncate. The family includes:
- **Ganoderma**: reishi/lingzhi mushrooms, the most commercially important medicinal mushrooms. *G. lucidum* (sensu stricto) and *G. tsugae* are perennial shelf fungi with a lacquered appearance. Used in traditional medicine across Asia for millennia.
- **Amauroderma**: tropical ganodermataceous fungi with non-laccate (non-glossy) surfaces
- **Ganoderma species** are significant pathogens of tropical crops, causing basal stem rot of oil palm and butt rot of rubber trees

### Fomitopsidaceae
Fomitopsidaceae includes the brown-rot fungi:
- **Fomes**: *F. fomentarius* (tinder conk), used historically as fire-starting tinder and for felt-making. A large perennial bracket fungus on birch and beech.
- **Fomitopsis**: *F. pinicola* (red-belted conk), a common brown-rot fungus of conifers. *F. betulina* (birch polypore) is edible when young.
- **Piptoporus**: *P. betulinus* (birch polypore/razor-strop fungus), used historically for sharpening tools and as a medicinal fungus. Ötzi the Iceman carried P. betulinus.

### Meruliaceae
Meruliaceae includes fungi with merulioid (wrinkled, folded) or odontoid hymenophores:
- **Phlebia**: resupinate fungi with wrinkled pore surfaces
- **Bjerkandera**: *B. adusta* (smoky polypore), a common wood decomposer
- **Merulius**: the type genus, with strongly wrinkled, gelatinous fruiting bodies

### Phanerochaetaceae
Phanerochaetaceae includes white-rot fungi of considerable industrial and ecological importance:
- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium**: a model white-rot fungus used in biotechnology for lignin degradation studies and bioremediation. Its lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase enzymes have been extensively characterized.
- **Phanerochaete velutina**: aggressive wood decomposer, studied for competitive interactions with other fungi

### Hymenochaetaceae
Hymenochaetaceae includes fungi with setae (thick-walled, dark, pointed hyphal ends) in the hymenium:
- **Hymenochaete**: crust fungi causing canker diseases on trees
- **Inonotus**: *I. obliquus* (chaga), a sterile conk on birch trees widely used medicinally. *I. hispidus* causes canker and white rot.
- **Phellinus**: a large genus of woody, perennial polypores, some causing heart rot. *P. igniarius* was historically used as a fire-starting tinder.

### Steccherinaceae
A recently defined family including *Steccherinum* and *Antrodia*. These include both poroid and odontoid forms and are important wood decomposers.

## Wood Decay Types

### White Rot
White-rot fungi degrade all major wood components: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. The removal of lignin leaves the wood bleached (white) and fibrous. This is the most efficient form of wood decay and is characteristic of most Polyporales. Key enzyme systems include:
- **Lignin peroxidase (LiP)**: oxidizes non-phenolic lignin units
- **Manganese peroxidase (MnP)**: oxidizes phenolic lignin and generates Mn(III) chelates
- **Laccase**: oxidizes phenolic substrates with molecular oxygen

### Brown Rot
Brown-rot fungi primarily degrade cellulose and hemicellulose while largely leaving lignin modified but not removed. The wood becomes brown and crumbly as it loses its structural polysaccharides. Brown rot uses a Fenton chemistry-based system generating hydroxyl radicals to depolymerize cellulose. Major brown-rot polyporales include *Fomitopsis pinicola*, *Serpula lacrymans*, and *Wolffiporia cocos*.

### Simultaneous vs. Selective Decay
White-rot fungi may remove all wood components simultaneously or selectively target lignin first (selective delignification). Selective delignification is of interest for biopulping in the paper industry, as it reduces the chemical inputs needed for pulp processing.

## Ecological and Economic Importance

### Forest Ecosystem Dynamics
Polyporales are primary agents of wood decomposition in forests, driving carbon cycling and nutrient release. They facilitate gap dynamics by killing weakened trees and creating habitat for other organisms. Dead wood colonized by polyporales supports specialized beetle and insect communities.

### Timber and Structural Damage
Brown-rot polyporales cause enormous economic damage to timber in service and standing trees. *Serpula lacrymans* (dry rot) is the single most damaging fungus to buildings in temperate regions. *Ganoderma* species cause butt rot and heart rot in timber trees, reducing wood quality.

### Medicinal Applications
Several Polyporales have extensive medicinal use:
- **Ganoderma lucidum**: immunomodulation, anti-cancer adjunct, hepatoprotection
- **Trametes versicolor**: PSK/PSP polysaccharides as cancer therapy adjuvants (approved in Japan and China)
- **Inonotus obliquus** (chaga): antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, potential anti-cancer properties
- **Fomes fomentarius**: traditional medicine, antibacterial properties
- **Piptoporus betulinus**: antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory

### Biotechnological Applications
Polyporales enzymes have applications in:
- **Bioremediation**: degradation of pollutants including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, dyes, and explosives
- **Biopulping**: pretreatment of wood chips to reduce chemical use in paper production
- **Biofuel production**: enzymatic conversion of lignocellulose to fermentable sugars

## Cultivation

Several Polyporales species are commercially cultivated:
- **Lentinula edodes** (shiitake): second most cultivated mushroom globally after Agaricus bisporus, grown on supplemented hardwood sawdust or natural logs
- **Pleurotus ostreatus** (oyster mushroom): easily cultivated on diverse substrates, important food source
- **Ganoderma lucidum**: cultivated for medicinal products, using wood substrates or synthetic media

## Biogeography

Polyporales are cosmopolitan, found in all terrestrial ecosystems where wood is available. Tropical regions harbor the highest diversity, with many species restricted to specific tree hosts or forest types. Temperate and boreal forests have diverse assemblages adapted to conifer and hardwood substrates. Island biogeography has produced endemic polypore floras, particularly in oceanic archipelagos.
