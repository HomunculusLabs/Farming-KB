## Winter Harvest Handbook Index M-W: Comprehensive Topic Reference

This page expands the M–W index entries of *The Winter Harvest Handbook* into a structured topical reference for winter vegetable production systems.

## Market Gardening History

### Paris Market Gardens (pp. 13–18, 21–24)
The 19th-century Parisian *maraîchers* (market gardeners) operated on small plots within and around Paris, producing extraordinary volumes of fresh vegetables year-round. Their system relied on three pillars: **horse manure** (abundant from city stables), **glass cloches** (for individual plant protection), and **hotbeds** (manure-heated growing frames). At peak production, Parisian market gardens fed the city"s population on roughly 6% of its land area.

### British Market Gardens (pp. 18–21)
British growers adapted French intensive techniques to their climate, though with less intensive inputs. Key historical figures include Weathers, Smith, and Thick. British gardens typically relied more on cold frames and [[dutch-lights]] than on manure-heated hotbeds.

### Louis Savier's Garden (pp. 21–24)
A documented case study of a French-style intensive market garden. Savier's operation demonstrated the adaptability of intensive techniques to different cultural and economic contexts.

## Mobile and Movable Greenhouses

### Design and Construction (pp. 106–113, 116–117)
Mobile greenhouses are structures mounted on rail systems that can be moved between separate growing areas. This allows one greenhouse to serve multiple plots sequentially — summer crops get full sun exposure, then the house moves to cover winter crops on an adjacent plot. Coleman's design uses steel pipe rails with the greenhouse structure on wheeled carriers.

### Advantages (pp. 7–8, 105–106, 122)
- **Single structure, multiple plots** — cost efficiency of one greenhouse serving 2–3 growing areas
- **Built-in crop rotation** — the structure itself moves, breaking pest and disease cycles that accumulate in permanent greenhouses
- **Summer/winter dual use** — tomatoes, cucumbers, and peppers in summer; greens in winter
- **Soil building** — each plot gets a rest period with cover crops between greenhouse occupations

### Ventilation and Vole Problems
Adequate ventilation via roll-up sidewalls and end-wall vents prevents overheating — a greater risk than underheating. Moving the greenhouse periodically disrupts meadow vole habitat and reduces rodent damage (pp. 106, 113, 116–117, 175–176).

## Protected Cropping Structures

### Quick Hoops (pp. 94, 122, 124–127, 129–130)
Low-cost tunnel structures made from PVC conduit or wire hoops covered with row cover fabric or polyethylene. Supported with sand bags for anchoring. The most accessible entry point for protected cultivation.

### Row Covers (pp. 5–6, 29, 56, 58–65, 74, 158)
- **Spun-bonded polyester** — standard floating row covers for frost protection (1–4°F per layer) and insect exclusion
- **Aluminized cloth** (pp. 60–61) — reflective covers that increase light levels and provide additional warmth
- **PVA (polyvinyl alcohol)** (pp. 62–63, 158) — permeable plastic film that allows gas exchange while providing frost protection

### Wiggle Wire / Spring-Lock Channels (pp. 110, 113, 116)
Metal tracks mounted on greenhouse frames that hold plastic coverings securely using preformed spring wire. Enables easy attachment and removal of coverings for ventilation.

### Tunnel Greenhouses
Including high tunnels (pp. 2, 54, 56–59, 65, 93, 106) and quick hoops. The photograph on p.4 illustrates a typical tunnel greenhouse installation.

## Crop Management and Scheduling

### Harvest Scheduling (pp. 34, 48–50)
Planning harvest timing across multiple protected environments. Coleman's system extends fresh vegetable availability from approximately October through April in Zone 5, with the period from December through February (Persephone Period) focused on harvesting already-mature crops rather than active growth.

### Planting Schedules (pp. 28, 39, 44, 48–50, 130, 164)
Detailed sowing calendars organized by crop, planting site (outdoor, early house, late house, cool house), and succession timing. The fundamental principle: get crops to harvestable size before daylight drops below 10 hours.

### Succession Planting
Staggered sowings ensuring continuous availability. Chard succession in cool houses (p. 88, 102), cucumber succession (p. 102), radish succession (p. 85), and salad mix succession (p. 75, 85) are all documented.

### Microclimates (pp. 21, 27, 51, 57, 59, 65, 92, 105)
Protected structures create distinct microclimates that differ significantly from ambient conditions. Understanding and managing these microclimates — temperature, humidity, light, and air movement — is essential for successful winter production.

### Vertical Production (pp. 94, 98, 99, 101, 103, 107)
Growing vining crops (tomatoes, cucumbers) vertically on trellises to maximize space efficiency in greenhouses. Vertical production is primarily used for summer greenhouse crops.

## Soil and Fertility

### Soil Types and Amendments
- **Sandy acid podzol** (pp. 131–132) — Coleman's native soil type at [[four-season-farm]] in Maine
- **Limestone** (p. 102, 132) — for pH adjustment
- **Greensand** (p. 132) — potassium source and soil conditioner
- **Phosphate rock** (p. 132) — slow-release phosphorus
- **Rock powders** (p. 132) — broad-spectrum mineral amendments
- **Nitrogen** (pp. 101, 134, 182, 185) — managed through compost, green manures, and crop rotation
- **Wood shavings** (p. 138) — carbon source for compost

### Soil Preparation (pp. 103, 134–137)
Broadforks (pp. 135–137) are preferred over power tillers for maintaining soil structure. The Tilther (pp. 134–135) provides shallow tillage for surface preparation without deep soil disturbance.

### Crop Debris Management (pp. 139–140)
Post-harvest residue is managed carefully to prevent disease carryover while returning organic matter to the soil. Scythes (pp. 139, 156, 192) are used for cutting back mature crop residue.

## Pests and Weed Control

### Insect Pests
- **Aphids** (pp. 184–185) — primary greenhouse pest, especially on spinach
- **Cucumber beetles** (p. 182) — damage cucurbits and can spread disease
- **Flea beetles** (p. 93) — brassica pest, controlled with floating row covers
- **Meadow voles** (pp. 175–178) — rodent pest that gnaws stems and roots under snow and mulch
