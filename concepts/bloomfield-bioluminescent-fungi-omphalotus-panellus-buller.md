## Panellus Stypticus and Buller's Experiments

A. H. Reginald Buller made a systematic study of fungal luminescence,
working extensively with Panellus stypticus, a little bracket fungus
that grows on tree stumps. In his Researches on Fungi, Buller describes
rising at 4:00 A.M. and walking to his laboratory before sunrise so that
his eyes were adapted to low light conditions. He chronicles his
observations, recording how long it took at different times of the day
before he could perceive the glow from the fruiting bodies in his
darkroom.

To avoid the lights from street lamps during his commute, Buller muffled
his head in horse blinders so that his eyes would arrive at the
laboratory in peak condition for observing the faint bioluminescence.
This eccentric dedication exemplifies Buller's approach to mycological
research, which combined rigorous methodology with personal
adventurousness.

## Photographic Evidence and Physiological Studies

Buller took photographs using only the light from Panellus mycelia and
fruiting bodies, demonstrating that fungal bioluminescence was
sufficiently intense for photographic exposure. He studied the
dependence of luminescence on oxygen availability and temperature,
establishing that the biochemical light-producing reaction requires
oxygen and proceeds more rapidly at moderate temperatures.

Buller also discovered a genetic component to luminescence. Collections
of Panellus from [[huckleberries-and-wild-berries-of-north-america]] were luminous, but a strain sent from
England did not glow. When the transatlantic strains were paired on
agar, their hyphae fused, producing a hybrid mycelium with luminous and
non-luminous sectors. This heterokaryon experiment demonstrated that the
luminescence trait was genetically determined and could be expressed or
suppressed depending on the nuclear composition of the mycelium.

## Practical Applications and Wartime Use

Some luminescent fruiting bodies emit enough light for reading. It is
claimed that in the trenches of the First World War, soldiers attached
mushrooms to their helmets to avoid nighttime collisions without drawing
the attention of a sniper. While this account may be apocryphal, the
underlying principle is sound: [[bioluminescent-fungi]] produce visible
light without generating heat or requiring any external energy source
beyond oxygen.

## The Function of Fungal Luminescence

The function of luminescence for fungi remains an enigma. For fireflies
and deep-sea fish, light emission serves to attract mates and lure prey.
Flashes of light allow some planktonic organisms to outwit their
predators. Single-celled dinoflagellates flash in response to
disturbance by fish swimming through their blooms. Crustaceans feed on
dinoflagellate blooms, and experiments show that by illuminating their
immediate surroundings, dinoflagellates expose these predators to the
fish.

Fungal luminescence consumes very little energy and may have no
ecological value at all. However, some authors have speculated that
glowing fruiting bodies might attract insects that would serve as
vectors for [[adaptive-anemotropism-spore-dispersal-optimization-mushroom-evolution]]. Their greenish light is certainly visible
to insects, and the possibility that insects could augment the chief
mechanism of spore dispersal by wind is intriguing.

## Insect Attraction Hypothesis

Any mycologist who has spent time [[studying-fungi-living-plants-macrofungal-diversity]] outside the laboratory
has noticed the activity of insects around fruiting bodies. Nicholas
Money describes a white bracket fungus dissolving a fallen beech tree
near his home, with midges flitting around the stacks of spore-producing
shelves jutting from the decaying trunk. The question of whether these
insects are carrying spores remains open for investigation.

If bioluminescence does attract insects, it would represent a convergent
evolution with the insect-pollinated flowers of angiosperms. In both
cases, a visual signal (light or color) draws mobile organisms to a
reproductive structure, facilitating the dispersal of genetic material.
The difference is that flowers offer nectar as a reward, while luminous
fungi appear to offer nothing to their insect visitors beyond the light
itself.

## Biochemical Mechanism

Fungal bioluminescence involves the oxidation of a luciferin molecule by
a luciferase enzyme, with molecular oxygen as the substrate. This is the
same general mechanism found in firefly bioluminescence, though the
specific luciferin and luciferase molecules differ. The reaction
produces oxyluciferin, carbon dioxide, and light in the blue-green
portion of the visible spectrum (approximately 520-530 nanometers
wavelength).

The low energy cost of bioluminescence is notable. Unlike incandescent
light sources, which convert most of their energy to heat, the
biochemical light-producing reaction is nearly 100% efficient at
converting chemical energy to visible light. This efficiency suggests
that even if luminescence has no current [[phytochrome-ecological-function-dusk-dawn-red-far-red-light-switch]], it is
inexpensive enough to maintain that natural selection has not eliminated
it.
