# Bigwood and Beug Psilocybe cubensis Cultivation Methodology

## Overview

Bigwood and Beug (1982) developed a controlled "miniculture" system for
cultivating Psilocybe cubensis that allowed systematic study of alkaloid
[[cannabis-sativa-indoor-cultivation-techniques]] into a standardized, reproducible
protocol using half-pint mason jars. This miniculture approach proved
effective for producing multiple flushes over extended periods while
maintaining the [[bigwood-beug-amazon-strain-origin-pucalpa-peru]] cultivation methodology system evolved through several prior
contributions. The basic rye-grain method was first described by San
Antonio (1971) for cultivating [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]] without excess moisture that could promote
bacterial contamination. The jars were autoclaved to achieve full
sterilization before inoculation. This small scale allowed for numerous
replicate cultures while minimizing the space and resource requirements
of the experiment.

## Inoculation and Colonization

Under sterile conditions, each jar was inoculated with mycelium culture
transferred from agar plates. The source strain was an Amazonian
collection from near Pucallpa, Peru, originally obtained as a spore
print and maintained as a [[inoculation-point-taping-technique-sterile-bag-inoculation-jarrold]] and instead
promoted colonization throughout the entire grain bed.

## Casing and Fruiting Initiation

After 28 days of colonization, when the mycelium had fully permeated
the rye grain, the jars were opened and the grain surface was cased
(covered) with a layer approximately 2 cm deep. The casing mixture
consisted of 2 parts peat moss, 1 part calcium carbonate, and 2 parts
perlite and/or vermiculite. The calcium carbonate served to buffer pH
and provide calcium, while the perlite or vermiculite improved moisture
retention and aeration of the [[suppressed-incomplete-fruit-body-formation-brassinosteroid-treatment]].

## Moisture Maintenance

After casing, the minicultures were watered once every two days with
1 ml of sterile water delivered via syringe. This precise moisture
management prevented the casing layer from drying out while avoiding
over-saturation that could promote contamination. The use of sterile
water delivered by syringe maintained the aseptic conditions established
during autoclaving and inoculation, extending the productive lifespan
of each culture.
