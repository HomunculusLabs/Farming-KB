# Permaculture Village Infrastructure and Land Allotment

## Overview

Infrastructure planning for a [[permaculture]] village encompasses energy systems, commerce, land allotment, community tools, dwelling diversity, and the integration of life, work, and recreation. Mollison argues that modern zoning practices — separating industrial, commercial, residential, and recreational areas — are the antithesis of energy-conserving [[cool-climate-house-and-settlement-design]].

## Planning Priority Order

In permaculture village planning, infrastructure should be developed in this sequence:

1. **Water and energy** systems first
2. **Access** (roads, pathways) second
3. **Dwellings and other structures** third
4. **Land use** indication fourth
5. **Legal, social, and financial systems** discussed last

## Reserved Areas for Future Use

A general plan of village resources must be published for all participants. The following areas should be reserved:

### Educational and Cultural
- School, seminar, and workshop rooms
- Computers and library
- Artwork and some crafts areas

### Food and Commerce
- Food processing centre
- Café, coffee shop
- Home baked goods
- Some commercial or surplus sale products

### Industrial and Workshop
- Noisy/oily work areas
- Woodwork and metal-work
- Machine shop and repairs
- Bulk fuels, oils, grease, air pump
- Vehicle service

### Retail and Visitor Services
- Retail shops (including plant nursery retail)
- Reception for visitors

### Agricultural and Processing
- Dairy and [[faires-dairy-processing-cheese-and-butter]] centre
- Domestic livestock housing (chickens, goats, pigs, sheep, rabbits)
- Methane generator and ferment tanks
- Sewage, sullage, biomass conversion systems
- Water clean-up sites
- Glasshouses, commercial crop, and special crop areas

### Visitor Accommodation
- Camp area for casual visitors (toilets and showers)
- Can serve as a youth or student hostel

## Community Tools

Some tools are needed for the site at large and should be held communally:

- Tractor and slasher (fire control)
- Posthole borer (fencing)
- Trailer
- [[chisel-plough-soil-conditioning]]/soil conditioner
- Chipper (biomass and mulch provider, fire control)
- Tow truck (mulch and goods)
- Van (goods transport)
- Back-pack slasher (blackberry and brush)

### Workshop Tools

Essential workshop tools available on lease or time-share:
- Drill press
- Lathe
- Radial-arm saw
- Welder
- Planer or thicknesser
- Router

Trust B can undertake charges for replacement cost, wear and tear, fuel/power, and a service charge.

## Dwelling Diversity

Villages need a mix of dwelling types to accommodate varying needs and age differences:

| Type | Ownership | Bedrooms | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family homes | Individual or trust | 2–5 | Primary housing |
| Singles quarters | Strata title flats | 1–2 | Singles, guest accommodation |
| Elderly/hospital quarters | Strata and community trust | Single/double | Aged care, medical needs |
| Terrace housing | Various | Various | Higher density where appropriate |

Every village should maintain one empty strata title for emergencies such as family break-up, or to stage people into permanent housing.

## Energy-Efficient Design

Every house built today can be close to 100% energy-efficient by design alone, at no extra cost in construction. Key principles:

- Solar hot water systems as routine installations
- Photovoltaics becoming increasingly viable
- Energy needs solved mainly in the home through good design and hardware
- Some American towns now require 60% self-sufficient space heating compliance

## Integration of Life, Work, and Recreation

### Critique of Modern Zoning

Modern urban planning that designates sectors as industrial, commercial, residential, or recreational is described as "the very antithesis of good planning for transport [[desert-house-water-energy-conservation]]." It bears little relationship to the zonation of function and available time around a settlement.

### The Pre-1930 Model

Older cities that evolved in energy-conservative environments — Florence, Vienna, older parts of Berlin, and almost every village that functioned before 1930 — integrated cultural, crafts, trades, commercial, and domestic functions:

- Housing over street-level shops on the sunny side
- Trades and work in easily accessible interior courtyards (Hofs in Berlin)
- Market or supply depot close to the assembly

### Benefits of Integration

Wherever possible, life, work, and recreation should be integrated in a dwelling:
- Households are better informed
- Children are less alienated
- Adults are less isolated from social contact
- Complex transport systems are eliminated
- Complementary skills develop in the neighbourhood

### Craft Streets Model

In Istanbul and India, trades are grouped in streets or market areas, facilitating new materials, assembly, and sale. The total market or neighbourhood contains all trades except those based on rare resources or needed only on a regional basis.

Young people growing up in such environments develop a capacity to use many materials or make whatever they require, through informal everyday association with the "open shops" of small tradespeople.

### Davis, California Model

Davis is cited as a modern town where energy-conserving legislation is in place:
- People are encouraged to conduct all non-polluting businesses from their homes
- Bikeways are available throughout the settled area

## Land Allotment Categories

Land not attached to dwellings should be allotted in these categories:

1. **Public buildings and services** — administrative and civic infrastructure
2. **Recreation, trails, and walks** — leisure and movement corridors
3. **Water storages and reserves, energy sites** — utility infrastructure
4. **Productive land for food** — livestock, orchard, nursery, greenhouses
