# Polydrug Use Patterns and Hallucinogenic Mushroom Consumption

The use of hallucinogenic mushrooms in Europe does not occur
in isolation but is embedded within broader patterns of polydrug
use, particularly among young people who frequent dance music
settings. Survey data consistently show that mushroom users
are far more likely to have used other illegal substances than
non-users, placing mushroom consumption within a well-established
sequence of drug involvement.

## The Drug Sequencing Pattern

Analysis of data from 11 EU Member States together with Norway
and Croatia demonstrates that school students aged 15 to 16
are much more likely to have used hallucinogenic mushrooms if
they have already used LSD or other hallucinogens, ecstasy,
amphetamines, or cocaine. Among students who had used LSD or
other hallucinogens, lifetime prevalence of mushroom use was
approximately 50 percent for males. By contrast, students who
had only used cannabis or legal substances showed markedly lower
rates of mushroom use.

This sequencing pattern is significant because it suggests that
hallucinogenic mushrooms function as a secondary or tertiary
substance within drug use careers rather than an initiation drug.
The data indicate that exposure to and familiarity with other
psychoactive substances, particularly those associated with
recreational settings, is a strong predictor of subsequent
mushroom experimentation. Following common gender distributions
for illegal drug use, male students generally showed higher
prevalence rates than females across all substance categories.

## Clubbing Settings and Polydrug Clustering

Within club sample populations, polydrug use tends to cluster
in the same individuals. A survey of 760 clubbers from six
nightclubs in south-east England conducted in 2000 found that
overall lifetime prevalence for hallucinogenic mushroom use was
24 percent. However, among current drug users within these
nightclub samples, defined as those who had used cannabis or
any other drug during the past month, mushroom prevalence rose
to 44 percent. In comparison, lifetime prevalence among 16 to
19 year olds in the general population was only 11 percent.

This clustering effect demonstrates that mushroom use is
concentrated within populations that already engage in multiple
substance use. The fourfold difference between general population
prevalence and current drug user prevalence within the same age
group underscores the degree to which mushroom consumption is
confined to experienced drug-using subcultures.

## Cross-National Variations in Polydrug Contexts

Non-probability surveys conducted in dance music settings across
Europe revealed substantial variation in mushroom prevalence that
correlates with local drug cultures and market conditions. Lifetime
prevalence estimates ranged from 9 percent of clubbers in Budapest
to 55 percent in France. The French figure, while notably high,
likely reflects the specific survey methodology and settings rather
than a uniquely high rate of mushroom consumption.

In Italy, a survey of 2015 respondents at a rave parade found
intermediate prevalence levels. The Czech Republic reported data
from 1652 respondents in dance party settings. These diverse
settings, ranging from dedicated rave events to mainstream
commercial nightclubs, produce different prevalence estimates
partly because the polydrug profiles of attendees vary by event
type and regional drug culture.

## Comparison with Other Drug Prevalence

Among school students aged 15 to 16 across 27 European countries,
ever-in-lifetime use of hallucinogenic mushrooms was equal to or
higher than lifetime use of ecstasy in nine countries. This is
a striking finding given that ecstasy was far more widely
discussed in drug policy circles and media coverage during this
period. The equivalence suggests that mushroom use was more
prevalent than public attention indicated, particularly in
countries such as the Czech Republic, the Netherlands, France,
and Belgium where school survey prevalence was highest.

However, prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use remained
considerably lower than cannabis use across all surveyed
countries. Cannabis lifetime prevalence among 18 to 36 year
olds ranged from 15 percent in Poland to 45 percent in Denmark,
while mushroom prevalence among 15 to 24 year olds ranged
from less than 1 percent to 8 percent. The proportion of
current users among those who had ever used mushrooms was also
lower than for cannabis and ecstasy, consistent with reports
that the effects of mushrooms limit the appeal of regular use.

## First Use Age and User Demographics

The average age for first use of hallucinogenic mushrooms was
between 18 and 19 years old, according to the 2004 Mixmag survey.
This is slightly older than typical initiation ages for cannabis
and alcohol, consistent with the drug sequencing pattern in which
mushroom experimentation follows earlier exposure to more common
substances. Among the 305 individuals who had used hallucinogenic
mushrooms in this survey, over half planned to take them again,
suggesting that while repeat use was not universal, a substantial
minority of users found the experience worthwhile.

## Implications for Prevention

The polydrug context of hallucinogenic mushroom use has
important implications for drug prevention strategies.
Mushroom use is unlikely to respond to prevention approaches
that target initiation of drug use in general, as mushroom
users are typically already experienced with other substances.
Instead, effective prevention would need to address the
specific risks associated with mushroom use within the context
of broader polydrug lifestyles, including the dangers of
combining mushrooms with alcohol or other substances. The
clustering of mushroom use within club and party settings
also suggests that targeted interventions in these venues
may be more effective than general population campaigns.
