# Right Livelihood, Commonwork, and Balanced Living

The concept of **right livelihood** in permaculture extends far beyond employment — it encompasses the fundamental question of what constitutes meaningful, ethical, and balanced human work. [[bill-mollison]] frames right livelihood as work that assists in caring for the earth and other people, work congruent with one's beliefs, and work that is self-determined rather than imposed by external economic structures. This concept is intimately connected to the principle of **commonwork** — the idea that individuals in a village or community hold diverse, rotating roles rather than being confined to a single occupational identity.

## The Two Unhappy States of Human Existence

Mollison identifies two fundamentally unsatisfying conditions in modern economic life:

1. **Unemployment** — being defined by one's community or nation as "of no use to anyone." In a world where enormous work remains to be done — repairing environmental damage, replacing forests, securing soils, housing people, building local self-reliance — Mollison considers unemployment "an obscene concept." Relief benefit systems, he argues, effectively reward people for accepting the role of non-participation and penalize them if they attempt to work.

2. **Employment without autonomy** — going daily to a job that may be useless, boring, or actively destructive, being paid to exist in a single role (teacher, clerk, process worker, labourer) with no say in policy, social value, hours, product quality, or environmental worth. The worker is defined entirely by their function and stripped of agency.

In both cases, other people define the individual's life. Since all such money ultimately derives from the pool of public wealth, Mollison argues that both salaried employees and relief recipients are effectively on "relief payments" — and that companies supported by public subsidies (particularly [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]] systems in the Western world) occupy the same category. The only people who are truly **self-defined** are those who are self-employed or who work in community work cooperatives, where consumers pay directly for products and services and where housing, products, and choice of work are self-determined.

## Commonwork: The Portfolio of Occupations

The **commonwork** model stands in direct opposition to the industrial specialization paradigm. In a village setting, no person is just a miner, clerk, or banker — on different days, one can be a forester, beekeeper, writer, printer, or carpenter. This portfolio of occupations provides:

- **Variety and stimulation** — daily work changes, preventing the monotony that leads to burnout and disengagement.
- **Skill breadth** — individuals develop competence across multiple domains, increasing both personal capability and [[permaculture-community-resilience]].
- **Reduced dependency** — when no single employer or role defines a person, economic shocks to any one sector are absorbed by the diversity of the individual's activity.
- **Holistic understanding** — people who work across production, processing, and service gain a systems-level view of how their community functions.

Mollison attributes the confinement of people to single occupational roles to the combined pressure of trade unions and monoculture industry, which bind people with what he calls "invisible shackles" to industrial suburbs characterized by malnutrition, poor housing, and human suffering. The commonwork model liberates individuals from this confinement by restoring the natural human capacity for diverse, meaningful contribution.

## The Three Pillars of a Balanced Life

A healthy and balanced life, according to Mollison, consists of being able to access three fundamental areas of human experience:

1. **Physical exertion** — walking, gardening, sport, manual labor, and other bodily activities that maintain health and provide tangible accomplishment.

2. **Intellectual pursuits** — design, research, education, planning, problem-solving, and creative thinking that engage the mind and advance understanding.

3. **Emotional-sensual areas** — celebration, contemplation, love, art, music, and other experiences that nourish the heart and senses.

Mollison observes that when people are deprived of intellectual life through boring or repetitive work, emotional life tends to dominate, and their lives become "a drama or series of dramatic events" — a pattern familiar in communities where meaningful work is scarce. A balanced life distributes energy across all three outlets, preventing any single dimension from overwhelming the others.

### The Aboriginal Function Group Model

Mollison draws on the social organization of Central Australian Aboriginal tribes, where at every event three independent "function groups" operate alongside totem or kinship affiliations:

- One group **"knows" or records time** — they orchestrate, plan, and maintain the intellectual framework (the intellectual function).
- One group **carries out dances, increase ceremonies, or activity** — they execute the physical dimension of cultural practice (the physical function).
- One group **encourages, applauds, and appreciates** — they provide emotional support and aesthetic response (the emotional function).

Every person thus fits within a matrix of **totem (identity) and function (role)**, ensuring that the community's intellectual, physical, and emotional needs are all met simultaneously. This model provides an anthropological precedent for the balanced-life framework Mollison advocates, demonstrating that the integration of physical, intellectual, and emotional work is not merely a modern ideal but a proven pattern in human social organization.

## The Ideal Day: Production, Service, and Celebration

In Mollison's vision of the good life, time is distributed across three categories:

- **Some time in primary production or manufacture** — growing food, building shelter, creating useful goods, directly engaging with the material world.
- **Some time in service to a wider group** — teaching, healing, maintaining infrastructure, participating in governance, contributing to the common good.
- **Some time in relaxation and celebration** — rest, play, artistic expression, social gathering, spiritual practice, and the enjoyment of life's pleasures.

This tripartite structure mirrors the Aboriginal model and the three pillars framework, applied at the level of daily and weekly rhythm rather than cultural ceremony. Few people in modern industrial societies achieve this balance, but Mollison presents it as both achievable and essential for genuine well-being.

## Self-Employment and Cooperative Work as Liberation

Mollison's analysis leads to a clear prescription: the path from the two unhappy states toward right livelihood runs through **self-employment** and **community work cooperatives**. These structures offer:

- **Direct relationship between effort and reward** — producers serve consumers directly, eliminating parasitic intermediaries.
- **Democratic control of work conditions** — workers set their own hours, standards, and methods.
- **Alignment of work and values** — people choose work that is congruent with their beliefs about caring for earth and people.
- **Community accountability** — products and services are judged by their real effects on real neighbors, not by abstract market metrics.
- **Freedom from destructive employment** — no one is compelled to participate in environmentally or socially harmful industries simply to survive.

The cooperative model, in particular, allows people who might lack the capital or confidence for full self-employment to participate in self-directed economic activity alongside others. The Mondragon worker cooperatives in Spain, which Mollison discusses elsewhere, demonstrate that this model can scale to thousands of worker-members while maintaining democratic governance and [[holmgren-ethical-principles-care-earth-people-fair-shares]].

## Voluntary Simplicity as Foundation

Throughout this discussion, Mollison returns to the theme of **[[voluntary-simplicity]]** — the deliberate choice to limit material consumption to modest levels. This is not asceticism but pragmatism: when basic needs are modest (shelter, sufficient food, small luxuries, travel funds, and friendship), the amount of paid work required to meet them is reduced, freeing time for the intellectual and emotional dimensions of life. Modest consumption also reduces ecological footprint, aligning personal economics with permaculture's ethical foundation of earth care.

The relationship between simplicity and right livelihood is reciprocal: simplicity makes right livelihood possible by reducing the economic pressure to accept any job regardless of its ethics, while right livelihood reinforces simplicity by directing energy toward productive, community-building activities rather than consumerism. Together, they form a virtuous cycle that supports both individual fulfillment and collective well-being.
