# Fungal Ligninolytic Enzyme Systems

The three major extracellular enzyme systems that white-rot fungi use to degrade lignin and pollutants. These nonspecific oxidative enzymes are the biochemical basis of [[mycoremediation-basics]]. See [[fungal-ecosystem-roles]] for the ecological context of fungal decomposition.

## Why Lignin-Degrading Enzymes Matter for Bioremediation

Lignin is an irregular, non-repeating polymer with no single cleavable bond. Fungi cannot use lignin as a sole carbon source (Kirk, Connors & Zeikus 1976) -- they degrade it only to access cellulose within the wood cell wall. The enzymes they evolved are therefore **nonspecific** and attack via free radical chemistry, making them effective against a wide range of anthropogenic pollutants including PAHs, PCBs, chlorophenols, dyes, and explosives (Evans & Hedger; Singh 2006).

## Lignin Peroxidase (LiP)

- Heme-containing peroxidase (~42,000 Mw), highly glycosylated
- First enzyme shown to attack lignin-type compounds (Tien & Kirk 1984), isolated from Phanerochaete chrysosporium
- **Unusually high redox potential** -- unique among peroxidases in its ability to oxidize methoxyl substituents on non-phenolic aromatic rings
- Generates cation radicals that undergo further reactions (oxidative cleavage producing quinones and ring-fission products)
- ph optimum below 3.0; natural wood cell environments are approximately pH 4.0, with localized acidic pockets from fungal oxalic acid secretion
- Produced by most but not all white-rot species (Hatakka 1994)
- Veratryl alcohol is a natural substrate that enhances LiP activity and protects the enzyme from inactivation by excess H2O2 (Chung & Aust 1995)
- **Not all white-rot species produce LiP** -- this is a key differentiator between species

## Manganese Peroxidase (MnP)

- Heme-containing enzyme, catalytic cycle similar to LiP
- **Considered essential for lignin degradation in vivo** -- all examined white-rot species produce MnP, whereas not all produce LiP
- Converts Mn(II) to Mn(III) during catalysis; Mn(III)-chelates (with oxalate) act as **diffusible oxidants** that penetrate into wood cell wall pores inaccessible to the enzymes themselves (Archibald & Roy 1992)
- Rate-limiting step in vivo may be availability of H2O2 or Mn(II) rather than the catalytic rate of MnP (Palma, Moreira & Feijoo Lema 1997)
- MnP activity can be independent of manganese with some substrates (dimethoxyphenol)
- Can generate H2O2 through oxidation of organic acids (Urzua et al. 1995)
- Litter-decomposing fungi have **significantly higher MnP activity** than wood decomposers (Ullah 2000) -- an important ecological distinction for species selection

## Laccase

- Copper-containing polyphenol oxidase that uses molecular oxygen (not H2O2) as electron acceptor
- Converts mono- and diphenolic groups to quinone radicals then quinones in multistep oxidation (Thurston 1994)
- **Dominant extracellular enzyme** in liquid cultures of T. versicolor and P. ostreatus
- Majority of white-rot fungi produce laccase; used as the basis for the Bavendamm test (rapid screening for white-rot potential)
- Blue and yellow isomer forms exist; yellow laccases from T. versicolor and Panus tigrinus are thought to bind soil phenolics
- With low-molecular-mass mediators (ABTS, HBT, 1-hydroxybenzotriazole), laccase can oxidize non-phenolic substrates, extending its effective range
- P. chrysosporium generally does NOT produce laccase under standard laboratory conditions

## Species Enzyme Profiles

| Species | LiP | MnP | Laccase | Notes |
|---------|-----|-----|---------|-------|
| Phanerochaete chrysosporium | Yes | Yes | Rarely | Primary model organism; high LiP |
| Trametes versicolor | Yes | Yes | Yes (dominant) | All three enzymes; widely used in bioremediation |
| Phlebia radiata | No | Yes | Yes | Laccase + MnP system |
| Pleurotus ostreatus | Variable | Yes | Yes (dominant) | Good for field deployment |
| Bjerkandera adusta | Yes | Yes | Yes | Effective on chlorophenols |
| Ganoderma, Fomes, Inonotus spp. | Understudied | Understudied | Understudied | Slow-growing stress-tolerant decomposers; persist for decades on wood |

## Enzyme Induction and Regulation

- **Nitrogen limitation** triggers ligninolytic enzyme expression in P. chrysosporium (C:N ratio 200:1 to 1000:1, mimicking wood)
- **Carbon limitation** also induces; LiP and MnP expression is suppressed by excess nitrogen and glucose
- H2O2 is required for LiP and MnP; generated by fungal enzymes including glucose oxidase, glyoxal oxidase, and aryl alcohol oxidase
- Specific laccase isomers can be induced by lignin-mimic compounds (e.g., 2,5-dimethylaniline)
- Excess H2O2 can destroy the catalytic site of both LiP and MnP

## In Vivo Operation

Immunogold-cytochemical labelling studies (Evans et al. 1991) showed that LiP, MnP, and laccase are **too large to penetrate intact secondary wood cell walls**. They remain close to hyphal surfaces. Degradation is initiated at a distance from the hyphae by diffusible small molecules: oxalate, H2O2, Fe2+/Fe3+, and Mn2+/Mn3+.

Brown-rot fungi use a fundamentally different mechanism: the **Fenton reaction** (Fe2+ + H2O2 -> hydroxyl radical + OH- + Fe3+) to generate hydroxyl radicals that depolymerize cellulose without degrading lignin (Koenigs 1974).

## Ecological Context

- Primary resource capturers (T. versicolor, Stereum hirsutum): fast-growing, combative, high enzyme activity. Rapidly colonize moribund trees
- Secondary resource capturers (Lenzites betulina): slower-growing, more combative, eventually replace primary capturers
- Stress-tolerant persisters (Ganoderma, Fomes): slow growth, defensive strategy, persist for decades on fallen trees
- Xylaria, Daldinia, Hypoxylon (ascomycetes): tolerate extreme water stress; lower laccase/MnP but cause extensive white rot in dry conditions
