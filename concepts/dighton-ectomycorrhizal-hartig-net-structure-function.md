# Ectomycorrhizal Hartig Net Structure and Function

## Overview

The Hartig net is a defining anatomical feature of
[[ectomycorrhizal-ecology]]|[[ectomycorrhizal-associations]], consisting
of a network of fungal hyphae that penetrate between the cortical cells
of the host root. Named after Robert Hartig, who first described the
structure in the 19th century, the Hartig net represents the primary
interface for nutrient and carbohydrate exchange between the fungal
symbiont and the host tree. Together with the fungal sheath (mantle)
that covers the root surface, the Hartig net constitutes the
structural basis of the ectomycorrhizal symbiosis.

## Anatomical Features

### Structural Position

In the ectomycorrhizal habit, the fungus does not penetrate into the
host cortical cells themselves, but instead grows between them, forming
the Hartig net. The Hartig net exists outside the endodermis of the
root, extending through the cortical cell layer. This intercellular
positioning distinguishes ectomycorrhizae from
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-network-visualization-anastomosis]], where fungal
structures (arbuscules) form within the cortical cells.

The ectomycorrhizal association is formed between mainly tree species
and a range of fungal taxa consisting of basidiomycetes, ascomycetes,
and some zygomycetes. The Hartig net forms alongside the development of
the fungal sheath or mantle on the root surface.

### Relationship to the Fungal Sheath

On the surface of the root, a sheath or covering of fungal material
develops alongside the Hartig net. This surface structure may vary from
a loose weft of hyphae to highly organized pseudoparenchymatous
structures. It is the structure of the sheath, the degree of branching
(induced by changes in cytokinins), and the nature of emanating hyphae
or hyphal strands that allow morphological identification of
ectomycorrhizae (Agerer, 1987 to 1999; Ingleby et al., 1990; Goodman
et al., 1996 to 2000).

### Fungal Partners

ectomycorrhizal-ecology|Ectomycorrhizal associations are formed
between a limited number of plant species and a huge diversity of
fungal species. This contrasts with arbuscular mycorrhizae, which
associate with many plant species but involve relatively few fungal
species. The taxonomic breadth of ectomycorrhizal fungal partners
contributes to the wide range of functional diversity observed in
ectomycorrhizal communities.

## Functional Significance

### Nutrient Exchange Interface

The Hartig net represents the primary site of nutrient and
carbohydrate exchange between the fungal partner and host plant. By
extending between cortical cells, the Hartig net creates an extensive
contact surface area that facilitates the bidirectional transfer of
mineral nutrients (particularly phosphorus and nitrogen) from fungus to
plant, and photosynthetically derived carbohydrates from plant to
fungus.

### Enhanced Nutrient Uptake

The development of the Hartig net, combined with the extraradical
mycelium that extends into the surrounding soil, dramatically enhances
the nutrient-absorbing capacity of the root system. Rousseau et al.
(1994) showed that for ectomycorrhizal pine seedlings colonized by
Pisolithus tinctorius, the extraradical mycelium accounted for only 5%
of the potential nutrient-absorbing system dry weight but represented
75% of the potential absorbing area and over 99% of the absorbing
length. The presence of both extraradical hyphae and rhizomorphs
further extends the influence of the fungal partner to great distances
from the root surface.

### Comparison with Arbuscular Mycorrhizae

The intercellular position of the Hartig net contrasts with the
intracellular arbuscules of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-network-visualization-anastomosis]]. In arbuscular mycorrhizae, fungal hyphae penetrate
within the host root cortical cells and develop a treelike branching
structure (the arbuscule) between the host cell [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]]
Both structures optimize the surface area of the interface between
plant host and fungus for nutrient and carbohydrate exchange, but they
achieve this through fundamentally different anatomical arrangements.

## Role in Ecosystem Processes

### Direct Nutrient Cycling

Many ectomycorrhizal fungi exhibiting enzyme activity have rapid hyphal
growth and large mycelial networks, many of which are aggregated into
cords or [[dighton-fungal-rhizomorphs-mycelial-cords]]
(Read, 1991a). These structures allow translocation of nutrients from
distal parts of the extraradical mycelial network to the root. The
distal parts of the extraradical hyphal structures are capable of
producing enzymes usually associated with saprotrophic decomposer
fungi, enabling direct cycling of nutrients from organic matter to the
host plant.

### Mycorrhizal Mats

The ectomycorrhizal mycelium can become so dense in humic soil horizons
that it forms "mats" (Griffiths et al., 1990). These mats may
constitute 10 to 20% of the top 10 cm of soil in temperate forest
ecosystems (Cromack et al., 1988) and account for 45 to 55% of total
soil biomass (Cromack et al., 1979). Mat-forming ectomycorrhizal
communities are important in progressively removing organic nitrogen
from the soil pool and immobilizing it into high C:N ratio fungal
tissue as forest growth proceeds.

## Comparative Mycorrhizal Anatomy

A comparison of mycorrhizal types reveals the diversity of structural
solutions to the challenge of plant-fungal nutrient exchange:

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizae**: Arbuscules within cortical cells; no
  sheath
- **Ectomycorrhizae**: Hartig net between cortical cells plus fungal
  sheath
- **Ericoid mycorrhizae**: Hyphal coils within cortical cells
- **Arbutoid mycorrhizae**: Hyphal coils within cortical cells
- **Orchidaceous mycorrhizae**: Fungal propagule carried in seed

The ectomycorrhizal condition, with its combination of Hartig net and
fungal sheath, represents a uniquely extensive interface between plant
and fungus that supports the diverse functional roles of
ectomycorrhizal fungi in forest ecosystem processes.
