## Characteristics of Pioneer Species

Pioneer species share several defining characteristics that make them
uniquely suited to colonizing difficult sites and preparing the ground for
longer-term species:

- They are usually nitrogen-fixing, drawing atmospheric nitrogen and
  converting it to forms available to other plants
- They are generally fast-growing and often short-lived
- They are highly variable and produce abundant seed
- Their seed is usually dispersed by animals and often requires
  scarification to germinate
- They can withstand the hostile conditions imposed on young trees,
  including exposure, wind, poor soil, and competition from grasses
- They act as nurse trees, providing shade, shelter, and mulch from
  leaf litter

## Seed Scarification

Many pioneer species produce hard-coated seeds that require scarification to
germinate. In nature, this scarification occurs when seeds pass through the
digestive tract of an animal or are exposed to fire. Both mechanisms
mechanically abrade or chemically soften the hard seed coat, allowing water
to penetrate and trigger germination.

In cultivation, seed scarification can be achieved by soaking seeds in
boiling water (then allowing to cool and soak for 12 to 24 hours) or by
mechanically filing or nicking the hard seed cases with sandpaper or a small
knife. This technique is especially important for species like
carob|carob and many [[honey-locust-pod-fodder-timber-production]]
and tagasaste are particularly valuable in this regard, producing high-
quality fodder while simultaneously improving soil and sheltering
establishment trees. This integration of permaculture insectary plants for pest control,
fodder, and soil-building functions exemplifies the multi-purpose plant
strategy central to permaculture design.
