# Pioneer Species and Succession in Permaculture Design

The pattern observed in nature where forest steps out into grassland is one of
the most important ecological models available to permaculture designers. At
the boundary between grassland and forest exists a set of species called
pioneers, and understanding their role in natural succession allows designers
to accelerate the establishment of productive [[permaculture-food-forests-design-course]] and permaculture food forests and structural forests on degraded or bare land.

## Characteristics of Pioneer Species

Pioneer species share several defining characteristics that make them
uniquely suited to colonizing difficult sites and preparing the ground for
longer-term species:

- They are usually nitrogen-fixing, drawing atmospheric nitrogen and
  converting it to forms available to other plants
- They are generally fast-growing and often short-lived
- They are highly variable and produce abundant seed
- Their seed is usually dispersed by animals and often requires
  scarification to germinate
- They can withstand the hostile conditions imposed on young trees,
  including exposure, wind, poor soil, and competition from grasses
- They act as nurse trees, providing shade, shelter, and mulch from
  leaf litter

## Seed Scarification

Many pioneer species produce hard-coated seeds that require scarification to
germinate. In nature, this scarification occurs when seeds pass through the
digestive tract of an animal or are exposed to fire. Both mechanisms
mechanically abrade or chemically soften the hard seed coat, allowing water
to penetrate and trigger germination.

In cultivation, seed scarification can be achieved by soaking seeds in
boiling water (then allowing to cool and soak for 12 to 24 hours) or by
mechanically filing or nicking the hard seed cases with sandpaper or a small
knife. This technique is especially important for species like
carob|carob and many [[permaculture-plants-legume-trees-acacia-carob-locust]]
species.

## Simulating Natural Succession

In nature, pioneers colonize bare ground and gradually modify the
environment, making it more suitable for longer-lived species. As canopy
trees mature, they often crowd out the pioneers, which may remain as a
minor component of the system or disappear entirely.

Permaculture designers can simulate and accelerate this succession by
deliberately planting pioneers onto bare or pastured areas. The pioneers
perform the heavy lifting of soil improvement, microclimate creation, and
grass suppression, while the designer's chosen long-term species (the
succession species) grow toward maturity under the protection of the nurse
crop. This approach can reduce the time needed to establish a productive
tree system from decades to years.

## Key Pioneer Species

### Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus palmensis)

permaculture-tagasaste|Tagasaste, or tree lucerne, is a nitrogen-fixing
legume native to the Canary Islands and one of the most useful pioneer
species available. It grows up to 7 meters tall and can productively grow
in areas receiving as little as 300mm of annual rainfall. By 1996, some
50,000 hectares of mostly marginal farmland in Western Australia had been
planted to tagasaste.

Tagasaste foliage contains 17 to 31 percent crude protein, comparable to or
exceeding lucerne. It is readily eaten by sheep, cattle, poultry, pigs,
horses, and rabbits. Research at the Martindale Research Project showed
that on near-useless sandy soils, tagasaste yielded the feed equivalent of
1,100 kg of wheat per hectare where the soil could previously support only
700 kg of wheat.

### Tree Lupin (Lupinus arboreus)

The tree lupin is an evergreen perennial shrub reaching about 2 meters,
native to California. It is nitrogen-fixing, frost-tolerant, and functions
well as a nurse crop. It flowers from late winter to early summer with a
second flush in autumn, providing valuable bee forage. While too toxic for
most livestock, it can be cut hard to supply nitrogen-rich mulch.

### Wattles (Acacia spp)

The genus Acacia includes some 850 species native to Australia. Many are
excellent pioneer species, being fast-growing, drought-tolerant, and
nitrogen-fixing. Acacia aneura (mulga) survives on 200 to 500mm annual
rainfall and is an outstanding fodder tree. Acacia ampliceps tolerates
alkaline, highly saline, and waterlogged conditions, making it invaluable
for difficult reclamation sites.

### Other Notable Pioneers

Additional pioneer species include Cassia spp, Gytisus spp, and Prosopis
spp (mesquite). Some Prosopis species have become problematic weeds in
Australia due to their thorns and aggressive spreading habit, illustrating
the importance of careful species selection.

## The Nurse Crop Role

Pioneers serve as nurse crops for longer-term tree species. In trials
conducted in New Zealand with various fruit and nut trees, trees growing
in close association with tagasaste (as close as 1 meter) consistently
grew better than controls without tagasaste. The nurse effect works
through multiple mechanisms: shade reduction, wind protection, grass
suppression, nitrogen fixation, and the addition of organic matter from
leaf litter.

If the nursed tree is grown for timber, the pioneer will tend to force it
upward for light, producing a straight, long timber tree. If the nursed
tree is needed for fruit or nut production, the pioneer should be clipped
and used as mulch or fed to livestock to prevent excessive shading.

## Integration with Fodder Systems

Many pioneer species produce edible foliage or seeds, making them dual-
purpose plants that provide both land reclamation services and direct
agricultural output. [[honey-locust-pod-fodder-timber-production]]
and tagasaste are particularly valuable in this regard, producing high-
quality fodder while simultaneously improving soil and sheltering
establishment trees. This integration of permaculture insectary plants for pest control,
fodder, and soil-building functions exemplifies the multi-purpose plant
strategy central to permaculture design.
