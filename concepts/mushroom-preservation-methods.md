Mushrooms are approximately 90% water and must be preserved within a few
hours of harvest or they will be consumed by fly larvae and bacteria.
Stamets and Chilton outline four primary preservation methods: air
drying, freezing, freeze drying, and canning. Each method has distinct
advantages and limitations regarding flavor retention, nutritional
value, storage life, and suitability for different [[psilocybin]], much of their active alkaloid content. The 110 degrees F
maximum temperature guideline preserves both nutritional and
pharmacological constituents. For home cultivators without dedicated
dehydrators, placing mushrooms on screens in a warm dry area with a
fan provides adequate air circulation. Desiccant packets can be added
to storage containers for extra protection against residual moisture.

## Freezing and Thawing Considerations

Freezing is another preservation method, but with significant
limitations unless the mushrooms are first dried. When frozen fresh,
the water constituting 90% of a mushroom's mass becomes crystallized.
Frozen mushrooms are held together more by ice crystals than by their
own cellular structure. Since ice expands upon crystallization, cells
break under the stress. Because frozen mushrooms disintegrate into a
formless mass when thawed, they are mostly used in soups or stews
where texture is less important.

The practical approach for freezing is to pre-dry the mushrooms to
leather-dry or fully dry consistency before freezing. This eliminates
the cellular damage from ice crystal formation. Pre-dried frozen
mushrooms retain better texture upon thawing than fresh-frozen ones.
Another option is to saute mushrooms in butter or oil before freezing,
which both preserves flavor and prevents the mushy texture problem.
Vacuum sealing before freezing further extends storage life and
prevents freezer burn.

## Freeze Drying: The Ideal Method

Freeze drying is the ideal method for preserving flavor, nutrition,
form, and/or pharmacological content of mushrooms. The process works
by first flash-freezing fresh mushrooms, then placing them onto heated
trays in a cooled, high-vacuum chamber. The frozen water within the
mushrooms begins to melt from the heat but is immediately transformed
into vapor that is pumped out of the freeze drier. This sublimation
process preserves much of the original cell structure, so freeze-dried
mushrooms are often life-like in appearance and reconstitute with
excellent texture.

Because commercial freeze driers are prohibitively expensive, few home
cultivators can afford them. Shiitake (Lentinus edodes) is one of the
few commercial mushrooms regularly preserved by freeze drying due to
its high market value. Many people have discovered that mushrooms
placed in a frost-free refrigerator are almost as well preserved,
though this method is not as thorough or long-lasting as true freeze
drying. For home cultivators seeking the best preservation possible
without specialized equipment, the combination of air drying followed
by frozen storage in vacuum-sealed bags provides an excellent
compromise.

## Canning and Pickling

Canning is a traditional method for storing mushrooms. Mushrooms must
be carefully cleaned before canning, then precooked for 3-4 minutes
in boiling water. They are inserted into glass jars with a small
amount of vinegar and sterilized in a pressure cooker at 10 psi for
30-40 minutes. The vinegar provides acidity that inhibits bacterial
growth and contributes a tangy flavor. Pickled mushrooms, especially
those prepared with herbs and garlic, are preferred by many epicureans
to those preserved by other methods.

Canning is particularly well suited to mushrooms with firm flesh like
Agaricus species and shiitake. Softer-fleshed species may not hold
their shape well through the canning process. The high heat of pressure
canning will degrade heat-sensitive compounds, so this method is not
recommended for species valued primarily for their pharmacological
constituents. Home canners should follow standard guidelines for
acidified foods to ensure safety against botulism and other foodborne
pathogens.

## Best Practices for All Methods

Regardless of preservation method, young mushrooms should always be
selected over old ones for better texture, flavor, and nutritional
retention. Each storage container should be labeled with the species,
the name of who grew or identified the mushrooms, the date, and the
place of origin. When eating wild mushrooms for the first time, always
retain one or two small specimens in case illness ensues and a
mycologist or doctor needs to be consulted.

For maximum preservation quality, process mushrooms as quickly as
possible after harvest. Delays allow enzymatic degradation and
bacterial growth. Clean mushrooms gently with a soft brush or damp
cloth rather than soaking in water, which can waterlog the flesh. For
drying, uniform slice thickness promotes even dehydration. For all
methods, minimize exposure to light and air during storage to prevent
oxidation of flavor compounds and nutritional constituents.

## Key Points

- Mushrooms are 90% water and must be preserved within hours of
  harvest
- Air drying at 110F maximum preserves nutrition and active compounds
- Good dehydrators complete drying in 24-48 hours
- Flash drying at high temperatures degrades nutritive and
  pharmacological content
- Freeze fresh mushrooms only if pre-dried; fresh freezing causes
  cellular damage from ice crystals
- Freeze drying is ideal but requires expensive commercial equipment
- Canning at 10 psi for 30-40 minutes with vinegar provides long-term
  storage
- Always label containers with species, origin, date, and identifier
- Young mushrooms preserve better than old ones in all methods
