From above, one can see how land consolidation has emptied landscapes. Regions
once covered with mixed forests are now bare monocultures. The humid habitats
-- lakes, ponds, bogs, hedges, and gardens -- are gone. Mountain forests die
from lack of water while roads and railways in the valleys drain water from
the soil.

## Reading at Ground Level

At ground level, the practitioner examines:
- The geology of the land and ground composition
- Existing flora and fauna as indicators of soil conditions
- Contour lines and natural water flow patterns
- Signs of erosion, gully formation, or soil compaction
- The condition of existing vegetation (healthy or stressed)
- Natural monuments such as old trees, wells, and rock formations

The vegetation indicates what nutrients are available in the soil. [[holzer-reading-nature-philosophy]] requires tuning into local
conditions that can differ widely just a kilometre apart. The reading of
nature and tuning in to the landscape cannot be learned from books alone; it
requires direct experience and observation over time.

## Recognising Causes vs Symptoms

A core principle of reading the landscape is distinguishing causes from
symptoms. When a tree is suffering, the cause may lie 50 or 60 years in the
past. It could be land consolidation, road building that disturbed the
hydrological balance, root damage from construction, or electromagnetic
radiation from power lines. Watering a stressed tree treats the symptom;
finding and addressing the cause creates lasting healing.

Similarly, insect overpopulation is a visible sign of incorrect cultivation,
usually indicating chemical use. The insect is not the pest; the human
imposing his will onto nature is the problem. Using pesticides treats the
symptom while destroying the natural predators that would restore balance.

## Communicating with Nature

Holzer's approach goes beyond physical observation into what he calls
communicating with nature. His most important rule is to put himself in the
position of the other: the tree, the pig, the earthworm, the ladybird, or
the sunflower. Would he feel good in their place? If the answer is no, he
finds what is wrong and changes it.

He talks to trees, rocks, and wells. These are places of power where he
feels drawn to stay and rest. He often falls asleep near them and dreams of
answers and solutions. These dreams give him keys to unlock parts of his
consciousness he cannot access when awake.

## Practical Assessment Questions

When assessing a new piece of land, Holzer recommends asking:
- How large is the area and how is it orientated?
- Is the land level, hilly, or sloping?
