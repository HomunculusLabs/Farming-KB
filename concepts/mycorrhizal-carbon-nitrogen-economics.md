# Mycorrhizal Carbon and Nutrient Economics

Mycorrhizal fungi form the largest biological interface between plants and soil, mediating massive fluxes of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients. The economics of these exchanges determine ecosystem productivity, soil carbon storage, and plant community composition. See [[mycorrhizal-networks]] and [[fungal-biogeochemistry]] for broader context.

## Carbon Flux to Mycorrhizal Fungi

### Ectomycorrhizas (EM)

EM associations dominate in boreal and temperate forests:
- 10-30% of net photosynthate passes into EM fungi in forest ecosystems
- EM mycelium accounts for over 70% of total soil microbial biomass in boreal forests
- EM mycelium has unusually high C:N ratios (18.5-21.9), making it a significant carbon sink
- 14C analysis shows ECM sporocarps contain carbon photosynthetically fixed only 0-2 years previously
- The "Gadgil effect": EM fungi suppress saprotrophic decomposition by competing for limiting nutrients, increasing C:N and C:P ratios of remaining residues

See [[ectomycorrhizal-ecology]] for detailed treatment of EM symbioses.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizas (AM)

AM associations dominate in grasslands, tropical forests, and many agricultural systems:
- External AM mycelium receives more than 9% of net C fixation by grassland in summer
- Glomalin: a hydrophobic, recalcitrant protein secreted by AM hyphae, with residence time of 6-42 years, contributing up to 15% of stable soil organic carbon in grasslands
- AM fungal biomass represents 54-58% of total microbial biomass in some tropical soils
- AM networks can interconnect multiple plant individuals, facilitating carbon transfer between plants

See [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] for further details on AM biology.

### Ericoid Mycorrhizas (ERM)

ERM associations dominate in heathlands and acidic soils:
- ERM fungi enable incomplete decomposition of lignocellulose
- This produces recalcitrant humic compounds that accumulate as soil organic matter
- ERM associations contribute to the characteristic thick organic horizons of heathland soils

See [[ericoid-and-orchid-mycorrhizae]] for additional information on ericoid systems.

[[glomalin]] is the recalcitrant glycoprotein produced by AM hyphae that contributes significantly to stable soil carbon pools.

## Nitrogen Economics

### The Saprotrophic-Mycorrhizal Divide

Stable isotope studies (δ15N) reveal a consistent pattern: ECM fungi are enriched in 15N compared to both their host plants and saprotrophic fungi. This reflects the distinct nitrogen sources used by each guild:
- **Saprotrophs**: Decompose organic matter, acquiring nitrogen from diverse sources
- **ECM fungi**: Directly access organic nitrogen (amino acids, proteins) from soil organic matter, fractionating isotopes differently
- **Plants**: Receive nitrogen from mycorrhizal partners, showing the most depleted 15N values

This nitrogen partitioning is a key component of [[fungal-role-in-nitrogen-cycling]] and [[fungal-nutrient-cycling-mineral-transformations]].

### Mycoheterotrophs

Non-photosynthetic plants (e.g., *Cryptothallus mirabilis*, some orchids) obtain all carbon and nitrogen from mycorrhizal fungi. These "cheaters" show the highest 15N enrichment and the most depleted 13C values, reflecting their complete dependence on fungal intermediaries.

[[ectomycorrhizal-ecology]] provides comprehensive coverage of EM fungal biology and their dominant role in boreal and temperate forest nutrient cycling.

## The Gadgil Effect

The Gadgil effect describes the suppression of saprotrophic decomposition by ectomycorrhizal fungi through competition for limiting nutrients (particularly nitrogen and phosphorus). When EM fungi sequester these nutrients into their biomass, saprotrophs are deprived of the resources needed to decompose organic matter efficiently. The result is elevated C:N and C:P ratios in remaining residues, slower decomposition rates, and increased soil carbon storage. This creates a positive feedback loop: greater EM dominance leads to slower decomposition, which retains more organic matter and further favours EM-associated plant communities. The Gadgil effect is a key mechanism linking [[mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration]] to [[fungal-decomposition-processes]].

## Phosphorus Economics

Mycorrhizal fungi are the primary phosphorus scavengers for most plants:
- External mycelium explores soil volumes far beyond root reach
- Phosphorus is translocated through mycelial cords at rates sometimes exceeding 25 cm/hour
- 32P tracer experiments show proportional allocation to new resources based on size and type
- AM fungi access poorly available phosphorus sources through phosphatase secretion and organic acid excretion
- In some systems, EM fungi act as phosphorus conduits, connecting organic matter decomposition with plant uptake

Phosphorus mediation by mycorrhizae is detailed in [[fungal-role-in-phosphorus-cycling]].

## Resource Translocation

Cord-forming basidiomycetes demonstrate sophisticated resource allocation:
- 14C and 32P tracer studies show preferential translocation to new, uncolonized resources
- Translocation follows pulsatile patterns with synchronous oscillations
- Network theory (graph analysis) reveals efficient transport pathways
- "Physiological division of labour" occurs when different parts of the mycelium specialize in resource acquisition versus resource consumption

## Carbon Flow and Plant Community Effects

Carbon flow through mycorrhizal networks has profound effects on plant community composition:
- **Direct effects**: Differential carbon supply to neighbouring plants through common mycorrhizal networks can alter competitive hierarchies. Seedlings of EM-associated species may receive carbon from established trees, conferring a survival advantage.
- **Indirect effects**: The Gadgil effect reduces litter decomposition rates under EM-dominated stands, creating soil conditions that favour EM-associated plants over those dependent on rapid nutrient turnover. This feedback mechanism helps maintain distinct vegetation types.
- **AM vs EM strategies**: AM-associated plant communities (grasslands, early-successional forests) and EM-associated communities (late-successional boreal and temperate forests) represent alternative stable states driven partly by mycorrhizal-mediated carbon and nutrient economics. The [[soil-food-web-structure]] differs substantially between these mycorrhizal regimes.

## Mycorrhizal Carbon Pools and Persistence

The carbon invested in mycorrhizal fungi has unusually long residence times in soil:
- EM mycelium has high C:N ratios (18.5-21.9), which retards microbial decomposition after fungal death and makes EM biomass a disproportionately large carbon sink relative to its nitrogen content
- Persistence of mycorrhizal carbon after senescence is facilitated by the chemical nature of fungal cell walls (chitin and glucans resistant to decomposition) and associations with soil minerals that provide physical protection
- Glomalin, a glycoprotein produced by AM fungi, has a residence time of 6-42 years in soil and can contribute up to 15% of stable soil organic carbon in grassland ecosystems
- These persistent carbon pools represent a significant component of [[fungal-role-in-carbon-cycling]] and are relevant to understanding [[fungal-responses-climate-change]] as warming may accelerate turnover of even recalcitrant fungal residues

## Climate Change Implications

Warming affects mycorrhizal economics through multiple pathways:
- Temperature sensitivity of decomposition versus mycorrhizal nutrient acquisition
- Altered plant C allocation under elevated CO2
- Shifts in EM versus AM dominance with vegetation change
- Impacts on glomalin production and soil carbon stability
- Changes in mycorrhizal-mediated [[mycorrhizal-plant-defense]] under climate stress
