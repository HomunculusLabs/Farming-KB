## Fungal Education Curriculum and Mushroom Science Activities

A structured, grade-level approach to teaching [[mycology]] and mushroom science in
educational settings, from kindergarten through college, covering fungal ecology,
cultivation, composting, and [[mycoremediation]] through hands-on experiments.

## Grades K–4: Introduction to Fungi

### Mushroom Dissection and Anatomy

Dissecting a mushroom introduces students to basic fungal anatomy. Using wild
or store-bought specimens with distinct cap, stem, and gill features, students cut
mushrooms from top to bottom and draw and label the parts. After dissection, the
separated caps and stems serve as paint stamps for creative art projects, reinforcing
the connection between science and art.

### Fruit Cups for Young Growers

Younger students can grow mushrooms in cups using inoculated substrate. This simple
exercise demonstrates the full mushroom life cycle from [[mycelium]] colonization to
fruiting body production in a classroom-friendly format.

### Gas Exchange Experiments

Building on the fruiting cup lesson, students can observe how carbon dioxide pools
in a covered aquarium "like invisible water." By placing half the cups in an empty
aquarium and half in one containing low-growing plants, students observe dramatic
differences: mushrooms without plants develop elongated stems searching for fresh air,
while those with plants show shorter, stockier stems. Students measure and graph stem
length and cap width, developing hypotheses about plant-fungal gas exchange
symbiosis.

### Recycling with Mushrooms

Students bring recyclable items from home and interview school personnel about waste
disposal, brainstorming ways to redirect biodegradable waste to on-site mushroom
composting operations. Soaking materials, adding mushroom cultures, and creating
compost introduces concepts of [[decomposition]] and [[wild-mushroom-harvest-sustainability]].

### Plate Contamination Observations

Students monitor agar plates containing bacteria alone, fungi and bacteria on
opposite sides, and fungi on agar treated with herbicides or fungicides. They also
expose uncultured agar plates in different locations (classroom, bathroom) to observe
mold and bacterial growth, counting and describing different colony types.

## Grades 5–8: Intermediate Mycology

### Gravitropism Experiments

Most plants exhibit [[gravitropism]]: roots grow toward gravity (positive
gravitropism) while stems grow against it (negative gravitropism). To test how
gravity affects mushroom growth, students cultivate [[query-how-to-grow-reishi-mushrooms]]
(Ganoderma lucidum), which form antlers — long, thin, sturdy pins that are slow-
growing and easy to observe. Half the cups serve as controls in a plastic bin; the
other half are attached to a turntable platter. The spinning creates centrifugal
force (a sideways gravitational pull), and students compare growth patterns over
one to two weeks.

### Expanding Biomass on Cardboard

After a mushroom has fruited several times, the mycelium remains alive and active.
Students spread mycelium on wet cardboard, roll it into "burritos," and store them
in lidded containers with holes for gas exchange. Within days, the burritos are fully
colonized and usable as spawn for new experiments — a practical demonstration of
fungal cloning and biomass expansion.

### Mushroom Dissection with Identification

Building on the basic dissection, students use dichotomous keys and field guides to
identify mushroom genera. Spore prints and general dissections assist with making
distinctions between species.

## Grades 9–12: Advanced Classroom Mycology

### Identifying Mushrooms with Dichotomous Keys

Students use basic keys (online or from field guides) to identify wild mushrooms to
genus level. Teachers introduce dichotomous keys using basic gilled fungi
(Agaricales) as a starting point, supplemented by spore prints and dissections.

### Mycorrhizal Relationship Experiments

Students perform controlled experiments with vegetable seeds known to form
[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] relationships (fast-growing plants such as beans). Seeds are
surface-sterilized with ethanol or isopropyl alcohol, rinsed, and planted in
autoclave-sterilized potting soil. Groups of five seeds are planted as controls (plain
sterile soil), while treatment groups receive mycorrhizae inoculation or nitrogen
fertilizer (with and without mycorrhizae). Students observe growth rates, plant
height, leaf node spacing, and can add soil pathogens as a variable to test disease
resistance imparted by mycorrhizal partners. Mycorrhizae can be stained and viewed
under a microscope to confirm fungal bonding inside roots.

### Isolating and Cloning Fungi

Students make oyster mushroom [[macrofungal-documentation-photography-spore-prints]] on aluminum foil, transfer spores to
agar plates (with penicillin to minimize bacterial contamination), and culture
mycelium. Alternatively, oyster mushrooms clone easily from cap tissue — students
take small fragments of the interior (away from gills and outside surfaces) and
transfer them to agar plates. Cultured plates are stored in individual ziplock bags
at room temperature or in an incubator at approximately 75°F (24°C).

### Space Lab: Building an Enclosed Ecosystem

Students create small-scale enclosed ecosystems with plants and fungi that provide
mutual gas exchange and produce food. Mushrooms serve as bioindicators of gas levels:
high oxygen produces short, thick stems; low oxygen produces elongated, deformed
stems. Students must balance biomass proportions and manage ethylene, a plant hormone
that accelerates fruit ripening and can become toxic in enclosed environments. A
small duct fan filtering ethylene through potassium permanganate crystals can
mitigate this. Projects can be competitive, with teams documenting daily changes and
comparing favorable growth of both plants and mushrooms.

### Soil Erosion and Filtration Experiments

Students construct biological soil erosion and pollution control units in trays,
planting varying ground covers, plants, and fungi. After allowing root establishment
and fungal colonization, they simulate water runoff through angled channels filled
with different erosion treatments (bare soil, erosion fabric, shredded wheat straw,
mushroom substrate). Silt and soil erosion levels are measured by dry weight. For
contaminated water testing (diluted herbicides, fertilizers), students use test kits
or low-tech methods like the dilution "bean test" or "worm assay" to measure
remaining contaminants.

### Remediation of Oil Spills

Students set up mock oil spills using vegetable oil in small aquariums. Shredded
dried grass absorbs the oil, and the oiled plant matter is layered between
colonized oyster mushroom cardboard sheets in bins. Weekly inspections reveal
mycelial enzyme production (large yellow metabolite droplets). After approximately
one month, red wiggler worms are added to test biological recovery. The experiment
can span up to three months.

## College Level: Research and Advanced Techniques

### Entomopathogenic Fungi

College students study [[entomopathogenic-fungi]] — fungi that parasitize and kill
insects by colonizing their bodies, sometimes even reaching the brainstem to control
behavior. Students collect sluggish or "mummified" insects from gardens and farms,
isolate and identify the fungal parasites, and test pathogenicity in controlled
behavioral or mortality galleries.
