## JADAM Indigenous Microorganism Cultivation

## Overview

A cornerstone of the JADAM system is the cultivation of indigenous microorganisms (IMO)—locally adapted beneficial bacteria and fungi that are collected from the farm itself rather than purchased as commercial inoculants. This concept page describes the principles and methods of JADAM microorganism cultivation.

## The Indigenous Principle

### Local Adaptation

JADAM's approach to microorganism management is fundamentally local: the beneficial microorganisms best suited to a particular farm are already present in that farm's soil. The task is not to import exotic strains but to create conditions that favor the indigenous beneficial populations.

### Grass as Microorganism Habitat

"You will observe that the soil beneath the grass turns soft. This is sign of indigenous microorganisms propagating. Use this soft soil as starter for microorganism culture."

The technique is elegantly simple: find an area of healthy grass, collect the soft soil beneath it, and use it as a starter culture. The grassland soil naturally contains a diverse community of beneficial microorganisms that have been selected by local conditions.

### Universal Applicability

"You can see how JADAM method can be used anywhere in the world." Because the method uses locally available materials and indigenous microorganisms, it requires no imported inputs and can be applied in any climate or soil type.

## The Philosophy of Balance

### Too Much of Anything Is Bad

"Water is an absolute need for plants but too much will drown them. Anything that is good will turn bad if not applied in the appropriate amount."

JADAM's approach to soil biology is guided by the principle of balance, not maximization. The goal is not the highest possible microorganism count but a balanced community in which no single population dominates.

### The Non-Dualistic View

"No life can be labeled good or evil; this is the mystery of life." This philosophical stance has direct practical implications for microorganism management. The JADAM farmer does not seek to eliminate "bad" microorganisms but to create conditions in which beneficial organisms naturally outcompete harmful ones.

### Specific Examples

- **Nematodes**: "Soil nematodes are not always bad, as adequate number of them helps maintain soil health."
- **Ladybugs**: "Ladybugs are not always good; too many and they will become pests."
- **Apple snails**: "A moderate number of apple snails in rice paddies control weeds but when they over-populate, they become a nuisance."

## The Metabolic Perspective

### Cellular Damage from Excess

"Oxygen will form in the course of metabolism which may inflict serious damage on your cells." This observation about the dangers of excess applies equally to agricultural inputs: even beneficial substances become harmful when applied in excess.

### The Goldilocks Principle

JADAM farming is essentially an exercise in finding the "just right" level for every input—not too much, not too little. This requires careful observation and responsiveness to the farm's conditions rather than adherence to a fixed recipe.

## Practical Methods

### The JADAM Microorganism Solution

The JADAM system teaches farmers to cultivate microorganism solutions from locally collected soil and organic materials. The process typically involves:
1. **Collection**: Gathering soil from beneath healthy vegetation
2. **Feeding**: Providing food sources (rice bran, sugar, etc.) for microbial growth
3. **Cultivation**: Allowing the microorganisms to multiply in a controlled environment
4. **Application**: Diluting and applying the solution to crops and soil

### Cost Elimination

By producing microorganism solutions on-farm, JADAM eliminates one of the most significant recurring costs of organic farming: the purchase of commercial biological inoculants. A single collection can produce enough starter culture to maintain microorganism production indefinitely.
