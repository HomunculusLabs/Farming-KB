# Permaculture Self-Regulation, Energy Hierarchy, and Personal Responsibility

This page extends Principle 4 (Apply Self-Regulation and Accept Feedback) into social and political dimensions: tripartite altruism, energy hierarchy, elite failure, personal responsibility, self-audit, addiction, self-reliance as political action, and [[williams-pdc-disaster-preparedness]].

## Tripartite Altruism in Design

[[howard-odum]]'s tripartite altruism describes how organisms allocate energy:
- **One-third** to metabolic self-maintenance
- **One-third** to maintaining lower-order system providers
- **One-third** to higher-order system controllers

### Applying to Garden Design

Choose elements that provide for their own needs: deep-rooted plants accessing deep moisture, breaking subsoil, providing food for animals or humans. Dock eaten by goats and [[daikon-radish]] for human consumption both break up subsoils — feeding energy back to soil while providing yield to higher-order controllers.

### Modern Economy's Failure

Modern economies fail to provide critical feedback signals for: how much to consume, how much to work, how to pay and measure, what to contribute to the greater good. A practical application: allocate one-third time to material needs, one-third to self-development and reflection, one-third to wider societal benefit.

## The Energy Hierarchy

### Trophic Pyramid

In nature and traditional societies, a trophic pyramid shows: producers (numerous, little power) at the base, consumers (fewer, more powerful) at the top. All levels are functional and complementary; none is more important than another.

### Traditional Structure

```
Spiritual & temporal leaders
    Royalty & managerial elite
        Specialised trades & professions
            Peasants and other producers
```

"Too many chiefs and not enough Indians" describes imbalance. Industrial energy and affluence made this imbalance possible because energy slaves support everyone.

### The Middle-Class Bulge

Industrialization created a great bulge of middle-class consumers — a "normal distribution curve" that has come to dominate thinking about natural [[permaculture-community-philosophy-and-social-structure]]. Evidence of nature and history reveal its novelty. The middle-class is a bulge in the low-energy curve created by the pulse of fossil fuel energy.

### Global Reality

When considering all people globally, the middle-class bulge is very modest: rural and industrial workers in poor countries far outnumber the billion or so middle-class consumers mostly in rich countries.

## Failure of Self-Regulation by Elites

### The Elite Corruption Pattern

The historically recurring problem: elites start to believe the whole social ecosystem is for their benefit. This corrupts acknowledging higher power and fails to provide functional feedback control. Breakdown cascades through the hierarchy until corruption at all levels leads to breakdown and reform.

Since the Enlightenment, social democratic and Marxist ideologies have sought to overcome this, but achieving effective guidance without corruption has not been great. The energetic foundation of these failures: so many hierarchical levels in response to abundant energy, all occupied by fallible humans. "Power corrupts, absolute power corrupts absolutely."

### The Network Problem

People at the highest level function as a self-reinforcing community — like global villages of parochial citizens unable to step outside their norms, yet stratospherically removed from effects of their actions on lower levels.

### Permaculture as Ecological Rationalism

[[permaculture]] recognizes design rules and measures of value in [[energy-descent-action-planning]], providing ethical and positive pathways. Rather than an economic stick forcing people off the "gravy train," we need a deep cultural revolution recognizing society's increasing dependence on nature.

## Top-Down Thinking, Bottom-Up Action

### Three Historical Modes

1. **Traditional (steady state):** Integrated understanding → top-down action (elites), Fragmented understanding → bottom-up action (masses)
2. **Industrial (expansion):** Fragmented understanding → top-down action (reductionist science + managerial power)
3. **Post-industrial (contraction):** Integrated understanding → bottom-up action (the most effective mode)

### The Environmental Slogan

"Top-down thinking, bottom-up action" restates "think globally, act locally." It means awareness of the whole and our relationship to it, informing reorganization of how we live so impacts are closer to home.

## Personal Responsibility

### Four Reasons for Emphasis

1. **Individual wealth and power:** Each person in rich countries commands ~100 energy slaves. A single American baby-boomer's lifetime consumes over half the US conventional oil reserves. The billion middle-class people are the engine of global destruction.

2. **Institutional decay:** Churches, parliamentary democracy, legal systems fail due to cultural decay and change too fast to adapt. When they fail, individuals and small groups carry culture forward. Average life expectancy of great corporations is less than a human lifetime. William Irwin Thompson's historical examples: Pythagoras taking knowledge from decaying [[egyptian-mystery-schools-and-pyramid-initiation]], Lindisfarne monks converting Britain before the institutional church gained hold.

3. **Lack of direct feedback:** Globalization accumulates benefits at centers of affluence and adverse impacts in hinterlands and poor countries. Normal negative feedback mechanisms fail. We need conscious awareness of dependence and impact, reorganizing lives to close the circle of [[fukuoka-nature-no-cause-and-effect]].

4. **The self as whole system:** Learning to think wholistically requires reversing cultural heritage of the last few hundred years. The self is the most accessible and potentially comprehensible whole system — crawl before you walk.

## Self-Audit Process

Self-audit is an ecological observation exercise, not a confession:
1. List all needs, wants, addictions, abilities, liabilities, responsibilities
2. Consider all influences and connections
3. Map material and energy flows and personal movement patterns
4. Take responsibility without guilt or blame on others
5. Look for easiest opportunities for reducing dependence, minimizing harm, improving quality of life
6. Make small changes and review regularly

Non-material needs are often met through material consumption but can be met in much simpler ways. Family upbringing, peer pressure, and advertising have linked emotional, intellectual, and spiritual needs to consumerism.

## Addiction in Modern Society

Holmgren describes permaculture as "aid for addicts," using pharmacological addiction frameworks to understand widespread addictions to shopping, television, driving cars, and other forces of unsustainability.

### The Hedonic Treadmill

Continuous increases in material wealth are needed to maintain happiness measures, but overall improvements are not achievable — addiction by another name. The irrational anger of people unable to get petrol during strikes indicates addiction to driving.

### Lessons from Drug Addiction Recovery

1. Acknowledge our own addictive behaviors as barriers
2. Recognize emotional benefits/yields from the addiction
3. Avoid guilt and blame of others including parents
4. Disconnect from reinforcing relationships with unwilling addicts
5. Connect with ex-addicts and form self-help groups

### Systemic Addiction

Governments dependent on gambling, alcohol, and tobacco revenue. High-yield agriculture addicted to inputs that maintain yields while destroying the productive base. Simply stopping input can cause "cold turkey" of collapsing yield.

## Self-Reliance as Political Action

### The Consumer Strike

Governments and corporations, while preaching self-reliance, actually need our dependence. A slackening in consumption frenzy is called a "consumer strike." Focused selective boycotts of corporations can force beneficial changes. Self-reliance works as a generalized, invisible consumer boycott.

### Resistance to Subversion

The amorphous, informal nature of self-reliance confounds efforts of media, corporations, and government to track, control, and subvert processes undermining their interests. This resistance is one of the great strengths of apparently disorganized movements.
