# Plants, Drugs, Ancient Pharmacology, and Astrological Medicine

## Plants as God's Offspring

In [[allegro-fertility-philosophy-of-the-ancient-near-east]] Eastern worldview, vegetation was not a mere natural
resource but a divine product. As Allegro notes, "Vegetation was the fruit
of god's union with earth. Like any other offspring, some of the children
were strong and vigorous, others weaklings." Plants were understood as
literal offspring of the divine — the semen of the sky-god fecundating
the earth-mother — and their properties reflected this sacred parentage.

"Some fruits were soft and sweet, but others bitter and full of some
strange power that could kill or cure." This duality made plants objects
of reverence and fear; the bitter, potent species bore an especially
concentrated portion of divine power, requiring specialized, often
priestly knowledge to handle. To harvest a potent herb was to interact
with a divine being; to consume it was to take the power of a god into
one's own body.

## Early Drug Discovery

"Man's first experiments in the use of plants as drugs must have been
extremely hazardous." Without systematic knowledge of dosage, preparation,
or contraindication, early humans risked death with every trial. The
process was driven by acute necessity and guided by careful observation
of animal behavior.

The figure of Melampus exemplifies this observational pharmacology: he
discovered Hellebore's purging properties by watching goats that had
eaten the plant and subsequently voided their ailments. Such stories
encode a real truth — that much early pharmacological knowledge came from
watching animals self-medicate. As experiential knowledge accumulated, it
became the province of a specialized class. These "wise men" transformed
folk observation into systematic practice, forming a privileged physician
class bound by strict secrecy to protect both professional status and
dangerous knowledge from misuse.

## Sumerian Medical Texts and Classical Botanists

The earliest known medical text is a Sumerian tablet from the end of the
third millennium BC. Its prescriptions draw from a sophisticated
pharmacopoeia already demonstrating compound preparation: milk,
snake-skin, tortoiseshell, salt, saltpetre, cassia, myrtle, asafoetida,
thyme, willow, pear, fir, fig, and date — spanning animal, mineral, and
vegetable sources.

By the classical period, botanical knowledge was highly systematized.
Theophrastus (372–287 BC), Aristotle's successor, catalogued some 400
plant species with detailed descriptions of their forms, habits, habitats,
and uses. He is said to have used over 2,000 students to gather specimens
and data across the Mediterranean. Dioscorides produced *De Materia
Medica*, systematically separating remedies into vegetable, animal, and
mineral sources — a classification scheme that endured for over a
millennium. Pliny's *Natural History* complemented these works with a
vast repository of folk-lore, superstitions, and stories about plant
qualities and names, preserving traditions that more rigorous writers
had excluded.

## Essenes and Solomonic Demonology

Among the Jewish sects of the Second Temple period, the Essenes displayed
a keen interest in pharmacology and natural philosophy. As Josephus
records, they showed "extraordinary interest in the writings of the
ancients... they investigate medicinal roots and the properties of
stones." This combination of textual scholarship and empirical
investigation positioned the Essenes at the intersection of religion
and medicine.

Solomon embodied this same union. Scripture credits him with knowledge
of "trees, from the cedar that is in Lebanon, to the hyssop that grows
out of the wall" — encompassing the full botanical world. His legendary
wisdom extended to controlling demonic forces, and in rabbinic and
Hellenistic tradition, botanical knowledge and demonology were
intimately connected.

The story of Eleazar illustrates this synthesis vividly: he cured
demonic possession using a ring set with a prescribed root under a seal,
holding it to the patient's nostrils to draw the demon out while speaking
Solomon's name. The ritual presupposes that "demon" and "root" are
aspects of the same potency — the plant drug physically expels the
spiritual affliction.

## Drug Identification and Dosage

The ancient pharmacist's art extended far beyond mere plant identification.
It required detailed knowledge of extraction, purification, and above all
correct dosage. Many powerful drugs were only considered safe on certain
days, after lengthy, ritualized preparation — reflecting both genuine
pharmacological caution and astrological timing.

When a drug proved too powerful, practitioners countered it with another
of opposite effect. A Hellebore purge might be followed by restoratives;
narcotics could be offset with stimulants. This principle of opposing
remedies suggests a sophisticated understanding of drug interaction and
physiological balance. Crucially, dosage depended on the patient's "fate"
— the constitutional disposition allotted at birth under the stars.
Medicine was therefore fundamentally dependent on astrology: the
physician had to know not only the drug but the patient's natal chart.

## Light vs Darkness — Medical, Not Moral

The [[allegro-dead-sea-scrolls]] contain a zodiac chart assigning physical and
spiritual characteristics to each birth sign, dividing human constitution
into "light" and "darkness" portions. There are nine such portions in
total, related to the months of gestation, each governing different
aspects of temperament and health. This was a practical medical
instrument, not a speculative curiosity.

The designations "Children of Light" and "Children of Darkness" must
therefore be understood not as moral categories but as constitutional and
medical ones. A person's "light" or "darkness" quotient was determined
by planetary influences during gestation and birth, dictating physical
vulnerabilities, mental tendencies, and spiritual predispositions.

The "fruits of the spirit of Truth" — healing, peace, longevity,
fruitfulness — describe physiological and spiritual balance. The "ways
of the spirit of Falsehood" — greed, wickedness, lies, haughtiness,
pride — are not abstract moral failings but the faults of intemperance
and arrogance, excesses of a constitution tilted toward the "darkness"
portions. As Allegro concludes: "Medicine was as much a part of righting
this imbalance of 'moral' character as religion; the two were, in fact,
inseparable." To heal the body was to restore spiritual equilibrium; the
physician and the priest were functionally identical.

## Demon = Drug Identity

The [[allegro-linguistic-evidence]] for this identity is striking. The Greek *daimōn*
derives from Sumerian *DA...U_NA*, "having power over fertility" —
describing both a spiritual being and a potent plant substance. Hebrew
*dawah* ("be sick") and Arabic "medicine" derive from the same root,
encoding the recognition that the agent of sickness and the agent of cure
are one. Allegro states it plainly: "The demon of health and sickness
and the drug are radically one and the same."

The Sumerian *A-ZU* / *I-ZU*, "water-oil-expert," designated
simultaneously the physician, the prophet, and the seer. The very name
"Essene" may derive from this same root, identifying the sect by its
primary vocation as healers of sacred substances. Even divine names
encode pharmacological meaning: Jesus/Joshua represents *JA_U_ShUSh* —
"semen which saves, restores, heals"; Dionysus represents
*IA-U-NU-ShUSh* — "semen, seed that saves." The god is the drug; the
drug is the god. "The arts of healing and religion were inseparable" —
to study ancient pharmacology is necessarily to study its theology, its
demonology, and its cosmology as one unified system of knowledge.
