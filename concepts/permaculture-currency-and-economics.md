# Permaculture Currency and Economics

Permaculture extends beyond physical landscape design into the realm of community economics. In Bill Mollison's Designers' Manual (Chapter 14), the financial and economic dimension is treated as inseparable from ecological design: money should serve the proliferation of life, not the accumulation of monuments.

## Core Philosophy

Money is not intrinsically a resource -- it represents (or should represent) a resource which lies "somewhere else." All money ultimately arises from the wealth of the natural world: plants, clean water, clear air, stored energy. The accumulation of unused wealth, or wealth that does not lead to the proliferation of life, is a pollution of the same nature as any unused resource. As Mollison writes, "Manure and money have much in common."

The goal of permaculture economics is to develop wealth the way we develop landscapes: by concentrating on conservation of energy and natural resources (reducing the need to earn), by developing procreative assets (forests, prairies, life systems), by reducing degenerative assets (roads, monuments, cities), and by constantly divesting surplus wealth toward these ends.

## Asset Categories

Mollison classifies assets into five categories (after Turnbull, 1975):

1. **Degenerative**: Assets that decay, rust, or wear out -- buildings, roads, cars, furnishings. Too many impoverish a region long-term.

2. **Generative**: Tools of society -- things which process raw materials into useful products (huskers, grinders, lathes, furnaces). These wear out but can repair each other in workshops.

3. **Procreative**: Trees, wildlife, fish, invertebrates, mammals, domestic livestock. People who maximize procreative assets can support tools and modest degenerative assets. Those who maximize degenerative assets eventually fail.

4. **Informational**: Education, data, applied intelligence, seeds, books, databases. Information makes the best use of all other assets.

5. **Conservative**: Insulation, dams, money recycling systems, good storage, strategic forests. These guard resources for future use and are essential to sustainability.

Expenditure on categories 3-5 creates and conserves wealth. Any society spending only on categories 1-2 will first pollute, then extinguish, its resource base.

## The Informal Economy

### Barter and Exchange

Barter is common in rural and neighborhood areas where people know one another. At household level, people exchange garden products, share labour, and exchange goods and services. Work groups of 3-5 people may form round-robin patterns until all present needs are met.

The **Bendigo Home Builder's Club** (Victoria, Australia) is a group of 35 people building individual homes. Members pay $5/year for newsletter costs. Units of exchange are hours of labour; all labour is considered equal. Recipients are debited, donors credited. A Labour Organiser manages the balance of payments.

**Community Barter Clubs** work on debit/credit systems where residents offer goods, services, and skills. A credit equals one hour. People are not limited to one-to-one exchange; the organiser keeps records so transactions occur as long as services are desired. These clubs are assets for unemployed or underemployed community members.

### LETS (Local Employment Trading System)

The LET System centers in a community where every joining member must be willing to consider trading in local "green" dollars. Key features:

- Green dollars carry no interest
- Administration costs are charged on a "cost of service" basis
- Any member can know the turnover or balance of any other member
- The currency is equivalent to legal tender but cannot be cashed in
- Green dollars are "earned" by providing goods or services to others
- Unlike simple barter, a member in credit can spend across the whole range of services offered
- Production (time spent in service to others) is never limited by lack of money
- Businesses can charge federal currency for spare parts and green dollars for labour
- Local components increase; local businesses thrive
- Anyone who wants work can offer services without waiting for "jobs"

An ideal member has many transactions but accumulates modest debits and credits. In effect, any member issues their own currency by working or selling.

## The Formal Economy

Formal economies operate under a legal umbrella with accounting procedures. Community formal structures include:

- Cooperatives
- Community savings and loans
- Small business service offices
- Investment funds for special projects
- Leasing companies

### Cooperatives

Cooperatives are groups acting together for member benefit, with open membership, democratic organisation, limited interest on share capital, surplus belonging to members, education mandates, and cooperation between cooperatives.

The **Mondragon worker-cooperatives** in the Basque region of Spain demonstrate the model: 96 cooperatives employing 17,000 worker-members in less than 30 years. Each person invests ~$5,000 on joining (20% to collective funds, 80% to individual capital account). Profits: 10% returned to community, 20% held as capital reserves, 70% distributed to workers.

### Financial Institutions for Bioregions

Financial institutions should prevent leakage of essential resources and expedite export of local surplus. Every institution needs two foundation stones:

1. An ethic -- expressed as a published, legally binding, publicly known charter
2. Restriction to appropriate resource development and trade

Without ethics or restrictions, any financial institution is a danger to community.

## Money as Water

Mollison's key metaphor: "Money is to the social fabric as water is to landscape. It is the agent of transport, the shaper and mover of trade." What matters is not the total amount of money centering a community, but the number of uses or cycles of use. Leakage from the community must be prevented and recycling made the rule.
