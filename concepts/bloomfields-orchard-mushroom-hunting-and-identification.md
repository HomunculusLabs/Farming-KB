## The LBM Problem: Little Brown Mushrooms

Money devotes his chapter "Angels of Death" to the terror of Little Brown Mushrooms (LBMs) — "the multitude of dun, formulaic woodland fruiting bodies that rarely provoke a second glance." Among these unremarkable fungi lurk deadly species like the autumn skullcap (*Galerina autumnalis*), which contains the same amatoxins that make death caps lethal. Money recounts sitting on a log in the woods, flipping through guidebooks, unable to distinguish deadly galerinas from the edible two-toned scalecap (*Pholiota mutabilis*) — both grow on decaying wood, both have white rings on their stems, both are small and brown. The scalecap's stem is clothed with tiny scales (not prominent in older specimens), and the cluster coincap (*Collybia acervata*) is reportedly bitter. His eventual clue was a faint "farinaceous" (cucumbery) smell, noted in some descriptions of the deadly galerina.

This anecdote illustrates a broader truth: [[paddy-straw-mushroom]] (*[[pleurotus-ostreatus]])
Grows in shelf-like clusters on dead or dying hardwood trees, particularly beech and oak. Cap is fan-shaped to oyster-shaped, smooth, grey-brown to white (colour varies with age and light exposure). Gills run down a very short or absent stem, are white, and are closely spaced. Flesh is white and firm. Spore print is white to lilac-grey. One of the easiest wild mushrooms to identify, with no truly dangerous lookalikes. Fruits autumn through winter on dead wood. Compare with the angel's wing (Pleurocybella porrigens), which grows on conifers and has been associated with fatalities in people with kidney problems.

### Giant Puffball (Calvatia gigantea)
Fruit body is spherical to slightly irregular, 10-70cm diameter (specimens exceeding 30cm are common). Surface is smooth and white when young, becoming yellowish-brown with age. When cut open, the interior is solid, pure white, and marshmallow-like -- this is critical: if the interior shows any sign of gill formation or is not pure white, it should not be eaten. - **Microhabitat**: Mossy banks, stream edges, disturbed ground, animal paths, and rotting stumps each host characteristic fungal communities. The fairy ring mushroom (Marasmius oreades) grows in grasslands and lawns, never in forests.

## Spore Print Techniques

Making a spore print is one of the most important identification steps and should be done for every mushroom you intend to eat:
1. Cut the cap from the stem with a clean knife.
2. Place the cap gill-side (or pore-side) down on a sheet of paper (half white, half black to show spores of any colour). Alternatively, use a glass slide.
3. Cover with a bowl or cup to prevent air currents from dispersing spores.
4. Leave for 2-12 hours (overnight is ideal). Different species deposit spores at different rates.
5. Carefully lift the cap to reveal the spore print pattern. The colour and pattern are diagnostic: free gills produce distinct radial patterns; attached gills produce patterns connected at a central point; pores produce a dotted pattern.

Key spore print colours for identification: white (Amanita, Russula, Lactarius), cream (chanterelle, hedgehog), pink (Entoloma), brown (Agaricus, Psilocybe), chocolate-brown (Panaeolus, Cortinarius), rusty-brown (Gymnopilus), purple-brown (Psilocybe, some Agaricus), black (Coprinus).

## Dangerous Lookalikes: A Field Safety Guide

The most critical lookalike pairs for foragers to know:
- **Chanterelle vs. Jack O'Lantern (Omphalotus olearius)**: Jack O'Lanterns have true gills (not false gills), are brighter orange, grow in clusters on wood (not on soil), and are bioluminescent. Jack O'Lanterns cause severe gastrointestinal distress.
- **Porcini vs. Bitter Bolete (Tylopilus felleus)**: Bitter boletes have a pink pore surface when mature and pink reticulation on the stem. They are not poisonous but are intensely bitter and ruin any dish.
- **Hedgehog vs. no dangerous lookalikes**: The toothed underside of the hedgehog is unique among edible mushrooms. No toothed mushroom in temperate regions is dangerously poisonous.
- **Giant Puffball vs. immature Amanita**: Cut every puffball open before eating. If the interior is not pure white throughout, or if any gill-like structures are visible, discard it. This is the single most important rule for puffball foraging.
- **Morel vs. False Morel (Gyromitra esculenta)**: True morels are honeycomb-patterned with a continuous cap-to-stem connection. False morels have brain-like wrinkled caps attached to the stem at the top only, with a chambered interior when cut lengthwise. False morels contain gyromitrin, a compound that converts to monomethylhydrazine (rocket fuel) and can be fatal. Even "edible" preparations involve extensive parboiling and carry risk.

## Foraging Ethics and Best Practices

- **Harvest sustainably**: Cut mushrooms at the base with a knife rather than pulling them from the ground. Pulling damages the mycelial network. Leave at least one mushroom per cluster to ensure spore dispersal.
- **Use a mesh bag**: Carrying mushrooms in an open mesh bag allows spores to disperse as you walk through the forest, contributing to future fruiting. Avoid sealed plastic bags which cause rapid deterioration.
- **Know before you eat**: Never eat a mushroom unless you have identified it with 100% certainty using multiple features (cap, gills, stem, spore print, habitat, smell, bruising reaction). When in doubt, throw it out.
- **Respect private property**: Obtain permission before foraging on private land. Know the regulations for public lands -- some nature reserves prohibit all mushroom collection.
- **Avoid polluted sites**: Do not collect mushrooms near roadsides, industrial sites, or agricultural land treated with pesticides. Fungi accumulate heavy metals and other contaminants from their substrate.
- **Document your finds**: Photograph mushrooms from multiple angles (top, underside, stem, habitat) and record date, location, and associated trees. Build a personal reference library over time.
- **Learn from experts**: Join a local mycological society, attend foraging walks with experienced identifiers, and use multiple field guides. No single guide covers all species in all regions.
