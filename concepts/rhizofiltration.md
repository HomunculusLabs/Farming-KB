## Rhizofiltration

Rhizofiltration is a phytoremediation technology that uses plant roots to absorb, adsorb, concentrate, and precipitate contaminants from aqueous solutions. The technique exploits the natural ability of plant root systems to remove heavy metals, radionuclides, and certain organic pollutants from water, including groundwater, surface water, wastewater, and industrial effluents.

Unlike other phytoremediation methods that treat soil in situ, rhizofiltration typically involves growing plants hydroponically or in [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]]. This means that both living and dead root biomass can function as biosorbent material.

Root surface area is a critical factor in biosorption capacity. Fine, fibrous root systems provide greater surface area per unit volume than coarse roots. Root hairs further increase the available binding surface. The composition and density of surface binding groups vary among plant species, making species selection an important consideration for system design.

### Root Uptake and Translocation

Once adsorbed to the root surface, some metal ions are actively transported across the root cell membrane into the root interior. This process is energy-dependent and involves specific ion channels and transporter proteins. The extent of uptake and translocation varies considerably among plant species and metal types.

Some species are hyperaccumulators that not only take up metals efficiently but also translocate them to above-ground tissues at high concentrations. However, for rhizofiltration purposes, root accumulation is generally preferred because it avoids the need to manage contaminated above-ground biomass and reduces the risk of contaminant transfer to herbivores.

Uptake rates are influenced by water chemistry parameters including pH, redox potential, competing ions, dissolved organic matter, and the speciation and concentration of the target contaminant. Optimal pH for metal uptake typically falls between 4.5 and 6.5 for most plant species.

## Hyperaccumulator Plants

Hyperaccumulator plants are species capable of accumulating exceptionally high concentrations of heavy metals and other trace elements in their above-ground tissues without exhibiting toxic effects. These remarkable plants concentrate metals at levels that would be lethal to most other organisms, making them powerful tools for phytoremediation of metal-contaminated soils. The study and application of hyperaccumulator plants represents a convergence of plant physiology, biochemistry, ecology, and environmental engineering.

## Definition and Classification

Hyperaccumulator plants are defined by their ability to accumulate metals to concentrations exceeding certain threshold values. The most widely accepted thresholds are 100 mg/kg (0.01%) dry weight for cadmium, selenium, and other non-essential trace elements; 1,000 mg/kg (0.1%) for cobalt, copper, chromium, nickel, and lead; and 10,000 mg/kg (1.0%) for manganese and zinc. Plants exceeding these concentrations in their above-ground biomass are considered hyperaccumulators.

Approximately 500 plant species have been identified as hyperaccumulators, representing less than 0.2% of all known plant species. These species are distributed across many plant families, with notable concentrations in the Brassicaceae (mustard family), Fabaceae (legume family), Euphorbiaceae (spurge family), Asteraceae (daisy family), and Caryophyllaceae (carnation family). Most known hyperaccumulators are endemic to naturally metalliferous soils, where they have evolved metal tolerance and accumulation as an adaptation to their native environment.
