# Rhizofiltration

Rhizofiltration is a phytoremediation technology that uses plant roots to absorb, adsorb, concentrate, and precipitate contaminants from aqueous solutions. The technique exploits the natural ability of plant root systems to remove heavy metals, radionuclides, and certain organic pollutants from water, including groundwater, surface water, wastewater, and industrial effluents.

Unlike other phytoremediation methods that treat soil in situ, rhizofiltration typically involves growing plants hydroponically or in [[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]], then exposing their root systems to contaminated water. The roots act as a biological filter, removing dissolved contaminants through a combination of physical, chemical, and biological processes.

## Mechanisms of Contaminant Removal

Rhizofiltration operates through several complementary mechanisms that work together to remove contaminants from water. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for optimizing system design and predicting treatment performance.

### Biosorption on Root Surfaces

The primary mechanism of metal removal in rhizofiltration is biosorption, the passive binding of metal ions to functional groups on root cell wall surfaces. Plant root cell walls contain polysaccharides (cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin), proteins, and lignin that provide numerous binding sites including carboxyl, hydroxyl, amino, sulfhydryl, and phosphate groups.

Metal ions bind to these sites through ion exchange, complexation, electrostatic attraction, and surface precipitation. The binding is rapid, often reaching equilibrium within minutes to hours, and is largely independent of plant [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]]. This means that both living and dead root biomass can function as biosorbent material.

Root surface area is a critical factor in biosorption capacity. Fine, fibrous root systems provide greater surface area per unit volume than coarse roots. Root hairs further increase the available binding surface. The composition and density of surface binding groups vary among plant species, making species selection an important consideration for system design.

### Root Uptake and Translocation

Once adsorbed to the root surface, some metal ions are actively transported across the root cell membrane into the root interior. This process is energy-dependent and involves specific ion channels and transporter proteins. The extent of uptake and translocation varies considerably among plant species and metal types.

Some species are hyperaccumulators that not only take up metals efficiently but also translocate them to above-ground tissues at high concentrations. However, for rhizofiltration purposes, root accumulation is generally preferred because it avoids the need to manage contaminated above-ground biomass and reduces the risk of contaminant transfer to herbivores.

Uptake rates are influenced by water chemistry parameters including pH, redox potential, competing ions, dissolved organic matter, and the speciation and concentration of the target contaminant. Optimal pH for metal uptake typically falls between 4.5 and 6.5 for most plant species.

### Root-Mediated Precipitation

Plant roots can alter the chemical environment of the rhizosphere in ways that promote contaminant precipitation. [[root-exudates]] can raise or lower local pH, change redox conditions, and release ligands that form insoluble complexes with metal ions. Root-associated microorganisms further modify the rhizosphere chemistry through their metabolic activities.

Iron plaque formation on root surfaces is a well-documented phenomenon in wetland plants. Under flooded conditions, roots release oxygen that oxidizes ferrous iron (Fe2+) to ferric iron (Fe3+), which precipitates as iron oxyhydroxide coatings on the root surface. These iron plaques can adsorb and co-precipitate other metal contaminants including arsenic, phosphorus, and heavy metals.

## Plant Species for Rhizofiltration

Selection of appropriate plant species is critical for successful rhizofiltration. Ideal species exhibit rapid growth, extensive root systems, high root surface area, tolerance to target contaminants, and the ability to accumulate contaminants in root tissues without significant phytotoxicity.

### Aquatic and Semi-Aquatic Plants

Water hyacin eichhornia crassipes ipes) is one of the most studied species for rhizofiltration due to its extremely rapid growth rate and extensive fibrous root system. It has demonstrated effective removal of lead, cadmium, copper, zinc, nickel, chromium, and mercury from contaminated water. However, its invasive potential in warm climates requires careful management.

Duckweed (Lemna minor, Spirodela polyrhiza) species are small floating aquatic plants with high metal uptake capacity relative to their biomass. Their rapid multiplication rate allows quick [[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]] for continuous harvesting and contaminant removal.

Common re phragmites australis alis) is the dominant plant species in most constructed treatment wetlands worldwide. Its deep, extensive root system creates a large treatment zone and provides physical filtration through dense root mats. ## Factors Affecting Performance

Water chemistry parameters significantly influence rhizofiltration efficiency. pH affects both metal speciation and the ionization state of root surface binding groups. Most metals are more bioavailable and more readily adsorbed at slightly acidic pH (4.5 to 6.0), while very low pH can damage root tissues.

The presence of competing ions such as calcium, magnesium, and sodium can reduce target metal uptake by competing for binding sites. Dissolved organic matter can form soluble complexes with metals, reducing their availability for root adsorption. Water temperature affects plant growth rate, metabolic activity, and the kinetics of biosorption.

The concentration of contaminants affects both removal kinetics and the ultimate capacity of the root system. Very high concentrations may cause phytotoxicity, reducing plant health and treatment performance. Sequential treatment or dilution may be necessary for highly contaminated water.

## Applications

Rhizofiltration has been applied or proposed for treatment of groundwater contaminated with heavy metals from mining, smelting, and industrial activities. It has been used for treatment of [[singh-landfill-leachate-fungal-treatment]] containing mixed heavy metal contamination. Nuclear facility decommissioning has employed rhizofiltration for removal of radionuclides including uranium, strontium, cesium, and cobalt from contaminated surface and groundwater.

[[fungal-industrial-wastewater-treatment]] from electroplating, battery manufacturing, and metal finishing operations can be treated using rhizofiltration. The technology has also been investigated for treatment of agricultural runoff containing excess nutrients and pesticide residues, though organic contaminants are generally more effectively removed through rhizodegradation than biosorption alone.

## Limitations

Rhizofiltration is limited to water-soluble or suspended contaminants. Insoluble forms must be dissolved or desorbed before removal. Harvested plant biomass containing accumulated contaminants must be disposed of as hazardous waste or processed for metal recovery, adding to treatment costs. Seasonal climate variations affect plant growth and treatment performance, particularly in temperate regions.

The risk of contaminant release from decomposing plant material must be managed through timely harvesting and proper disposal. Invasive potential of fast-growing aquatic species requires monitoring and containment measures. Despite these limitations, rhizofiltration offers a low-cost, energy-efficient, and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional water treatment technologies for appropriate applications.

# Hyperaccumulator Plants

Hyperaccumulator plants are species capable of accumulating exceptionally high concentrations of heavy metals and other trace elements in their above-ground tissues without exhibiting toxic effects. These remarkable plants concentrate metals at levels that would be lethal to most other organisms, making them powerful tools for phytoremediation of metal-contaminated soils. The study and application of hyperaccumulator plants represents a convergence of plant physiology, biochemistry, ecology, and environmental engineering.

## Definition and Classification

Hyperaccumulator plants are defined by their ability to accumulate metals to concentrations exceeding certain threshold values. The most widely accepted thresholds are 100 mg/kg (0.01%) dry weight for cadmium, selenium, and other non-essential trace elements; 1,000 mg/kg (0.1%) for cobalt, copper, chromium, nickel, and lead; and 10,000 mg/kg (1.0%) for manganese and zinc. Plants exceeding these concentrations in their above-ground biomass are considered hyperaccumulators.

Approximately 500 plant species have been identified as hyperaccumulators, representing less than 0.2% of all known plant species. These species are distributed across many plant families, with notable concentrations in the Brassicaceae (mustard family), Fabaceae (legume family), Euphorbiaceae (spurge family), Asteraceae (daisy family), and Caryophyllaceae (carnation family). Most known hyperaccumulators are endemic to naturally metalliferous soils, where they have evolved metal tolerance and accumulation as an adaptation to their native environment.
