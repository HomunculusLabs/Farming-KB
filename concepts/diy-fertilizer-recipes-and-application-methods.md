## DIY Fertilizer Recipes and Application Methods

Organic gardeners can blend their own complete fertilizers from readily available ingredients. The recipes and application strategies below are drawn from *Teaming with Nutrients* by Jeff Lowenfels, synthesizing the approaches of several experienced growers. The goal is to supply plants with balanced macronutrients (N-P-K) and micronutrients while feeding the soil food web that makes those nutrients available.

## DIY Fertilizer Philosophy

Several principles underpin all of the recipes that follow:

- **Measure by volume, not weight.** Use the same scoop or container for every ingredient so that the part-ratios remain accurate regardless of ingredient density.
- **Match mix speed to garden maturity.** Fast-acting soluble mixes are appropriate for unprepared soils and transplanting situations where plants need an immediate nutrient supply. Slow-acting, meal-based mixes are better suited to established gardens with active soil biology.
- **Cover fertilized soil.** Applying a 0.5-inch layer of finished compost or 6 inches of grass clippings over fertilized ground significantly increases nutrient-use efficiency by moderating moisture and temperature and preventing volatilization.
- **Mulch always.** Nature does not maintain bare soil. Mulches conserve moisture, suppress weeds, and provide habitat for decomposer organisms that recycle nutrients.
- **Soil tests guide adjustments.** These recipes are general-purpose starting points. A professional soil test may reveal specific deficiencies or excesses that warrant modifying ingredient proportions.

Gardeners may choose to prepare a single all-purpose mix for most plants, maintain separate blends tailored to individual crop types, or shift formulations across the growing season as plant demands change.

## Grandpa Al's Can't Fail Recipe

A staple general-purpose organic fertilizer blend:

| Ingredient | Parts (by volume) |
|---|---|
| Fish meal (or soybean meal or cottonseed meal) | 4 |
| Kelp meal | 1 |
| Rock phosphate (or ¾ part bone meal) | 1 |
| Dolomitic limestone (or calcitic lime, or a mix) | 1 |

**Application rate:** 1–2 gallons of mix per 100 square feet at the start of the season, banded into the root zone. Side-dress under mulch every 4 weeks thereafter.

**Nutrient breakdown:** The seed meals supply nitrogen and some phosphorus. Rock phosphate or bone meal provides additional phosphorus and calcium. Potassium comes from sulfate of potash and the kelp meal. The limestone in this recipe is included primarily to counter the acidity of the other ingredients, not to adjust the soil's pH per se.

## Steve Solomon's Recipe

A higher-nitrogen alternative that includes blood meal for a rapid nitrogen boost:

| Ingredient | Parts (by volume) |
|---|---|
| Cottonseed meal | 3 |
| Blood meal | 1 |
| Dolomitic or calcitic limestone | 1 |
| Bone meal | ½ |
| Kelp meal | ½ |

**Application rate:** 6 quarts per 100 square feet. For quick results, band the fertilizer into the soil. In established natural systems, a simple top-dress is sufficient. Side-dress every 3–4 weeks after pulling back mulch.

The blood meal is the key differentiator here—it creates an immediately available nitrogen pool that young plants or nitrogen-hungry crops can draw on right away, while the cottonseed meal breaks down more slowly to sustain the supply.

## Quick Starter for Planting

This liquid blend is designed to give transplants and direct-sown seeds an immediate nutrient kick at planting time:

| Ingredient | Parts (by volume) |
|---|---|
| Fish emulsion or fish powder | 1.5 |
| Liquid kelp | 1 |

Alternatively, substitute 0.5 part bat guano for the fish product.

Dilute according to label instructions before use. Transplants can be soaked briefly in a 1:4 dilution before planting. **Starting reference rates:** 3 tablespoons bat guano per gallon of water; 1 teaspoon fish powder per gallon; 2 teaspoons fish emulsion per gallon. One gallon of diluted solution services roughly 4–5 square feet.

**Important warnings:**

- **Do not use in clay soils.** High-soluble-nitrogen liquids can deflocculate clay particles, destroying soil structure.
- High soluble nitrogen means the mix must be diluted correctly—over-concentration will burn plants.
- Always test on a few sample seedlings before applying broadly.

## Wayne Lewis's Graceland Lawn Food

A simple, burn-proof lawn fertilizer:

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Soybean meal (or chicken litter meal) | 50 lbs |
| Granulated molasses | 50 lbs |

This combination fertilizes approximately 2,000 square feet of lawn. Apply once weekly watering alone is no longer sufficient to keep the grass green. Because the ingredients are gentle and slow-release, they cannot burn the lawn even if over-applied.

Always leave grass clippings on the lawn—they return nitrogen and other nutrients to the soil, embodying the Law of Return. The soybean meal supplies nitrogen and serves as a fungal substrate, while the molasses acts as both a microbial stimulant and a direct fungal food source.

## Fertilizer Application Methods

How fertilizer is placed in the soil matters as much as what is applied. Different methods suit different situations and nutrient types.

### Broadcasting

Spreading fertilizer uniformly across the soil surface. This is the simplest method and works for large areas, but it is not very efficient: fertilizer is wasted between rows, surface-applied phosphorus remains largely unavailable to plants, and weed seeds in the broadcast zone are also fertilized.

### Banding

Placing fertilizer in a concentrated band approximately 2 inches away from and 2 inches deeper than where seeds will be planted. For transplants, the band should be 3–4 inches below the root ball. Banding can increase phosphorus and potassium uptake by roughly 50% compared to broadcasting, making it especially valuable for immobile nutrients that do not readily move through the soil profile.

### Spot Banding

A variation of banding in which a measured amount of fertilizer is placed in an individual hole and plants are arranged around it. This technique is particularly well suited to autumn application of immobile phosphates and potassium, giving the nutrients time to become available before spring growth begins.

### Side Dressing

Applying additional fertilizer alongside a row or beside individual plants at midseason. The material is placed under mulch or lightly buried in the top 1–2 inches of soil, where moisture and microbial activity can carry nutrients into the root zone.

### Foliar Spraying

Applying liquid fertilizer directly to plant leaves. Foliar feeding is useful only as a temporary quick fix for mobile micronutrient deficiencies—most commonly iron or zinc. It cannot supply enough macronutrients (N-P-K) to sustain healthy growth and should not be relied upon as a primary feeding strategy.

### Perennials, Trees, and Shrubs

Woody plants and other perennials require minimal fertilizer if the surrounding natural system is healthy and mulched. When fertilization is needed, apply materials on or near the soil surface where the majority of feeder roots reside in the top few inches. Avoid deep cultivation around established root zones.

## Mycorrhizal Inoculation

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots, dramatically extending the effective root surface area and improving nutrient and water uptake. In most outdoor soils, indigenous mycorrhizal populations are already present. Inoculation is primarily warranted in certain situations:

- **New gardens and disturbed sites** where native fungal populations have been depleted.
- **Indoor seed-starting mixes** and sterile potting media that contain no mycorrhizal spores.
- **Compost-heavy growing systems**, where the high microbial activity can suppress mycorrhizal spore viability.

Mycorrhizal spores germinate only upon contact with root exudates, so inoculants must be placed where roots will grow into them. Gardeners can also collect local mycorrhizal fungi by taking a small amount of soil from the root zone of thriving, healthy plants and incorporating it into new planting areas.

Similarly, rhizobia—the nitrogen-fixing bacteria that form nodules on legume roots—can be gathered by digging up root nodules from healthy legumes (pink contents inside indicate active nitrogen fixation) and using the nodule material to inoculate seeds or soil for similar legume crops.
