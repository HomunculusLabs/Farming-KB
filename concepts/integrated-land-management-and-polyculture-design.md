# Integrated Land Management and Polyculture Design

## Overview

Integrated land management in permaculture draws on traditional geomorphic and sociological units — such as the Maori marae and Hawaiian ohana — where land and people were integrated for sustenance. These systems evolved over centuries, often through early errors of over-clearing, excessive burning, and extinction of useful animals, before reaching equilibrium. Mollison's framework provides a comprehensive model for designing integrated hill-to-shore landscapes in the humid tropics, with principles applicable across all climates.

## The Hill-to-Shore Profile

The stable tropical landscape, often following a volcanic cone profile, requires a carefully zoned arrangement of features from the protected hilltop to the coastal shore:

### Protected Skyline and Hill Forests

The upper slopes play a critical role that extends far beyond timber production:

- **Soil and water protection:** These forests prevent erosion on steep slopes and maintain watershed function
- **Nutrient mining and release:** Trees on upper slopes access deep mineral nutrients and cycle them back into the system through leaf fall and decomposition
- **Limited forage and mulch provision:** Carefully managed, they can provide some forage and mulch without compromising their protective function
- **Iron-clad protection needed:** Clearing hilltop forests brings compound catastrophes — landslide, loss of nutrient in water and crop, desertification, and severe social disruption

At the base of these forests, as the slope eases to 15° or less, water can be diverted or harvested to replenish groundwater and irrigate terraces and crops.

### Midslope and Keypoint

The keypoint is a critical topographic feature where a diversion of stream water leads water out to ridges for terrace crop and village use. Cropping commences below this critical point. Human occupation and complex cultivated forests and gardens are established downslope from the keypoint.

The stable plateau, the hill rising above the valley, and bench sites above the reach of flood and sheltered from hurricane and tsunami are identified as prime cluster settlement (village) sites, with some scattered housing on higher ridges and at the forest edge.

### Low Slopes (2°-15°)

Low slopes are well suited to earth-shaping as terraces and padi (rice paddies), with limited grazing and innovative forestry. These are the primary sustainable agricultural areas where attention to sub-contoured agriculture, windbreaks, and access design helps direct runoff and water to crops.

### Coastal Flats and Valley Floors

Rich, deep humus soils accumulate on valley floors. Greywater and processed manures from settlement and livestock add to fertility, supporting extensive aquacultures or rich forest and orchard crops.

### Shorelines

Shorelines should be preserved for approximately 100 meters (330 feet) inland for essential windbreak, shore stability, and cloud generation over land. Selected quiet harbors and refuges accept fishing settlements based on sea resources. Shores with 15-20 year tsunami history need sacrificial palm or Casuarina barriers to reduce storm damage ashore. Lightly-built temporary shore housing is used only when working the coast, with more substantial homes and sheltered gardens safely inland or behind earth embankments.

## Lagoon and Estuary Systems

Ancient and innovative sea-walls — usually of semi-permeable coral blocks with entries, flood-tide gates, and a series of inner pens — represent an excellent use of shallows:

- **Mangrove stabilization:** Restricted mangrove plantings stabilize banks and provide sea mulch while catching silt washed from upslope
- **Warm-water aquaculture:** These warm-water lagoons are rich in algae and serve as good sites for cultivated seaweed
- **Fish impoundment:** They impound and fatten mullet, mangrove fish, oysters, eels, shellfish, and prawns
- **Phosphate fixation:** Mobile phosphates are fixed in plants and mud within a few days, becoming available for growth

## Marine Constructs

Artificial reef systems expand marine habitat:

- **Materials:** Tyres on sand, coral blocks, and boxes of palm trunks greatly enlarge habitat for sedentary fish and lobsters
- **Consolidation:** Within a year, constructs are consolidated or cemented by corals
- **Electrolytic accretion:** Weak electrolysis on metal mesh creates artificial but permanent coralline deposits more rapidly
- **Shore barriers:** In extensive sandy shallows, log barriers full of sand and stabilized with mangroves provide shore barriers, lagoon walls, and "edge" in the sea

## Erosion Control Systems

### Contour Planting

Contours at 10-meter intervals on medium slopes (2°-7°) and at 5-meter intervals on steep slopes can be planted with root sets of Canna, vetiver grass, lemongrass, or pampas grass. These are set out at 0.3-0.6 meter spacing and form:

- An unbroken cross-slope hedge
- A crown on earth walls or dam banks at spillways
- Both water-dispersing systems and silt traps

Behind these self-perpetuating walls, soil deepens and trees can be planted or crops grown. The system is cheap, effective, and provides mulch. Yuccas, agaves, and aloes may provide similar structural effects in desert areas.

### Windbreak Design

Primary tall windbreaks of Araucaria, Cupressus, Casuarina, Pinus, hardy Phoenix palms, and even mangroves may be needed in front-line locations, followed in the lee by hardy quickset species such as Euphorbia tirucalli, Coprosma repens, and Echium fastuosum. Local plant species that perform well in the district should always be preferred.

### Protective Hedging

Many desert species serve as protective hedges around compounds: *Euphorbia tirucalli* provides effective protection and can be propagated from cuttings even in arid conditions. *Acacia tortilis* is ideal for cut-and-build fences. When cutting Euphorbia, eye protection with goggles and skin coverage are necessary due to the irritating milky sap.

## Elements of a Village Complex

Based on traditional and modern villages in the humid tropics, a complex can be built from the following checklist:

- **Housing:** Well-arranged array of housing, often grouped around a compound
- **Compound facilities:** Processing and storage areas, threshing floors, dancing areas, meeting house, cooperative or retail store, bulk fuel depots, firewood and mulch depots
- **Water supply:** Well, piped water, tanks, or dam — sited and integrated for clean water
- **Nursery:** Plant nursery to serve gardens and forest
- **Home gardens:** 0.25-1.0 hectare per house, based on self-reliance
- **Domestic animal ranges:** Chickens, guinea pigs, ducks, rabbits, small pigs, pigeons at garden borders — these serve as manure resources for the home garden
- **Fish ponds:** In areas of plentiful water, fish ponds with some animals housed over them
- **Fuelwood plantation:** Sector or zone integrated with windbreak
- **Livestock yards:** Stockyard and sheds for cattle and pigs at commercial level
- **Tree polyculture:** Careful zonation of tree polyculture
- **Special facilities:** Log-trimming or boat-building area, fish net drying racks, canoe or small boat landing, wharf, freezer, solar pond installation, power house, community drying shed, craft work areas, vehicle or draught-animal park area

## The Ohana System

The Hawaiian ohana (Figure 10.28 in the source) illustrates the integrated model on one radial valley of a volcanic island:

- **A:** "Taboo" forest of upper slopes — protected from clearing
- **B:** Stream diversion to ridge or high terraces, main village
- **C:** Sacrificial forests of palm to modify hurricane damage
- **D:** Fish ponds to retain leached nutrients, growing mullet and shellfish
- **E:** Reef modified with coral blocks to shelter crayfish and fish

This system represents thousands of years of refinement, where each element serves multiple functions and waste from one component becomes the resource for another.

## Evolving a Polyculture

When establishing permaculture on grasslands or compacted soils, the first step is thorough site planning, followed by ripping, swaling, pitting, or damming every area to be planted. This ensures maximum wet-season soil water storage to carry over to dry periods. The process should commence at the highest point of the property and around the house or village site.

### Initial Planting Strategy

On loosened soils and in mulched swales, a co-planted mix includes:
- **Tree legumes** at 3 × 3 meter spacing
- **Bananas** at 2 × 2 meter spacing
- **Fruits** at 5 × 8 meter spacing
- **Palms** at 10 × 10 meter spacing
- **Smaller species** as gap fillers at 1-1.5 meter spacing

Larger planting holes are seeded with nasturtium, Dolichos, Haifa clover, broad bean (fava), buckwheat, Umbelliferae (dill, fennel), lupin, vetch (hairy or woolly), dun peas, chilies, pigeon pea, or any useful non-grass mix available and suitable to climate and landscape.

### Cover and Succession

The aim is to completely carpet and overshade the ground within the first 18-20 months of growth. Dense plantings should be grass or hay mulched using monsoon grasses, swamp grasses, and later the tops of arrowroot, comfrey, banana, and Acacia. Shade-loving species such as coffee and dry taro can be placed in open spots as the canopy develops.

Paths for access, openings for annual crops, bee plants on edges, flowers, and fire-resistant "wet" ground covers such as comfrey, Tradescantia, Impatiens, and succulents are placed progressively, while fruit trees are kept free of grass and mulched by cutting out crowded Acacia and banana as mulch.

### The Quarter-Hectare Principle

It is far better to occupy a quarter hectare thoroughly than to scatter trees over 2 hectares. Production is higher, maintenance is less, moisture is conserved, and frost is excluded in the intensively managed smaller area.

## Key Principles

1. **Hill-to-shore integration** — Every landscape element from skyline to shore plays a defined role
2. **Keypoint management** — The critical topographic point where water can be diverted determines the pattern of settlement and cultivation
3. **Shore preservation** — Coastal buffers of 100+ meters provide windbreak, stability, and cloud generation
4. **Lagoon and estuary aquaculture** — Marine impoundments fix nutrients and produce food
5. **Erosion control through vegetation** — Contour planting with grasses and perennials is cheaper and more effective than engineering alone
6. **Village-scale integration** — Housing, animals, water, and crops form a single interdependent system
7. **Intensive establishment** — A thoroughly managed quarter hectare outperforms a scattered two hectares
8. **Ground cover urgency** — Complete soil coverage within 18-20 months prevents erosion and conserves moisture
