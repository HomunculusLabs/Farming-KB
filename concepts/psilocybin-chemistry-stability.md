## Psilocybin Chemistry and Stability

The psychoactive compounds in neurotropic fungi are primarily indole alkaloids structurally related to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT). Understanding their chemistry, biosynthesis, and degradation is essential for [[non-psilocybin-neurotropic-fungi-and-ergot-alkaloids]] serves as a stable precursor that is enzymatically dephosphorylated in the body to produce the active compound psilocin. Psilocybin is more stable in air and is water-soluble, making it the predominant form in dried fungal tissue.

### Psilocin (4-HO-DMT)

Psilocin, or 4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, is the pharmacologically active compound. It acts on the serotonin system as a 5-HT2A post-synaptic agonist or partial agonist. Psilocin is less stable than psilocybin and is the compound responsible for the characteristic bluing reaction when fungal tissue is bruised or damaged — the blue color results from oxidation of psilocin and related hydroxylated indole compounds.

### Intraspecies Variation

Within a single species, potency is affected by:

- **Genetics** — different strains and collections show variable alkaloid profiles.
- **Growing conditions** — substrate composition, temperature, humidity, and light exposure all influence biosynthesis.
- **Age at harvest** — younger specimens are significantly more potent than mature ones (discussed below).
- **Part of the fungus** — alkaloid distribution is not uniform throughout the fruiting body.

### Indigenous Knowledge of Degradation

Mexican Indian users of sacred mushrooms possess traditional knowledge about specimen age that aligns with modern chemical findings. They say that old dried specimens kept for more than one year "are not good to use" and discard them. This empirical knowledge likely developed over centuries of use and represents an early understanding of alkaloid stability.

### Volatility of Active Compounds

Psilocybin and psilocin appear to be at least partially volatile. Guzmán experienced colored hallucinations without consuming any fungi while sleeping in a small room filled with fresh and semifermented specimens of multiple Psilocybe species (P. caerulescens, P. cordispora, P. cubensis, P. mexicana, and P. zapotecorum) that had been collected for commercial purposes. The room had a strong acrid fungal aroma. The hallucinations stopped upon leaving the room and breathing fresh air.

This observation has implications for storage, drying, and the interpretation of historical accounts of mushroom potency.
