# Root Cellaring Guide

A root cellar is a cold-storage structure that uses the natural insulating properties [[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-exopheromones-and-plant-communication]] earth to maintain consistent [[pf-tek-fruiting-chamber-temperature-and-heating-principles]] humidity for long-term food storage. [[comparison-root-cellaring-guide-vs-canning-and-preserving]] [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-fungal-dominant-and-bacterial-dominant-compost-tea]] simplest and most energy-efficient method for preserving fall harvests of root crops, hardy vegetables, and fruits through winter without electricity.

## Ideal Conditions

Different crops have different storage requirements. Understanding these ranges is essential for successful root cellaring.

### Temperature and Humidity by Crop

| Crop | Temperature (F) | Humidity (%) | Storage Duration |
|------|----------------|-------------|-----------------|
| Potatoes | 38-42 | 85-90 | 4-6 months |
| Carrots | 32-40 | 90-95 | 4-6 months |
| Beets | 32-40 | 90-95 | 3-5 months |
| Turnips | 32-40 | 90-95 | 4-5 months |
| Parsnips | 32-40 | 90-95 | 2-4 months |
| Onions | 32-40 | 60-70 | 6-8 months |
| Garlic | 32-40 | 60-70 | 6-8 months |
| Winter squash | 50-55 | 50-70 | 3-6 months |
| Sweet potatoes | 55-60 | 80-85 | 3-5 months |
| Apples | 30-40 | 85-90 | 3-6 months |
| Cabbage | 32-40 | 90-95 | 3-4 months |

Note: Onions, garlic, and winter squash require lower humidity and should not be stored [[query-what-are-the-benefits-of-straw-mulch-in-the-garden-and-how-do-i-use-it]] same area as root vegetables. Potatoes and apples should not be stored together -- apples release ethylene gas which causes potatoes to sprout.

## Root Cellar Design Types

### Excavated Root Cellar

The traditional root cellar is dug into a hillside or underground. The earth provides natural insulation, keeping temperatures cool in summer and above freezing in winter.

Key design elements:
- Depth: At least 4-6 feet below grade for earth insulation
- Ventilation: Two vents -- one high (exhaust) and one low (intake) for air circulation. Close both in extreme cold.
- Drainage: Sloped floor with gravel base or drain tile to prevent water accumulation
- Door: Insulated, weather-stripped door facing north to minimize solar heat gain
- Walls: Concrete block, stone, or treated wood
- Ceiling: Well-insulated (R-20+) to prevent heat loss from above
- Floor: Dirt or gravel (concrete can be too damp and cold)

### Basement Root Cellar

An exterior basement room can function [[query-can-bamboo-be-used-as-a-building-material-and-how-do-i-get-started]] root cellar if it meets temperature and humidity requirements. The room should have:
- Exterior walls (exposed to earth on at least two sides)
- A window or vent for temperature control
- A door that seals well
- Humidity management (damp sand floor or humidifier for high-humidity crops)

### Above-Ground Cold Storage

For sites without excavation potential, an insulated outdoor structure can work. Use straw bale walls (R-30+ insulation) with a buried or earth-bermed exterior. More challenging to maintain consistent temperatures.

## Construction Considerations

### Site Selection

- North-facing slope or shaded location
- Well-drained soil (sandy loam is ideal; avoid heavy clay that holds water)
- Above the water table
- Close to the house for winter access
- Away from trees whose roots could damage walls

### Ventilation

Proper ventilation is the most critical design element. Without it, ethylene gas and CO2 build up, accelerating spoilage. Install two 4-inch PVC pipes:

- Intake vent: Low on the wall near the floor, draws fresh cool air in
- Exhaust vent: High on the opposite wall near the ceiling, lets warm stale air out
- Both vents should have closable dampers for temperature regulation

### Temperature Control

Monitor temperature with a max-min thermometer. Adjust ventilation:
- Open vents at night in fall to pull cold air in, close during the day
- Close vents when temperatures drop below 32 F to prevent freezing
- Open vents on warm winter days to prevent overheating

### Humidity Management

- High humidity (90-95%): For root vegetables. Maintain by dampening gravel floors, placing pans of water, or using a humidifier.
- Low humidity (60-70%): For onions, garlic, and squash. Provide better ventilation or a separate, drier storage area.

## Harvesting for Storage

Proper harvesting is as important as proper storage:

- Harvest root crops after the first light frost for best sugar development
- Handle all crops gently -- bruises and cuts lead to rot
- Do not wash root vegetables before storage (brush off soil instead; moisture promotes rot)
- Cure potatoes by laying in a warm, dark, well-ventilated area for 10-14 days to toughen skins
- Cure onions and garlic by drying in a warm, dry, ventilated area for 2-3 weeks until necks are tight and papery
- Remove all damaged or diseased specimens -- one rotten item can spoil the batch

## Storage Methods

### Bulk Storage

Root vegetables can be stored in bulk in bins, crates, or mounds. Layer with damp sand, sawdust, or straw between layers to maintain humidity and prevent contact between vegetables (which spreads rot).

- Sand/sawdust method: Alternate 2-inch layers of vegetables with 1-inch layers of damp sand
- Crate method: Fill wooden crates with a single layer, stack with airspace between
- Pallet method: Store in mesh bags or open crates on pallets above the floor

### Hanging Storage

Onions, garlic, and braided corn can be hung from rafters for maximum airflow.

## Monitoring and Maintenance

- Check the root cellar weekly during storage season
- Remove any spoiled items immediately
- Monitor temperature and humidity daily during fall [[ice-house-and-spring-house]] transition periods
- Check for rodents -- set traps or use wire mesh over vents
- Ensure vents are clear of debris and frost

## Common Problems

- Freezing: Temperature dropped below 28 F. Improve insulation and close vents.
- Shriveling: Humidity too low. Increase humidity sources.
- Rotting: Humidity too high, poor ventilation, or damaged produce. Remove affected items and improve air circulation.
- Sprouting: Temperature too warm or ethylene exposure. Lower temperature and separate crops.
- Mold: Poor ventilation or excessive humidity. Increase airflow.
