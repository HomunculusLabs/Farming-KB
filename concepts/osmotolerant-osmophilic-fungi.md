## Overview

Osmotolerant and osmophilic fungi are defined by their ability to grow on
substrata whose low water activities result from osmotic effects -- high
concentrations of sugars, salts, or other solutes. This ecological group is
distinct from xerophilic/xerotolerant fungi, which tolerate low water
activity from low matrix potential rather than osmotic stress.
The water-activity value (aw) ranges from 0 to 1, with most
microorganisms restricted to aw >= 0.95. Osmophilic fungi can grow at
significantly lower aw values.

## Water Activity and Osmotic Stress

High osmotic concentrations outside a cell cause it to lose water,
potentially killing the fungus or preventing
[[growing-gourmet-spore-germination-strain-isolation]]. The ability to
preserve foods with concentrated sugar or salt solutions is based on this
principle.
Environments with naturally high salt concentrations (salt marshes, saline
soils, seawater -- average 0.5 mol/kg NaCl, aw 0.981) have restricted
microbial communities. Only a few
[[halotolerant-halophilic-fungi-saline-environments]] can grow in saturated
NaCl solution (6 mol/kg, aw 0.75).

## Mechanisms of Osmotolerance

Fungi employ several physiological strategies to survive and grow under
osmotic stress:

### Compatible Solutes

The primary mechanism of osmotolerance is intracellular accumulation of
compatible solutes -- [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]]
that do not interfere with normal metabolic functions even at high
concentrations:

- **Glycerol:** The most common compatible solute in fungi, accumulated
  via the HOG (High Osmolarity Glycerol) MAP kinase pathway. Glycerol
  concentrations in osmophilic fungi can reach molar levels, balancing
  external osmotic pressure without disrupting enzyme function. The HOG
  pathway senses osmotic stress through membrane receptors and activates
  transcription factors that upregulate glycerol biosynthesis genes
  (GPD1, GPD2).

- **Erythritol and arabitol:** Polyols produced by some osmophilic
  species as supplementary compatible solutes. *Zygosaccharomyces rouxii*
  accumulates significant erythritol alongside glycerol.

- **Trehalose:** A non-reducing disaccharide that stabilizes proteins and
  membranes under osmotic and thermal stress. While not a primary
  osmolyte in most species, it plays a critical role in spore
  desiccation tolerance and stress recovery.

### Ion Transport and Regulation

[[osmophilic-osmotolerant-fungi-low-water-activity]] maintain ionic
homeostasis through regulated ion transport systems. Under salt stress,
Na+/H+ antiporters in the plasma membrane actively extrude sodium ions
while the vacuolar Na+/H+ antiporter sequesters excess sodium into the
vacuole. Potassium uptake systems (Trk transporters) help maintain
favorable K+/Na+ ratios essential for enzyme function.

## Key Genera and Species

Important osmophilic fungi are confined to genera including:
*Aspergillus*, *Aureobasidium*, *Chrysosporium*, *Cladosporium*,
*Fusarium*, *Geotrichum*, *Myrothecium*, *Oidiodendron*, *Paecilomyces*,
*Penicillium*, *Rhizopus*, *Scopulariopsis*, *Trichoderma*, *Wallemia*,
and *Xeromyces* (Pitt 1981; Pitt and Hocking 1997).

### Aspergillus

Several Aspergillus species are notable osmophiles. *Aspergillus niger*
grows at aw as low as 0.77 and is a common contaminant of dried fruits,
jams, and confectionery. *Aspergillus restrictus* and *Aspergillus
penicillioides* are extreme xerophiles capable of growth below aw 0.75,
with *A. penicillioides* reported at aw 0.73.
These species are among the most desiccation-tolerant fungi known and
frequently dominate the mycobiota of stored grains and dried foods.

### Penicillium

*Penicillium* species are significant osmotolerant spoilage organisms.
*Penicillium chrysogenum* and *Penicillium roqueforti* can grow at aw
values around 0.80-0.82. *Penicillium citrinum* is frequently isolated
from high-sugar foods and can produce mycotoxins (citrinin) under these
conditions, raising food safety concerns.

### Zygosaccharomyces

*Zygosaccharomyces* species are osmophilic yeasts of major industrial
significance. *Z. rouxii* (syn. *Saccharomyces rouxii*) is essential in
soy sauce fermentation, tolerating salt concentrations up to 18-24% and
high sugar environments. *Z. bailii* is notorious as a spoilage yeast in
acidic foods, fruit juices, and condiments due to its exceptional
tolerance to weak-acid preservatives combined with osmotolerance.
*Debaryomyces hansenii* is halotolerant rather than sugar-tolerant,
thriving in salted fish and cheese environments.

## Ecological Niches

### Honey and High-Sugar Foods

Honey (aw 0.55-0.70) is naturally resistant to most microbial spoilage but
can support growth of osmophilic yeasts such as *Zygosaccharomyces* and
osmophilic *Aspergillus* species if moisture content increases through
absorption. Dried fruits (aw 0.60-0.75), candied products, and syrups are
also selective habitats favoring osmophilic species.

### Hypersaline Environments

Salterns, salt lakes, and marine environments support halophilic and
halotolerant fungi. *Wallemia sebi* is frequently isolated from salted
foods and hypersaline soils. *Hortaea werneckii*, a black yeast, can grow
across an exceptionally broad salinity range (0-32% NaCl) and dominates
microbial communities in some hypersaline environments.

### Desert Soils

In a study of desert soil in Saudi Arabia, 100 species belonging to 38
genera were isolated on 40-60% sucrose Czapek's agar. Although the number
of colonies per gram was low, species richness was high. *Aspergillus* was
the most common genus isolated (Abdel-Hafez 1982b).

## Spoilage of Low-Water-Activity Foods

Foods with low aw subject to fungal spoilage include salted fish, jams,
jellies, and candy. The fungal species responsible are predominantly
ascomycetes or their anamorphs (Pitt and Hocking 1997).
*Xeromyces bisporus* is the most xerophilic fungus known, with a minimum
aw for growth of approximately 0.61, and is a critical spoilage organism
for dried foods and confectionery. These spoilage dynamics intersect with
