# Common Names and Epithets for Magic Mushrooms in Australia and New Zealand

## Introduction

Most recreational users of psychoactive visionary mushrooms have very little
knowledge of their scientific names. Instead, they have given their favorite
species local epithets which are commonly used by those who collect and ingest
them. Some of these popular names are also known and applied by users outside
of Australia and New Zealand. The study of these common names provides insight
into the [[mushroom-folklore-and-culture]] surrounding psychoactive fungi in
the Australasian region.

## "Magic Mushrooms"

The term "Magic Mushrooms" is the most common and widely applied epithet for
any mushroom which contains psilocybine and/or psilocine. The term was coined
by a Life Magazine editor in 1957, in connection with [[gordon-wasson-mushroom-discovery]] on the ceremonial use of hallucinogenic mushrooms among the
Mazatec Indians of Southern Mexico (see Wasson, 1957). In New Zealand, the term
"magic mushroom" is certainly more popular than any other term used in
identifying psilocybian mushrooms.

## Gold Tops and Gold Caps

[[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]] is known in Australia as "golden tops," "gold tops," or
sometimes "gold caps." These Australian epithets may have originated with
members of a local drug-using group of surfers who frequented the Gold Coast
region of eastern Australia. However, some of these names have apparently been
used to describe several different species of Psilocybe by users in Australia
(see Allen, 1997). It is important to note that [[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]] is not
known to occur naturally in New Zealand.

Those who ingest [[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]] -- known in both Australia and New
Zealand as "blue meanies" -- also refer to this species as "Blue Legs," "golden
tops," or "gold caps." The latter two nicknames, as well as "dimple tops" and
"cone heads," are common terms applied to [[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]] in the
Hawaiian Islands. Some of these same popular names have also been used by
visiting surfers from both New Zealand and Australia to describe the
macroscopic characteristics of the species.

The terms "gold tops" or "golden caps" often refer to the color of the pileus
(cap) of both [[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]] and [[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]], which are
hygrophanous -- meaning they change color as they dry.

## Blue Meanies

The epithet "blue meanies" refers primarily to [[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]] and
carries a dual significance:

1. The relative potency of the species and the intense blue oxidation
   (indicating the presence of psilocine) caused by damage to the cap and/or
   stem when the mushroom has been bruised by human handling

2. A cultural reference to the Beatles' animated film "Yellow Submarine,"
   which featured characters called "Blue Meanies"

The term is used in both Australia and New Zealand, though it is occasionally
applied in the Hawaiian Islands as well. The blue bruising reaction is a
characteristic shared by many psilocybian species and is discussed in the
context of [[metol-testing-bluing-reaction-mushrooms]].

## Liberty Cap (Psilos)

In the United States, Canada, Great Britain, and Europe, [[psilocybe-semilanceata-profile]]
is often referred to as the "liberty cap" (see Allen 1997a and 1997b) and is
also known to many Europeans simply as "Psilos." In New Zealand, this term has
not been widely adopted. Many New Zealand users knew that "gold tops" referred
to an "Australian" mushroom ([[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]]) which was different from
those used in New Zealand. See [[liberty-cap-british-discovery-1970s]] for the British
history of the liberty cap.

## Surfer Culture and Mushroom Terminology

There is a significant connection between surf culture and magic mushroom use in
both Australia and New Zealand. Many visiting surfers from both countries have
reportedly ingested mushrooms prior to surfing, as do many locally based surfers
in Australia and NZ. Surfing subculture at the Gold Coast region of eastern
Australia appears to have been a major vector for the spread of mushroom
knowledge and terminology.

An Australian physician suggested that the general public in Australia, as well
as members of its drug-using subculture, first became aware of the visionary
properties of psychoactive mushrooms through visiting surfers who came from
either New Zealand or the United States (Hawaii). These surfers most likely
provided ethnomycological information to local Australian surfers (McCarthy,
1971).

## Regional Terminology Differences

There are notable differences between Australian and New Zealand mushroom
terminology:

- In Australia: "gold tops" and "blue meanies" are the most common terms
- In New Zealand: "magic mushrooms" is the most popular umbrella term, with
  "blue meanies" occasionally used for [[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]]
- Neither the Australian "gold tops" nor the English/American "liberty caps"
  has been widely adopted in New Zealand

## Impact of Media on Terminology

The media has played a significant role in popularizing these terms. A 1972
newspaper report from Brisbane described how "children at a suburban school are
getting high on mushrooms called 'Gold Tops.' The mushrooms are common along the
Brisbane River near Toowing High School, and children in search of 'kicks'
have been experimenting with them." Such reports simultaneously popularized
the terminology and provided location information to would-be foragers.
