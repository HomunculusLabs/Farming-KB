# Shanin — *Petunia violacea*

## Overview

Shanin is the common name for *Petunia violacea*, a South American plant reportedly used by indigenous peoples in Ecuador to induce the sensation of flight. It is one of the most recently reported hallucinogenic plants from the New World and remains poorly characterized chemically and pharmacologically. Despite its widespread horticultural popularity as a garden flower, the psychoactive properties attributed to it in indigenous practice have received remarkably little scientific attention.

## Taxonomy and Distribution

*Petunia violacea* is one of approximately 40 species of petunias, which grow across South America and in warmer parts of North America. The genus *Petunia* belongs to the nightshade family, Solanaceae, and is closely allied to the genus *Nicotiana* (tobacco). This taxonomic placement is significant, as the Solanaceae is well recognized for containing numerous species with toxic and psychoactive properties, including *Datura*, *Brunfelsia*, *Latua*, and others used as hallucinogens by indigenous South American peoples.

*Petunia violacea* and several other species are horticulturally important. Cultivated varieties, with their attractive funnel-shaped blooms, are popular garden flowers that bloom profusely throughout the summer months. The ornamental petunias sold commercially are hybrids and selections derived primarily from *P. axillaris* and *P. integrifolia*, with *P. violacea* contributing to some breeding lines.

## Indigenous Use

The indigenous peoples of Ecuador reportedly take shanin to induce the sensation of flight. This specific effect—the subjective experience of levitation or aerial movement—is unusual among hallucinogenic plants and distinguishes shanin from most other known psychoactive species, which typically produce visual hallucinations, altered perception, or delirium rather than a focused somatic sensation.

Unfortunately, detailed ethnographic documentation of shanin use is sparse. The original reports come from ethnobotanical fieldwork in the Ecuadorian highlands, where the plant was identified as a component of local psychoactive practice. It is unclear whether shanin is used alone or in combination with other plants, what parts of the plant are employed, how they are prepared, or what the broader ritual context of its use might be.

## Chemistry

An alkaloid of unknown identity has been reported from *Petunia violacea*, but the compound has not been characterized and its pharmacological properties remain undetermined. Given the plant's placement in the Solanaceae, it is plausible that the active constituents may include tropane alkaloids (such as hyoscyamine, scopolamine, or atropine) or other biodynamic compounds common to the nightshade family.

Phytochemical investigation of petunias is urgently needed. The genus has been studied primarily from a horticultural perspective—breeding, cultivation, and ornamental characteristics—while its phytochemistry has been largely neglected. Modern analytical techniques could readily identify and characterize the alkaloid(s) responsible for the reported psychoactive effects.

## Pharmacological Context

Within the Solanaceae, several genera are known to produce hallucinogenic effects through tropane alkaloids:

- **Datura species** (*D. stramonium*, *D. inoxia*, *D. suaveolens*, and others): Contain hyoscyamine and scopolamine, producing characteristic deliriant intoxication.
- **Brunfelsia species** (*B. grandiflora*, *B. chiricaspi*): Used as hallucinogenic additives in South American ceremonial drinks.
- **Latua pubiflora**: The "sorcerers' tree" of Chile, containing hyoscyamine and scopolamine.
- **Iochroma fuchsioides**: Suspected hallucinogen of the Sibundoy Valley in Colombia.

The reported sensation of flight induced by shanin does not clearly align with the typical tropane alkaloid intoxication profile, which is more commonly characterized by delirium, visual disturbances, dry mouth, tachycardia, and amnesia. This discrepancy suggests that either the active compound(s) in *Petunia violacea* differ from the classical tropane alkaloids, or that the reported effects reflect a specific cultural interpretation of a more general altered state.

## Related Species

Other members of the Solanaceae used as hallucinogens by South American indigenous peoples include:

- **Borrachera** (*Iochroma fuchsioides*): Used in the Sibundoy Valley of Colombia in hallucinatory drinks.
- **Arbol de los Brujos** (*Latua pubiflora*): Used by Mapuche medicine men of Chile to cause delirium and hallucinations.
- **Chiric-Caspi** (*Brunfelsia grandiflora*): Used by the Kachinaua of Brazil as a source of hallucinogenic drink.
- **Culebra Borrachera** (*Methysticodendron amesianum*): An aberrant tree-form Datura from Sibundoy with extremely potent tropane alkaloid content.

## Research Gaps

Shanin represents a significant gap in ethnopharmacological knowledge. Key unanswered questions include:

- What is the identity and structure of the alkaloid(s) reported from *P. violacea*?
- What is the complete phytochemical profile of the species?
- Are other *Petunia* species psychoactive?
- What is the full ethnographic context of indigenous use in Ecuador?
- How do the reported effects compare pharmacologically to other Solanaceous hallucinogens?
- Could the sensation of flight be a culturally specific interpretation, or does it reflect a distinct pharmacological mechanism?
