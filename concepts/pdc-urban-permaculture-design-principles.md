# Urban Permaculture Design Principles

The history of urban design -- from LeCorbusier's skyscraper city centers to Levittown's suburban sprawl -- created a resource hyper-consumptive system reliant on the automobile that destroyed both food security and local ecologies. Working together, the International Style of architecture, LeCorbusier-inspired city centers, and the Levittown model of suburban expansion created a system that destroyed local food resources, chewed up ecosystems that ringed the cities, and caused a cultural degeneration. Permaculture Design offers principles to retrofit and redesign urban environments toward ecological health.

## Urban Forests

Urban forestry is the planting of indigenous trees in an effort to rejuvenate something close to the original forest conditions before urbanization. With proper design the trees and forest can be interwoven with urban infrastructures. Trees can line the roads, they can be planted to delineate property lines. Around the bases of the trees we can recreate the layers of the forest, planting shrubs and ground covers around their bases.

Urban forests provide cooling, increase air quality, block wind which aids maintaining a comfortable temperature, help naturally clean runoff water before it percolates back into the environment, and reduce storm water runoff. Urban food forests can also be created, combining permaforestry concepts with food production to yield edible resources.

The designer must choose climate-appropriate trees. Trees not suited for the climate have a much greater chance of becoming sick or fragile and becoming hazards than trees indigenous to the environment. While an urban forest will never reach the richness of a true climax forest, urban forestry restores some habitat, species diversity and ecological health to the urban landscape.

## Zones in Urban Environments

Be very careful when making Zones in an urban environment. Many neophyte Permaculture Designers believe every Zone analysis has to have all five zones, which is not true. In dense urban environments a typical yard may only have a Zone 0 and Zone 1. Perhaps Zone 2 and 3, but rarely Zone 4 and never Zone 5.

Larger urban neighborhoods may have up to Zone 4, but the odds of there being a Zone 5 -- true wilderness -- in an urban area are pretty much zero. Urban designers should adapt the zone model to fit the reality of the site rather than forcing a complete five-zone model where it does not apply.
