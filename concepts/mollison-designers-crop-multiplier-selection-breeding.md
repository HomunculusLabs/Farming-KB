# Crop Multiplier Selection Breeding

Crop multiplier selection is a practical plant breeding approach described by
Bill Mollison in the [[permaculture-designers-manual-bamboo]] that empowers
gardeners and farmers to develop locally adapted crop varieties through
systematic selection and seed saving. Rather than depending on commercial
seed suppliers, permaculture practitioners observe their growing conditions,
identify the best-performing individual plants, and save seed exclusively
from these elite performers to progressively improve their crop populations
over successive generations.

## Philosophical Foundation

Mollison frames crop multiplier selection as an expression of [[permaculture-ethics]] in action. By selecting and saving seed from the best plants adapted
to local conditions, growers develop varieties that become increasingly
resilient to local pests, diseases, climate patterns, and soil types. This
decentralised approach to plant breeding maintains genetic diversity
and reduces dependence on industrial seed supply chains. Over time, each
farm or garden develops its own unique, locally optimised crop populations
that outperform commercial varieties in their specific context.

## Principles of Mass Selection

Mass selection is the simplest and most accessible form of plant breeding.
The basic process involves:

1. **Grow a large population:** Plant enough individuals of each crop to
   ensure meaningful genetic variation. Populations of 50 to several
   hundred plants provide adequate diversity for effective selection.

2. **Rogue out inferior plants:** Remove plants that show poor vigour,
   disease susceptibility, undesirable growth habits, or other negative
   traits before they can cross-pollinate with superior individuals.

3. **Select elite performers:** At harvest time, identify the individual
   plants that perform best according to the desired selection criteria.
   These may include highest yield, earliest maturity, pest resistance,
   drought tolerance, fruit quality, or any combination of traits.

4. **Save seed from the best:** Harvest seed exclusively from the selected
   elite individuals. Mix seeds from the best plants to maintain genetic
   diversity while shifting the population average toward desired traits.

5. **Repeat annually:** Each generation of selection moves the population
   further toward local adaptation and improved performance.

## Selection Criteria

Mollison emphasises that selection criteria should reflect the actual needs
and conditions of the growing site. In permaculture systems, selection
typically prioritises:

- **Adaptation to local climate:** Heat tolerance, cold hardiness, drought
  resistance, or tolerance of local rainfall patterns.
- **Pest and disease resistance:** Plants that remain healthy under local
  pest and disease pressure without chemical intervention.
- **Low-input performance:** Varieties that thrive under organic or
  minimal-input conditions rather than requiring high fertility or
  irrigation.
- **Yield stability:** Consistent production across variable seasons,
  rather than maximum yield under ideal conditions.
- **Multiple use:** Plants that provide food, fodder, mulch material, or
  other useful products simultaneously.

## Maintaining Genetic Diversity

A critical distinction between permaculture crop multiplication and
industrial plant breeding is the emphasis on maintaining genetic diversity
within crop populations. Mollison warns against over-selection, which narrows
the genetic base and increases vulnerability to novel stresses. Best practice
is to save seed from a minimum of 20 to 50 superior plants to preserve
sufficient diversity for ongoing adaptation.

This approach contrasts with the industrial model of developing uniform
pure-line varieties through repeated inbreeding and selection of single
plants. While pure lines maximise uniformity, they sacrifice the adaptive
capacity that broad-based populations retain. Permaculture crop populations
are dynamic, evolving systems rather than fixed varieties.

## Techniques for Different Crop Types

Selection methods vary depending on the crop's reproductive biology:

### Self-pollinating crops (tomatoes, beans, peas, wheat)

These crops are easier to maintain as distinct lines because cross-pollination
rates are low. Select the best individual plants, save their seed separately,
and grow them in isolation from other varieties of the same species to
maintain purity. Even small populations of 10 to 20 plants can be effectively
improved through selection.

### Cross-pollinating crops (squash, corn, brassicas, carrots)

These crops require larger populations (minimum 50 to 100 plants) to
maintain genetic health and avoid inbreeding depression. Isolation from
other varieties of the same species is essential if varietal purity is
desired. Alternatively, crops can be allowed to interbreed freely and
selected for broad adaptation across the resulting diverse population.

### Vegetatively propagated crops (potatoes, sweet potatoes, cassava)

Selection involves choosing the best-performing tubers, cuttings, or sets
from each harvest and using them as planting material for the next season.
Disease-free material should be prioritised. Over successive seasons,
the population adapts to local conditions while maintaining clonal identity.

### Perennial crops (fruit trees, berries)

Selection in perennials is slower but equally valuable. Identify the best-
performing individuals in orchards or hedgerows and propagate from them
by cuttings, grafting, division, or layering. [[seed-saving-vegetables]] from superior
perennials also produces new genetic combinations that may exceed parent
performance.

## Regional Adaptation

One of the most powerful outcomes of sustained crop multiplier selection is
the development of crop populations uniquely adapted to the microclimate,
soil, and management regime of a specific site. After 5 to 10 generations of
selection, locally developed populations typically outperform commercial
varieties by significant margins under local conditions. This is a form of
local adaptation that industrial breeding programmes cannot replicate
because they select under centralised research station conditions.

## Integration with Permaculture Systems

Crop multiplier selection integrates naturally with [[seed-saving-vegetables]], [[permaculture-zone-planning]], and community food systems. Mollison advocates for
community-scale seed exchange networks where locally selected varieties are
shared between growers, multiplying the adaptive benefits across wider
areas while maintaining local distinctiveness.
