# Acquired Tastes and the Chemical Addiction Syndrome

## Overview

In *Food of the Gods*, [[terence-mckenna]] develops a theory of "acquired tastes" that connects the
mundane phenomenon of learning to enjoy initially unpleasant foods with the deeper mechanisms of
chemical addiction. His central insight is that the process of acquiring a taste for a bitter or
otherwise aversive substance follows the same neurological pathway as the development of drug
dependence. This framework allows McKenna to place addiction within a broader evolutionary
context, suggesting that the human capacity to form chemical dependencies is not a modern
pathology but an ancient and once-adaptive feature of our relationship with the plant world.

## The Biology of Acquired Taste

Humans are both attracted and repelled by substances whose taste skirts the edges of
acceptability. Foods that are highly spiced, bitter, or aromatic arouse strong reactions. The
common expression that one must "acquire a taste" for such foods describes a genuine
neurological process. This is true of foods such as soft cheese or pickled eggs, but it is
even more true of drugs. The first cigarette or first shot of bourbon is, for most people,
an experience of an organism violently rejecting the acquisition of a particular taste.

Repetition of exposure is the key to acquiring a taste. This process is complex and involves
both behavioral and biochemical adaptation. Something foreign to the body is nevertheless
repeatedly introduced into it by conscious decision. The body adjusts to the new chemical
regimen and then does more than adjust: it accepts the new regimen as right and proper and
produces signals of alarm should the regimen be threatened.

## The Addiction Spectrum

McKenna traces a continuum from habit through obsession to addiction, describing these as
"signposts along a path of ever-decreasing free will." Habits are "a settled tendency or
practice," familiar, repetitious, and largely unexamined. They are simply the things that
we do. Culture itself is largely a matter of habit, learned from parents and those around us
and then slowly modified by shifting conditions and inspired innovations.

When habits consume us, when our devotion to them exceeds culturally defined norms, we label
them as obsessions. An obsession with the [[guzman-magic-mushrooms-chemical-constituents]] of foods and drugs, also
called metabolites, is labeled an addiction. McKenna notes that addictions and obsessions are
unique to human beings. While anecdotal evidence supports preference for intoxicated states
among elephants, chimpanzees, and some butterflies, these animal behaviors are enormously
different from the depth and complexity of human chemical dependence.

## The Body's Acceptance of the Chemical Regimen

The critical transition in the addiction process occurs when the body not only tolerates but
actively defends the presence of a foreign chemical. These signals of alarm when the chemical
environment is imperiled may be both psychological and physiological, and they manifest
whenever the new chemical environment within the body is threatened for any reason, including
a conscious decision to discontinue use.

This defensive response is what distinguishes true addiction from mere habit. The body has
reorganized its biochemical equilibrium around the presence of the foreign substance. The
withdrawal symptoms that accompany cessation are not simply the absence of a pleasurable
stimulus but the active disruption of a new homeostatic state that the body has constructed
around the drug. McKenna's framing of this process emphasizes its biological sophistication:
the body does not merely fail to resist the drug, it actively incorporates the drug into its
regulatory systems.
