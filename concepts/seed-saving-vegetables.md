# Seed Saving Vegetables

Practical guide to saving vegetable seeds for the home gardener, distilled from [[steve-solomon]]'s "[[gardening-when-it-counts]]." Home-garden seed production is one of the most valuable self-reliance skills, but it requires understanding the biology of each crop species.

## Self-Pollinating vs. Outcrossing Species

### Self-Pollinating Species (easy to save)
These species [[natural-farming-seed-saving|natural]] ly pollinate themselves before the flower opens, so seed saved from a single plant remains genetically true:

- Tomatoes
- Peppers
- Eggplants (aubergines)
- Lettuce
- Peas
- Beans (most common varieties)
- Tomatillos

Self-pollinating species are the best starting point for beginners. A small number of plants (even 10-20) can maintain genetic diversity.

### Outcrossing Species (require isolation)
These species are pollinated by insects or wind and will cross with other varieties of the same species:

- Brassicas (cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, radish, turnip)
- Corn (maize)
- Beets and chard
- Carrots
- Onions
- Squash and pumpkins
- Cucumbers
- Spinach

Saving seed from outcrossers requires either isolation distance (often 0.5-1 mile), staggered flowering times, or hand pollination with bagging. Working from too small a gene pool (too few plants) with outcrossers will ruin the variety within two generations.

## Drying Seed (Dry-Seeded Crops)

For beans, peas, and other crops that produce dry seed in pods:

1. Allow pods to dry on the plant until the stem end withers — this indicates the plant's sap is no longer flowing into the seed
2. At this point, any further nutrition added to the seed's food reserve comes from the pod itself as it withers
3. Pick each pod individually at optimal maturation for the highest quality seed
4. Finish drying in a large bowl or on screens in a dry, well-ventilated space
5. Seed is ready when a thumbnail cannot dent it

If ripening continues over weeks and drying seed is at risk of being remoistened, yank the entire plant, shake soil from roots, and move it under cover — spread on a tarp or hang upside down to catch scattering seeds.

## Wet Seed (Tomatoes, Melons, Squash)

The key to getting vigorous wet seed is to let the fruit become fully dead-ripe on the plant before harvesting — allow it to ripen nearly to the point of rotting:

- **Tomato**: Take seeds from overripe fruit. Let a bowl of soft ripe fruit sit on the counter for a few more days before extracting seed.
- **Melon**: Make sure the fruit chosen for seed honestly slips the vine.
- **Squash**: Allow the fully ripe fruit to cure for a month or so before extracting seed.

If the seed is not dry, it is moist. At high moisture contents and temperatures, seed ages rapidly. Dry it down as soon as possible after extraction.

## Hybrid Seeds: Myths and Realities

### Common anti-hybrid myths (and the truth)
Much anti-hybrid propaganda circulates. The claim that gardeners can't save seed from hybrids is a half-truth:

- **Self-pollinating species**: Hybrids are produced painstakingly by hand. Hybrid tomato seed wholesales for close to $1,000/lb because every flower must be hybridized before it self-pollinates. Hybrid solanums offer minor advantages in productivity and disease resistance but are not threatening OP variety availability.
- **Outcrossing species**: Breeders mass-produce hybrid seeds economically by creating "self-sterile parent lines" grown alongside a second uniform variety. Bees cross-pollinate them. The result is uniform, vigorous hybrid seed.

### Converting hybrids to OP varieties
It IS possible to breed an open-pollinated variety out of a hybrid, but it takes skill and multiple generations:

- Tim Peters bred OP broccoli from Green Valiant (a hybrid). The first generation from the self-sterile parent produced only a few hundred seeds. Subsequent generations set seed normally, and within a few generations a uniform OP variety resulted.
- Steve Solomon similarly bred an OP carrot from an overwintered hybrid. The key: plant the hybrid alongside an OP variety so bees can cross-pollinate. The first generation may be self-sterile, but the next will set seed normally.

### Warning on seed-saving scale
The average gardener saving seed for insect- or wind-pollinated varieties usually works from too small a gene pool (too few plants) and makes poor decisions about which plants to remove and which to let remain. The result: the variety is ruined within two generations. For this reason, starting with hybrid seed of outcrossers is not recommended for beginners unless collaborating with a network of seed savers.

### Sometimes hybrids are actually OP
Some seed sold as hybrid and priced at hybrid rates may actually behave as OP. When Solomon grew 100 plants of Kolibri F1 purple kohlrabi, it produced seed that grew kohlrabi as uniform, productive, and tasty as Kolibri F1 had been. Similar stories exist about so-called hybrid varieties.

## Space Requirements

Cabbage-family seed production requires roughly 4 sq ft (0.4 sq m) per plant. To maintain a variety properly, you need 200-400 plants — that's 800-1,600 sq ft (75-150 sq m). This is why seed saving for outcrossers is better suited to collaboration than individual gardeners.
