# Citric Acid and Chlorine Removal for AACT Water Treatment

## Summary

Chlorine and its derivatives in municipal water supplies are lethal to
the beneficial bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes that are the
primary value of actively aerated compost tea (AACT). While citric acid
can neutralize chlorine chemically, Ingham identifies aeration as the
preferred and most reliable method for dechlorination. Citric acid
has a secondary role in adjusting water pH but carries risks of
killing beneficial organisms if overdosed. Understanding the correct
approach to water treatment is essential for producing viable compost
tea. (see [[aact-brewing-methodology]]).

## Why Chlorine Kills Beneficial Microbes

Chlorine and hypochlorite compounds in municipal water are powerful
oxidizing agents. Ingham explains that "un-reacted oxygen radicals take
out the membranes of the bacteria, fungi and protozoa we want in the
tea." The chlorine damages the cell membranes of these organisms,
destroying their viability. This is by design -- municipal water
treatment intentionally kills microbes to ensure water safety for human
consumption. (see [[ingham-micronized-compost-aact-brewing-technique]]).

However, the organisms that make compost tea effective are precisely the
aerobic bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes that chlorine targets. (see [[compost-tea-brewing-methods]]).
Ingham's observations of fully chlorinated water on organisms in tea are
unequivocal: "Activity is destroyed." She cautions that anyone relying
on plate counts to assess the damage will miss most of the impact, since
"99.99% of the bacteria in soil, compost or compost tea won't grow on
plates." Plate counts measure viability, not activity, and "we need
active organisms in tea."

Most water treatment plants can provide data on chlorine concentration
kill rates of indicator microbes in water. The kill rates are
substantial and rapid, meaning that even brief exposure to chlorinated
water during the initial stages of brewing can devastate the microbial
communities you are trying to cultivate. (see [[compost-tea-recipe-guide]]).

## Citric Acid as a Chlorine Neutralizer

Ingham addresses citric acid directly: "I'm going to agree that citric
acid is not your best choice, or most effective method, of removing
chlorine." She explains the fundamental problem: "Citric acid works only
if you know how much chlorine you are working with in your water, and
you add enough to neutralize the chlorine in your water, which can be
quite variable."

Chlorine levels in municipal water fluctuate significantly by season,
time of day, and location. Ingham cites Las Vegas as having "some of the
highest chlorine levels in their water that I've ever experienced," and
notes that water in Toowoomba and Brisbane, Australia is "even more
chlorinated." Because the concentration is variable, it is nearly
impossible to dose citric acid accurately without testing each batch of
water. (see [[compost-tea-guide]]).

The risk of under-dosing is that residual chlorine will kill beneficial
organisms. The risk of overdosing is that citric acid itself "kills
SOME organisms" at higher concentrations. Ingham notes that citric acid
and citrus products can reduce E. coli and other human pathogens at low
concentrations, but the line between pathogen suppression and beneficial
organism damage is narrow and machine-dependent.

## Dosage Rates for pH Adjustment

While citric acid is not recommended as the primary chlorine removal
method, it does have a role in adjusting water pH for brewing. Ingham
provides specific guidance: "3 teaspoons of citric acid to drop 100 gal
of water one pH unit." Ferric citrate was the compound tested, but she
notes that Tang (a citrus-flavored drink powder containing citric acid)
"should give about the same results."

For international practitioners, she advises: "forget Tang. Get citric
acid, can be found in health food stores, or buy ferric citrate from a
chemistry supply." Catalogues like Sigma or Fisher carry ferric citrate.
A warning about Tang is important: "Tang also has some sugar in it,"
and "in mid-summer, addition of sugar may get the bacteria growing fast.
They may use up the oxygen in the water."

Vinegar is another option for pH adjustment, but "you would have to
test to determine how much vinegar would drop the pH per unit volume of
water." The safest approach is to add citric acid to the spray tank to
drop pH before adding the tea.

## Aeration: The Preferred Method

Ingham states unequivocally: "The best way to remove the chlorine is to
aerate the water and let the chlorine de-gas." This approach avoids the
uncertainty of chemical dosing entirely. When you aerate water, chlorine
gas (Cl2) evaporates from the water surface naturally.

Practical approaches include filling the sprayer with water that has
"aerated overnight," or putting water into the tank "after letting it
'fountain'" to drive off chlorine through agitation and air exposure.
Even water applied through irrigation can benefit from dechlorination:
"if the water flies through the air as small droplets before landing on
the plant, generally de-chlorination has occurred."

Ingham also notes that "your nose can be a good indicator of not-enough
de-gassing if you take just a little time to train your nose." The
characteristic chlorine smell indicates residual chlorine remains.

## Why Chemical Dechlorination Is Problematic

Ingham raises a broader concern about chemical approaches to chlorine
removal: "When you start playing water chemistry, it gets difficult. If
you add a chemical to complex the chlorine, what does that complexed
chlorine-chemical do to the bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes?" There
is a whole set of additional tests needed to determine the safety of
any chlorine-chemical complex on beneficial organisms.

This uncertainty extends to other chemical dechlorination methods. While
ascorbic acid (vitamin C) is sometimes used for dechlorination, Ingham
does not specifically endorse it in the Field Guide. The fundamental
principle remains: if you must add a chemical, you must test to confirm
that it does not harm the organisms you are trying to grow.

## Testing for Chlorine

Testing water for chlorine levels before brewing is strongly recommended.
Simple chlorine test kits are widely available and inexpensive. Test both
free chlorine and total chlorine, as some municipalities use chloramine
(a chlorine-ammonia compound) which is more stable and harder to remove
by aeration alone. For chloramine, extended aeration or activated
carbon filtration may be necessary.

## Alternatives to Citric Acid

The safest alternatives for water treatment include: aeration overnight
or through fountain/agitation methods; reverse osmosis for highly
chlorinated or otherwise contaminated water sources; activated carbon
filtration; and collecting rainwater or using well water when available.
For pH adjustment specifically, citric acid remains useful but should be
dosed carefully based on testing, not estimated.
