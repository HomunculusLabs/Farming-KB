## Clay Seed Pellet Composition and Aerial Seeding Method

The clay seed pellet (sometimes called a "seed ball" or "tsuchi dango") is [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] formed into spheres — that solves several problems simultaneously: seed predation, moisture retention, germination triggering, and distribution logistics.

## Core Composition

The clay seed pellet is composed of three essential ingredients in approximately equal proportions by volume.

### Clay

The clay serves as the protective outer shell and moisture buffer. It should be a relatively fine-grained clay — not gravelly or sandy — that forms a cohesive mass when wet and hardens to a durable crust when dried.

The clay should not be so dense that water cannot penetrate it at all. It needs to absorb moisture slowly from rainfall or dew and release it gradually to the seed inside.

Local clay is always preferred over imported material. The clay should ideally come from the target site or a nearby location with similar mineral composition. This ensures compatibility with the local soil chemistry and reduces the risk of introducing foreign minerals that might create unfavorable conditions for germination.

### Seeds

The seed mix is the biological core of the pellet. Fukuoka emphasized that seed selection should follow the principle of "many seeds, many varieties." A single pellet might contain seeds of 10 to 50 different species.

This diversity serves multiple purposes:

- Different species have different germination requirements, so the mixed pellet will produce seedlings across a range of conditions
- Species that fail to germinate in the current season remain dormant and may germinate in a future season when conditions are more favorable
- The resulting plant community is diverse from the start, which provides better ground cover, more rapid soil building, and greater resilience against [[clay-seed-pellets]] offer several decisive advantages:

- **No nursery phase**: Seeds go directly from pellet preparation to field deployment, eliminating months of nursery care
- **No irrigation**: Once distributed, pellets rely entirely on natural rainfall
- **No hole-digging**: Pellets rest on the soil surface; the seedling emerges and roots downward naturally
- **No ongoing maintenance**: The pellet's protective shell eliminates the need for protective fencing, watering, or pest control
- **Dramatically lower cost**: Labor and material costs are a fraction of conventional approaches
- **Species diversity**: A single pellet contains many species, producing diverse plant communities rather than monocultures
- **Scalability**: Aerial distribution can treat thousands of hectares in a single operation

## Limitations and Considerations

The method is not universally applicable. In areas with extremely low rainfall (true deserts receiving less than 100mm annually), even clay pellets may not provide sufficient moisture protection for germination. In areas with heavy clay or compacted soils, seedling roots may struggle to penetrate the surface.

In areas with severe ongoing disturbance (overgrazing, frequent fire, intensive agriculture), the emerging seedlings may be destroyed before they can establish.

Fukuoka acknowledged these limitations and emphasized that the clay seed pellet is a tool within a broader philosophy of working with nature, not a universal technological solution. It works best when combined with changes in land management practices that reduce ongoing disturbance.

## Scaling Production for Large Operations

For national-scale revegetation programs, hand-rolling individual pellets is impractical. Fukuoka described two approaches to scaling pellet production.

The first is a simple mechanical tumbler: a rotating drum that coats seeds with successive layers of clay-organic mixture, building up pellets of consistent size. A tumbler operated by a small team can produce thousands of pellets per day.

The second approach is to mix the clay-seed-organic paste at higher volume and use a simple extrusion device — essentially a screen or die through which the paste is forced, with blades cutting the extruded material into uniform segments. These segments are then tumbled to round them into spheres.

Both approaches require the same fundamental ingredients in the same proportions. The advantage of mechanization is consistency of pellet size and density, which leads to more uniform aerial distribution and more predictable germination rates.
