# Flood-Outs and Claypan Rehabilitation in Drylands

Dryland landscapes contain a complex array of degraded landforms
including flood-outs, claypans, saltpans, gilgais, and badlands, each
requiring specific rehabilitation approaches. Mollison provides
detailed strategies for restoring these features, connecting to
[[mollison-arid-lands-design-and-erosion-control]]
and [[mollison-designers-gabions-and-stone-weirs-for-gully-control]].

## Flood-Outs and Gully Formation

Also called flat-outs or run-ons, flood-outs are the ever-widening
flattish floors of valleys as they leave the hills. These "streams" are
only 2 to 8 cm deep and 20 m or more wide, braided, and stable. Water
absorbs over a wide area of such plains. Overgrazed, the streams
commonly first erode some deeper channels, then become steep gullies.

At this stage, diversion of water from gully heads, water spreading
from large canals, and gully planting must be applied. Small gullies
can be filled, and larger gullies planted behind stone walls or gabion
barricades once the feed water has been diverted or spread.

## Controlling Gullies

Controlling gullies is essentially a matter of relieving the causes,
usually those of overuse and lack of water control, and preventing
further cut-back. Fragile soils, loose shaley ground, and recent
sediments exposed to sudden overland flows may gully in a few
downpours.

Such areas need fencing out, light or no stocking of animals, pest
control, and above all, spreader banks for the water. In the valleys
above the gullies, stream flow can be diverted to flood spreader
channels, and a series of rip-lines drawn downhill from the gully bank
towards the ridges (sloping at 1:1000 or so).

## Claypans

Unlike scalds, claypans rarely overflow, but receive silty waters from
clay soils. After they pool for a while, clay settles out and water
evaporates. In the wet phase, tall canegrass (Eragrostis australisica)
can grow, duck and waterfowl nest, and as the swamps dry out,
marsupials and rodents take refuge.

These swamp areas were frequently burnt by Aborigines in autumn, or as
the canegrass dried out, but regenerated in wet periods. Non-saline
eroded claypans have been revegetated by pitting, by building flood
retention banks, and by chequerboard ridging.

A low bank of 0.5 to 1 m is sufficient to support adapted trees in
saltpan areas. The trees then assist desalination. Ploughed or ripped
clay allows faster water infiltration, and vegetation keeps the initial
crevices open if dry-leaf mulch species are avoided, and herbaceous or
needle-leaf groups substituted.

## Saltpans and Salt Lakes

Saltpans can hold many metres of water in floods. When this water
evaporates, great depths of salt may accumulate, sometimes with a thin
clay top. Vegetation of salt-tolerant plants occurs only on the
margins.

Salt enters the freshwater zone in sandy deserts and atolls from these
sources:

- Salt washed down by infiltration of water through saline surface
  sands
- Salt diffusing up from deep, heavily-salted waters (usually saltier
  than the sea)
- Salt advecting in rains from the sea or saltpans after rain, most
  pronounced if freshwater has been overdrawn from the ground
- Salt drawn up and in by forceful pumping locally, which then pollutes
  surface soils

## Gilgais

Gilgais are "puff and hollow" areas formed by patches of clay 3 to 5 m
across, which expand into 6 to 20 cm deep hollows on a "swell-shrink"
regime. The hollows occur in solonetic clay soils which shed their
water into the hollows. The hollows can link up in chains or patterns.

Where the area is overgrazed, the gilgais become sand-filled, the
solonetic clays wash away, and a series of sandy mounds evolve. The
area loses most of its plants and becomes unstable.

## Rehabilitation Strategies

Non-saline eroded claypans have been successfully revegetated by:

- Pitting the surface to create micro-catchments for seed and water
- Building flood retention banks at 0.5 to 1 m height
- Chequerboard ridging to spread water and trap seed
- Ripping compacted clay to allow faster water infiltration
- Selecting herbaceous or needle-leaf plant groups that keep
  infiltration crevices open
- Avoiding dry-leaf mulch species that seal the surface

## Integration with Stock Management

If we consider stocking on deserts, Mollison recommends having at
least one-fourth of the area free of stock at any one time, and letting
the area regenerate if rains occur. This may be assisted with spring
fire, pelleted seed, or broadscale water harvesting.

On the wider range, highly selected and controlled meat and milk flocks
also thrive if they can be herded or penned in about 15 rotated runs,
allowing 2 to 8 years for each run to recover and re-seed. Some
successful Australian sheep graziers allow 7 to 9 years rest per run
and never suffer the animal stress brought on by drought.

It is infinitely preferable to run small, high-value herds on copious
range than to risk the inevitable collapse of range and flocks by
stressing the vegetation. This connects to [[mollison-animal-range-management]] and [[mollison-rangelands-and-natural-productivity]].
