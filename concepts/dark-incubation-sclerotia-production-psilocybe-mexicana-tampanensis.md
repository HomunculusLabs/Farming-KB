# Dark Incubation Sclerotia Production in Psilocybe mexicana and P. tampanensis

## Overview

Sclerotia (singular: sclerotium) are dense, compact masses of mycelium that serve as survival structures for certain fungi during adverse environmental conditions. In *Psilocybe mexicana* and *Psilocybe tampanensis*, sclerotia are of particular interest because they contain the psychoactive alkaloids [[tryptamine-chemistry-psilocin-and-psilocybin-synthesis]] at concentrations comparable to or sometimes exceeding those of the mushroom fruit bodies. These sclerotia are commonly known as "magic truffles" or "philosopher's stones" in the commercial market. A key advantage of [[sclerotia-cultivation]] is that they form **entirely within sealed jars** during dark incubation, without any need for casing, fruiting chambers, or environmental controls — making them the simplest method for producing psychoactive fungal biomass.

## Species That Produce Sclerotia

Among the *Psilocybe* species, the following are known to form sclerotia:

- ***Psilocybe mexicana*:** The type species for psilocybin, first identified by Roger Heim in the late 1950s during his ethnobotanical investigations in Mexico. Heim's work with *P. mexicana* led directly to [[albert-hofmann]]'s isolation and identification of psilocybin and psilocin in 1958–1959. *P. mexicana* produces both mushrooms and sclerotia. The sclerotia tend to be small, firm, and dark brown. *P. mexicana* usually produces one large flush of mushrooms and a small second flush when cased, but the sclerotia pathway offers a more reliable and productive harvest. The species is subtropical in origin and prefers warm incubation temperatures (22–26 °C).
- ***Psilocybe tampanensis*:** Originally collected near Tampa, Florida (hence the name) by Steven Pollock and later described by Guzmán and Pollock in 1978, this species is particularly prolific in sclerotia production. The original Tampa specimen remains the only confirmed wild collection, and all cultivated material is descended from this single collection. sclerotia of *P. tampanensis* will continue to enlarge until approximately **4 months after inoculation** when grown on sterilised grass seed in jars. This extended growth period allows substantial biomass accumulation without any grower intervention beyond the initial inoculation. The sclerotia of *P. tampanensis* are typically larger than those of *P. mexicana* and have a smoother, more regular shape.
-psilocybe atlantis ntis* and *P. galindoi*:** Other species known to produce sclerotia, though less commonly cultivated than the two principal species. *P. galindoi* (sometimes considered synonymous with *P. mexicana* var. *galindoi*) is native to Mexico and produces sclerotia that are intermediate in size between *P. mexicana* and *P. tampanensis*. *P. atlantis*, reportedly from Fulton County, Georgia, USA, has been cultivated for both mushrooms and sclerotia but reliable genetic material can be difficult to obtain.

## Why Dark Incubation?

Sclerotia form as a response to nutrient accumulation and environmental stress. In nature, they allow the fungus to survive periods of drought, temperature extremes, or nutrient depletion. In cultivation, dark incubation p [[growing-gourmet-sclerotia-formation-biology-mushrooms]] ormation because:

1. **Absence of light cues:** Light is a primary trigger for fruiting (mushroom formation) in most agarics, including *Psilocybe* species. The mycelium senses light through blue-light receptors (homologous to the WC-1/WC-2 system in *Neurospora*) and initiates the geneti [[fruiting-body-development]] body development. By maintaining complete darkness, the mycelium is prevented from entering the reproductive fruiting cycle and instead channels metabolic energy into sclerotial biomass accumulation.
2. **Reduced gas exchange:** Sealed jars with loosened lids provide limited gas exchange, creating elevated CO₂ levels that favour vegetative (sclerotial) growth over reproductive (fruiting) development. High CO₂ environments suppress mushroom formation and encourage the compact hyphal aggregation that characterises sclerotial development.
3. **Stable moisture:** The sealed jar environment prevents moisture loss, maintaining optimal hydration for sustained mycelial growth over the extended incubation period of 3–4 months. This moisture stability is critical because sclerotia form gradually and require consistent hydration throughout their development.
4. **Nutrient density:** Grass seed provides a nutrient-rich substrate with readily available carbohydrates (starches and simple sugars in the endosperm) and proteins, allowing the mycelium to accumulate substantial reserves in the sclerotial tissue over time.

## Substrate: Grass Seed in Jars

The substrate for sclerotia production is sterilised grass seed in glass jars. Grass seed is an excellent substrate for sclerotia formation because the individual seeds provide numerous discrete colonisation points, and the gaps between seeds create air pockets that promote healthy mycelial growth. The standard formula for 720 ml jars is:

- **110 grams grass seed** (typically rye grass, *Lolium perenne*)
- **180 ml water**

The seed should **not** be treated with fungicides — seed sold for agricultural planting is often treated, while seed sold as animal feed (bird seed) from farm supply stores is typically untreated and inexpensive. Different varieties and even different batches of the same variety can differ greatly in their ability to absorb water, so some experimentation with the water-to-seed ratio may be necessary. Alternatively, the grass seed can be soaked in water overnight before loading into jars, which produces a more homogenous substrate.

The jars are fitted with lids that are **not screwed tight** — they must remain loose to allow gas exchange during colonisation. A double layer of aluminium foil is crumpled over the lid and the top part of the jar before sterilisation in a pressure cooker (1 hour for 720 ml jars, 2 hours for 1.5 litre jars or spawn bags).

## Colonisation and Sclerotia Development

After inoculation with spore syringe, mycelium syringe, or agar squares, the jars are shaken to distribute the inoculant. The lids are loosened (but left in place) and the jars are placed in a clean, draft-free location at room temperature (20 °C) or slightly higher. Key points during this phase:

- **Early colonisation:** When mycelium starts growing in only a few spots, the jars are shaken to redistribute colonised kernels, dramatically speeding up colonisation. Full colonisation of grass seed typically takes 5–20 days depending on temperature, species, and inoculation method.
- **Over-incubation warning:** When jars are incubated too long or at too high a temperature, the mycelium will excrete yellowish metabolites. This stress response indicates that the mycelium should be cased (for mushroom production) or that conditions need adjustment. For sclerotia production, this is less critical since the extended incubation is expected.
- **Sclerotia initiation:** After the grass seed is fully colonised, sclerotia begin to form as small, hard, white-to-brown nodules within the substrate. Over the following weeks and months, these nodules grow larger and darken.

## Timeline for Sclerotia Maturation

The timeline differs between species and depends on substrate volume, temperature, and strain genetics:

- ***P. tampanensis*:** Sclerotia continue to enlarge until approximately **4 months** (120 days) after inoculation. Growth rate slows noticeably after 3 months but measurable increases in sclerotial mass continue until approximately the 4-month mark. Growth essentially ceases after this point and the sclerotia enter a dormant state. The 4-month mark represents the practical harvest point for maximum biomass per jar.
- ***P. mexicana*:** Sclerotia formation follows a similar but somewhat compressed timeline. Maximum biomass may be reached slightly earlier (3–3.5 months) depending on the strain. The optimal harvest window should be determined empirically for each strain by periodically opening a test jar to assess sclerotial development.
- ***P. galindoi*:** Similar to *P. mexicana*, with harvest typically at 3–4 months.
- **Post-maturation:** Sclerotia can be left in the jars beyond the optimal harvest point and will remain viable and psychoactive for extended periods (6 months or more). However, no significant additional biomass accumulation occurs, and the risk of eventual contamination slowly increases. The jars should be kept in a clean, preferably dark location throughout the entire incubation and storage period.

## Sclerotia in Cased Trays

The source notes that sclerotia of *P. tampanensis* and *P. mexicana* also form in the casing layer of cased trays, alongside mushroom production. This means that cultivators who case colonised grass seed for mushroom harvest can obtain sclerotia as a secondary product. However, for maximum sclerotia yield, the dedicated jar method (no casing, extended dark incubation) is preferred because all metabolic resources are directed to sclerotial biomass rather than being split between sclerotia and fruit bodies. The dual-production approach (casing for mushrooms + harvesting sclerotia from the casing layer) can be useful for cultivators who want to maximise total alkaloid yield from a single culture, but the individual yields of both mushrooms and sclerotia will be lower than what could be achieved by dedicated cultivation of either pathway separately.

## Harvesting and Storage

Sclerotia are harvested by opening the jar and separating the hard, dense nodules fro grass seed substrate s seed substrate. A clean fork or spoon works well for this purpose. The colonised grain itself may contain significant alkaloid content from the mycelium, but the concentrated sclerotia are the primary harvest. After separation, sclerotia should be rinsed briefly with cold water to remove adhering substrate particles and patted dry. Post-harvest options include:

- **Fresh consumption:** Sclerotia have a firm, somewhat crunchy texture and a mild, earthy flavour that is generally considered less unpleasant than fresh mushrooms.
- **Dried:** Sclerotia can be air-dried or desiccated for long-term storage. Drying concentrates the alkaloids by weight. Sclerotia typically lose 60–70% of their weight during drying, similar to mushroom fruit bodies.
- **Refrigerated:** Fresh sclerotia can be stored in the refrigerator for several weeks in a paper bag or sealed container. For longer storage, freezing is an option, though the texture may change upon thawing.
- **Extraction:** Sclerotia can be used for ethanol or water-based extractions, following protocols similar to those used for dried mushroom material.

## Alkaloid Content

Sclerotia of *P. mexicana* and *P. tampanensis* contain psilocin and psilocybin in concentrations comparable to the fruit bodies of the same species. The total alkaloid content varies depending on strain, substrate composition, incubation time, and environmental conditions. Baeocystin and norbaeocystin, the lower homologues of psilocybin, may also be present as minor constituents. The alkaloid profile of sclerotia is generally similar to that of the corresponding mushroom species, though some analytical studies have reported differences in the relative ratios of psilocin to psilocybin between sclerotia and fruit bodies. This may reflect differences in the enzymatic environment during sclerotial versus fruit body development, or in the degree of post-harvest dephosphorylation of psilocybin to psilocin.

## Comparison with Mushroom Cultivation

Compared to standard mushroom cultivation, sclerotia production offers several practical advantages:

1. **No fruiting chamber needed:** Sclerotia form inside sealed jars, eliminating the need for humidity-controlled fruiting bins, misting schedules, or air exchange management.
2. **No casing required:** The grass seed substrate is used directly without any casing layer, reducing materials and labour.
3. **Minimal contamination risk:** Once fully colonised, the sealed jar is highly resistant to contamination. The long incubation period (4 months) occurs entirely within a closed system.
4. **No light required:** Dark incubation is the standard condition, so no lighting equipment or light cycle management is needed.
5. **Low maintenance:** After inoculation and the initial shake, no intervention is required until harvest at 4 months.
6. **Compact:** Multiple jars can be incubated in a small space (cupboard, closet, drawer), making this method suitable for cultivators with limited space.
7. **Predictable yield:** Sclerotia production is generally more consistent than mushroom fruiting, which can be sensitive to environmental fluctuations.

The primary disadvantages are the long incubation time (4 months versus 4–6 weeks for mushrooms) and the generally lower total alkaloid yield per unit of substrate compared to a well-managed multi-flush mushroom grow. Sclerotia also cannot be cloned or subcultured as readily as mushroom tissue, since the sclerotial mass must be opened and exposed to potential contamination for harvesting.

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

Several issues can arise during sclerotia cultivation:

- **No sclerotia formation:** This may indicate the wrong species (not all *Psilocybe* species form sclerotia), insufficient incubation time, or suboptimal substrate moisture. Ensure the seed is properly hydrated but not waterlogged.
- **Contamination during incubation:** Usually caused by improper sterilisation or poor [[pf-tek-inoculation-technique-and-syringe-management]]. If contamination appears before full colonisation, the jar should be discarded. Contamination appearing after full colonisation can sometimes be managed by removing the affected area, but the jar should be monitored carefully.
- **Mushroom formation in the jar:** If light reaches the jar or the lid is too loose, the mycelium may initiate fruiting inside the jar rather than forming sclerotia. This wastes metabolic resources on mushroom production in an unsuitable environment. Ensure jars are kept in complete darkness.
- **Small sclerotia:** May indicate insufficient incubation time (harvest too early), low substrate nutrient content, or suboptimal temperature. Extending the incubation period and maintaining consistent temperature (20–25 °C) typically improves sclerotial size.
- **Soft or mushy sclerotia:** This indicates bacterial contamination or excessive moisture. Properly formed sclerotia should be firm and hard, similar to nuts or small potatoes.

## Biological Function of Sclerotia

In nature, sclerotia serve as long-term survival structures that allow the fungus to persist through adverse conditions. They are composed of densely packed hyphae with thick cell walls, significant reserves of glycogen and lipids, and reduced [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] compared to actively growing mycelium. The outer rind (cortex) of the sclerotium is typically heavily melanised, providing protection against UV radiation, desiccation, and microbial degradation. The inner medulla contains the nutrient reserves and viable hyphae. When environmental conditions become favourable (adequate moisture, suitable temperature, nutrient availability), sclerotia can germinate to produce either mycelium (vegetative growth) or fruiting bodies directly (reproductive growth), depending on the species and conditions. This dual germination capacity makes sclerotia highly effective at propagating the fungus across both temporal (seasonal) and spatial gaps in favourable conditions. In *Psilocybe* species, sclerotia are primarily a means of surviving dry seasons in subtropical and tropical grasslands, where the fungus fruits after seasonal rains.

## Commercial and Legal Context

In the Netherlands, psilocybin-containing mushrooms were banned in 2008, but sclerotia (truffles) remained legal until they were also banned in 2019. During this eleven-year window, a significant commercial industry developed around the cultivation and sale of "magic truffles," primarily *P. tampanensis* and *P. mexicana* sclerotia. Dutch smart shops sold truffles under various brand names with standardised packaging and dosing guidelines (typically 15 g fresh as a standard dose). This commercial scale was made possible largely by the simplicity of the jar cultivation method described here, which allows predictable, scalable production without the complexity of mushroom fruiting infrastructure. The Dutch truffle industry developed sophisticated packaging, branding, and dosing standards for sclerotia, establishing conventions that persist in the grey market today. The legal distinction between mushrooms and sclerotia that existed during this period was biologically arbitrary — both contain the same active alkaloids — but reflected the specific wording of the Dutch ban, which prohibited "mushrooms" (paddo's) but did not initially address sclerotia (truffels).

## Historical Note: Steven Pollock and P. tampanensis

*Psilocybe tampanensis* has a unique place in the history of psychedelic mycology. It was discovered in 1977 near Tampa, Florida, by Steven Pollock, a physician and mycologist who was one of the pioneers of  mushroom cultivation. Pollock developed many of the techniques that would later become standard in the cultivation community, including improved methods for growing *P. cubensis*. His 1977 discovery of *P. tampanensis* was significant because it was only the second *Psilocybe* species known to produce sclerotia (after *P. mexicana*). Tragically, Pollock was murdered in 1981, and the original Tampa collection of *P. tampanensis* remains the only confirmed wild specimen ever found. All cultivated *P. tampanensis* material worldwide is descended from spores collected by Pollock from that single fruiting body. This [[cervantes-genetic-bottleneck-and-diversity-loss]] makes the species particularly vulnerable to loss, and underscores the importance of maintaining cultivated cultures of this historically and biologically significant fungus.
