# Homestead Emergency Preparedness

Homesteads face unique emergency scenarios: extended power outages, severe weather, wildfire, flooding, and isolation from emergency services. Preparation is not paranoia — it is responsible risk management that distinguishes a resilient homestead from a vulnerable one.

## Power Outage Preparation

Most homesteads experience multiple power outages per year. Prepare for outages lasting from hours to weeks:

### Short-Term (Hours to 2 Days)
- Flashlights and headlamps with extra batteries — avoid candles due to fire risk
- Battery-powered or hand-crank radio for weather updates
- Phone chargers (portable battery banks, 20,000 mAh minimum)
- Cooler with ice for refrigerated items — open refrigerator only when necessary (a full freezer holds temperature for 48 hours; half-full for 24 hours)
- Manual can opener

### Medium-Term (2 Days to 2 Weeks)
- Portable generator — 3000-5000W running watts covers most needs. Run extension cords to critical loads (refrigerator, well pump, sump pump, lights). NEVER run a generator indoors or in an attached garage — carbon monoxide kills.
- Transfer switch — if you install a generator, a manual transfer switch lets you power your breaker panel safely and legally
- Propane or butane camp stove for cooking — use outdoors or in a well-ventilated area
- Battery bank and inverter for lights and small electronics
- Solar panels and charge controller — even a small portable setup (100-200W) keeps communications charged

### Long-Term (Weeks to Months)
- Full solar power system with battery storage — see [[food-dehydration-guide]] for small-scale solar applications
- Backup well pump (hand pump or 12V DC pump with battery bank)
- Wood stove for heating and cooking — [[rocket-stove]] for efficient outdoor cooking
- Fuel storage — stabilized gasoline (6-month shelf life with stabilizer), diesel (12-month shelf life), propane (indefinite shelf life)

## Water Storage and Purification

Water is the most critical resource. The rule of thumb is one gallon per person per day minimum (2 gallons in hot climates or for medical needs).

### Storage
- Store in food-grade containers only — never use milk jugs or used bleach bottles (they degrade and leak)
- WaterBricks (3.5 gallons each) are stackable and durable
- 55-gallon food-grade drums with bung wrenches for bulk storage
- Rotate stored water every 6-12 months — label with date
- Treat stored tap water with 8 drops (1/8 teaspoon) of unscented bleach per gallon

### Purification Methods
- **Boiling** — bring to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6500 feet elevation). Most reliable method.
- **Filtration** — Sawyer Squeeze or Katadyn filters remove bacteria and protozoa. Some models remove viruses.
- **Chemical treatment** — Aquatabs (chlorine dioxide) or 2 drops of unscented bleach per quart. Wait 30 minutes before drinking.
- **UV treatment** — SteriPEN and similar devices are effective but require batteries.
- **Berkey-style gravity filters** — good for home use, remove a wide range of contaminants. See [[water-management]] systems for homestead-scale filtration.

## Food Storage

A well-stocked homestead pantry provides food security beyond the growing season. Layer your approach:

### Short-Term Pantry (1-3 Months)
- Canned goods (commercial and home-canned)
- Dry goods: rice, beans, pasta, oats, flour
- Cooking oil, salt, sugar, spices
- Coffee, tea, powdered milk

### Medium-Term Storage (3-12 Months)
- Freeze-dried meals (Mountain House, Augason Farms) — 25-year shelf life
- Dehydrated vegetables and fruits — [[food-dehydration-guide]] for off-grid drying
- Cured meats, hard cheeses, fermented foods
- Bulk grains in sealed buckets with oxygen absorbers

### Long-Term Storage (1-25 Years)
- Wheat berries, white rice, dried beans in mylar bags with oxygen absorbers inside food-grade buckets
- Salt, sugar, honey (indefinite shelf life)
- Freeze-dried foods in #10 cans
- Home-canned goods (1-2 year shelf life, inspect before use)

Track all stored food with date and rotation schedule — see [[homestead-record-keeping]].

## Communication

When the grid goes down, communication becomes critical:

- NOAA weather radio (hand-crank/solar powered)
- HAM radio (amateur radio) — requires license ( Technician class is entry-level, no Morse code required). Provides communication when all other systems fail.
- Two-way radios (FRS/GMRS) — for communication around the property. GMRS requires a license but has longer range.
- Satellite communicator (Garmin inReach, SPOT) — for emergencies when cell service is unavailable. Subscription required.
- Establish a communication plan with neighbors and family — designated check-in times and a rally point

## Go Bags (Bug-Out Bags)

Even if you intend to shelter in place, prepare go bags in case evacuation becomes necessary. One per person plus one per vehicle.

### Essentials (Per Person)
- Water (1 liter minimum, purification capability)
- Food (2400 calories per day for 3 days — energy bars, jerky, dried fruit, nuts)
- First aid kit — see [[homestead-first-aid-guide]]
- Clothing (layered, including rain gear and sturdy shoes)
- Shelter (emergency bivvy or space blanket)
- Fire-starting (lighter, matches in waterproof case, ferro rod)
- Navigation (map and compass, not just GPS)
- Light (headlamp with extra batteries)
- Multi-tool or knife
- Cash in small denominations
- Copies of important documents in a waterproof bag

### Homestead-Specific Additions
- Livestock feed and water for 72 hours if you have animals
- Animal crates, leashes, halters
- Fuel for vehicles and generator
- Critical tools (chainsaw chain, fuel mix, repair parts)
- Seeds — a seed bank of open-pollinated varieties as a long-term food security measure

## Wildfire Preparedness

If you live in a fire-prone area:
- Maintain defensible space (30 feet minimum, 100 feet ideal) around all structures
- Use fire-resistant building materials (metal roof, no wooden decks attached to the house)
- Have evacuation routes planned and practiced — more than one route
- Keep hoses connected and pumps ready during fire season
- Know your local alert system (Reverse 911, Nixle, CodeRED)

## Flood Preparedness

- Know your flood risk (FEMA flood maps)
- Elevate critical systems (electrical panel, HVAC) above expected flood levels
- Store go bags and critical supplies on upper floors
- Know evacuation routes that do not cross low-lying areas
- Sandbags and a sump pump with battery backup

## Documentation

Keep physical copies (waterproof bag) and digital copies (encrypted cloud backup) of:
- Insurance policies
- Property deeds and vehicle titles
- Medical records and prescriptions
- Financial account information
- Emergency contacts
- Photos/video of property and possessions for insurance claims

Review and update your emergency preparedness plan annually. Run drills with your household at least twice a year. Preparedness is a practice, not a one-time task.
