# Primary Nutrients and Mineral Sources in Soils

## Overview

Soil nutrients are the foundation of plant health and agricultural productivity. In permaculture design, understanding the sources, cycles, and distribution of primary nutrients—phosphorus, potassium (potash), and nitrogen—is essential for creating self-sustaining food systems that minimize external inputs. Rather than relying on synthetic fertilizers, permaculture emphasizes biological cycling, local mineral sources, and strategic design to maintain soil fertility.

## The Three Primary Nutrients

### Phosphorus (Phosphates)

Phosphorus is one of the most commonly deficient nutrients in heavily leached soils. Natural sources include:

- **Guano**: Bird manures from dry islands, concentrated by seed-eating birds. However, marine guano may contain elevated levels of cadmium and uranium from oceanic food chains, making heavy use potentially polluting.
- **Phosphatic rock**: Sedimentary rock deposits containing 8–15% phosphorus in various combinations with oxygen or water (hydrated forms). Distribution is geographically restricted.
- **Rock phosphate**: Contains 8–15% phosphorus but releases nutrients very slowly. On highly leached clays and clay-loams, it may be completely absorbed before plants can access it.

The permaculture strategy is to use phosphate resources conservatively and to develop local cycling systems, particularly by encouraging bird populations whose manures concentrate phosphorus.

### Potassium (Potash)

Potash deficiency is widespread in leached areas. Sources include:

- **Kainite**: A mineral formed in areas of evaporated waters, found in desert salts typically containing 20–25% potash.
- **Orthoclase**: A major constituent of granite containing potash, representing large but slowly available reserves.
- **Burnt and rotted plant material or compost**: Locally producible sources through managed burning and composting.

### Nitrogen

Nitrogen can be supplied biologically through several pathways:

- **Rhizobia-inoculated legumes**: Both water plants (e.g., *Azolla*, *Scirpus*) and land plants fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiotic relationships with rhizobia bacteria.
- **Nitrogen-fixing trees**: Species such as *Acacia*, *Albizia*, and *Eleagnus* serve as pioneer cover crops. Even after cutting, their root zones continue releasing nitrogen for up to 6 years.
- **Algae**: Aquatic and semi-aquatic systems contribute nitrogen fixation.

A key insight from permaculture is that legumes may not be needed in mature forests, where they typically die out under canopy. Only a few large leguminous trees (e.g., *Samanea*, *Acacia melanoxylon*) persist in mixed forest systems, suggesting that nitrogen cycling becomes self-sufficient in established ecosystems.

## Sources of Minerals in Soils

### The Sea as a Mineral Reservoir

As soils are leached and become mineral-poor, those minerals concentrate in the sea, in marine organisms, and in inland saltpans. This creates a cascading resource system:

1. **Seaweeds and seagrasses**: Traditionally used as agricultural fertilizers and still valued in modern systems.
2. **Fish residues**: Long-standing fertilizer sources.
3. **Evaporite sequences**: As seawater evaporates, minerals precipitate in order of solubility:
   - First: **Calcite and dolomite** — used for soil conditioning and pH adjustment
   - Next: **Gypsum and anhydrite** — restore soil crumb structure
   - Then: **Rock salts** — common nutrient, lacking only in wet tropical uplands
   - Finally: **Potash, magnesium salts, and trace elements** — concentrated in the remaining liquid

#### Bitterns

The liquid remaining after common salt deposition is called **bitterns** — a rich source of minor minerals and trace elements including bromine and rare minerals. Applications include:

- Dilution and incorporation into crop soils
- Foliar sprays at 1–20 L/ha (diluted 100–500:1)
- Must be handled in non-corrosive vessels (polyethylene pipes and drums) due to extreme corrosiveness

Bitterns are inexpensive and easily transported to leached areas, but their effects must be validated through local trials on specific crops.

### Rocks and Rock Dusts

Geological sources provide slow-release mineral nutrition:

| Rock Type | Primary Nutrients |
|-----------|------------------|
| Granites | Potash and sodium salts (from feldspars) |
| Limestone | Calcium |
| Dolomite | Calcium and magnesium |
| Mineral deposits/ores | Trace elements |
| Basalt dusts | Broad spectrum, especially useful on leached tropical soils |

**Key principles for rock dust application:**

- Cheap ores, finely ground, are as effective as refined sulphates or oxides (Leeper, 1982).
- Trace elements (zinc, copper, cobalt, molybdenum) are needed in small quantities — as low as 5–7 kg per 100 hectares.
- Rock dusts are often cheaply available from quarries or gravel pits.
- **Caution**: Unselective rock dust application can do harm — excessive or poisonous nutrients may be added. Always match the rock type to local soil needs.

Animals can also serve as mineral distributors: salt licks, molasses supplements, water additives, injections, and slow-release rumen pellets (cobalt) all move minerals through biological pathways into the soil via urine and manure.

## Distribution of Elements in the Soil Profile

The vertical distribution of nutrients through the soil profile (surface to approximately 2 meters deep) is governed by two primary determinants:

### 1. Water Penetration

Water acts as a universal solvent, enabling compounds to dissociate into ions and transporting them to microsites throughout the soil. This works in three dimensions:

- **Infiltration**: Water soaks in from the surface downward, carrying dissolved minerals.
- **Capillary rise**: Water moves upward from the water table through capillary action.
- **Flooding**: Periodic flooding redistributes nutrients vertically.

### 2. Biological Activity

Root networks, soil organisms, and microbial communities create channels and exchange surfaces that redistribute minerals. Mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective reach of root systems, while earthworms and other soil fauna create vertical mixing through their burrowing activities.

## Permaculture Design Implications

The mineral nutrition of soils in a permaculture system is managed through several integrated strategies:

1. **Biological nitrogen fixation** through strategic placement of leguminous plants and nitrogen-fixing trees in pioneer and succession stages.
2. **Local mineral cycling** through composting, mulching, and controlled burning to recycle potash and phosphorus.
3. **Bird integration** to concentrate phosphorus through their manures.
4. **Sea and ocean resource utilization** where appropriate — seaweeds, fish residues, and bitterns for trace elements.
5. **Strategic rock dust application** matched to specific soil deficiencies identified through observation and testing.
6. **Design for mineral retention** — contour banks, swales, and vegetated barriers to slow water movement and reduce leaching.

The overarching ethic is one of conservation and local production. External mineral inputs should be used judiciously, while biological systems are designed to capture, cycle, and retain nutrients within the site. This approach not only reduces dependence on mined or imported resources but also builds long-term soil health and resilience.
