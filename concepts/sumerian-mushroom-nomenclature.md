## The Sumerian Religious Context
Allegro placed [[allegro-death-and-resurrection-in-the-mushroom-cult]] within the broader context of
Sumerian religion. Civilization, he argued, began in Sumer in
the Land of the Two Rivers, Mesopotamia. About 4000 BCE, the
Sumerians were already developing a culture that would affect
the whole world for over five thousand years.
In Sumerian city-states, the land was the god's, and the king
was his bailiff. The administrative centre of each district
was the temple, with priestly officials whose control was
absolute. The temple was the seat of justice, land
administration, scientific learning, and theological
speculation, as well as the theatre of religious ritual.
It was in such temple colleges that tutors built, over two
thousand years, some of the richest libraries of the ancient
world. From the ruins of ancient Nippur came several thousand
literary texts covering theology, botany, zoology, mineralogy,
geography, mathematics, and philology.
## Problems with the Etymologies
The methodological problems with Allegro's approach are
particularly acute in his Sumerian etymologies:
**Hypothetical roots**: Many of the Sumerian words Allegro
cited were his own constructions rather than attested words
in the cuneiform record. Professional Assyriologists could not
verify the existence of many of his proposed roots.
**Phonological flexibility**: Allegro's method allowed him to
manipulate Sumerian syllables freely, adding, removing, or
modifying sounds to achieve the desired connection. This degree
of flexibility rendered the method essentially unfalsifiable.
**Anachronism**: Even where Allegro's Sumerian readings had
some merit, the enormous temporal gap between Sumerian
civilization (4000-2000 BCE) and the composition of the New
Testament (first century CE) made direct transmission of
specific coded meanings extremely improbable.
**Ignoring semantic development**: Words change meaning over
time, often dramatically. Allegro's method assumed that
Sumerian meanings could survive essentially unchanged across
thousands of years and multiple language translations, a claim
that no serious linguist would support.
## Scholarly Reception
Allegro's work was overwhelmingly rejected by specialists in
Sumerian, Hebrew, and biblical studies. The response from
academic reviewers was uniformly negative, with linguists
pointing out fundamental errors in his understanding of
Sumerian grammar, phonology, and semantics.
