# Composting Toilets

Composting toilets treat human waste through aerobic decomposition,
converting it into safe, nutrient-rich compost rather than flushing it
into water-based waste systems. [[bill-mollison]] strongly advocated for
composting toilets as a fundamental permaculture technology.

## The Problem with Flush Toilets

Mollison frequently pointed out that a single flush toilet contaminates
approximately 13,000 liters of clean water per year to transport a few
kilograms of human waste. The resulting sewage contains nitrogen,
phosphorus, potassium, and [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] that could fertilize
[[fukuoka-textdoc-agricultural-land-law-obstruction-return-farming]].

Instead, this nutrient stream is diluted, mixed with industrial
chemicals, and discharged into waterways where it causes eutrophication
and ecosystem damage. A composting toilet reverses this flow, keeping
human waste separate from the water supply and processing it on-site
through biological decomposition.

## Types of Composting Toilets

Batch composting toilets use two or more chambers or containers. While
one chamber is in active use, the other is composting. When the active
chamber fills, it is sealed and left to decompose while the second
chamber is put into use, ensuring waste composts for at least several
months.

The most common batch design uses two 50-liter drums mounted on a
rotating frame. When one drum fills, it is rotated out of service and a
fresh drum is rotated into position. The sealed drum decomposes over 6
to 12 months.

Continuous composting toilets have a single large chamber where waste
moves gradually through the decomposition process. New waste enters at
the top and finished compost is removed from the bottom. These systems
are larger and more expensive but can handle higher volumes.

## How They Work

Popularized by Joseph Jenkins, the dry toilet approach is the simplest
form. A toilet seat is mounted over a container. After each use, the
user covers deposits with a carbon-rich cover material such as sawdust,
straw, peanut shells, or rice hulls.

The covered material is deposited in an outdoor composting bin where it
decomposes for 6 to 12 months along with yard waste and other organic
materials. Mollison described similar systems in his work on village
scale sanitation, noting that properly managed dry composting produces
pathogen-free compost suitable for fruit [[fukuoka-textdoc-shelterbelt-design-environmental-tree-planting]].

The compost should feel like a wrung-out sponge: moist but not dripping
wet. Too dry and decomposition stalls; too wet and the system goes
anaerobic, producing foul odors. Urine diversion systems significantly
reduce [[mushroom-casing-moisture-management]] challenges.

## Pathogen Destruction

Pathogen destruction in composting toilets depends on three factors:
time, temperature, and microbial competition. Even without sustained
high temperatures, extended composting times of 6 to 12 months reduce
pathogen populations to safe levels through competitive exclusion.

When composting temperatures reach 55 degrees Celsius for at least
three days, pathogens including Salmonella, Shigella, and hookworm eggs
are destroyed. Most backyard composting toilets rely primarily on time
rather than sustained heat for pathogen reduction.
