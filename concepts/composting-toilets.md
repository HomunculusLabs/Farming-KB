# Composting Toilets

Composting toilets treat human waste through aerobic decomposition,
converting it into safe, nutrient-rich compost rather than flushing it
into water-based waste systems. Bill Mollison strongly advocated for
composting toilets as a fundamental permaculture technology, arguing
that conventional flush toilets represent one of the greatest design
errors in modern civilization: they mix clean water with valuable
nutrients and create a pollution problem that requires enormous
infrastructure to manage.

## The Problem with Flush Toilets

Mollison frequently pointed out that a single flush toilet contaminates
approximately 13,000 liters of clean water per year to transport a few
kilograms of human waste. The resulting sewage contains nitrogen,
phosphorus, potassium, and organic matter that could fertilize
agricultural land. Instead, this nutrient stream is diluted, mixed with
industrial chemicals, and discharged into waterways where it causes
eutrophication and ecosystem damage.

A composting toilet reverses this flow. It keeps human waste separate
from the water supply, processes it on-site through biological
decomposition, and returns the resulting compost to the soil as a
fertilizer. This closes a major nutrient loop that conventional systems
permanently break.

## Types of Composting Toilets

### Batch Systems

Batch composting toilets use two or more chambers or containers. While
one chamber is in active use, the other is composting. When the active
chamber fills, it is sealed and left to decompose while the second
chamber is put into use. This approach ensures that waste composts for
at least several months before being handled, which significantly
reduces pathogen levels.

The most common batch design uses two 50-liter drums mounted on a
rotating frame. When one drum fills, it is rotated out of service and
a fresh drum is rotated into position. The sealed drum decomposes over
6-12 months while the user fills the second drum.

### Continuous Systems

Continuous composting toilets have a single large chamber where waste
moves gradually through the decomposition process. New waste enters at
the top and finished compost is removed from the bottom. These systems
are larger and more expensive but can handle higher volumes. They
require careful management of moisture, temperature, and aeration to
function properly.

### Dry Toilet (Humanure) Systems

Popularized by Joseph Jenkins, the dry toilet approach is the simplest
and most accessible form of composting toilet. A toilet seat is mounted
over a container (bucket or vault). After each use, the user covers the
deposits with a carbon-rich cover material such as sawdust, straw,
peanut shells, or rice hulls. The covered material is deposited in an
outdoor composting bin where it decomposes for 6-12 months along with
yard waste and other organic materials.

Mollison described similar systems in his work on village-scale
sanitation, noting that properly managed dry composting produces
pathogen-free compost suitable for fruit tree and ornamental planting.

## Science of Pathogen Destruction

Pathogen destruction in composting toilets depends on three factors:
time, temperature, and microbial competition. Even without sustained
high temperatures, extended composting times of 6-12 months reduce
pathogen populations to safe levels through competitive exclusion by
thermophilic and mesophilic microorganisms. When composting temperatures
reach 55 degrees Celsius for at least three days, pathogens including
Salmonella, Shigella, and hookworm eggs are destroyed. Most backyard
composting toilets rely primarily on time rather than sustained heat.

## Cover Materials

The carbon-rich cover material is critical to the system's success:
- **Sawdust**: Excellent moisture control and carbon addition. The
  gold standard for dry toilet systems.
- **Peat moss**: Good moisture absorption but less sustainable to
  harvest.
- **Straw and hay**: Effective but may introduce seeds.
- **Rice hulls**: Widely available in tropical regions.
- **Shredded leaves**: Free and abundant in autumn but may mat down.
- **Coco coir**: Excellent moisture management, renewable resource.

The cover material must be added in sufficient quantity to completely
cover fresh deposits and control odors. A ratio of roughly equal
volumes of waste and cover material works well in most systems.

## Managing Moisture and Aeration

Composting toilets require careful moisture management. The compost
should feel like a wrung-out sponge: moist but not dripping wet. Too
dry and decomposition stalls. Too wet and the system goes anaerobic,
producing foul odors.

Excess moisture can be managed by adding more carbon material, improving
ventilation, or installing a small drainage system. Urine diversion
systems, which separate urine from feces, significantly reduce moisture
management challenges. Diverted urine can be diluted and used directly
as a high-nitrogen fertilizer.

## Using the Finished Compost

Compost from well-managed composting toilets is safe for agricultural
use after the recommended aging period. Best practices include:
- Apply compost primarily to fruit trees, berry bushes, and ornamental
  plants rather than root vegetables or leafy greens consumed raw
- Ensure a minimum of 6 months composting time, preferably 12 months
- Mix the finished compost into soil rather than surface-applying it
- Combine with other compost materials for a more balanced product

## Integration with Permaculture Design

Mollison placed composting toilets within the broader context of
waste-free design. Combined with greywater systems, they eliminate the
need for conventional sewage infrastructure entirely. The resulting
compost feeds fruit trees and ornamental plantings, completing the
nutrient cycle on-site.

In larger-scale applications, composting toilets can serve community
buildings, eco-villages, and rural settlements. Mollison described
examples where community-scale [[composting-toilet-systems]] provided all
the fertilizer needed for adjacent food forests and orchards.
