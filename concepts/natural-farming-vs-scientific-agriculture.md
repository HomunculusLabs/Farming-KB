# Natural Farming vs Scientific Agriculture

The contrast between natural farming and scientific agriculture is the central philosophical and practical theme of [[Masanobu Fukuoka]]'s work. Fukuoka argued that these two approaches are not merely different techniques but fundamentally opposed worldviews with divergent trajectories: natural farming moves toward simplification, convergence, and harmony with nature, while scientific agriculture moves toward increasing complexity, divergence, and ultimately destruction. This comparison underpins his critique of modern civilization and his proposal for an alternative relationship between humanity and the natural world.

## Fundamental Differences

### Direction of Development

Fukuoka described the two systems in spatial and dynamic terms. Natural farming is "centrifugal and convergent": its objective is non-action and a return to nature. It seeks to reduce human intervention to zero. Scientific farming is "centripetal and divergent": it breaks away from nature through the expansion of human wants and desires, requiring ever more technology, energy, and labor. Because this outward expansion cannot be stopped, Fukuoka argued, scientific farming is ultimately doomed to extinction.

### Energy Efficiency

Fukuoka presented compelling energy calculations to support his claim that natural farming is more productive than scientific agriculture. His natural farming method produced approximately 130 pounds of rice (200,000 kilocalories of energy) per man-day of labor, without any input of outside materials. This was about 100 times the daily caloric intake of a farmer on a natural diet.

In contrast, traditional farming using horses and oxen required approximately ten times as much energy input as natural farming. Small-scale mechanization doubled this energy requirement, and large-scale mechanization doubled it yet again. Modern scientific agriculture thus represented a geometric progression in energy consumption.

### Yield Comparison

Fukuoka consistently harvested approximately 22 bushels (1,300 pounds) of winter grain and 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre using his natural method. He noted that scientists often claimed they could improve on these yields by adding technical know-how, but argued that this reasoning contained a basic contradiction: the additions of technology to natural farming, which already achieved strong yields, only served to move it back toward the scientific paradigm.

He argued that high-yield practices do not actually exceed natural productivity but merely attempt to artificially restore full productivity after man has hamstrung nature so that it cannot exercise its full powers. In his view, high-yield technologies are "glorified attempts to stave off reductions in productivity" caused by the very practices of scientific agriculture itself. The productivity of Japanese agricultural land had actually declined over the fifty years of scientific advancement, a fact obscured by focusing on labor productivity rather than land productivity.

### The Labor Trap

Fukuoka observed that the claim that mechanization has increased the efficiency of work is misleading. Farmers must use the extra hours freed from field labor to earn outside income to pay for their equipment. "All they have done is exchange their work in the fields for a job in some company; they have traded the joy of working outdoors in the open fields for dreary hours of labor shut up inside a factory." The modern farmer has become, in Fukuoka's phrase, "a hired hand of industrialized society," spinning around like a top in a futile effort to make money with synthetic chemicals.

## The Three Types of Farming

Fukuoka categorized farming approaches into three distinct types:

### Mahayana Natural Farming

This type of farming and scientific agriculture exist in entirely different worlds. Mahayana natural farming represents a pure, philosophically grounded approach that begins from a state of "no knowledge" and seeks to work in complete harmony with nature. It is the approach Fukuoka himself practiced. In this framework, nature is perceived as whole and complete, and human intervention is understood as inherently disruptive. The practitioner seeks to minimize action rather than optimize it.

### Hinayana Natural Farming

This type belongs in the same world as scientific farming but represents a reduced-input version. It still operates within the framework of human knowledge and intervention but attempts to minimize them. Fukuoka considered this an improvement over scientific farming but still fundamentally limited because it accepts the basic premises of scientific agriculture while merely reducing the dosage of its interventions.

### Scientific Farming

Pure scientific farming operates from the premise that nature must be understood, controlled, and improved through human intellect and action. It breaks nature into component parts, studies each in isolation, and attempts to optimize each factor individually. Fukuoka argued that this approach, while appearing rational, leads inevitably to chaos because the interactions between optimized parts cannot be predicted or controlled. The more factors that are optimized in isolation, the more complex and unstable the overall system becomes.

## Critique of Scientific Method in Agriculture

### The Fallacy of Analysis

Fukuoka argued that the living, holistic biosystem that is nature cannot be dissected or resolved into its parts. Once broken down, it dies. Scientists who collect data piecemeal on a "dead and fragmented nature" and claim to know, use, or conquer nature are examining something that is no longer what they think it is.

He illustrated this with the metaphor of Mt. Fuji. A scientist who climbs the mountain, studies its rocks, wildlife, geology, and meteorology, and concludes he now has a complete picture of Fuji has, in Fukuoka's view, understood less than the artist Hokusai, who captured the mountain's essence from a distance without examining it. "One must see it and yet not examine it, and in not examining it, know it."

### The Problem of Cause and Effect

Fukuoka rejected the scientific framework of cause and effect as applied to natural systems. He argued that in nature, there is no cause and effect, only an endless chain of interwoven events and factors that exceeds human comprehension. Scientific agriculture isolates individual factors, assigns causation, and develops interventions, but this approach necessarily ignores the vast web of relationships that sustains living systems.

He illustrated this with his analysis of [[Pine Disease and Matsutake Mushroom Decline|pine forest decline]]: soil acidity was blamed for tree death, but the acidity came from air pollution, which came from industrial activity, which was driven by economic policy, which was shaped by cultural values. Tracing causes backward leads to an infinite regression with no identifiable origin. The notion that eliminating one "cause" can restore health to a complex system is, in Fukuoka's view, a fundamental error of the scientific mindset.

### The Insignificance of Human Knowledge

Fukuoka's most radical claim was that human knowledge, far from being a tool for understanding nature, is actually an obstacle. He argued that the living and holistic biosystem that is nature cannot be dissected or resolved into its parts. Once broken down, it dies. Those who break off a piece of nature lay hold of something dead, and, unaware that what they are examining is no longer what they think it to be, claim to understand nature.

"Because he starts off with misconceptions about nature and takes the wrong approach to understanding it," Fukuoka wrote, "regardless of how rational his thinking, everything winds up all wrong. We must become aware of the insignificance of human knowledge and activity, and begin by grasping their uselessness and futility."

### The Creation of Artificial Needs

Fukuoka observed that every time someone identifies something as "useful" or declares that a certain practice "ought to be done," it is because human intervention has already created the conditions that give that thing its value. Farmers create situations in which they become dependent on tools and inputs they never needed, then celebrate new discoveries that address the very problems their earlier interventions created.

He offered the example of plowing: flood a field with water, stir it up with a plow, and the ground sets as hard as plaster. The soil dies and hardens, so it must be plowed each year to soften it. "All we are doing is creating the conditions that make a plow useful, then rejoicing at the utility of our tool."

## The Illusion of Organic Farming

Fukuoka reserved some of his sharpest criticism for organic farming, which he considered merely another form of scientific agriculture. He acknowledged that organic farming avoids synthetic chemicals, but argued it still operates within the same framework of human knowledge and intervention. Large amounts of organic material are moved from place to place, processed, and treated. Compost is prepared through elaborate procedures. The gains from all this activity are, in Fukuoka's view, local and temporal, and when examined from a broader perspective, many efforts to protect the natural ecology through organic methods are actually destructive.

He pointed out that the compost taken from one field to fertilize another represents a depletion of the source field. The organic farmer is essentially mining fertility from one location to concentrate it in another, not unlike the way scientific agriculture mines petroleum to produce synthetic fertilizers. True natural fertility, Fukuoka argued, comes from within the soil itself through the activity of microorganisms, plant roots, and natural decomposition cycles.

## Critique of Selective Pesticides

Fukuoka was deeply skeptical of so-called safe or selective pesticides. He argued that animal cells, plant cells, and microorganisms are fundamentally similar, and any substance effective against a target insect or pathogen also affects other organisms to some degree. What is called "phytotoxicity" in plants and "pollution" in animals and humans are merely different words for the same phenomenon: the disruptive effect of a biologically active chemical on living systems.

He cited the antibiotics blasticidin S and kasugamycin, released onto the Japanese market as new non-polluting pesticides effective against rice blast disease. Pesticides prepared from biological components such as amino acids, fatty acids, and nucleic acids were similarly promoted as safe. But Fukuoka argued that claiming such substances are harmless to plants and animals is merely "a word game that plays on a very minor difference in resistance."

He extended this critique to biological control using natural predators. While many people feel reassured that releasing beneficial insects to control pests is a safe, biological method, Fukuoka argued that to someone who understands the chain of being linking all living organisms, there is no way to tell which organisms are beneficial predators and which are pests. By meddling with controls, all humanity accomplishes is the destruction of the natural order.

## Food Quality and the Degradation of Diet

Fukuoka argued that scientific farming produces "artificial, deformed food" by making quantitative and qualitative changes in certain aspects of nature without understanding the whole. Modern agriculture has created nothing from nature but has only fabricated synthetic food products that are crude, expensive, and further alienate humanity from the natural world.

He traced this degradation to the shift in Japanese rice culture, where rice changed from being "the very soul of the Yamato people" — grown in a relationship of gratitude toward heaven and earth — to a mere commodity, valued only for its starch content and commercial potential. The object of rice production gradually shifted from cultivation of a sacred food to starch manufacturing and profit pursuit.

## The "Do-Nothing" Movement

Fukuoka envisioned natural farming as one branch of a broader spiritual and cultural movement he called the "do-nothing" movement. He argued that the age of aggressive expansion in materialistic culture had ended and that a new age of consolidation and convergence had arrived. Humanity must, he urged, rid itself of the false visions and concepts that permeate society and establish a new way of life founded on communion with nature.

This was not passivity or laziness, he emphasized, but an active choice to simplify and reduce human knowledge and effort in keeping with the laws of nature. Human knowledge and effort expand and grow increasingly complex and wasteful without limit. Natural farming seeks to halt this expansion, to converge, simplify, and reduce. "Natural farming is more than just a revolution in agricultural techniques," Fukuoka wrote. "It is the practical foundation of a spiritual movement, of a revolution to change the way man lives."

### Become One with Nature

Fukuoka extended his critique of scientific agriculture into a broader epistemology. He argued that to truly know nature, one must not analyze it but become one with it. "Farming is an activity conducted by the hand of nature," he wrote. "We must look carefully at a rice plant and listen to what it tells us." This does not mean studying the plant as an object but putting oneself in its place. When one does this, the self looking upon the rice plant vanishes. "This is what it means to 'see and not examine and in not examining to know.'"

He acknowledged that those without experience of this mode of perception would find it abstract and unconvincing. But he insisted it was the only authentic way to understand the living world. The scientist who dissects nature and concludes that it consists of rocks, trees, and measurable processes has understood less than the farmer who works with detachment, free of worldly concerns, and grasps the whole through direct intuition.
