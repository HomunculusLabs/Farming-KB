# Altitude and Latitude Effects on Climate

The interaction of altitude and latitude creates the fundamental framework for understanding climatic variation. These two factors, combined with continentality (distance from the sea), determine the range of conditions a permaculture designer must work with on any site.

## Altitude Effects

### Temperature Gradient
Temperature falls with altitude at approximately:
- **9.8°C/km** (5.4°F/1000 feet) in rainless or dry air
- **4–9°C/km** (2.2–5°F/1000 feet) in humid and saturated conditions

### The Latitude-Altitude Equivalence
As a rough approximation, **every 100 m (330 feet) of altitude is equivalent to 1° of latitude**. This means:
- At 1000 m on the equator, temperatures are about equivalent to a climate 10° off the equator
- At 10° latitude, a plateau at 1850 m has a climate more like 30° latitude, with probable wind chill to below freezing
- High islands or mountain ranges enable growing a wide range of plant species from ocean to mountain-top

### High Altitude Complications
Mountains are not strictly "latitude equivalents." The air is more rarefied, atmospheric pressure is lower, and radiation is higher. Above 4000 m (13,000 feet):
- **Oxygen deficiency** (mountain sickness) causes fatigue, insomnia, and laboured breathing in visitors
- **Snow and radiation blindness** can occur
- **Extreme day-night temperature fluctuations** challenge plant and animal life

In the Peruvian Andes, day temperatures remain at 16–19°C (61–66°F) year-round, but night temperatures plunge to **-10°C (14°F)**. Water boils at lower temperatures due to reduced atmospheric pressure, and snow may sublime directly to water vapour rather than melting.

### Slope Effects
The effect of slope is even more pronounced than altitude alone. Slopes of just 5° towards the poles reduce soil temperatures as much as **168 km distance** towards the poles. Cold ravines in near-permanent shadow represent extreme microclimates — one can stand in hot sunlight in the Himalayas and gaze into icy depths where only the hardiest life forms exist.

## Valley Climates

### Temperature Inversions
Valleys exhibit dramatic diurnal temperature swings. Hot air builds rapidly in sun-facing wider valleys sheltered from winds, generating strong winds — upslope and up-valley by day, downslope and down-valley at night. At moderate altitudes (3,000–4,000 m), these valley winds follow a daily cycle used by many bird species for daily migration.

### Cloud Formation
In large valleys, especially in cool moist climates, upslope winds generate chains of cumulus clouds at the valley head from mid-morning to evening. In tropical humid climates, cloud may be continuously held on mountain tops, forming the standing cloud of high islands. Forest on valley sides and ridges actively humidifies air streams through transpiration, accentuating cloud and rainfall effects.

### Valleys as Refugia
Valleys in tundra and desert support tree populations absent from surrounding plains:
- **Tundra valleys** are protected by driven deep snow cover, preserving warm or sub-lethal soil temperatures in winter and providing excess summer melt moisture
- **Desert valleys** (wadis) remain moist due to deep detritus filling their floors; shaded soils lose less moisture to evaporation, and lethal soil temperatures are avoided by partial shading

The sudden pre-dawn coldness in valley areas represents the maximum intensity of cold air that has pooled all night flowing down from mountains. It is at this time that glacier-fed winds reach maximum speed.

### Frost in Valleys
Without wind or air flow, **radiation frost** forms in sheltered hollows and tree clearings. Opening up clearings or draining them of cold air helps reduce frost. Growing crops inside open tree canopies can prevent frost but may cause losses from low light levels and lack of wind or insect pollination — solutions include shade-side reflector plants and bee-attracting species around clearings.

## Latitude Effects

### Light Availability at High Latitudes
Despite weak light and short growing seasons at sub-polar latitudes, very long summer days provide sufficient light for vigorous plant growth. Daily radiation totals in late summer:
- **Madras, India (13°N):** 440 Langleys
- **Fresno, California (36°N):** 680 Langleys
- **Fairbanks, Alaska (64°N):** 450 Langleys

Average radiation in temperate areas is **1–5 times that of the tropics** (Chang, 1968).

### Photosynthetic Efficiency by Latitude
Moderate temperatures at high latitudes suit photosynthesis, conferring efficiency that enables considerable production of cereals, berries, tomatoes, potatoes, and vegetables tolerant of short-season/long-day conditions. Deep periglacial soils produce gigantic lettuce, cabbage, spinach, and root crops. These conditions prevail in Alaska, Ireland, Scotland, and parts of Norway.

### Tropical Light Inefficiency
At equatorial latitudes, the ample light is **inefficient** due to extremely high temperatures. Plants may become **light-saturated** — photosynthesis can actually decline in intense light, and energy built into plants may be less in sunlight than in partial shade. Shade (down to 20% sunlight) is of great benefit in tropical deserts and sunny equatorial areas. Shadecloth with 50–70% light transmission may greatly increase production.

### Optimal Temperature Ranges
- Above 25°C (77°F): [[photosynthetic-efficiency]] **sharply decreases**
- Arctic/high latitudes: **15°C (59°F)** is optimum for adapted species
- Many food plants: **20–24°C (68–75°F)** is optimum

The normal desert or equatorial condition of high light and temperature is very inefficient for plant material production. This explains why temperate areas often out-produce the tropics for the same crops — rice, for example, yields 4–5 times better in temperate areas, although up to three crops per year in the tropics helps compensate.

## Carbon Dioxide and Photosynthesis

Photosynthetic efficiency is limited by the leaf's ability to obtain CO₂. At high light intensity, supplying CO₂ to the saturation level of **0.13%** yields a 2–3 times increase in photosynthetic rate. CO₂ can be supplied by composting or by housing animals in greenhouses where light is more than sufficient. True tropical plants can maintain photosynthesis at low (0.10%) CO₂ and tolerate much higher light levels before saturation — a key reason why adapted species outperform temperate introductions in the tropics.

## Design Implications
Understanding altitude and latitude effects on climate is essential for effective site
planning in permaculture and sustainable agriculture. Climate factors determine
plant selection, water management strategies, and the placement of structures
and growing areas within a designed landscape.

## Practical Applications
These climatic principles guide decisions about species selection, microclimate
creation, wind protection, and solar access optimization. Designers use this
knowledge to extend growing seasons, reduce energy consumption, and create
favorable conditions for cultivated ecosystems in diverse climate zones.
