# Forest Mycelial Biomass: The Hidden Majority of Topsoil Ecosystems

## Overview

The mycelial networks of fungi constitute a remarkably large proportion of total forest biomass, particularly in the topsoil horizon. In Pacific Northwest Douglas fir forests, the mycelial component of topsoil approaches 10% of total biomass — a figure that challenges conventional understanding of forest ecosystem structure and carbon allocation.

## Quantifying Mycelial Biomass

### Douglas Fir Forest Estimates

In typical Pacific Northwest Douglas fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*) forests:
- Mycelial biomass in the topsoil approaches 10% of total ecosystem biomass
- This estimate is considered conservative, as it does not fully account for endomycorrhizal fungi and yeast-like organisms in the topsoil
- The actual mycelial contribution may be substantially higher when considering the full depth of the rooting zone and the three-dimensional extent of mycelial networks

### Why Biomass Estimates Are Difficult

Accurate quantification of mycelial biomass faces inherent methodological challenges:
- Mycelium is distributed throughout soil matrix and cannot be easily separated
- Hyphal diameters are microscopic (typically 2-10 μm) but networks extend across meters
- Biomass fluctuates seasonally with moisture and temperature
- Living and dead hyphae are difficult to distinguish without vital staining
- Standard soil sampling methods fragment and underestimate mycelial networks

### Comparison with Other Organisms

To contextualize the mycelial biomass figure:
- Total aboveground tree biomass dominates forest carbon accounting in standard forestry
- Fine root biomass (tree + fungal) is typically estimated at 5-20% of aboveground biomass
- Mycelial biomass alone may rival or exceed fine root biomass of host trees
- Bacterial biomass in forest soils is typically 1-3 orders of magnitude lower than fungal biomass
- Soil faunal biomass (earthworms, arthropods) is negligible in comparison

## The Mycelial Network as Living Infrastructure

### Network Architecture

Mycelial networks in forest topsoil function as a distributed living infrastructure:
- Individual mycelial mats can cover thousands of square meters
- Networks connect multiple trees, facilitating resource sharing between individuals
- The system operates as both a transport network (water, nutrients, carbon) and a communication network (chemical signals)
- Network connectivity varies seasonally, with peak connectivity during moist periods

### Carbon Storage

Mycelium represents a significant and often overlooked carbon pool:
- Fungal cell walls contain chitin, a durable polymer that resists decomposition
- Turnover rates for mycelial biomass range from months to years, depending on species and conditions
- Dead hyphae contribute to stable soil organic matter pools
- Glomalin — a glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi — persists in soil for decades and contributes significantly to soil carbon sequestration

### The Hidden Forest

The concept of a "hidden forest" beneath the visible one has important implications:
- The belowground fungal network processes the majority of ecosystem nutrient fluxes
- Tree-to-tree carbon transfer through common mycorrhizal networks has been demonstrated isotopically
- The fungal network may determine which tree seedlings survive and which die
- Forest management that ignores the mycelial component is managing only the visible fraction of the ecosystem

## Mycorrhizal Contribution to Mycelial Biomass

### Ectomycorrhizal Networks

In Douglas fir forests, ectomycorrhizal fungi dominate the mycelial biomass:
- Short roots of Douglas fir are almost invariably colonized by ectomycorrhizal fungi
- The fungal mantle and Hartig net add significant biomass at the root-soil interface
- Extramatrical mycelium extends centimeters to meters from root surfaces
- Multiple fungal species typically associate with a single tree, creating a diverse network

### Endomycorrhizal Contribution

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) also contribute substantially:
- AMF associate with an estimated 80% of plant species globally
- Their fine hyphal networks are difficult to quantify but ubiquitous in topsoil
- Understory plants, shrubs, and herbs in the forest rely primarily on AMF associations
- The endomycorrhizal component may substantially increase total mycelial biomass beyond the 10% ectomycorrhizal estimate

### Saprophytic Contribution

Saprophytic fungi contribute mycelial biomass primarily through:
- Wood-decomposing basidiomycetes growing on coarse woody debris
- Leaf litter decomposers in the organic horizon
- Soil-inhabiting ascomycetes and zygomycetes
- Their contribution varies with the availability of dead organic material

## Factors Affecting Mycelial Biomass

### Environmental Controls

- **Moisture**: Mycelial growth is water-limited; biomass peaks during wet seasons
- **Temperature**: Most forest fungi are mesophilic, with optimal growth at 15-25°C
- **Soil pH**: Mycorrhizal fungi have specific pH requirements; acidification reduces diversity
- **Nutrient availability**: Both excess nitrogen and phosphorus limitation alter mycelial biomass
- **Root exudates**: Plant-derived carbon compounds stimulate mycorrhizal mycelial growth

### Disturbance Effects

- **Timber harvest**: Removes the primary carbon source (roots) for mycorrhizal fungi
- **Soil compaction**: Physically disrupts mycelial networks and reduces porosity
- **Fire**: Can eliminate surface mycelial networks, though some species survive underground
- **Pollution**: Acid rain and heavy metals reduce mycelial biomass and diversity
- **Invasive species**: Can replace native mycorrhizal networks with less diverse associations

## Implications for Forest Management

### Carbon Accounting

Standard forest carbon inventories typically ignore mycelial biomass:
- Including mycelial carbon would significantly increase estimates of forest carbon storage
- Soil carbon monitoring protocols should account for the fungal contribution
- Forest management practices that maintain mycelial networks enhance long-term carbon sequestration

### Sustainable Harvest Practices

Understanding mycelial biomass changes management recommendations:
- Clear-cutting eliminates mycorrhizal networks, requiring decades for recovery
- Selective harvesting preserves root systems and associated mycelial networks
- Retaining coarse woody debris supports saprophytic fungal biomass
- Reduced-impact logging techniques minimize soil compaction and network disruption

### Reforestation

- Inoculation of seedlings with mycorrhizal fungi improves establishment success
- Natural mycelial networks facilitate seedling recruitment near mature trees
- Site preparation that preserves existing mycelium accelerates reforestation
- The choice of tree species affects the type and diversity of mycorrhizal networks that develop
