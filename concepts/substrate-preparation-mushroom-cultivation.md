# Substrate Preparation for Mushroom Cultivation

The substrate is the material on which mushroom mycelium grows and from
which it derives its nutrition. Proper substrate preparation is
fundamental to successful cultivation. The right substrate provides the
correct balance of carbon, nitrogen, moisture, and structure for the
target species.

## Nutritional Requirements

Mushroom mycelium requires three primary nutritional components:

### Carbon Sources

Carbohydrates form the bulk of the substrate. Cellulose, hemicellulose,
and lignin from plant materials serve as the primary carbon and energy
sources. Different mushroom species have evolved to degrade different
plant polymers. Oyster mushrooms aggressively break down cellulose,
while shiitake is better adapted to lignin degradation.

### Nitrogen Sources

Nitrogen is required for protein synthesis and enzyme production. The
carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N ratio) of the substrate significantly
affects growth speed and yield. Most cultivated mushrooms prefer C:N
ratios between 25:1 and 40:1. Grain spawn typically provides the
initial nitrogen boost, while the bulk substrate supplies carbon.

### Mineral Supplementation

Calcium (from gypsum), phosphorus (from bran or phosphate), and
potassium support healthy mycelial growth. Gypsum (calcium sulfate) is
added to most substrates at 2 to 5 percent by weight to buffer pH and
provide calcium without raising the pH like lime would.

## Common Substrate Types

### Straw

Wheat straw is the most widely used substrate for oyster mushroom
cultivation worldwide. It is inexpensive, widely available, and has an
ideal structure for mycelial colonization. Straw should be chopped to
2 to 5 centimeter lengths to improve colonization speed and packing
density.

Preparation involves pasteurization by soaking in hydrated lime water
(alkaline pasteurization) or by hot water treatment at 65 to 80
degrees Celsius for 1 to 2 hours. After pasteurization, the straw is
drained to approximately 65 to 70 percent moisture content and packed
into containers or bags.

### Hardwood Sawdust

Hardwood sawdust from oak, beech, maple, or alder is the standard
substrate for shiitake, lion's mane, maitake, and reishi cultivation.
Softwood sawdust is generally avoided because the resins and phenolic
compounds inhibit mycelial growth, though some species tolerate it.

The sawdust is typically supplemented with 10 to 20 percent wheat bran
or rice bran to increase nitrogen content and speed colonization. The
mixture is hydrated to 60 to 65 percent moisture content and sterilized
in an autococlave or pressure cooker at 15 PSI for 90 to 120 minutes.

### Wood Chips and Logs

Whole log cultivation is the traditional method for shiitake and reishi.
Hardwood logs 10 to 20 centimeters in diameter are inoculated by
drilling holes and inserting spawn plugs. The logs colonize over 6 to
18 months and then fruit naturally in response to seasonal temperature
changes and rainfall.

### Grain Spawn Substrates

Grain (rye, wheat, millet, or brown rice) serves as the medium for
expanding mycelium before it is transferred to bulk substrates. Rye is
considered the best all-around grain for spawn production because its
kernel size and nutrient profile promote vigorous growth. Millet is
preferred when many inoculation points are needed because its small
size provides more individual colonization sites per volume.

Grain spawn preparation involves soaking or boiling the grain, draining
to achieve approximately 50 percent moisture content, adding a small
amount of gypsum, and sterilizing at 15 PSI for 90 minutes.

### Supplemented Substrates

Adding nitrogen-rich supplements to base substrates dramatically
increases yield but also increases contamination risk. Supplemented
substrates require full sterilization (autoclaving) rather than simple
pasteurization. Common supplements include wheat bran, rice bran, soy
hulls, and coffee grounds.

The "master's mix" is a popular high-yield formulation: 50 percent
hardwood sawdust, 50 percent soy hulls, hydrated to 60 to 65 percent
moisture. This formulation can produce biological efficiencies exceeding
100 percent (yielding more fresh mushrooms than the dry weight of the
substrate).

### Coffee Grounds

Spent coffee grounds are an excellent substrate for oyster mushrooms.
They are already pasteurized during the brewing process and have a
favorable C:N ratio and moisture content. Coffee grounds should be mixed
50/50 with straw or sawdust to prevent compaction and used within 24
hours of brewing to avoid bacterial fermentation.

## Moisture Content

The target moisture content varies by substrate type but generally falls
between 55 and 75 percent. The squeeze test provides a practical field
measurement: the substrate should hold together when squeezed firmly but
not drip water. Excess moisture promotes anaerobic conditions and
bacterial growth; insufficient moisture slows or prevents colonization.

## pH

Most cultivated mushrooms prefer slightly acidic conditions, with an
optimal pH range of 5.5 to 6.5. During colonization, mycelium produces
organic acids that naturally lower the substrate pH. Starting with a
slightly higher pH (6.0 to 6.5) accounts for this natural acidification.

Gypsum is the preferred pH buffer because it provides calcium without
significantly raising pH. Hydrated lime is used for alkaline
pasteurization of straw, raising the pH to 12 or higher to kill
contaminants before it gradually returns to neutral during drainage.
