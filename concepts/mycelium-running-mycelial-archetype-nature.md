# The Mycelial Archetype in Nature

The mycelial archetype, a branching, netlike organizational pattern,
can be seen throughout the universe: in the patterns of hurricanes,
dark matter, neural networks, and the Internet. Nature tends to build
upon its successes, and the mycelial form represents one of the most
successful and energy-efficient architectural strategies ever evolved.
This pattern may not be merely a coincidence but rather a consequence
of fundamental physical laws that favor threadlike, interconnected
structures.

## Mycelium as Nature's Internet

The mycelial network is composed of a membrane of interweaving,
continuously branching cell chains, only one cell wall thick.
Interlacing mosaics of mycelium infuse habitats with information-
sharing membranes. These membranes are aware, react to change, and
collectively have the long-term health of the host environment in
mind. The mycelium stays in constant molecular communication with
its environment, devising diverse enzymatic and chemical responses
to complex challenges.

When the Internet was designed, its weblike structure maximized the
pooling of data and computational power while minimizing critical
points upon which the system is dependent. The structure of the
Internet is simply an archetypal form, the inevitable consequence of
a previously proven evolutionary model, which is also seen in the
human brain. Diagrams of computer networks bear resemblance to both
mycelium and neurological arrays in the mammalian brain.

## Evolutionary Significance

Animals are more closely related to fungi than to any other kingdom.
More than 600 million years ago we shared a common ancestry. Fungi
evolved a means of externally digesting food by secreting acids and
enzymes into their immediate environs and then absorbing nutrients
using netlike cell chains. Fungi marched onto land more than a
billion years ago. Many fungi partnered with plants, which largely
lacked these digestive juices. Mycologists believe that this alliance
allowed plants to inhabit land around 700 million years ago.

One evolutionary branch of fungi led to the development of animals.
The branch of fungi leading to animals evolved to capture nutrients
by surrounding their food with cellular sacs, essentially primitive
stomachs. The branch that remained fungal retained its netlike
mycelial architecture, an approach that has proven extraordinarily
successful over geological time scales.

## Cosmic and Universal Patterns

The architecture of mycelium resembles patterns predicted in string
theory, and astrophysicists theorize that the most energy-conserving
forms in the universe will be organized as threads of matter-energy.
The arrangement of these strings resembles the architecture of
mycelium. Spiral galaxies conform to the same archetypal pattern as
hurricanes and mycelium. This convergence of form across such vastly
different scales, from subatomic to cosmic, suggests that the mycelial
pattern represents a fundamental organizing principle of nature.

## Mycelium in the Web of Life

These fungal fabrics run through the top few inches of virtually all
landmasses that support life, sharing the soil with legions of other
organisms. If you were a tiny organism in a forest's soil, you would
be enmeshed in a carnival of activity, with mycelium constantly moving
through subterranean landscapes like cellular waves, through dancing
bacteria and swimming protozoa with nematodes racing like whales
through a microcosmic sea of life. Fungi outnumber plants at a ratio
of at least 6 to 1.

Year-round, fungi decompose and recycle plant debris, filter microbes
and sediments from runoff, and restore soil. Mushrooms themselves are
nourishment for worms, insects, mammals, bacteria, and other parasitic
fungi. The occurrence and decomposition of a mushroom pre-determines
the nature and composition of downstream populations in its habitat
niche. Wherever a catastrophe creates a field of debris, many fungi
respond with waves of mycelium.

## Scale and Longevity

These networks not only survive, but sometimes expand to thousands
of acres in size, achieving the greatest mass of any individual
organism on this planet. That mycelia can spread enormous cellular
mats across thousands of acres is a testimonial to a successful and
versatile evolutionary strategy. One honey mushroom (Armillaria
ostoyae) mycelial mat found on a mountaintop in Oregon covers more
than 2,400 acres and is possibly more than 2,200 years old. What
makes mushroom mycelia different from mold fungi is that some mushroom
species can grow into massive membranes, thousands of acres in size,
hundreds of tons in mass, and thousands of years old.

## Key Principles

- Archetypal form: The branching net pattern recurs across
  scales from mycelium to galaxies as an energy-efficient
  organizational strategy
- Information sharing: Mycelial membranes function as
  biological networks that sense and respond to environmental
  change at the molecular level
- Evolutionary success: The one-cell-wall-thick mycelial
  architecture has persisted for over a billion years
- Universal connectivity: A single cubic inch of topsoil
  contains enough fungal cells to stretch more than 8 miles

## Applications

- Biomimetic design of computer networks and distributed systems
  based on mycelial redundancy and resilience patterns
- Ecological monitoring using mycelial networks as indicators of
  environmental health and change
- Understanding fungal networks as models for sustainable
  infrastructure and resource distribution systems
- Inspiring architectural and engineering solutions through
  mycelial branching patterns
