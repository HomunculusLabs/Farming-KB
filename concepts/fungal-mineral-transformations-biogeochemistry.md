# Fungal Mineral Transformations and Biogeochemical Cycles

Fungi play a central role in the transformation of rocks and minerals, driving processes collectively known as geomycology. These mineral transformations are fundamental to biogeochemical cycling — the movement of elements through the biosphere, lithosphere, hydrosphere, and atmosphere. As decomposer organisms, plant pathogens, and mutualistic symbionts, fungi influence carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and metal cycles at every scale from individual soil particles to global ecosystems.

## Geomycology: An Overview

Geomycology is the study of fungal interactions with minerals and geological substrates. Rocks and minerals represent a vast reservoir of essential elements including Na, K, Mg, Ca, Mn, Mo, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, and crucially, phosphorus. All these elements must be released into bioavailable forms through weathering — the physical, chemical, and biological breakdown of rock substrates.

Fungi have been components of terrestrial microbial communities since the Ordovician period (480–460 million years ago), inhabiting deserts, metal-rich habitats, and hypersaline environments. Their filamentous growth habit, high surface-area-to-mass ratio, and ability to translocate nutrients within [[fungal-ecology-decomposition-cycles]] is especially significant in forest soils where mycorrhizal fungi dominate microbial biomass.

The precipitation of carbonates, phosphates, and hydroxides by fungi increases soil aggregation and organic matter stabilization. ## Fungal Weathering and the Carbon Cycle

Fungal weathering of minerals has significant implications for the global carbon cycle. The production of organic acids (particularly oxalic acid) during mineral dissolution creates a pathway for carbon transfer from the biosphere to the geosphere. When oxalate is oxidized by fungal enzymes or soil bacteria, it releases CO2 but can also precipitate calcium carbonate if calcium is available — a process called the oxalate-carbonate pathway. This pathway, studied extensively in tropical soils and karst environments, represents a net carbon sink: atmospheric CO2 is fixed by plants, transferred to soil through root exudates and fungal metabolites, and ultimately sequestered as stable carbonate minerals. Some estimates suggest this pathway could sequester several tons of carbon per hectare per year in suitable environments, making it a potentially significant but often overlooked component of terrestrial carbon budgets.

## Fungi in Extreme Environments

Fungi have been isolated from some of the most extreme environments on Earth, including Antarctic soils, deep-sea sediments, hot springs, acid mine drainage, and the interiors of nuclear reactors. These extremophilic fungi demonstrate remarkable metabolic versatility and stress tolerance. Radioresistant fungi such as *Wallemia* and *Cladosporium* species have been found growing inside the Chernobyl reactor building, where they appear to harness ionizing radiation through melanin-mediated energy transduction — a process analogous to photosynthesis but using radiation rather than light. In acidic mine drainage environments, fungi tolerate pH values below 2 and high concentrations of dissolved metals, contributing to the formation of iron-rich precipitates that can be harvested for metal recovery. The study of extremophilic fungi has practical applications in biotechnology, including the discovery of novel enzymes, antibiotics, and bioremediation agents adapted to harsh conditions.

## Fungal Interactions with Heavy Metals in Mining Environments

Mining activities create extreme environments characterized by high concentrations of toxic metals, low pH, and nutrient-poor substrates. Fungi are among the first organisms to colonize mine tailings and waste rock piles, initiating biological succession that eventually leads to soil development. Metal-tolerant fungi accumulate heavy metals in their biomass through biosorption to cell wall components (chitin, glucans, melanin) and intracellular sequestration mechanisms. Species of *Aspergillus*, *Penicillium*, and *Trichoderma* have been isolated from mine sites with tolerance to copper, zinc, lead, cadmium, and arsenic at concentrations lethal to most organisms. Some fungi actively precipitate metals as insoluble oxalates, phosphates, or sulfides, effectively immobilizing contaminants and preventing their leaching into groundwater. These capabilities are being harnessed in bioremediation strategies: fungal inoculation of mine tailings can accelerate the formation of stable soil-like substrates, reduce metal mobility, and create conditions favorable for plant establishment. The combination of fungal weathering (which releases nutrients from mineral substrates) and fungal metal immobilization (which reduces toxicity) makes fungi uniquely suited as pioneer organisms for the ecological restoration of contaminated mining landscapes.

## Lithic Fungi and Rock Coatings

Rock surfaces in diverse environments are often colonized by fungal communities that produce visible coatings, stains, and crusts. Lithic (rock-dwelling) fungi contribute to the formation of rock varnish — thin, dark coatings composed of clay minerals, iron and manganese oxides, and organic matter found on exposed rock surfaces in arid and semi-arid regions. While bacterial contributions to rock varnish have long been recognized, fungal roles in manganese oxidation and organic matter incorporation are increasingly appreciated. In urban environments, fungi contribute to the biological weathering of building stone, concrete, and masonry, producing discoloration, surface etching, and structural degradation. The ability of fungi to colonize and transform rock surfaces — from natural outcrops to human-made structures — underscores their significance as geological agents operating at the interface between the biosphere and the lithosphere.
