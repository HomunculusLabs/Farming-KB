# Anaerobic Conditions and Oxygen Thresholds in Compost Tea

## What Anaerobic Organisms "Breathe"

Most problematic anaerobic organisms in [[compost]] tea are **autotrophs** — meaning they use inorganic carbon (CO₂) or other oxidized compounds as their energy source rather than organic carbon. In practical terms, these organisms use:

- Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
- Nitrate (NO₃)
- Nitrite (NO₂)
- Sulfate (SO₄)
- Other materials containing oxygen in chemical (non-gaseous) form — what [[elaine-ingham]] calls "solid oxygen"

While these organisms can respire using these alternative electron acceptors, the real problem is not what they breathe but **what they produce** as metabolic byproducts.

## Why Plants Don't Tolerate Anaerobic Conditions

### It's Not About Oxygen Deprivation

A common misconception is that plant roots need oxygen directly. In fact:

- Plant roots can function fine in conditions that simply lack oxygen
- Remember that plants take up CO₂ and release oxygen — they don't "breathe" oxygen the way animals do
- Lack of oxygen alone is not the problem for plant roots

### The Real Problem: Toxic Byproducts

There are two primary reasons anaerobic conditions harm plants:

1. **Toxic metabolic byproducts** — anaerobic bacteria produce substances (alcohols, organic acids, phenols, etc.) that are directly harmful to plant roots and tissues
2. **Loss of beneficial competitors** — the beneficial organisms that normally outcompete and suppress disease organisms require aerobic conditions; when oxygen drops, the good guys die off and the pathogens take over

### The Competition Principle

Almost all disease organisms perform much better when oxygen is reduced, because the organisms that would normally outcompete them are no longer able to function. This is simple competitive exclusion — not a toxic effect.

Aerobic enzymes are generally better at capturing food resources than enzymes produced by facultative or true anaerobes. This means aerobic organisms grab nutrients faster, leaving less available for pathogens. There are exceptions (certain anaerobes from extreme environments like ocean floors and thermal pools are superior competitors), but other environmental factors keep these organisms out of typical agricultural soils.

## The Oxygen Cut-Off Level

### The Critical Threshold: 5.5–6 mg/L

Based on extensive observation of plant responses to compost tea, the safe minimum dissolved oxygen level is:

**5.5 to 6 mg of oxygen per liter**

Below this threshold, problems regularly observed include:

- **Leaf curl**
- **Root death**
- **Burning of plant tissue**

### Why the Threshold Varies

The exact cut-off depends on which organisms are present in the soil or compost:

- **Well-composted material** — pathogens have been killed during the [[composting]] process (either by proper thermal composting temperatures or by passage through earthworm guts), so there's less risk even if oxygen dips
- **Poorly composted material** — more pathogens survive, and any drop in oxygen gives them an advantage

The statement "it depends" is not useful in practice, but the 5.5–6 mg/L threshold provides a reliable working guideline.

## Fermentative vs. Putrefactive Anaerobes

### Fermentative Bacteria

When fermentative (rather than putrefactive) bacteria dominate in low-oxygen conditions:

- Directly toxic materials (alcohols, phenols) may not be produced in high quantities
- However, **low-pH organic acids** are still produced
- These organic acids **harm beneficial fungi, kill protozoa, and kill beneficial nematodes**

### The Slow Decline Effect

The damage from fermentative conditions is insidious — rather than an acute burn or kill of plant tissue, it causes a **gradual decline in soil health**:

- Beneficial fungi (all strict aerobes) are eliminated
- Protozoa that consume bacteria and cycle nutrients are killed
- Beneficial nematodes that control fungal pathogens are killed
- The organisms that combat major diseases are progressively lost:
  - **Pythium** (wilts)
  - **Phytophthora** (blights, mildews)
  - **Rhizoctonia** (root rots)

### The Backward Progress Problem

With fermentative anaerobic conditions in compost tea:

- You may get some short-term benefit from bacterial competition on leaf surfaces
- But in the soil, you are actively **going backward** — losing the organisms that provide long-term disease suppression
- The effort of making and spraying tea produces minimal benefit while degrading soil health
- This is why anaerobic teas cannot build soil health over time

## The Goal: Soil Self-Regulation

The ultimate objective of compost tea application is to **restore soil to a condition of health where ongoing intervention is unnecessary**:

- With good (aerobic) teas applied consistently, soil biology rebuilds
- Over time, weekly or even monthly tea applications can be reduced
- Eventually, [[soil-food-web]] becomes self-sustaining
- **This outcome is impossible with anaerobic teas** — they destroy the very organisms needed for self-regulation

## Nutrient Availability from Organic Sources

### Available Forms Matter

When evaluating organic amendments like kelp, the critical question is not what nutrients the material contains in total, but **what forms plants can actually take up**:

- **Nitrogen in kelp** — present as proteins and amino acids (organic forms), NOT as nitrate
- **Phosphorus in kelp** — present in organic forms within plant cytoplasm, NOT as phosphate or inorganic phosphorus
- Plants can access nutrients inside plant cells only after **microbial decomposition** breaks the organic material down into plant-available forms

This underscores the importance of having active soil biology — without the organisms to decompose organic inputs, the nutrients remain locked in unavailable forms.

## Antibiotics: Definitions and Misconceptions

### What Is (and Isn't) an Antibiotic?

From a regulatory and pharmaceutical perspective, an antibiotic has a **specific chemical action on the enzymatic or membrane function** of another organism. This is distinct from general antimicrobial effects.

### Honey and Osmotic Shock

Honey is sometimes claimed to have "antibiotic" properties, but this is a misconception:

- Honey kills microorganisms primarily through **osmotic shock** — its high sugar concentration draws water out of cells
- This is the same mechanism by which salt kills organisms
- If honey were classified as an antibiotic, then salt and most inorganic fertilizers would also need EPA registration as antibiotics
- **Osmotic shock ≠ antibiotic activity**

### Why the Distinction Matters

The practical importance of this distinction is regulatory:

- If natural compounds are classified as pharmaceutical antibiotics, they would require **EPA registration as pesticides or inhibitory substances**
- This would impose significant regulatory burdens on [[natural-farming]] inputs
- The mechanisms should be clearly understood and correctly described to avoid triggering unnecessary regulation

### True Pharmaceutical Antibiotics

To legitimately claim antibiotic activity, a compound must be demonstrated to:

- Destroy membrane function through specific biochemical interaction
- Complex with enzymes so they cannot function
- Disrupt enzymes involved in respiration or other critical metabolic processes

Simple concentration-dependent effects like osmotic shock do not qualify.
