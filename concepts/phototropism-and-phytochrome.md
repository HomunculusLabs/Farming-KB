# Phototropism and Phytochrome in Plants

Plants perceive light through multiple photoreceptor systems that serve
fundamentally different purposes: directional light sensing (phototropism) and
day-length measurement (photoperiodism). These two systems use different
wavelengths of light, different molecular mechanisms, and different parts of
the plant to accomplish their respective tasks. The discovery of these systems,
from Darwin's elegant experiments to the identification of phytochrome as the
molecular switch governing flowering, represents one of the great arcs of
botanical research and has transformed both basic plant science and practical
agriculture.

## Darwin's Discovery of Phototropism

Charles Darwin, best known for his work on evolution by natural selection,
spent the twenty years following the publication of On the Origin of Species
investigating plant movement. In his final book, The Power of Movement in
Plants (1880), co-authored with his son Francis, Darwin established the
experimental foundation for understanding how plants perceive directional light
— effectively demonstrating that plants can "see."

Darwin's key insight was that phototropism — the bending of a plant toward
light — was not a product of photosynthesis but rather of a dedicated light-
sensing system. In a series of simple but definitive experiments using canary
grass ([[Phalaris canariensis]]) seedlings grown in total darkness, Darwin and
his son demonstrated five critical findings:

1. Untreated seedlings bent toward a dim gas lamp within three hours, even
   when the light was so dim that "they could not see the seedlings themselves,
   nor see a pencil line on paper"
2. Seedlings with their tips removed did not bend toward light at all
3. Seedlings with opaque (lightproof) caps on their tips did not bend
4. Seedlings with clear glass caps on their tips bent normally toward light
5. Seedlings with lightproof tubes around their middle sections bent normally

The conclusion was unambiguous: the "eyes" of the plant were located at the
tip of the shoot, not at the bending point. The tip sensed the light and
transmitted some kind of signal downward to the midsection, which responded
through differential cell elongation on the shaded versus illuminated sides,
causing the plant to curve toward the light source. This represented the first
experimental demonstration of rudimentary vision in plants and remains a
landmark in the history of plant physiology.

Subsequent work by Julius von Sachs in 1864 established that blue light is the
primary color inducing phototropism. Plants are generally insensitive to other
colors with respect to directional bending. The blue-light photoreceptors
responsible, later identified as phototropins, are distinct from the chlorophyll
pigments used in photosynthesis and represent a separate, evolutionarily
ancient light-sensing system.

## Photoperiodism: Measuring the Length of Darkness

The discovery of photoperiodism emerged from a practical agricultural problem.
In 1906, farmers in southern Maryland noticed a new tobacco strain, later
named Maryland Mammoth, that grew to extraordinary heights — reaching 4.5
meters and producing nearly 100 leaves — but rarely flowered. Without flowers,
farmers could not harvest seed for the next year's crop.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of
Agriculture discovered the explanation. Maryland Mammoth was a short-day plant:
it flowered only when exposed to days shorter than a critical threshold. The
long summer days of Maryland kept it in continuous vegetative growth. When the
researchers artificially shortened the light period by moving plants to a dark
shed each afternoon, the plants stopped growing and began flowering. Farmers
in Florida found they could grow the variety for many months and it would
eventually flower in midwinter when natural day length was shortest.

Plants are classified by their flowering response into three categories:

- **Short-day plants** flower when days are shorter than a critical length
  (e.g., chrysanthemums, soybeans, Maryland Mammoth tobacco)
- **Long-day plants** flower when days exceed a critical length (e.g., irises,
  barley, spinach)
- **Day-neutral plants** flower regardless of day length (e.g., tomatoes, rice)

## The Red/Far-Red Switch: Phytochrome

A series of experiments around World War II revealed a counterintuitive
finding: plants measure not the length of the day but the length of the
continuous dark period. A brief flash of light in the middle of the night
could prevent short-day plants from flowering and could induce flowering in
long-day plants that would not normally bloom during short winter days.

Harry Borthwick and colleagues at the USDA in the early 1950s discovered that
only red light (approximately 660 nm wavelength) was effective in interrupting
the dark period. Blue or green flashes had no effect. Then came the remarkable
finding that far-red light (approximately 730 nm, barely visible at dusk) could
completely reverse the effect of red light. A pulse of red followed immediately
by far-red was as if neither had been applied. Red-far-red-red would induce
flowering; red-far-red-red-far-red would not. The system behaved as a
molecular on/off switch, and only a few seconds of illumination sufficed.

By 1960, Warren L. Butler and colleagues had identified the single photoreceptor
responsible: phytochrome (Greek: phyton = plant, chroma = color). Phytochrome
exists in two interconvertible forms:

- **Pr** (red-absorbing form): the default state that absorbs red light (~660
  nm) and converts to Pfr
- **Pfr** (far-red-absorbing form): the biologically active form that absorbs
  far-red light (~730 nm) and converts back to Pr

Pfr is the biologically active form that triggers downstream signaling cascades
affecting germination, stem elongation, leaf expansion, and flowering. In
nature, the last light of each day is enriched in far-red wavelengths (because
atmospheric scattering selectively removes red light at low solar angles),
converting phytochrome to inactive Pr — the plant's signal that darkness is
beginning. In the morning, red-rich sunlight converts Pr back to active Pfr,
"waking up" the plant. The plant effectively measures how long ago it last
received red light and adjusts its growth and development accordingly.

## Location of the Photoreceptors

A striking difference exists between the phototropism and photoperiodism
systems in terms of where in the plant the light sensing occurs:

**Phototropism** uses a localized apical sensor. The "eye" is at the shoot tip
(apex). Covering the tip eliminates the phototropic response entirely;
covering the stem does not. The tip senses the direction of light and sends a
hormonal signal (auxin) to the stem, where differential distribution causes
cells on the shaded side to elongate more than those on the illuminated side.

**Photoperiodism** uses a distributed leaf-based sensor network. Any single
leaf is sufficient to perceive the red/far-red light signal and regulate
flowering for the entire plant. If all leaves are removed, leaving only stem
and apex, the plant becomes completely blind to photoperiodic cues, even if
the entire remaining plant is illuminated. Phytochrome in the leaves receives
the light signal and initiates a mobile chemical signal (later identified as
FT protein, or florigen) that travels through the phloem to the shoot apex,
where it triggers the transition from vegetative to floral development.

This architectural distinction means that plants use fundamentally different
sensory arrangements: a localized directional sensor for guiding growth toward
light, and a distributed whole-plant sensor network for tracking seasonal time.

## Ecological and Agricultural Significance

The phytochrome system allows plants to integrate multiple types of
environmental information beyond simple day-length measurement. Plants growing
under a forest canopy receive light filtered through overstory leaves. Since
chlorophyll absorbs red strongly but transmits far-red, the ratio of red to
far-red light under a canopy shifts dramatically toward far-red. This shift
keeps phytochrome predominantly in inactive Pr form, triggering shade-avoidance
responses: elongated stems, smaller leaves, reduced branching, and accelerated
flowering — all adaptations to escape competition for light.

The practical applications of photoperiodism knowledge are vast. Greenhouse
operators manipulate day length to produce poinsettias for Christmas,
chrysanthemums for Mother's Day, and strawberries year-round. Chrysanthemum
growers keep plants from flowering during autumn and winter by providing brief
nighttime illumination, then trigger synchronized flowering by discontinuing
the light treatment approximately two weeks before the target market date.
Understanding phytochrome has informed the development of photoselective
greenhouse films that filter specific wavelengths to control plant height and
flowering without chemical growth regulators.
