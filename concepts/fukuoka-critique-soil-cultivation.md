# Fukuoka's Critique of Soil Cultivation

## Overview

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] considered the plow to be the single most destructive
invention
in agriculture. In "The Natural Way of Farming," he devotes extensive analysis
to
why cultivation — the turning and aerating of soil — is not merely unnecessary
but
actively harmful to long-term agricultural productivity.

## The Plow's History

The earliest agriculture consisted of burning forest cover and planting seeds
in
the ash-enriched soil. Cultivation began as an attempt to extend the productive
period
of these naturally fertile plots. Over time, it became an article of faith
that soil
must be worked before planting. Fukuoka argues this is a self-perpetuating
error:
cultivation degrades soil, which then "requires" further cultivation to restore.

## Physical and Biological Damage

Cultivation disrupts the soil in multiple ways:

- **Destroys soil structure:** The natural aggregation of soil particles
into crumb
structure is broken apart. This crumb structure, built by [[soil-moisture-management-and-root-growth]] and
microbial
activity, is what gives soil its water-holding capacity and aeration.

- **Kills soil organisms:** Earthworms, fungi, and beneficial bacteria are
disrupted
or killed by the physical action of plowshares and rotary tillers. These
organisms
are essential to [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] and soil health.

- **Accelerates [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] decomposition:** Turning soil exposes buried
organic
matter to oxygen, causing rapid oxidation and CO2 release. Fukuoka compares
this
to burning humus. The soil's organic reserves are depleted faster than they
can be
rebuilt.

- **Promotes erosion:** Exposed, loosened soil is vulnerable to wind and
water erosion.
The [[fukuoka-natural-protective-forests-appendix]] layer of undisturbed soil surface is destroyed.

## The Nutrient Fallacy

Conventional agriculture teaches that plants cannot grow without cultivated,
fertilized
soil. Fukuoka observed the opposite: plants grow abundantly on uncultivated
ground,
along roadsides, on mountain slopes. The belief that soil needs "loosening"
comes
from a misunderstanding of how roots interact with earth.

When soil is undisturbed, it develops a natural porous structure through
root channels,
worm tunnels, and microbial aggregation. This natural porosity is far superior
to
the artificial porosity created by tillage. Roots penetrate undisturbed soil
more
easily than freshly tilled soil because the natural structure has been developed
in place over years.

## Self-Cultivation by Nature

Nature cultivates soil continuously and more effectively than any machine.
Plant
roots penetrate and create channels. Earthworms burrow and aerate. Microorganisms
decompose [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]] situ, building humus. Fungi create networks that
transport
nutrients. This process operates 24 hours a day, every day of the year.
