## Anaerobic Conditions and Oxygen Thresholds in Tea Brewing

## Overview

Maintaining adequate oxygen levels during [[compost-tea-brewing]] is one of the most critical factors determining tea quality. When oxygen drops below [[ingham-dissolved-oxygen-critical-thresholds-compost-tea]], [[anaerobic-organisms-compost]] produce harmful metabolites that can damage plants, kill beneficial organisms, and undermine the entire purpose of applying [[compost]] tea.

## What Anaerobic Organisms "Breathe"

Anaerobic organisms — the harmful ones that should not be present in compost tea — are primarily **autotrophs**. In non-technical terms, they use:

- **Carbon dioxide** (CO2)
- **Nitrate** (NO3)
- **Nitrite** (NO2)
- **Sulfate** (SO4)
- Other materials containing oxygen in chemical (non-gaseous) forms — sometimes described as "solid oxygen"

The critical issue is not so much what these organisms breathe, but **what they produce** as metabolic waste products.

## Why Plant Roots Cannot Tolerate Anaerobic Conditions

### The Real Problem Is Metabolites, Not Oxygen Itself

A common misconception is that plant roots need gaseous oxygen. In fact:

- Plant roots can do fine in conditions that simply lack oxygen
- Roots do not need oxygen per se — plants take up CO2 and release oxygen
- **Lack of oxygen is not the direct problem**

The actual problems with anaerobic conditions are:

1. **Toxic metabolites**: Anaerobic bacteria produce substances harmful to plant roots (alcohols, organic acids, hydrogen sulfide, etc.)
2. **Loss of competition**: The beneficial organisms that outcompete disease organisms **require aerobic conditions**. Without them, pathogens "win" through simple competitive advantage

### Aerobic Advantage

Aerobic enzymes are generally better at grabbing food resources than enzymes produced by facultative or true anaerobes. This means aerobic organisms dominate in well-oxygenated environments and keep pathogens in check through competitive exclusion.

**Exceptions** exist — a few anaerobic organisms are better competitors than aerobes — but these are typically found at the bottom of the ocean or in thermal pools. Environmental factors (extreme pH, high reduced iron, high sulfate) normally keep them out of agricultural soils.

## The Oxygen Cut-Off Level

### Critical Threshold: 5.5 to 6 mg O2 per Liter

Based on extensive observations of plant responses to compost tea:

| Oxygen Level | Expected Outcome |
|---|---|
| Above 6 mg/L | Safe — beneficial organisms thrive, pathogens suppressed |
| 5.5-6 mg/L | Borderline — may still be acceptable |
| Below 5.5 mg/L | Risk zone — harmful substances may be produced |

**Below 5.5 to 6 mg/L**, production of substances that can cause:

- **Leaf curl**
- **Root death**
- **Burning of plant tissue**

### Not Always Immediately Toxic

The severity of problems below the cut-off depends on the **type of anaerobic bacteria** present:

- **Fermentative bacteria** may not produce the most toxic substances (alcohols), so damage may not be immediately obvious
- **Putrefying bacteria** produce the worst metabolites and cause rapid damage

However, even with fermentative bacteria, the outcome is still harmful:

### The Slow Decline Effect

Even when fermentative bacteria do not produce directly toxic materials, low-oxygen conditions in tea still cause significant problems:

1. **Low pH organic acids** are produced, which harm fungi
2. **Protozoa are killed**
3. **Beneficial nematodes are eliminated**
4. The net effect is a **slow decline in soil health** as you lose the organisms that combat major pathogens:
   - *Pythium* (wilts)
   - *Phytophthora* (blights, mildews)
   - *Rhizoctonia* (root rots)

### The Backward Step

Using anaerobic tea means you did a lot of work making and spraying tea, got a small benefit from competition for food resources on leaf surfaces, but **went backwards in the soil**. This is counterproductive because the goal is to build soil health so that over time you need fewer and fewer tea applications.

### The Aerobic Tea Advantage

With good aerobic teas, you should be able to **reduce and eventually stop spraying** — weekly or even monthly applications — because the soil returns to a condition of health where it supports its own biological defenses. This self-sustaining state **cannot happen with anaerobic teas**. This is why aerobic tea is essential.

## Factors Affecting Anaerobic Risk

### Pathogen Load in Compost

The cut-off level"s practical impact depends on how many pathogens and harmful organisms survived the composting process. The more pathogens killed during composting — through:

- **Proper heating regime** in thermal composting, or
- **Sufficient passes through earthworm guts**

— the less risk during the tea brewing cycle.

### Key Principle

Better compost = wider safety margin in tea brewing. Start with high-quality, properly composted material that has been through adequate thermophilic temperatures or vermicomposting.

## Nutrient Available Forms in Compost Tea

### Plant Material vs. Inorganic Nutrients

Understanding the form nutrients are in is critical. In plant materials like kelp:

- **Nitrogen is NOT nitrate** — plant material should not have high nitrate levels
- **Phosphorus is NOT phosphate** — it is in organic forms (proteins, nucleic acids, phospholipids)
- Nutrients in plant material are in the form of **proteins, plant cytoplasm, and other organic compounds**

### Why This Matters

Plants cannot directly access nutrients locked inside plant cells. The nutrients must first be **released through biological decomposition** — bacteria and fungi break down organic matter, [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] release nutrients through their feeding cycles, and only then do minerals become available for plant uptake.

This is why adding raw kelp or other plant materials directly to soil provides relatively little immediate nutrition — the organisms must first process the material before nutrients become plant-available.

## Antibiotics: Osmotic Shock vs. True Antibiotic Action

### The Honey Distinction

There is an important legal and scientific distinction between **true antibiotics** and substances that kill through **osmotic shock**:

| Property | True Antibiotic | Osmotic Agent (e.g., honey, salt) |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Specific chemical action on enzymes or membrane function | Removes water through [[fungal-fruiting-body-mechanics-osmotic-pressure]] |
| Legal status | Regulated by EPA as pharmaceutical | Not an antibiotic in pharmaceutical sense |
| Specificity | Targets specific biochemical pathways | Non-specific physical effect |

### Why This Matters

Calling honey an "antibiotic" could trigger EPA regulatory action requiring it to be registered as a pesticide or inhibitory substance. The same logic would classify salt and most [[ingham-inorganic-fertilizers-soil-biology-damage]] as antibiotics.

### True Pharmaceutical Antibiotics

A true antibiotic has a **specific chemical action** on the enzymatic or membrane function of another organism. To claim [[amanita-muscaria-antibiotic-properties-nanobacteria-fungal-medicine]] for a substance, you must demonstrate that it contains bioactive compounds that:

- Destroy membrane function, or
- Complex with enzymes to prevent their function

Honey and similar substances that "suck up water" and kill through osmotic shock do not meet this definition.
