# Kykeon and the Eleusinian Mysteries in Food of the Gods

In [[terence-mckenna]] devotes significant attention to the [[dionysian-cults]] to Eleusis itself. The Dionysian theme entered Greece from the south, from "island cultures with roots ten thousand years deep in the religion of the mushroom Mother Goddess," and from Asia Minor, "but via four millennia of incubation within Minoan civilization." The mysteries planted on Grecian shores at Eleusis were "the latest, last, and most baroque adumbrations of the great Archaic religion of the Goddess, cattle, and ecstatic intoxication by indole hallucinogens."

The Dionysian and [[mckenna-eleusinian-mystery-and-psychedelic-sacrament]] cults coexisted as "the last frail outposts in the west" of the Archaic partnership tradition. They were, from the perspective of the emerging patriarchy, "something wild, ancient, and potentially dangerous."

## The Psychedelic Hypothesis

McKenna notes that "not until 1964 did someone make bold to suggest that a hallucinogenic plant must have been involved" in the Mysteries. That person was the English poet Robert Graves, who argued in his essay "The Two Births of Dionysus" that Triptolemus's secret -- traditionally said to be the art of sowing grain -- could not have been grain cultivation, which had been practiced since 7000 B.C., some five millennia before Triptolemus.

Graves concluded: "Triptolemus's secret seems therefore concerned with [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-emerging-trend-europe]], and my guess is that the priesthood at Eleusis had discovered an alternative hallucinogenic mushroom easier to handle than the [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]; one that could be baked in sacrificial cakes, shaped like pigs or phalloi, without losing its hallucinogenic powers."

Graves further suggested that recipes in classical sources for the ritual Eleusinian beverage contained ingredients whose first letters, when arranged as an ogham (an Irish poetic cipher), spelled out the word "mushroom" -- identifying the secret ingredient. Graves "readily grants that 'you are at liberty to call me crazy,' but then goes on to defend his thesis very well."

## Wasson and Hofmann's Ergotized Beer Theory

The most developed version of the psychedelic Eleusis hypothesis was proposed by R. Gordon Wasson, gordon-wasson, and Carl Ruck, unveiled at a mushroom conference in San Francisco in 1977. Their theory held that the source of intoxication was not mushrooms directly but an ergotized beer brewed from [[ergot-fungus]] that infects edible grains.

Several lines of evidence support this theory. Grain was centrally important to the cult; the festival was a harvest celebration. Ergot produces powerful alkaloids capable of causing hallucination, triggering labor, and vasoconstriction. The purple traditionally associated with Demeter's robe may signify the distinctive purple color of ergot sclerotia, the asexual resting stage in the organism's life cycle.

In 1900, Sir Arthur Evans excavated vessels adorned with ears of barley in relief near the palace of Knossos, suggesting that "a kind of beer had preceded wine on Crete." Kerenyi believed that the small size of these vessels indicated they were used for a special kind of barley drink -- "the visionary sacrament of the Eleusinian mysteries" -- in rites "allegedly performed without secrecy at Knossos."

Wasson and Hofmann argued eloquently for the symbolic resonance of their theory: "Clearly ergot of barley is the likely psychotropic ingredient in the Eleusinian potion. Its seeming symbiotic relationship to the barley signified an appropriate expropriation and transmutation of the Dionysian spirit to which the grain, Demeter's daughter, was lost in the nuptial embrace with earth."

## The Alcibiades Scandal

Perhaps the strongest evidence that the Eleusinian sacrament was a tangible psychoactive substance comes from the scandal of 415 B.C., in which the Athenian noble Alcibiades was prosecuted for "having the Eleusinian sacrament in his home and using it for the entertainment of his friends." As McKenna notes, this makes clear "to even the most resistant skeptic that whatever the catalyst for ecstasy at Eleusis was, it was tangible" -- not merely symbolic or theatrical.

## The Unresolved Question

McKenna acknowledges that the Wasson-Hofmann theory remains "well-argued speculation" because no one has subjected it to what he calls "the acid test": actually brewing a hallucinogen from cereal grain infected with some strain of ergot. He also notes a problem: in documented instances of mass ergot consumption (ergotism, or St. Anthony's Fire), the effects were horrific -- gangrene, convulsions, hallucinations, and death -- rather than visionary. The key question is whether a specific ergot strain existed that could produce the desired visionary effects without toxicity, a question that remains unresolved.

## The Eleusinian Mysteries as Archaic Survival

For McKenna, the Eleusinian Mysteries are significant not merely as a historical curiosity but as evidence for his broader thesis about the suppression of partnership societies by [[mckenna-dominator-culture-and-suppression-of-ecstasy]]. The Mysteries represent a direct line of descent from the Minoan religion of the Great Goddess, one of the "last frail outposts in the west" of Archaic plant-based religion, proof that Western civilization's own ancestors practiced and revered ecstatic, plant-induced religious experience, and a counterexample to the claim that dominator values are natural or inevitable.

The eventual suppression and disappearance of the Mysteries -- like the parallel suppression of Dionysian rites -- marks for McKenna the final triumph of the dominator paradigm in the West, severing Western civilization from its own ecstatic roots.

## Archaeological Evidence from Eleusis

The archaeological site of Eleusis, located approximately 18 kilometers northwest of Athens, has yielded significant material evidence supporting the scale and importance of the Mysteries. Excavations conducted primarily by the Greek Archaeological Society beginning in the 1880s revealed a complex of structures including the Telesterion (the main initiation hall), the Anaktoron (the innermost sacred chamber), and various auxiliary buildings including stoas, treasuries, and fountain houses.

The Telesterion itself was rebuilt and expanded multiple times over the course of nearly two millennia, reflecting the cult's growing importance. The most significant rebuilding occurred under the Athenian statesman Pericles in the 5th century BCE, when the architect Ictinus (who also worked on the Parthenon) designed a massive square hall capable of holding thousands of initiates. The presence of seating tiers along the walls suggests that the initiation involved a ritual performance or spectacle visible to all participants, though the specific content of what occurred inside the Telesterion was protected by the strictest secrecy under penalty of death.

Grave stelae and votive offerings found at the site attest to the Mysteries' broad appeal across social classes. Unlike most Greek religious rites, initiation at Eleusis was open to any Greek-speaking person who was not guilty of murder — including women, slaves, and foreigners. This remarkable inclusivity contributed to the Mysteries' reputation as a universal religious experience transcending the normal boundaries of Greek society.

## The Persephone Myth and Its Agricultural Roots

The central myth of the Eleusinian Mysteries — the abduction of Persephone (Kore) by Pluto and Demeter's grief-stricken search for her daughter — is fundamentally an agricultural allegory. Persephone's descent into the underworld and periodic return to the surface mirrors the annual cycle of seed burial, germination in darkness, and emergence as a new plant. Demeter's withdrawal of fertility from the earth during her grief corresponds to the Mediterranean summer drought, when grain fields lie fallow and barren.

This myth-ritual complex predates the Greek classical period by millennia. Parallels to the Kore-Persephone narrative exist in Near Eastern and Anatolian fertility cults, particularly the Sumerian Inanna-Dumuzi cycle and the Phrygian Cybele-Attis myth. In each case, a deity descends to the underworld, causing fertility to fail, and returns to restore life. The persistence of this narrative pattern across cultures and millennia suggests that it encodes fundamental observations about the relationship between seasonal cycles, seed burial, and agricultural renewal — observations that would have been intimately familiar to the Neolithic farmers who first developed these rites.

McKenna argues that the psychoactive dimension of these rites — the visionary experience that accompanied the ritual dramatization of the myth — transformed what might otherwise have been a simple agricultural ceremony into a genuinely transformative religious experience. The combination of mythic narrative and chemical revelation created a synergy that neither element could achieve alone, explaining why the Eleusinian Mysteries retained their power and prestige for nearly two thousand years.

## Comparison with Other Mystery Cults

The Eleusinian Mysteries were the most prestigious but not the only mystery cult in the Greco-Roman world. The Cult of Isis, imported from Egypt, offered similar promises of salvation and personal transformation. The Cult of Mithras, popular among Roman soldiers, centered on the slaying of a cosmic bull and the ascent of the soul through planetary spheres. The Orphic mysteries promised liberation from the cycle of reincarnation through ritual purification and the recitation of sacred formulas.

What distinguished Eleusis from these competitors was its antiquity, its official sanction by the Athenian state, and the universality of its promise. While other mystery cults were often associated with specific ethnic groups, social classes, or gender restrictions, Eleusis was open to all and was explicitly protected by Athenian law. The Athenian state subsidized the cost of initiation for citizens and maintained the physical infrastructure of the sanctuary, giving the Mysteries a degree of institutional stability that other cults lacked. This state support also meant that the suppression of Eleusis by the Christian emperor Theodosius I in 392 CE represented not merely the closing of a religious site but the deliberate destruction of an institution that had been central to Western religious life for longer than Christianity had existed.

## The Role of Fasting and Purification

Initiates at Eleusis underwent a period of fasting and purification before the central rite, a practice that has pharmacological significance for the psychedelic hypothesis. Fasting for 24-48 hours before consuming a psychoactive substance increases its bioavailability and intensity, as the absence of food in the digestive system allows for more rapid and complete absorption of active compounds. The Eleusinian preparatory rites, which included ritual bathing in the sea, abstention from certain foods, and walking the Sacred Way from Athens to Eleusis, would have created physiological conditions optimal for the absorption of a psychoactive sacrament.

The specific food restrictions observed by initiates are also relevant. Classical sources indicate that initiates abstained from pomegranates, eggs, poultry, and certain fish — all foods that are rich in amino acids that might compete with the absorption of ergot alkaloids if present in the kykeon. Whether these restrictions were deliberately designed to enhance the sacrament's potency or evolved for other reasons, their practical effect would have been to maximize the bioavailability of any psychoactive compound in the ritual beverage.

## Modern Attempts to Reconstruct the Kykeon

Several modern researchers and experimental archaeologists have attempted to reconstruct the Eleusinian kykeon based on the available evidence. The standard formulation described in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter includes barley water, mint ( pennyroyal, *Mentha pulegium*), and possibly honey. Reconstruction experiments have focused on two approaches: brewing barley infected with carefully selected ergot strains, and preparing kykeon with the addition of other psychoactive substances known to be available in the ancient Mediterranean world, including opium poppy extracts and claviceps-containing preparations.

The difficulty of these reconstructions lies in identifying ergot strains capable of producing psychoactive effects (specifically, lysergic acid amide or ergonovine) without the toxic alkaloids (primarily ergotamine) that cause the symptoms of ergotism. Modern analytical chemistry has identified over 40 different alkaloids in ergot sclerotia, and their proportions vary dramatically between strains and growing conditions. The question of whether a naturally occurring ergot strain could have produced the desired effects without toxicity remains unresolved, though some researchers argue that the alkaloid profile of [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]] growing on barley under Mediterranean conditions may differ significantly from the strains that caused ergotism epidemics in Northern European rye fields.

## Enduring Significance

The Eleusinian Mysteries remain one of the most compelling case studies in the relationship between psychoactive substances and religious experience. Whether or not the Wasson-Hofmann ergotized beer theory is ever conclusively proven, the example of Eleusis demonstrates that Western civilization's own ancestors practiced and revered plant-based ecstatic religion for nearly two millennia. This fact alone challenges the assumption that psychoactive spirituality is a marginal or exotic phenomenon and supports McKenna's argument that the suppression of such practices represents a historical rupture rather than a natural progression.
