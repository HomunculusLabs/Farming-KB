## The Alcibiades Scandal

Perhaps the strongest evidence that the Eleusinian sacrament was a tangible psychoactive substance comes from the scandal of 415 B.C., in which the Athenian noble Alcibiades was prosecuted for "having the Eleusinian sacrament in his home and using it for the entertainment of his friends." As McKenna notes, this makes clear "to even the most resistant skeptic that whatever the catalyst for ecstasy at Eleusis was, it was tangible" -- not merely symbolic or theatrical.

## The Unresolved Question

McKenna acknowledges that the Wasson-Hofmann theory remains "well-argued speculation" because no one has subjected it to what he calls "the acid test": actually brewing a hallucinogen from cereal grain infected with some strain of ergot. He also notes a problem: in documented instances of mass ergot consumption (ergotism, or St. Anthony's Fire), the effects were horrific -- gangrene, convulsions, hallucinations, and death -- rather than visionary. The key question is whether a specific ergot strain existed that could produce the desired visionary effects without toxicity, a question that remains unresolved.

## Enduring Significance

The Eleusinian Mysteries remain one of the most compelling case studies in the relationship between psychoactive substances and religious experience. Whether or not the Wasson-Hofmann ergotized beer theory is ever conclusively proven, the example of Eleusis demonstrates that Western civilization's own ancestors practiced and revered plant-based ecstatic religion for nearly two millennia. This fact alone challenges the assumption that psychoactive spirituality is a marginal or exotic phenomenon and supports McKenna's argument that the suppression of such practices represents a historical rupture rather than a natural progression.
