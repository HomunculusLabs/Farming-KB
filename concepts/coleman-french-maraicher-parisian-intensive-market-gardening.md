# French Maraîcher System: Parisian Intensive Market Gardening History

## Overview

The French maraîcher system of intensive [[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]] was one of the
most remarkable horticultural achievements in history. Practiced in and around
Paris from the early [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]] through the early [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]],
this system produced fresh vegetables year-round on tiny plots of land using
horse manure for heat, glass cloches and cold frames for protection, and
intensive [[decomposition]], heat for
protected cultivation.

The climate of central France is equivalent to USDA Zone 8, with winter
minimum temperatures in the range of 10°F to 20°F, possibly warmer due to
the urban heat island effect. Despite this relatively mild winter climate,
the maraîchers developed elaborate systems of protection that allowed them
to produce tender vegetables like lettuce, radishes, and early carrots
throughout the coldest months. Their ability to ship fresh salads to
Britain all winter — while British growers produced nothing — was a source
of both admiration and consternation among English horticulturists.

## Horse Manure: Fuel of the System

Horse manure was the foundation of the maraîcher system. It served two
critical functions. First, fresh horse manure mixed with straw was used as
a heat source for hotbeds. When piled beneath cold frames, decomposing
manure generated temperatures sufficient to protect cold-sensitive crops
through the winter. The manure was also packed into the walkways between
glass-covered frames to provide additional warmth to the growing area.
Second, once the manure had completed its decomposition cycle and lost its
heat, it was shoveled out as finished compost — a crumbly, nutrient-rich
soil amendment that was considered the finest possible fertilizer for
vegetable production.

The quantities of manure used were enormous. Coleman reports that the
intensive Parisian system consumed between 100 and 400 tons of horse
manure per acre annually, depending on the number of hotbeds in operation.
This manure was delivered daily by the wagons that hauled vegetables to
market, creating a closed nutrient loop between city and farm. The
maraîchers' preference for horse manure over all other fertilizers was
shared by practitioners across Europe. Many growers of the era expressed
the opinion that composted horse manure was the only suitable manure for
vegetable production.

## Cold Frames, Hotbeds, and Cloches

The maraîchers used three primary structures for protected cultivation.
Cold frames were shallow, glass-topped boxes set directly on the ground,
used to harden off transplants and grow cold-tolerant crops through the
winter. Hotbeds were cold frames built over pits filled with decomposing
horse manure, which provided bottom heat that allowed earlier planting
and the cultivation of more cold-sensitive species. Glass cloches were
eighteen-inch-diameter bell jars set in rows over individual plants or
small groups of plants. Each cloche had to be individually vented on
sunny days by propping up one edge with a notched stick, and many
growers owned up to three thousand cloches, requiring an almost
unbelievable amount of hand labor.

Additional insulation was provided by one-inch-thick mats made of rye
straw, which could be rolled out over the glass covers on cold nights.
The combination of manure heat, glass protection, and straw insulation
created a [[cannabis-growing-environment-setup]] that was effectively several climate zones
warmer than the surrounding landscape. The maraîchers also used south-
facing walls, typically six feet high, which surrounded each garden and
provided both wind protection and a warm microclimate through solar
radiation absorption.

## Intensive [[succession-planting]]

The maraîchers practiced extremely intensive [[query-what-is-succession-planting-and-how-do-i-plan-a-succession-planting-schedule]] to
maximize production from their limited land. Multiple crops occupied the
same cold frame bed in a single year. For example, an early crop of
lettuce might be followed by carrots, which in turn were followed by
cauliflower transplants. The timing was precise: each crop was harvested
just in time for the next crop to be planted, so that no bed was ever
empty and every square foot was producing food at all times.

The access paths through the garden were only ten inches wide — too narrow
for wheelbarrow use — because every inch of ground was considered too
valuable to waste on pathways. Manure and compost were delivered to the
beds in woven willow basket backpacks called hottes, which had an extension
arching up and over the worker's head so the load could be deposited by
bending forward and letting the manure tumble out over the head. This
labor-intensive system required enormous skill and physical endurance.
