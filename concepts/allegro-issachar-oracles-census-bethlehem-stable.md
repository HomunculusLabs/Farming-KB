## Older Oracles of Issachar

### Deborah"s Taunt (Judges 5:16)

The Song of Deborah contains a barbed question aimed at the tribe of Issachar:
"Why did you lie between the sheep-folds?" Allegro resolves this as a Sumerian
name-phrase, *\*LI\_MASh\_BA(LA)\_ANTA\_TAB\_BA\_RI*, which he reads as "why are
you resting (sh-b-kh) in the pasture?" The oracle is not a tribal insult but a
double entendre — "resting" in the pasture evokes both the indolence of a
sheepherder and the languid posture of [[allegro-death-and-resurrection-in-the-mushroom-cult]] initiate reclining
between the two barrier-walls of a sheepfold, itself a visual metaphor for the
mushroom"s flared cap.

### Jacob's Blessing (Genesis 49:14f)

Jacob's deathbed oracle says: "Issachar is an ass, lying between the
sheepfolds…he saw a resting-place, and it was good…he put his shoulder to the
burden and became a slave at forced labour." Allegro unpacks several
interlocking puns here. The "resting-place" (m-n-w-h) plays on Na"iman
(n-ʾ-m, "be sweet"), a Semitic mushroom epithet. The word rendered "forced
labour," mas-ʿōbēd, Allegro traces back to an earlier mas-palakh — itself a
phonetic echo of the [[allegro-cross-and-mushroom-symbolism]] in the Issachar oracles is not purely verbal. The
"sheepfold" — two barriers arranged in an open V — mirrors the shape of a
mushroom cap seen from below. The tribal description carries an unmistakable
sexual charge: Issachar is a "mighty penis lying between opened legs." The
phrase "take up the yoke" or, in the New Testament, "bear his cross" (Greek
stauros, "stake") continues the same phallic-stalk imagery. The mushroom"s
upright stalk supporting its broad cap becomes a potent emblem of virility and
divine potency, linking the old covenant oracles to Christian Passion
typology.

## Census and the Bethlehem Story

### MASh-BALAG and the Lucan Census

The census theme carries directly into the New Testament. In Luke 2:3, Mary
travels from Nazareth to Bethlehem "because of the census" — Greek apographē.
Allegro reads this as a continuation of the MASh-BALAG "census" wordplay
already present in the Issachar material and David"s numbering. The entire
Bethlehem journey exists to set up the mushroom birth-story in the proper
location — the "house of bread" (Beth-lehem) echoing the mushroom as the
true bread of the Presence.

### Quirinius and Greek Mushroom Names

The Roman governor Quirinius (Greek Kūrēnios), credited with ordering the
census, is himself a wordplay target. His Greek name echoes Grunon and
Geraneion — known Greek names for [[hallucinogenic-mushroom-terminology-european-languages-emcdda-2006]], not for their
historical accuracy.

### The Stable and Manger (Luke 2:7)

Luke 2:7 places the newborn Jesus "in a manger, because there was no room for
them in the inn." Allegro reads the manger scene as another spin on the
Issachar rest-place formula *\*LI\_MASh\_BA(LA)\_ANTA\_TAB\_BA\_RI*, now
reinterpreted as "resting-place in an animal"s stall." Additional puns layer
on top: pitrāʾ, the Hebrew word for "mushroom," connects with Peter ("first-
born"), and the manger itself becomes a visual metaphor for the mushroom.
The sheltering canopy of the cap, held aloft by the stalk, sits above the
"cradle" formed by the lower volva half — a perfect manger in miniature.

### Euripides on Dionysus in the Manger

Allegro cites Euripides" *Bacchae*, where Dionysus is described as "tied where
the steeds are bound; let him lie in a manger." The Greek god of ecstasy and
the mushroom are already fused in classical tradition; placing the newborn god
in a manger is an established cultic motif long before the Gospel writers
adopted it for their own purposes. The Christian nativity thus inherits a
pagan template whose original referent was the fungal sacrament.

## Adonis, David, and Fact versus Fiction

### Adonis as Fertility Hero-God

Adonis is the prime fertility hero-god of [[egyptian-mushroom-symbolism]]" — a reference to a particular variety or
source of the sacred fungus. The story of enslavement and liberation, like the
Issachar forced-labour oracles, encodes the mushroom"s role as both burden and
saviour.

### How Much Biblical Tradition Is History?

Allegro poses the central question: how much of the biblical tradition, if any,
is genuine history? Over the centuries, he argues, characters were made "more
and more believable" — rough mythological figures were progressively clothed in
realistic detail, given plausible genealogies, situated in identifiable
geography, and attached to datable events. Figures like Jacob and Esau, Cain
and Abel, exist only within mushroom mythology; there is "little point in
arguing they were ever real people."
