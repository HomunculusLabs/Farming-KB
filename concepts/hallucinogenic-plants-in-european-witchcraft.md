# Hallucinogenic Plants in European Witchcraft

## Overview

Michael J. Harner's essay "The Role of Hallucinogenic Plants in European Witchcraft" argues that the seemingly fantastical elements of late medieval and Renaissance witchcraft—flying through the air, Sabbats with demons, transformation into animals—become logically consistent once the use of psychoactive plants of the nightshade family (Solanaceae) is taken into account. This concept page summarizes Harner's pharmacological interpretation of European witchcraft beliefs and practices.

## The Scholarly Debate

### The "Fiction" Theory

A prevalent position among historians and scholars of religion holds that late medieval and Renaissance witchcraft was "essentially a fiction created by the Church." This view argues that the Inquisition had an a priori conception of witchcraft and tortured accused persons until they produced confessions matching Church dogma. The seemingly impossible acts confessed to by accused witches—flying through the air, engaging in orgies with demons at Sabbats—are cited as evidence that the confessions were fabricated under duress.

### Modern Witches

Modern practitioners of formal witchcraft covens do not support the fiction theory through their practices. Contemporary "witches" engage in what they believe are traditional rituals, but they do not claim to fly through the air or consort with supernatural creatures at Sabbats. Their activities tend to be "sober and highly ritualistic." Neither modern scholars nor modern practitioners have generally comprehended the importance of hallucinogenic plants in historical European witchcraft.

### Harner's Counter-Argument

Harner contends that once the use and effects of natural hallucinogens are understood, "the major features of past beliefs and practices suddenly seem quite logical and consistent." The confessions of accused witches were not fabricated but described genuine experiences induced by psychoactive plant preparations.

## The Solanaceous Hallucinogens

### The Potato Family (Solanaceae)

"The single most important group of plants used by mankind to contact the supernatural belongs to the order Solanaceae (the potato family)." Hallucinogenic members of this family are widespread in both Old and New Worlds. Besides food crops (potato, tomato, chile pepper, tobacco), the family includes numerous psychoactive species.

### Key Psychoactive Species

- **Datura** (genus): Called by many names—Jimson weed, devil's apple, thorn apple, mad apple, devil's weed, Gabriel's trumpet, angel's trumpet. Used widely and apparently from ancient times in shamanism, witchcraft, and vision quest in Europe, Asia, Africa, and among American Indian tribes.
- **Mandrake** (*Mandragora*): One of the most famous magical plants in European tradition, associated with fertility, divination, and protection.
- **Henbane** (*Hyoscyamus*): Used in magic and medicine since antiquity.
- **Belladonna / Deadly Nightshade** (*Atropa belladonna*): Perhaps the most notorious of the witchcraft plants, widely used in folk medicine and magic.

### Tropane Alkaloids

Each of these plants contains varying quantities of atropine and the closely related tropane alkaloids hyoscyamine and scopolamine. These compounds have hallucinogenic effects and can be "extremely dangerous in their mental and physical effects," with toxicity that "can result in death."

## The Flying Ointment and Transdermal Absorption

### The Broomstick Symbol

The witch's broomstick is "actually a very serious and central aspect of European witchcraft." The European witches rubbed their bodies with a hallucinogenic ointment containing solanaceous plants. The witch then "indeed took a trip"—the witch on the broomstick is a representation of the imagined aerial journey to a rendezvous with spirits or demons, called a Sabbat.

### Atropine and Skin Absorption

"One outstanding feature of atropine is that it is absorbable even by the intact skin." Medical literature documents toxic effects produced by belladonna plasters. This transdermal absorbability is "of considerable significance for the study of shamanism and witchcraft"—it explains how witches could achieve psychoactive effects without necessarily ingesting the plants.

### The Staff as Applicator

The use of a staff or broom was "undoubtedly more than a symbolic Freudian act, serving as an applicator for the atropine-containing plant to the sensitive vaginal membranes as well as providing the suggestion of riding on a steed, a typical illusion of the tropane experience."

This pharmacological explanation resolves one of the enduring mysteries of witchcraft iconography: why witches were always depicted riding broomsticks. The broom was not merely a symbol but a practical tool for applying psychoactive ointments to mucous membranes.

## Historical Evidence

### Roman Times

The use of hallucinogenic plants in European magic was known "at least as early as Roman times." The practice was thus not a medieval innovation but had deep roots in European pre-Christian religion.

### Lady Alice Kyteler (1324)

In an investigation in Ireland, authorities found "a Pipe of ointment, wherewith she greased a staffe, upon the which she ambled and galloped through thick and thin, when and in what manner she listed." This account, from 1324, provides clear evidence of the ointment-and-staff method.

### Somerset Witches (1664)

Elizabeth Style testified: "Before they are carried to their meetings, they anoint their Foreheads and Hand-wrists with an Oyl the Spirit brings them (which smells raw) and then they are carried in a very short time." Alice Duke confirmed the testimony, noting the oil was greenish in color. Ann Bishop, the Officer of the Somerset covens, described having her forehead anointed with a feather dipped in oil.

### Bergamo (c. 1470-71)

A fifteenth-century Italian source states: "the witches confess, that on certain days or nights they anoint a staff and ride on it to the appointed place or anoint themselves under the arms and in other hairy places." The specification of "hairy places" suggests knowledge that hair follicles enhance transdermal absorption.

### The Magical Words

The Somerset witches used specific words when "flying": "Thout, tout a tout, tout, throughout and about." When returning, they said: "Rentum, Tormentum." These formulaic utterances served as verbal components of the ritual—incantations that accompanied the application of the ointment and the onset of its psychoactive effects.

## The Sabbat as Psychedelic Experience

### The "Trip"

Harner's central argument is that the Sabbat was not a literal gathering but a visionary experience induced by the flying ointment. What accused witches described—the flight through the air, the arrival at a remote location, the encounters with demons and other witches, the orgiastic behavior—were all elements of a psychedelic experience that felt subjectively real.

### Lewin's Testimony

The pharmacologist Louis Lewin (1924) wrote that magic ointments "produced effects which the subjects themselves believed in, even stating that they had intercourse with evil spirits, had been on the Brocken and danced at the Sabbat with their lovers." The hallucinations were so powerful "that mentally uncultivated persons believed them to be reality."

### The Erotic Component

"The hallucinations are frequently dominated by the erotic moment." Tropane alkaloids are known to produce sexual fantasies and sensations of physical pleasure, which would account for the reports of orgiastic behavior at Sabbats.

## The Inquisition's Role

The Inquisition, "at the cost of the torture and execution of perhaps hundreds of thousands of believed and real witches," has supplied the bulk of historical data on the role of hallucinogenic plants in late medieval Europe. While the Inquisition's methods were brutal and its conclusions were shaped by theological assumptions, the confessions it extracted contain descriptions of genuine plant use and genuine visionary experiences.

The tragedy of the witch hunts is that a genuine folk practice—the use of psychoactive plants for spiritual purposes—was reinterpreted through the lens of Christian demonology. What had been a shamanic tradition was transformed into evidence of devil worship, with catastrophic consequences for the practitioners.

## Connection to Shamanism

Harner explicitly frames European witchcraft as a form of shamanism: "Datura has been used widely and apparently from ancient times in shamanism, witchcraft, and the vision quest in Europe, Asia, Africa, and among American Indian tribes." The use of psychoactive plants to contact the supernatural is a human universal, and European witchcraft represents the European expression of this universal shamanic practice.

## Pharmacological Notes

### Toxicity

The tropane alkaloids in solanaceous plants are significantly more dangerous than the tryptamine alkaloids found in *Psilocybe* mushrooms. While psilocybin has a very high therapeutic index (it is virtually impossible to fatally overdose), atropine, hyoscyamine, and scopolamine can easily cause death. This toxicity may explain the fearful reputation of witches' potions and the association of witchcraft with poison.

### Effects Profile

Tropane alkaloid intoxication is characterized by:
- Dry mouth and skin
- Dilated pupils
- Increased heart rate
- Visual and auditory hallucinations
- Sensations of flying or floating
- Confusion and disorientation
- Amnesia
- Erotic fantasies
- In high doses: delirium, coma, death

The combination of flying sensations, sexual arousal, and visionary encounters with supernatural beings accounts for the core elements of the witchcraft experience as described in historical records.
