# Mycorrhizae in Aquatic and Estuarine Ecosystems

## Overview

Fungi are fundamentally aerobic organisms whose physiological functions are inhibited by inundation. Despite this constraint, [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] successfully colonize plants growing in aquatic, semiaquatic, and estuarine ecosystems worldwide. The degree of colonization is controlled primarily by water depth, duration of inundation, salinity, and [[phosphorus-availability]]. These mycorrhizal associations play important roles in nutrient acquisition, plant community establishment, and ecosystem restoration.

## Physiological Constraints of Waterlogging

Waterlogged soils are generally anoxic except at the water–soil interface. Anaerobic bacterial communities produce iron, sulfur, and manganese compounds that, together with reduced oxygen availability, impair both root and mycorrhizal growth (Khan and Belik, 1995). Gadgil (1972) demonstrated that 7 weeks of inundation reduced phosphate uptake by radiata pine and [[douglas-fir]], while 14–16 weeks of waterlogging rendered P uptake and succinic dehydrogenase activity of mycorrhizae negligible. Despite these constraints, Khan (1993) showed that *[[casuarina-cunninghamiana]]* roots became increasingly colonized by both [[arbuscular-mycorrhizae]] (AM) and ectomycorrhizae (EM) progressing from stream edge up the bank.

## Colonization in Aquatic Systems

### Prevalence

Approximately 50–100% of plants in aquatic or developing shoreline habitats harbor AM colonization of their root systems (Khan and Belik, 1995; Cooke and Lefor, 1998). Semiaquatic grasses maintain some level of mycorrhizal infection even at maximum inundation depths, though colonization decreases with increasing water depth (Miller, 2000). Water depth is the major controlling factor, outweighing differences among plant species.

### Phosphorus Interactions

White and Charvat (1999) investigated *Lythrum salicaria*, an emergent aquatic plant, and found that AM colonization increased significantly with rising phosphate levels up to a threshold of 1000 mg PO₄ L⁻¹, above which mycorrhizae failed to form. At low P levels, mycorrhizal infection had no positive effect on total biomass. At the highest P level, mycorrhizae significantly reduced growth (at which point no colonization was observed). At intermediate P levels, however, mycorrhizal colonization preferentially increased root growth over shoot biomass, elevating the root:shoot ratio.

## Mycorrhizae in Salt Marsh Ecosystems

### Global Distribution

AM fungi occur in salt marshes worldwide (Rozema et al., 1986; van Duin et al., 1989; Sengupta and Chaudhuri, 1990; Hildebrandt et al., 2001), including along the East Coast of the United States (Cooke et al., 1993; Hoefnagels et al., 1993).

### Species-Dependent Colonization

Colonization patterns in salt marsh grasses are strikingly species-dependent:

- ***Spartina alterniflora***: Dominant in high-salinity marshes; largely nonmycorrhizal
- ***Spartina cynosuroides***: Dominant in brackish marshes; frequently mycorrhizal

Both species occupy similar intertidal elevations and overlap in brackish water tolerance, yet differ markedly in mycorrhizal status. Reports of nonmycotrophy in *S. alterniflora* may reflect seasonal variation in colonization rather than true absence.

### Seasonal Patterns

The greatest fungal biomass and mycorrhizal colonization coincide with periods of active root growth during summer months (van Duin et al., 1989; Padgett and Celio, 1990; Mansfield and Bärlocher, 1993). This suggests a link between nutrient depletion during maximal plant growth and the development of mycorrhizae to enhance scavenging efficiency.

### Salinity Effects

Soil salinity reduces AM spore germination and hyphal growth (Juniper and Abbott, 1993). Hyphal colonization of *S. alterniflora* roots decreased under higher saline conditions, though a similar decrease was not observed for *S. cynosuroides* (McHugh, 2001). Cooke and Lefor (1990) showed that vegetation establishment on disturbed salt marsh was negligible without mycorrhizal inoculation, indicating that disturbance removes residual inoculum from sediments.

### Depth Limitations

Cooke et al. (1993) found vesicles in roots of *Spartina patens* and *Distichlis spicata* down to 42 cm depth, but arbuscules only to 37 cm, with significant reduction in abundance below 25 cm. McHugh and Dighton found few differences in colonization with effective depth (inundation duration) for either *Spartina* species.

### Nitrogen Assimilation

Mycorrhizal colonization of *S. cynosuroides* roots significantly increased total nitrogen assimilation by the plant, though no effect on shoot biomass or P content was observed for either species. In both species, inoculation resulted in more shoots per pot (increased tillering).

## Functional Significance

### Tillering and Soil Stabilization

Increased tillering from mycorrhizal colonization has been observed in dune grass (Gemma and Koske, 1997) and wetland rice (Solaiman and Hirata, 1998). This enhances lateral spread rates in field plantings and accelerates soil stabilization in restoration projects.

### Restoration Roles

Jasper (1994) identifies four key roles of mycorrhizae in revegetation:

1. **Enhanced plant establishment** through improved nutrition
2. **Maintenance of diversity** and alteration of plant competitive fitness
3. **Resource recycling** and increased ecosystem stability
4. **Soil stabilization**

### Salt Tolerance

Mycorrhizae may enhance oxygen uptake by host plants, improving resistance to salt stress (Hyde et al., 1998). *Aster tripolium* colonized by AM fungi had lower shoot sodium concentrations when grown in 300 mM NaCl (Rozema et al., 1986), though this could reflect dilution from increased biomass rather than direct exclusion.

### Phosphorus as Limiting Nutrient

In many salt marsh ecosystems, phosphorus—rather than nitrogen—is the primary limiting nutrient (Valiela and Teal, 1974), providing a possible evolutionary rationale for AM associations enhancing P acquisition. Buresh et al. (1980) showed that P-fertilized plants accumulated "luxuriant" P without growth increase, while N-enriched plants showed decreased tissue P with successive growth.

## Edaphic Factors

Brown and Bledsoe (1996) found that colonization of *Jaumea carnosa* was related to sediment nitrogen levels rather than redox potential, with higher colonization in channels and creeks where faunal activity increased aeration and litter decomposition. The benefits, costs, and controlling factors of salt marsh mycorrhizae remain poorly understood overall.
