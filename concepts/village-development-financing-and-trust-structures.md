## Village Development Financing and Trust Structures

The financial and legal architecture of a [[permaculture]] village is as important as its physical design. [[bill-mollison]] outlines a systematic approach to developing ecovillages that balances individual ownership with collective commons, using trust structures to ensure long-term [[permaculture-community-resilience]] and self-reliance.

## Development Process

Establishing a [[permaculture-village-development-trusts]] follows a careful sequence of steps:

1. **Select suitable land** based on climate, water, soil, and accessibility criteria.
2. **Prepare a subdivision plan** showing all allotments, commons, roads, and reserves.
3. **Plan staged development** if the local shire (municipality) requires building completion in phases.
4. **Establish pricing** based on roading, water supply, and sewerage infrastructure costs.
5. **Prepare a detailed site plan** and proposal for the village.
6. **Convene prospective buyers** through advertisement and obtain firm commitments.
7. **Obtain sealed permission** for subdivision from the local authority.
8. **Sell to buyers** using a trust fund for road, water, and site preparation.
9. **Reinvest profits** from cleared costs into future projects.

## Financing Models

Several approaches can fund the development process:

- **Self-funded developer**: The developer holds all capital for survey and planning.
- **Investor-funded**: The developer raises funds from investors for the proposal stage.
- **Buyer trust account**: Potential buyers form a trust to purchase and develop the land collectively.
- **Mixed model**: A combination of developer and investor funds, with trust funds established by developers that are released when the shire council fully approves the development.

## Dual Trust Structure

Mollison recommends two complementary trust structures that separate commons stewardship from commercial risk:

### Trust A — The Land Bank (Commons Trust)

Trust A holds common land and assets for the public good. Its key characteristics include:

- Holds assets for community benefit without taking commercial risks
- Leases or rents property to Trust B for trading operations
- Administers common lands, recreation reserves, and sites for future structures (schools, restaurants, machine shops, [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]] areas)
- Is governed by 3–4 elected trustees once the village reaches 10–12 residents
- The original developer acts as founding director (settlor) and should retire once sufficient residents are established

### Trust B — Trading Trust

Trust B handles all commercial and trading operations:

- Currently immune from company tax in many jurisdictions
- Reduces family tax obligations
- Enables a wide variety of enterprises under one organizational umbrella
- Can duplicate or triplicate itself to accommodate new enterprises and insulate successful operations from risk
- Handles financial systems such as leasing and lending
- Has Trust A as one of its beneficiaries

This dual structure ensures that **commons are protected from commercial failure** while allowing entrepreneurial activity to flourish.

## Title Systems

While cluster and strata titles provide privacy, negotiability, and autonomy to individuals and families—allowing titles and houses to be resold, traded, or transferred to Trust A—a land trust remains necessary to:

- Administer common lands and recreation reserves
- Manage sites for future structures
- Oversee conservation areas and productive landscapes

## Development Group

The developmental group works best as a small core of 2–4 people, each with specialized skills:

- Real estate expertise
- Design and planning knowledge
- Legal competency
- Surveying and engineering capability

Surveyors, road-builders, builders, and landscapers are typically locally available. Some contractors may work for equity in the project—either as village occupants or for resale at a later date.

## Handover and Design Evolution

The development group should transfer a **site design and user's manual** to the directors of Trust A, who then:

- Display and circulate the initial design
- Record changes and modifications
- Keep essential land areas clear for productive use

Mollison emphasizes that all initial designs are made to be changed. The challenge is to change the design for the better—a design is a starting point, not an end point.

## Optimal Village Size

Human settlements vary in their ability to provide resources, develop self-reliance, and foster neighborly behavior according to population size:

| Population | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| ~30 adults | Good starting point for social cohesion |
| ~100 income-producing people | Can support a significant village-based financial institution |
| ~500 people | Everyone can know each other; social affairs are manageable |
| ~2,000 people | Theft and competitiveness become more common; sectarian divisions emerge |

### The Neighborhood Factor

Mollison considers the **neighborhood factor**—knowing each other's names—to be primary for survival. This was demonstrated in the 1967 Hobart fires, where casualties in "anonymous" commuter suburbs were many times higher than in neighborhoods where people knew and cared for each other.

### Growth Strategy

The recommended approach is to:

1. Start small at about 30 adults
2. Build gradually to 200–300 people
3. Proceed slowly and by choice to 500
4. "Calf" into new neighborhoods or villages when appropriate

### Alliances and Confederation

Alliances of 200–500 household-size hamlets can form viable manufacturing or trading networks while maintaining a safe genetic base. Many traditional tribes of ~200 confederated into alliances of 5,000–7,000, sharing special products by trade and arranging out-marriages.

The **[[mondragon-cooperatives]] of Spain** initially grew large (3,000–5,000 members) but later restructured into cooperatives of 300–500 to preserve individual identity. Nevertheless, a group of such small cooperatives can manufacture any vehicle or machine if each produces a part—this is organized by the smaller cooperatives in the Mondragon system.

## Infrastructure Allotment

New villages need to reserve land and areas for future priority development. The following areas should be set aside:

- **Educational**: School, seminar, and workshop rooms; computers; library; artwork; crafts areas
- **Food processing**: Cafe, coffee shop, home baked goods, commercial surplus products
- **Industrial/Workshop**: Noisy/oily work, woodwork, metalwork, machine shop, repairs, bulk fuels
- **Retail**: Shops including plant nursery, visitor reception
- **Agricultural**: Dairy and [[faires-dairy-processing-cheese-and-butter]], domestic livestock housing, glasshouses, commercial and special crops
- **Energy/Water**: Methane generator, ferment tanks, sewage systems, biomass conversion, water cleanup
- **Accommodation**: Camp area for casual visitors, potential youth hostel

A general plan of these resources must be published and accessible to all participants.
