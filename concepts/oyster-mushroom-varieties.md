## Yield Conversion
In decomposing dry straw: ~50% mass liberated as CO2, 20% lost as water, 20% spent compost, 10% converted to dry mushrooms. This equals ~25% conversion of wet substrate mass to fresh mushrooms. Oyster mushrooms have 15-35% protein (dry weight basis).

## Critical Cultivation Notes
- Biological efficiencies often exceed 100% -- among the highest of all cultivated mushrooms
- Nitrogen supplementation (e.g., 20% alfalfa hay to wheat straw) boosts yields but increases contamination risk
- Lack of light causes coral-like malformations
- Strong light alters pigment: some strains darken, others pale
- Mushrooms that rot at maturity generate hundreds of primordia directly on the decaying fruitbodies
- Harvest timing is critical: P. pulmonarius grows so quickly that overripe mushrooms rot quickly if gas exchange is limited

## Containers and Growing Systems
Perforated plastic bags, columns, vertical racks, trays, and bottles all work. For P. pulmonarius, most economical growth is on pasteurized cereal straws rather than wood-based substrates. Vertical "totem" systems using straw columns are popular for small-scale commercial operations.

## Contamination and Pest Management
Oyster mushrooms are susceptible to Trichoderma (green mold), Dactylium (cobweb mold), and various fly species. Proper pasteurization, clean technique, and adequate fresh air exchange are the primary defences. The aggressive colonization speed of Pleurotus species is itself a defence against competitors.

## Nutritional Profile
Oyster mushrooms are rich in protein (15-35% dry weight), fibre, B vitamins, and minerals including potassium, iron, and zinc. They contain ergothioneine, a potent antioxidant, and beta-glucans with immune-modulating properties. Low in calories and fat, they are valued both for culinary quality and nutritional content.

## Strain Selection and Genetics

Selecting the right strain is critical for commercial success with oyster mushrooms. Within each Pleurotus species, significant genetic variation exists in growth rate, yield, fruiting temperature range, color, shelf life, and substrate preference. Commercial spawn laboratories maintain strain libraries and typically offer multiple strains per species. For indoor cultivation, strains selected for compact fruiting bodies, reduced spore production, and tolerance of high CO2 are preferred. Sporeless or low-spore strains of P. ostreatus and P. pulmonarius are especially important for worker health, as heavy spore loads can cause respiratory irritation and allergic reactions (mushroom worker's lung). Strain stability is maintained through dikaryotic tissue culture rather than spore propagation, which would produce genetic recombinants with unpredictable characteristics. Periodic strain evaluation through comparative fruiting trials is recommended to identify superior performers and detect senescence in older stock cultures.

## Environmental Triggering of Fruiting

Oyster mushrooms require specific environmental cues to initiate fruiting body formation. The primary triggers include a rapid drop in temperature (cold shock), increased fresh air exchange (lowering CO2 from >5000 ppm to 400-800 ppm), introduction of light (1000-1500 lux), and high humidity (95-100% for primordia formation). These triggers simulate the natural conditions of autumn weather changes that signal the fungus to reproduce. The cold shock requirement varies among species: P. ostreatus responds to a 10-15°F drop, while tropical species like P. djamor require minimal temperature change but respond strongly to increased air exchange. Light quality matters: blue spectrum light (450-495 nm) is most effective for promoting normal fruiting morphology. Insufficient light causes etiolation (elongated stems, small caps), while excessive light can cause premature cap darkening. CO2 concentration directly affects morphology: high CO2 during fruiting produces thick-stemmed, small-capped mushrooms, while low CO2 produces well-developed caps with short stems.

## Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling

Proper harvesting technique significantly affects both yield and shelf life of oyster mushrooms. Harvest when the cap margin begins to uncurl from parallel to perpendicular, before full expansion and spore release. Cut or twist the entire cluster at the base rather than picking individual mushrooms, which damages remaining developing fruiting bodies. Sharp knives minimize tearing and reduce infection risk at the cut surface. Post-harvest cooling to 2-4°C within 2 hours of harvest extends shelf life to 7-14 days. Humified packaging (modified atmosphere with 3-5% O2 and 10-15% CO2) further extends shelf life. Oyster mushrooms are highly perishable and continue to respire after harvest; proper cold chain management from harvest to retail is essential. Drying at 35-45°C preserves oyster mushrooms for long-term storage, though texture changes significantly compared to fresh. Freeze-drying produces the highest quality dried product but requires specialized equipment.

## Medicinal and Bioactive Properties

Oyster mushrooms possess several bioactive compounds of therapeutic interest. Pleurotus species produce beta-glucans (particularly pleuran from P. ostreatus) that have demonstrated immunomodulatory, anti-tumor, and cholesterol-lowering effects in clinical studies. Lovastatin, a statin compound naturally produced by P. ostreatus and P. citrinopileatus, lowers blood cholesterol by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase. The species also produces mevinolin and other secondary metabolites with antioxidant properties. P. eryngii contains ergothioneine at concentrations among the highest of any mushroom, and this antioxidant compound has been linked to reduced inflammation and cellular protection against oxidative stress. Research on P. ostreatus extracts has shown antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and potential antiviral properties. The nutritional value combined with these medicinal properties makes oyster mushrooms one of the most important cultivated mushrooms for both food and nutraceutical applications.

## Small-Scale and Home Cultivation

Oyster mushrooms are the most accessible mushroom species for home cultivation due to their aggressive growth, broad substrate tolerance, and minimal equipment requirements. The simplest method uses inoculated straw logs: pasteurized wheat straw packed into mesh bags or poly tubing, inoculated with grain spawn, and incubated in a warm, dark location for 2-3 weeks. After full colonization, cutting holes in the bag exposes the mycelium to fresh air and light, triggering fruiting. Ready-to-grow oyster mushroom kits using pre-colonized substrate blocks are widely available and provide an easy introduction to mushroom cultivation. Coffee grounds from cafes provide a free, already-pasteurized substrate that supports vigorous oyster mushroom growth. A bucket fruiting system using drilled 5-gallon buckets filled with coffee grounds or straw is one of the most productive and low-cost methods for home growers. Even experienced commercial cultivators often start with oyster species before advancing to more demanding varieties like shiitake or lion's mane.

## Bioremediation Potential

Oyster mushrooms possess remarkable abilities to degrade and detoxify environmental pollutants, making them a focus of mycoremediation research. Pleurotus species produce lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases — extracellular enzymes that break down lignin in wood and can similarly decompose a wide range of xenobiotic compounds. P. ostreatus has been demonstrated to degrade polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in contaminated soils, including benzo[a]pyrene and anthracene.

The white-rot fungi mechanism involves the same enzymatic systems used for natural wood decomposition, repurposed for breaking down aromatic pollutants with similar molecular structures. Research has shown that P. ostreatus can also degrade polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), organophosphate pesticides, and synthetic dyes from textile wastewater. The spent substrate from commercial oyster mushroom cultivation retains significant enzymatic activity and has been used as a biofilter material for treating contaminated water.

Paul Stamets popularized the concept of using oyster mycelium mats ("mycofilters") to intercept and degrade agricultural runoff, including coliform bacteria and excess nutrients before they reach waterways. P. eryngii has shown particular promise in degrading pharmaceutical residues in wastewater, including antibiotics and endocrine-disrupting compounds. The combination of pollutant degradation with edible mushroom production creates an economically viable model for environmental cleanup.

Practical applications include deploying inoculated straw bales or woodchip mats in drainage swales to filter stormwater, using spent oyster substrate as a bioremediation layer in constructed wetlands, and applying colonized substrate directly to petroleum-contaminated soil. Research at the University of Minnesota demonstrated that P. ostreatus could reduce E. coli and total coliform bacteria in runoff by over 99%. These capabilities make oyster mushrooms valuable tools in ecological restoration and sustainable agriculture.

The enzymatic systems of Pleurotus species are particularly effective because they operate extracellularly, meaning the enzymes are secreted into the surrounding environment where they can interact directly with pollutants in soil and water. This differs from bacterial remediation approaches that require direct contact between the organism and the contaminant. P. ostreatus mycelium can extend hyphal networks over large areas, effectively creating a distributed enzyme delivery system throughout contaminated substrates.

In practical permaculture and homesteading applications, spent oyster mushroom substrate from cultivation can be repurposed as a mulch or soil amendment that continues to provide enzymatic activity in garden beds, contributing to both soil health and passive pollutant degradation. Mycoremediation with oyster mushrooms is accessible to home growers because the species are easy to cultivate and the basic techniques — inoculating straw or wood chips and placing them in contact with contaminated material — require no specialized equipment beyond standard mushroom cultivation supplies.

Compared to traditional remediation methods such as excavation, chemical treatment, or thermal desorption, mycoremediation with Pleurotus species is significantly less expensive and less disruptive to the treated site. It can be implemented in situ without removing contaminated soil, making it particularly suitable for large areas with low to moderate contamination levels. The technology is most effective when combined with other bioremediation approaches and proper site management to optimize conditions for fungal growth and enzymatic activity.
