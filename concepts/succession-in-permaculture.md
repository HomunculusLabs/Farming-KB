# Succession in Permaculture

Nature shows us that a sequence of processes arise in the establishment of "new" systems on devastated landscapes such as basalt flows and ice-planed or flood-swept sites. Understanding and accelerating natural succession is one of the most powerful tools in the permaculture designer's toolkit.

## Natural Succession

In natural successions, grasses slowly give way to shrubs, which eventually give way to trees. The first living components are hardy pioneer species, which establish on damaged or impoverished environments. Thus we see "weeds" (thistles, Lantana) occupying overgrazed, eroded, or fired areas. These pioneer species assist the area by:

- Stabilizing water flow in the landscape
- Providing shelter for subsequent species
- Providing mulch as they die back
- Improving soil quality for their successors (the longer-term forest or tree crop species)
- Fixing nitrogen (in the case of pioneer legumes)
- Breaking up compacted soils with their root systems

## Working With Nature

We can actively assist natural succession, not by slashing out weeds and pioneers, but by using them to provide microclimate, nutrients, and wind protection for the exotic or native species we want to establish. The permaculture principle "Work with Nature, Rather Than Against It" applies directly to succession.

As Masanobu Fukuoka said: "If we throw nature out the window, she comes back in the door with a pitchfork." For example, if we spray for pest infestations, we end up destroying both pests and the predators that feed on them, so the following year we get an explosion of pests because there are no predators to control them.

## Accelerated Succession in Design

To enable a cultivated system to evolve towards a long-term stable state, we can construct a system of mixed tree, shrub, and vegetable crop, utilizing livestock to act as foragers, and carefully planning the succession of plants and animals so that we receive short-, medium-, and long-term benefits.

For example, a forest will yield:
- **First**: coppice and mulch material
- **Then**: pole timbers
- **Eventually**: honey, fruit, nuts, bark, and plank timber

This evolution occurs over a period of 15-50 years from a pioneer and young, or crowded, plantation to a well-spaced mature stand.

## Simultaneous Planting

Unlike the processes of nature, however, we can place most of the elements of such a succession in one planting, so that the pioneers, ground covers, under-story species, tree legumes, herbage crop, mulch species, the long-term windbreak and the tree crop are all set out at once.

So many species and individuals of each species are needed to do this that it is usually necessary to first create a small plant nursery to supply the 4,000-8,000 plants that can be placed on a hectare. While these are growing in their pots, we can fence and prepare the soil, and then plant them out to a carefully-designed long-term plan.

## Results of Accelerated Succession

Where this approach is used, as it has been by many permaculturalists on their properties, quite remarkable changes occur over two to three years:

- Mulch is produced on site for the long-term crop
- Weed competition, wind, and frost effects are nullified or moderated
- Cropping can be continuous as the annuals or herbaceous perennials effectively control unwanted grasses and weed species

For instance, radish or turnip planted with tree seedlings control grasses until the small tree provides its own grass control by shading.

## Using Livestock in Succession

A succession of animal species can follow each other through developing systems. A cow/sheep/goose/pig succession (or some such) over a period of years is common, choosing a succession of animal species following each other in the developing tree crop. This allows different animal species to perform different functions:

- Pigs to root and clear ground for planting
- Chickens to follow and clean up pest larvae
- Grazing animals to manage pasture between tree rows
- Geese to control specific weeds

## Succession in Soil Development

Soil improvement follows succession. The use of legumes in a similar proportion to that occurring in natural plant associations in the area is important at all stages of the succession. Ideal conditions for soil health include:

- Permanent pasture
- Forests
- Orchards with permanent green crop as mulch
- No-dig or mulched gardens
- No- or low-cultivation of field crop, or field crop between strips of forest
- Legumes integrated throughout

Under these conditions, soil mineral availability is made possible and soils do not lock up nutrients in oxides or produce pollutants.

## Establishment Sequence

The practical sequence for establishing a permaculture system follows a successional approach:

1. **Fencing or hedgerow** -- to exclude unwanted animals and define zones
2. **Soil rehabilitation by mulch** or loosening by tools
3. **Erosion control** -- swales, contour planting, ground covers
4. **Water supply** -- tanks, dams, swales before planting
5. **Zone 1 and Zone 2** first -- these support the household and save the most expense
6. **Nursery establishment** -- propagate all needed species
7. **Pioneer planting** -- fast-growing species for immediate soil protection
8. **Under-story planting** -- shrubs, herbs, ground covers
9. **Canopy planting** -- long-term tree crops and windbreaks
10. **Animal integration** -- introduce livestock as the system matures

## Key Principle

The golden rule is to develop the nearest area first, get it under control, and then expand the perimeter. A single perimeter will then enclose all your needs. Start with a nucleus and expand outwards -- this is the most successful, morale-building, and easily-achieved way to proceed.
