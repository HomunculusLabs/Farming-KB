# Desert Food Systems and Vine Integration

A staple food is defined as one supplying 50% or more of the diet when in season. Today, some 70% of the food in Western societies is supplied by just 8 staple crops, whereas no European family has access to 20–30 basic staple foods. A well-managed home garden and livestock situation can produce about 20 vegetables, 6–9 fruits, and 3–6 staples—well beyond the choice range of an average family.

## The Loss of Indigenous Desert Knowledge

The question of how to improve desert food yield is deeply tied to the history of colonisation. By introducing cattle and sheep, and releasing rabbits, the bulk of useful vegetation and almost every common marsupial or adapted mammal of medium size were destroyed. Indigenous ways of living and the profound knowledge of desert management held by tribal peoples were suppressed for an undeserved profit by a very few disintegrated pastoralists.

This makes no ecological, economic, genetic, informational, or social sense. People who managed deserts well for 10,000–40,000 years may have known a great deal about them. Both genetic resources and information on management have been destroyed by what Mollison calls "the-right-to-ruin" mentality claimed by private owners and governments.

Even the plants developed by the Papago and Hopi Indians are being lost as store foods replace desert foods. And yet desert plant lists show a present potential for several staple foods and an untapped potential for developing better cultivars of many desert plants.

## Fruit Tree Spacing

The spacing of fruit trees in Zones 1 and 2 can be as usual, but it is essential to provide high shade from interplants of tall thin-foliage legumes or palms, and to interplant fast-growing small and large woody legumes that either die out (pigeon pea), can be coppiced (tagasaste), or can be felled or ring-barked to rot to humus (many Acacia species).

Such well-planned systems are very productive, drought resistant, cool, and eventually humus-rich. They demonstrate how quickly a barren or hard soil area can be brought into production as a result of intensive biomass production and wastewater use.

## Vines in Desert Gardens

Vines have a key role to play in desert gardens. Correctly spaced and pruned, they provide both a productive crop and shade cover to mulched and watered gardens. They are also a key element in moderating climate in designed houses or in retrofitting uncomfortably hot homes.

### Vine Over Garden

Horizontal trellis bars at 1–2 metre spacing furnished with grape vines can throw a shade system over the greater part of the vegetable garden, relieving light saturation. The sides of this trellis can be more completely closed with herbaceous vines (beans, climbing tomatoes, yams, cucurbits).

It is more important to defend from the early (eastern) sun, as dew, distillation, and guttation moisture is important to plants early in the day, and soil temperatures then remain cooler for longer periods. The western side of the trellis needs fleshy vines, as great heat can build up on the western aspect late in the afternoon.

### Vines for House Cooling

Considerable comfort can be brought to over-hot houses by attaching trellis:

- Stand dense evergreen vine crop out from the west wall (Mikania, Dolichos, Pelargonium), and a vine trained above and out from any existing west roof.
- Grow vines (winter deciduous or summer leaf) in a screen fixed out from the eastern sun.
- These are well placed at the edge of a verandah if one exists. Washing down the verandah and spraying vines with water rapidly cools the shaded verandah areas.
- Build extensive closed trellis over the rear door on the shade side to provide a cool air source, preferably with a thick bark mulch below the vine and fine sprays to damp it down. Roof vents via the indoor ceiling or a small sun-side glasshouse are necessary to draw in the cool air.
- Run a perennial non-invasive vine (Mikania, Pyrostegia) completely across the roof.
- Place a water tank under the vine crop to keep the garden air cool and to cool the water.

### Vines as Mulch and Forage

Vines can be vigorous producers of foliage for garden mulch and for feeding small domestic livestock. Well-chosen shade vines can also provide some stick fuel wood for efficient cook-stoves.

### Desert-Adapted Vines

Several vine crops are desert-adapted. If gourds and yam legumes (ground vines) are included, some very basic reserve foods can be set out in shaded, moist, wastewater, or soakage situations. Some vines survive dry, harsh, stony, or dune conditions. All, however, thrive and yield better if a few cubic feet of humus is pitted below the plant when set out.

## The Role of Teachers and Designers

The role of teachers and designers is to help plan and place trial systems in villages, to locate and provide seeds and propagules of new species, to teach the benefits of soil humus and rainwater harvest, and to stress food preparation and good nutrition.
## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
Field trials provide essential data for validating theoretical approaches and refining methodologies.

Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
