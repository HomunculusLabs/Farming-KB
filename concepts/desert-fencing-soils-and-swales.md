## Desert Fencing, Soils, and Swales

Over much of the world's drylands, the great impediment to home garden production of food is the presence of wild, feral, and domestic browsing animals. Fencing, soil management, and water harvesting through swales form the three pillars of productive desert agriculture.

## Fencing as a Primary Requisite

It is much cheaper to fence out devastating and ever-hungry animals from settlements and gardens than it is to airlift emergency food aid to starving people, or to withstand the social costs of poverty and famine. Fencing is thus a primary requisite for intensive food production in deserts.

### Fenced Corridors

Fenced corridors or "stock routes" can permit milk and draft animals to enter some restricted areas of settlements, but sound fences (preferably electrified) are needed to exclude goats, camels, cattle, donkeys, and sheep from home gardens.

### Natural Regeneration Within Fences

Within fenced areas, sometimes surprising natural regeneration of trees can occur, and hardy food plants can be set out in unirrigated areas. These form a firewood, mulch, and medicinal resource for hard times. Poultry, domestic rabbits, bees, guinea pigs, and pigeons are all relatively harmless livestock for dryland homes.

### Low-Cost Fencing Alternatives

Where money is unavailable for post-and-wire fencing, alternatives include:

- **Ditch and bank systems**, either rock-faced or thorn-crowned
- **Living fences** of plant materials: reeds (Caspian area), cactus (Mexico), *[[euphorbia-tirucalli]]* and *[[saharawi-people]] of North Africa, as refugees in Algeria, have developed two methods of soil treatment under irrigation that leaches salt. Both involve heroic or total soil rehabilitation:

### Method 1 (Where Shingle or Pebbles Are Available)

Salt soil is removed to 1–2 metres deep, a layer of shingle is laid down, and non-salted soil is carted in. The shingle base allows drainage while preventing capillary rise of salt from below.

### Method 2 (Where No Stones Are Available)

Topsoil is removed (if clayey) to 20 cm, salt-free sand is brought in, and deep canals are cut to drain off surplus salt with the irrigation water.

## Plants in Difficult Soils

Specialised techniques exist for establishing plants in extremely difficult desert soils:

- **Calcrete layers** can be cracked open with a pit of humus and organic wastes that allows roots to reach lower damp sands
- **Cracking clay soils** can be filled with a sand-gypsum mix before seedlings are planted
- **Gels mixed with trace elements and sulphur** can be used before planting in alkaline sites
- **Plastic liners with holes** in deep sands hold minerals, nutrients, and gels
- **Small explosive charges** can shatter hard shales for tree planting
- **Ripping** opens hard pans for tree lines and water infiltration

These techniques demonstrate that even the most hostile desert substrates can be made productive with appropriate intervention.
