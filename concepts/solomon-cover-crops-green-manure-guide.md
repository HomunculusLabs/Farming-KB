# Solomon [[green-manure-guide]]

## Overview

Green manures and [[cover-crops]] are interchangeable terms for growing something whose sole purpose is enriching the soil. Solomon considers cover cropping one of the most important skills for the serious gardener, but warns that practicing it can be tricky and hazardous.

## The Fundamental Principle

If a plot of land is put to work growing as much biomass as can be formed during every day of the year that biomass will grow, and if almost all that biomass is recycled back into the land, then that bit of earth will attain a relatively stable and high organic matter content and will maintain it indefinitely. In that condition of maximum organic matter content the soil will have good tilth and grow good crops.

Example: A pasture growing a mixture of grasses and clovers has a thick sward intercepting every bit of sunlight and converting it to biomass every day of the growing season. Over years of such treatment, fertility builds.

The [[fukuoka-dutch-priest-vegetable-garden-spiritual-renewal]] is the opposite -- plants start small, spaced far apart, and by the time the crop canopy thickly covers the ground (formed), much of the growing season has already passed. The canopy persists and then the crop matures and dies back. Considerable growth could happen between the first frost and hard freezing, but the veggie garden, growing mainly frost-tender species, is bare then too.

Cover cropping means making sure the ground produces more biomass by covering it with a crop canopy for as much of the growing season as possible.

## The Downside -- Critical Warnings

Green-manuring practices have filtered down from farmers to vegetable growers. Farmers use big tractors (or once used teams of horses). Gardeners use shovels and sometimes light powered equipment. Gardeners can be overwhelmed trying to handle a six-foot-tall stand of woody-stalked grain rye by hand.

### The Decomposition Problem

When you dig tender, low C/N green material into warm, moist earth, it will decompose almost entirely in two weeks. However, give that vegetation a few more growing weeks (let it get woody) and dig it in -- it will now take four or five weeks to decompose enough to allow seeds to sprout.

The reason: When a great deal of decomposition is going on in the soil, the microbial population enormously increases. These microcritters breathe in heaps of oxygen and exhale heaps of carbon dioxide gas. The soil atmosphere becomes so filled with CO2 that seeds cannot even germinate. Transplanted seedlings either die outright from suffocation or are not allowed to grow. All this settles back to normal as soon as most decomposition has finished.

### The Timing Trap

Imagine having heavy soil and a thriving overwintered green crop (rye grass or winter wheat) coming out of the snow. Spring weather goes against you with more rain than expected. When it's time to dig in the knee-high grass, the soil is too wet to work. If you dig, you make a mess of clods. If you wait for the land to dry out, the grass will be waist-high (or chest-high for rye) and forming seeds before the earth is dry enough for tillage. Now you have an additional four to five weeks to wait after turning in the cover crop. That land might not be ready to plant until time for your cool-weather autumn crops.

### Semi-Arid Risk

In semi-arid conditions without irrigation, if you have a relatively dry winter followed by a rainless spring, the green-manure crop might suck so much moisture out of the soil before you turn it in that the ground will be deprived of moisture needed to germinate your vegetable crop.

### Allelopathy

One crop can put residues into the soil that harm the growth of the crop that follows. The cover crop you select could prevent some of your garden crops from growing well -- the opposite of what you hoped to achieve.

## The Upsides

If all goes well, cover cropping provides:
- Soil thickly filled with rapidly decomposing roots, leaving the land loose, airy, and totally open to rapid root penetration
- [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] have had a place to overwinter
- If the cover crop was a legume, it will have put nitrogen and organic matter into the soil
- If not a legume, it still contributes organic matter
- An overwintering cover crop incorporated nutrients that might otherwise have leached out; these are now part of the decomposing mass and steadily released

## Summer Green Manures

### Buckwheat (Recommended Without Hesitation)
- Grows best during early summer
- Local varieties adapted to your situation are important -- use seed grown at similar latitude
- Sown thickly before the solstice, grows waist-high in less than five weeks, then begins blooming
- Seedmaking ends [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] -- dig in or yank out before more than first few flowers appear
- Stalks are tender and brittle, easily dug in or rototilled without tangling
- Decomposes in just one week if dug into moist soil before flowering
- Puts soil into magnificent, fine-textured condition -- perfect seedbed for any following crop
- Any time you harvest an early spring sowing (peas, salads, radishes, mustards) before midsummer and nothing is scheduled for at least five weeks, scatter seeds about 1 inch apart and chop in shallowly with a hoe

WARNING: Buckwheat seed from a very different latitude may perform poorly. Solomon once used Australian buckwheat seed from semi-tropical Queensland at 41 degrees South latitude -- it hardly bloomed before stopping growth.

### Southern Peas
Function much like buckwheat in summer, with the additional benefit of being a legume ([[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]]). Promptly till in or yank out the crop when it begins flowering.

### Garden Peas as Green Manure
Whole beds of ordinary garden peas can serve as green manure, with the benefit of filling the freezer with peas. If beds are needed sooner than the peas mature, the green manure can be composted anytime. Peas leave the soil in the most beautiful condition.

## Autumn Green Manures

### Brassicas
Fodder kale, oilseed radish, tyfon (a turnip x Chinese cabbage hybrid), rape, and field turnips make excellent autumn green manures. In cold climates they freeze out, posing no spring problem. In maritime or mild-winter climates they will almost certainly overwinter and make lush spring growth that blooms early.

On clay soils in mild areas, don't overwinter brassicas unless you can yank them out by hand when blooming begins. Sow at least 40 days before the end of the autumn growing season. Their leaves protect the ground in autumn; their huge taproots really open up the soil.

### Winter Legumes
Annual legumes are the mainstay for overwintering in mild climates. Beware of perennial legumes which grow more slowly their first year and may be a bother the next. [[compost]] is widely used in Oregon. Hairy vetch is popular. Lupins and small-seeded fava beans are also suited.

## No-Till from Cover Crops

Solomon discovered the practice of transplanting directly into a mulch made from rye (sometimes intercropped with hairy vetch) grown right on the site. The rye is cut off at ground level just after forming seed heads. The dead vegetation remains as a thick mulch. The decomposing rye releases allelopathic chemicals that block weed seed sprouting.
