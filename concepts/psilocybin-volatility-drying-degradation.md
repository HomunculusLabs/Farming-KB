# Psilocybin Volatility and Drying Degradation

## Overview

The potency of psilocybin-containing mushrooms is not fixed at harvest but
undergoes significant changes over time due to the volatile nature of the active
compounds. This phenomenon, documented by both indigenous practitioners and
modern mycologists, has profound implications for the preparation, storage, and
use [[distribution-of-neurotropic-fungi]]. Gastón Guzmán's 1998 global distribution study provides
some of the most compelling evidence for psilocybin volatility, including a
personal account of passive inhalation exposure.

## Chemical Volatility of Indole Alkaloids

Psilocybin (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) and its dephosphorylated
cousin psilocin are indole alkaloids that degrade over time, even in dried material.
Guzmán concluded from observational evidence that "it seems that psilocybin and
psilocin are volatiles"—capable of sublimating or evaporating from dried fungal
tissue at room temperature.

This volatility is not merely theoretical. Repke et al. (1977) demonstrated that
specimens of [[psilocybe-baeocystis]] and P. cyanescens analyzed 20-60 days after
collection showed "no trace of indoles." The active compounds had degraded or
evaporated entirely within two months of drying.

Ohenoja et al. (1987) provided quantitative evidence from [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]] of
[[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybe-semilanceata-temperate-cosmopolitan-distribution]]. They found progressively lower psilocybin concentrations
corresponding to specimen age: 0.84% dry weight in specimens from 1976, 0.67% in
specimens from 1954, and only 0.014% in specimens from 1869. This represents a
60-fold decrease over roughly a century of storage, with the steepest losses
occurring in the first few decades.

## Indigenous Knowledge: The One-Year Rule

Mexican Indian practitioners of the [[maria-sabina-and-mazatec-mushroom-ceremony]] have long maintained that
old dried specimens lose their efficacy. Guzmán reported that "the Mexican Indians
wisely say in relationship with the use of the sacred mushrooms, that the old
dried specimens kept for more than one year are not good to use, and they throw
them out."

This traditional knowledge is now supported by chemical evidence. Specimens dried
for "almost a year" were found to produce effects that were "only slightly
neurotropic or entirely inactive" when consumed at normal doses. The Mazatec and
other indigenous groups had no chemical instruments but arrived at the same
conclusion through centuries of empirical observation.

## Passive Inhalation Exposure: Guzmán's Account

The most dramatic evidence for psilocybin volatility comes from Guzmán's personal
experience in Oaxaca, Mexico, in 1958. While conducting fieldwork collecting
neurotropic fungi with local Indian assistants, he gathered a large quantity of
mixed Psilocybe species (P. caerulescens, P. cordispora, P. cubensis, P.
mexicana, and P. zapotecorum) for commercial purposes.

The mushrooms were stored in sacks in a small, closed-door room of an Indian
home where Guzmán was trying to sleep. The air was "heavy with a strong acrid
aroma of the fungi." Despite having consumed no mushrooms, Guzmán experienced
"colored hallucinations" from prolonged exposure to the [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]] in the
closed space. When he left the room for fresh air, the hallucinations stopped
immediately.

This account demonstrates that psilocybin or related compounds can reach
pharmacologically active concentrations in the air of a poorly ventilated room
containing large quantities of fresh or semi-fermented neurotropic fungi. The
acrid aroma noted by Guzmán suggests that volatile tryptamine derivatives were
actively off-gassing from the fungal material.

## Comparison with Other Amanita Alkaloids

The volatility phenomenon is not unique to psilocybin. Julius A. Palmer documented
a parallel experience with Amanita specimens in 1870s Boston: merely smelling
Amanitas in a closed box on his desk produced vomiting and stomach oppression
three hours later, with livid eye discoloration lasting through the following day.

The similarity between Palmer's Amanita experience and Guzmán's Psilocybe
experience—both involving passive exposure to fungal volatiles in enclosed
spaces—suggests that fungal alkaloid volatility may be a general phenomenon across
multiple genera, not limited to the psilocybin-containing species.

## Implications for Storage and Commerce

The illegal trade in neurotropic fungi, documented by Guzmán as occurring in the
USA, Canada, Europe, and Australia, faces an inherent quality-control problem.
Specimens that are dried, shipped, and stored for extended periods may arrive at
their destination with significantly reduced potency. The tropical species
[[anemotropism-in-psilocybe-cubensis]], the most commonly traded species, is frequently found in
Europe as illegally cultivated or dried specimens imported from tropical
countries—but their potency at point of sale depends entirely on [[spawn-storage-conditions]]
and elapsed time since harvest.

Guzmán noted that this trade frequently leads to reports of P. cubensis being
"found" in [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]], when in fact the specimens were imported rather
than locally collected. This confuses distribution data and may account for some
discrepancies in the mycological literature regarding species ranges.

## Implications for Chemical Analysis

The age-dependent degradation of psilocybin has significant implications for
the reliability of chemical studies. Guzmán pointed out that Hofmann found no
indoles in specimens of [[cordyceps-capitata-ophioglossoides-mexican-ceremonial]] and C. ophioglossoides that Wasson had
gathered from a Mexican market. These specimens had been "preserved for a long
time" before analysis, potentially explaining the negative result despite the
species' importance in indigenous ceremonial use.

Chemical studies that report negative results for psilocybin in species
previously found to contain it may reflect specimen age rather than genuine
absence of the compound. This variable must be controlled for in any comparative
study of fungal alkaloid content.

## Practical Guidelines

Based on the accumulated evidence:

1. Consume dried specimens within one year of harvest for reliable potency
2. Store dried material in airtight containers, ideally with desiccant, in cool
   dark conditions to slow degradation
3. Be aware that specimens of unknown age may have severely reduced potency
4. Do not store large quantities of fresh [[ethnobotanical-use-of-neurotropic-fungi-in-mexico]] poorly ventilated
   living spaces—the [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] can reach active concentrations
5. Chemical analysis of old herbarium specimens may yield false negatives for
   psilocybin content due to degradation over time
