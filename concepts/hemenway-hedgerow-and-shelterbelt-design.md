# Hedgerow and Shelterbelt Design

Hedgerows and shelterbelts are among the most versatile and
multifunctional elements in [[hemenway-ecological-garden-design]]. A
well-designed hedgerow is far more than a screen or property boundary.
It can serve simultaneously as a windbreak, deer barrier, wildlife
corridor, food source, mulch producer, insectary, nitrogen-fixer, and
microclimate modifier. This approach to hedgerow design embodies the
principle of stacking functions central to
[[hemenway-ecological-principles-niche-succession-biodiversity]].

## Multifunctional Hedge Design

Through creative choice of plants, a simple hedge becomes not merely a
screen but a deer-blocking, wind-reducing, wildlife- and people-feeding,
mulch-producing, insect-attracting source of medicinal plants and craft
materials. Curve it around the north side of a garden, and it becomes a
sun-trap. This is the power of stacking functions applied to a single
linear element.

### Roxanne Swentzell's Edible Hedge

In her New Mexico desert garden, Roxanne Swentzell blended berry bushes
and small fruit trees into an edible hedge along the north border. This
provided food for the family while blocking the fierce canyon winds that
roared down from nearby hills. The hedge was part of a larger strategy
to build fertile soil, cast shade, damp wild temperature swings, and
conserve water. Together with swales, mulch, and nurse plants, this
hedgerow helped transform bare desert into a young, multistoried food
forest.

### Hemenway's Deer-Deflecting Food Hedge

Hemenway designed a deer-deflecting food hedge with wildlife plants on
the outside and food-bearing species on the inside. The hedge includes
Manchurian apricots, currants, Siberian pea shrub, buffaloberry, and
other species. Siberian pea shrub and buffaloberry provide nitrogen
fixation and wind protection, while the apricots and currants supply
food. Wildlife plants on the outer edge feed birds and attract
beneficial insects. The deer can nibble on the outside to their heart's
content but cannot get through.

## Windbreak Design Principles

Shelterbelt species are limited only by the designer's imagination, as
these hedges can be delightfully multifunctional. Key design principles
include:

- **Porosity**: Windbreaks should allow 40 to 70 percent of the wind to
  pass through them. If they are denser, they create turbulence
  downwind rather than calm air
- **Protected area**: A windbreak will protect an area two to five
  times as long as the windbreak is high
- **Species mix**: Combine evergreens for year-round protection with
  deciduous species for seasonal variety. Junipers and hollies provide
  constant cover, while fruiting trees and shrubs add food production
- **Nitrogen fixers**: Include black locust, laburnum, Siberian pea
  shrub, or other nitrogen-fixing species that are also beloved by bees
  and wildlife
- **Layered structure**: A tall windbreak hedge with understory shrubs
  creates more habitat niches than a single-height planting

## Sun-Traps and Microclimate Modification

Hedges can be arranged in a U-shape, open to the sunny south side but
closed to winds, creating a warm, protected microclimate suitable for
tender plants. Evergreens planted on the north side serve as a
year-round windbreak, while deciduous plants on the south side provide
summer shade but allow winter sun to reach the garden. This is a
practical application of [[hemenway-zone-and-sector-planning]] principles
for managing wind sectors.

Deciduous plants trellised on the south or west side of a building, over
a deck, or even over a roof will drop the temperature substantially in
summer while letting in sunlight in bare-branched winter.

## Deer-Deflecting Hedges

A tangle of thorny shrubs can thwart plant-munching deer or discourage
trespassers. Effective deer-deflecting species include osage orange,
hawthorn, native roses, Manchurian plum, gooseberries, and nonthorny
wildlife species mixed in for filler. Once established, deer can nibble
on the outside but cannot penetrate the hedge. This approach connects to
[[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] by creating wildlife corridors while
solving a common garden problem without fencing.

## Hedgerows as Wildlife Corridors

Hedgerows serve as critical wildlife corridors in the ecological garden.
They connect isolated habitat patches, allowing birds, insects, and
small mammals to move safely through the landscape. This connectivity
supports the [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] and [[hemenway-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insects]]
strategies by providing shelter, nesting sites, and food sources.

Many beneficial insects, including parasitic wasps and predatory beetles,
need weedy or sheltered spots to complete their life cycles. Hedgerows
with diverse species provide these refuges, ensuring that pest
controllers remain in the garden year-round.

## Species Selection for Hedgerows

A multifunctional hedgerow might include:

- **Nitrogen fixers**: Siberian pea shrub, black locust, sea buckthorn,
  buffaloberry, laburnum
- **Fruit producers**: Manchurian apricot, currants, gooseberries,
  elderberry, blueberry, wild cherry
- **Wildlife plants**: Dogwood, hawthorn, ceanothus, native roses,
  chokeberry
- **Thorny barriers**: Osage orange, gooseberry, wild roses, hawthorn
- **[[hemenway-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insects]]**: Species with
  umbelliferous flowers that attract predatory wasps and butterflies
- **[[hemenway-mulch-makers-and-plant-functional-roles]]**: Species that
  produce abundant leaf litter for soil building

## Integration with Broader Design

Hedgerows fit naturally into [[hemenway-food-forest-design]] as boundary
defining elements. They also work within [[hemenway-guild-design-roles-and-assembly]]
as linear plant communities. Their role as wildlife corridors connects
them to [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]], and their wind-modifying
function makes them tools for [[hemenway-zone-and-sector-planning]] sector
analysis. A hedgerow is, in essence, a linear guild: a multifunctional
plant community designed to perform many ecological services simultaneously.
