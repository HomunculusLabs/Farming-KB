# Lichen Sample Site Selection and Within-Site Sampling Strategies

[[lichen]] biodiversity inventories require careful decisions about where to sample and how to sample within chosen sites. Effective survey design integrates statistical rigor with practical field constraints and conservation objectives. This page covers the principles and practical considerations for selecting sample site locations, designing within-site sampling protocols, choosing plot sizes and shapes, estimating species abundance, using permanent photoplots for monitoring, and employing lichens as biodiversity indicators.

## Sample Site Location

### Stratification by Classification Units

Rather than random site selection, lichen surveys benefit from **stratification by vegetation or habitat classification units** (Grieg-Smith 1983). This approach selects sites that represent each class of interest, which is useful for relating lichen communities to other organisms and environmental variables, and encourages management application of results (McCune and Antos 1981a,b; Wolseley and Aguirre-Hudson 1991; Wolseley et al. 1995).

A key practical advantage is that accessible sites can be chosen to represent more remote areas. However, sample units should be **dispersed over the geographic area they are intended to represent** (Hurlbert 1984), because geographically closer samples from the same habitat tend to be more similar than distant ones due to spatial autocorrelation (Legendre and Fortin 1989).

### Lichen Communities vs. Macrovegetation Classifications

Survey designers should **not assume** that lichen community variation is adequately represented by macrovegetation classifications. Lichen diversity patterns at the 0.1–1.0 ha scale frequently diverge from those of vascular plants (McCune and Antos 1981a, 1981b), and there is no reason to expect lichen patterns to always match vascular plant patterns across scales ranging from centimeters to hundreds of kilometers.

Lichen community variation operates at multiple scales:
- **Microhabitat/habitat** scale (centimeters)
- **Successional and disturbance gradients** (various scales)

Macrovegetation classes with similar composition but different stand ages (e.g., old-growth vs. young forest) may host markedly different lichen communities (Lesica et al. 1991; Wolseley and Aguirre-Hudson 1991; Tibell 1992; Selva 1994; Wolseley et al. 1995). Additionally, lichen sensitivity to air pollution means diversity patterns may differ from those of less pollution-sensitive organisms.

### Practical Site Selection Considerations

Surveying lichens and macrovegetation at the same sites provides a strong basis for relating environmental variables. Preexisting macrovegetation sample sites offer logistical advantages, and additional lichen-only sites can supplement these. Important lichen habitats that may be missed by plant-focused classifications include **rock outcrops, talus piles, coarse woody debris, and desert pavements**.

In practice, statistical sampling considerations are often subordinated to conservation goals—such as monitoring areas considered for development—or to establishing **benchmark inventories for protected areas**.

## Within-Site Sampling Protocols

Within-site protocols designate the site size, whether and how to subsample, and what information to record. They range from **time-constrained qualitative surveys** (1–2 hours) to **intensive quantitative subsample surveys** requiring several days. Protocols must be firmly constrained to meet project goals with available resources.

There is no single best way to inventory lichens; even the most intensive protocol is unlikely to find all species present. **Repeated sampling by different observers or methods** can estimate "species capture" rates (McCune and Lesica 1992). Smith et al. (1993) provide a detailed discussion of within-site design, including tradeoffs between few-large vs. many-small subsample units, abundance estimation approaches, and types of data analysis.

## Selecting Size of Sample Plot

Lichens occupy substratum surfaces at microhabitat scales. At scales greater than 0.01–0.25 m², lichen distribution becomes heterogeneous, and there is no single "best" scale for measuring lichen diversity. Important microhabitat variables influencing distribution include **light regime, moisture status, and the type, hardness, chemistry (especially pH and calcium), and age of the substratum**.

Fixed-area plots at each site are **strongly recommended** for comparability, particularly when using diversity indices or species frequency data. For habitat or vegetation class sites, the typical range is 0.1–5 ha, with sites larger than 0.5 ha usually subsampled. Published lichen surveys for independently chosen sites range from 0.05–1 ha. One hectare represents the practical upper limit for a circumscribed search (the largest used is ~0.8 ha; Wetmore 1993, 1995). At sites larger than 1 ha, multiple plots can be located, but they are not independent and thus do not increase the statistical sample size.

### Plot Shape Considerations

- **Compact shapes** (squares, circles) are more internally homogeneous, faster to lay out, more efficient for correlating with ecological factors, and circular quadrats can be precisely relocated from a single center marker—recommended for field efficiency.
- **Extended shapes** (rectangles) capture more environmental heterogeneity and may be preferred for biodiversity studies seeking to maximize heterogeneity within plots.

## Within-Site (Within-Plot) Sampling Strategies

Subsample unit shape and size should be tailored to the specific habitat or microhabitat being sampled (Goldsmith et al. 1986; Longman and Jenik 1987; Wolf 1993; Lücking and Lücking 1996; Sipman 1996b; Nimis et al. 2002).

### Standard Subsample Units by Substratum

| Substratum | Unit Type | Size |
|---|---|---|
| **Tree trunks** | Trunk plots | 0.1–0.5 m² |
| **Tree branches** | Branch lengths | 0.25–1 m |
| **Rock/ground surfaces** | Ground plots | 0.2–1 m² |

Tree trunk and branch measurements follow standards from Johansson (1974), Will-Wolf (1980, 1988), Cornelissen and ter Steege (1989), McCune (1990), and McCune and Lesica (1992). Rock and ground surface plot sizes follow Anderson et al. (1982), Rosentreter (1986), James and Wolseley (1992), and McCune and Lesica (1992); ground plots are often arranged along transects.

### Difficult Microhabitats

Upper trunks, canopy surfaces, and cliffs are sampled only in very intensive surveys. Surveyors should take advantage of low-effort opportunities such as recently fallen or felled trees. **Canopy lichens differ from lower-trunk lichens** in both species composition and abundance (Yarranton 1972; Pike et al. 1975; Lang et al. 1980; Oksanen 1988; McCune and Lesica 1992; McCune et al. 2000), though canopy sampling is less successful in moist tropical forests where thalli decay rapidly.

## Estimating Abundance

Abundance estimation is constrained by the ability to identify and distinguish species in the field. When field identification is not possible, complete collections are needed and abundance must be estimated from specimen proportions—which can generate massive numbers of specimens. A higher proportion of macrolichens than microlichens are distinguishable in the field.

### Approaches by Effort Level

- **Minimum:** qualitative notes about apparent abundance.
- **Moderate:** assign abundance classes. McCune et al. (1997b) used 2 density + 2 abundance classes; Geiser et al. (1994a) used 5 density classes. Alternatively, species presence in subplots provides frequency as an abundance estimate (efficient despite known problems).
- **Grouped:** abundance classes for morphological groups (e.g., small foliose rock lichens, hanging fruticose bark lichens; McCune 1993). Vagrant macrolichens are important in grasslands and steppes (Rosentreter 1993); Kaltenecker et al. (1999) used cover classes for morphological groups in these habitats.
- **Quantitative:** cover classes or percent cover in replicated fixed-area subplots are simpler and more accurate than individual counts (Will-Wolf 1988; James and Wolseley 1992; Marcelli 1992), but cover estimation is generally too labor-intensive for general biodiversity inventories.

### Functional Groups and Biomass

Morphological groups may serve as surrogates for functional groups (Pike 1978; Rosentreter 1995). **Gelatinous ground lichens** (*Collema*, *Leptogium*, *Polychidium*) fix nitrogen and protect the soil surface. **Biomass measurement** is labor-intensive, requiring subsampling and weighing (Lang et al. 1980; McCune 1993; Hayward and Rosentreter 1994; Rosso and Rosentreter 1999), but canopy macrolichen biomass can be efficiently estimated from litterfall analysis (McCune 1994) and lower canopy branches (Esseen et al. 1996).

## Permanent Photoplots

Permanent photoplots offer an alternative to field abundance assessment and are valuable for **monitoring trends over time** (Will-Wolf 1980; Hale 1982; Bureau of Land Management 1996). Individual species may or may not be recognizable in photographs, but morphological types usually are; identifications and cover estimates can be completed in the laboratory. This approach has been greatly simplified by advances in digital cameras and spatial analysis software.

## Biodiversity Indicators

### Single Species vs. Species Groups

The **indicator-species approach is not recommended** for baseline biodiversity studies, as it presupposes detailed ecological and physiological knowledge that currently exists only in Europe (Seaward 1988). Common lichen species are seldom present at all monitoring sites. Smith et al. (1993) also concluded that single species should not be recommended as air pollution indicators.

**Species groups as community indicators** show more promise, though they require knowledge of inter-species relationships. An identifiable subset of lichen species (combining rare and common taxa) is appropriate as biodiversity monitors for baseline surveys.

### Established Indicator Groups

| Indicator Group | Significance | Key References |
|---|---|---|
| **Macrolichen communities on bark** | Response to sulfurous air pollutants; forest ecosystem biodiversity and function | Ferry et al. 1973; Nash and Wirth 1988; Bates and Farmer 1992; Smith et al. 1993; McCune et al. 1997b; Will-Wolf et al. 2002 |
| **Nitrogen-fixing lichens** (cyanobacterial photobionts) | More common and diverse in older, less disturbed forests with cleaner air | Eldridge and Rosentreter 1999; Gauslaa 1995; Kondratyuk and Coppins 1998; Sillett et al. 2000 |
| **"Pin-lichens"** (former Caliciales) | Indicate long continuous occupation by old-growth forest in northern temperate/boreal regions | Tibell 1992; Selva 1994 |
| **Ground lichens** | Indicators of ecosystem function in grassland/steppe | Anderson et al. 1982; Brotherson et al. 1983; Rosentreter 1986; Eldridge and Tozer 1996 |

Selva (1994) defined a forest continuity indicator species group for northeastern North America. *Lobaria pulmonaria* is a particularly notable species with both green algal and cyanobacterial photobionts, nitrogen-fixing capability, old-growth association, and air pollution sensitivity.

## Habitat Information

### Minimum Recording Requirements

At minimum, the following should be recorded for each site: **location, vegetation description and classification** (life forms and structure), **vegetation history, landform, topography, and rock/soil type**. Where possible, this information should be acquired from existing literature and previous studies rather than from the sampling protocol itself.

### Detailed Habitat Data

More detailed habitat information includes the relative importance of plant species and quantitative structural estimates: shrub and ground cover, standing dead stems, dead wood, and canopy cover across forest layers. For rock and ground habitats: rock types, patch sizes, aspect exposures, and percent sun/shade. Habitat information should match the microhabitat subdivisions used for lichen sampling.

Lichenologists with limited ecological training should **consult an ecologist** during protocol design to ensure habitat data are adequate and appropriate.

## Summary of Key Principles

1. **Stratify site selection** by habitat or vegetation classes rather than relying on random placement.
2. **Do not assume** lichen diversity patterns mirror vascular plant patterns at any scale.
3. **Use fixed-area plots** for comparability; 0.05–1 ha is the standard range for lichen surveys.
4. **Tailor subsample units** to the specific substratum (bark, rock, ground) being surveyed.
5. **Match abundance estimation effort** to project goals and field identification capacity.
6. **Use species groups, not single species**, as biodiversity and environmental indicators.
7. **Record habitat information** at a level matching the lichen microhabitat subdivisions used.
