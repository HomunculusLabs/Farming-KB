# Soil Mycobiome

## Overview

The soil mycobiome refers to the total fungal community inhabiting soil
environments. Soil is the primary reservoir of fungal diversity on Earth,
containing an estimated 1.5 million fungal species across virtually every
major taxonomic group. Despite its ecological importance, the soil
mycobiome remains largely uncharacterized, with the vast majority of soil
fungi known only from DNA sequences rather than formal species descriptions.
A single gram of forest soil may contain hundreds of fungal species
represented by millions of hyphal meters and billions of spores.

## Functional Guilds

### Decomposers (Saprotrophs)

Saprotrophic fungi are the primary agents of organic matter decomposition
in soil, breaking down cellulose, lignin, and other complex polymers.
Basidiomycetes such as white-rot and brown-rot fungi are particularly
important for lignin degradation, a capacity relatively rare among
microorganisms. White-rot fungi employ lignin peroxidases, manganese
peroxidases, and laccases to oxidatively cleave lignin, while brown-rot
fungi use a non-enzymatic Fenton chemistry mechanism. Decomposer activity
drives nutrient cycling and carbon flux in terrestrial ecosystems, and
the balance between fungal and bacterial decomposition pathways has
significant implications for soil carbon storage.

### Mycorrhizal Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots, exchanging
soil nutrients (especially phosphorus and nitrogen) for plant-derived
carbon. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (Glomeromycota) associate with the
majority of terrestrial plant species and are obligate biotrophs that
cannot be cultured without a host. Ectomycorrhizal fungi (predominantly
Basidiomycota) partner with trees in temperate and boreal forests and
produce conspicuous fruiting bodies. Mycorrhizal networks, sometimes called
common mycorrhizal networks or "wood wide webs," connect multiple plants
and facilitate resource sharing, seedling establishment, and stress signal
transmission.

### Pathogens

Soil-borne fungal pathogens cause diseases in plants, animals, and other
fungi. Genera such as Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, and Verticillium cause
significant agricultural losses worldwide. Soil fungal pathogens maintain
their inoculum potential through resilient structures like sclerotia,
chlamydospores, and melanized hyphae that persist for years in the absence
of a susceptible host. Suppressive soils — those that naturally inhibit
pathogen establishment — are of particular interest for understanding
microbial mechanisms of disease control.

### Endophytes and Epiphytes

Fungi colonizing roots and belowground plant parts without causing visible
disease symptoms include dark septate endophytes and various root-associated
Ascomycota. These fungi may transition between endophytic, saprotrophic,
and pathogenic lifestyles depending on environmental conditions and host
physiological status. Their ecological roles are complex and context-dependent.

## Spatial Structure

Soil fungal communities are highly spatially structured. Diversity and
composition vary with soil depth (declining sharply below the organic
horizon), pH, moisture, organic matter content, and vegetation type.
Horizontal spatial autocorrelation means that fungal communities can change
significantly over distances of meters to centimeters. Rhizosphere soil —
the zone of soil directly influenced by plant root exudates — harbors
distinct fungal communities enriched in mycorrhizal and endophytic taxa
compared to bulk soil. The rhizosphere effect can extend several
centimeters from the root surface.

## Sampling and Detection Challenges

Studying the soil mycobiome is complicated by the heterogeneous, opaque,
and three-dimensional nature of soil. DNA-based methods have revolutionized
soil fungal surveys, but extracting high-quality DNA from humic-rich soils
remains technically challenging due to co-extracted PCR inhibitors. The
vast majority of soil fungal sequences in public databases do not match
described species, reflecting the magnitude of undocumented diversity.
Fruit body surveys capture only a small fraction of the soil community
because most soil fungi rarely or never produce visible aboveground
structures. Spore traps and root washing complement DNA-based approaches.

## Seasonal and Successional Dynamics

Soil fungal communities shift seasonally in response to temperature,
moisture, and plant phenology. Mycorrhizal fungi peak in activity during
the growing season when host plants allocate more carbon belowground.
Decomposer communities respond to litter inputs, with peaks following leaf
fall in temperate forests. Ecosystem succession — from bare ground to
mature forest — involves predictable shifts in fungal community composition
and functional guild dominance, with early successional communities
dominated by fast-growing Ascomycota and late successional communities
enriched in Basidiomycota.

## Global Biogeography

Broad-scale patterns in soil fungal biogeography are shaped by climate,
soil properties, and historical factors. Tropical soils tend to have
higher fungal diversity but lower biomass compared to temperate forest
soils. Arid and semi-arid soils are dominated by melanized fungi adapted
to desiccation and UV stress. Island biogeography principles apply: larger
and less isolated landmasses harbor more diverse soil fungal communities.
Elevational gradients in fungal diversity often show mid-elevation peaks
in species richness.
