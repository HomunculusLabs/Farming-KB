# Hallucinogens and Shamanism

The use of hallucinogenic agents to achieve trance states for perceiving and contacting the supernatural world is an ancient and widespread human practice. The foundational text edited by Michael J. Harner (1973), *[[hallucinogens-and-shamanism]]*, represents a landmark in anthropological research, bringing together ethnographic accounts of psychoactive plant use among indigenous peoples—particularly those of the Upper Amazon—and exploring the relationship between psychedelic substances and shamanistic practice.

## The Role of Hallucinogens in Shamanism

In non-literate societies, the experts who directly confront the supernatural are called "shamans." The term derives from the Tungus tribe of Siberia and is preferred in anthropology because it lacks the sensational or negative connotations of "witch" or "witch-doctor." A shaman is defined as a man or woman who is in direct contact with the spirit world through a trance state and has one or more spirits at their command to carry out their bidding for good or evil. Shamans may bewitch persons, cure the ill, influence the course of events, find lost objects, divine the identity of criminals, communicate with the dead, foretell the future, and practice clairvoyance.

The use of hallucinogenic agents is only one method of achieving trance-like states conducive to supernatural contact. Other methods include fasting, flagellation, sensory deprivation, breathing exercises, yogic meditation, and ritual dancing and drumming. However, the use of hallucinogens appears to be the easiest and fastest technique for reaching what is believed to be a supernatural experience.

## Fly Agaric and Siberian Shamanism

Any discussion of hallucinogens and shamanism must consider northeast Asia—the home of what has been termed "classic" shamanism. In Siberia, a close relationship exists between the psychoactive mushroom **fly-agaric** (*Amanita muscaria*) and the shamanistic act. *Amanita muscaria* contains muscarine and muscimol, both with demonstrated hallucinogenic properties. It was used by shamans among the Koryak, Chukchi, Yukagir, Yakut, Ostyak, Samoyed, and Kamchadal tribes.

The ethnographer Jochelson (1905–1908) described the effects among the Koryak: light intoxication produces animation and spontaneity of movement, while strong intoxication causes sensory distortion, hallucinations, spontaneous movements, and convulsions. Periods of great animation alternate with deep depression. The Koryak discovered that the active alkaloid is quickly excreted in urine, and the urine of intoxicated persons was consumed to prolong the hallucinogenic effects.

## Anthropological Neglect of Hallucinogens

The theoretical literature had largely overlooked the fact that even "classic" shamanism often involved the use of hallucinogens. Entire books on shamanism or primitive religion could be read without finding any reference to hallucinogens except for peyote. Yet patient library research reveals overwhelming evidence of the use of such substances in connection with the supernatural in scores of cultures.

Anthropologists had long been interested in the peyote cactus (*Lophophora williamsii*) in North American Indian cultures, but generally neglected the ethnological importance of other natural hallucinogens, including psychotropic mushrooms and solanaceous plants. One major reason for this neglect was that very few anthropologists had themselves partaken of native psychotropic materials or undergone the resulting subjective experiences—experiences critical, perhaps paradoxically, to an empirical understanding of their meaning to the peoples being studied.

## Social and Political Implications

The widespread utilization of hallucinogenic substances in a population to obtain direct revelations very probably tends to compete with the authority of priestly hierarchies. The authority of shamans themselves as special agents of contact with the supernatural has been reported threatened by the general adoption of peyote in some societies. Conversely, certain types of social structures may be more conducive than others to the development of conditions leading to widespread hallucinogen use.

An intriguing possibility raised by the research is that hallucinogenic experiences may have played a role in the innovation of religious beliefs about the supernatural world and the existence of disembodied souls. There can be little doubt that the use of powerful hallucinogens tends to strongly reinforce a belief in the reality of the supernatural.

## The New World as a Focus of Research

The New World is overwhelmingly emphasized as a region of research on hallucinogens and shamanism. The Western hemisphere is unusually rich in hallucinogenic plants, a factor which facilitated their use by North, Middle, and particularly South American Indians. The Upper Amazon remains one of the last areas where hallucinogenic drugs are used under essentially aboriginal conditions, with shamanistic practices typically involving the ingestion of a hallucinogenic tea or brew made from the **Banisteriopsis** vine.

## Key Contributors

The book draws on contributions from pharmacologists such as Lewin (1964, originally 1924) and botanists such as Schultes (1940, 1955) and Wasson (1961, Wasson and Wasson 1957), as well as younger anthropologists describing first-hand field research among tribes including the Cashinahua, Jivaro, Sharanahua, Campa, Apache, and others. The work spans four thematic sections: the primitive Upper Amazon world, cultures undergoing Westernization, the traditional Western world (European witchcraft), and the question of trans-cultural hallucinogenic experience.
