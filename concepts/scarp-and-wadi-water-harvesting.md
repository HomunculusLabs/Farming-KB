# Scarp and Wadi Water Harvesting

## Overview

Scarps and wadis are among the most productive and strategically important desert landforms for [[water-management]].

## Scarp Profile Formation

Scarp profiles develop through a predictable sequence when previously forested or vegetated land is cleared and overgrazed:

1. **Humid profile:** The original rounded landscape with vegetation
2. **Headslope develops:** As vegetation is removed, erosion begins
3. **Scree slope develops:** Material accumulates at the cliff base
4. **Angular desert scarp profile emerges:** The final characteristic desert form

In true deserts, the scarp face is typically straight or gently curved, and wadis are at near right-angles to the scarp face. Side valleys leave at near right-angles to the main valley, following patterns of parallel fault weakness and compensating joints in the rock. Scarps are capped with durable materials—ironstones, hard sandstone bands, [[silcrete]] crustal material. Cliff faces are of softer, sometimes bauxitic material, and the lower erosion surface is covered with silts and sands.

## Water Flow Patterns

When rain falls on the scarp system, the hard rock upper surface sheds water rapidly. Water follows gentle valleys behind the scarp and plunges as sudden waterfalls over cliff ends of the wadis. Just before falling, turbulence scours deep holes (scour holes) in the upper surface. Water may rush through the system for 3–4 days after rain, with discharge volume depending on catchment area.

A curtain of water or rills may fall along the whole scarp face from the downslope before the cliff. Over time, this seepage may partly undercut the soft scarp, forming caves (notches) at the base. These notches range from tiny (shade for a kangaroo) to enormous (large enough for a 6-storey house). They provide critical shade habitat for pigeons, owls, swallows, swifts, rodents, reptiles, and insects.

## Water Storage Strategy

### Upper Surface (Top of Scarp)

1. **Stone and cement dams** across gentle valleys to hold clean water, fitted with plugs or base pipes to moderate flood flow
2. **Gutters** cut across the scarp top above useful caves to prevent water splashing onto cave-house sites
3. **Scour holes** cleared of silt, deepened, and sealed to serve as tank storages
4. **Side valley diversion** to spread water to multiple valleys, reducing torrent concentration in any single channel
5. **Concrete or stone chutes** leading into excavated pools at cliff base for remaining overflow

### Wadi Floor

The wadi floor contains deep sands, gravels, and silts that spill out onto the lower erosion surface. Management strategies include:

1. **Strong rock walls** across the wadi floor, allowing silt fields to build up behind them
2. **Minor wadis:** The whole width can be dammed with spillways cut in pediment rock
3. **Wide wadis:** A central channel left open with fields built to the sides, fed by side valleys
4. **Broad earth-and-stone banks** on the lower plain to soak flood water into contained fields, stabilised with unpalatable shrubs

## The Nabatean System

The most extensively studied wadi development is the ancient Nabatean system of the Negev desert (documented by Michael Evenari and D. Koller, 1956). Key features include:

- **80–90% run-off capture** behind dykes in wadis, spread to lower dykes on the plains
- **20:1 catchment ratio:** For every irrigated hectare, 20 hectares of run-off surface are reserved as catchment
- **Highly coordinated flood and soak regimes** delivering water to orchards
- **Reliable winter rains** in the Negev area around Petra requiring close attention during rain events

The ideal is to absorb 0.5–1.0 m of water into each field, providing enough moisture for grain crops or to maintain palms and fruits. Upper reservoirs can be released after torrents cease to top up the system.

## Settlement Design

Settlements in scarp-and-wadi systems should be:

- **Cut into the pediment** of the scarp itself, well above valley floors
- **Located in base caves** of the cliffs for natural shade and flood protection
- **Protected by box canyon walls** that naturally contain animals and provide easy defence against feral animals

A windmill or pump close to the scarp can lift fresh water from wells or bores to tanks or rock cisterns on the upper peneplain, from where gravity flow delivers water to cliff-base houses.

## Maintenance

All wells, pools, and dams gather silt and need periodic cleaning. Dams can be cleaned by leaving base pipes open during alternative flows. Cliff pools self-clean through turbulence. With modern machines, fencing, windmills, and solar panels, many currently neglected wadi systems could be productively occupied on a modest scale, supporting desert forestry systems. Large trees already grow in most wadis, protected by cliffs and immune from sand blast—restricting hoofed animals would allow many more trees to establish.
