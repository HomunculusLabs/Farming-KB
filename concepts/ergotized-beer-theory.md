# Ergotized Beer Theory

## Overview

The ergotized beer theory proposes that the kykeon — the sacred potion
consumed during the Eleusinian Mysteries of ancient Greece — was a beer
brewed from barley infected with ergot fungus (*Claviceps purpurea*). The
theory was developed by R. Gordon Wasson in collaboration with Albert
Hofmann and Carl Ruck, and publicly unveiled at a mushroom conference in
San Francisco in 1977.

## Historical Context of Eleusis

For nearly two thousand years, the Eleusinian Mysteries were the most
sacred religious rites of the classical Greek and Roman civilizations.
Each September, initiates gathered on the Eleusinian plain near Athens
to participate in a ceremony centered on the goddesses Demeter and
Persephone (Kore).

The power of the Mysteries lay in the fact that they possessed no dogma
but involved sacred acts that engendered religious feeling. Something was
drunk and something was seen by each initiate that was utterly
unexpected, transformative, and capable of remaining as a powerful memory
for the rest of their life.

Cicero called the Mysteries the greatest gift Athens gave to civilization.
Pindar wrote: "Happy is he who, having seen these rites, goes below the
hollow earth; for he knows the end of life and its god-sent beginning."

## The Wasson-Hofmann-Ruck Proposal

Wasson and his colleagues argued that Eleusis was a rite of visionary
intoxication, but that mushrooms were not directly involved. Instead,
the source of intoxication was an ergotized beer brewed from a strain of
ergot fungus infecting barley.

Grain was central to the cult at Eleusis. The festival was a harvest
celebration, and barley was one of Demeter's sacred plants. *Claviceps
purpurea*, a small fungus that infects edible grains, produces ergot — a
source of powerful alkaloids capable of causing hallucination, triggering
labor, and producing strong vasoconstrictive effects.

The purple traditionally associated with the robe of Demeter may signify
the distinctive purple color of ergot sclerotia, the asexual resting
stage in the life cycle of the organism.

## Archaeological Evidence

In 1900, Sir Arthur Evans, excavating near the palace of Knossos in
Crete, unearthed vessels adorned with ears of barley in relief. He
concluded that a kind of beer preceded wine on Crete. Karl Kerenyi
believed the small size of these vessels indicated they were used for a
special kind of barley drink — the visionary sacrament — in rites
"allegedly performed without secrecy at Knossos."

The Minoan origins of the Eleusinian Mysteries are well established.
The oldest cult structures are pre-Hellenic, and many ritual objects
are common to both Minoan and Eleusinian cults.

## The Scandal of 415 B.C.

Wasson and Hofmann's discussion of the scandal of 415 B.C. provides
circumstantial evidence that the Eleusinian sacrament was tangible. The
Athenian noble Alcibiades was fined for having the sacrament in his home
and using it for the entertainment of his friends. Whatever the catalyst
for ecstasy at Eleusis was, it was a physical substance.

## The Toxicity Problem

The theory faces a significant challenge: ergot is toxic. Documented
instances of mass ergot consumption have produced horrific results. An
outbreak in A.D. 994 killed nearly 40,000 people in France. An outbreak
in 1129 killed about 1,200. Ergot-infected flour has been proposed as a
factor in the Salem witch trials and the decline of the Roman Empire.

Two clinical types of ergotism have been described. Gangrenous ergotism
begins with tingling in the fingers, then vomiting and diarrhea, followed
by dry gangrene and limb separation. Convulsive ergotism starts similarly
but progresses to painful spasms and epileptic-like convulsions, with many
patients becoming delirious.

## Proposed Solutions

Wasson and Hofmann identified two potential solutions to the toxicity
problem.

First, *Claviceps paspali*, which preferentially infects barley instead
of rye, may contain a higher proportion of psychoactive but less toxic
simple ergot alkaloids (similar to those in morning glories) and a lower
proportion of the toxic peptide-containing ergot alkaloids.

Second, macerating the ergotized grain in water would effectively
separate the water-soluble psychoactive alkaloids from the fat-soluble
toxic alkaloids. This simple preparation step could have been discovered
empirically by ancient brewers.

## Verification

The theory remains unproven. The "acid test" would be the actual brewing
of a superior hallucinogen from cereal grain infected with some strain of
ergot. As of the theory's publication, no one had subjected it to this
test. Until such an experiment is conducted, the theory remains well-
argued speculation.

## Alternative Theories

If ergot played no part at Eleusis, Robert Graves's proposal that
psilocybin mushrooms constituted the mystery requires reconsideration.
Knowledge of *Stropharia cubensis* or other psilocybin-containing
mushrooms may have survived from Minoan times until the final destruction
of Eleusis in 396 A.D.

Graves believed that recipes in classical sources for preparing the
ritual Eleusinian beverage contained ingredients whose first letters
could be arranged to spell "mushroom" — an ogham, a poetic cipher
device.

## Legacy

With the passing of Eleusis, the great river of partnership society,
Goddess worship, and hallucinogenic ecstasy that had flowed for over ten
thousand years sank into the chthonic realm of forgotten religions. The
Christian triumph ended the glorification of nature as a supreme
spiritual force and established dominator social models of paternalism
and patriarchy in its place.
