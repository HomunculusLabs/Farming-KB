# Homestead Tax Planning

Understanding farm tax obligations and deductions is essential for homestead businesses. Farm income is reported differently than wages, and agricultural operations qualify for numerous deductions, exemptions, and special tax treatment. This guide covers Schedule F, deductions, agricultural exemptions, and record keeping.

## Farm Income Reporting

### Schedule F (Form 1040)
- Farm profit or loss is reported on IRS Schedule F (Profit or Loss from Farming)
- Filed as part of individual income tax return
- Report all farm income: crop sales, livestock sales, product sales, fees, co-op payments
- Report all farm expenses (see deductions below)
- Net farm profit flows to Schedule SE for self-employment tax (15.3%)
- Net farm loss may be limited: hobby loss rules apply if IRS determines operation is not profit-motivated

### Determining Farm vs. Hobby
The IRS presumes an activity is a business if it shows profit in 3 of 5 consecutive years. For farming, the rule is 3 of 5 years. If you don't meet this test, you must prove profit motive using:
- Time and effort devoted to the activity
- Expertise of the taxpayer
- History of income/losses and changes to improve profitability
- Changes in methods of operation
- Dependence on income from the activity
- Personal pleasure or recreation (if present, hobby classification more likely)

### Self-Employment Tax
- 15.3% on net farm profit (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare)
- Deduct half of self-employment tax on Form 1040 (above the line)
- Estimated quarterly payments required if you expect to owe $1,000+ in tax for the year
- Due dates: April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15

## Business Structure

### Sole Proprietorship (Most Common for Homesteads)
- Simplest: no separate filing, everything on personal return
- Schedule F for farm income/expenses
- Schedule SE for self-employment tax
- Personal liability for debts and lawsuits

### LLC (Limited Liability Company)
- Protects personal assets from business liabilities
- Can be single-member or multi-member
- Single-member LLC: default taxed as sole proprietor (Schedule F)
- Multi-member LLC: default taxed as partnership (Form 1065)
- Can elect S-Corp taxation to reduce self-employment tax on profits
- Cost: $50-500 filing fee depending on state
- Annual reporting requirements vary by state

### S-Corporation
- Officer receives reasonable salary (subject to employment tax)
- Remaining profit is not subject to self-employment tax
- Saves 15.3% on profit distributions
- Requires payroll setup and formal corporate governance
- Best when net profit exceeds $30,000-50,000

## Farm Deductions

### Common Farm Expenses (Deductible on Schedule F)
- Seeds, plants, and trees
- Feed for livestock
- Fertilizer and lime
- Pesticides and herbicides
- Fuel and oil (farm use)
- Repairs and maintenance (equipment, buildings, fences)
- Supplies (tools, twine, containers, labels)
- Veterinary expenses and livestock medicines
- Insurance (farm liability, crop, equipment)
- Hired labor and contract labor
- Rent or lease payments (land, equipment)
- Interest on farm loans
- Property taxes on farm land and buildings
- Depreciation on equipment, buildings, and improvements
- Marketing and advertising expenses
- Business travel (mileage at IRS rate, conferences)
- Office supplies and software
- Internet and phone (business portion)
- Education and training related to farming

### Vehicle and Equipment Expenses
- Standard mileage rate: 67 cents/mile (2024) for farm business driving
- Or actual expenses: gas, oil, repairs, insurance, depreciation (keep detailed logs)
- Heavy equipment: depreciate over 5-7 years (Section 179 allows full deduction in year of purchase, limits apply)
- Bonus depreciation: 60% in 2024 (phasing down)

### Home Office Deduction
- If you have a dedicated space used regularly and exclusively for farm business
- Simplified method: $5/sq ft up to 300 sq ft ($1,500 max)
- Or actual expenses: proportional share of rent/mortgage, utilities, insurance

## Agricultural Exemptions

### Property Tax Exemptions
- Agricultural or "greenbelt" valuation: land assessed at agricultural use value (often 90%+ less than market value)
- Requirements vary by state: typically 5-20 acres in active agricultural production
- Some states have minimum income thresholds from farm activities
- Apply through county assessor's office
- Can save thousands per year in property taxes

### Sales Tax Exemptions
- Most states exempt agricultural inputs from sales tax:
  - Feed, seed, fertilizer, pesticides
  - Farm equipment and machinery
  - Building materials for farm structures
- Require a farm tax exemption number (apply through state department of revenue)
- Keep exemption certificates on file for purchases

### Income Tax Credits
- Conservation Reserve Program (CRP) payments: taxable but may qualify for conservation credits
- Renewable energy credits: solar panels, wind turbines on farm
- State-level agricultural credits vary widely
- Earned Income Tax Credit: may apply if farm income is modest

## Record Keeping

### What to Track
- All income: date, source, amount, what was sold
- All expenses: date, vendor, amount, category, what was purchased
- Mileage: date, destination, miles, business purpose
- Inventory: beginning and ending inventory of feed, supplies, seeds
- Capital purchases: equipment, improvements (separate from operating expenses)
- Bank statements and receipts (keep for 7 years)

### Recommended Systems
- Spreadsheet: Google Sheets or Excel (free, flexible)
- Accounting software: QuickBooks Self-Employed ($15/month), Wave (free), FarmBooks
- Mileage tracking: MileIQ, Everlance, or paper log
- Receipt management: Shoeboxed, or physical accordion folder by category
- Farm management: Traction, FarmLogs, or paper notebooks

### Record Retention
- Tax returns and supporting documents: 7 years minimum
- Property records: keep for duration of ownership + 7 years
- Employee records: 4 years after employment tax due date
- Depreciation records: for duration of depreciation period + 7 years

## Quarterly Estimated Taxes

Calculate estimated tax liability and pay quarterly to avoid penalties:
1. Estimate annual farm income and expenses
2. Calculate expected tax (income tax + self-employment tax)
3. Pay 25% each quarter: April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15
4. Safe harbor: pay 100% of prior year's tax liability (110% if AGI over $150,000)
5. Underpayment penalty: calculated on Form 2210

## Common Mistakes

- Not tracking expenses: leaving deductions on the table
- Commingling personal and farm finances: open a separate farm bank account
- Missing quarterly payments: penalties add up
- Not claiming depreciation: equipment is a major deduction
- Ignoring home office deduction: if applicable, it's free money
- Not filing as a farm: if you have agricultural income, use Schedule F, not Schedule C

## When to Hire a Tax Professional

- Annual farm income exceeds $25,000
- You have employees or hire seasonal labor
- You're considering changing business structure
- You have complex deductions (depreciation, conservation programs)
- You receive a notice from the IRS
- Investment income from farm exceeds operating income

A tax professional who specializes in agriculture pays for themselves in deductions found. Budget $300-800 for farm tax preparation.
