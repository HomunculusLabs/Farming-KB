# Mycorrhizal Nitrogen Nutrition and Litter Decomposition Dynamics

## Overview

The ability of [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] to access and mobilize nitrogen from diverse organic sources represents one of the most important aspects of the [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]]. Research spanning several decades has revealed that different [[mycorrhizal-types]] employ distinct strategies for nitrogen acquisition, from peptide utilization by [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] to protein-polyphenol complex decomposition by ericoid mycorrhizal fungi. Simultaneously, mycorrhizal fungi interact with litter decomposition processes in complex ways that influence ecosystem nutrient cycling.

## Nitrogen Acquisition from Organic Sources

### Ectomycorrhizal Peptide and Protein Utilization
Abuzinadah and Read conducted a landmark series of studies on protein nitrogen nutrition in ectomycorrhizal plants:

- **1986a**: Demonstrated that ectomycorrhizal fungi can utilize peptides and proteins as nitrogen sources directly
- **1986b**: Showed protein utilization by birch (*Betula*), spruce (*Picea*), and pine (*Pinus*) in mycorrhizal association with *Hebeloma crustuliniforme*
- **1989**: Found that birch infected with different mycorrhizal fungi varies in its capacity to utilize peptides

This work established that ectomycorrhizal fungi bridge the gap between plant nitrogen demand and the large pool of organic nitrogen in soil, bypassing the mineralization bottleneck.

### Ericoid Mycorrhizal Nitrogen Strategies
Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi have evolved particularly effective mechanisms for accessing nitrogen bound in complex organic compounds:

- **Bajwa and Read (1985)**: Showed that peptides serve as nitrogen sources for the ericoid endophyte and for both mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal ericaceous plants
- **Bending and Read (1996)**: Demonstrated nitrogen mobilization from protein–polyphenol complexes by both ericoid and ectomycorrhizal fungi, a capability critical in heathland and boreal forest soils where nitrogen is predominantly in polyphenol-bound forms
- **Kerley and Read (1995)**: Showed chitin degradation by the ericoid endophyte *Hymenoscyphus ericae* and transfer of chitin-derived nitrogen to the host plant

### Variation Among Ectomycorrhizal Species
Anderson et al. (2001) demonstrated that different isolates of the ectomycorrhizal genus *Pisolithus* vary significantly in their nitrogen source utilization, highlighting the importance of fungal species identity in determining nitrogen cycling outcomes.

## Phosphorus Acquisition and Phosphatase Activity

### Ectomycorrhizal Phosphatases
Phosphatase enzymes produced by mycorrhizal fungi are critical for accessing organic phosphorus:

- **Dighton (1983)**: Documented phosphatase production by mycorrhizal fungi
- **Antibus et al. (1992)**: Showed phosphatase activities and phosphorus uptake from inositol phosphate by ectomycorrhizal fungi
- **Antibus et al. (1997)**: Demonstrated root surface phosphatase activities and uptake of radiolabelled inositol phosphate in field-collected gray birch and red maple roots
- **Kroehler et al. (1988)**: Found that organic and inorganic phosphorus concentrations affect acid phosphatase activity of ectomycorrhizal fungi

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Phosphorus Dynamics
- **Bolan (1991)**: Provided a critical review of the role of mycorrhizal fungi in phosphorus uptake by plants
- **Jayachandran et al. (1992)**: Demonstrated mineralization of organic phosphorus by [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]
- **Clark and Zeto (2000)**: Reviewed mineral acquisition by arbuscular mycorrhizal plants broadly

### Phosphodiesterase Activity
Leake and Miles (1996) showed that the ericoid mycorrhizal fungus *Hymenoscyphus ericae* produces phosphodiesterases and can utilize DNA as a phosphorus source, expanding the known range of organic phosphorus compounds accessible to mycorrhizal fungi.

## Litter Decomposition and Mycorrhizal Interactions

### Mycorrhizal Suppression of Decomposition
The Gadgil and Gadgil studies established a foundational paradox in mycorrhizal ecology:

- **Gadgil and Gadgil (1971)**: First showed that mycorrhiza presence is associated with reduced litter decomposition rates
- **Gadgil and Gadgil (1975)**: Demonstrated suppression of litter decomposition by mycorrhizal roots of *Pinus radiata*
- **Berg and Lindberg (1980)**: Asked whether litter decomposition is retarded in the presence of mycorrhizal roots

The proposed mechanism is that ectomycorrhizal fungi compete with free-living saprotrophic fungi for nutrients, thereby indirectly slowing decomposition.

### Mycorrhizal Litter Exploitation
In contrast to suppression, mycorrhizal fungi can also directly exploit litter:

- **Bending and Read (1995a)**: Described foraging behavior and translocation of nutrients from exploited litter by ectomycorrhizal mycelium
- **Bending and Read (1995b)**: Showed activities of nutrient-mobilizing enzymes in birch litter colonized by *Paxillus involutus*
- **Dighton et al. (1987)**: Studied interactions between tree roots, mycorrhizas, a saprotrophic fungus, and decomposition of organic substrates

### Litter Quality Effects
Conn and Dighton (2000) demonstrated that litter quality influences decomposition rates, ectomycorrhizal community structure, and mycorrhizal root surface acid phosphatase activity, linking above-ground litter chemistry to below-ground mycorrhizal function.

## Ectomycorrhizal Mats and Soil Chemistry

### Nitrogen Cycling in Mats
Cromack et al. (1979) discovered calcium oxalate accumulation and soil weathering in mats of the hypogeous fungus *Hysterangium crassum*, while Griffiths et al. (1990) documented seasonal variation in nitrogen chemistry and nitrogen cycle transformation rates in Douglas fir forest soils colonized by ectomycorrhizal mats.

Aguilera et al. (1993) found elevated nitrogen levels in ectomycorrhizal mat soils compared to non-mat soils in different-age Douglas fir forests.

### Soil Animal Interactions
Cromack et al. (1988) investigated interactions between soil animals and ectomycorrhizal fungal mats, revealing that the dense mycelial mats create unique microhabitats for soil fauna.

## Mycorrhizae in Specialized Habitats

### Salt Marshes
Mycorrhizal fungi colonize plants in extreme environments:
- **Brown and Bledsoe (1996)**: Documented spatial and temporal dynamics of mycorrhizas in the salt marsh halophyte *Jaumea carnosa*
- **Cooke and Lefor (1990, 1998)**: Compared mycorrhizae in disturbed and undisturbed salt marsh regions
- **Cooke et al. (1993)**: Observed vertical distribution of arbuscular mycorrhizae in roots of salt marsh grasses in saturated soils

### Heavy Metal Resistance
Bradley et al. (1982) showed that ericoid mycorrhizal infection plays a role in heavy metal resistance of ericaceous plants, a finding with implications for phytoremediation and mine spoil reclamation.

### Successional Dynamics
- **Boerner et al. (1996)**: Documented spatial patterns of mycorrhizal infectiveness along a successional chronosequence
- **Barni and Siniscalco (2000)**: Studied vegetation dynamics and arbuscular mycorrhiza in old-field successions in the western Italian Alps
- **Fleming et al. (1986)**: Described ectomycorrhizal succession in Scottish birch woodland

## Fungal Succession Theory
Frankland (1992, 1998) explored mechanisms and predictability of fungal succession, concluding that fungal succession remains inherently unpredictable, a theme that complicates our understanding of how mycorrhizal communities develop and function over time.

## Isotopic Insights
Hobbie et al. (1999) used isotopic evidence to gain insights into nitrogen and carbon dynamics of ectomycorrhizal versus saprotrophic fungi, establishing a powerful tool for distinguishing the ecological roles of different fungal functional groups in situ.
