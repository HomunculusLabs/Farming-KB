## Flame Weeding and Cultivation for Weed Control

Weed management is a critical challenge in intensive vegetable production, particularly in greenhouse systems where crops are planted at close spacing on fertile soils. The high fertility and intensive planting that drive exceptional yields also create ideal conditions for weed germination and growth. Eliot Coleman's approach to weed control emphasizes prevention first, followed by targeted mechanical and thermal intervention, with a philosophy of working early, often, and shallow.

## The Weed Challenge in Intensive Systems

When crops are planted at 2.25-inch row spacing to maximize yield per square foot, mechanical cultivation between rows becomes extremely difficult. No conventional cultivating tool works effectively at such narrow spacing. Even baby-leaf salad crops, which occupy the ground for only a short period, can be significantly impacted by weeds. Quick-growing species like chickweed slow harvest dramatically by contaminating the mix and forcing additional sorting time.

The yield increase possible from close spacing on fertile greenhouse soil makes any effort the grower devotes to solving weed problems a worthwhile investment of time. Every square foot lost to weed competition directly reduces the economic return from expensive protected growing space.

## Prevention: The First Line of Defense

The most effective weed control strategy is prevention. Coleman's approach centers on a simple but powerful principle: **never allow any weed to go to seed**. This means hand-weeding in fields and greenhouses whenever necessary, even weeding [[cover-crops]] to prevent seed production. In cases where weed pressure becomes excessive in a green manure stand, the entire stand may be tilled under and replanted rather than allowing weeds to mature.

This zero-tolerance approach to weed seed production is the foundation upon which all other weed control methods build. Each weed that goes to seed can produce hundreds or thousands of offspring, potentially creating years of additional work. The time spent preventing seed production is always less than the time that will be spent dealing with the consequences of neglect.

Research has demonstrated that the serious weed-seed load in a soil can be greatly reduced over a period of three to five years if no new weed seeds are introduced to the area. This long-term approach to seed bank management is one of the most powerful tools available to the persistent grower. While complete eradication is unrealistic, systematic prevention can dramatically reduce weed pressure over time, making all other control methods more effective.

## Preemergence Flaming

Given that weeds will inevitably appear despite preventive efforts, the second line of defense is to dispatch weeds after they germinate but before the crop emerges. The most effective method for this is **preemergence flaming** — using a propane flame to kill weed seedlings on a seeded bed just before the crop seedlings break through the soil surface.

The principle is straightforward: the not-yet-emerged crop seeds remain insulated from the heat of the flame by the soil, while the small weed seedlings exposed at the surface are killed. This creates a clean bed for the crop to emerge into, free from competition during the critical establishment period.

### Timing the Flame

Timing is the single most important factor in successful flaming. Two approaches have been used:

**The glass pane method** was taught to Coleman by European growers in the 1970s. After sowing, square-foot panes of glass are placed in a wooden frame about one inch above the soil surface in random locations throughout the field. The glass creates a warm microclimate that accelerates crop seed germination. The grower monitors the glass panes and flames the field immediately upon seeing the first crop seed germination beneath the glass. This method works well in early spring but becomes impractical in summer when temperatures under the glass become too hot.

**The calendar method** is simpler and more adaptable. The grower pays careful attention and keeps detailed notes on days-to-germination for different crops at different seasons. When sowing a new bed, the germination notes are consulted, a one- or two-day fudge factor is subtracted, and the flaming date is set accordingly. This method works year-round and becomes more accurate as the grower accumulates seasons of data.

### Bed Preparation Timing

Whenever possible, the seedbed should be prepared up to two weeks before the planned sowing date. During this pre-sowing period, the area is irrigated if necessary to encourage weed-seed germination. The crop is then sown without further cultivation, and the bed is flamed at the appropriate time. This approach maximizes the number of weed seeds that germinate and can be killed before the crop emerges.

### Crop Suitability for Flaming

Preemergence flaming is most effective for crops that take a relatively long time to germinate — carrots, onions, and parsley are classic examples. The longer germination period gives weeds a head start that can be exploited with well-timed flaming.

For quick-germinating crops planted in close succession — particularly lettuce and arugula — there is often not enough time for weeds to germinate ahead of the crop, making flaming difficult to time effectively. However, even for these crops, flaming can still be worthwhile when the bed was prepared well in advance of sowing.

### Chickweed: The Winter Greenhouse Nemesis

Chickweed (*Stellaria media*) is identified as the most troublesome weed in winter greenhouses. It thrives on fertile soils and possesses the remarkable ability to germinate and set seed at surprisingly low temperatures. Coleman has observed chickweed plants in full bloom in an unheated greenhouse on January 15. This cold tolerance means chickweed can complete its entire life cycle within the winter growing season, continuously replenishing the seed bank.

Because chickweed is a cool-season specialist, winter greenhouse conditions are ideal for its growth. The combination of fertile soil, regular irrigation, and protection from extreme weather creates an environment where chickweed can outcompete even intentionally planted crops if not managed aggressively.

Coleman explicitly notes that chickweed is not considered a desirable addition to baby-leaf salad mix, unlike some salad growers who tolerate it. This makes control even more important for growers whose market depends on clean, consistent salad mixtures.

### Flaming and Bed Preparation Timing

The interaction between bed preparation timing and flaming effectiveness deserves emphasis. When a bed is prepared two or more weeks before sowing, the grower gains several advantages:

1. Multiple flushes of weed seeds can germinate during the waiting period
2. Irrigation can be used to trigger germination of additional weed seeds
3. The grower has more flexibility in choosing the optimal flaming date
4. By the time the crop is sown, the first wave of weeds has already been eliminated

This approach transforms flaming from a reactive measure into a proactive strategy that dramatically reduces the weed seed bank before the crop is even planted.

## The Science of Flaming

Understanding the physics of [[flame-weeding]] helps the operator use propane efficiently and effectively. The goal of flaming is **wilting, not burning** the weeds.

Research has established that exposure to a temperature of 160°F (71°C) for one second is sufficient to melt the protein cells in small weed seedlings, ideally those less than one inch tall. The weeds continue to look normal for an hour or two after being flamed, but then wilt and die as the cellular damage takes effect.

This delayed response is important for the operator to understand. New practitioners may be tempted to re-flame areas where weeds still appear healthy immediately after treatment, wasting propane in the process. Patience and trust in the science are essential.

A common mistake among new operators is to burn the weeds to a crisp, which is wasteful and counterproductive. Burning rather than wilting uses approximately three times as much propane. Additionally, oversized burners — common on commercially available hand flamers — burn far more fuel than necessary. Excessive burn rates can cause the propane tank to freeze up (since evaporation is a cooling process), which then inhibits propane flow and further reduces efficiency.

## Flamer Design Considerations

Coleman has identified several design improvements for flame weeders that would increase efficiency and practicality:

- **Properly sized nozzles** matched to the area being covered, rather than oversized burners
- **A hood over the flame** to contain heat and direct it downward toward the soil surface
- **A roller behind the burner** to maintain consistent height above the soil and reduce operator fatigue by taking weight off the arms
- **A 15-inch-wide model** for efficient coverage of 30-inch beds with one pass down and back

The hood design also makes the flamer safer to use near greenhouse sidewalls, where large open flames pose a risk of burning or melting the plastic covering. Coleman encourages other growers to design and fabricate improved tools and share the designs for the benefit of the farming community. This collaborative approach to tool development has been a hallmark of the small-farm movement and continues to drive innovation in appropriate-scale equipment.

## Cultivating Tools: The Collineal Hoe and Wire Weeder

Once weeds appear within the crop, the control strategy shifts to careful cultivation with appropriately designed hand tools. Coleman designed two hoe models specifically for this purpose: the **collineal hoe** and the **wire weeder**. These tools address a real gap in the market — Coleman had never found a commercially available hoe that satisfied his requirements for accurate, efficient weed control in closely spaced crops.

Both tools share the same fundamental design principle: they are meant to be used like razors rather than axes. Their blades are perfectly angled to shave small weeds at the surface of the soil. The residues quickly wilt afterward in sunlight. Shallow cutting minimizes damage to the roots of crop plants, which is critical when crops are growing at close spacing where even minor root disturbance can reduce yield.

Blade sharpness is essential. Dull blades tear rather than cut, increasing the likelihood that weeds will re-root. Coleman recommends sharpening the blades regularly and choosing the morning of a sunny day for cultivation work.

### Cultivation Technique

The proper technique for using these hoes involves several key principles:

**Timing**: Always cultivate before weeds exceed one inch in height. Small weeds are far easier to kill and less likely to re-root after cutting.

**Grip and posture**: Walk in the path between beds, standing upright. Hold the hoe handle with thumbs pointing up the handle, similar to holding a broom for sweeping. Always work forward toward the uncut weeds.

**Cutting motion**: Position the hoe blade 3 to 6 inches away from you (depending on weed size and density) over the area to be weeded. Place the blade on the soil surface and shave just under the surface back toward you. Check that all weeds have been cut, then move the blade forward another 3 inches and repeat. This motion is similar to mowing with a scythe — the blade moves into the uncut area and the cutting stroke returns it to the already-cut area.

**Speed cultivation for tiny weeds**: When weeds are very small and conditions are sunny, a faster technique is possible. Hold the collineal hoe or wire weeder between rows just slightly under the soil surface and walk down the path, adjusting the blade's forward angle to compensate for slight discrepancies in row spacing. Keeping depth consistent at no more than 0.25 inch, all tiny weeds are cut off or tipped out to wilt on the surface. This eliminates the forward/return stroke and takes significantly less time.

**Adaptability**: The overarching principle is to always adjust technique to the specific conditions — the size and type of weeds, the weather, and the crop stage. Experience teaches the grower to automatically make small efficiency adjustments based on conditions encountered during previous cultivations.

The guiding philosophy for successful cultivation can be summarized as: **early and often, shallow and efficient, accurate and rapid**. With the right tools and proper technique, consistent weed control is achievable even in intensive planting systems.

## The Broader Philosophy of Attention

Coleman connects weed control technique to a broader principle about farming efficiency. The ability to adjust actions to conditions — choosing the right moment to flame, selecting the appropriate cultivation stroke for the weed size, adapting to weather — is an example of the greater efficiency shown by a worker who is truly familiar with their craft.

With experience, the grower automatically makes small efficiency adjustments based on having performed the task many times before. The path to becoming that proficient worker is simple: pay attention while working, no matter what the job. Attention to detail is identified as the major secret to success in any endeavor, and weed control is no exception.

The right time to cultivate is always the morning of a sunny day, when cut weeds will desiccate rapidly. If rain comes unexpectedly and cut weeds re-root, or another flush of seeds germinates, the solution is simple: do it again. Persistence and regular attention to the weed situation, combined with the right tools and technique, will keep weed pressure manageable season after season. It is worth teaching cultivation skills to employees explicitly, as the technique does not come naturally to many people — especially executing it with the speed and efficiency that commercial production demands.
