# St. John's Wort Growing Guide (Hypericum perforatum)

## Overview

St. John's Wort is a perennial herbaceous plant native to Europe, western
Asia, and North Africa, now naturalized throughout much of North America.
Named for flowering around St. John's Day (June 24), it has been used
medicinally for over 2,400 years and is one of the most clinically studied
herbal remedies, particularly for mild to moderate depression. It also
produces a beautiful red oil infusion valued in topical [[herbal-medicine]].

## Botanical Profile

- Scientific name: [[hypericum-perforatum]]
- Family: Hypericaceae
- Hardiness: USDA zones 3-8
- Lifecycle: Perennial
- Height: 1-3 feet
- Bloom period: June to August

The name "perforatum" refers to the translucent oil glands visible in the
leaves when held up to light. Flowers are bright yellow with five petals
and prominent stamens. When flower buds or flowers are crushed, they release
a reddish-purple pigment ([[hypericin]]), which is the signature of this plant.

## Medicinal Uses

St. John's Wort contains several active compounds: hypericin, hyperforin,
pseudohypericin, flavonoids, and xanthones. Hyperforin is now believed to
be the primary compound responsible for the antidepressant effect, acting on
[[serotonin]], dopamine, and norepinephrine reuptake.

Internal uses:
- Mild to moderate depression: The most extensively studied use. Numerous
  clinical trials and meta-analyses support its efficacy, showing it is
  comparable to standard SSRIs for mild to moderate depression with fewer
  side effects for many people.
- Anxiety: Often co-occurs with depression treatment; may help with
  generalized anxiety.
- Seasonal affective disorder (SAD): Some evidence supports its use for
  winter depression.
- Nerve pain: Historically used for sciatica and neuralgia.
- Topical wounds and burns: Antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties.

Topical uses:
- St. John's Wort oil (red oil): Made by infusing fresh flowers in a carrier
  oil. Used topically for nerve pain, sciatica, muscle aches, minor wounds,
  burns, and sunburn. The red color comes from hypericin extraction.
- Anti-inflammatory and analgesic: Applied to sore muscles, joints, and
  bruised areas.

## Critical Drug Interactions

St. John's Wort is notorious for drug interactions and must be used with
caution. It is a potent inducer of cytochrome P450 enzymes (particularly
CYP3A4) and P-glycoprotein, which speeds up the metabolism of many drugs:

- SSRIs and SNRIs: Risk of serotonin syndrome -- NEVER combine
- Birth control pills: Reduces effectiveness; risk of unintended pregnancy
- Blood thinners (warfarin): Reduces anticoagulant effect
- Immunosuppressants (cyclosporine): Can cause organ rejection in transplant
  patients
- HIV protease inhibitors: Reduces drug effectiveness
- [[digoxin]]: Reduces cardiac drug levels
- Benzodiazepines: Reduces effectiveness
- Many other medications: Always check with a pharmacist or practitioner

Additionally, St. John's Wort causes photosensitivity in some individuals,
particularly at high doses or in fair-skinned people. Avoid excessive sun
exposure while taking high doses internally.

Always inform healthcare providers of St. John's Wort use. Discontinue 2+
weeks before surgery due to interaction with anesthetics.

## Cultivation

### Site Selection
St. John's Wort thrives in full sun to partial shade. It is highly adaptable
to soil conditions, tolerating poor, sandy, gravelly, and even clay soils.
Prefers well-drained soil with a pH of 5.5-7.0. Very drought-tolerant once
established. Naturally colonizes disturbed areas, meadows, and roadsides.

### Propagation

From seed:
- Seeds require light to germinate; surface sow
- Cold stratify for 4-6 weeks or sow in fall
- Germination: 10-30 days at 60-70 degrees F
- Start indoors 6-8 weeks before last frost or direct sow in spring or fall
- Seedlings are slow-growing initially; transplant when 3-4 inches tall

From division:
- Divide established clumps in spring or fall
- Each division should have roots and several shoots
- Space divisions 12-18 inches apart

From cuttings:
- Softwood cuttings in early summer root under mist
- Semi-hardwood cuttings in late summer also work

### Spacing
- Space plants 12-18 inches apart
- Rows 18-24 inches apart
- Plants will spread slowly by rhizomes to form colonies

### Care and Maintenance
- Minimal care required once established
- Water during extended droughts; otherwise, no supplemental irrigation needed
- No fertilizer required; performs well in poor soils
- Cut back spent flower stems to maintain neat appearance
- Divide every 4-5 years to maintain vigor

### Invasive Potential

St. John's Wort is considered an invasive or noxious weed in many areas,
particularly in western North America and Australia. It can aggressively
colonize pastures and rangelands, and it is toxic to livestock (causing
photosensitivity in grazing animals, especially white-faced sheep and cattle).

Management:
- Remove seed heads before they mature to prevent spread
- Monitor for unwanted spread and remove promptly
- Check local regulations before planting -- it is legally restricted in some
  areas
- Keep away from pastures where livestock graze
- Do not [[compost]] mature seed heads

## Harvesting

For medicinal use, harvest the upper 6-12 inches of the plant (flowering
tops including buds, flowers, and upper leaves) during peak bloom.

Optimal harvest timing:
- Harvest on a dry, sunny day in mid-morning after dew has dried
- The highest hypericin content is when approximately 50% of flowers are
  open and 50% are in bud
- Snip stems above a leaf node to allow regrowth
