Fungal exopolysaccharides (EPS) and extracellular polymeric substances represent a diverse group of high-molecular-weight compounds secreted by fungal hyphae into their surrounding environment.

These polymers play essential roles in fungal ecology, including soil aggregation, water retention, mineral weathering, metal binding, and protection against environmental stress.

## Types of Fungal Extracellular Polymers

Fungal extracellular polymers encompass several major classes of compounds.

Capsular polysaccharides form discrete gelatinous layers surrounding individual hyphae or yeast cells.

Mucilage refers to the slimy, hydrated polysaccharide matrix that surrounds fungal colonies and hyphal networks.

Slime layers are less organized than capsules and may be continuously shed into the surrounding medium.

Glomalin is a specific glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi that has received considerable attention for its role in soil aggregation and carbon storage.

Melanin, while primarily considered a pigment, also functions as an extracellular polymer with metal-binding and protective properties.

The chemical composition of fungal exopolysaccharides varies among species but commonly includes glucose, galactose, mannose, glucuronic acid, and galacturonic acid residues.

Many fungal EPS are heteropolymers containing multiple sugar types, and some are modified with acetyl, pyruvyl, or succinyl groups that affect their physical and chemical properties.

## Functions in Soil Aggregation

Exopolysaccharides are critical binding agents in soil aggregate formation.

The polysaccharide capsule surrounding many bacteria, especially Gram-negative bacteria, physically causes clay particles to adhere to the bacteria.

Similarly, fungal mucilage and capsular materials bind soil particles together through several mechanisms.

Cation bridges form between negatively charged carboxyl and hydroxyl groups on polysaccharide chains and negatively charged clay mineral surfaces, mediated by divalent cations such as calcium and magnesium.

Direct hydrogen bonding between polysaccharide hydroxyl groups and clay mineral surfaces provides additional binding strength.

The physical entanglement of polysaccharide chains with mineral particles creates a three-dimensional network that resists disaggregation.

Fungal hyphae coated with exopolysaccharides are particularly effective aggregate stabilizers.

The combination of physical enmeshment by hyphae and chemical binding by their associated polysaccharide coatings creates stable soil aggregates that resist water erosion and mechanical disruption.

## Water Retention and Hyphal Networks

The hydrated mucilaginous sheaths surrounding hyphae provide an interconnected microenvironment within the fungal network.

These hydrated polymer matrices maintain moisture around hyphae during dry periods, allowing continued metabolic activity when bulk soil has dried below the wilting point.

In arid and semi-arid environments, fungal exopolysaccharides contribute significantly to soil water-holding capacity.

The hydrophilic nature of polysaccharides allows them to absorb and retain water, creating local moisture reservoirs around hyphal networks.

This property is particularly important for maintaining fungal activity in soils subject to intermittent drying.

## Rock Weathering and Biogeochemical Cycling

Fungi achieve protection by embedding colonies into mucilaginous polysaccharide slime that may entrap clay particles, providing extra protection against environmental stress.

In rock-inhabiting communities, these extracellular polymers also serve as agents of mineral weathering.

The organic acids and polysaccharides secreted by fungi can dissolve mineral surfaces, releasing nutrients and contributing to soil formation.

The combination of hyphal penetration into rock crevices, acid excretion, and polysaccharide-mediated retention of weathering products creates microenvironments favorable for further weathering and eventual soil development.

Rock-inhabiting fungi in extreme environments such as Antarctic deserts and hot drylands rely heavily on their extracellular polymer coatings for both protection and mineral nutrient acquisition.

## Metal Binding and Bioremediation

Fungal exopolysaccharides have significant metal-binding capacities due to the presence of carboxyl, hydroxyl, sulfate, and phosphate functional groups.

These groups can chelate metal ions through coordinate bonding, effectively immobilizing metals in the fungal microenvironment.

The metal-binding properties of fungal EPS have implications for bioremediation of contaminated soils.

Extracellular polymers can sequester heavy metals and radionuclides, preventing their uptake by plants and movement into groundwater.

The hydrated mucilage surrounding hyphal networks provides a large surface area and numerous binding sites for metal ion complexation.

In lichens, which produce abundant extracellular polysaccharides, metal accumulation is particularly effective.

Lichens can accumulate metals such as lead and copper, as well as radionuclides, forming metal-organic biominerals during growth on metal-rich substrates.

## Protection Against Environmental Stress

Exopolysaccharide coatings provide multiple forms of environmental protection.

The slimy matrix surrounding fungal colonies creates a diffusion barrier that slows the penetration of toxic compounds, including heavy metals and organic pollutants.

The polysaccharide layer also provides a buffer against rapid changes in osmotic potential, helping fungi maintain cellular water balance in fluctuating environments.

In rock-inhabiting and extremotolerant fungi, the combination of melanin pigments and mucilaginous polysaccharide slime provides comprehensive protection against UV radiation, desiccation, temperature extremes, and chemical stressors.

These fungi represent some of the most stress-resistant organisms known, and their extracellular polymers are key to their survival strategy.

## Role in Fungal-Faunal Interactions

Fungal extracellular polymers influence interactions with soil fauna.

The mucilage coating of fungal hyphae affects palatability and digestibility for fungal grazers such as collembola and nematodes.

Some soil animals preferentially feed on fungal species with particular EPS compositions, while others are deterred by the physical or chemical properties of the slime layer.

In wood-decomposing systems, the association between marine fungi and wood-boring crustaceans such as the gribble (Limnoria tripunctata) depends on fungal modification of wood through enzymatic activity and EPS production.

The gribble has increased longevity when feeding on fungus-colonized wood and is incapable of reproduction without fungal components in its diet, possibly due to enhanced availability of proteins, essential amino acids, and vitamins provided by fungal EPS.
