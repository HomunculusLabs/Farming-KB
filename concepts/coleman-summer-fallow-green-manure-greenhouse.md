# Summer Fallow Green Manure Crops in Winter Harvest Greenhouses

## Overview

During the years of the backwards farm experiment at Four Season Farm, Eliot Coleman developed
a systematic approach to summer soil management using long-term green manure crops. When the
winter harvest ended in late May and the greenhouses were emptied, the exposed soil was
planted with deep-rooting leguminous cover crops that occupied the ground for up to thirteen
months before the next winter crop cycle began. This practice became a cornerstone of the
farm's soil fertility program during its early years and demonstrated the critical role that
planned fallow periods play in sustainable intensive vegetable production.

## The Soil Fertility Challenge

Intensive winter vegetable production extracts significant nutrients from the soil. Without
intentional replenishment, the soil would quickly become depleted and crop quality would
decline.

The conventional approach to this problem is to apply fertilizers — either synthetic or
organic — to replace what has been removed. But Coleman's deep-organic philosophy led him to
seek solutions that worked with natural soil biology rather than simply adding external
inputs.

Green manure crops offered the ideal solution. Rather than importing fertility, the farm
could grow its own.

## The Backwards Farm Context

The summer fallow green manure practice arose directly from the backwards farm experiment —
the six years when Four Season Farm produced vegetables only during the winter months, from
October through May.

When the winter harvest concluded and the greenhouses were cleaned out in late June, the
growing beds sat empty. Rather than leaving the soil bare, Coleman planted green manure
crops. This was not merely a soil conservation measure but a deliberate fertility-building
strategy that transformed the fallow period from dead time into productive soil-building time.

## Characteristics of the Green Manure Crops

The green manure crops used during the summer fallow were chosen for several specific
characteristics.

They were leguminous, meaning they could fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiotic
bacteria in their root nodules. This biological nitrogen fixation provided a natural source
of the nutrient that vegetable crops need in the greatest quantity.

They were deep-rooting, which meant their roots penetrated deeply into the soil profile,
breaking up compaction, improving drainage, and drawing up minerals from subsoil layers that
shallow-rooted vegetable crops cannot reach.

They produced substantial above-ground biomass that, when turned under, added significant
organic matter to the soil. This organic matter improved soil structure, water-holding
capacity, and microbial activity.

## Duration of the Green Manure Phase

The length of the green manure phase depended on the farmer's willingness to forgo summer
vegetable production.

If the farmer chose to skip summer crops entirely, the green manure could occupy the
uncovered soil for as long as thirteen months — from June through the following July. This
extended period allowed the cover crop to mature fully, produce maximum biomass, and
contribute the greatest possible soil improvement.

If the farmer wanted to grow some summer crops before the greenhouse returned for winter,
the green manure period was shorter — about ten months, from September through July. Even
this reduced period provided significant soil benefits.

All green manures were turned under three to four weeks before the planting date of the
winter crops that followed them, allowing time for decomposition and nutrient release.

## Benefits Beyond Nitrogen

While nitrogen fixation was the most obvious benefit of the leguminous green manures, the
practice provided numerous additional advantages.

The living root systems of the cover crops maintained populations of beneficial soil
microorganisms through the summer months. Bare soil, by contrast, quickly loses its
microbial diversity and activity.

The above-ground biomass protected the soil surface from erosion by wind and rain, and
from the drying and crusting effects of direct sun exposure.

The dense canopy of the cover crop suppressed weed germination and growth, reducing the
weed seed bank in the soil for subsequent crops.

When the green manure was turned under, the decomposition process stimulated a burst of
microbial activity that made nutrients more available to the following vegetable crops.

## Connection to the Mobile Greenhouse System

The summer green manure practice also played a role in the development of the mobile
greenhouse system. One of the advantages of moving greenhouses between two sites is that
for one year out of every two, the growing beds are uncovered and exposed to the cleansing
powers of sun, rain, wind, and snow.

During this uncovered year, a long-term green manure crop can be planted on that section.
The green manure thus occupies the soil for an extended period without interfering with the
covered production cycle.

The mobile greenhouse system effectively institutionalized the summer fallow, making it a
permanent feature of the rotation rather than an ad hoc response to empty beds.

## Lessons from the French Maraichers

The use of green manures for soil building during fallow periods has deep historical roots.
The nineteenth-century Parisian maraichers, whose techniques so strongly influenced Coleman,
also understood the importance of soil rest and renewal.

Vincent Gressent wrote in 1870 that insect pests only attack weak, sickly plant specimens
lacking proper nutrition, and pointed to the market gardens of Paris — where copious compost
use and rational crop rotations were practiced — as proof that properly nourished plants
resist pests naturally.

The summer green manure practice at Four Season Farm can be seen as a modern continuation
of this principle: building soil health as the foundation of crop health, and crop health as
the foundation of pest resistance.

## The Evolution Toward Year-Round Production

When Coleman eventually transitioned from the backwards farm model to year-round production,
the summer green manure practice was not abandoned but adapted.

The mobile greenhouse system preserves the benefits of the summer fallow by alternating
covered and uncovered sites. The uncovered site each year receives the same soil-building
treatment that the entire farm received during the backwards farm years.

In this way, the lessons learned during six years of winter-only growing continue to inform
the farm's management even under the more intensive year-round schedule.
