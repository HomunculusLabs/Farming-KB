# Quarter-Acre Farming and Self-Sufficiency
One of Masanobu Fukuoka's most provocative proposals in *The Natural Way
of Farming* is his vision of quarter-acre farming for all people. This
concept challenges the foundational assumptions of modern agricultural
economics and calls for a radical rethinking of the relationship between
land, labor, food, and money.

## The Original Image of Farming

Fukuoka traced the historical reality of Japanese farming to argue that
small-scale agriculture was never inherently inadequate. Before the
postwar transformation, farmers were said to grow a hundred crops. In the
paddy field and vegetable gardens, they raised rice, barley, and other
grains, as well as sweet potato and many different vegetables.
Fruits ripened on trees next to the farmhouse, which was surrounded by a
shelterbelt. A cow was kept under the same roof and chickens ran about
loose in the farmyard, protected by a dog. A beehive hung from the eaves.
All peasants were totally self-supporting and enjoyed the richest and
safest possible diet. That they are seen as having been poor and hungry,
Fukuoka argued, may well reflect the envy of modern man more than anything
else. People today have never had the experience of living independently
by their own devices, so they know neither spiritual nor material poverty
and abundance.
The perception of peasant poverty is a projection of modern alienation
onto a way of life that was, in truth, self-sufficient and rich in ways
that money cannot measure.

## A Quarter-Acre Is Enough

A quarter-acre of land, Fukuoka insisted, is enough to supply the food
needed for supporting a family. If anything, a full acre is too large.
Had the peasants been exuberant at heart and ruled over by a benevolent
government, instead of living in mean poverty, they could have lived like
princes on their acre of land.
The poverty of historical peasant farmers was not of their own making.
They were the victims of outside forces: an oppressive social system and
political and economic mechanisms beyond their control. Their poverty was
structural, not agricultural. The land itself was abundantly productive;
it was the social and economic arrangements surrounding it that kept
farmers impoverished.

## The Paradox of Scale

Following World War II, farms in Japan increased steadily in size, going
from one to two to four acres. Although farmland increased, more peasants
abandoned farming. Today, full-time farms have grown to fifteen or even
twenty-five acres, comparable to Western farms, yet they have become
increasingly unstable. This paradox reveals the error of equating scale
with viability: larger farms produced greater dependency on external
inputs, greater debt, and greater vulnerability to market fluctuations.
The expansion was driven not by agricultural necessity but by economic
theories measuring success in monetary terms rather than well-being.

## The Critique of Agricultural Economics

Fukuoka launched a sustained attack on the economic framework used to
evaluate farming, challenging its most basic premises:
**Income-based viability assessment** makes no sense. Japan has the world's
highest land productivity but low labor productivity per worker. Fukuoka
inverted the conventional logic entirely.
**The international division of labor argument** holds that Japan should
import food from countries with lower production costs. Fukuoka regarded
this as the core of Japan's destructive agricultural policy, sacrificing
food sovereignty on the altar of economic efficiency.
**Low labor productivity is cause for pride, not shame.** It indicates that
prices for produce are unreasonably low or that farming materials are
unfairly high. Farmers never calculated wages for their labor because
farming was done aside from any considerations over money.

## Agriculture Has Nothing to Do with Profitability

Fukuoka's most radical claim was that agriculture has nothing to do,
fundamentally, with profitability. The overriding concern is how to make
use of the land. The goal of farming is to produce plentiful harvest by
bringing out the full forces of nature, because this also happens to be
the shortest road to knowing and approaching nature.
Farming is not centered on income or on man; at its core are natural
fields that transcend man. The fields of nature are the representatives
of nature; they are God. The farmer is in the service of God, so immediate
gain is a secondary concern. He should rejoice and feel gratified when
his fields bear richly.
This renders conventional economic analysis fundamentally misguided.
Applying industrial metrics to what is essentially a spiritual practice
and a relationship with nature produces actively destructive conclusions.

## Zero Production Costs

When Fukuoka said that crops do not need to be priced, he meant that
whether they have prices or not makes no difference to the farmer who
dedicates himself to natural farming. Because he has no use for various
chemical-based farming materials and does not reckon household labor into
his expenses, his production costs are zero.
If all farmers thought along these lines, crop prices everywhere would
settle to the same level and would no longer be needed. Prices are a
man-made device that do not exist in nature. Nothing has less to do with
the crops of nature than money.

## Mu Economics

Fukuoka proposed a new economics founded on Mu — the state where
willful human actions are cast aside. Crops grown by natural farming
should be assessed on a natural economy, not a monetary one. Establishing
Mu economics requires discarding false values and unearthing the true
value of agriculture. Mu natural farming must be supported with Mu
economics and Mu government.
In a nation of small farmers, some consignment farming, sharecropping,
or cooperative cultivation might arise, but trade would be limited to
occasional surplus exchange in open-air markets. The emphasis is always
on local self-sufficiency rather than commercial production.

## The Land Problem

Fukuoka identified the systematic theft of farmland as a central injustice:
taxes on farmland became unjustly high when assessed on the same scale as
housing land, clearly designed to drive farmers off their land. The oases
of green left in towns and cities are vanishing from the reach of the
farmer, and someday these hardships will return as calamity to threaten
the well-being of city dwellers.

## The Minimum Law

Fukuoka proposed that if people scattered out and built a small house on
a quarter-acre wherever they pleased, the food problem would take care of
itself, water and sewage lines would not be needed, and pollution would
be licked. When people can do without a law, it is best not to have it.
Were a single law necessary, it should be: one shall build one's house at
least sixty feet from one's neighbor. Only scoundrels and those in power
gain from capricious laws that take land out of farmers' hands.
