# Vegetable Transplanting and Hardening Off

Setting out healthy transplants at the right time and with proper
technique is critical to success in [[home-vegetable-garden-planning]].
Many vegetables, including tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, broccoli,
cabbage, and cauliflower, are best started indoors and then
transplanted to the garden. This page covers selecting transplants,
preparing planting holes, the hardening off process, and protecting
young plants from frost.

## Selecting Quality Transplants

Buy only sturdy, green transplants of known cultivars. Avoid tall,
spindly plants that have been grown in insufficient light. Look for
transplants with:
- Stocky, thick stems rather than tall, leggy growth
- Healthy green foliage without yellowing, spots, or wilting
- Well-developed root systems that are not pot-bound
- No signs of insect pests or disease

Transplants in individual peat or plastic pots are easy to work with
and generally perform better than bare-root plants.

## Handling Peat Pots

When planting a peat pot, be sure to tear off the rim and bottom of
the peat pot before planting. The edges, when exposed to the air, act
as a wick and draw moisture away from the root area. Removing the
bottom of the peat pot will allow easier root penetration. Also, be
sure the peat pot is moist at planting time. In sandy soil, carefully
remove the entire peat pot. All plastic pots should be removed before
planting.

## Timing of Transplanting

To avoid wilting and possible death of plants, set them out during the
late afternoon or early evening, when the sun's intensity and the wind
have died down. This gives transplants the overnight period to recover
before facing full sun the next day. Avoid transplanting on extremely
hot or windy days whenever possible.

## Hardening Off

Transplants can be "hardened off" by gradually exposing them to the
cooler, brighter, and windier outdoors for 1 to 2 weeks before final
planting. Hardening off is the process of gradually acclimating
indoor-grown seedlings to outdoor growing conditions.

### How to Harden Off Transplants

The hardening off process should be gradual. Follow these steps:
1. Begin about 1 to 2 weeks before your planned transplant date.
2. On the first day, place plants outdoors in a sheltered, shady
   location for just 1 to 2 hours.
3. Gradually increase the time outdoors each day over 7 to 14 days.
4. Slowly move plants into increasing amounts of direct sunlight.
5. Begin exposing plants to light wind as the week progresses.
6. Reduce watering slightly during the hardening off period to
   strengthen stems.
7. By the end of the process, plants should be able to stay outdoors
   overnight, as long as frost is not expected.

### Important Hardening Off Notes

Hardening off should not be a substitute for producing or buying stocky,
vigorous plants rather than tall, spindly ones. Even well-hardened
spindly plants will not perform as well as sturdy transplants. The goal
of hardening off is to prepare already healthy plants for outdoor
conditions, not to compensate for poor growing conditions indoors.

## Preparing the Planting Site

First, mark the rows as you would for seed, following proper spacing
as noted in [[vegetable-seed-and-transplant-guide]].
Then dig a hole for each plant roughly twice as wide and twice as deep
as the soil ball, keeping in mind proper planting distance between
plants.

## Planting Technique

Follow these steps when setting out transplants:
1. Set each plant at the same level it grew before. For tomatoes, you
   can plant deeper as they will root along the buried stem.
2. Place soil around the roots and firm gently to eliminate air pockets.
3. Water with starter solution to help roots establish quickly.
4. If the next 2 to 3 days call for sunshine, shade the new transplants
   to prevent wilting.

## Protecting Newly Transplanted Plants

Newspaper tents, coffee cans, or similar materials can be used to
shade and protect newly set transplants. Water as needed, but water
thoroughly each time. Light, frequent watering encourages shallow root
growth, while deep, less frequent watering promotes deeper, stronger
roots.

## Frost Protection

Cover tender crops such as tomatoes, peppers, and eggplants when frosty
nights are forecast. Suitable coverings include:
- **Cardboard boxes**: Easy to place and remove, good for individual
  plants.
- **Blankets or old bed sheets**: Can cover multiple plants at once.
- **Hot caps**: Wax-coated paper or plastic domes placed over
  individual plants.
- **Fabric row covers**: Spun-bonded polyester fabrics that allow
  light and moisture through while trapping heat.
- **Newspaper tents**: Simple and effective for small plants.

### Frost Protection Rules

- Do not allow the coverings to touch the plants, for they could
  injure the plants where contact is made. Use stakes or wire hoops to
  support coverings above the foliage.
- Remove the covers in the morning once temperatures rise above
  freezing to prevent overheating.
- Do not use metal or plastic sheets to protect plants from frost
  damage. They actually conduct cold to the plants and can cause more
  harm than no protection at all.
