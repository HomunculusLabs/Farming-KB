# Soil Rehabilitation Methods

## Overview

Soil rehabilitation is the process of restoring degraded, compacted, or eroded soils to productive health. Permaculture approaches rehabilitation through a combination of water control, soil conditioning, biological fertilisation, and careful species selection. No matter the starting substrate, rich and well-structured soils can be created through organic methods.

## The Four Pillars of Soil Rehabilitation

### 1. Water Control

Drainage and irrigation management are the first priority, particularly for salted or waterlogged areas. Drought is reframed as a design failure rather than an inevitable condition — it only becomes a problem where:

- Poor or no water storage has been developed
- Tree crops have been sacrificed for fodder or fuel
- Grain crops depend entirely on annual rains

#### Drainage Methods for Waterlogged Soils

Where water lies over impermeable layers or backs up behind barriers, three remediation techniques apply:

**Raised garden beds**: Paths are dug down as drains while beds are raised. In very wet areas, paths receive a 1:500 slope to prevent erosion.

**Deep open drains**: Installed every 10-80 m (for clay-sand soils), positioned upslope and downslope or on either side of garden beds.

**Underground pipes**: Fluted plastic pipes (tile drains) laid in trenches 1.5 m deep, spaced 10-80 m apart, starting from a drain or stream with a gentle fall of 1:1000 to 1:600.

#### Water Retention

Long-term soil additives in the form of **hydrogels** absorb and release water over many rain cycles. These are practical only for gardens or high-value tree crops where the cost amortises over time.

### 2. Soil Conditioning

Compacted, collapsed, and eroded soils require **rehabilitative aeration** combined with a change in land use. Mechanical conditioning (chisel ploughing, subsoiling) opens compacted earth, followed by biological agents that maintain and deepen the loosened structure.

### 3. Fertilisation

Wasteful or polluting fertilisation can be replaced by:

- **Foliar sprays** of trace elements in very small amounts, greatly assisting plant establishment
- **[[seed-pelleting]]** using key elements that are locally deficient in plants
- **Legume integration** to create total nitrogen for food crops via interplanting and green manures
- Unlocking phosphate locked up in clays through biological activity

### 4. Crop and Plant Species Selection

Many older crop varieties yield with less fertiliser and water than recently developed varieties. Additional benefits include:

- Superior flavour (e.g., heritage Pippin and Russet apples versus market varieties)
- Pest and disease resistance
- Medicinal value
- Reliable production in sub-optimal conditions

**Pioneer species** — including so-called noxious weeds — colonise eroded landscapes and begin the slow march towards stabilisation. These can be harvested as mulches.

## Building Soil at Garden Scale

Gardeners build soil through three integrated processes:

1. **Shape the earth**: Raise or lower beds to facilitate watering or drainage, carefully levelling surfaces
2. **Mix amendments**: Incorporate compost, humus materials, clay, sand, or nutrients to bring soil to balance
3. **Mulch**: Reduce water loss, sun exposure, and erosion with surface organic matter

Accessory systems include growing compost materials as hedgerows, herbs, or plantation within and around the garden, combined with trellis, shadecloth, glasshouses, and trickle irrigation.

## Building Soil at Farm Scale

Large-scale systems require different approaches unless producing high-value product:

- **Broad-scale drainage** and soil conditioning
- Primary aeration by machinery or biological agents
- Careful plant and livestock management to maintain open, humus-rich soil
- Introduction of deep-rooted species: [[daikon-radish]], comfrey, chicory, lucerne, tree legumes
- Worm species introduction to pastures
- Livestock management for minimal compaction and over-grazing

## Biological Chisel Ploughs

Where mechanical implements cannot reach, deep-rooted plants serve as living soil openers:

| Region | Biological Chisel Species |
|---|---|
| Japan (Fukuoka) | Radish, Acacia |
| Africa | [[acacia-albida]], Glyricidia |
| New Guinea | Casuarina |
| Mediterranean | Tamarix |

For difficult terrain — boulder fields, dunes, steep slopes, laterites — **mixed legume/non-legume forests** (citrus, olive, pine, oak) provide the best permanent solution.

## Evidence of Effectiveness

Research by [[erik-van-der-werf]] at the Agomeda Agricultural Project in Ghana measured soil improvement through bulk density (g/cc):

| Soil Treatment | Bulk Density |
|---|---|
| Annually burnt bush | 1.35 |
| Bush left 2 years without fire | 1.27 |
| Farmland, cultivated 2 years | 1.29 |
| Farmland, permanently mulched and cropped for 3 years | 0.92 |

The mulched soils showed dramatically improved aeration, soil temperatures 10°C lower, threefold increases in organic matter production, and improved grain yields — all achieved **without tillage**.

## Key Principles

1. **Water control is always the first step** — drainage before anything else on waterlogged sites
2. **Organic matter is the universal soil conditioner** — mulching alone restores structure
3. **Older crop varieties are more resilient** — preserve and cultivate heritage varieties
4. **Weeds are pioneers, not enemies** — they initiate the rehabilitation sequence
5. **Scale determines method** — gardens use sheet mulch; farms use chisel ploughs and managed grazing
