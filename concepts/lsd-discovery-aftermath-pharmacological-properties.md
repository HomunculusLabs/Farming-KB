# LSD Discovery Aftermath — Pharmacological Properties and Initial Reactions

## Overview

Following Albert Hofmann's self-experiment with LSD-25 on April 19, 1943, several remarkable properties of the new substance became apparent. This concept page covers the immediate aftermath of the discovery, including LSD's unique pharmacological profile, the reactions of Hofmann's colleagues, and the early recognition of its potential applications in medicine and psychiatry.

## The No-Hangover Property

### Absence of Aftereffects

One of the most surprising aspects of LSD was "its ability to produce such a far-reaching, powerful state of inebriation without leaving a hangover." On the day after his self-experiment, Hofmann felt himself to be "in excellent physical and mental condition."

This was remarkable because virtually all known inebriants—alcohol, opiates, barbiturates—produced characteristic hangover effects: headache, nausea, fatigue, cognitive impairment. LSD's lack of aftereffects suggested that its mechanism of action was fundamentally different from conventional intoxicants.

### Implications

The absence of hangover had important implications for potential medical use: if LSD could produce profound alterations of consciousness without subsequent impairment, it could be used in clinical settings without requiring extended recovery periods. This property distinguished LSD from all other psychoactive substances known at the time.

## The "Terrifying, Demonic Aspect"

### Hofmann's Initial Perception

Hofmann's first LSD experience was overwhelmingly negative—what he later called its "terrifying, demonic aspect." The loss of control, the visual distortions, the sense of being in the grip of an alien force—all of these were experienced as threatening rather than pleasurable.

Given this initial experience, "the last thing I could have expected was that this substance could ever find application as anything approaching a pleasure drug." Hofmann's perception of LSD as fundamentally dangerous and uncontrollable colored his attitude toward the substance for the rest of his life.

### The Delayed Connection to Visionary Experience

Hofmann "failed to recognize the meaningful connection between LSD inebriation and spontaneous visionary experience until much later, after further experiments, which were carried out with far lower doses and under different conditions." The connection between the laboratory-induced psychedelic state and the spontaneous mystical experiences reported throughout human history was not immediately apparent to a chemist whose frame of reference was pharmacological rather than spiritual.

## Reactions at Sandoz

### Incredulous Astonishment

Hofmann's report of his experience was met with "incredulous astonishment." Professor Stoll immediately called to ask: "Are you certain you made no mistake in the weighing? Is the stated dose really correct?" The suggestion was that Hofmann must have taken far more than the 0.25 mg he reported—surely no substance could be active at such a minuscule dose.

### Verification

The dose was confirmed, and a second self-experiment was conducted under controlled conditions with the same results. Only then did Sandoz management accept that a new and extraordinary pharmacological agent had been discovered.

## Early Recognition of Medical Potential

### Pharmacology, Neurology, and Psychiatry

Hofmann immediately recognized that "LSD, a new active compound with such properties, would have to be of use in pharmacology, in neurology, and especially in psychiatry, and that it would attract the interest of concerned specialists."

This prediction proved accurate. Within a few years, LSD was being investigated by researchers around the world for applications ranging from psychotherapy to the study of consciousness.

### Beyond Medical Science

What Hofmann did not anticipate was "that the new substance would also come to be used beyond medical science, as an inebriant in the drug scene." The recreational use of LSD—beginning in the 1960s and continuing to the present—was an outcome that Hofmann viewed with deep ambivalence and concern.

## The Letter to Stoll

On the day after his self-experiment, Hofmann wrote a detailed report to Professor Stoll and sent a copy to Professor Rothlin, director of the pharmacological department. This letter is one of the primary documents in the history of psychopharmacology—a firsthand account of the discovery of a new class of psychoactive substances.

The letter's tone is notably restrained and scientific, despite describing an experience that must have been profoundly disorienting. Hofmann's commitment to objective reporting, even in the face of an experience that challenged his understanding of the relationship between chemistry and consciousness, is characteristic of his approach throughout his career.

## The Sandoz Response

### Development of Delysid

Sandoz eventually developed LSD under the trade name **Delysid** for research and limited clinical use. The drug was supplied to qualified researchers in ampoules containing precise doses, along with detailed instructions for its administration.

The Delysid prospectus listed two primary indications:
1. Analytical psychotherapy, as an adjunct to facilitate access to unconscious material
2. Experimental investigations on the nature of psychoses, based on the similarity between LSD-induced states and certain psychiatric conditions

### The Burden of Responsibility

As the use of LSD spread beyond medical settings in the 1960s, Sandoz found itself in an increasingly difficult position. The company had patented LSD but could not control its use once it entered the illicit market. The "burden of responsibility" that Hofmann's discovery placed on Sandoz ultimately led the company to cease production and distribution of the substance in 1965.
