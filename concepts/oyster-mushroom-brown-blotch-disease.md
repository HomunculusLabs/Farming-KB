# Oyster Mushroom Brown Blotch Disease

Brown blotch disease causes significant crop loss in [[cotter-oyster-mushroom-cultivation]] and is very common in mushroom houses worldwide. The disease manifests in two distinct forms — **bacterial brown blotch** and **fungal brown blotch** — each with different pathogens, symptoms, and control measures. Yellowing of fruiting bodies can also be caused by environmental factors, particularly rapid humidity changes from excessive ventilation. Understanding the distinction between these conditions is essential for effective management.

## Bacterial Brown Blotch

### Pathogen

- **Primary pathogen:** *[[pseudomonas-tolaasii-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]]*
- **Secondary reported agent:** *Pseudomonas agarici* (importance as a pathogen of brown blotch disease is questionable)
- *P. tolaasii* has been isolated from all observed symptomatic mushrooms, though mixed infections may contribute to the variety of symptoms seen

The bacterium is a Gram-negative rod that produces a characteristic lipopeptide toxin called tolaasin, which is the primary virulence factor responsible for tissue damage and lesion formation. Tolaasin disrupts cell membranes, causing the characteristic brown discoloration and tissue maceration.

### Symptoms

Bacterial brown blotch presents with various symptoms on [[oyster-mushroom-fruiting-chamber-environment]] bodies:

**Typical brown spot symptoms:**

- Brown spots appear on caps and stipes, initially as small (1-3 mm) darkened areas
- Spots enlarge and coalesce with other spots, forming large irregular lesions
- Affected areas become sunken and covered with sticky, slimy material
- A distinctive **rotten fish smell** is evident at this stage — this is a diagnostic feature that distinguishes bacterial from fungal blotch
- In severe cases, the entire cap surface may be affected, rendering mushrooms unmarketable

**Severe symptoms:**

- The entire fruiting body may become discolored **reddish-brown** and appear waterlogged
- Young fruiting bodies are covered with a clear, glossy material and **stop growing** entirely
- Affected pins may abort before reaching maturity, resulting in significant yield loss
- Secondary fungal infections may colonize damaged tissue, complicating diagnosis

### Epidemiology and Spread

*Pseudomonas tolaasii* spreads through multiple vectors in the mushroom growing environment:

- **Water splash:** The primary mechanism of dispersal. Irrigation water that contacts infected tissue picks up bacterial cells and deposits them on healthy mushrooms.
- **Insect vectors:** Mushroom flies (phorid and sciarid species) carry bacteria on their bodies and in their gut, transmitting the pathogen between substrate blocks and fruiting bodies.
- **Physical contact:** Handling infected mushrooms and then touching healthy ones transfers bacteria.
- **Air movement:** Aerosolized droplets containing bacteria can travel short distances through air currents.
- **Contaminated equipment:** Tools, containers, and surfaces that contact infected material become sources of ongoing contamination.

## Fungal Brown Blotch

### Pathogen and Symptoms

Fungal brown blotch is caused by *[[mycogone-perniciosa-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]]* (syn. *Sepedonium perniciosum*), a pathogenic fungus that produces distinct symptoms:

- Small, brownish spots on the cap surface that may develop white, powdery spore masses
- Distorted, malformed fruiting bodies — particularly "balloon-shaped" caps with thick, shortened stipes
- Internal tissue becomes spongy and brown
- A characteristic musty or earthy odor (distinct from the fishy smell of bacterial blotch)

Fungal brown blotch is less common than bacterial brown blotch in modern oyster mushroom cultivation but can cause significant damage when it occurs. It is more frequently associated with button mushroom (*[[agaricus-bisporus]]*) cultivation, where it is known as "wet bubble disease."

## Control Measures

### Sanitation and Cultural Practices

Sanitation is the foundational control measure — follow strict disease and pest management protocols throughout the growing cycle:

- **Pasteurize substrates thoroughly** and use only healthy spawn from reliable sources. Inadequate pasteurization allows both bacterial and fungal pathogens to survive in the substrate and emerge during fruiting.
- **Control mushroom flies** — they are well-known vectors of both bacterial and fungal blotch pathogens. Fly screens on ventilation openings and regular monitoring with sticky traps are essential components of [[mushroom-growing-room-environmental-control]] systems.
- Maintain **constant humidity and temperature** in growing houses; abrupt changes increase brown blotch incidence. Rapid humidity fluctuations stress mushroom tissue and create micro-wounds where bacteria can enter.
- **Avoid free water** on fruiting bodies, as it allows pathogenic bacteria to multiply rapidly. Standing water droplets on the cap surface provide the ideal microenvironment for *P. tolaasii* proliferation.
- **Ventilate after watering** to remove standing water from mushroom surfaces. Allow adequate time for evaporation before reducing airflow.
- **Do not over-water** — brown blotch incidence increases dramatically with excessive moisture. Target substrate moisture at 65-70% and avoid surface saturation.

### Environmental Management

- **Relative humidity control:** Maintain 85-90% RH during fruiting without allowing condensation on mushroom surfaces. Use fogging systems rather than misting to avoid large droplets.
- **Air circulation:** Ensure adequate horizontal air movement across the fruiting surface. Dead air zones promote bacterial accumulation and humidity pockets.
- **Temperature management:** *P. tolaasii* proliferates most rapidly at 20-25°C. Slightly cooler fruiting temperatures (15-18°C) can slow bacterial growth, though this must be balanced with the species' optimal fruiting temperature.

### Chemical and Biological Controls

For organic and conventional operations alike, chemical options for bacterial blotch are extremely limited:

- **Chlorine-based sanitizers:** Sodium hypochlorite solutions (100-200 ppm available chlorine) can be used for surface disinfection but are not approved for direct application to mushrooms in most jurisdictions.
- **[[cervantes-hydrogen-peroxide-sterilization]]:** Dilute H₂O₂ solutions (0.5-1%) can reduce bacterial loads on surfaces and equipment.
- **Copper-based products:** Some copper formulations have demonstrated efficacy against *P. tolaasii* but may leave residues and are generally not approved for mushroom crops.
- **Biological control:** Certain *Bacillus* species produce antibiotics that inhibit *P. tolaasii*. Research into biocontrol agents for mushroom diseases is ongoing but commercial products are not yet widely available.

The relationship between environmental conditions and brown blotch incidence makes this disease a useful indicator of overall [[mushroom-cultivation-problems]] related to humidity management. Growers who consistently experience brown blotch should review their watering practices, air exchange rates, and ventilation patterns as the first step in developing an effective prevention strategy.
