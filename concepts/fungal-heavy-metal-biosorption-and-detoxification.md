Fungi possess remarkable abilities to accumulate, tolerate, and detoxify heavy metals through biosorption, bioaccumulation, intracellular sequestration, and chemical transformation. These capabilities make fungi valuable agents for [[fungal-bioremediation]] of metal-contaminated environments and [heavy metal remediation.

## Metal Contamination Problem

Heavy metal contamination arises from industrial activities including mining, smelting, electroplating, battery manufacturing, pesticide application, and fossil fuel combustion. Problem metals include lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), mercury (Hg), arsenic (As), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), and nickel (Ni). Unlike organic pollutants, metals cannot be degraded and persist indefinitely in the environment.

## Fungal Metal Tolerance Mechanisms

Fungi employ multiple strategies to survive in metal-contaminated environments:

### Extracellular Mechanisms
- **Biosorption**: Binding of metal ions to fungal cell wall components (chitin, glucans, proteins)
- **Extracellular precipitation**: Metal precipitation through metabolite excretion (oxalate, phosphate)
- **Extracellular complexation**: Organic acids and siderophores chelate metals, reducing bioavailability
- **Cell wall binding**: Chitin and glucans in the cell wall provide binding sites for metal cations

### Intracellular Mechanisms
- **Bioaccumulation**: Active uptake and intracellular concentration of metals
- **Sequestration**: Compartmentalization in vacuoles
- **Metallothionein production**: Small cysteine-rich proteins that bind metals
- **Phytochelatin synthesis**: Metal-binding peptides for detoxification
- **Enzymatic transformation**: Reduction, oxidation, or methylation of metal species
- **Efflux pumps**: Active transport of metals out of cells

## Fungal Cell Wall as Biosorbent

The fungal cell wall is the first line of defense and primary biosorption site:

| Cell Wall Component | Metal Binding Mechanism |
|--------------------|------------------------|
| Chitin | Amine and hydroxyl groups coordinate metal cations |
| Glucans | Hydroxyl groups provide binding sites |
| Proteins | Carboxyl, amine, and thiol groups bind metals |
| Melanin | Multiple functional groups; high metal affinity |
| Phosphates | Precipitate metals as insoluble phosphates |

Dead fungal biomass (spent from industrial fermentation) retains biosorption capacity and can be used as low-cost biosorbent material.

## Key Fungal Species for Metal Biosorption

### White-Rot Fungi
- **[Phanerochaete chrysosporium**: Biosorbs Cd, Cu, Pb, Zn; tolerant to multiple metals
- **[Trametes versicolor**: Effective biosorbent for Cu, Pb, Cd; laccase activity maintained under metal stress
- **[Pleurotus ostreatus**: Used in PAH-heavy metal co-contaminated soils; tolerant of Cd and Hg

### Yeasts
- **Saccharomyces cerevisiae**: Well-characterized biosorbent; effective for Cu, Pb, Cd, Zn
- **Candida species**: Effective biosorbent for various metals

### Molds
- **Aspergillus niger**: High biosorption capacity; citric acid production enhances metal leaching
- **Penicillium species**: Effective for Cu, Pb, Cd biosorption
- **Rhizopus species**: Chitin-rich cell walls provide high metal binding capacity
- **Mucor species**: Significant biosorption of Pb and Cd

### Mycorrhizal Fungi
- **Ectomycorrhizal species**: Accumulate metals from soil, protecting host plants
- **Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi**: Enhance plant metal tolerance; sequester metals in fungal tissues

## Fungal Biotransformation of Metals

Beyond biosorption, some fungi can chemically transform metal species:

| Transformation | Fungal Species | Significance |
|---------------|---------------|-------------|
| Mercury methylation/demethylation | Various fungi | Alters mercury toxicity and mobility |
| Chromium reduction (Cr(VI) to Cr(III)) | Various fungi | Converts toxic Cr(VI) to less toxic Cr(III) |
| Uranium precipitation | Aspergillus niger, others | Reduces uranium mobility |
| Selenium volatilization | Various fungi | Removes selenium from contaminated sites |
- Arsenic transformation | Various fungi | Alters arsenic speciation and toxicity |

## Factors Affecting Biosorption

- **pH**: Optimum pH varies by metal and fungal species; generally 4-6 for cationic metals
- **Temperature**: Moderate temperatures (25-35C) typically optimal
- **Biomass concentration**: Higher biomass increases total metal uptake but may reduce per-unit efficiency
- **Initial metal concentration**: Affects isotherm type and biosorption capacity
- **Competing ions**: Other metals and cations compete for binding sites
- **Biomass pretreatment**: Chemical pretreatment (acid, base, heat) can enhance capacity
- **Contact time**: Equilibrium typically reached within minutes to hours

## Biosorbent Preparation

Fungal biosorbents can be prepared from:
- Spent biomass from fermentation industries (waste utilization)
- Cultured mycelia grown specifically for biosorption
- Mushroom cultivation waste (spent compost)
- Fungal pellets from bioreactors

Immobilization on inert supports (alginate, polyurethane, silica) enhances handling, reusability, and mechanical strength for column applications.

## Applications

- **Industrial wastewater treatment**: Removal of metals from electroplating, mining, and textile effluents
- **Soil remediation**: In situ metal immobilization through fungal inoculation
- **Drinking water purification**: Point-of-use metal removal systems
- **Metal recovery**: Precious metal recovery from electronic waste leachates
- **Mine tailing treatment**: Stabilization of metals in mining waste
