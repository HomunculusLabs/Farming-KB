# Ayahuasca Tourism: Evaluating Shaman Character and Trustworthiness

## Overview

Ayahuasca tourism in Peru presents significant challenges for visitors seeking authentic spiritual experiences. R. Stuart's 2001 observations in the Ucayali region revealed that evaluating a shaman's character is one of the most difficult and consequential tasks facing ayahuasca tourists. The article documents encounters with multiple practitioners demonstrating a wide range of personal integrity, from honorable community healers to opportunistic hustlers preying on foreign visitors.

## The Problem of Finding a "Good" Ayahuasquero

Almost every shaman in Amazonia claims to be the only person who knows how to properly brew ayahuasca. This creates a paradox for tourists: with no reliable external verification system, visitors must rely on word of mouth, intuition, and limited personal interaction to evaluate practitioners. The linguistic barriers and cultural misunderstandings that confound communication even in ordinary consciousness make this evaluation even more precarious in altered states.

The question foremost in many prospective tourists' minds is: "How do I find a good ayahuasquero?" Unfortunately, there is no simple answer. Stuart's fieldwork demonstrates that reputation within the local community is perhaps the most reliable indicator, but accessing that reputation requires time, language skills, and connections that most short-term tourists simply do not have.

## Case Studies from Ucayali, Peru

### The Elderly Shaman "B" (Yarina)

Stuart lived for a week in the ceremonial hut of an elderly shaman near Pucallpa, observing nightly ceremonies attracting up to two dozen visitors. Over time, B was revealed to be:

- A **frequent liar** who misrepresented his abilities and history
- A **shoplifter** with petty criminal habits
- Someone who **regularly asked for money or gifts** at every opportunity
- A practitioner whose son (the family's religious practice director) was giving workshops in the United States, suggesting the family had expanded their commercial reach internationally

Despite these character flaws, B continued to host ceremonies for both Peruvians and foreign tourists. His case illustrates that spiritual reputation and personal integrity are not always correlated in the commercialized ayahuasca tourism context. A practitioner can maintain a functioning ceremonial practice and attract visitors while simultaneously engaging in dishonest behavior in their daily life.

### The Visiting Training Shamans

Two ayahuasqueros visited B's group for a two-week training period:

- One was described as "a sweet and wonderful guy" with genuine healing intent
- His partner, however, "always hustled me for spare change"
- This pairing demonstrates that even within training relationships, character varies dramatically
- The contrast suggests that ethical conduct is individual rather than institutional in Amazonian shamanism

### B's Nephew: The Enterprising Shaman

B's nephew represents the economic dimension of ayahuasca tourism:

- Earned **a couple hundred dollars per month** from tourists
- His neighbors could **not even afford a thirty-cent taxi ride**
- Charged **$30 for tourists** vs. approximately **$2 for Peruvians** (15× markup)
- His income came entirely from foreign visitors seeking traditional medicine

This dramatic price disparity raises ethical questions about cultural extraction and whether traditional spiritual practices are being commodified for Western consumption. The income differential also creates social tensions within communities where one family's ayahuasca tourism income can dwarf their neighbors' entire livelihoods.

### "E" — The Honorable Practitioner (San Francisco)

In the Shipibo town of San Francisco, Stuart met "E," who was:

- **Widely regarded as an honorable man** by the community
- Observed singing icaros in the darkness of his home during private family services
- Charged approximately **$10 per tourist ceremony** — modest by comparison
- Maintained a separation between commercial tourist services and authentic family practice
- His private family ceremonies, conducted at night with icaros sung in darkness, suggested a depth of practice that extended beyond his commercial offerings

"E" represents the ideal that many tourists hope to find: a practitioner who maintains genuine spiritual commitment while also serving visitors, and whose pricing reflects a sense of fairness rather than maximum extraction.

### "A" — The Swindler

Another practitioner, "A," invited Stuart to rituals but was rejected after he:

- **Swindled Stuart on the price of a tobacco pipe**
- Demonstrated that commercial motives can override spiritual authenticity
- Showed how difficult it is to distinguish genuine practitioners from opportunists based on initial encounters alone

Even a seemingly warm invitation to ceremony can come from someone whose primary interest is financial rather than healing-oriented.

## Factors Complicating Evaluation

### Cultural and Linguistic Barriers

Even without the complications of altered consciousness, tourists face:

- **Language differences** that prevent nuanced communication about intentions, expectations, and boundaries
- **Cultural norms** around money, gifts, and obligation that differ from Western expectations
- **Limited time** to develop the trust relationships that would normally precede ceremonial participation
- **No regulatory framework** or licensing system for practitioners
- **Power dynamics** where the tourist holds economic power but the shaman holds knowledge power

### The Commercialization Effect

Commercialized ayahuasca ceremonies differ fundamentally from authentic community rituals:

- Doses tend to be **milder** as shamans err on the side of caution with foreigners
- Ceremonial depth may be **shallower** than rituals conducted for family and community
- The motivation shifts from **community healing** to **income generation**
- Tourists are treated as **customers** rather than spiritual seekers or community members
- Shamans may moderate the experience to avoid problems rather than pushing for deeper healing work

### The Information Asymmetry

Tourists have virtually no way to verify:

- A shaman's training lineage or authentic credentials
- Whether the brew contains the full complement of traditional admixture plants
- Whether safety protocols are followed (particularly regarding MAOI interactions with medications)
- Whether the practitioner has a history of complaints or incidents
- How the ceremony compares to authentic non-commercial rituals

## Practical Guidance for Tourists

Based on Stuart's observations, prospective ayahuasca tourists should:

1. **Spend extended time** getting to know a practitioner before participating in ceremonies
2. **Seek community reputation** — ask locals rather than relying solely on tourist reviews
3. **Be wary of practitioners** who aggressively solicit business or charge dramatically different rates for foreigners
4. **Consider the motivation** — is the shaman primarily serving their community or catering to tourists?
5. **Have realistic expectations** about what can be gained from a brief cross-cultural encounter
6. **Observe daily behavior** — character revealed outside ceremony matters as much as ceremonial conduct
7. **Compare pricing** within a community to identify outliers who may be exploiting tourists

## The Ethics of Ayahuasca Tourism

Stuart's account raises fundamental questions about whether spiritual traditions can be meaningfully accessed through commercial tourism. While some practitioners undoubtedly come to their profession to help others, ayahuasca tourism is fundamentally a business in Peru, and tourists will likely be treated accordingly. The economic disparity between practitioners and their neighbors, combined with the significant price markup for foreign visitors, suggests that the industry functions partly as a form of cultural extraction.
