## Aquaculture Design Checklist and Taro Field Integration

## Overview

Effective aquaculture design requires careful attention to a wide range of biological, physical, and management factors. This page synthesizes Mollison"s designer's checklist for aquaculture systems with advanced taro field modifications that demonstrate integrated land-water polyculture principles at their best.

## Taro-Eel Integration

### Eel Rearing with Taro

In climates such as the western Pacific (including New Zealand), eel rearing combines very well with taro culture. Taro is set out in wet mud or shallow water, but at maturity can be kept flooded at mid-thigh depth, or with 0.5 m of free water above the softer muds of the terrace. In this deeper water phase, prawns and grass carp can be stocked—these species coexist very well together in flooded taro terraces.

This combination illustrates the principle of temporal stacking: the same terrace shifts from shallow taro production to deeper aquaculture as the crop matures, maximizing the productive use of both space and time.

### Taro as a Flexible Polyculture Center

Taro is a very productive and flexible plant for basic starchy food production, and a rich polyculture can be developed around it. The crop is itself well protected from browsers by its oxalic acid spicules, reducing the need for fencing or other protective measures. Every tropical garden deserves a patch or 50 of taro. It is often grown as a mulched patch or groundcover below fruit trees in higher rainfall areas, or as a subsidiary crop in banana, guava, avocado, and macadamia nut orchards.

The ideal taro polyculture environment includes taro, [[comfrey]], papaya, guava, and banana, with chilis, ginger, peppers, ducks, pigs, and fish—supplemented by visits to the sea for limu (seaweed) and crab, and a good earth oven (imu) for cooking.

## Advanced Taro Field Modifications

Mollison describes several modifications to traditional taro fields that enhance productivity:

- **Animal houses** positioned above or adjacent to terraces to supply nutrient-rich manures
- **Logs rotting** in upper terraces (especially *Aleurites*) to produce edible fungi, converting up to 80% of log biomass to food
- **Deep channels** for shrimp and fish running alongside the taro paddies
- **Fish-taro paddies** with peripheral channels providing refuge and breeding areas
- **Irrigation channels** passing through papaya crops, bananas, cucurbits, and beans on bunds
- **Margin crops** such as comfrey, taro, and *[[tradescantia]]* for fish food
- **Coconut** on margins providing additional yield and shade

The modified system diverts water from a stream, fertilizes it through crop and animal systems, uses it in production, and returns it to the stream free of pollutants—demonstrating closed-loop [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]].

## Aquaculture Designers' Checklist

### Fundamental Principles

Due to the special susceptibility of water life, minimal to zero biocide use is essential near or in waterways. This is the non-negotiable foundation of all aquaculture design.

### Fish Stocking and Management

- **Stock rates**: Test below, at, and above suspected optimum (plus or minus 2,000/ha) to find the true [[fukuoka-textdoc-land-requirements-per-diet-type-carrying-capacity]] of each pond
- **Water chemistry**: Fertilize pond and bring pH to 7+ before stocking
- **Stock health**: Select disease-free stock from reputable sources
- **Predator exclusion**: Exclude or guard against predators using nets, fences, or deep water refuges
- **Edge maximization**: Maximize edge and natural foods within the pond system
- **Predator fish ratios**: Introduce predator fish at about 1:5 ratio to control populations
- **Aeration strategy**: Select fish species that require no pond aeration, or match species to the aeration method provided
- **Economic assessment**: Select fish for good local value, but assess food costs for each species

### Brood Stock and Breeding

- **Shelter provision**: Provide shelter from predators and excess light and heat
- **Brood selection**: Select fast-growing brood stock for the particular site, from fry to adults
- **[[aquatic-polyculture-design-mollison]]**: Devise a fish/plant polyculture for the ponds rather than monoculture

### Site Analysis and Landscape Integration

- **Food resource survey**: Analyze landscape for natural or cultivated food resources available to the aquaculture system
- **Pond diversity**: Vary pond depth, pond size, and pH to suit a set of food species and productive fish for the district
- **Configuration analysis**: Carefully analyse pond configurations for specific polycultures, easy management, site, and weather effects
- **Holistic balance**: Pay attention to aquatic and marginal crops, downstream crop, and total landscape balance

### Support Systems

- **Accessory food**: Devise accessory food systems for fish or invertebrates, as vegetation, root crop, and invertebrate sources
- **Wetland inclusion**: In any design, include some appropriate (small or large) wetlands, even if it is from waste water
- **Downstream utilization**: Plan for downstream irrigation and nutrient capture from pond discharge

## Water Quality Factors

### pH Management

Water pH is a critical factor in aquaculture productivity. Before stocking, ponds should be fertilized and pH brought to 7+ (neutral to slightly alkaline). Acidic waters can be buffered using agricultural lime, wood ash, or crushed shell. Marginal plants also contribute to pH modification through mulch and leaf [[decomposition]], buffering extreme pH levels naturally.

### Dissolved Oxygen

Dissolved oxygen levels determine how many fish a pond can support. [[factors-affecting-mycoremediation-efficiency]] oxygen include:

- **Temperature**: Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen
- **Organic loading**: Decomposing matter consumes oxygen
- **Algal blooms**: Can cause oxygen fluctuations (high during day, depleted at night)
- **Water depth**: Deeper ponds stratify, with bottom waters often anoxic
- **Aeration**: Can be provided mechanically, through water movement, or via trompe systems

### Nutrient Management

Pond fertility is managed through a combination of external inputs and internal cycling:

- **Animal manures** from ducks, pigs, or chickens provide balanced nutrients
- **Green manures** including [[azolla]], duckweed, and other aquatic plants
- **Algal growth** supports zooplankton and detritus feeders
- **Edge vegetation** contributes leaf fall, insect material, and dissolved organics
- **Biogas digestion** of green feed or wastes produces a flow-on slurry not one whit less fertile than the original substances

## The Trompe: Aeration Without Energy

Mollison includes a note on the trompe—a device used to compress underground air for workshop use or to directly aerate fish ponds and spawning beds. The trompe uses falling water to entrain and compress air, providing aeration without any external energy input. This is particularly valuable for aquaculture systems where aeration is needed but electricity or fuel is unavailable or uneconomical.

The trompe principle has been used historically in mining operations (notably the Ragged Chutes trompe in Ontario, Canada) and can be adapted for pond aeration by directing the compressed air through diffusers at the pond bottom. The only requirement is a sufficient head of falling water.

## Predator Management

Predator control in aquaculture requires a multi-layered approach:

- **Deep water refuges** where fish can escape surface-feeding birds
- **Netting or wire covers** over particularly valuable ponds
- **Water moats** surrounding production areas (as in chinampa systems)
- **Dogs or other guard animals** to deter birds and mammals
- **Balanced predator-prey ratios** including predator fish at approximately 1:5 ratio
- **Shade structures** to reduce visibility of fish from above

## Harvesting Strategies

### Catch-and-Restock Systems

For non-carnivorous species, catch-and-restock systems can greatly increase yields over batch or total removal approaches. Many permanent ponds operate on this basis, or on that of natural breeding in the case of crayfish (where a maximum size limit is used to return large, fast-growing breeders to the pond or breeding area). It is always wise to consider returning or keeping the largest fish and eating the slightly smaller grades—a strategy unfortunately not well-applied in sea fisheries.

### Processing and Preservation

Processing of fish on-farm has many advantages. Drying, smoking, freezing, salting, or reducing to pastes and sauces are all small-scale activities. More everyday manufacture of clear plastic drying tents or boxes is needed rather than emphasis on increasing fishing efficiency or production, as up to 40% of a catch can be lost to spoilage and damage by rodents and birds. Ferment of fish wastes (as practiced in Indonesia) produces a protein-rich, salt-stabilized liquid sauce of great value, and such techniques are essential for salvaging fish wastes for market dispersal.

## Design Philosophy

The overarching principle behind these checklist items is integration. Aquaculture should never be designed as an isolated fish-production system. Instead, every pond should be conceived as one element in a larger landscape that includes land crops, livestock, water treatment, and [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]]. The greatest yields—both in quantity and diversity—come from systems where the waste products of one element become the inputs for another, creating self-reinforcing cycles of fertility and production.

True aquacultural planning should always include diverse plant products with emphasis on local staples. Fish are then accessory to a broad product base and to tree crops. The disease problems that build up in aquacultures are due to over-emphasis on one species or class of food and high yields, and also to a neglect of shade, wild foods, and the beneficial effects of plants on water quality.
