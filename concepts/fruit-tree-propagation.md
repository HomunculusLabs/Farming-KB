# Fruit Tree Propagation

Propagating your own [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]] saves money, preserves heritage varieties, and allows you to create custom combinations of rootstock and scion wood. Most fruit trees do not grow true from seed -- vegetative [[vegetative-propagation-methods-mollison]] are required to reproduce exact copies of a desired variety.

## Why Propagate Vegetatively

- Seeds produce unpredictable offspring ([[fungal-life-cycles-sexual-reproduction-metamorphosis]] shuffles genetics)
- Grafted trees combine a desirable fruiting variety (scion) with a rootstock that controls size, disease resistance, and soil adaptation
- Cloning preserves the exact characteristics of the parent tree
- Cost: a grafted tree from a nursery costs $25-60; propagating your own costs $2-5 per tree

## Grafting

Grafting joins two pieces of living plant tissue so they grow together as one. The scion (top portion) provides the [[old-fruit-variety-selection]]; the rootstock (bottom portion) provides the root system.

### When to Graft
Most grafting is done in late winter to [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] (February-April) before buds break. The scion wood should be collected during dormancy (January-February) and stored in the refrigerator in a sealed plastic bag with damp paper towels.

### Whip and Tongue Graft
The most common graft for small-diameter material (1/4 to 1/2 inch). Used for new trees.

1. Collect scion wood from the previous year's growth (pencil-thick, with 2-4 buds)
2. Make a smooth, slanting cut 1-2 inches long on both the scion and rootstock
3. Make a tongue cut (a small downward slice into the slanting cut) on both pieces
4. Interlock the tongues so the pieces fit together snugly
5. Match the cambium layer (thin green layer just under the bark) on at least one side -- both sides is ideal
6. Wrap tightly with grafting tape or parafilm, covering the entire joint
7. Seal the scion tip with grafting wax or parafilm to prevent drying
8. Plant the grafted tree or store in moist sawdust until planting

### Cleft Graft
Used for top-working existing trees (changing varieties) or grafting onto larger rootstock (1/2 to 2 inches).

1. Cut the rootstock or branch off squarely
2. Split the stump down the center with a cleft grafting tool or knife and mallet, about 2 inches deep
3. Prepare two scion pieces with long wedge-shaped bases (1-2 inches)
4. Insert one scion into each side of the cleft, matching cambium layers
5. The wedge should push the cleft open slightly -- natural pressure holds the scions in place
6. Seal all exposed surfaces with grafting wax
7. Remove wrapping once the graft has healed (usually by midsummer)

### Side Veneer Graft
Used for grafting onto larger trees without cutting back the top. Good for adding varieties to established trees.

1. Make a shallow cut (1-2 inches long) through the bark and into the wood of the rootstock
2. Make a short inward cut at the bottom of the long cut to create a flap
3. Prepare the scion base with a long, shallow taper on one side only
4. Insert the scion under the flap, matching cambium layers
5. Wrap tightly and seal exposed wood
6. Cut back the rootstock above the graft once the scion is growing well

## Budding

Budding uses a single bud rather than a scion with multiple buds. Performed in late summer (July-August) when the bark is slipping (easily separates from the wood).

### T-Budding
The most common budding method. Works on most fruit trees.

1. Select a healthy bud from the current season's growth on the scion variety
2. Make a T-shaped cut in the bark of the rootstock: a vertical cut 1-2 inches long and a horizontal cut across the top
3. Lift the bark at the intersection to create flaps
4. Cut a bud shield from the scion: a thin slice of bark with the bud in the center, starting 1/2 inch below the bud and ending 1/2 inch above
5. Slide the bud shield under the bark flaps of the T-cut, matching the bud facing upward
6. Wrap with budding rubber or parafilm, leaving the bud itself exposed
7. In spring, cut the rootstock back just above the bud to force growth from the scion bud

## Air Layering

Air layering encourages roots to form on a branch while it is still attached to the parent tree. Works well for figs, citrus, mangoes, and some stone fruits.

1. Select a healthy branch from the previous or current year's growth, about pencil-thick
2. Find a section 12-18 inches from the branch tip
3. Make two parallel cuts around the branch, 1-2 inches apart, through the bark and cambium
4. Remove the bark ring between the cuts (exposing the white wood)
5. Apply rooting hormone to the exposed wood
6. Wrap the area with a handful of moist sphagnum moss
7. Cover the moss with plastic wrap, securing tightly at both ends with tape or wire
8. Check periodically to ensure moss stays moist (add water with a syringe if needed)
9. Roots will form in 4-12 weeks depending on species
10. When roots are visible through the plastic and fill the moss ball, cut the branch below the layer
11. Pot the new plant in a sheltered location and harden off gradually before planting out

## Rooting Cuttings

Some fruit species root readily from cuttings, avoiding the need for grafting.

### Softwood Cuttings (June-July)
Used for figs, grapes, currants, gooseberries, and some berries.

1. Take cuttings from current season's growth, 4-6 inches long
2. Remove lower leaves, keeping 2-3 leaves at the top
3. Dip the cut end in rooting hormone
4. Insert 2-3 inches into moist [[solomon-potting-mix-and-seedling-soil-recipe]] (perlite/peat or sand/peat)
5. Cover with a humidity dome or plastic bag
6. Keep in bright, indirect light at 65-75F
7. Mist regularly. Roots form in 3-6 weeks
8. Transplant to individual pots once rooted and growing

### Hardwood Cuttings (Late Winter)
Used for grapes, figs, currants, gooseberries, and mulberries.

1. Take cuttings during dormancy from the previous year's growth, 8-12 inches long
2. Make a straight cut at the base (below a bud) and an angled cut at the top (above a bud) so you know which end is up
3. Dip base in rooting hormone (optional for grapes -- they root readily without)
4. Bury horizontally or at an angle in moist sand or nursery bed, with only the top 2 buds exposed
5. Keep moist through spring
6. Roots form by midsummer; transplant in fall or the following spring

### Root Cuttings
Used for some species that resist standard cuttings: blackberry, raspberry, horseradish.

1. Dig roots during dormancy (2-4 inches from the crown)
2. Cut into 2-4 inch sections
3. Remember orientation -- cut straight on the top end, angled on the bottom
4. Lay horizontally in moist sand or potting mix, 2 inches deep
5. New shoots emerge in spring

## Rootstock Selection

Choose rootstock based on your goals:

- Dwarf (8-10 feet at maturity): good for small spaces, easy harvesting, begins bearing in 2-3 years
- Semi-dwarf (12-16 feet): balance of size and yield, begins bearing in 3-4 years
- Standard (20-30 feet): largest yield, longest-lived, begins bearing in 5-7 years
- Disease-resistant rootstocks: essential in areas with specific disease pressures (fire blight, nematodes, Phytophthora)

## Aftercare for Grafted Trees

- Protect the graft union from physical damage (staking, tree guards)
- Remove any suckers growing below the graft union -- these are from the rootstock, not the scion
- Do not let the scion fruit in the first 2 years -- remove flowers to direct energy into establishment
- Water regularly for the first year
- Mulch around the base (do not pile against the graft union)
