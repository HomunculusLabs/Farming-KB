# Lichen Ecology and Soil Formation

Lichens are symbiotic associations between fungi (mycobionts) and photosynthetic partners (photobionts), typically algae or cyanobacteria, that play pioneering roles in primary succession, soil formation, and nutrient cycling in extreme environments. As some of the first organisms to colonize bare rock, sand, and other newly exposed substrates, lichens initiate the process of soil development and create conditions that allow subsequent colonization by vascular plants and other organisms.

## Lichen Symbiosis

The lichen symbiosis is a close, long-term association between a fungus and a photobiont in which the fungal partner provides protection, water retention, and mineral nutrient acquisition, while the photobiont provides carbohydrates through photosynthesis. The fungal partner (mycobiont) is typically an ascomycete, although some basidiomycete lichens are known. The photobiont can be a green alga (chlorolichens), a cyanobacterium (cyanolichens), or both (tripartite lichens).

The morphological integration of the symbiotic partners varies among lichen types. In crustose lichens, the fungal thallus is tightly appressed to the substrate surface. In foliose lichens, the thallus is leaf-like and loosely attached. In fruticose lichens, the thallus is shrub-like and erect or pendulous. These different growth forms influence the lichen's interaction with the substrate and its capacity for soil formation.

## Role in Rock Weathering and Soil Formation

Lichens are among the most effective biological agents of rock weathering. Through both physical and chemical mechanisms, lichens break down rock surfaces and contribute to the formation of proto-soils. Physical weathering by lichens occurs through the penetration of fungal hyphae into rock cracks and fissures, where expansion and contraction of the thallus with wetting and drying cycles widens cracks and detaches mineral fragments.

Chemical weathering by lichens involves the production of lichenic acids (also called lichen acids), which are secondary metabolites that facilitate rock solubilization. These organic acids, including usnic acid, oxalic acid, and various depsides and depsidones, chelate mineral cations and dissolve rock minerals, releasing calcium, iron, aluminum, and other elements into solution.

The combined effects of physical and chemical weathering by lichens gradually convert bare rock into a thin layer of mineral particles mixed with organic matter derived from the lichen thallus. This proto-soil provides a substrate for the colonization by mosses, liverworts, and eventually vascular plants, initiating the successional development of a mature soil profile.

## Soil Development Succession

The process of lichen-mediated soil development follows a characteristic successional sequence. Crustose lichens are typically the first colonizers of bare rock, as their tightly adherent growth form allows them to withstand desiccation and temperature extremes. Foliose and fruticose lichens colonize later as the developing soil provides sufficient moisture and nutrient retention.

Once lichen communities have become established and their weathering action has created a sufficient depth of mineral soil, other organisms can colonize. The accumulation of organic matter from dead lichen tissue, along with the mineral particles released by weathering, creates a soil horizon that can support mosses and herbaceous plants. The increasing organic matter content and moisture retention capacity of the developing soil creates conditions favorable for further biological activity and soil development.

The hydrophobicity of the lichen community and the leaching of lichen acids influence the hydrological properties of the developing soil. These effects on water infiltration and retention can influence the trajectory of soil development and the composition of the establishing plant community.

## Nutrient Contributions

Lichens contribute significantly to nutrient cycling in the ecosystems they colonize. Nitrogen fixation by cyanobacterial photobionts in cyanolichens provides an important input of fixed nitrogen to nitrogen-poor substrates. In boreal and subarctic ecosystems, cyanolichens can contribute substantial quantities of nitrogen through biological nitrogen fixation.

The leaching of lichen acids and other organic compounds from lichen thalli adds dissolved organic matter to the developing soil, contributing to the formation of soil organic matter and providing carbon substrates for microbial activity. The mineral elements released by rock weathering contribute to the nutrient pool available for plant uptake.

In approximately 28 kilograms of carbon per hectare was fixed by the lichen crust community in a pinyon-juniper woodland, demonstrating the capacity of lichen communities to contribute to ecosystem carbon fixation even in relatively productive environments.

## Lichen Communities as Bioindicators

Lichen communities are sensitive to air quality and are widely used as bioindicators of atmospheric pollution. Epiphytic lichen communities on tree bark are particularly responsive to sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxide pollution, with sensitive species declining and tolerant species increasing along pollution gradients.

Knops and colleagues (1996) demonstrated that the diversity and composition of epiphytic lichen communities reflect air quality conditions. Brown (1996) outlined the ways in which lichen communities have been reported to change along gradients of air pollutant loading. The recovery of lichen communities has been observed where air quality has improved following the implementation of pollution control measures.

The sensitivity of lichens to atmospheric pollution reflects their direct absorption of nutrients and pollutants from the atmosphere (they have no roots and absorb through their entire surface), their slow growth rates, and their limited capacity for detoxification.

## Lichens in Extreme Environments

Lichens are dominant organisms in many extreme environments including arctic tundra, alpine zones, hot and cold deserts, and coastal cliffs. Their ability to tolerate desiccation, extreme temperatures, and high light intensity allows them to occupy niches where vascular plants cannot survive.

In arctic and boreal ecosystems, lichens are important food resources for herbivores. Mushroom-forming fungi, along with lichens, are staple food items for reindeer and moose in boreal ecosystems. Severe threats to lichen communities, including overgrazing by reindeer, can have cascading effects on the sustainability of viable food webs in these ecosystems.

## Carbon Fixation and Ecosystem Productivity

The contribution of lichen communities to total ecosystem primary productivity varies with ecosystem type. In lichen-dominated ecosystems such as arctic tundra and desert crust communities, lichens can account for a significant proportion of total carbon fixation. In more productive ecosystems, the lichen contribution is relatively smaller but can still be ecologically significant.

The question of whether total net primary production of an ecosystem drops if the lichen community is removed has been raised, highlighting the need to consider the often-overlooked contribution of lichens to ecosystem carbon budgets. The removal of lichens could affect nutrient cycling, soil stability, and microclimate in ways that indirectly affect vascular plant productivity.

## Lichen-Dominated Ecosystems

In certain ecosystems, lichens are the dominant primary producers and play a central role in ecosystem functioning. Arctic tundra, Antarctic desert, and hot desert ecosystems all support lichen communities that contribute significantly to primary production and nutrient cycling. In these environments, lichens may be the primary or sole source of fixed carbon for heterotrophic organisms.

The water relations of lichens are unique among photosynthetic organisms. Most lichens are poikilohydric, meaning they lack mechanisms for controlling water loss and rapidly equilibrate with atmospheric humidity. This adaptation allows them to photosynthesize during brief periods of hydration, such as morning dew or fog events, even in arid environments where vascular plants cannot maintain positive water balance.

## Lichen Secondary Metabolites

Lichens produce a diverse array of secondary metabolites, many of which are unique to lichenized fungi. These lichen acids, including usnic acid, atranorin, and various depsides and depsidones, serve multiple ecological functions. They provide protection against UV radiation, deter herbivory, and have antimicrobial properties that protect the lichen thallus from pathogenic microorganisms.

The role of lichen acids in rock weathering has been discussed extensively in the ecological literature. These organic acids chelate mineral cations from rock surfaces, accelerating the dissolution of silicate minerals and the release of calcium, magnesium, iron, and other elements. The weathering action of lichen acids is one of the primary mechanisms by which lichens contribute to soil formation on rock surfaces.

## Climate Sensitivity

Lichens are sensitive to climatic conditions, particularly temperature and moisture availability. Their growth rates are generally slow, typically ranging from a few millimeters to a few centimeters per year, depending on species and environmental conditions. Climate warming may shift the distribution of lichen communities, with potential consequences for the ecosystems they support.

Changes in precipitation patterns can also affect lichen communities. Increased frequency of drought events may reduce lichen cover and productivity, while changes in snow cover duration can affect the length of the growing season for lichens in arctic and alpine environments.
