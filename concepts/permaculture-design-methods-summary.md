# Summary of Permaculture Design Methods

[[permaculture-design]] is not a single technique but a comprehensive methodology that integrates multiple analytical approaches into a coherent whole. Mollison emphasizes that all the approaches outlined — zone analysis, sector analysis, slope and orientation assessment, incremental adaptation, guild assembly, and ethical consideration — are not merely useful but **necessary**. Only through a sensible combination of all these methods can a designer select and assess every element that enters into a total design.

## The Integrated Design Process

### Foundation: Reading the Landscape

Whole farm planning begins with mapping and understanding what already exists. Outlining areas of like soil, slope, or drainage will suggest sensible crops, treatments, fence lines, and land use generally. The principle is to **accept what is there** — ethical land use dictates conservative and appropriate usage of existing conditions rather than imposing arbitrary plans.

This does not mean accepting degradation. It means working with the inherent capabilities of each site zone rather than forcing uniform treatment across diverse conditions. As Mollison notes, one can probably grow bananas in Antarctica given enough money, or persuade penguins to heat a glasshouse — but this represents energy-wasting domination rather than intelligent design.

### The Analytical Stack

The design process builds upward through layers of analysis:

1. **Climate and broad pattern** — understanding macro-scale environmental forces
2. **Water and topography** — slope, elevation, water flow, keypoint identification
3. **Zone placement** — arranging elements by visit frequency and management intensity
4. **Sector analysis** — mapping directional energy flows (sun, wind, fire, water, views)
5. **Slope and aspect** — three-dimensional placement using elevation and orientation
6. **Orientation** — fine-tuning structural alignment to sun, wind, and views
7. **Guild design** — assembling beneficial species communities around central elements
8. **Incremental refinement** — adapting the design based on observation and feedback

Each layer builds on the previous ones. Skipping a layer — as conventional design routinely does — produces systems that fail to capture available efficiencies and often require costly retrofit.

## The Requirements of a Complete Design

A complete [[permaculture]] design, according to Mollison, must include:

- **A large degree of self-management** — systems that maintain themselves with minimal human intervention
- **Attention to details on site** — specific soil conditions, microclimates, existing vegetation, water patterns
- **Suiting the ethics and resources of people** — matching the design to the values, skills, and available energy of its inhabitants
- **Integrated ground features** — placing water, access, structures, and vegetation in cooperative relationship rather than isolation
- **Proper location of natural systems and access routes** — ensuring that ecological functions and human movement patterns work together rather than conflicting

## The Social Dimension

### Settlement Size and Human Needs

Village design introduces financial and space constraints on upper and lower sizes — a "break-even" point and an "optimum" number of houses per unit area. But design that neglects the social effects of settlement size and the local functions related to that size may be designing for human misery, under-servicing of needs, and even sociopathologies such as riot and crime.

Such designs may be economic in cash terms and efficient for one use only, but they can be **totally inhuman**. The Bruce effect — where shared airstreams cause physiological stress and pathology — illustrates how physical design decisions have profound social and psychological consequences. Yet in almost all cities, people are crammed into tall office buildings with no opening windows and only a single airstream.

### The Bio-Social Approach

Site designing needs not a specialist approach but rather a **multi-disciplinary and bio-social approach** that considers the effects the environment has on its intended occupants. This means integrating knowledge from ecology, sociology, psychology, hydrology, soil science, architecture, and community planning into a unified design process.

## Permaculture Design vs. Conventional Design

Mollison provides a detailed comparison of the two paradigms:

### Stimulus
- **Permaculture**: A perceived social problem — responding to real human and ecological needs
- **Conventional**: A drive to erect monuments or make money — responding to economic opportunity

### Subject to
- **Permaculture**: Values of energy conservation, self-reliance, and harmonious human occupancy
- **Conventional**: Economic (cash) considerations and the desire for profit

### Mediated by
- **Permaculture**: Consideration of long-term biosocial factors
- **Conventional**: Purely functional values for short-term cost and material factors

### Achieved by
- **Permaculture**: Research and consultation with clients, assisting people to gain education in design
- **Conventional**: External funding, little consultation, little client or community education

### Refined by
- **Permaculture**: Allowing space, finance, and feedback to adjust activity; accommodating new or overlooked needs as they occur
- **Conventional**: Selling off and not taking responsibility for results

### Leading to
- **Permaculture**: A dynamic and healthy area inhabited by people with the power and understanding to make necessary changes — the stability of dynamic local adjustment
- **Conventional**: A dislocated population, powerless to effect change easily — the instability of perceived individual powerlessness, dependency, and anxiety

## Toward Computer-Assisted Design

Mollison anticipates the potential for computer-aided design systems that could assemble diverse and effective knowledge of both parts and whole into a search or game-playing program. Such systems could handle the fussy detail that slows design — from plant list specifications to home construction details — leaving the designer free to address the imponderables: processes observed on the land, likely trends of future societies, future needs, and measures of human satisfaction.

This vision recognizes that the complexity of integrated design exceeds what any single human mind can optimize without assistance, while preserving the essential role of human judgment for the qualitative, unpredictable, and ethical dimensions of design.

## The Designer's Responsibility

The ultimate message is one of responsibility. The designer is not merely arranging physical elements on land but shaping the relationship between people and their environment for generations. A design that ignores energy conservation, fails to provide for self-reliance, neglects the social dimensions of settlement, or destroys natural systems to serve short-term economic goals is not merely bad design — it is unethical design.

The permaculture designer's task is to produce systems where **good things naturally follow** from good initial placement — where the inherent logic of the design produces benefits beyond what was explicitly planned, and where inhabitants are empowered rather than made dependent.
