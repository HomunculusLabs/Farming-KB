# JADAM Herb Solution (JHS) - Fermented Plant Juice for Pest Control

JADAM Herb Solution (JHS) is a boiled herbal extract used primarily for
its insect-controlling effect in the JADAM natural pesticide system.
Unlike Korean Natural Farming's [[master-cho-knf-fpj-fermented-plant-juice-guide]], which ferments plant material in sugar, JHS is
produced by boiling herbs in water without any added sugar or
fermentation step. This approach is simpler, faster, and avoids the
costs and acidity problems associated with sugar-based fermentation.

## Core Method: Boiling Herbs in Water

The fundamental JHS production method follows a straightforward process:

1. Cut fresh plant material into pieces and place in a mesh bag with a
   rock to keep it submerged
2. Place the bag in a pot and add soft water (water in which JWA mixes
   clearly)
3. For 1 kg fresh plant: add 4 L water for pressure pot, 5 L for
   ordinary pot. For 1 kg dried plant: add 20 L for pressure pot, 25 L
   for ordinary pot
4. Boil for 4 hours in a pressure pot (5 hours in an ordinary pot).
   Bring to boil on high heat, then reduce to medium and maintain a
   gentle boil
5. The final volume will be approximately 70% of the water added
6. After boiling, lift the mesh bag and drain remaining liquid
7. For storage, pour the still-boiling solution into heat-resistant
   bottles until completely full, seal tightly, and lay on their side.
   The hot liquid sterilizes the entire bottle including the air pocket
   around the lid
8. Store in a cool, dark place or cold storage for up to 1-2 years

When using JHS, discard sediments and filter if not clear. If a bottle
inflates during storage, sterilization has failed and microbes have
begun feeding on the solution. Use small bottles for storage since they
are easier to fill completely, use up quickly, and are less prone to
microbial attack. Once opened, use within 1-2 days.

## Why Boiling Over Fermentation

JADAM explicitly chose boiling over fermentation methods for several
reasons. Boiling is cheap, easy, and produces stronger solutions. The
analogy given is boiling chicken for soup versus soaking it in vinegar
or alcohol: boiling extracts far more nutrients. Boiled JHS also mixes
well with [[jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]], forming foam readily, whereas
herbal solutions brewed in vinegar or wood vinegar do not mix as well.

Fermented extracts from vinegar or alcohol can work but add complexity
and cost. A "fresh juice" method using blended raw herbs is explicitly
discouraged as hard work, less effective, and prone to leaving sticky
residue on fruit surfaces.

## Herb Selection and Potency Scale

JHS herbs are ranked by increasing pesticidal strength:

### Basic Level: Jerusalem Artichoke

Jerusalem artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus) is the foundational JHS
herb. Despite being edible food for humans, it possesses significant
pesticidal properties. The entire plant including branches, leaves, and
roots is used. It is assumed that inulin, a substance good for diabetes
found in the plant, somehow acts as a pesticide. Jerusalem artichoke is
easily grown anywhere in the world, grows tall (eliminating the need for
weeding), and can be cut in autumn, dried, and stored. It controls
aphids, mites, and most moth species.

### Medium Level: Gingko

The gingko tree has what Cho calls "the perfect defense mechanism
against pests and diseases." Fresh leaves, fallen leaves, and fruits are
all used for JHS production.

### Strong Level: Korean Pasque Flower Root (KPFR)

Korean pasque flower (Pulsatilla koreana) provides the strongest
pesticidal effect of all JHS herbs. The root is used; seeds are
collected in mid-May, sprouted in trays (approximately 10 days), and
transplanted. It requires full sun. Commercially available dried KPFR
can also be purchased.

### Other Common JHS Herbs

- **Red spider lily** (Lycoris radiata): roots used, repels moles and
  snakes; traditionally used in oriental medicine for tumors
- **Foxglove** (Digitalis purpurea): leaves collected spring to autumn
- **Tobacco**: leaves collected spring to autumn; can also be extracted
  by soaking in water for 15 days instead of boiling
- **Garden balsam**: leaves and roots collected spring to autumn
- **Monkshood**: roots collected autumn to winter, highly poisonous
- **Oleander**: leaves and roots collected in autumn, highly poisonous
- **Pokeweed**: common plant with strong repelling properties
- **Water pepper** (Persicaria hydropiper): readily available
- **Bracken** (Pteridium): fern with pesticidal properties

## Application Rates

Mix 3 to 20 liters of JHS with water to make 500 liters (132 gal) of
finished spray solution. The concentration is adjusted based on pest
severity. For fog machines, use 10 L of JHS per 17 L of water.

JHS also functions as an excellent liquid fertilizer, providing nutrients
unimaginable with chemical pesticides. [[jadam-jhs-nutritional-analysis]] confirm significant nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and
micronutrient content. This dual function as both pesticide and
fertilizer is a distinctive advantage of the JHS system.

## Self-Sufficiency Strategy

Cho recommends growing JHS herbs around the farm perimeter to achieve
complete self-sufficiency in pesticide production. Jerusalem artichoke,
gingko, Korean pasque flower, and red spider lily are the four primary
species. After the autumn harvest, farmers should prepare JWA, JS, and
multiple JHS varieties for the following year's farming.

## Alternative Extraction Methods

Herbs with strong scent such as peppermint, Korean mint, cinnamon bark,
star anise, rosemary, lavender, hot pepper, and mustard can be deep-fried
in canola oil. The poisonous substances melt into the oil, which also
preserves the active compounds. This oil can potentially replace canola
oil in making JWA, creating a herbal JADAM wetting agent (H-JWA) that
would simplify pesticide production to just water plus H-JWA.
