# Phytochrome and Photoperiodism in Plants

## Overview

Phytochrome is a photoreceptor pigment protein that allows plants to detect red and far-red light, enabling them to measure the length of the dark period (night) and regulate critical developmental transitions, most importantly flowering. The discovery of phytochrome and the photoperiodic response revealed that plants possess a sophisticated light-sensing system that functions as a biological clock, tracking [[coleman-persephone-months-winter-day-length]] with remarkable precision to time seasonal events like flowering.

## Historical Discovery

### [[maryland-mammoth-tobacco-and-the-discovery-of-photoperiodism]] Mammoth Tobacco

The story of photoperiodism begins with a mutant tobacco strain discovered in southern Maryland around 1906. This strain, named *[[chamovitz-maryland-mammoth-photoperiodism-discovery]]*, grew continuously to heights of 4.5 meters with nearly a hundred leaves, but rarely flowered — a serious problem for farmers who needed seed for the next year's crop.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of Agriculture discovered the cause: Maryland Mammoth was a **short-day plant** that flowered only when exposed to short day lengths. Under the long summer days of Maryland, it never received the flowering signal. But when the researchers moved plants into a dark shed each afternoon, artificially shortening the perceived day length, the plants stopped growing and began to flower.

This experiment established the concept of **photoperiodism** — the ability of plants to measure and respond to the duration of light and dark periods.

### The Night-Length Discovery

A crucial follow-up question emerged: do plants measure day length or [[photoperiodism-plants-measure-night-length]]? Experiments during the World War II era provided the answer. Scientists found that:

- A **short-day plant** (like soybean) would not flower in naturally short autumn days if a light was turned on for just a few minutes in the middle of the night.
- A **long-day plant** (like iris or barley) would flower in the middle of winter if given a brief flash of light during the night.

This proved that plants measure the **length of the continuous dark period**, not the day length. A brief interruption of darkness resets the plant's night-length counter.

## The Red/Far-Red Light Switch

### Discovery

Scientists then investigated which color of light was responsible for the photoperiodic response. The finding was surprising: plants responded only to **red light** (~660 nm wavelength). Blue or green flashes during the night had no effect on flowering, but even a few seconds of red light was sufficient to disrupt or induce flowering depending on the plant type.

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues made the remarkable discovery that **far-red light** (~730 nm, wavelengths slightly longer than red, barely visible at dusk) could **reverse** the effect of red light:

- Red light flash during the night → long-day plant flowers (acts as if night was short)
- Far-red flash immediately after red → effect cancelled, plant does not flower
- Red flash after far-red → flowering induced again
- Far-red after that → cancelled again

This red/far-red reversibility could be toggled back and forth indefinitely with just seconds of each light type. The system acts as a **bistable molecular switch**.

### Phytochrome Structure and Function

By 1960, Warren L. Butler and colleagues demonstrated that a single photoreceptor protein was responsible for both the red and far-red effects. They named it **phytochrome** (Greek: *phyton* = plant, *chroma* = color).

Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms:

- **Pr (P660):** The red-light-absorbing form. When Pr absorbs red light (~660 nm), it converts to Pfr.
- **Pfr (P730):** The far-red-light-absorbing form. When Pfr absorbs far-red light (~730 nm), it converts back to Pr.

**Pfr is the biologically active form.** It is Pfr that triggers downstream signaling cascades leading to flowering (in long-day plants) or flowering suppression (in short-day plants).

## Ecological Significance

The red/far-red system has deep ecological logic:

- **End of day:** The last light a plant sees at sunset is enriched in far-red (because red light is scattered more by the atmosphere at low sun angles). This converts Pfr to Pr, signaling the plant to "wind down" for the night.
- **Beginning of day:** Morning light is enriched in red light. This converts Pr to Pfr, "waking up" the plant's metabolic systems.
- **[[phytochrome-red-far-red-ratio-shade-detection-plants]]:** Leaves absorb red light for photosynthesis but transmit/reflect far-red. A plant growing under a canopy receives a high far-red:red ratio, signaling that it is shaded and triggering shade-avoidance responses (stem elongation, upward leaf movement).
- **Night length measurement:** Pfr slowly reverts to Pr in darkness over several hours. The amount of Pfr remaining at the end of the night indicates how long the dark period was. If enough Pfr persists (short night), long-day plants flower. If Pfr has fully decayed (long night), short-day plants flower.

## Where Phytochrome Senses Light

Unlike [[phototropism-darwin-plant-light-sensing]] (where Darwin showed the "eye" is at the shoot tip), phytochrome-mediated photoperiodism is sensed primarily in the **leaves**:

- Illuminating any single leaf with red light during the night is sufficient to regulate flowering in the entire plant.
- A plant with all leaves removed, leaving only stem and apex, is "blind" to photoperiodic light signals even if the entire shoot is illuminated.

After phytochrome in a leaf detects the red/far-red signal, it initiates the production of a mobile signaling molecule (the "florigen" signal, later identified as a protein called FT — FLOWERING LOCUS T) that travels through the phloem to the shoot apex and triggers or suppresses the floral transition.

## Photoperiodic Plant Classifications

| Category | Flowering Trigger | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Short-day plants | Flower when night exceeds critical length | Chrysanthemum, soybean, [[garner-allard-maryland-mammoth-tobacco-experiment]], Cannabis (some cultivars) |
| Long-day plants | Flower when night is shorter than critical length | Iris, barley, spinach, Arabidopsis |
| Day-neutral plants | Flower regardless of day length | Tomato, rice, maize, cucumber |

In practice, the classification is really about **critical night length** — short-day plants require a long night, long-day plants require a short night.
