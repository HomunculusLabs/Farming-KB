# Fungal Succession on Decomposing Leaves Fungal succession on decomposing leaves is one of the most well-documented patterns in microbial ecology, involving predictable sequences of species colonization, resource utilization, and community turnover as leaf material progresses from fresh litter to humus. ## The Successional Framework The study of fungal succession on decomposing leaves was pioneered by Hudson (1968) and further developed by Cooke and Rayner (1984) and Dix and Webster (1995). These researchers established that fungal communities on decomposing leaf material change predictably over time, driven primarily by changes in the availability and quality of organic substrates. Succession on leaves occurs through three overlapping phases: ### Phase 1: Initial Colonization (Sugar Fungi) Freshly fallen leaves support opportunistic "sugar fungi" that rapidly exploit soluble carbohydrates, amino acids, and other readily available nutrients leached from the leaf tissue. These early colonizers are often characterized by rapid spore germination, fast hyphal extension, and high reproductive rates. Many Mucorales are typical of this ruderal strategy. ### Phase 2: Secondary Decomposition As labile nutrients are depleted, secondary colonizers capable of degrading structural polysaccharides (cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin) become dominant. These fungi possess the enzymatic machinery to break down more complex molecules. Species of hyphomycetes and coelomycetes are common in this phase. ### Phase 3: Late-Stage Decomposition The final phase involves fungi capable of degrading lignin and other recalcitrant compounds. ## Temporal Scale

The temporal scale of fungal succession varies dramatically between ecosystems:

- **Humid tropical forests**: complete decomposition may occur in months, with rapid species turnover

- **Temperate deciduous forests**: succession spans months to years

- **Boreal and alpine forests**: decomposition may take years to decades, with very slow successional change

## Upper Versus Lower Leaf Surfaces

Hudson and Webster (1958) discovered that fungal succession varies even between upper and lower surfaces of leaves and between upper and lower internodes of grass stems. A. Rambelli observed that primary colonizers of newly fallen Pistacia lentiscus leaves in Sardinia differ between the upper and lower surfaces of individual leaves. This microhabitat specificity must be accounted for in sampling.

## Interactions with Soil Fauna

Fungal succession on leaves is influenced by interactions with soil invertebrates. Collembola, mites, and other microarthropods feed on fungal mycelium and spores, selectively grazing certain species and thereby shaping [[ligninolytic-enzyme-production-bioremediation]] including laccases, peroxidases, and oxidases that attack the most recalcitrant plant polymers.
