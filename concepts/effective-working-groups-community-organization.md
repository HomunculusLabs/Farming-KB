## Effective Working Groups and Community Organization

A core insight from [[permaculture-design]] is that human social systems are as subject to [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]] as landscapes. [[bill-mollison]] argues that most failures in community cooperation stem not from a lack of will, but from the failure to adopt sensible legal, administrative, and social mechanisms that enable cooperation. This concept page synthesizes Mollison"s guidance on structuring groups for effectiveness, drawing on decades of study of [[holmgren-community-design-and-intentional-communities]].

## Portfolio of Occupations

In a well-designed community web of function, individuals participate in multiple small teams rather than committing to a single role. This creates a **portfolio of occupations** — each person may serve on two or three teams, spreading skills, preventing burnout, and fostering cross-functional understanding. The key dependencies are structural: each group relies on every other group performing its function, which creates mutual accountability rooted in shared ethics and values rather than assumed personal affection or trust.

Mollison is explicit that groups should not prescribe personal behavior, diet, or lifestyle beyond alignment with core ethics. People are never assumed to be perfect — the standard is movement toward improvement.

## Group Sizes and Their Optimal Functions

Group size is perhaps the single most critical variable in community design. Different scales of human gathering exhibit fundamentally different characteristics. Mollison provides a detailed taxonomy:

### Micro-Groups (1-6 People)

- **1-3 people (Troika model):** Executive decisions, minimal meeting time, high pressure to act, fast changes possible, rapid replacement of key people. This is the optimal unit for management and coordination — a cell of one to three can run a very large network.
- **4-6 people:** Ideal for volunteer work, cooperative projects, single-purpose task groups, and work exchange systems. This is the most effective working size for concrete tasks.

### Small Groups (7-40 People)

- **7-20 people:** Function well only in social or recreational contexts. At around 7 members, a chairperson becomes necessary, decisions slow down, and dissent often emerges. Not recommended for operational work.
- **30-40 people:** The minimal group size in which most human functions can be covered. If well-chosen, such a group can cope with almost any type of problem.

### Community Scale (40-600 People)

- **40-200 people:** Rarely found as a single settlement, but a good size for regional organization.
- **200-300 people:** The basic number for genetic variability — a tribe that can maintain its numbers through careful breeding despite disease losses. This is probably the minimal viable village size (a hamlet).
- **300-600 people:** About the limit where people know every other person by name — the limit of "identity." This is the largest satisfactory size for educational systems that value personal attention, and is acknowledged as the upper limit for successful cooperatives with real participation.

### Larger Scales (1,000+ People)

- **1,000-5,000 people:** Upper limit of tribal federations; good for bioregional groups. Cliques, theft, and cheating become common; hierarchies become necessary.
- **7,000-40,000 people:** Town scale. Chinese communes begin around this size. Unsatisfactory unless broken into small cooperatives and villages.
- **15,000-10,000,000 people:** Cities — largely disorganized on every level, with effective anarchy, crime, and social isolation.

### The Strategic Implication

Since groups of 4-6 people are highly effective, it is far better to establish **independent but friendly alliances of small groups** than to coalesce into unwieldy groups of 600 or more. An alliance of 4-10 villages (12,000-50,000 people) can, by agreement, run sophisticated trade and travel organizations while preserving local autonomy.

## Troika Management and Autonomous Delegation

The preferred [[troika-management-approach-mollison]] is delegation to **small autonomous subgroups**. When a large number of people is needed for a job, a calendar is set and conveners inform participants of when and where personpower is required. Each subgroup is trusted to execute its work and is only replaced if it persistently fails.

This structure has several critical consequences:

- Every person who wants to work controls their own work
- Non-involved people have no say in tasks they are not familiar with
- Control by inactive people is eliminated
- Power-seeking behavior is nullified

Dissenters face no impediment — they are free to set up their own parallel system or work group to demonstrate a better approach. Mollison insists: "Above all, there is no one way to do anything." Systems that enforce a single solution arise from concentrated power in some form of dictatorship.

## Abolishing Consensus Decision-Making

One of Mollison"s most provocative positions is that **group consensus on all decisions** is a primary error — a "tyranny of the dissenter." His framework distinguishes sharply between:

- **Initial ethical and value consensus:** Agreed upon once, during the planning phase, and clearly articulated
- **Everyday operational decisions:** Made by small autonomous groups without requiring broader consensus

Once functions are assigned or chosen, no group meetings for business are necessary. Each subgroup is small enough to reach fast agreements and stay aware of each member"s work. All group meetings can therefore be social, convivial, and focused on information exchange — replacing frustrating "group decision" meetings with pleasant gatherings people actually look forward to.

## Work-Netting

On a wider scale, cells of one to three people can coordinate a very large network through **work-netting** — a term Mollison distinguishes from aimless networking. Work-netting involves periodic (approximately every 2 years) meetings of affinity or work integration groups, attended by small autonomous groups. The principle is straightforward: no person can force another group to cooperate; they must offer reasonable, rewarding, and fair cooperation.

This creates a resilient, self-organizing structure where coordination happens through voluntary alignment around shared goals rather than top-down mandates.

## Intentional Community History and Voluntary Simplicity

Mollison observes that throughout history, intentional communities have emerged during economic downturns — the 1890s, 1930s, and 1960s. The most recent wave (1960-1990) represents the longest sustained period of out-migration: a movement of skilled family people toward smaller, more self-reliant societies.

Studies of effective communities reveal that the most important success factor was the adoption of **[[voluntary-simplicity]]** as an ethic. The relationship between community cooperation and economic constraint is not coincidental — it reflects a fundamental truth that modest consumption enables genuine interdependence.

## The Legal and Ethical Basis for Cooperation

The foundation for successful community must stress three principles:

1. **Sharing** — cooperative access to resources and knowledge
2. **Trusteeship** — responsibility for shared assets held in trust, not ownership
3. **Modest consumption** — the most important of the three, as individual power over land, assets, finance, or group membership inevitably leads to power over others

Mollison regards the habit of **frugality** as perhaps the single most important behavior for assisting other life forms and maintaining cooperative social structures.

## Common Primary Errors in Community Design

Like errors in [[slope-analysis-landscape-planning]], certain community design choices cause more conflict than can ever be recovered. Mollison identifies these as **primary errors** — structural mistakes that lead to constant problems and expense:

- **Group consensus on all decisions** — tyranny of the dissenter; paralyzes action
- **Group leadership by one or a few** — tyranny of the leader; concentrates power
- **Rules about lifestyle** — tyranny of proscription; restricts autonomy unnecessarily
- **Large group households** — lack of privacy erodes well-being and creates tension
- **Overscheduling of meetings** — consumes time and energy needed for productive work
- **Neglect of income-producing activities** — financial unsustainability undermines everything
- **Poor financial accounting** — leads to poor maintenance of facilities and equipment

## Organic Growth and Selective Recruitment

Most groups start with 1-3 people and recruit slowly. Slow, organic growth is easily managed, while sudden influxes of members can be highly disruptive. As a group approaches 30 members, it may become possible to pursue selective recruitment to address deficiencies in skills, sex ratio, age diversity, or needed specialties.

## Finite Purpose

Finally, Mollison emphasizes that no group or community needs to last forever. A group formed to achieve specific ends can disband with a clear conscience once those ends are substantially achieved. Members can then take on new tasks or adopt a different level of effective action informed by their experience. This pragmatic approach prevents institutional stagnation and honors the natural lifecycle of human organizations.
