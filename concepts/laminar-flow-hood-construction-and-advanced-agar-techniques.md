# Laminar Flow Hood Construction and Advanced Agar Techniques

The [[laminar-flow-hood]] is the centerpiece of any mycology laboratory. It provides a sterile work environment by filtering ambient air through a [[hepa-filter]] and blowing a constant wall of clean air across the work surface, preventing contamination from airborne microorganisms. Beyond building the hood itself, advanced agar techniques -- including enrichment media, split gallery plates, and antibiotic supplementation -- allow cultivators to fine-tune their culture media for specific mushroom species and experimental purposes.

## Laminar Flow Hood Function

A laminar flow hood is essentially a work surface surrounded by an open cabinet or hood with its own HEPA-filtered air supply. The unit pulls in ambient air at its side or base, filters it through the HEPA filter, and gently blows clean air across the work surface. This constant outward-moving filtered airflow prevents ambient air from entering the work area, blocking contamination from airborne bacteria and fungal spores. In most home and small-scale commercial laboratories, laminar flow hoods handle round-the-clock air purification that maintains the integrity of the space.

## Building a Laminar Flow Hood

### HEPA Filter Selection

Look for a gasketed HEPA filter with an efficiency rating of 99.997 percent at 0.3 microns. The gaskets allow a perfect seal in the box enclosure, eliminating leakage of unfiltered air into the work area. For a full-size laminar hood, a filter approximately 2 feet high and 3 feet wide is preferred. A separate smaller laminar flow hood with a smaller HEPA filter is useful for isolating wild specimens or potentially contaminated cultures that could threaten the cleanliness of the main work area.

Purchasing a prefabricated kit costs over $1,000. Building one yourself costs approximately $400 for a high-quality HEPA filter and fan or blower unit, plus additional materials for the enclosure. The investment can last years with proper maintenance.

### Blower or Fan Selection

The blower is typically a furnace-type "squirrel-cage" blower available at hardware stores. It must have the proper electrical rating for the workspace and a force rated at cubic feet per minute (CFM) appropriate for the filter. If the blower lacks sufficient force, the laminar will not maintain an even stream of sterile air. If too powerful, it can damage delicate tissues or create a whirlwind that blows contamination throughout the lab. The ideal airflow rate for a commercial laminar flow hood is approximately 550 CFM, with an acceptable range of 100-600 CFM for home-built units.

### Box Construction

Measure the exact dimensions of the HEPA filter and construct a basic box structure around it. The box extends roughly 8-10 inches from the back of the filter and approximately 18-24 inches from the front. The deeper front space serves as the actual work area; the shallower rear enclosure houses the blower, typically mounted on top through a small hole. The blower can be housed in its own separate box with an open top where a coarse prefilter catches larger particulates before they reach the HEPA filter.

The work surface should be stainless steel or a comparable surface that withstands disinfection without staining or rusting. Old tabletops or commercial kitchen components can be converted for this purpose. Fasten the box together using small bolts with wing nuts on compression washers, allowing easy access for HEPA filter replacement. Designing the filter housing with a removable lid allows the filter to be slipped out and replaced without disassembling the entire unit.

The box can be built from any sturdy material -- wood, scrap metal, or galvanized steel. If using wood, seal it with epoxy or laminate for wipe-down protection. The interior of the work space must be impervious to water.

### Sealing and Testing

Seal all joints and seams to create an airtight enclosure. Aluminum tape (used in the HVAC trade for sealing ductwork) is preferred over silicone caulk. Line all box joints and junctions between the box and HEPA filter with tape. Extend the tape up and over the filter frame edges and along the entire inside perimeter of the filter housing where it connects to the work area to prevent contaminated air from being sucked in from behind the unit.

To test for air leaks, turn on the blower and hold a smoldering stick of incense close to each seam. If the smoldering end brightens, air is being pulled into the box at that location, indicating a seal needs attention. Check the entire construction systematically. With a solid wall of sterile air at 100-600 CFM and a waterproof work space enclosure, the hood is ready for tissue culture work. A well-maintained HEPA filter with a prefilter lasts two to three years; without a prefilter, annual replacement is typical.

## Enrichment Medium (Substrate Agar)

Although the goal of lab work is to recreate a perfect growing environment, it is incredibly difficult to duplicate the complex chemistries and interconnectedness of natural habitats. One approach to making agar attractive to mushrooms that favor particular food sources is to add a small amount of their preferred fruiting substrate to the agar, allowing it to dissolve and release its chemical signature into the solution. The resulting enrichment medium or substrate agar helps hard-to-clone or picky fungi "feel at home."

### Practical Application

Drying and powdering samples of various growing substrates provides a library of materials to add to agar plates as needed. Adding a tablespoon of finely ground sawdust or screened wheat straw powder (sieved to the smallest particle obtainable) from a fruiting formula to agar helps cultures adapt and express the enzymes needed for metabolic activity. Substrate agar plates also serve as experimental mini-trials -- testing whether a particular mycelium strain can grow on an unusual substrate, such as shiitakes on wheat straw.

Alternatively, samples of pesticides or other pollutants can be added to agar plates to gauge a fungus's ability to break down contaminants. Alternating media through successive culture transfers keeps the mycelium's enzyme production strong, providing valuable information about substrate preferences and capabilities.

## Split Gallery Plates

Split plates create transitional or behavioral galleries for observing how fungi react to different concentrations of supplements or additives. One side contains an agar formula the mushroom has adapted to and prefers (such as [[potato-agar]]), while the other side presents a more challenging medium.

### Construction Technique

To create a split gallery plate, set the petri dish base at an angle by resting one end on a dowel. Pour enough agar to fill the raised half. Once gelled, flip the base and pour the second formula to fill the other side. When both sides are set, transfer mycelium to the "safe side" to give it opportunity to expand on familiar medium before reaching the middle, where it senses the change and the observer can record its reaction.

With additives creating the challenging medium, concentration can be increased with each consecutive transfer to determine tolerance levels for particular mycelial strains. This technique is especially useful for acclimating strains to new substrates or screening for pollutant degradation capacity.

Dyes can also be used to mark zones or galleries on agar plates, allowing observation of mycelial behavior and growth in response to different formulas. Colored agars provide a more prominent background when photographing or observing species that produce faint mycelium, such as lion's mane (*[[hericium-erinaceus]]*).

## Adding Antibiotics to Agar

Professional lab agar formulas sometimes require antibiotics to minimize or eliminate contaminants early in the cultivation process. Ampicillin and streptomycin are commonly used to limit bacterial contaminants across a broad spectrum. Powdered antibiotics are economical but require rehydration, vortex mixing, centrifuging, and syringe filtering -- a labor-intensive process. Pre-mixed agar formulations with heat-stable antibiotics exist but are expensive.

### Penicillin G for Agar

The most practical solution is sterile, injectable penicillin G used for livestock inoculations. Since the solution is already sterile, it can be added directly to cooling agar media when the temperature drops below 110 degrees F (43 degrees C) but before gelling, swirling to distribute evenly. Adding antibiotics above 110 degrees F or autoclaving them destroys their effectiveness.

A sterile syringe is used for addition. The same syringe can be reused if both the antibiotic jar and syringe are wiped with alcohol before the first use, and both are stored together in the same ziplock bag in the refrigerator. Antibiotics can also be used in liquid cultures, especially for longer-term storage, to prevent bacteria from flourishing in nutrient suspensions used for repeated transfers.

## Record Keeping

Maintaining detailed records is essential for any laboratory operation. A laboratory notebook should track agar formulas, additives and their concentrations, species cultured, dates, transfer histories, contamination events, and performance observations. Color-coding systems for agar plates and consistent labeling conventions help manage the complexity of running multiple species and experiments simultaneously. Over time, these records become invaluable for optimizing formulas and troubleshooting problems.
