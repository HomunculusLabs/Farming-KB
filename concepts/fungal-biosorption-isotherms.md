## Overview

Biosorption is the non-metabolic binding of metal ions to microbial

typically exceed metabolism-dependent bioaccumulation by a significant
margin. Fungal biosorption has been studied since the 1980s, when

generally falls in the range of 0.1 to 1.0 mmol metal per gram dry
weight, making [[diffusion-osmosis-and-active-transport-in-plants]] into
the cell interior. For most filamentous fungal species, metabolism-
dependent uptake is negligible compared with biosorption values.
Biosorption is largely independent of whether cells are living,
denatured, or dead, because binding depends on the physicochemical
nature of the cell wall rather than cellular metabolism. This
distinction is practically important because dead or processed fungal
biomass can be used as a biosorbent without the need to maintain
living cultures, simplifying industrial application.

## Key Fungal Biosorbents

Members of the order Mucorales, particularly Rhizopus and Mucor
genera, consistently exhibit high uptake levels across a range of
cations. Rhizopus arrhizus has shown maximum uptake values of 0.56
mmol per gram for cadmium, 0.61 for lead, 0.60 for copper, and 0.53
for zinc. Rhizopus oligosporus achieved 1.1 mmol per gram for lead,
among the highest reported values. Mucor meihi showed 1.15 mmol per
gram for chromium(VI). While Penicillium and Aspergillus species were
initially reported as poor biosorbents, more recent studies have
shown contrasting evidence with improved performance. Yeasts including

## Metal Selectivity Patterns

Uranium, specifically the uranyl ion (UO2(2+)), is consistently
taken up to high levels by most fungal biomass types, with uptake
exceeding 0.8 mmol per gram dry weight (200 mg per gram). Lead is
another metal repeatedly found to be biosorbed to high levels.
