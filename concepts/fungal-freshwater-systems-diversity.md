## Aquatic Hyphomycetes

Freshwater habitats support specialized communities of aquatic hyphomycetes, also known as Ingoldian fungi, which produce distinctive tetraradiate or sigmoid conidia adapted for dispersal in flowing water. These fungi are the primary decomposers of leaf litter entering freshwater systems.

Aquatic hyphomycetes convert allochthonous leaf material into fungal biomass that supports aquatic food webs, playing a crucial role in energy flow from terrestrial to aquatic ecosystems.

## Leaf Decomposition in Streams

When leaves enter streams, they are rapidly colonized by aquatic hyphomycetes. The succession of fungal species on decomposing leaves follows patterns analogous to terrestrial leaf litter succession, beginning with rapid colonizers and progressing through species with different enzymatic capabilities.

The rate of leaf decomposition in streams depends on water temperature, nutrient availability, and the composition of the fungal community. Fungal diversity on decomposing leaves in streams can be assessed through conidium trapping, direct observation, and molecular methods.

## Root-Associated Aquatic Fungi

Aquatic hyphomycetes also occur as endophytes in plant roots near water bodies. Fisher and colleagues (1991) found 46 species including 14 common aquatic hyphomycetes as endophytes in Alnus glutinosa aquatic roots. Sridhar and Barlocher (1992) found seven species of aquatic hyphomycetes as endophytes in Acer spicatum roots.

This dual lifestyle as both free-living decomposers and root endophytes demonstrates the ecological versatility of aquatic hyphomycetes.

## Filamentous Fungi from Freshwater

Various investigators have isolated filamentous fungi from soils, sands, and sediments of estuaries, mangroves, and freshwater habitats. Almost all taxa isolated from freshwater habitats, with the exception of a few facultative marine mitosporic fungi, appear to be terrestrial soil fungi.

This finding suggests that many freshwater fungi are primarily terrestrial organisms capable of tolerating aquatic conditions, blurring the distinction between truly aquatic and facultatively aquatic fungal species.

## Water as a Limiting Factor

The considerable diversity of fungi in aquatic environments is indicative of the importance of water as a factor limiting fungal existence. Conversely, some species are adapted to xeric environments. The diversity of microfungi closely associated with plants is small in arid areas compared with mesic areas.

Water availability directly affects rates of decomposition. Abundance of water promotes fungal growth in general and may affect diversity because fungi vary in their tolerances to water stress.

## Factors Affecting Diversity

Temperature, water relations, seasonality, and physical factors all influence freshwater fungal diversity. The effects of seasonality are connected intimately with the effects of temperature and water.

Plant diversity at a site exerts the greatest influence on microfungal diversity because a large proportion of microfungi have at least some degree of host and substratum preferences. In freshwater systems, the diversity of leaf inputs from riparian vegetation strongly influences aquatic fungal diversity.

## Fungal Roles in Stream Ecosystems

Aquatic hyphomycetes play crucial roles in stream food webs by converting recalcitrant leaf material into more palatable fungal biomass. Invertebrate shredders preferentially consume fungus-conditioned leaves over fresh leaves, demonstrating the central role of fungi in making terrestrial carbon available to aquatic consumers.

The diversity of aquatic hyphomycete communities affects decomposition rates and therefore the availability of food resources for higher trophic levels. More diverse fungal communities may decompose leaf litter more completely and efficiently.

## Sampling Methods

Sampling freshwater fungi requires specialized approaches. Conidium traps placed in flowing water capture the characteristic spores of aquatic hyphomycetes. Incubation of submerged substrata in the laboratory allows direct observation of fungal fruiting structures.

Molecular methods including metabarcoding of environmental DNA from water and sediment samples are increasingly used to document freshwater fungal diversity without the need for cultivation.
