## Two Mechanisms of Dryland Salting

Dryland salting manifests through two distinct but often interacting mechanisms, each tied to how water moves through the landscape after native vegetation is removed.

### Mechanism A: Soil Collapse in Clay-Fraction Soils

In soils with a significant clay fraction, the removal of deep-rooted perennial vegetation leads to structural collapse of the soil profile. Without the stabilizing effect of tree roots and the constant transpiration draw that kept water tables in check, the soil compacts and loses its capacity to infiltrate and drain water effectively. This compaction produces waterlogging at the surface, which in turn accelerates gully erosion. Crops die not directly from salt toxicity in the early stages, but from the combined effects of physical compaction and anaerobic (oxygen-deprived) conditions in the root zone. As the cycle progresses, salts concentrate in the waterlogged zone, compounding the damage.

### Mechanism B: Rising Saline Groundwater in Permeable Soils

In deforested areas underlain by permeable soils and rock, a different process dominates. Floodwaters that would once have been intercepted and transpired by trees now infiltrate past the root zone, recharging deep saline aquifers. Over time, these saline groundwaters rise toward the surface through capillary action and hydraulic pressure. Trees are far more effective than crops or fallow land at removing excess soil water, and their removal eliminates the primary mechanism for keeping the water table at depth.

## Cautious Approaches for Dryland Designers

Designers working in salt-affected drylands should adopt a conservative, incremental approach rather than implementing large-scale interventions without adequate site understanding. The following principles are essential:

- **Start small**: Try a variety of small systems on one slope and soil type before scaling up.
- **Monitor continuously**: Track infiltration rates, evaporation, salinity changes, and crop health across all trial systems.
- **Avoid inappropriate storage**: Store no runoff in locations where tree roots cannot remove excess water, as this will contribute to further water table rise.
- **Match strategy to groundwater depth**: Where groundwater is already shallow, concentrate on diverting floodwater to natural streams. Where soils are free-draining, focus on increasing infiltration via swales.
- **Use ridge trickle irrigation** during dry periods, and allow floodwaters to flush furrows periodically.
- **Prefer surface water sources**: Favor roof tanks and surface swales over wells and bores, which can draw saline water and accelerate water table decline in quality.
- **Establish local conditions**: Always use back-hoe or deep pit samples to understand local drainage conditions before committing to any design.

## Strategy Set for Farmers

Mollison outlines a prioritized five-part strategy for farmers confronting dryland salting:

1. **Swales at high levels**: Construct swales on upper slopes to establish tree systems that reduce net infiltration into the groundwater. These trees act as perennial pumps, intercepting rainfall before it reaches the saline aquifer.

2. **Interceptor drains at midslope and lowlands**: Install interceptor drains leading to natural waterways. These capture lateral water flow that would otherwise contribute to waterlogging and salinization at lower elevations.

3. **Intensive tree planting at intake areas**: Plant salt-tolerant and high-water-use species at the zones where water enters the system. Recommended species include *Eucalyptus camaldulensis*, *E. sideroxylon*, *E. wandoo*, *E. globulus*, and *Casuarina cunninghamii*. Native tea-tree species (*Leptospermum*, *Melaleuca*, *Banksia*) are killed by salt accumulation but can re-establish naturally once uphill salinization effects are brought under control.

4. **Subsurface drains for high-value crops**: Where high-value horticultural or agricultural crops justify the investment, subsurface drainage can protect root zones from waterlogging and salt accumulation.

5. **Bores and pumps as last resort**: Pumping saline groundwater is energy-intensive, expensive, and addresses the symptom rather than the cause. It should only be employed when all other strategies have been implemented and additional water table management is still needed.

## Additional Salinity Control Methods

Beyond the core five-part strategy, several complementary approaches can further reduce salinization risk:

- **Shift from annuals to perennials**: Replace shallow-rooted annual crops and pastures with deep-rooted forage tree crops that transpire water year-round and maintain a deeper root zone.
- **Eliminate leaky infrastructure**: Replace open irrigation channels with impermeable pipes to prevent water losses that recharge the water table.
- **Precision irrigation**: Use sophisticated irrigation systems that deliver water directly at root level, minimizing deep percolation below the root zone.
- **Salt-tolerant species**: For soils exceeding **0.4% NaCl**, establish salt-tolerant plants including *Atriplex* (saltbush), *Acacias*, *Prosopis* (mesquite), *Puccinella*, *Maireana*, *Salicornia*, *Agropyrum*, and *Lolium rigidum*.
- **Economic assessment**: Conduct careful economic analysis that accounts for the long-term costs of salinization and favors naturally managed systems over intensive engineering solutions.

## Long-term Recovery

Recovery from dryland salting is a long-term process that unfolds over decades. As remediation measures take effect and trees lower the water table, salt stored in the soil profile is flushed out — but this flushed salt enters streams and waterways, causing a **temporary increase in stream salinity** before conditions improve. During this recovery period, fresh water for stock and domestic use may need to be supplied by **rainwater tanks** and **hill sand storage dams** until natural streamflows regain acceptable quality. Designers and land managers must plan for this transitional phase and communicate realistic timelines to stakeholders, as full recovery of stream health can take **decades**.
