# Fungal Diversity in Temperate and Boreal Forests

Temperate and boreal forests represent major biomes that
harbor distinctive and highly diverse fungal communities.
While tropical forests often receive greater attention in
biodiversity studies, temperate and boreal ecosystems support
remarkable fungal diversity, including many species that are
unique to these regions. The seasonal dynamics, plant
composition, and soil characteristics of these forests create
heterogeneous habitats that sustain complex fungal assemblages.

## Overview of Temperate Forest Fungi

Plant-species diversity in temperate forests generally is
lower than in tropical forests, yet the fungal diversity can
be remarkably high. Temperate forests are characterized by
pronounced seasonality, with distinct fungal fruiting periods
in spring and autumn. Foliar parasites of temperate trees
include genera such as *Coccomyces*, *Discula*,
*Mycosphaerella*, *Rhytisma*, and *Venturia*. The life cycle
of a typical foliage parasite of temperate trees involves the
production of ascomata on infected leaves during fall or early
spring, completing the cycle through saprobic overwintering.

Common leaf-colonizing fungi in temperate regions include
species of *Alternaria*, *Cladosporium*, *Botrytis*,
*Epicoccum*, and *Stemphylium*. These fungi tend to be more
numerous on lower leaves and on more peripheral leaves in the
canopy. Fungal growth is often better on the abaxial leaf
surface because it is more sheltered and has a higher number
of stomata.

## Boreal Forest Fungal Communities

Boreal forests present unique conditions for fungal
communities, including long cold winters, short growing
seasons, and often acidic, nutrient-poor soils. Terrestrial
and arboreal lichens are important regular components of
boreal forest ecosystems, serving as food for reindeer,
caribou, and deer. In temperate and boreal forests, lichens
may alter the availability of nutrients and influence system
dynamics from atmospheric deposition via canopy throughfall.

When compared, the boreal zone currently appears to be the
richer in terms of certain fungal groups. Boreal regions
support distinct assemblages of mycorrhizal fungi, including
many *Cortinarius* and *Russula* species that form
ectomycorrhizal associations with coniferous trees.

## Needle-Cast and Conifer-Associated Fungi

Needle-cast fungi occur predominantly in temperate regions
where conifers are common. Many needle-cast diseases are
caused by facultative parasites in the Rhytismataceae
(Ascomycota). Species commonly causing needle blights and
casts of conifers include members of *Lophodermium*,
*Lophodermella*, *Elytroderma*, *Hypodermella*, and
*Lirula*. They often produce symptoms and initiate fruiting
bodies in the first growing season, but those fruiting bodies
may not sporulate until the foliage is a year old or older.

The genus *Taphrina* appears to occur almost exclusively in
temperate regions. These biotrophic microfungi cause galls,
witches' brooms, leaf curl, and fruit deformations on a
variety of hosts. In temperate regions, young leaves and
flowers are infected each spring, and collections must be
made at this time or infections may be overlooked entirely.

## Soil Fungi in Temperate and Boreal Systems

The Wisconsin surveys provide foundational data on soil fungal
diversity across temperate vegetation types. Northern upland
conifer-hardwood forests yielded more than 476 taxa from
8,061 isolates, representing the highest species richness of
any vegetation type surveyed. Northern conifer swamps and
bogs supported more than 133 taxa.

Species diversity in tropical soils appears similar to that
in temperate soils, contrary to the intuitive expectation that
tropical soils would be more species rich. The species
richness of fungi in humid tropical soils is comparable to
that of fungi in temperate forests. This finding has important
implications for global estimates of fungal diversity and
suggests that temperate regions may harbor more undiscovered
fungal species than previously assumed.

## Seasonal and Successional Dynamics

Seasonal patterns strongly influence fungal communities in
temperate and boreal forests. The periodicity of spore
production and fruiting is closely tied to temperature and
moisture availability. Temperatures in temperate forest
soils influence which species dominate at different times of
year. Psychrotolerant fungi coexist with mesophiles in these
soils, and their spores can survive warm periods.

Distinct guilds of fungi are associated with different
successional stages of plant debris decomposition, with
lignified tissues such as wood and bark supporting more
extensive fungal succession than non-lignified remains.
Understanding these patterns is critical for predicting
how fungal communities will respond to environmental change
and forest management practices.

## Conservation and Research Needs

Despite decades of study in temperate regions, many fungal
species remain undescribed. The ratio of fungi to
phanerogams in temperate regions ranges from 6:1 in
well-characterized areas like Britain to 1:1 in less-studied
regions like the United States. This disparity underscores
the need for continued inventory work even in comparatively
well-surveyed biomes. Climate change poses particular risks
to boreal fungal communities, which are adapted to narrow
temperature ranges and may be unable to track shifting
vegetation zones.
