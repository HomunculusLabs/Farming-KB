# Right Livelihood in Permaculture

Right livelihood is the permaculture principle of earning a living in
ways that align with ecological ethics, personal values, and
sustainable systems design. Living in a sustainable way is more than
just an issue of architecture and infrastructure systems; it is also
an issue of lifestyle choices and personal habits. One of the most
important lifestyle choices we all make is how we earn our livelihood.

## Defining Livelihood Beyond Money

In modern times most people find livelihood to be synonymous with
earning money. However the true definition of livelihood means
securing the necessities of life. While in the modern system money is
the means by which we acquire necessities, there are many more ways
to acquire these necessities than by the exchange of money. With
[[permaculture-design]] one can actually create systems that generate the
necessities of life directly, reducing dependence on the cash economy.

A permaculture design should endeavor to have systems that yield the
resources that one can base their livelihood on. The idea is not to
pull from the system, but to have the system yield at a sustainable
rate for your livelihood. The ultimate goal of any permaculture
design is first to ensure the health of the earth, then take care of
one's personal needs, then generate abundance. You can use the
abundance to create stores for lean times, and when you have enough
stores then share your abundance with others.

## The Five Economic Input Streams

A permaculture economist understands that there are five economic
input streams that can be integrated into a right livelihood. Rather
than depending solely on any one stream, the permaculture approach
seeks to integrate all five to create resilience and abundance.

The market economy is the typical economy we all think of, goods and
services traded for legal currency. Participating in the market
economy typically entails having a paying job or some other form of
gaining legal tender. While important, one should not be solely
dependent on this stream.

The barter economy is simply trade, exchanging goods or services
directly. Trades are often a tit-for-tat equal exchange, though
sometimes what constitutes equal is in the eye of the beholder, as
one person's junk may be another person's treasure.

The gift economy looks like a barter economy but is of a wholly
different ethical nature. The gift economy is simply an act of taking
something you have in abundance and giving it to someone else.
Typically in the gift economy one never asks for anything in return;
things are simply given freely.

Locally produced natural resources represent another input stream. If
one can produce natural resources such as food, water, medicines, and
building materials through the use of high or low yield permaculture
systems, then one gains the necessities of life directly from the
land without monetary exchange.

Skill shares are when people get together and share their skills with
others, essentially teaching and passing on skillful knowledge. With
skills one is also able to acquire the necessities of life, either
by doing things yourself or trading your skills for what you need.

## Integration of Economic Streams

Permaculture Principle number 5 calls for us to integrate rather than
segregate. A person trying to live a right livelihood would attempt
to integrate all five forms of economic input streams into their
livelihood. You should not eschew any of them; however you should
also not be solely dependent on any one of them. Permaculture is all
about harnessing multiple input streams into your system, and these
five economies are all input streams. You should utilize them each as
much as possible without being dependent on any one of them.

The more of your immediate needs your system can provide directly, the
less money you need to make to survive. For example, grow as much food
as you can, harvest water, and create your own local infrastructures
that support your needs. The permaculture designer understands that
the system itself can generate many of life's necessities.

## Practical Applications of Right Livelihood

There are no easy answers to what livelihood will be exactly right
for you. However, the framework of five economic input streams
provides a practical guide for thinking about how to structure your
life. A person who grows their own food, harvests rainwater, builds
with local earthen materials, trades skills with neighbors, and has
a modest income from a market economy job has integrated multiple
streams and is far more resilient than someone dependent on a single
source.

The permaculture approach to livelihood also emphasizes creating value
rather than just earning money. Growing food, building community
infrastructure, teaching skills, and sharing abundance all create
value that circulates through the community and strengthens the
overall system. Right livelihood is not about austerity or
self-deprivation; it is about designing systems that yield abundance
while maintaining ecological health.

## Key Principles

- Livelihood means securing necessities, not just earning money:
  the five economic streams provide multiple pathways to meet basic
  needs beyond the cash economy
- [[integrate-rather-than-segregate]] economic streams: combine market,
  barter, gift, local production, and skill shares for maximum
  resilience
- Do not depend solely on any single stream: diversity in economic
  input mirrors ecological diversity and creates stability
- First earth care, then self care, then fair share: the hierarchy of
  [[permaculture-ethics]] guides how abundance should be generated and
  distributed
- The system yields at a sustainable rate: livelihood comes from
  system output, not from extracting or depleting resources

## Applications

- Designing homestead food systems that reduce grocery expenses and
  provide surplus for gift economy sharing
- Joining skill share networks where community members exchange
  knowledge in gardening, building, food preservation, and other
  practical skills
- Creating barter arrangements with neighbors for goods and services
  that reduce cash dependence
- Harvesting rainwater and building with local earthen materials to
  reduce utility and construction costs
- Participating in Free Cycle groups and other gift economy platforms
  to circulate surplus resources
