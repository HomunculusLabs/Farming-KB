# Permaculture Zone One Design

Zone one is the area of closest human interaction in a permaculture system — the space
immediately surrounding the home that is visited most frequently and managed most
intensively. It is the site of the kitchen garden, small animal systems, glasshouses,
and ponds. [[bill-mollison]] describes zone one as a **tightly controlled, weed-free,
dig-free area** designed for maximum productivity with minimum effort.

## Mulch Gardening Techniques

Zone one gardens are maintained using **full mulch** rather than traditional
cultivation methods like triple digging. Mulch gardening eliminates the need to turn
soil, preserves soil structure, retains moisture, suppresses weeds, and feeds the soil
biota continuously. Layers of organic material — straw, leaves, compost, and green
manure — are applied to garden beds and replenished as they decompose. This approach
contrasts sharply with the labor-intensive triple-digging methods common in
conventional organic gardening.

## Annual Garden Plant Classes

Mollison categorizes annual garden plants into two functional classes based on how they
are harvested:

- **Continually picked crops**: Plants that are harvested repeatedly over a season —
  herbs, leafy greens, pole beans, tomatoes, zucchini, peppers, and berries. These
  belong closest to the kitchen door because they require frequent attention and
  daily picking.
- **Harvested-once crops**: Plants harvested in a single pass — root crops like
  potatoes, onions, garlic, and grains. These can be placed further from the house
  since they are visited only at planting and harvest time.

This classification directly informs garden layout, ensuring the most-used plants
occupy the most accessible positions.

## Garden Layout with Paths and Borders

Effective zone one design uses **narrow beds separated by permanent paths** to avoid
soil compaction. Mollison recommends parsley as a living border plant for garden
beds — it is hardy, useful, and defines bed edges clearly. Paths should be mulched
or paved with stepping stones to remain weed-free and navigable in wet weather.
Garden beds should be designed so that all plants can be reached from the path
without stepping into the bed.

## Invasion Control and Border Plants

Zone one must be defended against invasion by weeds, grass, and wandering animals.
**Border plant systems** serve as living barriers. Criteria for effective barrier
plants include:

- **Fireproof**: Species with high moisture content and low flammability.
- **Dark underneath**: Dense canopies that suppress light and prevent weed
  germination below.
- **Low light requirements**: Plants that thrive in the partial shade of their own
  canopy.
- **Wind defense**: Species that form dense windbreaks.
- **Nitrogenous**: Plants that fix nitrogen or produce high-nitrogen leaf litter to
  enrich surrounding soils.

Recommended barrier plants include **Siberian pea tree** (*[[caragana-arborescens]]*), a
nitrogen-fixing shrub that provides edible seeds and chicken forage; **Jerusalem
artichoke** (*Helianthus tuberosus*), a tall, dense perennial tuber that forms
impenetrable screens; and **comfrey** (*Symphytum officinale*), whose deep taproot
mines minerals and whose broad leaves produce abundant mulch material.

## Fencing for Zone One

Zone one requires robust fencing to exclude animals and pests. Mollison specifies a
three-layer fence system:

1. **Netted fence**: The primary above-ground barrier, tall enough to exclude
   rabbits and poultry.
2. **Electric wire**: A deterrent wire at the top or middle of the fence to
   discourage climbing animals.
3. **Underground layer**: Wire mesh or hardware cloth buried at the base to prevent
   burrowing animals from entering.

## The Glasshouse Connection

Glasshouses and polytunnels should be **attached directly to the kitchen** whenever
possible. This eliminates the need to go outside in poor weather to harvest herbs,
salads, or seedlings. The glasshouse serves as a thermal buffer for the house,
capturing solar heat that can be vented indoors. It also extends the growing season
for tender crops and provides a protected environment for seed raising.

## Small Animal Systems

Zone one integrates **small animal systems** for protein and pest control:

- **Quail**: Compact, quiet birds suitable for small spaces. They produce eggs
  and eat insect pests. Quail can be housed in moveable pens that rotate through
  garden beds, providing manure and scratching services.
- **Turtles**: Turtles can be integrated into zone one pond systems as part of
  the aquatic ecosystem.

## Zone One Ponds

A small pond in zone one provides irrigation water, aquatic food crops, and habitat
for beneficial wildlife. Mollison recommends stocking ponds with **watercress** for
edible greens and **tadpoles** for mosquito control. An important design insight is
that **tree frog and ground frog tadpoles stratify by water depth** — tree frog
tadpoles occupy shallow surface zones while ground frog tadpoles prefer deeper water.
Designing pond edges with varied depth profiles supports both species and maximizes
biological pest control.

## Critique of Lawn Culture

Mollison offers a pointed critique of the **British lawn tradition** as a symbol of
wasted space and misplaced values. Lawns, he argues, serve primarily as **status
symbols** signifying wealth and leisure — they consume enormous resources in
maintenance while producing nothing edible or ecologically useful. The manicured lawn
represents what Mollison calls the **"nuclear family trap"**: a private, isolated
landscape that replaces productive community space with sterile monoculture. Zone one
design rejects this paradigm entirely, replacing decorative lawns with productive
gardens, food forests, and shared growing spaces.

## The Davis Village Project

Mollison references the **Davis village project** as a real-world example of zone one
principles applied at community scale. In this California development, homes were
designed with zone one gardens integrated into shared commons, glasshouses connected
to kitchens, and productive landscapes replacing traditional suburban lawns. The
project demonstrates that intensive zone one design works at both household and
neighborhood scales.

## Design Principles Summary

Key principles for zone one design:

- **Intensity**: Zone one is the most intensively managed area — tight weed control,
  high productivity, frequent harvest cycles.
- **Proximity**: Place the most frequently used elements closest to the door.
- **Mulch, don't dig**: Use sheet mulching and no-till methods exclusively.
- **Integrate animals**: Small livestock and aquaculture belong in zone one.
- **Defend boundaries**: Use multi-layer fencing and living barrier plants.
- **Connect structures**: Attach glasshouses, sheds, and processing areas to the
  home for efficiency.
- **Reject ornamental waste**: Every element in zone one should serve multiple
  functions — food, medicine, mulch, habitat, or soil building.
