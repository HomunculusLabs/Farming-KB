# Natural Farming Five Principles

## Overview

Natural farming is an agricultural philosophy developed by Japanese farmer and
philosopher **Masanobu Fukuoka** (1913–2008) over more than fifty years of
practice on his farm in Ehime Prefecture, Japan. Also called **"do-nothing
farming"** (無農薬自然農法), it represents a radical departure from both
conventional chemical agriculture and organic farming. Fukuoka proposed that
nature itself is the best farmer, and that human intervention in growing food —
through tillage, fertilizers, pesticides, weeding, and pruning — is largely
unnecessary and often destructive.

The philosophy emerged from Fukuoka's youth, when a certain turn of events set
him on what he called "the proud and lonely road back to nature." He came to
believe that people were "no longer truly human, and nature no longer truly
natural," and devoted his life to demonstrating that farming could be done in
harmony with natural processes rather than in opposition to them. His hilltop
orchard overlooking the Inland Sea became a proving ground where young people
from cities and other lands came to learn what he called "quarter-acre farming"
— growing enough food on a small plot to be self-sufficient.

## The Five Principles

Fukuoka distilled his approach into five foundational principles that, taken
together, define natural farming. These are not merely techniques but
expressions of a worldview in which human knowledge is seen as a source of
disorder rather than order. He sought to demonstrate the validity of these
principles through decades of practice and consistent harvests.

### 1. No Tillage

Plowing and tilling disrupt the soil ecosystem, destroying fungal networks,
earthworm channels, and the natural structure that plants depend on. Fukuoka's
fields were not plowed for over thirty years, yet consistently produced
above-average yields. The soil is worked instead by the roots of living plants,
the burrowing of small animals, and the decomposition of organic matter on the
surface. When seeds are broadcast onto undisturbed ground — particularly within
a living mulch of clover — they germinate and establish without mechanical soil
preparation.

The no-tillage principle extends beyond convenience to touch on fundamental
questions about soil health. Tilling oxidizes organic matter, accelerates
erosion, and disrupts the [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]] through which plants share
nutrients and chemical signals. By leaving the soil undisturbed, natural
farming preserves the complex biological architecture that conventional
agriculture destroys. Flood a field with water, stir it up with a plow, and
the ground sets as hard as concrete — a lesson Fukuoka learned by observing
the degraded soils of neighboring conventional farms.

### 2. No Fertilizer

Neither [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]] nor prepared compost are applied. Fukuoka argued
that fertilizers, even organic ones, deplete the land from which their
constituent materials are taken. The soil is enriched naturally through the
decomposition of straw mulch returned to the field after harvest, nitrogen
fixation by clover and other legumes, and the activity of an estimated
**100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria per gram** of healthy soil.

Rather than feeding the crop, the farmer's task is to maintain the conditions
in which soil microorganisms can sustain fertility. Fukuoka noted that chemical
fertilizers only became considered "indispensable" over roughly the last fifty
years of the [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]] — a blink of an eye in the long history of
agriculture. Before that, civilizations grew food for millennia without them.
Even the ancient practice of using manure and compost, while it does speed
crop growth, ultimately impoverishes the source land from which organic
material is harvested.

### 3. No Pesticides

Fukuoka observed that although thousands of diseases and insects exist in any
field or forest, nature maintains a dynamic balance among them. The concept of
"pest" is itself a human invention — an organism is labeled a pest only when
it threatens a monoculture grown under artificial conditions. In a diverse,
healthy ecosystem, no single species proliferates unchecked.

By eliminating pesticides, beneficial predatory insects and microorganisms are
allowed to thrive, and the natural checks on potential pest species remain
intact. Fukuoka wrote that "man grew confused when he identified these diseases
as insect damage; he created with his own hands the need for labor and toil."
