# Natural Farming Five Principles

Natural farming, as developed by Masanobu Fukuoka, is founded upon five major
principles that distinguish it from both conventional scientific agriculture and
organic farming. These principles are: **no tillage**, **no fertilizer**, **no
pesticides**, **no weeding**, and **no pruning**. Fukuoka spent over fifty years
demonstrating the validity of these principles on his farm in Ehime Prefecture,
Japan, consistently achieving yields comparable to or exceeding those of
conventional methods while dramatically reducing labor inputs.

## Philosophical Foundation

Natural farming is rooted in the philosophy of "Mu," or nothingness. It
originates from the insight that nature is perfect as it is and cannot be
improved by human knowledge or intervention. Fukuoka described natural farming
as "the methodless method of nature, the unmoving way of Bodhidharma." The
approach is centrifugal and convergent, seeking to return to nature rather than
diverge from it through ever-increasing complexity.

The core conviction is that human knowledge and action have harmed nature, and
that by renouncing these instruments of chaos and destruction, nature will
recover its ability to nurture all forms of life. Unlike organic farming, which
Fukuoka considered simply another form of scientific farming requiring
extensive processing and transport of materials, natural farming eliminates
unnecessary practices entirely.

## No Tillage

Fukuoka argued that no plant on earth is so weak as to germinate only in plowed
soil. Seeds in nature have always germinated and grown without tillage. The
soil is naturally worked by the roots of grasses and green manure crops, which
penetrate to depths of twelve to fifteen inches or more, far deeper than the
four to eight inches achieved by a plow.

When roots penetrate the earth, air and water follow. As roots wither and die,
microorganisms proliferate, increasing humus and softening the soil. Earthworms
appear where there is humus, and moles begin burrowing. This natural process
of soil aeration and enrichment is continuous and self-sustaining. Plowing,
by contrast, kills soil life, destroys soil structure, and creates conditions
that make further plowing necessary.

Fukuoka's paddy fields were not plowed or turned for over thirty years, yet
consistently produced close to 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre. The key
insight is that flooding a field with water and stirring it with a plow causes
the ground to set hard as plaster. The farmer then believes plowing is needed
to soften soil that was hardened by plowing in the first place.

## No Fertilizer

Crops depend on the soil, not on fertilizers applied by human hand. Forest
trees grow to magnificent heights without fertilizer, producing two to four
tons of new growth per quarter-acre annually. Fukuoka demonstrated that fruit
trees and grain crops could be cultivated without fertilizers while achieving
yields equal to those obtained with heavy fertilization.

Fertilizers create a cycle of dependency: they speed crop growth temporarily,
but weaken plants, lower disease resistance, acidify soil, suppress
microorganisms, and create trace element deficiencies. Approximately 70% of the
"big three" chemical fertilizers is concentrated sulfuric acid, meaning 1.8
million tons of sulfuric acid were being dumped annually on Japanese farmland.
Furthermore, research revealed that about 30% of nitrogenous fertilizer is
denitrified by soil microbes and escapes into the atmosphere, and that
phosphate fertilizers penetrate only two inches into the soil surface.

## No Pesticides

Fukuoka observed that although a thousand diseases attack plants in fields and
forests, nature strikes a balance without any need for pesticides. When
pesticides are applied, the first casualties are the tens of thousands of
spiders and fireflies per square yard that serve as natural predators, followed
by chalcid wasps, dragonfly larvae, tadpoles, and loaches. The natural balance
is destroyed, and the pests return in greater numbers because their predators
have been eliminated.

In his own fields, whenever a large outbreak of leafhoppers occurred, spider
populations multiplied as well, restoring balance without human intervention.
The widely accepted belief that trees will succumb without spraying does not
hold under natural conditions, provided varieties closer to their natural
ancestors are selected and grown in a proper environment.

## No Weeding

Nature does not arbitrarily classify one plant as a weed and try to eradicate
it. Weeds serve important ecological functions: their roots break up compacted
soil, they cycle nutrients, provide habitat for beneficial insects, and
protect soil from erosion. One can understand the nature of the soil more
quickly by examining the weeds growing in it than by examining the soil itself.

Fukuoka used straw mulching and cover crops such as white clover to suppress
unwanted vegetation while allowing beneficial plants to thrive. Rather than
eradicating weeds, his approach managed the plant community as a whole. The
emergence and succession of different weeds over the years is more natural and
more conducive to soil enrichment than maintaining a monoculture ground cover.

## No Pruning

A tree grows best in its natural habit. In natural forests, the trunks of
pines and cedars never branch or twist as long as they are not cut or harmed.
Branches do not crisscross, there are no dense lower branches that die back,
and every leaf receives sunlight. The confused, tangled forms commonly seen in
fruit trees are the result of haphazard pruning followed by neglect, not of
natural growth patterns.

Pruning creates an endless cycle of intervention: cutting branches disrupts
the tree's natural balance, stimulating excessive growth that requires further
pruning. Fukuoka argued that the tree shape confusion justifies pruning only
because pruning caused the confusion in the first place.

## Energy Efficiency

Natural farming produces approximately 130 pounds of rice, or 200,000
kilocalories of energy, per man-day of labor without any outside material
inputs. This is about 100 times the daily caloric intake of a farmer on a
natural diet. By comparison, traditional farming with animal power required ten
times as much energy input, small-scale mechanization doubled that again, and
large-scale mechanization doubled it yet again. When measured by energy
efficiency rather than gross output, natural farming is the most productive
agricultural method in existence.
