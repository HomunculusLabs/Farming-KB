## Natural Farming Five Principles

## Overview

Natural farming is an agricultural philosophy developed by Japanese farmer and
philosopher **Masanobu Fukuoka** (1913–2008) over more than fifty years of
practice on his farm in Ehime Prefecture, Japan. Also called **"do-nothing
farming"** (無農薬自然農法), it represents a radical departure from both
conventional chemical agriculture and organic farming. Fukuoka proposed that
nature itself is the best farmer, and that human intervention in growing food —
through tillage, fertilizers, pesticides, weeding, and pruning — is largely
unnecessary and often destructive.

The philosophy emerged from Fukuoka's youth, when a certain turn of events set
him on what he called "the proud and lonely road back to nature." He came to
believe that people were "no longer truly human, and nature no longer truly
natural," and devoted his life to demonstrating that farming could be done in
harmony with natural processes rather than in opposition to them. His hilltop
orchard overlooking the Inland Sea became a proving ground where young people
from cities and other lands came to learn what he called "quarter-acre farming"
— growing enough food on a small plot to be self-sufficient.

## The Five Principles

Fukuoka distilled his approach into five foundational principles that, taken
together, define natural farming. These are not merely techniques but
expressions of a worldview in which human knowledge is seen as a source of
disorder rather than order. He sought to demonstrate the validity of these
principles through decades of practice and consistent harvests.

### 1. No Tillage

Plowing and tilling disrupt the soil ecosystem, destroying fungal networks,
earthworm channels, and the natural structure that plants depend on. Fukuoka's
fields were not plowed for over thirty years, yet consistently produced
above-average yields. The soil is worked instead by the roots of living plants,
the burrowing of small animals, and the decomposition of organic matter on the
surface. When seeds are broadcast onto undisturbed ground — particularly within
a living mulch of clover — they germinate and establish without mechanical soil
preparation.
