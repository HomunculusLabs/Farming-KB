# Soil Fungal Isolation Techniques — Suspension Plating and Selective Media

Soil fungal surveys rely on indirect isolation techniques in which fungal propagules in soil are dispersed on artificial media. Multiple methods exist, each with strengths and biases.

## Soil Collection

- Collect 10-30g soil per sample; 6-20 samples per site
- Use soil-coring tube sterilized in 70% ethanol
- Take samples from mineral soil surface, after removing overlying litter and humus
- Place in labeled sterile bags; transport in ice chest
- Plate within 4 days or freeze for examination within 6-9 months
- Phytosanitary permits required for foreign soils

## Suspension Plating

The most commonly used method (often incorrectly called "dilution plating"):

### Basic Protocol
1. Prepare dilution series from 5-20g soil in sterile water (ratios of 1:100 to 1:200,000)
2. Goal: 5-30 colonies per 100mm Petri plate
3. Add 0.05-0.2% agar or carboxymethyl cellulose to retard sedimentation
4. Pipet suspension onto plates; spread with glass rod
5. Incubate at 15-20C for up to 3 weeks

### Advantages
- Simple, rapid, reasonably repeatable
- Yields excellent comparative data
- Low colony densities minimize intercolony interactions

## Direct Inoculation Methods

### Drechsler's Method
Sprinkle finely divided soil onto water agar, cornmeal agar, or carboxymethyl cellulose medium. Incubate at 15-20C, review daily, transfer emerging hyphal tips.

### Soil Swabbing and Stamping
Works well with low-propagule-density soils (e.g., deserts):
- Touch sterile cotton swab to soil surface
- Streak onto isolation medium in bacteriological purification pattern
- Or stamp soil onto series of plates — later plates have manageable colony counts

### Warcup Soil Plates
- Place 0.2-5.0mg pulverized soil in sterile Petri dish
- Disperse with sterile water
- Overlay with 8-10ml molten medium at ~45C
- Thin agar layer facilitates colony dissection from beneath

## Particle Filtration

Developed as alternative to suspension plating; favors isolation from mycelial fragments rather than spores. Developed in Canadian, German, and Swedish laboratories. Requires specialized apparatus.

## Selective Media and Antibiotics

### Key Agents
- **Pimaricin (natamycin):** Toxic to true fungi; used for oomycete isolation (Aphanomyces, Phytophthora, Pythium)
- **Cycloheximide (actidione):** Eliminates most filamentous fungi at 0.25-0.5g/liter; used for Ophiostomatales isolation
- **Benomyl:** Toxic to ascomycetes but less so to basidiomycetes, Pleosporales, and Mucorales
- **Rose bengal:** Colony-limiting agent; increased toxicity in light

## Colony Selection Strategy

### Option 1: Quantitative Survey
Select 25-35 isolates per sample from low-density plates after 3-5 days incubation. Suitable for compositional comparisons.

### Option 2: Thorough Community Characterization
Remove ALL isolates from limited number of plates. Rank by abundance. Start selection within 1-2 days of plating. Eliminate fast-growing colonies (Trichoderma, Mucor) to allow slow-growing species to develop.

### Critical Warning
Discourage practice of taking only "different-looking" colonies arbitrarily. Strongly advocate systematic collection of all isolates, ranked by abundance. Beginners tend to overselect pigmented, heavily sporulating colonies (Epicoccum, Stachybotrys) over featureless but potentially more significant taxa.

## Incubation Considerations

- Match incubation temperature to prevailing soil temperatures
- Cool temperatures (15-20C) slow colony expansion, allowing more time for species separation
- High temperatures (>40C) reveal thermotolerant and thermophilic fungi
- Light effects not systematically investigated but can affect growth and sporulation
