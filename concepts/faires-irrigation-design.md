# Irrigation Design

Permaculture irrigation is half species choice and half terrain. Only a small part involves piping, pumps, or equipment. By studying slope and topography, gravity directs water to gardens or individual plants. Irrigation is simply a way of supplementing natural water flow and possibly rehabilitating soil — it should NOT be used for growing watermelons in the desert or for watering lawns and washing cars.

## A Self-Reliant Water System

Even a small property can become self-reliant in water with a well-developed diversion system:
- Dams at top of slopes leading down to mid-slope dams
- Connected with diversion drains collecting rainwater
- Water directed into swales and reserve ponds
- Ponds with irrigation pipes to gardens and orchards
- Gardens and orchards also have swales and diversion drains

## Desert Irrigation

With careful planning, enough water can be captured to grow food even in the desert:

- Recycle ALL graywater to a wetland marsh
- Use drip hoses buried at least 6 inches under mulch or soil
- Drip hoses can be expensive; DIY alternatives:
  - **Ollas:** Earthenware pots sunk into the ground
  - **Bottles:** Punctured holes in the sides, filled with water
  - **Pipes:** Filled with gravel for slow seepage
- In orchards: use sprinklers to spray mist over roots ONLY in shady tree areas
- Water only in early morning, late evening, or at night
- **Mulch and swales are the secret** — both trap and preserve water runoff

## Designing an Irrigation System

1. Study the slope and topography of the land
2. Use gravity to direct water to gardens or individual plants
3. Choose species suited to the specific location and water availability
4. Harvest barn roof rainwater into a large tank
5. Gravity-feed to gardens below if possible
6. Mulch extensively
7. Choose drought-tolerant species for backup

The less likely your garden is to dry out, the better. Topography and layout conducive to water retention, extensive mulching, and drought-tolerant species are the keys.

## Key Principles

- Irrigation supplements natural flow — it doesn't replace it
- Gravity is free energy for water movement
- Species selection is as important as water delivery
- Mulch reduces evaporation dramatically
- Swales capture and store water in the soil
- Graywater recycling doubles your effective water supply

## Practical Applications

The principles outlined on this page connect directly to hands-on work in gardens,
farms, and homesteads. Integrating these concepts with complementary techniques from
related disciplines can significantly improve outcomes. Start with small-scale
implementation and scale up as understanding deepens through direct observation.

## Design Considerations

Effective implementation requires careful attention to site-specific factors
including climate, topography, available materials, and household needs. Each
homestead presents unique challenges and opportunities that must be addressed
through thoughtful design rather than generic solutions. The goal is to create
integrated systems where each element serves multiple functions and supports
the others, reducing overall workload while increasing resilience and output.

## Materials and Resources

Sustainable homesteading prioritizes locally available and renewable materials.
This reduces both costs and environmental impact while creating systems that
can be maintained without dependence on distant supply chains. Understanding
the properties and appropriate uses of local materials is a foundational skill
for self-reliant living. Many traditional building and craft techniques were
developed specifically to work with regional materials and climatic conditions.

## Seasonal Planning

The rhythm of seasonal change drives all aspects of homesteading. Planning
activities around seasonal patterns maximizes efficiency and aligns human
effort with natural cycles. Spring preparation, summer production, autumn
harvest, and winter processing each have their own requirements and rhythms.
Understanding these patterns allows for more effective use of time and energy
throughout the year, reducing stress and improving overall system performance.

## Integration with Broader Systems

No homesteading element exists in isolation. Water systems connect to growing
areas, which connect to food preservation, which connects to waste management.
Designing these connections intentionally creates synergies that multiply the
benefits of each individual component. The most successful homesteads are those
where every output becomes an input for another process, closing loops and
minimizing waste while maximizing productivity and self-reliance.
