# Compost Feedstock Selection

[[elaine-ingham]]'s field guide provides detailed guidance on selecting and managing compost feedstocks, comparing thermal composting with vermicomposting and addressing specific materials including paper, wood chips, and [[mushroom-compost]].

## Thermal Composting Fundamentals

Thermal (hot) composting relies on thermophilic microorganisms to decompose organic matter at elevated temperatures, typically 55-70°C (131-158°F).

The heat generated during active decomposition is critical for pathogen destruction, weed seed mortality, and the rapid breakdown of organic materials.

Proper thermal composting requires a balance of carbon-rich (brown) and nitrogen-rich (green) materials, adequate moisture (50-60% by weight), and sufficient oxygen through turning or passive aeration.

Ingham emphasizes that the biological diversity of the starting materials directly determines the biological diversity of the finished compost. Using a wide variety of feedstocks produces compost with richer microbial communities.

## Vermicomposting Fundamentals

Vermicomposting uses earthworms (primarily [[eisenia-fetida]], the red wiggler) to process organic matter at mesophilic temperatures, typically 15-25°C (59-77°F).

The worm gut acts as a specialized microbial reactor. As organic matter passes through the worm digestive system, it is inoculated with beneficial bacteria and enzymes that accelerate decomposition.

Vermicompost typically has higher concentrations of plant growth hormones, humic acids, and available nutrients compared to thermal compost, because the worm processing creates more biologically active material.

However, vermicompost may have lower total microbial biomass and less fungal diversity than well-made thermal compost, since the process operates at lower temperatures.

## Paper as Worm Feedstock

Paper and cardboard are commonly recommended as carbon sources for vermicomposting systems, but Ingham cautions that not all paper products are suitable.

Uncoated, unprinted paper (newspaper without glossy inserts, brown cardboard, paper egg cartons) provides good carbon substrate for worms. The cellulose in paper is broken down by bacterial and fungal enzymes in the worm gut.

Printed paper with petroleum-based inks may introduce contaminants including heavy metals and volatile organic compounds that accumulate in vermicompost and can harm plants or soil biology.

Glossy or coated papers contain clay coatings and plastic films that worms cannot digest and that may introduce microplastics into the finished vermicompost.

Paper should be shredded or torn into small pieces before adding to worm bins to increase surface area for microbial colonization and worm processing.

A balanced worm diet combines paper carbon sources with nitrogen-rich materials like kitchen scraps, coffee grounds, or manure to maintain appropriate C:N ratios.

## Wood Chips in Compost

Wood chips are a valuable but challenging compost feedstock due to their high carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (typically 200:1 to 400:1) and the lignin content that resists microbial breakdown.

Ingham notes that wood chips in compost primarily support fungal growth. The high carbon content and recalcitrant lignin select for lignin-decomposing fungi, which is desirable when fungal-dominated compost is the goal.

For compost tea production targeting fungal-dominated soils (orchards, perennial systems, [[forest-gardens]]), wood chip compost provides an excellent fungal inoculum base.

Wood chips decompose slowly — a hot compost pile with wood chips may take 6-12 months to fully mature, compared to 2-4 months for finer materials.

Fresh wood chips from conifers may contain allelopathic compounds (terpenes and phenolics) that can temporarily suppress microbial activity. Aging chips for several weeks before composting mitigates this effect.

## Worm Castings versus Worm Compost

Ingham distinguishes between worm castings (pure vermicast, the material that has passed completely through the worm digestive tract) and worm compost (the mixture of castings and partially decomposed organic matter from a vermicomposting system).

Pure worm castings have the highest concentration of plant growth hormones, beneficial microbes, and available nutrients, but represent only a portion of the total vermicomposting output.

Worm compost contains castings mixed with partially decomposed feedstock and microbial-rich material from the bedding. It has good biological activity but less concentrated plant growth stimulants than pure castings.

For compost tea production, Ingham generally recommends using worm compost as part of the compost base, because it provides both microbial diversity and readily available food sources for the tea brewing process.

## Thermal versus Worm Compost for Compost Tea

The choice between thermal compost and vermicompost as the base for AACT brewing depends on the target application.

Thermal compost typically has higher total microbial biomass and greater species diversity, including a broader range of thermally-tolerant fungi. It is preferred for general-purpose compost tea.

Vermicompost has higher concentrations of plant growth hormones (auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins) and more bioavailable nutrients. It is preferred when the tea will be used for plant growth stimulation or [[foliar-feeding]].

Many practitioners blend thermal and worm compost to combine the microbial diversity of thermal compost with the growth-promoting properties of vermicompost.

## Mushroom Compost

Spent mushroom compost — the substrate remaining after commercial mushroom cultivation — can be used in composting and as a compost tea ingredient.

Mushroom compost has already undergone significant microbial decomposition during the mushroom growing process, making it partially stabilized and biologically active.

However, Ingham cautions that spent mushroom compost may have reduced microbial diversity because the growing process selects for the cultivated mushroom species and its associated microbes.

Commercial mushroom compost may also contain residual fungicides or other chemical treatments used during cultivation, which could harm beneficial organisms in compost tea.

If using spent mushroom compost, it should be re-composted with diverse feedstocks to rebuild microbial diversity before use in compost tea production.

## Making Good Compost

Ingham's criteria for quality compost include:

Adequate diversity of starting materials — at least 5-10 different feedstock types to ensure broad microbial diversity.

Proper temperature management — achieving and maintaining thermophilic temperatures (55°C+) for at least 3 consecutive days to ensure pathogen destruction.

Sufficient oxygen — regular turning or passive aeration to maintain aerobic conditions throughout the pile.

Moisture management — maintaining 50-60% moisture content throughout the composting process.

Maturity testing — finished compost should have an earthy smell, dark brown color, and no recognizable feedstock materials.

## Compost Desired Biological Levels

Ingham specifies target levels of microbial biomass for quality compost used in tea production:

Total bacterial biomass: 150-300 µg/g
Total fungal biomass: 100-200 µg/g (higher for fungal compost)
Active bacterial biomass: at least 70% of total
Active fungal biomass: at least 70% of total
Protozoa: 10,000-50,000/g
Nematodes: diverse community with bacterial-feeding, fungal-feeding, and predatory types

Compost that falls below these levels may need remediation — additional feeding, aeration, or re-composting — before it is suitable for compost tea production.

## Compost Longevity

Finished compost remains biologically active for months to years depending on storage conditions.

Compost stored in cool, moist conditions retains biological activity much longer than compost that dries out or is exposed to extreme temperatures.

Ingham recommends using compost within 6-12 months of production for compost tea, as microbial populations gradually decline during storage even under ideal conditions.

## Key Takeaways

- Feedstock diversity determines compost microbial diversity — use 5-10+ different material types.
- Paper is acceptable worm feedstock only if uncoated, unprinted, and shredded.
- Wood chips favor fungal growth and are ideal for fungal-dominated compost teas.
- Thermal compost has broader microbial diversity; vermicompost has more growth hormones.
- Spent mushroom compost should be re-composted to rebuild diversity before tea use.
- Compost biological quality should be verified through microscopy before tea brewing.
