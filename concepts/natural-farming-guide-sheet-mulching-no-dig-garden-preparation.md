# Sheet Mulching No-Dig Garden Preparation

Sheet mulching is a method of layering different sheets of materials together,
something like a layer cake. The beauty of sheet mulching is that you can create
a new garden without the hard labor of digging and turning dirt. It works for
every soil type except for the hardest dirt, for which you would need to build
a [[natural-farming-guide-composting-methods-and-techniques]].

5. Water it well until it is completely soaked.

6. Add 8 to 12 inches (20 to 30 cm) of old straw from a horse stable, old
   chicken coop sawdust, raked or old mashed up leaves, seaweed, or seagrass.
   These all contain vital nutrients and can be moist. These should be brown
   materials rather than green, which would turn into mush and smell bad.

7. Water everything again until well soaked.

8. Add another 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 cm) of compost and manure, and another
   2 inches (5 cm) of dry material like straw or leaves. The only rule of thumb
   is the thicker the better. If you don't have enough material, make your bed
   smaller rather than spreading it thin over a wide area.

9. Plant your largest seeds, potatoes, seedlings, and small potted plants. Make
   a hole through the mulch to the sheet material. Use a knife to cut an X in
   the cardboard or carpet, and use your hands to put dirt in the hole. Stick
   the seeds or seedling in the soil. For tiny seeds, sprout them first and
   make a line rather than an X. Cover them with mulch.

10. Roots won't do very well in the first year, but if you plant roots that
    grow down deep, they will begin to break up the soil under the mulch. The
    second year you can grow more of them.

## Comparison to Traditional Tilling

Sheet mulching preserves soil structure and the complex web of soil organisms
that tilling destroys. It prevents erosion, retains moisture, suppresses weeds
naturally, and builds organic matter from the top down, exactly as forests do.
Traditional tilling exposes soil to sun and rain, accelerates decomposition of
organic matter, and disrupts fungal networks that plants depend on for nutrient
exchange.

## Soil Type Considerations

Different soil types benefit from sheet mulching in different ways:

- **Clay soil:** Adding gypsum in step 2 helps break up clay particles.
  The organic layers improve drainage and prevent crusting.
- **Sandy soil:** Sheet mulching dramatically improves water retention in sandy
  soils. The organic layers act as a sponge, holding moisture that sand alone
  cannot retain.
- **Compacted soil:** The layers gradually soften compacted earth as roots
  grow downward and organisms create channels. Over multiple seasons, even
  heavily compacted areas can be restored.

## Materials Sourcing

Finding enough organic material for sheet mulching can be a challenge. Sources
include: arborist wood chip deliveries, coffee shop grounds, brewery spent
grains, stable cleanings, grass clippings from neighbors, seaweed from beaches,
and shredded cardboard from businesses. Many of these materials are available
free or at very low cost, making sheet mulching an economical choice for large
areas.

## Timing and Seasonal Planning

Sheet mulching can be started at any time of year, but autumn application is
ideal. Layers break down over winter, and the bed is ready for spring planting.
Summer mulching helps protect soil from heat and retain moisture during dry
periods. Winter mulching insulates soil and prevents frost heaving of
perennials. For immediate planting, ensure the bottom layers contain enough
finished compost to support root growth.
