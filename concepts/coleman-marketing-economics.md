# Marketing and Economics of Winter Produce

Eliot Coleman's approach to marketing winter produce centers on the
principles of freshness, locality, and quality differentiation. Having
been involved in organic agriculture since 1965, Coleman observed that
small growers lost their unique niche when organic became industrialized.
The word "organic" is no longer an adequate distinction. The important
words became "fresh" and "local."

## The Freshness and Local Advantage

No matter who grew it or how it was grown, produce shipped from
faraway is at least a week old by the time it reaches the customer.
Long-distance produce is not "fresh" by any definition. Astute eaters
who shop at roadside stands and farmers markets know the superiority
of truly fresh vegetables and seek out local growers.

When Coleman walked into stores and restaurants in October 1995
with the first commercial winter-harvest crops, the wholesale price
was set at $2 per pound higher than California organic salad mixes.
The local food co-op placed Coleman's product side by side with the
West Coast competition. A day later, Coleman's had all sold while
the competition remained. Chefs immediately recognized both the
quality and the marketing cachet of serving a "local" salad, further
gratified by the freshness since the week-old imported product had
a short remaining shelf life.

## Packaging and Branding

Produce is packed for delivery in distinctive homemade 12-by-16-by-
8-inch wooden boxes made from native Maine cedar trees cut on the
farm and milled on a neighbor's sawmill. The reusable box concept
was adopted after learning that 25 percent of the volume in the
average landfill was food-packaging waste. Store managers approved
of the idea and save and return the boxes.

After construction, boxes are sanded smooth and coated with a
food-grade finish. The trademark "Seasonal Salads" is burned into
the end boards with a branding iron. The boxes were sized by
measuring produce coolers in stores and the back of the family
car, allowing up to 48 boxes in a Subaru for early deliveries.

Boxes are lined with a sheet of pure cellophane (made from wood
fibers, a farm product) that folds over the top and is held by
rubber bands. Cellophane was chosen over plastic because it can
be composted after use. Empty boxes are collected on each delivery
trip, cleaned with hot water, and stored for reuse.

## Setting Equitable Prices

When it comes to food prices, "fair" has two meanings: "affordable"
and "equitable." The most difficult challenge for the small-scale
grower producing high-quality vegetable crops is charging an
equitable price sufficient to make a decent living without being
accused of elitism.

Coleman argues that biological quality differences between
properly grown crops and conventional produce are real and
significant. He compares the quality difference between his
carrots and supermarket carrots to that between a Volvo and a
Corvair. A similar difference exists between local "organic" and
imported "organic." The small-scale local grower can produce a
more finely crafted product through meticulous attention to soil
care, superior compost, and variety selection, plus the obvious
benefit of delivery within 12 to 24 hours of harvest.

## Nutritional Superiority

Every day more peer-reviewed articles attest to the nutritional
superiority of food raised with organic and other natural
techniques. The winter harvest may offer additional benefits:
highly colored foods grown under cool conditions have been shown
to be much higher in anthocyanins, one of the most valuable
antioxidants. This is easily noticed in the much deeper red color
of red lettuces and beet leaves from the winter greenhouse.
Green leaves can also be high in anthocyanins since plants
utilize them to resist cold.

## Economic Targets and Returns

When commercial winter production began, Coleman set a target return
per square foot for the 12,000 square feet of greenhouse space.
The plan called for three crops per year from each square foot of
unheated production with a target of $5 per square foot annually,
about one-third of expected returns in large heated commercial
greenhouses growing flowers. In the cool houses, the target was
six crops per year aiming for $10 per square foot annually. Each
crop needed to return just over $1.50 per square foot.

A five-year ramp-up period was allowed for reaching target gross,
accounting for inevitable early mistakes in timing, cultivar
selection, and regrowth knowledge. Gross returns from both field
and greenhouse crops together eventually reached $80,000 per acre
per year. Coleman emphasizes that net income, not gross, makes a
living for the farmer, and much current effort focuses on
developing new tools to improve the net by cutting labor costs.

## The Farm Stand

The Four Season Farm stand features perennial flowerbeds in front
and operates with regular hours (Monday through Saturday, closed
Sunday). The sign at the entrance serves as both a marketing tool
and a statement of the farm's philosophy. Beautiful crops and
professional presentation are the keys to selling everything that
can be grown.

Coleman has suggested that the average American spending hundreds
of dollars per month on sickness insurance would be wiser to
invest in "health assurance" by spending an additional $10 per
day on high-quality local fruits and vegetables. This would
enormously increase the market for local growers while benefiting
customer health.

## Growing the Market

In one local store that had never carried these types of products
before, the quality of Coleman's salad and other vegetables created
an entirely new market. For customer convenience, salad is packed
in quarter-pound bags rather than bulk boxes. That account
eventually expanded to eight times the initial quantities.

One year, when extra outdoor carrots were available in late fall,
"Thanksgiving Gift Packs" helped triple sales, demonstrating the
value of creative marketing approaches tied to seasonal occasions.
