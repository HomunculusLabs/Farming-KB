## Mushroom Environmental Requirements — Temperature, Moisture, and Light

## Overview

The relationship of the cultivated mushroom (*[[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]].

## Temperature

### Optimal Range

For commercial mushroom growing, a more or less **uniform air temperature** in the growing house is highly desirable. The optimum range is:

- **Optimum**: 54° to 56°F
- **Good working range**: 50° to 65°F

Cultivation at temperatures more or less continuously **above 60°F or below 50°F** is not commercially successful, though much depends upon other environmental factors besides temperature.

### High Temperature: More Injurious Than Cold

Of the two extremes, high temperature is significantly more dangerous to mushroom crops. The key findings include:

- Brief heating to 70°F or above will not render the bed useless
- The bed will withstand low temperature, or even being **frozen for a considerable period of time**
- The amount of heat and cold that may permanently injure the bed depends largely on the extent of mycelial growth already made and on moisture conditions
- Any severe changes—whether heating or cooling—either retard growth or act more injuriously
- A high temperature long continued—**72°F for 24 hours**—is fatal to pin heads and injurious to spawn

### The Biological Mechanism of Heat Injury

Critically, the injurious effects of high temperature are **not due to direct thermal damage** to the mushroom [[compost]] permits continuous cultivation at temperatures somewhat above normal

If competing organisms could be largely eliminated from the mushroom bed, mushroom production might be possible at considerably higher temperatures than currently practiced.

### Effect of Temperature on Mushroom Quality

High temperature has an unfortunate effect upon the **form and quality** of the mushroom. At elevated temperatures:

- Sporophores expand and elongate so rapidly that large, heavy buttons (the highest market quality) are not produced
- Mushrooms resemble those produced when the bed is nearly exhausted—small and open
- This effect is partially due to a decrease in atmospheric humidity as temperature rises

### Benefits of Cold Treatment

Conversely, cold or freezing can produce beneficial effects under certain conditions. In beds spawned in November that experienced temperatures never above 52°F for several months (including periods at 32°F or below with frozen beds), subsequent warming in March caused abundant bearing with **unusual mushroom size**. Although the productive life of these beds was relatively brief due to subsequent above-normal temperatures, they produced heavy mushrooms much later into warm weather than beds spawned later and held at growing temperatures continuously.

### Practical Temperature Recommendations

- To produce heavy mushrooms: Do not "push" the beds by regulating to the highest permissible temperature
- If there is no necessity of hastening bearing: A temperature below 50°F for a week or two before buttons begin to form is advantageous
- There are unquestionably species of edible mushrooms that mature at temperatures unfavorable for *A. campestris*—some at higher, some at lower—but their economic cultivation potential remained undetermined

## Moisture

### Atmospheric Humidity

Moisture is second only to temperature in importance among environmental factors. The key principles are:

- **Saturation is harmful**: An atmosphere saturated with moisture, with water dripping from walls everywhere, creates an unfavorable environment
- **Optimal relative humidity**: 60 to 75 percent is satisfactory
- **Gradual evaporation is desirable**: A slow but gradual evaporation from the surface of the beds is altogether desirable and can usually be regulated by controlling ventilation

### Undesirable Extremes

The extremes to be avoided are:

1. **Stagnant, saturated air**: As found in caves with no air circulation—promotes excessive wetness, bacterial growth, and disease
2. **Drying conditions**: Exposure to drying winds or excessive ventilation that desiccates bed surfaces and casing material

### The Moisture-Ventilation Relationship

Moisture management is inseparable from ventilation management. Proper ventilation facilitates the gradual evaporation from bed surfaces that is desired, while preventing the accumulation of stagnant, moisture-saturated air. The balance between these two factors determines whether the growing environment is favorable or unfavorable.

### The Hand Test for Bed Moisture

A simple and reliable practical test for sufficient moisture in the compost involves compressing some material in the hand: under such pressure, it should not be easy to squeeze out any drops of water, yet the hand will be distinctly moistened. This condition usually corresponds to a water content of approximately 65 percent, though the qualitative hand test is more meaningful for practical purposes than the quantitative figure.

## Light

### The Myth of Light Injury

Since mushrooms are ordinarily grown in cellars or other light-proof houses, it has been widely assumed that light is an injurious factor in mushroom cultivation. This assumption is **incorrect**.

### The Real Situation

In pastures and fields, where mushrooms are ordinarily found, the young buttons are exposed to direct light as soon as they begin to emerge through the sod. Light has **no directly injurious effect** upon the quality or productiveness of cultivated mushrooms. Mushrooms can be, and indeed are, grown in greenhouses, both under the benches and on the benches, under proper conditions of temperature and moisture.

### Why Mushrooms Are Grown in Darkness

The association of mushroom growing with dark environments is practical rather than biological:

- **Sunlight brings heat**: Sunlight is accompanied by thermal radiation, and where the sun shines directly into a window, the temperature is appreciably raised
- **Temperature management**: In cellars with many windows, the temperature factor might be variable and, during bright weather, too high for successful work
- **Convenience**: Dark environments happen to be cool, humid, and protected—all conditions that favor mushroom growth

If the temperature can be adequately regulated, light can do no harm. The perceived "injurious effects of light on mushrooms under glass" are wholly a matter of temperature, not illumination. This understanding opens possibilities for mushroom cultivation in environments that are not completely dark, provided that temperature control is maintained.

## Interdependence of Factors

The three environmental factors—temperature, moisture, and light—are deeply interdependent:

- Raising temperature decreases atmospheric humidity, potentially desiccating beds
- Increasing ventilation for moisture control also affects temperature
- Light affects temperature through radiant heating
- Temperature affects the rate of evaporation from beds

Successful mushroom cultivation requires managing all three factors as an integrated system, not as isolated variables. The skilled grower must continuously monitor and adjust the balance among these factors to maintain conditions within the optimal range for each stage of the mushroom's life cycle.
