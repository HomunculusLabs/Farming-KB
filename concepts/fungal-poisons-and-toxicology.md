## Amatoxins (The Deadliest)

Found in: Amanita phalloides (death cap), A. virosa
([[galerina-marginata]], and Lepiota
species. The lethal dose is approximately 6-7mg,
roughly equivalent to one medium death cap mushroom.

Amatoxins inhibit RNA polymerase II, halting protein
synthesis in liver cells. The clinical timeline is
insidious: symptoms are delayed 6-12 hours with
initial gastrointestinal distress, followed by a
honeymoon period of apparent recovery, then liver
failure at 48-72 hours.

Treatment involves supportive care, silibinin (milk
thistle extract), and liver transplant in severe
cases. Mortality ranges from 10-15% with treatment to
60% without treatment.

The destroying angel is described by Money as
ravishingly beautiful with a pure white fruiting
body, membranous volva at the stem base, and a
floppy ring below the cap. The delayed onset of
symptoms is particularly dangerous, as patients may
feel well enough to leave the hospital before
catastrophic liver damage becomes apparent.

## The Death Cap Problem

The death cap, Amanita phalloides, is responsible for
most fatal mushroom poisonings throughout Europe and
[[paddy-straw-mushroom]],
[[galerina-autumnalis]], is a common
wood-rotting fungus that produces a small brown
mushroom known as an LBM (little brown mushroom).
The stems are never taller than a finger, and the
caps expand to just a few centimeters in diameter.

This unassuming woodland fruiting body contains the
same amatoxins as the destroying angel. The
identification challenge is formidable: edible
Pholiota mutabilis are roughly the same size and
color and grow on the same types of decaying wood.
Both may have white rings that often disappear with
age. Even professional mycologists admit to unease
when confronting LBMs in the field.
