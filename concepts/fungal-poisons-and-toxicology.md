# Fungal Poisons and Toxicology

Mushroom poisoning represents one of the most dangerous aspects of mycophagy. While the vast majority of mushroom species are harmless, a small number contain potent toxins that can cause organ failure, neurological damage, or death. Understanding these toxins is essential for anyone engaged in wild mushroom foraging.

## Amatoxins (The Deadliest)

Found in: *Amanita phalloides* (death cap), *A. virosa* (destroying angel), *Galerina marginata*, *Lepiota* species

- **Lethal dose**: 6-7mg (approximately one medium death cap)
- **Mechanism**: Inhibits RNA polymerase II, halting protein synthesis in liver cells
- **Timeline**: Symptoms delayed 6-12 hours (gastrointestinal); "honeymoon" period of apparent recovery; liver failure at 48-72 hours
- **Treatment**: Supportive care, silibinin (milk thistle extract), liver transplant in severe cases
- **Mortality**: 10-15% with treatment; up to 60% without

The destroying angel (*Amanita virosa*) is described by Money as "ravishingly beautiful" -- a pure white mushroom with a membranous cup (volva) at the stem base and a floppy ring below the cap. "To a reckless chef, the destroying angel is a formidable siren. Misused as a cooking ingredient, its alabaster flesh has wiped out whole families." In textbook cases, the unlucky patient feels fine for some time after consuming the mushrooms. First symptoms begin eight hours later, or even after a delay of two days, with abdominal cramping, vomiting, and violent diarrhea. But then symptoms abate -- the so-called "honeymoon" -- and the patient may appear well enough to leave the hospital. Unfortunately, liver damage continues, coma ensues, and death is a frequent outcome.

No antidote for amatoxin is available, although French investigators have been claiming miraculous cures since the early 1800s, using "potions ranging in refinement from minced rabbit brains and stomachs (sweetened with jam to increase palatability of course -- ah, the joys of French cuisine!) to mixtures of antibiotics and vitamin C."

## The Death Cap Problem

The death cap, *Amanita phalloides*, is responsible for most fatal poisonings throughout Europe and probably in North America. Many recent cases in the United States have involved immigrants who misidentified a lethal mushroom as an edible fruiting body from their homeland. Destroying angels and death caps resemble the paddy straw mushroom, *Volvariella volvacea*, widely cultivated in Asia. Vietnamese and Laotian immigrants have been felled by this mistake. Recently, a family in Ohio added slices of a pure white mushroom to their soup; the poisonings were so severe that two young sisters required liver transplantation.

## The LBM Problem: Deadly Galerina

The autumn skullcap, *Galerina autumnalis*, is a common wood-rotting fungus that buries its mycelium in fallen logs and resurfaces as a small brown mushroom -- a perfect example of an LBM (little brown mushroom). The stems are never taller than a finger or thicker than a pencil, and the caps expand to just a few centimeters in diameter. This dun, formulaic woodland fruiting body rarely provokes a second glance, yet it contains the same amatoxins as the destroying angel.

The identification challenge is formidable: edible fruiting bodies of the two-toned scalecap, *Pholiota mutabilis*, are roughly the same size and color, and grow on the same types of decaying wood. Both mushrooms may have white rings that circle the stem beneath the cap, but these often disappear with age. Even professional mycologists admit to unease when confronting LBMs in the field.

## Gyromitrin

Found in: *Gyromitra esculenta* (false morel), *G. infula*

- **Mechanism**: Hydrolyzes to monomethylhydrazine (MMH), which inhibits GABA and damages liver
- **Symptoms**: Headache, nausea, vomiting, hemolysis, seizures
- **Timing**: 6-12 hours after ingestion
- **Note**: Toxins are volatile and water-soluble; thorough cooking and multiple water changes reduce but do not eliminate risk
- **Geographic variation**: Finnish false morels have lower gyromitrin levels; North American specimens are more toxic

False morel poisoning parallels the clinical course of amatoxin poisoning in its severity. Fortunately, it is difficult to mistake a false morel for a true morel once the differences are understood.

## Coprine

Found in: *Coprinopsis atramentaria* (common inkcap)

- **Mechanism**: Not toxic alone, but inhibits acetaldehyde dehydrogenase
- **Effect**: Antabuse-like reaction when consumed with alcohol (flushing, nausea, palpitations, tachycardia)
- **Timing**: Symptoms appear within 30 minutes to 2 hours of alcohol consumption
- **Duration**: Sensitivity persists for 48-72 hours after mushroom ingestion

## Orellanine

Found in: *Cortinarius orellanus*, *C. rubellus* (webcaps)

- **Mechanism**: Causes irreversible kidney damage through oxidative stress
- **Timeline**: Extremely long latency -- symptoms may not appear for 3-14 days
- **Symptoms**: Thirst, polyuria, kidney pain progressing to renal failure
- **Treatment**: Dialysis; no specific antidote; damage may be permanent

## Muscarine and Ibotenic Acid

Found in: *Amanita muscaria* (fly agaric), *A. pantherina*, *Inocybe*, *Clitocybe* species

- **Muscarine**: Activates parasympathetic nervous system (SLUDGE syndrome). Atropine is the antidote.
- **Ibotenic acid/muscimol**: CNS effects including confusion, hallucinations, ataxia, drowsiness. Cooking or parboiling reduces toxicity.

## GI Irritants

Many mushrooms cause nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea without containing specific named toxins. These include:
- *Chlorophyllum molybdites* (green-spored parasol) -- most commonly consumed toxic mushroom in North America
- *Hebeloma crustuliniforme* (poison pie)
- Various *Entoloma*, *Tricholoma*, and *Russula* species

## Why Mushrooms Produce Toxins

Mushroom toxins are described as secondary metabolites. The delayed action of amatoxins makes them poor deterrents for mammalian predators -- the animal has already digested the mushroom by the time symptoms appear. A better explanation lies in their effects on developing insect larvae. Boletes and some Russula species become riddled with insect grubs, and high rates of cell division make embryos very prone to genetic damage. Insect larvae hatching within toxin-saturated flesh of an *Amanita* are likely to be highly vulnerable. Insects and fungi have interacted for 400 million years or more, offering ample opportunity for an evolutionary arms race.
