## Mescalero Apache Shamanism and Peyote Use

## Overview

The ethnographic study of [[mescalero-apache]] [[shamanism]] and peyote use was conducted by an interdisciplinary team — L. Bryce Boyer, Ruth M. Boyer, and Harry W. Basehart — combining psychiatric, psychological, and anthropological perspectives. Their fieldwork centered on the Mescalero Apache Reservation in southern New Mexico, home to three Apache groups: the Mescaleros, Chiricahuas, and Lipans. Peyote was used among the Mescalero from approximately 1870 until after 1910, when it was ultimately abandoned. The study provides a detailed account of why peyote — uniquely among all supernatural powers — came to be regarded as uniformly evil and was discarded by the community.

## Apache Supernatural Power Concepts

### The Nature of Power

In aboriginal Apache cosmology, the world is permeated by supernatural power (*biyáti* or *bijii*) that carries no intrinsic attribute of good or evil. The sole criterion of significance is potency — power's virtue resides entirely in its efficacy. This is a radically amoral ontology: power is neither benevolent nor malevolent in itself. Its moral character is determined exclusively by how it is wielded by the person who possesses it.

### Acquisition of Power

Power approaches individuals through a medium — a plant, animal, or natural phenomenon — by means of a dream or hallucinatory experience. Acceptance of the power is frequently accompanied by an ordeal, testing the recipient's resolve and worthiness. Any person is understood to be a possible recipient of power. The anthropologist Morris Opler characterized the Mescaleros as "a tribe of shamans, active or potentially active," reflecting the broadly distributed access to supernatural authority.

### Multiple Powers and Witchcraft

An individual might own any number of powers, each with its own associated rituals, songs, and instructions. The relationship between a person and their power is personal and idiosyncratic — there is no standardized body of shamanic knowledge, no canon of practice that all practitioners share. However, if a person uses power for purposes not oriented toward the common good, that person is classified as a witch. The boundary between shaman and witch is thus not a matter of the power itself but of its application. This creates a social landscape saturated with suspicion: shamans who save lives through curing ceremonies are themselves believed to incur a cost, sacrificing their own or a loved one's life in exchange. Jealousies, enmities, and mutual suspicion abound among practitioners.

Each shaman possesses private instructions and individually owned rites. There has never been a chief shaman among the Mescalero — the shamanistic system is fundamentally individualistic and decentralized, with no institutional hierarchy to mediate conflicts or standardize practice. This decentralization means that each practitioner operates in relative isolation, competing for clients and prestige without any overarching authority to resolve disputes or authenticate claims to power.

## Peyote Camp Structure and Practice

Peyote power was acquired through personal contact with power invested in peyote flowers or "buttons." Various shamans became leaders of peyote camps, organizing curing ceremonies centered on peyote ingestion. These camps were not informal gatherings but structured ceremonial settings in which peyote served as both the source of power and the medium of the curing rite.

The peyote camp leader directed the ceremony, but the nature of peyote itself — producing psychoactive effects in all who consumed it — created a fundamentally different social dynamic than that of ordinary shamanistic practice.

## The Fundamental Incongruity

### Individualistic vs. Communal Religious Experience

The core theoretical insight of the Boyer, Boyer, and Basehart analysis is the identification of a fundamental incongruity between two modes of Apache religious experience:

- **Ordinary shamanistic ceremonies**: A single shaman serves as the principal actor. Religious ecstasy — the direct experience of supernatural power — is his exclusive prerogative. The shaman's uniqueness and special status are central to the ceremony's meaning and efficacy.

- **Peyote rites**: The psychoactive effects of peyote are shared by all participants. The shaman's uniqueness disappears; every person present undergoes the experience of power. The shaman is no longer the sole intermediary with the supernatural.

This incongruity was socially destabilizing. Peyote meetings became places of rivalry and witchcraft rather than communal healing. Instead of producing social cohesiveness, peyote ceremonies generated disruption. The hostilities, rivalries, and accusations that emerged in the peyote camp context were ultimately ascribed to peyote itself.

## Peyote as Uniformly Evil Power

### A Unique Classification

Peyote power is unique among the Mescalero in being uniformly considered bad. This is remarkable precisely because the underlying Apache framework holds that power has no intrinsic moral valence — only potency matters. Peyote is the only power that, despite having no inherent attribute of good or evil, is nonetheless deemed entirely negative. Its classification represents an exceptional departure from the general ontological principle.

### The Process of Abandonment

During the 1959–60 study period, there were 13 accredited shamans and 15 pseudoshamans on the reservation. Of these, only one shaman — referred to as "Ancient One" — had ever participated in the peyote camp. This near-total disaffiliation underscores the completeness of peyote's delegitimization. The pseudoshamans who continued to use peyote were believed to have suffered "turning back" of the power, a concept denoting the reversal or punitive reversal of supernatural power against its user. Specific instances were cited: one pseudoshaman lost a leg, another had relatives killed. These outcomes were interpreted as confirming peyote's uniformly evil nature, reinforcing the community's collective decision to abandon peyote ceremonies entirely. The abandonment was thus not a response to external prohibition but to internally generated conclusions about peyote's inherent malevolence, arrived at through the accumulation of perceived evidence of its harmful effects.

## Apache Personality Structure

### Character Disorder Profile

The psychiatric dimension of the study produced a detailed personality profile of the Mescalero Apache, corresponding in Western diagnostic terms to a character disorder with hysterical and impulsive attributes. Key features include:

- **Impulse-ridden behavior**: Difficulty modulating drives and impulses, particularly aggressive and hostile urges.
- **Fear of loss of control**: Persistent anxiety about the consequences of uncontrolled hostile impulses.
- **Suggestibility**: Heightened susceptibility to influence, including supernatural beliefs.
- **Phobic tendencies**: Avoidance patterns linked to fear and anxiety.
- **Avoidance of introspection**: Resistance to self-examination or psychological insight.
- **Suspiciousness**: Pervasive mistrust, consistent with the cultural emphasis on witchcraft and envy.
- **Dependency**: Reliance on others for emotional and practical support, coupled with insecurity.
- **Unstable libidinal attachments**: Difficulty maintaining consistent and secure interpersonal bonds.

Men were described as caught between passive and aggressive urges with insecure sexual identities, reflecting the tensions inherent in the socialization process.

### Socialization Practices

Apache child-rearing practices were identified as producing this personality configuration. Key features include:

- **Gross inconsistency in maternal care**: Caregiving is unpredictable, creating insecure attachment patterns.
- **Abrupt displacement of children**: The birth of a sibling leads to sudden withdrawal of maternal attention, generating intense feelings of rejection and rivalry.
- **Intense but disapproved sibling rivalry**: Competition between siblings is fierce but culturally condemned, creating internal conflict.
- **Feeble repression of hostilities**: Aggressive impulses receive weak social containment, leading to poorly regulated hostility.

In the aboriginal nomadic situation, these hostilities were externalized toward outsiders, ghosts, and witches, which had the paradoxical effect of strengthening group solidarity. Under reservation conditions, however, the targets of externalized aggression shifted inward, resulting in anomie, self-destruction, and interpersonal conflict within the community.

## Shamans vs. Pseudoshamans

### The Distinction

The study drew a clear empirical distinction between accredited shamans and pseudoshamans on the reservation. During the study period, 13 shamans and 15 pseudoshamans were identified. The critical difference lay not in the personality structure — both groups shared the typical Apache character disorder profile — but in the presence or absence of specific psychological capacities necessary for successful [[amazonian-boa-spirits-and-water-beings-in-shamanic-practice]].

### Psychological Requirements for Shamanism

The authors proposed that successful shamans are distinguished by two key psychological capacities:

1. **Creative potential**: Shamans possess greater creative ability than the general Apache population. This creativity manifests in the composition of songs, the innovation of rituals, and the theatrical performance of curing ceremonies. It also enables the effective use of imposture — the deliberate construction of convincing shamanic performances.

2. **Regression in the service of the ego**: Drawing on Ernst Kris's (1952) psychoanalytic formulation, the authors argued that shamans possess the capacity to regress to primary process thinking — the mode of thought characterized by illogical, associative, and hallucinatory content — while maintaining ego control. This ego-controlled availability of primary process material is related both to creativity and to the showmanship necessary for successful shamanism. The shaman can enter altered [[mckenna-food-gods-designer-states-of-consciousness]], experience visions, and communicate with supernatural entities, yet return to oriented functioning and direct the ceremonial performance.

Pseudoshamans, by contrast, lacked both the creative potentials and the capacity for regression in the service of the ego. They could neither produce convincing ceremonial innovations nor maintain the ego control necessary to integrate visionary experiences into effective practice.

### Not Autocultural Deviants

Importantly, the authors argued that shamans are not autocultural deviants who resolved pre-existing psychopathology through the shamanic role. Instead, shamans share the typical Apache personality configuration but are distinguished by their enhanced creative capacity and their ability to channel primary process thinking in controlled, socially productive ways. The shamanic role does not cure psychopathology; it provides a cultural framework within which specific psychological capacities — creativity and controlled regression — can be expressed and valued.

## Theoretical Significance

The Mescalero Apache case offers several contributions to the comparative study of shamanism and hallucinogen use:

- It demonstrates that the adoption or rejection of a hallucinogenic substance is not determined solely by its [[lsd-discovery-aftermath-pharmacological-properties]] but by the fit between the social dynamics the substance produces and the pre-existing structure of religious authority.
- It illustrates how a fundamentally amoral ontology of power can nonetheless produce a moral classification — peyote as uniformly evil — when the substance disrupts established [[patterns-of-social-hierarchy]] and religious organization.
- It provides empirical support for the psychoanalytic theory that shamanic capacity depends on specific psychological functions (creativity and controlled regression) rather than on psychopathology per se.
- It shows how changes in social conditions — specifically the transition from nomadic to reservation life — can alter the functional consequences of personality structures that were adaptive in the original [[ayahuasca-pharmacology-and-cultural-context]].
- It challenges the common assumption that hallucinogen use in indigenous societies invariably produces social cohesion, demonstrating instead that communal visionary experience can intensify pre-existing rivalries when it undermines individualized authority structures.

The case thus underscores the importance of analyzing hallucinogenic religious practices within their specific sociocultural and psychological matrices rather than treating them as self-explanatory phenomena.
