Key species:
- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium**: Most studied; effective for many azo, anthraquinone, and triphenylmethane dyes
- **Trametes versicolor**: Broad dye decolorization capability; high laccase production
- **Pleurotus ostreatus**: Effective decolorization; edible mushroom enabling safe biomass disposal
- **Bjerkandera adusta**: High manganese peroxidase production; effective for recalcitrant dyes
- **Coriolus versicolor**: Strong decolorization of textile effluents

### Mechanisms of Dye Decolorization

1. **Biosorption**: Dye molecules adsorb to fungal cell wall components (chitin, glucans) without chemical transformation. Rapid process; effective for initial color removal but does not destroy dye molecules

2. **Enzymatic degradation**: Ligninolytic enzymes chemically transform dye molecules:
   - **Laccase**: Oxidizes phenolic and non-phenolic dye structures (with mediators); produces phenoxy radicals that undergo non-enzymatic coupling, polymerization, or ring cleavage
   - **Manganese peroxidase**: Generates Mn(III) chelates that oxidize dye molecules
   - **Lignin peroxidase**: Direct one-electron oxidation of dye molecules with high redox potential

3. **Combined biosorption and biodegradation**: Initial rapid biosorption followed by slower enzymatic degradation of adsorbed dyes

## Metabolic Products and Pathways

### Azo Dye Degradation
- Azo bond cleavage produces aromatic amines (toxic intermediates)
- Further oxidation by ligninolytic enzymes converts amines to less toxic products
- Complete mineralization to CO2 achievable for many azo dyes by white-rot fungi

### Anthraquinone Dye Degradation
- Initial oxidation of aromatic rings
- Ring opening and formation of smaller organic acids
- Eventual mineralization possible

### Triphenylmethane Dye Degradation
- N-demethylation of triphenylmethane structure
- Oxidative cleavage producing smaller aromatic compounds
- Malachite green degradation products include malachite green carbinol and leucomalachite green

## Factors Affecting Decolorization

### Media Composition
- Carbon source: Required for fungal growth and enzyme production; glucose most commonly used
- Nitrogen: Nitrogen limitation enhances ligninolytic enzyme production in some white-rot fungi
- C/N ratio: Optimal ratio varies between species; typically 20-40 for ligninolytic activity

### Culture Conditions
- Static vs. agitated: Static conditions generally promote better ligninolytic enzyme production and dye decolorization; agitated conditions favor biomass production
- pH: Optimal range typically pH 4-6 for white-rot fungi
- Temperature: 25-30 C optimal for most species

### Initial Dye Concentration
- Higher concentrations may inhibit fungal growth and enzyme activity
- Many white-rot fungi tolerate and decolorize dyes at concentrations up to 200-500 mg/L
- Sequential or multi-stage treatment enables handling of higher concentrations

## Enzyme-Based Decolorization

### Laccase-Mediator Systems
Laccase alone cannot oxidize high-redox-potential dyes. Mediators extend the substrate range:
- **Natural mediators**: Syringaldehyde, acetosyringone, vanillin (from lignin degradation)
- **Synthetic mediators**: ABTS (2,2'-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)), HBT (1-hydroxybenzotriazole)
- Laccase-HBT system effective for decolorization of anthraquinone, azo, and triphenylmethane dyes

### Peroxidase Systems
- Manganese peroxidase with Mn(II) and organic acid chelators (malonate, lactate)
- Lignin peroxidase with veratryl alcohol as mediator
- Horseradish peroxidase (HRP) for treatment of phenolic dye intermediates

### Bioreactors for Enzymatic Treatment
- Immobilized laccase in packed-bed reactors
- Membrane reactors for continuous enzyme treatment
- Enzyme recycling systems with ultrafiltration

## Agricultural Relevance

While primarily an industrial concern, dye contamination affects agricultural environments through:
- Irrigation with contaminated water: Dyes can accumulate in soil, affect soil microbial communities, and potentially be taken up by crops
- Textile sludge application: Sludge from dye treatment may contain dye residues; composting with white-rot fungi can degrade residual dyes
- Wetland treatment: Constructed wetlands with fungal-augmented substrates can treat agricultural runoff containing dye contaminants
