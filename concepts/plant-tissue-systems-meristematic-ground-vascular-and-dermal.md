# Plant Tissue Systems: Meristematic, Ground, Vascular, and Dermal

Plants are organized into four types of tissue, each composed of specialized cells that carry out distinct functions. All plant cells start as undifferentiated meristematic cells, which then differentiate into the three mature tissue types: ground, vascular, and dermal. Understanding these tissue systems is essential for understanding how plants grow, transport nutrients, and protect themselves.

## Meristematic Tissue

Meristematic tissue is the source of all new cells. Meristematic cells are small, undifferentiated, and capable of division -- a trait most plant cells lose at maturity. They have large nuclei, small vacuoles, thin [[alpha-1-3-glucan-fungal-pathogen-cell-walls]], and are closely packed together with undeveloped cytoplasm.

### Apical Meristems

Located at the tips of stems (shoot apical meristem) and just above root tips (root apical meristem). These produce cells for primary growth (increasing height and root length). Growth mostly occurs through [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]] rather than cell division.

Hormones signal shoot meristem tissue to produce cells in one direction (upward), while root meristem tissue grows in two directions (downward and outward).

### Lateral Meristems

Responsible for secondary growth (increasing diameter), producing growth rings in woody plants. Not all plants have lateral meristems -- monocots (maize, grasses) lack them.

### The Differentiation Process

When a meristematic cell receives a signal, an extraordinary transformation begins:
- RNA copies DNA instructions from the nucleus
- Messenger RNA constructs proteins in ribosomes (on the endoplasmic reticulum or free in cytosol)
- Proteins transfer to the Golgi apparatus for packaging
- Proteins move to where needed -- plasmalemma, other cell parts, or other plant locations
- The vacuole grows, mitochondria provide energy, microtubules and actin fibers form a transit system

Each meristematic cell is capable of cloning the entire plant. All ground, vascular, and dermal tissues -- all leaves, stems, and roots -- come from meristematic tissue.

## Ground Tissue

Ground tissue provides most of the plant's mass and support, and serves as the site for photosynthesis, food storage, protection, and regeneration. Three cell types:

### Parenchyma Cells

The most common ground tissue cells. They have thin primary walls and large vacuoles, closely resembling the typical plant cell. They retain the ability to divide (enabling regeneration) and can become more specialized. Functions include photosynthesis, storage, and metabolism. Found abundantly in leaves, roots, fruit flesh, and tubers like potatoes.

### Collenchyma Cells

Form bundles and strands (the strings in celery) that provide flexible support. Lacking secondary walls and lignin, they allow plants to bend gently in wind. They are living cells that provide [[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]] without rigidity.

### Sclerenchyma Cells

Develop lignified secondary walls and cannot elongate. Most die at maturity, leaving hollow lignin shells. Sclereids (a type of sclerenchyma cell) form hard coatings on seeds, nut shells, and the gritty skin of pears.

## Vascular Tissue

The transport system of the plant, consisting of [[xylem-and-phloem-transport-systems-in-plants]] running side by side throughout the plant, separated by the vascular cambium (lateral meristem tissue).

### Xylem

Transports water and dissolved nutrient ions upward from roots to leaves. A one-way system of specialized cells:

- **Tracheids**: Dominant in gymnosperms (conifers, cycads, ginkgoes). Tapered cells with pitted end walls that overlap. About the width of a human hair.
- **Vessel elements**: Dominant in hardwoods. Connected end-to-end with perforated end walls. About six times wider than tracheids (500 microns), forming vessels up to 10 cm long.

Both types have lignin-strengthened walls. Xylem cells die at maturity but lignin preserves their shape and function, creating a waterproof, unidirectional plumbing system.

Water moves through xylem without energy expenditure, driven by transpiration pull, water cohesion, and adhesion to xylem walls. Speed: up to 30 meters per hour.

### Phloem

Transports sugars, amino acids, RNA, and other synthesized compounds. Operates bidirectionally (not simultaneously) through living sieve elements connected in vertical stacks.

- **Source-to-sink movement**: Sap flows from areas of high sugar concentration (leaves in summer, roots in spring) to areas of [[ultra-low-concentration-brassinosteroid-efficacy-hormetic-dose-response-fungi]]
- **Hydrostatic pressure**: Unlike xylem, phloem movement depends on pressure, not transpiration. Sugar loading creates [[fungal-fruiting-body-mechanics-osmotic-pressure]] that pushes sap through the system
- **Companion cells**: Each sieve element pairs with a companion cell that performs functions for its missing nucleus and organelles

## Dermal Tissue

The plant's protective skin, keeping water in and defending against injury.

### Epidermis

A single layer of cells covering all plant surfaces (flowers, leaves, stems, roots, seeds). Usually transparent and lacking chloroplasts. Produces cutin, a waxy substance forming a cuticle that prevents water loss. Contains:

- **Stomata**: Pores with guard cells that regulate [[mushroom-cultivation-jar-lid-management-loose-fit-vacuum-gas-exchange]] (CO2 in, H2O and O2 out)
- **Trichomes**: Hair-like structures that aid transpiration and some produce anti-herbivore compounds
