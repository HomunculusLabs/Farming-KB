## Shared Mechanism: Water Vapor and Evaporative Demand

### The Johnson and Gamow Finding (1971)

Johnson and Gamow demonstrated that wind and **water vapor** are both necessary for the anemotropic and avoidance responses in *Phycomyces*. Their key findings:

- Sporangiophores grow toward regions of greater water vapor (higher humidity).
- Wind creates asymmetric humidity gradients around the sporangiophore.
- The growth response is to the humidity differential, not to the mechanical force of the wind itself.
- Removing water vapor from the system abolishes the anemotropic response.

### Application to Psilocybe cubensis

Badham proposed that the same water-vapor-based mechanism explains Phase 1 anemotropism in *P. cubensis*:

- Wind redistributes the boundary layer around the stipe, creating a thicker (more humid) boundary layer on the leeward side.
- Cells on the windward side experience greater drying (higher evaporative demand) than cells on the leeward side.
- Differential cell expansion rates on opposite sides of the stipe produce curvature toward the wind.
- This is essentially the same mechanism as *Phycomyces* anemotropism, operating on a larger structure.

### Gravitropic Sensitivity

Dennison (1961) showed that *Phycomyces* sporangiophore growth responds to both gravity and centrifugal force, similar to how higher plants respond to gravitational stimuli. *P. cubensis* shows [[mushroom-negative-geotropism]] only during Phase 2, and this response is coupled to spore production — a feature not described in *Phycomyces*.

## The Ethylene Hypothesis

### Russo et al. (1977) and Phycomyces

Russo, Halloran, and Gallori proposed that **ethylene** may be involved in the autochemotropic (avoidance) response of *Phycomyces blakesleeanus*. Ethylene is a gaseous plant hormone involved in:

- Fruit ripening
- Leaf abscission
- Stress responses
- Growth regulation
