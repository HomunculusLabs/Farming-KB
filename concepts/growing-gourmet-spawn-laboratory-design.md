# Spawn [[stamets-sterile-laboratory-design]] and Clean Room Principles

The design of a spawn laboratory is critical to successful
[[contamination-mushroom-cultivation]]. Whether retrofitting a home room or
building a dedicated facility, specific design principles
dramatically reduce contamination and improve success rates.
For [[growing-gourmet-autoclave-retort-selection]], see that companion page.

## HEPA and ULPA Filtration

HEPA (High Efficiency Particulate Air) filters eliminate
particulates down to 0.3 microns with an efficiency rating
of 99.99%. ULPA (Ultra-Particulate Air) filters screen out
particles down to 0.1 microns with 99.9999% efficiency. A
new generation of micron filters, the ULPA filters, provide
even greater protection than HEPA alone.

The use of HEPA filters makes a home laboratory quite
functional for the small and mid-size cultivator. Once
airborne contamination is eliminated, the other vectors of
contamination become much easier to control.

## Positive Pressurization

The laboratory should be continuously positive-pressurized
with fresh air. The fresh outside air is serially filtered:

1. First through a coarse pre-filter (30% efficient at
   1 micron)
2. Then an electrostatic filter (95-99% efficient at
   10 microns)
3. Finally a HEPA filter (99.99% efficient at 0.3 microns)

Blowers must be properly sized to overcome the cumulative
static pressures of all the filters. In most cases, the
combined static pressure approaches 1.25 inches. Air
velocity off the face of the final filter should be at
least 200 feet per minute. For a 400 sq. ft. clean room,
2 ft. x 2 ft. x 6 in. filters should be employed.

Fresh air exchange is essential to displace the carbon
dioxide and other gases being generated by the mushroom
mycelium during incubation. Sensitive cultivators can
determine the quality of the laboratory immediately upon
entering using their sense of smell.

## Turbulent vs. Still Air

A strong belief in creating a laboratory characterized by
turbulent air streams with a high rate of impact through
micron filters is recommended. Turbulent, filtered air in
the laboratory is far more desirable than a still air
environment.

If airborne particles are introduced, they are kept
airborne from turbulence. If kept airborne, particles are
soon impacted into the micron filters. This reduces the
stratification of contaminant populations and temperature
variations in the laboratory. This concept is diametrically
opposite to the "old school" concept that still air is the
ideal environment for handling pure cultures.

## Stand-Alone Laminar Flow Bench

A laminar flow bench constantly recirculates the air within
the laboratory. The air entering the laboratory has been
already filtered from the positive pressurization system.
By having two independent systems, the lifespan of the
filter in the laminar flow bench is greatly extended.

A home-made laminar flow bench measuring 2 ft. high by
12 ft. long gives ample freedom of hand movement and
surface area. Fresh outside air is serially filtered and
positive-pressurizes the lab from overhead through a coarse
pre-filter, an electrostatic filter, and finally a 24 x 24
HEPA filter.

## Multi-Door Entry System

There should be at least two doors, preferably three,
separating the clean room from the outside environment.
Double door entries are standard in the industry. Doors
with windows have obvious advantages in preventing
accidents. Furthermore, the doors should be gasketed with
dirt skirts.

When doors swing outwards as you exit the innermost clean
room, the export of mature spawn or blocks is made easier.
As workers travel towards the clean room, they enter rooms
hygienically cleaner than the previous, and into
increasingly higher pressure zones.

Floor decontamination pads, otherwise known as "sticky
mats," are usually placed before each doorway to remove
debris from feet.

## Decontamination Chambers

With ultra-modern clean rooms, a double door anteroom
called a "Decontamination Chamber" utilizes the down-flow
of HEPA filtered air over the worker who stands on a metal
grate. The air is pushed from above and actively exhausted
through the floor grate to the outside. The constant
descension of airborne particulates improves laboratory
integrity.

## Separation of Laboratory and Growing Rooms

If the laboratory and growing rooms are housed in the same
building, contamination is much more likely. Since the
activities within the laboratory and growing rooms are
distinctly different, separate buildings are preferred.
The [[mushroom-pests-insect-control-stamets]] strategies described by Stamets are essential
for keeping both laboratory and growing rooms clean.

The positioning of growing room exhaust fans should be
oriented so as not to direct a "spore stream of
contaminants" into the laboratory filtration system. The
design of a mushroom farm's buildings should take into
account prevailing wind direction, sunlight exposure,
shade, and the overall flow patterns of raw materials and
finished goods.

## Humidity and Temperature Control

A laboratory run at 50% relative humidity and 75 degrees F
(24 degrees C) prevents condensation from forming on the
upper inside of petri dishes. These water droplets carry
otherwise-dormant contaminants down to the rich media.
Bacteria particularly love condensation surfaces.

Temperature fluctuations cause the outer walls of the
laboratory, especially those made of concrete, to sweat,
potentially growing contaminant colonies.
