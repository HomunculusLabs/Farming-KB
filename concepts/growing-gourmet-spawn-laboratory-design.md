## HEPA and ULPA Filtration

HEPA (High Efficiency Particulate Air) filters eliminate
particulates down to 0.3 microns with an efficiency rating
of 99.99%. ULPA (Ultra-Particulate Air) filters screen out
particles down to 0.1 microns with 99.9999% efficiency. A
new generation of micron filters, the ULPA filters, provide
even greater protection than HEPA alone.

The use of HEPA filters makes a home laboratory quite
functional for the small and mid-size cultivator. Once
airborne contamination is eliminated, the other vectors of
contamination become much easier to control.

## Positive Pressurization

The laboratory should be continuously positive-pressurized
with fresh air. The fresh outside air is serially filtered:

1. First through a coarse pre-filter (30% efficient at
   1 micron)
2. Then an electrostatic filter (95-99% efficient at
   10 microns)
3. Finally a HEPA filter (99.99% efficient at 0.3 microns)

Blowers must be properly sized to overcome the cumulative
static pressures of all the filters. In most cases, the
combined static pressure approaches 1.25 inches. Air
velocity off the face of the final filter should be at
least 200 feet per minute. For a 400 sq. ft. clean room,
2 ft. x 2 ft. x 6 in. filters should be employed.

Fresh air exchange is essential to displace the carbon
dioxide and other gases being generated by the mushroom
[[mushroom-pests-insect-control-stamets]] strategies described by Stamets are essential
for keeping both laboratory and growing rooms clean.

The positioning of growing room exhaust fans should be
oriented so as not to direct a "[[spore]] stream of
contaminants" into the laboratory filtration system. The
design of a mushroom farm's buildings should take into
account prevailing wind direction, sunlight exposure,
shade, and the overall flow patterns of raw materials and
finished goods.

## Humidity and Temperature Control

A laboratory run at 50% relative humidity and 75 degrees F
(24 degrees C) prevents condensation from forming on the
upper inside of petri dishes. These water droplets carry
otherwise-dormant contaminants down to the rich media.
Bacteria particularly love condensation surfaces.

Temperature fluctuations cause the outer walls of the
laboratory, especially those made of concrete, to sweat,
potentially growing contaminant colonies.
