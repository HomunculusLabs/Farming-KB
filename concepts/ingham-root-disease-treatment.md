# Root Disease Treatment - AACT Protocols

Extracted from Elaine Ingham's AACT Field Guide, covering root disease
suppression using [[ingham-aact-disease-suppression]] and biological
approaches.

## The Mycorrhizal Connection

The data collected by SFI shows that when VAM (vesicular-arbuscular
mycorrhizal) colonization is less than 40%, root disease problems, root-
feeding nematodes, and other root pests appear. Once VAM or ecto-
mycorrhizal colonization gets above 30 to 40%, these root problems begin
to go away.

If colonization is less than 10 to 15%, an inoculum of mycorrhizal fungi
will be needed. Inocula are available from EPM, Mycorrhizal Applications,
and other suppliers.

See [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] for background on mycorrhizal biology.

## Root Rot Diseases

Fungi must be brought back into the soil in order to suppress root rot
diseases. This requires:

1. Adequate fungal biomass in compost
2. Adequate fungal extraction into the tea
3. Proper oxygen maintenance (above 5.5-6 ppm) to keep fungi alive
4. Proper application to get fungi into the root zone

The benefits of restoring fungal dominance go beyond root rot suppression:
- Holding calcium, nitrogen, and phosphorus
- Building soil structure for 30-50% water use reduction
- Supporting the complete [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]]

## Banana Root Rot and Root Borer (Australia)

A case study with an organic banana grower in Australia showed that
compost and AACT worked well on:

- Root borer
- Root rots
- Weeds (couch grass replaced by ryegrass)

The grower achieved ryegrass establishment in the banana plantation
instead of couch grass -- a significant shift in the weed spectrum
indicating improved soil biology.

## Brown Rot (Cherries)

No replicated data on brown rot, but AACT has done a good job in
practice. The recommendation is to make the tea as fungal as you can
manage.

## The Pathogen Triad

The primary root disease organisms combated by healthy soil biology:

- **Pythium** -- causes wilts
- **Phytophthora** -- causes blights and mildews
- **Rhizoctonia** -- causes root rots

These organisms are naturally present in most soils but are kept in check
by the competition from beneficial organisms. When pesticides kill the
beneficials, these pathogens proliferate.

The impact of pesticides is not so much a direct burn of plant tissue as
a slow decline in soil health as you lose the organisms that combat these
pathogens.

## Anaerobic Conditions and Root Disease

Anaerobic soil conditions create the perfect environment for root
diseases:

1. High nitrate concentrations from inorganic fertilizers
2. Undigested sugars, proteins, and short-chain organic acids
3. Compacted soil limiting oxygen diffusion to roots
4. Loss of beneficial fungal biomass

The conditions are perfectly balanced for fusarium, and other blights,
wilts and root rots. Turning anaerobic conditions around requires
getting a soil sample to determine the biology present, then applying the
appropriate biology through compost and AACT.

## Trichoderma for Root Disease

[[ingham-trichoderma-biocontrol-fungi-aact]] does not follow
along the root the way a mycorrhizal fungus does. Thus, with Trichoderma,
you need to get the fungus growing through the soil, so a soil drench
works better, or addition to the soil of a compost to which you have
added T-22.

## Laminated Root Rot (Douglas-fir)

Ingham's published research includes work on laminated root rot in
Douglas-fir forests, studying soil foodweb responses following
chloropicrin application. This research documented how biological
approaches can replace fumigant treatments for root disease control in
forestry systems.

## Treatment Protocol Summary

1. Test soil biology to identify gaps
2. If VAM colonization below 10-15%, apply mycorrhizal inoculum
3. Apply fungal-dominant AACT as soil drench
4. Add fungal foods to support establishment
5. Reduce compaction to allow oxygen penetration
6. Stop applying pesticides that kill beneficial fungi
7. Monitor over time -- expect 6+ months for significant root-feeder
   reduction

## Key Takeaways

- VAM colonization below 40% correlates with root disease problems
- Fungal-dominant AACT is essential for root rot suppression
- Pythium, Phytophthora, and Rhizoctonia are the primary root pathogens
- Anaerobic soil conditions favor root disease organisms
- Pesticides cause slow decline by removing competing beneficial organisms
- Trichoderma requires soil drench (doesn't follow roots like mycorrhizae)
- Root-feeder reduction from ~100/gram to undetectable achievable in 6
  months with full foodweb restoration
