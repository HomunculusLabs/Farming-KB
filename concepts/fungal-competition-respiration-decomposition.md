## Species Interactions on Straw

Robinson et al. (1993a) studied colonization patterns of individual straw resource units
by multiple fungal species and correlated these patterns to relative growth rates of the
fungi on agar. These rates allowed species to be ranked in combative order. Mixtures of
fungal species caused significant reductions in the rate of growth of less combative
fungal species in the presence of combative species, demonstrating that competitive
hierarchies influence both community structure and decomposition dynamics.

The cascade of decomposition is thus related to colonization of a substrate by fungi
based on their enzymatic competence in relation to available chemical resources and the
outcome of interactions with other potential colonizers. The initial species composition
on a resource unit can therefore determine the long-term trajectory of decomposition.

## Interactions Between Mycorrhizal and Saprotrophic Fungi

There is evidence of both antagonism and facilitation between mycorrhizal and
saprotrophic fungi. Moller et al. (1999) showed that the cellulolytic fungus Humicola sp.
caused double the carbon utilization from beech leaves when decomposing alone compared to
in combination with a mixed inoculum of soil bacteria, suggesting that interspecific
interactions can reduce decomposition efficiency.

Ectomycorrhizal and ericaceous mycorrhizal fungi can compete with saprotrophic fungi for
organic nitrogen and phosphorus in forest soils. These competitive interactions may slow
overall decomposition rates, contributing to the accumulation of soil organic matter in
boreal and heathland ecosystems. The balance between mycorrhizal and saprotrophic
activity therefore influences whether ecosystems function as net carbon sources or sinks.

## Faunal Modulation of Competitive Interactions

Soil fauna that graze on fungi can modulate competitive outcomes among fungal species. By
selectively grazing certain species, collembola and nematodes can alter the relative
abundance of competing fungi, effectively reducing competitive intensity and potentially
increasing overall decomposition efficiency. Lussenhop and Wicklow (1985) demonstrated
that the introduction of mycophagous fly larvae to complex fungal communities on rabbit
dung increased decomposition rates, likely by reducing the energy expended on
inter-fungal combat.

## Implications for Carbon Budgets

The energetic costs of fungal competition have important implications for ecosystem
carbon budgets. In systems with diverse fungal communities, a greater proportion of
decomposed carbon may be released as carbon dioxide through competition-driven
respiration rather than being incorporated into fungal biomass or stable soil organic
matter. This effect may partially explain why decomposition efficiency (mass loss per
unit respiration) varies among ecosystems with different levels of fungal diversity.
