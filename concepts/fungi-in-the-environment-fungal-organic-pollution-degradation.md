# Fungal Organic Pollution Degradation

Fungi possess remarkable capabilities for transforming and degrading organic pollutants, making them important agents in bioremediation. Their filamentous growth habit, metabolic versatility, and ability to translocate resources through mycelial networks position them uniquely for degrading contaminants in heterogeneous environments where other organisms may fail.

## Fungi as Bioremediation Agents

Fungi transform toxic metals and are important factors in bioremediation. As nutrient recyclers, biocontrol agents and bioremediation agents, fungi grow in environments exhibiting spatio-temporal nutritional and structural heterogeneity. Their mycelial networks can translocate resources between different regions of the mycelium. This means that hyphae growing through nutritionally impoverished zones or deleterious regions -- including localized deposits of organic pollutants, toxic metals, dry or waterlogged zones -- can be supplemented by resources imported from distal regions of the mycelium.

This ability to translocate nutrients has profound implications for growth and functioning of mycelia and attendant effects upon the environment in which they grow. Mathematical modelling combined with experimental data can yield considerable insight into the functional consequences of fungal bioremediation activities. Modelling is predicted to play a central role in the successful application of fungi to biotechnological areas such as biocontrol and bioremediation, as well as providing better understanding of their significant roles in environmental biogeochemistry.

## Mycelial Translocation in Contaminated Environments

A key advantage of filamentous fungi in pollution degradation is their ability to grow through contaminated zones while maintaining physiological function through resource importation from uncontaminated or less contaminated areas. This is particularly important because:

- Contaminated zones often lack readily available nutrients, being locked in recalcitrant material
- Toxic conditions may inhibit local metabolism, including enzyme production
- The mycelial network provides a continuous transport system for carbon and energy
- Hyphae can grow out of one resource to search for others (non-resource-unit restricted growth)
- Different regions of the mycelium can simultaneously perform different functions

Cord-forming basidiomycetes at the soil-litter interface exemplify this capability. They interconnect disparate litter components and provide most examples of non-resource-unit restricted growth. Their mycelial networks are continuously re-modelled in response to local nutritional or environmental cues through a complex combination of growth, branching, hyphal fusion and regression of different regions of the mycelial system.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi and Pollutant Interactions

### Plant Protection Mechanisms

Interactions between mycorrhizas and metal pollution have raised increased interest because of the potential of mycorrhizas to:

- Enhance plant growth on contaminated soils
- Remediate and remove pollutants
- Protect host plants from metal toxicity
- Enable re-vegetation and re-forestation of degraded sites

Ectomycorrhizal mycobionts filter toxic metals through multiple mechanisms:
- Sorption in the hyphal sheath or Hartig net
- Restriction of metal mobility due to hydrophobicity of the fungal sheath
- Complexation of metals by released organic acids and other substances
- Arbuscular mycorrhizas reduce toxic metal (e.g., Zn) uptake by plants

### Metal-Resistant Symbionts

Most studies demonstrating amelioration of metal toxicity to host plants are most efficient when the mycobiont was metal resistant. Such associations could be used in re-vegetation and re-forestation programmes as well as in phyto- and rhizo-remediation strategies. However, these mycorrhizal interactions are variable and context-dependent, with outcomes varying in response to biotic and abiotic factors.

### Phytoremediation Applications

When mycorrhizas enhance accumulation of toxic metals in host plants, this may be relevant for phytoextraction techniques. Zinc-tolerant Paxillus involutus employed a toxic metal avoidance strategy under phosphorus-replete conditions but mobilized and accumulated the highest amount of zinc under phosphorus deficiency, demonstrating the importance of environmental context in remediation outcomes.

Under phosphorus-replete conditions, total zinc mobilization from zinc phosphate and zinc accumulation in pines was least for the ectomycorrhizal association with a zinc-tolerant strain. Under phosphorus deficiency, zinc-tolerant ectomycorrhiza mobilized and accumulated the highest amount of zinc. This demonstrated that zinc phosphate dissolution and host plant protection against zinc was strongly dependent on the phosphorus status of the matrix.

## Degradation of Specific Pollutant Classes

### Metal-Contaminated Soils

In metal-contaminated soils, fungal mineral-solubilizing activity may affect chemical remediation methods. Phosphate-induced metal stabilization (PIMS), which leads to increased deposition of toxic metal phosphates (hopeite, pyromorphite), has been proposed as a cost-effective remediation technology. However, fungi may transform the final mineral products of such remediation. Assessment of long-term environmental consequences of chemical remediation technologies should therefore take into account the biogeochemical activity of soil fungal communities.

### Organic Pollutants and Decomposition

The mycelial growth habit enables fungi to penetrate soil matrices and reach pollutants that are inaccessible to bacteria. Fungi growing through zones containing organic pollutants can be supplemented by resources imported from distal mycelial regions, maintaining metabolic activity in otherwise energetically unfavourable conditions. Saprotrophic basidiomycetes decompose lignin, one of the most recalcitrant natural organic compounds, using oxidative enzyme systems that can also attack many xenobiotic pollutants.

### Saltmarsh Decomposition Systems

Fungal communities in salt marshes demonstrate temporal and spatial dynamics in organic matter decomposition. Molecular studies using T-RFLP to investigate fungal communities in southeastern US salt marshes found considerable temporal variability with little to moderate spatial variability. Dominant decomposer fungi (Phaeosphaeria spartinicola, P. halima, Mycosphaerella sp.) were present across all samples collected over one year, suggesting persistent roles in organic matter processing.

Ascomycetous laccase genes have been identified in salt marsh decomposition systems, indicating a role for fungal oxidative enzymes in the degradation of complex organic matter in coastal ecosystems. Laccases are copper-containing oxidases capable of degrading a wide range of phenolic compounds and are potentially important for degradation of anthropogenic pollutants including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons.

## Challenges and Considerations

- Fungal degradation of remediation products may undermine long-term stability of chemical treatments
- Mycorrhizal interactions with pollutants are variable and context-dependent, requiring site-specific assessment
- Only approximately 5% of environmental fungi can be cultivated, limiting study of degradation potential
- Mathematical modelling is needed to predict functional consequences of fungal bioremediation strategies
- Success depends on matching fungal capabilities to specific contaminant profiles and environmental conditions
- Understanding the synergistic effects of combined organic and metal contamination on fungal degradation capacity remains a research priority
