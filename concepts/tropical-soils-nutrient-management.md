# Tropical Soils and Nutrient Management

## Overview

Tropical soils present unique challenges [[mushroom-cultivation-for-sustainable-agriculture]]. Unlike temperate soils that are renewed by glaciation, most tropical soils are ancient, deeply weathered, and heavily leached. Understanding their chemistry and developing appropriate [[forest-management-strategies]] is essential for any [[cation-exchange-capacity]] (CEC) — the soil's ability to hold nutrients against leaching — is directly affected by pH. As pH increases (becomes more alkaline), the negative charge on soil particles increases, improving their ability to retain calcium, sodium, and phosphates. This relationship between pH and CEC is a critical factor in [[tropical-soil-management-and-restoration]].

## Strategies for Restoring Tropical Soil Fertility

### Humus Restoration

Humus provides a good CEC and must be a priority. Key approaches include:

1. **Perennial legumes:** Species such as *Leucaena* and other tree legumes are the primary tools for maintaining soil health. Any cultivation loses humus as carbon dioxide, so intercropping is preferred over monoculture.

2. **Frequent light fertiliser applications:** Small quantities of superphosphate applied at frequent intervals allow plants to absorb nutrients before leaching occurs. Light applications at 6-week intervals are recommended until plants are well established.

3. **[[cervantes-organic-mineral-amendments]]:** Fine crushed basalt, cement powder, shredded bamboo, and cane mulches supply silica and calcium. Increasing pH with lime after trees and green crops are growing well further enhances nutrient retention.

4. **Perennial over annual crops:** Substituting perennial for annual crops reduces soil disturbance and humus loss. Frequent cultivation should never be practised.

### Coral Cay Soils

On coral cays, calcium-rich sands bind with phosphate to form insoluble calcium triphosphate, creating a sort of cement (platin or calcrete). Superphosphate accelerates this process due to its greater solubility. Fine [[rock-phosphate]], which releases more slowly, provides better long-term benefits on calcium-rich tropical soils. Returning crop wastes as mulch can reduce pH in coral sands from 8–9 to 6.5–7, which is suitable for gardens.

### Volcanic Soils

In areas with recent vulcanism or volcanic dust deposits (such as Indonesia), soils are sufficiently rich to sustain intensive agriculture. However, constant cropping will eventually exhaust even these soils. The alkaline volcanic soils of Indonesia support rich terrace and palm polyculture systems, and many tropical high-island soils where dolomite tops or forms a mosaic with recent volcanics support excellent gardens with mulch-manure mixes.

## Managing Deep Granitic Sands

In soils over rotted granites — found on high islands of the Indian Ocean, the Deccan in India, and where granites form inselbergs (domed hills) — a peculiar problem arises: open, coarse granitic sands, often very deep, will not retain mulch beyond one growing season.

### Broadscale Approach

1. Plant palms (*Albizzia* spp., *Inga* spp., *Acacia* spp.) and adapted leguminous trees with small, regular nutrient additions
2. Establish a light canopy of leaves; plant palms in mulch-filled pits for high crown cover
3. Use legume detritus to establish valuable fruit trees while retaining a complete root web of legumes
4. Utilise palm trunks as trellis for vanilla and passionfruit
5. Plant in niches and clefts in the granite mass itself — vines will cover granite slabs, which maintain heat and ripen crops effectively

**Key insight:** The mycelial web of pioneer legume roots enables maintenance of applied nutrients, reduces water use, and establishes fruiting trees (mango, cashew, pomegranate, persimmon, citrus, tamarind, lychee, custard apple, avocado) in sparse, drought-prone soils. Palms suited to these sites include date, coconut, doum, and *Borassus* palms.

### Garden-Scale Approach

1. **Trench gardens:** Excavate long trenches (1–2 m wide, 1–1.5 m deep) in loose sand, line with sheet plastic (upturned at one end only) or thickly with cardboard, paper, and carpet, then backfill with sandy loam. The sealed layer holds water and leached mulch; household greywater can be directed into these trenches.

2. **Raised bed boxes:** Build log-boxes above ground, carpet the base with plastic or thick paper, fill with humus-sand mixes for green crop and vegetables, top-mulching as needed.

3. **Water storage:** Large domestic water tanks, gleyed ponds, solid granite dams, and underground plastic-lined cisterns back-filled with sand.

## The Critical Role of Tree and Ground Legumes

In the long term, sustainable [[termite-and-ant-ecology-in-tropical-agriculture]] must rely on tree and ground legumes to maintain soil health. The destructive approaches — now well demonstrated — combine forest clearing, bare-soil cropping, and careless water runoff management to create desolate, baked clays from once-rich tropical forests. The loss of soil fertility through inappropriate management represents one of the most significant environmental crimes of the modern era.

## Soil Life in the Tropics

Termites, ants, and certain worms are the primary soil mesofauna in humid subtropical areas. Both ants and termites actively transport rotted rock and subsoil to the surface, open galleries for water infiltration, and break down woody and leafy plant material. Some species create large mounds, others build underground compost heaps for fungal culture.

Termites play a decisive role in the balance between surface soil erosion and soil replacement by subsoil and rotted rock particles. They contribute to plant succession and distribution in savannah areas, and their mounds often provide the only well-drained or elevated sites in flood-prone landscapes or areas with impermeable clays underlying thin peats.

### Mound Gardening

Ant and termite mounds present rich deposits of calcium and potash, better soil aeration, and faster water infiltration that releases minerals from rocks such as granites. Planting in these mounds is a widely applicable example of niche gardening:

- Leaf-cutter ants in South America and termite mounds in Uganda assist forest spread, establishing islands of taller vegetation in grasslands
- Succession sequences: tall grasses (*Pennisetum*, *Eragrostis*) → shrubs (castor oil bean, *Prosopis*, thorny legumes) → understory and forest (tamarind, *Vitex*, *Sapium*, palms)
- Crops such as sisal, cotton, and tobacco are deliberately cultivated on large mounds in grasslands
- Palms and coffee can have much of their outer bark removed by termites without suffering production loss
- Termites greatly assist primary breakdown of logs, coarse stems, and hard leaf material used as mulch

The practical approach is to observe and report which useful crops are left alone on mounds, which are attacked but remain productive, and which benefit by association with local termite or ant species.
