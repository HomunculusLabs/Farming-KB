From observing such natural associations, permaculture derives a resolve to cooperate and take support roles within society, fostering interdependence that values each individual's contributions rather than organizing around opposition or competition. This cooperative ethic extends progressively outward: from helping family and friends (which clearly assists personal survival), to recognizing all humankind as family, and ultimately to regarding all life as allied associations sharing common origins.

## Three Models of Permanence

Mollison identifies three distinct historical approaches to permanent agriculture, each with different social, political, and ecological characteristics:

### Feudal Permanence

Well-documented by F.H. King in *Farmers of Forty Centuries* for old China, feudal permanence involved hauling nutrients from canals, cesspits, pathways, and forests to sustain annual grain cultures. People were bound to the landscape through unremitting toil in service to a state or landlord. Despite its productivity, this system ultimately led to famine and revolution because the social structure was unsustainable.

### Baronial Permanence

Characteristic of the prairie, pampas, and modern Western ranches, baronial permanence involves large landholdings and few people managing vast grazing leases, typically for a single species of animal. These near-regal properties of immense extent operate at the lowest possible level of land use—pasture and cropland represent the least productive use of land conceivable. Once mechanized, such systems destroy whole landscapes and soil complexes, creating what Mollison calls "agricultural deserts."

### Communal Permanence

Forests represent the oldest form of permanent agriculture, but they require generations of care and knowledge, and therefore a tribal or communal reverence found only in stable communities. This is the model many permaculturalists seek: the ability to plant a pecan or citrus tree in old age and know it will not be cut down by one's children's children. Communal permanence, where land is held in common and needs are met from within the system, is the form most compatible with long-term ecological and social stability.

The further society departs from communal permanence, the greater the risk of tyranny, feudalism, and revolution—and the more work required for less yield. Any error or disturbance in a simplified system can bring disaster: a drought year in a desert grain crop, or a distant political decision on tariffs can devastate an entire region.

## The Energy Characteristic of Permanent Agriculture

The defining characteristic that unites all permanent agricultures is that the energy needs of the system are provided by that system itself. Modern crop agriculture, by contrast, is totally dependent on external energy inputs—hence the fundamental vulnerability of fossil-fuel-dependent farming. This dependency on external energy is what Mollison calls "the oil problem and its associated pollution."

Selected forests not only yield more total biomass than annual crops but also provide a diverse nutrient and fuel resource that can support those crops. The energy relationship is critical: industrial agriculture starts at a 10:1 energy ratio against the farmer (a net loss of ten calories of input for every calorie of food produced), while conservation farming and permaculture can reach a 1:120 energy gain when firewood and fuels are produced on-site.

## The Transition Pathway: From Industrial Agriculture to Permaculture

Mollison outlines a transition period of three to eight years from contemporary industrial agriculture to a permaculture system. The basic changes involved include:

- Replacing animal forage grains with tree crops
- Increasing forest cover significantly
- Adopting low-tillage or no-tillage practices on remaining croplands
- Retrofitting the house for energy conservation
- Producing some or all fuel on the farm

By year eight of this transition, approximately 70% of former cropland can be devoted to forage farming and tree systems, with the remaining 30% supporting intensive food production.

## Social Stability and the Necessity of Permanence

Without permanent agriculture, Mollison argues, there is no possibility of a stable social order. The historical move from productive permanent systems—where land was held in common—to annual commercial agriculture, where land is treated as a commodity, represents a departure from a low-energy to a high-energy society. It involves the exploitative use of land and a demand for external energy resources, primarily extracted from the third world.

The real risk is that the needs of people working "on the ground"—the inhabitants—are overthrown by the needs or greeds of commerce and centralized power. The forest is cut for warships or newspaper, and people are reduced to serfs in a barren landscape. This has been the fate of peasant Europe, Ireland, and much of the third world throughout history.
