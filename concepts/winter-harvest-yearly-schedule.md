## Winter Harvest Yearly Schedule

The winter harvest yearly schedule is a comprehensive annual cropping plan that integrates summer and winter vegetable production into a continuous twelve-month farming system. Developed by Eliot Coleman at Four Season Farm in Maine, this schedule maximizes the productivity of mobile greenhouses through carefully timed crop rotations, succession plantings, and strategic use of both heated and unheated growing spaces. The system evolved from separate summer-only and winter-only experiments into an integrated year-round approach.

## Evolution of the Schedule

Coleman"s approach went through several phases:

1. **Summer-only growing**: Traditional production from spring through fall
2. **Winter-only experiment**: For several years, the farm ran as a "backwards farm," producing salads and vegetables from October 1 through May 31, then taking a long summer vacation
3. **Full year-round production**: The current system combining the best of both, with a scheduled vacation period during the lowest-production weeks

The winter-only phase revealed a valuable eight-month harvest season but showed that the dream of a long summer vacation was unrealistic — cleanup from the winter season and preparation for the next winter consumed most of June and July.

## The Annual Cycle

### Summer Season

During summer, the greenhouses are moved off the winter-crop beds and used for warm-season crops:

- **Tomatoes**: The primary summer greenhouse crop, transplanted in April or May
- **Peppers and eggplants**: Grown in the warm greenhouses alongside tomatoes
- **Cucumbers**: Often included in the summer greenhouse rotation
- **Green manures**: Grown in outdoor beds during summer to boost soil fertility for the next winter cycle

### Fall Planting and Harvest

Fall planting is critical to the success of the winter harvest system. Sowing dates are timed to maximize fall greenhouse production from November 1 through December 21 (the winter solstice). Key activities include:

- **Sowing winter crops** in August, September, and October for greenhouse transplanting
- **Harvesting from outdoor fields** through the end of October
- **Moving greenhouses** over outdoor-planted crops in mid-to-late October
- **Marketing intensively** to the pre-Christmas high-demand period

Fall-harvested greenhouse crops include head lettuce, radishes, turnips, baby pak choi, celery, fennel, and scallions. As these once-and-done crops are harvested in November and December, the spaces they free up are re-prepared and re-sown to crops that will mature from mid-February onward.

### The Persephone Months (Late December through January)

This is the low point of the year. Plant growth has essentially stopped due to short day lengths, customer interest is at its seasonal low, and grower energy levels need replenishment. The schedule addresses this by:

- **Scheduling a four- to five-week vacation** for the crew starting at the winter solstice
- **Simplifying production** to minimize labor demands
- **Relying on storage crops and minimal fresh harvesting** (primarily "candy carrots" and overwintered leeks) for sales
- **Hiring someone** to handle the minimal harvesting and sales during the vacation period

By aiming greenhouse production to maximize November/December sales and running all greenhouses as cold (unheated) houses, the farm sells just as much total volume while cutting expenses and labor during the most difficult weeks.

### Late Winter and Spring

As days lengthen past ten hours in early February, growth resumes and the system ramps back up:

- **New sowings** planted during winter begin maturing
- **Overwintered crops** (especially spinach) resume active growth
- **Cool houses** with minimal heat provide an even wider variety of crops
- **Spring crops** like arugula, baby beets, baby new potatoes, broccoli, fennel, kale, lettuce, and turnips come into production
- **Greenhouses transition** from cold houses to warm houses as propane heaters are activated for summer crops

## The Dependable Spinach Crop

Overwintered spinach has become the major winter green crop for three reasons:

1. **Continuous production**: Unlike kale and other cold-hardy crops that stop producing new leaves during winter, spinach continues growing new leaves throughout the Persephone months
2. **Strong demand**: Spinach is enormously popular as a winter crop, and demand consistently exceeds supply
3. **Perfect rotation timing**: Mid-September-sown outdoor spinach is hardy enough to not need protection until the greenhouse is freed from summer crops. The house is moved over the spinach in the second half of October.

The spinach crop timeline:
- **Mid-September**: Sown outdoors
- **Through October**: Harvested from the field
- **November**: Harvested from another greenhouse
- **Late October**: Mobile greenhouse moved over remaining outdoor spinach
- **Thanksgiving onward**: First greenhouse harvest begins
- **December**: Heavy harvest to meet holiday demand; spinach needs a month to recover
- **Late January**: First post-vacation harvest
- **Monthly intervals**: Each bed provides approximately four harvests
- **Late March**: Final harvest before spinach begins bolting
- **Early April**: Beds cleaned and prepared for earliest tomato transplanting

## Crop Rotation in Cold Houses

The goal is to harvest at least three crops per year from every square foot of the cold houses — two in winter and one (or more) in summer.

### Example Rotation 1 (48-foot cold house)
1. **Winter**: Carrot crop harvested through winter
2. **March 15**: First baby new potatoes planted
3. **After potato harvest**: Charantais melons transplanted (late May)
4. **After melons**: Fall-harvested spinach crop
5. **Late November**: House moved to cover later-planted spinach for spring harvest

### Example Rotation 2 (48-foot cold house)
1. **Winter**: Fall stir-fry crops followed by early spring lettuce
2. **May 10**: Six-week-old tomato plants set out, protected with inner layer
3. **After frost danger**: Inner covers removed, tomatoes trellised to overhead supports
4. **September 1**: Red clover green manure undersown in standing tomatoes
5. **Day before move**: Tomato vines unclipped from supports
6. **Late October**: House moved to cover winter carrot crop

Rotations repeat on the uncovered site for a second year before being exchanged with a different rotation in another house, maintaining a four-year cycle overall.

## Cool House Rotation

With supplementary heat, the cool house supports four to five crops during winter alone, plus warm-weather crops in summer. The rotation alternates between:

- **Lettuce family** (Compositae/Asteraceae)
- **Cabbage family** (Brassicaceae)
- **Warm-weather crops** (cucumbers, peppers, eggplants)

This complex alternation helps prevent pest and disease buildup. Since the original cool house is non-mobile, extra attention is paid to soil preparation, compost quality, crop rotation, and irrigation to avoid the soil degradation problems that historically plagued permanent greenhouses.

## Summer and Winter Balance

After six years of winter-only production, Coleman added summer crops back into the rotation. The subsequent years of full year-round production were "truly exhausting" but revealed an important economic insight: the gross revenue from four summer months was slightly higher than from eight winter months. However, the winter months contributed critically by getting crops ready earlier in spring, allowing the farm to establish new markets and maintain existing ones through the summer transition.

## Adaptation Guidelines

Growers at different latitudes should adjust the schedule based on their local day-length patterns. The key date ranges shift with latitude:

- **44°N (Maine)**: Persephone period November 5 – February 5
- **39°N (Washington, D.C.)**: Persephone period November 17 – January 24
- **35°N (Charlotte, NC)**: Persephone period December 1 – January 10
- **33°N (Atlanta, GA)**: Persephone period December 7 – January 5

Planting dates should be calculated to ensure crops reach harvestable size before the Persephone period begins at each location.
