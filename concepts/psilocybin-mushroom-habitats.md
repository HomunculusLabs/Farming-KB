# Psilocybin Mushroom Habitats

The six classic habitat types where psilocybin mushrooms flourish, as described by [[paul-stamets]] in Psilocybin Mushrooms of the World. Understanding habitat is one of the most effective ways to narrow identification candidates in the field. See [[mushroom-identification]] for general identification methods and [[mushroom-species-guide]] for species profiles.

## The Ecological Basis

All psilocybin-producing mushrooms are **saprophytes** -- they decompose dead organic matter. This means they can exploit a broad range of ecological niches and are geographically widely dispersed. About 100 species are known or suspected active (producing psilocybin, psilocin, baeocystin, or nor-baeocystin). More than half of the genus Psilocybe contains psilocybin. Mexico is the richest in psilocybin mycoflora.

Stamets has not found a single temperate or tropical habitat with high annual rainfall that lacks psilocybin mushrooms. However, in every region they are greatly outnumbered by toxic species.

## The Six Classic Habitats

### 1. Grasslands

Species: P. strictipes, P. liniformans, P. semilanceata, P. mexicana, P. samuiensis, P. inquilina, P. kashmeriensis

- Tall, thin, small, conic-capped Psilocybes dominate
- Wet swampy lowlands are most productive
- Associated grasses: fescues, bent grasses, canary grasses, perennial ryes, sedges, duneland grasses
- Grasses grazed by sheep, horses, cattle, bison, yaks, water buffalo tend to be most productive
- P. semilanceata exploits dying rhizomes of field grasses including fescues
- Canary (Phalaris) grasses, high in DMT, may potentiate psilocybin production (Gartz 1989)
- Many grassland species form **sclerotia** (hardened nutlike dormant structures): P. mexicana, P. semilanceata, P. tampanensis, Conocybe cyanopus
- Borders along forestlands are best -- cooler, more humid, longest fruitings during dry weather

### 2. Dung Deposits

Species: P. cubensis, P. coprophila, Panaeolus cyanescens (= Copelandia cyanescens), Panaeolus subbalteatus, P. angustispora

- Dung is a short-lived habitat -- mushrooms flourish for days as dung disintegrates
- Co-occurrence with grassland species common (e.g., P. mexicana with P. cubensis in Mexico)
- P. angustispora favors marmot and elk dung in Cascade Mountains
- Noble dung heaps provide dependable, easy-to-find habitat worldwide

### 3. Riparian Zones

Species: P. cyanescens, P. azurescens, P. yungensis

- Created by flooding rivers that erode soils and trees
- Alluvial plains: high in sandy silt, abundant broken wood fragments
- Trees: cottonwoods (Populus), alders (Alnus), willows (Salix)
- Characteristically open and sunny
- Hot springs near Whistler, BC: Psilocybe similar to P. cyanofibrillosa grows in sedge grass
- Hikers distribute spores from these remote habitats back to urban areas

### 4. Disturbed Habitats

Species: P. caerulescens ("landslide mushroom" -- derrumbes), P. cyanescens, P. stuntzii

- Roads punched into forest, construction sites, landslides
- First 2 years are most prolific; third year declining; after 4-5 years, habitats virtually expire
- Re-introducing debris or upturning soils can rebound populations
- Blackberry and Scotch broom thickets: open, moist, punctuated with deciduous trees
- Rodent burrows can be sheathed with mycelium; rodent pellets can sprout P. azurescens (observed near Astoria, Oregon)
- P. caerulescens has such strong association with disturbed ground it is called "derrumbes" (landslide mushroom) by Mexicans

### 5. Gardens

Species: P. cyanofibrillosa, P. baeocystis, P. caerulescens, P. stuntzii, P. subaeruginosa, Panaeolus subbalteatus, Panaeolus cyanescens

- Tilling practices make gardens disturbed habitats
- Importing exotic plants, amending soils, composting, and watering all encourage mushroom growth
- Manured gardens sprout P. subbalteatus (temperate) and Panaeolus cyanescens (subtropical)
- Vegetable gardens support terricolous (soil-dwelling) Psilocybes
- Floral gardens (richer in wood debris) support lignicolous (wood-dwelling) species
- Rhododendron and rose gardens: annually renewed by caretakers. One gardener placed cardboard around rhododendron bases every year for 12 years straight, supporting P. cyanescens continuously

### 6. Woodlands

Species: P. baeocystis, P. pelliculosa, P. serbica, P. caerulipes, P. muliercula, P. cyanescens

- The most expansive category; delimited by altitude, temperature, and rainfall
- **Deciduous**: cottonwoods, alders, willows, box elders, sweet gums, hornbeams -- moist soils along streams, ponds, lakes
- P. serbica (central Europe) grows in forests with European beech (Fagus sylvatica)
- P. caerulipes (northeastern US) associates with American beech (Fagus grandifolia)
- **Coniferous**: P. baeocystis from Douglas fir cones; P. pelliculosa from Douglas fir needles
- P. cyanescens fruits from Montberny squirrel seed caches at tree bases
- P. muliercula grows exclusively in Abies and Pinus forests at 2600-3500m (Mexico)
- P. caerulescens: red soils and pines; first reported near Montgomery, Alabama, 1923
- Psilocybes are rare in deep PNW forests but frequent in pine forests closer to equatorial subtropics
- Few psilocybin mushrooms in true tropics

## Human-Mediated Habitat Expansion

The use of wood chips (beauty bark) for landscaping has dramatically expanded the range of wood-loving Psilocybes into densely populated areas. P. stuntzii frequents football fields, school grounds, and parks in Washington and Oregon. Students dusted with spore mass disperse spores as they leave these sites, spreading populations along their travel routes.
