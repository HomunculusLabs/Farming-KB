# Beneficial Insect Habitat

Creating [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] insects is one of the most important and rewarding aspects of ecological gardening. Most insects are either helpful or harmless, and only a tiny fraction are true pests. By designing gardens that attract and sustain predatory and parasitic insects, gardeners can let nature handle [[biological-pest-control-balance]] rather than relying on chemicals.

## The Ecological Balance

Research has shown that in natural ecosystems, pest insects rarely reach damaging levels because they are held in check by predators, parasites, and disease. The widespread use of broad-spectrum insecticides disrupts this balance by killing beneficial insects along with pests -- and beneficials are slower to recover, leaving the garden defenseless when pests return.

The key insight: having a few pests around is better than having none at all. If you temporarily eliminate all pests, good bugs disappear too, leaving you defenseless when the faster-breeding pests return. Weedy spots and trap crops harbor a few aphids and other prey, encouraging beneficials to stay -- their value as snack bars for good bugs far outweighs any possible harm.

### Why Pests Outpace Predators

Insects that feed on plants reproduce at staggering rates, quickly surging to astronomical numbers. But the insects that prey on these pests reproduce more slowly and are far fewer in number. A predator's food supply is less abundant -- there are more leaves to eat than bugs -- and it takes more energy to hunt than to graze. Less energy is available for predator breeding than for their prey.

Predators always occur in much smaller numbers than their prey. This makes them vulnerable to extinction when conditions are bad. A time lag also falls between the breeding of prey and their predators: an outbreak of aphids can reach pestilential proportions in a week or two (aphids can give birth to already-pregnant young), but lady beetles need time to lay and hatch eggs. The young larvae, which look like alligators, are voracious predators but take days to grow. By the time predator numbers build, the gardener has often already sprayed insecticide -- killing both pests and predators, while the fast-breeding pests recover first.

### How Habitat Was Lost

Historically, farmers divided fields with many-specied hedgerows and left wild vegetation along creeks and back pastures. The diversity of plants in these untamed places was home to a wide variety of insects that sheltered among leaves and fed on nectar and pollen. Spiders and birds thrived in these thickets. Whenever pests swarmed into adjacent fields, predators were waiting. Then herbicides, powerful tractors, and the drive to maximize farmland profit destroyed this habitat. Hedgerows were replaced with wire fence, and wild places were eliminated. Combined with widespread insecticide use, this placed gardens on an expensive and toxic treadmill.

## What Beneficial Insects Need

Like all animals, beneficial insects need food, shelter, water, and the right conditions to reproduce.

### Food

Beneficial insects need two types of food:

- **Pest prey**: Without a reliable supply of prey, predators and parasitoids won't stay. Hedgerows and weedy spots that harbor a few aphids encourage beneficials to lurk about.
- **Nectar and pollen**: Adult parasitic wasps, hover flies, lacewings, and many predators feed on flower nectar and pollen. This is where insectary plants are essential.

### Shelter

Dense foliage, mulch, dead brush and leaves, rock piles, and walls all provide shelter. Many beneficial insects overwinter or lay eggs in dead vegetation, so gardeners should delay postseason cleanup until spring. That fastidiously neat garden is poor habitat for beneficials.

### Water

Many insects get moisture from nectar or foliage, but some bees and wasps drink from open water. Having a pond or other water feature is never a bad idea.

### Reproduction Conditions

Food needs to be available at the right time. Grow many species of flowers so that several types are always in bloom. Many-specied hedgerows, wild and weedy spots, mixed perennial borders, and flowers sprinkled in vegetable beds all provide the right conditions.

## Insectary Plants

Insectary plants are those that attract beneficial insects by offering nectar, pollen, foliage for larvae, or habitat for prey species. Beneficial insects fall into two main groups: pollinators, needed for fruit and seed set, and predators, which consume the bugs that damage plants.

Key plant families include:

- **Umbelliferae / Apiaceae** (carrot family): Dill, fennel, coriander, parsley, Queen Anne's lace -- nearly all members are excellent insectary plants
- **Asteraceae** (composite/sunflower family): Calendula, chamomile, sunflowers, coneflowers, cosmos, golden marguerite
- **Lamiaceae** (mint family): Bee balm, lavender, thyme, sage, rosemary -- especially loaded with insect-attracting species
- **Liliaceae** (onion/lily family): Also contains insect-attracting members

Choice insectary plants include yarrow, buckwheat, lavender, golden marguerite, bee balm, and many clovers. Many insectary plants are multifunctional -- they are also edible (calendula, bee balm, borage), medicinal, or produce herbs for cooking. The flash and shimmer of multicolored pollinators will both delight the eye and attract many varieties of birds, further increasing biodiversity.

## Key Beneficial Insect Groups

- **Lady beetles (ladybugs)**: Both adults and larvae consume aphids, mealybugs, and other soft-bodied pests. Larvae are particularly voracious, resembling tiny alligators.
- **Ground beetles**: Large, fast-running predators that hunt at night, consuming slugs, cutworms, and [[cervantes-nematodes-root-maggots-soil-pests]]. Shelter in mulch, stones, and [[fungal-succession-woody-debris-ecology]].
- **Lacewings**: Adults feed on nectar; larvae are active predators of aphids, thrips, and small caterpillars.
- **Hover flies (syrphid flies)**: Adults visit flowers for nectar; their larvae consume large numbers of aphids.
- **Parasitic wasps**: Tiny wasps that lay eggs inside or on pest insects. Adults need nectar from small flowers, especially umbels.
- **[[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol]]**: Hunt pest mites and thrips in leaf litter and on plants.
- **Spiders**: Generalist predators found in all garden habitats.

## Design Strategies

- **Diversity is the key**: Plant everything under the sun and enjoy the resulting orgy of buzzing, flittering, pollinating, and garden health
- **Continuous bloom**: Ensure something is flowering from early spring through late fall
- **Cluster plantings**: Group insectary plants in masses of at least 3-5 feet across so beneficials can find them easily
- **Integrate throughout**: Sprinkle insectary flowers in vegetable beds, along paths, and at the edges of [[fruit-tree-guild-roles]] -- guilds include insectary plants as a key role
