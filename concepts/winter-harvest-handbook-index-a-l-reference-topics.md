# Winter Harvest Handbook Index A-L: Key Reference Topics

This page serves as a topical glossary expanding the A–L index entries of *The Winter Harvest Handbook* into a structured reference guide for winter vegetable production.

## Greenhouse Systems and Structures

### Cold Frames (pp. 16–17, 25–27)
The simplest protected growing structure — a bottomless box with a transparent lid, traditionally glazed. Cold frames provide 5–10°F of frost protection and are the historical precursor to modern greenhouses. Coleman traces their use from Roman times through the Parisian *maraîchers* (market gardeners) to present-day practice.

### Cold Houses
Unheated greenhouses that rely on solar gain and thermal mass for temperature moderation. Coleman distinguishes cold houses from cool greenhouses: cold houses receive no supplemental heat whatsoever, while cool greenhouses may be minimally heated. Cold houses are the backbone of his [[winter-harvest-system]].

### Cool Greenhouses
Minimally heated structures (maintained just above freezing) that extend the growing season significantly compared to unheated houses. Key advantages include earlier spring planting and protection during extreme cold events. Energy costs are modest — Coleman reports minimal heating inputs compared to conventional greenhouse operations.

### Cloches (pp. 17–18)
Individual glass or plastic covers placed over plants, originating from French intensive gardening. The Parisian *maraîchers* used thousands of bell-shaped glass cloches to protect crops. Modern equivalents include plastic tunnel cloches and hotcaps.

### High Tunnels (pp. 2, 54, 56–59, 65, 93, 106)
Large hoop-supported structures covered with polyethylene film. Unlike traditional greenhouses, high tunnels are typically unheated and lack permanent foundation systems. They represent the most cost-effective approach to season extension.

### Mobile Greenhouses
Greenhouses mounted on rails or skids that can be moved between growing areas. This innovation, which Coleman adapted from earlier European designs, allows one greenhouse structure to serve multiple plots — providing summer protection for heat-loving crops and winter protection for cold-hardy vegetables in different locations.

### Quick Hoops (pp. 94, 122, 124–127, 129–130)
Low-cost, small-scale tunnel structures made from PVC conduit or metal wire hoops covered with row cover fabric or plastic. Quick hoops are the most accessible entry point for protected cultivation, costing a fraction of permanent greenhouse structures.

### Wiggle Wire Channels (pp. 110, 113, 116)
Also called spring-lock channels or poly-lock channels. Metal tracks mounted on greenhouse frames that hold plastic coverings securely using wiggle wire (preformed spring wire). This system allows easy attachment and removal of coverings for ventilation.

## Crop Management

### Braising Mix (pp. 12, 76–78)
A blend of sturdier greens intended for cooking rather than raw salad. Typically includes chard, kale, Asian greens, and other greens that benefit from brief cooking. Braising mix complements salad mix in a diversified winter harvest operation.

### Salad Mix / Mesclun (pp. 50, 75–78, 84–85, 86, 88, 161)
The primary winter harvest product — a blend of tender leafy greens cut at baby stage and sold fresh. Coleman's salad mix may include lettuce, arugula, mizuna, tatsoi, claytonia, mâche, endive, frisée, beet greens, mustard greens, and more. [[cut-and-come-again]] harvesting allows multiple harvests from a single planting.

### Cut-and-Come-Again Crops (pp. 3, 50, 85)
Crops harvested by cutting above the growing point so the plant regrows for subsequent harvests. This method is central to winter production economics — a single sowing can yield 3–5 harvests over several months.

### Crop Rotation (pp. 4, 15, 34, 36–40, 94, 99–100, 117–124)
Systematic sequencing of crop families across growing areas to break pest and disease cycles and manage soil fertility. Coleman rotates between summer crops (tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers) and winter crops (greens, root vegetables) within the same greenhouse using mobile structures.

### Succession Planting
Staggered sowings of the same crop at intervals to ensure continuous harvest availability. Critical for winter production where the harvest window for any single planting may span weeks to months.

### Intensive Cropping
Growing crops in close spacing with careful attention to soil fertility. Drawing on the French intensive tradition, Coleman achieves high per-square-foot productivity through close plant spacing, relay planting, and double-cropping (summer and winter crops in the same space annually).

### Daylight Requirements (pp. 2–4, 41–44, 48–50, 54, 57–58, 62, 66)
Perhaps the most important environmental factor in winter production. When daylight drops below 10 hours per day (the **Persephone Period**), plant growth essentially ceases for most vegetables. Coleman's entire scheduling system is built around getting crops to harvestable size before this period begins.

### Harvest Scheduling (pp. 34, 48–50)
Planning harvest timing across multiple protected environments to ensure continuous supply through winter. Coleman's system extends fresh vegetable availability from approximately October through April in Zone 5.

## Soil and Fertility

### Compost (pp. 15–16, 95, 98, 100–101, 137–139, 182)
The primary fertility source in Coleman's system. He emphasizes high-quality compost production and application, often incorporating horse manure from local sources. Compost provides both nutrients and organic matter for soil biological health.

### Green Manure Crops
Cover crops grown specifically to be incorporated into the soil for fertility building. Used between cash crop cycles to add organic matter, fix nitrogen (legumes), prevent erosion, and suppress weeds.

### Horse Manure (pp. 14–16, 19, 23, 138)
The traditional fertility backbone of French intensive gardening. The Parisian *maraîchers* built their entire system around horse manure from the city's thousands of horses. Coleman continues to use horse manure as a primary compost feedstock.

### Soil Aeration (pp. 8, 132, 135–136, 210)
Maintaining soil structure and air exchange through appropriate tillage. Coleman uses broadforks rather than power tillers where possible, preserving soil structure while improving drainage and root penetration.

### Fertility Maintenance (pp. 101, 132, 134, 137–138, 182, 185)
A holistic approach combining compost, green manures, mineral amendments (greensand, phosphate rock, limestone), and careful crop rotation to maintain productive soil without synthetic chemical inputs.

## Pests and Diseases

### Aphids (pp. 184–185)
The primary insect pest in winter greenhouse production. Aphids thrive in the protected, relatively warm greenhouse environment. Coleman favors biological and cultural controls over chemical interventions.

### Flea Beetles (p. 93)
A significant pest of brassica crops, particularly during spring and fall transitions. Floating row covers provide effective exclusion.

### Meadow Voles (pp. 175–178)
Small rodents that can cause significant damage by gnawing plant roots and stems, especially under snow cover or mulch. Vole damage is a particular concern in mobile greenhouse systems where the structure may sit in one location for extended periods.

### Prevention vs. Palliatives (pp. 179–180)
Coleman's pest management philosophy emphasizes preventing problems through healthy soils, proper crop rotation, and environmental management rather than treating symptoms with pesticides after problems arise.

## Tools and Equipment

### Broadforks (pp. 135–137)
A hand tool with long tines used to aerate and loosen soil without inverting the soil profile. Preferred over power tillers for maintaining soil structure.

### Flame Weeders (pp. 100, 151–154)
Propane-powered tools that pass a flame over weed seedlings to kill them before crop emergence. Effective for pre-emergence weed control in direct-seeded crops.

### Floating Row Covers (pp. 61, 63)
Spun-bonded polyester or polypropylene fabrics laid directly over crops for frost protection and insect exclusion. Available in various weights providing different levels of protection. Used extensively inside cold houses for a double-layer protection system.

### Pinpoint Seeders (pp. 141–143, 145–147)
Precision seeding tools that singulate seeds for accurate in-row placement. Coleman uses these for efficient direct seeding of salad greens and other small-seeded crops.

### Collineal Hoes (pp. 154, 156)
Hoes designed for efficient weed control between crop rows, minimizing labor while maximizing weed removal effectiveness.

### Wire Wickets (pp. 5–6, 29, 60–61, 129)
Bent wire supports used to hold floating row covers above crops, preventing abrasion and providing better air circulation than fabric laid directly on plants.

## Cultural and Historical Context

### Celtic Festivals / Pagan Calendar (p. 47)
Coleman uses the ancient Celtic Wheel of the Year — Imbolc (Feb 1), Beltane (May 1), Lughnasadh (Aug 1), and Samhain (Nov 1) — along with the solstices and equinoxes as a framework for understanding seasonal rhythms and planning agricultural activities.

### Demeter and Persephone Myths (p. 45)
The Greek myth of Persephone's descent to the underworld (and the resulting winter) provides the metaphorical framework for Coleman's concept of the "Persephone Period" — the time each winter when daylight drops below 10 hours and plant growth ceases.

### French Garden Systems (pp. 13–18, 21–24)
The 19th-century Parisian market gardening tradition (*la culture maraîchère*) that serves as Coleman's primary historical inspiration. These growers produced extraordinary quantities of fresh vegetables year-round using horse manure, glass cloches, and hotbeds on small urban plots.

### Chemical Inputs — Historical Shift (pp. 21, 195–197, 201–212)
Coleman documents the 20th-century shift from biological farming to chemical-dependent agriculture and argues for a return to biological principles based on the accumulated evidence of soil science and practical experience.
