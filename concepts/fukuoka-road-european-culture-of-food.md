## The Western Meal: Functional and Self-Service

Fukuoka visited farming families in the European countryside
and observed what they normally eat: some kind of soup or
stew made of vegetables and a bit of grain floating in milk
and wheat flour. To him, it looked very unappetizing. After
a meal of this, he did not feel as if he had really eaten.

People eat or don't eat breakfast as they feel like, and the
woman of the house does not rush about busily in the kitchen
before lunch preparing the meal as Japanese wives do. People
serve themselves one or two dishes, sprinkle some condiments
on top and mix, essentially preparing the food on their own
plate. Once finished eating, they wipe the plate clean with
bread. The whole process struck him as dreary.

## Why Everything Is Self-Service

The reason for self-service, Fukuoka discovered, is that
everywhere in Europe it is "ladies first." The wife doesn't
cook, so everyone prepares his food to suit his own taste.
Women take no interest in cooking on account of the poor
variety of materials available. He wondered whether it is
because men and women eat separately that meals have become
totally self-service.

## The Deficiency of Root Vegetables

Europe and America have too small a variety of vegetables,
and the quality is poor. There seemed to be far too few root
vegetables in particular. Burdock grows wild but no one
realizes this is a food. There are no taros. The yams are
sticky and not fit for consumption. People view devil's
tongue and other "barbarian" foods that grow in mud, such as
arrowhead and lotus root, as outlandish. There is no bamboo,
so no one knows of bamboo shoots. The sweet potatoes grown
are hard and tasteless.

Westerners consume very few root vegetables, which as a
group are essential to proper physical growth. Perhaps this
is one of the reasons why they differ so from Orientals. The
dearth of roots in the diet may have something to do with
the inability of people in the West to see the depth of
nature's roots.

## East Versus West: Two Philosophies of Eating

Western plates are large so you can mix all ingredients on
one plate, making for efficient eating. Cups have handles
for easy holding. Spoons are conveniently designed for
carrying food to the mouth. In Japan, individual dishes are
served in an assortment of plates and bowls. One serves a
small amount of choicely prepared food in a small plate and
savors it.

Even the size of Japanese teacups differs with the type of
tea. Teacups have no handles, so one balances the cup in
both hands, reverently savoring the taste. With chopsticks,
