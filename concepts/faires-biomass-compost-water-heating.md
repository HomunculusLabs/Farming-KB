## How It Works

As microorganisms break down organic material, they generate significant heat. A compost pile at least 3 feet (1 meter) square will heat up within 24 hours of being assembled. The temperature rises as bacteria, fungi, and other decomposers feast on the materials, often exceeding 120°F (49°C).

## Greenhouse Biomass Heating

The most common application of biomass heating is in the greenhouse, where the compost provides both heat and CO2 for growing plants. The compost pile is placed inside or adjacent to the greenhouse, warming the air directly.

A water hose laid under the floor of a greenhouse can circulate warm water through the compost, distributing heat more evenly. When combined with hay bale insulation walls, the system creates a very effective heated growing space.

## Biomass Compost Water Heater Design

A more sophisticated system uses a ring of chicken wire or mesh fencing to contain a compost pile, with a coil of plastic pipe wrapped around it. A second ring of wire is set up at least four feet (1 meter) outside the first, and more compost is added between the rings.

### Construction Steps

1. Build a circular enclosure from chicken wire or mesh fencing, approximately 4 feet (1.2 meters) in diameter
2. Fill the inner circle with layers of compost and organic material (alternating green and brown materials)
3. Wrap a coil of plastic pipe around the outside of the inner pile
4. Set up a second ring of wire fencing at least 4 feet (1 meter) outside the first
5. Fill the space between the rings with more compost material
6. Connect the pipe ends to a water tank or hot water system
7. As the compost heats up, it heats the water circulating through the pipe

### How the Water System Works

- Cold water enters one end of the pipe coil
- As it passes through the hot compost, the water absorbs heat
- Warm water exits the other end and flows to the hot water tank
- A thermosiphon effect can drive circulation (hot water rises naturally), or a small pump can be used
- The heated water supplements the household hot water supply

## Compost Pile Management for Maximum Heat

### Material Ratios

- Half brown materials (carbon-rich): paper, leaves, dry grass, straw, sawdust
- Half green materials (nitrogen-rich): manure, garden waste, kitchen scraps, grass clippings
- The pile should be about as moist as a damp sponge

### Getting Maximum Heat

- Pile must be at least 3 feet (1 meter) square to generate sufficient heat
- Chop materials before adding to speed decomposition
- Keep pile sheltered from excessive rain (unless in a very dry climate)
- Layer materials rather than mixing randomly
- Add manure to boost nitrogen and microbial activity
- If pile doesn't heat up: it may be too small, too wet, too dry, or imbalanced

### Temperature Cycle

- Phase 1 (0-24 hours): Initial heating as bacteria multiply rapidly
- Phase 2 (days 1-7): Peak heating, temperatures exceed 120°F (49°C)
- Phase 3 (weeks 2-4): Temperature drops as initial food source is consumed
- Action: Stir the pile to introduce oxygen; insects and worms move in
- Phase 4 (months 1-3+): Slow decomposition, lower temperatures, stable compost

## Advantages of Biomass Heating

- Uses waste materials that would otherwise be discarded
- Produces heat with zero fossil fuel input
- The end product is finished compost for garden use
- Simple to build and maintain with basic materials
- Can be scaled from small greenhouse heaters to whole-house systems
- Works in any climate where decomposition occurs

## Limitations

- Heat output is lower and less controllable than combustion systems
- Requires regular feeding with organic material
- Space-intensive compared to conventional water heaters
- Best used as a supplement rather than primary heat source
- May attract pests if food scraps are used in the compost
