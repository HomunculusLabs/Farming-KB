# Fungi in Elevated CO2 and Global Carbon Cycling

The increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration and the
consequent predicted temperature rise present complex challenges for
understanding fungal roles in [[fungal-ecosystem-processes-overview]]. Dighton and Jansen
(1991) proposed a model of possible effects of increased atmospheric
CO2 and elevated temperature on tree-ectomycorrhizal symbiosis. Under
elevated CO2, increased photosynthesis leads to greater carbon supply to
roots and fungi, supporting greater nitrogen access in N-limiting
systems. Elevated temperature increases decomposition and nutrient
mineralization, potentially reducing host dependency on mycorrhizae.

## Effects on Decomposition and Nutrient Availability

The effect of enriched atmospheric CO2 on soil organisms is
inconsistent. Increased CO2 and elevated temperature increased microbial
carbon but decreased the metabolic quotient when temperature alone was
increased. Zak et al. (2000) summarized results from 47 publications on
soil C and N cycling under elevated CO2 across graminoid, herbaceous,
and woody plant ecosystems. The basic generalities were: (1) greater
plant growth with more carbon entering the below-ground component, and
(2) greater [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] of soil microbial communities. Changes in
C and N cycling between life forms showed coefficients of variation
between 80 and 800%.

De Angelis et al. (2000) showed that elevated CO2 increased C:N and
lignin:N ratios of oak leaf litter in Mediterranean forests, retarding
decomposition by 5 to 8% in field experiments and 12.5% in microcosms.
Cotrufo et al. (1998) showed nitrogen content reduction in leaves
ranged from 50% in [[holzer-sweet-chestnut-and-nut-trees]] to 19% in sycamore under enhanced
CO2. This reduction in leaf litter quality reduces activity of the
saprotrophic community, including fungi.

## Below-Ground Carbon Stores

Roots and their mycorrhizal components are frequently overlooked as
carbon sources. Vogt (1991) shows below-ground biomass carbon accounts
for between 16% in Pinus menziesii to 64% in Pinus elliottii of total
tree biomass carbon. Nearly two-thirds of soil respiration in temperate
mixed hardwood forests comes from root activity. Root litter
decomposition contributes 70 to 80% of total soil respiration across a
range of forested ecosystems.

Coniferous trees appear to allocate twice as much carbon to roots as
deciduous species, a trade-off in [[fungal-bacterial-predators-nitrogen-acquisition-soil-ecology]]. Leaf litter of
coniferous trees is characteristically low quality, leading to low N
mineralization rates and low N demand. In mor soils with distinct
surface organic horizons, carbon investment in roots supports soil
exploitation. In mull soils with incorporated organic matter and limited
N supply, photosynthates stimulate N mineralization by priming the
[[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]] through rhizodeposition.

## Temperature Effects on Carbon Balance

Predicted atmospheric temperature increases raise saprotrophic microbial
activity, increasing decomposition rates. Bonan and Van Cleve (1992)
compared consequences of a 5-degree C rise in boreal forest ecosystems.
Soil temperature increases of 300 to 500 degree days would cause
increased soil respiration loss of 439 g C per square meter in birch
and 675 g C per square meter in black spruce. Total carbon gain from
increased photosynthesis effectively counteracted decomposition loss
except in white spruce ecosystems.

## Mycorrhizal Response Scenarios

The Dighton-Jansen model presents three scenarios for mycorrhizal
function under changing climate. In scenario A (elevated CO2 alone),
increased photosynthesis supports greater C supply to roots and fungi,
enhancing N access. In scenario B (elevated temperature), increased
litter decomposition and nutrient mineralization reduces plant
dependency on mycorrhizae. In scenario C (combined effects with altered
leaf chemistry), enhanced N content increases [[fungal-decomposition-resource-quality-determinants]], forcing
mycorrhizal fungi to compete with saprotrophs by increasing diversity
and extraradical hyphal exploitation.
