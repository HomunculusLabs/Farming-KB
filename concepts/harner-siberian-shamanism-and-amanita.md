# Siberian Shamanism and the Amanita Muscaria

In the Introduction to "Hallucinogens and Shamanism," Harner establishes that what anthropologists call "classic" shamanism--as practiced by native Siberians and first described in detail in the ethnological literature--is fundamentally intertwined with psychoactive plant use, specifically the fly-agaric mushroom (Amanita muscaria). This connection had been largely overlooked in the theoretical literature on shamanism, a gap Harner's volume seeks to address.

## The Definition of Shamanism

Harner provides a foundational definition that frames the entire volume: a shaman is "a man or woman who is in direct contact with the spirit world through a trance state and has one or more spirits at his command to carry out his bidding for good or evil." The term "shaman" comes from the language of the Tungus tribe of Siberia and was preferred in anthropology because it "lacks the sensational or negative connotation of 'witch' or 'witch-doctor.'"

Typically shamans bewitch persons with the aid of spirits or cure persons made ill by other spirits, whether sent by another shaman or acting on their own volition. Depending on their traditions, a shaman may also influence the course of events, find lost or stolen objects, divine the identity of criminals, communicate with the spirits of dead relatives and friends, foretell the future, and practice clairvoyance. While contemporary anthropology tends to view the shaman as a psychotherapist, Harner notes that "the people of the cultures in which he operates believe him to be able to contact and deal with an invisible spirit world."

## Amanita Muscaria: Botanical and Pharmacological Background

Amanita muscaria, the fly-agaric mushroom, is one of the most recognizable fungi in the world--the iconic red-and-white spotted mushroom of fairy tales and video games. It is known to contain muscarine and muscimol, both with demonstrated hallucinogenic properties, as well as other psychotropic substances in limited quantities or of undetermined effects. The mushroom is widely distributed across temperate and boreal forests of the Northern Hemisphere, making it readily available to the indigenous peoples of Siberia.

## Siberian Use of Amanita

In Siberia, Amanita was used by shamans and laypersons among numerous tribes: the Koryak, Chukchi, Yukagir, Yakut, Ostyak, Samoyed, and Kamchadal. Harner quotes extensively from Waldemar Jochelson, who traveled among the Koryak in 1900-1901:

"Fly-agaric produces intoxication, hallucinations, and delirium. Light forms of intoxication are accompanied by a certain degree of animation and some spontaneity of movements. Many shamans, previous to their seances, eat fly-agaric to get into ecstatic states. Under strong intoxication the senses become deranged; surrounding objects appear either very large or very small, hallucinations set in, spontaneous movements and convulsions. So far as I could observe, attacks of great animation alternate with moments of deep depression. The person intoxicated by fly-agaric sits quietly rocking from side to side, even taking part in conversations with his family. Suddenly his eyes dilate, he begins to gesticulate convulsively, converses with persons whom he imagines he sees, sings, and dances. Then an interval of rest sets in again."

### The Urine-Drinking Practice

Jochelson documented the remarkable Koryak practice of drinking the urine of persons intoxicated with fly-agaric to prolong the hallucinations: "There is reason to think that the effect of fly-agaric would be stronger were not its alkaloid quickly taken out of the organism with the urine. The Koryak knows this by experience, and the urine of persons intoxicated with fly-agaric is not wasted. The drunkard himself drinks it to prolong his hallucinations, or he offers it to others as a treat." This practice of recycling the active compounds through urine consumption is well-attested across Siberian cultures and represents a sophisticated understanding of the mushroom's pharmacology.

## The Overlooked Connection

Harner's central argument about Siberian shamanism is provocative: "The theoretical literature has largely overlooked the fact that even this 'classic' shamanism often involved the use of hallucinogen." One could read entire books on shamanism or primitive religion without finding any reference to hallucinogens except for peyote. "Yet by patient library research one can find overwhelming evidence of the use of such substances in connection with the supernatural in scores of cultures."

This omission is particularly significant because Siberian shamanism serves as the type case for the entire anthropological concept of shamanism. If even the foundational example involved hallucinogenic plants, then the role of such substances in shamanism worldwide has been systematically underestimated. Harner suggests that anthropologists, "not free of ethnocentrism," too often passed over the significance of some unidentified "noxious herb" that native peoples claimed to use for trance, much as explorers and missionaries had done before them.

## Methods of Achieving Altered States

Harner contextualizes hallucinogen use within a broader framework of shamanic techniques for achieving trance. Multiple methods are documented across cultures: fasting (water and food), flagellation and self-torture, sensory deprivation, breathing exercises and yogic meditation, and ritual dancing and drumming. Harner suggests that "a common psycho-physiological basis for the similarity of effects produced by all of these methods may exist," but argues that "the use of hallucinogens appears to be the easiest and fastest technique for reaching a believed supernatural experience and visions."

## Implications for Understanding Shamanism

The recognition of Amanita use in Siberian shamanism carries several important implications:

1. **Shamanic personality**: Long-standing controversies over the personality and psychopathology of shamans may need reconsideration in light of "the potentiality of native psychedelics to temporarily transport almost any individual to another state of consciousness." The special psychological makeup attributed to shamans may be less innate than previously assumed.

2. **Worldview and belief**: Discussions of worldview and supernatural belief systems may benefit from investigating "the extent to which these substances are used in religious rites and vision quests." The "more powerful hallucinogens" strongly reinforce belief in the reality of the supernatural world and in the existence of a disembodied soul.

3. **Origins of belief**: Harner raises the "intriguing possibility that hallucinogenic experiences may have also played a role in the innovation of such beliefs," calling for comprehensive cross-cultural and interdisciplinary research on this question.

## The New World Emphasis

Harner notes that the New World is "overwhelmingly emphasized" in the volume's geographic coverage, for several reasons. The New World is "unusually rich in hallucinogenic plants," a factor that facilitated their use by indigenous peoples. Additionally, the Western hemisphere is one of the best regions for studying shamanism, because American Indian cultures "have often preserved an emphasis on shamanism, perhaps because the overwhelming majority were untrammeled by a state religion."

## Social Implications

The social dimensions of hallucinogen use are also addressed. Widespread utilization of hallucinogens to obtain direct revelations "very probably tends to compete with the authority of priestly hierarchies." The authority of shamans themselves has been threatened by the general adoption of peyote in some societies. Conversely, certain types of social structures may be more conducive than others to the development of widespread hallucinogen use. This dynamic between individual revelation and institutional authority remains an important area for future research.
