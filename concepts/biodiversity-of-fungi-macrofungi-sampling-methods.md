# Macrofungi Sampling Methods

Sampling conspicuous macrofungi requires standardized protocols that yield
quantitative data comparable across sites, seasons, and studies. Mueller et al.
(2004) provide detailed recommendations for inventory and monitoring of
macrofungi, emphasizing the need for well-planned research with clearly stated
goals, pilot studies, and systematic data collection. This page summarizes the
key methods for [[recommended-protocols-sampling-macrofungi]] from terrestrial ecosystems, including
plot-based surveys, transect protocols, and [[fungal-biodiversity-data-analysis]] approaches.

## Fixed-Size Plot Protocol

The recommended protocol for sampling conspicuous macrofungi using fixed-size
plots involves establishing permanent plots that represent important forest or
grassland types at the study site. Each plot should be set up in a homogeneous
area, easily accessible but not susceptible to edge effects, since it will be
visited repeatedly. The plot should optimize the diversity of habitat types
sampled by the entire study. At least one plot should be established in each
major habitat type if the study covers grasslands, open woodlands, and dense
forest.

## Transect Design

Each plot consists of 10 transects that are 100 meters long. Transects are
typically laid out parallel to one another at 10-meter intervals, though the
shape of the sampling area may require some transects to be laid end to end.
Transects are marked every 5 meters with a flag or stake. Each transect is
assigned a unique letter, and each flag is numbered sequentially within a
transect (e.g., A1 through A20, B1 through B20, continuing to J1 through J20).
This yields 20 sampling stations per transect and a total of 200 stations per
plot, covering a sampling area of 1,000 square meters (0.1 hectares).

## Field Collection Procedures

Each person sampling carries a plastic pipe or wooden pole and a rope that is
1.262 meters long; the rope is used to circumscribe 5-square-meter circular
subplots around each flag in a transect. Care should be taken not to walk in or
unnecessarily disturb the subplots. At each station, all macrofungal sporocarps
within the subplot are recorded. A minimum of two to three sporocarps of each
species should be collected as vouchers. If many sporocarps of a particular
species are present, a pin or other marker can be used as a reminder not to
collect the same species again on the next trip.

## Specimen Handling and Voucher Preparation

Small specimens, such as those of corticoid fungi and ascomycetes, are
collected whole. Large polypores are sectioned radially at a width of
approximately 1.0 to 1.5 cm to enhance drying and discourage mold. Specimens
are labeled with the log number and placed in appropriate containers. At the
end of the collecting day, specimens are transported to the field station for
sorting, describing, photographing, and drying. When possible, the genus or
species of the [[ectomycorrhizal-host-tree-partnerships]] is noted. Written descriptions, photographs, spore
prints, cultures, and macrochemical tests should be used to fully document each
specimen.

## Downed Log Sampling

Restricting quantitative sampling to the 0.1-hectare plots would exclude most
fungi found on large [[biodiversity-macrofungi-woody-substrata]]. A separate protocol addresses this: 30
logs are selected at each site in each of three decay classes. Class 1 logs
are relatively newly fallen, usually retaining bark. Class 2 logs are medium
rotten with bark fallen off; a knife can penetrate approximately 2 cm without
undue pressure. Class 3 logs are thoroughly rotten; the wood can be partly
destroyed with the fingers. Each log must be more than 20 cm in diameter and
more than 2 meters in length, lying on the ground.

## Log Documentation and Measurement

Each log is marked with a colored plastic band and given a number, and its
position is mapped. If possible, the tree is identified to genus or species.
The length and diameter of each log are measured and recorded. If the diameter
varies greatly from one end to the other, measurements are taken near both ends
and at the middle. Major branches are also measured. Generally, only two to
three sporocarps of each common species are collected from each log; multiple
sporocarps of species not or only infrequently encountered previously are
collected in greater numbers.

## Substratum and Host Classification

Recording the substratum for each specimen is essential for ecological analysis.
Key substratum categories include living tree (with part specified: trunk,
branch, root), dead wood (with decay class), soil, [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]], dung, and other
organic material. For wood-inhabiting fungi, the diameter class and decay stage
of the substratum should be recorded. Host tree species associations are
particularly important, as many macrofungi show strong [[fungal-endemism-host-specificity]]. Data
on the tree species, its condition, and the position of the fungus on the tree
provide critical ecological information.
