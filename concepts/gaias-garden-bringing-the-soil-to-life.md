# Bringing the Soil to Life

Soil is not merely an inert substrate for plants. It is alive -- the thin boundary between inanimate rock and the planet's green carpet where matter crosses and recrosses the line between living and dead. One key to a garden bursting with healthy plants, balanced insects, and thriving wildlife is to fill the soil with as much life as possible.

## The Soil Life Pyramid

Soil life forms the base of an ecological pyramid. Stacked upon this base are plants, then insects, and finally animals, each dependent on the creatures below. The broader the pyramid's base -- the greater the number and diversity of soil organisms -- the more plants will be supported, attracting more insects and animals. Diversity builds diversity.

A teaspoon of good pasture soil may contain a billion bacteria, a million fungi, and ten thousand amoebae. An acre of good pasture may support a half-ton of aboveground animals but contains 2 tons of worms and another 2 tons of bacteria, fungi, and soil animals below ground -- equivalent to eight to ten horses' worth of animals living beneath the surface.

## How Soil Life Creates Fertility

From a plant's perspective, the main role of soil organisms is to chew up matter that plants cannot eat and transform it into palatable food. Soil inhabitants furnish almost all the fertilizer that plants need through their activities of eating, excreting, reproducing, and dying.

Fertility comes from flow. A more vigorous soil life heaves more nutrients into the flux for plants to absorb. The process -- the briskness and depth of flows and interconnections -- is paramount, not just the presence of nutrients.

### The Leaf Decomposition Cycle

The decomposition of a single leaf illustrates soil's alchemy:

1. **Bacteria and fungi** colonize the moistened leaf, secreting enzymes that tear apart cellulose and lignin. Fungi hold a broad spectrum of enzymes able to digest lignin -- without them, the earth might be neck-deep in undecomposable tree trunks.
2. **Primary decomposers** -- earthworms, millipedes, pill bugs, springtails, mites, fly larvae, and enchytraeid worms -- shred the leaf into small scraps. Soil invertebrates principally comminute (physically reduce) litter, while their tunneling mixes leaf particles with soil, keeping fragments moist and palatable.
3. **Earthworms** carry fragments into burrows, grinding them with soil in their gizzard, where gut bacteria assist digestion. Worm casts coat burrows with fertile, organically enriched earth.
4. **A second wave of microbes** descends on the remains, snapping large molecules into small, edible fragments. Bacteria and fungi perform the real chemical alchemy, transforming cellulose, lignin, chitin, and keratin into simpler sugar-like components and ultimately into humus.
5. **Worm tunnels** loosen and aerate soil, letting rain penetrate deeper and roots extend to late-season moisture. Soil near worm burrows stays damp longer between rains.

Worms turn over up to 25 tons of soil per acre per year -- the equivalent of one inch of topsoil over Earth's land surface every ten years.

## Nature's Recycling Efficiency

In a northern forest, researchers found that of 365 kg of calcium in one hectare, only 8 kg (2 percent) was lost annually to runoff. The remaining 98 percent was recycled: raining down in falling leaves, decomposed by soil life, and transferred back to plant roots. This far exceeds the 30 percent recycling rate of urban programs or the 25-60 percent losses on fertilized agricultural land.

## Sheet Mulching: Instant Soil Building

Sheet mulching (also called lasagna gardening) is the fastest method to convert barren ground into fertile soil without tilling. The process layers organic materials directly on top of existing soil or lawn: first a weed-suppressing layer of overlapping cardboard or newspaper (4-6 sheets, thoroughly wetted), followed by alternating 2-3 inch layers of nitrogen-rich material (compost, manure, [[query-which-cover-crops-are-best-for-breaking-hardpan-and-compacted-soil]] one of the primary tools for [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]]**: Legumes and certain other species (alder, Russian olive, ceanothus) host symbiotic bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen
- **Carbon addition**: Grasses and non-legumes add carbon and build soil structure. Soil organisms consume 10 to 50 times more carbon than nitrogen, so cover crop mixes should be 60-90 percent non-legume
- **Mineral mining**: Deep-rooted species like chicory bring potassium, sulfur, calcium, and magnesium from subsoil to the surface
- **Soil opening**: Rapeseed, mustard, daikon radish, and alfalfa punch through compacted subsoil
- **Insect attraction**: Buckwheat, phacelia, fava beans, clovers, and vetch blossoms attract [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] builds humus deep in the soil -- something no plow can ever achieve. Many cover crops send roots 3 to 6 feet deep.

Interlocked loops like this reveal nature's complexity: the wet/dry cycle drives root-dieback, which in turn tempers the rhythm of plant and soil organism growth. The thirsty plants themselves, as they consume soil water, alter the wet/dry cycle. Wheels within wheels spin as humus, soil life, and plants all grow together.

### Nitrogen Fixation in Action

Legumes don't only release nitrogen when they die. During the wet/dry soil cycle, clover roots slough off along with their nitrogen-fixing nodules. Surrounding plants and microbes absorb these nutrients as roots and nodules decay. Research using radioactive tracers showed that 80 percent of nitrogen in interplanted ryegrass came from companion clover.

Ancient farmers knew this: Roman farm texts of Virgil and Cato both recommend plowing legumes into fallow fields. Ecological gardeners can take a lesson from prairie research: adding more legumes to a seed mix increases total species survival. Nitrogen-fixing species abound in early successional ecosystems -- when building soil or feeding hungry plants, go heavy on the nitrogen fixers.

## Sharing the Wealth of the Soil

A living, fertile soil is the cornerstone of a sustainable garden. By packing the growing earth with organic matter via thick mulch, self-renewing roots, and buried debris, gardeners beckon worms, beetles, mites, bacteria, fungi, and other helpers. Life builds on life. Whatever is planted in rich earth has a far greater chance of thriving.
