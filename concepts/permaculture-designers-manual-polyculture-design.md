# Polyculture Design

Source: Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Bill Mollison, 1988), Chapter 10

Updated: 2026-04-18

Polyculture is the cultivation of many plant and animal species together in a designed system that mimics natural ecosystems. In permaculture, polyculture replaces monoculture to achieve higher total yields, greater stability, reduced pest problems, and more efficient resource cycling.

## Principles of Polyculture

In a polyculture, we try to maximise the benefits of each species to the others. The number of productive, managed, and effectively-harvested species in a polyculture is decided by a complex of factors:

1. The number of people responsible for managing one or two of these crops or animal species (labour)
2. The proximity of the complex to village or settlement (zoning)
3. The relative cost-benefit balance on increasing inputs to optimum levels
4. The need for effective [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]] (harmonious ecological assemblies)
5. The method of marketing and processing
6. The total area which is controlled (larger areas demand increasing simplicity)
7. The maturity or stage of evolution of the plant system (older systems provide more niches)

## Species Richness

In practice, the gardens, walls, roof areas, trellis systems, and compounds of villages are the most complex and rich areas of cultivated species. A complex village situation can manage 200-400 species, including:

- Basic food species: 70-90
- Fodder provision: 10-30
- Medicinals and biocides: 20-50
- Structural and craft: 5-20
- Culinary herbs: 20-30
- Beverages and export specialties: 10-15
- Fuelwood and coppice: 12-30
- Special uses, sacred uses: 8-15
- Total cultivated species: 155-280
- Plus wild-gathered species: 40-80
- Total species utilised: 195-360

While the fiscal return peaks at about 6-8 species in a system, the nutritional-total yield system peaks at a much higher number of species.

## Establishing a Polyculture

If starting to evolve a permaculture on grasslands or compacted soils, the very first step is to thoroughly plan the site, and rip, swale, pit, or dam every area to be planted, ensuring maximum wet-season soil water storage to carry over to dry periods.

On loosened soils and in mulched swales, plant a mix of:
- Tree [[permaculture-designers-manual-nitrogen-fixing-trees]], fruits, bananas, papayas, arrowroot (Canna), cassava, sweet potato, and comfrey at 1-1.5 m spacing
- Acacia at 3 x 3 m spacing
- Banana at 2 x 2 m
- Fruits at 5 x 8 m
- Palms at 10 x 10 m
- Smaller species as gap fillers

All larger planting holes should be seeded with nasturtium, Dolichos, Haifa clover, broad bean (fava), buckwheat, Umbelliferae (dill, fennel), lupin, vetch, dun peas, chilies, pigeon pea, or any useful non-grass mix available. The end aim is to completely carpet and overshade the ground in the first 18-20 months.

## Mulch and Ground Cover

Ideally, dense plantings should be grass or hay mulched, using monsoon grasses, swamp grasses, and later the tops of arrowroot, comfrey, banana, Acacia, and green crop. Shade-loving species such as coffee and dry taro can be placed in any open spots.

Paths for access, openings for annual crop, bee plants on the edges, flowers, and fire-resistant "wet" ground covers such as comfrey, Tradescantia, Impatiens, and succulents can be placed as time goes on.

It is far better to occupy a quarter hectare thoroughly than to scatter trees over 2 ha, as production is higher and maintenance less, moisture is conserved, and [[permaculture-designers-manual-frost-protection]] is excluded.

## Species for Immediate Stabilisation

Have seed, divisions, cuttings, and potted plants ready before [[permaculture-designers-manual-rabbit-housing]] begin:

- Seed mixes: sunflower, millets, pulses, chard, parsley, lupin, clovers; parsnip, salsify, daikon radish, turnip; bulbs of lilies, grape hyacinth
- Roots: sunroot, comfrey, chicory, horseradish, ginger, sweet potato, turmeric
- Divisions: bamboo, banana grass, pampas grass, aloes, agave
- Cuttings: elderberry, willow, poplar, mulberry, Pride of Madeira
- Tubed seedlings: Acacia, Prosopis, tagasaste, Coprosma, pines, eucalypts

## Nitrogen Fixing Legumes in Polyculture

Leguminous trees such as Leucaena, Acacia, Albizia, Gliricidia, and Tephrosia may contain high nitrogen levels. Cut green material from such trees supplies much of the nitrogen for crops. It is necessary to inoculate trees with the correct root associates in the [[permaculture-designers-manual-plant-toxins-dryland]] or in the field.

Management considerations:
- Spacing: With shrubs and small trees, 0.5 m apart is best for foliage production, trimming height 0.5-1.5 m
- Season: Only in frost-free tropics can all-year trimming occur (4-5 cuts)
- Forage: Young coppice shoots may have higher metabolic poison levels than older shoots
- Shelter: Trees at 2-20 m spacing for seed production, bee forage, and in-crop shelter
- Replacement: Any sign of loss of vigour indicates need to replant

## Pioneering in Difficult Terrain

On landslide or volcanic areas, first pocket the area with soil-mulch mixtures (nut husks from coconut and macadamia are excellent). Species such as Inga edulis, Leucaena leucocephala, various Acacias, Scalesia pedunculata, Prosopis pallida, and legumes (Dolichos, Desmodium) prepare the area for palms, figs, and useful fruit trees. It is better to plant small assemblies than to space out a lot of species on their own.

## Fiscal vs Nutritional Yields

While a complex polyculture delights both the naturalist and the householder, it becomes difficult to control an extensive rich polyculture and collect its products. Very complex polycultures work best at small scale and with close attention from people. When analysing dollar economics, there will be an optimum number of species for broadscale cash yields; but analysing for total nutrition and total yield (ignoring dollar returns) indicates a different and richer species assembly.
