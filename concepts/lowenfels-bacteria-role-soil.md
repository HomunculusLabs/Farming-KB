# Bacteria and Their Role in Soil

## Overview

Bacteria are the most numerous organisms in soil -- no other organism has more
members, not even close. These single-celled prokaryotes appeared at least 3
billion years ago and are so small that 250,000 to 500,000 can fit inside the
period at the end of a sentence. They come in three basic shapes: coccus
(spherical), bacillus (rod-shaped), and spiral. A teaspoon of good garden soil
contains roughly a billion bacteria. Under laboratory conditions, a single
bacterium can produce 5 billion offspring in 12 hours, but natural conditions
(moisture requirements, predators, slower reproduction) keep populations in check.

## Primary Decomposers

Bacteria are among the earth's primary decomposers of organic matter, second only
to fungi. They decompose plant and animal material to ingest nitrogen, carbon
compounds, and other nutrients, which are then held immobilized inside their
bodies. Different kinds of soil bacteria survive on different food sources. Most
do best decomposing young, still-fresh "green" plant material, which contains lots
of sugars that are easier to digest than the complex carbon compounds of "brown"
material (leaves, wood). Bacteria take in food directly through their cell walls
via molecular transport -- membrane proteins act as pumps, using energy to push
nutrients in and waste products out.

## Feeding and Root Exudates

Root exudates are favorite foods for certain soil bacteria, and huge populations
concentrate in the rhizosphere, where bacteria also find nutrition from cells
sloughed off during root-tip growth. Bacteria use enzymes to break bonds holding
organic chains together before ingestion. They have adapted over millennia to
attack all manner of organic and even inorganic matter. Once nutrients are inside
the bacteria, they are locked up until the bacteria are consumed by protozoa or
nematodes, at which point excess nitrogen is released as ammonium waste in the
rhizosphere.

## Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Bacteria

Two main groups exist. Anaerobic bacteria live without oxygen; their by-products
include hydrogen sulfide (rotten eggs), butyric acid (vomit), ammonia, and
vinegar. Facultative anaerobes like E. coli prefer anaerobic environments but
can survive in aerobic conditions. Aerobic bacteria require air and are the
beneficial group. The actinomycetes (order Actinomycetales, genus Streptomyces)
produce the volatile chemicals that give soil its clean, earthy aroma. They grow
filaments almost like fungal hyphae and are particularly adept at decaying
cellulose and chitin -- difficult-to-digest carbon compounds in plant cell walls
and fungal/arthropod exoskeletons.

## The Nitrogen Cycle

Bacteria play crucial roles in the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria
(Azotobacter, Azospirillum, Clostridium) live free in soil, while Rhizobium
species live in root tissues of legumes, forming visible nodules. These convert
atmospheric N2 into plant-available ammonium. Nitrifying bacteria then convert
ammonium to nitrites (Nitrosomonas spp.) and nitrates (Nitrobacter spp.).
Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrogen salts back to atmospheric N2. Nitrifying
bacteria require pH above 7, which is maintained by the slightly alkaline
bacterial slime (biofilm) in areas with sufficient bacterial populations.

## Biofilms

Bacterial slime, or biofilm, is a matrix of sugars, proteins, and DNA. It
enables bacteria to stick to soil particles (binding them into aggregates), saves
bacteria from desiccation, provides transportation channels for nutrients and
wastes, and offers defense against antibiotics. Biofilm-protected colonies are
1,000 times more resistant to antibiotics and microbicides than individual
bacteria. Some bacteria squirt biofilm as a means of propulsion, though most
travel using whip-like flagella that operate like propellers.

## Nutrient Retention

Bacteria play a major role in plant nutrition by locking up nutrients that might
otherwise leach away. They retain nutrients in their cellular structures while
decomposing organic matter. Since bacteria are attached to soil particles,
nutrients remain in the soil near the root zone. Protozoa consume bacteria and
release excess nitrogen as ammonium waste in the rhizosphere. Without bacteria,
most applied fertilizer would drain through soil to the water table.

## Disease Suppression

Soil bacteria compete with pathogens for space and nutrients, keeping them in
check. High bacterial diversity means more nonpathogenic bacteria outcompeting
pathogenic bacteria. Some bacteria produce broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g.,
Pseudomonas produces phenazines to control fungal wheat diseases). Bacterial
activity also breaks down pollutants and toxins, including oil and gasoline
spills. A healthy soil food web's natural defenses are the best way to control
pathogens.

## Cellulose and Lignin Decay

Specialized bacteria like Cellulomonas carry cellulose-breaking enzymes, but
most bacteria reach their limit with lignin -- the tough brown component of bark
and woody materials. Lignin consists of chains of interlinked alcohols resistant
to most bacterial enzymes and is left for fungi to decay. This division of labor
between bacteria (simple sugars, green material) and fungi (complex compounds,
brown material) is fundamental to soil food web dynamics.
