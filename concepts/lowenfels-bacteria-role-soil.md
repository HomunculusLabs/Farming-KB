## Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Bacteria

Two main groups exist. Anaerobic bacteria live without oxygen; their by-products
include hydrogen sulfide (rotten eggs), butyric acid (vomit), ammonia, and
vinegar. Facultative anaerobes like E. coli prefer anaerobic environments but
can survive in aerobic conditions. Aerobic bacteria require air and are the

## The Nitrogen Cycle

Bacteria play crucial roles in the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria
(Azotobacter, Azospirillum, Clostridium) live free in soil, while Rhizobium
species live in root tissues of legumes, forming visible nodules. These convert
atmospheric N2 into plant-available ammonium. Nitrifying bacteria then convert
ammonium to nitrites (Nitrosomonas spp.) and nitrates (Nitrobacter spp.).
Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrogen salts back to atmospheric N2. Nitrifying
bacteria require pH above 7, which is maintained by the slightly alkaline
bacterial slime (biofilm) in areas with sufficient bacterial populations.

## Biofilms

Bacterial slime, or biofilm, is a matrix of sugars, proteins, and DNA. It
enables bacteria to stick to soil particles (binding them into aggregates), saves
bacteria from desiccation, provides transportation channels for nutrients and
wastes, and offers defense against antibiotics. Biofilm-protected colonies are
1,000 times more resistant to antibiotics and microbicides than individual
bacteria. Some bacteria squirt biofilm as a means of propulsion, though most
travel using whip-like flagella that operate like propellers.

## Nutrient Retention

Bacteria play a major role in plant nutrition by locking up nutrients that might
otherwise leach away. They retain nutrients in their cellular structures while
decomposing organic matter. Since bacteria are attached to soil particles,
nutrients remain in the soil near the root zone. Protozoa consume bacteria and
release excess nitrogen as ammonium waste in the rhizosphere. Without bacteria,
most applied fertilizer would drain through soil to the water table.
