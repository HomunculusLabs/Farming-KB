# Tissue Culture Method for Mushroom Spawn Production

## Overview

The tissue culture method for producing [[mushroom-spawn]] represents a landmark achievement in mycological technique and agricultural practice. Developed at the Bureau of Plant Industry, U.S. Department of Agriculture, around 1902, this method enabled growers to select specific mushroom varieties and propagate them as pure cultures—free from contamination by other fungi or bacteria. The technique transformed [[mushroom-cultivation]] from a chance-dependent art into a systematic, repeatable science.

## Scientific Background

### The Spore Germination Challenge

The initial approach to controlled [[spawn-production]] focused on [[spore]] germination. In 1899, while working in the laboratory, Miss Ferguson determined certain conditions governing spore germination, raising hopes that a method of using spores in spawn-making would be practicable. However, certain difficulties arose that could not be satisfactorily overcome:

- Spores of *[[agaricus-campestris]]* do not germinate readily
- Even with suitable stimulation, only a sporadic percentage of spores grow
- Methods of stimulating germination that were developed were far less easily applicable than the alternative tissue culture approach
- Many attempts by practical growers to develop spawn from spores by sowing gill portions of mature mushrooms in specially constructed beds were not satisfactory

### The Tissue Culture Discovery

While the spore approach was being pursued, the author made a critical observation: **fragments of growing mushrooms, obtained under aseptic conditions, may serve as the starting point for pure cultures of spawn**. This is based on the biological fact that a small piece of the inner tissue of a fresh mushroom will, when placed on any suitable sterile nutrient medium, promptly develop mycelium, yielding a pure culture of the spawn.

The principle is straightforward: the inner tissue of a living, healthy mushroom is naturally free from contamination by other organisms. If this tissue can be transferred to a sterile growing medium without introducing contaminants, it will grow into a pure culture of the desired mushroom strain.

## Making Pure Cultures: Detailed Procedure

### Equipment and Materials

- **Large test tubes or wide-mouthed bottles** for culture vessels
- **Cotton batting** for tube plugs
- **Sterilization equipment**: dry oven, steam boiler, or autoclave
- **Sterile instruments**: scalpels, forceps, needles
- **Growing medium**: manure, half-decayed leaves, or a mixture of leaf mould and well-fermented manure
- **Gas flame or alcohol lamp** for sterilizing instruments

### Step 1: Vessel Preparation

1. Clean test tubes or wide-mouthed bottles carefully
2. If possible, give a preliminary sterilization by **dry heat** (approximately 150°C for nearly an hour)
3. Plug the tubes with cotton batting, which prevents the entrance of germs from the air when carefully manipulated

### Step 2: Medium Preparation and Sterilization

1. Partially fill tubes (about two-thirds) with the growing medium
2. Insert a cotton plug in each tube
3. Sterilize using one of these methods:
   - **Autoclave**: About 15 pounds pressure for 15 minutes to half an hour
   - **Steam boiler or open water bath**: Boil for at least one hour on each of two or three successive days (intermittent sterilization at 100°C)

For containers more than about one inch in diameter, longer steaming times are required, and the exact duration should be carefully determined in advance.

### Step 3: Selection of Source Mushrooms

This is the critical stage where **selection** may be made. The grower should procure from a bed of mushrooms in full bearing specimens representing the most desirable qualities. Selection criteria include:

- **Size**: Large mushrooms preferred for heavy-yielding varieties
- **Quality**: Firm texture, good color, desirable flavor characteristics
- **Prolificness**: Evidence of abundant production from the parent bed
- **Other characteristics**: Depending on breeding goals—long bearing period, resistance to higher temperatures, etc.

### Step 4: Tissue Extraction and Inoculation

1. **Wash the mushroom** first to remove dust and surface contaminants
2. Select specimens **as young as possible**; those showing the **veil still intact** are especially desirable
3. Sterilize a scalpel or pair of forceps by passing through a gas flame or alcohol lamp flame
4. Break the mushroom open **longitudinally**
5. Remove small pieces of the **internal tissue** using the sterile instrument
6. Transfer tissue pieces to the sterile tubes without contacting any unsterilized surface
7. Replace the cotton plug immediately

### Step 5: Incubation

1. Place inoculated tubes out of the dust
2. Maintain temperature at approximately that of an ordinary living room
3. In several days, slight growth should be evident from the tissue if conditions have been perfectly sterile
4. In a week or more, growth should become very evident
5. In three weeks, the mold-like development of mycelium should spread to practically all parts of the medium in the tube

### Aseptic Technique Essentials

- Tubes and apparatus should be held in a more or less horizontal position while open, to reduce contamination risk
- Work should be done in a room free from currents of air
- The cotton plug, if removed, should not contact any unsterilized substances
- Sterile needles or scalpels must be used for all transfers

## From Pure Culture to Commercial Spawn

### Using Pure Cultures to Inoculate Bricks

When the tubes are thoroughly "run" (fully colonized by mycelium), the contents may be removed and used in spawning bricks. The contents of one large tube may spawn several bricks when carefully employed.

### Transfer Limitations

An important principle: **no further transfers should be made from inoculated bricks to other bricks** under any circumstances in spawn making. Such continuous transference is injurious to the vigor of the spawn and diminishes the quantity of mushrooms produced. The author later came to regard it as wiser to use larger containers for the spawn and make no transfers whatever.

### Improved Growing Medium

A more rapid growth is obtained if **leaf mould and well-fermented manure are mixed together** as a substratum for the cultures, rather than using either material alone.

## Selection and Variety Maintenance

### Advantages of Pure Culture Methods

The advantages for systematic variety improvement are fundamental:

- **Any vigorous, disease-free specimen** can serve as the source of a pure culture, whether from fields, woods, or commercial beds
- **Varieties can be perpetuated** indefinitely once a desirable strain is identified
- **Individual differences** indicating a potentially permanent desirable characteristic may be selected and propagated
- **Stable characteristics** can be maintained through successive seasons

### Testing for "Running Out"

There was initial concern that even with tissue culture methods, "running out" (gradual deterioration of spawn vigor) might occur. However, the author tested one strain for five years by continuous propagation from season to season through tissue cultures, and it consistently showed high yield and quality. It was concluded that "running out" would more likely result from improper care of the pure cultures rather than from any inherent limitation of the tissue culture method.

### Comparison with Analogous Systems

The concern about running out has analogues in other mycological systems—for example, continuous culture of certain pathogenic fungi in the laboratory can materially lower their pathogenicity. This phenomenon underscores the importance of proper technique and periodic re-selection from high-performing specimens.

## The Broader Significance

The tissue culture method was the primary cause of the rapid development of mushroom growing in the United States in the early 20th century. By enabling reliable selection, propagation, and distribution of superior mushroom varieties, it transformed the industry from one dependent on chance and mystery to one built on scientific principles and systematic improvement. The method remains the foundation of modern commercial spawn production worldwide.
