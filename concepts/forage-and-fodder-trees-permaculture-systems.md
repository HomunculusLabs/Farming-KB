# Forage and Fodder Trees in Permaculture Systems

Forage and fodder trees are among the most strategically important plant
categories in permaculture design. They serve multiple functions simultaneously:
providing livestock feed, improving soil through nitrogen fixation and leaf
litter, offering shade and shelter, producing timber and fuel, and supporting
permaculture insectary plants guide populations. The
inclusion of fodder trees in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]]
and [[mollison-designers-food-forest-succession-design]] designs
transforms grazing systems from degradative to regenerative.

## The Role of Trees in Livestock Systems

Conventional grazing systems often degrade land through continuous exposure
of soil to hooves, sun, and rain. Integrating trees into grazing systems
addresses multiple problems simultaneously. Tree canopies moderate
temperature extremes for livestock, reducing heat stress and improving
animal welfare. Root systems penetrate deep soil layers, bringing nutrients
to the surface through leaf fall. Fallen leaves add organic matter and
create favorable conditions for soil organisms.

Trees can be arranged into lock-up fodder systems that allow trees time to
recover from grazing while avoiding the labor-intensive cut-and-carry
method. Well-fenced hedgerows can be constantly grazed, providing a
self-renewing fodder bank that requires minimal management once
established.

## Key Fodder Tree Species

### Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus palmensis)

permaculture-tagasaste|Tagasaste is the benchmark fodder tree in
permaculture systems. Native to the Canary Islands, it grows up to 7 meters
tall and produces foliage containing 17 to 31 percent crude protein on a dry
matter basis. It is a [[permaculture-plants-nitrogen-fixing-trees]] capable of productive growth on as little as 300mm annual rainfall.

Stock carrying capacity research at Margaret River, Western Australia, showed
that tagasaste can support up to 35 sheep per hectare by year six, compared
with a declining capacity of only 1 sheep per hectare on unimproved pasture
over the same period. The edible yield increases from 2.7 tonnes of dry
matter per hectare in year two to 19 tonnes by year six.

Tagasaste is readily eaten by sheep, cattle, poultry, pigs, horses, and
rabbits, and can comprise a major portion of their diet. It also provides
valuable winter bee forage and produces seed that is excellent free-range
poultry forage. As a [[permaculture-pioneer-species]], it
serves as a nurse crop for longer-term establishment trees.

### Honey Locust (Gleditsia triacanthos)

[[honey-locust-pod-fodder-timber-production]] produces pods
with 14.3 to 16 percent protein and 15.5 to 26.5 percent sugar, making them
a high-energy livestock feed. The pods fall from female trees in autumn and
are readily consumed by cattle, sheep, goats, and pigs. Propagation from
seed is similar to carob|carob, though honey locust seed
requires scarification to germinate.

### Mulga (Acacia aneura)

Mulga is an outstanding Australian fodder tree surviving on 200 to 500mm
annual rainfall across all states except Victoria and Tasmania. Some trees
reach 9 meters with straight trunks. During drought conditions, mulga
provides critical emergency fodder for cattle and sheep. The foliage is
palatable and nutritious, and the tree's deep root system allows it to
remain productive when surface pastures have completely dried.

### Leucaena (Leucaena leucocephala)

[[mollison-designers-leucaena-multipurpose-nitrogen-tree]] is a
tropical legume tree used extensively in alley cropping systems. Rows of
leucaena are intercropped with corn, tobacco, yams, and rice, with the
trees regularly pruned to provide mulch and fodder. Leucaena foliage
contains mimosine, a toxic amino acid, so it should constitute no more
than 30 percent of the long-term diet. Sheep fed exclusively on leucaena
for a few days develop symptoms of toxicity, but rotational grazing with
grass species eliminates this risk.

### White Mulberry (Morus alba)

The [[permaculture-mulberry]] serves a dual role as a fodder
tree and the primary food source for silkworms in sericulture systems. The
leaves are highly nutritious and palatable to livestock. Mulberry grows
rapidly, coppices readily, and produces abundant leaf biomass that can be
harvested multiple times per growing season.

## Designing Fodder Tree Systems

Successful integration of fodder trees requires attention to several design
principles. Trees should be planted at appropriate densities for the
targeted stocking rate, with alleys wide enough for machinery access if
needed. Nurse species like tagasaste can be interplanted with longer-term
fodder trees to accelerate establishment. Fodder trees should be chosen
to fill seasonal feed gaps, with species selected for winter, summer, and
autumn production to extend the year-round availability of green feed.

Integration with bee forage is an important secondary function. Many
fodder trees, including tagasaste and various Acacia species, flower
during periods when little else is available, supporting honey production
and pollinator populations that benefit the entire farm system.

## Nutritional Considerations

When designing fodder tree systems, growers should be aware of toxicity
issues. Some otherwise excellent fodder species contain secondary compounds
that limit their use. Leucaena's mimosine, tree lupin's alkaloids, and
the tannins found in many Acacia species all require management through
appropriate species selection, grazing rotation, or processing methods.
Testing new fodder species with small numbers of livestock before large-
scale planting is always advisable.
