## Economics of Winter Greenhouse Production

Understanding the economics of winter greenhouse vegetable production is essential for making informed crop choices, setting realistic income targets, and building a sustainable small-farm business. Eliot Coleman"s financial framework at [[four-season-farm]] provides concrete benchmarks for unheated and cool greenhouse production.

## Revenue Targets Per Square Foot

When beginning commercial winter production, Coleman established target returns per square foot for the farm's 12,000 square feet of greenhouse space:

### Unheated (Cold) Greenhouses

- **Three crops per year** per square foot
- **Target: $5 per square foot per year**
- This is approximately one-third of the expected return per square foot in large heated commercial greenhouses growing flowers
- No heating expenses offset the lower gross revenue

### Cool Greenhouses

- **Six crops per year** per square foot (double the cold-house rotation)
- **Target: $10 per square foot per year**

### Per-Crop Revenue Requirement

Under either system, each individual crop must return **just over $1.50 per square foot** to meet annual targets. These computations serve as a practical filter for evaluating which crops are paying their way and which should be dropped.

## Realistic Timeline for Profitability

Coleman gave himself a **five-year ramp-up period** to reach target gross revenue, acknowledging the steep learning curve of winter production. Early years included many costly mistakes:

- Poor timing of plantings
- Poor choice of cultivars
- Inadequate knowledge of regrowth characteristics
- Many square feet devoted to trial crops that never produced salable harvests

Despite these setbacks, income increase was steady, and the farm eventually achieved a combined gross return from field and greenhouse crops of **$80,000 per acre per year**.

## Gross vs. Net Income

While $80,000 per acre gross sounds impressive, Coleman emphasizes that **net income, not gross income, is what makes a living for the farmer**. Much of the farm's current effort in developing new tools (such as improved seeders and harvesters) is aimed at improving net income by reducing labor costs.

### Key Economic Insight

The economics of winter greenhouse production work because:

1. **No heating costs** in unheated houses—the [[passive-solar-design]] and row-cover insulation eliminate the largest expense of conventional greenhouse operations.
2. **Intensive spacing** maximizes revenue per square foot through precision seeding.
3. **Premium pricing** for unmatched freshness and quality justifies higher per-unit costs.
4. **Year-round production** spreads fixed costs (greenhouse infrastructure, land) across more harvest cycles.

## Crop Economics and Decision-Making

The per-square-foot revenue target provides a clear decision framework:

- Any crop that cannot reasonably return $1.50+ per square foot should be replaced with one that can.
- Trial crops are an investment in knowledge but represent a direct cost against targets.
- Regrowth characteristics matter economically—a crop that can be harvested multiple times from a single planting spreads the planting and seed costs across more revenue events.

## The Biotic Farming Vision

Coleman grounds his economic approach in the philosophy expressed by Aldo Leopold in *A Sand County Almanac* (1949): poundage is no measure of food value. Products of fertile soil may be qualitatively as well as quantitatively superior. Pouring imported fertility onto depleted soils may boost tonnage without improving nutritional quality.

This philosophy has direct economic implications:

- **Quality differentiation** allows premium pricing that compensates for lower volume.
- **Soil investment** in compost and careful management produces crops that command higher prices.
- **Local freshness** provides a competitive advantage that industrial-scale shipping cannot match.
- **Nutritional density** of properly grown crops adds health-value that supports price premiums.

## Scaling Considerations

### Labor and Efficiency

- The original operation was two people (Coleman and his wife Barbara). They expanded to five summer employees (fewer in winter) due to demand but insist on maintaining quality standards.
- Efficiency improvements are continuously pursued to avoid expanding staff further—each new hire reduces per-worker productivity margins.
- Infrastructure investments (larger sink pipes, better tools) that reduce labor time per unit have high ROI.

### Startup Constraints

- At the beginning, a small business cannot afford a separate delivery vehicle. Coleman's initial delivery capacity was 48 boxes in the back of a Subaru.
- Growth to the point of needing multiple delivery trips justified purchasing a used van.
- Reusable cedar boxes and compostable cellophane reduced ongoing packaging costs while building brand identity.

## Additional Revenue Opportunities

Creative marketing approaches can supplement regular greenhouse income. One example: when the farm had extra outdoor carrots in late fall, "Thanksgiving Gift Packs" helped **triple sales** for that period. Such seasonal promotions leverage existing production capacity and customer relationships to generate incremental revenue without additional growing costs.
