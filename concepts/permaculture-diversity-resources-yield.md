# Permaculture Principles: Diversity, Resources, and Yield

The core intellectual framework of [[permaculture]] rests on a set of principles, directives, and observations derived from the study of natural systems. These principles govern how energy flows through systems, how resources are categorized and managed, what diversity truly means in functional terms, and how yield is defined and maximized through design.

## The Cooperative Ethic

Permaculture is explicitly a cooperative, not a competitive, system. The movement requires rapid dissemination of knowledge — no patenting, no hoarding of information, no protecting of jobs. The scale of [[global-ecological-crisis-soils-and-deforestation]] demands that hundreds of experts be set loose on the ground, each creating productive systems in their own bioregions.

Mollison identifies a necessary ethical shift: "to stop admiring the people who have money." The educational system must shift from passive learning to active application. All permaculture methodologies arose from observation of natural systems, but the critical "mind twist" is recognizing that this knowledge can be actively applied — moving from passive understanding to active design intervention.

## Diversity: Connections, Not Numbers

The most commonly misunderstood concept in permaculture is diversity. Diversity is not about the number of elements or species in a system. It is about the **number of [[component-analysis-and-functional-connections]] between elements**. A system with 3,000 species but few interactions between them is less diverse (and less stable) than a system with 30 species that are richly interconnected.

### The Greenhouse-Chicken Example

Mollison catalogued 129 beneficial connections arising from placing just two elements together — a greenhouse and a chicken coop. The greenhouse provides warmth for chickens in winter; chickens provide body heat and carbon dioxide for plant growth; chicken manure fertilizes greenhouse plants; the greenhouse shelters the coop from wind; scratching chickens aerate greenhouse beds; and so on through dozens of synergistic interactions. Each connection adds stability, productivity, and resilience to the system.

### Diversity Without Harmony Is Not Stability

It is possible to have diverse interests that are not beneficial. Social groups, ecological communities, and agricultural systems can be diverse yet chaotic and unproductive. Diversity of itself does not confer stability. What matters is the **guild** — a group of elements that work harmoniously together through beneficial connections.

### Designing for Connections

[[permaculture-design]] involves establishing rules for placement based on orientation, zonation, and interactions. The goal is not to maximize species count but to maximize beneficial functional relationships. There are physical relationships, energy-exchange relationships, defensive relationships (protection from wind, pests, or sun), and structural relationships (trellising, shade provision).

## Energy: Source, Storage, and Sink

### The Energy Flow Principle

The Earth receives a fairly constant energy input from the sun. Between the energy source and the energy sink (where energy is ultimately lost or dissipated), designers intervene. The more useful storages that can be created between source and sink, and the more complexity that can be built into the flow, the more effective the design.

### Water as Energy

The principle applies directly to water. We can store water where it falls or let it leave. If we let it leave, it becomes unavailable and must be recovered at great expense. Engineers typically dam valleys where water collects at its lowest point — where it is least useful. Storing water at its source (on ridge tops and upper slopes) allows it to pass through multiple productive systems — forests, gardens, pastures — before reaching the valley.

The closer to the source that designers can intervene, the greater the network of uses they can create. Not the amount of rainfall matters, but the number of duties that water is induced to perform.

### Energy Cannot Be Destroyed

Energy can be transferred between forms but cannot disappear or be created. The choice is in what type of flow is allowed through the system — stored or released. Energies that are unusable in a mechanical sense may be perfectly usable in a biological sense. Permaculture therefore requires both biological and mechanical storages.

## Resource Categories

Mollison identifies five categories of resources based on their response to use:

### 1. Resources That Increase With Use
These resources grow or improve the more they are used. Some forms of browse, certain categories of plants and animals that increase each other through interaction, and information all fall into this category. Using them actively enhances the system.

### 2. Resources Unaffected by Use
A beautiful view can be appreciated all day without degradation. Information, when shared, is preserved rather than consumed (though Mollison's editor Dan Hemenway notes that information is specifically *preserved* by use — unused information is forgotten).

### 3. Resources That Decrease If Not Used
Quick-turnover resources like annual grass pastures decline if not grazed or harvested. Firewood in fire-prone ecosystems accumulates as dangerous fuel load if not regularly harvested. These resources require active management to maintain their productive state.

### 4. Finite Resources (Decrease With Use)
Most conventional resources fall here — soil, fossil fuels, minerals. They decrease when used and must be carefully managed. The critical insight is that continued use beyond the point of productivity causes system collapse. Whether the resource is money or manure, you can put too much of it in. Beyond a threshold, yield decreases and eventually lethal factors emerge.

### 5. Resources That Decrease Everything Else
Uranium, plutonium, dioxins — resources whose use actively degrades the broader resource base. Using them makes everything else worse. These are fundamentally anti-design resources that should be avoided entirely.

## The Yield Concept

Yield in permaculture is not measured as pounds of tomatoes or bushels of grain. It is defined as **the sum of useful energy stores** — the total of energy conserved and generated within the system. This includes food, fiber, water, soil, fertility, biodiversity, knowledge, and social capital.

### Yield Is a Function of Design

Yield is theoretically unlimited — there is no system where better design cannot increase the yield. As design understanding improves, yield increases. This makes yield a function of the designer's comprehension of the system and its elements.

### The Global Biological-Chemical Cycle

Much depends on maintaining the global cycles of essential elements: carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, sulphur, and phosphorus. Disruption of these cycles — through deforestation, pollution, or unsustainable extraction — undermines the foundation upon which all yield depends.

### The Over-Run Thesis

A key statement governing resource use: "Our ability to change the face of the Earth increases at a faster rate than our ability to foresee the consequences of that change." This principle argues for extreme caution and for designing systems that are resilient to unforeseen consequences.

## Tidiness vs. Creative Disorder

Mollison makes a sharp distinction between order and tidiness. **Tidiness** is a symptom of frontal brain damage — it is mechanical, rigid, and produces "straight up and down" landscapes with straight rows. The British garden is a sign of extraordinary tidiness and functional disorder.

**Creativity** is symptomatic of a whole brain and is usually a disordered affair. The tolerance for disorder is one of the few healthy signs in life. If you can tolerate disorder, you are probably healthy. Creativity is seldom tidy.

What permaculture seeks is **creative disorder** — not chaos, but a functional complexity where elements interact in beneficial ways that a tidy, monocultural system cannot achieve.

## Necessitous, Harmful, and Beneficial Acts

Tribal lore prescribes carrying out only necessitous acts — non-necessitous behavior tends to be destructive. Every action should be justified by necessity. Mollison recounts that around 1952, he decided as an experiment not to cut down a tree unless he had to — and he never had to. The constraint of necessity forced creative solutions that a more profligate approach would have missed.

## The Landscape Responds

A fundamental principle of permaculture is that **living systems push back harder than they are pushed**. This is not simple Newtonian equal-and-opposite reaction but an escalating response. If you push someone out the door, they re-enter with a pitchfork, not just pushing back but ready to do real damage.

### Ecological Examples

- **Fish and algae**: When fish are placed with palatable algae, they consume the preferred species until it is gone, then the unpalatable algae proliferate, starving out the fish. Fish eats algae; algae destroys fish.
- **Cattle and pasture**: Cattle preferentially graze their favorite plants, which allows unpalatable (and sometimes poisonous) species to increase, eventually closing the landscape to cattle. The landscape actively responds to [[gadd-mycelial-network-resilience-grazing-pressure]].

This principle means that every intervention in a living system produces consequences that are disproportionate and often opposite to the intended outcome. Good design works with these responses rather than against them.

## Niches and Design

The number of species a system can support depends on the number of niches available, and the number of niches depends on the design of the system. The number of breeding pairs of pigeons on a cliff depends on the number of ledges — it is easy to increase the ledges.

Often, what limits yield is not the basic factor of food (food ceilings are rare) but some other factor entirely. There may be tons of acorns around an environment with nothing eating them — the limiting factor is not food but access, habit, or some other condition that design could address.

## Density and Genetic Health

The probability of species extinction is greatest at both very high and very low population densities. High density causes rapid transmission of disease through exhaustion of critical resources. Low density is more difficult to observe but equally critical — small populations lose [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] and become vulnerable to random events.

No human society dependent on its own genetic health can persist below approximately 300 individuals, and not even at that number without rigorous genetic management. High-density populations accumulate genetic disasters and mutations. Both extremes are dangerous, and the factor of number is one that most [[holmgren-community-design-and-intentional-communities]] ignore.
