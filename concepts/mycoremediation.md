# Mycoremediation

Mycoremediation is the use of fungi to degrade, sequester, or otherwise remove
environmental contaminants from soil, water, and other substrates. Coined and
popularized by mycologist Paul Stamets, the term describes a suite of
bioremediation strategies that harness the natural enzymatic capabilities of
mycelial networks to break down pollutants that are resistant to conventional
cleanup methods. Fungi produce a remarkable array of extracellular enzymes,
including lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases, which are
capable of degrading complex organic molecules including many persistent
environmental toxins.

## Mechanisms of Fungal Degradation

Fungi degrade contaminants through several complementary mechanisms. Enzymatic
breakdown is the primary pathway: white rot fungi such as Phanerochaete
chrysosporium secrete lignin-degrading enzymes that incidentally oxidize a wide
range of aromatic pollutants, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs),
polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), and various dyes and explosives. Because these
enzymes are extracellular and nonspecific in their action, they can attack
pollutants that bacteria cannot access, particularly large, insoluble, or
recalcitrant molecules. Biosorption is another mechanism, where fungal cell walls
bind and concentrate heavy metals such as lead, cadmium, mercury, and arsenic
through chelation with chitin, glucans, and other structural polymers.
Bioaccumulation goes further by actively transporting metals into fungal tissue.
Additionally, mycorrhizal fungi can alter the chemical speciation of contaminants
in the rhizosphere, making them more or less bioavailable to plants.

## Applications

### Petroleum Hydrocarbon Cleanup

One of the most dramatic demonstrations of mycoremediation involves the cleanup
of petroleum-contaminated soils. Stamets and collaborators have shown that
inoculation of oil soaked substrates with oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)
mycelium can significantly accelerate the breakdown of diesel fuel, motor oil,
and other petroleum products. The mycelium colonizes the contaminated matrix,
secreting enzymes that fragment hydrocarbon chains into simpler, less toxic
compounds. In field trials, mycelially treated soil showed visible fungal
fruiting and subsequent recolonization by plants and invertebrates, whereas
untreated control plots remained barren. The process is particularly effective
when combined with biostimulation techniques such as nutrient amendment and
aeration.

### Heavy Metal Sequestration

Certain fungi exhibit strong affinity for heavy metals, making them useful for
remediating mining tailings, industrial effluent, and contaminated groundwater.
Species of Pisolithus, Suillus, and other mycorrhizal fungi can accumulate
metals in their fruiting bodies, effectively concentrating and removing them
from the substrate. Oyster mushrooms have demonstrated the ability to
biosorb cadmium, lead, and chromium from aqueous solutions. Spent mushroom
substrate from commercial cultivation operations has been investigated as a
low-cost biosorbent material for treating wastewater.

### Pesticide and Herbicide Degradation

Fungal enzymes can degrade organophosphate insecticides, carbamates, triazine
herbicides, and other agricultural chemicals. White rot fungi have been shown
to mineralize DDT, atrazine, and glyphosate under laboratory conditions. The
non-specific nature of ligninolytic enzymes makes them broadly effective against
xenobiotic compounds that share structural features with lignin.

### Chemical Warfare Agent Degradation

Stamets has proposed and provided preliminary evidence that certain fungi can
degrade chemical warfare agents such as sarin and VX nerve agents. This
application emerged from Defense Department funded research exploring biological
countermeasures to bioterrorism. The enzymatic pathways involved include
phosphotriesterases and organophosphate hydrolases that can hydrolyze the
phosphoester bonds characteristic of nerve agents.

## Advantages and Limitations

Mycoremediation offers several advantages over conventional remediation: it is
relatively low cost, can be applied in situ without excavation, works under a
wide range of environmental conditions, and produces no secondary waste streams.
However, it also has limitations. Degradation rates are typically slower than
physical or chemical methods, and performance depends heavily on environmental
factors such as temperature, moisture, pH, nutrient availability, and the
presence of competing microorganisms. Not all contaminants are equally
susceptible to fungal degradation, and the metabolic end products must be
characterized to ensure they are not themselves toxic. Scaling laboratory
results to field conditions has proven challenging, and regulatory frameworks for
mycoremediation are still under development.
