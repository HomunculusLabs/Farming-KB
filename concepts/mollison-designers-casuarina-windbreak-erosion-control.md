## Species Overview

Key Casuarina species referenced by Mollison:

- Casuarina equisetifolia: coastal she-oak; salt-tolerant, fast-growing,
  reaching 15-25m; excellent for coastal windbreaks and sand stabilisation
- Casuarina cunninghamiana: river she-oak; found along waterways, grows
  to 20-30m; excellent for riparian planting and farm windbreaks in
  inland areas
- Casuarina glauca: swamp she-oak; tolerates waterlogging and saline
  soils; useful for reclamation of degraded land
- Allocasuarina species: smaller she-oaks (5-12m) suited to hedgerows,
  wildlife habitat, and smaller properties

All Casuarina species share distinctive needle-like branchlets (often
mistaken for leaves) and a conifer-like growth habit that makes them
exceptionally effective wind filters.

## Nitrogen Fixation

Casuarina forms a symbiotic relationship with Frankia bacteria in root
nodules, fixing atmospheric nitrogen and converting it to forms available
to plants. This nitrogen fixation capacity rivals that of leguminous trees
like [[acacia]], carob, and locust, and Leucaena.

Mollison emphasises several advantages of Casuarina as a nitrogen fixer:

- Frankia-nodulated trees fix nitrogen across a wider pH range than
  Rhizobium-nodulated legumes, performing well in acidic and alkaline
  soils alike
- Casuarina leaf litter is nitrogen-rich and breaks down relatively
  quickly, contributing significant nitrogen to the soil surface
- [[permaculture-designers-manual-windbreak-species]] because its dense, conifer-like
foliage provides year-round wind filtration. Unlike deciduous windbreak
trees that lose effectiveness in winter, Casuarina maintains its
protective canopy year-round.

Windbreak [[permaculture-design-principles]] using Casuarina:

- Height: plant 2-3 rows of Casuarina as the windward component of a
  multi-row windbreak; the tallest row should be on the windward edge
- Density: 2-4 metre spacing within rows creates a dense barrier; the
  foliage is sufficiently fine-textured to filter wind without creating
  severe turbulence downwind
- Length: windbreaks should extend 10-15 times the height of the trees
  beyond the area being protected to account for wind curling around the
  ends
- Distance: the protected zone extends approximately 10-15 times the
  height of the windbreak downwind, so a 20m Casuarina belt protects
  a zone 200-300m long
- Composition: combine Casuarina with understory species in a multi-strata
  windbreak; the lower layers catch wind that passes through the canopy

## Erosion Control

Casuarina excels at stabilising soils on slopes, riverbanks, and coastal
dunes. Its erosion control mechanisms include:

- Deep and extensive root systems that bind soil to depths of 3-5 metres,
  anchoring slopes that would otherwise fail during heavy rain
- Dense surface root mats that hold topsoil and resist sheet erosion
- High tolerance for waterlogged, saline, and nutrient-poor soils,
  allowing establishment on degraded sites where other trees fail
- Rapid growth providing quick ground cover; Casuarina equisetifolia
  reaches 3-5m within 3 years from seed
- Leaf litter that forms a protective mulch layer on the soil surface,
  reducing raindrop impact and promoting infiltration

Mollison recommends Casuarina for stabilising dam walls, road cuttings,
gully heads, and coastal foredunes where erosion threatens infrastructure
or productive land.

## Mulch and Fodder

Casuarina provides useful mulch and limited fodder:

- Mulch: the fine branchlets (needle-like cladodes) that shed naturally
  form a nitrogen-rich surface mulch. Pruned branches can be chipped for
  concentrated mulch application around garden plants and fruit trees.
- Fodder: Casuarina branchlets have moderate protein content (8-12%)
  and are browsed by cattle, sheep, and goats in times of feed shortage.
  They are not a primary fodder species but provide emergency forage
  during droughts when other feed is scarce.
- Bedding: fallen branchlets provide clean, aromatic bedding for poultry
  and livestock.

## Timber and Fuel

Casuarina produces dense, hard wood (specific gravity 0.8-1.0) that
sinks in water. Uses include:

- Firewood: extremely dense and hot-burning; one of the best firewood
  species in the tropics, producing high heat with low smoke when seasoned
- Fence posts: naturally durable in the ground due to high tannin content;
  posts last 10-20 years without treatment
- Tool handles: the hard, straight wood makes excellent handles for
  tools and implements
- Craft wood: the fine grain and natural finish suit turnery and carving
- Charcoal: Casuarina charcoal is high-quality and was historically used
  in iron smelting in Australia

## Establishment

Casuarina is easily established from seed or nursery seedlings:

- Seed: tiny winged seeds are sown in nursery beds; germination occurs
  within 2-4 weeks. Seedlings are ready for transplanting at 20-40cm
  height, typically 3-6 months after sowing.
- Direct seeding: successful on sandy soils where weed competition is
  low; broadcast seed into prepared ground at the start of the wet season
