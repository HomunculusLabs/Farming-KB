# Mycoremediation of Soil Erosion and Oil Spills

Using fungal mycelium to control soil erosion, filter contaminated water, and
remediate hydrocarbon-based pollution through extracellular enzyme production and
substrate colonization.

## Soil Erosion Control with Mycelium

### The Problem of Soil Erosion

Soil erosion is a major environmental concern affecting agricultural productivity,
water quality, and ecosystem stability. Conventional erosion control methods include
geotextile fabrics, straw mulching, and chemical stabilizers, but [[mycoremediation]]
offers a biological alternative that can simultaneously improve soil structure and
filter contaminants.

### Constructing Erosion Control Units

Students and researchers can construct small-scale biological erosion control units
in trays or containers by planting varying ground covers, plants, and fungi in an
appropriate substrate. Key principles include:

- **Consistent soil base**: All units use the same soil to ensure comparability
- **Root establishment period**: Allow 2+ weeks for plants to root and fungi to
  colonize before testing
- **Control groups**: Set up identical containers with bare soil as baseline
- **Replication**: Run trials in replicates, recording and averaging data

### Water Run Simulation

To simulate site runoff, a "water run" channel is constructed — a long trough angled
at the desired slope and filled with [[mycelium|myceliated]] mushroom biomass. The
procedure involves:

1. Measuring the rate of flow using clean water before introducing contaminated water
2. Adding a known volume of water at the upper channel
3. Collecting runoff at the base in a collection tub
4. Timing the runoff and measuring the collected volume (should be near-identical to
   the original input)

### Retention Time and Contact Time

Water filtration in mycoremediation experiments is based on retention and contact
time — the duration that water interacts with the mycelium in the treatment zone.
Rate of flow directly indicates contact time, making it a critical measurement for
evaluating remediation effectiveness.

### Treatment Comparisons

Erosion treatments tested in comparative experiments include:

| Treatment | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Bare soil | Control — no intervention |
| Erosion fabric | Commercial geotextile |
| Shredded wheat straw | Organic mulch layer |
| Mushroom substrate | Myceliated fungal biomass |

### Measuring Erosion

Silt and soil erosion levels are measured by **dry weight** after each water run.
The ideal treatment minimizes or eliminates silt and soil runoff. Students can also
analyze cost-effectiveness by comparing material costs against erosion prevention
performance.

## Oil Spill Remediation with Fungi

### How Fungi Degrade Hydrocarbons

Fungi remediate oil spills using their extracellular enzymes to break apart and
cleave hydrocarbon molecules into smaller, more biodegradable units. This degradation
occurs in a sequence where fungi and bacteria work cooperatively. The mechanism works
because hydrocarbons are chemically similar in architecture to [[lignin]] — the woody
component of wood that fungi naturally decompose. This structural similarity allows
fungi to adapt and begin decomposing oils relatively quickly, typically within a few
weeks.

The process begins when fungal mycelium exhausts its natural food source (usually
prepared substrate such as spent oyster mushroom media) and shifts to metabolizing
available hydrocarbons as an alternative carbon source.

### Classroom Oil Spill Experiment

A mock oil spill can be set up using common materials:

**Materials needed:**
- Small aquarium
- Plastic bins with drilled lids
- Shredded dried grass (hay or straw)
- Vegetable oil (motor oil requires environmental permits)
- Colonized oyster mushroom mycelium (cardboard sheets, prepared 2 weeks ahead)
- Large metal spoon or stick for stirring

**Procedure:**
1. Fill the aquarium approximately half full with water
2. Pour a measured amount of oil onto the water surface
3. Add shredded dried grass, which floats, and stir with a spoon
4. The oil adheres to the plant matter
5. Remove oiled plant matter and layer it between colonized cardboard sheets in bins
6. Pack tightly, cover with lids that have holes for gas exchange

### Observing the Remediation Process

Students inspect bins weekly by gently peeling back layers to photograph and observe
the mycelium in action. Key observations include:

- **Mycelial colonization**: White fungal growth spreading through oiled material
- **Metabolite production**: Large yellow droplets forming on the myceliated surface,
  indicating the mycelium is exuding the enzymes needed to break down the oil
- **Timeline**: Visible enzymatic activity typically begins within a few weeks

### Biological Recovery Testing

After approximately one month of fungal remediation, students add red wiggler worms
to test biological recovery of the treated material. Multiple bins can be set up for
sequential testing at weeks 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 to track the remediation timeline.
Worm survival indicates that toxicity has decreased to tolerable levels.

### Full Experiment Duration

The complete oil spill remediation experiment can span up to three months from
initial setup to final biological recovery assessment, making it important to start
early in the academic year.

## Contaminated Water Testing

### Types of Contaminants Tested

Water contamination experiments can include:

- Diluted herbicides
- Fertilizers (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium compounds)
- Other environmental pollutants

### Testing Methods

**Professional test kits** measure specific contaminant concentrations remaining in
treated water. For low-tech alternatives:

- **Dilution bean test**: Seeds are germinated in treated water at various dilutions
  to assess phytotoxicity
- **Worm assay**: Organisms are exposed to treated water to assess biological toxicity

### Safety Protocols

When working with chemicals in mycoremediation experiments:

- Always wear gloves and safety glasses
- Use appropriate ventilation
- Follow institutional chemical safety guidelines
- Vegetable oil substitutes for motor oil in classroom settings to avoid hazardous
  waste permitting requirements

## Data Analysis and Reporting

### Graphing Results

Results from all experimental modules can be graphed and analyzed to determine:
- Best treatments for erosion prevention
- Variables contributing most to remediation success
- Optimal retention times for different contaminant types
- Cost-effectiveness of biological versus conventional methods

### Key Variables to Track

- Flow rate and retention time
- Silt/soil dry weight in runoff
- Contaminant concentration before and after treatment
- Mycelial growth rate and health
- Biological recovery indicators (worm survival, seed germination)

## Real-World Applications

### Engineering Mycoremediation Prototypes

At the college level, interdepartmental collaborations between engineering and
microbiology students can design and test full-scale mycoremediation systems,
progressing from data collection through prototype testing to construction and
implementation of filtration systems for contaminated sites.

### Screening Native Fungi

Researchers can collect fungi at known contaminated sites, clone them to reproduce
strains already acclimated to local conditions, and compare their remediation
effectiveness against lab-trained samples. These native strains can be further
improved through selective screening for favorable mutations that enhance
remediation potential.
