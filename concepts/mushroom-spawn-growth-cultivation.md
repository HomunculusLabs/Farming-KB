# Mushroom Spawn, Growth, and Cultivation

## Overview

The subterranean life of fungi was poorly understood in the 19th century, and Palmer devoted significant attention to describing the nature of mushroom spawn, the process of fungal growth, and the challenges of artificial cultivation. His observations, based on years of careful watching, corrected many popular misconceptions about how mushrooms grow and reproduce.

## The Nature of Spawn

### What Spawn Is

Spawn is the subterranean vegetative part of the fungus, analogous to the vine of a grape plant. The visible mushroom above ground represents only the fruiting body, while the spawn is the persistent living tissue from which new mushrooms arise year after year.

Palmer described observing spawn during earth-moving operations near the Brunswick Hotel in Boston:

> "As the pick of the workman broke up the soil, a white substance ran through every piece. Starting with large branches, it divided and sub-divided like the veins on the back of the hand."

This "white vine" was the spawn of the [[Coprinus comatus]]. The smell was strong enough to be noticed from across the street.

### Microscopic Structure

Under the microscope, spawn appears as "a collection of oblong cells united at the ends." The stalk and the whole plant seem to be "but a mass of these cells, extended into chains interlaced and united." However, the most powerful microscope fails to show any germ of the future plant in the spores, and the starting point of spawn growth from spores remained a mystery.

## The Mystery of Reproduction

### The Spore Problem

Unlike seeds of higher plants, fungal spores contain no trace of an embryo. Millions of spores may be scattered on suitable ground without producing a single mushroom. Palmer observed:

> "On the grass-plot in front of my office I have thrown the refuse of hundreds of fungi, and the soil must have received millions of spores, yet not a toadstool of any kind has ever grown there."

The propagation of spawn from spores was the "true mystery in the growth of the fungi." The name "cryptogamy" (hidden marriage) aptly reflects this ignorance.

### Evidence for Sexual Reproduction

The microscope shows in some fungal spores a conjugation indicating sexual difference, suggesting reproduction "must be analogous to that of animal rather than vegetable nature." Palmer further supported this argument with the observation that the taste, odor, and constituents of edible fungi "resemble no form of vegetable food," and that mushrooms absorb oxygen from the air "after the manner of animals."

## Growth Timeline

### Speed of Growth

The popular belief that mushrooms spring up in a single night is a "decided error." The common mushroom takes about 10 days to mature from a button the size of a pin-head to a disk three or four inches in diameter. Other species require similar periods, though the [[Coprinariae]] develop in about half that time.

### The Role of Moisture

Most development is subterranean. The fruiting body forms below the soil surface for days. A heavy dew or evening shower straightens the stem and expands the cap, causing the mushroom to break through the earth overnight. The gatherer finds white buttons in the morning where nothing was visible the day before. This overnight appearance led to the proverbial misconception about instantaneous growth.

### Seasonal Succession

Mushroom varieties follow each other in rotation through the growing season, "exactly analogous to the order of the different families of flowers." Some species produce more than one crop between May and November. Each variety tends to reappear in the same spot year after year, allowing the gatherer to develop regular haunts.

### Perennial Return

Palmer documented finding the identical varieties on the same spots where he had gathered them five years earlier:

> "The lover of fungus may have his regular harvests with all the certainty of the farmer who looks for the return of his wheat crop."

However, like any crop, mushroom spawn can fail in unfavorable years or even become extinct under adverse conditions.

## Cultivation of the Common Mushroom

### Why Only One Species Is Cultivated

The common mushroom ([[Agaricus campestris]] / [[Agaricus arvensis]]) is the only fungal species ever successfully propagated by artificial means. Palmer proposed a plausible explanation: it follows the horse and grows from horse manure. The spores are probably swallowed in large numbers during grazing, and some necessary change for reproduction occurs either in the intestinal canal or in the manure. This explains why old manure heaps eventually generate their own spawn and produce fine mushrooms.

### The Cultivation Process

1. **Spawn preparation**: Use spawn from the edge of an old mushroom bed rather than purchased material (much commercial spawn contained "not a trace of the fungus")
2. **Bed composition**: About two-thirds horse manure, one-third from other grazing animals or rich loam
3. **Temperature management**: The bed must reach 60 to 80 degrees Fahrenheit before inserting spawn blocks
4. **Spawn insertion**: Small blocks of spawn are inserted into the prepared bed
5. **Colonization**: In about three weeks, the white vine permeates the entire bed
6. **Fruiting**: About ten days later, mushrooms appear

### Common Causes of Failure

Palmer identified three primary reasons for cultivation failure:

1. **Poor spawn quality** — much commercial spawn was worthless
2. **Planting the entire bed at once** — the bed should be planted in sections "like a Pullman car," so that when one end is bearing, the other is just preparing
3. **Atmospheric neglect** — fungi are extremely susceptible to atmospheric changes

### The Moisture Problem

Watering the ground directly is "worse than useless." Moisture must come from beneath, like dew. Mushroom beds should have nothing between them and the earth. The solution is to produce an atmosphere similar to "dog-days" through evaporation. In greenhouses, steam pipes with vents can be used to collect condensation on the soil surface, keeping it moist and warm.

### Economic Factors

The difficulty of cultivation kept prices high: one to two dollars per pound in Palmer's time. Even successful beds typically produced only one picking before becoming barren. The gardener often failed because "he does not know his subject," treating mushroom spawn as if it were seeds to be planted like peas.

## Environmental Influence on Quality

Palmer documented several cases showing how environment affects mushroom quality:

- [[Boletus pachypus]] specimens communicated their strong nutty flavor to nearby potatoes through the refrigerator air
- [[Agaricus melleus]] (maned mushroom) from lawns was delicious, but specimens from dust-heaps, brick-yards, or city offal had no flavor and would be "doubtless deleterious" in quantity
- Mushrooms forced with potash cooked "like pieces of soap" with liquor resembling "strong lye"
- [[Boletus aestivalis]] from certain locations was impregnated with the odor of decayed fish

These observations demonstrate the extreme susceptibility of mushrooms to their growing conditions, reinforcing the need for careful selection and freshness.

## The Truffle Exception

Palmer noted that the celebrated truffle differs from all other mushrooms in that both the spawn and the fruiting body remain entirely underground. He proposed importing French swine and dogs trained to detect truffles beneath oak plantations to determine whether the species was indigenous to America. Isolated reports of truffles found in America existed but could not be verified.
