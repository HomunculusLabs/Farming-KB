# Ayahuasca and Caapi in Ethnobotany

## Introduction

Ayahuasca and caapi are two of many local names for either of two
species of South American vine: [[banisteriopsis-caapi]] or B. inebrians.
These gigantic jungle lianas with tiny pink flowers belong to the
family Malpighiaceae. An hallucinogenic drink prepared from the bark of
these vines is widely used by indigenous peoples throughout the western
Amazon, including Brazil, Colombia, Peru, Ecuador, and Bolivia. The
drink occupies a central role in the spiritual, medicinal, and social
life of dozens of South American Indian tribes.

## Botanical Description

Banisteriopsis caapi and B. inebrians are among approximately 100
species in the genus Banisteriopsis, though their botany remains poorly
understood. Both are large forest vines that can grow to enormous size.
Local names for the vines or the drink include ayahuasca, caapi, yaje,
dopa, natema, and pinde. The hallucinogenic drink is intensely bitter
and nauseating, often producing purgative effects considered part of
the healing process by indigenous users.

## Methods of Preparation

Preparation methods vary significantly across regions:

- **Peru and Ecuador**: The bark is rasped from the vine and boiled
  in water to produce the drink.
- **Colombia and Brazil**: The scraped bark is squeezed in cold water
  rather than heated.
- **Orinoco region**: The bark is simply chewed directly.
- **Northwestern Amazon**: Recent evidence suggests the plants may
  be used in the form of snuff.

Some tribes add other plants to alter or increase the potency of the
basic preparation. The diversity of additives is remarkable, including
even ferns.

## Historical Discovery

The earliest published reports of ayahuasca date from 1858, but in 1851
the English explorer Richard Spruce had already discovered the plant
from which the intoxicating drink was made and described it as a new
species. Spruce also reported that the Guahibos along the Orinoco River
in Venezuela chewed the dried stem for its effects. He collected
flowering material and stems for chemical study; remarkably, these
stems were not analyzed until 1969, yet after more than a century they
still showed the presence of active alkaloids.

## Effects of Ayahuasca

The effects of drinking ayahuasca range from a pleasant intoxication
with no hangover to violent reactions with sickening after-effects.
Usually there are visual hallucinations in color. In excessive doses,
the drug produces nightmarish visions and a feeling of reckless
abandon. Importantly, consciousness is usually not lost, nor is there
impairment of the use of the limbs. In fact, dancing is a major part
of the ayahuasca ceremony in some tribes. The intoxication typically
ends with a deep sleep and vivid dreams.

The experience varies from person to person and is influenced by the
preparation method and plant additives used across different regions.

## Ceremonial Uses

Ceremonial uses of ayahuasca are of major importance in the lives of
South American Indians:

- **Eastern Peru**: Medicine men take the drug to diagnose and treat
  diseases, serving as the primary pharmacological tool of the
  Amazonian shaman.
- **Colombia and Brazil**: The drug is employed in deeply religious
  ceremonies rooted in tribal mythology.
- **Yurupari ceremony**: Among the Tukanoan Indians of Amazonian
  Colombia, ayahuasca is given to adolescent boys undergoing
  initiation into manhood, fortifying them for a severely painful
  ordeal.

The intoxication is thought to represent a return to the origin of all
things. Users report "seeing" tribal gods and the creation of the
universe, of man, and of animals. This experience convinces the
Indians of the reality of their religious beliefs, because they have
"seen" everything that underlies them. To them, everyday life is
unreal, and what caapi brings them is the true reality.

## Chemistry of the Ayahuasca Vines

Chemical studies have been complicated by botanical confusion, but both
species owe their hallucinogenic activity primarily to harmine, the
major beta-carboline alkaloid. Harmaline and tetrahydroharmine,
present in minor amounts, may also contribute. Early chemical studies
isolated these alkaloids without recognizing their identity and gave
them names as "new" alkaloids. One name, "telepathine," reflects the
widespread belief that the drink gave medicine men telepathic powers.

## The DMT Synergy: Plant Additives

Two plant additives are especially significant and widely used across
many tribes:

1. **Banisteriopsis rusbyana**: The leaves (but not the bark) of this
   third Banisteriopsis species are added "to lengthen and brighten
   the visions." Called oco-yaje in the westernmost Amazon, it is
   cultivated alongside B. caapi and B. inebrians.

2. **[[psychotria-viridis]]**: Over a much wider area including Amazonian
   Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru, leaves of Psychotria species
   are added. This 20-foot forest treelet belongs to the coffee
   family (Rubiaceae) and has been found to contain the strongly
   hallucinogenic N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT).

The combination is pharmacologically ingenious: the harmine and
harmaline from Banisteriopsis are MAO (monoamine oxidase) inhibitors
that prevent the breakdown of DMT in the digestive tract, making the
orally active DMT from Psychotria bioavailable. This synergy between
beta-carboline alkaloids and DMT represents one of the most remarkable
examples of indigenous pharmacological knowledge.

## Tetrapteris methystica: Another Kind of Caapi

Another caapi is prepared from Tetrapteris methystica, a forest vine
also in the family Malpighiaceae. The Maku Indians of the
northwesternmost Brazilian Amazon prepare a cold-water drink from the
bark. It is very bitter with an unusual yellow hue, possibly the
"painted caapi" mentioned by early explorers. Though its chemistry
remains unknown, its close relation to Banisteriopsis suggests similar
alkaloids are present.
