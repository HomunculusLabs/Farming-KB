# Mushroom Strain Preservation and Species Banking

Preserving mushroom strains through stock cultures is essential for any
cultivator who invests effort in [[mushroom-cultivator-strain-selection]], [[stamets-spore-germination-and-tissue-culture]], or breeding. Paul Stamets details multiple
methods for maintaining viable cultures over periods ranging from months
to decades.

## The Species Bank Concept

Once a pure strain has been created and isolated via [[stamets-agar-media-preparation]] techniques, saving it as a "stock culture" is
wise. Stock cultures — or "slants" — are agar-filled glass test tubes
sterilized and inoculated with mushroom mycelium. A suitable tube size
is 20 mm x 100 mm with a screw cap.

Every experienced cultivator maintains a collection of stock cultures,
known as a **species bank**. With it, a cultivator may preserve strains
for years and always have backup material available.

## Preparing Slants

1.  Mix any of the standard [[stamets-agar-media-preparation]]
    formulas (PDY, MEA, or MPG are recommended).
2.  Fill test tubes one-third full, plug with cotton and cover with
    aluminum foil, or screw on the cap.
3.  [[sterilization-techniques-mushroom-cultivation]] in a pressure cooker for
    30 minutes at 15 psi.
4.  Allow the cooker to return to atmospheric pressure before opening.
5.  Remove slants, gently shake to distribute the liquefied media, and
    lay at a 15-30 degree angle to cool and solidify.
6.  Inoculate with a fragment of mushroom mycelium.
7.  Label each tube with the date, agar type, species, and strain.
8.  Make at least three slants per strain to insure against loss.
9.  Incubate for one week at 75°F (24°C).

## Standard Refrigerated Storage

Once the mycelia has covered a major portion of the agar surface and
appears contamination-free, store slants at 35-40°F (2-4°C). At these
temperatures, metabolic activity is lowered to the point where growth
and nutrient absorption virtually stop.

Check the vitality of stored cultures every six months by transferring
fragments to fresh [[stamets-agar-media-preparation]]. Once
colonized two-thirds across, select for strandy growth (rhizomorphism)
and reinoculate fresh slants.

Growing out mini-cultures is also a good way to verify a stored strain's
vitality and [[mushroom-cultivator-fruiting-conditions]].

## Mineral Oil Preservation

An excellent method for long-term preservation involves overlaying the
live mycelium with a thin layer of sterile mineral oil once established
in a test tube:

-   The mineral oil is non-toxic to mycelium.
-   It greatly reduces metabolism and inhibits water evaporation from
    the agar base.
-   Store at 37-41°F until needed.

In a study by Perrin (1979), all 30 wood-inhabiting species stored under
mineral oil for 27 years produced viable cultures. To reactivate, invert
slants to drain oil, then incubate at 77°F. Within three weeks, renewed
growth was evident, and subculturing yielded uncontaminated cultures.

Strains stored more than 5 years may show signs of degeneration, though
viability at 2.5 years was equal to the day of preservation. Storing up
to 2 years without serious impairment is reasonable.

## The Buddy System

An excellent backup method: pass duplicates of each species or strain
to a cultivator friend. Mushroom strains are more easily lost than one
might expect. Once lost, they may never be recovered, making this simple
redundancy invaluable.

## Additional Preservation Methods

Four other methods extend culture lifespan:

1.  **Liquid nitrogen immersion** — expensive but highly effective for
    indefinite storage.
2.  **Sterilized compost inoculation** — inoculate washed, sterilized
    horse manure/straw compost and keep at 36-38°F. See [[stamets-compost-preparation]].
3.  **Sawdust/bran media** — for wood decomposers, using the [[mushroom-cultivator-wood-based-substrates]] method.
4.  **Aseptic spore storage** — perhaps the simplest method for home
    cultivators; save [[mushroom-spore-printing-and-storage]] under refrigerated conditions.

## Strain Degeneration

The mushroom's nature is to fruit, sporulate, and evolve. Cultivation
techniques should evolve with the mushroom. Cultivators must selectively
isolate and maintain promising strains as they develop. Do not be
surprised if a stored strain poorly resembles the original in fruiting
potential or form after five years.
