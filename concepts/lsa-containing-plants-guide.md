# LSA-Containing Plants Guide

Lysergic acid amide (LSA), also known as ergine or d-lysergic acid amide, is a naturally occurring alkaloid found in several plant species. It is a close chemical relative of LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), sharing the same lysergic acid core structure but with a simpler amide group rather than the diethylamide of LSD. LSA-containing plants have a long history of traditional use and remain legally accessible in many jurisdictions.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

LSA is an ergoline alkaloid, part of a family of compounds that includes LSD, ergotamine, and the ergot alkaloids used in migraine medications. The structural similarity between LSA and LSD means that LSA acts on similar serotonin receptor sites, particularly 5-HT receptors, though with significantly lower potency.

The hallucinogenic activity of LSA occurs at doses of 2-5 mg, providing an intoxication lasting 4-8 hours. The subjective effects are described as qualitatively and quantitatively different from LSD. LSA is generally considered milder, with more pronounced sedative and physical effects (nausea, lethargy, vasoconstriction) and less intense visual hallucination compared to LSD.

Some researchers and users distinguish between the effects of LSA and those of the whole seed preparation, which contains additional alkaloids including iso-LSA (isolysergic acid amide), chanoclavine, elymoclavine, and lysergol. These companion alkaloids may contribute significantly to the overall experience, including the sedative and physical side effects.

## Argyreia Nervosa (Hawaiian Baby Woodrose)

Argyreia nervosa is a perennial climbing vine native to the Indian subcontinent but now widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. The plant produces large, heart-shaped leaves and attractive purple flowers, making it popular as an ornamental. The seeds are the primary psychoactive part, containing approximately 0.14% LSA by dry weight.

Five to ten seeds of Argyreia nervosa yield an average dose of LSA. The seeds are brown and woody in appearance. Commercially available seeds may be coated with pesticides or anti-fungal treatments that should be removed by washing before preparation.

Common preparation methods include grinding the seeds and soaking them in water, crushing the seeds and consuming the pulp directly, or performing a simple cold water extraction. Some users scrape off the fuzzy seed coating before grinding, believing it contains compounds that contribute to nausea.

The experience from Argyreia nervosa seeds is consistently described as more sedating and physically heavy than LSD. Nausea is common during the onset phase. Visual effects are generally mild, with more pronounced changes in thought patterns, emotional sensitivity, and sensory perception than in visual hallucination.

## Ipomoea Violacea (Morning Glory)

Ipomoea violacea and related Ipomoea species are common ornamental climbing vines grown for their abundant, colorful trumpet-shaped flowers. Several varieties produce seeds containing LSA, with concentrations of approximately 0.02% LSA by dry seed weight.

The Heavenly Blue and Pearly Gates varieties of Ipomoea tricolor are the most commonly used. Flying Saucers and Blue Star varieties are also known to contain LSA. Because concentrations are lower than in Argyreia nervosa, a much larger quantity of seeds is needed: approximately 150-200 seeds (3-6 grams) for an average dose.

Commercial morning glory seeds sold by garden suppliers are often coated with emetic poisons to discourage ingestion. These coatings are not always effective but add to the gastrointestinal discomfort. Organically grown or untreated seeds are strongly preferred for this reason.

Historically, Ipomoea violacea and related species were used in Central America for shamanic and ceremonial purposes. The Aztec and related Mesoamerican cultures used ololiuqui, identified as the seeds of Turbina corymbosa (a close relative of Ipomoea), in divinatory and healing rituals. The Mazatec people of Mexico continue traditional use of these seeds.

## Turbina Corymbosa (Ololiuqui)

Turbina corymbosa is a perennial climbing vine native to Mexico and Central America, where it has been used ceremonially for centuries. The Nahuatl name ololiuqui means round thing, referring to the small, round seeds that are the psychoactive part of the plant.

Ololiuqui holds a significant place in Mesoamerican ethnopharmacology. Spanish chroniclers documented its use by Aztec priests and healers for divination, diagnosis of illness, and communication with spirits. The seeds were considered sacred and their use was restricted to trained practitioners.

The LSA content of Turbina corymbosa seeds is comparable to or slightly lower than Ipomoea species. The traditional method of preparation involves grinding the seeds and soaking them in water or alcoholic beverages. The experience profile is similar to other LSA-containing seeds, with sedative and oneiric (dream-like) qualities.

## Stipa Robusta (Sleepygrass)

Stipa robusta, also known as Achnatherum robustum or sleepygrass, is a perennial grass native to the southwestern United States. It has been found to contain the highest concentrations of LSA among known plant sources, though with significant variability between individual plants and populations.

The high LSA content in Stipa robusta is associated with infection by an Acremonium endophytic fungus, which produces the alkaloids within the plant tissues. This fungal symbiosis is responsible for the psychoactive properties of the grass, similar to how ergot fungi produce ergoline alkaloids in grain crops.

Despite its high LSA content, Stipa robusta has no known history of traditional shamanic or ceremonial use, and there is no evidence that it is commonly ingested for intoxication. The variability of alkaloid content and the absence of traditional preparation knowledge make it a less practical source than the better-documented seeds.

## Preparation Methods

Several methods exist for extracting or preparing LSA from seeds, with varying degrees of complexity and effectiveness.

Cold water extraction is the simplest method. Seeds are thoroughly crushed or ground and soaked in cold water for several hours or overnight. The solid material is then filtered out, and the liquid is consumed. This method extracts water-soluble alkaloids including LSA while leaving behind some of the non-water-soluble compounds that may contribute to nausea.

Non-polar solvent extraction uses substances like petroleum ether or naphtha to remove non-polar compounds from the crushed seeds before a water extraction. The theory is that many of the nausea-causing compounds are non-polar and can be removed in this step, leaving a cleaner LSA solution. However, some LSA itself may be removed by non-polar solvents, reducing potency.

Ethereal extraction using diethyl ether can produce a relatively pure alkaloid extract, but this method requires handling volatile, flammable solvents and is impractical for most users.

Direct consumption of crushed seeds is the simplest approach but produces the most gastrointestinal discomfort. The seed coatings and plant material contain compounds that irritate the stomach.

## Effects and Experience

The LSA experience is typically described as dreamy, sedating, and introspective, with less visual intensity than classical psychedelics like LSD or psilocybin. Common effects include altered perception of time, enhanced emotional sensitivity, mild visual distortions (color enhancement, pattern recognition, trailing), and a general sense of relaxation or lethargy.

Physical side effects are more prominent with LSA than with most classical psychedelics. Nausea and gastrointestinal discomfort during the onset phase are nearly universal. Vasoconstriction (narrowing of blood vessels) can cause coldness in the extremities and a sense of physical heaviness. Some users report uterine contractions, making LSA inadvisable during pregnancy.

The sedative quality of LSA makes it unsuitable for situations requiring alertness. Users often find themselves inclined to lie down and enter a dream-like state rather than engaging in active exploration. This quality has led some to classify LSA more as a oneirogen (dream-inducing substance) than as a classical psychedelic.

## Legal Status

LSA itself is a Schedule III controlled substance in the United States under the Controlled Substances Act. However, the plants that contain LSA are generally legal to grow, possess, and sell. The seeds of Argyreia nervosa and Ipomoea species are widely available from garden suppliers and online vendors.

This legal paradox, where a controlled substance occurs naturally in an uncontrolled plant, creates a gray area. The seeds themselves are not scheduled, but extracting the LSA would constitute manufacturing a controlled substance. Most users who prepare LSA-containing seeds are technically in violation of the law, though prosecution for personal use of commonly available garden seeds is essentially unknown.

## Safety Considerations

The primary safety concern with LSA-containing seeds is accurate identification. While Argyreia nervosa and Ipomoea species are generally safe, consuming unknown seeds can be dangerous. Seeds from garden suppliers may be treated with pesticides, fungicides, or other chemicals that are harmful if ingested.

Individual sensitivity varies considerably. Some people experience minimal nausea while others find the physical side effects intolerable. Starting with a low dose and having a sober companion present are standard harm reduction practices.

Pregnant women should avoid LSA due to its uterine-contracting properties. Individuals with cardiovascular conditions should exercise caution due to the vasoconstrictive effects. As with all psychedelics, individuals with a personal or family history of psychosis should approach with caution.
