# Cyanide Biodegradation by Fungi

Cyanide is one of the most rapidly acting metabolic poisons known, yet certain fungi possess remarkable abilities to degrade and detoxify both free cyanide and its metal complexes. Fungal cyanide biodegradation represents an important capability for remediation of industrial sites contaminated with this compound.

## Cyanide Chemistry and Environmental Significance

Hydrogen cyanide (HCN) may account for more human deaths throughout history than any other toxin due to its use in executions and genocide. Key environmental facts:

- HCN inhibits respiration by binding to Fe3+ in cytochrome c oxidase
- Inhalation of concentrations greater than 0.3 ppm is toxic; 300 ppm is fatal without prompt treatment
- Free cyanide (HCN/CN-) and simple cyanides (KCN, NaCN) are the most toxic forms
- Twenty-eight elements can react with cyanide to form 72 different metallocyanides
- Iron cyanide complexes (hexacyanoferrates) are the most stable but can dissociate under acidic conditions
- Nitriles (organic cyanides, RCN) are used widely in industry as chemical precursors

Major sources of environmental cyanide contamination include:

- Gold and silver mining (cyanide leaching processes)
- Metal plating and finishing industries
- Gas works and coking plants
- Iron and steel manufacturing
- Petroleum refining
- Photographic film processing
- Pharmaceutical manufacturing

## Fungal Cyanide Degradation Pathways

Fungi can degrade cyanide through several biochemical routes:

### 1. Hydrolytic Pathway
Cyanide is converted to formamide and then to formic acid, eventually yielding carbon dioxide and ammonia. This pathway involves the enzyme cyanidase (cyanide dihydratase).

### 2. Oxidative Pathway
Cyanide is oxidized to cyanate (OCN-) and then hydrolyzed to carbon dioxide and ammonia. This involves cyanide mono-oxygenase.

### 3. Rhodanese Pathway
Cyanide is converted to thiocyanate (SCN-) by transfer of sulfur from a sulfur donor. While thiocyanate is less toxic than cyanide, it can still be environmentally problematic.

### 4. Direct Assimilation
Some fungi can incorporate cyanide into amino acids and other biomolecules, effectively using it as a nitrogen source.

## Cyanogenic Fungi: Production and Degradation

An important paradox is that many fungi both produce and degrade cyanide. Certain fungal species are cyanogenic -- they produce HCN as a secondary metabolite, often as an antimicrobial defense mechanism. These same species frequently possess the enzymatic machinery to degrade cyanide, creating a self-regulating system.

Species known to be involved in cyanide degradation include various genera of filamentous fungi and yeasts isolated from contaminated sites.

## Advantages of Fungal Cyanide Bioremediation

Compared to bacterial and chemical treatment methods, fungal approaches offer:

- **Broader substrate range**: Many fungi can handle both free and metal-complexed cyanides
- **Tolerance**: Fungi often tolerate higher cyanide concentrations than bacteria
- **Robustness**: Filamentous growth allows fungi to colonize heterogeneous contaminated environments
- **Metal complex degradation**: Some fungi can degrade stable iron cyanide complexes that resist bacterial attack
- **Acid tolerance**: Fungi generally tolerate the acidic conditions under which iron cyanide complexes dissociate

## Metal-Cyanide Complex Challenges

The strongest cyanide complexes (hexacyanoferrates, hexacyanocobaltates) are the most stable and least toxic but also the most difficult to degrade. Under acidic conditions typical of some contaminated sites, these complexes can dissociate to release free cyanide. The fungal ability to operate across a range of pH conditions makes them valuable for sites where cyanide speciation varies.

## Comparison with Bacterial Cyanide Degradation

Bacterial cyanide degradation is generally better characterized than fungal degradation. However, bacteria often struggle with:

- High cyanide concentrations (toxicity)
- Metal-cyanide complexes (limited enzymatic capability)
- Acidic pH conditions
- The heterogeneity of contaminated environments

Fungi complement bacterial systems by handling conditions where bacteria fail. Combined bacterial-fungal approaches may offer the most effective remediation strategy.

## Practical Applications

- Treatment of gold mining tailings and leach pad effluents
- Remediation of former gasworks sites
- Detoxification of metal plating wastewater
- Treatment of coke oven effluent
- Bioreactor systems for continuous cyanide removal
