## Loss of Ballistospore Discharge

The spores of [[gasteromycete-fungi]] are spherical or ellipsoidal with no
hilar appendix, the small protruding attachment point found on spores
discharged by the catapult mechanism. The lack of this appendage accords
with the absence of the ballistospore discharge mechanism. Evidence
that gasteromycetes evolved from ancestors that launched spores from
their basidia is very strong, suggesting that the enclosed fruiting
body is a derived rather than primitive condition.

Genetic data show that some false truffles are closely related to boletes,
which have exposed pore surfaces beneath their caps. The fertile tubes
of boletes develop in the embryonic stage of the fruiting body beneath
the soil and become exposed as the mushroom surfaces and expands its
cap. Very few genes specify the emergence of the fruiting body, and when
these genes mutate, the bolete remains buried. It seems that an
important theme in the evolution of false truffles and other
gasteromycetes is the gradual loss of the unfolding capacity of the
fruiting body.

## Puffballs

Puffballs are among the most recognizable gasteromycetes. They produce
spores within a spherical or pear-shaped fruiting body enclosed by a
leathery or papery outer wall (the peridium). When mature, the spores
are released through an apical pore or by the disintegration of the
outer wall, often in response to physical disturbance. Raindrops,
passing animals, or even a curious human finger can trigger the
explosive release of a cloud of powdery spores.

Puffballs are most plentiful after rainfall, but after they have
expanded, many can function perfectly during drought. The loss of the
water-dependent mechanism of spore propulsion enables puffballs to
colonize much drier habitats than other basidiomycetes, and many are
found on sandy soils and even in deserts. For these fungi, water
conservation makes more sense than evaporative cooling, and not
surprisingly, the temperature of puffballs is the same as the air that
surrounds them, unlike gilled mushrooms which cool during spore release.

## Earthstars

Earthstars (Geastrum species) are puffballs with a distinctive star-
shaped outer layer that splits and folds back as the fruiting body
matures. The inner spore sac sits elevated on the star-like base,
raised above the ground surface and better positioned for spore
dispersal. Some earthstar species are hygroscopic: the outer rays
fold over the spore sac in dry conditions, protecting the spores, and
open when wet to allow dispersal. This behavior represents a
sophisticated adaptation that allows these fungi to time spore release
to periods of optimal humidity.

## Stinkhorns and Phallic Mushrooms

Stinkhorns (Phallus and related genera) produce a slimy, foul-smelling
spore mass called the gleba at the tip of an erect fruiting body. The
odor, which resembles decaying flesh or carrion, attracts flies and
other insects that land on the gleba, become coated with spores, and
carry them to new locations. [[spore-dispersal]] in stinkhorns depends
entirely upon animal vectors. The fruiting body develops rapidly from
an egg-like structure (the volva) that remains visible at the base of
the mature mushroom. The expansion of the stinkhorn is driven by
turgor pressure within the hyphae, a remarkable example of hydraulic
engineering in fungi.

## Bird's Nest Fungi

Bird's nest fungi (Cyathus and related genera) produce tiny cup-shaped
fruiting bodies containing packets of spores called peridioles. These
look remarkably like miniature eggs in a nest. When raindrops strike
the cup, the peridioles are splashed out and can land several feet
away, stuck to vegetation or other surfaces where herbivorous animals
may encounter and ingest them. The spores pass through the animal's
digestive tract unharmed and are deposited in dung, a nutrient-rich
substrate for the next generation of fungi.

## Cage Fungi

Cage fungi (Clathrus species) produce striking lattice-like fruiting
bodies that resemble red or orange cages. Like stinkhorns, they develop
from an egg-shaped volva and produce a malodorous gleba that attracts
insect dispersers. The open lattice structure maximizes the surface
area of spore-bearing tissue exposed to insects, enhancing the
efficiency of animal-mediated dispersal.

## False Truffles

False truffles are [[gasteromycete-fruiting-bodies]] that mature beneath
the soil surface. They serve as attractive lures for rodents, which
disperse their spores for the nutritional reward of the fungal flesh.
The mammalian dispersal strategy of false truffles parallels that of
true truffles (Tuber species, which are ascomycetes), representing a
convergent evolution toward hypogeous fruiting and animal-mediated
spore dispersal. False truffles are particularly common in arid and
semi-arid regions where the water-dependent ballistospore mechanism
would be unreliable.

## The Evolutionary Theme

An important theme in gasteromycete evolution is the gradual loss of
the unfolding capacity of the fruiting body. This results in the
production of spores within enclosed hymenial tissues and precludes
discharge by the catapult mechanism. The shift from ballistospory to
various forms of passive or animal-mediated dispersal has allowed
these fungi to colonize habitats where water is scarce and the
evaporative cooling needed for Buller's drop cannot be sustained.

Spore dispersal in gasteromycetes depends upon animal vectors in
phallic mushrooms, cages, and false truffles, while puffballs and
earthstars expel their spores in response to physical disturbance of
any kind. Each of these strategies represents an independent solution
to the problem of spore release without the ballistospore catapult,
and the diversity of gasteromycete forms testifies to the evolutionary
flexibility of the basidiomycete fruiting body.

## Temperature and Water Relations

Unlike gilled mushrooms, which cool during spore release due to
evaporative water loss, gasteromycetes maintain temperatures equal to
the surrounding air. This is because they have abandoned the water-
dependent spore discharge mechanism. [[water-conservation]], rather than
evaporative cooling, is the thermodynamic priority for these fungi,
and this fundamental difference in water economy underpins their
ability to colonize dry habitats that are inhospitable to gilled
mushrooms.
