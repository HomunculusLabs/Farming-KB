# Permaculture Designers Manual - Vegetable Gardening

updated: 2026-04-18

Vegetable gardening is treated primarily in Section 12.6 "The Home Garden" of Chapter 12, with additional material in Chapter 10 for tropical systems. Mollison presents the home garden as a central element of permaculture design, zoned for frequency of harvest and integrated with the broader farm or homestead system.

## Cool Climate Home Gardens

One special feature of cool area home gardens is the need to grow and store crops (in the field or home) over winter months, as growth effectively ceases from late autumn to early spring. The middle of spring is known as the "hungry gap," when winter crops are finished and new crops are not yet yielding.

### Bridging the Winter Food Gap

The winter food gap is bridged by growing root crops, cucurbits, and processed crops for preservation. These mature in late summer and are stored or preserved. Key crops include:

Staple root crops: potato, parsnip, carrot, oca, sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke), and bulbs such as onions.

Field crops left to over-winter: fava bean, kale, chard (silver beet), parsley, and varieties of cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, and sprouts under straw or shelters.

### Planting Periods

There are two main planting periods:
- Early spring months: salad vegetables and all summer crops
- Last month of summer or first month of autumn: all over-winter crops and all root crops of the year

In milder areas, a green crop (green manure) can be sown before winter using oats, tares, or fava beans, slashed or dug in during spring.

### Storage Systems

Cellar or pit storage is extensively used in cool areas:
- Potatoes: stored in straw or ash in dark boxes or humid cellars
- Carrots and parsnips: tops removed, stored in mounds of sand or ash
- Apples: separately wrapped or spread on attic floors (never stored with root crops due to ethylene production causing root crops to shoot)
- Cabbage or kale: stored uprooted under hay, as sauerkraut, or sterilised/frozen

### Garden Layout

Planning means small beds for spring planting with main crops of potato, tomato, and sweet corn, then reversion to larger mass beds in autumn for winter storage crops. Chard and parsley are important all-year foods with several successions sown at irregular intervals.

The layout is similar to tropical gardens but omits overshaded areas or tree crop interplant, as light levels can be low in cloudy weather. Mulch is appropriate throughout, with the sole exception of spring carrot planting where mulch is pulled from seedbeds to allow warming. All other mulched crops catch up with bare-soil crops and mature at the same time.

### Protected Growing

A small glasshouse for seedling trays and a series of cloches for early transplants are invaluable. Potatoes can be sprouted on old hessian bags indoors or in shadehouses, then transplanted complete with leaves and roots by cutting the bags into squares.

## Perennialising Annuals

The manual describes several techniques for converting annuals to perennial management:

- Carrots: cut a disc off the top, sprout on soft wet paper in a shallow tray, replant; some gardeners report eating the same carrots for 9 years
- Leeks: let one in four go to seed, remove small bulbils from base and replant; cut off 2 cm below ground when harvesting so central shoot regrows; perennial bunching leek varieties exist
- Lettuce: pick single leaves, allow to go to seed, scatter seed; mignonette is a good self-seeder
- Cabbage: cut the head 8 cm above ground, deeply crosscut the stem; each quarter regrows a small cabbage
- Garden fennel, chard, parsley, parsnip: self-seed if allowed to go to seed; judicious thinning, weeding, and transplanting is in order

## Seed Saving

When saving seed from umbelliferous plants, collect the strong, mature seed from the terminal panicle only, as this germinates best. Side panicles contain immature and small seeds and can be pruned or rejected.

Despite the enormous loss of locally adapted seed (estimated at 85% of European varieties) from monopoly control, the encouraging result is that most gardeners can assemble more species and varieties than ever before in history by using seed exchanges and local resources.

## Trellis and Small Fruit

Trellis crops are important in mesothermal climates. Peas, beans, cucurbits, and tomato varieties will trellis. Vertical zigzag, inverted V, or house wall/fence trellis aligned north-south is preferred over trellis over the garden.

Small fruit -- currants, gooseberry, raspberry, trellis blackberry -- is a feature of cool-area gardens. Six to fifteen plants of 6-8 species yield substantial fresh or frozen food of high vitamin C value, and rows can act as windbreaks within the garden.

## Tropical Home Gardens

Tropical gardens (Chapter 10) follow similar principles but with continuous year-round production, emphasis on mulching, and tree crop integration. Bed geometry, path placement, and species selection follow zone-and-sector analysis. Kitchen gardens in the tropics combine root crops, greens, small fruit, and climbing vegetables in intensive polyculture beds.
