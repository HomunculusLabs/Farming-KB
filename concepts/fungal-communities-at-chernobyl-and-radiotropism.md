# Fungal Communities at Chernobyl and Radiotropism

## Overview

The Chernobyl nuclear disaster of 1986 created an unprecedented natural laboratory for studying fungal responses to intense and sustained ionizing radiation. Research over the following decades revealed that fungi are remarkably resistant to radiation, exhibit behavioral adaptations including radiotropism, and can serve as bioindicators of radioactive contamination levels. These findings have profound implications for understanding [[fungal-ecology]], evolution, and potential applications in bioremediation.

## Community Structure Changes

### Diversity Reduction with Increasing Radiation

Zhdanova et al. (1994) demonstrated that [[mineralogical-controls-fungal-community-structure]] in soils around Chernobyl differed systematically with the degree of radionuclide exposure. Using pleiads (diagrammatic representations where points represent fungal species and the distances and connections between them indicate similarity and community complexity), they showed two key patterns:

1. **Decreased complexity**: Community diversity decreased with increasing radioactive dose, as measured by the number of linear connections between species
2. **Increased melanization**: The proportion of melanin-containing (pigmented) fungal species within communities increased with dose rate

Over 15 years of monitoring, approximately 2,000 strains of 180 species from 92 genera were isolated from the Chernobyl region.

### Melanin as a Protective Mechanism

Melanin pigment in fungal cell walls appears to provide protection against ionizing radiation, analogous to its known role in protecting lichen fungi against ultraviolet light (Gauslaa and Solhaug, 2001). Melanized species dominate at higher radiation levels, suggesting strong selective pressure favoring this trait.

## Fungi from the Reactor Room

Zhdanova et al. (2000, 2001) isolated microfungi directly from the walls of the reactor room at the Chernobyl Atomic Electric Station under conditions of 1.5 to 800 mR h⁻¹, in the presence of alpha- and beta-emitting ²³⁹Pu, ²⁴⁰⁺²⁴¹Pu, ²⁴¹Am, and ²⁴⁴Cm, and gamma-emitting ¹³⁷Cs. Thirty-seven species from 19 genera were isolated, with higher isolation frequencies at greater radiation intensities.

### Dominant Species

| Fungal Species | Isolation Frequency at 1.5–25 mR h⁻¹ | Isolation Frequency at 40–220 mR h⁻¹ |
|----------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| *[[cladosporium-sphaerospermum]]* | 73.7% | 99.9% |
| *[[alternaria-alternata]]* | 40.2% | 44.4% |
| *Aureobasidium versicolor* | 26.8% | 55.5% |
| *[[aureobasidium-pullulans]]* | 20.1% | 44.4% |
| *Cladosporium herbarum* | 20.1% | 44.1% |
| *Acremonium strictum* | 33.5% | 22.2% |
| *Aspergillus niger* | 13.4% | 22.2% |

*Cladosporium sphaerospermum* showed near-complete dominance at the highest radiation levels (99.9% isolation frequency).

## Decomposition of Radioactive Debris

Some fungi isolated from high-radiation zones can actively grow into and decompose carbon-based radioactive debris from the reactor, known as "hot particles." Zhdanova et al. (1991) showed that *[[cladosporium-cladosporioides]]* and *Penicillium roseo-purpureum* could overgrow hot particles with less than 1,147 Bq of activity and destroy them within 50 to 150 days. This demonstrates that certain fungi can metabolize radioactive carbon substrates.

## Radiotropism

One of the most intriguing discoveries from Chernobyl research is the phenomenon of **radiotropism**—the active directional growth of fungi toward sources of radioactivity (Vember et al., 1999; Zhdanova, 1994).

### Key Characteristics

- **Strain-dependent**: Fungi isolated from high-radiation areas direct growth toward collimated radiation sources in culture, while the same species from low-radiation areas do not
- **Behavioral adaptation**: This appears to be an evolved behavioral response rather than a passive physical effect
- **Mechanism unknown**: The triggers and physiological mechanisms underlying radiotropism remain poorly understood and require further investigation

Radiotropism suggests that some fungi may derive metabolic benefit from radiation, possibly through melanin-mediated energy transduction, though this hypothesis remains debated.

## Bioindicator Species

Zhdanova et al. (1990, 1991, 1994, 1995, 2000) characterized fungal bioindicator traits for different levels of radioactive contamination:

### High Contamination Indicators
- *Chaetomium aureum*
- *[[paeciliomyces-lilacinus]]*
- High proportion of melanized species
- Reduced community diversity

### Community-Level Indicators
- Pleiad complexity (number of interconnections between species)
- Ratio of melanized to non-melanized species
- Frequency of isolation of radiation-tolerant genera

These bioindicators provide a practical ecological tool for assessing radioactive contamination levels based on fungal community composition, which can be more informative than single-point radiation measurements.

## Lichens as Radionuclide Accumulators

The fungal component of the lichen symbiosis is responsible for elevated radionuclide content. Heinrich et al. (1999) reported post-Chernobyl ¹³⁷Cs levels in Austrian lichens rising from ~0.4 kBq kg⁻¹ to over 50 kBq kg⁻¹. *Pseudovernia furfuracea* exceeded natural ⁴⁰K activity by 430 times.

### Biological Half-Lives in Lichens
- ¹³⁷Cs in foliose arboreal lichens: ~1.3 years
- ⁹⁰Sr in foliose arboreal lichens: 1.2–1.6 years
- ¹³⁷Cs in *Cetraria islandica* (terricolous): 2.5 years

The relatively long biological half-lives allow for extended transfer to animal grazers. In Norway, where reindeer densities reach ~4 per hectare and lichens form ~40% of annual food intake, mean ¹³⁷Cs activity in lichens between 1986 and 1988 ranged from 6,700 to 24,000 Bq kg⁻¹.

## Genetic Changes

Sustained intense radiation at Chernobyl has led to detectable shifts in the genetic composition within some fungal species (Mironenko et al., 2000). This suggests that radiation acts as both a selective pressure and potentially as a mutagenic agent, driving rapid evolution in these communities.

## Implications

The Chernobyl fungal communities demonstrate several important principles:

1. **Fungal resilience**: Fungi can survive and function under radiation levels lethal to most organisms
2. **Evolutionary adaptation**: Behavioral traits like radiotropism can evolve in response to radiation pressure
3. **Bioremediation potential**: Fungi that decompose radioactive debris or accumulate radionuclides could be harnessed for cleanup
4. **Biomonitoring utility**: Fungal community composition serves as a sensitive indicator of radioactive contamination
5. **Ecological concern**: The long biological half-lives of radionuclides in lichens and fungi create persistent pathways for food chain contamination, particularly in Arctic and subarctic ecosystems where lichen-reliant herbivores are important food sources
