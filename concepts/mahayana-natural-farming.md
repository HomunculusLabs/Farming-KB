# Mahayana Natural Farming

## Overview

Mahayana natural farming is Masanobu Fukuoka's term for the highest form
of agricultural practice, in which the farmer becomes one with nature and
abandons all purposeful human intervention. The name derives from the
Buddhist concept of the Mahayana ("Great Vehicle"), the path of universal
compassion that transcends individual effort.

Fukuoka distinguished Mahayana natural farming from both [[fukuoka-mahayana-and-hinayana-natural-farming]] farming. Together, these three
approaches form a philosophical spectrum for understanding humanity's
relationship to the natural world through agriculture.

## The Three Ways of Farming

Fukuoka organized all agricultural practice into three categories, each
representing a fundamentally different relationship between human beings
and nature.

**Mahayana natural farming** occupies the "absolute world." The farmer
does nothing at all — no tilling, no fertilizing, no weeding, no
pesticides, no pruning. The farmer lives freely as part of the natural
world, subsisting on its bounty without purposeful effort.

**Hinayana natural farming** occupies the "relative world" alongside
scientific farming. The practitioner earnestly seeks the condition of
Mahayana farming but has not yet attained it. This corresponds broadly to
[[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]], by contrast, relies on "[[non-discriminating-knowledge-and-critique-of-scientific-reductionism]]"
that splits the world into subject and object, fragmenting a living whole
into isolated facts.

## Comparison with Scientific Farming

Scientific agriculture extracts knowledge and forces from nature, then
attempts to surpass nature through accumulated human ingenuity. But human
knowledge, however vast, remains a tiny fraction of the "infinitude of the
natural world." Since imperfection can never equal perfection, scientific
farming must always be inferior to Mahayana natural farming.

Scientific farming produces results that are always distorted and of only
local utility. A fruit grown with scientific methods may be large in a
relative sense, but Fukuoka considered it "deformed fruit." The
unnaturalness of scientific farming leads to incompleteness in all its
outputs.
