# Aquifer Intake Areas and Recharge

## Overview

Aquifer intake areas are the specific landscape features and geological formations where water enters underground storage systems. Understanding where, how, and why aquifers recharge is critical for [[humus]] enhance infiltration.
- **Cultivation on ridges** that compacts soil and reduces permeability.
- **Road construction** across intake areas that creates impermeable surfaces.
- **Overgrazing** that destroys ground cover and leads to soil compaction and erosion.

## Assisting Aquifer Recharge

A range of design strategies can enhance aquifer intake and maintain groundwater systems:

### Vegetation-Based Approaches

- **Planting trees on ridges and slopes** — trees are the long-term solution. They keep intake systems open to infiltration and reduce or eliminate dust from loess and sand deposits in surrounding areas. Root systems create preferential flow paths for water penetration, and canopy cover reduces the impact of raindrops on soil surfaces.

### Earthwork-Based Approaches

- **Deep interceptor drains on contour** near ridge tops and on plateaus to capture and direct water into permeable substrata.
- **Ripping rock pavement or capstone areas** to allow water penetration through otherwise impermeable surface layers.
- **Leading surface drains to wells** cut in shattered rock, concentrating water flow into known permeable zones.
- **Creating banks of loose surface boulder** or opening pits and swales in gravels to increase surface area for infiltration.

### Protective Measures

- **Stabilising the landscape** with windbreaks and protected reserves to prevent dust accumulation.
- **Excluding livestock** from intake areas to prevent compaction.
- **Maintaining forest cover** on all ridges, slopes, and plateau areas within catchments.

## Quanats: Ancient Aquifer Tapping Systems

Quanats (also spelled qanats) are ancient underground galleries used for thousands of kilometres across many deserts, with particularly wide use in Southwest Asia. They represent one of humanity's oldest and most sophisticated water harvesting technologies.

### How Quanats Work

Quanats tap into aquifers by means of gently sloping underground tunnels that convey groundwater by gravity flow from the water table at higher elevations to the surface at lower elevations. The mother well is dug at the upstream end where the water table is close to the surface, and the tunnel extends downslope, gradually emerging at the surface where water can be distributed for irrigation and domestic use.

### Advantages

- Water is delivered by gravity, requiring no pumping energy.
- The underground conduit minimises evaporation losses.
- Air from the tunnels can be vented to cool houses — a secondary benefit in hot climates.
- The system taps deep, clean groundwater relatively free of surface contamination.

### Disadvantages

- Quanats have the significant disadvantage of being non-stoppable once constructed — if the aquifer is depleted, the entire system fails.
- Construction requires specialised knowledge and significant labour.
- Maintenance of tunnel sections can be hazardous.

## The Hydrological Cycle in Forested vs. Desertified Areas

When areas were forested, ungrazed, uncultivated, and lacked the hard pavements of roads and settlements, little runoff occurred. Instead, throughflow in soils was significant, and clean streams flowed steadily. Desertified areas lack these characteristics:

- Surface runoff increases dramatically.
- Infiltration decreases.
- Springs dry up.
- Streams become ephemeral.

We need to intercede in the hydrological cycle to **store, infiltrate, and convert rainwater to vegetative growth** — in short, to create soil storages that assist forest regeneration. This is the fundamental challenge and opportunity of dryland [[permaculture]] design.

## Infiltration Rates by Soil Type

Understanding infiltration rates is essential for designing effective recharge systems:

| Soil Type | Minimum Rate | Time to Infiltrate 25mm |
|-----------|-------------|------------------------|
| Sands and loess (dust) | 11-17 mm/hr | 2-3 hours |
| Clay loams | 4-14 mm/hr | 6-20 hours |
| Alkaline swelling clays | ~1 mm/hr | ~24 hours |

### Design Implications

If neutral or acidic sands about 0.5 m deep or more can be spread over alkaline clay pans, the result is an excellent surface that first infiltrates and then holds rain — creating a good garden or tree site capable of storing up to 1 metre of water for long periods. This effect occurs naturally at the discharge end of some gullies or canyons where sand deposits overlie clay substrates.

Another approach is to open deep swales in alkaline soils in front of sands and gravels on the move, allowing them to fill with coarser material and improve infiltration capacity.

## Key Principles

1. **Identify and protect** all natural aquifer intake areas within a catchment.
2. **Enhance infiltration** through vegetation, earthworks, and soil management.
3. **Prevent clogging** of intake areas by dust, silt, and compaction.
4. **Design for recharge** — every drop of rainwater that can be directed into the ground is an investment in long-term water security.
5. **Use water frugally** — withdrawal from groundwater must not exceed recharge rates if permanent habitation is the goal.
