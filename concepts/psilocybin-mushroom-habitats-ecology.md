### 2. Woodlands
The most expansive habitat category, broadly delimited by altitude, temperature, and rainfall.

**Deciduous woodlands**: Cottonwoods (Populus), alders (Alnus), willows (Salix), box elders (Acer), sweet gums (Liquidambar), and hornbeams (Carpinus) along streams, ponds, and lakes. Psilocybe serbica in European beech forests (Fagus sylvatica); P. caerulipes in American beech forests (Fagus grandifolia). Generally rare unless collector/cultivator activity creates localized surges.

**Coniferous forests**: P. baeocystis grows directly from Douglas fir cones (Pseudotsuga menziesii). P. pelliculosa has strong affinity for Douglas fir needles. P. cyanescens fruits from Monterey pine (Pinus radiata) duff. P. muliercula grows exclusively in Abies and Pinus forests at 3150-3500m and 2600-2800m respectively.

### 3. Riparian Zones
Areas along rivers and streams. Geysers and hot springs represent a blending of riparian and disturbed habitats. A Psilocybe similar to P. cyanofibrillosa occurs in sedge grass at hot springs near Whistler, British Columbia. These geologically unstable microcosm environments undergoing radical renewal are perfect springboards for riparian Psilocybes.

### 4. Disturbed Habitats
Roads punched into forests, construction sites, and landslides. P. caerulescens is classic ("demumbes" - landslide mushroom in Meiji-era Japan). Blackberry and Scotch broom thickets provide open, moist environments with deciduous trees. Rodent burrows can be sheathed with mycelium; rodent pellets have been observed sprouting P. azurescens near Astoria, Oregon.

### 5. Gardens
By accident or design, gardens are rich environments. Tilling, importing exotic plants, composting, and watering create favorable conditions. Gardens with cow or horse manure support [[stamets-panaeolus-cyanescens-copelandia-identification-ecology]] (subtropical). Vegetable gardens support terricolous Psilocybes; ornamental gardens with wood debris support lignicolous species. Rhododendron and rose gardens annually renewed with cardboard or mulch have supported P. cyanescens for 12+ consecutive years. Other species: P. baeocystis, P. caerulescens, P. stuntzii, P. subaeruginosa.

### 6. Mosslands
Generally poor habitats for psilocybin species. Can exist as island ecologies within woodlands or grasslands. Psilocybe atrobrunnea, P. montana, and Mythicomyces cornipes (formerly P. cornipes, Smith and Redhead) are representative. P. baeocystis and P. cyanofibrillosa occasionally found in moss islands within rhododendron parks. Hypholoma species (H. elongatum, H. ericaeum, H. dispersum, H. polytritica, H. udum) are lookalikes.

### Burned Lands
Not good places initially, but lead to good fruitings with time. In central Oregon, burning grass-seed fields followed by regrowth has produced enormous fruitings of P. strictipes in subsequent fall. Without revegetation, burned lands suffer erosion, exposure, and cracking. Subsequent flooding and new disturbance can stimulate Psilocybe growth.

## Key Principle
Comparatively few psilocybin mushrooms grow in the woodlands of the true tropics. They occur with frequency in pine forests of Mexico and elsewhere closer to the equatorial subtropics. Deciduous woodlands devastated by recurring cataclysms tend to be better habitats than those in stasis.
