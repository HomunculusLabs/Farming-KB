# Sporeless Mutant Tropism Deficiency in Psilocybe cubensis

## Overview

A critical finding from Badham's 1982 wind tunnel study of Psilocybe
cubensis is that sporeless (sterile) mutant basidiocarps fail to exhibit
the negative geotropic curvature that characterises normal Stage 2-3
development. While fertile basidiocarps turn their caps upward to orient
the gills for downward spore release, sporeless mutants continue growing
in the direction established by earlier tropic responses (wind or light)
and never undergo the upward recurvature. This observation establishes a
biochemical link between spore production and [[coprophilous-habitat-negative-geotropism-spore-dispersal-adaptation]] is produced in the gill tissue during sporulation.

## The Sterile Mutant Phenotype

The sporeless mutant of Psilocybe cubensis studied by Badham was derived
from the same wild-type isolate used for all experiments. It produced
basidiocarps that appeared morphologically normal through Stage 1 and
early Stage 2: stipes elongated, pilei swelled and became spherical,
and the mushrooms grew to similar sizes as their fertile counterparts.
However, the mutant never produced mature spores on its lamellae. When
placed in the wind tunnel, sterile basidiocarps exhibited normal
anemotropic growth during Stages 1-2, curving into the wind just like
fertile mushrooms. The critical difference appeared at the Stage 2-3
transition: while fertile basidiocarps underwent negative geotropic
recurvation (turning the cap upward), sterile basidiocarps continued
growing in the wind-directed orientation indefinitely. The cap opened
but remained oriented by the wind rather than by gravity.

## Linking Sporulation and Geotropism

The absence of geotropic recurvature in sporeless mutants provides strong
evidence that the two processes are biochemically linked. Several lines
of reasoning support this interpretation. First, the timing coincidence:
negative geotropism appears precisely when spore production begins in
fertile basidiocarps. Second, the dependency on light: both sporulation
and geotropic recurvature require light, and in darkness, fertile
basidiocarps produce few spores and show reduced [[adaptive-significance-mushroom-tropisms-spore-dispersal]], proposed by
Gruen (1963) and Hagimoto (1963), suggests that the lamellae produce a
growth-regulating substance that influences stipe elongation and curvature.
Hagimoto and Konishi (1960) demonstrated that agar diffusates from gills
could partially substitute for the pileus in maintaining normal growth
patterns. The sporeless mutant data strengthens this hypothesis by
providing a natural experiment: if the geotropic signal originates in the
gills and is tied to sporulation, then a mutant that cannot sporulate
should also lack the geotropic signal. This is exactly what Badham
observed. The alternative possibility, that the mutation affects both
sporulation and geotropism independently (a dual defect), is less parsimonious
but cannot be ruled out without biochemical identification of the signalling
molecule.

## Practical Significance in Mushroom Cultivation

Sporeless mutants are commercially valuable in mushroom cultivation because
they eliminate spore deposition on neighbouring mushrooms and on the
casing surface. Spore deposits create a dark, messy appearance, trigger
allergic responses in some workers, and can accelerate casing
contamination by providing nutrient material for competitor organisms.
However, the tropism deficiency of sporeless strains has practical
consequences. Caps may not orient optimally for harvest, and stems may
show less uniform upright growth compared to fertile strains. Cultivators
working with sporeless strains should expect different morphological
outcomes and may need to adjust growing conditions (particularly air
circulation patterns) to compensate for the absent geotropic correction.
The tendency of sporeless caps to orient by wind rather than gravity means
that even air currents from fans or ventilation systems will influence
cap direction.

## Molecular Basis: Speculation

The molecular identity of the geotropic signal remains unknown but several
candidates can be proposed based on analogous systems. Auxin-like compounds
have been detected in mushrooms, though no evidence links them to tropism
regulation. Ethylene has been implicated in the avoidance response of
Phycomyces (Russo et al., 1975) and is produced by many fungi during
reproductive development. Gibberellin-like compounds have been found in
mushroom tissue but their function is unclear. The most intriguing
