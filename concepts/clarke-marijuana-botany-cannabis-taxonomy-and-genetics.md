## Genetic Fundamentals

Cannabis has a **diploid chromosome number of 2n = 20** (haploid n = 10), with each chromosome containing hundreds of genes influencing every phase of [[oner-cannabis-breeding-fundamentals]]:

**Law I (Segregation)**: Each gene in a related pair segregates from the other during gamete formation. A cross between homozygous WW and ww parents produces F1 offspring that are all Ww (heterozygous). Crossing two F1 individuals yields F2 offspring in approximately a 3:1 phenotype ratio (3 dominant : 1 recessive), or 1:2:1 genotype ratio.

**Law II (Independent Assortment)**: Unrelated pairs of genes are inherited independently. A dihybrid cross (e.g., tracking tall/short and early/late maturation simultaneously) yields F2 phenotypes in a 9:3:3:1 ratio with complete dominance, creating new recombinant phenotypes not present in either parent.

**Gene interaction**: When two or more gene pairs control a single trait, standard ratios are modified. Clarke gives the example of anthocyanin pigmentation controlled by two gene pairs (P and C), where both must be present for purple calyxes, producing distorted phenotype ratios like 9:3:4 or 9:7.

## The Back-Cross Technique

The back-cross is Clarke's primary tool for determining genotypes and reinforcing parental characteristics:

- Crossing an F1 individual back to a homozygous recessive parent produces a 1:1 phenotype ratio, confirming the F1 was heterozygous.
- Back-crossing to reinforce a desired parental trait involves crossing offspring with the parent expressing that trait, providing chances for recombination and expression of the selected characteristic.

## Polyploidy

Cannabis has 20 chromosomes in its normal diploid state. Polyploidy (multiple chromosome sets) does not occur naturally in Cannabis but can be induced with **colchicine**, a poisonous compound from Colchicum species that inhibits chromosome segregation.

- **Tetraploids (4n = 40)**: Can be 25-30% taller than diploids with intense dark green coloration. However, they tend to revert to diploid in subsequent generations.
- **Triploids (3n = 30)**: Created by crossing tetraploids with diploids; generally inferior to both parent types.
- **Aneuploids** (unbalanced chromosome sets): Inferior in every economic respect, characterized by extremely small seeds.

De Pasquale et al. (1979) reported treated plants showed 166-250% increases in THC with decreases in CBD (30-33%) and CBN (39-65%). Clarke notes that [[berry-growing]]
- Even in a greenhouse, it may not be possible to mature plants to their full native potential
- A breeder may hybridize to combine early maturation with high THC content, but this risks losing unique imported genetics
- Alternatively, a pure cross between individuals of the same strain that bloom earliest can preserve genetics while adapting to local conditions
- After establishing true-breeding lines for both traits, a dihybrid pure cross can produce acclimatized drug strains

Clarke warns that crosses made without a clear goal lead to strains that acclimatize while losing many favorable characteristics. He emphasizes the imperative of preserving original imported Cannabis genetics intact to protect the species from loss of genetic variety through excessive hybridization. A currently unrecognized gene may be responsible for controlling resistance to a pest or disease, and it may only be possible to breed for this gene by back-crossing existing strains to original parental gene pools.
