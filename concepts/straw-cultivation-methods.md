# Straw Cultivation Methods

From Chapter 10 of *Mycelium Running* by [[paul-stamets]]. Despite only a few fungi being native to straw, many mushrooms can be grown on cereal straws due to their powerful fiber-degrading enzymes. Wheat, barley, rye, and rice straws are preferred.

## The Core Challenge

Mycelium must be given an advantage over competing microbes. The cultivator's role is to set the stage so mycelium predominates and forms a contiguous colony. Three recommended methods:

## Method 1: Cold Incubation

Takes advantage of cold-weather mushroom strains that grow at temperatures below competitors' thresholds.

**Procedure:**
1. Choose a cold-loving strain (oyster, enoki, Hypholoma species)
2. Use the most vigorous mycelium available
3. Broadcast mycelium onto wet straw at 10-30% inoculation rate (spawn mass = 10-30% of total substrate mass)
4. Mix thoroughly; place in plastic bag, box, tray, or old bathtub
5. Lay parallel spawn layers to speed colonization
6. Cover with shade cloth, cardboard, or plastic (if shaded)
7. Incubate outdoors until fully colonized (straw turns white with mycelium)
8. Bring to suitable fruiting temperature

**Best for:** Hypholoma, Stropharia, Psilocybe, Agrocybe, and other wood chip-loving terrestrial species that also favor straw.

**Pros:** Simplest method, no equipment needed
**Cons:** Highest risk of failure; but much lower risk for cold-tolerant species

## Method 2: Hydrogen Peroxide Treatment

Mushroom mycelium secretes peroxidases — free radical enzymes that break plant fibers and kill foreign spores. Hydrogen peroxide mimics this natural defense.

**Procedure:**
1. Soak straw with water until thoroughly wet; drain, rinse, drain again
2. Gather 5-10 lbs straw in a waterproof container
3. Pour 1 liter of 3% hydrogen peroxide into 1 gallon fresh water
4. Pour onto straw; thoroughly mix and submerge. Cover and soak 24 hours
5. Rinse with fresh clean water (submerge, drain); rinse and drain again
6. Introduce mycelium immediately after second rinse; distribute evenly
7. Perforate container: holes every few inches on sides/bottom/top (drainage + air exchange)
8. Incubate 1-3 weeks depending on species
9. Initiate fruiting (temperature, watering, indirect light)

**Alternatives:**
- **One Step** (sodium carbonate + hydrogen peroxide): Commercial product
- **Iodaphor**: 120 ml in 10 gallons water (~50 ppm). Soak overnight, flush twice with cold water

**Pros:** Only produces water and oxygen as byproducts; inexpensive for small batches
**Cons:** Less practical at commercial scale

## Method 3: Heat Pasteurization

The method with the longest history and preferred by most cultivators. Heat straw to ~160F to kill competitors while preserving beneficial organisms.

### Hot Water Submersion
1. Place straw in a food-grade metal drum partially filled with water
2. Heat from underneath to 160F (propane, wood-fired, or solar ovens)
3. Submerge straw for 1 hour
4. Remove and spread on a cleaned or plastic-covered table for rapid cooling
5. A 55-gallon drum yields 100-150 lbs moist straw after inoculation and drainage
6. Once cooled below 100F, spread spawn evenly and mix in by hand

### Steam Injection
1. Spread straw on cement or plastic tarp; wet down with hose, turning periodically
2. Achieve 75-80% saturation
3. Place straw in insulated container; inject steam from underneath
4. Insert long-shafted thermometer to measure core temperature (coldest spot = lower center)
5. When core reaches 160F, slow steam to maintain 160-170F for 1-2 hours
6. Stop steam; allow to cool in container

**Cooling precautions:**
- As substrate mass increases, keeping air clean during cooling becomes critical
- Install fan-powered HEPA filters to maintain spore-free air
- Or make container practically airtight with only 1 air entry port
- Window of opportunity: ~1 week to 10 days before competitor molds emerge
- **Do NOT overpasteurize** (>190F): black and pink molds can appear within days

### Inoculation
- Use grain spawn at 10-20% inoculation rate
- Mix thoroughly by hand immediately after cooling
- If enough grain spawn is used, colonization completes within a week

## Alternative Substrates for Mycelium

Beyond straw, mycelium can decompose an enormous range of materials (break into 1/4 to 2 inch pieces, saturate 60-80% with water):

- Bamboo, brewery waste, cacao shells, coconut/coir, coffee grounds
- Corn, corncobs, cornstalks, cotton waste, fabrics, garden waste
- Hair, hemp, leaves, manure, nut casings/seed hulls
- Paper products (newspapers, cardboard, money, books)
- Soybean roughage, sugarcane/bagasse, tea waste, textiles
- Tobacco stalks, trees/shrubs/brush, construction wood waste, water hyacinth

**Mycelium loves complex mixtures with fractal-like microstructures.** Mixing fine and coarse materials creates a friendly matrix.

## Materials NOT Recommended

Formaldehyde, chlorine, methyl bromide, and gamma radiation — effective against molds but pose health risks to people, nontarget organisms, and the environment. Unfortunately still common in developing countries.
