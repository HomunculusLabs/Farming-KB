# Gourmet and Medicinal Mushroom Spawn Grain Production

Grain spawn is the critical bridge between laboratory culture and bulk cultivation. Each grain kernel becomes an individual mycelial capsule, a platform from which mycelium leaps into the surrounding substrate. The goal of grain spawn is to boost mycelium to a state of vigor sufficient for launch into bulk substrates.

## Grain Selection

Virtually all cereal grains can be used for spawn production. The choice depends on availability, cost, kernel size, and cultivator preference:

- **Rye**: The most popular grain for [[growing-gourmet-mushroom-dehydration-methods-equipment]] cultivation. Provides consistent results.
- **Wheat**: Widely available alternative, used extensively in India and other regions.
- **Millet**: Small kernels provide more inoculation points per pound. Preferred by many large spawn producers.
- **Sorghum (milo)**: Suitable for tropical cultivation; used commercially in Asia.
- **Corn**: Less commonly used but functional.

Smaller grain kernels provide more points of inoculation per unit weight, which can accelerate colonization of bulk substrates. The grain must be free of fungicides and ideally organically grown.

## Moisture Content

Moisture is the most critical variable in grain spawn production. The optimum moisture content falls within 45-55%, with an ideal around 50%:

- **Too dry**: Growth is retarded; mycelium forms fine threads and grows slowly.
- **Too wet**: Grains clump, dense slow growth occurs, and bacterial blooms are encouraged. Exploded grain kernels expose internal nutrients, making them susceptible to contamination and creating sites of depressed gas exchange.

Since grain has an inherent moisture content of 8-15% when purchased, less water must be added than might be expected. For accurate measurement, weigh 100 grams of prepared grain, dry in an oven at 300 degrees F (150 degrees C) for 8 hours, and re-weigh. The percentage of weight lost is the moisture content.

## Grain Preparation Methods

Two primary approaches exist for preparing grain spawn:

### Method 1: Boiling/Steeping

Grain is submerged in boiling water for approximately 1 hour (or steeped in hot water). After boiling, the saturated grain is drained and scooped into spawn containers fitted with microporous filter discs. Advantages include even moisture absorption and consistency. A 12-24 hour soak before boiling allows heat-resistant bacterial endospores to germinate, making them sensitive to subsequent heat sterilization.

### Method 2: Dry Loading

Dry grain is placed directly into glass spawn jars, the recommended amount of hot water is added, and jars sit overnight before sterilization. This is a simpler one-step process with less fuel consumption and handling, but produces uneven moisture distribution between kernels.

Both methods require jars or bags fitted with microporous filter discs (3/8 to 1/2 inch hole covered by filter) for adequate gas exchange. Filter discs should be pre-soaked in 5% bleach solution before use.

## Grain Formulas for Spawn Production

Standard rye grain formulas scaled to common container sizes:

### Small Containers (16 oz bottles, quart/liter jars, 1/2 gallon jars)

| Container | Rye | Water | Gypsum | Approx. Moisture |
|-----------|-----|-------|--------|-----------------|
| 16 oz bottle | 100g (125 ml) | 150 ml | 0.5g | 60% |
| Quart/Liter jar | 200g | 200 ml | 1g | 50% |
| 1/2 gallon jar | 480g | 400 ml | 2g | 45% |

### Large Containers (gallon jars, 2.5 gallon jars, spawn bags)

| Container | Rye | Water | Gypsum | Approx. Moisture |
|-----------|-----|-------|--------|-----------------|
| Gallon/4L jar | 800g | 600 ml | 4g | 43% |
| 2.5 gallon/10L jar | 2200g | 1500 ml | 8g | 40% |
| Standard spawn bag | 3300g | 1400 ml | 12g | 38% |

Gypsum is added to keep kernels separated after sterilization and to provide calcium and sulfur. These moisture percentages are approximate since inherent grain moisture varies. Larger containers require proportionally less water because anaerobic environments develop more readily in large grain masses.

## Sterilization Times

Sterilization time increases with container size:

- **16 oz / Quart jars**: 1 hour at 15 psi (250 degrees F)
- **1/2 gallon jars**: 1.5 hours at 15 psi
- **Gallon jars**: 2 hours at 15 psi
- **Standard spawn bags**: 4 hours at 15 psi

Pressure adjustments must be made for elevations above 3,000 feet. Sterilization indicator test strips should be placed in a few jars per run to verify effectiveness.

## Spawn Generation Hierarchy

Mushroom spawn is produced through a progressive expansion of mycelial mass:

### First Generation: Grain Masters

The first transfer of mycelium from petri dish to grain creates the Grain Master. A single 100 x 15 mm petri dish can inoculate 4-20 cups of sterilized grain using the [[agar-to-grain-wedge-transfer-technique]] technique. The culture should be no more than two weeks old, with a margin of uncolonized media along the peripheral edge (about 1/2 inch) to verify purity. Since the Grain Master is used to generate 100 to 1,000 times its mass, absolute purity is essential.

### Second and Third Generations

Each generation exponentially expands the mycelial mass. For Shiitake specifically, Stamets recommends:
- 1st and 2nd generation: Rye, wheat, sorghum, or corn grain
- 3rd (final) generation: Sawdust spawn

### Inoculation Rates

The recommended rate of inoculation from commercial spawn is 3-7% of substrate mass (wet weight). Cultivators producing their own spawn frequently use 8-15%, which accelerates colonization, narrows the window for competitor invasion, and boosts yields. Higher spawning rates increase thermogenesis (self-heating), which must be anticipated and controlled.

## Spawn from the Oyster Mushroom Handbook

The India method described in the MushWorld handbook provides an alternative approach:

1. Boil 10 kg wheat grains in 15 liters of water for 15 minutes
2. Soak for another 15 minutes without heating
3. Drain and cool, turning grains several times
4. Mix with gypsum (calcium sulfate) and 30g calcium carbonate
5. Fill into half-liter milk bottles or polypropylene bags (150-200g each)
6. Autoclave at 121 degrees C for 2 hours
7. Target pH after sterilization: 7.0
8. Inoculate with grain or agar mycelium
9. Incubate at 22-24 degrees C in darkness
10. Full colonization achieved in approximately 2 weeks

## Contamination Awareness

The greatest enemy of spawn production is exponential contamination. A single contaminated grain kernel surrounded by thousands of clean kernels makes the entire container useless. Contamination amplifies not by a factor of 10 per transfer, but by thousands. Molds have a particular advantage: within 2-4 days, every spore can produce hundreds of conidiophores, each bearing dozens more spores. Fresh grain with low endemic bacteria and mold spore counts is essential for consistent spawn production.
