## Coffee Houses and Revolution

Coffee was widely blamed for the death of the French minister Colbert, who died of stomach cancer. Goethe blamed his habitual cafe latte for his chronic melancholia and his attacks of anxiety.

Coffee was also blamed for causing "an excessive state of brain-excitation which becomes manifest by a remarkable loquaciousness sometimes accompanied by accelerated association of ideas."

The prince of Waldeck pioneered an early version of the drug-snitch program when he offered a reward of ten thalers to anyone who would report a coffee drinker to the authorities.

Even servants were rewarded if they informed on employers who had sold them coffee.

By 1777 authorities in continental Europe recognized the suitability of coffee for use by the pillars of dominator society — the clergy and the aristocracy. Punishment for a coffee offense by less privileged classes was usually a public caning followed by fines.

Coffee was once widely suspected of causing impotence. The Princess Palatine Elizabeth Charlotte of Orleans wrote to her sister: "Coffee is not so necessary for Protestant ministers as for Catholic priests, who are not allowed to marry and must remain chaste. I think it tastes exactly like foul breath."

Coffee was introduced in Paris in 1643, and within thirty years there were over 250 coffee houses in the city. In the years immediately preceding the French Revolution there were nearly 2,000 coffee establishments operating.

McKenna observes that "if wild talk is the mother of revolution, then certainly coffee and coffee houses must be its midwife."
