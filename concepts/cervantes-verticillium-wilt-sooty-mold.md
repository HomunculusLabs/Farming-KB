# Verticillium Wilt and Sooty Mold on Cannabis

## Verticillium Wilt

### Overview

Verticillium wilt is a vascular fungal disease that is less
common than [[cervantes-fusarium-vascular-wilt-diseases]] but produces similar devastating symptoms. The fungus
invades the plant's water-conducting tissue (xylem), blocking
fluid flow and causing progressive wilting.

### Identification

Lower leaves develop chlorotic yellowing on margins and between
veins before turning dingy brown. Plants wilt during the day
when transpiration demand is highest and may partially recoup
overnight when the light goes off. Wilt soon overcomes parts of
the plant or the entire plant.

To confirm diagnosis, cut the main stem in two and look for
brownish discoloration of the xylem tissue. The fungus blocks
the flow of plant fluids through the vascular system, causing
the characteristic wilting.

### Control and Prevention

Cleanliness is paramount. Use fresh, sterile growing medium for
every crop. Good drainage prevents waterlogged conditions that
favor the fungus. Use ammonical nitrogen as a nitrogen source
rather than nitrate forms. Do not overfertilize, as excess
nitrogen produces tender growth susceptible to infection.

Biological controls include Bio-Fungus (Trichoderma species) and
Rhizo-Plus (Bacillus subtilis). No chemical spray is effective
against verticillium wilt once the fungus is established inside
the vascular system. Prevention through sterile practices and
healthy root zone management is the only reliable approach.

### Relationship to Fusarium Wilt

Both verticillium and fusarium wilts share similar symptoms:
yellowing leaves, wilting, and brownish xylem discoloration.
The key difference is that fusarium wilt progresses faster and
is more common in warm grow rooms with recirculating nutrient
solutions above 75 degrees F (24 degrees C). Verticillium wilt
tends to develop more slowly. Both require immediate plant
removal and destruction to prevent spread.

## Sooty Mold

### Overview

Black sooty mold is a surface-growing fungus that colonizes
sticky honeydew excreted by sap-sucking insects including
[[cervantes-aphids-identification-control]],
[[cervantes-mealybugs-scale-insect-control]], scale
insects, and [[cervantes-whitefly-pest-control]].
Sooty mold is only a problem on indoor plants when honeydew
is present as a food source.

### Identification

Black sooty mold appears as a dark, soot-like coating on leaf
surfaces, stems, and growing surfaces beneath infested plants.
Unlike other fungal diseases that penetrate plant tissue, sooty
mold grows only on the surface. However, it blocks light from
reaching leaf surfaces, restricting photosynthesis and plant
development.

### Damage

Sooty mold restricts plant development, slows growth, and
diminishes harvest. The dark coating reduces light absorption
by leaf tissue, effectively starving the plant of the light
energy needed for [[cervantes-photosynthesis-chlorophyll-plant-science]].
While the mold itself does not directly attack plant tissue,
the combined effect of insect feeding and light deprivation can
significantly reduce yield.

### Control

The fundamental control strategy is to remove the source of
honeydew by controlling the sap-sucking insects that produce it.
Once honeydew is eliminated, the sooty mold dies without its
food source. Wash away existing honeydew and mold with a
biodegradable soapy solution. Rinse away soapy water a few
hours after applying to prevent any soap residue from affecting
plant tissue.

[[cervantes-homemade-pest-disease-sprays]]
containing insecticidal soap are effective for cleaning sooty
mold from affected surfaces while simultaneously controlling
the underlying insect problem.

### The Honeydew Cascade

Sooty mold illustrates an important ecological chain in the
cannabis garden. Sap-sucking insects feed on plant phloem,
extracting more nitrogen-rich sap than they need. They excrete
the excess as sugar-rich honeydew, which coats leaf surfaces
below. Ants are attracted to honeydew and will farm aphids to
maintain the supply. Sooty mold fungi colonize the honeydew,
reducing photosynthesis. The combined stress of insect feeding,
reduced light, and possible disease entry through feeding wounds
can severely impact plant health and yield.
