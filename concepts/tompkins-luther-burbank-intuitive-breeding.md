# Luther Burbank: Intuitive Plant Breeding

Luther Burbank (1849-1926), known as the "Wizard of Horticulture,"
created over eight hundred new varieties of plants during his lifetime,
including the Burbank potato, the Shasta daisy, and the Santa Rosa plum.
His feats were so extraordinary that his name became a transitive verb
-- "to burbank" -- meaning to modify and improve plant species. Yet
his methods baffled conventional scientists, relying heavily on an
intuitive connection with plants that defied explanation.

## The Secret Garden Method

Burbank was deeply impressed by Charles Darwin's The Variation of
Animals and Plants Under Domestication. When the eminent geneticist
Hugo de Vries visited Santa Rosa, California, to discover Burbank's
methods, he was dumbstruck to find that in the little cottage where
Burbank worked there was neither library nor laboratory. As for his
work notes, De Vries found only "a few knotty old envelopes" with
scrawled records. When asked about his laboratory, Burbank told De
Vries: "I keep it in my head."

His methodology, often branded as wizardry or even charlatanry by
jealous competitors, rested on an extraordinary sensitivity to plants.
In 1901, Burbank told the [[clay-pellet-seed-ball-method-for-desert-revegetation]] in his
patch of potatoes -- a vegetable which almost never sets seed. Knowing
that potato seeds would not reproduce the exact qualities of the parent
plant but would instead yield something new, Burbank recognized an
extraordinary opportunity. One of the twenty-three seeds in the ball
gave rise to a potato variety of exceptional quality. He received $150
from a Marblehead seedman for his discovery. Christened the "Burbank,"
it was later widely planted and helped alleviate the Irish potato
famine's legacy.

## Intuitive Communication with Plants

Burbank's approach to plant breeding was distinctly unscientific by
the standards of his day. He described his process as one of
"conversing" with his plants, of understanding their inner nature and
potential. He believed that plants possessed a form of consciousness or
sensitivity that could be perceived by a sufficiently attuned human
mind.

This intuitive approach put him at odds with the rising Mendelian
genetics establishment, which sought to reduce heredity to mathematical
laws. Burbank insisted that the living organism could not be fully
understood through dissection and analysis alone, but required an
appreciation of the whole plant in its environment.

## The Calla Lily Experiment

One of Burbank's most remarkable feats involved the calla lily. The
conventional wisdom held that the calla lily's characteristic spathe
(the showy white "petal") was immutable. Burbank, through an intuitive
understanding of the plant's potential, was able to produce calla lilies
with spathes in a range of colors including pink, yellow, and even
striped varieties -- something his colleagues considered impossible.

He achieved this not through any conventional hybridization technique
that he could clearly explain, but through a process he described as
"telling the plant what he wanted it to do." Whether this was
metaphor, wishful thinking, or genuine attunement to plant capacities
remains a matter of debate.

## Legacy and Philosophical Impact

Burbank's work embodied a philosophy that was both ahead of its time
and at odds with the direction biology would take in the twentieth
century. He believed that all life was interconnected, that plants
responded to human thought and emotion, and that the breeder's
relationship with his subjects was as important as any technical
knowledge.
