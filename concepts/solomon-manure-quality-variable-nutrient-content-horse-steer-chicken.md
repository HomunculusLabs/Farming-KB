# [[solomon-manure-nutrient-variability-and-quality|Manure]] Quality and Variable Nutrient Content

**Concept:** Why [[solomon-manure-nutrient-variability-cof]] content is wildly unpredictable, the range of [[solomon-manure-types-and-nutrient-values|values]] across animal types, and how to use manure safely as a supplement rather than a primary fertility source.

## The Manure Variability Problem

One of the most persistent myths [[hamilton-kelp-and-seaweed-in-organic-gardening]] is that "manure grows stuff." The reality is far more nuanced. The nutrient content of animal manures varies enormously depending on the animal's diet, the bedding material mixed in, how the manure was stored, and how long it has decomposed.

Solomon presents data showing the extraordinary range:
- **Horse manure:** Nitrogen can range from 0.5% to 1.3% — a 2.6× difference
- **Steer manure:** Can go as high as 2% nitrogen, but may be as poor as 0.5% — a 4× difference
- The same spread occurs for phosphorus and potassium

This variability exists because there is no telling what balance of nutrients was in the feed the horse, cow, sheep, or other animal consumed. Animals are not [[manure-quality-variability-and-fertilizer-value|fertilizer]] factories with consistent output — they are what they eat, and their manure reflects their diet.

---

## Why the Range Is So Wide

Several factors contribute to this unpredictability:

### Diet Quality
Animals fed high-quality feed with balanced protein, minerals, and energy produce manure with higher and more consistent nutrient content. Animals fed poor-quality hay, crop residues, or minimal supplementation produce correspondingly poor manure.

### Bedding Material
There is no telling how much straw, sawdust, wood chips, or other bedding material was mixed into any given batch of manure. Bedding dilutes nutrient concentration significantly. A "load of manure" that is 70% straw and 30% actual manure has a very different fertilizer value than one that is mostly manure with minimal bedding.

### Storage and Aging
Fresh manure has different properties than [[comparison-wood-chip-mulch-vs-leaf-mold-vs-composted-manure-soil-building]]. As manure ages, nitrogen can be lost through volatilization (especially as ammonia), while phosphorus and potassium become more concentrated relative to the remaining nitrogen. The length and method of storage (composted, stacked, spread, etc.) all affect the final nutrient profile.

## Solomon's Manure Case Studies

### The Racehorse Stable (High Quality)
One neighbor obtained manure from a stable keeping bred racehorses. These animals are fed like endurance athletes — their hay is purchased from knowledgeable farmers who ensure proper protein content and drying. The horses also receive a broad range of protein supplements, including oilseed meals and abundant vitamins. The resulting manure is genuinely high-quality, and the garden it produces reflects that investment in animal nutrition.

### The Backyard Horse (Low Quality)
Another neighbor sold horse manure from horses kept in her back pasture. The vegetation in their pasture was pathetic despite all the horse urine the pasture received. To get decent grass, she had to buy most of the horse's food, and she fed mainly local grass hay of such low quality that the agricultural expert consulted wouldn't use it for anything but bedding straw. The resulting manure was barely adequate — rose bushes and other low-demand plants grew a little better, but it would not sustain [[query-how-do-i-save-water-in-a-vegetable-garden]] producing highly nutritious food.

### The Lesson
Both sources are "horse manure." Both look roughly similar. But their fertilizer value is dramatically different because of the underlying diet and management. Without knowing the animal's diet, you cannot know the manure's quality.

## Nutrient Ranges by Animal Type

The following ranges represent typical N-P-K values (percentages), but remember that actual values within these ranges depend entirely on diet and management:

| Manure Type | Nitrogen (%) | Phosphorus (%) | Potassium (%) |
|-------------|-------------|----------------|---------------|
| Dairy cow | 0.5-0.7 | 0.2-0.3 | 0.5-0.7 |
| Horse | 0.5-1.3 | 0.2-0.5 | 0.4-1.0 |
| Sheep | 0.8-1.2 | 0.3-0.5 | 0.6-1.0 |
| Chicken | 1.0-2.5 | 0.5-1.0 | 0.5-1.8 |
| Rabbit | 1.5-2.0 | 0.7-1.2 | 0.8-1.5 |
| Steer | 0.5-2.0 | 0.2-0.5 | 0.4-1.0 |
| Duck | 0.8-1.2 | 0.4-0.6 | 0.5-1.0 |
| Hog | 0.4-0.6 | 0.2-0.4 | 0.3-0.5 |
| Goat | 0.7-1.0 | 0.2-0.4 | 0.6-1.0 |

**Note:** These ranges are compiled from numerous sources. The extraordinary range for each type should make any gardener cautious about relying on manure as a primary nutrient source.

## Solomon's Recommendation

Given this unpredictability, Solomon's position is clear:

> It is not easy to obtain manure that you can be confident in — that you can be sure will grow a great garden and produce highly nutritious food.

His recommendation: **Use only the minimum amount of manure necessary to supply the soil ecology's requirement for organic matter.** Do not attempt to use manure as your primary fertilizer. Instead:

1. Use modest amounts of manure or compost for soil ecology (microbial food, humus, structure)
2. Use a [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof]] (COF) blend for actual plant nutrition
3. The COF provides known, consistent quantities of N-P-K plus [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-trace-minerals]]
4. Manure provides the organic matter that feeds [[soil-life]] and improves structure

This dual approach — COF for nutrition, manure for ecology — avoids the gamble of relying on unknown manure quality while still benefiting from what organic matter provides.

### COF: The Complete Organic Fertilizer

Solomon's COF recipe provides consistent, known nutrition:
- 4 parts seed meal (cottonseed, soybean, or canola)
- 1 part agricultural lime (finely ground)
- 1 part gypsum (or double the agricultural lime if no gypsum)
- 1 part dolomite lime
