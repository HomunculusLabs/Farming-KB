The reality is far more nuanced. The nutrient content of animal manures varies enormously depending on the animal's diet, the bedding material mixed in, how the manure was stored, and how long it has decomposed.

Solomon presents data showing the extraordinary range:
- **Horse manure:** Nitrogen can range from 0.5% to 1.3% — a 2.6× difference
- **Steer manure:** Can go as high as 2% nitrogen, but may be as poor as 0.5% — a 4× difference
- The same spread occurs for phosphorus and potassium

This variability exists because there is no telling what balance of nutrients was in the feed the horse, cow, sheep, or other animal consumed. Animals are not [[comparison-wood-chip-mulch-vs-leaf-mold-vs-composted-manure-soil-building]]. As manure ages, nitrogen can be lost through volatilization (especially as ammonia), while phosphorus and potassium become more concentrated relative to the remaining nitrogen. The length and method of storage (composted, stacked, spread, etc.) all affect the final nutrient profile.

## Solomon's Manure Case Studies

### The Racehorse Stable (High Quality)
One neighbor obtained manure from a stable keeping bred racehorses. These animals are fed like endurance athletes — their hay is purchased from knowledgeable farmers who ensure proper protein content and drying. The horses also receive a broad range of protein supplements, including oilseed meals and abundant vitamins. The resulting manure is genuinely high-quality, and the garden it produces reflects that investment in animal nutrition.

### The Backyard Horse (Low Quality)
Another neighbor sold horse manure from horses kept in her back pasture. The vegetation in their pasture was pathetic despite all the horse urine the pasture received. To get decent grass, she had to buy most of the horse's food, and she fed mainly local grass hay of such low quality that the agricultural expert consulted wouldn't use it for anything but bedding straw. ### COF: The Complete Organic Fertilizer

Solomon's COF recipe provides consistent, known nutrition:
- 4 parts seed meal (cottonseed, soybean, or canola)
