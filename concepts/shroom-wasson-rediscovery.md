# Wasson and the Rediscovery of Psilocybin Mushrooms

updated: 2026-04-24

R. Gordon Wasson (1898-1986) was a Wall Street banker and amateur
ethnomycologist whose investigations in Mexico led to the Western
rediscovery of psychoactive psilocybin mushrooms. His 1957 Life magazine
article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" introduced millions of readers to
the existence of these fungi and inadvertently kick-started the
psychedelic revolution of the 1960s.

## From Banking to Ethnomycology

Robert Gordon Wasson was born on 22 September 1898, the son of an
Episcopalian minister. After active service in the First World War, he
graduated from Harvard with a degree in literature and began his career
as a journalist before crossing into banking, eventually becoming vice-
president at J. P. Morgan & Co. His consuming interest in mushrooms was
stimulated by a widely retold incident from his honeymoon with Valentina
Pavlovna Guercken in 1926. While strolling through the Catskill
Mountains, Wasson's horror at the sight of wild mushrooms was
intensified by his bride's evident delight as she gathered them for
cooking. Their extreme reactions suggested a fundamental cultural
difference, and they embarked on a lifelong quest to investigate and
understand the varied relationships between humans and fungi.

## The Safford Controversy

The Western rediscovery of Mexican mushrooming practices began,
ironically, with a vigorous scholarly denial that they had ever existed.
In 1918, the American botanist William Safford published a paper arguing
that teonanacatl had been none other than the peyote cactus, and that
Sahagun and his native informants had been wrong. Safford's failure to
find psychoactive fungi in Mexico was because, having misread Sahagun,
he had been looking in the arid north rather than the humid south.

The Austrian physician Blas Pablo Reko was the first to challenge
Safford, publishing in 1919 that teonanacatl "is actually a fungus
which grows on dung-heaps and which is still used under the same old
name by the Indians of the Sierra Juarez in Oaxaca." In 1936, linguist
Robert J. Weitlaner set out to conduct research amongst the Mazatec
Indians in the remote town of Huautla de Jimenez in Oaxaca, where he
discovered that mushrooms were indeed used in curative divinatory rites.

## Scientific Identification and Psilocybin Isolation

In 1938, Reko was joined by the brilliant young Harvard ethnobotanist
Richard Evans Schultes. Together they collected samples from which
Schultes made the first formal identification. In 1938, a party led by
Jean Bassett Johnson became the first Westerners to witness an
indigenous mushroom curing ceremony. The mounting evidence was
incontrovertible, and the peyote hypothesis was finally abandoned.

Wasson enlisted notable scientists in his endeavours, most importantly
Roger Heim (1900-1979), Professor of Mycology at the prestigious Musee
National d'Histoire Naturelle in Paris, who took on the task of
identifying and describing the mushrooms used in Mazatec ceremonies.
Several species, primarily of the genus Psilocybe (but also Stropharia
and Conocybe), were identified. Sufficient samples were cultivated in
Heim's laboratory for [[albert-hofmann]], the Swiss chemist famous for the
discovery of LSD, to isolate the psychoactive alkaloids. In 1958, while
working for the Basle-based Sandoz pharmaceutical company, Hofmann
first synthesised psilocybin and psilocin, which he named after the
mushrooms' Latin epithet.

## The Life Article and Its Consequences

Wasson popularised his discoveries in a 13 May 1957 Life magazine
article, eye-catchingly captioned "Seeking the Magic Mushroom: A New
York banker goes to Mexico's mountains to participate in the age-old
rituals of Indians who chew strange growths that produce visions." The
article was read by millions. Here, for the first time, a positive
understanding of the psychoactive properties of fungi was broadcast to
the Western world, captured in the snappy and memorable term "Magic
Mushroom."

The article played a pivotal role in kick-starting the whole psychedelic
revolution. Key figures of that movement, including [[timothy-leary]],
experimented with psychedelics as a direct result. Wasson had
inadvertently revealed the name and whereabouts of Maria Sabina, and
within months of the article going to press, Western seekers were
knocking on her door. The trickle of visitors in the early 1960s became
a flood, until Oaxaca was awash with hippies on the magic mushroom
trail.
