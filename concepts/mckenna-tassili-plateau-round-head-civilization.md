## The Tassili Plateau and the Round Head Civilization

## Archaeological Evidence for Psychedelic Paradise

Archaeological evidence for the partnership paradise can be found in the Sahara Desert of southern Algeria in an area called the Tassili-n-Ajjer Plateau. A curious geological formation, the plateau is like a labyrinth, a vast badlands of stone escarpments cut by wind into many perpendicular narrow corridors. Aerial photographs give the eerie impression of an abandoned city.

In the Tassili-n-Ajjer, rock paintings date from the late Neolithic to as recently as two thousand years ago. Here are the earliest known depictions of shamans with large numbers of grazing cattle. The shamans are dancing with fists full of mushrooms and also have mushrooms sprouting out of their bodies. In one instance they are shown running joyfully, surrounded by the geometric structures of their hallucinations. The pictorial evidence seems incontrovertible.

Images similar to those of the Tassili occur in pre-Columbian Peruvian textiles. In these textiles the shamans hold objects that may be mushrooms but may also be chopping tools. With the Tassili frescoes, however, the case is clear. At Matalen-Amazar and Ti-nTazarift on the Tassili, the dancing shamans clearly have mushrooms in their hands and sprouting from their bodies.

## The Bee-Faced Mushroom Shaman

One of the most remarkable images from the Tassili is the "bee-faced mushroom shaman" — a dancing figure wearing what appears to be a bee mask, clutching mushrooms, surrounded by hallucinatory geometric patterns. This figure has become iconic in discussions of [[mckenna-nostalgia-for-paradise]], the idea of a lost golden age of plenty, partnership, and social balance.

The contention is that the rise of language, [[dominator-culture-and-partnership-society]] arose and flourished; there hunter-gatherer culture slowly gave way to domestication of animals and plants. In this milieu the psilocybin-containing mushrooms were encountered, consumed, and deified. Language, poetry, ritual, and thought emerged from the darkness of the hominid mind.

Eden was not a myth — for the prehistoric peoples of the high plateau of the Tassili-n-Ajjer, Eden was home.

## Archaeological Evidence at Tassili n"Ajjer

The Tassili n'Ajjer plateau, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1982, contains one of the most important collections of prehistoric rock art in the world. Over 15,000 engravings and paintings have been documented across the plateau, spanning a period from approximately 12,000 BCE to the first centuries CE. The rock art is distributed across several distinct stylistic periods, of which the Round Head Period is the oldest and most enigmatic.

The site was first brought to widespread scholarly attention by French ethnographer Henri Lhote, who undertook extensive expeditions to the plateau between 1956 and the 1970s. Lhote documented hundreds of paintings and published his findings in a series of influential works, though his interpretations — particularly his tendency to romanticize the Round Head figures — have been critiqued by later researchers. More recent surveys by archaeologists such as Jean-Loïc Le Quellec and Malika Hachid have provided more measured assessments of the site's significance.

The geological and climatic context of the rock art is crucial. During the African Humid Period (approximately 11,500 to 5,500 years ago), the Sahara was a green landscape of lakes, rivers, and grasslands — an environment that would have supported large human populations, abundant cattle, and, crucially, the growth of psilocybin-containing mushrooms in the dung of grazing herbivores. As the climate dried, the Tassili plateau's rock shelters preserved a record of this vanished world.

## The Round Head Figures: Interpretations and Debates

The Round Head style is named for the distinctive rendering of human figures with rounded, featureless or minimally detailed heads. These paintings typically depict scenes of communal activity: dancing, processions, hunting, and ritual performances. The figures are often shown in elaborate costumes or with animal attributes — masks, horns, tails — suggesting shamanic practices involving the transformation of human identity through ritual.

The mushroom imagery that McKenna emphasized is present but not universal in the Round Head paintings. Some figures clearly hold mushroom-shaped objects, while others hold objects that could be mushrooms, roots, rattles, or ceremonial staffs. The most famous image — the so-called "bee-faced mushroom shaman" from the site of Sefar — depicts a masked figure with what appear to be mushrooms clutched in both hands, surrounded by geometric patterns consistent with the visual phenomena reported in psychedelic experiences.

Scholarly interpretation of these images varies considerably. Some researchers, including ethnomycologist Gastón Guzmán, have supported the identification of the objects as mushrooms and the paintings as evidence of prehistoric psilocybin use. Others, including Le Quellec, have argued that the mushroom interpretation relies on selective reading of ambiguous imagery and that the objects could represent a variety of ceremonial or utilitarian items. The debate remains unresolved, though the consensus among researchers who have visited the site acknowledges the striking visual similarity between the painted objects and known psychoactive mushroom species.

## Other Researchers' Views

Henri Lhote, the site's principal documenter, was cautious about the mushroom interpretation, focusing instead on the broader cultural and artistic significance of the Round Head paintings. He noted the clear connections between the Round Head style and later Egyptian artistic conventions but did not endorse the specific claim that the paintings depicted mushroom use.

Riane Eisler, in *The Chalice and the Blade*, cited the Tassili paintings as evidence of pre-patriarchal, partnership-oriented societies in North Africa, emphasizing the prominence of female figures and the absence of warfare imagery in the Round Head period. This reading aligns with McKenna's interpretation but focuses on social organization rather than psychoactive use.

## Egyptian Connections

The many Round Head paintings showing unmistakable Egyptian influence in content and style, at a time when the Nile valley was nearly deserted, suggest that these motifs were first introduced into Egypt by the dwellers of the Western Desert. If proven, this would indicate the central Sahara as the source of what later became the high civilization of pre-Dynastic Egypt.
