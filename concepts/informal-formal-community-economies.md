## Informal and Formal Community Economies

[[permaculture]] economics operates at two complementary levels: the **[[informal-economy-and-community-currencies]]** of barter, labor exchange, gift systems, and community currency, and the **formal economy** of legally structured cooperatives, savings and loans, and investment funds. [[bill-mollison]] treats both as essential components of community self-reliance, arguing that robust local economies need both the flexibility of informal exchange and the accountability of formal financial structures. Together, they create a financial ecosystem that retains wealth within communities, provides meaningful work regardless of external economic conditions, and aligns economic activity with [[permaculture-ethics]].

## The Informal Economy

### Household and Neighborhood Barter

Barter is the most fundamental form of community exchange, practiced particularly in rural or neighborhood settings where people know one another. At the household level, people exchange garden products and plants, share labor, and trade goods and services informally. These exchanges build social bonds alongside material flows — the act of trading with a neighbor reinforces the relationship that makes future exchanges possible and desirable.

Occasionally, people form small work groups of three to five people to build houses, create gardens, or tackle household projects. These groups may be episodic, operating in a round-robin pattern until all participants" needs are met. The informal nature of such arrangements keeps administrative overhead near zero while ensuring that everyone's contributions are visible and reciprocated through direct experience.

### The Bendigo Home Builder's Club

Mollison provides a detailed case study of the Bendigo Home Builder's Club in Victoria, Australia — a group of 35 people building individual homes through organized labor exchange. The system operates as follows:

- Members pay **$5 per year per family**, primarily covering newsletter printing and distribution costs.
- Each member can be either a **recipient or donor** of labor.
- The **unit of exchange is one hour of labor**, and all labor is considered equal regardless of skill level.
- A standard **labor exchange form** — legally binding — records every transaction, debiting the recipient and crediting the donor for each hour worked.
- A **Labour Organiser** manages the balance of payments and dispatches laborers to recipients, who must have at least **60 hours in credit** before receiving help.

This system demonstrates that complex construction projects — normally requiring significant capital — can be accomplished through organized mutual aid. The equal valuation of all labor hours is significant: it prevents the emergence of hierarchy based on skill differentials and reinforces the principle that every person's time and effort are equally valuable to the community.

### Community Barter Clubs

Community Barter Clubs extend the labor exchange model to a broader range of goods and services. Residents offer everything from landscaping and massage to mowing and printing, all tracked through a system of debits and credits. Key features include:

- The **club secretary or organizer is paid in credits**, not cash, ensuring the administrative function itself is sustained within the barter system.
- A **credit equals one hour** of service, with the donor and recipient agreeing on the value of specific jobs.
- Members are **not limited to one-to-one exchanges** — because the organizer maintains records of all individuals' debits and credits, transactions can occur across the entire membership.
- The system is especially valuable for **unemployed or underemployed** people and those who need services but cannot afford cash payment.

### Brokerage and Trade Units

At larger scales, brokerage houses now deal in surplus barter systems for industry using a **Trade Unit (T.U.)** valued at approximately $1 for pricing and exchange. These trade units can be placed in smaller blocks for a variety of exchanges in goods and services, effectively turning a large surplus of one commodity into a range of needed services and goods. Exchange newsletters, computer services, and classified advertisements facilitate these transactions, with barter centers charging only a proportion of successful swaps as commission.

### The LET System (Local Employment Trading System)

The **[[let-system]]** represents the most sophisticated informal economy model Mollison describes. It centers on a community currency — "green dollars" — with these characteristics:

- Every joining member must be willing to trade in local green dollars.
- Green dollars carry **no interest**, and administration costs are charged on a "cost of service" basis.
- Taxes applicable to transactions are the **responsibility of individual members**.
- Any member can know the **turnover or balance** of any other member — full transparency.
- Every member receives **periodic account statements**.
- The currency is equivalent to legal tender in value but is **not issued by any central authority** and cannot be cashed in for federal currency.
- Green dollars are "earned" by providing goods or services to others and "spent" by using services or goods.

Unlike simple barter, a member in credit can spend across the entire range of services and goods offered by the community. Production is never limited by the lack of money — anyone who wants work can offer services and need not wait for conventional "jobs." Businesses can charge federal currency for spare parts while pricing labor in green dollars, making imported goods relatively more expensive and encouraging local sourcing.

The system ensures the community account is always balanced, as only members can trade with each other. In effect, every member issues their own currency through productive activity, and the system could return any community to full employment. Mollison notes that an ideal member has many transactions but accumulates modest debits and credits — active participation matters more than accumulation.

### Volunteer Labor and Community Projects

The informal economy also encompasses purely volunteer labor, gift exchanges, and community project adoption. Mollison advocates for "convivial tree-planting on community common areas" as a responsibility of every household, potentially achieved through the "adoption" of a few acres of community forest. Other community projects — infrastructure, events, education — benefit from volunteer efforts, gifts of materials, and donated time as advisors or entertainers.

## The Formal Economy

### Why Formal Structures Are Needed

"Formal" means that goods or services are conducted under a legal umbrella and regulated by accounting procedures. Formal economics become necessary when people (managers) act for a group of members or investors rather than just for themselves or their households. Legal procedures must also be followed by self-employed people or family businesses where cash is received for goods or services rendered publicly.

### Cooperative Principles

A cooperative is a group of people acting together for the benefit of members, operating as a legal entity with limited liability and perpetual succession. Mollison outlines the cooperative principles:

1. **Open membership** — open to all who can use the services and accept the responsibilities of membership.
2. **Democratic organization** — members participate in decisions affecting the cooperative, with affairs administered by elected representatives.
3. **Limited interest on share capital** — fair but limited return on capital investment, restricting the influence of those holding large share positions.
4. **Member ownership of surplus** — savings from operations belong to members, who decide whether to develop the business, provide common services, or distribute among members based on degree of involvement.
5. **Education** — cooperatives should educate members, officers, employees, and the public on cooperation principles and techniques.
6. **Cooperation between cooperatives** — encouraging the development of more cooperatives through mutual support.

### The Mondragon Model

Mollison highlights the worker-cooperative system centered on **Mondragon** in the Basque region of Spain as a premier example of formal cooperative economics. In less than 30 years, 96 worker cooperatives emerged, employing 17,000 worker-members. Key features include:

- Each person invests approximately **$5,000 when joining**, which can be borrowed from the cooperative bank or paid in installments deducted from wages over two years.
- Of this investment, **20% goes to collectively owned funds** and 80% purchases an individual shareholding or capital account.
- Individual accounts are normally not drawn upon except on retirement, death, or extreme hardship.
- The cooperative can partially **fund itself** through member capital contributions, supplemented by a supportive cooperative banking sector.

The Mondragon model demonstrates that formal cooperative structures can achieve significant scale — thousands of workers across dozens of enterprises — while maintaining democratic governance, member ownership, and alignment with community values.

### Other Formal Economic Structures

Beyond cooperatives, Mollison identifies several formal economic structures appropriate for community self-reliance:

- **Community savings and loans** — locally governed financial institutions that retain deposits for local investment.
- **Small business service offices** — shared administrative, legal, and accounting support for community enterprises.
- **Investment funds for special projects** — pooled capital for infrastructure, land acquisition, or [[enterprise-development-mushroom-growers]].
- **Leasing companies or systems** — community-owned equipment available for hire, spreading capital costs across many users.

## The Complementary Relationship

The informal and formal economies are not alternatives but complements. The informal economy provides flexibility, low barriers to entry, and social cohesion — it is the daily fabric of exchange that keeps a community functioning even when external economic conditions are adverse. The formal economy provides legal protection, accountability, scale, and access to capital — it enables communities to undertake projects that require investment, liability management, or contractual relationships beyond the scope of informal trust.

Together, they create a **financial ecosystem** that is far more resilient than either alone. When external money is scarce, the informal economy keeps goods and services flowing through barter, labor exchange, and community currency. When capital investment is needed, the formal economy provides the legal and financial structures to mobilize and protect community resources. The goal is a community where no one is excluded from economic participation by lack of conventional money, and where all financial activity serves the long-term health of both the community and the ecosystems that sustain it.
