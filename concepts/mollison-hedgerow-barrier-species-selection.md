Farm woodlots and hedgerows serve multiple on-farm purposes that extend far beyond commercial timber production. While farmers are often encouraged to plant trees suited to central processing for wood pulp or off-farm markets, Bill Mollison emphasizes the many on-farm needs that woodlots can satisfy: fuel from high sugar crops, [[mollison-farm-forestry-and-woodlot-design]] is selecting species that serve multiple functions within the system. Conifers, while useful as timber, are generally slow-growing and less broadly useful in permaculture systems compared to multi-use species. This approach mirrors the broader [[windbreak-shelterbelt-species-selection]] for integrated land use.

## Timber Durability Ratings

For structural applications, species with long natural durability in the ground are the first choice. Mollison provides two categories of durability ratings drawn from standard references.

### High Durability (70+ Years in Ground)

These species are recommended as multi-use selections for farm woodlots:

- Strawberry Jam Acacia (*Acacia acuminata*)
- Black Locust (*Robinia pseudoacacia*)
- Catalpa (*Catalpa speciosa*)
- Almost all cedars (*Cedrus* spp.)
- Juniper (*Juniperus communis*)
- River Red Gum (*E. camaldulensis*)
- Huon Pine (*Athrotaxis franklinii*)

Species marked with an asterisk in the original source are recommended specifically as multi-use species, suitable not only for timber but also for forage, shelter, and other farm functions.

### Moderate Durability (30-70 Years in Ground)

A second tier of durable species provides options across different [[bloomfield-ghosts-of-evolution-osage-orange-anachronism]] (*Maclura pomifera*)
- Bald Cypress (*Taxodium distichum*)
- Redwood (*Sequoia sempervirens*)
- [[free-range-poultry-forage-design-mollison]] species, bee forage plants, and species yielding foliage or fruit for distillation as oils or alcohols. Some species suit arid conditions, others riverine or coastal environments.

## Plant Barriers for Livestock Containment

Plant barriers may be erected with or without supporting fences for a variety of reasons:

- To contain or exclude livestock
- To shelter gardens and houses from wind
- To increase the efficiency of wind and sun
- To prevent re-invasion by unwanted plants
- To screen unwanted views and sounds

The most difficult barrier function to satisfy is livestock containment. Almost the only tree that stops everything but needs no pruning is *Lycium ferocissimum*, or African boxthorn. It will stop bulls and resist salt spray and gales, while also feeding poultry. In coastal sandy areas, the crown spreads gradually to about 7 m wide, and plants may seed down, but the cost of uprooting seedlings every 50 years is small compared with the frequent attention needed to keep less ferocious hedgerows tight.

This makes African boxthorn the ultimate hedge-barrier for broadscale, large livestock containment. Cautious browsing by stock sometimes occurs, but an established Lycium hedge forms a stable, wide fence. In pastures and on heavy soils it has no tendency to spread as seedlings.

Lycium compounds or bomas of dead branches will protect plantings of more useful trees, and clipped branches deter rabbits when strewn around seedlings. Other species recommended for formidable barriers include jujube and *Rosa multiflora* or *Rosa rugosa*, though both need hard stopping or pruning in their first few years.

## Traditional and Supplementary Hedgerow Species

Hawthorn (*Crataegus*) species, live-set at 60-90 cm and later layered or interwoven with dead trimmings, represent the traditional livestock hedge of Europe and Tasmania. These require cutting to shape every 4-6 years. All such hedges are made more efficient by a few strands of barbed wire strained through them in their infancy, by ditch and bank approaches, or by permanent electric wire protection.

Additional barrier species include the redoubtable red cedar, honey locust, and shingle oak (*Quercus imbricaria*). Cacti are effective in dry areas, as are unpalatable or thorny local species. Inside more impenetrable hedges, only woven or netted palisades repel small livestock.
