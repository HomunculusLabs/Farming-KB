# Mollison Design Criteria and Aboriginal Ecological Wisdom

## Why Design Matters

If there is a single claim that distinguishes [[permaculture-climatic-zones-design]] from other systems of agriculture, with the notable exception of keyline concepts, it is that permaculture is primarily a consciously designed agricultural system.

The main reasons for designing a plant system are:

- To save our energies in the system
- To cope with energies entering the system from outside (sun, wind, fire)
- To arrange plants so that they assist the health and survival of other plants
- To place all units (plants, earthworks, and artifacts such as houses) in the best possible arrangement in landscape
- To suit climate and site (specific design)
- To integrate with man and society, and save heating fuel and cooking energy
- To provide a wide range of necessities for man, in a way every man can achieve

## Critique of Ornamental Design

The imposed zonation of functions around human settlements, recorded by Von Thunen in his analysis of pre-industrial northern European settlements, has the appearance of design but is in fact an unconscious result of the limitations of that community in its foot-and-horse-bound economy. Such arrays are patterns forced on people and landscape, not conscious designs chosen for their relevance to society and energy savings.

Conventional landscape design is described as "a forcing of nature and landscape into a salute to wealth and power, and has no other purpose or function." The formal lawns of the Taj Mahal, maintained by widows with small knives, and the British gardener clipping hedges for nouveaux riches, both represent wasted energies in controlled, meaningless toil.

## Design as Energy Conservation

The characteristic that typifies all permanent agricultures is that the needs of the system for energy are provided by that system. Modern crop agriculture is totally dependent on external energies -- hence the oil problem.

Without permanent agriculture there is no possibility of a stable social order. Overpopulation itself may be a response to increased energy needs in annual (peasant) crops; it was never a problem in forested areas, nor where energy excess is locally available.

## Aboriginal Ecological Wisdom

### True Signs
The Aborigines of Tasmania left to their descendants a legend of "true signs" -- something that happens to you here means something else is happening there. Something that happens now means something else will happen later. Ghosts pluck at the muscles of the shoulder as someone dies, waves rise from a smooth sea to signal illness, and the ti-tree breaks into flower as the swans lay their first eggs.

Virgil too speaks of such things: "A heavy corn crop follows a heavy walnut crop, and a leafy corn crop the leafy walnut." The ti-tree and the walnut indicate the complexity of relationships that may exist between species.

### Tribal Ecology
European man in Tasmania occupies just that area where stringy bark (Eucalyptus obliqua) grows. Aboriginal tribes were limited in range by "brother" trees like the ironbark, the native cherry, or the cider gum. The tribal ecology was the ecology of that tree.

To injure a tree was to injure a brother; this outlook is one of sophisticated conservation. The most important man of any tribe, chosen for long and true memory, was the crop-master -- not the chief of warfare, not the doctor of the spirit and flesh, but the living computer, one of a long line of accurate memories, who orchestrated the taking of food, arranged taboos and prohibitions, feasts and celebrations, and ensured that the tribal permaculture endured.

### Aboriginal Agricultural Practices
The Aborigine acts as a de facto agriculturalist. In gathering seeds and small fruits, he rakes clean the leaves from under selected trees, spreads skins or makes a funnel in sand, then beats the trees to bring down fruit or seed. By doing so, he has incidentally protected the tree from fire, provided a drip-line mulch, and thus altered the chance of survival of high-yielding trees.

They stored seed in clay-lined pits, baskets, wood or stone hollows, and transported seed over great distances, trying out such plants as native tobacco (Mingkulpa) at selected sites. Waterholes were thatched over to prevent evaporation. Mulching was demonstrated with old blankets, cardboard, clothes, and mulga or tamarisk "hay" to persuade Aboriginal gardeners that mulch conserves water.

The ashes, bones, and litter around camps are converted into rich garden soil by soil fungi, termites, and bacteria in the heat of central Australia, quickly reducing potentially noxious wastes to soil, hence to a food resource.

### A Very Large Vocabulary
A very large (and largely unrecorded) vocabulary exists, detailing food plants, fire effects and control, the links between species, and general ecological patterning. Combined efforts by linguists, botanists, astronomers, ecologists, and generalists are needed to recover the detailed information of the older people of the deserts.

### Native Animal Management
Shooting kills many kangaroo rejected for food because of no fat or yellow fat. Baited compounds or traps at water holes make far more sense, as fat-free, female, and old male animals can be released to breed again, and only the immature and well-conditioned animals taken for food. Active planting of emu berry, honey locust, tree lucerne, and like forage may increase native animal numbers, but only if very selective trapping -- not indiscriminate broadscale shooting -- is practiced.

## The Synthesis Possible Now

What is now possible is a totally new synthesis of plant and animal systems, using a post-industrial and even computerised approach to system design, applying the principles of whole system energy flows and the principles of good ecologies. It is a whole new ball game to devise permaculture systems for local, regional, and personal needs.

## Design as Reading Landscape

Mollison emphasizes that the designer must learn to read the landscape before attempting to modify it. Every landscape tells a story through its vegetation patterns, water flow lines, soil types, erosion evidence, and microclimate indicators. The position of trees, the direction of slope, the accumulation of organic matter, and the behavior of water all provide information that should inform design decisions. A competent permaculture designer can walk onto a piece of land and, by reading these indicators, determine where water collects, where frost pockets form, which areas are most fertile, and where wind exposure is greatest. This observational skill is the foundation upon which all design work rests.

## The Role of the Designer

Mollison distinguishes between the farmer or gardener who works by habit and the designer who works by analysis. The designer asks fundamental questions: What are the energies entering this site? What resources are available? What are the limiting factors? What are the client's real needs? The designer then assembles components so that each element performs multiple functions and each function is supported by multiple elements. This principle of functional interconnection is what distinguishes designed permaculture systems from conventional agriculture, where each component operates independently and external energy inputs compensate for missing connections.

## Integration of Traditional and Modern Knowledge

The synthesis Mollison proposes is not a romantic return to pre-industrial methods but an integration of traditional ecological wisdom with modern analytical tools. Aboriginal fire management, tribal resource partitioning, and intergenerational knowledge transmission can be combined with satellite mapping, soil analysis, and systems modeling. The key insight is that traditional practices often encode sophisticated ecological understanding that has been tested over centuries or millennia, even if the practitioners themselves could not articulate the underlying principles in scientific terms. The permaculture designer's task is to recognize, validate, and adapt these practices for contemporary conditions.

## Energy Accounting in Design

Every design decision involves energy flows that must be accounted for. Mollison insists that designers trace the energy pathways through their systems: where does energy enter, how is it transformed, where is it stored, and where is it lost? A well-designed permaculture system minimizes energy losses by cycling resources internally. Composting returns nutrients from harvested plants to the soil. Animal manure feeds the food garden. Water captured from roofs irrigates crops. Pruned material becomes mulch. This energy accounting approach reveals that many conventional agricultural practices are fundamentally wasteful, exporting nutrients from farms as produce and replacing them with energy-intensive synthetic fertilizers.

## Scale and Context in Design

Design criteria change with scale. A window herb garden, a suburban food forest, a community farm, and a broadacre farm all require different approaches, yet all can apply the same underlying principles. Mollison stresses that small-scale, intensive systems are often more productive per unit area than large-scale extensive systems. This has important implications for land use planning: a region that converts a portion of its agricultural land from extensive monoculture to intensive permaculture can produce equal or greater food yields while freeing the remainder for ecological restoration, timber production, and wildlife habitat.

## Site Analysis and Data Collection

Before design work begins, the permaculture designer conducts a thorough site analysis that documents the existing conditions. This includes mapping topography, water flows, existing vegetation, soil types, microclimates, wind patterns, sun exposure, existing structures, access routes, and any hazards or constraints. Mollison's Designers' Manual provides detailed checklists for site analysis, emphasizing that the quality of the design is directly proportional to the quality of the information on which it is based. Modern tools including satellite imagery, GIS mapping, and drone surveys have expanded the designer's toolkit, but the fundamental principle remains: thorough observation precedes intelligent design.

## The Design Process

Mollison outlines a structured design process that moves from broad analysis to specific detail. The process begins with client interview and goal definition, followed by site analysis and sector mapping, conceptual design, detailed design, and implementation planning. Each stage builds on the previous one, and the designer frequently returns to earlier stages as new information emerges. This iterative approach ensures that the final design is both comprehensive and responsive to site-specific conditions. The designer's role is not to impose a preconceived pattern on the landscape but to discover and enhance the patterns already present.

## Energy Accounting in Design

A critical component of Mollison's design methodology is energy accounting: ensuring that every element in the design produces more energy (in usable forms) than it consumes. This principle extends beyond mechanical energy to include human labor, financial investment, and ecological energy flows. A properly designed system should require decreasing energy inputs over time as biological processes take over functions that initially required human labor. This energy accounting approach distinguishes permaculture design from conventional landscape design, where ongoing energy inputs are assumed and rarely questioned. The aboriginal societies that Mollison studied achieved remarkable productivity with minimal energy input by designing systems that worked with natural processes rather than against them.

## Integration of Design Elements

Mollison emphasizes that individual design elements should serve multiple functions and be supported by multiple elements. This principle of functional interconnection creates resilient systems where the failure of any single component does not collapse the whole. For example, a chicken coop might provide eggs, manure for compost, pest control in gardens, and heat for a greenhouse. Each function is supported by multiple elements: pest control comes from chickens, beneficial insects, and plant diversity. This approach to integration, derived in part from Mollison's observation of how aboriginal societies wove multiple functions into every element of their landscape management, creates systems that are both productive and resilient.

## Tools for Design Analysis

Mollison provides several practical tools for analyzing landscape patterns and developing designs. Sector analysis maps the flows of energy (sun, wind, fire, water, wildlife) across a site, identifying both opportunities and hazards. Zone analysis organizes design elements by frequency of use, placing the most intensively managed elements closest to the center of activity. Slope analysis examines how water and nutrients move across the landscape, identifying keypoint positions for water management interventions. Overlay mapping combines multiple analytical layers to reveal the optimal placement of design elements. These analytical tools, while systematic and quantitative, are grounded in the pattern understanding that Mollison developed through studying aboriginal ecological wisdom, representing a fusion of traditional knowledge and modern analytical methods.
