## Windbreak Mechanics and Effects

Wind approaching a barrier is deflected upward, creating a zone of reduced
wind speed on the leeward side. The most effective windbreaks have a
porosity of approximately 40 to 60 percent, allowing some wind through while
creating enough turbulence behind the barrier to prevent wind acceleration
at ground level. Solid barriers such as walls cause severe turbulence
immediately behind them and are less effective for agricultural protection
than permeable living windbreaks.

The protected zone extends approximately 10 to 15 times the height of the
windbreak on the leeward side and 2 to 5 times on the windward side. Within
this zone, wind speed is reduced by 20 to 50 percent depending on distance
from the barrier and its porosity. The practical effect is a significant
reduction in evapotranspiration, which Mollison quantifies as a water saving
of 20 to 30 percent for sheltered crops compared to fully exposed sites.

## Design Principles

### Orientation

Windbreaks should be oriented perpendicular to the prevailing or most
damaging winds. In most temperate regions this means planting on the western
and northwestern boundaries. Where winds come from multiple directions,
a network of cross-planted windbreaks may be necessary. Mollison advises
mapping seasonal wind roses for the site before finalizing windbreak layout,
as summer drying winds and winter storm winds may come from different
directions, requiring strategic placement of multiple belts.

### Height and Density

The height of the windbreak determines the extent of the sheltered zone.
Taller windbreaks protect a larger area but take longer to establish.
Mollison recommends designing windbreaks with a mix of tall canopy trees
(15 to 25 meters at maturity), understory trees (8 to 12 meters), and
shrub layers to create a dense, multi-strata profile. The root-to-shoot
ratio should favor deep-rooting species that do not compete aggressively
with adjacent crops for water and nutrients in the surface soil horizon.

### Width and Layout

A windbreak should be at least 5 to 10 meters wide to provide adequate
density and structural diversity. Multiple rows planted in staggered
formation create better density than single-row plantings. Mollison
recommends a minimum of three rows: a windward row of hardy shrubs, a
central row of tall canopy trees, and a leeward row of medium-height
trees. Where space is limited, a single dense row of multi-layered
planting can still provide meaningful protection for [[acacia]] species in warmer climates. These species establish quickly,
have dense foliage, and tolerate pruning and shaping. They form the
structural backbone of the windbreak and should be selected for longevity
and wind firmness at maturity.

### Secondary and Understory Species

The inner rows can include productive species that benefit from the
protection of the outer windward row. Fruit and nut trees such as
Citrus, [[tagasaste]] (Chamaecytisus proliferus) or [[mollison-designers-free-range-poultry-systems]] and livestock grazing systems.

### Shrub and Ground Layer

Low-growing shrubs and groundcovers complete the windbreak profile by
closing gaps at the base where wind can channel through. Species such as
Coprosma, Hibiscus, Tamarix, or native leguminous shrubs are effective.
This layer also provides habitat for insectivorous birds that contribute
to pest control in adjacent cropping areas, linking windbreak design to
[[mollison-designers-fuelwood-forestry-systems]] through periodic coppicing, and create
sheltered microsites for intensive garden production. Mollison emphasizes
that windbreaks are not isolated features but connected elements in a
whole-site design that manages energy flows, water, and nutrient cycles
across the landscape.

## Summary

Effective windbreak design requires attention to orientation, height,
density, width, and species composition. A multi-strata planting of
wind-tolerant trees, productive understory species, and dense shrubs
creates a permeable barrier that reduces wind speed, conserves water,
protects crops and buildings, and yields food, fodder, and timber as
secondary products. Properly integrated windbreaks are foundational
infrastructure for productive and resilient permaculture landscapes.
