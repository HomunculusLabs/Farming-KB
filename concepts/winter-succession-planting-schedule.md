# Winter Succession Planting Schedule

Succession planting for [[cold-house-winter-harvest-system]] is fundamentally different from spring and
summer succession [[coleman-succession-planting-winter-harvest]] planting. In spring, conditions are improving — day length is
increasing, temperatures are rising, and growth accelerates. In fall, conditions
are deteriorating. Day length is decreasing, temperatures are falling, and plant
growth progressively slows. Understanding and accounting for this reversal is the
key to successful [[coleman-beet-greens-cold-house-winter-production]]. (see [[coleman-greenhouse-summer-crops]]).

## The Day Length Factor

The critical threshold for winter planting is ten hours of daylight. When day length
drops below ten hours (around November 5 at Coleman's latitude of 44°N), most cold-
hardy crops essentially stop producing new growth. They enter a state of dormancy or
semi-dormancy, maintaining their existing leaves but not adding significant new
biomass until day length increases past ten hours again in early February. (see [[lettuce-and-leafy-greens]]).

This means that all crops intended for winter harvest must reach at least 75% of
their mature size before this threshold is crossed. Planting too late results in
crops that are too small to harvest through the winter. Planting too early results
in oversized plants that are less cold-hardy than younger, more compact specimens.
Getting the timing right requires careful observation and record-keeping specific to
each growing location.

The dates given here are for coastal Maine (approximately 44°N latitude, USDA Zone
5). Growers at other latitudes must adjust these dates. In general, the farther south
you are, the later you can plant, because the ten-hour day-length threshold occurs
later in the fall and the return to ten-hour days occurs earlier in the spring.
Growers in Zones 6 and 7 can shift all dates 1-3 weeks later; growers in Zones 8-9
can shift them 3-6 weeks later.

## The Planting Calendar

### August: The Second Spring

August 1 marks the beginning of the winter planting season. The first sowings include
crops that need the longest growing period or that benefit from maximum size before
winter: carrots (for winter harvest, not storage), leeks, chicory, and the first
plantings of spinach. These early August sowings will be harvestable by late October
and will provide the first winter crops.

In mid to late August, additional plantings include tatsoi, mizuna, pak choi, radish,
and the first succession of head lettuce. Scallions can also be started from seed or
transplanted.

### September: Peak Planting Month

September is the most [[intensive-planting-spacing]] month for winter production. Mid-September
is the ideal time for sowing [[coleman-overwintered-spinach-cold-house-production]] — this crop will become the
backbone of the winter harvest, providing four or more cuttings at approximately
monthly intervals from late November through late March. The September-sown spinach
is hardy enough to grow outdoors without protection until the greenhouse is moved
over it in mid to late October.

Other September plantings include claytonia, mache, arugula, additional lettuce
successions, beet greens, and radishes for November and December harvest. Asian
greens such as tatsoi and mizuna can be succession-sown every two to three weeks
through September for a continuous harvest.

### October: Final Plantings

Early October is the last opportunity for sowing crops that will germinate and reach
harvestable size before winter dormancy sets in. The last plantings of mache, claytonia,
and spinach can go in during the first week or two of October. These late sowings will
be small at the onset of winter but will provide harvests beginning in February as
day length increases.

By mid to late October, the focus shifts from planting to maintenance. The inner row
cover layer is added to the cold houses as nighttime temperatures consistently drop
below freezing. Crops sown in August and September should be approaching or at
harvestable size.

### November-December: Harvest Season

November and December are peak harvest months. The fall-sown crops reach full maturity
and quality is at its highest. Spinach, tatsoi, mizuna, pak choi, radishes, baby
lettuces, and carrots are all harvested. These months typically generate the highest
winter sales, particularly around the pre-Christmas holiday period.

As crops are harvested, beds are cleared and replanted with crops intended for the
late winter [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] harvest. These winter sowings will germinate slowly in
the cold greenhouse and reach harvestable size in February and March as day length
increases.
