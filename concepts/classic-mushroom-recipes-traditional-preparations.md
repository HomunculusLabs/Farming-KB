# Classic Mushroom Recipes and Traditional Preparations

## Overview

The early 20th century tradition of mushroom cookery, as preserved in the Benjamin Minge Duggar manual, reflects a sophisticated European-influenced culinary approach to *[[agaricus-campestris]]*. The recipes demonstrate the remarkable versatility of the cultivated mushroom as an ingredient, ranging from simple breakfast preparations to elaborate French-inspired entrées. These recipes represent foundational techniques that remain relevant to modern mushroom cookery.

## Stuffed Mushrooms

One of the most celebrated mushroom preparations, stuffed mushrooms transform large caps into elegant vehicles for seasoned fillings:

**Stuffed Caps**: Prepare 12 large cleansed caps by removing stems. Sprinkle the interior with salt and pepper, also add a few drops of olive oil and vinegar. Cut up the cleansed mushrooms and mix with a dressing prepared as follows: to the yolk of one egg add a little oil and onion juice, a few drops of anchovy sauce (if convenient), vinegar, salt, pepper, and capers. Beat these together without forming a mayonnaise, and add this sauce to the mushrooms. Fill the caps with the dressing thus prepared. Prepare the day before serving for best flavor development.

## Stuffed Tomatoes with Mushrooms

A preparation that combines mushrooms with another complementary vegetable:

Hollow out tomatoes, remove the pulp, and sprinkle the interior with salt, pepper, a few drops of olive oil and vinegar. Cut up cleansed mushrooms and mix with the prepared dressing (as above). Fill the tomatoes with the mushroom mixture. Prepare the day before serving to allow flavors to meld.

## Chicken à la Bressanne

A classic French preparation combining mushrooms with poultry:

Put a dozen large cleansed mushrooms in a casserole with the same weight of chicken. Add 2 ounces of butter and salt and pepper to taste. Cover and let cook for ½ hour. When the chicken is tender, add ½ cup of cream and 1 tablespoon of flour. Reheat and serve with mushroom croustades. The croustades are made by cutting stale bread in strips 1¼ by 3 inches and frying in butter. Chop some mushrooms and sauté in butter. When the croustades are golden brown, drain and cover with the chopped mushrooms.

## Stewed Mushrooms for Breakfast

A simple, practical preparation suitable for everyday use:

Use all sizes including younger stems. Cleanse and peel where necessary. Cover with cold water containing a little vinegar. After standing half an hour, drop the mushrooms into a stew pan containing a tablespoon of butter at the boiling point. Cook for a minute or two and season with salt and black pepper. Cook again slowly for 5 minutes while shaking the pan, then pour in cream to cover, and let cook 8 or 10 minutes longer.

## À la Cuore (Marinated Buttons)

A marinated preparation that showcases the delicate flavor of small buttons:

Cleanse ¼ pound of button mushrooms, cover with a spoonful of salt, and let stand for 1 hour. Add 2 soup spoons of white vinegar and let macerate for 1½ hours. Pour on 3½ tablespoons olive oil, a little pepper, and a trace of mustard. Serve as desired—as a salad component, appetizer, or garnish.

## Cream of Mushroom Soup

Two variations demonstrate the principles of mushroom soup preparation:

### Method 1 (Simple)

¼ pound mushrooms cut up fine, 2 cups water, 1 teaspoon salt. Cook in a double boiler for 30 minutes. Strain (or rub through sieve if desired). Rub together 1 tablespoon butter and 3 tablespoons sifted flour, then add this and 1 pint full milk to the stock. Cook 20 minutes, season to taste, and serve with or without whipped cream.

### Method 2 (With Chicken Stock)

¼ pound young mushrooms, 4 cups chicken stock, 1 slice onion, ½ cup butter, ¼ cup flour. The use of chicken stock as a base produces a richer, more complex soup than the simple water-based version.

## Culinary Principles from the Recipes

Several principles emerge from these traditional preparations:

### Use of Stems

Many recipes explicitly incorporate stems, recognizing their value. For soups, stems are preferred over gill surfaces because they do not yield a dark-colored broth—a practical consideration for presentation.

### Marination Techniques

The à la cuore recipe demonstrates the traditional approach to marinating raw mushrooms: salting to draw out moisture, followed by acid (vinegar) to "cook" the mushrooms chemically, then oil and seasonings. This technique predates modern ceviche-style preparations.

### Combination with Cream and Butter

The predominance of cream and butter in these recipes reflects both the European culinary tradition and the complementary relationship between dairy fats and the earthy, savory flavors of mushrooms. Cream particularly helps to moderate and round out the mushroom's natural umami character.

### Cooking in Butter at the Boiling Point

The technique of dropping mushrooms into butter already at the boiling point—a form of quick searing—appears in several recipes. This rapid initial cooking helps to develop flavor through browning while preventing the mushrooms from becoming waterlogged in their own juices.

### The Double Boiler

For soups and delicate preparations, the double boiler (bain-marie) provides gentle, even heat that prevents scorching and preserves the mushroom's delicate flavor compounds. This technique is particularly appropriate when extended cooking times are required.

### Advance Preparation

Several recipes recommend preparing the dish the day before serving. This practice, common in French cuisine, allows flavors to develop and meld, producing a more complex and integrated result than immediate service would provide.

## The Versatility of the Cultivated Mushroom

The range of these preparations—spanning appetizers, soups, main courses, side dishes, and marinated salads—demonstrates the cultivated mushroom's remarkable culinary versatility. From simple breakfast dishes requiring only butter and cream to elaborate French-inspired compositions with poultry, the mushroom serves as both a featured ingredient and an enhancer of other flavors. This versatility, combined with the mushroom's ability to be stored for several days when properly handled, makes it one of the most practical and rewarding ingredients in the cook's repertoire.
