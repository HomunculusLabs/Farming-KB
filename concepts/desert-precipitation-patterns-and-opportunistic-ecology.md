## Runoff and Stream Flow

A rainstorm of just 8-12 mm produces sufficient runoff to start headwater stream flow. Only occasional torrential convectional downpours or heavy monsoon/tradewind rains over remote headwaters produce river flow down the braided or sandy water networks threading through the desert. Key hydrological facts:

- **35% of runoff** occurs in headwater areas
- Main stream flow carries only about 5% unless rains are widespread
- Retardation basins and catchment form moderate flood sequences

## The Opportunistic Response to Rain

### The "Walkabout" as Ecological Intelligence

For Central Australian Aboriginal peoples, "walkabout" is not arbitrary or whimsical—it is a sophisticated response to environmental information. It translates roughly as:

> Our scouts saw thunderstorms to the far north: if we go now we can arrive in time to harvest some of the birds, animals, and plants that will respond to rain, and to follow the water to the waterholes that will fill with freshwater and give us fish, turtle, and frogs for a few months. We must go now, in time to celebrate the cycle of plenty that comes from the rain.

Such movements are sensible, planned, and appropriate to an environment that presents rare opportunities to harvest varied resources, visit newly-regenerated country, and lighten the burden of resource pressure on favored home areas so they can regenerate as reserves for hard times.

### The Ephemeral Explosion

The desert response to rain is truly remarkable in its speed and abundance:

- **Ephemeral plants** carpet the ground, producing flowers and seed in abundance
- **Buried tubers** throw out great patches of melon, bean, and yam vines
- **Shrubs and trees** may produce numerous seedlings in areas where few young plants previously existed
- **Pools and streams** attract frogs and fish emerging from mud-packed retreats or invading from rare permanent pools
- **Fish** may breed and grow to harvestable size in just a few months
- **Sea-birds** migrate inland: pelicans, flamingos, herons, terns, gulls, waders, and ducks occupy the waters
- **Terrestrial animals** flock to waterholes: antelope, kangaroo, desert quail, pigeon, finches, and parrots spread across new forage areas

For a few months, life is riotous and breeding unconfined.

### The Inevitable Collapse

The longer-term collapse of this explosion of life is slower but usually inevitable:

- Some new tree generations may have established (the lasting positive outcome)
- Waters turn salty, then dry up
- Life scatters, dies, or returns to rest in mud and sand
- Seeds are blown and buried, and the desert waits again
- Birds disperse, as do migratory animals
- Desert fringes experience sudden irruption of migrating species

### Recruitment Cycles

New generations of shrubs may experience favorable seedling conditions as rarely as **every 7-20 years**. This then becomes the period of recruitment for new forests or shrublands. Understanding and protecting these rare recruitment events is critical for long-term [[desert-revegetation]].

## Temperature Patterns in Deserts

### Daily Cycle

The air temperature over deserts follows a characteristic daily pattern:

- **6 AM**: 8-30 degrees C depending on location and season
- **Peak (12-3 PM)**: Rise of approximately 10-25 degrees C over morning temperature
- **Night**: Rapid cooling, often dropping 20-30 degrees C from afternoon highs

### Soil Temperature

Soil temperatures follow the same general curve but peak about one hour earlier than air temperature. Critical relationships:

- At 30 cm (12 inches) depth, there is little effect of daily temperature changes
- Peak surface temperature reduces about 2 degrees C for every 5 cm depth (15 degrees C in 30 cm)
- Soils at 30 cm deep maintain fairly constant daily temperatures approximately 5 degrees C above the lowest surface temperature and 15 degrees C below the highest

Soil effectively evens out the heating that affects air for a considerable distance above the ground. Both air and soil follow a slower annual cycle depending on latitude and seasonal radiation, affecting soils to about 2 meters depth with approximately 5 degrees C annual fluctuation—similar to conditions in underground houses and cellars.

### Extreme Heat

Soils can gain and hold much higher heat levels than air:

- At 5 cm depth in favorable sun slopes: **60-70 degrees C** at peak
- Surface air temperatures frequently reach 30 degrees C; isolated records of 52 degrees C (125 degrees F)
- Layers of super-heated air above soil may become unstable, forming rapid convectional currents, especially on sun-facing slopes
- Cumuliform clouds can arise from thermal effects, or as clear-air turbulence in very hot dry periods

### Animal Thermal Refuge Strategies

Desert animals escape extreme temperatures through:

- **Burrowing**: To below 30 cm depth (most common strategy)
- **High refuges**: Low shrubs, stumps, or posts in hot weather
- **Shade-seeking**: The limiting factor for larger species survival (e.g., cave shelter availability may limit numbers of large desert marsupials)
- **Nocturnality**: Deserts have a far greater proportion of subterranean and nocturnal fauna than other biomes

### Soil Moisture Gradient

Soil humidity rises steeply with depth in desert environments:

| Depth | Water Content |
|---|---|
| Top 1 meter | ~4% average |
| 2-6 meters | 10-20% |
| 20-60 meters | Potentially saturated |

For both plants and animals, drawing from these deeper, cooler, and more humid soil layers is strongly preferable to surface resources.
