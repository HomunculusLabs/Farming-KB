# Figs (Ficus spp)

Figs are a large genus of about 600 species in the family Moraceae, all
from tropical to subtropical regions, often found in rainforest
conditions, although many extend into arid areas. They are among the
most versatile [[food-forest-establishment]] plants in [[permaculture]] systems.

## General Characteristics

Fig fruits are pollinated by tiny wasps and many species produce fruit
year-round to support these wasps. A sprinkling of various Ficus spp
among the more flavourful [[ficus-carica]] should ensure good populations of
these pollinating wasps. Seed is readily transported in nature by fruit
bats and birds. The fruits are generally good [[integrated-poultry-forage-systems-zone-two]] feed.

The Ficus spp are generally considered good [[fire-resistant-plants]].
The milky latex that exudes from broken leaf, stem, or unripe fruit is
used to treat infections such as ringworm and warts. The Perth zoo grows
various Ficus spp as stock fodder for large herbivores.

## Ficus carica (Common Fig)

The fig is one of the most ancient plants in civilisation, existing in
as many as 700 varieties. It is a deciduous tree growing to 5-10 m with
a spread up to 25 m, although most cultivars tend to be smaller. It grows
best in warm temperate and subtropical regions and is frost tolerant to
-10 degrees C. Figs can live for 100 years and begin bearing fruit at 2-3
years.

Fig trees do well in a wide range of soils, although they dislike
alkaline or salty soils. Periodic lime applications are beneficial. They
are tolerant of drought but produce better quality fruit when water is
plentiful. They often produce a better crop if roots are restricted,
making them a good tree for container growing in small gardens.

## Fruit Production

Fruits are produced singly or in pairs in leaf axils, varying
considerably in shape and skin colour between varieties. Some cultivated
varieties require no pollination. In some climates, figs produce two
crops: one in early summer (larger fruits) and one in late summer to
early autumn (smaller but more numerous).

## Nutritional Composition (Fresh)

Per 100 grams of edible portion:
- Water: 77.5%
- Energy: 80 calories
- Carbohydrate: 20.3 g
- Fat: 0.3 g
- Protein: 1.2 g
- Calcium: 35 mg
- Potassium: 194 mg
- Iron: 0.6 mg
- Phosphorus: 22 mg
- Vitamin A: 80 I.U.
- Ascorbic acid: 2 mg
- Digestion time: 2.25 hours

## Nutritional Composition (Dried)

Per 100 grams of edible portion:
- Water: 23%
- Energy: 274 calories
- Carbohydrate: 69.1 g (about 50 g is dextrose)
- Fat: 1.3 g
- Protein: 4.3 g
- Calcium: 126 mg
- Potassium: 640 mg
- Iron: 3.0 mg

Sugar content rises from 12% to 50% on drying. The best drying method
is to let them partially dry on the tree and drop naturally.

## Medicinal Uses

Figs are used medicinally for their mild laxative effect. Demulcent
decoctions treat catarrhal infections of the nose and throat. Roasted and
split figs provide a poultice for dental abscesses, gum boils, and boils.
The milky juice is applied to warts, causing them to dry and fall off.

## Propagation

Propagation is by layering or cuttings. Layers should root in a season.
Cuttings (15-25 cm) should be selected from short-jointed shoots of the
previous year's growth, taken in spring, and do best in sandy soil in
warmth.

## Pruning

Little pruning is required unless the tree is to be kept as a dwarf.
Pruning consists mainly of cutting out dead, deformed, or weak branches.
Tips of young shoots can be pinched out to ensure strong growth, as
fruit only matures on new or recently ripened wood. Figs can be trained
against walls or along fences.

## Ficus macrophylla (Moreton Bay Fig)

A tall (to 50 m) evergreen forest tree of the tropical and subtropical
rainforests of eastern Australia and New Guinea. Given the space, it is
a spreading tree often twice as wide as it is tall, with a wide
buttressed trunk. It prefers deep, moist, fertile, alluvial soil in
rainfalls between 1000-1700 mm.

Moreton Bay figs have been planted extensively as park trees and street
trees but have fallen into disfavour for the latter because their root
system destroys paths, roads, drains, fences, and buildings. The fruit
is very variable but in some individuals can be outstanding in size and
flavour, quite reminiscent of Ficus carica.

The Australian Aborigines made string from the bark fibres for dilly
bags and fishing nets. The timber is open-grained, easily worked, and
dresses with a mild sheen reminiscent of cedar.

## Companion Planting

Figs work well in [[food-forest-establishment]] systems as a mid-to-upper canopy
element. They provide shade for understorey crops, produce copious leaf
litter for mulch, and support pollinator wasp populations. Avoid
planting large Ficus species near buildings or infrastructure due to
aggressive root systems. Some species (curtain fig, strangler fig) can
engulf host trees and should be managed carefully.

## Key Cultivars

- Brown Turkey: Medium fruit with mahogany brown skin, rich flavour.
  Cold hardy. Best eaten fresh.
- Celeste: Violet coloured with light coloured, firm, juicy, sweet flesh.
- Conadria: Vigorous tree adapted to warmer climates. Best for drying.
- Desert King: Widely adapted, large dark green fruit, excellent quality.
- Genoa (White Genoa): Best adapted to cooler areas, very hardy.
- Kadota: Vigorous tree for hot climates, popular for commercial canning.
- Mission (Black Mission): Dependable, excellent flavour, eaten fresh,
  dried, or canned.
