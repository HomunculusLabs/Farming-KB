# Fukuoka Breitenbush Natural Garden

The Breitenbush natural garden in Oregon provides one of the most compelling demonstrations of Fukuoka's natural farming principles. A garden started by a young Japanese man using natural farming techniques, then left untended for two years, appeared at first to be a total failure but revealed itself as a thriving ecosystem when examined closely.

## The Setting

A colloquium on nature was held at Breitenbush, a hot springs resort in Oregon. Several hundred people from all over the world gathered for three days. The resort was situated in a large virgin forest, with buildings scattered here and there. Tucked off in the grounds was a natural garden for growing the food used in the dining hall kitchen. The person in charge had gone, and the garden had been left alone.

## Initial Appearance

The garden had been started two years earlier by a young Japanese man named Katsu. He had sown clover over the entire surface and scattered many different types of vegetables in little clusters within the garden. After he left, the garden grew wild. At first sight, the place appeared overrun with weeds and in total disarray. "Anyone would take this for a failure."

## Fukuoka's Close Inspection

Upon entering the garden to take a closer look, Fukuoka found:

- Clover had spread throughout the entire garden, creating a living mulch ground cover
- The number of weeds was not as large as it had appeared from a distance
- All kinds of vegetables were growing to respectable sizes, demonstrating natural viability

This moment exemplifies one of Fukuoka's core principles: that human perception of disorder often masks natural order. What appeared chaotic from a distance was in fact a self-regulating polyculture developing according to ecological principles.

## The Three-Year Process

Fukuoka summarized what he saw to the workshop participants:

"Man sowed the seed the first year, nature made some adjustments the second, and the third year God built us a natural garden."

The process was clearly evident:

1. **Year 1 (Man)**: Katsu sowed clover and scattered vegetable seeds using the natural farming approach
2. **Year 2 (Nature)**: Left untended, birds and mice were attracted by the fruit and seeds, spreading them throughout the garden — essentially sowing the seeds of different vegetables everywhere through natural dispersal mechanisms
3. **Year 3 (God)**: Natural selection and ecological balance produced a functioning garden where only the most adapted plants thrived in their optimal microenvironments

This three-year timeline aligns with Fukuoka's broader observation that natural farming requires patience. The first year establishes the foundation, the second year allows natural adjustments and animal-mediated seed dispersal, and the third year reveals the mature self-sustaining system.

## The Survival Test

The garden had varied growing conditions: dry spots and wet spots, areas of poor soil and shady spots. Not all seeds that fell germinated. Only seeds that fell in the right time and place germinated. Fukuoka called this the "survival test."

"There is nothing wrong with this seemingly disordered state in which those seeds which are doomed to perish do so, and only the survivors thrive. But nature eventually shows us in this way where each plant will grow best. All one has to do is look and follow nature's example."

This concept connects directly to Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy]] — the idea that human intervention often disrupts the natural selection process that produces the strongest, most resilient plants. The survival test eliminates weak plants and identifies optimal growing locations without human judgment.

## Specific Observations

- A clump of wheat and a clump of naked barley had appeared that were not sown by man, demonstrating natural seed recruitment
- The kernels on these volunteer plants had more kernels than normal, possibly due to natural selection for vigorous individuals
- Garlic was growing very well in its self-selected location
- Fukuoka suggested mixing in naked barley and other grains with the vegetable seed for greater diversity

He recommended a succession pattern: grow naked barley in winter and spring, follow with plants of the sourd family, then plant onions and broad beans where cucumbers and melons had been. This succession mirrors natural rotation patterns and avoids the depletion associated with monoculture.

## Restored Self-Confidence

The practical impact was significant. Katsu had felt that discussions of natural farming tended to be abstract and vague, making them difficult to understand. When Fukuoka went to America and gave practical instruction, people grasped the concepts quickly. Even those who remained unconvinced by the theory were ready at once to give the method a try.

The Breitenbush garden served as a tangible proof of concept that converted skeptics and inspired practitioners. It demonstrated that natural farming was not merely philosophical but produced real, edible results without ongoing human labor.

## Americans vs Japanese Attitude

Fukuoka noted a key difference: in Japan, even those who tend a home garden do so either as a pastime or to produce food free of pesticides. In America, people had the urge to move to the mountains and enjoy a life of freedom and autonomy. "If the Japanese, like the Europeans and Americans, were to get the urge to move to the mountains and enjoy a life of freedom and autonomy, Japan has quite a lot of mountains and it is almost certain that the bottom would fall out of land prices in the cities."

This observation reflects Fukuoka's broader critique of modern Japanese society's disconnection from natural living, a theme developed more fully in his [[fukuoka-european-civilization-standstill]] discussions.

## Significance for Natural Farming

The Breitenbush garden remains one of the most cited examples of natural farming working in a temperate Western climate. It demonstrates that the principles developed in Fukuoka's subtropical Japanese farm are transferable across climates and cultures. The key insight is that nature does not need human management to produce food — it needs only the initial seed input and the patience to allow ecological succession to take its course.
