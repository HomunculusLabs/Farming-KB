# Incremental Design and Design Methods Summary

[[permaculture]] aims to create. This concept synthesizes the design methods presented in Mollison's framework and examines the role of incremental design within the broader methodology.

## Incremental Design: The Historical Foundation

### How It Works

Almost all engineering design throughout history has been based on **small changes to existing designs**, gradually pushing toward an ultimate limit in efficiency or performance. The process can span centuries, and the end result can be mass-produced if necessary.

Kevin Lynch (1982), in *Site Planning*, describes site design by **incremental adaptation of already-existing designs** — following physical systems that have been shown to work. He identifies this as the basis of the best site planning of the past, noting it works very well unless some external and important condition (such as market or land ownership) changes.

### Strengths of Incremental Design

- **Proven reliability**: Known effective design units and specifications — whether of roads, culverts, houses, garden beds, or technologies — have been subjected to long tests and evolved from trials (or prototypes) to working, reliable standards
- **Simplicity and efficiency**: Even "old-fashioned" solutions (like overshot water wheels) may represent a simplicity and efficiency hard to beat without considerable increase in expense and complexity
- **Feedback-driven**: Continual adaptation is the basis of feedback in systems undergoing establishment, where additions or changes are made to houses or plant systems based on observed results

### Limitations of Incremental Design

Incremental adaptation alone **cannot cope with**:

- Complex systems that must satisfy a **complex set of priorities** simultaneously
- **Changing futures** and evolving conditions
- **New information and new sets of values**
- The demands of **self-reliance and self-governance**

Fine-tuning of successful design for a specific climate and purpose can be **totally inappropriate if transferred** out of culture, climate, or if applied to a different purpose. This is a critical warning against blindly copying designs across contexts.

## Summary of All Design Methods

Mollison stresses that **all the design approaches outlined in permaculture are not only useful but necessary**. Only by some sensible combination of all methods can a designer:

1. Select and assess all elements entering into a total design assembly
2. Evolve a design that includes a large degree of **self-management**
3. Take account of **details on site**
4. Suit the **ethics and resources of people**
5. Locate ground features in an **integrated way**
6. Provide for **natural systems and access routes** to be properly located

### The Methods in Combination

The complete [[zone-and-sector-analysis]]** for energy-efficient placement
- **Slope, aspect, and elevation analysis** for three-dimensional optimization
- **Guild [[garden-guilds-and-polyculture-design]]** for species interaction benefits
- **[[water-conservation]] and keypoint planning** following Yeomans' principles
- **Incremental adaptation** of proven design elements
- **Ethical assessment** ensuring wilderness is preserved and home systems are efficient
- **Client consultation** and education for appropriate, adopted designs
- **Feedback loops** allowing space, finance, and observation to adjust activity over time

## Site Design as a Bio-Social Process

### Beyond Specialist Approaches

Site designing needs not a specialist approach but a **multi-disciplinary and bio-social approach** that takes into account the effects the environment has on its intended occupants. The consequences of ignoring this are severe.

### The Bruce Effect and Crowding

Research found that rats subjected to breathing the same airstream as their fellows experienced severe instability, physiological stress, and consequent pathologies akin to crowding stress — the **"Bruce effect."** This may apply equally well to people crammed into 16–40 storey office buildings with no opening windows and only a single airstream.

Neglecting social effects of settlement size and the local functions related to settlement size may result in designing for:

- **Human misery**
- **Under-servicing of needs**
- **Sociopathologies** such as riot and crime

Such designs may be economic in cash terms and efficient for one use only, yet be **totally inhuman**. But they are built every day.

### The Village Scale

In [[integrated-land-management-and-village-design]], financial and space constraints create upper and lower size limits — a "break-even" point and an "optimum" number of houses per unit area. Neglecting social effects at this scale produces the same dysfunctions as building-scale failures.

## The Permaculture Design Contrast

Mollison's comparison between permaculture and conventional design reveals fundamentally different value systems:

### Permaculture Design Is:
- **Stimulated by** a perceived social problem
- **Subject to** values of [[desert-house-water-energy-conservation]], self-reliance, and harmonious human occupancy
- **Mediated by** consideration of long-term biosocial factors
- **Achieved by** research and consultation with clients, assisting people to gain education in design
- **Refined by** allowing space, finance, and feedback to adjust activity, and allowing for new or overlooked needs
- **Leading to** a dynamic and healthy area inhabited by people with power and understanding to make changes

### Conventional Design Is:
- **Stimulated by** a drive to erect monuments or make money
- **Subject to** economic considerations and the desire for profit
- **Mediated by** purely functional values for short-term cost and material factors
- **Achieved by** external funding, little consultation, little client education
- **Refined by** selling off and not taking responsibility for results
- **Leading to** a dislocated population, powerless to effect change — dependency and anxiety

## Toward Computer-Assisted Design

Mollison envisions assembling all considerable, diverse, and effective knowledge of both the parts and the whole order of design into a **type of computer search or game-playing programme** that would advance the whole design process as a realm of continuing and additive human knowledge, available to everyone.

Such programmes could deal with the fussy detail that now slows design — from plant list specifications to home construction details — leaving the designer with the **imponderables**: the processes observed on the land, the likely trends of future societies, future needs, and a measure of human satisfaction.

## Accepting What Is There

In whole farm planning, outlining areas of like soil, slope, or drainage will suggest sensible crops, treatments, fencelines, and land use generally. **If we accept what is there**, ethical land use dictates conservative and appropriate usage.

The alternative — forcing a single use (like wheat) across all land regardless of variations — is possible only with sufficient energy or resources. As Mollison dryly notes: "I am sure we can [[query-how-do-i-grow-bananas-in-a-subtropical-climate]] Antarctica if we are prepared to spend enough money, or can persuade penguins to heat a glasshouse!"

## Summary

Incremental design is a powerful but incomplete methodology. It provides proven, reliable design elements that have evolved through centuries of trial and adaptation. However, permaculture demands more: the integration of zone-sector placement, slope analysis, guild design, water conservation, ethical assessment, and social consideration into a holistic design process. The result of combining all methods is a design that is self-managing, site-specific, ethically grounded, and adaptable — producing the stability of dynamic local adjustment rather than the instability of perceived individual powerlessness.
