## Informal and Formal Community Economics

Community economics, as described in [[permaculture-design]], encompasses the full spectrum of economic activity that sustains a settlement — from simple household-level barter to formally incorporated cooperative enterprises. [[bill-mollison]]'s framework recognises that resilient communities need both informal exchange networks and formal economic structures working in tandem. Conventional money flows inward from external agencies but tends to accumulate in cities and multinational corporations, draining wealth from local areas. By contrast, community-based money and credit systems keep value circulating indefinitely within the community, supporting local employment and reducing dependence on the external cash economy.

## Informal Economy: Household and Neighbourhood Level

### Household Barter

The most fundamental unit of community economics is direct exchange between households. At this scale, neighbours trade garden produce, propagate and share plants, and exchange goods and services without any formal accounting. This peer-to-peer barter requires no administration, builds trust between neighbours, and ensures that surplus production (common in productive [[permaculture]] gardens) is redistributed rather than wasted.

### Small Work Groups

When tasks exceed what a single household can accomplish, informal work groups of three to five people naturally form. These groups take on projects such as building houses, establishing gardens, and conducting community cleanups. Work typically follows a round-robin pattern: each member contributes labour in turn, so that every participant eventually receives the benefit of the group's collective effort. No money changes hands; the currency is mutual obligation and reciprocity.

## Community-Level Labour Exchange

### Scaling Beyond the Household

Once a group exceeds six to eight people, informal coordination becomes insufficient. At this threshold, some form of labour exchange organisation is needed to track who has contributed what and who is owed assistance. Several real-world models demonstrate how this can be achieved.

### The Bendigo Home Builder's Club

The Bendigo Home Builder's Club in Victoria, Australia, provides a well-documented example of organised community labour exchange. Thirty-five participating families pooled their efforts to build homes collectively. The club operated on remarkably simple terms:

- An annual membership fee of $5 per family
- Hours of labour recorded as exchange units
- Legally binding exchange forms to formalise commitments
- A designated Labour Organiser to coordinate schedules and match skills to needs
- A requirement that recipients accumulate 60 hours of labour credit before receiving assistance on their own home

This structure ensured fairness, accountability, and a sustainable flow of mutual aid. The result was that families who could never afford conventional construction were able to build homes through community cooperation.

## Community Barter Clubs

### Debit and Credit Systems

Community Barter Clubs extend the labour-exchange concept to encompass goods, services, and specialised skills. Participants earn credits by providing services — landscaping, massage, lawn mowing, printing, carpentry, childcare — and spend those credits on goods and services offered by other members. Key features of a well-run barter club include:

- **One credit equals one hour** of labour or service, providing a stable, intuitive unit of value
- **No upper limit on exchanges**, allowing members to accumulate substantial credit balances
- **The organiser is paid in credits**, making the system self-sustaining without external funding
- **Broad accessibility** — particularly valuable for unemployed or underemployed community members who have time and skills but lack conventional income

### Barter Media

Effective barter systems require communication channels. Exchange newsletters, community computer bulletin boards, and classified advertisements all serve as barter media, enabling members to broadcast what they need and what they can offer. In the [[mycology-modern-era]], digital platforms and mobile applications have expanded these possibilities considerably.

### Brokerage Houses and Trade Units

For communities generating large surpluses, brokerage houses can formalise barter at greater scale. These organisations issue **Trade Units (T.U.)**, each valued at approximately one dollar, which function as a community currency redeemable for goods and services within the network. Brokerage houses allow surplus producers — farmers, craftspeople, manufacturers — to trade excess output without converting to conventional currency.

## Community Money and the LET System

### The Problem with Conventional Money

Conventional money enters communities from external agencies — governments, corporations, banks — but it does not remain. It flows outward to pay for imported goods, taxes, debt service, and shareholder returns, ultimately concentrating in cities and multinational corporations. This perpetual drain undermines local economic resilience and creates dependency on external economic forces.

### Community Currency Principles

Community money, by contrast, is designed to circulate indefinitely within the local area. Because it cannot be spent outside the community network, it stays in circulation, supporting local transactions and employment. The more a community relies on its own currency, the more local economic activity is stimulated.

### The LET System (Local Employment Trading System)

The **[[let-system]]** is one of the most thoroughly developed models for community currency. Its key characteristics include:

- **Local "green" dollars** serve as the unit of account, distinct from national currency
- **No interest is charged or paid** on account balances, eliminating the debt-driven dynamics of conventional banking
- **Administration is funded on a cost-of-service basis**, not by extracting value from users
- **Account turnover and balances are publicly visible**, ensuring transparency and trust
- **Green dollars are not issued by a central authority, nor can they be cashed out** — they are created when one member provides a service and another's account is credited
- **Value is earned by providing services** to other members and spent on services and goods within the system
- **The community account always balances** — every credit has a corresponding debit
- **Full employment is theoretically achievable**, since anyone willing to work can earn credits by serving community needs

The LET System's self-balancing nature means that the community collectively determines how much currency exists. As local components of the economy increase, local businesses thrive because a larger share of spending remains within the community.

## Volunteer Labour and Gift Economy

Not all community economic activity involves accounting or reciprocity. **Volunteer labour** and **gift exchanges** represent the non-transactional dimension of community economics. When community members take shared responsibility for projects — maintaining public spaces, organising festivals, caring for vulnerable neighbours — they create social capital that strengthens the fabric of the community.

### Community Forestry

A particularly permaculture-relevant example is **convivial tree-planting**, where community members collectively establish and maintain forest areas. Households can adopt designated acres of community forest, taking responsibility for planting, tending, and harvesting from their adopted plots. This model blends [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]] with community bonding and long-term economic benefit through timber, fruit, and [[biodiversity-of-fungi-ecosystem-services-agriculture]].

## Formal Community Economic Structures

### The Role of Formal Economics

While informal exchange systems operate on trust and reciprocity, the **formal economy** provides goods and services under a legal umbrella, regulated by accounting procedures and subject to government oversight. For permaculture communities, formal structures are essential for interfacing with the broader economy, securing [[sacramental-psychedelic-legal-protections]], accessing capital, and scaling beyond what informal networks can support.

### Essential Formal Institutions

Mollison identifies several formal structures that communities should develop:

- **Cooperatives** — member-owned enterprises operating for mutual benefit
- **Community savings and loans** — pooled capital for local investment without reliance on banks
- **Small business service offices** — shared administrative infrastructure for local enterprises
- **Investment funds** — community-controlled capital directed toward local development
- **Leasing companies** — shared ownership of expensive equipment and infrastructure

## Cooperatives

### Cooperative Principles

Cooperatives form the backbone of formal community economics. Their operating principles ensure that economic power remains distributed among members rather than concentrated in external shareholders:

- **Open membership** — anyone who can use the cooperative's services and accepts the obligations may join
- **Democratic organisation** — each member has one vote, regardless of capital contribution
- **Limited interest on share capital** — returns to investors are capped, preventing profit extraction
- **Surplus belongs to members** — profits are distributed proportionally to use, not investment
- **Commitment to education** — cooperatives educate their members and the public about cooperative principles
- **Cooperation between cooperatives** — local co-ops federate for mutual support and greater economic power

### The Mondragon Model

The **Mondragon worker-cooperatives** in the Basque region of Spain represent the most successful large-scale implementation of cooperative economics. Over thirty years, the Mondragon network grew to encompass:

- **96 cooperatives** operating across diverse sectors
- **17,000 worker-owners** participating in the network
- **A $5,000 investment per person** as the entry threshold, making ownership accessible
- **A 20% collective / 80% individual shareholding** structure, balancing community solidarity with personal stake

Mondragon demonstrates that cooperative economics can scale to industrial levels while maintaining democratic governance and local wealth retention. The model is directly relevant to permaculture communities seeking to build formal economic structures that align with their values of equity, sustainability, and local self-reliance.

## Integrating Informal and Formal Systems

A healthy community economy integrates informal and formal systems into a mutually reinforcing whole. Informal barter, volunteer labour, and gift exchanges build the trust and social cohesion that make formal institutions viable. Formal cooperatives, credit systems, and savings pools provide the infrastructure and legal protection that allow informal activity to scale. Together, they create an economic ecosystem that keeps wealth circulating locally, provides meaningful employment, and reduces dependence on the extractive external economy — the economic dimension of a truly sustainable [[permaculture-settlement-design]].
