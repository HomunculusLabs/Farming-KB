# Falconer Topdressing with Loam

Chapter XIV of William Falconer's *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* (1891)
describes the practice of applying a fresh layer of loam to bearing
mushroom beds that are in full production or showing signs of declining
vigor. This maintenance technique was used to support the development of
small "pinhead" mushrooms and address problems in the bed surface.

## When to Topdress

Topdressing was applied to beds in full bearing or a little past their
best. Such beds often contained multitudes of very small "pinhead"
mushrooms sitting right on the top of the loam, or clumps ("rocks") that
had been raised slightly above the surface by growing in bunches. A
topdressing of finely sifted fresh loam, about one-fourth to one-half inch
thick, spread evenly over the bed, helped these mushrooms materially
without harming them.

## Effects on Fertility

Falconer was careful to note that while topdressing assisted all visible
mushrooms, he was "not convinced that it induces greater fertility in the
spawn, or, in other words, induces the spawn to spread further and produce
more mushrooms than it would were no topdressing applied." This contradicted
the opinions and writings of many contemporaries, but was, he stated,
"according to my own observation."

## Bed Preparation Before Topdressing

Before applying the topdressing, the grower was to go over the bed very
carefully and:

- Pick out every soft or "fogged-off" mushroom, no matter how small.
- Root out every bit of old mushroom stem or tough spongy material.
- Fill up all holes caused by pulling mushrooms or rooting out old stumps.
- Level the whole surface.

Then the topdressing was applied evenly, avoiding as much as possible
burying well-advanced mushrooms. The dressing could not be packed smoothly
over the whole bed because of the growing mushrooms. On bare spots between
mushrooms the grower could press gently with the back of the hand, but
never directly over the mushrooms, as many pinheads would be injured and
cause fogging off.

## Firming by Watering

The topdressing could be firmed to the bed by watering it, applied over
the whole surface including the mushrooms. Clear water was used, applied
gently through a water-pot rose. Falconer states he always did this and
had never known it to injure young mushrooms.

## Topdressing Against Black Spot

In beds where [[falconer-mushroom-diseases-treatment]] had
appeared, Falconer found that a topdressing of fine, fresh earth applied
evenly all over the bed acted "to a certain extent, as a preventive of
further attack." However, it had no effect upon already affected mushrooms,
large or small.

## The Broader Context of Bed Maintenance

Topdressing was part of a broader system of bed maintenance that included
regular gathering of mushrooms by pulling (not cutting), prompt removal of
old stumps, and keeping the bed surface clean. When [[falconer-gathering-and-marketing-mushrooms]], the
leftover stumps decayed and produced a fluffy, spongy substance that
destroyed surrounding mushrooms and mycelium threads. These stumps had to
be scooped out with a knife before decay set in, and the resulting holes
filled with fresh loam.

## Connection to Bed Longevity

Topdressing was sometimes seen as a way to extend the productive life of
a bed, but Falconer's view was that beds had a natural lifespan determined
by the vitality of the [[falconer-mushroom-spawn-preparation]]. A bed
could bear in a desultory way for many months and show "spurts of
increased fertility," but this was merely a prolonged dribbling of the
original crop, not a true second crop. Once the mycelium was exhausted,
no amount of topdressing or stimulation could revive production.
