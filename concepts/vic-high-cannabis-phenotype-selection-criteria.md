## [[oner-cannabis-breeding-fundamentals]]. Many desirable traits -- flavour, potency, flowering characteristics -- can only be evaluated in female plants because male plants do not produce female flowers.

This forces half-sib breeding where males contribute genes randomly, and the impact is quantified throughout the text. The difference between full-sib and half-sib outcomes at the F5 generation (96% vs 87% phenotypic uniformity for dominant traits) represents a significant loss of efficiency.

### Strategies for Male Evaluation

- **[[cervantes-progeny-testing-combining-ability]]**: Growing out test crosses from candidate males to determine which males pass on the most desirable genes. Vic High describes this as the gold standard but notes it requires additional time and resources.
- **Large male numbers**: Using many males per cross increases the probability that the pollen pool includes the desired alleles, making outcomes more closely match theoretical predictions.
- **Indirect markers**: Using linked visible traits as proxies for desired invisible traits -- but this carries significant risk due to gene linkage complications.

## The Danger of Linked Genes

Vic High highlights a subtle but critical trap in selection: gene linkage. Cannabis has several thousand genes residing on just 10 chromosome pairs (20 individual chromosomes). Each chromosome contains hundreds of genes that generally travel together during [[fungal-life-cycles-sexual-reproduction-metamorphosis]].

### Implications for Selection

- Selecting for one trait may inadvertently select for or against hundreds of other traits on the same chromosome.
- A breeder might select for spikey leaves while actually selecting against grapefruit flavour if those traits are on opposite chromosomes of the same pair.
- Each selection decision has far-reaching consequences beyond the target trait.
- Each time you select for a trait, you are selecting against several hundred genes.

The exception is crossing over during meiosis, where linked genes can be broken apart, but Vic High notes this is not reliable enough for practical selection purposes.

## Methodical Single-Trait Selection

Because of linkage effects and the compounding difficulty of polygenic traits, Vic High strongly recommends:

- Working on one or two traits at a time, especially with inbreeding projects such as selfing and backcrossing.
- Taking small, methodical steps rather than trying to fix many traits simultaneously.
- Ignoring superficial traits that have nothing to do with the real breeding objectives.
- Understanding that each selection for a trait is also a selection against several hundred other genes.
