# The Maze of Relative Subjectivity

## Overview

Through the parable of Okuninushi's sack, Fukuoka presents a profound
epistemological critique of how human beings attempt to understand nature. Man
jumps into the great sack called nature, grabs whatever he can, turns it over
and examines it, asking what it is and how it works, and drawing his own
conclusions. But no matter how careful his observations and reasoning, each
interpretation carries the risk of grievous error because man cannot know
nature any more than he can know the uses for the objects in Okuninushi's sack.

## The Parable of Okuninushi's Sack

When people want to know what Okuninushi no Mikoto, the Shinto deity of
agriculture, carries around in the huge sack on his shoulder, they immediately
open the sack and thrust their hands in. They find strange objects made of wood
and bamboo and begin making pronouncements: "This is a tool used by travelers,"
"No, it is a decorative carving," "No, it is definitely a weapon."

Yet the truth, known only to Okuninushi himself, is that the object is an
instrument fashioned for his amusement. Moreover, because it is broken, he
carries it merely for use as kindling. Every interpretation is wrong because
each interpreters projects their own framework onto something whose purpose
lies entirely beyond their comprehension.

## The Flea in the Sack

A flea born and raised in the sack without ever having seen the world outside
will never be able to guess that the object is an instrument hung from
Okuninushi's belt, no matter how much it studies the object. Similarly, man,
who is born within nature and will never be able to step outside of the natural
world, can never understand all of nature merely by examining that part of
nature around him.

Man's answer is that, although he may not be able to view the world from
without, if he explores the furthest reaches of the vast universe and learns
what there is and what has happened, is this not enough? But the frog in the
well, however content, remains in the well.

## The Stage of Subjectivity

When man observes and judges, there is only the thing called "man" and the
thing being observed. It is this thing called "man" that verifies and believes
in the reality of an object, and it is man who verifies and believes in the
existence of this thing called "man." Everything in this world derives from
man and he draws all the conclusions. But he runs the risk of acting out a
drunken role on the stage supported by the crazed subjectivity of his own
despotic existence.

Before laughing at blind faith in God, man should take note of his blind faith
in himself. His subjectivity holds up the stage on which his knowledge
performs, but if his subjective view is all wrong, the entire performance is
nothing but delusion.

## Connection to Non-Discriminating Knowledge

This epistemological critique connects directly to Fukuoka's concept of
non-discriminating knowledge. An infant sees things intuitively. When observed
without intellectual discrimination, nature is entire and complete, a unity.
In this non-discriminating view, there is no cause for doubt or discontent.
