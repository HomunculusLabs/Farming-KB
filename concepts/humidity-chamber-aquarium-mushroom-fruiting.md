# Aquarium Humidity Chamber for Mushroom Fruiting

## Overview

The aquarium-based humidity chamber is a simple, effective fruiting
environment for indoor [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] described in Dennis Jarrold's
[[gourmet-medicinal-mushroom-oyster-cultivation-methods]]. Using a standard glass fish tank as the enclosure, this
design creates the high-humidity conditions that mushroom [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]]
require to develop properly. The chamber maintains relative humidity levels
of 90-95% through the use of soaked hydro pellets (hydroton or expanded clay
aggregate) placed at the bottom of the tank, combined with a sealed plastic
sheet cover that minimizes moisture loss. The design is inexpensive, requires
no specialized equipment beyond the aquarium itself, and can be assembled
from materials readily available at [[solomon-garden-centers-seedling-traps]] and hardware stores.

## Basic Chamber Construction

The foundation of the humidity chamber is a standard glass fish tank,
ideally measuring approximately 4 feet long by 2 feet wide and 12-18 inches
deep, though smaller tanks can be used for smaller-scale cultivation. The
bottom of the tank is covered with a layer of hydro pellets — expanded clay
aggregates commonly used in [[cannabis-grow-hydroponic-growing-systems]] — soaked thoroughly in water.
These pellets serve as a passive humidification system: water evaporating
from their porous surfaces maintains high humidity within the enclosed tank.
The pellets provide a large surface area for evaporation while keeping the
bottom of the tank from becoming waterlogged, which would create conditions
favorable to bacterial contamination. A layer of water at the very bottom
of the tank, below the pellets, ensures that the pellets remain saturated
throughout the fruiting period.

## Sealing and Humidity Maintenance

The top of the aquarium is sealed with a sheet of clear plastic (polyethylene
or similar) to trap moisture inside the chamber. The plastic sheet should be
large enough to drape over the entire top of the tank with some overhang on
each side. It can be secured with tape, weights, or clamps to ensure a
tight seal. The seal does not need to be airtight — some gas exchange is
necessary to prevent the buildup of [[carbon-dioxide-retention-jar-lid-mushroom-cultivation]] from mushroom metabolism
— but it should be tight enough that the humidity level inside the chamber
remains consistently high. Condensation forming on the inside of the plastic
sheet is a positive indicator that the humidity level is adequate. If the
plastic sheet shows little or no condensation, the hydro pellets may need
to be re-soaked or additional water added to the bottom of the tank.

## Fresh Air Exchange Protocol

While high humidity is critical for mushroom development, fresh air exchange
(FAE) is equally important. Mushrooms produce carbon dioxide during
metabolism, and elevated CO2 levels inside the sealed chamber can lead to
abnormal [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] — particularly elongated stems and small
or absent caps, a condition known as "leggy" growth. In the basic Jarrold
method, FAE is provided manually by removing the plastic sheet cover and
fanning fresh air into the chamber with a piece of cardboard or a small
fan. This fanning is performed approximately twice daily, with each session
lasting long enough to completely replace the air inside the tank. After
fanning, the plastic sheet is resealed. This [[leary-psychedelic-manual-approach]] requires
regular attention but is effective and gives the cultivator direct control
over the FAE schedule.

## Self-Humidifying Chamber Design

For cultivators who prefer a more automated approach, Jarrold described a
self-humidifying version of the [[jarrold-self-humidifying-aquarium-chamber]] that maintains humidity
without relying solely on passive evaporation from hydro pellets. This
design incorporates an air stone (the type used in fish aquariums) placed
at the bottom of the tank, connected via flexible tubing to a standard
aquarium air pump positioned outside the chamber. The air stone generates
bubbles in the water layer at the bottom of the tank, and the humidified air
rises through the hydro pellets and into the chamber above. This active
humidification system provides a more consistent and controllable humidity
level than passive evaporation alone, and the gentle air movement from the
bubbles also contributes to fresh air exchange, reducing the need for
manual fanning. The air pump runs continuously or on a timer, depending on
the desired level of humidity and air exchange.

## Angled Drip Lids

An important refinement in the Jarrold [[mushroom-humidity-chamber-design]] is the use
of angled drip lids to manage condensation. Condensation that forms on the
plastic sheet cover tends to accumulate into large droplets that eventually
fall back into the chamber, potentially dripping directly onto developing
mushrooms and causing waterlogging or bacterial rot. To prevent this,
Jarrold recommends constructing drip lids from 2mm plastic sheet cut to the
dimensions of the tank top. These lids are positioned at a slight angle —
higher on one side and lower on the other — so that condensation runs down
the angled surface and drips off at the low edge rather than forming large
drops that fall randomly. The drip lid replaces the flat plastic sheet cover
and serves the dual purpose of humidity retention and controlled condensation
management, significantly reducing the incidence of water damage to the
developing mushroom crop.
