# Compost Quality Grades

## Overview

Steve Solomon challenges the widely held belief that all compost is inherently good compost. Drawing on decades of experience and analytical data, he classifies compost into three distinct quality grades based on nutrient content, carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, and suitability for vegetable production. Most home compost, according to Solomon, falls into the low-grade category — adequate for maintaining soil humus but insufficient as a standalone fertilizer for most vegetable crops. Understanding these grades is essential for gardeners who rely on compost as their primary or sole fertility source.

The quality of finished compost depends on the raw materials going into the heap, the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of those materials, the management of the decomposition process, and the soil temperature during application (which determines how quickly nutrients become available to plants).

## The Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio (C/N)

Every decomposable material can be characterized by the ratio of carbon (carbohydrate) to nitrogen (protein) it contains, written as C:N or C/N. This ratio is the single most important predictor of compost quality and decomposition behavior.

The C/N of finished soil humus is approximately 10:1. For compost to effectively feed vegetables, its C/N must approach this natural soil ratio. Materials with high C/N ratios (50:1 or higher, such as sawdust, straw, paper, and woody waste) decompose slowly and tie up soil nitrogen during the process. Materials with low C/N ratios (10:1 to 20:1, such as fresh manure, seedmeal, and kitchen scraps) decompose rapidly and release nutrients quickly.

A critical misconception Solomon addresses is the belief that mixing high-C materials with low-N materials and letting them decompose somehow preserves the nitrogen. In reality, much of the nitrogen is lost to the atmosphere as ammonia gas during the high-temperature phase of decomposition. The longer and hotter the composting process, the more nitrogen escapes. This is why municipal composting operations — which rely heavily on woody waste and paper — produce material with C/N ratios around 25:1, far too high for effective vegetable growing.

## Low-Grade Compost

### Characteristics
- **Nitrogen content**: Around 1.5 percent
- **C/N ratio**: Approximately 20:1
- **Total N+P+K**: Under 3 percent
- **Phosphorus content**: Low

### Suitability
Low-grade compost is barely adequate for the vegetable garden. When shallowly worked into the soil at a rate of about a quarter to half inch thick (6 to 12 millimeters), it provides enough nutrition for [[vegetable-nutrient-demand-levels|low-demand vegetables]] in warm climates where soil temperatures promote rapid decomposition.

In cool maritime climates, low-grade compost may not release nutrients fast enough to support even low-demand crops during the limited growing season. It is, however, perfectly adequate for maintaining soil humus levels, improving soil structure, and feeding ornamental plants or fruit trees.

### How It Is Made
Low-grade compost is the inevitable product of simply piling up garden and kitchen waste and waiting. Even a huge heap will eventually become something resembling soil — given about half a year in warm conditions, longer in cool ones. No particular skill, attention, or recipe is required. This is what most home gardeners produce, and what most gardening books present as perfectly adequate.

### The Problem
Relying solely on low-grade compost means your medium and high-demand vegetables will underperform. The phosphorus content is too low for good root development, the total nutrient content is insufficient for heavy-feeding crops, and the C/N ratio means that soil microorganisms will continue decomposing the material rather than releasing nutrients to plants for an extended period after application.

## Medium-Quality Compost

### Characteristics
- **Nitrogen content**: Approximately 2 to 3 percent
- **C/N ratio**: Around 15:1
- **Total N+P+K**: 3 to 6 percent
- **Phosphorus content**: Moderate

### Suitability
Medium-quality compost can adequately grow medium-demand vegetables in almost any soil and climate when applied at the same rate as low-grade compost. It may also support low-demand vegetables with excellent results. However, it still falls short of what high-demand vegetables like celery, cauliflower, and eggplant require for full production.

### How It Is Made
Producing medium-quality compost requires active management of the composting process. Solomon describes this as the "once-a-year heap" method:

1. **Accumulate materials** over weeks or months, segregating nitrogen-rich materials (kitchen scraps, fresh manure, green garden waste) from carbon-rich materials (dry leaves, straw, paper)
2. **Build the heap** with careful layering, aiming for an overall C/N of approximately 25:1 to 30:1 in the initial mix
3. **Add amendments**: Incorporate agricultural lime, rock phosphate, and possibly seedmeal or other nitrogen-rich materials to boost the final nutrient content
4. **Manage moisture**: The heap should be as damp as a wrung-out sponge throughout the process
5. **Monitor temperature**: A properly built heap will heat to 130-160°F (55-70°C) within days
6. **Turn the heap** at least once, preferably twice, to ensure even decomposition and introduce oxygen
7. **Allow curing**: After active decomposition subsides, let the compost cure for several weeks to months before use

The key difference from low-grade compost is intentionality: choosing materials, layering deliberately, supplementing with mineral amendments, and monitoring the process.

## High-Quality Compost

### Characteristics
- **Nitrogen content**: Exceeding 3 percent
- **C/N ratio**: No higher than 12:1
- **Total N+P+K**: Exceeding 6 percent, with a significant proportion as phosphorus
- **Trace minerals**: Significant amounts of calcium, magnesium, and trace elements in proper balance

### Suitability
High-quality compost can serve as the sole fertility source for all but the most demanding vegetable crops, provided it is applied in sufficient quantity and the soil is warm enough to decompose it. In hot climates where organic matter decomposes rapidly, it is possible to grow high-demand vegetables with only high-quality compost. In cool climates, supplemental [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof|COF]] may still be needed for high-demand crops.

### How It Is Made
Producing high-quality compost is a highly skilled activity requiring significant work, close attention, and the ability to observe and respond to what is happening inside the heap. Solomon compares it to serious winemaking: just as homebrew can range from foul to superb depending on the skill and attention of the brewer, compost quality varies enormously based on the compost-maker's knowledge and engagement.

Key requirements include:
- Starting with nitrogen-rich materials (manures, seedmeals, leguminous green matter)
- Supplementing with rock minerals (rock phosphate, lime, kelpmeal)
- Maintaining optimal moisture and aeration throughout decomposition
- Multiple turnings to ensure complete and even breakdown
- Adequate curing time for microbial stabilization

## The Fermentation Analogy

Solomon draws a direct parallel between composting and fermentation (beer, wine, yogurt, sauerkraut). Both are microbial processes that can be done casually with erratic results, or done with precision and control for consistently high-quality outcomes. The two essential differences between casual and skilled composting mirror those between casual and skilled fermentation:

1. **Control of conditions**: Temperature, moisture, aeration, and ingredient ratios must be managed
2. **Standardization of inputs**: The starting materials and their proportions matter enormously

Just as my grandmother's annual kosher wine varied wildly from year to year depending on grape quality, sugar content, yeast strain, and fermentation temperature, casual composting produces unpredictable results. The serious composter, like the serious winemaker, controls every variable possible.

## Common Composting Pitfalls

- **Too much woody material**: Paper, cardboard, chipped tree trimmings, and sawdust have C/N ratios of 100:1 to 500:1. Heaps dominated by these materials produce low-grade compost at best, with most nitrogen lost to the atmosphere during the prolonged high-temperature phase
- **Insufficient nitrogen sources**: Without manure, seedmeal, or other nitrogen-rich inputs, the heap cannot reach temperatures sufficient for complete decomposition
- **Poor moisture management**: Too dry and decomposition stalls; too wet and anaerobic conditions produce foul odors and poor-quality compost
- **Inadequate turning**: Oxygen-starved heaps decompose slowly and incompletely
- **Impatience**: Using compost before it is fully cured can tie up soil nitrogen and introduce pathogens

## Municipal Compost

Solomon is particularly critical of municipal composting programs. The raw materials going into municipal heaps — paper, cardboard, chipped tree trimmings, and other woody waste — have very high C/N ratios. The composting process causes most of the nitrogen to disappear as gas. The final product typically has a C/N around 25:1 and insufficient nutrients for vegetable growing. Municipal compost is suitable only as mulch along roadsides and under ornamentals. Gardeners who try to grow vegetables with it are invariably disappointed.

## Climate Considerations

The effectiveness of any grade of compost depends heavily on soil temperature. Nutrient release from organic matter is governed by microbial activity, which approximately doubles for each 10°F (5°C) increase in soil temperature. In cool maritime climates (such as the Pacific Northwest, the UK, and Tasmania), soil may never get warm enough for even high-quality compost to release nutrients rapidly. In these conditions, concentrated nutrient sources like COF provide more reliable results, especially for medium and high-demand crops.
