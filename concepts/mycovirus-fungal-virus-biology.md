Mycoviruses are viruses that infect and replicate within fungal cells,
found across all major groups of fungi including Ascomycota,
Basidiomycota, Zygomycota, and Chytridiomycota. Unlike plant and
animal viruses, most mycoviruses lack an extracellular transmission
phase and are transmitted vertically through spores or horizontally
during hyphal anastomosis (cell fusion between compatible fungal
strains). Mycoviruses are of particular interest for their ability to
modulate fungal virulence, including the phenomenon of hypovirulence
where viral infection reduces or eliminates the pathogenicity of
[[bloomfield-fungal-plant-pathogens]].

## Classification and Genome Diversity

Mycoviruses exhibit remarkable genomic diversity, with representatives
in most major viral taxa. The majority have double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)
genomes, traditionally classified into families including
Totiviridae, Partitiviridae, Chrysoviridae, Quadriviridae, and
Reoviridae. Single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) mycoviruses include positive-
sense members of the Hypoviridae, Narnaviridae, and Endornaviridae,
along with negative-sense viruses recently identified in the
Mononegavirales.

DNA mycoviruses are relatively rare but include the ssDNA virus
[[sclerotinia-sclerotiorum]] hypovirulence-associated DNA virus (SsHADV-1)
in the Genomoviridae family and the dsDNA mycovirus found in
 Cryphonectria parasitica. The discovery of DNA mycoviruses has
expanded the known host range and transmission mechanisms, as some
DNA mycoviruses can be transmitted extracellularly, unlike their RNA
counterparts.

## Transmission Mechanisms

Mycovirus transmission is fundamentally constrained by the fungal cell
wall, which limits extracellular transmission. The primary routes are
vertical transmission through asexual and sexual spores, and
horizontal transmission through hyphal anastomosis between
vegetatively compatible fungal strains. Vegetative compatibility
groups (VCGs) determine whether hyphal fusion occurs, and
incompatibility reactions involving programmed cell death prevent
virus transmission between incompatible strains.

This transmission bottleneck has important epidemiological consequences.
In natural populations, mycovirus spread is limited by VCG
diversity, and the viruses persist primarily through vertical
inheritance in clonal lineages. However, the hypovirus in
Cryphonectria parasitica demonstrates that mycoviruses can spread
effectively in populations with sufficient VCG diversity, reducing
the impact of chestnut blight across European forest ecosystems
over decades.

Recent discoveries of extracellular transmission mechanisms, including
the ssDNA mycovirus SsHADV-1 and some encapsidated RNA viruses,
suggest that the strict intracellular paradigm is incomplete. These
viruses may exploit insect vectors, soil water, or plant wound sites
for movement between fungal individuals, broadening their
epidemiological reach.

## Hypovirulence and Biological Control

The most celebrated application of mycovirus biology is the use of
hypovirulence for biological control of [[fungal-plant-diseases]]. The
prototypical example is Cryphonectria hypovirus 1 (CHV1), which
infects Cryphonectria parasitica, the causal agent of chestnut blight.
CHV1 infection reduces fungal growth rate, sporulation, and
pigmentation while dramatically attenuating canker expansion on
infected chestnut trees.

The molecular basis of hypovirulence involves disruption of fungal
[[plant-signaling-pathways]]. CHV1-encoded papain-like protease p29 suppresses
the cAMP-PKA signaling pathway, while the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase
p29 modulates G-protein signaling. These disruptions impair the
fungus's ability to sense environmental cues, form sexual structures,
and mount effective pathogenicity programs. The result is a weakened
pathogen that competes poorly with uninfected strains in the absence
of continued virus transmission.

Biological control using hypovirulent strains has been deployed in
Europe with notable success against chestnut blight, where CHV1-infected
strains spread naturally through forest populations. Application to
other pathosystems has been more limited, primarily because VCG
incompatibility restricts virus transmission in many fungal species.
Efforts to overcome this barrier include engineering transmissible
virus particles and selecting for broadly compatible hypovirulent
strains.

## Effects Beyond Hypovirulence

Not all mycovirus effects are deleterious to the host. Some mycoviruses
are entirely asymptomatic, while others confer adaptive benefits. The
Curvularia thermal tolerance virus (CThTV) enables its fungal host
Curvularia protuberata to confer heat tolerance to a symbiotic grass
 Dichanthelium lanuginosum, allowing colonization of geothermal
soils in Yellowstone National Park. This three-way symbiosis between
virus, fungus, and plant represents a remarkable example of beneficial
viral ecology.

Mycoviruses have been shown to alter [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]]
in fungi, including reduction of aflatoxin synthesis in Aspergillus
species. The mycovirus [[aspergillus-flavus]] 1 (AfV1) reduces aflatoxin
B1 production by modulating expression of the aflatoxin biosynthetic
gene cluster. Other documented effects include modulation of
cryptococcal virulence factor production, changes in fungal
morphology, and alteration of stress response pathways.

## Discovery and Detection Methods

Mycovirus discovery historically relied on purification of dsRNA from
fungal mycelium using phenol-chloroform extraction and cesium chloride
density gradient ultracentrifugation. The presence of dsRNA, absent in
uninfected eukaryotic cells, served as a diagnostic indicator of
mycoviral infection. Electron microscopy of purified preparations
revealed icosahedral virus particles, though many mycoviruses lack
conventional capsid structures and exist as naked RNA replication
complexes.

Modern detection employs metatranscriptomic sequencing of fungal
RNA, which has dramatically accelerated mycovirus discovery. Deep
sequencing of total RNA extracted from fungal cultures reveals viral
sequences without prior purification, enabling identification of
previously unknown viral taxa. This approach has revealed that
mycovirus infection is far more prevalent than previously estimated,
with some fungal species harboring multiple distinct viruses
simultaneously. Bioinformatic tools for viral contig identification
have expanded the known mycovirus diversity by an order of magnitude
in recent years.
