# Teonanacatl and Aztec Mushroom Use in Colonial Codices

## The Word Teonanacatl

The Nahuatl word "teonanacatl" -- variously translated as "flesh of the
gods," "[[ceremonial-shamanic-use-psychoactive-mushrooms]] employed in their religious
ceremonies. The word itself is a compound of "teo-" (divine, sacred, or
god) and "nanacatl" (mushroom or flesh). This translation has been the
subject of considerable scholarly debate: some have argued that "nanacatl"
in this context means "mushroom" while others contend it means "flesh,"
giving the word the powerful translation "flesh of the gods" -- a
theologically loaded phrase that resonated with Christian sacramental
language and consequently drew the intense hostility of Spanish
missionaries.

## Bernardino de Sahagun's Florentine Codex

The most important primary source on Aztec mushroom use is the work of
Bernardino de Sahagun (c. 1499-1590), a Franciscan friar who arrived in
New Spain in 1529 and spent over fifty years studying Aztec culture.
Sahagun's Florentine Codex, compiled between approximately 1545 and 1590,
is an encyclopedic work in twelve books covering Aztec religion, history,
medicine, and natural history.
