
# Green Manure and Cover Crop Techniques for Gardeners

## Overview

[[cover-crops]] are interchangeable terms for growing plants whose
sole purpose is enriching the soil. On a farm scale, cover crops are plowed in
or knocked down and left to rot on the surface. In gardens, they may be mowed,
with tops hauled to the compost heap and root systems dug in, or the entire plant
yanked out by hand and composted. Solomon presents [[vegetable-garden-fertilization]], the situation is the opposite of a permanent pasture. When
vegetables are growing their fastest, the gardener plants seedlings or sows seeds
in widely spaced rows. The ground is bare between plants until the crop canopy
closes, and considerable growth could happen between first frost and hard freezing,
but the garden is bare then too. Cover cropping fills these gaps.

## The Downsides — Risks for Gardeners

Solomon starts with the negatives, which he says how-to books rarely mention.
Green manuring practices filtered down from farmers to vegetable growers, but the
translation is imperfect. Farmers use tractors to handle tough, woody cover crops.
Gardeners use shovels and can be overwhelmed by a six-foot stand of cereal rye.

### The Timing Trap

The most dangerous scenario: an overwintered cover crop like cereal rye or winter
wheat emerges from snow in spring. Spring rains keep the soil too wet to work.
By the time the soil dries enough for tillage, the grass is waist-high and forming
seeds. After digging it in, the woody, high C/N material takes four to five weeks
to decompose. During decomposition, soil microbes consume enormous oxygen and
exhale CO₂, making the soil atmosphere so CO₂-rich that seeds cannot germinate
and transplanted seedlings suffocate.

Key rule: tender, low C/N green material dug in root and all decomposes in about
two weeks. Let the crop grow a few extra weeks until it starts forming seeds, and
the wait extends to four to five weeks.

### Allelopathy Risk

One cover crop may put residues into the soil that harm the subsequent crop — the
opposite of the intended effect. Solomon warns gardeners to be alert to this
possibility, especially with certain grain crops.

### Moisture Depletion in Arid Climates

In semi-arid regions without irrigation, a winter cover crop may deplete soil
moisture so thoroughly that the following crop cannot establish.

## Safe Practices

The safest approach for gardeners, especially those in cold winter climates:

- **Autumn-sown, winter-killed crops**: Sow cover crops that will be killed by
  winter frost. Their roots decompose in early spring, giving a much higher
  chance of a fine, loose seedbed by spring planting time.

- **Light soil advantage**: Light soils dry out quickly after rain, making spring
  incorporation of overwintered crops more feasible than with heavy clay.

- **Avoid "forward" crops**: If an overwintered crop gets too far ahead (farmers
  call this being "forward"), the woody material creates prolonged decomposition
  delays that can push planting past the viable window.

## The Upsides

When cover cropping goes well, the benefits are substantial:

- The soil is thickly filled with rapidly decomposing roots, leaving land loose,
  airy, and open to rapid crop root penetration.
- [[cover-crop-species-comparison]] has different freeze tolerances,
temperature preferences, and establishment requirements that vary by latitude.
Cover cropping is something gardeners must work out for themselves with initial
guidance, developing intuitive sense over a few years. Local agricultural
extension agencies and farm supply stores (not mail-order seed companies) are the
best sources for locally adapted cover crop seed and advice.
