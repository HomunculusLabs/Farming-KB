# Respiratory Herbs

Respiratory herbs address conditions of the lungs, bronchi, throat, and sinuses including coughs, colds, bronchitis, asthma, allergies, sinusitis, and congestion. Respiratory complaints are among the most common reasons people seek herbal treatment, and herbal medicine offers a rich pharmacopoeia for respiratory support. Herbs for the respiratory system are classified by their primary actions: expectorants, demulcents, antitussives, bronchodilators, antimicrobials, and decongestants.

## Expectorant Herbs

Expectorants help clear mucus from the respiratory tract by either increasing mucus production (stimulating expectorants) or thinning existing mucus (relaxing expectorants). The choice between stimulating and relaxing expectorants depends on the type of cough and the character of the mucus.

Stimulating expectorants are used for productive coughs with thick, sticky mucus that is difficult to expectorate. They increase the volume and fluidity of respiratory secretions through reflex stimulation. Common stimulating expectorants include licorice root ([[glycyrrhiza-glabra]]), ipecac (Cephaelis ipecacuanha, in very small doses), senega (Polygala senega), and grindelia (Grindelia spp.).

Relaxing expectorants soothe irritated respiratory mucosa and reduce spasmodic coughing while promoting gentle mucus clearance. They are used for dry, irritating coughs and for conditions where the airways are inflamed. Common relaxing expectorants include mullein (Verbascum thapsus), marshmallow ro althaea officinalis alis), licorice root, and wild cherry bark (Prunus serotina).

Mullein leaf is one of the most important respiratory herbs in Western herbalism. Its mucilage content soothes inflamed respiratory tissue, while its saponins help loosen mucus. Mullein leaf tea is a traditional remedy for coughs, bronchitis, and asthma. The leaves can also be smoked for acute respiratory distress, though this is not recommended as a regular practice. ## Antitussive (Cough-Suppressing) Herbs

Antitussives reduce coughing, either by suppressing the cough reflex or by soothing the irritated tissue that triggers coughing. Herbal antitussives generally work through soothing mechanisms rather than suppressing the cough reflex directly, which is considered safer than pharmaceutical cough suppressants that work centrally on the brain.

Wild cherry bark (Prunus serotina) contains hydrocyanic acid (in very small, safe amounts) that suppresses the cough reflex. It also has antispasmodic and sedative properties. Wild cherry bark is traditionally used as a syrup for dry, hacking coughs, particularly at night when coughing interferes with sleep.

Iceland moss ([[cetraria-islandica]]) is a lichen with demulcent and mild antitussive properties. Its polysaccharide content soothes inflamed throat and bronchial tissue. Iceland moss is prepared as a decoction or cold infusion and can be combined with honey for cough relief.

Plantain (Plantago major) leaf is a gentle but effective antitussive and demulcent for dry coughs and sore throats. It also has mild [[mycelium-running-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties-species-chart]]. Plantain tea or tincture is used for coughs, bronchitis, and irritated throats. This common yard weed is one of the most accessible respiratory herbs.

Thyme (Thymus vulgaris) combines antitussive, expectorant, antimicrobial, and antispasmodic properties. It is one of the best all-around respiratory herbs. Thyme tea with honey is a classic remedy for coughs, bronchitis, and colds. Thyme's thymol content provides antimicrobial activity against respiratory pathogens.

## Bronchodilator and Antispasmodic Herbs

Bronchodilators relax smooth muscle in the bronchi and bronchioles, widening the airways. They are particularly important for asthma and bronchospasm. Antispasmodic herbs reduce the spasmodic coughing that accompanies bronchial irritation.

Elecampane (Inula helenium) is a traditional respiratory tonic with expectorant, bronchodilator, and antimicrobial properties. Its inulin content provides soothing effects on respiratory mucosa, while its [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]] content (alantolactone) has antimicrobial activity. Elecampane root is prepared as a decoction or tincture for chronic bronchitis, asthma, and persistent coughs.

Zingiber officinale) has bronchodilator properties in addition to its anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects. Ginger tea helps relax bronchial smooth muscle and reduces inflammation in the respiratory tract. Fresh ginger is preferred for respiratory use because of its higher volatile oil content.

Chamomile (Matricaria recutita) has mild bronchodilator and antispasmodic effects that are useful for respiratory conditions with a spasmodic component. Its anti-inflammatory properties reduce airway inflammation, and its calming effect can reduce anxiety associated with breathing difficulty.

## Decongestant Herbs

Decongestant herbs reduce nasal and sinus congestion by promoting drainage of mucus and reducing mucosal inflammation. Many decongestant herbs contain volatile oils that can be inhaled as steam or used in aromatherapy.

Peppermint (Mentha x piperita) contains menthol, which provides a cooling sensation and helps open congested nasal passages. Peppermint tea, steam inhalation, and essential oil (diluted) are used for nasal and sinus congestion. Menthol also has mild antitussive and analgesic properties.

## Respiratory Steams and Inhalations

Steam inhalation is a traditional method of delivering volatile oils from herbs directly to the respiratory mucosa. A simple steam inhalation involves adding [[comparison-essential-oils-vs-neem-oil-vs-insecticidal-soap]] or fresh herbs to a bowl of hot water, covering the head with a towel, and inhaling the steam for five to ten minutes.

Effective herbs for steam inhalation include eucalyptus, peppermint, thyme, rosemary, and tea tree. The volatile oils are carried by the steam to the nasal passages, sinuses, and bronchi, providing decongestant, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory effects directly where they are needed.

Steam inhalation should be used with caution in children (risk of burns) and in individuals with asthma (some people find steam triggers bronchospasm). See [[honey-based-herbal-preparations]] for honey-based medicine making.

# Herbal Syrups

Herbal syrups are sweet, viscous liquid preparations made by combining a concentrated herbal extract or tea with honey, sugar, or other sweeteners. Syrups are particularly valuable for respiratory conditions, coughs, sore throats, and children's medicine because their sweetness masks bitter or unpleasant-tasting herbs and their viscous consistency coats and soothes irritated throat tissue.

## Why Syrups as a Preparation

Syrups occupy a unique position in the herbal apothecary. Their primary advantage is palatability: even very bitter or strong-tasting herbs become acceptable when combined with sufficient sweetener. This makes syrups ideal for children, elderly individuals, and anyone who struggles with the taste of tinctures or teas.

The viscous consistency of syrups provides a physical soothing effect on the throat and upper respiratory tract. As the syrup slowly coats the back of the throat, it delivers antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory herbs directly to irritated tissue while providing a protective barrier. This coating action is particularly valuable for dry, hacking coughs and sore throats.

Syrups also offer convenience. A bottle of herbal syrup in the refrigerator is ready to use at any time without the need for preparation. This is especially valuable during acute illness when the patient (or caregiver) may not have the energy or time to prepare teas or tinctures.

## Basic Syrup Preparation Methods

The simplest method involves preparing a strong herbal decoction or infusion, then adding honey or sugar while the liquid is still warm. A standard ratio is one part strong herbal tea to one to two parts honey or sugar by volume.

The decoction method is used for roots, barks, and seeds. Simmer one to two ounces of dried herb in one pint of water for 20 to 30 minutes. Strain the liquid and return it to the pot. Add one to two cups of honey or sugar. Warm gently until the sweetener is fully dissolved. Do not boil honey, as this damages its enzymes and therapeutic properties. Cool, bottle, and refrigerate.
