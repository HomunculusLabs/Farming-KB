# Mushroom Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling

Harvesting is the culmination of the cultivation cycle, but it is also a critical
determinant of final product quality, shelf life, and market value. Mushrooms are
highly perishable, with respiration rates among the highest of any harvested crop.
Proper harvesting technique and post-harvest handling can extend shelf life from days
to weeks and preserve the texture, flavor, and nutritional value that make fresh
mushrooms premium products. For medicinal mushrooms, correct handling preserves the
bioactive compounds that define therapeutic quality.

## Harvest Timing

The optimal harvest window varies by species and market requirements but generally
occurs when the mushroom cap has expanded but before the veil tears (for species with
a partial veil) or before spores are released. Key indicators of harvest maturity:

- **Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.)**: Harvest when the cap margin begins to
  flatten from convex to plane, typically 3-5 days after pinning. Waiting too long
  results in dry, tough textures and massive spore release that covers everything in
  the growing area.
- **Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)**: Harvest when the cap is 70-80% open with a
  thick, rolled margin. The gills should be white to pale pink. Fully opened shiitake
  have lower market value and shorter shelf life.
- **Button mushrooms (Agaricus bisporus)**: Harvest at button stage (closed veil)
  for the premium market, or at the cup stage (partially open) for processing.
- **Lion's mane (Hericium erinaceus)**: Harvest when the spines are 0.5-1.5 cm
  long, before they begin to brown from the tips downward.
- **Reishi (Ganoderma lingzhi)**: Harvest after the spore-producing surface has
  developed a significant white growth margin, typically 2-4 months after pinning.

## Harvesting Technique

Mushrooms should be harvested by cutting with a sharp knife or scissors at the base,
or by gently twisting and pulling from the substrate. The twist method is faster but
can damage the substrate surface and disturb adjacent pins. Cutting is preferred for
species that flush multiple times from the same block, as it minimizes substrate
disturbance. For oyster mushrooms growing in clusters, the entire cluster is typically
removed at once. Workers should wear clean gloves to prevent introducing bacteria from
skin contact, and tools should be sanitized between blocks to prevent cross-
contamination. Harvesting should be done in the morning when mushrooms are at peak
turgidity and before the fruiting room warms up.

## Flushing Patterns

Most cultivated mushrooms produce multiple "flushes" or crops of mushrooms from a
single substrate block. The first flush is typically the largest (50-60% of total
yield), with subsequent flushes declining in size. Oyster mushrooms usually produce
2-4 flushes, shiitake 3-5 flushes over a 6-8 week fruiting period, and button
mushrooms 2-3 flushes. Between flushes, the substrate needs a rest period of 1-2
weeks during which it is kept at high humidity but with reduced fresh air exchange to
allow the mycelium to recover and consolidate nutrients. Some growers soak blocks in
cold water for 12-24 hours between flushes to rehydrate the substrate and stimulate
the next fruiting cycle.

## Post-Harvest Cooling

Rapid cooling after harvest is the single most important step for extending shelf life.
Mushrooms should be cooled to 0-4 degrees C within 2 hours of harvest. Field heat
 accelerates respiration, water loss, and microbial growth. Precooling methods include
cold room storage, forced-air cooling, and hydrocooling (brief immersion in cold
water, suitable only for species not damaged by water contact). The target storage
temperature varies: oyster mushrooms store best at 1-2 degrees C, shiitake at 2-4
degrees C, and lion's mane at 0-2 degrees C. Freezing should be avoided as it damages
cell walls, turning the texture to mush upon thawing.

## Packaging

Proper packaging maintains high humidity around the mushroom while allowing gas
exchange to prevent anaerobic conditions that cause off-flavors and rapid decay. The
industry standard is micro-perforated polyethylene or polypropylene film overwrapped
trays. The micro-perforations allow O2 in and CO2 out at rates matched to the
mushroom's respiration rate, creating a modified atmosphere that extends shelf life.
For premium markets, flow-wrapped trays with a small headspace of air provide good
protection. Bulk packaging for food service uses ventilated crates lined with damp
paper to maintain humidity without waterlogging.

## Shelf Life

Expected shelf life at optimal storage temperatures (0-4 degrees C):

- **Oyster mushrooms**: 5-9 days. The most perishable of the common gourmet species.
  Yellow oysters have the shortest shelf life; blue and grey oysters last slightly
  longer. King oysters can last up to 14 days.
- **Shiitake**: 14-21 days. Among the best-keeping fresh mushrooms due to their
  dense flesh and natural antimicrobial compounds.
- **Button mushrooms**: 7-14 days in proper packaging.
- **Lion's mane**: 7-10 days. Degrades rapidly, developing a fishy or ammonia odor
  when past prime.
- **Maitake (hen of the woods)**: 5-7 days. Extremely fragile and prone to browning.

## Drying and Preservation

Drying is the primary long-term preservation method, especially for medicinal
mushrooms. Air drying at temperatures below 40 degrees C preserves heat-sensitive
bioactive compounds like beta-glucans and triterpenes. Dehydrators set to 35-38
degrees C with good airflow produce consistently high-quality dried product. Shiitake
are traditionally dried at moderate temperatures (30-40 degrees C) which actually
enhances their umami flavor by converting lentinic acid to lentinione. Vacuum sealing
dried mushrooms in oxygen-barrier bags with desiccant packets extends shelf life to
2+ years. Freeze-drying produces the highest quality but is prohibitively expensive for
most operations. Some growers also freeze mushrooms after brief blanching or sauteing
for value-added products.
