# Fungal Extreme Environments

## Overview

Fungi are remarkably adaptable organisms that colonize some of Earth's most extreme environments. From polar deserts to hot arid zones, from metal-contaminated soils to radioactive concrete, fungi have evolved diverse strategies to survive and function under conditions that preclude most other life forms. Key fungal groups in extreme environments include lichens, rock-dwelling microcolonial fungi, melanized microfungi, and various stress-tolerant saprotrophs and symbionts.

## Lichens in Extreme Environments

Lichens dominate approximately 10% of terrestrial ecosystems, encompassing regions where higher plants are at their physiological limits: polar, alpine and desert ecosystems. The lichen symbiosis between the fungal partner (mycobiont) and photosynthesizing partner (photobiont) is one of the most successful means for fungi to survive in extreme sub-aerial environments.

### Desert Lichens

Lichen mats cover thousands of square kilometres of Arctic tundra (Cladonia spp., Cetraria islandica) and extensive fields in the southern Namib Desert (Teloschistes capensis). In the Negev and Namib Deserts, lichen photobionts photosynthesize during a short period at sunrise when thalli are partly hydrated from fog or nocturnal dewfall, before drying out and entering dormancy. Annual gas exchange measurements in Ramalina maciformis in the Negev Desert showed positive carbon balance on 218 days, negative on 88 days, and zero (drought dormancy) on 49 days. Radial size increase of approximately 0.4 mm per year was recorded in Caloplaca aurantia. The most conspicuous chlorolichen communities develop in desert areas with very low precipitation but frequent high atmospheric moisture, such as coastal fog oases (Atacama Desert, Namib Desert), harbouring high proportions of fog comb lichens with high surface-to-volume ratios.

### Polar and Alpine Lichens

Temperature and water availability are limiting factors for polar and alpine lichens. Frost is the main water source in high Andean Paramos. Trebouxia photobionts of polar lichens are photosynthetically active at sub-zero temperatures, low photon flux densities and partly hydrated states. Covered by snow with ice windows from melting/freezing cycles above darkly pigmented thalli, lichens live in greenhouse-like conditions well protected from harsh climate. Very low growth rates occur in continental Antarctic lichens, but surprisingly fast growth occurs in maritime Antarctic communities.

### Water Relations and Desiccation Tolerance

The key to understanding impressive environmental stress tolerance of lichen-forming fungi lies in their poikilohydric water relations and ability of desiccated cells to survive temperature extremes unharmed in dormancy. Desiccated thalli of Xanthoria parietina grew normally after cryoimmobilization in sub-cooled liquid nitrogen (approximately -200 degrees C), freeze-fracturing and examination under high vacuum, but hydrated thalli did not survive. Both symbionts are fully adapted to continuous wetting and drying cycles, which drive passive fluxes of solutes between partners. Lichens die off under continuously moist conditions or when prevented from drying at elevated temperatures.

## Rock-Dwelling Fungi

Rock-inhabiting fungal communities must deal with varying extremes in light, salinity, pH and water potential over considerable periods of time. Fungi achieve protection through melanin pigments and mycosporines in cell walls, and by embedding colonies into mucilaginous polysaccharide slime that entraps clay particles providing extra protection. Some fungi are polymorphic, occurring as filamentous mycelium and unicellular yeasts or yeast-like cells -- the black meristematic or microcolonial rock-dwelling fungi. Microcolonial black-coloured fungi occur on rock surfaces as spherical clusters of tightly packed cells with thick pigmented walls or as moniliform thick-walled hyphae. These are common inhabitants of limestone, sandstone, marble and granite in hot and cold deserts and semi-arid regions, associated with pitting and etching of rock surfaces. Fungi are also common inhabitants of biogenic oxalate crusts on granitic rocks. Fungal communities have been documented in the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica, extreme hot and cold deserts, and rock substrates buried several kilometres below the Earth's surface, spanning limestone, soapstone, marble, granite, sandstone, andesite, basalt, gneiss, dolerite, amphibolite and quartz across a wide range of geographical and climatic zones.

## Metal-Rich and Radioactive Environments

Fungi colonize metal-rich and hypersaline habitats, reflecting their long evolutionary
history in geochemically active environments since the Ordovician period (480-460 MYBP).
Fungal communities have been components of terrestrial environments including deserts
and metal-rich habitats throughout this period. Melanized strains of Alternaria,
Cladosporium and Aureobasidium survived and colonized highly radioactive concrete
at the Chernobyl nuclear power plant (alpha = 500 Bq/cm2, beta = 20,000 Bq/cm2,
gamma = 700 mR/h). Studies of the Shelter over Reactor No. 4 demonstrated microfungi
could colonize and corrode barrier concrete, with myco-corrosion potentially reducing
the theoretically calculated service life of concrete and causing risk from
radionuclide release into the environment. Fungi can also adapt to severe radioactive
contamination, raising concerns about both existing and future nuclear waste storage
integrity. Fungi tolerate metal toxicity, UV radiation, and desiccation through a
combination of structural defenses (melanin, thick cell walls, polysaccharide slime)
and physiological mechanisms (compatible solutes, antioxidant systems, metal
complexation and precipitation).

## Structural Adaptations to Environmental Stress

### Hydrophobins and Wall Surface Hydrophobicity

Wall surface hydrophobicity in lichen thalli is generated by class 1 hydrophobins that form semicrystalline rodlet layers on hyphal surfaces. This protein-based hydrophobicity channels the flow of solutes from the thallus surface to interior and vice versa during wetting and drying cycles. Hydrophilic amorphous fungal wall components such as lichenin, a linear beta-(1,3),(1,4)-glucan, can temporarily retain water in the thallus.

### Drought-Stress Responses

Drought-stressed lichen thalli shrink without wilting, revealing different consistency and colour from the fully hydrated state. The cortex of hydrated thalli is elastic and glassy-translucent, allowing light to reach the algal layer. In the desiccated state the cortex is opaque and brittle. The severity of shrinkage depends on thalline morphology: crustose and squamulose thalli shrink mainly in the vertical axis, while thalli with no tight substrate contact shrink in all dimensions. Cells with thin hyaline walls undergo cytorrhysis (collapse), globose or ovoid spores often achieving a boat-like shape during desiccation. Cells with thick rigid walls cannot be sufficiently deformed during water loss and therefore cavitate (implode). Reversible symplastic cavitation is a regularly occurring feature in lichen-forming fungi under drought stress. Ascomal shrinkage during desiccation is functionally important, generating pressure on flanks of mature asci required for ascospore ejection. Concentric bodies, found in cells of lichen-forming and numerous desiccation-tolerant fungi, are considered to be involved in membrane protection during drying and rehydration cycles. Mycobiont-derived ice nucleation activity facilitates water management during freeze-thaw transitions.

### Biological Soil Crusts

Lichens are key components of biological soil crust communities in arid ecosystems worldwide, covering very large areas. These crusts are ecologically important stabilizers that reduce wind erosion in steppe, semi-desert and desert ecosystems, lowering the severity of large-scale intercontinental dust storm events. Approximately 85% of lichen-forming ascomycetes have green algal photobionts, 10% have cyanobacterial photobionts, and 4% have both simultaneously in triple symbioses where the fungal partner creates microaerobic conditions enhancing cyanobacterial nitrogen fixation.

## Molecular Basis of Stress Tolerance

The molecular basis of desiccation- and stress-tolerance in lichen-forming fungi and their photobionts remains poorly investigated. In natural habitats, constitutively induced protective mechanisms for cellular membrane and enzyme systems are essential. Acyclic polyols from fungal and green algal partners serve as compatible solutes under drought stress and are centrally important mobile carbohydrates in the symbiotic interaction. A glutathione-based antioxidant system was found to be activated in wild-collected thalli but not in sterile cultured isolates of Cladonia vulcani and its photobiont Trebouxia excentrica, suggesting that stress responses are naturally induced by multifactorial environmental parameters that are difficult to simulate in laboratory culture. Mycobiont-derived protein-based ice nucleation activity at -5 degrees C or warmer was found in symbiotic and aposymbiotic phenotypes of lichen-forming ascomycetes, but not in cultured photobionts, suggesting the fungal partner plays a key role in freeze tolerance.

## Physiological Diversity of Photobiont Partnerships

Approximately 85% of lichen-forming ascomycetes have green algal photobionts (chlorolichens), 10% have cyanobacterial photobionts (cyanolichens), and about 4% have both simultaneously. In triple symbioses (cephalodiate lichens), the fungal partner creates microaerobic conditions around the cyanobacterial colony, increasing heterocyst frequencies and enhancing nitrogen fixation. Drought-stressed chlorolichens with Trebouxia photobionts (symbionts of over 50% of lichen-forming ascomycetes) can rehydrate with water vapour alone, but cyanolichens require liquid water for full recovery. Accordingly, chlorolichens dominate in arid ecosystems while cyanolichens with internally stratified thalli (Peltigera, Sticta, Nephroma) are abundant in moist habitats. Gelatinous cyanolichens (Collema spp.) dominate soil crust communities in arid ecosystems with erratic rainfall. Tropical chlorolichens often contain Trentepohliales as photobionts, which are less cold-tolerant than Trebouxia species.

## Fungal Strategies for Coping with Extreme Conditions

Fungi combine features of animal and plant cells by being both non-motile and heterotrophic. Being non-motile has introduced strong selection pressures for fungi to evolve mechanisms responding rapidly to changing environmental conditions. Hyphae respond extremely sensitively and quickly to signals within heterogeneous microenvironments. Hyphal growth direction and branching patterns change rapidly, and hyphae can selectively grow from unfavourable (nutrient-depleted or desiccated) to more favourable environments. Sporulation can be initiated quickly, producing spores adapted to resist adverse conditions. Many fungi form differentiated hyphal aggregates such as sclerotia that resist adverse environments, rhizomorphs for long-distance transport of water and nutrients through adverse environments, or fruit bodies that protect and promote spore production.

Filamentous fungi respond to four types of environmental signals: (a) abiotic and global (light, temperature, humidity), (b) abiotic and local (nutrients, oxygen, CO2, pH, osmotic shock), (c) biotic signals from other organisms (chemical and physical signals from fungi, bacteria, plants and animals), and (d) biotic autoregulatory signals from the fungus itself that regulate colony morphogenesis, hyphal avoidance, homing, yeast-hyphal dimorphism and reproduction. Fungi produce a diverse range of extracellular enzymes efficiently secreted into the environment, and possess a wide range of transport membrane processes for nutrient uptake.
