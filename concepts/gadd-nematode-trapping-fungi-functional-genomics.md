Nematode-trapping fungi are soil-dwelling predators that capture and
digest nematodes using specialised morphological structures called
traps. Over 200 species of nematophagous fungi have been identified
across all major taxonomic groups of fungi, including deuteromycetes,
basidiomycetes, chytridiomycetes and zygomycetes. They inhabit soils
worldwide, from the tropics to Antarctica, and occupy agricultural
and forest soils alike. Their predatory lifestyle makes them
fascinating subjects for understanding how fungi evolve complex
infection mechanisms, and functional genomics approaches have
revolutionised the study of these organisms.

## Diversity of Nematophagous Fungi

Three broad groups of nematophagous fungi are recognised based on
their infection mechanisms. Nematode-trapping fungi and endoparasitic
fungi attack free-living nematodes using specialised structures, while
egg- and cyst-parasitic fungi infect these stages with their hyphal
tips. The nematode-trapping fungi are the best-known group owing to
their remarkable morphological adaptations and dramatic infection
process. With few exceptions, including the mushroom Hohenbuehelia
(asexual state Nematoctonus), most identified species belong to a
monophyletic clade among the apothecial ascomycetes, as determined
by 18S rDNA phylogenetics.

Trap morphology varies enormously even between closely related species.
Some produce erect branches covered with adhesive material. Others,
such as the well-studied Arthrobotrys oligospora, form complex
three-dimensional adhesive nets. A third type is the adhesive knob,
a morphologically distinct cell produced on the apex of a slender
hyphal stalk, coated with a layer of adhesive polymers absent from
the supporting stalk. Finally, some species capture nematodes using
mechanical constricting rings that snap shut when triggered.

## Infection Sequence

Regardless of trap type, infection follows a conserved sequence of
events. First, the trap cells attach to the surface of the nematode.
Second, the fungus penetrates the cuticle using a combination of
physical force and enzymatic activity. Third, the nematode tissues are
digested. Fourth, the nutrients from the killed nematode are
assimilated by the fungus. This sequence parallels the infection
process of plant pathogenic fungi, where appressoria attach to and
penetrate host surfaces, suggesting convergent evolution of predatory
and parasitic strategies.

Traps develop from hyphal branches and can form either spontaneously
or in response to environmental signals, including peptides and other
compounds secreted by host nematodes. This inducible trap formation
is ecologically significant because it allows the fungus to switch
between saprophytic growth in soil and an active predatory phase
when nematodes are detected.

## Serine Proteases and Virulence

The serine protease PII, a subtilisin, was one of the first
pathogenicity factors characterised in nematode-trapping fungi.
Isolated from culture filtrates of Arthrobotrys oligospora, the
protein was sequenced and its corresponding gene cloned. Transformants
with deleted PII genes produced fewer traps and showed slightly
reduced virulence, while strains over-expressing PII developed more
infection structures and killed nematodes more rapidly than wild type.
Heterologously produced PII in Aspergillus niger demonstrated direct
nematicidal activity when applied to free-living nematodes, confirming
the protease as a bona fide virulence factor.

A carbohydrate-binding lectin designated AOL was also studied in
detail. AOL belongs to a family of low-molecular-mass, saline-soluble
lectins that appear unique to filamentous fungi. Although AOL gene
deletion did not affect the infection phenotype, expression studies
suggested its function relates to nitrogen storage: large amounts of
AOL are synthesised during nematode digestion and later transported
from the infected nematode to other parts of the mycelium.

## EST Database for Monacrosporium haptotylum

Monacrosporium haptotylum (synonym Dactylaria candida) was selected
as a model for functional genomic studies because its adhesive knobs
can be easily separated from mycelium by filtration of liquid
cultures. Isolated knobs retain their function as infection structures,
capable of capturing and infecting nematodes independently.

Four directional cDNA libraries were constructed from mycelium,
isolated knobs, and knobs infecting Caenorhabditis elegans for 4
and 24 hours respectively. In total, 8463 expressed sequence tags
(ESTs) were sequenced and assembled into 3121 contigs representing
putatively unique genes or transcripts. Between 5 and 37 percent of
assembled sequences showed significant similarity to entries in the
GenBank nr protein database, while a large fraction of 38 to 60
percent (orphans) showed no homology to known proteins. The
proportion of orphans was highest in the knob (58 percent) and
24-hour infection (60 percent) libraries, suggesting that trap cells
and infection stages express many novel or highly divergent genes.

From these ESTs, a cDNA microarray was constructed containing 3518
PCR-amplified clones: 2822 of fungal origin, 540 from C. elegans,
and 156 of unknown origin. The use of C. elegans as a host was
strategic because its fully sequenced genome allows transcripts from
the fungus and the nematode to be readily separated in mixed cDNA
libraries.

## Gene Expression in Trap Cells Versus Vegetative Hyphae

Comparing knobs and mycelium of M. haptotylum revealed substantial
differences in gene expression patterns. Using mixed-model ANOVA
at a significance threshold of p less than 0.05, 23.3 percent
(657 of 2822) of putative genes were differentially expressed in
knobs versus mycelium. Genes with roles in transcription, cellular
transport, signal transduction, cell rescue and defence, and cell
growth were generally expressed at lower levels in knobs than in
mycelium, consistent with the knob being a differentiated,
non-growing cell type.

The transition from vegetative hypha to adhesive knob represents a
shift in cell polarity. Several differentially expressed genes showed
homology to known regulators of cell polarity in fungi, including
actin-binding proteins profilin and cofilin, and members of the
Ras and Rho families of small GTPases. These molecular switches
act through GTP binding and hydrolysis to control actin cytoskeleton
organisation during polarised growth.

## Parallels with Plant Pathogenic Appressoria

Striking similarities exist between the transcriptional profiles of
nematode-trapping knobs and appressoria of plant pathogenic fungi.
Both are specialised infection structures that develop as spherical
cells at hyphal tips, possess adhesive outer layers for host
attachment, and penetrate hosts using physical force combined with
extracellular enzymes.
