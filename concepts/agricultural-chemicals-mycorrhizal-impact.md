## Overview

Many common agricultural practices and chemical inputs can damage or destroy [[mycorrhiza]]l fungal populations, undermining the benefits these symbionts provide to crops. Understanding which practices are harmful and how to mitigate their effects is essential for farmers and gardeners seeking to maintain healthy [[mycorrhiza]]l communities.

Source: [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi]]

***

## Fungicides

### Direct Toxicity

Some fungicides, inoculants, and fumigants can be deadly to [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] because they attack fungal components such as:
- Unique cell walls
- Metabolic processes specific to fungi

Agricultural seed is often coated with fungicides, and fungicides are routinely applied to agricultural soils. These biocides do not distinguish between pathogenic fungi and beneficial [[mycorrhizal-fungi]].

### Timing and Application Method

The impact of fungicides depends on how they are applied:
- **Soil drenching** during [[mycorrhiza]]l formation: HIGH impact — directly exposes fungi to the biocide
- **Foliar spraying** on plant leaves: LOWER impact — less direct exposure to root-zone fungi
- **Seed coating**: Variable impact — depends on whether fungicide persists into the root zone

### Not All Fungicides Are Harmful

Interestingly, not all fungicides impact [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] negatively. At least one class of fungicides containing metalaxyl actually stimulates [[mycorrhiza]]l formation, perhaps because it eliminates competing fungi. This illustrates the complexity of the [[soil-food-web]] and the difficulty of making blanket recommendations.

## Herbicides

### Indirect Effects

Although herbicides are not usually directly toxic to [[mycorrhizal-fungi]], they have nonselective reach and can impact [[mycorrhiza]]l populations indirectly:
- If a [[mycorrhiza]]l host plant is killed by herbicide application, the number of spores in the soil can be reduced
- This has varying detrimental impacts depending on timing, extent, and frequency

### Glyphosate Considerations

While Lowenfels notes that herbicides and pesticides are not usually directly toxic to [[mycorrhiza]]e, the elimination of host plants removes the carbon source that [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] depend on. Organic solutions are always preferred over chemical ones when it comes to maintaining [[mycorrhiza]]l health.

***

## Pesticides

### Mixed Effects

Some pesticides may actually increase [[mycorrhiza]]l colonization:
- Killing the pest results in healthier plants
- Healthier plants produce more [[root-exudates]]
- More exudates attract and support more [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]

However, this benefit is incidental rather than intentional, and the overall ecological cost of pesticide use typically outweighs this indirect benefit.

## Fertilizers

### The Phosphorus Problem

The amount of phosphorus in soil greatly affects [[mycorrhiza]]l establishment:
- **Too much phosphorus** — spores are less likely to germinate, [[mycorrhiza]]l growth is hindered
- **High-phosphorus synthetic fertilizers** — can inhibit [[mycorrhiza]]l growth
- Recovery from phosphorus excess can take **decades**

### The Nitrogen Problem

Excess nitrogen similarly affects [[mycorrhiza]]l formation. The dose-dependent relationship:
- More fertilizer = less [[mycorrhiza]]l colonization
- Less [[mycorrhiza]]l colonization = less phosphorus concentration in plant tissues
- Paradoxically, more fertilizer can lead to less nutrient uptake efficiency

### Organic Fertilizer Considerations

Even organic fertilizers require careful management:
- Manure-laden composts can have very high phosphorus levels
- Animal manures may contain phosphate salts exceeding 1,000 ppm
- Soil amended with manure may still have inhibitory phosphorus levels 100 years after application
- Compost itself is free of [[mycorrhiza]]l spores (no live roots to support them, and composting temperatures kill propagules)

## Tillage and Soil Disturbance

### Physical Destruction

Any surface disturbance of plants and soil can negatively impact the belowground [[mycorrhiza]]l network:
- Stock grazing
- Plowing
- Tilling (especially fall tilling)
- Root damage from equipment

### Mechanism of Damage

Since arbuscular [[mycorrhiza]]l spores are abundant in the top 6-15 inches (15-38 cm) of soil:
- Tilling destroys or displaces spores deeper into the soil profile
- Spores moved deeper may not be reachable by roots or [[root-exudates]]
- Germination and growth may be delayed by unsatisfactory deeper soil conditions
- Hyphae are physically torn apart by tillage, especially fall tillage

### Consequences

If fungal hyphae are damaged or destroyed:
- They will not be available to partner with cover crops or spring-planted crops
- The amount of phosphorus available for the next crop is reduced
- A new [[mycorrhiza]]l network must form from scratch (taking up to 2 months)

## Crop Rotation Impact

### Rotation with Non-Host Crops

Crop plants differ in their [[mycorrhiza]]l dependence:
- **Highly dependent:** corn, flax, legumes, potatoes
- **Somewhat dependent:** wheat, oats, barley
- **Non-hosts (do not form [[mycorrhiza]]e):** brassicas (mustards, cabbages, broccoli), beets, buckwheat

Planting non-host crops means no host plants provide carbon to the fungi. Without carbon, [[mycorrhiza]]l populations decline and spore banks diminish.

### Allelopathic Crop Residues

Some crops release allelopathic chemicals when they decompose:
- Mustards, rapeseed, and radishes release chemicals that prevent [[mycorrhiza]]l spore formation or propagation
- These chemicals can persist in soil even after plants are removed

### Fallowing Problems

Leaving fields fallow (cropless) reduces [[mycorrhiza]]l populations because:
- No host plants provide the carbon fungi need to survive
- Fallow fields are usually plowed or tilled, further destroying the mycelial network
- Reestablishing [[mycorrhiza]]e after fallow can take up to 2 months
