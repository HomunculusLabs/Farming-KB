# Dryland Building and Settlement

Dryland environments present extreme challenges for human settlement—intense solar radiation, temperature extremes, water scarcity, dust storms, and limited vegetative cover. [[permaculture]] design addresses these through earth-sheltered architecture, underground food production, strategic use of desert-adapted plants, and traditional building techniques that exploit the thermal mass and insulation properties of earth.

## Earth-Sheltered and Underground Architecture

Underground and earth-sheltered structures are among the most effective solutions for dryland habitation. In Fresno, California, hand-dug underground caves open into shaded pits containing fig, grape, citrus, and pomegranate orchards. Looking up from underground, the sky is framed by fruit tree canopy. The root zones in the pits remain cool and are easily mulched, while the ground surface can be shaped into funnel forms to catch and direct water to plant containers. One such house covers 2.8 hectares and was entirely hand-dug.

Traditional earthed-up barns in high cold deserts (such as near Aspen, Colorado) provide winter hay storage and livestock shelter, insulated naturally by accumulated snow. At St. Theresa Mission in Central Australia, house verandahs are screened with climbing plants—Bryonia (a cucurbit), lab-lab bean, and tomato vines—which cool west-facing and sunward walls and are sustained by waste wash-water and greywater.

The Village Homes development in Davis, California, demonstrates earth-sheltered housing with solar hot water, turf roofs, and slab floors providing excellent thermal comfort. At Old Snowmass, Colorado (7,500 feet elevation), an early Biodome greenhouse uses solar electricity, heating ponds, and biological pest control (frogs and finches) to grow tender crops year-round in an area where the growing season averages only 60 days.

## Desert-Adapted Plant Species for Settlement

Several desert plant species serve multiple functions around settlements:

- **Opuntia** (prickly pear) provides fruit (tuna), vegetable food, cattle forage, and living hedges. In the Canary Islands, it also hosts cochineal scale insects used locally as a food colorant.
- **Acacia species** create desert mounds (nebkas) through dust accretion. Gidgee (Acacia cambagei) forms mounds about 1.5 meters high and 12 meters across. Many sessile or weeping desert trees perform this function, providing deep absorptive soils and mulch for understory vines, other trees, and burrowing animals.
- **Ocotillo** (Fouquieria splendens) is cultivated for fencing, can be live-set to grow as gated barriers, and is widely used as reinforcement for mud wall construction—making it a useful structural crop plant for desert construction.
- **Solanum species** provide abundant fruit and seed suitable for poultry forage, while woolly Solanum species serve as dryland soup or pot herbs. Lycium species similarly offer poultry seed and edible berries.

## Deep Mulch Techniques

On volcanic islands such as the Canary Islands (Las Palmas), a deep mulch technique using volcanic cinder (arendo, approximately 0.3 meters deep) sustains apple and pear crops in areas receiving primarily night fog and condensation rather than rainfall. This technique also produces potatoes and other food crops.

## Traditional Cooling Systems

In Greenock, South Australia, traditional buried dairy sheds exploit earth thermal mass to keep milk and food cool, assisted by vine, earth, or tree shade. At the Bronx, New York, ice plants and herbs on rooftops provide active cooling through transpiration and shade—a principle applicable to any dryland building.

## Dust Storm Mitigation

Extensive disc pitting over 600 square kilometers around Alice Springs, Central Australia, has significantly reduced dust storms around the town, demonstrating how broad-scale earth-shaping can address regional environmental problems while improving conditions for settlement.

## Design Principles for Dryland Settlement

1. **Earth-shelter first**: Bury or partially bury structures to reduce temperature extremes and conserve water.
2. **Capture every drop**: Shape surfaces to funnel water to plants and storage.
3. **Use thermal mass**: Earth, stone, and water provide passive temperature regulation.
4. **Plant multi-functional species**: Select plants that provide food, forage, shelter, and structural materials simultaneously.
5. **Exploit microclimates**: Use walls, rocks, and plantings to create cooler, moister pockets within the harsh landscape.
6. **Greywater recycling**: Direct all household wastewater to productive plantings around structures.
7. **Living structures**: Use living plants (Ocotillo, Opuntia hedges) as fencing, shade, and windbreak rather than imported materials.

## Radon Considerations

In areas with known radon levels, underground structures require ventilation planning. This is a site-specific hazard that must be assessed during the design phase, particularly for earth-integrated buildings in certain geological formations.
