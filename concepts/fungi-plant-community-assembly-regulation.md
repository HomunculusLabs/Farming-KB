## Overview

Fungi play central roles in the assembly, maintenance, and regulation of plant communities. From the initial colonization of bare ground through successional dynamics to the establishment of stable climax communities, fungal interactions — through mycorrhizae, pathogens, endophytes, and saprotrophs — shape the composition, diversity, and stability of vegetation. The interplay between patchy environmental resources, differential fungal benefits to host plants, and fungal-fungal interactions creates a complex mosaic that determines community structure.

## Coevolution of Fungi and Land Plants

The evolution of fungi is intimately linked with the emergence of plants onto dry land (Kidston and Lang, 1921; Pirozynski and Malloch, 1975). These ancient associations persist today in the forms of mycorrhizae, endophytes, and plant pathogens. The fact that these close associations still exist suggests mutual benefit to each partner, and this mutualism fundamentally shapes how plant communities assemble.

## Primary Succession and Mycorrhizal Inoculum

### Patchy Distribution of Benefits

During primary succession onto new land, mycorrhizal inocula carried into newly colonized areas benefit plants in terms of nutrient and water acquisition. The patchy distribution of these pockets of inoculum, combined with patchy distributions of suitable rooting media, leads to spatial variability in the benefits plants derive from mycorrhizal associations. In very resource-rich areas, non-mycorrhizal ruderals are capable of surviving independently.

### Community Structure from Heterogeneity

Plant community structure develops from the combination of:

1. **Environmental heterogeneity**: Patchy distribution of resources, soil conditions, and rooting media
2. **Patchy beneficial fungi**: Irregular distribution of mycorrhizal inoculum
3. **Differential fitness**: Increased growth and fitness of individual species within this mosaic

This heterogeneity-driven model explains how diverse plant communities can develop even on apparently uniform substrates.

## Fungal Succession During Plant Succession

### Linked Successions

As plant communities develop from initial colonization toward a stable climax, successions of fungi occur simultaneously — both as saprotrophs and mycorrhizae (and probably pathogens). Compelling evidence suggests these successions of plants and fungi are linked through changing environmental conditions.

### Edaphic Feedback Mechanisms

Most edaphic (soil) changes during succession relate to the resource quality of materials providing plant nutrients during decomposition. Conn and Dighton (2000) suggest a link among edaphic factors, nutrient supply, and the development of different functional groups of ectomycorrhizal fungi:

- Where leaf litters immobilize phosphorus in early decomposition stages, ectomycorrhizal fungal communities develop higher proportions of acid phosphatase-producing types
- These fungi effect mineralization of phosphorus tied up in organic resources
- Although not proven for nitrogen, feedbacks among nutrient dynamics, plant demand, and the ability to "select" appropriate mycorrhizae likely maintain optimal communities of both plants and associated fungi

This model of feedback between plant litter quality, nutrient availability, and mycorrhizal community composition remains to be fully evaluated.

## Mycorrhizal Networks and Interplant Transfer

### Carbon and Nutrient Sharing

The development of a mycelial web or network in the environment benefits the establishment of new recruits into the plant community. Simard et al. (1997a,b,c) demonstrated transfer of carbon between paper birch (Betula papyrifera) and Douglas fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii) in both partial and deep shade, which considerably supplemented photosynthetically derived carbon in seedling recipient plants. This mechanism enhances survival of tropical ectomycorrhizal tree seedlings under parent tree canopies.

### Implications for Community Assembly

If plants can "plug into" an existing mycorrhizal network, their chance of survival is enhanced. This is especially important during initial stages of colonization of bare ground. The demonstration of interplant carbon and nutrient transfer in the field is altering the understanding of plant community assembly:

- Communities were previously thought to develop solely through competition among species
- The new finding implies synergistic activities exist that need incorporation into models of plant community dynamics
- The question of how much interplant connection contributes to establishing and maintaining ecosystem stability remains open

## Differential Mycorrhizal Effects on Plant Fitness

### Variable Fungal Benefits

Many lines of evidence point to different fungi having different properties and efficiencies (Dighton et al., 1990). By chance, some plants in the community associate with a "better" mycorrhizal fungus and receive greater benefits. Whether this variability is smoothed out over the population and community (as suggested by Allison and Goldberg, 2002) or the asymmetry of beneficial effects persists is unresolved.

### Asymmetric Effects and Community Trajectories

If asymmetric mycorrhizal benefits persist, they may be sufficient to alter the trajectory of plant community development — changing the sequential pattern of dominant plant species or functional groups. This represents a fungal-mediated mechanism for community change that operates alongside classical competition-based models.

## Cost of Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

Fitter (1991) estimated the relative cost of maintaining arbuscular mycorrhizal symbioses compared to roots:

| Parameter | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Fungal biomass | 10% of root biomass (may be up to 20%) |
| Cost of fungal growth and maintenance | 1–10% of fungal biomass per day (0.1–1% of root biomass per day) |
| Cost of root maintenance | 1.5% of root biomass per day |

The cost of maintaining mycorrhizal fungus is less than root maintenance cost, suggesting the burden on the plant to sustain a mycorrhizal habit may be less than previously thought. Compared to producing additional roots, mycorrhizal associations may confer multiple benefits at relatively low cost.

## Fungal Pathogens and Community Heterogeneity

### Natural Ecosystem Effects

In natural ecosystems, less acute fungal pathogens (Alexander, 1990; Paul, 1990; Hansen and Goheen, 2000) increase heterogeneity in the gene pool of host plant species, leading to patchy dispersion of disease-susceptible and disease-resistant plants. This patchiness is maintained by:

- The rate of spread of the fungal pathogen
- The rate of recolonization of blighted areas by the host plant

### Gap Replacement and Biodiversity

Where patches of diseased plants are not recolonized by the same species, gap replacement by other species leads to increased biodiversity. Non-conspecific replacement also increases:

- Community architectural diversity
- Chemical diversity of the leaf community
- Diversity of resources available to grazing animals

Gap replacement by a different species thus leads to increases in both plant diversity and grazing faunal diversity — a positive cascading effect on community complexity.

### Exotic Pathogen Effects

The most extreme examples of fungal pathogen effects occur when exotic plants meet fungi or exotic fungi meet plants. Without time for disease resistance to evolve, dramatic and rapid changes in plant communities result:

- **Chestnut blight**: Elimination of a dominant canopy tree from eastern North American forests
- **Oak decline**: Phytophthora-induced mortality in European oak populations
- **Dutch elm disease**: Near-elimination of American elm from the landscape

## Fungal-Fungal Interactions

### Interactions Between Functional Groups

Fungi interact with each other in multiple ways:

- **Plant pathogens vs. saprotrophs**: Competition on leaf surfaces
- **Plant pathogens vs. mycorrhizae**: Interference that may be physical (resource competition) or chemical (biocide production)

### Natural Balance and Agricultural Disruption

A fine balance has likely evolved in natural ecosystems between plant pathogens and other fungal functional groups that regulate them. In agroecosystems, this balance is disrupted by:

- Monospecific crop cultivation
- Physical disturbance
- Fertilizer and chemical applications

### Emerging Biocontrol Science

The disruption of natural fungal balances in agriculture has given rise to biocontrol science, seeking to harness saprotrophic and mycorrhizal fungi to control disease-causing fungi. This extends to invertebrate pest control using nematophagous and entomopathogenic fungi. In natural ecosystems, the effects of these fungi may not be apparent until host invertebrate populations increase to high levels, at which point density-dependent regulation by fungal pathogens becomes evident.

## Climate Change Implications

Predicted climate change — warmer and wetter conditions — will increase conditions favorable for fungal pathogen propagation. This has dual implications:

1. **Increased pathogen pressure** on both plant and animal populations
2. **Exacerbated amphibian declines** in tropical regions where chytrid fungal diseases are already causing population collapses

The net effect on community structure depends on how different fungal functional groups respond to changing conditions and whether natural regulatory mechanisms can maintain community stability under altered pathogen pressures.
