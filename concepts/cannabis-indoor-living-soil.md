# Cannabis Indoor Living Soil

Living soil for indoor cannabis cultivation mimics natural soil ecosystems by establishing a complete soil food web. Instead of feeding plants directly with liquid nutrients, the grower feeds the soil microbiome, which in turn makes nutrients bioavailable to cannabis roots. This approach produces terpene-rich, flavorful flower while reducing input costs and waste.

The cannabis microbiome is now understood to be shaped by a two-tier selection model (Winston et al. 2014): cannabis recruits specific microbial communities from the broader soil environment, with strong cultivar-specificity especially in the root zone (endorhiza). This means building a diverse, healthy soil ecosystem is critical — cannabis will selectively enrich the microbes it needs. See [[cannabis-root-microbiome]] for the full research breakdown.

## The Two-Tier Microbial Selection Model

Recent research (Wagner et al.) has revealed how cannabis assembles its microbiome:

### Tier 1: Bulk/Rhizosphere Soil
Microbial communities in bulk soil and the rhizosphere (soil immediately around roots) are primarily shaped by:
- **Soil type** (N, salinity, C, water content, pH) — the strongest predictor of community composition
- **Geographic location** and climate
- **Cultivation practice** (organic vs conventional has small but measurable effect)

### Tier 2: Endorhiza (Root Interior)
Microbial communities inside cannabis roots (endorhiza) are primarily shaped by:
- **Cannabis cultivar** (genotype) — the dominant factor for root-interior communities
- Plant immune system selectively allowing certain microbes inside root tissue
- Strong cultivar-specificity: different cannabis varieties recruit different endorhiza communities

**Key insight**: This means you cannot simply "inoculate" a specific endorhiza community — cannabis selects its own internal partners. What you CAN control is building a diverse, healthy soil ecosystem (Tier 1) from which cannabis can recruit. The richer the soil microbial pool, the more choices cannabis has.

## Core Cannabis Endorhiza Community

Research has identified a core set of microbial taxa consistently found inside cannabis roots across cultivars and growing conditions:

### Bacteria
- **Pseudomonas** — plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), phosphate solubilization, disease suppression
- **Cellvibrio** — cellulose degradation, nutrient cycling
- **Oxalobacteraceae** — organic acid metabolism, nutrient cycling
- **Xanthomonadaceae** — diverse metabolic capabilities
- **Rhizobiales** — nitrogen fixation potential
- **Burkholderiaceae** — plant growth promotion, biocontrol

### Fungi/Actinomycetes
- **Actinomycetales** — filamentous bacteria that decompose complex organic matter, produce antibiotics
- **Sphingobacteriales** — organic matter degradation
- **Mortierellaceae** — early successional fungi, promote plant growth, suppress pathogens

These core taxa appear across most cannabis cultivars, suggesting they play essential functional roles. Enriching your living soil for these groups improves outcomes regardless of cultivar choice.

## Edaphic Factors — What Matters Most

Research ranked soil factors by their influence on the cannabis microbiome (Winston et al. 2014, BEST/Mantel analysis, most to least important):

1. **Nitrogen (N)** — strongest predictor of microbial community composition (r-stat: 0.465 weighted)
2. **Salinity** — high salt reduces microbial diversity (r-stat: 0.437)
3. **Carbon (C)** — organic matter content drives microbial biomass (r-stat: 0.330)
4. **Water content** — moisture levels shape aerobic vs anaerobic communities (r-stat: 0.281)
5. **pH** — moderate influence on community structure (r-stat: 0.221)
6. **Phosphorus (P) and Potassium (K)** — less influential on community composition than expected

BEST analysis found the optimal three-factor combination (N, C, Water) explains community variance at rho = 0.632. Notably, P and K had minimal effect on *which microbes thrive*, even though they are critical for plant nutrition.

**Actionable insight**: Focus on building soil organic matter (carbon) and maintaining appropriate nitrogen levels rather than chasing specific P/K ratios. The microbes will handle nutrient availability.

## Building Living Soil Indoors

### Base Mix (per cubic yard)

| Ingredient | Volume | Function |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| Sphagnum peat moss | 40% | Base, CEC, water retention |
| Aeration (pumice/perlite/rice hulls) | 30% | Drainage, air porosity |
| High-quality compost/earthworm castings | 20% | Microbial inoculation, slow-release nutrients |
| Castings | 10% | Dense microbial life, enzymes |

### Amendments (per cubic yard)

| Amendment | Amount | Function |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| Kelp meal | 1-2 cups | Trace minerals, cytokinins, microbial food |
| Crab/crustacean meal | 1-2 cups | Chitin (feeds beneficial fungi), slow N |
| Neem seed meal | 1-2 cups | Slow N, pest deterrence |
| Bone meal | 2 cups | Slow P, calcium |
| Rock phosphate | 2 cups | Slow P |
| Gypsum | 2 cups | Calcium, sulfur, soil structure |
| Oyster shell flour | 1 cup | Long-term calcium, pH buffer |
| Alfalfa meal | 1 cup | N, growth hormones (triacontanol) |
| Malted barley grain | 1/2 cup | Enzymes, fungal food |
| Inoculant (mycorrhizae, trichoderma) | per label | Fungal establishment |

### Mulch Layer (top dressing)
- Strips of untreated cardboard, rice straw, or alfalfa hay
- 1-2 inches deep over entire soil surface
- Maintains moisture, feeds microbes, creates fungal highways
- Replace or add to each cycle

## Microbial Inoculation Strategy

### For Maximum Diversity (Tier 1 enrichment)
- Use [[indigenous-microorganisms-imo]] collected from healthy local soil
- Add [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] to suppress pathogens during early stages
- Inoculate with [[effective-microorganisms-em]] or commercial consortia containing Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Trichoderma species
- Add Actinomycete-rich compost (forest humus or worm castings aged 6+ months)
- [[fermented-plant-extract-fpe]] as microbial food (sugar source for rapid colonization)

### For Cultivar-Specific Recruitment (Tier 2)
- Start seeds or clones in the same living soil they will flower in
- Avoid sterile media for seedling stage — this resets microbial recruitment
- Keep root zone undisturbed ([[no-till-farming]] approach preferred)
- The longer the soil has been active, the richer the microbial pool for cannabis to select from

## Watering Living Soil

### Compost Tea Protocol
- Aerate compost tea (ACT) every 1-2 weeks during vegetative growth
- Use high-quality [[vermicompost]] as tea base
- Add kelp, molasses, and [[fermented-plant-extract-fpe]] as microbial food
- Apply as soil drench (not foliar in flower to avoid bud rot risk)
- During flower: reduce tea frequency to monthly, use only unsulfured molasses

### KNF Inputs in Living Soil
KNF inputs (see [[knf-preparations]]) work well with living soil but require adjustment:
- Use lower dilution rates for soil drenches (1:300-500 vs standard 1:1000)
- [[indigenous-microorganisms-imo]] — IMO-2 as soil drench for rapid microbial boost
- [[fermented-plant-extract-fpe]] — FPJ as microbial food source, apply to soil
- [[fish-amino-acid-faa]] — use sparingly, living soil should provide adequate N
- [[oriental-herbal-nutrient-ohn]] — as IPM foliar spray, not soil drench
- [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] — suppresses pathogens, improves soil structure

### Water Quality
- Dechlorinate all water (let sit 24h or use air stone)
- Avoid RO water — lacks minerals that feed microbes
- Aim for 6.0-6.8 pH in runoff
- Don't overwater — living soil needs aerobic conditions (see [[soil-food-web]])

## The No-Till Living Soil Cycle

Living soil improves over multiple grows if managed correctly:

1. **After harvest**: Remove main stem and root ball (or leave root ball to decompose)
2. **Top-dress amendments**: Re-apply kelp, neem, crab meal, castings
3. **Add fresh compost**: 1-2 inches of fresh worm castings
4. **Re-mulch**: Fresh straw/cardboard layer
5. **Moisten and wait**: 2-4 weeks before next planting for microbial recovery
6. **Plant next cycle**: Seeds/clones directly into existing soil

Soil typically improves for 3-6 cycles before needing significant amendment. After 6+ cycles, consider adding fresh base mix (30% new soil, 70% recycled).

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| Slow growth, pale leaves | Low microbial activity, N deficiency | ACT with molasses, top-dress alfalfa/neem |
| Bud rot (botrytis) | High humidity, poor airflow | Improve ventilation, OHN spray at 1:1000 |
| Nutrient lockout | pH drift, salt buildup | Flush with compost tea, check pH |
| Soil compaction | Overwatering, lack of aeration | Add perlite, reduce watering frequency |
| Fungus gnats | Overwatering, organic matter on surface | Dry top inch, add nematodes, sand mulch |
| Root aphids | Infested clones, soil not hot enough | Beneficial nematodes, hydrogen peroxide drench |
| Poor terpene production | Over-fertilization, harvest timing | Reduce inputs in late flower, monitor trichomes |

## Key Metrics to Track

- Soil temperature: 65-75F (18-24C) optimal
- Soil moisture: 40-60% field capacity (squeeze test)
- Runoff pH: 6.0-6.8
- Runoff EC: 1.0-3.0 mS/cm (lower is better in living soil)
- Soil respiration: visible steam/fog when watering indicates active biology
