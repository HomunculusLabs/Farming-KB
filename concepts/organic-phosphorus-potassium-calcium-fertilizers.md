# Organic Phosphorus, Potassium, and Calcium Fertilizers

Organic gardeners rely on naturally derived mineral and biological sources for phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and calcium (Ca). Understanding the characteristics, application rates, and soil conditions required for each source is essential for effective nutrient management without disrupting the soil food web.

## Phosphorus Sources

Phosphorus is critical for root development, flowering, fruiting, and energy transfer (ATP). In organic systems, phosphorus is supplied through several natural materials, each with different release rates and soil-condition requirements.

### Animal Bone Meal (N–P–K 3–15–0)

Bone meal is produced by steam-processing and grinding animal bones. The phosphorus it contains is very readily available and persists for 1 to 4 months in the soil. However, bone meal is only effective when soil pH is below 7.0. Above this threshold, phosphorus becomes locked in unavailable forms. Gardeners must read product labels carefully—some brands list phosphorus content while others list phosphate (P₂O₅), which is the industry standard.

Bone meal can attract animals due to its odor, though this dissipates 1 to 2 weeks after application. It remains one of the most popular organic phosphorus sources for established gardens with acidic to neutral soils.

### Bat Guano (N–P–K 3–10–1)

Bat guano (the accumulated feces and urine of cave-dwelling bats) is available in two forms: a high-nitrogen version and a high-phosphorus version. The high-phosphorus guano lasts 1 to 4 months because phosphorus is rapidly immobilized in soil. Mycorrhizal fungi are the best releasers of this nutrient from guano.

An important caveat: excessive phosphorus limits mycorrhizal fungal presence, so gardeners should apply guano conservatively and inform their soil testing laboratory that they rely on mycorrhizal associations when receiving fertilization recommendations. Guano is best used when starting a new garden or when a testing laboratory specifically recommends it as a phosphorus source.

### Colloidal Rock Phosphate (N–P–K 0–2.5–0)

Also called soft rock phosphate, this material consists of clay particles surrounded by phosphate minerals. The clay component improves both cation and anion exchange capacities in the soil. Colloidal rock phosphate is extremely slow-releasing—it remains active for 3 to 5 years depending on rainfall and watering patterns. Some gardeners report that it works better in the second and third years after application, as the phosphorus gradually becomes available through mycorrhizal fungal networks or diffusion.

Placement is critical with rock phosphates. They perform best when positioned where roots will directly intercept the particles, rather than being broadcast on the soil surface where phosphorus quickly becomes unavailable.

### Crab Shell Meal (N–P–K 2–3–0)

Crab shell meal provides phosphorus along with significant calcium and trace elements. Its standout feature is high chitin content, making it excellent fungal food. Chitin also suppresses high nematode populations, offering dual benefits as both a fertilizer and a biological pest control.

## Potassium Sources

Potassium regulates water movement, activates enzymes, strengthens cell walls, and improves disease resistance. Organic potassium sources vary widely in release speed and duration.

### Greensand (N–P–K 0–0–7)

Also known as New Jersey Greensand or glauconite, this mineral formed on ancient seabeds and contains more than thirty elements, including calcium, magnesium, iron, and other micronutrients alongside potassium. Greensand requires weathering to release nutrients, making it very slow-acting. It persists in the soil for 2 to 3 years, providing a long-term potassium and micronutrient supply.

### Wood Ashes (N–P–K 0–1–3)

Wood ashes (historically called "pot ash") have been used for centuries as a potassium source. They are free for anyone with a fireplace but must come from untreated, natural wood—treated charcoals or woods may leave harmful chemical residues. Because ashes are alkaline and raise soil pH, they should not be used on soils that are already alkaline. Ashes last only 1 to 2 months in the soil, making them a short-term potassium supplement.

### Sulfate of Potash (N–P–K 0–0–2)

Often labeled as Sul-Po-Mag, this mineral salt is highly water-soluble and contains 23% sulfur, 22% potash, and 11% magnesium. Its high solubility makes it quick-acting but short-lived, lasting approximately 2 months. Some organic gardeners debate its inclusion in organic programs because it does not add bulk organic matter to soils, though it is a valuable tool when rapid potassium correction is needed.

## Calcium Sources

Calcium is essential for cell wall structure, membrane integrity, root growth, and cell division. It is relatively immobile within plants, so a continuous supply is important.

### Calcitic Limestone (N–P–K 0–0–0)

Calcitic limestone is sedimentary rock containing calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). While a solid limestone slab persists for thousands of years, when powdered and exposed to water and carbon dioxide (which form carbonic acid), it breaks down and releases calcium ions. The duration of calcium availability depends heavily on soil pH, organic matter content, and clay content.

A critical limitation: gardeners can typically raise soil pH by only about one point per growing season. The optimal application time is autumn, allowing the limestone to react over winter for the following growing season.

### Dolomitic Limestone (N–P–K 0–0–0)

Dolomitic limestone (CaMg[CO₃]₂) provides both calcium and magnesium, containing approximately 10% magnesium. However, excessive magnesium can bind soils and interfere with the uptake of other nutrients. Gardeners should only use dolomitic limestone when soil tests confirm a magnesium deficiency. As with calcitic limestone, effectiveness depends on existing pH, water availability, and CO₂ levels—another reason why regular soil testing is essential.

## Key Principles

- **Soil pH governs availability**: Phosphorus, in particular, becomes locked in alkaline soils. Calcium sources shift pH upward and must be applied carefully.
- **Mycorrhizal fungi are central**: These symbiotic fungi are the primary mechanism by which plants access phosphorus from slow-release sources like rock phosphate.
- **Placement matters**: Immobile nutrients like phosphorus and potassium should be banded in the root zone rather than broadcast on the surface.
- **Source selection depends on soil test results**: Never apply a nutrient without knowing your soil's current levels and pH.
- **Release rates vary enormously**: From liquid kelp lasting weeks to rock phosphate lasting years, understanding duration helps plan seasonal feeding programs.
