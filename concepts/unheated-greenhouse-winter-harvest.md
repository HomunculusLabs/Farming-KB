# Unheated Greenhouse Winter Harvest

The unheated greenhouse winter harvest system, developed and refined by
[[eliot-coleman]] at Four Season Farm in Harborside, Maine, enables year-round
production of fresh vegetables in Zone 5 conditions (temperatures to -20F /
-29C) without supplemental heat. The system relies on a double-layer protection
strategy inside standard plastic-covered hoop houses, using the soil itself as
the heat-storage medium. This approach produces harvestable vegetables from
October through May using no fossil fuel inputs for heating.

## The Cold House Structure

Winter-harvest cold houses are standard plastic-covered gothic-style hoop
houses, typically 30 feet wide and 96 feet long, aligned on an east-west axis.
They require only a single-layer covering of UV-resistant polyethylene plastic,
unlike heated greenhouses which benefit from two air-inflated layers to minimize
heat loss. The gothic arch design sheds snow effectively, which is critical
given the snow loads experienced in coastal Maine winters.

The hoop house frame is typically constructed from galvanized steel tubing bent
into gothic arches, with end walls of plywood or polycarbonate sheeting.
Roll-up side curtains provide ventilation during shoulder seasons. The structure
is simple enough that a small farm crew can erect one in a few days, and the
cost per square foot of growing space is a fraction of a conventional glass
greenhouse. The east-west orientation is deliberate, maximizing solar gain
during winter months when the sun traces a low arc across the southern sky.

## The Inner Layer Innovation

The key innovation of the winter-harvest system is the **inner layer of
protection**: lightweight floating row cover material (such as Agribon or Reemay)
suspended over crops on flat-topped wire wickets approximately 12 inches (30
cm) above the soil. Wickets are spaced every 4 feet (120 cm) along 30-inch-wide
(75 cm) growing beds. The wire wickets are made from 9-gauge galvanized wire
bent into an inverted U shape.

Any type of lightweight floating row cover that allows light, air, and moisture
to pass through is suitable. The fabric is laid over the wickets and the edges
are weighted down with sandbags or buried in the soil. During harvest, the
fabric can be pulled back from individual beds and replaced afterward. This
creates a twice-tempered microclimate: the outer greenhouse layer blocks wind
and traps solar heat, while the inner row cover further reduces radiant heat
loss at night and creates a still-air boundary layer around the crops.

## Temperature Performance

The double-layer system provides substantial freeze protection through a
combination of thermal mass, solar gain, and reduced heat loss. When outdoor
temperatures drop to -15F (-26C), the temperature under the inner layer drops
only to 15-18F (-10C to -8C) on average. This represents a temperature
differential of approximately 30-33 degrees Fahrenheit between outside and the
crop zone.

The cold-hardy vegetables can survive temperatures down to 10F (-12C) or lower
as long as they are not exposed to the additional stresses of outdoor conditions
like wind and desiccation. Wind chill is a major factor in winter crop damage,
and eliminating it through the double-layer system is arguably more important
than the temperature differential alone. The higher relative humidity maintained
under the double cover also provides additional protection against freezing
damage.

## The Soil as Heat Storage

The soil itself serves as the heat-storage medium. During sunny winter days,
even when air temperatures are below freezing, solar radiation penetrates the
greenhouse plastic and row cover and warms the soil. The soil retains this heat
and releases it slowly through the night. This is fundamentally different from
heated greenhouses, which use forced-air heaters or radiant floor heating.

Coleman's system requires no water storage tanks, no stone ballast, no pumps,
no buried insulation, and no thermostats. The soil does all the work naturally.
Properly irrigated soil has higher thermal mass than dry soil, making adequate
irrigation a factor in winter performance as well as summer.

## Suitable Crops

Approximately 30 cold-hardy vegetable species thrive in this system. The most
reliable winter crops include:

- **Spinach**: the star performer, producing sweet tender leaves all winter
- **Mache** (corn salad): extremely cold-tolerant, grows during the shortest
  days
- **Claytonia** (miner's lettuce): reliable producer with succulent leaves
- **Minutina**: hardy green that persists through deep winter
- **Mizuna, tatsoi, and pak choi**: Asian greens with compact growth habit
- **Carrots**: sweeten significantly in cold soil, harvested as needed
- **Leeks**: stand in the field for pulling throughout winter

A critical insight is that cold-hardy vegetables actually **improve in eating
quality** during cool and cold months. The sugars produced in response to cold
temperatures make them sweeter, more tender, and more flavorful than their
summer counterparts. This is not a compromise crop but genuinely superior
produce.

## Cold House Harvest Calendar

The harvest season follows a predictable pattern in Zone 5:

- **October to November**: peak harvest of all crops sown during the second
  spring planting window
- **December to January**: reduced but continued harvest of spinach, mache,
  claytonia, leeks, carrots, and kale
- **February to March**: harvests resume as day length increases and
  overwintered crops begin active regrowth
- **April to May**: full production transitioning into spring and summer crops

## The Second Spring

Winter crops are not leftovers from summer cultivation but part of a dedicated
year-round production cycle. Planting begins August 1, which Coleman calls the
"second spring." The timing of sowing dates is far more critical for fall
planting than for spring because day length is contracting and temperatures
dropping simultaneously. The goal is to get plants almost to maturity before
day length becomes shorter than ten hours, after which growth effectively stops.
[[winter-succession-planting]] is the scheduling discipline that makes this
possible.

## Bed Management

A core operational principle is that greenhouse beds are never left unplanted.
Within twenty-four hours after a crop is harvested, residues are removed, the
soil is re-prepared with compost, and a new crop is planted. Careful records
ensure varied crop rotation across beds. Soil fertility is maintained through
annual applications of high-quality compost, typically an inch or two worked
into the bed surface between plantings.

## Historical Origins

The concept was pioneered in the 1950s by E.M. Emmert, a professor of
horticulture at the University of Kentucky, and was largely ignored at the time.
Coleman revived and commercialized the approach starting in the 1990s. The
approach also has deep roots in the [[parisian market gardening]] tradition of
19th-century Paris.
