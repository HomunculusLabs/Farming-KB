# Excavation Tools and Drilling Methods

Beyond the primary earthmoving machines used for large-scale grading and terracing, permaculture earthworks rely on a range of specialized excavation and drilling tools for creating wells, trenches, post holes, foundations, and bore holes. These tools range from simple hand-operated augers to sophisticated hydraulic systems, and even include the judicious use of explosives for otherwise intractable ground conditions.

## Augers and Drills

Augers and drills are essentially post-hole diggers suited to most soils, available in both hand-operated and mechanical forms. Their applications extend well beyond fencing:

- **Post holes and fencing** — the primary current use
- **Tree planting** — though care must be taken not to compact surrounding soils
- **Tuber planting** — for deep-rooted crops
- **Well construction** — wells to 30–60 m (100–200 feet) are still hand-dug worldwide
- **Foundation work** — piers and footings in stable ground

### Depth Limitations

Augers have a limited lift and typically operate to about 2 m (6 feet) depth. Drills with extension tubes can operate to much greater depths. Borings are removed by liquid flushing or simply compacted and tube-lined. Purely hydraulic drills work to great depths, and bores are often combined with hydraulic nozzles.

### Large-Bore Applications

Special large-bore augers up to 11 m in diameter are used at mines, carrying extensions to create 2 m wide shafts. Very large pneumatic blowers are crane-mounted to remove loose materials. At the Coober Pedy opal mines in Australia, large-bore drills called "Caldwells" (after the inventor) are used for underground housing, excavation, shafts, and tunnels up to 1.5 m diameter.

Some of the largest vertical and horizontal earth holes — mines and qanats — were once hand-cut with primitive tools, demonstrating that even without modern machinery, determined human labor can achieve remarkable underground construction.

## Explosives in Earthworks

Explosives serve specific, valuable purposes in earthmoving where conventional mechanical methods are impractical:

- **Assisting auger holes** in hard or rocky ground
- **Creating holes in unstable ground** such as marshes and swamps
- **Loosening rock** in quarries for extraction
- **Solving otherwise intractable earth-moving problems** quickly and economically

With the advent of the swamp tractor, marsh blasting has become less common, but the basic ease and effectiveness of explosives should not be overlooked where appropriate.

### Types and Availability

Nitroglycerine-based explosives (cellulose treated with acid in glycerine, absorbed into wood dust or clay earths) as gelignite, dynamite, or plastique provide an inexpensive solution to difficult earth-moving problems. Even cheaper is the mixture of ammonium nitrate fertilizer and dieseline, known in the trade as "chickenshit" — a reference to the ancient practice of gathering nitrates from manures, toilet pit soils, or extracting them from guano by washing and evaporation.

### Safety Considerations

A "powder monkey" certificate (licensed explosives handler qualification) is required to legally set explosives in most jurisdictions. Many older reference books contain recipes for cheap explosives, and common household materials (custard powder, flour, face powder, ether fumes, domestic gases) can produce dangerous explosions — making formal training and licensing essential for safety.

## Hydraulic Jets

The quiet but powerful action of water jets assists earthworks in several ways:

### Water Spear Drilling

A simple jet nozzle fixed inside a pipe and connected to a garden tap can drill a water bore (water spear) in sands, gravels, or deep soils within minutes or hours. This is one of the simplest methods of accessing shallow groundwater.

### Jet Piling

An old hose-pipe fastened to a sharpened pile or post and connected to a water supply will sink jetty pilings into silt or sand very quickly. This technique eliminates the need for heavy driving equipment in soft substrates.

### Jet Pumps

Commercially available jet pumps remove silt, sediment, mulch, and even large gravel from dams, depositing these materials behind retaining walls as rich terrace soil while water flows back to the dam. This converts a maintenance operation (desilting) into a constructive one (creating growing substrate).

### Sediment Washing

Jets fired at loose sediment wash down gravels, sands, and soils for mining operations, terrace construction, or removing landslips from roads and fields. Precise control of jet action is achievable with appropriate nozzle design and pressure regulation.

### Deep Drilling Guidance

Hydraulic nozzles, drills, and jet pumps are manufactured by industry to steer deep drills in boreholes to specific underground locations when tapping oil, thermal energy, or venting mines and caves. A jet combined with a cutting drill cools the cutting area and removes waste material in a single operation.

### Safety Warning

Inadvertent jet drilling has a powerful pulling action. Jet pumps with their motors, rafts, or platforms can be dragged below water or across the ground if left unattended. A garden hose will even bury itself in sandy soils and become lost if left running uncontrolled.

## Pneumatic Systems

Powerful air jets serve a parallel role to hydraulic jets, removing gravels from mines and performing similar excavation tasks. A whole series of turbines and powerful pistons operate from pressure applied by water, air, or oil — the science of applied hydraulics or pneumatics. These systems provide the force for compacting, cutting, lifting, and moving earth and materials in situations where direct mechanical contact is impractical.

## Finishing Tools

Rakes and rollers are the essential tools for finishing excavated earth:

- **Rakes** — fine-level surfaces to prepare for planting or construction
- **Rollers** — compact earth where traffic or water retention is intended

### Compaction Principles

Compaction is only necessary where traffic or water retention is intended. Where required, it may need to be accompanied by water sprinkling in dry conditions. Critical rules include:

- Compact only 15–30 cm (6–12 inches) of soil thoroughly at a time
- Even very heavy machines can seldom compact depths greater than 38 cm (15 inches)
- Impact or hand compaction is effective for less than half that depth
- Strict adherence to careful layer-by-layer compaction is essential for dams and house foundations

Modern compacting machines often vibrate, using a pneumatic or mechanical eccentric weight to impact and shake soil as it is compacted. This vibration significantly improves compaction density at shallower depths compared to static weight alone.
