## Contrast with Scientific Rice Farming

Modern rice production involves repeated pesticide applications, flooding
regimens that rot roots, heavy nitrogen fertilization, transplanting from
seedbeds, and extensive mechanization. Each intervention creates conditions
requiring further intervention: tilling hardens soil, necessitating more
tilling; fertilizers weaken plants, requiring pesticides; pesticides kill
beneficial insects, triggering pest outbreaks. The natural farming approach
breaks this cycle entirely by refusing the first intervention.

Fukuoka noted that toxic chemicals were applied at least eight times per year
on Japanese rice fields, yet the amount of insect damage remained similar to
fields where no pesticides were used. The first pesticide application would
eliminate tens of thousands of young spiders per square yard and cause
fireflies to vanish, while the second application killed off chalcid wasps and
dragonfly larvae. The destruction cascaded through the ecosystem while the
original pest problem persisted.

## Philosophical Foundation

This cultivation method is rooted in the Buddhist concept of Mu, or
nothingness. Rather than adding techniques, Fukuoka progressively eliminated
unnecessary practices. After thirty years of simplification, he reduced all
farming labor to essentially sowing seed and spreading straw. The method
demonstrates that human effort is unnecessary because nature, not man, grows
the rice and wheat. Farmers do not produce food; only nature has the power to
create something from nothing. The farmer's role is to assist nature by
working within its processes rather than imposing human designs upon them.
