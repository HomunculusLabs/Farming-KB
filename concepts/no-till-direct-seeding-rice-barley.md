# No-Till Direct-Seeded Rice/Barley Succession in Clover Cover

No-till direct-seeded rice and barley (or wheat) succession grown in a living
cover of clover represents the centerpiece of Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming
method. This system eliminates plowing, chemical fertilizers, pesticides,
compost preparation, and weeding while consistently producing above-average
yields. Fukuoka described it as a "methodless method" that lets nature grow the
crops rather than relying on human knowledge and labor.

## The Five Principles Behind the Method

Fukuoka's natural farming rests on five foundational principles that this
cultivation system embodies simultaneously:

1. **No tillage**: The soil is never plowed or turned. Microorganisms and small
   animals serve as nature's tillers. Tilling kills soil life, hardens the
   earth, and creates the conditions that make a plow seem necessary in the
   first place, generating a self-perpetuating cycle of intervention.

2. **No fertilizer**: Chemical fertilizers and prepared compost are not applied.
   Soil enriches itself year after year through the decomposition of straw
   mulch, clover, and green manure plants. Rice straw scattered over the field
   provides organic matter half a year before the rice is sown, activating
   latent soil fertility.

3. **No pesticides**: No chemicals are sprayed for insect or disease control.
   When leafhopper outbreaks occur, spider populations rise to meet them.
   Dragonflies, frogs, and loaches maintain natural balance in the paddy
   ecosystem. Healthy rice plants grown in balanced soil have no need for
   chemical protection.

4. **No weeding**: Weeds are not eradicated. Clover serves as a living ground
   cover that suppresses unwanted weeds through competition rather than
   elimination. When weeds do appear, they are recognized as part of the
   ecosystem performing valuable functions in soil biology and nutrient cycling.

5. **No pruning**: Fruit trees and other perennials are left to grow in their
   natural form rather than being shaped by human intervention.

## Annual Cycle of Operations

The cultivation cycle begins in autumn when clover and winter grain are
broadcast over the ripening heads of rice before the fall harvest. After the
rice is harvested, it is left to dry for three days, then threshed. The entire
rice straw is scattered uncut over the field. If chicken droppings are
available, they are scattered over the straw. Next, clay pellets containing
rice seed are prepared and scattered over the straw before the New Year.

Through winter, the clover and grain grow beneath the straw mulch. There is
nothing left to do until the winter grain harvest. The labor of one or two
people suffices for a quarter-acre.

In late May, during the winter grain harvest, the clover grows luxuriantly at
ground level while small rice shoots emerge from the clay pellets. After the
winter grain is harvested, dried, and threshed, its straw too is scattered
uncut over the entire field. The field is flooded for four to five days to
weaken the clover and give rice shoots a chance to break through. From June
through July, the field is left unirrigated. In August, water is run through
the drainage ditches once every week or ten days.

## The Role of Clover

Clover is the living engine of this system. As a leguminous ground cover, it
fixes atmospheric nitrogen and makes it available to the grain crops. Its dense
growth suppresses weeds through light competition rather than eradication. The
clover mat protects the soil surface from erosion and desiccation while
maintaining soil structure and moisture.

During the flooding period, the clover weakens sufficiently to allow rice
shoots to establish dominance, but it does not die completely. The clover's
nitrogen contribution, combined with the nutrient release from decomposing
straw, provides all the fertility the rice crop requires. This symbiotic
relationship between clover and grain eliminates the need for any external
fertilizer inputs.

## Clay Pellet Seed Protection

Fukuoka's innovation of encasing seeds in clay pellets serves multiple purposes.
The clay coating protects seeds from being eaten by birds and mice. It delays
germination until conditions are optimal, with rain softening the clay and
triggering sprouting only when moisture and temperature are suitable. The
pellets can be broadcast casually over the field without precise placement,
and the seeds within will germinate reliably.

The pellet mixture incorporates soil, clay, and beneficial microorganisms,
giving each seed a starter inoculum of soil life. This technique is not limited
to rice cultivation and was later adapted by Fukuoka for his desert
revegetation work, where the protective qualities of clay pellets become even
more critical.

## Yields and Energy Efficiency

Fukuoka consistently harvested approximately 22 bushels (1,300 pounds) of
winter grain and 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre using this method, with a
goal of reaching 33 bushels per quarter-acre. His field yielded roughly 250
heads of grain per square yard with an average of 200 grains per head.

More significantly, natural farming produces 130 pounds of rice, or 200,000
kilocalories of energy, per man-day of labor without any outside material
inputs. This is approximately 100 times the daily caloric intake of a farmer
on a natural diet. By comparison, traditional farming with draft animals
required ten times the energy expenditure, small-scale mechanization doubled
it again, and large-scale mechanization doubled it yet again, producing the
energy-intensive methods of today.

## Contrast with Scientific Rice Farming

Modern rice production involves repeated pesticide applications, flooding
regimens that rot roots, heavy nitrogen fertilization, transplanting from
seedbeds, and extensive mechanization. Each intervention creates conditions
requiring further intervention: tilling hardens soil, necessitating more
tilling; fertilizers weaken plants, requiring pesticides; pesticides kill
beneficial insects, triggering pest outbreaks. The natural farming approach
breaks this cycle entirely by refusing the first intervention.

Fukuoka noted that toxic chemicals were applied at least eight times per year
on Japanese rice fields, yet the amount of insect damage remained similar to
fields where no pesticides were used. The first pesticide application would
eliminate tens of thousands of young spiders per square yard and cause
fireflies to vanish, while the second application killed off chalcid wasps and
dragonfly larvae. The destruction cascaded through the ecosystem while the
original pest problem persisted.

## Philosophical Foundation

This cultivation method is rooted in the Buddhist concept of Mu, or
nothingness. Rather than adding techniques, Fukuoka progressively eliminated
unnecessary practices. After thirty years of simplification, he reduced all
farming labor to essentially sowing seed and spreading straw. The method
demonstrates that human effort is unnecessary because nature, not man, grows
the rice and wheat. Farmers do not produce food; only nature has the power to
create something from nothing. The farmer's role is to assist nature by
working within its processes rather than imposing human designs upon them.
