## Wild Rice and Taro Polycultures

## Overview

Traditional water polycultures represent some of the most sustainable and productive food systems ever devised by human cultures. Two particularly important examples are wild rice (*[[zizania-lacustris]]*) culture in cold climates of North America and taro (*[[colocasia-esculenta]]*) terraces in tropical and subtropical Pacific regions. Both systems demonstrate how integrated land-[[water-management]] can produce staple foods while supporting diverse associated species.

## Wild Rice Culture (*Zizania lacustris*)

### Species Description

Wild rice is a tall annual grass reaching up to 2.5 meters in height, with several perennial species within the same genus. It has been gathered as a grain for centuries by Amerindian tribes across a vast range stretching from Minnesota in the north to Florida in the south. Species or seed collected for culture trials should be gathered from selected plants at the climatic and water provenance suited to the culture site.

### Production and Economics

Approximately 3 million kg of wild rice are produced annually, with about 50% still wild-gathered in traditional fashion by Amerindians who beat the grains into canoes. The remainder is produced in extensive cultivated fields of up to 100 hectares. Wild rice has been brought into cultivation only within the last 30 years, making it one of the most recent grains to be developed, but one peculiarly suited to cold swamps and shallows with intense summer heat.

Commercial yields are relatively low at only 50-100 kg/ha, but prices are high—at $10/kg in 1984—so that fields of 100 hectares could yield cash returns of up to $100,000 annually from rice alone. The original culture was developed for duck flyway wildlife seed, and wild ducks harvest perhaps 70% of the total production, as the seed ripens slowly over 3-4 weeks. Mechanical harvesting can only gather seed that is ripe at the moment of harvest.

### Cultivation Practices

Seed is saved from selected plants and held over winter in bags under water in icy lakes, or in water in refrigerators at 40°F. It is sown in spring in fields flooded to about 16-20 cm deep and harvested from mid-autumn. The fields are dried out for 2-3 weeks before machine harvesting and must be rested or sown to another crop to clean the ground if a new cultivar is to be introduced, as self-seeding otherwise perpetuates the first-sown variety.

Wildfowl, in dabbling the fallen seed in fields, help thin out seed that will germinate after winter stratification. Plants crowd if not so thinned. Ideal spacing is 20 × 35 cm, and for small plots with selected heads, yield can reach 200-500 g/head. Home plots gathered over 3-4 weeks are in fact very productive, and only 200-800 plants should supply a family if well cared for.

### Associated Polyculture

In the large fields and deep margin ditches of Minnesota, a self-generated polyculture of crayfish (*Homarus*), mink, beaver, and thousands of waterfowl has evolved alongside the wild rice culture. Coyote also range the bunds, and packrats store much of the seed. The crayfish live in ditches and browse 5-10 m into the crop, causing some damage, but as they are themselves a potential crop or food for mink, this is tolerated.

Duck potato (*Sagittaria*) can become a weed of the system but is also waterfowl food and prefers somewhat deeper water than the rice plant. There is obvious potential for coldwater fish-rearing in the canals and ditches of the system, with species such as trout and bullheads.

### The Packrat Harvesting Strategy

An intriguing potential is the propensity of packrats to carry and store large quantities of sound, cleaned grain into artificial shelters (like engine manifolds), insulated and plugged by Typha (cumbungi, cattail) seed silk. Narrow fields and a set of such artificial storage sites may well be the best way to harvest. Breeding packrats could be supplied with alternative food for the winter or allowed to keep 15% of their stores. They may well be the most efficient, as well as the cleanest, harvesters for the crop, and can scarcely do worse than the 50-100 kg (less than 10%) gathered by machines costing $300,000 or more.

A strategy used by Indians to save grain from predation and shattering is to bundle and tie 10-20 heads together, and to untie them periodically for threshing into canoes.

### Supplementary Products

Duck fattening, crayfish harvest, mussel meats, fish production, and vine crops of hops or silverberries (*[[actinidia-arguta]]*) are obvious supplementary products. Together with restricted fur production, some special forestry, and recreational fishing or tour potential, these form a richly productive system. A small terrace or pond of wild rice should be the aim of every home gardener who can obtain seed and who enjoys a nutritious grain.

## Taro Culture (*Colocasia esculenta*)

### Species Description

Taro is an herbaceous perennial reaching 1 meter in height, with large arrow-like leaves and a swollen stem base or tuber that can reach 10 kg but is normally marketed at about 1 kg after an 8-15 month growing period. There is no "off season" in the subtropics. There were approximately 1,100 Hawaiian varieties, and perhaps 100 or so are still preserved. It is a staple food in Hawaii and parts of Polynesia and Southeast Asia, where rice or breadfruit are also staples.

### Propagation and Cultivation

Taro is propagated by small side tubers or more commonly by a cutting based on a crown disc about 1 cm thick and the lower 18 cm of stalk from the old tuber at harvest. Cuttings have the leaves removed by a slant cut just above the first leaf node. There are special varieties for boiling, baking, ferment to poi, or cooking as green leaf spinach (luau taro). All taro must be cooked due to the stinging crystals of oxalic acid in the leaves and tubers.

### Traditional Hawaiian Integration

There has always been a fish culture tradition with taro, of local prawns, shellfish, and freshwater fish. Tilapia and introduced prawns have merely extended the potential, as have crayfish. What is missing is a good variety of edible freshwater mussels to fix phosphates in the terrace mud. In older times, nutrient supply came from forest leaves (chiefly *Aleurites* or *Hibiscus*). The stems of kukui trees (*Aleurites*) were stood to rot in the upper terraces where they produced a local edible ear fungus. About 80% of the bulk of a log was converted to fungus in this way, and the remainder broken up as water mulch.

### Integrated Polyculture Design

Accessory margin crops include ti (*Cordyline*)—the Polynesian "wax paper" and wrapper for baked foods—papaya, banana, coconut, and sugar cane. As taro appreciates partial shade, there is potential for marginal or wide-spaced overhead kiwifruit, passionfruit, pole bean, or cucurbit crop. Bunds planted to *Dolichos hosei* or *Phyla nodosa* would be useful nitrogenous mulch sources, remaining short and trimmed at the water level by tilapia or grass carp.

Pigs and ducks are traditional livestock. Construction of their pens over feeder canals or the upper terraces would add substantially to nutrient supply, although ducks need confinement to a few ponds as they too appreciate the edible Chinese snails, which are a high-priced local delicacy.

### Dryland and Irrigated Taro

Taro is also grown on dryland areas under irrigation, responding very well to thick mulch and green crop. In these situations, intercrop of *Brassica*, melons, ginger, or lettuce is viable. Beds are normally 1.3 m wide at 30 beds/ha, with taro spaced at 40 × 60 cm in diamond pattern. There are 55,500 bulbs/ha, averaging about 1 kg each, for a yield of approximately 55 t/ha.

Spacing in wet terrace is similar, but the "intercrop" there is more profitably prawns, although kangkong and watercress are spot-planted in many taro terraces for greens. Both are planted as low mound crops in special shallow terrace or canal systems with faster flow than taro systems.

### Taro in Mixed Orchards

Taro is often grown as a mulched patch or groundcover below fruit trees in higher rainfall areas, or as a subsidiary crop in banana, guava, avocado, and macadamia nut orchards. Its large leaves provide effective weed suppression and the constant leaf fall contributes to mulch. The ideal taro polyculture environment described by Mollison includes taro, comfrey, papaya, guava, and banana, with chilis, ginger, peppers, ducks, pigs, and fish—supplemented by visits to the sea for limu (seaweed) and crab, and cooked in a traditional earth oven (imu).

### Eel Integration

In climates such as the western Pacific (including New Zealand), eel rearing would combine very well with taro culture. At maturity, taro can be kept flooded at mid-thigh depth, or with 0.5 m of free water above the softer muds of the terrace, where prawns and grass carp can be stocked. These species coexist very well in the flooded taro environment, and eels can utilize the deeper channels as both habitat and foraging grounds.

## Traditional Water Cultures as Models

The wet terraces of the Ifugao people of the Philippines have been in use for at least 3,000 years. The flood cultures of the Nile are probably 17,000 years old. The chinampa system of Mexico and the flood-plain, lakeside, and river cultures of Asia all demonstrate the sustainability of integrated water-based food production over millennia. These systems share common characteristics:

- **Community self-governance** arising from shared irrigation and canal systems
- **No external authority** recognized by the creators and maintainers
- **Intensive labor** concentrated in short periods (the Ifugao family of 5 spends only 400 person-days/year, or 80 days per able individual)
- **Polycultural diversity** preventing disease buildup and ensuring food security
- **Continuous soil building** rather than soil mining

The Ifugao terraces have only 2 cm of soil, and half the mass of the bunds is in stone. Canal and ditch maintenance necessitates annual earth and stone repairs. The equivalent value of this work is one day of rice for one hour of work—a remarkably efficient return on labor investment.

## Aquatic Plant Potential

Beyond the major cultivated species, there is an obvious potential for wetland forestry, craft product, and specialised forage crop assemblies for species such as bees, ducks, waterfowl, and fish. Pigs utilize seasonal swamps and water margins for a variety of fodders. Cattle enter lagoons and shallow waters to browse, while species like the Soay sheep, turtles, and the Galapagos iguana browse large seaweeds and seagrasses. In samphire pastures, geese, swan, and fish species browse at varying tide levels.

Only some 20–30 species of water plants are in constant culture or have been subject to varietal selection. Many species are still wild-gathered or derived from unselected wild stock, representing an enormous opportunity for domestication and improvement. There is an obvious role for seed, vegetative, and general aquatic nurseries and suppliers, which are undeveloped in most countries.

### Plant Assemblies for Specific Purposes

Anyone interested in specific plant assemblies can evolve a fairly complex array of forbs, shrubs, vines, and trees to suit specific or specialised purposes. Rather than enumerate all the cultured species, Mollison recommends dealing with a few selected polycultures of plants, briefly treating the marginal plantings, and suggesting plant assemblies for specific sites. The approach is to select one cold-area system and one subtropic system to illustrate the aquatic potential—wild rice for cool areas and taro for the tropics—with rice (*Oryza*) itself grown from temperate to tropical areas and very well described by Fukuoka.

### Key Species Companions

For wild rice systems, important companion species include:

- **Crayfish** (*Homarus*): Browse into the crop from ditches, serve as harvestable crop themselves
- **Duck potato** (*Sagittaria*): Can become a weed but also serves as waterfowl food; prefers deeper water
- **Typha** (cattail/cumbungi): Seed silk used by packrats for insulation; shoots and pollen are edible
- **Mussels**: Potential for phosphate fixation in the system mud
- **Mink and beaver**: Furbearers that coexist with the rice culture
- **Waterfowl**: Primary harvesters (consuming ~70% of seed) and seed-thinners

For taro systems, key companions include:

- **Tilapia**: Weed control in canals and nutrient cycling
- **Chinese edible snails**: High-priced local delicacy in Hawaii, controlled by duck predation
- **Prawns**: Primary intercrop in wet terraces, profitable addition
- **Kangkong** and **watercress**: Spot-planted greens in many taro terraces
- **Kukui** (*Aleurites*): Logs stood in upper terraces to produce edible ear fungus (80% biomass conversion)
- **Ti** (*Cordyline*): Polynesian "wax paper" for wrapping baked foods

## Design Implications

These traditional polyculture systems demonstrate several key principles for permaculture designers:

- **Multi-species integration** maximizes total yields across all trophic levels
- **Animal harvesters** (ducks, packrats, wildfowl) can replace expensive mechanical systems
- **Nutrient cycling** through fish, manures, and decomposing vegetation sustains fertility indefinitely
- **Edge effects** between land and water zones are particularly productive
- **Traditional knowledge** accumulated over millennia provides proven design templates
- **Polyculture diversity** reduces disease risk compared to monoculture approaches
- **Temporal stacking** allows the same space to produce different yields at different times
- **Low technology solutions** (bundling rice heads, [[packrat-harvesting]], ear fungus from logs) can outperform expensive mechanical systems
- **Community organization** is essential for managing complex integrated systems effectively
- **Species selection for site**: Always match species to climatic and water provenance suited to the culture site
- **Value-added processing**: Fermentation, drying, and other preservation techniques increase total economic yield
- **Wildlife integration**: Rather than excluding wildlife, design systems where wildlife contributes to productivity
