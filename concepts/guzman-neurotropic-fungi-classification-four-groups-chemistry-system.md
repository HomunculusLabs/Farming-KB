# Neurotropic Fungi Classification: Four Groups System

## Overview

Guzmán, Allen & Gartz (2000) organized the world's known neurotropic (psychoactive) fungi into four distinct groups based on their chemistry [[ayahuasca-pharmacology-and-cultural-context]]. This classification system, presented in their landmark paper "A Worldwide Geographical Distribution of the Neurotropic Fungi," provides a systematic framework for understanding the chemical and cultural diversity of mushrooms that affect the human nervous system. The four groups encompass over 250 reported species across the Ascomycotina and Basidiomycota, with the authors considering 216 species as confirmed or probable neurotropic fungi.

## Group 1: Indole Alkaloid-Containing Fungi

### Definition
Fungi containing indolic substances, primarily psilocybin, psilocin, baeocystin, and norbaeocystin. This is by far the largest group and includes the majority of confirmed neurotropic species.

### Key Characteristics
- Many species exhibit "bluing" (a distinctive blue or blue-black bruising reaction) when damaged, caused by the oxidative degradation of psilocin
- The bluing reaction is considered a reliable field indicator of neurotropic potential, though not all bluing species have been confirmed to contain indole alkaloids
- Psilocybe represents the majority of species in this group, with 116 species listed in Table I

### Genera Included
**Primary genera** (bluing species with confirmed indole alkaloids):
- **Psilocybe** — The dominant genus, with species in sections Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae, Cubensis, Mexicanae, Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae, and Zapotecorum
- **Conocybe** — Several species confirmed to contain psilocybin
- **Copelandia** — Twelve species, all tropical and subtropical, containing psilocybin and related compounds

**Secondary genera** (species containing or probably containing indole alkaloids, typically non-bluing):
- **Agrocybe** — A. farinacea confirmed from Japan
- **Galerina** — Some species contain or are suspected to contain indole compounds
- **Gerronema** — G. fibula, uncertain status
- **Gymnopilus** — Multiple species confirmed, including G. spectabilis and G. aeruginosus
- **Inocybe** — Several species, including I. corydalina and I. aeruginascens
- **Mycena** — Some species reported
- **Panaeolina** — Four species, including the cosmopolitan P. foenisecii
- **Panaeolus** — Thirteen species confirmed or suspected
- **Pluteus** — Several species, including P. salicinus and P. cyanopus

### Excluded Species
Guzmán, Allen & Gartz explicitly excluded several species that had been erroneously reported as hallucinogenic:
- **Psilocybe atrobrunnea** and **P. coprophila** — Do not contain indole alkaloids
- **Psilocybe pseudobullacea** — Confusedly reported; excluded from this group
- **Psathyrella sepulchralis** — Wrongly confused with Psilocybe zapotecorum; does not contain psilocybin (Ott & Guzmán, 1976)
- **Panaeolus antillarum** — Often erroneously identified as Copelandia by users; this edible species does not turn blue and is widely distributed in the tropics

## Group 2: Ibotenic Acid-Containing Fungi

### Definition
Fungi containing ibotenic acid and/or muscimol as their primary psychoactive compounds. These compounds act as GABA receptor agonists, producing a distinctly different pharmacological profile from the [[serotonin-receptor-interference-psychedelic-mechanism]] agonism of the indole alkaloids.

### Species Included
- **Amanita muscaria** — The "fly agaric," the most widely known species in this group. Contains ibotenic acid, muscimol, and muscarine (though muscarine is not the primary psychoactive compound). Its distinctive red cap with white spots makes it one of the most recognizable mushrooms in the world.
- **Amanita pantherina** — The "panther cap," contains similar compounds to A. muscaria but is generally considered more toxic.
- **Amanita regalis** — The "royal fly agaric," a closely related species with confirmed psychoactive properties.

### Ecological and Chemical Notes
These species are mycorrhizal, forming symbiotic associations with tree roots, primarily with Pinus and Betula (birch) in the Northern Hemisphere. They are found in temperate and boreal forests, and also in pine plantations [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] (e.g., Brazil, Colombia, Tanzania, Australia, New Zealand).

The pharmacology differs fundamentally from Group 1: ibotenic acid is a glutamate receptor agonist, while its decarboxylation product muscimol is a GABA-A receptor agonist. Effects include sedation, dissociation, visual distortions, and in some cases nausea and confusion. Traditional Siberian use involved the practice of "urine drinking," as the active compounds are excreted largely unchanged and the urine contains a higher proportion of muscimol (the more desirable compound) relative to ibotenic acid (which causes nausea).

## Group 3: Ergot Alkaloid-Containing Fungi

### Definition
Fungi containing ergot alkaloids, primarily produced by species of Claviceps and possibly Cordyceps. Ergot alkaloids include compounds related to lysergic acid, the structural basis of LSD.

### Claviceps Species
- **[[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]]** — The classic ergot fungus, parasitizing grasses and cereals worldwide. Contains ergotamine, ergocristine, and related alkaloids. Historically responsible for ergotism (St. Anthony's Fire) outbreaks when infested grain was consumed.
- **Claviceps nigricans** — Less commonly reported
- **[[claviceps-paspali]]** — Parasitizes Paspalum grasses, distributed across Africa, Asia, and Australasia
- **Claviceps rolfesii** — Reported from Africa
- **Claviceps tripsicii** — Parasitizes Tripsacum grasses

### Cordyceps Species (Probable)
- **Cordyceps capitata** — Parasitizes Elaphomyces (deer truffles)
- **Cordyceps ophioglossoides** — Another Elaphomyces parasite

The neurotropic status of the Cordyceps species is classified as "probable" rather than confirmed. These species have been used traditionally in East Asian medicine and were included in sacred fungi traditions, but reliable chemical studies confirming the presence of ergot-like alkaloids are limited.

## Group 4: Sacred Fungi Without Reliable Chemical Studies

### Definition
Fungi used as sacred or ceremonial by indigenous peoples around the world, but for which no reliable chemical analyses have confirmed the presence of known psychoactive compounds. This is the most controversial and uncertain group.

### Included Taxa

**Boletus (6 species):**
- B. flammeus, B. kumaeus, B. manicus, B. nigerrimus, B. nigerimus, B. nigroviolaceus
- All reported from New Guinea, used by indigenous highland communities

**Heimiella (2 species):**
- H. anguiformis, H. retispora
- Both from New Guinea

**Russula (6 species):**
- R. agglutina, R. kirinea, R. maenadum, R. nondorbingi, R. pseudomaenadum, R. wahgiensis
- All from New Guinea; Ott (1993) stated there is no scientific evidence that any are neurotropic

**Gasteromycetes (6 species in Lycoperdales and Phallales):**
- Puffballs and stinkhorns used ceremonially in various cultures
- These include species of the orders Lycoperdales (puffballs) and Phallales (stinkhorns)
- Chemical studies confirming psychoactive compounds are absent

### The Uncertainty Problem

Group 4 represents the most problematic aspect of the classification system. The inclusion of these species is based entirely on ethnographic reports rather than chemical evidence. Jonathan Ott (1993) was particularly critical, explicitly denying neurotropic status for several species (B. flammeus, B. nigroviolaceus, H. anguiformis) and noting the absence of scientific evidence for the Russula species.

Possible explanations for the reported effects include:
1. **Genuine novel chemistry**: These species may contain as-yet-unidentified psychoactive compounds
2. **Placebo/nocebo effects**: Cultural expectations may produce subjective psychoactive experiences
3. **Species confusion**: Indigenous users may be combining these mushrooms with confirmed psychoactive species
4. **Contamination**: Specimens may be contaminated with ergot or other fungi growing nearby
5. **Confounding factors**: Effects may result from secondary compounds (e.g., gastrointestinal irritants) rather than true psychoactive compounds

## Taxonomic Framework and Validation Criteria

Guzmán, Allen & Gartz (2000) established specific criteria for including species as neurotropic, following Singer & Smith (1958), Guzmán (1983), and Singer (1986):
1. Species demonstrating the bluing reaction, OR
2. Species confirmed to contain indolic substances, OR
3. Species for which available evidence suggests the presence of such substances

This framework allowed for a degree of flexibility, including species based on suggestive (but not conclusive) evidence while explicitly flagging uncertain cases. The exclusion of several well-known misidentified species (Psilocybe atrobrunnea, P. coprophila, Psathyrella sepulchralis) demonstrates the authors' commitment to taxonomic rigor.
