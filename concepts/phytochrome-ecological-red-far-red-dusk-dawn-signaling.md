Phytochrome serves as the primary photoreceptor mediating plant responses to red and
far-red light. Its [[bloomfield-fungal-fruiting-body-morphology-ecological-function]] is elegantly attuned to the natural light
transitions that occur at dawn and dusk, functioning as a biological switch that
allows plants to measure the length of continuous darkness and regulate critical
developmental processes including flowering, seed germination, [[phototropism-and-shade-avoidance]].

## The Red-Far-Reversible Switch

Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms. Red light (approximately 660 nm)
converts inactive Pr (P-red) into the active Pfr (P-far-red) form. Far-red light
(approximately 730 nm, wavelengths slightly longer than bright red and most visible
at dusk) reverses this conversion, returning Pfr back to the inactive Pr state. This
reversibility means that phytochrome functions as a light-activated molecular switch
that can be toggled on and off by alternating wavelengths.

The discovery that far-red light could cancel the effect of red light on plants was
made by Harry Borthwick and colleagues at the US Department of Agriculture in the
early 1950s. If irises (long-day plants) are given a shot of red light in the middle
of the night, they produce flowers. But if far-red light is shone on them immediately
after the red pulse, the effect is nullified as if they never saw the red light at
all. A subsequent red pulse reactivates the [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]], and another far-red
pulse cancels it again. This alternating pattern can be repeated indefinitely, with
only a few seconds of either color being sufficient to toggle the state.

## Ecological Logic of Dusk and Dawn

In nature, this toggle mechanism has clear [[lichen-biology-guide]]. The last light
any plant perceives at the end of the day is dominated by far-red wavelengths, which
tells the plant it should "turn off" its active phytochrome state. In the morning,
the incoming sunlight is rich in red light, which activates phytochrome and signals
the plant to "wake up." By measuring the ratio of Pfr to total phytochrome, a plant
effectively tracks how long ago it last saw red light and adjusts its growth and
developmental programs accordingly.

The red-to-far-red ratio (R:FR) is a critical environmental signal. Direct sunlight
has a relatively high R:FR ratio, while light filtered through or reflected from
green vegetation has a much lower ratio because chlorophyll absorbs red light and
reflects far-red light. This makes phytochrome a sensitive detector of neighboring
vegetation, enabling shade avoidance responses before direct competition for light
becomes severe.

## Philosophical Implications: The Plant Remembers the Last Color It Saw

On a more philosophical level, the red-far-red reversibility demonstrates that
plants possess a form of short-term memory. The phytochrome system retains the state
imposed by the last light signal it received, and this state persists in darkness.
The plant does not simply respond to light in a reflexive manner; it integrates
information about light quality over time, maintaining a record of the most recent
photonic experience. This is one of the clearest demonstrations that the boundary
between plant "perception" and animal "perception" is more a matter of degree than
of kind.

## Applications in Agriculture and Horticulture

Understanding phytochrome's ecological role has direct practical applications.
Growers can manipulate flowering by controlling the red-far-red environment.
Chrysanthemum farmers, for example, use [[chrysanthemum-greenhouse-photoperiod-manipulation]] to delay flowering
until just before Mother's Day. During autumn and winter, greenhouse lights are
turned on for a few minutes each night to interrupt the long dark period, keeping
the plants vegetative. Two weeks before the desired harvest date, the night
interruption is stopped, and all plants flower simultaneously.

The same principle applies to [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-cannabis-cultivation]], where photoperiod control is
essential for transitioning between vegetative growth and flowering. Indoor growers
use timers to maintain specific dark periods (typically 12 hours) to trigger and
sustain flowering in photoperiod-sensitive cultivars. The red-far-red dynamics of
the lighting system, including the choice of LED spectra, can influence the speed
and quality of this transition.

## Relationship to Other Photoreceptor Systems

Phytochrome works alongside other photoreceptor systems in plants. Blue light
photoreceptors (cryptochromes and phototropins) mediate phototropism (directional
bending toward light) and other blue-light-dependent responses. While phytochrome
measures the length of darkness and regulates flowering, blue light receptors tell
the plant which direction light is coming from. Together, these systems give plants
a surprisingly sophisticated ability to monitor their light environment across
multiple dimensions: direction, quality (color), quantity (intensity), and duration.

## Molecular Basis

Warren L. Butler and colleagues demonstrated by the early 1960s that a single
photoreceptor molecule was responsible for both the red and far-red effects. They
named this receptor "phytochrome," meaning "plant color." The protein consists of
an apoprotein covalently bound to a chromophore (a light-absorbing molecule). The
chromophore undergoes a conformational change upon absorbing either red or far-red
light, switching the protein between its two stable states. This molecular switch
is one of the most studied signal transduction mechanisms in plant biology.

Phytochrome exists in multiple gene families (phyA through phyE in Arabidopsis),
with each isoform playing distinct roles. PhyA is the primary photoreceptor for
continuous far-red light and is responsible for de-etiolation responses in
germinating seedlings. PhyB is the dominant phytochrome for red/far-red reversible
responses in mature plants and mediates shade avoidance and photoperiodic flowering.
The diversification of phytochrome gene families represents an evolutionary
elaboration that allows plants to fine-tune their responses across different
ecological contexts.

## The Shade Avoidance Syndrome

One of the most dramatic phytochrome-mediated responses is the shade avoidance
syndrome (SAS). When a plant detects a reduced R:FR ratio, indicating the
proximity of neighboring vegetation, it initiates a suite of responses designed
to outcompete its neighbors: elongation of stems and petioles, hyponastic leaf
movement (raising leaves toward light), accelerated flowering, and reduced branch
formation. In dense plant communities, SAS can dramatically alter canopy
architecture and competitive dynamics. Understanding SAS is critical for
agriculture, as excessive shade avoidance in crop monocultures can reduce yield
by diverting resources from reproductive structures to stem elongation.

## Phytochrome and Seed Germination

Many seeds, particularly small-seeded species, require light for germination. This
is mediated by phytochrome: red light promotes germination while far-red inhibits
it. In natural environments, seeds buried deep in soil receive no light and remain
dormant. When soil disturbance brings seeds to the surface where they can perceive
red-rich sunlight, phytochrome activates the germination program. This ecological
strategy ensures that seeds germinate only when conditions at the soil surface are
favorable for seedling establishment.
