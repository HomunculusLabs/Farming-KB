# Fungal Inventory and Sampling Methods

## Overview

Documenting fungal biodiversity [[fungal-biodiversity-forest-ecosystems]] [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-ecosystems]] requires specialized sampling methods that account for the unique biology of fungi — their cryptic life cycles, seasonal fruiting patterns, diverse growth forms, and the need for both field observation and laboratory isolation. Standardized protocols are essential for producing comparable data across studies, sites, and time periods.

## Macrofungal Inventory Methods

### Plot-Based Sampling

For macrofungi (mushrooms and other large fruiting bodies), plot-based methods provide quantitative, reproducible data:

- **Forest plots**: Recommended minimum 1,000 m² per plot
- **Grassland plots**: Recommended minimum 500 m² per plot
- **Minimum plot number**: At least 5 plots per site
- **Survey frequency**: Every 2-3 weeks during fruiting season(s)
- **Minimum survey duration**: 3 years, preferably 5 years

### Seasonality Considerations

Macrofungal fruiting varies by season, geographic area, and elevation. In areas with pronounced spring and autumn fruiting, different species fruit in each season. At high elevations, the season is confined to a few summer months. Surveys must span the full fruiting period to capture seasonal specialists and rare species.

### Sequestrate Fungi

Hypogeous (underground) fungi require specialized methods: raking near animal digs, searching compacted soil areas (campgrounds, road margins, old skid trails), and using trained dogs for truffles. A crew of six can survey approximately 10 hectares per day.

### Species-Effort Curves

Species-effort curves (analogous to species-area curves in plant ecology) help determine when sampling is sufficient. The Pacific Northwest old-growth survey mandate identified 225 macrofungal species requiring documentation. New species continue to appear with additional effort, making it difficult to define a definitive endpoint.

## Microfungal (Soil) Inventory Methods

### Soil Plating Techniques

Three principal methods are recommended for soil fungal isolation:

- **Warcup soil plates**: Small soil samples placed directly on agar surface
- **Suspension plating**: Soil suspended in water, diluted, and spread on agar
- **Particle filtration**: Soil particles filtered and placed on agar

### Sample Size Determination

Species-isolate curves indicate that 600-1,000 isolates per site are needed to capture the majority of species obtainable by standard methods. For comparative quantitative studies, 180-250 isolates per site provide repeatable data on principal species. The maximum number of isolates manageable by two full-time researchers is approximately 2,000-3,000.

### Selective Isolation

Selective techniques extend species lists by targeting specific groups:

- **Ethanol pasteurization**: Suppresses rapidly growing fungi
- **Soil steaming**: Eliminates sensitive taxa, revealing tolerant specialists
- **Dichloran (botran) addition**: Restricts radial hyphal extension
- **High-temperature incubation**: Selects for thermotolerant species
- **Low-temperature incubation**: Selects for psychrotolerant species
- **Low water activity media**: Selects for xerophilic species
- **Antibiotic-supplemented media**: Selects against bacterial contamination

### Collection Protocol

Soil is collected from the A1 horizon (upper mineral soil surface): remove litter and humus, sterilize tools in 70% ethanol, collect 10-30 g plugs in sterile bags. Transport in cooler (0-10 degrees C) in warm weather. Process within hours or air-dry for storage.

## Lichen Inventory Methods

Lichen surveys use a combination of plot-based and targeted search methods. All growth forms (crustose, foliose, fruticose) and substrates (rock, bark, soil, wood) should be examined. Lichen community composition is influenced by air quality, making standardized methods essential for environmental monitoring.

## Molecular and Culture-Independent Methods

### Environmental DNA

Soil DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing (metabarcoding) of ITS amplicons reveal fungal diversity far exceeding that captured by culture-based methods. This approach detects non-sporulating, non-fruiting, and unculturable taxa. However, results depend on primer choice, DNA extraction efficiency, and the completeness of reference databases.

### Endophyte Isolation

Endophytes are isolated by surface-sterilizing plant tissue (sequential immersion in ethanol and sodium hypochlorite), plating on nutrient media, and identifying emerging colonies. Molecular identification (ITS sequencing) is now standard practice.

## Data Analysis

### Community Metrics

Standard ecological metrics are applied to fungal inventory data:

- **Species richness**: Total number of species per site or sample
- **Species diversity**: Combines richness and evenness (e.g., Shannon index)
- **Species composition**: Ranked relative abundance of species
- **Similarity indices**: Compare communities across sites or treatments

### Species-Isolate Curves

These mycological analogues of species-area curves help determine sampling sufficiency. Asymptotes for soil fungi typically range from 60-150 species for 600-1,000 isolates, but new species continue to be encountered indefinitely.

## Merged: Mordecai Cubitt Cooke

# Mordecai Cubitt Cooke

Victorian mycologist and science writer (1825-1914), author of "The Seven Sisters of Sleep" (1860) and numerous mycological works. A founding figure of British mycology who came closest to starting a Victorian magic mushroom craze.

Cooke's career spanned nearly five decades during which mycology transformed from a gentleman's hobby into a recognized scientific discipline. His prolific output — over 30 books and hundreds of articles — helped drive this transformation, even as his lack of formal credentials limited his acceptance by the academic establishment.

## Overview

Mordecai Cubitt Cooke was a prolific mycological writer and a member of the Woolhope Naturalists' Field Club in Hereford, which organized the first systematic fungus forays in Britain beginning in 1868. Despite his humble origins (which prevented him from entering the gentlemanly classes establishing academic mycology), Cooke made significant contributions to popularizing mycology. He is shown seated in the middle of the front row of the founding members of the British Mycological Society (c. 1896).

Cooke's career spanned the formative decades of British mycology, bridging the gap between the gentleman naturalists of the early Victorian period and the professional scientists who would establish mycology as an academic discipline. His popular writings brought fungal biology to a wide audience, though his lack of formal academic credentials limited his recognition within the emerging scientific establishment.

His background was distinctly non-gentlemanly for the era. Born in Horning, Norfolk, Cooke worked as a clerk, a teacher, and a journalist before finding his calling in natural history. This working-class origin set him apart from the wealthy amateurs who dominated Victorian science, and may explain both his prolific output (he needed to write for income) and his sometimes uneasy relationship with the scientific establishment.

## Key Contributions

### "The Seven Sisters of Sleep" (1860)

Cooke's first and most unconventional book was a defense of drug use, written when he was in his mid-thirties. The book treated seven narcotic plants (tobacco, opium, hashish, betel, coca, belladonna, and fly-agaric) as "sisters" ruling over different parts of the world. Cooke argued for the "moral equivalence, and therefore legitimacy" of the world's regional drug preferences, a concept partly plagiarized from James Johnston's "The Chemistry of Common Life" (1855).

Of most interest to mushroom history, Cooke devoted significant space to the fly-agaric (Amanita muscaria). He not only popularized the myths of Siberian Amanita use but retold the story of Mr Glen's encounter with a bemushroomed man staggering through Knightsbridge. He prophetically wrote that "if future generations do not deem it desirable to indulge in a narcotic of this kind for the purposes of producing pleasurable sensations... yet they may learn more than we do at present know of the peculiar characteristics which distinguish this [mushroom] from all the others of the 'Seven Sisters of Sleep.'"

This passage is remarkable for its prescience — Cooke recognised that Amanita muscaria had pharmacological properties that deserved serious scientific investigation, decades before [[gordon-wasson-mushroom-discovery]]'s systematic study of Siberian mushroom use. His framing of psychoactive mushrooms as a legitimate subject of inquiry was ahead of its time.

### Mycological Works

Cooke authored "Rust, Smut, Mildew and Mould" (1865), "A Plain and Easy Account of the British Fungi" (1862), and edited "Science Gossip." He served as editor of "Grevillea," a mycological journal. These works contributed to the [[victorian-mycology]] movement and the broader [[mycophiles-and-mushroom-culture]]. His "Plain and Easy Account" was one of the first accessible mycological texts for the general public, making [[fungal-biology-fundamentals]] available to amateur naturalists.

"A Plain and Easy Account of the British Fungi" went through multiple editions and remained in print for decades, serving as the primary introduction to mycology for generations of British naturalists. Its accessible style and clear illustrations set a standard for popular science writing that influenced later authors like gary lincoff and david arora.

### Role in Organized Mycology

Cooke's involvement with the Woolhope Naturalists' Field Club was instrumental in establishing the tradition of fungus forays in Britain. The Woolhope Club's annual "fungus forays," beginning in 1868, brought together amateurs and professionals to collect and identify fungi, creating a model for mycological fieldwork that continues today. These forays contributed to the [[fungal-herbarium-collection]] and fungal inventory methods used by British mycologists.

The Woolhope forays were social as well as scientific events, combining field collecting with communal meals featuring edible fungi (and, presumably, careful avoidance of the poisonous ones). This convivial tradition helped build the community of British mycologists that would eventually formalize as the British Mycological Society.

### Reversal on Psychoactive Mushrooms

By the time Cooke published "Poisonous Mushrooms" in 1894, published by the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge, he had completely reversed his earlier positions. Despite knowing how to identify Liberty Caps (Psilocybe semilanceata), he now warned the public to avoid them, reflecting the growing conservatism of his career.

This reversal is significant because it illustrates the shifting cultural attitudes toward psychoactive substances during the Victorian period. The same man who had enthusiastically defended drug use in his youth became a cautionary voice against it, mirroring the broader trajectory of [[psychedelic-mushroom-history]] in Western culture.
