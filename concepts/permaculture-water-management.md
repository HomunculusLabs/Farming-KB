# Permaculture Water Management

Water is typically the first priority in permaculture design. The permaculture approach to water management follows a sequence: slow it down, spread it out, sink it into the soil, and store it. Every drop of rain that falls on a site should be used as many times as possible before leaving the property. This approach builds drought resilience, reduces erosion, recharges groundwater, and creates productive aquatic ecosystems.

## Swales

Swales are shallow, level ditches dug on contour — the horizontal line across a slope where water would not flow in either direction. They are the most important water harvesting earthwork in permaculture.

### Function
- Capture runoff water and allow it to infiltrate into the soil
- Reduce erosion by slowing water flow downhill
- Recharge groundwater
- Create a moist planting zone along the berm (the soil piled on the downslope side)

### Design
- Spacing depends on slope, rainfall, and soil type; typically 10-50 feet apart vertically
- Depth: 6-18 inches; width: 2-4 feet
- Always perfectly level — use a laser level, A-frame, or water level
- The berm on the downhill side should be stabilized immediately with mulch and ground cover
- Plant the berm with trees, shrubs, and perennials that benefit from the moisture

### Construction
1. Mark the contour line across the slope
2. Dig the swale along the contour, piling soil on the downslope side
3. Level the bottom carefully
4. Compact the bottom lightly or leave rough depending on infiltration needs
5. Stabilize the berm with mulch and plant immediately
6. Do NOT put swales through existing tree root zones without consulting an arborist

### Limitations
- Not suitable for very steep slopes (erosion risk) or very flat land (no contour to follow)
- On highly compacted or clay soils, initial infiltration may be slow
- In high-rainfall areas, overspill must be designed to prevent swale failure

## Ponds

Ponds store water for irrigation, livestock, wildlife habitat, fire protection, and aquaculture. They also create microclimates and aesthetic value.

### Types
- **Excavated ponds** — dug into the ground, lined with clay or synthetic liner
- **Embankment ponds** — created by building a dam across a valley or drainage line
- **Spring-fed ponds** — fed by natural springs, the most reliable water source

### Design Considerations
- Minimum depth of 6-8 feet for temperature stability and to prevent freezing solid in winter
- Ratio of 3:1 or 4:1 (width to depth) for a stable shape
- Ensure adequate catchment area to fill the pond and maintain water levels
- Design spillways for overflow during heavy rains
- Plant edges with native wetland species for filtration and habitat
- See [[permaculture-duck-pond-systems]] for integrated duck and pond design

### Siting
- Below springs or above water use points for gravity feed
- In a natural depression or valley floor
- Away from large trees (roots can damage dam or liner)
- In zone 2-3 for regular access and maintenance

## Rain Gardens

Rain gardens are shallow, planted depressions designed to absorb stormwater runoff from impervious surfaces (roofs, driveways, patios). They are the urban/suburban equivalent of swales.

### Design
- Size: typically 10-20% of the drainage area (varies with soil infiltration rate)
- Depth: 4-8 inches below surrounding grade
- Fill with amended soil (compost + sand + topsoil mix) for fast infiltration
- Plant with native species tolerant of both wet and dry conditions
- Use river rock or gravel at inlet points to disperse flow and prevent erosion

### Benefits
- Reduces stormwater runoff and pollution
- Recharges groundwater
- Creates pollinator habitat
- Attractive landscaping feature
- Low maintenance once established

## Greywater Systems

Greywater is wastewater from sinks, showers, bathtubs, and laundry machines (not toilets). Reusing greywater reduces freshwater consumption and returns nutrients to the landscape.

### Greywater Quality Considerations
- Avoid greywater from kitchens with garbage disposals (high grease and solids)
- Use biodegradable, low-phosphate soaps and detergents
- Do not store greywater for more than 24 hours (pathogen risk)
- Do not use on root crops or food that contacts the soil surface
- Check local regulations before installing greywater systems

### System Types
- **Simple branched drain** — distributes greywater to multiple mulch basins around landscape plants
- **Laundry-to-landscape** — redirects washing machine discharge to the garden via a hose
- **Constructed wetland** — greywater flows through a planted wetland cell for filtration before reuse or discharge
- **Greywater greenhouse** — indoor greywater treatment with plants in a greenhouse environment

### Mulch Basins
The simplest and most effective greywater distribution method:
1. Create a basin around each recipient plant
2. Fill with coarse mulch (wood chips, straw)
3. Distribute greywater into the basin via a pipe or hose
4. The mulch filters particles and distributes water
5. Plant fruit trees, berries, or ornamentals in the basin

## Keyline Design

Keyline design, developed by P.A. Yeomans in Australia, is a landscape management approach that optimizes water distribution across agricultural land using the natural topography.

### Keyline Concept
- Identify the "keypoint" — the point where a valley floor transitions from convex to concave (where water naturally slows and begins to spread)
- The "keyline" is a contour line extending from the keypoint across the adjacent ridge
- Plowing or cultivating parallel to and below the keyline moves water from valleys (wet) to ridges (dry)

### Application
- Subsoiling along keyline patterns fractures compacted soil and improves infiltration
- Water that naturally concentrates in valleys is distributed across ridges
- Dams are sited in the upper valleys for maximum water storage with minimum earthworks
- Irrigation channels follow keyline contours for gravity-fed water distribution

### Benefits
- Dramatically improves soil moisture distribution across the landscape
- Reduces erosion by slowing water flow
- Increases groundwater recharge
- Can be applied to existing pasture, cropland, and forest without major earthworks

## Irrigation-Free Gardens

The ultimate goal of permaculture water management is gardens that do not require supplemental irrigation after establishment. This is achieved through:

### Techniques
- **Deep mulching** — 4-8 inches of organic mulch retains soil moisture, suppresses weeds, and builds soil
- **Swale and earthwork systems** — passively distribute rainfall across the landscape
- **Dense planting** — canopy cover shades soil, reducing evaporation
- **Deep-rooted plants** — access moisture from deeper soil layers
- **Soil building** — organic matter increases soil's water-holding capacity
- **Microclimate creation** — windbreaks reduce drying winds; shade trees moderate temperature
- **Appropriate species selection** — drought-adapted species, particularly natives

### Establishment Period
Most irrigation-free gardens need 1-3 years of supplemental irrigation while plants establish deep root systems. After this establishment period, well-designed systems should survive on natural rainfall in most climates.
