"The soil that he knows is a discreet, isolated part of a whole" — and knowing the part while losing the whole is the characteristic error of
discriminating knowledge. The scientist who studies soil microorganisms in isolation understands less about soil than the child who plays in it.

The Buddhist framework makes this distinction explicit. "Discriminating knowledge" splits self and object, creating the illusion of separation that fuels both scientific overreach and
civilizational madness. "Non-discriminating knowledge" treats self and object as a unified whole — and this, Fukuoka argues, is the highest form of wisdom. It is not ignorance but a more
fundamental kind of knowing — one that grasps the whole before the intellect begins its work of division.

"The discriminating adult is inferior to the non-discriminating child, for the adult only plunges himself into ever-deepening confusion." The more he analyzes, the further he drifts from the
truth. The more he classifies, the more categories he creates — and the more reality eludes him. The pursuit of knowledge through dissection leads not to wisdom but to an ever-expanding labyrinth of partial truths, each one more distant from the living whole.

## Summary

Fukuoka's philosophy issues a fundamental challenge to the assumptions of modernity. Nature cannot be known through dissection because dissection destroys the unity that constitutes nature's
reality. Human subjectivity is not a problem to be overcome but an inescapable condition that renders all partial knowledge suspect.

The scientific method, for all its power, cannot deliver what it promises: an objective understanding of a world that is fundamentally indivisible.

The path forward — if there is one — lies not in more sophisticated analysis but in the recovery of a childlike, non-discriminating awareness that experiences the world as whole before the
intellect begins its work of fragmentation. This is not a retreat from knowledge but an advance toward a deeper, more complete form of knowing — one that honors the unity of nature rather than
destroying it in the name of understanding.
