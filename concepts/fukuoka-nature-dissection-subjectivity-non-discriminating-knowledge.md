# Fukuoka on Nature Dissection, Subjectivity, and Non-Discriminating Knowledge

[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s *The One-Straw Revolution* contains a devastating philosophical critique of Western science and modern civilization, articulated most forcefully in the chapter
"The [[fukuoka-illusions-of-natural-science]] Science." At its core, Fukuoka argues that the scientific method — dissection, analysis, and recombination — destroys the very thing it seeks to understand,
and that the discriminating intellect traps humanity in a maze of [[fukuoka-textdoc-maze-relative-subjectivity]] from which only non-discriminating awareness can offer escape.

## Western vs Eastern Approaches to Nature

Fukuoka draws a sharp contrast between Western and Eastern ways of relating to the natural world. The Western natural sciences are fundamentally materialistic, analytical, and dialectical.
They approach nature as an object to be studied — a collection of matter governed by discoverable laws. This orientation produces a rigid dichotomy between man and nature: man stands apart
from the natural world, and in many cases, opposes it.

The Western project rests on the conviction that human intellect can cast off subjectivity and comprehend nature objectively, as a reality independent of consciousness, knowable through
patient observation, reductive analysis, and reconstruction from parts.

This belief in nature as an objective reality — knowable, predictable, and ultimately controllable — has become the foundational assumption of the entire Western scientific enterprise.

The Eastern view, by contrast, holds that man should become one with nature — not stand apart from it, not dissect it, not dominate it, but participate in it as an inseparable part of the whole. Fukuoka regards the Western belief in objective knowledge of nature as not merely incomplete but dangerously illusory —
an error that has led humanity into ever-deeper alienation from the living world that sustains it.

## Nature Must Not Be Dissected

Fukuoka's central epistemological claim is radical: nature is a living, organic whole that cannot be divided and subdivided without losing its essential character. When man dissects nature
in order to "know" it — collecting data, classifying specimens, assembling facts into theories — he is not examining nature itself. He is examining fragments of nature, severed from the
relationships that give them meaning.

A single leaf, Fukuoka observes, offers nearly infinite opportunities for study. One can examine its cells, its nucleus, its chromosomes, its chlorophyll, the roots that feed it, the nutrients
it absorbs, the microorganisms that live upon it. Each avenue of investigation yields more data, more specialization, more fragmentation.

But nowhere in this process does the investigator encounter the leaf as it actually lives — as part of a unified, self-organizing whole. The deeper the analysis goes, the further it moves from the reality it purports to describe.

"Nature is a living, organic whole that cannot be divided and subdivided." When nature is divided into complementary halves and divided again, the unity of nature is irretrievably lost. What
remains are pieces — useful pieces, perhaps, from a technological standpoint, but pieces nonetheless.

Fukuoka's metaphor is deliberately shocking: "All science has succeeded in doing is to peel the skin off a [[fukuoka-scalpel-beautiful-woman-science-nature]] and reveal a bloody mass of tissue." The scientific method strips away
the form, the beauty, the wholeness — and presents the remains as truth. The living unity that was nature has been replaced by a catalogue of components.

[[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] exemplifies this error vividly. Chemical controls regulate bud formation so that plants bloom in all seasons, disrupting natural rhythms that evolved over millennia.

Now humanity challenges the synthesis of life itself through manipulation of nucleic acids, chromosomes, and genetic recombination — always pushing further into territory it cannot truly comprehend.

Yet "the fruits and creations of human knowledge can never exceed the limits of the human intellect." No matter how sophisticated the technology, it remains bounded by the same partial, fragmenting consciousness that produced it. The more we learn, the more we realize how little we can truly know.

## The Maze of Relative Subjectivity

Fukuoka illustrates the predicament of human knowledge through a parable of Okuninushi's sack. People find objects inside a great sack and misidentify their purposes at every turn. They guess,
debate, and build elaborate theories — but the truth is known only to the owner. The objects, in fact, are broken and carried only as kindling. Every interpretation, however clever, misses the
mark entirely.

"Man jumps into that great sack called nature," Fukuoka writes, "and grabbing whatever he can, turns it over and examines it." Every interpretation carries the risk of grievous error because
man cannot know nature's true purposes from inside it.

Yet man persists in the belief that knowledge will broaden without limit through observation and reasoning — that each new discovery brings
him closer to some final comprehension of the whole. This faith in the cumulative power of knowledge is itself a form of the subjectivity it claims to transcend.

Fukuoka offers a concrete example: shells attached to bamboo are found and interpreted first as a weapon, then as a musical instrument, then as a dancing accessory. Each stage is believed to
bring humanity closer to the truth, but all are wrong. The object's actual purpose remains inaccessible to those trapped inside the sack. "Man can know the world only by stepping outside of the
sack and meeting face-to-face with the owner." But this is precisely what the scientific method cannot do — it is, by definition, an investigation conducted from within.

A flea born in the sack cannot guess that the object hangs from Okuninushi's belt. Similarly, man — born within nature — can never understand all of nature by examining only the part around
him. His perspective is inherently limited, and every conclusion he draws is colored by the vantage point he cannot escape. The maze of relative subjectivity has no exit reachable through more
or better observation.

## Critique of Modern Civilization

Fukuoka extends this epistemological argument into a sweeping critique of modern civilization. "If we find our modern civilization to be insane," he writes, "we must conclude that the human
intellect which engendered it is also insane." The logic is inescapable: if the product is mad, the producer must be mad as well.

The products of technological progress — airplanes, automobiles — are held up as triumphs
of speed and comfort, but Fukuoka asks whether they are truly fast and comfortable or merely toys and
amusements that distract humanity from its estrangement from nature. What has been gained, and what has been lost? The question is rarely asked because the framework that produces the answer
also prevents the question from being formulated.

The conveniences of modern life mask a deeper impoverishment — a loss of direct connection to the living world that no technological advancement can restore.

"Man is unable to see the truth because his eyes are veiled by subjectivity." Everything in this world, as man perceives it, derives from man himself. He draws all conclusions, constructs all
frameworks, and acts out what Fukuoka calls "a drunken role on the stage supported by the crazed subjectivity of his own despotic existence." Modern civilization is not progress but performance —
a grand illusion sustained by the very intellect that claims to have transcended illusion.

## Non-Discriminating Knowledge

Fukuoka's alternative to the scientific worldview is rooted in what Buddhist philosophy calls "non-discriminating knowledge" — a mode of awareness that does not split the world into subject
and object, self and other, known and knower. He contrasts this with "discriminating knowledge," the habitual intellectual operation that classifies, compares, and judges — and in doing so,
severs the perceiver from the perceived.

"Science arose from doubt and discontent," Fukuoka acknowledges, but he insists that this origin in no way justifies science's claims to authority. The doubt that drives scientific inquiry is itself a product of the discriminating mind — it arises only after the original unity has been fractured.

An infant sees things intuitively. To the
infant, nature appears entire and complete — a unity that requires no improvement. There is no cause for doubt or discontent in this non-discriminating view of creation. The world simply is, and
it is whole. The infant does not separate himself from what he sees; he experiences it directly, without the mediation of concepts and categories.

The adult, by contrast, mentally picks things apart, classifies them, and perceives everything as imperfect. From this perception of imperfection arises the impulse to "improve" nature — and
the changes wrought by this impulse are celebrated as "progress."

But the child's understanding of "progress" reveals it for what it truly is: a march toward annihilation, manifesting as spiritual
decay and [[bloomfield-environmental-pollution-overcollection-impact-fungi]]. Each technological "advance" deepens the fracture between humanity and the living world. What is called progress is, in Fukuoka's view, the steady dismantling of the conditions that make life possible.

Fukuoka's example of children encountering mud is particularly illuminating. A country child jumps in and plays, experiencing mud as part of the living world. A city child, trained in the habits
of discriminating knowledge, fears "germs." The mother's knowledge — "dirt is filthy" — is, in Fukuoka's terms, more ignorant than ignorance itself, because it replaces direct experience with a
fragmentary, fear-based abstraction that severs the child from reality.

In truth, each gram of soil contains hundreds of millions of microorganisms. Harmful bacteria are killed by other bacteria, which are in turn killed by yet others. The soil under the sun is
healthy, whole, and essential to all life.

"The soil that he knows is a discreet, isolated part of a whole" — and knowing the part while losing the whole is the characteristic error of
discriminating knowledge. The scientist who studies soil microorganisms in isolation understands less about soil than the child who plays in it.

The Buddhist framework makes this distinction explicit. "Discriminating knowledge" splits self and object, creating the illusion of separation that fuels both scientific overreach and
civilizational madness. "Non-discriminating knowledge" treats self and object as a unified whole — and this, Fukuoka argues, is the highest form of wisdom. It is not ignorance but a more
fundamental kind of knowing — one that grasps the whole before the intellect begins its work of division.

"The discriminating adult is inferior to the non-discriminating child, for the adult only plunges himself into ever-deepening confusion." The more he analyzes, the further he drifts from the
truth. The more he classifies, the more categories he creates — and the more reality eludes him. The pursuit of knowledge through dissection leads not to wisdom but to an ever-expanding labyrinth of partial truths, each one more distant from the living whole.

## Summary

Fukuoka's philosophy issues a fundamental challenge to the assumptions of modernity. Nature cannot be known through dissection because dissection destroys the unity that constitutes nature's
reality. Human subjectivity is not a problem to be overcome but an inescapable condition that renders all partial knowledge suspect.

The scientific method, for all its power, cannot deliver what it promises: an objective understanding of a world that is fundamentally indivisible.

The path forward — if there is one — lies not in more sophisticated analysis but in the recovery of a childlike, non-discriminating awareness that experiences the world as whole before the
intellect begins its work of fragmentation. This is not a retreat from knowledge but an advance toward a deeper, more complete form of knowing — one that honors the unity of nature rather than
destroying it in the name of understanding.
