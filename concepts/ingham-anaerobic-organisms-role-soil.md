# Anaerobic Organisms and Their Role in Soil Systems

## What Anaerobic Organisms Breathe

Most of the problematic anaerobes are autotrophs, which means they
use carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrate (NO3), nitrite (NO2), sulfate
(SO4), and other materials containing oxygen tied up in chemical
forms. If you notice, all those chemical formulas contain oxygen,
but not in gaseous form. They use what could be called "solid oxygen."

The problem is not so much what the anaerobes breathe, as what they
produce. Plant roots do not tolerate anaerobic conditions, but not
because roots need oxygen per se. Plants take up CO2 and release
oxygen. The real problem is what anaerobic bacteria produce: toxic
metabolic byproducts that kill root tissue and harm beneficial
organisms.

## The Critical Oxygen Cutoff

Based on plant responses to tea, 5.5 to 6 mg oxygen per liter is a
safe cutoff level. Below 5.5 to 6 mg/L we often see production of

## Anaerobic Pockets in Soil

Even healthy soils contain anaerobic microsites. Inside soil
aggregates, at the center of compacted zones, and within saturated
areas, conditions can become temporarily anaerobic. This is normal
and natural. The key is that these pockets remain small and
temporary, and the surrounding aerobic community can recolonize
them when conditions improve. Problems arise when anaerobic
conditions expand to dominate the soil profile.
