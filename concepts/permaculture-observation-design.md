# Observation-Based Design in Permaculture

Observation-based design is one of the foundational methodologies in [[permaculture]], championed by [[bill-mollison]] as an approach that begins directly on and around a site rather than at a desk with [[maps]] and data. Unlike purely analytic methods, observation-based design relies on direct, sensory engagement with the landscape to reveal processes, patterns, and dynamic interactions that no static dataset can capture.

## The Limits of Analytic Design

Analytic design methods can produce useful schematics—showing how a chicken house might heat a greenhouse, or how livestock manure might cycle through an orchard—but these remain "information" rather than "resources" until implemented. Mollison calls this distinction critical: **resources are practical and useful energy storages, while information is only a potential resource until put to use.** The academic fallacy of "I think, therefore I have acted" can mislead designers into believing that assembling data is equivalent to creating real-world solutions.

The integrated design approach demonstrates this principle: placing a chicken house to heat (and be heated by) a glasshouse, with chickens ranging in the orchard for manure and pest control, and a glasshouse also heating the house, creates functional interconnections that minimize work and imported energy. But until those chickens are actually performing these functions, the system exists only as potential.

## Observation as a Design Tool

No complex of map overlays, library research, computer data, or remote analysis will ever supplant field observation for dependability and relevance. Observation is not easily directed and is sometimes regarded as unscientific, but processes and events encountered on a real site—such as heavy rain and subsequent runoff—are never revealed just by maps or fixed data. It is from observing these processes that designers can devise strategies of "least change," saving energy and time.

### Four Approaches to Observation

Mollison outlines four attitudes that observers can adopt when approaching a site:

1. **A Child-Like and Non-Selective Approach**: Prefacing observation with "I wonder why..." opens the mind to unexpected patterns and connections that a trained expert might filter out.

2. **A Thematic Approach**: Focusing observation on a specific theme such as water flow, potential energy sources, or conditions for natural regeneration allows deeper understanding of particular systems.

3. **An Instrumental Approach**: Using equipment to measure specific factors such as temperature gradients, wind patterns, or light reflection from trees provides quantitative data to complement qualitative observation.

4. **An Experiential Approach**: Using all senses as instruments—feeling soil moisture, hearing bird activity, smelling decomposition—develops a holistic awareness of site conditions and ambient qualities.

A camera, notebook, and video recorder are invaluable aids, allowing re-examination of information. A good memory for events helps tie seasonal patterns together over time.

## The Five-Stage Observation Procedure

### Stage 1: Value-Free Recording

Make non-interpretative notes about what is seen, measured, or experienced. For example, note that "moles have thrown up earth mounds on the field" without guessing at causes or implications. This requires discipline but yields rich primary data.

### Stage 2: Speculation

Select observations of interest and list speculations about possible meanings. A single observation about molehills might generate multiple hypotheses:

- Molehills may be equally common in non-field areas but are only conspicuous on bare soil
- Fields may be particularly attractive to moles due to soil conditions or food availability
- The distribution pattern may relate to moisture gradients or soil type

Speculations are a species of hypothesis—guesses about which more information can be gathered.

### Stage 3: Testing Speculations

Several strategies help confirm or deny speculations:

- **Library research** on the observed organism or phenomenon, including allied species
- **Asking others** about their observations and experience with similar situations
- **Devised observations** targeting a specific theme to test particular ideas
- **Recalling prior knowledge** about the subject from other areas or circumstances

### Stage 4: Pattern Recognition

Examine all evidence to identify patterns and modes of operation. What other creatures share the mole's niche? What predators, prey, or symbiotic partners are connected? How does the observed phenomenon link to broader systems like water percolation, soil production, or plant communities?

### Stage 5: Design Strategy Application

The final and decisive step asks: **How can we find a use for all this information?** What design strategies does the accumulated knowledge suggest? The mole may emerge as a valuable soil aerator and seed-bed provider to be encouraged, or a pest to be excluded in certain areas. Practical strategies—control methods, food uses, integration techniques—may surface from the research process.

## From Observation to Strategy

As research and observation progress, individual observations reveal their interconnectedness. The mole connects to earthworms, upturned soils, water percolation, and soil production. Dozens of useful strategies can evolve from simple initial observations, and the site begins to "design itself."

Cautious trials and further observation confirm the benefits (or drawbacks) of integrating observed organisms into the total system. Practical information gathered carries over to other sites and allied observations. Co-evolution of understanding—such as simultaneously studying earthworms alongside moles—reveals the interconnectedness of natural systems.

## The Unique Value of Observation

No analytic method can involve a designer in the world as deeply as observation. While observation methods require practice and development (unlike analysis, which needs no prior practice), observers are very likely to stumble on unique and effective strategies and thus become innovators. The uses and strategies derived from observation, experience, or experiments on-site are the basic tools of aware, long-term residents. A set of reliable strategies built up this way is transferable to other locations.

The end result of systematic observation is two-fold: evolved strategies for application in design, and a personal, involved understanding of the totality of natural system interdependence. Nature itself becomes the teacher—supplying answers to all questions in time, at no cost, and without bureaucratic involvement.

## Practical Applications

- **Soil aerators**: Moles and other burrowers can be encouraged or excluded based on their net effect on soil health
- **Pest management**: Understanding predator-prey relationships through observation reveals natural pest control mechanisms
- **[[water-management]]**: Direct observation of runoff patterns, absorption rates, and seasonal flow informs swale, dam, and drainage placement
- **Plant succession**: Watching how pioneer species colonize disturbed areas guides [[succession-planting]] strategies
- **Microclimate creation**: Observing natural shade, wind protection, and moisture retention patterns informs placement of structures and plantings
