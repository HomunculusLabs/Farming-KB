# Water Runoff Factors and Swale Systems

## Overview

Water runoff is the movement of water across the land surface that has not infiltrated into the soil. In arid and semi-arid environments, understanding the factors that control runoff is critical for designing effective water harvesting and soil conservation systems. Runoff transforms from a destructive erosion force into a valuable resource when properly managed through earthworks such as swales, contour banks, and retardation basins.

## Factors Affecting Runoff

### Vegetation Cover

Vegetation is the single most important factor controlling runoff. Different land cover types produce dramatically different runoff coefficients:

- **Forests (10-15% runoff):** Deep tree root systems, leaf litter, and [[soil-collapse]] leading to salted soils. The seasonal dimension is critical for design—earthworks must be in place *before* the destructive season arrives.

### Artificially Sealed Areas

Roads, parking areas, house roofs, bare rock, concreted drains, and compacted settlement areas all produce flash floods in nearby streams. The total area of these impervious surfaces within a catchment must be calculated when planning flood retardation systems. In urban and peri-urban settings, even small sealed areas can generate disproportionately large runoff volumes.

### Soil and Rock Type

Rapid rainfall infiltration occurs in:
- Coarse or open sands
- Sandy loams
- Kraznozems (deep, well-structured red soils)
- Soils with 15-20% or more humus content

Slow infiltration or rapid runoff occurs on:
- Non-wetting sands (hydrophobic soils)
- Sealed clay soils
- Compacted soils
- Sheet rock or massive rock domes

Aquifer intake areas include gravels, boulder banks, shattered rock ridges, unconsolidated pediment wastes, limestone outcrops, and eroded fold crests. These remain open and reduce runoff unless fine dust and sands bury crevices and seal the aquifer intakes.

### Time Required for Infiltration

Water infiltration times vary dramatically by soil type:

- **Sands:** 30 minutes to achieve significant infiltration
- **Clay-fraction soils:** 3-4 days for full infiltration
- **Best plant growth:** Requires 0.5-1.2 m of saturated soil profile

Infiltration rates should be tested by digging a pit, wetting it first, then filling it to measure how long it takes for the desired depth of soil to become saturated. Given this data, the width and depth of swales or contour banks can be calculated. Most plants can withstand 2-3 days of temporary flooding.

### Water Viscosity

Water density is relatively unaffected by temperature, but temperature significantly affects viscosity and thus friction against surfaces. Daytime rains on warm surfaces run off faster than night rains or rain during cool periods. In desert regions where rain arrives in summer monsoon regimes, floods peak earlier and with greater erosive force than in winter-rain areas.

## Halting and Absorbing Runoff with Swales

### Design Principles

The two main aims of runoff interception are:
1. Converting water from a destructive erosion force into a life-creative energy that supports forest development
2. Recharging groundwaters so that freshwater sheets develop above deeper saltwater tables

### Swale Specifications

- **Width:** 4-5 m for hand labour; 5-18 m with tractor and blade (or road grader)
- **Silt management:** Place permanent steel pegs with clear graduations in swales; remove silt when more than 20 cm accumulates
- **Spacing:** Terraces spaced downhill at intervals of 20 times their width
- **Infiltration aids:** Rip the [[gypsum]] in clay soils

### Evolution into Terraces

Where low-slope soils are subject to steady erosion, swale banks can be progressively exaggerated to form terraces with trees on the outer slopes. This evolution occurs naturally on sandy slopes or where cultivation takes place between swales. As terraces with treed edges develop, slopes stabilize and silt removal becomes less necessary.

### Creating Springs from Swale Systems

A succession of 5-7 swales operating effectively on runoff slopes can create springs and maintain low streamflow. This effect occurs naturally where receded lakes (such as Pyramid Lake, Nevada) leave stepped bands of beach boulders across slopes, halting runoff and enabling freshwater springs to emerge below.

## Runoff Estimation

### Desert Runoff

Runoff in deserts ranges from 5-80% of rainfall depending on surface permeability. A practical average is approximately 20%, but conservative design uses a ratio of **20 hectares of catchment to 1 hectare of crop** to ensure sufficient soakage for long-term growth. Only in areas of reliable rainfall can this ratio be reduced to 15:1.

### Design Calculations

Any designer can estimate potential runoff by considering:
- Rain days per year and season
- Catchment area and shape
- Vegetation cover percentage
- Soil type and infiltration rate
- Slope and aspect
- Existing sealed or compacted areas

## Bleed-Off Systems for Turbid Streams

For silt-laden and turbulent streams, a restricted water exit should open into a much wider drain (the silt trap), which leads into a broad reed bed. From the reed bed, relatively clear water can be led to a dam or swale sited well away from the stream line and not above any valley settlement area.

Swales from these systems can extend several kilometres to the next fast stream, growing extensive forest lines with only occasional silt clearing and periodic road grader maintenance. Seasonal silt clearing in the silt trap may be necessary until trees are well established.
