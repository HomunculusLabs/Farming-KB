## Overview
First-pass metabolism is the loss of an orally absorbed drug before it reaches systemic circulation.
It is also called presystemic metabolism.
After swallowing, a drug must dissolve, cross the intestinal wall, enter portal blood, pass through
the liver, and escape into the systemic circulation. Loss at any of these steps reduces oral
bioavailability.
Bioavailability, written F, is the fraction of the administered dose that reaches systemic blood as
unchanged active drug. Intravenous dosing has F equal to one by definition.
Oral dosing can have F near one, near zero, or anything between. The value depends on absorption,
intestinal metabolism, hepatic extraction, transporters, formulation, food, disease, and genetics.
First-pass metabolism is clinically important because it explains why oral and intravenous doses can
differ by orders of magnitude.
## Portal Route
Nutrients and many drugs absorbed from the gut enter the portal vein before they enter general
circulation.
This anatomical arrangement sends absorbed molecules directly to the liver, a metabolic filter rich
in CYP enzymes, UGTs, SULTs, esterases, and transporters.
The intestine is not passive. Enterocytes express CYP3A4, UGTs, sulfotransferases,
carboxylesterases, P-glycoprotein, BCRP, OATPs, and other uptake or efflux systems.
A drug may be metabolized in enterocytes, pumped back into the lumen, metabolized again by the
liver, excreted in bile, or recycled through enterohepatic circulation.
The first-pass effect is therefore a gut-liver process rather than a purely hepatic process.
## The Bioavailability Equation
A useful conceptual equation is F = Fa × Fg × Fh.
Fa is the fraction absorbed across the intestinal lumen. Poor dissolution, low permeability,
degradation in stomach acid, or binding to food can reduce Fa.
Fg is the fraction escaping intestinal metabolism and efflux. CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein in
enterocytes are major determinants for many drugs.
Fh is the fraction escaping hepatic extraction. It depends on hepatic blood flow, protein binding,
enzyme activity, transporter uptake, and intrinsic clearance.
A drug with excellent membrane permeability can still have low F if Fg or Fh is small.
## Hepatic Extraction Ratio
The hepatic extraction ratio is the fraction of drug removed during one pass through the liver.
High-extraction drugs are cleared efficiently when hepatic blood flow delivers them to enzymes and
transporters. Examples often include morphine, propranolol, nitroglycerin, and lidocaine.
For these drugs, clearance is flow-limited. Liver enzyme capacity matters, but changes in hepatic
blood flow can dominate systemic exposure.
Low-extraction drugs are removed slowly relative to liver blood flow. Their clearance is more
sensitive to enzyme inhibition, induction, protein binding, and intrinsic metabolic capacity.
This distinction helps predict interactions. A CYP inhibitor may transform the exposure of a
low-extraction oral drug, while a meal or cirrhosis may strongly affect a high-extraction drug.
## Enzymes and Transporters
[[cytochrome-p450-enzymes-drug-metabolism]] are central to many first-pass processes, especially
CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP1A2.
CYP3A4 is abundant in both liver and small intestine, so CYP3A substrates often show strong
presystemic loss and strong food or drug interactions.
[[phase-ii-conjugation-reactions-drug-metabolism]] also contribute. Morphine, acetaminophen,
raloxifene, and many phenolic compounds undergo substantial glucuronidation or sulfation.
Transporters shape exposure by controlling entry and exit. P-glycoprotein can pump drug back into
the gut lumen, while OATP transporters can increase hepatic uptake and extraction.
Metabolism and transport are coupled. A transporter can repeatedly expose a drug to enterocyte
CYP3A4, creating an absorption-metabolism cycling effect.
## Routes That Avoid First Pass
Intravenous injection bypasses absorption and presystemic metabolism entirely. This is why IV dosing
is used as the reference for absolute bioavailability.
Sublingual, buccal, transdermal, inhaled, intranasal, and rectal routes can partly bypass portal
flow, though each has its own barriers and variability.
Nitroglycerin is a classic example. Oral nitroglycerin is extensively metabolized before systemic
circulation, while sublingual administration rapidly reaches the bloodstream.
Transdermal delivery avoids gut and liver first pass, but it requires drugs with suitable potency,
lipophilicity, molecular size, and skin permeability.
Alternative routes are not automatically better. They trade first-pass loss for issues such as local
irritation, slower onset, patch adhesion, device technique, or variable mucosal absorption.
## Food and Interaction Effects
Food can increase, decrease, or delay oral bioavailability. Fatty meals may improve dissolution of
lipophilic drugs, slow gastric emptying, or alter bile secretion and lymphatic transport.
Grapefruit juice inhibits intestinal CYP3A4 and can raise exposure to susceptible oral drugs such as
some calcium-channel blockers, statins, and immunosuppressants.
Enzyme induction works in the opposite direction. Rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, and St. John's
wort can lower exposure by increasing CYP or transporter expression.
Inhibition and induction can affect gut and liver differently. A local intestinal inhibitor may
raise Fg without changing systemic clearance much.
This is why oral drug interactions are often stronger than intravenous interactions for the same
substrate.
## Genetics, Age, and Disease
Genetic variation in CYP2D6, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, UGT1A1, transporters, and other proteins can alter
first-pass loss.
A poor metabolizer may have higher oral exposure to a parent drug, while an ultrarapid metabolizer
may have lower exposure or more active metabolite.
Neonates have immature hepatic and intestinal enzymes, while older adults may have reduced hepatic
blood flow, polypharmacy, and altered protein binding.
Liver disease can reduce first-pass extraction by lowering functional enzyme mass, changing blood
flow, or creating portosystemic shunts.
Intestinal disease, bariatric surgery, diarrhea, cholestasis, and inflammation can also change Fa,
Fg, and bile-dependent absorption.
## Dose and Saturation
First-pass metabolism can be nonlinear when enzymes, transporters, or conjugation cofactors
saturate.
If presystemic pathways saturate, a small increase in dose can produce a larger-than-proportional
rise in systemic exposure.
This matters for drugs with narrow therapeutic windows and for toxins where protective conjugation
becomes overwhelmed.
Saturation may occur in the gut wall, in the liver, or during transporter-mediated uptake and
efflux.
Dose-dependent bioavailability is a warning that simple proportional dose scaling may be unsafe.
## Formulation Strategies
Pharmaceutical design can improve oral exposure by changing salts, particle size, solid state,
prodrugs, lipid vehicles, enteric coatings, and controlled-release systems.
A prodrug may improve permeability, stability, or transporter uptake, then release the active
compound after absorption.
Lipid formulations can move some highly lipophilic molecules into lymphatic transport, partially
avoiding portal first pass.
Controlled release may lower peak gut concentrations or shift absorption to regions with different
enzyme expression, but it can also reduce absorption if transit time is too short.
Good formulation strategy begins by identifying whether the main limitation is solubility,
permeability, gut metabolism, hepatic extraction, or instability.
## Clinical Interpretation
Low oral bioavailability is not always a problem. If exposure is predictable, safe, and dose is
practical, an oral drug can still be useful.
High first-pass metabolism becomes problematic when it causes large variability, serious
interactions, active toxic metabolites, or impractically high oral doses.
Comparing oral and IV area-under-the-curve values estimates absolute bioavailability, while
metabolite ratios can reveal whether gut or liver pathways are responsible.
First-pass concepts also explain why crushing tablets, changing diet, combining herbs, or switching
routes can alter drug response.
For clinical pharmacology, F is not just a number. It is a summary of anatomy, chemistry, enzyme
biology, transport, patient variability, and formulation design.
## Key Takeaways
First-pass metabolism is presystemic loss in the intestine and liver before an oral dose reaches
general circulation.
Bioavailability can be described as Fa times Fg times Fh: absorption, gut escape, and hepatic
escape.
CYP enzymes, Phase II enzymes, transporters, hepatic blood flow, food, genetics, age, disease, and
formulation all influence oral exposure.
Routes such as IV, sublingual, inhaled, transdermal, and some rectal dosing can bypass part or all
of the portal first-pass pathway.
The concept is essential for dose selection, drug-interaction prediction, formulation design, and
understanding why oral pharmacology differs from plasma chemistry alone.
