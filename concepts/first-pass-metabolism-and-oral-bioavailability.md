## Overview
First-pass metabolism is the loss of an orally absorbed drug before it reaches systemic circulation.
It is also called presystemic metabolism.
After swallowing, a drug must dissolve, cross the intestinal wall, enter portal blood, pass through
the liver, and escape into the systemic circulation. Loss at any of these steps reduces oral
bioavailability.
Bioavailability, written F, is the fraction of the administered dose that reaches systemic blood as
unchanged active drug. Intravenous dosing has F equal to one by definition.
Oral dosing can have F near one, near zero, or anything between. The value depends on absorption,
intestinal metabolism, hepatic extraction, transporters, formulation, food, disease, and genetics.
First-pass metabolism is clinically important because it explains why oral and intravenous doses can
differ by orders of magnitude.

## Portal Route
Nutrients and many drugs absorbed from the gut enter the portal vein before they enter general
circulation.
This anatomical arrangement sends absorbed molecules directly to the liver, a metabolic filter rich
in CYP enzymes, UGTs, SULTs, esterases, and transporters.
The intestine is not passive. Enterocytes express CYP3A4, UGTs, sulfotransferases,
carboxylesterases, P-glycoprotein, BCRP, OATPs, and other uptake or efflux systems.
A drug may be metabolized in enterocytes, pumped back into the lumen, metabolized again by the
liver, excreted in bile, or recycled through enterohepatic circulation.
The first-pass effect is therefore a gut-liver process rather than a purely hepatic process.

## The Bioavailability Equation
A useful conceptual equation is F = Fa × Fg × Fh.
Fa is the fraction absorbed across the intestinal lumen. Poor dissolution, low permeability,
degradation in stomach acid, or binding to food can reduce Fa.
Fg is the fraction escaping intestinal metabolism and efflux. CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein in
enterocytes are major determinants for many drugs.
Fh is the fraction escaping hepatic extraction. It depends on hepatic blood flow, protein binding,
enzyme activity, transporter uptake, and intrinsic clearance.
A drug with excellent membrane permeability can still have low F if Fg or Fh is small.

## Hepatic Extraction Ratio
The hepatic extraction ratio is the fraction of drug removed during one pass through the liver.
High-extraction drugs are cleared efficiently when hepatic blood flow delivers them to enzymes and
transporters. Examples often include [[morphine]], propranolol, nitroglycerin, and lidocaine.
For these drugs, clearance is flow-limited. Liver enzyme capacity matters, but changes in hepatic
blood flow can dominate systemic exposure.
Low-extraction drugs are removed slowly relative to liver blood flow. Their clearance is more
sensitive to [[cytochrome-p450-enzymes-drug-metabolism]] are central to many first-pass processes, especially
CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP1A2.
CYP3A4 is abundant in both liver and small intestine, so CYP3A substrates often show strong
presystemic loss and strong food or [[cannabis-drug-interactions-and-contraindications]].
