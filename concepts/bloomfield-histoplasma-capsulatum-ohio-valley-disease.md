## Association with Bird Droppings and Starling Roosts

*Histoplasma capsulatum* thrives in nitrogen-rich soils, and the most potent
natural source is the [[fungal-accumulation-of-radionuclides]] bird and bat droppings (guano). Starling
roosts are particularly notorious sites [[six-vectors-of-contamination]]. Massive
congregations—sometimes numbering millions—deposit tons of nitrogen-rich feces
beneath their roosting sites, creating ideal [[fruiting-conditions-for-mushrooms]] *Histoplasma*
growth. Barns, attics, church steeples, bridges, and cave systems inhabited
by bats are all potential hotspots.

The spores become airborne when contaminated soil or guano is disturbed by
construction, excavation, farming, or spelunking. Once airborne, the
microscopic conidia (2–5 μm) are small enough to reach the deep alveoli of
the lungs. Money captures this exposure vividly: he describes sitting in a
shed surrounded by starlings, acutely aware that *Histoplasma* spores are
entering his lungs with every breath.

## The Ubiquity of Fungal Spores

A central theme in Money's account is the ubiquity of fungal spores in the
human environment. As he writes: "Every day of your life other kinds of
fungal spores pass into your nostrils and lungs." This is literal—the average
person inhales thousands of fungal spores daily from dozens of species. Most

## Ohio Valley Disease: Epidemiology

Histoplasmosis is endemic throughout the Ohio and Mississippi River Valleys
and parts of the southeastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]]—from Ohio and Indiana south
through Kentucky, Tennessee, and Arkansas to the Gulf Coast, and west through
Missouri to Texas. Skin test surveys indicate that **most residents of Ohio
and surrounding states have been infected** at some point, though the vast
majority of infections are entirely asymptomatic.

Millions of Americans carry dormant *Histoplasma* organisms in their lungs,
held in check by their immune systems. Most will never develop symptomatic
disease, but the fungus remains latent—a potential threat that can reassert
itself if immune surveillance breaks down.

## Clinical Manifestations

### Asymptomatic Infection and Calcified Nodules

by alveolar macrophages, transform into yeast cells, and trigger a
granulomatous immune response. The granulomas heal over time, often calcifying
into small dense nodules visible on chest X-rays. These calcified pulmonary
nodules are so common in Ohio Valley residents that radiologists routinely
encounter them and must distinguish them from tuberculosis or malignancy. They
are, in effect, scar tissue marking sites where the immune system won a battle
the patient never knew was fought.

### Progressive Disseminated Histoplasmosis

In immunocompromised individuals—particularly those with advanced HIV/AIDS or
receiving immunosuppressive therapy—the granulomatous response fails.
*Histoplasma* yeasts escape from the lungs, enter the bloodstream, and
disseminate to virtually any organ: liver, spleen, bone marrow, adrenal
glands, CNS, skin, and mucous membranes. Progressive disseminated
histoplasmosis is often fatal and can mimic tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, or
lymphoma, frequently causing diagnostic delays. As Money emphasizes, the
fungus's capacity to leave the lungs and penetrate any body tissue is what
makes it so dangerous in the context of immune suppression.
