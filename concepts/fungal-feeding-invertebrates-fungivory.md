# Fungal-Feeding Invertebrates and Fungivory

Updated: 2026-04-18

Fungal-feeding invertebrates (fungivores) form a critical trophic link between fungal decomposers and higher consumers in soil food webs. Their grazing activities influence fungal community composition, decomposition rates, and nutrient cycling in all terrestrial ecosystems.

## Overview of Fungal Feeding

Some invertebrate animals are entirely fungivorous, while others ingest fungi inadvertently along with plant remains or soil (Shaw, 1992; McGonigle, 1997). For vertebrates, fungi serve as primary or temporary food during seasons when little other food is available. The nutritional quality of fungi varies: they contain approximately 40% lipids and 8-10% protein by weight, and have higher nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and micronutrient content than many plant parts (Kinnear et al., 1979; Fogel and Trappe, 1978).

## Vertebrate Mycophagy

Claridge and May (1994) identified 37 species of native and 5 species of feral mammals in Australia exhibiting mycophagy. The brushtailed potoroo (Potorus longipes) obtains more than 25% of its diet by volume from fungi at all times of year, with fungi occurring in feces more than 90% of the time in most months. Body size limits fungal species diversity consumed: small mammals under 150g feed mainly on arbuscular mycorrhizal spores of the Endogonaceae, while large animals like feral pigs eat a wide variety of fungal species (Cheal, 1987).

Hypogeous (subterranean) fungi are particularly important. Fogel (1976) estimated 11,052 to 16,753 fruiting bodies per ha per year in old-growth Douglas fir forests, yielding 2.3-5.4 kg/ha dry mass of fungus. Hypogeous fungi have higher nutrient content than epigeous fungi, making them a higher-quality food resource for mammals.

Reindeer in Fennoscandia rely heavily on lichens during winter, consuming up to 2.6 kg lichens per day during intensive digging periods, with each reindeer requiring approximately 1000 kg/ha dry weight of lichens to sustain through winter (Kumpula, 2001). Reindeer grazing increases lichen nitrogen content from 0.43-0.91% and increases bacterial and fungal-feeding nematode abundance (Stark et al., 2000).

## Invertebrate Fungal Feeding: Collembola

Collembola are among the most important fungal-feeding invertebrates. Their feeding preferences show no consistent pattern across all faunal groups. Key findings:

- Collembola preferred mixed microfungal diets over single species cultures (Reddy and Das, 1983)
- Different fungi had different food values, affecting population growth (Trichoderma, Cladosporium, and Pythium had varying effects on arthropod numbers over 9 weeks)
- The collembolan Onychiurus amatus actively avoided a basidiomycete fungus that caused death without ingestion (Parkinson et al., 1979)
- Collembola show distinct species-specific preferences that differ among genera and species within the same faunal taxon

## Effects on Fungal Community Composition

Fungivorous invertebrates profoundly affect fungal community structure:

- Collembola grazing altered the vertical distribution of competing mycelia of Mycena species in Sitka spruce forests (Newell, 1984a,b)
- Exclusion of fungivorous soil arthropods reduced litter dry mass loss by 23% (Setala and Huhta, 1991)
- At high collembola densities, Mycena galopus was constrained while Marasmius grew at greater depth, suggesting collembola can shift competitive hierarchies among fungal species
- Moderate collembola grazing on extraradical arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphae stimulated root colonization, but high densities reduced it -- the relationship is curvilinear rather than linear (Klironomos and Ursic, 1998)
- At intermediate collembola densities, arbuscular mycorrhizal effects on plant growth of Pulsatilla robertianum were maximal compared to both high densities and absence of collembola (Klironomos et al., 1999)

## Fungal Chemical Defenses Against Grazing

Not all fungi are equally palatable to animals. Specific secondary metabolites act as deterrents:

- The selection by animals must be based on characteristics other than poisons affecting humans, since many Drosophila species tolerate alpha-amanitin from Amanita spp. (Jaenike et al., 1983)
- Fungi producing deterrent compounds can influence the spatial distribution of fungivores
- The spatial distribution of fruiting bodies influences above-ground fungivorous insect feeding activities (Hanski, 1989)
- Most fungal-feeding insects are polyphagous rather than monophagous, increasing the chance of finding at least one fungal species fruiting at any time

## Fungal Responses to Grazing

Grazing effects on fungi are complex and sometimes beneficial:

- Grazing can stimulate fungal growth rates (compensatory growth)
- Fungal spore dispersal by invertebrate vectors (mycophagy facilitates spore dispersal, particularly for hypogeous fungi)
- In systems with diverse fungivorous invertebrate populations, fungi may benefit from the net effect of grazing on fungal community dynamics

## Diptera as Fungal Feeders

Diptera larvae breeding in Agaricales show broad polyphagy: Russula hosts 17 dipteran species, Lactarius 14, while smaller genera collectively host 37 species (Hanski, 1989). Large fleshy mushrooms are heavily invaded by dipteran larvae, and the seasonal appearance of basidiomycete fruit bodies influences insect larval growth, adult development and fecundity.

# Fungal Biomass in Ecosystems

Fungal biomass represents a critical pool of carbon and nutrients in terrestrial ecosystems, varying dramatically across biomes and playing central roles in decomposition, nutrient cycling, and plant-microbe interactions. Understanding fungal biomass dynamics is essential for quantifying [[fungal-role-in-carbon-cycling]] and [[fungal-role-in-nitrogen-cycling]].

In many soil ecosystems, fungi can comprise the largest pool of biomass, even exceeding that of other microorganisms and invertebrates (Gadd, 2004). Fungi also play a fundamental role in the maintenance of soil structure owing to their filamentous branching growth habit and exopolymer production.

## Measuring Fungal Biomass

Fungal biomass is estimated through several metrics, including hyphal length (m per gram dry weight soil), direct biomass measurements, and biochemical markers. Kjøller and Struwe (1982) measured fungal hyphal abundance across different ecosystems, finding that the appropriate metric depends on environmental context.

Hyphal length appears to be a more important fungal investment of energy than biomass in cooler environments where resources are recalcitrant, while biomass with less hyphal extension appears more important in warmer environments with higher quality resources. This trade-off between exploration and exploitation reflects the fundamental [[mycelial-foraging-strategies-nutrient-translocation]] that fungi employ.

Mathematical modelling of fungal growth has focused on variables such as biomass yield and hyphal tip growth, enabling the study of biomass distribution within mycelia in both homogeneous and heterogeneous conditions (Davidson, 2006).

## Biomass Across Ecosystem Types

Tundra ecosystems show the importance of fungal biomass in high-organic-matter-content soils. Schmidt (1999) found hyphal lengths of 393 and 27 m/g dry weight soil in the Levinson-Lessing Valley of Siberia (supporting typical tundra plant communities), versus only 9 m/g dry weight in more fertile, lower-organic-content brown earth soils at Labaz.

Key patterns across ecosystems:

- **[[fungal-biodiversity-alpine-arctic]]**: Low plant biomass but comparatively high litter fall relative to standing biomass; decomposition slow due to poor litter quality and narrow windows for microbial activity; organic matter accumulates, peaty soil profiles evolve; systems dominated by fungi (Heal and Dighton, 1986).

- **[[fungal-biodiversity-forest-floor]]**: High plant biomass, low litter fall, but accumulation occurs due to recalcitrance of litter (low resource quality, high C:N and lignin:N ratios). [[fungal-ecology-forest-floor]] fungal biomass is a major component of soil organic matter.

- **[[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]]**: Very high plant biomass, high litter fall, but sparse litter on soil indicating rapid decomposition from favorable climate and high resource quality. Tropical forests input more litter than temperate (9 t/ha/yr vs 3 t/ha/yr), with 75% of phosphorus and 41% of potassium flux in the litter.

## Nutrient Immobilization in Fungal Biomass

Fungal hyphae colonizing resources incorporate mineral elements into their biomass (immobilization phase), converting these materials to growth. The system is leaky, however, with some nutrients lost as the hyphal front advances. Nutrients temporarily locked up within fungal biomass become available upon fungal death or through grazing by invertebrate fungivores.

In a temperate Douglas fir forest ecosystem, fungal biomass accounts for 5 t/ha of the total soil organic matter and immobilizes significant quantities of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. This immobilization creates a substantial nutrient reservoir within the [[nutrient-retention-soil-food-web]].

## Carbon-Nutrient Ratios

Fungal tissues have characteristically low C:N (6:1) and C:P (15:1) ratios compared to plant litter. Woody litter has C:N of 300-500:1 and C:P of 1850:1. This means fungi must mineralize very large amounts of carbon relative to the nitrogen and phosphorus they require, releasing excess carbon as CO2 while incorporating limited nutrients into biomass.
