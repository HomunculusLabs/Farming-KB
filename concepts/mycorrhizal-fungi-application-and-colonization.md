## Mycorrhizal Fungi Application and Colonization

## Terminology

Precise terminology matters when discussing mycorrhizal associations:

- **Mycorrhizal fungus**: refers to the fungus alone, not in association with a plant root.
- **Mycorrhiza**: a single association of one root with a mycorrhizal fungus (singular).
- **Mycorrhizae**: plural form meaning "fungus-root," referring to all associations of roots and fungi collectively.

## Application Methods

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are applied in many different ways depending on the plant species, the growing system, and the requirements for getting spores (the typical application form) next to the root. There is no one-size-fits-all approach — the method must match the conditions.

### When Surface Application Works

There are places and conditions where applying spores to the soil surface works perfectly well for getting spores next to roots. Colonization can be rapid and quite high under these favorable conditions.

### When Surface Application Fails

There are certain soils and situations where surface application would never be considered because the spores would never reach the roots and therefore would never establish colonization. Key factors that affect spore viability and movement include:

- **Soil compaction**: dense soils prevent spore migration
- **Moisture levels**: spores need adequate moisture to germinate
- **Soil chemistry**: extreme pH or salinity can kill spores
- **[[soil-biota]]**: certain soil organisms may consume spores before they reach roots

### Application Best Practices

The core principle is to **get the spores next to the root without harming the roots or killing the spores**. Additionally:

1. **Place spores in direct contact with root zones** — use seed coatings, root dips, or banding near root systems
2. **Provide fungal foods near the root** — fungi need carbon sources (complex carbohydrates, humic acids) to establish and grow
3. **Avoid harming roots during application** — any root damage reduces colonization potential
4. **Avoid killing spores** — protect spores from UV exposure, extreme temperatures, and chemical residues

## The Role of the Whole Soil Food Web

The entire [[soil-food-web]] is needed to prevent both easy-to-prevent and harder-to-prevent diseases. Bacteria alone can account for only a few of the preventative mechanisms that work in soil. The rest of the food web — fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods — provides the remaining protective functions.

### The "Stall" Effect

When any single species or small group of organisms is added back into a really sick soil, you will typically see initial improvement. However, this improvement **"stalls"** because you must build the entire food web back to reach a state where no more pesticides or inorganic fertilizers are required. A compost tea that delivers the whole food web — not just bacteria — is essential for sustained improvement.

### Why Failures Occur

Where people have had failures with compost tea, it is consistently because they were not getting the full set of organisms extracted into the tea. The tea may not have contained all the organisms needed for comprehensive disease prevention. It is important to:

- Identify the specific disease you are trying to combat
- Know which organisms (bacteria, fungi, protozoa, or nematodes) are needed
- Ensure those organisms reach and establish on leaf surfaces and in soil

## Fungal Biomass Minimums

The **2 microgram minimum** for good fungal biomass in compost tea was established based on empirical observations of where tea consistently prevented disease (mostly mildew and blight) versus where the fungal component was lower and problems occurred. Prevention consistently works above 2 micrograms; below this threshold, results become unreliable.

### More Is Better

Higher fungal biomass correlates with better disease protection. Some systems have achieved fungal biomass levels of **800 micrograms** (not a typo — eight hundred micrograms of fungal biomass per unit volume). At such concentrations, application rates can be reduced — instead of 5 gallons per acre, 2 gallons per acre may still provide excellent protection.

## Organism Dormancy in Tea

To put organisms in compost tea into a dormant (sleep) state, you must induce them to produce dormant stages without killing them. This can be accomplished by:

- **Manipulating osmotic factors** (e.g., adding sugars or salts to change osmotic pressure)
- **Adjusting pH** to levels that trigger dormancy responses

However, approximately **50% of species are lost** during this process — specifically those organisms that lack dormant stages or require longer time to complete dormancy transitions. This significant species loss means that dormant teas will always have reduced diversity compared to fresh teas.

## Soil Biology Diversity

Diversity is critically important in soil microbial communities. Key points:

- **More compost types** (with greater organism diversity) produce better teas
- Most decent composts already contain a huge diversity of organisms, so a single compost source is not quite equivalent to a monoculture
- **Morphotyping** (examining organism shape and structure under a microscope) differentiates many more species of bacteria and fungi than traditional plate counts
- **DNA testing** reveals approximately **25,000 unique DNA strands per gram** of good, healthy soil and compost

### DNA Testing Considerations

There is ongoing scientific discussion about how many base-pair differences in DNA are necessary to define a different species. Some plant pathogens differ from non-pathogens by only a single base pair. Additionally, plasmid DNA complicates species counting. Despite these uncertainties, DNA-based assessment provides far more comprehensive species enumeration than morphological or plate-count methods.

### Compost Source Considerations

The species present in compost generally mirror those in soil, since the organic matter typically comes from materials that were in contact with soil (leaves, tree debris, grass clippings). Exceptions include:

- **Indoor composting** with grocery store vegetable matter (no environmental inputs) — this produces a bizarre set of organisms
- **Desert southwest regions** — these may require deliberate inoculum addition
- **Urban/downtown areas** — depleted soil biology may require inoculation
- **Clopyralid-contaminated materials** — must be excluded from compost (this herbicide persists through composting and kills broadleaf plants)
- **European soils** — may have different organism profiles than North American soils

## Glomalin

Glomalin is a sticky substance produced by many soil organisms — not just vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi. It serves as a general term for various compounds that bind soil particles together.

### Production

- Many bacteria produce glues that hold themselves to soil particles
- VAM fungi also produce these compounds
- R.C. Foster's work in Australia ("The Ultrastructure of the Rhizosphere") documented these sticky materials long before they were named glomalin

### Significance

Glomalin represents only the tip of the iceberg in terms of sticky materials in soil, but its recognition has been important for understanding how soil aggregation and structure develop. The key questions remaining include whether glomalin from different sources (bacterial vs. fungal) shares the same pH characteristics and chemical structure.
