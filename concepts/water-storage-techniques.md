# Water Storage Techniques

Water storage is one of the most critical elements of [[permaculture-design]]. Mollison devotes extensive attention to the design and construction of water storage systems, from small tanks to large dams, arguing that water is the primary limiting factor in most landscapes and that its capture and storage transforms the productive potential of any site.

## The Fundamental Principle

"The flatter the floor that you flood, the more water that you get for dollars spent." This single rule governs dam siting: the best dam sites are at the heads of valleys where the catchment is small and the storage potential is large relative to the wall size, or on ridges where the terrain flattens. On point-of-ridge sites, "that may be flatter than the valley floor," with the added advantage of no flood-rush risk.

## Soil as the Primary Storage

Mollison's most important insight about water storage is that "we store most of our water in our soil." Soil conditioning through [[solomon-humus-building-and-soil-life]] creates enormous subsurface water storage capacity. Conditioned soils commonly average 15-19 degrees Fahrenheit above surrounding soil temperatures in winter because wet soil is "an enormous heat mass" with much air space. This extends the growing season at both ends.

When water drains across conditioned soil, "it encounters a series of ribbed systems that run it out and store it up." Roots follow these crevices and create channels for further water penetration. The goal is to return soil to its "forest absorption capacity" -- the capacity it had before compaction by grazing animals.

## Earth Tank (Dam) Construction

Mollison provides detailed guidance on building earth tanks using the puddled clay core technique:

1. **Site selection**: Choose locations where the natural catchment concentrates water flow, typically in valleys or saddles between ridges. "You walk the valley floor and find where it levels. At the point where it starts to level, you often find that it tightly constricts, and you will find the logical valley dam site."

2. **Key trench**: Dig a trench along the centerline of the dam wall down to impervious clay or rock. This locks the wall into the foundation and prevents water seeping under the dam.

3. **Clay core**: Build a central core of puddled clay within the dam wall. This is the waterproof membrane. The clay core should extend down into the key trench and up above the expected water level.

4. **Compacted earth walls**: Build the embankment on either side of the clay core using compacted earth from the excavation. The wall should have a minimum 3:1 slope on the downstream face and a broader slope on the upstream face.

5. **Spillway**: Design an adequate spillway to safely pass flood flows without overtopping the dam wall. The spillway should be located at the natural edge of the water storage area, excavated into solid ground.

### The Bunyip Level

Mollison describes the Bunyip level, "a sophisticated bit of equipment invented by the ancient Chinese and originally made of pig's guts, but adaptable to modern materials." It is used to find dead level lines across the landscape for swales and dam walls. "You start at your knoll, or you descend across the landscape on your keyline. Or you strike a dead level thing for a swale."

## Tank Siting and Design

Key considerations include:
- **Catchment area**: The ratio of catchment to storage area determines fill rate. "Just when everything is melting, you can follow all the trickles across the landscape and work these little things out."
- **Evaporation**: In arid areas, evaporation can exceed inflow; deeper tanks with smaller surface area minimize losses
- **Seepage**: All dams seep to some degree; clay-rich sites minimize this
- **Siltation**: Design sediment traps upstream to extend the dam's useful life
- **Where water speeds up**: That is where your dam wall goes. "Where it is moving slowly, that is the floor of your dam."

## Biological Nutrient Capture

A critical function of water storage is capturing dissolved nutrients before they leave the property. "Water that looks perfectly clear may carry a heavy weight of dissolved solids." Mollison describes a cascading system:

1. Start with clean high dams
2. Gradually dirty the water with manurial nutrients -- ducks on slowing flows, pig manure washed into ponds
3. Fodder plants, algae, mussels, and snails absorb nutrients at the molecular level
4. Run the enriched water through wetland plant systems for high growth
5. Water that finally leaves the property is clean again

These are "very efficient little biological machines, working at the molecular level, straining out the nutrients before the nutrients leave your property." This approach produces duck manure, fish, and [[wild-foraging-guide]] as additional yields while cleaning the water.

## Lower Dams as Production Systems

Lower dams -- below fields, animal houses, and the house -- serve as production dams rather than energy dams. "Here we produce the highest amount of yield from water. They are the best dams for our fish and our wild life and water chestnuts, crayfish, all those little creatures." These lie where nutrient flow into the dam is highest, supporting productive aquaculture-in-permaculture|aquaculture.

## Sand-Fill Water Storage

In sandy or calcareous landscapes where clay is unavailable, Mollison describes the sand storage technique. Water is stored in sand-filled tanks where it cannot evaporate. Sand stores approximately 50% of its bulk as water. The technique is ancient and particularly valuable in arid regions and coral atolls.

## Roof Water Collection

All buildings should capture roof water for domestic and garden use. Mollison recommends sizing tanks to capture as much roof runoff as possible, with first-flush diverters to exclude debris from the initial rainfall.

## Gley Ponds

Gley is a biological sealing technique for ponds. Organic material that ferments rapidly (banana leaves, grape leaves, papaya) is placed in the pond basin and covered with water. The anaerobic decomposition creates a biological seal that prevents water seepage. This technique is especially useful where clay is not available.

## Hydraulic Rams and Energy Dams

Where sufficient fall exists, hydraulic rams can lift domestic water 10 feet for every foot of fall -- requiring no external energy. These "energy dams" at lower elevations can power small-scale machinery but generally provide less utility for the overall design than high storage dams.

## Integration with Landscape Design

Water storage should be integrated with the overall [[permaculture-zones-system]] design. High dams in zone 3-4 provide irrigation for lower gardens and [[permaculture-fire-protection-design]]. Small ponds in zone 1-2 provide duck habitat, irrigation, and aesthetic value. The [[ohana-watershed-system]] treats the entire water cycle from mountain to sea as a single design unit.
