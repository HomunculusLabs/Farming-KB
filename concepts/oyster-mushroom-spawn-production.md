# Oyster Mushroom Spawn Production

Spawn production is the critical first step in
oyster mushroom cultivation. Spawn serves as the
living inoculum that introduces vigorous mushroom
mycelium into the fruiting substrate. The quality
of spawn directly determines colonization speed,
contamination resistance, and ultimate yield.
Producing reliable, clean spawn requires attention
to detail at every stage from culture maintenance
and strain selection to final spawn preparation
and quality verification before use in production.

## Culture Maintenance

### Stock Cultures on Agar
Mushroom strains are maintained on nutrient agar
media, typically malt extract agar (MEA) or potato
dextrose agar (PDA). Agar slants stored at 2-4
degrees C provide medium-term storage of viable
cultures for 6-12 months. For long-term
preservation, cultures can be stored in distilled
water at room temperature (1-5 years of viability)
or in liquid nitrogen (indefinite storage).
Regular subculturing every 3-6 months maintains
viability but introduces cumulative risks of
genetic drift and contamination over successive
transfers.

### Strain Selection
Different oyster mushroom strains vary
significantly in growth rate, yield potential,
temperature preference, cap color, and shelf life.
Commercial spawn suppliers offer tested strains
optimized for specific growing conditions and
market requirements. Strains are typically
identified by alphanumeric codes. For home
cultivators, maintaining multiple strains allows
experimentation and identification of the best
performers for local conditions.

## Grain Spawn Preparation

### Grain Selection and Preparation
Rye grain is the traditional choice for spawn
production due to its favorable nutritional
profile, moisture retention capacity, and
appropriate grain size. Millet, sorghum, and wheat
are also widely used with good results. Grains
must be hydrated to approximately 50% moisture
content before sterilization. Common hydration
methods include 24-hour soaking followed by
boiling until grains are plump but not bursting,
or direct simmering. Grains should be drained and
surface-dried before loading into containers.

### Container Selection
Quart (1 L) mason jars with modified lids
(inoculation ports with self-healing rubber and
gas exchange filters) are standard for home
production. Commercial operations use autoclavable
polypropylene bags with filter patches that offer
advantages in scalability and easier shaking for
even distribution during colonization.

### Sterilization
Grain spawn requires complete sterilization at 121
degrees C (15 PSI) for 90 minutes in a pressure
cooker or autoclave. Shorter times risk survival
of bacterial endospores (particularly from
Bacillus species), which are extremely heat-
resistant. The large thermal mass of grain means
longer sterilization times are needed compared to
agar media. Slow natural depressurization prevents
grain from boiling inside containers during
pressure release.

### Inoculation
Grain spawn is inoculated under sterile conditions
(laminar flow hood or still air box) with agar
culture wedges, liquid culture, or previously
prepared grain spawn. A single agar wedge per
quart jar is sufficient. When expanding spawn, a
transfer rate of 5-10% by volume provides rapid
colonization while conserving parent stock.
Containers should be gently shaken after
inoculation to distribute mycelium evenly.

### Colonization
Jars are incubated at 24-26 degrees C in darkness.
Oyster mycelium typically colonizes quart jars in
10-21 days. Containers should be shaken once at
approximately 30% colonization to redistribute
mycelium. Fully colonized grain appears uniformly
white with mycelium firmly binding the kernels.
Spawn is ready to use immediately or can be stored
at 2-4 degrees C for 2-4 months.

## Liquid Culture Spawn

Liquid culture offers faster spawn production and
more uniform inoculation compared to grain spawn.
Mycelium is grown in nutrient broth (typically 4%
light malt extract) with gentle agitation. Liquid
culture is inoculated from agar wedges or existing
culture and is ready in 7-14 days. Advantages
include faster preparation, hundreds of
inoculation points per milliliter, and easy serial
expansion. Disadvantages include shorter shelf
life, difficulty detecting contamination visually,
and unsuitability as a standalone fruiting
substrate.

## Spawn Quality Assessment

High-quality spawn exhibits rapid, uniform
colonization; clean white mycelium without
discoloration; firm grain binding; pleasant
mushroomy odor; and absence of colored patches or
slime. Before production use, a small test jar
should be inoculated to verify viability and
purity. Contaminated spawn causes cascading losses
that are costly in both time and materials.
