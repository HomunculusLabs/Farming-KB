## Soil Moisture Monitoring and Field Capacity

Understanding when to water and how much water to apply requires knowledge of two fundamental soil physics concepts: field capacity and the wilting point. Between these two extremes lies the "available water" — the moisture that plants can actually use. Monitoring soil moisture and managing irrigation within this range is the key to productive, water-efficient gardening.

## Field Capacity

When you irrigate or when it rains, each soil particle attracts to itself all the water that will stick to it before the force of gravity overcomes this attraction and pulls surplus water deeper into the ground. A soil that has absorbed all the water it can hold against the force of gravity is said to be at **field capacity** — like a sponge retaining all the water it can.

### What Happens During Irrigation

1. The surface few inches of soil quickly become saturated while deeper layers may still be dry
2. Continuing the irrigation brings layer after layer to field capacity
3. Moisture seeps ever deeper
4. Each irrigation also leaches water-soluble plant nutrients out of the surface layers and moves them down to the full depth the water has reached
5. If you water too long, nutrients are moved so far down that vegetable roots can no longer access them — result: poor growth

## The Wilting Point

The opposite of soil at field capacity is totally dry soil — something rarely if ever seen on this planet. As soil particles dry, the moisture they hold becomes an ever-thinner film on their surfaces. The thinner the film, the more tightly it is held, until moisture clings so tenaciously that vegetable roots can't extract it.

### Three Levels of Wilting

| Level | Description | Impact on Plants |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Permanent wilting point** | Soil so dry that plants can't extract moisture at all | Plants die |
| **Temporary wilting point** | Soil dry enough that plants wilt during the hottest hours but recover at night | Significant stress, reduced quality |
| **Subtle moisture stress** | Soil dry enough that plants don't visibly wilt but growth is slowed | Almost unnoticeable, but quality and yield suffer |

### The Danger of Subtle Stress

Well before soil reaches the temporary wilting point, vegetables — being fragile, highly inbred, weakly rooting creatures — experience subtle, almost unnoticeable moisture stress. It is your obligation to prevent this stress; any stress reduces the quality and amount of production.

## Modern Vegetables and Water Stress

Most [[comparison-heirloom-vegetable-varieties-vs-seed-saving-techniques]] have been bred over the last 50 years to put less energy into [[root-development-and-plant-competition]] more into producing their edible portions more quickly. This was done to maximize profit in an industrial age of oil-driven irrigation. The result: modern vegetable varieties are even weaker at surviving moisture stresses than their predecessors.

**Implication**: Gardeners should try to maintain moisture levels above 70% of field capacity in the top foot (30 cm) of soil. When the top foot has dried to about 60%, bring it back up to capacity.

## The Soil Ball Test

A simple, practical way to determine soil moisture content:

1. Dig down 5-6 inches (12-15 cm) below the surface
2. Take a handful of soil and squeeze it into a ball (the classic ready-to-till test)
3. **If the soil feels damp and sticks together solidly** (unless you have over 20% sand, in which case this test won't work at all): moisture is above 79%
4. **If the moist soil ball sticks together firmly but breaks apart easily**: moisture is around 70%
5. **If the soil feels damp but won't form a ball when you squeeze it hard in your fist**: moisture is below 50% — vegetables are experiencing moisture stress
6. **If the soil feels dry and crumbles**: below 40% — severe stress

## Estimating Moisture Loss

### Daily Evapotranspiration Rates

The amount of water that sun, wind, and heat remove from soil varies with the season and the amount of vegetation, but does not vary with the type of soil. Regardless of texture, all soils lose water at about the same rate because it is not the sun shining on the earth that dries soil out — it is the sun evaporating moisture from plants' leaves (transpiration).

### Adjustments

When estimating moisture loss from reference charts:

- **Cloudy days**: Far less loss — reduce estimate significantly
- **Rain received**: Even a drizzly fraction of an inch that stops the soil from losing moisture counts
- **Bare soil**: Hardly loses moisture at all, especially if cultivated with a dust mulch
- **Canopy coverage**: Dense crop canopy loses much more moisture than sparse plantings

## How Much to Water

### The Basic Plan

- Maintain the top foot (30 cm) of soil above 70% of field capacity
- Bring it back up to capacity every two to five days
- More frequently (giving less water) on sandy soil
- Less often but applying more when you do water on heavier soils
- Replace lost moisture at roughly the rate it is lost, without overwatering (which leaches out soil fertility)

### Practical Guidelines

| Soil Type | Water Per Application | Frequency | Notes |
|-----------|----------------------|-----------|-------|
| Sandy | 0.5-0.75 inch | Every 2-3 days | Apply less per watering, more often |
| Loam | 0.75-1.0 inch | Every 3-5 days | Balanced approach |
| Clay | 1.0-1.5 inches | Every 5-7 days | Apply more per watering, less often |

### Special Cases

- **Seed beds**: Keep surface consistently moist for germination
- **Recently transplanted seedlings**: Keep [[cervantes-root-zone-heating]] moist until established
- **High-moisture crops** (celery, lettuce): May need extra irrigation
- **Drought conditions**: Reduce plant density to stretch available water

## The Water Gauge Test

Most gardeners who irrigate grossly overwater far more often than they underwater. Consumer-grade "lawn and garden" sprinklers spread water thick and fast:

- **Most lawn sprinklers**: Well in excess of 2 inches (5 cm) per hour
- **Oscillating sprinklers**: 2-4 inches (5-10 cm) per hour
- **Soaker hoses and spot sprinklers**: Even higher rates

### How to Test Your Sprinkler

1. Set out a few water gauges — empty tin cans or cylinders with straight sides
2. Put one near the sprinkler, one at the outer limit, and a couple in between
3. Run the sprinkler for exactly 20 or 30 minutes
4. Measure the depth of water in each container
5. Average those amounts and do arithmetic to derive the application rate per hour

This test also reveals the uniformity (or lack thereof) of your sprinkler's distribution.
