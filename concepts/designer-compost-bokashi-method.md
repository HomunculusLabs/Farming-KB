# Designer Compost (Bokashi) Method

Designer compost, known as bokashi in Japan, is a fermentation-based composting method that produces a more potent and biologically diverse soil amendment than traditional aerobic composting.

Developed and refined by [[indigenous-microorganisms]] with bionutrients to create a fortified compost rich in macro and micronutrients.

The method represents a bridge between Korean [[soil-fertility-management]].

## Bokashi vs. Traditional Composting

Traditional composting relies on aerobic decomposition with regular turning to maintain oxygen and temperatures above 130°F.

While effective, it loses significant nitrogen through ammonia volatilization and can take months to produce finished compost.

Bokashi uses [[jadam-anaerobic-fermentation-principles]] instead, inoculating organic materials with [[fish-amino-acid]] or leguminous plant extracts.

For general-purpose compost, a balanced bionutrient blend works well.

The ability to tailor the nutrient profile of bokashi through bionutrient selection is what gives it the "designer" designation.

## Fermentation Process

To prepare bokashi, all dry ingredients are mixed thoroughly in a large container or on a clean surface.

The BIM and molasses are diluted in water and mixed evenly through the dry materials.

The bionutrient is added last and mixed in.

The moisture content should be approximately 60% — the mix should feel damp but not dripping when squeezed in the hand.

The mixture is then packed tightly into a sealed container, pressing out as much air as possible.

The anaerobic environment is essential for the lactic [[lactic-acid-bacteria-knf]] to dominate the fermentation.

The container is left sealed at room temperature for two to four weeks.

During this time, the mixture will develop a sweet-sour smell, indicating successful fermentation.

A foul or putrid smell indicates that undesirable anaerobic bacteria have dominated, usually because of insufficient inoculant or too much moisture.

## Application in the Garden

Finished bokashi is not a direct soil amendment in its fermented state.

It should be buried in garden beds or compost piles, where it will complete decomposition rapidly.

A common method is to dig trenches 6 to 8 inches deep, add the bokashi, and cover with soil.

Within two to four weeks, the bokashi will have decomposed into rich, dark humus teeming with beneficial microorganisms.

This finished material can be used as a top dressing, mixed into planting holes, or incorporated into potting mixes.

Bokashi also makes an excellent [[comparison-biofertilizers-and-soil-inoculants-vs-compost-tea-guide]] base because of its high microbial diversity.

## Integration with Natural Farming Systems

In the natural [[cho-natural-farming-philosophy-of-non-interference]], bokashi represents the practical application of several core principles.

It uses locally available materials, keeping farmers self-reliant and reducing input costs.

It harnesses beneficial microorganisms rather than synthetic chemicals.

It completes the nutrient cycle by converting waste materials into fertility.

As Carandang emphasizes, living soil depends on biodiversed microbial populations and nutrients that create stable, balanced conditions.

Bokashi is one of the most effective methods for establishing and maintaining this living soil.
