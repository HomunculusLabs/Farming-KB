# Designer Compost (Bokashi) Method

Designer compost, known as bokashi in Japan, is a fermentation-based composting method that produces a more potent and biologically diverse soil amendment than traditional aerobic composting.

Developed and refined by [[natural-farming]] practitioners, bokashi combines beneficial [[indigenous-microorganisms]] with bionutrients to create a fortified compost rich in macro and micronutrients.

The method represents a bridge between Korean [[natural-farming-philosophy]] and practical [[soil-fertility-management]].

## Bokashi vs. Traditional Composting

Traditional composting relies on aerobic decomposition with regular turning to maintain oxygen and temperatures above 130°F.

While effective, it loses significant nitrogen through ammonia volatilization and can take months to produce finished compost.

Bokashi uses anaerobic fermentation instead, inoculating organic materials with [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] and other beneficial microorganisms.

The fermentation process preserves more nutrients, particularly nitrogen, because the acidic environment created by lactic acid bacteria suppresses ammonia volatilization.

Bokashi also proceeds faster — the active fermentation phase typically takes two to four weeks, compared to several months for hot composting.

The resulting pre-compost is then buried in soil where it completes decomposition within two to four weeks.

Bokashi can also process materials that are difficult to compost aerobically, including meat, dairy, and cooked foods, making it valuable for household food waste recycling.

## The General Formulation Principle

Gil Carandang's bokashi formulation follows a general principle borrowed from animal feed formulation: 80% carbohydrate (carbon source), 17% protein (nitrogen source), and 3% vitamin/mineral (micronutrients).

When applied to compost, this translates to 80% carbon source, 17% nitrogen source, and 3% trace elements.

This ratio provides balanced nutrition for microbial proliferation while maintaining the correct carbon-to-nitrogen ratio for fermentation.

The real key is not the precise ratios but the inclusion of biodiverse beneficial microorganisms and bionutrients with plenty of carbon and organic matter as substrate.

## The Philippine Basic Recipe

The bokashi recipe developed at Herbana Farms in the Philippines uses locally available materials and serves as a template that can be adapted to any region.

The ingredients and proportions are:

Rice bran — 10 kilograms, serving as the primary carbon source.

Copra meal — 20 kilograms, providing protein from coconut residue after oil extraction.

Coco peat — 20 kilograms, adding carbon, structure, and moisture retention.

Chicken manure — 30 kilograms, the primary nitrogen source, rich in NPK and micronutrients.

Charcoal dust — 20 kilograms, serving as a carbon substrate that provides surface area for microbial colonization.

BIM (Beneficial Indigenous Microorganism) — 1 liter, the microbial inoculant.

Molasses — 1 kilogram, feeding the microorganisms and initiating fermentation.

Bionutrient — 1 liter, a fermented plant extract providing additional macro and micronutrients.

The total batch weighs approximately 100 kilograms when all ingredients are combined.

## Ingredient Substitutions

One of the strengths of the bokashi method is its adaptability to locally available materials.

Rice bran can be substituted with wheat bran or any inexpensive grain bran.

Copra meal (coconut residue) can be replaced with corn meal, soybean meal, or any legume-based meal with ample protein.

Coco peat can be substituted with peat moss, sawdust, or any material high in carbon and lignin.

Any kind of grain hay can also work as the carbon component.

Any animal manure can replace chicken manure, though chicken manure is preferred because of its higher concentrations of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and calcium.

Charcoal dust is preferred over other carbon forms because its porous structure provides an ideal substrate for microbial proliferation.

## The Role of Molasses

Molasses plays a critical role in bokashi fermentation.

As a sugar source, it feeds the beneficial microorganisms, dramatically increasing their population during the fermentation period.

The high sugar content also lowers the pH, creating the acidic environment that suppresses pathogens and preserves nutrients.

Crude sugar or molasses must be used — refined white sugar is chemically bleached and can harm the beneficial microorganisms.

Molasses also provides trace minerals including iron, calcium, magnesium, and potassium, supplementing the micronutrient component of the formulation.

## Bionutrient Customization

The bionutrient component of bokashi can be customized based on the intended use of the finished compost.

If the goal is to promote flowering and fruiting, the bionutrient should emphasize fermented extracts high in potassium, such as banana-squash-papaya extract or seaweed extract.

If the goal is vegetative growth, bionutrients rich in nitrogen should be emphasized, such as [[fish-amino-acid]] or leguminous plant extracts.

For general-purpose compost, a balanced bionutrient blend works well.

The ability to tailor the nutrient profile of bokashi through bionutrient selection is what gives it the "designer" designation.

## Fermentation Process

To prepare bokashi, all dry ingredients are mixed thoroughly in a large container or on a clean surface.

The BIM and molasses are diluted in water and mixed evenly through the dry materials.

The bionutrient is added last and mixed in.

The moisture content should be approximately 60% — the mix should feel damp but not dripping when squeezed in the hand.

The mixture is then packed tightly into a sealed container, pressing out as much air as possible.

The anaerobic environment is essential for the lactic acid bacteria to dominate the fermentation.

The container is left sealed at room temperature for two to four weeks.

During this time, the mixture will develop a sweet-sour smell, indicating successful fermentation.

A foul or putrid smell indicates that undesirable anaerobic bacteria have dominated, usually because of insufficient inoculant or too much moisture.

## Application in the Garden

Finished bokashi is not a direct soil amendment in its fermented state.

It should be buried in garden beds or compost piles, where it will complete decomposition rapidly.

A common method is to dig trenches 6 to 8 inches deep, add the bokashi, and cover with soil.

Within two to four weeks, the bokashi will have decomposed into rich, dark humus teeming with beneficial microorganisms.

This finished material can be used as a top dressing, mixed into planting holes, or incorporated into potting mixes.

Bokashi also makes an excellent compost tea base because of its high microbial diversity.

## Integration with Natural Farming Systems

In the natural farming philosophy, bokashi represents the practical application of several core principles.

It uses locally available materials, keeping farmers self-reliant and reducing input costs.

It harnesses beneficial microorganisms rather than synthetic chemicals.

It completes the nutrient cycle by converting waste materials into fertility.

As Carandang emphasizes, living soil depends on biodiversed microbial populations and nutrients that create stable, balanced conditions.

Bokashi is one of the most effective methods for establishing and maintaining this living soil.
