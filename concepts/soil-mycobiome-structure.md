# Soil Mycobiome Structure

The soil mycobiome encompasses the total fungal community inhabiting soil
environments, representing the most diverse fungal habitat on Earth. Soil
fungi drive critical ecosystem processes including organic matter decomposition,
nutrient cycling, plant symbiosis, and soil aggregation. Understanding the
structure of soil fungal communities is essential for predicting ecosystem
responses to environmental change.

## Community Composition

Soil fungal communities are dominated by a few major functional groups.
Saprotrophic fungi, which decompose organic matter, typically comprise the
largest fraction, accounting for 40-60% of sequences in most surveys.
Mycorrhizal fungi, which form mutualistic associations with plant roots,
represent 20-40%, with the balance made up of plant pathogens, animal
parasites, and endophytes.

Taxonomically, Ascomycota dominates most soil environments, particularly
in agricultural and disturbed soils. Sordariomycetes, Eurotiomycetes, and
Dothideomycetes are the most abundant classes. Basidiomycota, especially
Agaricomycetes, are proportionally more important in forest soils and
undisturbed ecosystems. Early-diverging lineages including Mucoromycota and
Chytridiomycota are present at lower abundances but play unique ecological
roles.

## Vertical Distribution

Soil fungal communities change dramatically with depth. The organic horizon
and upper mineral soil layers harbor the highest diversity and biomass, driven
by inputs of plant litter and root exudates. Diversity typically declines
exponentially with depth, though some specialized fungi, including certain
Mortierellomycota and deep-soil Ascomycota, are preferentially found at depth.

The transition from organic to mineral horizons involves a shift from
predominantly aerobic, fast-growing decomposers to slower-growing, stress-
tolerant taxa. Mycorrhizal fungi are distributed throughout the soil profile,
following host root distributions, while saprotrophs concentrate near organic
matter hotspots.

## Drivers of Community Structure

Soil fungal community composition is shaped by interacting biotic and abiotic
factors. Soil pH is often the strongest single predictor of fungal community
composition across broad spatial scales, consistent with patterns observed in
bacterial communities. However, fungal communities show stronger responses to
vegetation type and land use than bacterial communities.

Climate variables including mean annual temperature and precipitation strongly
influence fungal diversity patterns. Tropical soils consistently show higher
fungal diversity than temperate or boreal soils, though the relationship is
modulated by soil properties and vegetation. Aridity filters out moisture-
sensitive taxa, leading to distinct arid-land fungal assemblages.

Plant community composition directly influences soil fungi through litter
quality, root exudates, and mycorrhizal host specificity. Changes in plant
communities due to invasion, succession, or land use change propagate through
the soil mycobiome with measurable effects on ecosystem function.

## Spatial Scales and Patterns

Soil fungal communities exhibit distance-decay patterns similar to those
observed in plant and animal communities. Community similarity decreases with
geographic distance, reflecting both dispersal limitation and environmental
sorting. The spatial scale at which environmental factors dominate over
dispersal limitation varies by fungal group and habitat connectivity.

Global-scale surveys have revealed distinct biogeographic regions for soil
fungi, broadly corresponding to major biome types. Tedersoo et al. (2014)
identified biome-specific fungal assemblages that were more strongly
differentiated than those of plants or bacteria, suggesting that fungal
distributions may be more tightly coupled to climate and soil conditions.

## Functional Implications

The structure of the soil mycobiome directly influences ecosystem function.
Saprotrophic community composition affects decomposition rates and nutrient
mineralization patterns. Mycorrhizal fungal diversity influences plant
community productivity and stability through complementarity in nutrient
acquisition strategies. Pathogenic fungi regulate plant population dynamics
through density-dependent mortality effects.

Loss of fungal diversity, whether through land use change, pollution, or
climate change, can reduce ecosystem resilience. Experimental studies have
shown that reduced fungal diversity leads to slower decomposition, lower
plant productivity, and increased susceptibility to plant invasions.

## Sampling and Methodological Considerations

Characterizing soil mycobiome structure requires careful attention to sampling
design. Soil fungal communities are spatially heterogeneous at scales from
millimeters to kilometers, necessitating composite sampling strategies.
DNA extraction methods favor different fungal groups depending on cell wall
composition and hyphal density, introducing methodological bias. Primer choice
for ITS amplification significantly affects community composition estimates.
