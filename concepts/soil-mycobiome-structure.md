# Soil Mycobiome Structure
 The soil mycobiome encompasses the total fungal community inhabiting soil environments, representing the most diverse fungal habitat on Earth. Soil
fungi drive critical [[fungal-ecosystem-processes-overview]] including organic matter decomposition, [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]], plant symbiosis, and soil aggregation.
Understanding the structure of soil fungal communities is essential for predicting ecosystem responses to environmental change.

## Community Composition
 Soil fungal communities are dominated by a few major functional groups.
Saprotrophic fungi, which decompose organic matter, typically comprise the
largest fraction, accounting for 40-60% of sequences in most surveys.
Mycorrhizal fungi, which form mutualistic associations with plant roots,
represent 20-40%, with the balance made up of plant pathogens, animal
parasites, and endophytes.

Taxonomically, Ascomycota dominates most soil environments, particularly in
agricultural and disturbed soils. Sordariomycetes, Eurotiomycetes, and
Dothideomycetes are the most abundant classes. Basidiomycota, especially
Agaricomycetes, are proportionally more important in forest soils and
undisturbed ecosystems. Early-diverging lineages including Mucoromycota and
Chytridiomycota are present at lower abundances but play unique ecological
[[dighton-fungal-roles-in-nutrient-cycling]] cycling and decomposition.

## Vertical Distribution

Soil fungal communities change dramatically with depth. The organic horizon and
upper mineral soil layers harbor the highest diversity and biomass, driven by
inputs of plant litter and root exudates. Diversity typically declines
exponentially with depth, though some specialized fungi, including certain
Mortierellomycota and deep-soil Ascomycota, are preferentially found at depth.

The transition from organic to mineral horizons involves a shift from
predominantly aerobic, fast-growing decomposers to slower-growing,
stress-tolerant taxa. Mycorrhizal fungi are distributed throughout the soil
profile, following host root distributions, while saprotrophs concentrate near
organic matter hotspots at the soil surface.

## Drivers of Community Structure

Soil fungal community composition is shaped by interacting biotic and abiotic
factors. Soil pH is often the strongest single predictor of fungal community
composition across broad spatial scales. However, fungal communities show
stronger responses to vegetation type and land use than [[cultivar-endorhiza-bacterial-communities-cannabis]].

Climate variables including mean annual temperature and precipitation strongly
influence fungal diversity patterns. Tropical soils consistently show higher
fungal diversity than temperate or boreal soils, though the relationship is
modulated by soil properties and vegetation. Plant community composition
directly influences soil fungi through [[dighton-litter-quality-fungal-decomposition-rates]], root exudates, and
mycorrhizal host specificity.

## Spatial Scales and Patterns

Soil fungal communities exhibit distance-decay patterns similar to those
observed in plant and animal communities. Community similarity decreases with
geographic distance, reflecting both [[fungal-dispersal-limitation-biogeographic-barriers]] and environmental
sorting. Global-scale surveys have revealed distinct biogeographic regions for
soil fungi, broadly corresponding to major biome types.

Tedersoo et al. (2014) identified biome-specific fungal assemblages that were
more strongly differentiated than those of plants or bacteria, suggesting that
fungal distributions may be more tightly coupled to climate and soil
conditions. These findings have important implications for predicting how soil
fungal communities will respond to [[decomposition]] dynamics under changing
climate regimes.

## Functional Implications

The structure of the soil mycobiome directly influences ecosystem function.
Saprotrophic community composition affects decomposition rates and nutrient
mineralization patterns. Mycorrhizal fungal diversity influences plant
community productivity and stability through complementarity in nutrient
acquisition strategies.

Pathogenic fungi regulate plant population dynamics through density-dependent
mortality effects. Loss of fungal diversity, whether through land use change,
pollution, or climate change, can reduce ecosystem resilience. Experimental
studies have shown that reduced fungal diversity leads to slower decomposition,
lower plant productivity, and increased susceptibility to plant invasions.
