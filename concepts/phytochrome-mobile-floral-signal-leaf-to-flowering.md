# Phytochrome Mobile Floral Signal: From Leaf to Flowering

A surprising finding in photoperiod research is that the phytochrome responsible
for measuring day length is located in the leaves, not in the shoot tip. This
contrasts with phototropism, where Darwin showed that the light-sensing "eye" is
at the shoot apex. The discovery that leaves perceive photoperiodic signals and
communicate flowering information to the rest of the plant revealed the existence
of a mobile floral signal, later identified as the protein florigen.

## The Locality Surprise

Darwin's phototropism experiments established that the shoot tip perceives
directional light and signals the stem to bend. It would be natural to assume
that the same tip-based system mediates photoperiodic flowering. However,
experiments demonstrated that this is not the case.

When researchers shone light on different parts of a plant in the middle of the
night, they discovered that illuminating any single leaf was sufficient to
regulate flowering in the entire plant. A single leaf could perceive a red light
flash and transmit a signal that initiated or suppressed flowering at the shoot
apex, many centimeters away. Conversely, if all leaves were pruned from a plant,
leaving only the stem and the shoot apex, the plant became completely blind to
photoperiodic light signals — even if the entire remaining plant was illuminated,
no flowering response occurred.

## Implications for Plant Architecture

This finding has profound implications for understanding plant architecture. The
leaf is the primary photoreceptor organ for photoperiodism, serving as a
distributed sensor array. Each leaf independently monitors the light environment
and contributes to the plant's overall assessment of day length. The system is
redundant: no single leaf is essential, but the collective input from all leaves
provides robust photoperiodic perception. If one leaf is shaded by a neighboring
plant while others receive full light, the plant can still make an accurate
photoperiodic assessment.

## The Mobile Signal: Florigen

The existence of a mobile signal traveling from leaf to shoot apex was
hypothesized for decades before its molecular identity was discovered. This
hypothetical signal was named "florigen" (flower-inducing substance). The long
search for florigen ended in 2007 when multiple research groups independently
demonstrated that FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) protein is the long-sought mobile floral
signal. FT is produced in leaves under inductive photoperiods and travels through
the phloem to the shoot apical meristem, where it interacts with FD protein to
activate floral identity genes.

FT belongs to the phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein (PEBP) family and
functions as a component of a "florigen activation complex" (FAC) at the shoot
apex. The discovery of FT as florigen was a landmark achievement in plant
biology, resolving one of the oldest questions in the field.

## Phytochrome in the Leaf

Phytochrome proteins are expressed throughout the plant, but the leaves are the
critical site for photoperiod perception. Phytochrome in leaf cells absorbs red
and far-red light and initiates a signaling cascade that ultimately regulates
CONSTANS (CO) gene expression. CO protein, in turn, activates FT transcription.
The entire pathway from light perception to mobile signal production occurs within
leaf tissue, making the leaf the computational center for photoperiodic decisions.

## Contrast with Phototropism

The contrast between phototropism and photoperiodism illustrates the modularity
of plant sensory systems. Phototropism uses blue-light photoreceptors (phototropins)
localized at the shoot tip to detect light direction, with the response occurring
locally through asymmetric auxin redistribution. Photoperiodism uses red/far-red
photoreceptors (phytochromes) distributed throughout the leaves to detect day
length, with the response occurring remotely through a protein signal that travels
through the vascular system. Different stimuli, different receptors, different
locations, different signaling mechanisms — yet both systems serve the plant's
need to optimize its light environment for growth and reproduction.

## Agricultural Relevance

Understanding that leaves are the photoperiodic sensor has practical implications
for crop management. Defoliation (leaf removal) can delay or prevent flowering in
photoperiod-sensitive crops. In cannabis cultivation, the number and health of
leaves influences the efficiency of photoperiodic transition. Light must reach
the leaves, not just the flowers, for effective photoperiodic control. Plant
spacing and canopy architecture that ensures adequate leaf exposure to light are
important considerations for maximizing flowering response.

## Grafting Experiments

Grafting experiments provided some of the earliest evidence for a mobile floral
signal. When a leaf from a plant induced to flower was grafted onto a
non-induced plant, the non-induced plant often flowered as well, demonstrating
that something mobile from the flowering leaf could transmit the flowering signal
across the graft junction. These experiments, conducted in the early 20th century,
strongly suggested a transmissible substance but could not identify it. Modern
molecular techniques finally resolved this question with the identification of
FT protein as the mobile signal.

## Antiflorigen

The existence of florigen implies the possible existence of an "antiflorigen" —
a mobile signal that suppresses flowering. Indeed, a related protein called
TERMINAL FLOWER 1 (TFL1) in Arabidopsis functions as an antiflorigenic signal,
maintaining the shoot apical meristem in a vegetative state. The balance between
florigen (FT) and antiflorigen (TFL1) at the shoot apex determines whether the
plant remains vegetative or transitions to flowering. This antagonistic
relationship adds another layer of regulation to the photoperiodic flowering
pathway.

## Evolutionary Conservation

The FT-florigen pathway is remarkably conserved across flowering plants. FT
homologs have been identified in rice (Hd3a), poplar, tomato, and many other
species, all functioning as mobile flowering signals. This conservation suggests
that the leaf-to-apex floral signaling system evolved early in flowering plant
history and has been maintained with relatively little modification for over 100
million years. Even in day-neutral plants, which do not require specific day
lengths to flower, the FT pathway still mediates the floral transition in
response to other cues such as age or temperature.
