# Fukuoka's Inductive vs Deductive Reasoning in Agricultural Research

## Source
*[[masanobu-fukuoka]] — The Natural Way of Farming (1985)*

## Summary
Fukuoka drew a sharp philosophical distinction between inductive and deductive approaches
to agricultural knowledge, arguing that [[fukuoka-illusions-scientific-farming-centrifugal-centripetal-model]]'s reliance on inductive
experimentation can only prevent crop losses while true deductive — and ultimately
intuitive — reasoning is required for genuine yield improvements. He likened the two
methods to two climbers ascending a rock face, where induction checks footing while
deduction pulls upward, but argued that both remain trapped within a limited framework
that fukuoka [[fukuoka-critique-inductive-deductive]] [[fukuoka-textdoc-intuitive-reasoning-beyond-induction-deduction]] agricultural research alone can transcend.

## Inductive Experimentation

Inductive agricultural research proceeds by studying all aspects of crop production and
conducting many different tests in an attempt to develop increasingly economical and
high-yielding methods of cultivation. Scientists run experiments oblivious to the
direction in which their research takes them. They may be pleased with the results and
confident that the amassing of new data leads to steady progress and scientific
achievement.

But in the absence of a clear goal by which to set their course, Fukuoka argued, this
activity is just aimless wandering — not progress. The scientist is well aware of the
restrictive and circumstantial nature of inductive research, and does give some thought
to deductive reasoning, but ends up relying on the inductive approach because it leads
more directly to practical and certain success.

## Deductive Reasoning as Conventionally Understood

Deductive experimentation has never had much appeal to scientists because they are
unable to get a good handle on what appears to many a whimsical process. In addition,
as this requires a great deal of time and space, it runs counter to the natural
inclinations of scientists, who like to hole up in their laboratories.

In practice, deduction in science is merely defined as the inverse of induction.
Scientists take an oddball idea from an eccentric farmer, analyze it, study it,
reconstruct it, and verify it through inductive experimentation. They then raise the
idea to the level of a universally applicable technique. But this "deduction" is still
trapped within the inductive framework — it merely provides the initial inspiration
that induction then processes.

## The Climbing Analogy

Fukuoka likened induction and deduction to two climbers ascending a rock face. The
lower of the two, who checks his footing before giving the climber in the lead a
boost, plays an inductive role, while the lead climber, who lets down a rope and
pulls the lower climber up, plays a deductive role.

The two are complementary and together form a whole. Although [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]]
has relied primarily on inductive experimentation, progress has been made as well in
deductive reasoning. But because deduction here is merely a concept defined in
relation to induction, we may see a gradual increase in yields but are unlikely to see
a dramatic improvement. The two climbers make only slow progress and will never go
beyond the peak they have already sighted.

## True Deduction and Intuitive Reasoning

To attain dramatically improved yields, one would have to rely not on this restricted
notion of deduction, but on a broader deductive method — namely, intuitive reasoning.
In addition to the two climbers with a rope, other radically different methods of
reaching the top of the mountain are possible, such as descending onto the peak by
rope from a helicopter.

True deduction originates at a point beyond the world of phenomena. It arises when one
has acquired a philosophical understanding of the true essence of the natural world and
grasped the ultimate goal. All that man sees is a superficial image of nature. Unable
to perceive the ultimate goal, he assumes deduction to be merely the inverse of
induction and can go no further.

Experiments in which deduction is treated as the counterpart of induction have brought
us the confusion [[fukuoka-road-critique-of-modern-science]]. Even in agriculture, farmers and scientists are
confounding measures for preventing crop losses with means for raising yields, and by
discussing both on equal terms, are only prolonging the current stagnation.

## The Role of Eccentric Farmers

The initial inspiration that lays the rails for agricultural progress is often the
deductive notion of a progressive farmer or a hint left by someone who has nothing to
do with farming. Generally lacking scope and universality, such an idea tends to slide
back into oblivion unless the scientist recognizes it as a clue.

The creative roots of [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] lie in true intuitive understanding. The point
of departure must be a true grasp of nature gained by fixing one's gaze on the natural
world that extends beyond actions and events in one's immediate surroundings. An
infinitude of yield-improving possibilities lie hidden here. One must look beyond the
immediate.
