# Soil Collapse and Desertification

[[desertification]]. Understanding this process is essential for anyone working in dryland agriculture, land reclamation, or [[crumb-structure]]:

1. **Deforestation** — removal of tree cover exposes soil to erosion and eliminates deep-rooted water uptake
2. **Over-grazing** — compacts soil and removes the grass cover that protects surface structure
3. **Fire** — burns dryland grasses and wheat stubble, reducing [[humus]] content and baking surface soils into fine dust
4. **Mechanical compaction** — heavy or fast farm machinery compresses pore spaces
5. **Repeated cropping** — wheat cropping reduces soil crumb structure from 92% (in free-draining uncleared soils) to as little as 12%

### The Cascade of Degradation

When humus is removed, the following cascade occurs:

1. **Clay deflocculation** — sodium ions displace calcium in the clay complex, causing clay particles to disperse
2. **Pore space elimination** — dispersed silty clay particles clog the air spaces that once constituted 40–60% of soil volume
3. **Compaction** — the soil crumb structure collapses into a cemented surface layer
4. **Vertical compression** — a full 1-metre depth of soil can compress into just 10–20 cm of residual fine sand and dust
5. **B-horizon cementation** — a concrete-like "B" horizon forms beneath the collapsed surface, impermeable to roots and water

### Salt Concentration

Salt that was originally dispersed throughout the full depth of the open soil layer becomes concentrated in the narrow collapsed zone. This creates highly saline conditions that are lethal to most plants. In West Australian soils over granites and gneissic areas, salt reserves to 20 metres deep range from 3,400 to 19,000 tonnes per hectare, with most lying below 2 metres — below the forest root zone.

### Anaerobic Conditions

Collapsed soils become anaerobic (oxygen-deprived), producing several toxic conditions:

- **Elevated CO₂** — soil carbon dioxide from root respiration can reach 5% (compared to 1% in healthy soils and 0.03% in atmospheric air), which is lethal to plant roots
- **Methane and sulphur dioxide** — anaerobic bacteria produce these gases as byproducts of humus decay
- **Ethylene** — another product of anaerobic decomposition that further inhibits plant growth
- **Osmotic stress** — concentrated salt water prevents roots from taking up water even when moisture is present

### Water Cycling Disruption

[[soil-collapse]] disrupts the natural water cycle in several ways:

- **Wind-borne silt** from dust storms fills rock crevices that once fed underground aquifers, sealing off water intake areas
- **Perched water tables** form above the cemented B-horizon during rains, while subsoils remain dry
- **Overland flow increases dramatically** — cropped soils absorb only 5–16 cm of rain over 5 hours, compared to 30 cm in adjacent uncropped soils
- **Surface evaporation intensifies** — shallow water tables (around 1.8 m) allow capillary action to bring salt to the surface, forming white salt crusts (efflorescences)

## The End State: Desertification

The final result is a self-generating cycle of degradation:

- Collapsed soil areas sink 1–2 metres, producing sunken salt pan areas in valleys
- **Peripheral collapse** — salt pans cause further edge swamping and salting of adjacent soils
- **Salt lakes** may develop from winter salt pans, with increasingly severe collapse effects on surrounding soils
- **Salt transport** — salt is washed downstream and into dams, spreading the problem
- **Mottled and waterlogged soils** with iron stone layers or "buckshot" concretions appear at the base of slopes
- **Tree death** — dying trees and shallow salt pans mark the progression

## The Timescale Problem

The natural process of building an argillic (clay) skin plus iron and silica cements into an impermeable layer is believed to take 10,000 or more years of alternate wet and dry seasons. Deforestation and the subsequent solubility of clay due to mobile salt can speed up this natural process considerably. This means that what took nature millennia to build can be destroyed in decades of poor land management.

## Why Tree Planting Alone Fails

A critical insight from the soil collapse model is that planting trees in soils already undergoing collapse is futile. The trees cannot survive because:

- Root growth is inhibited by lethal CO₂ levels and anaerobic conditions
- Roots face osmotic stress from concentrated salt
- Surface evaporation causes rapid drought stress
- The cemented B-horizon prevents root penetration to deeper moisture

Until the process of soil collapse itself is stopped — typically through [[interceptor-banks]] that halt overland flow and seepage — trees will not survive long enough to reverse the degradation.

## The Role of Humus

The major factor in soil crumb formation is the presence of **polysaccharides** (long-chain sugars) and gels produced by humus. This humic material is critical to good soil structure. While burning stubble further reduces soil humus, adding green crop material to neutral or alkaline soils will restore crumb structure — but only if salt-water deflocculation can be stopped simultaneously.

## Reversal Requirements

Effective reversal of soil collapse requires addressing the root causes:

- **Stop overland flow** — through interceptor banks or contour earthworks
- **Restore humus** — through green manuring, composting, and eliminating stubble burning
- **Cease compaction** — by reducing heavy machinery use and controlling grazing
- **Manage salt** — through drainage, flushing, and tree planting (once flow is intercepted)
- **Replace sodium with calcium** — using gypsum where pH exceeds 8.5
