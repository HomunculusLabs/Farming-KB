    10|# Edible Mushroom Families — Boleti, Russula, Lactarius, Clavaria, and Puffballs (Palmer)
    11|
    12|Updated: 2026-04-18
    13|
    14|Source: Julius A. Palmer, Jr., *About Mushrooms* (1894). Chapters on edible species identification and the foraging tradition.
    15|
    16|## Palmer's Approach to Edible Mushroom Identification
    17|
    18|Palmer advocated a conservative, knowledge-based approach to mushroom eating: learn to identify a limited number of easily recognized edible species, and leave all others alone. He stated: "I may select twenty varieties, easily recognized, advise that all others be let alone, and follow my own counsel."
    19|
    20|His personal testing protocol was methodical: take a small piece of cap flesh (avoiding the spore-bearing gills), eat it first thing in the morning. If no ill effects follow, increase the dose daily until a whole specimen is consumed. After this, never reverse the decision.
    21|
    22|Key principle: a variety once found edible never changes its properties — what is true of other plants is true of these.
    23|
    24|## Puffball Family (Lycoperdaceae)
    25|
    26|### Identification
    27|Puffballs are among the safest edible mushrooms for beginners. Palmer noted that "most persons will readily recognize the puff-ball family — those round, white balls which come after rains."
    28|
    29|### Edibility Rule
    30|"Wherever they cut open, perfectly white, they may be eaten; there are no poisonous varieties."
    31|
    32|### Critical Safety Notes
    33|- Only eat puffballs that are PURE WHITE throughout when cut open
    34|- Any discoloration (yellow, purple, green, black) indicates the specimen is past prime or is an immature egg stage of an Amanita
    35|- The outer skin should be removed before cooking
    36|- Small puffballs tend to be more tender and desirable

    45|## Boleti (Boletaceae)
    46|
    47|### Identification
    48|Boleti are distinguished by the under-surface of the cap being composed of a "porous or sponge-like stratum" instead of gills. This tubular hymenophore is the key diagnostic feature separating them from gilled mushrooms.
    49|
    50|### Habitat and Foraging
    51|Palmer observed Italians making a harvest of Boleti in American forests, gathering "like varieties to those they eat at home." He found them "generally delicious" — this reflects the strong European foraging tradition for porcini and related species.
    52|
    53|### Color Change and Edibility
    54|Palmer addressed the common belief that color change indicates poison: "Nothing could be more false." He noted:
    55|- *Boletus cyaneuscens* turns "throughout the most beautiful blue" when bruised
    56|- Many species assume blue shades in lighter degrees when cut
    57|- One species turns blood red wherever bruised or cut
    58|- All of these color-changing species are edible
    59|
    60|### Contrast with Amanita
    61|The dangerous Amanita "remains the purest white under all conditions" — color change is NOT an indicator of poison.
    62|
    63|See [[porcini-boletus-guide]] for modern identification details.

    65|## Russula (Russulaceae)
    66|
    67|### Identification
    68|Palmer segregated the Russula family by three features:
    69|1. **Brittle flesh** — the mushroom breaks cleanly and crisply, unlike the fibrous texture of many other genera
    70|2. **No juice** — unlike the related Lactarius, Russula species do not exude latex when broken
    71|3. **Equal gills** — the gills (thin lines or rays) are of equal length, not interspersed with shorter ones
    72|
    73|### Evidence of Edibility
    74|Palmer's confidence in the edibility of pleasant-tasting Russula species came from multiple lines of evidence:
    75|- Forest animals (squirrels, snakes, land-tortoises) were observed eating them
    76|- Partly gnawed specimens were commonly found
    77|- He tested many species personally following his gradual-dose protocol
    78|
    79|His correspondent Paul Casamajor confirmed this approach: "acting on this suggestion, he had gathered with his children a basket full, being careful to exclude all not Russula, and then, tasting each specimen, he cooked them and made an evening meal with perfect safety."
    80|
    81|### Caution
    82|Some Russula species are acrid or peppery in taste. Palmer advised tasting a small piece raw — if unpleasant, avoid the species. While Palmer never encountered dangerous Russula species, modern mycology recognizes some mildly toxic members. See [[russula-edible-species]].
    83|
    84|## Lactarius (Milk Mushrooms)
    85|
    86|### Identification
    87|Closely resembling Russula but distinguished by the production of a milky or colored latex when the flesh is broken. Palmer described this as "an albuminous juice, holding resin in solution."
    88|
    89|### Edibility
    90|When the latex is naturally mild, the mushroom is edible. In most cases where it is not mild, "the action of salt and water changes its nature, and leaves the substance of the mushroom unhurt and good for food."
    91|
    92|### Preparation
    93|Acrid or peppery Lactarius species can be rendered edible by:
    94|- Soaking in salted water (drawing out the acrid latex)
    95|- Thorough cooking, which destroys the irritating compounds
    96|
    97|### Modern Note
    98|See [[edible-wild-mushroom-guide]] and [[lactarius-cultivation-attempts]] for modern perspectives.

   100|## Clavaria (Coral Fungi)
   101|
   102|### Identification
   103|Clavaria species appear as "club-moss, or coral-shaped group, growing in our forests." They are characterized by:
   104|- Little branches dividing and subdividing from a common base
   105|- Height ranging from a few inches to two feet
   106|- Multiple varieties with different branching patterns
   107|
   108|### Culinary Quality
   109|Palmer praised Clavaria highly: "more delicate food than cauliflower, nicer in soup or stew than vermicelli." He recommended eating them "when not bitter, as either of these, daily through the season."
   110|
   111|### Foraging Notes
   112|- Cut off just below the top
   113|- Gather perfectly clean specimens
   114|- Choose only young, hard specimens
   115|- They soon become full of maggots
   116|- Very abundant around Boston during fall
   117|
   118|## Foraging Safety Principles (Palmer)
   119|
   120|### The Discrimination Principle
   121|"There is only one way to tell a mushroom from a toadstool; the same discrimination which is applied to other foods." Palmer compared mushroom identification to knowing currants from grapes or wild parsnip from domestic — it requires learning, not magic tests.
   122|
   123|### The Amanita Avoidance Principle
   124|Learn the three Amanita marks (volva, ring, scurfy warty cap). If all three are present, avoid the mushroom. This single rule eliminates virtually all deadly poisoning risk.
   125|
   126|### The Gradual Testing Principle
   127|For any species not positively identified as safe, use Palmer's gradual dose protocol: small piece first, increasing daily, never eat an unidentified species in quantity.
   128|
   129|### The Consistency Principle
   130|"A variety once found edible never changes its properties." An edible species remains edible regardless of season, location, or growing conditions. This has been confirmed by modern mycology.
   131|
   132|### Historical Context
   133|Palmer noted the state of public knowledge in 1877: "It seemed to me in 1874, and it appears still, that such general ignorance as exists in regard to a large, well-defined, and important natural kingdom is nothing less than a disgrace." He credited his work with increasing public interest: "its most valuable fruit is in the number now interested compared with those who cared anything about it fifteen years ago."
