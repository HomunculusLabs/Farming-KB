## Winter Preparation of Droppings

Winter droppings required active fermentation management:

- Placed in a heap and allowed to heat gently to 80-90 degrees F.
- Turned over twice daily to release heat and steam.
- If neglected, the natural spawn in the droppings was destroyed.
- Fresh dung, when trodden very hard, stopped over-heating and retained
  heat longer, preserving the ammonia and other salts.

## Bed Construction

### Indoor Beds

- Depth: one foot to one foot six inches of manure.
- Width: typically four feet (in his vinery houses, twenty or forty-five
  feet long).
- The surface (top six inches) consisted of unheated droppings.
- The condition of the dung for the lower part was less critical.

### Outdoor Ridge Beds

- Short material shaken out for the surface; long strawy material used
  for the interior and for winter covering.
- Foundations five to six feet wide, rising in ridge form to three feet
  or more in height, all trodden as hard as possible.
- Moulding began at the bottom and was worked up on both sides.

### Flat vs. Sloped Beds

Cuthill preferred flat beds (higher in the centre by six or eight
inches) over the traditional ridge system. A flat bed was "more
natural," gave more surface area for cropping, and a foot-plank at the
front kept dung and mould firm for watering.

## Heat Management During Construction

Cuthill described two methods for managing bed heat:

### Old Method

- Make up the bed and wait for heat to subside to about milk heat
  (approximately 98 degrees F).
- Spawn, mould, plaster down, and finish.
- This drove remaining heat out of the bed; without some heat the spawn
  could not grow and run.

### New Method (Cuthill's Improvement)

- Spawn and finish the beds as soon as heat reached 100 degrees F or
  more, before the bed reached its greatest heat.
- Insert spawn in the dung, cover over, put on mould, tread down hard,
  water, plaster, and finish all at once.
- By the next morning the heat had moved nearly to the bottom, so no
  injury to spawn could occur.
- The cold mould, hard treading, watering, and plastering all drove
  heat downwards. Heat could be recalled by covering beds with hay.
- This preserved the ammonia and strength of the manure and produced
  crops two weeks earlier, though the crop must not be forced sooner
  than six weeks.

### Ventilation Holes

To manage heat in beds spawned at lower temperatures:

- Make holes six inches deep and one foot apart in regular rows.
- When heat reduced to 80 degrees, fill holes to within three inches
  of the surface with droppings.
- Insert spawn in each hole.

## Moulding (Casing)

The mould ([[casing-layer-principles]] layer) was critical:

- Best soil: soft, soapy, loamy, yellow, sandy soil, or rich light
  dark sandy soil.
- Never use clay, chalky, or limey soil.
- No worms were permitted in the bed at moulding time.
- Thickness: three inches if loamy soil (reduced to about two inches
  after treading); not less than four inches if light sandy mould.
- Applied immediately after spawning, not when the bed was first made
  up.
- Tread down hard with the feet, water all over, and plaster the
  surface with the back of a spade.
- An excellent plan: scatter a little loam on the surface of light
  sandy beds just before plastering, making mushrooms more solid with
  finer flavour.
- Shallow moulding (about two inches finished) produced finer crops
  than deep moulding.

## Watering

Watering was the most delicate aspect of bed management:

- First watering: six to eight weeks after bed formation.
- Never water until at least the sixth week after spawning.
- Too much heat and too much water were "the greatest enemies to young
  spawn."
- Water at intervals (e.g., three times in one day) to allow penetration
  into solid parts, rather than all at once (which ran into cracks).
- Quantity: not less than half a gallon per square yard; liquid manure
  water (half and half) was preferred.
- Soft water only; in winter, always take the chill off.
