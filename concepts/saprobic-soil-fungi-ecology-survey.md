# Saprobic Soil Fungi -- Ecology, Biomass, and Survey Methods
Fungi are among the most abundant organisms in soil, comprising an estimated
78-90% of total decomposer biomass in grassland soils and approximately 89%
of total living microbial biomass in British deciduous forest.

## Ecological Roles

### Decomposition

Soil fungi function as primary, secondary, and tertiary decomposers in the
complex process of degrading organic matter. They are significant elements in
food webs involving soil fauna and microbes.

### Trophic Relationships

- **Saprobic:** Degrading organic matter in various stages of decay
- **Mutualistic:** Mycorrhizal associations with plant roots
- **Parasitic:** Attacking living members of soil food webs and remains of
  virtually all organisms

### Consumers

Soil fungi are consumed by mycophagous nematodes, protozoans, collembolans,
oribatida, enchytraeids, dipteran larvae, and to a lesser extent by gnats,
earthworms, water bears (Tardigrada), and mites. Many soil animals feeding on
fungi are species-specific or species-selective.

### Fungivore-Fungus Symbioses

Where the fungivore facilitates fungal growth, documented for wood-boring
beetles, attine ants, and certain termites.

## Taxonomic Composition

All major taxonomic groups of fungi are encountered in soils. Especially
prevalent among saprobic filamentous forms:

- Saprolegniales, Pythiales (Oomycota)
- Mucorales (Zygomycota)
- Eurotiales, Microascales, Hypocreales, Sordariales, Onygenales, Leotiales,
  Pezizales (Ascomycota)
- Numerous mitosporic fungi (anamorphs of ascomycete or basidiomycete
  species)

### The "Missing Link"

Chesters (1949) referred to basidiomycetes as "the missing link in soil
mycology" -- a comment still accurate more than 50 years later. Basidiomycetes
accounted for ~60% of total living microbial biomass in British deciduous
forest but are under-represented or absent in general surveys due to
technique-based bias.

## Soil Environments

### Grassland Soils

Enriched by organic accretion throughout the soil profile, especially from
annual, finely divided grass roots contributing biomass exceeding above-ground
biomass.

### Forest Soils

Enriched by debris from massive above-ground biomass plus substantial input
from mycorrhizae. Superficial debris underlain by amorphous, aromatic humus
layer on mineral soil.

### Desert Soils

Organic matter often primarily in the form of relatively intact litter only.

## Survey Challenges

- Direct observation usually reveals only indistinct, featureless mycelia
- In situ identification requires species-specific probes (nucleic acids or
  immunofluorescent labeling)
- Indirect isolation techniques (soil plating) remain preferred for
  inventorying species composition
- Slight variations in technique result in different perceptions of fungal
  activity
- Multiple propagules do not necessarily represent separate fungal individuals
- Chytridiomycetes overlooked because their zoospores require water for
  dispersal
- No complete inventory of soil fungi has been undertaken for any single
  geographic region

## Seasonal and Spatial Dynamics

Soil fungal communities are not static -- they fluctuate significantly across
seasons and with soil depth. [[soil-fungal-isolation-techniques]] and screening
programs contribute to pharmaceuticals, [[chamovitz-photoperiodism-agricultural-applications]], and
industrial enzymes.
The vast majority of soil fungal species remain uncultured and uncharacterized,
representing an enormous reservoir of potentially useful biochemical diversity.

## Fungal Biomass and Carbon Cycling

Soil fungi are central players in terrestrial carbon cycling, processing
enormous quantities of organic matter annually. Estimates of global fungal
biomass in soil range from 1-5 gigatons of carbon, making fungi one of the
largest living carbon reservoirs on Earth.
Key aspects of fungal contributions to carbon cycling include:

- **Enzymatic diversity**: Soil fungi produce a wider range of
  [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]] than bacteria, enabling them to degrade complex
  plant polymers including cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin
- **Hyphal foraging**: [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]] explore soil volumes far larger
  than bacterial colonies, connecting spatially separated nutrient sources
- **Carbon storage**: Fungal cell walls contain chitin and melanin, both
  of which are relatively resistant to decomposition, contributing to
  long-term soil carbon sequestration
- **Priming effects**: Fungal activity can stimulate or suppress
  decomposition of existing soil organic matter through substrate
  interactions and [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] shifts
The [[biodiversity-of-fungi-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]] relationship
remains an active area of research, with implications for climate change
modeling and soil carbon management.

## Soil Depth Profiles and Vertical Stratification

Fungal communities change dramatically with soil depth, reflecting gradients
in oxygen availability, organic matter quality, moisture, and root
distribution:

- **Organic horizons (O layers)**: Highest fungal biomass and diversity;
  dominated by cellulolytic and ligninolytic species that decompose fresh
  leaf litter and woody debris
- **Surface mineral soil (A horizon)**: Transition zone with mixed
  communities including both decomposers and mycorrhizal fungi associated
  with plant roots
- **Subsoil (B horizon)**: Reduced fungal biomass with communities
  increasingly dominated by oligotrophic species adapted to low nutrient
  conditions
- **Deep soil (C horizon)**: Sparse fungal populations, primarily
  endolithic species and weathering fungi associated with rock surfaces
Root distribution is the primary driver of fungal [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]] with
depth, as most soil fungi are either directly mycorrhizal or dependent on
root exudates and root-derived organic matter.
