## Time, Yield and Succession in Ecological Systems

The relationship between time, system age, and productive yield is a critical but often overlooked dimension of ecological design. [[kenneth-watt]] and [[permaculture-design]]:

- **Young systems**: High energy flow, rapid growth, increasing diversity
- **Mature systems**: Peak complexity, maximum connections, highest functional yield
- **Over-mature systems**: Dominated by large individuals, declining energy flow, reduced yield

Only local disturbance (fire, flood, death) renews the flow of energy through old systems. The natural cycling time may be very long, but in annual cropping it may be reduced to a single season or less.

## Death as Essential Renewal

Death in over-mature systems is viewed not in the negativistic sense of the fatalist, but in a positivistic and natural way:

- It is better that elements die and are renewed by other species than that the system simplifies to extinction
- It is better for a community (or "tribe") if its components change than if it turns inward, ages, and decays as a whole
- Life is seen as a preparation for succession and renewal, rather than a journey to extinction

This perspective reframes disturbance, death, and turnover as productive forces within ecological design, rather than as losses to be prevented.

## Time as a Resource

Watt identified time as a resource, and like all resources, both excess and deficiency are counterproductive:

- A system in which too much time has accumulated becomes chronically polluted
- A system in which not enough time has accumulated is below peak yield
- A strawberry seedling and an old strawberry bush are equally unproductive
- The very young and the very old in society are analogous: both require more inputs than they produce

There are age-specific diseases in whole systems, just as there are in people (whooping cough in the young, prostate hypertrophy in the old). A **mixed-age stand** is the best insurance against complete failure or epidemic disease of this nature. Mollison extends this biological observation to society: if efforts to prevent ageing succeed, the result may be a crowded, unstable, unproductive society subject to "gerontocratic peevishness."

## Permaculture's Advantage Over Annual Systems

[[permaculture]] uses the time resource much more effectively than annual gardening alone, and therefore uses solar energy to better effect. The mixed ecology of annuals and perennials:

- Maximizes product yield
- Maximizes the resourcefulness of the people who establish, control, and harvest the system
- Distributes yield across time through multiple harvest windows

Species diversity (richness) works to make the most of incoming energy, counteracting the tendency of aging systems to channel energy into maintenance rather than yield.

## Design Implications

- Design for mixed-age polycultures, not monoculture or single-cohort plantings
- Plan for succession stages, not just the mature system
- Incorporate disturbance (pruning, grazing, harvest) as a productive force
- Evaluate systems on energy yield, not just monetary return
- Manage natural yields rather than replacing complex systems with simplified ones
- Recognize that turnover and renewal are essential to sustained productivity
