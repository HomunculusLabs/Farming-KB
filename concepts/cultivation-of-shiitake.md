# Cultivation of Shiitake Shiitake (Lentinula edodes) is the second most cultivated mushroom worldwide and the most widely cultivated specialty mushroom in terms of economic value. Native to East Asia, it has been grown on logs for centuries and is now produced commercially using both traditional log methods and modern sawdust block technology. Shiitake is prized for its rich umami flavor, meaty texture, and well-documented medicinal properties, particularly its immune-modulating polysaccharides. ## Two Cultivation Approaches Shiitake cultivation diverges into two distinct methods, each with different equipment requirements, timelines, and product characteristics: **Log cultivation** (traditional): Hardwood logs are inoculated with shiitake spawn and left to colonize outdoors for 6-18 months before fruiting. This produces premium-quality mushrooms with thick flesh and long shelf life. The method is low-tech but slow and weather-dependent. **Sawdust block cultivation** (modern): Supplemented hardwood sawdust is sterilized, inoculated, and incubated indoors under controlled conditions. Time from inoculation to first harvest is 8-14 weeks. This method offers faster turnaround, higher and more predictable yields, and year-round production capability. However, mushrooms tend to have thinner flesh and shorter shelf life compared to log-grown. ## Log Cultivation Suitable tree species include oak (preferred), maple, beech, ironwood, hornbeam, and sweetgum. Softwoods and fruit trees are generally avoided. Logs should be 10-20 cm in diameter and 90-120 cm long, cut from healthy, living trees during dormancy (late fall through early spring). Logs should be inoculated within 2-6 weeks of cutting, before the natural anti-fungal defenses of the wood degrade too much. ## Fruitbody Types

Shiitake produces two distinct [[comparison-fruiting-body-vs-mycelium-extracts-medicinal-mushrooms]] morphologies:

**Donko** (thick, winter type): Produced under cooler temperatures
(8-15 C), higher CO2, and slower growth. Caps are thick, convex, and
heavily cracked. Premium market quality with excellent shelf life.

**Koshin** (thin, summer type): Produced under warmer temperatures
(15-20 C), lower CO2, and faster growth. Caps are thinner, flatter,
and lighter in color. Lower price but faster crop turnaround.

Many commercial operations adjust environmental conditions seasonally to
produce the appropriate type for market demand.

productive life of 3-6 years, though individual flushes are modest.

## Spawn Selection and Preparation

Choosing the right spawn strain is critical for shiitake success.
Hundreds of cultivated strains exist, broadly categorized by their
fruiting temperature preferences into wide-range, warm-weather,
and cold-weather varieties.
Wide-range strains (fruiting at 10-25 C) are most versatile for
hobby growers and small operations with limited climate control.
Cold-weather strains produce the highest-quality donko mushrooms
but require careful temperature management below 15 C.
Spawn types include sawdust spawn (most common for logs), plug spawn
(wooden dowels colonized with mycelium), and grain spawn (used for
sawdust block inoculation).
Sawdust spawn generally colonizes logs faster than plug spawn and
allows more precise dosing per inoculation hole.
See [[cultivator-strain-selection-and-stock-culture]] for general
principles of strain selection and [[spawn-production-techniques]]
for methods of producing quality spawn in-house.

## Sawdust Block Formulation

The substrate formulation for sawdust block cultivation significantly
affects yield, mushroom quality, and contamination resistance.
A standard mix consists of hardwood sawdust (75-85%) supplemented
with wheat bran or rice bran (15-25%) for additional nitrogen.
Some growers add 1-2% gypsum (calcium sulfate) to buffer pH and
improve substrate structure, and small amounts of calcium carbonate
to adjust the initial pH to approximately 5.5-6.5.
Moisture content at inoculation should be 60-65%, measured by weight.
Excessive moisture creates anaerobic pockets that favor bacterial
contamination, while insufficient moisture slows mycelial growth.
Oak and alder sawdust are preferred; avoid cedar, redwood, and
other resinous or antimicrobial woods.
See [[mushroom-substrates]] for detailed information on substrate
materials and [[three-pillars-mushroom-cultivation-spawn-substrate-environment]]
for the foundational principles of substrate management.

## Fruiting Chamber Requirements

Sawdust block cultivation requires controlled fruiting environments
to produce consistent, high-quality crops.
Temperature management is the primary environmental variable, with
optimal fruiting occurring between 12-20 C depending on the target
mushroom type.
Relative humidity must be maintained at 85-95% to prevent cap
cracking and drying of developing fruitbodies.
Fresh air exchange (FAE) is critical: elevated CO2 levels above
1000 ppm cause elongated stems and small, underdeveloped caps.
Light intensity of 500-1000 lux for 8-12 hours daily stimulates
normal fruiting body development and cap pigmentation.
Misting systems, exhaust fans, and intake vents work together to
balance humidity and air exchange without causing waterlogging
or excessive drying of the block surface.

## Common Pests and Contaminants

Shiitake cultivation faces several common pest and contamination
challenges that growers must monitor and manage.
Green molds (Trichoderma species) are the most frequent competitors,
appearing as rapidly spreading green patches on logs or blocks.
Bacterial blotch (Pseudomonas tolaasii) causes brown, sunken lesions
on mushroom caps, particularly when humidity is excessive or
water sits on cap surfaces for extended periods.
Mites can vector mold spores between cultivation blocks and feed
directly on mycelium, causing slowed growth and reduced yields.
Sciarid flies (fungus gnats) lay eggs in moist substrate, and their
larvae damage mycelium and create entry points for contamination.
Phorid flies are another common pest in indoor cultivation facilities.
Maintaining clean work areas, proper pasteurization or sterilization,
and good air circulation are the primary defenses against these
problems. See [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]]
for a comprehensive treatment of mushroom cultivation pests.

## Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling

Proper harvesting technique directly affects shiitake quality and
subsequent flush productivity.
Mushrooms should be harvested when the cap is 70-90% expanded but
before the veil at the cap margin begins to lift significantly.
Twist or cut the stem cleanly at the block surface rather than
pulling, which can damage the mycelium and reduce future yields.
Donko-type mushrooms are harvested when the cap surface develops
characteristic cracks, which indicate optimal maturity and flavor.
After harvest, mushrooms should be cooled quickly and stored at
2-4 C with high humidity to maintain freshness.
Fresh shiitake has a shelf life of 2-3 weeks under proper cold
storage, though log-grown mushrooms typically last longer than
sawdust-grown due to their denser tissue.
Drying is an excellent preservation method for shiitake and actually
enhances the umami flavor by converting lentinic acid to the more
potent lentinic acid lactone during the drying process.
