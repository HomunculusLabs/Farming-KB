# Plant Tissues, Dermal and Ground Systems, and Root Structure

Plants are composed of three major tissue systems — dermal, ground, and vascular — each with specialized cell types that work in concert. Understanding these tissues and how roots are organized provides the foundation for understanding nutrient uptake.

## Dermal Tissue

The dermal tissue forms the **skin** of the plant, serving as the primary barrier against water loss and external injury. It has two components: **epidermis** and **[[periderm]]**.

### Epidermis
The epidermis is typically a single layer of cells covering all plant surfaces — flowers, leaves, stems, roots, and seeds. Key characteristics include:

- **Transparency**: Epidermal cells generally lack chloroplasts, allowing light to reach photosynthetic tissues beneath
- **Cuticle**: Epidermal cells produce **cutin**, a waxy substance that forms a protective **cuticle** on all aboveground surfaces. The cuticle is thicker on the upper surface of leaves (greater sun exposure) than on the lower surface
- **Stomata**: Pores containing **guard cells** that open to allow CO₂ diffusion in and water vapor and oxygen out. Potassium is essential for stomatal operation — when potassium ions move into guard cells, water follows by osmosis, causing the cells to swell and bend, opening the pore
- **Trichomes**: Tiny hair-like cells on leaf surfaces. Some trichomes possess glands producing **anti-grazing compounds** that deter herbivores. Others decrease airflow over the leaf surface, slowing water loss through transpiration

### Periderm (Bark)
As some plants age, the epidermis is replaced by periderm, formed from **lateral meristematic cells**. The periderm:

- Replaces the epidermis on stems and roots of woody plants
- Provides protection while allowing airflow into the plant
- Produces **cork** — dead cells filled with **suberin**, the same waxy substance found in the [[casparian-strip]]

## Root Hairs

[[root-hairs]] are among the most important structures for nutrient uptake. They are specialized epidermal cells that dramatically increase the root's contact area with soil.

### Formation and Structure
- Each root hair is a **single epidermal cell** that extends outward from the root surface
- They can grow to lengths of up to **1,500 microns** (0.06 inch) with diameters of about **15 microns** (0.0006 inch)
- Only a small zone just below the growing root tip contains meristematic cells coded to become root hairs
- Root hairs are short-lived — lasting from a few days to a couple of weeks — and are constantly replaced as the root tip advances

### Density
Some plants produce tens of thousands of root hairs in just 1-2 square inches of soil surface. A single rye plant can develop **miles of root hairs** growing in 2 cubic feet of soil.

### Self-Regulation
Not all epidermal cells become root hairs. Those that do secrete chemicals that **prevent neighboring cells** from also developing into root hairs. Without this regulation, plants would develop tangled masses of ever-expanding epidermal cells.

### Calcium-Directed Growth
Root hairs act as the "eyes" of the root system. Their growth direction is guided by calcium uptake:

1. Calcium enters the root hair through the plasmalemma
2. The presence of calcium on a particular surface allows continued elongation in that direction
3. When an obstacle (e.g., a rock) blocks calcium uptake on one side, elongation stops on that side and shifts to another area
4. Once the obstacle is passed, the root hair reorients to its original direction

This calcium-dependent guidance system operates across thousands of root hairs simultaneously. Calcium shortages therefore have obvious consequences for root exploration.

### Nutrient-Dependent Development
Root hair production is **suppressed when soil nutrients are abundant**. Producing root hairs requires energy and nutrients, so plants only invest in them when additional foraging capacity is necessary.

## Mucilage and the Rhizosphere

Root tips produce **mucilage**, a gel-like substance composed of sloughed-off root cap cells, [[root-exudates]] (sugars and amino acids), and microbial populations with their by-products. Mucilage serves multiple critical functions:

- **Lubrication**: Helps roots penetrate soil
- **Nutrient uptake**: Particularly important for metal nutrients (phosphorus, zinc, iron, magnesium). The gel oozes into soil crevices, increasing contact with nutrient-bearing surfaces
- **[[fungal-roles-in-phosphorus-solubilization]]**: Contains acids that dissolve phosphorus, allowing it to diffuse through the gel to the root
- **pH-mediated uptake**: Chemical reactions in the mucilage and the gel's pH influence metal ion uptake
- **Microbial habitat**: The mixture of microbes living in and digesting sloughed-off cellular material generates wastes containing essential nutrients, particularly nitrogen
- **[[fungal-chemical-signaling]]**: Plant exudates in the mucilage can be produced in direct response to nutrient needs, recruiting specific [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]]

## Root Zones

Roots are organized into distinct functional zones:

### Zone of Cell Division (Apical Meristem)
New cells are produced here and pushed toward the root tip, where they differentiate into **columella cells** — rectangular cells in columns that form the **root cap**. The root cap contains **statoliths**, heavy organelles that sink to the bottom of cells and help detect gravity, triggering auxin production that directs downward growth.

### Zone of Elongation
Cells above the meristem elongate, creating **hydraulic pressure** as vacuoles fill with water. These cells function as biohydraulic pumps forcing the root through soil.

### Zone of Maturation
Just above the elongation zone, meristematic cells differentiate into their mature forms and become part of the dermal, ground, or [[water-transport-vascular-tissues-plants]]. Root hairs form in this zone.

## Root Cross-Section Anatomy

From outside to inside:

1. **Epidermis**: Outer skin (except at the root cap)
2. **Cortex**: Thick layer of parenchyma cells storing food (especially starch)
3. **Endodermis**: Tight cell layer containing the Casparian strip — the gateway controlling all entry into the vascular system
4. **Pericycle**: Thin layer inside the endodermis with cells that retain dividing ability. Forms **lateral (branch) roots** — structurally different from root hairs — and contributes to root thickening
5. **Vascular tissue**: At the center, containing xylem (water transport upward) and phloem (sugar transport to/from roots)

## Leaf Structure

Leaves provide the platform for photosynthesis and house the stomata. A typical leaf comprises:

- **Lamina** (leaf blade): The photosynthetic surface
- **Petiole**: The stalk connecting leaf to stem, containing [[xylem-and-phloem-nutrient-transport-systems]]
- **Leaf axil**: Where the petiole attaches to the stem

Leaves are covered with epidermal cells on both surfaces, with **chloroplasts** residing in the **mesophyll** between the two epidermal layers. The mesophyll also contains parenchyma and collenchyma cells, plus xylem (water supply) and phloem (sugar export) tissues that form the **veins**.

### Guttation
At night, when excess soil water creates [[guttation-root-pressure-and-maple-sap-physiology]] stomata are closed, water is forced into leaves and escapes through **hydathodes** — tiny holes at leaf tips — in a process called **guttation**.

## Key Takeaways

- Dermal tissue (epidermis and periderm) forms the plant's protective barrier, with the cuticle preventing water loss
- Root hairs are single cells that can extend 1,500 microns, dramatically increasing root-soil contact area
- Root hair growth direction is guided by calcium uptake, making calcium availability critical for soil exploration
- Mucilage lubricates roots, solubilizes phosphorus, and hosts beneficial microbes in the rhizosphere
- The endodermis with its Casparian strip is the critical control point for selective nutrient uptake
- Root hair production is energy-dependent and suppressed when nutrients are abundant
