# Marketing Local Winter Produce

Marketing high-quality local winter vegetables requires distinguishing small-farm produce from both conventional and industrial organic alternatives. [[eliot-coleman]]'s approach centers on the concepts of **freshness** and **local identity** as the key selling points that command premium prices and build loyal customer relationships.

## The Case for Fresh and Local

Coleman has been involved in organic agriculture since 1965, when the distinctiveness of organic farming gave small growers a unique marketing advantage. That niche disappeared when organic became industrialized. Nowadays, organic produce from distant sources fills store shelves, but industrial organic products are nowhere near as well grown or flavorful as those from small family farms.

The word "organic" is no longer an adequate distinction. The important words are **fresh** and **local**:

- Produce shipped from far away is at least a week old by the time it reaches local markets—it is not "fresh" by any definition.
- Astute eaters who shop at roadside stands and farmers markets know the superiority of truly fresh vegetables and actively seek out local growers.
- Long-distance produce has a short remaining shelf life after delivery, whereas locally harvested produce can be delivered within 12–24 hours.

## Packaging as Brand Identity

### Cedar Delivery Boxes

[[four-season-farm]] uses distinctive reusable wooden boxes for delivery:

- **Dimensions**: 12 × 16 × 8 inches, homemade from native Maine cedar trees cut on the farm and milled at a neighbor's sawmill.
- **Motivation**: After learning that 25% of the volume in the average landfill is food-packaging waste, the farm chose to create permanent reusable boxes rather than contribute to the waste stream.
- **Finish**: Sanded smooth and coated with food-grade finish. The trademark "Seasonal Salads" is burned into the end boards with a branding iron.
- **Size rationale**: Determined by measuring produce coolers in stores and the cargo space of the family car. At startup, up to 48 boxes fit in the back of a Subaru.

### Interior Packaging

Before filling, each box is lined with a sheet of **pure cellophane** that folds over the top and is secured with two rubber bands:

- Cellophane was chosen over plastic because it is made from wood fibers—an appropriate choice since wood is a farm product.
- Pure cellophane can be composted after use.
- Empty boxes are collected from customers on each delivery, cleaned with hot water at the farm, and reused.

## Setting Prices

### The First Sale

In October 1995, Coleman walked into stores and restaurants with the first commercial winter-harvest crops, priced at **$2 per pound higher** than California organic salad mixes. The local food co-op set the local and West Coast products side by side; the local produce sold out within a day while the competition remained unsold.

### Customer Response

- **Chefs** immediately recognized quality and the marketing cachet of serving "local" salad, plus the freshness advantage over week-old imported product.
- **Retail stores** that had never carried such products found that quality alone created demand. For customer convenience, salad was packed in quarter-pound bags rather than bulk boxes, and one account expanded to eight times the initial quantities.

### The "Fair Price" Challenge

"Fair" has two meanings in food pricing—**affordable** and **equitable**. The most difficult challenge for the small-scale grower is charging an equitable price sufficient for a decent living without being accused of elitism.

Coleman argues that the public myth that "a carrot is a carrot" is patently untrue. There is as much quality difference between carefully grown local carrots and supermarket carrots as between a Volvo and a Corvair. Most people easily discern quality differences in automobiles or appliances but do not apply the same standard to vegetables—partly because the myth serves homogeneous supermarket interests.

## Biological Quality and Nutritional Superiority

### Evidence for Quality Differences

In forty years of growing crops and feeding livestock and people, Coleman has observed many striking examples of superior biological quality in properly grown crops. The small-scale local grower can produce a more finely crafted product through:

- Meticulous attention to soil care
- Superior compost
- Careful variety selection
- The obvious benefit of delivering within 12–24 hours of harvest

### Winter-Harvest Nutritional Bonus

Peer-reviewed research increasingly documents the nutritional superiority of organically raised food. Winter-harvested crops may offer an additional benefit:

- Highly colored foods grown under cool conditions have been shown to be much higher in **anthocyanins**, one of the most valuable antioxidant compounds.
- This is visibly demonstrated in the much deeper red color of red lettuces and 'Bull's Blood' beet leaves harvested from winter greenhouses.
- Green leaves can also be high in anthocyanins, which plants utilize to resist cold.

### Health Assurance

Coleman suggests that Americans spending hundreds of dollars monthly on sickness insurance would be wiser to invest in "health assurance" by spending an additional $10 per day on high-quality local fruits and vegetables. This would dramatically expand the market for local growers while equally benefiting customer health.

## The "Seasonal Salads" Brand

Building a reputation for quality is central to the marketing approach. The reusable branded cedar boxes, consistent delivery schedule, and unmatched freshness combine to establish brand loyalty. The brand identity communicates:

- **Craftsmanship**: Attention to detail at every stage from soil to delivery.
- **Sustainability**: Reusable packaging and compostable cellophane.
- **Place-based identity**: Maine cedar boxes and local farm provenance.
- **Premium quality**: Freshness that cannot be matched by any shipped-in competitor.
