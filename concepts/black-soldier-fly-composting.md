# Black Soldier Fly Composting

Black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) composting is a bioconversion method that uses larvae to rapidly break down organic waste into protein-rich animal feed and frass fertilizer. Adult BSF do not bite, sting, or transmit disease, making them safe for homestead-scale operations.

## Lifecycle Overview

The BSF lifecycle has four stages:

1.  **Egg** — Females lay 500-900 eggs near decaying organic matter. Eggs hatch in 3-4 days.
2.  **Larva** — The active feeding stage lasts 14-21 days. Larvae grow from 1 mm to 25 mm and pass through 6 instars. This is the composting workhorse stage.
3.  **Prepupa** — Larvae stop feeding, turn dark, and seek dry pupation sites. They migrate out of the compost on their own, enabling self-harvesting.
4.  **Adult** — Adults live 5-8 days, do not feed, and focus solely on mating and egg-laying. They are drawn to composting bins by volatile organic compound cues.

## Bin Design

A basic BSF bin has these components:

-  **Feeding chamber** — A 40-100 liter container with drainage holes in the bottom. Food scraps go in here.
-  **Ramp system** — A 45-degree ramp with a collection bucket at the top. Prepupae climb the ramp seeking a dry pupation spot and fall into the bucket.
-  **Drainage tray** — Beneath the feeding chamber to collect leachate (liquid runoff). This can be diluted and used as fertilizer.
-  **Lid with mesh** — Allows adult flies to enter and lay eggs while keeping out rain and larger pests.
-  **Egg-laying attractant** — Corrugated cardboard or flaps near the top of the bin give females crevices to lay eggs in.

Commercial options like the BioPod simplify this design but are not required. A DIY bin can be built from a plastic tote, PVC pipe, and hardware cloth for under $30.

## Feeding

BSF larvae accept an extremely wide range of inputs:

- Kitchen scraps (fruit, vegetable, bread, dairy, meat)
- Brewery and distillery waste
- Manure (poultry, swine, cattle — avoid if medicated)
- Restaurant food waste
- Coffee grounds and filters

Feed at a rate the colony can process. A mature colony can consume 1-2 kg of waste per day per square meter of bin surface area. Do not overfeed — excess food will sour and attract pests. Bury fresh food under a thin layer of existing compost to reduce odor and fruit fly attraction.

Avoid: heavily salted foods, large amounts of citrus (can lower pH), and anything treated with pesticides.

## Environmental Conditions

- Temperature: Optimal larval growth occurs at 27-30 C (80-86 F). Below 15 C (59 F), development slows dramatically. Above 40 C (104 F), larvae die.
- Moisture: Maintain 60-70% moisture in the substrate. Too dry and larvae desiccate; too wet and anaerobic conditions develop.
- Aeration: Ensure drainage is adequate. Stagnant, waterlogged bins produce foul odors.
- Light: Adults need UV light to trigger mating. Outdoor bins typically receive enough ambient light.

## Harvesting Larvae

The self-harvesting ramp design is the simplest method. When prepupae are ready to pupate, they naturally migrate upward and fall into the collection bucket. Collect daily.

Alternative harvest methods:

-  **Screen separation** — Push finished compost through a screen. Larvae are caught on top.
-  **Water flotation** — Place compost in water; larvae float and can be skimmed off.
-  **Hand sorting** — Labor-intensive but works for small batches.

Harvested larvae can be fed live to poultry and fish, dried for storage, or processed into meal. See [[black-soldier-fly-larvae-as-feed]] for details on processing and feeding.

## Frass (Byproduct)

The residue left after larval digestion is called frass. It is a dark, odorless material rich in chitin and partially stabilized organic matter. Frass makes an excellent soil amendment:

- Apply at 5-10% by volume to potting mixes
- Top-dress garden beds at 1-2 cm depth
- Contains beneficial chitin that stimulates plant immune responses

## Scaling Considerations

- A single 100-liter bin can process 2-5 kg of food waste per day
- For larger operations, stack multiple bins or use multi-tier systems
- Overwintering is the main challenge in cold climates — larvae diapause below 10 C. Options include indoor bin placement, insulated bins, or purchasing new eggs each spring
- Start with a purchased colony of 5,000-10,000 larvae rather than waiting for wild adults to find your bin

## Common Problems

-  **Fruit flies** — Bury food waste, reduce moisture, add a fine mesh barrier over egg-laying area
-  **Sour bin** — Reduce feeding rate, add dry carbon (shredded cardboard), improve drainage
-  **Larvae escaping** — Ensure ramp angle is correct (40-45 degrees) and walls are smooth; escapees are usually a sign the bin is too wet or overcrowded
-  **Colony collapse** — Usually temperature-related. Move bins to a warmer location or reduce feeding

## Integration with Homestead Systems

BSF composting closes nutrient loops on the homestead. Food waste becomes protein for [[chickshaw-and-poultry-tractors|poultry]] and fish, and frass returns nutrients to garden beds. The system requires minimal labor once established and produces no foul odor when properly managed.

The leachate can be applied to gardens as a liquid fertilizer (dilute 1:10 with water), though it should be used with caution on edible crops due to potential pathogen load from meat and dairy inputs.
