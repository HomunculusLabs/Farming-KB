# Cannabis and Rheumatoid Arthritis

**Source:** The Cannabis Health Index, Uwe Blesching, PhD (2017)
**Updated:** 2026-04-19

## Evidence-Based Therapeutic Potential

- **Total Studies Reviewed:** 3
- **CHI Value:** 10

## Overview

Rheumatoid arthritis is the most crippling form of arthritis, deforming joints and bending bodies. It is considered an autoimmune disorder occurring when the body's immune system attacks healthy joint tissue. One can identify long-time sufferers from a distance by their gnarly joint appearance. Orthodox treatment focuses on suppressing pain and inflammation, primarily with NSAIDs, which carry serious risks — each year 41,000 older adults are hospitalized and 3,300 die from ulcers caused by NSAIDs.

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) affects approximately 1% of the global population, with women being 2–3 times more likely to develop the condition than men. RA typically presents between ages 30–60 and can affect any joint, though the small joints of the hands and feet are most commonly involved. The disease is characterized by synovial membrane inflammation, cartilage destruction, and bone erosion. Beyond joint damage, RA is a systemic condition that can affect the cardiovascular system, lungs, eyes, and blood vessels, contributing to increased mortality.

## Cannabis and Rheumatoid Arthritis

In 2006, UK researchers enrolled 58 rheumatoid arthritis patients in a double-blind, placebo-controlled study. Patients receiving cannabinoids (Sativex) slept better and experienced significant pain reduction with disease activity significantly suppressed. Boston scientists (2006) confirmed that habitual cannabis use positively affects the immune system and that regulation of the [[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]] can impact every major immune system function. A 2009 meta-analysis from Worcester, Massachusetts, showed all types of cannabinoids and non-cannabinoid plant components effectively reduce inflammatory pain in rheumatism, rheumatoid arthritis, chronic neuropathic pain, and fibromyalgia.

## Mechanisms of Action

Cannabinoids address rheumatoid arthritis through multiple well-characterized biological pathways:

- **CB2 receptor-mediated anti-inflammation:** CB2 receptors are expressed on immune cells within inflamed joints (macrophages, T-cells, B-cells). Activation of CB2 receptors reduces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-8) that drive synovial inflammation and joint destruction. This mechanism is central to the [[cannabis-anti-inflammatory-applications]] for RA.

- **CB1 receptor-mediated analgesia:** CB1 receptors in the central and peripheral nervous systems modulate pain signaling. In RA, chronic joint inflammation leads to peripheral and central sensitization, amplifying pain perception. CB1 activation reduces nociceptive transmission, providing analgesic effects that complement the anti-inflammatory action of CB2.

- **Immune system modulation:** The Boston study demonstrated that cannabinoid regulation can impact every major immune system function. In RA, where the immune system attacks healthy joint tissue, cannabinoid-mediated immunomodulation may help restore immune homeostasis. This involves shifting the immune response from a pro-inflammatory Th1/Th17 profile toward an anti-inflammatory Th2/Treg profile.

- **Synovial fibroblast inhibition:** RA is driven partly by abnormal synovial fibroblast behavior, where these cells proliferate excessively and produce inflammatory mediators and tissue-degrading enzymes. Cannabinoids have been shown to inhibit synovial fibroblast proliferation and reduce their production of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that destroy cartilage and bone.

- **Bone protection:** RA causes bone erosion through osteoclast activation. CB2 receptors on osteoclasts inhibit bone resorption, potentially slowing the bone destruction characteristic of RA. This mechanism parallels the protective effects of cannabinoids on bone observed in bone cancer research.

- **TRPV1 receptor modulation:** TRPV1 (vanilloid) receptors are involved in inflammatory pain signaling in joints. CBD and other cannabinoids modulate TRPV1 activity, contributing to pain relief through a non-CB1/CB2 pathway.

## The Sativex Study

The 2006 UK clinical trial is particularly significant because it was a rigorous double-blind, placebo-controlled study in actual RA patients:

- 58 patients received either Sativex (THC:CBD oromucosal spray) or placebo for five weeks
- Patients receiving Sativex experienced significant improvements in pain on movement, pain at rest, and morning stiffness
- Disease activity scores (DAS28) showed significant reduction in the Sativex group
- Sleep quality improved significantly in the cannabinoid group
- Side effects were generally mild and well-tolerated

Sativex is formulated as approximately equal parts THC (2.7 mg per spray) and CBD (2.5 mg per spray), which is relevant to [[cannabis-for-rheumatoid-arthritis]] dosing considerations.

## Additional Research

Research on cannabinoids and RA has continued to expand:

- Patient surveys consistently report that cannabis use improves RA symptoms, including pain, joint stiffness, and sleep quality.

- A 2019 Israeli study of RA patients using medical cannabis found significant improvements in pain scores, disease activity, and quality of life after six months of treatment.

- Pre-clinical research has demonstrated that CBD reduces inflammation and joint damage in animal models of RA.

- Studies have explored the combination of cannabinoids with conventional RA medications (methotrexate, biologics), with some suggesting potential synergistic effects.

## Dosage and Administration

For RA patients considering cannabinoid therapy, several factors are relevant:

- Oromucosal sprays (similar to Sativex) allow flexible, as-needed dosing for variable symptoms

- Oral preparations (capsules, tinctures) provide longer-lasting effects suitable for baseline pain management

- [[cannabis-topical-applications]] (creams, balms, oils) may provide localized relief for individual affected joints

- [[cannabis-edibles-guide]] recommendations should be followed for oral products

- Patients should consult [[cannabis-dosing-guidelines]] and be aware of [[cannabis-drug-interactions-and-contraindications]] with DMARDs (methotrexate), biologics (TNF inhibitors), and NSAIDs

## Strain-Specific Considerations

Sativex is an oromucosal spray of roughly equal parts THC (2.7 mg) and CBD (2.5 mg), similar to Cannabis sativa ratios. Both sativa and indica strains contain CB1- and CB2-activating cannabinoids. Indicas with lower THC:CBD ratios may favor CB2 activation, which is particularly relevant for autoimmune anti-inflammatory effects.

Strains rich in [[cannabis-minor-cannabinoids]] like CBG and CBC may provide additional anti-inflammatory benefits. The [[cannabis-terpene-profile]] of selected strains can enhance therapeutic effects, with myrcene (analgesic, muscle relaxant), beta-caryophyllene (anti-inflammatory, CB2 agonist), and pinene (anti-inflammatory) being particularly relevant for RA.

## Complementary Herbs and Spices

- **Cayenne:** Stimulates peripheral circulation; used topically in Cuba for chronic aches and pains

- **Myrrh:** Modulates inflammatory responses; University of Cincinnati confirmed benefits for arthritis

- **Nigella:** Volatile oil suppresses artificially induced arthritis in rats

- **Turmeric:** WHO Monographs describe uses for pain and inflammation due to rheumatoid arthritis

- **(E)-β-Caryophyllene:** FDA-approved food-based plant cannabinoid that activates cannabinoid-receptors CB2 and initiates potent anti-inflammatory actions; found in black pepper, cinnamon, rosemary, basil, clove, oregano, and caraway

## Limitations of Current Research

While the Sativex clinical trial was well-designed, it was relatively small (58 patients) and short-term (5 weeks). Long-term effects of cannabinoid therapy on RA disease progression are unknown. The immunosuppressive properties of cannabinoids could theoretically increase infection risk in RA patients already on immunosuppressive medications. Cannabis should be used as an adjunct to — not a replacement for — conventional RA treatment including DMARDs and biologics.
