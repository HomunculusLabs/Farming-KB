# Supercritical CO2 Cannabis Extraction

Supercritical carbon dioxide extraction [[cannabis-reflux-extraction-apparatus]] is a solvent-based method that uses CO2
in its supercritical state — where it exhibits properties of both a liquid and a
gas — to selectively dissolve and extract cannabinoids, terpenes, and other
compounds from cannabis biomass. The technique has become the industry standard
for large-scale commercial extraction due to its scalability, solvent safety [[humanure-composting-methods-and-safety]]
profile, and ability to produce clean, residue-free extracts without the use of
hydrocarbon or ethanol solvents.

## Supercritical Fluid Fundamentals

Every substance has a critical point defined by a critical temperature and
critical pressure above which the distinction between liquid and gas phases
ceases to exist. For carbon dioxide, the critical point occurs at 31.1°C
(304.1 K) and 73.8 bar (1071 psi). Above these conditions, CO2 enters a
supercritical state with unique [[lemon-cannabis-alchemy-solvent-properties-and-selection-guide]]: it has the density of a
liquid, enabling it to dissolve non-polar and moderately polar compounds, but
the diffusivity and viscosity of a gas, allowing it to penetrate plant matrices
rapidly and thoroughly.

The solvent power of supercritical CO2 can be tuned continuously by adjusting
temperature and pressure within the supercritical region. At lower pressures
(80-120 bar), CO2 selectively extracts lighter, more [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]]
including mono-terpenes like myrcene and limonene. At higher pressures (250-350
bar), the solvent strength increases, extracting heavier cannabinoids (THC, CBD)
along with waxes, lipids, and chlorophyll. This tunability allows operators to
design extraction profiles that target specific compound classes.

Near-critical CO2 at 50-80 bar and 15-25°C preferentially extracts delicate
terpenes while leaving cannabinoids largely in the biomass. Many commercial
operations use a multi-stage approach: subcritical extraction to capture terpenes,
followed by supercritical extraction for cannabinoids.

## Equipment and Process Design

A commercial supercritical CO2 extraction system consists of several key
components: a CO2 storage tank, a chiller, a high-pressure pump, heated extraction
vessels, separation vessels where pressure is reduced to precipitate the
extract, and a CO2 recovery system that condenses and recycles the gas. Systems
range from small benchtop units processing 100 grams of biomass per run to
industrial systems processing 50-100 kilograms per batch.

The extraction vessel is loaded with prepared cannabis biomass that has been
ground to a consistent particle size (typically 2-5mm) and dried to below 10%
moisture. The biomass is packed into the vessel with a specific bulk density,
as channeling — the formation of preferential flow paths through the biomass —
reduces extraction efficiency. Some systems use mechanical agitation or flow
reversal to improve solvent contact with the biomass.

Extraction times range from 1-4 hours depending on biomass load, flow rate, and
target [[2c-b-compound-profile]]. CO2 flow rates of 5-15 liters per minute are typical
for mid-scale systems. The total CO2 consumed per kilogram of biomass ranges
from 20-60 kg, though most is recovered and recycled. Make-up CO2 requirements
are typically 1-3 kg per kilogram of biomass to account for losses.

## Selectivity and Fractionation

One of the primary advantages of supercritical CO2 extraction is the ability to
fractionate the extract into separate compound classes during the extraction
process. Multi-vessel systems use cascading pressure reduction to selectively
precipitate different compounds at different pressures. The first separator,
operating at 50-80 bar, precipitates heavy waxes and lipids while allowing
cannabinoids and terpenes to remain dissolved in the CO2 stream. A second
separator at 30-50 bar precipitates cannabinoids, while a final separator at
near-ambient pressure captures volatile terpenes.

This in-line fractionation eliminates the need for post-extraction winterization
— the process of dissolving crude extract in ethanol and freezing to precipitate
waxes — though some operators still perform winterization as a polishing step.
The ability to produce separate terpene and cannabinoid fractions is valuable for
product formulation, allowing manufacturers to recombine them in precise ratios
or use them in different product lines.

## CO2 as a Solvent: Advantages and Limitations

Carbon dioxide is non-flammable, non-toxic, chemically inert, and generally
recognized as safe (GRAS) by the FDA. These properties make it the safest
industrial extraction solvent available. Unlike butane or propane, CO2 does not
leave residual solvent in the final product — any dissolved CO2 simply
evaporates at atmospheric pressure. Unlike ethanol, CO2 does not co-extract
water-soluble compounds like chlorophyll (at appropriate pressures), producing
a lighter-colored extract with less bitter flavor.
