## The Four Paradigms

### 1. Standard Western Psychotherapy

In this paradigm, the psychedelic drug is used to amplify and intensify the processes of internal self-analysis and self-understanding.

The experience is understood as a product of the patient's own psyche — the drug merely loosens defenses and amplifies unconscious contents.

The goal is therapeutic insight into emotional dynamics and behavior patterns, and potentially the transcendence of limiting self-concepts.

Reality is understood as singular and consensual — there is one objective reality, and the [[mckenna-food-gods-designer-states-of-consciousness]], and these worlds are considered just as real as the physical world.

The beings encountered in dreams and visions — spirits — are considered "just as real as the physical organism."

## Two Fundamental Differences

Metzner identifies two points where the shamanic worldview "differs radically from the accepted Western worldview":

### Multiple Realities

The shamanic paradigm assumes and perceives the existence of multiple realities or "worlds" that can be explored in expanded states of consciousness.

These are not understood as metaphors or subjective interpretations but as genuinely real dimensions of existence.

The physical world that we ordinarily perceive is one among many, and the shaman is trained to navigate between them.

This contrasts with the Western assumption of a single, objective reality that is modified subjectively in [[weil-the-natural-mind-altered-states-normalcy]].

### The Reality of Spirits

The shamanic paradigm holds that "spirits" — the beings one encounters in dreams and visions — are "just as real as the physical organism."

These spirits may include ancestors, animal powers, plant spirits, deities, and other entities.

In the Western paradigm, such beings are understood as projections of the unconscious mind — archetypes, complexes, or symbolic representations.

The shamanic understanding is ontological: spirits exist independently of the perceiver and can be interacted with, negotiated with, and in some cases opposed.

## The Role of the Practitioner

The differences between paradigms extend to the role and training of the practitioner:

**Western therapist**: Trained in psychology, often with no personal experience of non-ordinary states. The therapist guides the patient through the experience from outside.

**Shaman**: Trained through years of apprenticeship, including personal experience of non-ordinary states induced by the plants themselves. The shaman enters the same state as the patient, journeying alongside them.
