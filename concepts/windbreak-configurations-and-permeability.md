# Windbreak Configurations and Permeability

Windbreaks are essential in permaculture design, providing crop protection,
microclimate modification, and structural resilience. Their effectiveness
depends on two critical factors: **height** and **density**. Height determines
the **extent** of the protected zone leeward, while density and permeability
determine the **quality** of that protection. Excessive density creates
turbulent eddies that can damage crops more than moderate wind would.

## Height and Spacing Rules

The ideal windbreak height should be approximately **1/5 of the interspace**
(distance between windbreaks). Even windbreaks as low as **1/30 of the
interspace** still provide measurable shelter benefit. In severe wind areas,
spacing must follow the **20x hedgerow height rule** — a 10-metre windbreak
should be spaced no more than 200 metres apart. In less severe environments,
up to 30x height may be acceptable.

## Permeability: The 40-70% Wind-Through Guideline

Permeability is the most critical and commonly misunderstood aspect of windbreak
design. A windbreak allowing **40-70% of wind to pass through** provides optimal
protection:

- **Below 40%** (too dense): Wind is forced over the top, creating turbulent
  eddies on the lee side. These overturning vortices cause violent localised
  gusts and trap cold air, creating frost hollows.
- **Above 70%** (too open): Insufficient wind reduction; the system underperforms.
- **40-70%** (optimal): Wind is gently decelerated, creating a broad, stable
  shelter zone extending far leeward without turbulence.

### Frost Risk Reduction

Impermeable barriers block cold air drainage downslope, creating **frost
hollows** that devastate sensitive crops. Permeable windbreaks allow cold air
to drain through while still reducing wind speed — simultaneously protecting
against wind damage and radiative frost.

### Windbreak Extension

Permeable windbreaks extend effective shelter distance leeward. Because wind is
not completely redirected upward, it does not reconverge as quickly. A 50%
permeable windbreak protects significantly further than a solid barrier of the
same height.

## Configuration Types A Through E

Mollison identifies five principal configurations, each suited to different
site conditions and wind severities:

### Type A: Dense Windbreak
Single or multi-row dense barrier for maximum wind reduction in extremely
exposed sites. Uses tall evergreen conifers in close spacing. Excellent for
coastal or ridge-top applications; not ideal for frost-prone valleys.
**Species**: *Cupressus macrocarpa*, *C. arizonica*, *Pinus radiata*, *P.
pinaster*, *Casuarina equisetifolia*, *Araucaria cunninghamii*, *A.
heterophylla*.

### Type B: Zig-Zag Planting
Staggered pattern creating variable gaps that disperse wind energy over a wider
area. No direct wind tunnels form through the barrier. Excellent for moderate
wind zones.
**Species**: *Acacia melanoxylon*, *A. dealbata*, *Casuarina glauca*,
*Cupressus sempervirens*, *Pinus halepensis*.

### Type C: Compound Windbreak
Multi-strata system (tall trees, medium shrubs, low groundcovers) in a belt
10-30 metres wide. Mimics natural forest edge structure and provides excellent
permeability across all height layers. Most biologically productive type,
yielding fodder, fuel, timber, and habitat alongside shelter.
**Species**: Tall — *Pinus radiata*, *Araucaria bidwillii*, *Casuarina
cunninghamiana*; mid — *Acacia floribunda*, *Leucaena leucocephala*,
*Prosopis glandulosa*, *Albizia lebbeck*, *tagasaste*; low — *Glyricidia
sepium*, *Hibiscus rosa-sinensis*, *Coprosma* spp., *Atriplex* spp.

### Type D: Permeable Low Hedgerow
Low (1-3 m) permeable hedgerow for inter-row shelter within cropping systems.
Ideal for orchards, vineyards, and market gardens where tall windbreaks would
shade crops or compete for water.
**Species**: *Glyricidia sepium*, *Leucaena leucocephala*, *tagasaste*,
*Cassia floribunda*, *Tecomaria capensis*, *Buddleja davidii*, *Pittosporum*
spp., *Coprosma repens*.

### Type E: In-Crop Windbreak
Individual trees or clumps within the crop area creating a distributed shelter
network. Effective in broadacre systems and pastures where boundary windbreaks
alone are insufficient.
**Species**: *Acacia aneura*, *Prosopis cineraria*, *Albizia saman*,
*Casuarina decaisneana*, *Leucaena leucocephala*, *Glyricidia sepium*.

## Savannah-Style and Complete Crown Cover

Two landscape-scale configurations complement linear windbreaks:

- **Savannah-style**: Scattered trees at 10-30% crown cover over pasture or
  cropland. Provides distributed shelter and shade without significantly
  reducing productive area. Wind speed reductions of 20-40% are achievable.
- **Complete crown cover**: Closed-canopy woodland (80-100% cover) providing
  maximum shelter. Suitable where the tree system is the primary production
  element (forestry, agroforestry, food forests).

## Importance for Production Systems

- **Orchard crop yields**: 30-50% wind reduction increases fruit set by 10-30%,
  reduces fruit drop, improves pollinator activity, and decreases water loss.
  Sheltered orchards consistently outperform exposed sites.
- **Mechanical damage prevention**: Windbreaks reduce branch breakage, fruit
  abrasion, flower desiccation, and uprooting, extending productive tree
  lifespan and lowering maintenance costs.
- **Salt-burn reduction**: Coastal windbreaks intercept salt-laden air,
  depositing salt on salt-tolerant foliage. Protected zones can support
  sensitive crops that would otherwise fail.

## Design Principles

### Site-Specific Modification
No configuration should be applied as a template. Designers must consider
prevailing wind direction, seasonal variation, topography (ridges, valleys,
slopes), frost drainage paths, soil conditions, and the crop/animal system
being protected. Coastal zones require salt-tolerant species; arid areas must
avoid deep-rooted species that compete with crops.

### Succession Planning
Windbreaks are long-term investments. **Nurse species** (*Casuarina*, *Acacia*)
establish quickly and provide initial shelter while slower climax species
(*Cupressus*, *Araucaria*) develop. Nurse species are thinned or removed as
the permanent windbreak matures, ensuring shelter from year one.
