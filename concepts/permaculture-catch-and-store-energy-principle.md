# Permaculture Catch and Store Energy Principle

## Overview

"Catch and Store Energy" is the second of David Holmgren's twelve permaculture design
principles. It focuses on the capture and long-term storage of energy — savings and
investment to build natural and human capital. The principle deals with building storages
for the future, while the companion principle "Obtain a Yield" addresses generating income
for immediate needs.

The icon of sunshine captured in a bottle suggests preserving seasonal surplus and myriad
traditional and novel ways to catch and store energy. It reflects the fundamental lesson
of biological science: that all life is directly or indirectly dependent on solar energy
captured by green plants. The proverb "make hay while the sun shines" reminds us that we
have limited time to catch and store energy before seasonal or episodic abundance
dissipates.

## The Energetic Foundation

Holmgren grounds this principle in thermodynamics and systems ecology. Throughout the
universe, energy spreads from centres of concentration to vacant regions, where it tends
to remain dispersed. Energy of high quality degrades into lower-quality forms, reducing
its power to do work — this is entropy, affecting every living and non-living system.

However, self-organising systems (primarily living ones) can capture and transform a
limited proportion of absorbed energy into storages of varying form and durability.
Stored energy is generally higher in quality than its source and capable of driving a
wider range of processes.

Howard Odum's energy circuit language illustrates how energy flows through ecosystems:
reducing energy along food chains, increasing power per unit, essential dissipation
losses, and feedbacks of high-quality energy to stimulate inflows.

## The Modern Pattern and Its Vulnerability

In modern affluent societies, energy flow has become so reliably available that capture
— and especially storage — has ceased to be a major concern. In extreme urban living, no
food or fuel is stored in the house, and purchasing power depends on credit tied to
permanent employment. Economic rationalism has led to decline of large-scale stores of
food, fuels, materials and spare parts in the name of efficiency, increasing
vulnerability to disruption.

Holmgren warns that we have been living by consuming global capital in a reckless manner
that would bankrupt any business. We need to learn how to save and reinvest most of the
wealth currently consumed or wasted so that descendants might have a reasonable life.
Conventional notions of value, capital, investment and wealth are not useful in this
task.

## How Nature Catches and Stores Energy

For billions of years, terrestrial ecosystems have co-evolved to catch and store energy
in water, mineral nutrients and organic carbon as effectively as possible. Solar energy
drives photosynthesis and weather systems. Geothermal power from the earth drives
tectonic movement, delivering rock minerals critical to soil fertility.

The capacity of terrestrial systems to hold water, mineral nutrients and organic matter
is limited and always being eroded by gravity. All terrestrial ecosystems and landscapes
can be seen as design systems to overcome or limit these entropic forces. The four key
landscape energy storages that permaculture focuses on are water, living soil, trees,
and seed.

### Water Storage

Vegetation holds rainwater in tissues and humid air trapped by canopies. Forest litter
acts as an open sponge. Soil chemistry and humus provide stable moisture supply. The
pattern runs from ephemeral to permanent storage as water moves under gravity. Swamps
and wetlands serve as temporary storages and filters — the "kidneys of catchment
landscapes."

### Living Soil and Humus

Humus is perhaps nature's greatest invention, increasing soil capacity to store mineral
nutrients, water, and carbon. Under favourable conditions, humus storages can remain
stable for hundreds or thousands of years. Wes Jackson described the loss of ancient
prairie soil carbon as "the mining of young coal." Rebuilding humus in cropping soils is
one of the two great tasks for humanity, alongside reforestation.

### Trees and Carbon

Trees are especially significant as carbon stores because of their ability to keep
accumulating carbon as wood for hundreds or even thousands of years. Timber and fuel
forests can be grown on marginal soils lacking the fertility for food crops — this is
the single most important reason why tree planting is central to permaculture energy
strategies.

## Rebuilding Natural Capital

Permaculture strategies for catching and storing energy in landscape group under four
headings:

1. **Water**: Build reservoirs, dams, swales, tanks and cisterns. Small catchment dams
   have more benefits per unit than large reservoirs. High water provides potential
   energy for irrigation and micro-hydropower. Shallow nutrient-rich water supports
   productive aquaculture.

2. **Living soil**: Return all organic wastes to productive soils. Eliminate intensive
   livestock that deplete soil carbon. Use leguminous pasture rotations. Replace
   soluble fertilisers with rock minerals and coal humus. Integrate tree systems into
   all farm landscapes.

3. **Trees**: Establish timber, fodder, and fuel forests as integral parts of farm
   landscapes, especially in high-rainfall areas. Emphasise soil-building fodder
   shrubs and long-lived timber trees.

4. **Seed**: Maintain genetic diversity through saved seed, preserving a genetic and
   cultural lineage from ancestors to descendants.

## Key Insight: The Problem Is the Solution

Site-specific energy opportunities abound. Gully floods provide nutrients and sediment.
Hot drying winds are an opportunity to dry fruit. Blackberries and other weeds serve as
mulch and fodder. Every sunny winter day charges a passive solar house. These examples
reflect how ecosystems develop in response to diverse and irregular energy availability
across landscapes.

## Sources of Renewable Energy

Important sources of energy currently poorly used but commonly available for building
greater personal and local self-reliance include:

- **Solar energy**: simple drying of wood and crops, passive solar design, water
  heaters and photo-electric panels
- **Wind energy**: pumping and power generation
- **Biomass**: sustainable management of trees and forests for fuel and construction
- **Run-off water**: irrigation, aquaculture and power generation

In addition, wastes from agriculture, forestry and industry — especially organic matter —
can be used for soil improvement, fuel and animal feed. These manufactured resources are
not necessarily renewable but can be tapped in the transition to low-energy systems.

## Soil Humus as Carbon Sink

Alan Yeomans argued that loss of humus from agricultural soils contributes as much to
greenhouse emissions as motor cars, and that achievable humus increases could reabsorb
the damaging carbon dioxide imbalance. The greenhouse issue provides another reason to
rebuild natural capital of soil humus.
