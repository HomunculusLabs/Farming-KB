# Mushroom Legislation Worldwide

## Overview

The legal status of [[psilocybin]]-containing mushrooms varies dramatically across the world, ranging from complete prohibition to decriminalization to regulated medical use. This page provides a global overview as of 2026. Laws change frequently; always verify current local regulations.

See [[psychedelic-mushroom-history]] for the historical context of prohibition.

## International Framework

### UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971)

- Psilocybin and [[psilocin]] are listed in Schedule I (most restrictive)
- This convention requires signatory nations to criminalize possession, manufacture, and distribution
- However, the convention does not specifically name "mushrooms" — only the isolated compounds
- This legal ambiguity has been exploited in some jurisdictions (e.g., the Netherlands)

## North America

### United States

- **Federal**: Psilocybin and psilocin are Schedule I under the Controlled Substances Act (since 1968)
- **State-level reform**:
  - **Oregon**: Measure 109 (2020) legalized psilocybin-assisted therapy under regulated program; Measure 110 decriminalized personal possession
  - **Colorado**: Proposition 122 (2022) decriminalized personal use and cultivation and established a regulated therapy program
  - **California**: SB 58 (2023) decriminalized personal possession, use, and cultivation
  - **Washington**: several cities (Seattle, Port Townsend, others) have deprioritized enforcement
  - **Massachusetts**: decriminalization under consideration as of 2026
  - **Washington DC**: Initiative 81 made entheogenic plant use and cultivation lowest law enforcement priority
- **Cultivation**: illegal federally; spores are legal in most states (except California, Georgia, Idaho) as they do not contain controlled substances

### Canada

- Psilocybin is Schedule III under the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act
- **Medical exemptions**: Special Access Program allows healthcare practitioners to request psilocybin for patients with serious or life-threatening conditions
- **Decriminalization**: no federal decriminalization; some cities (Vancouver, others) have deprioritized enforcement
- Spores and grow kits exist in a legal gray area

### Mexico

- [[psilocybin-mushrooms]] ([[teonanacatl]]) are not specifically scheduled in federal law
- Sale and commercial distribution are prohibited under general drug laws
- Traditional indigenous use ([[mazatec]] and other groups) is culturally tolerated
- See [[maria-sabina-mazatec-mushroom-ceremony]] for indigenous context

## Europe

### Netherlands

- Fresh "magic mushrooms" (paddos) were banned in 2008 following several high-profile incidents
- However, **magic truffles** (sclerotia of Psilocybe species) remain legal and are sold openly in "smart shops"
- This is because truffles were not specifically named in the legislation
- The Netherlands remains the most accessible country in Europe for psilocybin

### United Kingdom

- Class A controlled substance under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971
- Possession: up to 7 years imprisonment
- Supply/production: up to life imprisonment
- Fresh mushrooms were technically legal until the Drugs Act 2005 closed this loophole
- No medical or religious exemptions exist

### Germany

- Psilocybin is controlled under the BtMG (Betäubungsmittelgesetz)
- Possession, acquisition, and sale are criminal offenses
- Small quantities for personal use may result in charges being dropped (prosecutorial discretion)
- No medical use program as of 2026

### Spain

- Psilocybin and psilocin are listed as controlled substances
- Cultivation for personal use occupies a legal gray area — Spanish courts have ruled that growing for personal consumption is not a crime, though processing or selling is
- Several cities have indicated deprioritization of enforcement

### Portugal

- Personal possession and use of all drugs were decriminalized in 2001
- This includes psilocybin mushrooms
- However, sale and distribution remain criminal offenses
- Decriminalization means administrative penalties (fines, referral to treatment) rather than criminal prosecution

### Czech Republic

- Since 2014, cultivation of "magic mushrooms" for personal use is legal (up to a threshold)
- Sale remains illegal
- This was the result of a court ruling that mushrooms themselves are not listed, only the alkaloids

### Switzerland

- Psilocybin is controlled under Swiss drug law
- However, Switzerland has a progressive medical research environment; clinical trials are active
- Compassionate use programs exist in some cantons
- Some therapists operate in legal gray areas offering guided sessions

### Other European Countries

- Most EU countries classify psilocybin as a controlled substance
- Finland, Norway, Sweden: strict prohibition
- Austria: controlled; fresh mushrooms were a gray area until recent clarification
- Poland: controlled since 1998
- Italy: controlled since 1993

### EU Legislation Timeline (2001-2006, EMCDDA)

Six EU member states tightened mushroom legislation in response to increasing prevalence. The EMCDDA documented these specific changes:

| Date | Country | Legislative Change |
|---|---|---|
| Jul 2001 | Denmark | Added "Mushrooms or spores of P. semilanceata, P. cubensis or other fungi/spores containing psilocin or psilocybin" as controlled substance |
| Nov 2002 | Netherlands | Supreme Court ruled Opium Act applies to dried/processed mushrooms (but not fresh) |
| Mar 2005 | Germany | Changed definition from "plant" to "organism" to include mushrooms (closing fungal loophole) |
| May 2005 | Estonia | Clarified that cultivation of hallucinogenic mushrooms is an offence |
| Jul 2005 | UK | Drugs Act 2005: "Fungus (of any kind) which contains psilocin or an ester of psilocin" became Class A — no longer only "if treated or prepared" |
| Feb 2006 | Ireland | Criminalized possession/sale of "fungus of any kind containing psilocin" (exempt if growing uncultivated) |

**Legal challenges with wild fungi**: Several countries struggled with whether to criminalize landowners where mushrooms grow wild. Solutions included specifying "prepared/treated" mushrooms (UK/Ireland approach), "dried/processed" (Dutch Supreme Court), or "organism" rather than "plant" (Germany). The Italian, Greek, Cypriot, and Lithuanian laws used catch-all terms about "plants from which narcotic substances can be extracted," which technically may not cover fungi.

**Smartshop era**: Prior to the 2005 UK ban, an estimated 300 shops/market stalls sold hallucinogenic mushrooms across the UK with annual turnover around GBP 1 million. In the Netherlands, 120-150 [[smartshops]] sold mushrooms (estimated 50% of smartshop turnover). The Dutch fresh mushroom loophole persisted from 2002 until full ban in 2008.

## Latin America

### Brazil

- Psilocybin mushrooms are not specifically scheduled under Brazilian drug law
- Sale is prohibited but possession and personal use are not criminalized
- Several religious groups use [[ayahuasca]] legally; psilocybin mushrooms are not part of these traditions
- Growing interest in therapeutic applications

### Costa Rica

- Not specifically scheduled; mushrooms grow naturally in the country
- No active enforcement against personal use
- Several retreat centers operate openly, primarily catering to international visitors

## Asia and Oceania

### Japan

- Psilocybin is controlled under the Narcotics and Psychotropics Control Law
