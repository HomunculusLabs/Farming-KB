# Dutch Smart Shop and Paddos Trade History

## Origins: Dutch Drug Policy Pragmatism

The Dutch are famous for their pragmatism, and this was never more evident
than in their approach to the matter of drugs. Recognising that a drug-free
society, though perhaps a desirable ideal, was unobtainable, the Dutch
government acted to separate "drugs with acceptable risks" from "drugs with
unacceptable risks," both in law and in the marketplace. Putting cannabis in
the first category led most famously to the opening of the Dutch coffee
shops, where small amounts could be consumed openly without fear of
prosecution.

Their hands tied by the UN Convention on Narcotic Drugs (1961), the Dutch
were unable to legalise cannabis, but the half-measure of decriminalisation
freed them to maximise their efforts in preventing unacceptable drug use of
heroin, cocaine, LSD, and later Ecstasy. It was within this liberal climate
that the magic mushroom trade was born.

## Opening of the First Smart Shop

A young man named Dirk van den Huerk opened Holland's very first smart shop,
called Dreams, in Amsterdam in 1993. A disillusioned IT worker, drugs
charity worker, and Rave enthusiast, van den Huerk envisioned a shop that
would sell a mixture of vitamins and minerals along with legal synthetic
"designer" drugs. Dutch smart shops quickly proliferated, selling everything
from natural plant stimulants such as guarana and ephedra, to psychoactive
botanicals like Salvia divinorum and the San Pedro cactus, alongside arcane
synthetic compounds with names like 2C-B and 2C-T-2.

A cat-and-mouse game ensued, in which new drugs would come onto the market,
only to be banned a few months later by the ever-vigilant authorities. The
hunt was always on to find legal loopholes to exploit.

## The Legal Loophole for Mushrooms

Not long after opening, van den Huerk was approached by a home-growing magic
mushroom enthusiast who offered to supply mushrooms. After legal
consultation, he concluded that the law was sufficiently grey to put
psilocybin mushrooms on the market. Following the 1971 UN Convention on
Psychotropic Substances, the Dutch Opium Act only listed psilocybin and
psilocin as controlled substances -- and not the mushrooms themselves.

## The Paddos Boom

Mushrooms, known as "paddos" in Dutch, became big business. They could be
sold openly from market stalls, in head shops, and in specialist smart
shops. Inundations of tourists flocked to Amsterdam to sample these unusual
wares. Enterprising enthusiasts in Holland spotted the loophole in the law
and hit upon the novel idea of growing mushrooms commercially.

Growing methods were refined from the cultivation techniques pioneered in
America by Paul Stamets and Jeff Chilton (The Mushroom Cultivator, 1983)
and Stephen Hayden Pollock. Sophisticated operations emerged using
climate-controlled rooms, sterile substrate preparation, and efficient
harvesting techniques. Industrial-scale cultivation was perfected and
mushrooms were transformed by the cold hand of capitalism from a sacred
sacrament to a commodity.

## Relationship with Authorities

Dutch smart-shop owners quickly established adequate organisation to give
them more lobbying power, and a productive relationship developed between
the authorities and the smart-shop industry. This was not to say that
arrests never took place, or that the owners never got their knuckles
publicly rapped. But Dutch policy concluded that it was better for mushrooms
to be sold openly and transparently, so that sales could be monitored and
levels of use assessed.

## Market Scale and Usage Statistics

Statistics from Holland suggested that, however visible magic mushrooms were
in the marketplace, the numbers of people actually using them remained
relatively small. In Amsterdam, the percentage of the city's population
that had ever tried mushrooms stood at 7.6 per cent in 2001, a rise from
6.6 per cent in 1997. This compared with 1.3 per cent for heroin, 8.7 per
cent for Ecstasy, 10 per cent for cocaine, and 38.1 per cent for cannabis.

Most users were in their twenties. The percentage of people who had taken
mushrooms during the previous thirty days stood at a mere 0.3 per cent.
Of those 7.6 per cent of lifetime users, only 8.4 per cent had taken
mushrooms more than twenty-five times. Terence McKenna was right: psychedelic
shamanism was a calling, and most found that it was not for them.

## Eventual Crackdown

The Dutch experiment with legal mushroom sales eventually came under
pressure. In the late 2000s, following high-profile incidents involving
foreign tourists, the Dutch government moved to close the loophole. Fresh
mushrooms were banned, though spores and grow kits initially remained legal.
Smart shops pivoted to selling other legal plant hallucinogens and began
looking to open new markets in other European countries where the law was
sufficiently vague.

## The Psilocybe atlantis Discovery

One notable development within the Dutch trade was the commercial
discovery and marketing of the "Atlantis" strain, later identified as
Psilocybe atlantis, a particularly potent truffle-forming species. Dutch
growers found that sclerotia -- the dense, hardened masses of mycelium
produced by certain Psilocybin species -- could be cultivated legally and
sold as "truffles" or "philosopher's stones." These became increasingly
popular as fresh mushrooms faced legal challenges, and the truffle trade
allowed the industry to pivot without entirely disappearing.

## International Influence

The Dutch model inspired attempts to establish similar trades elsewhere in
Europe. Belgian, German, and Czech entrepreneurs opened their own versions
of smart shops, though none achieved the scale or longevity of the Dutch
original. The Dutch experience also influenced the broader drug policy reform
movement, demonstrating that regulated sales of currently prohibited
substances could coexist with low rates of problematic use -- a finding that
challenged the prohibitionist orthodoxy.

## Assessment

The Dutch smart shop era demonstrated both the possibilities and the
limitations of pragmatic drug policy. While the system successfully monitored
sales and kept usage rates low, it proved politically vulnerable to external
pressures and isolated incidents. The average dose in Holland was a tolerably
low fifteen grams of Psilocybe cubensis -- a quarter of that preferred by
McKenna-type psychonauts -- suggesting that people had learnt how to take
mushrooms safely. The Dutch model remains influential in drug policy reform
discussions worldwide, and the truffle trade that succeeded the mushroom
trade continues to operate under its own set of legal ambiguities.
