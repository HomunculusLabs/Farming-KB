## Mycorrhizal Community Dynamics and Functional Diversity

Mycorrhizal fungal communities are not static assemblages but dynamic entities that change in composition, structure, and function over time, across space, and in response to environmental perturbations. Understanding these dynamics is essential for predicting how ecosystems will respond to disturbance, climate change, and management practices.

## Succession of Mycorrhizal Fungi

### Ectomycorrhizal Succession

ECM fungal communities undergo predictable successional changes as forest stands develop:

- Fruit body production of basidiomycete ECM species changes during forest stand development, with specific reference to mycorrhizal species showing distinct patterns (Dighton et al., 1986)
- ECM succession follows patterns described for Scottish birch woodlands (Fleming et al., 1986)
- Sheathing [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] show successional trends across stand age (Last et al., 1987)
- General mechanisms of fungal succession have been explored, though the process remains somewhat unpredictable (Frankland, 1992, 1998)
- Below-ground processes in forest succession involve complex interactions between mycorrhizal fungi and soil development (Cromack, 1981)

### Patterns Across Stand Age

- ECM colonization potential in Scots pine stands varies with stand age and manipulation of litter and humus layers (Baar and de Vries, 1995)
- *Pinus muricata* can be colonized from resistant ECM propagules after stand-replacing wildfire (Baar et al., 1999)
- Needle litter and grass extracts can have both stimulatory and inhibitory effects on ECM fungal growth (Baar et al., 1994)
- Mycorrhizal infectiveness of soils varies along successional chronosequences (Boerner et al., 1996)
- Nitrogen and phosphorus cycling in relation to stand age of *[[eucalyptus-regnans]]* shows changing [[mycorrhizal-phosphatase-activity-elf97-detection]] and labile P pools (Polglase et al., 1992)

### Disturbance and Recovery

- Defoliation of mixed *[[pinus-contorta]]/[[picea-engelmannii]]* stands affects ECM community composition (Cullings et al., 2001)
- Repeated harvesting in Swedish spruce forests alters ECM communities (Mahmood et al., 1999)
- ECM fungal communities in late-successional Swedish boreal forests change following wildfire (Jonsson et al., 1999a, b)
- Management of mycorrhizas in revegetation is critical for restoration success (Jasper, 1994)
- Continuity of ECM fungi in self-regulating boreal *Pinus sylvestris* forests is maintained by comparing mycobiont diversity on seedlings and mature trees (Jonsson et al., 1999a)

## Diversity and Ecosystem Function

### Importance of ECM Diversity

The question of whether ECM fungal diversity matters for ecosystem function has been directly addressed:

- ECM diversity alters growth and nutrient acquisition of gray birch seedlings in host–symbiont culture conditions (Baxter and Dighton, 2001)
- Context-dependent effects of ECM species richness on tree seedling productivity have been demonstrated (Jonsson et al., 2001)
- Patterns and regulation of mycorrhizal plant and fungal diversity have been reviewed (Allen et al., 1995)
- Functional diversity in fungi encompasses variation in nutrient use, [[fungal-enzymatic-capabilities]], and ecological roles (Miller, 1995)
- The importance of ECM diversity for ecosystem function has been specifically argued (Leake, 2001)

### Host Plant Effects on Mycorrhizal Communities

- Host plant species effects on AM fungal communities in tallgrass prairie are significant (Eom et al., 2000)
- Comparisons of ECM on pinyon pines across extremes of soil type and herbivory reveal community variation (Gehring and Whitham, 1994)
- Mycorrhizal dynamics during forest tree development follow species-specific patterns (Dighton and Mason, 1985)

## Helper Bacteria and the Mycorrhizosphere

### Mycorrhization Helper Bacteria (MHB)

A significant discovery in mycorrhizal ecology is the role of specific bacteria that assist in the establishment of mycorrhizal symbioses:

- Helper bacteria represent a new dimension to the mycorrhizal symbiosis (Garbaye, 1994)
- MHB associated with the *[[pseudotsuga-menziesii]]*–*[[laccaria-laccata]]* symbiosis show specificity and function (Garbaye and Duponnois, 1992)
- Stimulation of mycorrhizal infection of *Pinus radiata* by microorganisms associated with ECM mantles has been documented (Garbaye and Bowen, 1987, 1989)
- Mechanisms involved in growth stimulation of ECM fungi by bacteria include both nutritional and signaling effects (Duponnois and Garbaye, 1990, 1991)
- Biological interactions in the mycorrhizosphere are complex and multifaceted (Garbaye, 1991)

### Mycorrhizosphere Communities

- Bacterial community structure and colonization patterns in beech ECM have been characterized using fluorescence in situ hybridization (Mogge et al., 2000)
- Interactions between mycorrhizal fungi and other soil organisms affect plant health (Fitter and Garbaye, 1994)
- Interactions between mycorrhizal fungi and rhizosphere micro-organisms occur within the context of sustainable soil-plant systems (Barea et al., 1997)
- Interactions among soil animals and ECM fungal mats influence nutrient cycling (Cromack et al., 1988)

## Interplant Linkages and Resource Sharing

### Common Mycelial Networks

The existence of linkages among plants by mycorrhizal fungal hyphae in natural ecosystems has been conclusively demonstrated:

- ECM mycelial interconnections facilitate carbon and phosphorus transfer between plants (Finlay and Read, 1986a, b)
- The interconnectedness between trees and fungi has been described as "fountains of the forest" (Rayner, 1998)
- ECM mediation of competition between coniferous tree species occurs through shared mycelial networks (Perry et al., 1989)
- Translocation of 32P between interacting mycelia of wood-decomposing and ECM fungi occurs in microcosm systems (Lindahl et al., 1999)

### Niche Differentiation

- Negative associations among AM and some ECM fungi inhabiting the same root system suggest niche partitioning (Lodge and Wentworth, 1990)
- ECM vs. endomycorrhizal fungi within the same root system show distinct colonization patterns (Chilvers et al., 1987)
- Spatiotemporal distribution of ECM fruit bodies in fir forests follows predictable patterns (Matsuda and Hijii, 1998)

## Competition for Nutrients

- Competition for nitrogen between plants and soil microorganisms is a key process in ecosystem nutrient cycling (Kaye and Hart, 1997)
- Indigenous AM fungal populations and soil aggregate stability are major determinants of plant response to inoculation (Hamel et al., 1997)
- Responses of plants to mycorrhizal fungi, phosphorus, and soil organic matter have implications for land reclamation (Johnson, 1998)
- Nitrogen dynamics in the rhizosphere of pine seedlings are affected by [[mycorrhizal-colonization-thresholds]] (Norton and Firestone, 1996)

## ECM Identification and Classification

- Colour atlases of ectomycorrhizae provide [[mushroom-identification-resources]] (Agerer, 1987–)
- Manuals of concise descriptions of North American ectomycorrhizae have been published (Goodman et al., 1996–2000)
- Discrepancies exist between macroscopic and microscopic classification of spruce ECM (Egli et al., 1993)
- Identification of ectomycorrhizas follows standardized protocols (Ingleby et al., 1990)
- Experimental studies of ECM establishment on birch root systems show spatial patterns (Gibson and Deacon, 1988)
