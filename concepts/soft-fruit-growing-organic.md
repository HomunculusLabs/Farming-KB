# soft fruit growing organic

Updated: 2026-04-18

Soft fruits grow on bushes, stakes, or briars and are ideal subjects for the small garden. The fruits soon deteriorate after picking, so those you grow in your garden will be of superior quality to anything bought [[williams-pdc-climates-and-biomes]] conditions. All soft fruits are very attractive to birds, so it is essential to protect ripening fruit with netting.

## Strawberries (Fragaria x ananassa)

One [[permaculture-plants-herbaceous-perennials]] [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]] Site
Well-drained but moisture-retentive soil. For increased drainage, raise the growing area to form 4ft (1.2m) wide beds. Sunny site, avoiding areas with little air flow (increases mildew risk).

### Planting
Buy cold-stored runners from a reputable grower who can guarantee freedom from disease. Dress the soil with two handfuls of [[triptolemus-eumolpus-and-the-founding-families-of-eleusis]] plant will not establish; too low and the crown will rot.

Strawberries can also be planted through black plastic on raised beds. Cover a 4ft bed with black plastic, cut slits 18in (45cm) apart, and plant through the slits. This suppresses weeds and prevents soil-borne disease.

### Maintenance
- **Feeding:** Little fertilizer needed. Too much nitrogen produces excess leaf growth at the expense of fruit. After the crop has been picked, sprinkle a handful of rock potash along each yard/meter of row
- **Mulching:** Once fruits start to swell, mulch with straw, tucking it right under the plants to raise fruit off the ground. This protects from slug damage and mud splashing. Do not mulch too early -- straw can insulate flowers from rising warmth and lower the temperature around developing fruit
- **Propagating:** Pin healthy runners into pots sunk beside the parent plant. Once rooted, separate from parent. Do not use for restocking beds as this can spread virus disease
- **Protection:** Cover ripening fruit with netting. Cover fall-fruiting strawberries with a cloche against hard frost
- **After harvest:** Cut all plants to within 1in (2.5cm) of the crown. Compost old leaves and straw (remove diseased leaves first)

### Harvesting
Pick fruits when red all over, pulling off the central core as well. Pick over regularly as new fruits ripen each day.

### Pests and Diseases
Aphids, slugs, botrytis, mildew, virus diseases, red spider mite, and birds.

## Red and White Currants (Ribes sativum, Ribes rubrum)

Easy to grow and very prolific. Can be grown in very small spaces as cordons. White currants use the same [[solomon-garden-hoe-and-cultivation-techniques]]. Both are self-pollinating.

### Varieties
Red: Red Lake, Rovada. White: Blanka.

### Shapes
Freestanding bushes or single, double, or triple cordons against a fence, wall, or post-and-wire support. Red and white currants are grown on a "leg" (short stem), unlike black currants which are "stooled" (from ground level).

### Soil and Site
Soil pH about 6.5. Sunny location. May not suit very cold areas as they flower in early spring.

### Planting
Best planted in fall or early winter. Remove any suckers before planting. Add two handfuls of bone meal per square yard/meter. Freestanding bushes at 5ft (1.5m) apart with 6ft (1.8m) between rows. Cordons: single at 1ft (30cm) apart, double at 2ft (60cm), triple at 3ft (90cm). Tie arms at right angles to the wires. Mulch with well-rotted manure or compost.

### Maintenance
- **Feeding:** Apply one handful of rock potash per square yard/meter in early spring. A browning of leaf margins indicates potash deficiency -- spray with liquid kelp and feed with rock potash
- **Pruning:** First three winters: prune main branches to leave half the season's growth. Cut sideshoots to 3in (7cm). Remove dead, diseased, or overcrowded branches. After three years: prune in summer immediately after harvesting. Reduce all sideshoots to five leaves
- **Protection:** Cover with netting when in bud. Cover flowers with fleece or netting if hard frost threatens

### Harvesting
Pick stalks from the bush and remove individual currants later with a kitchen fork. Suitable for freezing and canning.

### Pests and Diseases
Aphids, birds, mildew, sawfly, and leaf spot.

## Black Currants (Ribes nigrum)

A rich source of Vitamin C. Black currants will produce heavy crops in a sunny location. They require plenty of minerals so prepare the soil well. Self-pollinating.

### Varieties
Carter's Black Champion, Black Naples (older). Better modern disease-resistant varieties: Ben Sarek, Ben Nevis, Noirama, Ben Connan.

### Shapes
Grown as "stooled" plants with branches arising from ground level or below. Plant 2in (5cm) lower than they grew at the nursery and cut all shoots back to ground level.

### Soil and Site
Will grow in part shade but produce heavier crops in sun. Rich soil; pH maintained at 6.5.

### Planting
Best as bare root plants in fall or early winter. Set 5ft (1.5m) apart with 6ft (1.8m) between rows.

### Maintenance
- **Feeding:** High nitrogen requirement -- apply two handfuls of blood, fish, and bone meal per square yard/meter in early spring. Mulch with well-rotted manure or compost. If growth is poor, apply a second dressing in early summer
- **Pruning:** Black currants produce most fruit on wood made the previous year. After the initial cut-back at planting, prune established bushes in winter by removing a quarter of the oldest wood to ground level each year. This encourages new growth and maintains productivity
- **Protection:** Cover with netting when in bud

### Harvesting
Pick individual berries or strip whole strigs (clusters). Ideal for freezing, juicing, jam-making, and wine.

### Pests and Diseases
Big bud mite (transmits reversion virus), aphids, birds, and mildew.

## Raspberries (Rubus idaeus)

Easy to grow in temperate climates and respond well to organic methods. Can suffer from iron deficiencies.

### Planting
Plant canes 18in (45cm) apart with 6ft (1.8m) between rows, supported on wire. Plant in fall or early winter.

### Maintenance
- **Feeding:** Apply blood, fish, and bone meal in spring. Mulch heavily with well-rotted manure or compost
- **Pruning:** Summer-fruiting varieties: cut out the canes that have fruited immediately after harvest, leaving the new canes for next year. Autumn-fruiting varieties: cut all canes to ground level in late winter
- **Support:** Run wires at 3ft (90cm) and 5ft (1.5m) heights. Tie canes to the wires

### Harvesting
Pick when berries pull away easily from the plug. Pick every 2-3 days during the season. Ideal for freezing, jam, and eating fresh.

## Gooseberries (Ribes uva-crispa)

The earliest soft fruits of the season. May not be suitable for very cold areas because they flower in early spring. Can be grown as bushes or cordons like red currants.

### Maintenance
- **Feeding:** Apply rock potash in early spring
- **Pruning:** Create an open-centered bush shape. In winter, remove dead, diseased, and overcrowded wood. In summer, shorten sideshoots to five leaves
- **Protection:** Net against birds. Protect from frost when flowering

## Blackberries and Hybrid Berries (Rubus sp.)

Bramble fruits take up room but are worth growing. Newer varieties taste much better than wild ones. Loganberries and tayberries are hybrids that grow best in cooler climates. They need regular attention to tying in and training on wire supports.

### Planting
Plant 10ft (3m) apart on wire supports. Plant in fall or early winter.

### Maintenance
- **Pruning:** After fruiting, cut out the canes that have borne fruit to ground level. Tie in the new canes to the wires
- **Feeding:** Apply blood, fish, and bone meal in spring and mulch with compost
