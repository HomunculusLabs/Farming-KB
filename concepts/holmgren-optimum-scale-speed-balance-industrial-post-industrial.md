# Optimum Scale and Speed: Balancing Industrial and Post-Industrial Systems

## Overview

Holmgren's principle "Use Small [[permaculture-principle-use-small-and-slow-solutions]]" is really about finding the
optimum scale — neither too small nor too big, neither too slow nor too fast.
However, the emphasis must be on small physical scale and slow speed because of
the built-in cultural bias in modern society toward growth in scale and speed as
indications of what is good, effective, and powerful. The sustainable balance
between these polarities is not achieved with equal amounts of each.

## The Beam Balance Model

Holmgren proposes a beam balance analogy (rather than a seesaw) for
understanding the appropriate balance between large/small and fast/slow. The
beam balance has arms of different lengths, comparing the weight of goods to
calibrated small weights — more useful than the seesaw with its equal arms.
Both are superior to the pendulum, which illustrates a final equilibrium
(dead state) rather than dynamic balance.

### Industrial Imbalance

In the modern world, the growth of speed and mobility has tipped the balance,
with consequent fracturing and decay of slow and sedentary systems. Long-
distance, high-speed transport of fresh food has displaced local production
due to cheap energy and subsidised road transport.

### Rebalancing Requires Effort

To deconstruct fast systems and move up the steep slope to the other side of
the balance requires high initial energy and commitment. The balance model
reinforces the idea that descent from an energy peak is not an easy coast down
— it requires deliberate effort to rebuild slow systems.

### Overshoot is Necessary

In rebuilding slow and small systems, overshoot is inevitable and necessary —
analogous to rebalancing a set of scales by weighting the other end until it
tips. The enthusiasm for home-grown food has increased awareness of local
commercial production through subscription farming — a modest example of
overshoot and rebalancing in action.

## The Asymmetrical Balance Across Multiple Polarities

The beam balance model extends beyond scale and speed to characterise
differences between industrial and sustainable culture across many dimensions:

- **Linear or cyclical** flows of materials
- **Non-renewable or renewable** resources
- **Positive or negative** feedbacks
- **Centralised or distributed** network organisation
- **Central or edge** focus
- **Consumption or storage** of assets
- **Reductionist or wholistic** thinking
- **Episodic or rhythmic** activity

Each of these polarities requires the same asymmetrical rebalancing — not a
50/50 compromise but a deliberate weighting toward the sustainable pole.

## Ethical Constraints on Size

The Permaculture ethic of recognising and acknowledging limits provides a clear
foundation. In effect, "big is better" is a form of greed. The slogan "live
simply so others may simply live" captures this idea.

Traditional societies had boundless examples of ethics and taboos constraining
growth — from simple immediate urges to catch another fish, to warnings of evil
consequences from barely imaginable worlds. Colin Turnbull documented a
remarkable example: Congo Pygmy histories describing ancestors who lived in
cities that failed due to technological hubris — their own "Atlantis" story,
told by people whose largest settlements had been small agricultural villages.
