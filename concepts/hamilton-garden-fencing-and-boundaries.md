# Garden Fencing and Boundaries

Garden boundaries serve multiple purposes: providing privacy,
protecting plants from wind and animals, and defining the overall
structure of the garden. You will almost certainly need some sort of
fencing or hedge around your garden. There are many types of man-made
fencing available, so choose one to suit your needs and to blend with
your house and style of garden. Fencing and hedging also protect
plants from damaging winds, which can be particularly harmful in
winter if plants are not protected by snow cover.

## Choosing Fence Types for Sloping Ground

The easiest materials to use for fencing on a slope are wire fences,
plastic windbreak, or posts and rails because they can follow the
general fall of the land. Do not try to follow every little rise and
fall or the top of the fence will go up and down like a switchback.
Instead, even it out to form one overall slope. Panel fencing is
difficult to put up on a slope because panels cannot follow the line
of the slope. On gently sloping ground, erect panels vertically in a
series of steps, ordering extra panels and longer fence posts as
needed. Panel fencing is unsuitable for steep slopes because gaps
appear at one end of each panel.

Good options for slopes include post-and-wire link fences and
post-and-rail fences. A post-and-rail fence can be planted with a
hedge in front, or climbing plants can be trained up a wire fence.
For all slope fencing, make sure the posts are exactly vertical
regardless of the angle of the fence itself.

## Frost Pocket Prevention

On sloping ground, fencing built across a slope can act as a barrier
that prevents frost-laden air from moving downhill and out of the
garden, creating a frost pocket. To avoid this problem, raise the
bottom of the fence slightly to allow cold air to flow underneath.
Leave at least 1ft (30cm) of space between the bottom of the fence
and the ground so that cold air can drain away. This is a critical
consideration for gardens on slopes where late spring frosts can
damage fruit blossom and tender vegetables.

## Wind Protection Barriers

The most effective windbreaks are those that merely slow the wind
down, such as hedges, slatted wooden fences, or barriers made from
perforated plastic windbreak material. Solid barriers like brick
walls can be worse than useless as wind protection unless they are
extremely high. When wind comes up against a solid obstruction, it
tends to whip over the top and swirl around on the other side,
increasing in speed. If using man-made barriers, ensure posts are
sunk deep into the ground. Fix plastic windbreak material to posts
with battens by laying it against the posts and nailing battens
over the top.

Hedges make the best windbreaks. You can choose formal hedges, kept
compact by clipping, or informal ones allowed to flower. Hedges take
up [[stamets-growing-room-design]] because they compete with other plants for water and
nutrients. Never choose privet (Ligustrum ovalifolium) unless
prepared to sacrifice at least 1 yard or meter along either side. In
small gardens, choose a formal hedge that can be kept compact. In
large gardens, informal hedges need at least 3 to 6ft (1 to 2m) of
growing room on either side.

## Enhancing Existing Walls

Existing walls can be softened and integrated into the garden by
removing individual bricks and filling the gaps with soil to plant
[[alpine-plant-cultivation-guide]]s such as pink lewisias. A wooden trellis serves as both
a practical barrier and a decorative feature when shrubs or climbers,
such as scented roses, are trained against it. This approach turns a
bare wall into a living feature that supports wildlife and adds
ornamental value to the garden boundary.

## Steps and Paths on Slopes

Where the garden has different levels, wooden steps made from
railroad ties provide an inexpensive and attractive link between
areas. Hammer stakes into the ground behind the ties and nail them
securely. Stone steps can also be constructed using stone or brick
for the risers and stone slabs for the treads. Paths should link the
main features of the garden logically. Gravel paths at least 2in
(5cm) deep provide good drainage and can be edged with bricks or
timber to keep the gravel contained.

## Key Points

- Wire fences, post-and-rail, and plastic windbreaks are best for
  sloping ground as they follow the land naturally
- Panel fencing needs extra panels and longer posts on slopes; erect
  in vertical steps
- Leave at least 1ft (30cm) under cross-slope fences for cold air
  drainage to prevent frost pockets
- Slatted or permeable barriers slow wind effectively; solid barriers
  create damaging turbulence on the lee side
- Hedges are the best windbreaks but need significant growing room on
  both sides
- Sink fence posts deep and fix windbreak material with battens for
  stability
- Enhance walls by planting in gaps or training climbers on trellis
- Wooden railroad tie steps are an inexpensive solution for linking
  different garden levels
- Protect fruit blossom and tender crops from wind by ensuring
  boundaries provide effective shelter without creating frost traps
- When planning fencing, consider how it will look from the house and
  whether it complements the overall garden style
