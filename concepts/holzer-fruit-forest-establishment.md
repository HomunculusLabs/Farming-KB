# Holzer Fruit Forest Establishment

### Stratification

Holzer leaves the pomace to ferment for around four to five weeks and then distributes it over the area. During the fermentation process, the layers which naturally delay germination are broken down. This natural stratification greatly increases the seeds' chance for success. The fermentation mimics the natural process that seeds would undergo passing through an animal's digestive system or decomposing on the forest floor.

### Natural Selection

The seeds are distributed and allowed to germinate and grow naturally. Many will sprout; not all will thrive. This natural selection process eliminates the weakest individuals and leaves the strongest, best-adapted plants. Because the trees are growing in their intended location from the start, they can best adapt themselves to the soil and climatic conditions.

### Grafting

After the trees have been growing for one to two years, they can be grafted. Holzer only selects the best trees for grafting, which ensures he gets the optimal plants for the location. This selective approach means that every grafted tree is growing on rootstock that has already proven its ability to thrive in that specific location.

## Advantages of the Sowing Method

### Low Cost

Planting fruit trees using this method costs no more than planting any other kind of tree. The costs are not higher when establishing an orchard, because Holzer simply sows the trees and then grafts the desired varieties. Seeds from pomace are essentially free, and the pig-powered soil preparation replaces expensive mechanical cultivation.

### Superior Adaptation

Trees grown from seed in situ adapt themselves to the soil and climatic conditions from when they were seedlings. This is fundamentally different from nursery-grown trees that have been raised in optimal conditions and then transplanted -- often suffering transplant shock and struggling to adapt to less favourable site conditions.

### Genetic Diversity

Not all seedlings are grafted. The ungrafted trees can also produce interesting fruit. Holzer has received very sweet fruit ideally suited to schnapps from cherry seedlings. Wild fruit trees have their own value for wildlife forage, processed products, and as a genetic resource. The diversity of seedlings also lessens the chance of browsing damage from deer, as the varied growth habits and flavours make the planting less attractive as a concentrated food source.

### Reduced Risk

This method is particularly well suited to unfavourable areas, because the trees have had time to adjust to the local conditions from when they were seedlings. Trees that survive the natural selection process are inherently resilient. Even if a grafted variety fails, the rootstock continues to grow and can be re-grafted with a different variety.

## Forest Integration

Holzer advocates for integrating fruit trees directly into forest systems:

I also plant cultivated and wild fruit trees in the forest to increase the diversity of species there and to increase the range of functions available for my woodland plots. From my point of view, there is no reason not to simply plant fruit trees (cultivated and wild) together in a mixed culture in the forest.
