Source: David Holmgren, *Permaculture: Principles & Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002)

David Holmgren's vision for village-scale permaculture design emerges from his critique of the nuclear family as too small for ecological living and his practical experience designing and living in the Fryers Forest eco-village community.

## The Scale Problem

The nuclear family household is too small to be efficient in food production and preparation, as well as many other functions. The traditional extended family household of between five and fifteen members was large enough to efficiently support many aspects of [[permaculture-self-reliance-and-political-action]]. The expanding interest in eco-villages and co-housing as part of the Permaculture vision is implicit recognition of this problem.

## Co-Housing

The Danish co-housing movement provides a model where a group of people jointly develop medium-density private apartments or houses integrated with commonly owned and used facilities. The successful Danish model is being widely used globally. Key features include:

- Common ownership structure enabling shared infrastructure
- Integrated design making ecological technologies practical
- Demonstrates that people can live together and effectively self-govern their community
- A powerful alternative to the view that cooperating with neighbours is not possible or desirable

Most co-housing projects are in urban areas, and many are mainstream in architectural style and cultural demeanour.

## Intentional Communities and Permaculture

Intentional communities provide the "invisible structures" of land ownership, economic relationships, social services and decision-making processes necessary for full development of diverse permaculture aspects. The original conception of Permaculture drew on intentional communities as sources of ideas on ethics, technical and social alternatives.

The Farm in the United States, a long-surviving intentional community from the late 1960s, influenced the design of the Tagari Permaculture community which Bill Mollison established in Stanley, Tasmania in 1979. The Tagari strategy of buying cheap houses in an existing small country town or village, with farmland owned by the community within walking or bicycling distance, has been replicated by other communities.

The [[homestead-education-resources]] has been closely associated with the Permaculture movement. However, for most people in intentional communities, Permaculture remains an environmentally friendly method of growing food rather than the design and philosophical basis of the community itself.

## Fryers Forest Eco-Village

Holmgren's own contribution to community design is the Fryers Forest eco-village, incorporating 100 hectares of managed box forest. Key design elements include:

- Body corporate (strata title) land tenure integrating residential and productive land uses
- [[mollison-permaculture-two-zone-planning-and-site-layout]] at village scale: residential clusters surrounded by common forest land
- Thin regrowth eucalypt forest management providing sustainable timber yields
- Residential amenity balanced with agricultural and forestry activities
- Integration of [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] principles into community governance

Lessons from Fryers Forest include the importance of:
- Incremental development with feedback
- Balancing individual autonomy with collective responsibility
- Appropriate legal structures for land use rights and conflict resolution
- Realistic assessment of the economic incentives for productive land use by residents

## Impediments to Rural Eco-Village Development

Despite the potential, rural eco-village development has been slow due to:

- Planning schemes that give no incentives and may actively discourage this type of rural resettlement
- Cheap rural land from low commodity prices and poor economic viability of agriculture
- Reluctance to deal with the complexities and constraints of community
- Cheap food relative to wages reducing incentive for land-based livelihoods
- Continued economic growth providing better income opportunities elsewhere
- Perceived conflicts between residential amenity and agricultural uses
- Limited knowledge of suitable legal mechanisms for land use rights

## The Feudal Future Warning

Holmgren warns that without models of common ownership, redevelopment and management for broadacre farmland commons, the default model for a low-energy future will be some form of feudalism. The aggregation of farmland into large holdings makes this future likely. Large farms will again become communities of some sort in energy descent.

The Enclosure Acts in England began the global process of privatising the commons. Reform of land tenure remains one of the central sustainability and justice issues worldwide.

## Patterns of Competition and Co-operation

Holmgren draws an important ecological parallel: in rapidly growing systems with surplus energy, competitive relationships dominate; in mature ecosystems with little surplus energy, mutualistic and symbiotic relationships prevail. This suggests that as energy descent progresses, co-operative social patterns become both more natural and more necessary.

In harsh environments, some hunter-gatherer societies regarded competition between group members as evil. The Inuit of the Canadian Arctic reportedly killed missionaries who attempted to introduce competitive team sports.

## Designing Community

The most integrated application of permaculture principles is possible in establishing intentional communities. Holmgren identifies key elements:

- Consensus decision-making and conflict resolution
- Shared ownership of land, infrastructure and facilities
- bioregionalism-in-permaculture|Bioregional awareness and adaptation
- Balance of skills, ages, needs and personalities (too much similarity encourages competition)
- Ecological terminology applied to social functions (with caveats about oversimplification)
