# The Global Environmental Crisis: Forests, Soils, and Water

In the opening lecture of his 1981 [[permaculture-design]] Course, [[bill-mollison]] presented a systematic account of the accelerating failure of Earth's life-support systems. Rather than attributing ecological collapse to a single cause, Mollison traced interconnected breakdowns across forests, soils, climate, and water — arguing that these systems share a common root: human industrial agriculture and forestry operating without design. This lecture, titled "The Terrible Time of Day," remains one of the most cited and influential summaries of the environmental crisis from a [[permaculture]] perspective.

## The Forest Crisis

Mollison identified forests as far more critical to planetary oxygen cycling than previously understood. Contrary to the prevailing assumption that oceans were the primary oxygen source, Mollison argued that oceans contribute less than 8% of atmospheric oxygen recycling — and many are becoming net oxygen consumers due to mercury pollution and nutrient loading. The burden of maintaining breathable atmosphere falls primarily on forests.

Two forest systems are especially critical: equatorial multispecies rain forests and cool evergreen forests (the Russian taiga and southern evergreen forests). These systems are also responsible for a large proportion of global precipitation. When forests are cleared from ridges, Mollison observed, measured rainfall can decline by 10–30%. But the hidden effect is far greater: total precipitation (including fog drip, stem flow, and condensation that never reaches rain gauges) can fall by up to 86%. On quiet, clear nights with no recorded rainfall anywhere, major precipitation events occur within forest systems through condensation and horizontal precipitation capture — a process that ceases entirely when trees are removed.

### The Cutting Rate

Of all forests that ever existed, Mollison estimated that as little as 2% remain in Europe (with no truly primeval forests surviving), 8% in South America, and approximately 15% in other regions. The global cutting rate exceeds planting by one million hectares per year, though this figure can double in months of major clearing events (such as Mississippi bottomland forests cleared for soybeans). Even in the best-managed commercial forests, a constant loss rate of 4% prevails, giving approximately 25 years before exhaustion. In the Third World and wherever multinational corporations obtain forest ownership, the rate is far worse.

### The Phasmid Conspiracy

Mollison challenged the conventional explanation that insects and pathogens are killing the forests. Chestnut blight eliminated chestnuts — which constituted 80% of their forest type. Dutch elm disease removed elms. Beeches, oaks, eucalypts, and even Japanese conifers are now failing. The standard response varies by country: Americans spray, British fell and burn, and Australians shrug and say "let it go."

Mollison proposed that these "pests" are not causes but symptoms. He demonstrated experimentally that a tree injured by an axe, a bulldozer, or even a car bumper will attract decomposer insects within three days. The phasmids and fungi detect the chemical signals of a dying tree and arrive to feed on a carcass that is already dead. The real cause of forest death, he argued, is "multiple insult" — profound changes in light penetration, atmospheric pollutants, and acid rain. People, not bugs, are killing the forests.

## The Soil Crisis

By Mollison's account, 50% of all soils that existed before 1950 had been lost by 1981, with another 30% of the remainder degraded. This loss is global — equally true of the Third World and the Western nations.

### Soil Creation vs. Soil Loss

Soils are created at a rate of approximately four tons per acre per year in humid climates (less in dry areas), through the interaction of rainfall and plant roots breaking down rock. At four tons per acre per year of loss, a soil is at break-even. In Australia, cultivated land loses approximately 27 tons per acre per year. In the American corn belt, losses reach 400–500 tons per acre per year. For every person on Earth who eats grain, Mollison calculated, it costs approximately 12 tons of soil per year — all of it a direct result of tillage.

### Deforestation-Driven Salinization

The most insidious soil loss mechanism operates through deforestation at a distance. When forests are cut from upper watersheds, the hydrological cycle reverses. Trees act as pumps, keeping deep saline water tables low through transpiration. When the trees are removed, the water table rises measurably — across vast areas of America, Africa, and Australia. When saline groundwater reaches approximately three feet below the surface, trees begin dying of "phasmids." At 18 inches below the surface, crops die. When it reaches the surface, it evaporates and the soil visibly turns to salt.

The Australian government's response — providing free pumps to farmers while simultaneously leasing additional wood-chipping licenses to multinationals — illustrated for Mollison the perverse logic of industrial resource management: selling pumps on one hand and wood chips on the other, creating a "happy circumstance for some people, but a catastrophe for the Earth."

### Urbanization and Highway Construction

Cities occupy 11% of the Earth's very best soils. Canada exemplified the pattern: cities expanding onto prime agricultural land, forcing agriculture onto less sustainable margins. In Britain, approximately one mile of highway exists for every square mile of surface, and highways account for permanent, irreversible soil loss. The American Southwest practices a "cut and run" bore-well agriculture: drill into semi-saline groundwater, pump it onto annual crops for four years until the surface mineralizes (pH rising from 8 to 11), then move on.

## The Water Crisis

Mollison identified water as the world's emerging rarest mineral. Ancient aquifer systems — some 40,000 years in evolution — are being depleted far faster than they can recharge. Surface sealing through urbanization prevents groundwater recharge. In many cities, dissolved salts in drinking water have reached 700 parts per million, approaching the human kidney's tolerance limit of 1,100 ppm (above which fainting, tissue water accumulation, and death occur).

Industrial pollution compounds the crisis. Deep bore disposal of atomic waste has begun contaminating aquifers in the Sacramento Valley, Maine, New Jersey, and California. PCB contamination has been linked to approximately 20% sterility rates among American males by age 20. The chemical burden — DDT, PCBs, dioxin, chlorine — represents what Mollison called a "broad-scale release of chemicals with unforeseen and long-term results."

## The Energy Absurdity

Mollison underscored the systemic inefficiency of industrial civilization with a striking observation: the entire output of atomic power in the United States is equivalent to the energy requirements of clothes-drying machines. The Chinese agricultural case provided another illustration: traditional hand-weeded farming using natural manures produced about three times as much energy as it consumed. After modernization with small tractors, artificial fertilizer, and flame weeders, energy input increased by 800% while yield increased only 15%. As more energy was poured in, yields actually declined. Modern Chinese agriculture now recovers only 4–6% of the energy invested.

## The Over-Run Thesis

Mollison articulated what he called the "over-run thesis": "Our ability to change the face of the Earth increases at a faster rate than our ability to foresee the consequences of that change." This encapsulated the core problem — not that humans lack technical capacity, but that the speed and scale of intervention vastly exceed the capacity for prediction and correction. The academic temptation to "gather more evidence" rather than act was, in Mollison's view, itself a form of complicity. By 1950, he argued, sufficient evidence existed to begin remedial action. Every additional study documenting accelerating decline only confirmed what was already clear.

## The Permaculture Response

Against this backdrop of systemic failure, Mollison proposed that design for permanence — moving toward forests, permanent pastures, lakes and ponds, and non-tillage agriculture — was not idealistic but necessary. The specific strategies he outlined included water storage in soils, earth tanks, and sealed catchments; conversion to self-reliant bioregions; legal and financial reorganization; and the rapid training of hundreds of permaculture designers. The goal was not merely to slow the rate of destruction but to reverse it through designed systems that mimic the stability, diversity, and resilience of natural ecosystems.
