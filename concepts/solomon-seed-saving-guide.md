# Solomon Seed Saving Guide

## Overview

Saving your own seed is one of the most important skills for the self-reliant gardener. Steve Solomon's "Gardening When It Counts" provides practical guidance on seed saving, with particular emphasis on understanding the genetics involved and avoiding common pitfalls. See also [[solomon-complete-organic-fertilizer-guide]].

## Dry Seed vs Wet Seed

There are two basic vegetable-seed divisions based on their method of pollinating: "wet seed" and "dry seed."

- **Dry seed** forms in pods, in clusters on the stalk, or in the dried flower structures -- examples are beans, peas, lettuce, mustard, spinach, beet, okra, etc.
- **Wet seed** forms in juicy fruit that is still full of moisture when the seed has matured. These include squash, pumpkin, cucumber, melon, tomato, pepper, etc.

## Getting Vigorous Dry Seed

The key to getting vigorous dry seed is to let it mature fully but to keep it drying down steadily while it matures. Should the ripening process continue over weeks, sometimes the only way to keep drying seed from being remoistened is to yank the entire plant, shake the soil from the roots, and move it under cover, spreading it out on a tarp or hanging it above one to catch shattering seeds.

If the species makes large seed in big pods, such as peas or beans, the gardener can produce seed of a quality no commercial grower could dream of. We can pick each pod at the point of maturation when the stem end of the pod withers and it is clear that the plant's sap is no longer flowing into the pod. The seed won't have dried down hard at that stage, but any further nutrition added to the seed's food reserve will be coming from material held in the pod itself, translocated into the seeds as the pod withers completely.

## Getting Vigorous Wet Seed

The key to getting vigorous wet seed is to let the fruit become fully ripe on the plant before harvesting it -- allow these dead-ripe fruits to nearly ripen to the point of rotting on the vine before you extract the seed.

- For tomato seed, take your seeds from overripe fruit that has been sitting around a few days
- For melons, make sure the fruit chosen for seed honestly slips the vine
- For squash, allow the fully ripe fruit to cure for a month or so before extracting the seed

## Seed Storage

The key to seed longevity is proper drying. If seed is not dry, then it is moist. At high moisture contents and temperatures, the seed ages rapidly. It's best to dry seed down to the point where no further moisture can be removed under ordinary conditions.

Beet and chard seeds remain viable for six years normally and ten isn't unusual. Other species vary -- brassica seed is also long-lived. Most legume seed lasts 3-5 years properly stored.

## Inbreeding Depression

The single biggest pitfall when growing seed from outbreeding species is "inbreeding depression of vigor." To avoid this, the variety needs to have a diverse gene pool. The making population is either too few in number and/or too uniform in genetics, and the variety will die out. After only a few generations of inbreeding, a variety can become so weak that its own seeds will barely sprout and will grow so poorly that the variety becomes nonproductive.

Minimum population sizes to avoid inbreeding depression:
- For a few extremely vigorous cross-pollinated vegetables like kale or rutabagas, as few as a dozen plants might suffice
- For corn, the minimum number of individuals needed to maintain reasonable vigor may be as low as 50
- For other species, 200 plants might be the minimum
- For a full-sized cabbage variety, the minimum sustainable number is probably best at 200

## Outbreeding vs Inbreeding Species

- **Outbreeding species** (cross-pollinated by wind or insects): corn, brassicas, beets, chard, carrots, onions, squash, cucumbers. These require large populations and isolation distances to maintain variety purity.
- **Inbreeding species** (self-pollinating): tomatoes, peppers, lettuce, peas, beans. These are much easier to save seed from because each flower pollinates itself. A single plant can maintain genetic purity.

## Isolation Distances

- **Beets and chard** (wind-pollinated): at least a quarter mile (400 meters) for rough purity
- **Corn** (wind-pollinated): pollen is heavy and won't blow much more than one mile (1,600 meters). Isolating varieties by a quarter mile (400 meters) is good enough for home-garden seed if the second patch is not upwind
- **Squash family**: Must understand which species cross -- all summer squash, delicata, acorn, and pumpkins are C. pepo. Different species within the family won't cross-pollinate

## Specific Seed-Saving Notes

### Beets and Chard
Beets must overwinter before making seed the next spring. Replant roots to crown depth at least 1 foot apart in rows 3-4 feet apart. Harvest whole plants in midsummer when seed is dry, finish drying under cover, then rub seeds off stalks and winnow to clean. Include at least 25 plants in the gene pool. Four to six plants will make several pounds of seed lasting a decade if properly dried.

### Corn
Corn is wind-pollinated with heavy pollen. Grow in a patch at least two rows wide with at least six plants per row. Allow seedmaking plants to stand until fully dried in the field. Select the best plants each year. Growing seed from 50 desirable plants out of 100 or more requires a patch of at least 100 square feet.

### Cucurbits
Saving seed from only a few cucurbit plants leads to inbreeding depression within a few generations. The seed is long-lasting though -- first generation seed will supply you and the whole neighborhood for ten years. Solomon recommends not trying to save seed for more than one generation unless the seed-producing population exceeds 25 plants.

### Celery
Flowering process is like carrots, but celery produces thousands of small seed-bearing flowers instead of only a few per plant. Do not save seed from plants that bolt in their first year (annual behavior is undesirable). Maintain at least 25 plants in the gene pool.

## Threshing and Winnowing

For dry-seeded crops like brassicas and beets: harvest whole plants when seed is dry on the stalk, finish drying under cover on a tarp, then rub seeds off stalks by hand or by light threshing. Winnow by pouring seed from one container to another in a light breeze or using a fan to separate seed from chaff.
