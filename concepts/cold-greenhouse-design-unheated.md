# Cold Greenhouse Design for Unheated Winter Growing

## Overview

Cold greenhouses (also called unheated greenhouses, high tunnels, or hoophouses) are structures that rely entirely on passive solar energy and thermal mass to maintain growing temperatures above freezing during winter months. Eliot Coleman pioneered the commercial use of unheated greenhouses in Harborside, Maine (latitude 44°N), demonstrating that year-round [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]] is feasible even in harsh northern climates without supplemental heating. The core principle is that plants can survive freezing nighttime temperatures as long as daytime temperatures rise sufficiently to allow photosynthesis and growth.

## Structural Design Principles

### Orientation and Placement

The greenhouse should be oriented with its long axis running east–west. This orientation maximizes the amount of southern exposure for the curved roof surface, allowing the structure to capture the maximum possible solar radiation during the short winter days. In the northern hemisphere, the sun stays low on the southern horizon from October through February, so every degree of south-facing glazing matters.

Site selection is critical. The greenhouse should be placed on level ground with good drainage. Avoid frost pockets (low-lying areas where cold air accumulates) and locations shaded by trees, buildings, or hills to the south. A windbreak to the north and west provides protection from prevailing winter winds without blocking sunlight.

### Frame Materials

Commercial cold greenhouses typically use galvanized steel tubing for the hoop frame. Steel provides the [[mycelium-running-rhizomorphic-mycelium-structural-strength-erosion-control]] needed to support snow loads and wind pressure while maintaining the curved shape that sheds snow effectively. The Gothic arch design is preferred over simple Quonset shapes because the peaked center sheds snow more reliably and provides more headroom along the sidewalls.

Wood-framed greenhouses are an alternative for smaller operations. They offer better insulation properties than metal but require more maintenance and have a shorter lifespan. Any wood in contact with soil should be naturally rot-resistant (cedar, locust) or pressure-treated.

### Glazing Options

The choice of covering material significantly affects performance:

- **Polyethylene film (poly)**: Single-layer or double-inflated poly is the most common and economical choice. Double-layer inflated poly provides an insulating air gap that reduces heat loss by approximately 30% compared to single-layer. Expected lifespan is 4–6 years. Coleman recommends a minimum of 6-mil greenhouse-grade poly with UV inhibitors.
- **Polycarbonate panels**: Rigid twin-wall or triple-wall panels offer superior insulation (R-value 1.5–2.5) and durability (15–20 year lifespan) but at significantly higher cost. They are more appropriate for permanent structures where the investment can be amortized.
- **Fiberglass**: Older technology with moderate insulation and light transmission. Yellowing over time reduces light quality. Generally not recommended for new construction.

## Thermal Management

### The Inertia Effect

The fundamental mechanism keeping plants alive in an unheated greenhouse is thermal inertia. The soil mass inside the greenhouse absorbs solar radiation during the day and releases it slowly at night. A properly managed cold greenhouse can maintain temperatures 10–15°F (6–8°C) above outdoor ambient temperatures on a clear winter night, even without supplemental heat.

Coleman's measurements at Four Season Farm showed that on a sunny January day with an outdoor high of 28°F (-2°C), the [[coleman-greenhouse-interior-design-and-management]] reached 65°F (18°C), while the outdoor low that night dropped to 10°F (-12°C). The greenhouse interior, however, only dropped to 28°F (-2°C) — above the damage threshold for cold-hardy crops.

### Interior Climate Zones

Within a single unheated greenhouse, distinct microclimates exist:

- **The warm zone** (center and south-facing areas): Receives the most direct sunlight and has the warmest daytime and nighttime temperatures. Best for less cold-tolerant crops.
- **The cool zone** (along the north wall): Receives less direct light and stays cooler. Suitable for the most cold-hardy crops.
- **The cold zone** (near doors and ventilation openings): Subject to temperature fluctuations from air exchange. Best used for storage or as a staging area.

Understanding and exploiting these zones allows growers to maximize the diversity of crops that can be grown simultaneously.

## Ventilation and Air Circulation

### Passive Ventilation

Even in winter, greenhouses require ventilation. On sunny days, temperatures can rise rapidly to levels that stress or kill plants (above 85°F/29°C). Roll-up sidewalls are the most effective passive ventilation system. They open from the bottom, allowing cool air to enter along the ground while warm air exits through ridge vents at the peak. This creates a chimney effect that exchanges the entire air volume in minutes.

### Automated Systems

Thermostatically controlled vent openers are inexpensive and reliable. They use a wax cylinder that expands with heat to push the vent open, requiring no electricity. Setting the opener to activate at 70°F (21°C) provides adequate protection while maximizing growing degree days.

## Snow and Wind Load Considerations

### Snow Management

The curved shape of hoophouses naturally sheds snow. However, wet, heavy snow (especially the mix of snow and rain known as "heart attack snow") can accumulate faster than it slides off. Coleman recommends:

1. Monitor snow accumulation during storms. If more than 4–6 inches accumulates without sliding off, manually remove it using a long-handled brush or by tapping the frame from inside.
2. Keep the air temperature slightly above freezing during wet snow events by closing vents early in the afternoon. The small amount of retained heat can prevent snow from sticking.
3. Install heat tape or a small heater in the gutters to create drainage channels that help snow slide.

### Wind Resistance

Proper anchoring is essential. Ground posts should be driven at least 24–30 inches into firm soil, or set in concrete footings in sandy or loose soil. End walls should be braced with diagonal supports, and all connections should use bolts rather than screws for critical structural joints. Wind straps connecting the ridge to ground anchors provide additional security against uplift.

## Size and Scale Considerations

### Minimum Viable Size

Coleman recommends a minimum width of 30 feet (9m) for commercial viability. Narrow structures have a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio, meaning they lose heat faster relative to the protected growing space. The 30-foot width has become a de facto standard because it allows four raised beds with adequate walkways, sufficient internal air mass for thermal inertia, reasonable snow-shedding geometry, and economical use of standard-length materials.

### Length

Length is primarily determined by available space and production needs. A common commercial size is 30 × 96 feet (9 × 29m), which provides approximately 2,400 square feet of growing space. Longer structures benefit from a lower end-wall-to-sidewall ratio, improving thermal efficiency.

## Cost Economics

The capital cost for a basic 30 × 96-foot unheated hoophouse ranges from $3,000 to $8,000 depending on materials and whether it is owner-built or professionally installed. Operating costs are minimal: replacement poly every 4–6 years ($500–$1,000), and occasional repairs to the frame and ventilation system.

The economic return comes from extending the growing season by 2–3 months on each end (spring and fall) and enabling winter harvest of high-value crops during periods when local, fresh produce commands premium prices. Coleman's economic analysis shows that a well-managed cold greenhouse can generate $3–$5 per square foot annually from winter crops alone.

## Water Management

Proper water management inside a winter greenhouse requires attention to several factors that differ from outdoor growing:

- **Reduced irrigation needs**: During winter, plants grow slowly and transpire minimally. Overwatering is a more common problem than underwatering. Check soil moisture before watering; the surface may appear dry while the root zone remains adequately moist.
- **Drip irrigation**: Overhead watering raises humidity and promotes fungal diseases. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone, keeping foliage dry. Even a simple soaker hose laid along the bed surface is preferable to overhead spraying.
- **Drainage**: Raised beds should be designed to drain freely. Standing water in winter greenhouses creates cold pockets that can freeze plant roots. Ensure the greenhouse floor has a slight slope (1–2%) toward drainage points.

## Integrated Pest Management in Winter

One advantage of winter growing is the natural reduction in pest pressure. Most insect pests are dormant or killed by sustained freezing temperatures. However, some pests persist:

- **Aphids**: Can survive and reproduce in the sheltered greenhouse environment. Introduce ladybugs or lacewings as biological controls. Yellow sticky traps provide monitoring and some control.
- **Fungus gnats**: Thrive in moist greenhouse potting media. Allow the soil surface to dry between waterings and [[query-how-to-use-beneficial-nematodes-for-soil-pest-control]] (Steinernema feltiae) as a preventive soil drench.
- **Slugs**: Active during mild winter spells. Beer traps and diatomaceous earth barriers around bed edges provide effective control.
