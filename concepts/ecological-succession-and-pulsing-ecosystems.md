# Ecological Succession and Pulsing Ecosystems

Ecological succession is the process by which biological communities of plants, animals, and microorganisms change in structure and species composition over time. In permaculture design, understanding succession is fundamental to working with natural systems rather than against them. David Holmgren's *Permaculture: Principles & Pathways Beyond Sustainability* extensively references succession as both an ecological model and a design framework for creating productive, resilient landscapes.

## Primary Succession and Pioneer Species

Primary succession begins on barren surfaces — lava flows, glacial moraines, or severely degraded land. Pioneer species are the first colonizers: lichens, mosses, and hardy annual plants that can tolerate extreme conditions. These species are typically short-lived, fast-growing, and adapted to nutrient-poor environments. They begin the process of soil formation by breaking down rock and accumulating organic matter.

In permaculture, pioneer plants are used strategically to prepare sites for more productive systems. Species like daikon radish, for instance, can break compacted subsoil and bring minerals to the surface. Understanding which plants serve as pioneers in different climates allows designers to accelerate natural succession toward desired endpoints.

## Secondary Succession and Agricultural Recovery

Secondary succession occurs on sites where an existing community has been disturbed — abandoned farmland, cleared forests, or burned areas. This is the most common context for permaculture design, since most degraded land has some residual soil biology and seed bank.

Fire plays a critical role in many ecosystems as a pulse that resets succession. Indigenous Australians practiced firestick farming for millennia, using controlled burns to manage landscapes and promote new growth. In Australia, fire interacts with drought in complex ways, sometimes driving ecological drift toward more fire-adapted communities. Forest regeneration after fire follows predictable successional patterns that permaculture designers can work with.

The fertility of landscapes follows successional patterns too. Indigenous Australian landscapes showed incremental fertility decline after disruption of traditional management practices. Understanding these patterns helps explain why conventional agriculture often degrades soil over time.

## The Four-Phase Model of Ecosystem Change

Holmgren references a four-phase model of ecosystem change (adapted from C.S. Holling) that describes how ecosystems cycle through phases of exploitation, conservation, release, and reorganization:

1. **Exploitation (or reorganization)**: Rapid colonization by pioneer species after disturbance. High energy flow, fast growth, and rapid nutrient cycling characterize this phase. In permaculture, this corresponds to the initial establishment phase of a design.

2. **Conservation**: The system matures, building complex structures and storing energy. Biomass accumulates, biodiversity increases, and nutrient cycles become more closed. Climax forests represent this phase. For permaculture, this is the mature food forest or established polyculture.

3. **Release**: External disturbance (fire, storm, harvest) releases stored energy and nutrients. This pulse of change is not inherently destructive — it is part of the natural cycle. Permaculture designs can incorporate planned pulses through coppicing, grazing rotations, and seasonal harvests.

4. **Reorganization**: The system reassembles from remaining resources, often with new species compositions. This phase creates opportunities for innovation and adaptation.

## Pulsing Agricultural Ecosystems

Rather than fighting against natural pulses, permaculture designs agricultural systems that work with them. Pulsing agricultural ecosystems are managed to cycle through productive phases while maintaining long-term system health. This contrasts with industrial agriculture, which seeks to maintain continuous maximum output — an approach that Holmgren argues is inherently unsustainable.

Key examples of pulsing in agricultural design:

- **Rotational grazing**: Moving livestock through paddocks allows pasture plants to recover between grazing pulses, maintaining root systems and soil biology.

- **Coppicing and pollarding**: Harvesting wood from trees on multi-year cycles allows the tree to regrow, providing continuous yields while maintaining the living root system and its soil-building functions.

- **Fallow periods**: Allowing land to rest periodically rebuilds soil organic matter and breaks pest cycles.

- **Fire management**: Controlled burns in appropriate ecosystems can maintain grasslands, reduce fuel loads, and promote new growth.

## Forest Succession and Permaculture Food Forests

Forest succession provides the primary model for permaculture food forests. The progression from grassland to shrubland to young forest to mature canopy forest mirrors the development of a designed food forest over time.

Understanding this progression allows designers to:

- **Anticipate changes**: Knowing that fast-growing pioneer trees will eventually be shaded out by slower-growing climax species means designers can plan for succession rather than being surprised by it.

- **Accelerate succession**: By planting nitrogen-fixing trees, adding organic matter, and introducing key species, designers can speed the transition from degraded land to productive polyculture.

- **Maintain productive stages**: Not all stages of succession are equally productive for human use. Pruning, grazing, and selective harvesting can maintain a system in a productive mid-successional state rather than allowing it to progress to a mature but less productive climax.

Different forest types show different successional patterns. Rainforests exhibit remarkable stability, while sclerophyll forests (dominated by eucalypts) are more dynamic, shaped by fire and drought. Mixed rainforest and wet sclerophyll forests in southeastern Australia represent particularly complex successional landscapes.

## Energy Storage in Successional Systems

As ecosystems mature through succession, they store increasing amounts of energy in biomass, soil organic matter, and complex food webs. Landscape storages of energy — in timber, soil carbon, water, and biodiversity — represent the accumulated capital of ecological succession.

Holmgren emphasizes that sustainable land management requires maintaining these storages while harvesting only the surplus. This is analogous to living on interest rather than drawing down capital. Industrial agriculture, by contrast, routinely depletes soil carbon, mineral nutrients, and biodiversity — effectively mining the accumulated wealth of past succession.

## Punctuated Equilibrium and Evolutionary Change

The concept of punctuated equilibrium — long periods of relative stability interrupted by rapid change — applies to both ecological succession and social systems. Holmgren draws parallels between ecosystem dynamics and cultural change, suggesting that societies, like ecosystems, go through phases of conservation and release.

This framework helps explain why gradual reforms often fail to address systemic problems: the system is in a conservation phase, resistant to change. Only when a release phase occurs — through crisis or collapse — does significant reorganization become possible. Permaculture design aims to build resilient systems that can survive release phases and reorganize in productive directions.

## Design Implications

For permaculture practitioners, the lessons of ecological succession include:

- **Work with succession, not against it**: Design for the expected trajectory of your site rather than trying to maintain a static state.

- **Use pioneer species strategically**: Fast-growing, nutrient-accumulating plants can prepare the ground for more desirable species.

- **Plan for disturbance**: Build redundancy and resilience into systems so they can recover from fire, drought, or other pulses.

- **Maintain soil biology**: The soil food web drives succession; protecting and feeding it accelerates recovery and productivity.

- **Observe before intervening**: Understanding the current successional stage of a landscape is essential before making design decisions.

- **Think in cycles**: Long-term thinking about cycles of change (decades to centuries) is necessary for truly sustainable design, not just seasonal planning.
