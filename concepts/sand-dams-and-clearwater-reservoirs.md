# Sand Dams and Clearwater Reservoirs

## Overview

Sand dams and clearwater reservoirs are among the most effective [[sand-dam]] system is elegantly simple:

1. **A barrier wall** is built across a wash or sandy watercourse in gently-rolling foothills.
2. **Floodwaters rush down and over the wall** during rain events.
3. **Silt and coarse sands settle out** behind the wall, filling the storage area over several rain events.
4. **Shingle or rocks placed against the upstream side** of the wall act as a filtered seepage to a clearwater dam.
5. The dam also **leaks water through the river bed** — this is intentional — recharging downstream areas.
6. **A subsurface reservoir** of moist sand is created, capable of storing enormous volumes of water with minimal evaporation.

## Sand Dam Construction

### Site Selection Criteria

- Ideally located in **gently-rolling foothills** where washes carry broad floodwater surges.
- The rubble and silt in the dry creek bed should be **very open and permeable**, composed of stones, pebbles, and coarse sands.
- Proximity to **headwater rocks** or foothills ensures adequate recharge material.
- The floor of the dam is ideally **clay or massive rock**, making it leak-proof at the base.

### Wall Construction

- The wall must be **keyed in for 4-6 metres** to the side banks, or water will scour around the ends.
- Before construction, a series of **2-metre deep bays** must be cut into the upstream banks.
- Several loads of **coarse stone** should be placed against the upstream side of the wall to form a filtered drain.
- The clearwater dam itself should be **less than one-ninth of the total area** backed up by the wall.

### Staged Construction for Large Dams

Extensive silt dams of 9-12 metres in height should always be built in **1.5 to 2 metre stages**, with the wall raised as earlier stages silt up. Behind each wall stage, gabions allow water to filter down to a base pipe that can be led to settlement. The entire catchment area needs protection from sprays, fertilisers, and browsing animals.

## The Clearwater Dam

### Siting Principles

Clearwater dams in deserts should be built **well away** from the main valley barrier dam sites. The turbulent flow from wadis or foothill sand-bed and boulder streams must be bled off into almost-level channels fitted with sand traps before reaching the dam. The dam should not be sited so that fast water can run into it over loose surface material. It should be fenced and the margins planted to rushes and shrubs.

### Design Specifications

- **Depth**: All clearwater dams should be **5-9 metres deep** with reduced surface area, rather than shallow and wide. Deep dams with small surface areas lose far less water to evaporation.
- **Sand trap upstream**: A weir and grill system allows water from a minor stream to be diverted to the clearwater dam via a sand trap that removes suspended sediment.
- **Fencing**: Essential to exclude livestock and protect water quality for settlement use.

## Downstream Effects

### Extended Forest Zone

The downstream forest may extend **1-2 kilometres** in the sands of the river, fed by a wall only 50 metres wide. This remarkable extension of productive land occurs because subsurface seepage through the sand creates a continuously moist zone that supports extensive tree growth.

### Tree Species Selection

- **Upstream forest** (on silt beds): pecan, palm, avocado, mango.
- **Dam forest** (around the clearwater dam): species tolerant of periodic waterlogging.
- **Downstream forest**: benefits from subsurface seepage, supporting a wide range of species.

### Barrier Dam Ecology

When the barrier dam is sand-filled, several sub-aquatic species grow in the moist substrate, including asparagus, ti palms, Chinese water chestnut, swamp taro, and others. The rubble and sand act as a buried reservoir that keeps the dam full — one documented sand dam dropped only 8 centimetres in level over a 7-year period.

## Agricultural Production

### Immediate Cropping

On the silt dam itself, melons grow very well as the first swampy condition recedes. A good crop of legumes and melons can be planted at that time, making productive use of the transitional period between full inundation and establishment of permanent tree cover.

### Guano Harvesting

Below roost trees around such dams, the guano from desert birds may be gathered as sheets of phosphate fertiliser. This is of great value in establishing dune or rock windrow trees when used as a dilute solution or mixed with 30-40 parts of damp sand.

### Village-Supporting Capacity

A few such dams can support a strong village population and keep scour holes downstream recharged with water for swimming holes. The Oodnadatta dam in South Australia, built by a railway gang in the 1920s, was still functioning well in the 1980s — demonstrating the extraordinary longevity of well-constructed sand dam systems.

## Pitting in Sands and Light Soils

### The Disc Pitting Method

By cutting a quarter-section slice off a set of disc blades, long pits are made with a quarter of their length as solid breaks. As discs are drawn along, a series of mini-swales is created, staggered like contour swales.

### Advantages

- **Not strictly on contour** — pits can be drawn by eye, simplifying implementation.
- **No single pit is large enough** to spill too much water and cause stream flow.
- **Seed can be added** to pits, broadcast, or blown in from residual vegetation.
- **Fast and effective** — can be completed on the broadscale.
- **Supports multiple species**: grasses, shrubs, trees, and forbs.

### Specifications

- **Spacing**: 0.5-0.75 metres apart.
- **Length**: 1.0-1.5 metres.
- **Depth**: 0.3 metres maximum.
- **Soil**: ideally sidecast downhill or downwind (not critical on near-flat sites).

### Broadscale Impact

Over many acres, these pits have enormous storage capacity. They effectively become small swales for areas of bare soil. The method acts as surface roughening and eliminates or greatly decreases dust storms as soon as it is made. Pelleted desert shrub seed plus a light fertiliser dressing creates conditions for permanent soil stability around settlements.

## Contour Furrow Systems

For slopes in excess of 2%, a series of contour furrows or mini-swales at 2-metre intervals is recommended, with only every 20-30 contours actually surveyed. The swales are "broken" to allow flooding through to the next series. Vegetation in the furrows remains green in drought.

## Rockholes and Gnammas

Rock basins, often filled with water, arise from several origins:

- **Rock scouring** (corrosion) in stream flow on stream beds.
- **Freeze-thaw and wet-dry** swelling and shrinking, plus rock mineral hydration.
- **Salt-eroded ponds** in rock, particularly near the sea.

### Water Conservation Methods

- Fill the hole with pebbles to lower evaporation (some water is lost).
- Thatch or cover the hole.
- Build gutters to direct water into the hole.
- Erect a metal mesh fence to direct dew condensation into the hole.
- Deepen the hole and plaster with cement if it leaks.

## Spilling Water Downslope in Fragile Soils

Steep-sided gullies often result from road culverts, animal tracks, or water discharged from diversion drains. Safe methods for spilling water downslope in deserts include:

1. **Very long spreader-banks** — maintained and inspected in rain.
2. **Grassed spillways with steering banks** — take a few years to fully evolve.
3. **Downslope sealed spillways or pipes** with splash-pools leading to a [[swale]] or dam.
4. **V-shaped channels** filled with boulder, smaller rock, then gravels — forcing water to follow multiple stable paths and imitating the rock sorting of dryland breccia slopes below cliffs.

## Evaporation and Evapotranspiration

### The Challenge of Open-Water Evaporation

In sub-humid to arid areas, open-pan evaporation always exceeds or equals precipitation over the year. This is the fundamental challenge that sand dams address — by storing water within sand and rubble, evaporation is dramatically reduced compared to open water surfaces.
