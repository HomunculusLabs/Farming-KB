# Soil Self-Plowing and Earthworm Ecology in Natural Farming

Fukuoka's radical assertion that "the soil lives of its own accord and plows itself" challenges the most fundamental assumption of conventional agriculture: that tillage is necessary to prepare soil
for crop production. He argued that the physical loosening, aeration, and enrichment of soil — the very purposes for which farmers plow — are accomplished more effectively and sustainably by the
combined action of plant roots, microorganisms, and soil animals, particularly earthworms. Human tillage, far from helping these processes, actively destroys them and creates the conditions that make
future plowing appear necessary.

## How Nature Plows the Soil

Fukuoka observed that in nature, grasses and green manure crops work the soil to depths of twelve inches, fifteen inches, or more — far deeper than the four to eight inches achieved by a farmer's
plow. When plant roots reach down deep into the earth, air and water penetrate the soil together with the roots. As these roots wither and die, many types of microorganisms proliferate. These
organisms die and are replaced by others, increasing the amount of humus and softening the soil.

The process follows a predictable ecological succession: wherever humus accumulates, earthworms eventually appear. As the number of earthworms increases, moles begin burrowing through the soil.
Together, these organisms create channels, loosen compacted layers, incorporate organic matter, and maintain soil structure — performing all the functions of a plow without any human effort.

Fukuoka summarized the principle succinctly: "In nature, the very grasses and trees, and the earthworms and moles in the ground, have acted the part of plow horse and oxen, completely rearranging and
renewing the soil. What can be more desirable to the farmer than being able to work the fields without pulling a plow or swinging a hoe?"

## Why Human Plowing Is Counterproductive

Fukuoka argued that human tillage does not merely fail to improve the soil — it actively creates the conditions that make future plowing necessary. The sequence of destruction works as follows:

**Weeding and plowing** each year deplete the fertility of the soil, create trace component deficiencies, diminish soil vitality, harden the topsoil, kill off microbes, and turn rich living organic
material into dead, inanimate, yellowish-white mineral matter whose only function is to physically support crops. Each time the soil is worked, soil particles become finer and finer, hardening the
ground.

**Clearing new land** by pulling up trees and bushes robs the deeper soil layers of a source of humus, halting the active proliferation of soil microbes. **Transplanting saplings** interferes with the
normal growth of the first generation of roots and weakens the tree's ability to send roots deep into the soil. **Applying chemical fertilizers** encourages shallow root structures that extend along
the topsoil. **Fertilizer application and weeding** bring a halt to the normal aggregation and enrichment of topsoil.

These very actions are what make plowing and turning the soil necessary in the first place. The farmer, having destroyed the natural soil-building processes, must then perform their functions
manually. Fukuoka called this "just man imitating nature" — all he has really gained is "a mastery at scientific exposition."

## The Mountain Forest as Proof

Fukuoka pointed to the mountain forest as living proof that trees grow without fertilizer. In the forest, the action of wind and snow over years weathers rock, a layer of humus forms and deepens with
the fall of leaves each year, microorganisms multiply in the soil — turning it a rich black — and the soil aggregates and softens, increasing water retention. No human intervention is needed, and the
trees grow on and on.

The evidence is not limited to natural forests. Planted cedar stands, though not virgin forest, demonstrate the same principle. Despite the damage caused by repeated planting of the same species and
the felling and harvesting of timber, anyone who sees black wattle planted in depleted soil on a mountainside and succeeded years later by giant cedars many times their size will be amazed at the
productive powers of the soil. When black wattle is planted among cedar or cypress, the latter thrive with the help of microbes present on the roots of the black wattle.

If the forest is left to itself, the action of the wind and snow over the years weathers the rock, a layer of humus forms and deepens with the fall of leaves each year, microorganisms multiply in the
soil — turning it a rich black — and the soil aggregates and softens, increasing water retention. There is no need for human intervention here. And the trees grow on and on. Nature is not dead. It
lives and it grows.

## Earthworms as Indicators and Agents of Soil Health

Earthworms occupy a critical position in Fukuoka's soil ecology. They function both as indicators of soil health — appearing only where sufficient humus has accumulated — and as active agents of soil
improvement. Their burrowing aerates the soil, their casts enrich it, and their presence supports the entire food web that maintains soil structure.

The role of earthworms in the broader ecosystem is illuminated by Fukuoka's description of the natural food chain: "Amoebae and nematodes feed on the bacteria, and the remains of nematodes are fed on
in turn by earthworms, which are relished by moles." This chain continues upward through weasels and eventually returns to the soil through microbial decomposition of carcasses, completing the
nutrient cycle.

Fukuoka's statement that "no species of bird flying in the air should become extinct. The earthworms that burrow through the soil must not vanish" reflects his understanding that the removal of any
single element from this web — whether earthworm, spider, or bacterium — throws the entire system out of balance. Tens of millions of species live on in perfect harmony without increase or decrease;
they are born and they die unseen by man.

## The Living Soil: Microorganisms in Every Gram

Fukuoka emphasized that the soil is not dead mineral matter but a living ecosystem of extraordinary complexity. He reported that one gram of ordinary topsoil contains about one hundred million
bacteria, yeasts, molds, diatoms, and other microbes. Far from being inanimate, the soil teems with life. These microorganisms do not exist without reason; each lives for a purpose, struggling,
cooperating, and carrying on the cycles of nature.

In his own farm, one gram of soil contained about 100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria and other soil-enriching microbes. He felt that soil containing seeds and these microorganisms could be the spark
that restores even the deserts.

Yet into this living soil, conventional agriculture throws powerful chemical fertilizers. Fukuoka noted that very little research had been done on the relationship between fertilizers and soil
microbes. At agricultural research stations, scientists placed soil in pots and ran tests, but most of the soil microbes in these pots died off. Results obtained under such fixed conditions cannot be
applied to situations under natural conditions. Clearly, results obtained from tests conducted under fixed conditions and within a limited experimental framework cannot be applied to situations under
natural conditions.

## The Illusion That Tilling Loosens Soil

For generations, farmers believed that tilling the soil softened it and let air in. Wet paddy fields were normally supposed to be tilled five, six, or even seven times during the growing season. The
more zealous farmers competed with each other to increase the number of plowings. Everyone thought this softened the soil and let more air in.

Then after World War II, herbicides became available. Farmers discovered that when they sprayed their fields with herbicides and reduced the frequency of plowing, their yields improved. This
demonstrated that intertillage had been effective as a weeding process but had been worthless as a means for loosening the soil.

Fukuoka stressed that saying tilling is worthless is not the same as claiming it is unnecessary to loosen the soil and increase its porosity. On the contrary, he emphasized more than anyone else how
important an abundance of air and water are to the soil. It is in the nature of soil to swell and grow more porous with each passing year. This is essential for microorganisms to multiply, for the
soil to grow more fertile, and for the roots of large trees to penetrate deep. But working the soil with plow and hoe actually interferes with these natural processes.

## The Five to Ten Year Timeline for Topsoil Building

Fukuoka was candid about the timescale of natural soil building. Five to ten years are needed to build up six inches of topsoil through the cultivation of cover crops. To current economic perceptions,
one disadvantage of natural farming methods is that they take too long. They may appear inferior in a world pressed for time, but if farmland were correctly understood as a legacy to be preserved for
future generations, the general opinion of natural farming would improve.

Physical improvement and the application of human effort alone have only temporary effect. Natural farming makes use of the forces of living organisms to physically and chemically improve the soil, a
process that goes hand-in-hand with the overall process of crop growing. The beneficial effects ultimately show up in the longer lifetime of fruit trees — two to three times that of trees grown by
scientific methods.

## Understanding Soil Through Weeds

Fukuoka offered a practical diagnostic principle: one can understand the nature of the soil more quickly by examining the weeds growing in it than by examining the soil itself. Weeds solve the
problems of both the soil and the weeds. A succession of different herbs takes place over the years as the soil becomes richer. The soil tends to become imbalanced when a stand of one type of plant is
grown year after year on the same land; the emergence and succession of different weeds is more natural and more conducive to soil enrichment and development.

We can either choose to see the soil as imperfect and take hoe in hand, or trust the soil and leave the business of working it to nature. No amount of research can teach man everything there is to
know about the soil, and he will certainly never create soils more perfect than those of nature. Because nature itself is perfect. If anything, advances in scientific research teach man just how
perfect and complete a handful of soil is, and how incomplete human knowledge.
