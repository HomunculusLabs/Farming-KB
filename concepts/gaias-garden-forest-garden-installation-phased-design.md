# Forest Garden Installation and Phased Design

In Gaia's Garden, Toby Hemenway provides a practical, phased approach to
installing a [[hemenway-food-forest-design]] on a typical
suburban lot. Rather than attempting to plant everything at once, the
phased design uses the [[hemenway-zone-and-sector-planning]]
to prioritize planting and lets the garden mature gradually, with each
phase building on the previous one. This approach makes forest gardening
accessible even to gardeners with limited budgets or time.

## The Design Process for a Forest Garden

The forest garden design process follows the same sequence as the
broader [[gaias-garden-many-gardens-garden-design-process]]:
observation, visioning, planning, development, and implementation. But
several additional considerations are specific to forest garden
installation.

## Key Design Principles

### Plant Trees and Woody Plants First

Trees and woody plants should go in first, since these take the longest
to mature and define the shape of the garden. Important guidelines:

- Design for the full mature size of trees, not their current spindly
  seedling size. Overcrowding is the most common mistake
- Leave room for sunlight to penetrate between full-grown trees; avoid
  creating a closed tall-tree canopy
- The more northerly the garden, the wider the spacing needed between
  trees to ensure adequate light penetration

### Include Soil-Building Plants Early

The dense plantings of a forest garden demand lots of nutrients during
their youth. Early inclusion of soil-building plants is essential:

- [[gaias-garden-nitrogen-fixers-guild-design]] and
  other soil-building plants should be abundant in the early years
- [[gaias-garden-nutrient-accumulators-dynamic-accumulators]] plants accelerate growth and speed succession
- [[gaias-garden-cover-crops-soil-building]] on open ground
  build soil while waiting for trees to fill in

### Provide Wind Protection

In exposed sites, wind barriers such as fences and
[[gaias-garden-hedgerows-and-windbreaks]] greatly speed the
establishment of all other plants. Wind protection reduces moisture
loss, prevents physical damage, and creates the sheltered conditions
that young trees and shrubs need to thrive.

### Set Up a Nursery

Buying all plants for a forest garden at once gets expensive. Hemenway
recommends setting aside a small area in [[hemenway-zone-and-sector-planning]] as a nursery for seed-starting, rooting cuttings, and general plant
propagation. From this nursery, gardeners can:

- Start perennials for the herb, root, and
  [[gaias-garden-living-mulches-ground-covers]] layers from
  seed en masse
- Propagate cuttings for trees, shrubs, and herbs
- Divide established plants to yield numerous progeny
- Create a swelling population of plants to colonize the new garden

A nursery is invaluable for furnishing huge numbers of plants very
cheaply.

## The Phased Installation Approach

### Phase 1: Zone 1 -- The Intensive Zone

Zone 1, closest to the house, is planted first with intensively managed
beds:

- Temporary, well-composted beds for fast, early food and flower
  production
- Salad greens, herbs, and dwarf fruit trees
- [[gaias-garden-sheet-mulching]] beds for quick soil
  building
- The patio and most-used outdoor areas

This phase provides immediate food production while the larger garden
establishes.

### Phase 2: Zone 2 -- The Productive Zone

Zone 2 holds the main productive plantings:

- Useful shrubs, a pond, chickens, and the beginnings of a food forest
- Blueberries, wild roses, native and insectary shrubs
- Espaliered or cordoned fruit trees along edges
- Berry bushes, shrub willows, and useful native shrubs
- Deep [[gaias-garden-sheet-mulching]] to quickly build soil
  beneath young trees and shrubs

### Phase 3: Zone 3 -- The Long-Term Zone

Zone 3 is the most extensive but least intensively managed area:

- Large fruit and nut tree seedlings
- [[gaias-garden-cover-crops-soil-building]] on the ground
  layer for long-term soil restoration
- The less-urgently needed plants that will take longest to mature
- This zone will take longest to fill in, so soil-building is the
  priority in early years

## Filling the Gaps

The open spaces between newly planted trees and shrubs can initially be
filled with:

- Annual vegetables, flowers, and herbs for immediate production
- [[gaias-garden-nitrogen-fixers-guild-design]] such as clover
- Nursery stock being grown for transplanting elsewhere
- [[gaias-garden-living-mulches-ground-covers]] to protect
  soil

As the upper layers grow and the nursery plants become ready to
transplant, these temporary fill beds gradually shrink.

## The U-Shaped Design Example

Hemenway provides a specific design example for a typical homeowner
taking out a lawn to install a more ecologically sound forest garden.
The U-shaped design opens toward the south for maximum solar gain and
creates a private, warm, and sheltered space.

### After Five to Ten Years

The garden has filled in. Since the perennial shrubs and trees are now
producing abundantly, the temporary keyhole beds have shrunk to narrow,
edge beds. A [[gaias-garden-living-mulches-ground-covers]]
of edible, flowering, and insectary herbs and shrubs fills the gaps
between and below major trees. The landscape has become a self-
sustaining food forest.
