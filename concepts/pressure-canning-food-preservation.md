# Pressure Canning for Food Preservation

## Overview

Pressure canning is a food preservation method that uses elevated temperature under steam pressure to safely process low-acid foods in sealed glass jars. It is the only recommended method for canning vegetables, meats, poultry, seafood, soups, stews, and other low-acid foods (pH above 4.6). Unlike water [[water-bath-canning-guide]], which reaches only 100°C (212°F) at sea level, pressure canning achieves temperatures of 116-121°C (240-250°F), sufficient to destroy Clostridium botulinum spores — the organism responsible for botulism, a potentially fatal form of food poisoning. Understanding and correctly applying pressure canning principles is essential for safe home food preservation.

## The Science of Pressure Canning

The fundamental safety requirement in canning low-acid foods is the destruction of Clostridium botulinum spores. These spores are remarkably heat-resistant and can survive the 100°C maximum temperature of boiling water. They are ubiquitous in soil and are commonly present on fresh vegetables and meats. In anaerobic (oxygen-free) environments — such as a sealed [[pf-tek-canning-jar-lid-configuration]] — the spores can germinate, grow, and produce botulinum toxin, one of the most potent biological toxins known.

Pressure canning solves this problem through the relationship between pressure and the boiling point of water:

- At sea level atmospheric pressure (14.7 psi / 101.3 kPa), water boils at 100°C (212°F)
- At 10 psi gauge pressure, water boils at approximately 115°C (240°F)
- At 11 psi gauge pressure, water boils at approximately 116°C (242°F)
- At 15 psi gauge pressure, water boils at approximately 121°C (250°F)

The critical threshold is 116°C (240°F), which is sufficient to kill C. botulinum spores within the processing times specified in tested [[homestead-canning-recipes]]. This temperature is achieved at 10 psi at sea level, but the required gauge pressure increases with altitude because atmospheric pressure decreases.

## Altitude Adjustments

Because atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude, the boiling point of water decreases as well. Pressure canners must be operated at higher gauge pressures at higher altitudes to achieve the same processing temperature:

| Altitude (feet) | Altitude (meters) | Weighted gauge | Dial gauge |
|----------------|-------------------|---------------|-----------|
| 0-1,000 | 0-305 | 10 psi | 11 psi |
| 1,001-2,000 | 306-610 | 15 psi | 11 psi |
| 2,001-4,000 | 611-1,219 | 15 psi | 12 psi |
| 4,001-6,000 | 1,220-1,829 | 15 psi | 13 psi |
| 6,001-8,000 | 1,830-2,438 | 15 psi | 14 psi |
| 8,001-10,000 | 2,439-3,048 | 15 psi | 15 psi |

Weighted gauge canners have only two or three weight settings (typically 10 and 15 psi), so altitude adjustments involve switching to the next weight setting. Dial gauge canners allow fine pressure adjustments for any altitude.

## Types of Pressure Canners

### Weighted Gauge Canners

Weighted gauge canners (e.g., Presto, All-American) use a weighted regulator that jiggles or rocks to maintain pressure. They are simpler to use and do not require calibration:

- **Advantages**: No calibration needed, easy to read (by sound and motion), foolproof pressure regulation
- **Disadvantages**: Limited pressure settings (typically 10 and 15 psi only), cannot make fine altitude adjustments, audible rocking can be noisy during long processing times
- **Operation**: Place the correct weight for your altitude on the vent pipe. When the weight begins rocking steadily (1-4 rocks per minute, depending on the canner model), the desired pressure has been reached. Adjust the heat to maintain steady rocking

### Dial Gauge Canners

Dial gauge canners (e.g., Presto with dial gauge, Mirro) use a pressure gauge with a needle indicator to show the exact pressure inside the canner:

- **Advantages**: Precise pressure reading, fine altitude adjustments possible, quieter operation
- **Disadvantages**: Gauge must be calibrated annually for accuracy, gauge can be damaged or become inaccurate, requires more attention during operation
- **Calibration**: Dial gauges should be checked for accuracy each year at a county extension office or using a tested master gauge. A gauge reading more than 1 psi high or low should be replaced

### Stovetop vs. Electric Pressure Canners

Electric multi-cookers (Instant Pot, etc.) are NOT approved for pressure canning by the USDA or National Center for Home Food Preservation. While some electric pressure cookers claim canning capability, independent testing has shown that they do not achieve consistent, uniform temperatures throughout the canning load, creating potential safety hazards. Only stovetop pressure canners that have been tested and approved for canning should be used.

## Essential Equipment

- **Pressure canner**: Must be large enough to hold at least 4 quart jars. Smaller pressure cookers (saucepans with pressure lids) are not safe for canning
- **Mason-type canning jars**: Standard jars with threaded mouths and self-sealing lids. Inspect for cracks, chips, or scratches before each use
- **Two-piece canning lids**: Flat metal lids with sealing compound and threaded metal screw bands. Lids are single-use; bands are reusable
- **Jar lifter**: Essential for safely removing hot jars from the canner
- **Canning funnel**: Wide-mouth funnel for filling jars without spilling on the rim
- **Bubble remover/headspace tool**: Non-metallic tool for removing air bubbles and measuring headspace
- **Clean cloths**: For wiping jar rims before applying lids
- **Timer**: Accurate timer for tracking processing time
- **Heat-resistant surface**: Towels or wooden board for cooling jars

## Step-by-Step Pressure Canning Process

1. **Prepare the canner**: Add 2-3 inches of water to the canner bottom. Place the rack in the bottom. Heat water to at least 140°F (60°C) for hot-packed foods, or 180°F (82°C) for raw-packed foods
2. **Prepare the food**: Follow a tested recipe from the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, the National Center for Home Food Preservation, or Ball Blue Book. Do not improvise or modify recipes, as processing times and pressures are specific to the tested formulation
3. **Fill the jars**: Pack food into hot jars using the specified method (raw pack or hot pack). Raw pack: pack raw food into jars and cover with boiling liquid. Hot pack: heat food to boiling before packing into jars
4. **Remove air bubbles**: Run a non-metallic bubble remover around the inside of the jar to release trapped air. Re-measure headspace and add liquid if needed
5. **Clean the rims**: Wipe the jar rim with a clean, damp cloth. Any food residue on the rim can prevent a proper seal
6. **Apply lids**: Place a flat lid on the jar, then screw on the band until fingertip-tight (snug but not over-tightened)
7. **Load the canner**: Place jars on the rack using a jar lifter. Ensure jars are not touching each other or the canner walls
8. **Vent the canner**: Lock the canner lid in place. Heat on high until steam flows freely from the vent pipe for 10 minutes. This venting step is critical — it removes air from the canner, allowing pure steam to build pressure
9. **Build pressure**: Place the weight on the vent pipe (weighted gauge) or close the petcock (dial gauge). The canner will pressurize over 5-10 minutes
10. **Process**: When the canner reaches the target pressure, start the timer. Maintain steady pressure throughout the processing time. If pressure drops below the target, bring it back up and restart the timer
11. **Depressurize naturally**: When the timer completes, turn off the heat and let the canner cool naturally to zero pressure. Do not rush this step — forced cooling can cause jar breakage, liquid loss, and seal failure. Wait 10 additional minutes after zero pressure, then open the canner
12. **Remove jars**: Use the jar lifter to carefully remove jars and place them on a towel-covered surface. Leave 1-2 inches of space between jars for [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]]
13. **Cool completely**: Let jars cool undisturbed for 12-24 hours. Do not tighten bands during cooling
14. **Check seals**: After cooling, press the center of each lid. Properly sealed lids are concave and do not flex. Remove bands, wipe jars clean, label with date and contents, and store in a cool, dark, dry place

## Common Safety Mistakes to Avoid

- Using a pressure cooker (not canner) for pressure canning
- Skipping the 10-minute venting step before building pressure
- Modifying tested recipes (adding ingredients, changing proportions, or omitting acid)
