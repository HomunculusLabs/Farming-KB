## Concentration Effects on Microbial Selection

The concentration of molasses in compost tea determines which organisms benefit. This relationship is governed by osmotic effects.

### Low Concentrations (Below 0.5%)

At very low concentrations, molasses provides simple sugars that serve as food for rapidly growing bacteria. The effect is modest, providing a gentle boost to bacterial populations without significantly altering the fungal-to-bacterial ratio.

### Moderate Concentrations (0.5% to 3%)

This range is actually the most problematic. The sugars fuel a rapid bacterial bloom that consumes oxygen faster than most aeration systems can replenish it. This creates the highest risk of the tea going anaerobic. Many compost tea failures can be traced to additions in this concentration range.

### Optimal Fungal Selection (3% to 5%)

Above 3%, osmotic effects begin to favor beneficial fungi over rapidly growing bacteria. As sugar concentration rises, water is held more tightly by the sugar molecules. Fast-growing bacteria struggle with these osmotic (water-holding) conditions, while beneficial fungi can utilize the complex sugars without competition. This range enhances fungal growth and reduces anaerobic bacterial growth.

### High Concentrations (5% to 15%)

Above 5%, the osmotic effect significantly restricts bacterial growth. Beneficial fungi that can tolerate high sugar concentrations gain a strong competitive advantage. The brew is less likely to go anaerobic because fewer rapidly growing bacteria are consuming oxygen. At these levels, molasses also functions effectively as a sticker-spreader.

## Molasses as a Sticker-Spreader

When blackstrap unsulfured molasses is used at concentrations above 3% to 5%, it serves a second critical function: helping beneficial organisms adhere to plant surfaces. The sticky sugar solution helps bacteria and fungi physically attach to leaves after spraying, improving coverage and persistence.

This dual function (fungal food plus sticker) makes high-concentration molasses additions particularly efficient. At these concentrations, a single amendment performs two jobs simultaneously, reducing the need for separate commercial sticker-spreader products.

## Practical Application Rates

For a 5-gallon brewer, a small amount of molasses (approximately one teaspoon) is sufficient for modest bacterial stimulation. For fungal-dominant teas, significantly more is needed. For a 500-gallon tea maker, approximately half a gallon of molasses is appropriate for fungal enhancement.

When using molasses as a sticker-spreader in the spray tank, the concentration should be above 5%. Ensure the sprayer can handle syrup at these concentrations without clogging.

## Molasses and Anaerobic Bacteria

An additional benefit of high molasses concentrations is the suppression of anaerobic bacteria. The osmotic effect that restricts rapidly growing bacteria also inhibits many anaerobic species, including potential human pathogens. Research submitted for publication by Ingham's group supports the finding that higher molasses rates are better for growing beneficial fungi and selecting against human pathogens, as long as conditions remain aerobic.
