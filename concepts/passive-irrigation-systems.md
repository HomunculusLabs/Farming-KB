# Passive Irrigation Systems

Passive irrigation delivers water to plants without pumps, timers, or electricity. These systems rely on gravity, [[capillary-action-in-soils]], and soil moisture dynamics to distribute water efficiently. They reduce labor, conserve water, and improve plant health by maintaining consistent soil moisture at the root zone.

## Ollas (Buried Clay Pots)

Ollas are unglazed terracotta pots buried in the ground with their necks exposed at soil level. Water seeps through the porous clay walls and is drawn into the surrounding soil by [[soil-capillary-action-and-water-movement]] root demand.

### How They Work

1.  Fill the olla through the exposed neck
2.  Water slowly seeps through the clay walls
3.  Soil moisture creates a suction that pulls water outward
4.  Plant roots grow toward the moisture source
5.  The olla automatically delivers more water as the soil dries

### Sizing and Placement

-  **Small ollas** (1-2 liters): Irradiate a 30-45 cm radius. Best for individual plants or small clusters.
-  **Medium ollas** (3-5 liters): Irradiate a 45-60 cm radius. Suitable for larger plants like tomatoes, peppers, squash.
-  **Large ollas** (5-10 liters): Irradiate a 60-90 cm radius. Good for raised beds and larger garden areas.

Spacing: Place ollas 45-90 cm apart in a grid pattern for full bed coverage. Closer spacing for thirsty crops, wider for drought-tolerant plants.

### Advantages

- Up to 70% water savings compared to surface irrigation
- Reduces weed growth (soil surface stays drier)
- Eliminates foliar wetting (fewer fungal diseases)
- Filled every 3-7 days depending on weather and plant demand
- Last indefinitely if protected from freezing

### DIY Ollas

Commercial ollas cost $15-40 each. DIY options:

-  **Two-pot method** — Glue two unglazed terracotta pots together at their rims with silicone. Plug one drain hole with silicone, leave the other open for filling. Cost: $3-5 per olla.
-  **Clay pipe sections** — Cut 15-20 cm sections of unglazed terracotta drainage pipe. Cap one end, fill through the other. Cost: $2-4 each.
-  **Test porosity** — Unglazed clay is essential. Glazed pots will not seep water.

## Wicking Beds

Wicking beds are self-watering garden beds with a built-in water reservoir below the soil surface. Water wicks upward from the reservoir through the soil via capillary action.

### Construction

1.  **Container** — A raised bed frame with a watertight liner (pond liner or food-grade plastic)
2.  **Reservoir** — 15-25 cm deep water-filled space at the bottom
3.  **Wicking medium** — 10-15 cm layer of coarse gravel, scoria, or sand above the reservoir
4.  **Geotextile barrier** — Separates wicking medium from growing soil, prevents soil washing into reservoir
5.  **Growing soil** — 25-35 cm of quality garden soil or compost blend on top
6.  **Overflow pipe** — Exits at the top of the reservoir to prevent waterlogging

### Watering

Fill the reservoir through a vertical PVC pipe that extends from above the soil surface down to the reservoir. Check water level through a sight gauge (clear tube alongside the fill pipe). Refill every 1-2 weeks depending on weather and plant demand.

### Advantages

- Consistent moisture at root zone reduces plant stress
- Very water-efficient (50-80% less than overhead watering)
- Excellent for raised bed gardens and urban settings
- Can be connected to [[rainwater-harvesting-systems]] for gravity-fed filling
- Reduced watering frequency — ideal for busy homesteaders

### Limitations

- Not suitable for root vegetables that need deeper, drier soil to develop properly
- Requires watertight container — leaks negate the system
- Soil depth is limited to 25-35 cm above the wicking zone
- [[jadam-salt-accumulation-in-soil]] reservoir over time — flush periodically

## Swale Irrigation

Swales are shallow, level trenches dug along the contour of a slope. They capture runoff water, slow it down, and allow it to infiltrate into the soil. On a homestead, swales serve as earthworks that hydrate entire planting zones.

### Design

-  **Depth**: 20-45 cm
-  **Width**: 60-120 cm
-  **Berm**: Excavated soil placed on the downhill side, creating a mound
-  **Spacing**: 3-15 meters apart depending on slope steepness (closer on steep slopes, wider on gentle ones)
-  **Level**: Use a laser level, A-frame level, or water level to ensure the swale bottom is perfectly level

### Planting

Plant the berm with deep-rooted perennials (fruit trees, berry bushes, comfrey) and the swale bottom with moisture-loving plants (willow, elderberry, rushes). The berm acts as a sponge that releases water slowly to plant roots downslope.

### Benefits

- Passively irrigates large areas from rainfall alone
- Reduces erosion by capturing and infiltrating runoff
- Recharges groundwater
- Creates productive planting zones on what may have been degraded slope
- Can integrate with [[backyard-pond-building]] — overflow from ponds can feed swale systems

## Buried Clay Pipe / Terracotta Irrigation Lines

A variation of the olla concept using buried terracotta pipes as irrigation lines:

- Run unglazed terracotta pipes (5-10 cm diameter) through garden beds at 15-20 cm depth
- Connect to a water reservoir (rain barrel or header tank) via gravity feed
- Water seeps through the pipe walls continuously
- Cover pipe joints to prevent roots from clogging the line

This system irrigates entire rows or beds from a single water source. More permanent than individual ollas.

## Deep Pipe Irrigation

Deep pipes deliver water directly to the root zone of established [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]]:

- Bury a perforated PVC pipe (5-10 cm diameter) vertically, 30-60 cm deep, next to the plant
- Fill the pipe with water; it drains into the deep root zone
- Encourages deep root growth, making plants more drought-resistant
- Especially useful for establishing fruit trees in food forests

## Choosing the Right System

| Factor | Ollas | Wicking Beds | Swales |
|--------|-------|-------------|--------|
| Best For | Individual plants, small beds | Raised beds, urban gardens | Sloped land, large areas |
| Water Source | Manual filling | Manual or gravity fill | Rainfall capture |
| Installation Effort | Low | Medium | High (earthworks) |
| Water Efficiency | Very High | High | High (landscape scale) |
| Cost | Low | Medium | Low (labor-intensive) |
| Permanence | Semi-permanent | Permanent | Permanent |

## Integration Notes

Passive [[irrigation-systems]] work best in combination. Use swales for landscape-scale water management, wicking beds for intensive [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]], and ollas for targeted plant watering. Connect all systems to [[rainwater-harvesting-systems]] for a gravity-fed, self-sustaining water supply. For more controlled delivery, [[drip-irrigation-guide]] can supplement passive systems during peak demand periods.
