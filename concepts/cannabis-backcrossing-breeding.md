# Cannabis Backcrossing Breeding

[[oner-cannabis-breeding-fundamentals]], this most often means crossing progeny back to the mother plant. The parent that is used repeatedly is called the recurrent parent, while the other contributing parent is called the donor parent. Backcross breeding has become one of the staple methods used by clandestine cannabis breeders because it is a simple, rapid method that requires only small populations and works well in controlled indoor environments.

The technique is widely used in conventional plant breeding as well as cannabis, and its predictability makes it especially valuable when the breeder wants to improve a proven cultivar without dramatically altering its characteristics. The method was formalized in the early 20th century by crop geneticists working with cereal grains, and has since been applied to virtually every major agricultural crop. In cannabis, where the breeding community has historically operated outside formal agricultural institutions, backcrossing was adopted independently and remains one of the most commonly employed techniques.

## Goals of Backcrossing

The principal goal of backcross breeding is to create a population of individuals derived mainly from the genetics of one single parent (the recurrent parent) while incorporating a specific desirable trait from the donor parent. The recurrent parent should be a highly acceptable or nearly ideal genotype — for example, an existing commercial cultivar or inbred line. The trait being introgressed from the donor parent should be simply inherited and easily scored for phenotype.

For example, a breeder might have an excellent yielding strain that lacks resistance to a particular mold. By crossing it with a mold-resistant donor parent and then repeatedly backcrossing to the high-yielding recurrent parent while selecting for mold resistance, the breeder can create a new line that retains most of the original genetics but now carries the resistance trait. Other commonly introgressed traits include altered flowering time, specific [[cannabis-disease-pest-resistance-breeding-clarke]]. The key requirement is that the trait must be simply inherited — controlled by one or a few genes — so that selection is reliable at each generation.

## Backcrossing Dominant Traits

When the desired trait from the donor parent is dominant, the backcross process is straightforward. At each generation, plants showing the dominant trait are selected and crossed back to the recurrent parent. The simplicity of this approach makes dominant traits the preferred target for most backcross programs.

**Step 1:** Cross the recurrent parent with the donor parent to produce the F1 hybrid generation. All F1 plants will express the dominant trait.

**Step 2:** Select desirable F1 plants showing the dominant trait and hybridize them to the recurrent parent. The resulting generation is denoted BC1.

**Step 3:** Select plants from BC1 showing the dominant trait and hybridize with the recurrent parent to produce BC2.

**Step 4:** Continue selecting and backcrossing. Each successive backcross generation recovers approximately half of the remaining donor parent genes, so the population increasingly resembles the recurrent parent while retaining the introgressed trait.

After several generations of backcrossing, the resulting population is composed almost entirely of recurrent parent genetics with only the targeted trait segment retained from the donor parent. Selection intensity at each generation determines how efficiently the donor genome is purged — breeders who evaluate more progeny per generation and select only the most recurrent-parent-like individuals achieve faster recovery of the desired genotype.

## Backcrossing Recessive Traits

Recessive traits are more difficult to work with in backcross breeding because their expression is masked by dominance in each backcross to the recurrent parent. An additional round of open pollination or sib-mating is needed after each backcross generation to expose homozygous-recessive plants.

**Step 1:** Cross the recurrent parent with the donor parent to produce the F1 generation.

**Step 2:** Select desirable F1 plants and create an F2 population via full sib-mating to expose the recessive trait.

**Step 3:** Select F2 plants showing the desired recessive trait and hybridize them to the recurrent parent, producing BC1.

**Step 4:** Select BC1 plants, create an F2 population via sib-mating to expose the recessive condition, then select and backcross to the recurrent parent for BC2.

**Step 5:** Continue this alternating pattern of backcrossing and F2 segregation until the recessive trait is stably fixed. After BC3F3, the resulting population consists of approximately 93.7% recurrent parent genes and only 6.3% donor parent genes, with the entire population homozygous for the recessive trait.

The additional F2 generation required at each backcross step substantially increases the time and population size needed for recessive trait introgression. Where a dominant trait might require only 3-4 years of work, a recessive trait can demand 6-10 years depending on generation time and the number of plants the breeder can evaluate. This time penalty is why recessive traits are less commonly targeted in cannabis breeding programs unless they offer exceptional value.

## Practical Considerations

Backcross derived lines are well-adapted to the environment in which they were bred, which is why backcrossing is particularly popular among [[cannabis-chemotype-classification-cannabinoid-breeding]] that rely on understanding the genetic basis of chemical phenotypes.

The most commonly used markers in cannabis include simple sequence repeats (SSRs) and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). SNP arrays and genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) have made high-density marker screening cost-effective for cannabis breeding programs. These tools allow simultaneous tracking of the target trait and monitoring of the overall recurrent parent genome recovery across all chromosomes, dramatically accelerating the pace of variety development.

## Cubing: A Simplified Backcross Variant

A related technique sometimes called "cubing" involves backcrossing a progeny to the same parent for three successive generations (BX1, BX2, BX3). After three backcrosses, the resulting population is approximately 87.5% identical to the recurrent parent. This informal method is widely used in underground cannabis breeding circles and produces results faster than formal backcrossing programs but with less precision. Cubing is typically applied to stabilize a particularly desirable clone by crossing it with itself through male progeny over three generations.

The term "cubing" derives from the mathematical fact that after three generations of backcrossing to the same parent, approximately (1 - 0.5^3) = 87.5% of the genome derives from that parent. Each backcross generation halves the remaining donor contribution: BX1 retains 50% donor genes, BX2 retains 25%, and BX3 retains only 12.5%. The technique is most effective when the recurrent parent is an outstanding clone that the breeder wishes to make available in seed form, since clones cannot be distributed as easily as seeds through commercial channels.

## Maintaining Genetic Stability During Backcrossing

A critical practical concern in cannabis backcrossing is preventing unwanted pollen contamination. Because cannabis is wind-pollinated and highly promiscuous, even a single stray pollen grain can compromise an entire backcross generation. Indoor breeders typically use dedicated flowering rooms with negative air pressure and careful isolation protocols. Some breeders employ colloidal silver or silver thiosulfate to induce male flowers on female plants (reversal), ensuring that all pollen used in the cross derives from a known genetic source rather than an unknown contaminant.

Pollen viability testing is another important quality control step. Cannabis pollen stored under cool, dry conditions can remain viable for several weeks to months, but viability degrades rapidly at room temperature or high humidity. Breeders performing multiple backcross generations in sequence must plan their timing carefully to ensure fresh, viable pollen is available for each cross. Some breeders maintain a frozen pollen bank, storing male flowers or extracted pollen at -20°C for longer-term preservation of valuable genetic lines.
