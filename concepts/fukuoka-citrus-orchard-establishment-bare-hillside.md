# Citrus Orchard Establishment from Bare Hillside

Masanobu Fukuoka described in detail how he established his citrus
orchards from bare hillside land, providing a practical model for
[[fukuoka-natural-orchard-development]]
without plowing, fertilizers, or pesticides.

## Starting from Scratch

After the war, when Fukuoka first began farming, he started with 1 3/4
acres of citrus orchard and 3/8 acre of rice fields. By the time of
his writing, the citrus orchards alone covered 12 1/2 acres. He came
by this additional land gradually, acquiring it piece by piece. In each
case, the land he took on was mountain land that had been left unused
and uncultivated for many years.

The method was simple: all he did was dig holes in a contour line and
plant the citrus seedlings. He did not plow the land or apply any
fertilizer.

## The Natural Succession Process

After planting the seedlings, Fukuoka let nature take its course.
Sprouts had been coming up, and over the years, various grasses and
weeds such as cogon grass and bracken began to thrive. The citrus tree
seedlings became lost in a tangle of weeds and grasses.

After six or seven years, the citrus trees finally bore fruit. At that
point, Fukuoka dug away the earth behind the trees to form terraces,
and the orchard then appeared little different from any conventional
orchard. The key difference was that the soil had been built up
naturally over those years through the action of the weeds and grasses,
without any artificial inputs.

## The Orchard Ecosystem

In conventional fruit orchards, there was not a blade of grass to be
seen in fields or orchards anywhere in the country. But seeing orchards
such as Fukuoka's, people came to understand that fruit trees could
grow quite well among the weeds and grasses. Today orchards with ground
cover have become common.

Fukuoka grew several varieties of citrus on the hillsides near his
home. The system worked as a complete [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] alongside rice, barley, and [[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-crop-rotation-vegetable-methods]].

## Connection to Pruning Philosophy

Fukuoka's approach to the orchard was inseparable from his
[[fukuoka-argument-against-pruning]]. If trees are
allowed to follow their natural form from the beginning, there is no
need for pruning or sprays of pesticides. The problems that arise in
conventional orchards -- insect attack, disease -- only appear after
the trees have been deformed by human intervention through pruning,
fertilizing, and cultivation.

Growing fruit without pruning, fertilizing, or using chemical sprays
is possible. It all begins with planting the seedling and allowing it
to develop its natural shape.
