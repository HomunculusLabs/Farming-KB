# Cordyceps and Claviceps as Sacred Neurotropic Fungi in Mexico

## Overview

While *Psilocybe* species dominate discussions of neurotropic fungi in
Mexico, Guzmán (1998) documented the significant role played by
*Cordyceps* and *Claviceps* species — ascomycetous fungi rather than the
typical basidiomycete mushrooms — in indigenous sacred fungal traditions.
These fungi are taxonomically related to each other through the order
Clavicipitales and share the ability to produce ergot alkaloids, though
their neurotropic properties in the Mexican context remain chemically
ambiguous.

## The Cordyceps Species of Mexican Ceremonies

Two *Cordyceps* species are documented as sacred fungi in Mexico:

- ***Cordyceps capitata*** (Holmsk.: Fr.) Link — A fungus that parasitizes
  subterranean truffle-like fungi of the genus *Elaphomyces*
- ***Cordyceps ophioglossoides*** (Fr.) Link — Another *Elaphomyces*
  parasite, found in similar habitats

These species were gathered by Wasson in popular markets in Mexico and
identified as sacred fungi. They are particularly important to the Indians
of the Nevado de Toluca region in central Mexico.

## Ceremonial Context and the Role of Elaphomyces

The ceremonial use of *Cordyceps* in the Nevado de Toluca region is
remarkably elaborate. During special nocturnal ceremonies, *Cordyceps*
specimens are eaten either together with *Psilocybe muliercula* or alone.
The ceremonies have a distinctive feature: a specimen of *Elaphomyces*
(the host fungus on which *Cordyceps* grows) is placed at the center of
the ceremonial room to serve as a **"director"** of the ceremony.

Three *Elaphomyces* species are used in this role:
- *E. granulatus* Fr.
- *E. muricatus* Fr.
- *E. reticulatus* Vitt.

The *Elaphomyces* species used in ceremonies are also employed by the
Trique Indians of Alta Mixteca in Oaxaca, Mexico, as a wound treatment and
to "rejuvenate the organism" (Trappe et al., 1979; Guzmán, 1994a, b).
This dual use — ceremonial and medicinal — suggests that indigenous
communities recognized distinct bioactive properties in these subterranean
fungi.

## Naming and Indigenous Classification

*Psilocybe muliercula* and its associated *Cordyceps* species have evocative
Nahuatl-derived names that reflect their morphological characteristics:

- *P. muliercula* (and by extension *C. capitata*) is called **«hombrecitos»**
  (little men)
- *C. ophioglossoides* is called **«mujercitas»** (little women)

This gendered naming system suggests a sophisticated indigenous taxonomy
that distinguishes between these closely related fungi based on subtle
morphological differences, despite both being collected from the same
habitat and used in the same ceremonies. The paired "male/female"
classification is common in indigenous Mesoamerican natural philosophy and
may reflect complementary ceremonial roles for the two species.

## Chemical Uncertainty

Despite their importance in [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]]*
— the famous ergot fungus that produces ergot alkaloids known to cause
hallucinations (Ramsbottom, 1954; Schultes and Hofmann, 1973, 1979;
Wasson et al., 1978) — provides a biochemical rationale for potential
neurotropic activity, though the specific alkaloid profiles of these
Mexican *Cordyceps* species have not been adequately characterized.
