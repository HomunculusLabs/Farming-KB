## Walnut Hackberry Guild Design

Updated: 2026-04-18

The walnut-hackberry guild, developed by Arizona permaculture designer Tim Murphy, is a nature-based guild centered on the allelopathic walnut tree (Juglans spp.). This guild demonstrates the armchair method of guild design: observing natural plant communities, understanding their interrelationships, and then building analogous human-designed assemblages that produce food and other useful products while maintaining [[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]] with walnuts particularly tricky. However, observing nature reveals that certain species naturally tolerate juglone and can form productive communities beneath walnuts.

## Core Guild Members

### Walnut (Juglans spp.) -- Central Element
- Provides nuts, premium timber, and [[permplant-carob-drought-tolerant-food-tree]]; grows in arid western states and less harsh locales
- Naturally open, spreading varieties (butternut, buartnut, heartnut) are excellent
- Decomposing walnut leaves and husks release insect-repelling citronella fumes
- Black walnut (J. nigra), butternut (J. cinerea), heartnut (J. ailantifolia cordiformis)

### Hackberry (Celtis spp.) -- Pioneer Shrub
- In Arizona, hackberry sprawls in the dry shade of walnut trees
- Like walnuts, hackberries secrete a competition-suppressing substance
- This allelopathic harmony is key: the toxins from the two species seem to complement each other
- Juglone doesn"t affect grass, while hackberry's toxins inhibit grasses and shallow-rooted plants
- Leaves and berries yield good wildlife forage
- Creates a highly specialized niche for other juglone-tolerant species

### Currant (Ribes spp.) -- Berry Producer
- Tim Murphy finds currants under walnut trees only when hackberries are present
- Benefits from absence of competing grasses under hackberries
- Dappled shade from walnuts and hackberries provides the half-gloom currants favor
- Aphids are a prime currant pest; citronella from decomposing walnut leaves may protect currants
- Possibly benefits from subterranean molecular commerce between microbial associates of walnut and hackberry roots
- Berries for fresh eating, jam, and wildlife

## Extended Guild Members

### Solanaceous Plants
Chiltepine (Capsicum aviculare) and wolfberry (Lycium spp.) are found naturally under hackberries in Arizona. Both are members of the Solanaceae (nightshade family), which "are narcissistic -- they thrive in the leaf litter of members of their own family." This suggests domesticated relatives could fit into the guild:
- Peppers (less sensitive to juglone)
- Tomatoes (less sensitive to juglone)
- Eggplants
- Note: potatoes are stunted by juglone and should be avoided

## Guild Assembly

### Arrangement
1. Begin with a single walnut tree at center
2. Within the roughly 20-foot circle of the future drip line: plant 1-3 each of hackberry, currant, wolfberry, and Elaeagnus or other N-fixer
3. Scatter solanaceous plants (peppers, tomatoes, eggplants) in the interstices
4. In northern climates: place solanaceous plants toward brighter margins
5. In southern climates: solanaceous plants appreciate the walnut's dappled shade
6. Radiating outside the circle: place mulberries and other buffer plants

### Plant Numbers
The larger the plant, the fewer in the guild: one walnut, a few each of hackberry and currant, several Elaeagnus, dozens of solanaceous vegetables.

## The Narcissistic Solanaceae Principle

A key ecological insight from this guild: certain plant families thrive in the leaf litter of their own family members. This principle suggests that when designing guilds, looking at what naturally grows together -- and what plant families are well-represented -- can guide substitution of domesticated relatives. The Solanaceae affinity for self-family litter is an example of this broader pattern.

## Proven Installations

Mary Zemach of Los Alamos has installed a variation containing currants, wolfberries, elderberries, silverberries, and Elaeagnus alongside walnuts and hackberries. While still experimental, the plants were reported as thriving.
