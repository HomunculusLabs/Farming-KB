## Strands and Rhizomorphs

The extraordinary destructive capacity of dry rot fungi lies in their root-like transport structures. These fungi form two types of organ:

**Strands (cords)** are assemblages of hyphae with a thick parent hypha at the core, surrounded by thinner hyphae growing along it in both directions. The parent produces tendrils that wind around the other hyphae to consolidate the structure. Strands are exploratory organs that develop in response to nutrient exhaustion, allowing the fungus to search across barren terrain. When a strand reaches a new food source, hyphae fan out to form new mycelia.

**Rhizomorphs** are more complex structures with waterproofed surfaces enclosing a central pipe for transmitting water and dissolved nutrients. Meruliporia generates both strands and rhizomorphs, while Serpula forms strands but not true rhizomorphs. Rhizomorphs can extend for many metres, piping water from wet soil to dry wood or masonry. In some situations, air passes along the rhizomorph, allowing the fungus to explore anoxic interiors of painted beams.

## The Dry Rot Invasion Process

The warmest invitation for dry rot is any wooden structure making direct contact with soil. Posts sunk into the ground without concrete protection, soil accumulating under exterior stucco, cracks in concrete slabs, and holes for pipes or electrical work all serve as entry points.

Once inside, hyphae digest the cellulose walls of hardwood beams, transforming springy lumber into parched brown cubes that crumble when touched — a pattern known as brown cubical rot. The brown colour results from progressive concentration of dark-pigmented lignin, which the fungus leaves behind as it digests cellulose. ## The Natural Habitat Mystery

The outdoor biology of Serpula lacrymans remains partly mysterious. The fungus has never been found growing on tree stumps in the British Isles. It has been found on stumps and fallen logs in the western Himalayas (documented by the Indian mycologist Bagchee over 20 years of searching beginning in 1929) and on roots of damaged Norway spruce in the Czech Republic, suggesting it sometimes survives as a wound parasite.

The true dry rot fungus is easily confused with the related Serpula himantioides, and both Serpula and the indoor mould Stachybotrys chartarum are weak competitors against closely related fungi in the wild — which may explain their rarity in natural habitats and their biological success in human-built environments.

## Climate Change and Modern Spread

Changing climatic conditions may aid the spread of dry rot fungi, particularly in areas experiencing increased rainfall, because elevated soil moisture benefits wood-rotting fungi. Dry rot tends to be more common in wet years because a high water table maintains the outdoor water supply for the indoor fungus.

The conversion of agricultural land to housing developments has been a major factor in the recent increase of Meruliporia damage in California. When orchards are cleared, trees are often snapped rather than pulled whole, leaving stumps and intact roots that are swiftly colonized by fungi. Once the buried wood is consumed, rhizomorphs extend through soil, around drainage pipes, and into basements. Imported topsoil and wood mulch used in landscaping serve as additional inoculum sources.

Homeowners can inadvertently worsen infestations by keeping rhizomorphs soaked with lawn sprinklers and using air conditioning to cool the fungus to its optimum growth temperature of approximately 23°C (73°F). This combination of moisture and temperature creates near-ideal conditions for Meruliporia establishment.
