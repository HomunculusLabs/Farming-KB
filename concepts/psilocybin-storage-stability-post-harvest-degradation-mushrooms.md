## Psilocybin vs. Psilocin Stability

**Psilocybin (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-DMT):**
- Relatively stable when dry. The phosphate group protects the 4-hydroxy
  position from oxidation.
- Stable at room temperature when kept dry and dark.
- Primary degradation: enzymatic dephosphorylation by alkaline
  phosphatase, converting psilocybin to psilocin. Greatly reduced
  in dried tissue with minimal residual moisture.

**Psilocin (4-hydroxy-DMT):**
- Significantly less stable. Prone to oxidation at the 4-hydroxy
  position to blue quinone polymers (blue bruising reaction).
- Degrades rapidly with oxygen, light, and moisture exposure.
- Slowly degrades over weeks to months at room temperature even when
  dried.

This stability difference explains why street samples consistently showed
near-zero psilocin (0-0.3 mg/g) vs. controlled-culture maximum of 2.9 mg/g.

## Degradation Pathways

### Enzymatic Dephosphorylation
Endogenous alkaline phosphatase converts psilocybin to psilocin
post-harvest until tissue is dry and enzyme activity ceases:

- Highest rate in fresh, moist tissue at room temperature.
- Slowed by refrigeration; stopped by freezing or freeze-drying.
- Irreversible — psilocin cannot revert to psilocybin.

### Oxidative Degradation of Psilocin
Psilocin oxidizes via the 4-hydroxy group to quinone intermediates that
polymerize into blue pigments:

- Accelerated by oxygen, light (especially UV), and heat.
- Slowed by vacuum sealing, darkness, [[root-cellars-and-cold-storage]].
- Complete oxidation renders psilocin pharmacologically inactive.
