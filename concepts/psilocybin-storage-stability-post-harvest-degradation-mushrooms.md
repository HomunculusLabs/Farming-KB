# Psilocybin Storage Stability and Post-Harvest Degradation

## Overview

The stability of [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] in harvested mushrooms critically
affects potency from harvest to consumption. Bigwood and Beug (1982) used
rigorous post-harvest protocols and documented extreme [[psilocybe-cubensis-street-sample-potency-variability]]
variability, providing evidence of [[fungal-pah-degradation-pathways]]. Understanding
storage stability is essential for research accuracy and predictable dosing.

## Post-Harvest Handling in Bigwood and Beug

The study used rigorous protocols to preserve alkaloid content:

- **Immediate freeze-drying:** Mushrooms freeze-dried after harvest,
  removing water by sublimation under vacuum at low temperature.
  Preserves heat-sensitive compounds and halts [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]].

- **Sealed packaging:** Dried mushrooms sealed in plastic, excluding
  atmospheric oxygen and moisture that promote psilocin oxidation.

- **[[root-cellaring-guide]]:** Samples stored at -5 C until analysis. Low
  temperature slows residual enzymatic activity and oxidation.

- **Voucher specimens:** Deposited at University of Washington Herbarium
  (WTU) for permanent reference and potential re-analysis.

Controlled cultures still showed 4-fold variability in psilocybin despite
these protocols, confirming biological variation as the primary source.

## Psilocybin vs. Psilocin Stability

**Psilocybin (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-DMT):**
- Relatively stable when dry. The phosphate group protects the 4-hydroxy
  position from oxidation.
- Stable at room temperature when kept dry and dark.
- Primary degradation: enzymatic dephosphorylation by alkaline
  phosphatase, converting psilocybin to psilocin. Greatly reduced
  in dried tissue with minimal residual moisture.

**Psilocin (4-hydroxy-DMT):**
- Significantly less stable. Prone to oxidation at the 4-hydroxy
  position to blue quinone polymers (blue bruising reaction).
- Degrades rapidly with oxygen, light, and moisture exposure.
- Slowly degrades over weeks to months at room temperature even when
  dried.

This stability difference explains why street samples consistently showed
near-zero psilocin (0-0.3 mg/g) vs. controlled-culture maximum of 2.9 mg/g.

## Street Sample Degradation Evidence

Five street samples showed striking degradation patterns:

| Sample | Psilocybin (mg/g) | Psilocin (mg/g) |
|--------|-------------------|-----------------|
| 1 | 5.6 | 0 |
| 2 | 6.2 | 0 |
| 3 | 0.7 | 0.3 |
| 4 | 0.7 | 0.3 |
| 5 | 1.3 | 0.3 |

The 10-fold psilocybin range (0.7-6.2 mg/g) exceeded controlled-culture
variability (3.2-13.3 mg/g). Consistent zero psilocin — even in samples
with moderate psilocybin — indicates degradation during distribution.

## Degradation Pathways

### Enzymatic Dephosphorylation
Endogenous alkaline phosphatase converts psilocybin to psilocin
post-harvest until tissue is dry and enzyme activity ceases:

- Highest rate in fresh, moist tissue at room temperature.
- Slowed by refrigeration; stopped by freezing or freeze-drying.
- Irreversible — psilocin cannot revert to psilocybin.

### Oxidative Degradation of Psilocin
Psilocin oxidizes via the 4-hydroxy group to quinone intermediates that
polymerize into blue pigments:

- Accelerated by oxygen, light (especially UV), and heat.
- Slowed by vacuum sealing, darkness, and cold storage.
- Complete oxidation renders psilocin pharmacologically inactive.
