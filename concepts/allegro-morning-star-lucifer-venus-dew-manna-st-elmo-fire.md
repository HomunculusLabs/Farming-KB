## Morning Star, Venus, Dew, Manna, and St Elmo's Fire

In [[allegro-star-of-the-morning-venus-and-the-sacred-mushroom]]. The celestial body, its moisture, and the fungus it was thought to engender
formed an interconnected symbolic system linking heaven and earth, semen and soil, divinity and
the underworld.

## Morning Star = Peristerēōn Word-Play

The Greek word for the morning star, *peristerēōn*, lends itself to a multilingual pun that Allegro
sees as central to its cultic significance. Spelled out through Aramaic and Greek components, it
becomes: **Bar-** (Aramaic: "son of") + **aster** (Greek: "star") + **heōs** (Greek: "of the
morning") = **"Son of the Star."**

This resonates with one of the most important messianic prophecies in the Hebrew Bible:
**"A star shall come forth out of Jacob"** (Numbers 24:17). Its political power was demonstrated
when the leader of the Second Jewish Revolt in the 2nd century CE — Simon bar Kokhba — adopted
the title **"Son of the Star"** (*Bar Kokhba*), explicitly claiming fulfillment of the Balaam
prophecy. For Allegro, the title's mushroomic substratum reveals that the "star" was not merely an
astral symbol but the phallic fungus that sprang from the earth at dawn, emerging from its volval
sheath in an event seen as both celestial descent and terrestrial birth.

## Isaiah's Oracle on the Fallen Star

Isaiah 14:12-15 contains one of the most famous "fallen star" passages in biblical literature:

> "How are you fallen from heaven, **Shining One, Son of the Dawn!**"

Allegro reads this as a mushroom epithet applied to the king of Babylon. The mushroom — a bright,
dome-capped organism that appears to fall from the sky and rise from the earth — was a natural
symbol for any figure of ambiguous celestial-terrestrial origin. The name **Babylon** itself,
Allegro suggests, is cognate with **Boubalion**, a fungus name attached to the Squirting Cucumber
(*Ecballium elaterium*), deriving from Sumerian **GU-BAR**, "top of the head; glans penis." The
Squirting Cucumber's explosive seed discharge mirrors the mushroom's [[spore]]-release, both understood
as celestial semen.

Pliny records a "Babylonian thorn bush" said to grow on the "Long Walls at Athens." Allegro
proposes that the original name derived from Sumerian ***GU_TIL_U_DUN***, meaning "ball-and-socket;
penis-and-vulva," which was subsequently misunderstood as the Semitic **kötel-ʾAttūnāʾ**, "long
wall of Athens." The semantic drift from a sexual-fungal designation to a fortification name
illustrates how the original cultic meanings were progressively obscured.

## Venus as Fertility Star

Venus, the Morning Star, held a unique position in ancient cosmology. Before the sun rises, Venus
"slips from the nuptial chamber" to herald the dawn — second in brightness only to the sun and
moon. Its dual visibility gave rise to paired names: **Lucifer** ("light-bringer," the morning
appearance) and **Vesper** (the evening appearance). It was also called **Juno, Isis,** and
**Mother of the Gods** across various traditions.

Ancient names for "star" reveal that luminaries were pictured as **penes in the sky**. The light
they shed was understood as the "glow" of the glans' fiery crown — a brilliant, moist-tipped
radiance descending upon the sleeping earth. Female names like Venus and Juno refer to this same
generative power from the receptive angle: the star was imagined as "slipping, dripping with the
semen of the terrestrial womb."

The sun eventually burns away the dew drops. Until then, they remain as dew — what Pliny calls
the **"most powerful conceptual fluid of Nature,"** the mediating substance between celestial fire
and earthly fertility. The dew was the visible evidence of the star's nocturnal visitation, proof
that heaven had consorted with earth.

## Dew as Seminal Fluid

For the ancients, dew was not merely condensation but a literal emission of divine semen. Pliny
writes that Venus "scatters a genital dew with which it fills the conceptive organs of the earth
and stimulates those of all animals." The identification of dew with semen pervades classical and
Near Eastern thought, forming the basis of fertility rituals across the Mediterranean world.

Pearls were believed to be "born" within shells by dew's influence: "The source and
breeding-ground of pearls are shells… they gap open… filled with dewy pregnancy." Pliny further
notes that **"pearls have more connexion with the sky than with the sea,"** confirming their
celestial-semenal origin rather than any merely aquatic explanation.

Drugs and medicines were thought to be produced from dew warmed by the sun after star-risings.
These were called **"heavenly gifts… equal to the Nectar of the gods"** — a direct link between
celestial moisture and divine sustenance. The dew-born drugs were the pharmacological counterparts
of [[soma-and-the-sacred-mushroom-debate]], so it was believed to give life to the
denizens of the underworld. The dew of light falling upon the land of shades promised resurrection
for the dead — the same fecundating power that quickened the earth could also revivify the departed.

Mushroom worship was intimately connected with **necromancy** — the practice of raising the spirits
of the dead for fortune-telling and prophecy. The fungus, with its ability to appear overnight as
if from nowhere and to produce visionary experiences, was the natural vehicle for contacting the
other world. The **mandrake**, another plant with powerful psychoactive properties, was said to have
its demonic power increased when touched with morning dew — the same celestial semen that animated
the mushroom.

## Dioscouroi as Storm Saviours

The **Dioscouroi** (Castor and Pollux) were identified with the constellation Gemini and, more
specifically, with the Morning and Evening Star as a pair. Their primary mythological function was
as saviours of sailors caught in storms — twin lights appearing at the masthead when all seemed
lost.

What the ancients described as the Dioscouroi's protective radiance corresponds to **atmospheric
electrical discharges** — the phenomenon now known as **St Elmo's fire**. These luminous plasma
discharges appear on pointed objects during thunderstorms, creating an eerie blue-violet glow.
During World War II, Allied airmen encountering these discharges dubbed them **"gremlins"** — modern
names for an ancient divine phenomenon.

Pliny provides a vivid account: "Stars come into existence at sea and on land… radiance clinging
to the javelins of soldiers… stars alight on the yards of ships… if two of them appear, they denote
safety and portend a successful voyage; their approach puts to flight the terrible star called
Helena." He concludes: **"For this reason they are called Castor and Pollux, and people pray to
them as gods for aid at sea."**

The Homeric Hymns invoke them directly: **"Bright-eyed Muses, tell of the Tyndaridae, the Sons
of Zeus"** — saviours of men in the darkest storms, appearing as twin lights above the masts of
endangered vessels. Their mushroomic connection lies in the sacred fungus's presumed ability to
control atmospheric phenomena.

## Mushroom as Bridge Between Worlds

The mushroom's dual nature — springing from the earth yet associated with the heavens — earned it
the title **"star of the ground"** (its modern Arabic name). It was understood as a **"child of
two worlds, heavenly and terrestrial,"** forming a bridge between man and god. No other natural
object so perfectly embodied the union of opposites: born of moisture from above yet rooted in
soil below; appearing overnight as if by magic yet possessing systematic biological structure.

Through consumption of the sacred fungus, men were given the power to **"become like gods, knowing
good and evil"** (Genesis 3:22). The fruit of the [[stropharia-cubensis-tree-of-knowledge]] was, in this reading, the
mushroom — the medium through which divine knowledge passed from heaven to earth. The Gospel of
John preserves the ascension-descent formula: **"No one has ascended into heaven but he who
descended from heaven"** (John 3:13). The mushroom, which "falls" from the sky as dew and "rises"
from the ground as fungus, perfectly embodies this reciprocal movement between realms. The
mushroom's primary celestial affinities lay in its presumed ability to **control the atmosphere,
winds, and tempests** — the same powers attributed to the Morning Star, the Dioscouroi, and the
storm-gods. As the visible link between sky and soil, semen and sustenance, [[shamanism-death-and-rebirth]], the mushroom stood at the center of an ancient cosmology that saw all fertility as a single, continuous process flowing from heaven to earth and back again.
