# Lime and Cement in permaculture: A Designers' Manual, presenting lime as the preferred
binding material for permaculture building while acknowledging the role
of cement where structural demands require it. He emphasises understanding
the chemistry and sourcing of these materials for self-reliant building.

## Lime Production

Lime is produced by calcining (burning) limestone (calcium carbonate,
CaCO3) at temperatures of approximately 900 to 1100 degrees Celsius in a
kiln. The burning drives off carbon dioxide, converting the limestone to
quicklime (calcium oxide, CaO):

    CaCO3 + heat --> CaO + CO2

Mollison notes that small-scale lime burning can be done in simple kilns
built from local stone or brick, using wood or [[fungi-in-composting-and-agricultural-waste-conversion]] as fuel.
This makes lime accessible to rural communities without industrial supply
chains, unlike cement which requires much higher kiln temperatures (1450C)
and precise raw material ratios.

## Slaking (Hydration)

Quicklime is highly reactive and must be slaked (hydrated) before use. The
slaking process involves adding water to quicklime in a controlled manner,
producing slaked lime (calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2) and releasing
significant heat. Mollison describes two main forms:

- Lime putty: quicklime slaked with excess water to form a thick creamy
  paste. This matures over weeks or months in sealed pits, improving
  workability and carbonation properties. Mature lime putty is considered
  the finest material for renders and mortars.
- [[cervantes-hydrated-lime-quicklime-ph-adjustment]] (bag lime): quicklime slaked with just enough water to
  form a dry powder. This is convenient for transport and storage but
  produces a less workable and potentially less durable mortar than putty.

The slaking process must be done carefully -- adding water to quicklime
can cause violent boiling and spattering of caustic material. Protective
equipment is essential.

## Lime Mortars

Lime mortar is made by mixing lime putty or hydrated lime with sand and
water. A typical ratio by volume is 1 part lime to 3 parts sand for
general building mortars. Lime mortars are:

- Flexible and self-healing: they accommodate slight building movement
  without cracking, unlike rigid cement mortars
- Breathable: they allow moisture vapour to pass through walls, preventing
  trapped moisture and associated decay
- Low energy: lime production requires far less energy than cement
  production
- Reversible: lime mortar can be removed and walls disassembled for
  recycling, supporting [[mollison-designers-earth-rammed-tire-construction]],
