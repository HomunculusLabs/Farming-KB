# Mycorrhizal Carbon Cycling and Soil Sequestration

Updated: 2026-04-18

Mycorrhizal fungi play dual roles in the global carbon cycle: as major conduits for plant-fixed carbon into soil systems, and as key regulators of soil carbon sequestration through their effects on decomposition processes and soil structure. The three main mycorrhizal types -- ericoid (ERM), ectomycorrhizal (EM), and arbuscular (AM) -- each influence carbon cycling through distinct mechanisms.

## Carbon Allocation Through Mycorrhizal Networks

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Carbon Flux

Early laboratory pulse-labelling studies (Jakobsen and Rosendahl, 1990; Pearson and Jakobsen, 1993) on pot-grown cucumber suggested the total carbon cost of mycorrhiza accounted for up to 20% of net fixation, with less than 1% found in external mycelium. However, these estimates significantly underestimated carbon flux:

- Up to 16% of AM mycelial carbon content can be replaced within 24 hours by new assimilate from a 5-hour labelling period (Staddon et al., 2003)
- AM mycelia comprise 50% of fungal length in soil under herbaceous plants (Rillig et al., 2002)
- AM fungi account for 20%-30% of total soil microbial biomass in temperate grassland (Miller and Kling, 2000; Olsson and Wilhelmsson, 2000)
- External mycelium receives more than 9% of net carbon fixation by grassland in summer, most within the first 3 days after photosynthesis (Johnson et al., 2002a, b)
- This exceeds typical carbon inputs through herbivore dung (Jones and Donnelly, 2004)
- Internal root colonization adds an additional 4%-10% of net fixation (Smith and Read, 1997)

## Glomalin and Soil Carbon Storage

AM fungi produce glomalin, a hydrophobic protein secreted into soil that plays a major role in soil aggregation:

- Glomalin strongly accumulates in soil with a residence time of 6-42 years
- It contributes up to 15% of total stable soil organic carbon in grasslands (Miller and Kling, 2000)
- It contributes 4%-5% of stable soil organic carbon in tropical rainforest soils (Rillig et al., 2001; Zhu and Miller, 2003)

This recalcitrant AM fungal product represents one of the most significant fungal contributions to long-term soil carbon sequestration.

## ERM and EM Effects on Decomposition and Carbon Storage

### Ericoid Mycorrhizae (ERM)

Plants with ERM produce extremely recalcitrant litter enriched in polyphenolic compounds, low-molecular-mass phenolics, and aliphatic acids that are fungistatic and fungitoxic (Jalal et al., 1982; Jalal and Read, 1983). This litter has extremely high C:N ratios and decomposes very slowly:

- ERM fungi are highly adapted to their host plant litter, tolerating and metabolizing toxic phenolic compounds (Leake and Read, 1991a)
- They express oxidative and hydrolytic enzymes to mobilize nitrogen and phosphorus from litter (Leake and Read, 1997)
- Decomposition is very incomplete, resulting in accumulation of recalcitrant humic compounds
- ERM effectiveness in nitrogen exploitation is reflected in absence of detectable mineral nitrogen (nitrate or ammonium) in soils under ericaceous plants (Adams, 1986)

### Ectomycorrhizae (EM)

EM mycelia provide intensive, spatially precise nutrient foraging through decaying litter rich in polyphenolics and tannins (Northup et al., 1995; Donnelly et al., 2004; Perez-Moreno and Read, 2000):

- EM fungi can use organic nitrogen sources (amino acids, proteins), short-circuiting ammonification and nitrification pathways (Leake et al., 2004)
- This results in very low rates of inorganic nitrogen release
- EM fungi compete effectively with saprotrophs due to large supplies of host photosynthate
- Selective nutrient removal increases C:N and C:P ratios of remaining residues (Bending and Read, 1995)
- The drying of soil under trees by EM water extraction has been implicated in reduced litter decomposition (Koide and Wu, 2003)

## Biome-Level Patterns

The association between mycorrhiza type and carbon cycling varies predictably across biomes (Raich and Tufekcioglu, 2000; Cornelissen et al., 2001; Read, 1991):

- **ERM-dominated heathlands**: Highest litter recalcitrance, slowest decomposition (C:N ratios 60-120)
- **EM-dominated forests**: High C:N litter, slow decomposition, significant soil carbon accumulation
- **AM-dominated grasslands**: Low-recalcitrance litter, faster decomposition, less carbon accumulation but significant contribution through glomalin and soil aggregation

## Litter Decomposition and Mycorrhiza Type

Litter mass remaining after 20 weeks of standard incubation shows clear patterns:
- ERM litter (Calluna vulgaris): slowest decomposition, highest C:N ratios, lowest pH
- EM litter (Pinus, Picea, Fagus, Quercus): intermediate
- AM litter (Acer, Fraxinus, Ulmus): fastest decomposition, lowest C:N ratios, highest pH

## Mycorrhizal Effects on Soil Aggregation

AM fungi affect soil carbon sequestration through soil macroaggregate stabilization:

- Both hyphal activities and glomalin secretions stabilize macroaggregates (more than 2 mm diameter) (Rillig et al., 2002)
- Organic matter entrapped inside macroaggregates is biologically and chemically stabilized to a greater degree than bulk soil organic matter (Jones and Donnelly, 2004)
- These effects are implicated in increased soil carbon storage (Zhu and Miller, 2003)

## Positive Feedback Mechanisms

Both ERM and EM create positive feedback mechanisms for soil carbon sequestration:

1. Mycorrhizal fungi outcompete saprotrophs for labile nutrients using host photosynthate
2. Selective nutrient removal from litter increases residue C:N and C:P ratios
3. Remaining residues become more recalcitrant
4. Saprotrophic decomposer communities are further restricted by reduced mineral nutrient supplies
5. Soil carbon accumulation continues

AM fungi contribute differently -- through glomalin production and aggregate formation rather than through decomposition suppression.
