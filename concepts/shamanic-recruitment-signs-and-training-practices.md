## Signs of Shamanic Potential

In preliterate societies, certain experiences mark an individual as a
candidate for shamanic training. These signs are understood not as random
misfortunes but as evidence of inner strength or a special relationship with
the spirit world. The most commonly cited signs include survival of near-death
experiences, particularly those involving illness. Individuals who nearly die
from a disease and fight their way back to health after weeks in what McKenna
calls an "indeterminate zone" are thought to possess exceptional strength of
soul.

Epilepsy is another frequent marker of shamanic potential in many traditional
cultures. The convulsive, trance-like states associated with epileptic
seizures are interpreted as evidence of a heightened sensitivity to altered
[[shamanic-trance-cross-cultural-patterns]].

## The Element of Uniqueness or Oddness

Beyond dramatic survival events, McKenna identifies a more subtle but equally
important sign: general oddness or uniqueness in an individual. This can
manifest in many ways. It is a quality that members of the community
recognize intuitively, an idiosyncrasy of temperament or behavior that sets
someone apart from their peers. Among aspiring shamans there must be some
demonstrable sign of inner strength or what McKenna terms "a hypersensitivity
to trance states." This sensitivity may express itself as a natural tendency
toward visionary experience, unusual dream life, or a spontaneous capacity
for entering deep meditative states without training.

## The Training Process

Once identified, the candidate undergoes training that McKenna characterizes
as involving instruction in breathing techniques, plant usage, and other
practices. He acknowledges that "we do not really know all of what goes on"
in these training processes, as the information systems involved are secret
and have not been well studied by outsiders. What is clear is that the
training is practical and technique-oriented rather than primarily
theological or devotional.

The emphasis on techniques rather than beliefs is a crucial distinction.
Shamanism, as McKenna understands it, is not a religion in the conventional
sense. It does not require adherence to a creed or participation in regular
worship. Instead, it provides a toolkit for navigating non-ordinary states
of consciousness, and the criterion for successful training is the ability
to use these tools effectively rather than the ability to articulate a
coherent theology.

## The Shaman as Phenomenologist

One of McKenna's most distinctive contributions to the understanding of
shamanism is his characterization of shamans as "the true phenomenologists
of this world." By this he means that shamans are engaged in systematic,
first-person investigation of consciousness and reality using the tools of
[[harner-shamanic-journey-and-altered-states]]. They possess deep knowledge of plant chemistry, yet they
describe the effects of psychoactive plants in terms of "spirits" rather
than molecular interactions. McKenna argues that the word "spirit," as used
by shamans, functions as what he calls a "technical gloss for a very
complicated concept," analogous to the way a quantum physicist might use the
term "charm" to describe a property of quarks.
