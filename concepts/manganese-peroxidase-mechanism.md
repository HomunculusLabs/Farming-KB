# Manganese Peroxidase Mechanism in Fungal Bioremediation

Manganese peroxidase (MnP; EC 1.11.1.13) is a glycosylated heme-containing extracellular peroxidase secreted by [[white-rot-fungi-effluent-decolorization]] and certain soil litter-decomposing fungi. MnP is one of the three principal [[ligninolytic-enzyme-systems-white-rot-fungi]] and plays a central role in [[fungal-lignin-degradation-enzymology]] and the [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]].

## Distribution Among Fungi

A comprehensive list of 56 fungi that produce MnP in liquid and/or solid-state fermentation has been compiled by Hofrichter (2002). MnP is secreted by a distinct group of Basidiomycetes belonging to the families Coriolaceae, Meruliaceae, Polyporaceae, and the soil litter families Strophariaceae and Tricholomataceae. The enzyme is widely distributed among lignin-degrading fungi and represents one of the most common peroxidases in [[white-rot-fungi-effluent-decolorization]] basidiomycetes.

The broad distribution of MnP across multiple fungal families reflects its fundamental importance in fungal lignin degradation strategies. Unlike [[fungal-lignin-peroxidase-veratryl-alcohol]], which is restricted to a smaller number of fungal species, MnP is found in the majority of lignin-degrading Basidiomycetes. This suggests that MnP represents a more evolutionarily conserved and versatile component of the [[fungal-enzyme-systems-xenobiotic-degradation]] for lignin degradation.

## Enzyme Properties

The molecular weight of MnP ranges between 38 and 62.5 kDa, with the molecular weight of the most purified MnP being approximately 45 kDa. About 11 isozymes of MnP are known to be produced by Ceriporiopsis subvermispora. Five isozymes have been detected in [[singh-cytochrome-p450-fungal-bioremediation]] MP-1. Like [[fungal-lignin-peroxidase-veratryl-alcohol]], nitrogen-deficient conditions favor the production of MnP.

MnP has a lower redox potential than LiP and differs from other peroxidases due to the structure of its Mn(II) binding site. Two calcium ions maintain the structure of the active site, which is critical for enzymatic function. The crystal structures of MnP have been determined for P. chrysosporium, C. subvermispora, and Pleurotus eryngii based on gene sequences.

The Mn(II) binding site of MnP is formed by three acidic amino acid residues (glutamate and aspartate) that coordinate the Mn(II) ion. This binding site is unique to MnP and is the structural basis for the enzyme's specificity toward Mn(II) as its primary substrate. The binding of Mn(II) at this site triggers the release of Mn(III) into the medium, where it can oxidize a wide range of phenolic compounds.

## Catalytic Cycle

The catalytic cycle of MnP is initiated by binding H2O2 or an organic peroxide to the native ferric enzyme, forming an iron-peroxide complex. Peroxide oxygen-oxygen bond cleavage produces MnP compound I, which is an Fe4+-oxo-porphyrin-radical complex. Subsequent reduction proceeds through MnP compound II, an Fe4+-oxo-porphyrin complex. During this reduction, Mn(II) is oxidized to Mn(III), and the native enzyme is regenerated with the release of a water molecule.

MnP compound I can be reduced by electron donors such as ferrocyanide, phenolics, and Mn(II). However, MnP compound II is very slowly reduced by substrates other than Mn(II), meaning that Mn(II) is essentially required for completion of the catalytic cycle. A high concentration of H2O2 can cause reversible inactivation of MnP by forming compound III, an inactive state.

The requirement for Mn(II) in the catalytic cycle is both a strength and a limitation of MnP. The Mn(III) produced by MnP is released into the medium as a diffusible oxidant, allowing MnP to act on substrates that are physically separated from the fungal hyphae. However, the availability of Mn(II) in the environment can limit MnP activity, and the enzyme is dependent on chelators such as oxalate or malonate to stabilize Mn(III) in solution.

## Oxidation Mechanism

One-electron oxidation of various substrates occurs through chelates of Mn(III) with organic acids. These chelates react with each other, forming alkyl radicals that undergo spontaneous reactions with molecular oxygen, producing superoxide and other radicals. The radicals generated by these autocatalytic reactions serve as a source of peroxides and can be employed by MnP even in the absence of exogenous H2O2.

An MnP system can oxidize organic sulfur-containing cooxidants such as glutathione and L-cysteine, unsaturated fatty acids and their derivatives including linoleic acid, and Tween 80 to reactive thiyl and peroxyl radicals. These radicals can attack lignin structures in the presence of dioxygen, enabling MnP to participate in the degradation of complex polymeric substrates.

The autocatalytic generation of peroxides by MnP/Mn(III) chelate systems represents an important self-sustaining mechanism. Once initiated, the MnP system can generate its own H2O2 supply through the oxidation of organic acids and other substrates, reducing the dependence on external peroxide sources. This autocatalytic capability may be particularly important for [[mycoremediation-field-applications-case-studies]] where exogenous H2O2 addition is impractical.

## Lignin Degradation by MnP

A reaction system containing MnP from [[singh-cytochrome-p450-fungal-bioremediation]], sodium malonate, Mn(II), and H2O2 catalyzes C alpha oxidation, C alpha-C beta cleavage, and alkyl-aryl cleavages of phenolic syringyl-type beta-1 lignin structures. Nematoloma frowardii MnP achieved 4 to 10% mineralization of labeled synthetic lignin as 14CO2 over 144 hours. Phlebia radiata MnP demonstrated 12 to 16% mineralization of labeled DHP.

A hypothetical pathway for MnP-catalyzed formation of CO2 from aromatic lignin structures involves initial MnP/Mn(II)/Mn(III) oxidation of phenolic lignin subunits to phenoxyl radicals, followed by carbon-centered radical formation, spontaneous reactions with oxygen, and eventual mineralization to CO2 and organic acids such as formic acid.

## Synergistic Enzyme Combinations

Increased depolymerization of lignin preparations occurs using combinations of MnP with other extracellular enzymes. MnP and [[brown-rot-fungi-biodegradation]] from Rigidoporus lignosus, MnP and laccase from Agaricus bisporus, MnP and [[fungal-lignin-peroxidase-veratryl-alcohol]] from P. chrysosporium, and LiP, MnP, and laccase from various sources all show enhanced lignin degradation compared to individual enzymes alone.

These synergistic effects reflect the complementary substrate specificities of the different [[ligninolytic-enzyme-systems-white-rot-fungi]]. MnP is particularly effective at oxidizing phenolic substructures, while LiP can attack nonphenolic compounds. Laccase with appropriate mediators can oxidize both phenolic and nonphenolic substrates. The combination of all three enzymes provides the broadest substrate coverage and the most effective lignin degradation.

## Degradation of Nonphenolic Compounds

Nonphenolic substructures are abundant in lignin but cannot undergo oxidation through direct attack by Mn(III)-chelates. Mediators or cooxidants generate reactive radicals that can oxidize such structures. MnP from Phlebia radiata cleaves nonphenolic beta-O-4 lignin model dimers and catalyzes the mineralization of synthetic lignin in a cell-free system.

An MnP-lipid peroxidation system is strong enough to degrade C alpha-C beta and beta-aryl ether bonds in nonphenolic lignin model dimers. Lipid peroxidation of unsaturated lipids forms lipoxyradical intermediates in the presence of Mn(II) that can oxidize nonphenolic model lignin compounds. Ceriporiopsis subvermispora produced benzyl fragments from beta-O-4 lignin structures due to MnP lipid-mediated peroxidation in the absence of LiP activity.

## Hybrid Peroxidases

Hybrid forms of MnP and LiP are known which can oxidize both phenolic and nonphenolic compounds. These enzymes have been identified in Bjerkandera sp. strain BOS55, Bjerkandera adusta, and Pleurotus eryngii. The existence of hybrid peroxidases suggests evolutionary convergence of catalytic capabilities and expands the substrate range beyond what either enzyme can achieve alone.

These versatile peroxidase (VP) enzymes possess both Mn(II)-oxidizing activity and the ability to directly oxidize nonphenolic aromatic compounds without mediators. VP enzymes represent a particularly promising group for [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] because of their broad substrate range and ability to function under a wider range of environmental conditions compared to individual LiP or MnP enzymes.

## Gene Structure and Regulation

DNA sequence and protein crystallographic examinations reveal the close similarity of MnPs to LiPs. MnP is regulated by carbon and nitrogen levels in P. chrysosporium. Transcription of MnP genes occurs in the presence of Mn(II), although Mn(II) does not regulate all mnp genes uniformly across species. Transcription of different MnP isozymes exhibits variable dependencies on Mn(II) concentration.

MnP gene sequences and cDNA have been cloned and sequenced from [[turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor]], [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]], Ceriporiopsis subvermispora, Dichomitus squalens, and Ganoderma applanatum. One MnP-encoding gene was found grouped within a 10-kb region in T. versicolor. Mature protein length shows differences between C. subvermispora and D. squalens (364 to 369 amino acids) and P. chrysosporium (357 to 358 amino acids).

## Role in Effluent Treatment

MnP plays a significant role in the decolorization and treatment of various industrial effluents. Production of MnP increases when [[singh-cytochrome-p450-fungal-bioremediation]] is cultured in media containing molasses, and the rate of color removal is directly proportional to MnP activity. MnP along with manganese-independent peroxidase (MIP) contribute to the decolorization of distillery and brewery wastes.

MnP is necessary for efficient decolorization of olive mill wastewater by several fungal species. The MnP/Mn(III) chelate system can oxidize the phenolic and melanoidin compounds responsible for the dark color of these effluents. The ability of MnP to function in the presence of organic acid chelators that are naturally present in many wastewaters makes it particularly suitable for industrial effluent treatment.

## Applications in Pollutant Degradation

MnP has been applied to the degradation of a wide range of xenobiotic pollutants. MnP from P. chrysosporium has demonstrated effectiveness in degrading bisphenol A (BPA), producing metabolites with no estrogen activity. Four metabolites are generated by one-electron oxidation of BPA by MnP of [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]], and a pathway of BPA metabolism by MnP of P. ostreatus has been proposed.

MnP from P. chrysosporium ME-446 has been shown to degrade methoxychlor with 28% efficiency without the formation of metabolites. MnP-Tween 80 and laccase-HBT treatment systems using white-rot fungi have demonstrated broad-spectrum degradation capabilities. A two-stage immobilized MnP bioreactor has been optimized for catalytic generation of Mn(III) and subsequent oxidation of chlorophenols using Lentinula edodes.
