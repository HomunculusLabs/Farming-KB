# Button Mushroom (Agaricus brunnescens and A. bitorquis) Overview

## Overview and Historical Context

The Button Mushroom (Agaricus brunnescens) is the most commercially
cultivated mushroom in the world. Its cultivation in caves near Paris
dates back to at least 1868. As a secondary decomposer, the Button
Mushroom occupies a unique ecological niche: it grows on substrates
that have already been partially broken down by other microorganisms.
Unlike primary decomposers such as Oyster mushrooms and Shiitake that
secrete enzymes to break down lignin-cellulose, the Button Mushroom
thrives on material that has been pre-digested by microbial communities.

The cultivation of Button Mushrooms is dependent upon composted
substrates. This complex composting process involves two distinct phases:
Phase I, where the raw materials are mixed and allowed to undergo
thermophilic microbial decomposition; and Phase II (pasteurization),
where the compost is further conditioned to select for beneficial
microorganisms while eliminating competitors and pests.

## Species Described

### Agaricus brunnescens (The Button Mushroom)
The standard commercial Button Mushroom, also known as the common
mushroom, is the most widely cultivated mushroom species worldwide. It
is dependent upon casing soils for fruitbody formation. Under sterile
conditions, this species will not fruit well or at all. Fruiting from
cased grain is the standard method. Mushrooms are best picked while the
partial veils are intact, in other words when the mushrooms are still
young.

### Agaricus bitorquis (=rodmanii) (The Warm Weather Button Mushroom)
A closely related species also requiring casing soils for fruiting. A.
bitorquis is more heat-tolerant than A. brunnescens, making it
suitable for cultivation in warmer climates. Both species require soil
microorganisms present in the casing layer to trigger the initiation
of fruitbody formation.

## The Composting Process

The Button Mushroom evolved to exploit a very specific niche in nature.
In forest ecosystems, plant tissue is first attacked by primary
decomposers. Once the enzymes of one mushroom species or group of
microorganisms have broken down the raw material, and the mass,
structure and composition of the compost is reduced, heat, carbon
dioxide, ammonia and other gases are liberated. After these
microorganisms have completed their life cycles, the compost is ready
for the Button Mushroom.

This two-stage decomposition model is fundamental to understanding
why the Button Mushroom requires such specialized cultivation methods
compared to primary decomposers. The compost must provide:
- A specific nutritional profile with available nitrogen
- The correct structure and moisture-holding capacity
- Beneficial microbial communities
- Freedom from competitors and pathogens

## Phase II Pasteurization Chamber

The "Phase II" chamber serves a dual purpose: steam pasteurization to
kill competitors, and controlled conditioning of the compost for
optimal mushroom growth. This is not to accomplish composting per se,
but rather a way of often-selecting for beneficial microorganisms while
eliminating pests. The process involves carefully controlled
temperature, air exchange, and humidity over several days.

## Casing Soil Dependency

Both A. brunnescens and A. bitorquis are absolutely dependent upon
casing soils for fruitbody formation. The casing layer provides:
- A moisture reservoir for the developing mushrooms
- Essential microorganisms that stimulate fruiting
- Minerals and salts necessary for mushroom metabolism
- Protection from humidity fluctuations

Without the microbial community present in casing soils, these species
will not form primordia even under otherwise ideal conditions. This
is in contrast to many gourmet mushrooms that can fruit without
casing, such as most Oyster species, Shiitake, and Lion's Mane.

## Pest Vulnerability

One significant challenge in Button Mushroom cultivation is the
economic devastation caused by nematodes. Unlike Oyster mushrooms,
which exude metabolites toxic to nematodes and can become predatory
toward them, Button Mushrooms have no such defense. Nematode
infestations are commonplace and economically damaging in commercial
Button Mushroom operations.

Stropharia ambigua has been noted as beneficial when introduced into
the composting process, as it can help suppress competitors before
the Button Mushroom is introduced. The relationship between different
fungal species in the compost ecosystem is complex and affects the
success of the final crop.

## Spent Compost Recycling

After Button Mushroom cultivation, the spent compost is a valuable
resource. It can be used for:
- Recycling into the cultivation of other mushroom species
- Feed for livestock (ruminants especially)
- Garden compost and soil amendment
- As a base substrate for species sequencing with Oyster mushrooms
  and other primary decomposers

The spent compost from Button Mushroom operations represents a
substantial volume of material that can be productively utilized
rather than discarded.

## Commercial Significance

The Button Mushroom industry is by far the largest segment of
commercial mushroom cultivation worldwide. The development of
mechanical harvesters in Holland has eliminated the largest labor
contingent in a mushroom farm, the pickers. In the United States,
Canada, and Europe, more Button Mushrooms are sold fresh than dried.
Marketing tactics include selling loose in trays with consumer self-
selection, or pre-packaged in 3, 5, or 7 ounce containers, a trick
employed to disguise the actual price per pound.
