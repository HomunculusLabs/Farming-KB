# Japanese Laughing Mushroom Tradition

Japan possesses a rich tradition of psychoactive mushroom lore dating back to the Middle Ages, centered on the legendary "Laughing Mushroom" (waraitake) and "Dancing Mushroom" (maitake). While the specific species responsible for these effects remain debated, Japan is confirmed to harbor several naturally occurring [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-psilocybin-and-psilocin]]-containing mushroom species, and the country's long history of mushroom cultivation provides important context for understanding psychoactive mushroom use in East Asia.

## The Legendary Laughing Mushroom

### The 11th Century Account

The most famous account dates from the 11th century:

Several lumberjacks from Kyoto got lost in the woods for unknown reasons. Suddenly they encountered four or five Buddhist nuns, whose behavior did not at all conform to expectations: instead of immersing themselves in their inner selves in a quiet quest for Nirvana, the search for Absolute Nothingness, these daughters of Buddha were found dancing and laughing. It turned out that the nuns had also gotten lost in the woods and dealt with their hunger by eating some delicious mushrooms. The faithful nuns soon discovered, however, that they could not stop dancing and laughing. The lumberjacks' stomachs, in the meantime, had also begun to growl and, thinking that what was good for the nuns was good enough for them, the lumberjacks ate some of the mushrooms as well. Soon after, they also succumbed to overwhelming fits of laughter and the urge to dance.

The linguistic result: since that time, the mushrooms have been referred to in Japanese as:

- **"Maitake"** (Dancing Mushrooms) -- and later
- **"Waraitake"** (Laughing Mushrooms)

### The Misidentified Species

For a long time, the species thought responsible were:

1. **Panaeolus papilionaceus** -- A species from Europe and North America that does NOT contain any psychoactive substances
2. **Gymnopilus spectabilis** ("giant laughing mushroom") -- Even Japanese authors have been unable, since 1980, to confirm the existence of psilocybin and its derivatives in this species. Only inactive substances have been found.

## Japan's Mushroom Cultivation Tradition

### 2,000 Years of Cultivation

Japan has a tradition of mushroom cultivation spanning at least 2,000 years. The method involves:

- Collecting naturally grown mycelia of Lentinus edodes (Berk.) Singer (shiitake)
- Transferring onto pieces of wood selected to serve as new substrates
- This technique of transferring colonized wood to fresh substrate is essentially the same principle used in modern [[psilocybin-mushroom-cultivation-basics]]

### Chinese Parallels

Literary sources from China also attest to knowledge about psychoactive mushrooms, where they were said to cause a "disease of dry laughter" -- a temporary condition of uncontrollable mirth.

## Confirmed Japanese Psychoactive Species

### Psilocybe venenata (formerly Stropharia caerulescens/venenata)

First described by S. Imai, who classified it as Stropharia caerulescens in 1932, later renamed Stropharia venenata Imai. It grows on top of wood and dung. Today classified within the genus Psilocybe as a close relative of [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]].

**Important taxonomic correction:** Singer and Smith mistakenly cited intoxication cases involving this species as examples of fatal effects in their 1958 monograph on the Psilocybe genus. For that reason, this species was unjustly branded for decades in the literature as a highly dangerous poisonous mushroom. No fatalities actually occurred.

### The 1929 Intoxication Case

Imai described an event from June 21, 1929: A 43-year-old woman collected about 13 oz. of mushrooms she erroneously thought were honey mushrooms (Armillaria mellea). The following day, she prepared a mushroom meal for her family. As effects became apparent, they sought medical attention.

The woman experienced the most potent effects: stomach pumped, laxatives administered, muscle twitches, hallucinations, and a comatose state. The son, who had eaten only the soup, also experienced hallucinations because the cooking process extracted water-soluble compounds. Both recovered completely.

### Psilocybe subcaerulipes Hongo

A bluing species found in Japan. In 1973, Yokoyama published systematic experimentation results with Psilocybe subcaerulipes. No "urge to dance" was noted in these controlled investigations.

### Psilocybe argentipes Yokoyama

Another bluing Japanese species. Intoxications occurred when three people mistook these mushrooms for honey mushrooms. These cases led to isolation of psilocybin in crystalline form from dried fruiting bodies, confirming the species' psychoactive status.

### Yokoyama's Controlled Experiments (1973)

Systematic experimentation with Psilocybe argentipes yielded detailed protocols:

**Subject J.H. (24-year-old male):** Ingested four cooked mushrooms after a meal, then three more fresh mushrooms 30 minutes later. Experienced regurgitation, profuse sweating, accelerated pulse and breathing, followed by visual hallucinations. Effects largely subsided within hours; by next morning, no side effects.

**Subject M.K. (22-year-old male):** Ate just one fresh mushroom with no effects at all.

**Subject K.Y. (31-year-old male):** Ate five mushrooms. Regurgitation after 30 minutes, sweating, slight paralysis of extremities lasting three hours. Had difficulty writing, depressed mood, hallucinations of colorful lights flooding from the sky. All effects dissipated by the following morning.

Fresh fruiting bodies were bitter, but this taste disappeared after cooking in water.

### Panaeolus subbalteatus

Known to grow on several Japanese islands, though its usage has not been extensively documented.

## The "Urge to Dance" Question

The medieval accounts describe an irresistible urge to dance -- a rather unusual effect of [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] from the modern pharmacological perspective. While psilocybin can cause initial fits of laughter, this phase is generally followed by relaxation and decreased physical activity.

Gartz proposes that "medieval Japanese mentality was a cultural factor that modified the specific expression of an altered state of consciousness." The set and setting of the experience -- lost in the woods, encountering dancing nuns, cultural expectations about mushroom effects -- likely shaped the behavioral expression.

## Knowledge Gaps

Much work remains to be done in Japanese psychoactive mycology:

- The geographic distribution of Japanese Panaeolus species requires further study
- Phytochemistry and taxonomy of many species remain inadequate
- The identification of the original "laughing mushroom" and "dancing mushroom" species has not been definitively resolved
- The connection between Japanese mushroom cultivation traditions and psychoactive species use has not been explored
