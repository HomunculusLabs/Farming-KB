# Lichenized Fungi: Taxonomy and Diversity

Source: Biodiversity of Fungi (Hyde et al., Ch. 9)

Lichenized fungi represent one of the most remarkable symbiotic
associations in nature. Lichenization has evolved independently several
times during fungal history. Of the 46 orders of Ascomycota, 14 include
lichen-forming representatives, and five of those comprise lichenized
species exclusively.

## Growth Forms

Three of five common growth forms (Figure 9.1 from Hyde et al.):

- **Crustose**: Several layers, medullary fungal hyphae attached
  directly to substratum
- **Foliose**: Distinct upper and lower cortex layers, attached by
  rhizines (rootlike fungal hyphae)
- **Fruticose**: Stalked, tufted, or pendulous, uniform outer layer
  (e.g., *Ramalina menziesii* on *Quercus garryana*)

### Basidiomycete Lichens

A few Basidiomycota form lichens, particularly in Agaricales
(*Omphalina*), Cantharellales (*Multiclavula*), and Stereales
(*Dictyonema*).

### Families of Uncertain Position

15 families including Aphanopsidaceae, Baeomycetaceae,
Gomphillaceae, Strigulaceae, Thelenellaceae, Thelocarpaceae,
Umbilicariaceae.

## Taxonomic Principles

Scientific names refer to the fungal partner (mycobiont); photobionts
have independent names. Officially, the lichen association itself has no
name under the International Code of Botanical Nomenclature. When more
than one species of photobiont occurs with the same fungus, the same
fungal name is used for all resulting lichen thalli even if they have
different morphologies.

Orders, families, and genera delimited primarily by characters of
fruiting bodies. Molecular approaches bring significant modifications
to classical morphological and chemical species concepts. Secondary
chemistry used extensively for identification.

## Ecological Roles

- Important winter foods for reindeer, caribou, deer in boreal forest
  and arctic tundra
- Emergency food during hard winters for many large mammalian herbivores
- Nest material for birds and mammals
- Significant proportion of autotroph biomass in desert and tundra
  ecosystems
- Fix significant nitrogen in Pacific Northwest conifer forests
- Buffer heavy metals from atmospheric deposition
- Industrial melanism in peppered moths: classic example of indirect
  lichen impacts on animals

## Photobiont Diversity and Specificity

The photobiont partner in lichens is typically either a green alga or
a cyanobacterium. Green algal photobionts are most commonly members of
the genus *Trebouxia* in temperate lichens, while tropical species more
frequently associate with *Trentepohlia* or other trentepohlioid algae.
Cyanobacterial photobionts, primarily *Nostoc* and *Scytonema*, are
especially common in lichens from humid or nutrient-poor habitats where
nitrogen fixation provides a significant advantage.

Photobiont specificity varies considerably among lichen-forming fungi.
Some species are highly specific, associating with a single photobiont
strain, while others are generalists that can form lichens with
multiple algal partners. This flexibility may be an adaptation to
changing environmental conditions or dispersal to new habitats. The
degree of specificity also affects biogeographic patterns, as
specialist species may be limited by the distribution of their
photobiont partners.

## Lichen Chemistry and Secondary Metabolites

Lichens produce an extraordinary diversity of secondary metabolites,
often called "lichen acids" or "lichen substances," that are
largely absent from non-lichenized fungi. Over 1,000 distinct
compounds have been identified, including usnic acid, atranorin,
gyrophoric acid, and various depsides, depsidones, and dibenzofurans.
These compounds serve multiple ecological functions: they deter
herbivory, protect against UV radiation, inhibit microbial growth on
the thallus surface, and may play roles in metal chelation and
mineral weathering.

Lichen chemistry has proven highly valuable for taxonomic
identification. Many morphologically similar species can be
distinguished by their secondary metabolite profiles using thin-layer
chromatography (TLC) or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).
Standardized TLC methods have become a routine part of lichen
identification in herbaria and field surveys worldwide.
