# Fermentative and Anaerobic Compost Tea Types

## Overview

Dr. Elaine Ingham's compost tea taxonomy distinguishes four distinct production
methods, each with fundamentally different biological outcomes. While Actively
Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) receives the most attention for its broad-spectrum
benefits, understanding the fermentative and anaerobic alternatives is critical
for growers who encounter these products or are tempted to brew them. The key
distinction lies in oxygen availability during the brewing process and the
[[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]] above 6 mg/L
throughout the brew cycle, typically 24-36 hours. This preserves the full
complement of beneficial organisms from the parent compost: aerobic bacteria,
fungi (including hyphal fragments), protozoa, and nematodes. AACT is the most
useful tea for improving plant-growing conditions because it delivers the
complete soil foodweb.

### 2. Fermentative Compost Tea (FCT)
Fermentative teas select for facultative [[bionutrients-lab-lactic-acid-bacteria-serum]] and fermentative yeasts.
While the aerobic organisms are not killed — they enter a dormant state and can
theoretically be resuscitated if conditions return to aerobic — the beneficial
fungi, protozoa, and nematodes are lost or severely diminished.

The fermentative pathway produces organic acids, bacteriocins, and other
metabolites that can inhibit specific pathogenic organisms. However, this is a
narrower tool than AACT. FCT acts more like a targeted antimicrobial rather than
a broad-spectrum biological inoculant. Ingham notes that FCT can be useful but
should not be confused with AACT in terms of expected outcomes.

### 3. Long-Brewing Compost Tea (LBCT)
Long-brewing teas start aerobic but transition through an anaerobic phase before
potentially returning to aerobic conditions by the end of the brewing process.
This multi-phase trajectory produces a complex and unpredictable mixture of
organisms and metabolites. The initial aerobic phase allows some beneficial
organism growth, the anaerobic middle phase produces fermentation products, and
the return to aerobic conditions may allow some recolonization.

The problem with LBCT is inconsistency. The exact trajectory depends on
temperature, starting organism density, nutrient additions, and brewer design.
Two batches brewed identically can yield very different biological profiles.
Ingham cautions that the beneficial communities never return to the full range
of diversity found in properly made AACT, even if the tea appears to recover.

### 4. Not-Aerobic Compost Tea (NACT)
These teas are predominantly anaerobic throughout the brewing process. Anaerobic
bacteria dominate, producing various toxic materials that can suppress the
growth of particular organisms. NACT products include alcohols, organic acids,
hydrogen sulfide, and ammonia — compounds that are biocidal at certain
concentrations.

Ingham is explicit: anaerobic liquids cannot be called compost tea. If the

benefit of the compost is gone. Additionally, anaerobic conditions can support
the growth of human pathogens including Clostridium species, E. coli,
Salmonella, and Shigella.

## Pathogen Risks of Anaerobic Conditions

One of Ingham's most emphatic warnings concerns food safety. When compost tea
goes anaerobic, the protective competitive exclusion provided by aerobic
organisms collapses. facultative and obligate anaerobes proliferate, including
potential human pathogens. This is not theoretical — the conditions that favor
putrefactive bacteria are the same conditions that favor Clostridium botulinum
and other serious pathogens.

The regulatory environment around compost tea reflects these concerns. Ingham
acknowledges the legitimate fear about E. coli and human pathogens while arguing
that properly made aerobic compost tea, from properly made compost, presents
minimal risk. The danger arises when teas are allowed to go anaerobic or when
starting materials (particularly uncomposted manures) contain pathogens.
