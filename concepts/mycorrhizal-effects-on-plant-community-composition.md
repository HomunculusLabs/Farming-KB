# Mycorrhizal Effects on Plant Community Composition

## Overview

Mycorrhizal fungi are increasingly recognized as key drivers of plant
community structure and composition in terrestrial ecosystems. By
differentially affecting the growth, survival, and competitive
ability of plant species, mycorrhizal fungi shape the composition
and diversity of plant communities across a wide range of habitats.

See [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]] for
related bacterial community research.

## Mechanisms of Community-Level Effects

### Differential Growth Promotion

Mycorrhizal colonization enhances growth more for some plant species
than others. Species that are highly mycorrhizal-dependent may
dominate in soils with active mycorrhizal networks, while non-
mycorrhizal species or those with low dependency may be competitively
suppressed. This differential growth response is a primary mechanism
by which mycorrhizae influence community assembly.

### Nutrient-Mediated Competition

By controlling access to soil phosphorus and other limiting nutrients,
mycorrhizal fungi can shift competitive hierarchies among plant
species. The species that most efficiently exploits the mycorrhizal
nutrient pathway gains a competitive advantage. Under phosphorus
limitation, mycorrhizal networks effectively determine which plant
species can thrive.

### Soil Disturbance Effects

Disruption of the external [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]] through soil disturbance
(tillage, erosion, or compaction) preferentially disadvantages
mycorrhizal-dependent species. Evans and Miller (1990) demonstrated
that the external mycelial network is directly responsible for much
of the effect of soil disturbance on vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal
colonization of maize. Agricultural intensification thus shifts
communities toward less mycorrhizal-dependent species.

### Modified Bacterial Communities

Mycorrhizal colonization modifies the bacterial community in the
rhizosphere, creating distinct microbial habitats that can favor
certain plant species over others. The hyphosphere -- the zone of
influence around individual hyphae -- harbors bacterial communities
distinct from bulk soil and the rhizosphere itself.

## Mycorrhizal Dependency and Community Outcomes

Plant species vary enormously in their degree of mycorrhizal
dependency -- the extent to which they require mycorrhizal
colonization for optimal growth. This variation is a primary driver
of mycorrhizal effects on community composition. Highly dependent
species include many tallgrass prairie forbs, temperate forest
trees, and most agricultural crops. Non-mycorrhizal species include
members of the Brassicaceae, Chenopodiaceae, and Cyperaceae
families.

The coexistence of mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal species within
communities suggests that mycorrhizal fungi do not simply eliminate
non-host species. Rather, the balance between mycorrhizal benefits
and costs creates niche differentiation that can maintain plant
diversity. Environmental conditions, particularly soil phosphorus
availability, modulate this balance.

## Succession and Disturbance Dynamics

### Primary Succession

Mycorrhizal fungi play crucial roles in primary succession on
volcanic deposits, glacial moraines, and mine spoils. Early
successional communities are typically dominated by non-mycorrhizal
or weakly mycorrhizal species, as mycorrhizal inoculum is limited in
newly exposed substrates. As fungal communities establish, later
successional stages increasingly include mycorrhizal-dependent
species, driving predictable shifts in plant community composition.

### Secondary Succession After Disturbance

Following disturbance events such as fire, flooding, or agricultural
abandonment, the rate of mycorrhizal recovery influences plant
community trajectories. Sites with intact mycorrhizal networks
recover faster and follow different successional pathways than sites
where the fungal community has been severely disrupted. This has
important implications for [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]], where
mycorrhizal inoculation can accelerate community recovery.

### Fire Effects

Fire can both destroy and stimulate mycorrhizal communities. While
surface fires may kill superficial hyphae, many ectomycorrhizal fungi
survive in deep soil layers or within protected root systems.
Post-fire environments often show enhanced mycorrhizal colonization
as plants benefit from increased nutrient availability from ash.
Prescribed fire regimes can thus be used to maintain mycorrhizal
diversity and the plant communities it supports.

## Ecosystem-Type Variation

### Grasslands

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi dominate [[biodiversity-fungi-grassland-ecosystems]], where
they influence the relative abundance of C3 and C4 grasses, forbs,
and legumes. Mycorrhizal suppression experiments in tallgrass prairie
have demonstrated shifts from mycorrhizal-dependent dominant species
toward non-mycorrhizal subdominants, altering both productivity and
species diversity.

### Forests

[[ectomycorrhizal-associations]] shape temperate and boreal forest
communities by favoring tree species capable of forming these
partnerships (Pinaceae, Fagaceae, Betulaceae). The mycorrhizal
community composition beneath different tree species creates feedback
loops that can maintain or alter forest composition over time.
Arbuscular mycorrhizal trees may be competitively excluded from
stands dominated by ectomycorrhizal species.

### Heathlands and Wetlands

[[comparison-orchid-mycorrhizae-vs-ericoid-mycorrhizae]] are critical in heathland and bog ecosystems,
where they enable plants to access nitrogen from organic sources in
acidic, nutrient-poor soils. The loss of [[singh-ericoid-mycorrhizal-fungi-remediation]]
can lead to heathland degradation and invasion by generalist plant
species. Wetland communities show variable mycorrhizal dependence
depending on water level, with flooded conditions often reducing
mycorrhizal colonization.
