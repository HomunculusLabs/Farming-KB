# Mycorrhizal Effects on Plant Community Composition

Mycorrhizal fungi are increasingly recognized as key drivers of plant community structure and composition in terrestrial ecosystems. By differentially affecting the growth, survival, and competitive ability of plant species, mycorrhizal fungi shape the composition and diversity of plant communities across a wide range of habitats.

## Mechanisms of Community-Level Effects

Mycorrhizal fungi influence plant communities through several interconnected mechanisms:

- **Differential growth promotion**: Mycorrhizal colonization enhances growth more for some plant species than others. Species that are highly mycorrhizal-dependent may dominate in soils with active mycorrhizal networks, while non-mycorrhizal species or those with low dependency may be competitively suppressed.

- **Nutrient-mediated competition**: By controlling access to soil phosphorus and other limiting nutrients, mycorrhizal fungi can shift competitive hierarchies among plant species. The species that most efficiently exploits the mycorrhizal nutrient pathway gains a competitive advantage.

- **Soil disturbance effects**: Disruption of the external mycelial network through soil disturbance (tillage, erosion, or compaction) preferentially disadvantages mycorrhizal-dependent species. Evans and Miller (1990) demonstrated that the external mycelial network is directly responsible for much of the effect of soil disturbance on vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization of maize.

- **Modified bacterial communities**: Mycorrhizal colonization has been shown to modify the bacterial community in the rhizosphere, creating distinct microbial habitats that can favor certain plant species over others.

## Mycorrhizal Dependency and Community Outcomes

Plant species vary enormously in their degree of mycorrhizal dependency -- the extent to which they require mycorrhizal colonization for optimal growth. This variation is a primary driver of mycorrhizal effects on community composition:

- **Highly dependent species**: Many woody plants, legumes, and grasses show strong positive responses to mycorrhizal colonization. These species tend to become more dominant in the presence of active mycorrhizal networks.

- **Non-mycorrhizal species**: Some plant families (e.g., Brassicaceae, Chenopodiaceae, Caryophyllaceae) include species that do not form mycorrhizae or form them rarely. These species may actually gain a competitive advantage when mycorrhizal fungi are absent or suppressed.

- **Facultative mycotrophs**: Many species show intermediate dependency, with mycorrhizal effects varying with soil nutrient levels, light availability, and other environmental factors.

## Soil Management and Mycorrhizal Communities

Agricultural practices significantly alter mycorrhizal communities and thereby influence the plant species composition of both crop and non-crop vegetation:

- **Crop rotation effects**: The choice of previous crop affects arbuscular mycorrhizal formation and growth of succeeding maize (Arihara and Karasawa 2000). Rotation with mycorrhizal crops maintains or builds AM fungal propagule levels, while rotation with non-mycorrhizal crops (such as canola) can deplete them.

- **Tillage effects**: No-till systems generally support higher AM fungal diversity and colonization than conventional tillage, favoring mycorrhizal-dependent crops and weeds while disadvantaging non-mycorrhizal species.

- **Soil sterilization**: Techniques used to create mycorrhizal-free soil for experimental purposes (including autoclaving, gamma irradiation, fumigation, and solarization) dramatically alter soil chemistry and biology, affecting not just mycorrhizal fungi but the entire soil microbial community.

- **Fungicide effects**: Benomyl and other fungicides used in agriculture can reduce mycorrhizal colonization and shift plant community composition, though the side effects vary with application rate and timing.

## Environmental Context and Mycorrhizal Effects

The impact of mycorrhizal fungi on plant communities is strongly context-dependent:

- **Soil fertility**: At high phosphorus levels, mycorrhizal benefits diminish and the competitive advantage shifts toward non-mycorrhizal species. Maluf de Souza and Ferreira (2004) found that plant community composition and mycorrhizal colonization patterns respond to phosphate pollution in field sites.

- **Light availability**: In shaded environments, the carbon cost of supporting mycorrhizal fungi may outweigh the nutrient benefits for some species, modifying the mycorrhizal dependency landscape.

- **Successional stage**: Mycorrhizal effects on community composition change through ecological succession. Early-successional communities often contain more non-mycorrhizal or facultatively mycorrhizal species, while late-successional communities are typically dominated by strongly mycorrhizal species.

## Research Approaches

Studying mycorrhizal effects on plant communities requires carefully designed experiments that account for the complexity of belowground interactions:

- **Inoculum reduction techniques**: Creating non-mycorrhizal controls is challenging because soil sterilization eliminates many other soil organisms. Kahiluoto et al. (2000) developed approaches using different methods to create non-mycorrhizal controls for bioassays of AM effectiveness.

- **Community-level measurements**: Techniques like the gridline intersect method for quantifying hyphal networks, combined with plant community surveys, help link belowground fungal structure to aboveground community patterns.

- **Field vs. controlled conditions**: Many mycorrhizal community dynamics observed in the field cannot be replicated in simplified laboratory systems, highlighting the need for field-based research.

## Successional Dynamics

The influence of mycorrhizal fungi on plant community composition changes through ecological succession. Early-successional communities, characterized by disturbed soils and high light availability, often contain species with low or variable mycorrhizal dependency. As succession proceeds and soils develop, mycorrhizal networks become more extensive and mycorrhizal-dependent species increasingly dominate.

This successional shift in mycorrhizal influence means that the same mycorrhizal manipulation (such as soil disturbance or inoculation) can have very different effects depending on the successional stage of the community. Understanding these dynamics is important for ecological restoration, where the goal is often to accelerate the transition from early-successional weed communities to late-successional mycorrhizal plant communities.

## Invasive Species and Mycorrhizal Disruption

Invasive plant species can disrupt native mycorrhizal communities and thereby alter plant community composition. Some invasive species are non-mycorrhizal and reduce mycorrhizal inoculum in the soil, disadvantaging native mycorrhizal species. Others form mycorrhizae with different fungal partners than native species, shifting the mycorrhizal fungal community composition. Still others may exploit existing mycorrhizal networks to parasitize native plants. These mycorrhizal-mediated mechanisms of invasion represent an important but often overlooked dimension of biological invasion ecology.

## Mycorrhizal-Mediated Succession

Mycorrhizal fungi can actively drive successional transitions by differentially promoting the growth of late-successional species over early-successional weedy species. In abandoned agricultural fields, the establishment of mycorrhizal networks can accelerate the replacement of weedy annuals by perennial grasses and eventually woody species. This mycorrhizal facilitation of succession is particularly important in nitrogen-limited systems where mycorrhizal nitrogen uptake provides a decisive competitive advantage to mycorrhizal-dependent species. Manipulating mycorrhizal communities through inoculation or soil management may therefore be a valuable tool in ecological restoration.

## Restoration Implications

The ability of mycorrhizal fungi to shape plant communities has direct applications in ecological restoration. Restoring degraded lands often requires rebuilding mycorrhizal networks that have been lost through soil disturbance, compaction, or long-term agricultural use. Inoculation with native [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] can accelerate the re-establishment of mycorrhizal-dependent native plant species and suppress invasive weeds that thrive in mycorrhiza-depleted soils. The choice of inoculant strains should consider both the target plant community and the local soil conditions, as [[mycorrhizal-specificity-and-host-range]] can significantly influence restoration outcomes.

## Climate Change Considerations

Global climate change may alter mycorrhizal effects on plant community composition through multiple pathways. Elevated atmospheric CO2 can increase the carbon available for allocation to mycorrhizal fungi, potentially strengthening mycorrhizal-mediated competitive effects. Changes in precipitation patterns and temperature regimes may shift the balance between mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal species in plant communities. Understanding these climate-mediated changes in mycorrhizal function is essential for predicting how plant communities will respond to ongoing environmental change.
