## Overview

[[soil-food-web]] is an interconnected system of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and other organisms that together maintain soil health, suppress disease, cycle nutrients, and build soil structure. Understanding the role of each organism group—and critically, how they function together as a whole—is essential for [[phosphate-accumulators-sustainable-agriculture]] and [[compost]] tea applications.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi: Terminology and Application

### Correct Terminology

The terminology surrounding [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] is often misused:

- **Mycorrhizal fungus** refers to the fungus itself, when not in association with a plant root.
- **A mycorrhiza** is a single association between one root and one mycorrhizal fungus.
- **Mycorrhizae** (plural of mycorrhiza, meaning "fungus-root") refers to all the associations of roots and fungi collectively.

### Application Methods

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are applied through spores, and the method of application depends heavily on the plant species, the growing system, and soil conditions. The critical factor is getting spores physically close to the root zone without harming either the roots or the spores.

**Successful conditions:** In some soils and situations, applying spores to the soil surface works perfectly well—colonization is rapid and extensive.

**Challenging conditions:** In other soils, surface application will never get spores close enough to roots for colonization to establish. These situations require more targeted placement strategies.

### Best Practices for Establishment

1. Ensure spores reach the root zone through appropriate placement methods.
2. Do not harm roots or kill spores during application.
3. Provide fungal food sources near the root zone to support establishment and growth.

## The Whole Food Web Concept

### Why Completeness Matters

The entire food web is necessary to prevent both easy-to-prevent and harder-to-prevent diseases. Bacteria alone can account for only a limited set of the preventative mechanisms that operate in soil. The remaining protective functions require protozoa, fungi, nematodes, and other organisms.

### The "Stalling" Effect

When only a subset of organisms is added back into degraded soil, initial improvement is typically observed. However, this improvement eventually **stalls** because the full food web has not been restored. To reach a point where no pesticides or minimal inorganic fertilizer is required, the entire food web must be rebuilt.

### Compost Tea Requirements

A compost tea that delivers the whole food web—not just bacteria—is essential. While a bacterial-only tea is not necessarily "bad," it will lack the complete organism complement needed for comprehensive disease prevention. When failures with compost tea are reported, they almost always trace back to not extracting the full set of organisms.

### Practical Guidance

- Identify the specific disease you are targeting.
- Determine which organism groups (bacteria, fungi, protozoa, or nematodes) are needed to combat it.
- Ensure those organisms reach leaf surfaces and establish in soil.
- Work on improving the tea machine, compost quality, food resources, and aeration to achieve complete organism extraction.

## Fungal Biomass Minimums for Disease Prevention

### The 2 Microgram Threshold

Based on extensive observations of compost tea effectiveness against foliar diseases (primarily mildews and blights), a minimum fungal biomass of **2 micrograms per milliliter** has been established. Above this threshold, disease prevention consistently works; below it, problems frequently occur.

### Higher Levels Are Better

More fungal biomass translates to greater protective capacity. Exceptionally rich sources like Highlands Soil and Water have been measured at approximately **800 micrograms per milliliter** of fungal biomass. At these concentrations, application rates can be reduced from 5 gallons per acre to approximately 2 gallons per acre while still achieving excellent [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]].

## Organism Dormancy and Preservation

### Inducing Dormancy

Putting tea organisms into a dormant (sleep) state is possible but comes with significant trade-offs. Methods typically involve manipulating osmotic factors or pH. However, approximately **50% of species** are lost in the process—specifically those organisms that either lack dormant stages entirely or require extended time to enter dormancy.

### Implications for Tea Storage

This 50% species loss means that stored or dormant teas will always have reduced diversity compared to freshly brewed applications. For maximum benefit, fresh application is preferred whenever possible.

## Microbial Diversity in Compost

### Why Diversity Matters

Diversity is critically important for soil and compost health. Using multiple compost sources with greater organism diversity produces better results. However, even a single good compost contains a huge diversity of organisms, so it is not equivalent to a biological monoculture.

### Assessing Diversity

[[soil-food-web]] Inc. (SFI) testing examines the morphology (shape and appearance) of bacteria and fungi to assess whether the community is balanced or skewed. This morphotyping approach differentiates many more species than traditional plate count methods.

### DNA Testing and Species Quantification

Molecular DNA assays represent the cutting edge of [[ingham-soil-food-web-diversity-assessment-dna-molecular-methods]]:

- **DNA testing** measures the number of unique DNA strands present in a sample.
- **Approximately 25,000 unique DNA strands per gram** have been found in good, healthy soil and compost (accounting for minor adjustments and plasmid DNA).
- The species present in compost closely mirror those in soil, since most compost ingredients (leaves, tree debris, grass clippings) were in contact with soil.
- Indoor composting using grocery store vegetables without environmental inputs produces a "bizarre" organism profile that diverges from natural soil communities.

### Controversies in DNA-Based Species Identification

There remains scientific debate about how many base-pair differences are necessary to define a separate species. Some plant pathogens differ from their non-pathogenic relatives by only a single base pair. Additionally, multiple copies of certain genome regions complicate counting. Despite these uncertainties, DNA methods are advancing rapidly and will eventually become the standard approach.

## Glomalin: The Sticky Substance That Binds Soil

### What Is Glomalin?

Glomalin is a general term for compounds produced by soil organisms that act as biological glues, binding soil particles together. While initially associated with vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi, glomalin-like substances are produced by many different organisms, including bacteria.

### Historical Context

Work by R.C. Foster in Australia (documented in "The Ultrastructure of the Rhizosphere") documented these sticky substances long before the term "glomalin" was coined. Bacteria produce their own glues to attach themselves to soil particles and surfaces.

### Significance

Glomalin represents the "tip of the iceberg" of biological soil-binding compounds. The recognition that living organisms produce the sticky materials responsible for soil aggregation is a major advance in understanding soil structure formation. Questions remain about whether fungal and bacterial glues share the same pH characteristics and chemical properties.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The whole food web** is required for comprehensive disease suppression—bacteria alone are insufficient.
2. **[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]]** depends on getting spores to the root zone with adequate fungal food.
3. **Fungal biomass above 2 μg/mL** is the minimum for reliable disease prevention.
4. **Dormancy induction** sacrifices approximately half of microbial species diversity.
5. **DNA testing** reveals roughly 25,000 unique species per gram of healthy soil/compost.
6. **Glomalin** and similar biological glues are produced by diverse organisms and are critical for soil structure.
