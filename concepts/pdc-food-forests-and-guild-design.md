## Forests as the Ultimate Model

Forests are dense, three-dimensional environments teeming with life and are perhaps the pinnacle achievement of the Earth's ecology. They recycle 100% of their resources, are solar powered, thermodynamically efficient, multi-layered, and produce food at rates per acre far greater than any human agricultural system. If Permaculture systems are modeled after nature, the forest is the greatest model we have.

## Forest Layers

Every forest follows the same basic seven-layer structure:

1. **Canopy** (overstory) -- large dominant trees in full sun (oaks, hickories, maples)
2. **Understory** -- shorter trees tolerant of shade
3. **Shrub layer** -- bushes and shrubs
4. **Herbaceous layer** -- non-woody plants, ferns, grasses
5. **Ground cover** -- low-growing plants protecting soil
6. **Root layer** -- plants producing below ground (carrots, potatoes)
7. **Vine layer** -- climbing plants growing through all layers (grapes, hops)

## Food Forests

Food forests are gardens modeled after the three-dimensional aspect of forests. They take advantage of the vertical dimension and combine many [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]] or polyculture.

### Principles of Guild Design

Guilds demonstrate several Permaculture principles simultaneously:
- **Integrate don't segregate**
- **Value diversity**
- **Stacking functions**

Designing guilds is more art than science. Factors include:

- **Physical/architectural** -- tall plants shade shorter ones; corn stalks serve as trellises for beans; leafy plants retain soil moisture
- **Biochemical** -- nitrogen-fixing plants convert atmospheric nitrogen into nutrients for other plants
- **Ecological** -- one plant attracts beneficial insects for another, or repels pests that prey on a guild member
- **Systemic** -- guilds may include animals, [[graywater-systems]], shade screens, windbreaks, micro-climates

### The Design Process for Guilds

1. Think of the **function** you want first, not the plant
2. Research plants indigenous to your area that perform that function
3. Look at all candidate plants to determine if they work together in guilds
4. Research potential guild partners to support your primary plant
5. Analyze micro-climate effects created by the plants
6. As a rule of thumb: for every function, find **three different species** and create guilds to support them

### Plant Categories

- **Native plants** -- most prized; perform the desired function and grow naturally in the area
- **Pioneer species** -- small, hardy perennials with rapid propagation; prepare barren areas for secondary succession
- **Invasive species** (weeds) -- high propagation rates that destabilize existing ecologies by out-competing
- **Niche species** -- complex plants thriving within narrow micro-climates
- **Appropriate species** -- not native but grow well locally without destabilizing the ecology

### Guilds Beyond Plants

Guilds are whole systems designs. They may include animals (chickens for pest control, bees for pollination), greywater systems, shade screens, windbreaks, and micro-climates. The only limit is the imagination and knowledge of the designer.
