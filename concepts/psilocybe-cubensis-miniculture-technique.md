# Psilocybe cubensis Miniculture Technique

The "miniculture" method developed by Bigwood and Beug (1982) is a scaled-down [[miniculture-rye-grain-cultivation-method-for-psilocybe-cubensis]] that uses small jars rather than large trays or monotubs. Originally designed for controlled research on alkaloid variation across flushes, this approach offers advantages for experimental reproducibility, space efficiency, and [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] levels across successive flushes. The method was based on:

- The rye-grain medium originally described by San Antonio (1971) for commercial mushroom cultivation.
- Refinements by Oss and Oeric (1976) for [[psilocybe-cubensis-cultivation]].
- Further adaptation by Bigwood and Beug to a "miniculture" scale using half-pint jars.

The primary goal was experimental control: each miniculture jar represents an independent biological replicate, allowing systematic comparison of alkaloid levels across flushes under identical conditions.

## Equipment and Materials

The miniculture setup requires minimal equipment:

### Jars and Substrate

- **Jars**: Wide-mouth half-pint (~250 ml) canning jars.
- **Grain**: Rye grain (10 g per jar).
- **Water**: 15 ml deionized water per jar.
- **Casing layer**: 2 parts peat : 1 part calcium carbonate : 2 parts perlite and/or vermiculite, applied ~2 cm deep.

### Sterilization and Inoculation

- **Autoclave or pressure cooker**: For sterilizing jars at 15 PSI (121°C) for 45–60 minutes.
- **Agar cultures**: Mycelium culture maintained on various agar media for stock and inoculation.
- **Inoculation tools**: Sterile syringe or inoculation loop for transferring mycelium from agar to grain.

### Maintenance

- **Sterile water**: 1 ml sterile water per jar, applied via syringe every two days for watering.
- **Shaking schedule**: Jars shaken every 4 days during colonization to distribute mycelium.

## Step-by-Step Procedure

### Phase 1: Preparation (Day 0)

1. Place 10 g of rye grain in each wide-mouth half-pint jar.
2. Add 15 ml of deionized water to each jar.
3. Close jars with lids fitted with filtered ventilation holes (or loosen lids and cover with foil).
4. Autoclave at 15 PSI for 45–60 minutes to sterilize.
5. Allow jars to cool to room temperature in a clean environment.

### Phase 2: Inoculation and Colonization (Weeks 1–4)

1. Under sterile conditions ([[laminar-flow-hood]] or still air box), transfer mycelium from agar culture to the sterilized grain.
2. Close jars and incubate at room temperature (24–27°C).
3. Every 4 days for 28 days, gently shake each jar to distribute the growing mycelium evenly across the grain surface.
4. After 28 days, the mycelium should have fully colonized the grain, appearing as a dense white mat throughout the jar.

### Phase 3: Casing and Fruiting Initiation (Weeks 4–6)

1. Open jars (aseptic conditions are no longer necessary after full colonization).
2. Apply a ~2 cm deep casing layer of peat:calcium carbonate:perlite/vermiculite mixture.
3. Place jars under cool white fluorescent light for fruiting initiation.
4. Water each jar with 1 ml of sterile water via syringe every two days.
5. The first flush (fruiting) typically appears 4–5 weeks after initial inoculation, or approximately 2 weeks after casing.

### Phase 4: Harvest and Continued Production (Weeks 6–20+)

1. Harvest sporocarps as soon as they are mature (pileus opening, gills exposed).
2. Immediately freeze-dry harvested mushrooms and store sealed at −5°C for analysis.
3. Continue watering every two days.
4. Additional flushes will appear every 1–3 weeks.
5. Minicultures can continue producing for at least 20 weeks if uncontaminated.

## Yields and Performance

Bigwood and Beug reported the following performance characteristics:

- **Average yield**: 2.7 g dry weight per miniculture jar over its productive lifetime.
- **Number of flushes**: Typically 4–5 flushes before the mycelium stops fruiting. One culture produced a sixth flush.
- **Productive period**: At least 20 weeks of continuous production from a single jar.
- **Flush timing**: Flushes occur at irregular intervals, typically every 1–3 weeks depending on conditions.
