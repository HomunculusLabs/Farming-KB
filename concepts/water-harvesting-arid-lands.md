# Water Harvesting in Arid Lands

## Overview

Water is the dominant theme for designers in arid landscapes. The quantity of fresh water (less than 700 ppm salt) is the final arbiter of successful settlement and sustainable agriculture. To obtain fresh water, it must be intercepted before it washes salt from soils, mixes with deeper saline groundwaters, or runs off roof areas and rocks. This concept covers the full spectrum of water harvesting techniques from domestic tank systems to broadscale landscape interventions.

## Core Aims of Water Harvesting

The aims of water catchment in arid landscapes can be summarised as four key objectives:

1. **Store fresh water** for cooking and drinking
2. **Divert sheet flow and waste water** to gardens
3. **Infiltrate water into soils** and produce plant growth on-site
4. **Give rainwater run-off time to soak** into the landscape where people live

## Rainwater Conservation

Two basic strategies apply to rainwater conservation:

### Domestic Tank Systems

Fitting tanks, cisterns, or sealed wells to capture house roof water. Every family roof should catch its own drinking water in tanks of 20,000–50,000 litres (5,000–12,000 gallons), depending on family size and regularity of general supply.

**Key yield figures:**
- Only 1 cm of rain produces 1,000 litres from a 100 m² roof
- 100,000 litres per hectare of sealed run-off surface

**[[water-conservation]] inside and outside the home:**
- Plant shrubberies instead of lawns, use sub-surface trickle irrigation, mulch, and shade to save ~50% of total water use
- Feed hand-basin and shower water to low-flush cisterns to save 40% of the remaining 50%
- Divert bathwater to pre-soak troughs for clothes
- Direct laundry water to gardens
- Install water-saving shower heads and timer taps to save 14–20%
- Divert kitchen sink water to gardens

### Public Cistern Systems

Provision of large public sealed and roofed reservoirs to hold water run-off from public buildings, paved areas, and roads. Many dryland settlements in Australia have public cisterns for domestic or agricultural use depending on run-off quality.

## Swales

A [[swale]] is a dead-level hollow built on contour (although the base need not be level) which creates temporary pools. Swales are ripped, gravelled, sanded, or planted at the base to assist rapid water infiltration.

### Design Specifications

Swales must be large enough to take:
- All tank or storage overflow
- Pavement run-off
- Additional harvested and diverted overland water flow from surrounding areas

They may need a capacity of 200% of village run-off, which then infiltrates locally as soil water storage to 3–5 m depth.

### Benefits of Swales

- **Cost reduction**: Eliminate stormwater drains, gutters, and kerbing
- **Tree establishment**: Grow trees needed to shade pavements, courtyards, parking, and market areas
- **Material production**: Provide dirt spoil to raise road-beds, sand for construction, and topsoil, loam, and clay for gardens
- **Rapid infiltration**: Many swales infiltrate water in 2–48 hours, providing ample tree irrigation after early establishment rains

### Swale Types

- **Roadside swales**: Provide tree water and shade after 2–3 rains
- **Limonias**: "Dry dams" in complex foothill sites where walls hold 1–1.5 m of water, overflowing via rock-based spillways; forests, citrus, olives, and palms establish in the base
- **Domestic swales**: Small stone-filled hollows or channels with vertical concrete edges, unsealed below for infiltration, providing shaded soil-water sources for vine trellises
- **Deep sand swales**: Long plastic-lined and sand-filled swales from which water can be pumped, with plants grown on or beside them

### Yeomans' Shallow Swales

At a scale larger than standard swales, 1–2 m banks thrown up by repeated side-casts back up water for miles, especially if sited to run from hill to hill in a pass between low ranges. Water can be left to soak in, or spilled under slide-gate control into smaller walled fields for crops of sunflower, millet, or beans. Trees and shrubs establish well on these bunds.

**Warning**: Such systems suit fairly permeable soils but can develop salt flats in areas with clay soils and salty surface water.

## Tank Design and Construction

### Tank Types and Materials

Tanks can be constructed from:
- Concrete (poured in central vertical sliding forms)
- Brick-lined and domed (rendered in sandy soils)
- "Stack sack" (plastic tubes of 1:9 cement:sand, pricked and soaked in water) in plastic-lined holes
- Cement plaster on wire-netting base
- Excavated in rock
- Galvanised steel, fibreglass, plastic, or plastic resins
- Earth-sheltered, partly or fully buried, covered with vines, or in shaded courtyards

### Water Quality in Tanks

Common myths about tank water are addressed:

1. **"It's stagnant"** — Untrue; tank water tastes fresh for years and remains clear and clean
2. **"It breeds mosquitoes"** — Not true of covered tanks; in open tanks, mosquito-eating fish can be stocked
3. **"It contains roof dust"** — True, but this settles as biological sediment and keeps water clear

**Water quality improvement**: Place limestone, [[dolomite]], or marble gravel (or mollusc shells) in a bag in every tank to offset acidity, fix metallic salts, and "harden" the water. Drinking alkaline or neutral water is associated with fewer heart attacks and less dietary metal intake.

### Siting Considerations

- Uphil from houses for gravity feed
- Large ground tanks can form foundations of barns or garages
- Space between tanks serves as cool storage for root and fruit crops
- On flat plains, tanks are raised on stands with windmill or solar pump lift

## Water Harvesting on Open Sites

### Catchment Surfaces

Wherever good clays are found, earth tanks can be made in the ground. Essentials for open-site cisterns:
- Tanks should be deep and narrow
- They should intercept a run-off area
- Ideally roofed to prevent evaporation

### Sealed Run-off Surfaces

A variety of deliberately sealed or compacted run-off surfaces have been constructed:
- Bitumen-sealed and fenced areas
- Roaded catchments of 300 m × 10 m (rounded and graded, side slopes at 1:20, drains at 1:100–1:200)
- Earth stabilised by gums, light cement mix, or well-compacted clay
- Sheet metal roofing areas up to 600 m² feeding 100,000 litre tanks
- Natural bare rock slabs with gutters (rock-cut or cemented)

### Clay Catchment Systems

In clay soils, square or circular spread-banks slope to a central cistern. Topsoil is first removed and excavated central material rolled down to create water run-off areas. Ramp access, base drains, or pumps provide water access.

## Oasis Creation

By instituting a system of cisterns, tanks, and swales, any desert settlement (wherever average rainfall exceeds 30 cm) can create for itself a reserve of soil and roof water that is, in effect, an oasis. The proportion of water supplied to swales by local run-off versus widespread interceptor banks changes as rainfall decreases. Below 30 cm annual rainfall, more extensive harvests of landscape run-off must be made and directed to village swales to support large trees.

## Water Strategy Summary by Site Type

The following landscape positions each require tailored water harvesting approaches:

| Site | Appropriate Strategies |
|------|----------------------|
| High hard plateau | Rock catchment, cisterns, contour banks |
| Stony country rills | Rill diversion, windrow capture |
| Shaded rocky valleys | Dam sites, tank storage |
| Upper valley systems | Swales, check dams, diversion drains |
| Wadis and wider valleys | Gabion weirs, flood spreading |
| Flood-outs | Spreader banks, silt traps |
| Low foothill slopes (4–8°) | Contour swales, tree planting |
| Plains | Circle swales, Yeomans banks |
| Flood plains | Overflow channels, raised fields |
| Dunes | Inter-dune swales, fog catchment |
| Pans | Pan-bottom cultivation, salt-tolerant species |
| Large valleys | Multiple storage, reticulation |
| Steep slopes (8–15°) | Terracing, diversion channels |
| Towns and settlements | Roof tanks, road swales, public cisterns |
