## Soil Capillary Action and Water Movement

Capillary action is one of the most important yet least understood physical
phenomena in gardening. It governs how water moves through soil, how seeds
germinate, and how deeply plant roots can draw moisture without irrigation.
[[steve-solomon]] emphasizes that understanding capillarity is essential for
successful [[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]] and efficient water use.

## What Is Capillary Action?

Capillary action (capillarity) is the movement of water through narrow spaces
against the force of gravity. In soil, this occurs through the tiny pores
between soil particles. Water molecules are attracted to soil particle surfaces
(adhesion) and to each other (cohesion), creating a combined force that pulls
water upward and laterally through the soil matrix.

The smaller the soil pores, the stronger the capillary force. Clay soils with
very fine particles have extremely strong capillary action but slow overall
water movement. Sandy soils with large particles have weak capillary action but
drain quickly. Loam soils strike a balance between the two extremes.

## Observing Capillarity in the Garden

Solomon provides a practical demonstration of capillarity. After tilling soil,
walk across it and leave footprints. Immediately after tilling, all the soil
appears uniformly damp. After the sun dries the surface, the footprint areas
remain damp longer because foot pressure compressed soil particles together
firmly enough to allow moisture to rise from below via capillary action.

This simple observation reveals a critical gardening truth: soil structure
directly determines how water moves through your garden. Compacted soil has
different capillary properties than loose soil.

## How Deep Does Capillary Water Rise?

The height to which capillary water can rise depends on soil [[vermiculite-particle-size-water-retention-calibration-pf-tek]]:

- **Coarse sand**: Water rises only 10-30 cm (4-12 inches)
- **Fine sand**: Water rises 30-60 cm (12-24 inches)
- **Silt loam**: Water rises 60-120 cm (24-48 inches)
- **Clay**: Water can rise 150-300+ cm (5-10+ feet)

However, the rate of rise matters as much as the ultimate height. Clay may
eventually pull water very high, but it does so extremely slowly. Coarser
soils move water faster but cannot lift it as high.

## Capillarity and Seed Germination

Seeds germinate in the capillary fringe — the zone where water is held by
capillary forces above the water table or above recently irrigated/fallen
moisture. The seedbed must have fine enough particles to create capillary
contact with the seed while maintaining enough air spaces for oxygen to reach
the germinating embryo.

This is why gardeners create fine seedbeds by raking soil to a crumb-like
texture. The fine crumbs maximize surface area for capillary contact while
preserving enough porosity for [[mushroom-cultivation-jar-lid-management-loose-fit-vacuum-gas-exchange]]. Solomon notes that overly fine,
powdery soil can actually hinder germination by creating a surface crust when
it rains, which blocks both water infiltration and gas exchange.

## Breaking Capillary Rise to Conserve Moisture

In dry gardening, understanding capillarity is essential for water
conservation. When soil surface is open and capillary channels connect the
surface to deeper moisture, water continuously evaporates upward through
capillary action. This is called capillary rise to the surface.

To interrupt this upward moisture loss:

1. **Cultivation**: Lightly stirring the top 2-5 cm of soil breaks capillary
   channels between the surface and deeper moist soil, dramatically reducing
   evaporation. This is the primary reason for frequent shallow cultivation
   in dryland farming.

2. **Mulching**: Any material placed on the soil surface slows evaporation
   directly, but also reduces the temperature gradient that drives capillary
   movement.

3. **Surface compaction**: Counterintuitively, a slightly compacted surface
   can sometimes reduce overall moisture loss by creating a thin dry crust
   that acts as its own mulch. However, this can go too far and create an
   impervious barrier.

## Capillarity and Root Growth

Plant roots follow moisture through capillary channels. When soil is evenly
moist throughout, roots distribute broadly and shallowly. When surface soil
dries but deeper soil remains moist (via capillary rise from below), roots
grow downward seeking that moisture.

Solomon argues that this downward root-seeking behavior is exactly what
gardeners want for drought resilience. By watering less frequently but more
deeply, or by relying on natural capillary rise from subsoil moisture,
gardeners encourage deep root systems that can withstand dry periods.

## The Plow Pan Problem

Rototilling creates a specific capillary problem. The tines loosen soil to
a certain depth, then compress the soil at the bottom of their reach into
a hard "plow pan." This compressed layer has very different capillary
properties than the soil above and below it:

- It may block downward water penetration
- It creates a perched water table above the pan
- It physically barriers root growth
- It creates [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] just above the pan

Solomon recommends using a spade or digging fork to break through this
compacted layer, rather than relying solely on rototilling which often
makes the problem worse over time.

## Practical Applications for Gardeners

1. **Test your soil's capillary rise**: Dig a hole 30 cm deep, fill it with
   water, and observe how quickly the water disappears. Fast drainage means
   weak capillarity (sandy soil); slow drainage means strong capillarity
   (clay soil).

2. **Time your cultivation**: Cultivate after rain or irrigation when soil
   is just dry enough to work but still moist enough to break into crumbs,
   not clods. This maximizes the capillary-breaking effect.

3. **Space plants appropriately**: Wider spacing means each plant can access
   a larger soil volume and deeper capillary moisture. Solomon's wide-spacing
   philosophy relies directly on capillary principles.

4. **Water deeply and infrequently**: This encourages capillary channels to
   develop vertically rather than horizontally, promoting deeper root growth.
