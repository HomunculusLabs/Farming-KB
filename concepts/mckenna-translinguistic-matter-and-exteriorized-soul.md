## Translinguistic Matter and the Exteriorized Soul — McKenna

## Overview

In his 1983 Esalen lecture, [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]], proposes that human
consciousness will eventually transcend biological embodiment through a
critical transformation at what McKenna called a "psychedelic Bar
Mitzvah."

The idea integrates elements of information theory, eschatology, and
psychedelic phenomenology into a singular vision of humanity"s ultimate
trajectory.

## The Nature of Translinguistic Matter

McKenna used the term "translinguistic matter" to describe a substance or
medium that lies beyond ordinary language but carries meaning. In the DMT
state, he reported encountering objects that are "feeling-toned, meaning-
toned, three-dimensional rotating complexes of transforming light and
color" — [[dmt-black-hole-effect-and-ineffability-of-tryptamine-experience]] suggests that human beings possess a latent
ability to perceive and generate meaning directly in three-dimensional
space, without the intermediary of spoken or written language.

## The Exteriorized Soul

McKenna"s vision of the "exteriorized soul" describes a future state in
which consciousness separates from the biological body.

He framed this as "an effort to exteriorize the soul and internalize the
body."

In this transformation, the soul would exist as a "superconducting lens of
translinguistic matter generated out of the body of each of us at a
critical juncture."

After this transition, the body image would exist as a "holographic wave
transform" while consciousness exists "at play in the fields of the Lord
and living in Elysium."

The metaphor draws on the holographic principle and the idea that
information, not matter, is the fundamental substrate of reality.

## The Psychedelic Bar Mitzvah

McKenna described the critical transition point as a "psychedelic Bar
Mitzvah" — a rite of passage into a new mode of being.

This is the moment when the exteriorization of the soul occurs, when
consciousness achieves the critical density or complexity needed to
transcend its biological matrix.

The Bar Mitzvah metaphor is apt: it marks a coming-of-age, an
initiation into mature capability.

McKenna linked this transition to his broader eschatological framework,
in which all of human history is a "shockwave" converging toward a
culminating moment of transformation.
