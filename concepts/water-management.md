# Water Management -- Swales, Keyline, and Rainwater Harvesting

Water is the most critical resource on a homestead. Permaculture water management aims to slow water down, spread it across the landscape, and sink it into the soil. The principle: "Make water walk, not run." Every drop that leaves your property is a missed opportunity.

## The Fivefold Water Strategy

From Gaia's Garden, effective water management combines five approaches:

1. **Rich soil** (high organic matter) -- stores water like a sponge. Every 1% increase in organic matter allows soil to hold roughly 20,000 more gallons of water per acre
2. **Contouring and swales** -- catch and redirect runoff
3. **The right plants** -- natives, Mediterranean-climate plants, and deep-rooted species that access groundwater
4. **Dense plantings** -- shade soil, reducing evaporation by over 60%
5. **Mulch** -- 2-4 inches of organic mulch dramatically reduces evaporation and moderates soil temperature

## Swales

A swale is a level trench dug on contour (the same elevation across the landscape) with a berm on the downhill side. Water collects in the swale during rain and slowly infiltrates into the soil.

### How Swales Work
- Rainwater runs downhill and fills the swale
- Water soaks into the soil over hours to days instead of running off
- The berm prevents overflow from eroding downhill
- Plantings on the berm and swale access this stored groundwater
- Below the swale, soil stays moist far longer than above it

### Designing Swales
1. Find contour lines using an A-frame level, laser level, or water level
2. Dig the swale on contour -- level across its entire length
3. Width: 1-3 feet wide, depth: 6-18 inches (scale to your property)
4. Pile excavated soil on the downhill side to form the berm
5. Plant the berm with trees, shrubs, and perennials
6. Plant the swale bottom with moisture-loving plants or fill with straw/gravel

### Types of Swales
- **Standard swale**: Trench + berm, full size
- **Straw swale**: Level trench stuffed with straw, topped with thin soil layer. Invisible once planted. Good for formal areas
- **Fishscale swales**: When obstacles prevent long swales, use short overlapping swales above and below obstacles

### When NOT to Use Swales
- On very flat land with no slope, water does not flow -- use rain gardens instead
- In areas with seasonal high water tables (swales can become bogs)
- On steep, unstable slopes (risk of landslides)
- Where bedrock is very close to surface

## Keyline Design

Keyline design, developed by P.A. Yeomans in Australia, manages water on broadacre land by identifying the "keypoint" -- the point in a valley where the slope changes from convex (steep) to concave (flattening). Plowing or subsoiling along the keyline contour from this point outward distributes water across the entire landscape.

For a small homestead, keyline principles simplify to:
- Identify where water naturally concentrates on your property
- Use shallow ripping/subsoiling along contour to break compaction and allow water infiltration
- Plant trees along these contour lines to stabilize and utilize the water channel
- Grade roads and paths to direct runoff into planting areas, not away from them

## Rainwater Harvesting

### Roof Catchment
Every roof is a water collection system. Calculate catchment:
- 1 inch of rain on 1,000 sq ft of roof = approximately 623 gallons
- A typical 2,000 sq ft house roof catches ~25,000 gallons/year in a 40-inch rainfall region

**Simple system**: Place 55-gallon drums at each downspout. Connect overflow from the first barrel to the second barrel. Use for garden irrigation.

**Larger system**: 1,000-5,000 gallon cistern or tank. Gravity-feed to irrigation. Can provide 2+ weeks of garden water during drought.

**Tips**:
- First flush diverters discard the first few gallons (contain roof debris)
- Screen inlets to prevent mosquitoes
- In cold climates, drain systems before freezing or insulate tanks
- Use overflow to fill swales or rain gardens

### Earthworks for Water
- **Ponds**: Store water at the highest practical elevation for gravity-fed irrigation. Sepp Holzer uses ponds extensively for microclimate creation and aquaculture
- **Rain gardens**: Shallow depressions planted with water-tolerant species in low spots
- **French drains**: Gravel-filled trenches to redirect excess water
- **Berm-and-basin**: Small berms create basins that catch runoff around individual trees

## Greywater Systems

Household wastewater from sinks, showers, and laundry can irrigate gardens:
- **Simple**: Drain washing machine hose to mulched swale or tree basin
- **Intermediate**: Drum system to temporarily hold and distribute greywater
- **Advanced**: Constructed wetland (gravel-filled marsh with bog plants) that purifies greywater before it reaches a pond or irrigation area

From Gaia's Garden: A family producing 100 gallons of greywater daily needs a wetland roughly 10x12 feet. Essential wetland plants: cattail, bulrush, reed canary grass, canna lily.

**Greywater rules**:
- Never store greywater more than 1-2 days (bacteria multiply)
- Only use biodegradable, low-phosphate soaps
- Do not use on root vegetables or leafy greens eaten raw
- Divert to sewer/septic in winter in freezing climates
- Check local codes before installing

## Drought-Proofing Strategies

1. Build soil organic matter (1% increase = 20,000 gal/acre more water holding capacity)
2. Install swales on any slope
3. Mulch everything (2-4 inches minimum)
4. Plant densely to shade soil
5. Harvest rainwater from every roof
6. Use greywater for irrigation
7. Choose Mediterranean-climate and drought-adapted plants (see [[permaculture-useful-plants-reference]])
8. Match plants to microclimates (thirsty plants in wet spots, drought plants on dry slopes)
