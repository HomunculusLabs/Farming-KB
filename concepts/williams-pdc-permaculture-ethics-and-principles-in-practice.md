# [[natural-farm-permaculture-ethics-principles]] in Practice

Permaculture is a design system for creating sustainable human
settlements that are in harmony with the natural cycles of the Earth.
As presented in William Ozier's [[permaculture-design]] Course (PDC), the
framework rests on three foundational ethics and twelve design
principles originally coined by [[david-holmgren]]. Understanding these
foundational elements is essential for any aspiring Permaculture
designer, as they inform every design decision from initial concept
through to long-term management.

## The Three [[permaculture-ethics]]

The three accepted central Permaculture ethics are adapted from the
work of Mollison and Holmgren: Earth Care, People Care, and Fair Share.

### Earth Care

All things, including humans, depend upon the Earth. Permaculture
begins with care for the health of the ecology and all its plants and
animals, solely and altruistically for their own sake. While
Permaculture is ultimately an anthropocentric discipline, it is not
only about the human animal. All Permaculture ethics, principles,
goals, and systems are intended to benefit the rest of the Earth as
much as they benefit the people of the Earth.

### People Care

This ethic involves showing compassion for all people and putting the
health and well-being of people as a first priority. People are not
expendable in a Permaculture system. A system is only as good as its
service to the needs of the people it supports.

### Fair Share

Fair share means that everything and everyone receives enough of the
resources to meet their needs. Anything past or superfluous to your
needs is considered 'abundance'. Abundance is to be shared as well.
All things on the Earth receive their fair share, and all people on
Earth receive their fair share. This means humans share in the Earth's
bounty and beauty, while also endeavoring to ensure the Earth itself
receives its fair share of care and resources.

## The 12 [[permaculture-principles]]

These principles serve as design tools but can also be applied to many
aspects of life. Understanding the underlying logic of each principle
and how to apply it as a designer is more important than memorizing
the wording. The twelve principles as presented by Ozier are:

1. Apply self-regulation and accept feedback
2. Catch and store energy
3. [[creatively-use-and-respond-to-change]]
4. [[design-from-patterns-to-details]]
5. [[integrate-rather-than-segregate]]
6. Observe and interact
7. Obtain a yield
8. Produce no waste
9. Use and value diversity
10. [[use-and-value-renewable-resources]] and services
11. [[use-edges-and-value-the-marginal]]
12. [[use-small-and-slow-solutions]]

## Self-Regulation and Accepting Feedback

Nature is a self-regulating system. If a herd of animals overgraze
their food supplies, the herd dwindles. By this process of regulation,
plants and animals maintain the peak health of the individual,
allowing the species to prosper over time. Humans have the capacity
to ignore natural balance, but eventually nature wins. The
Permaculture Designer must consciously control natural instincts for
ecological responsibility and accept feedback from their actions.

## Catch and Store Energy

In Permaculture, 'energy' is not limited to quantifiable scientific
forms such as watts or BTUs. Energy is a statement of functionality,
using an event or phenomenon to accomplish some goal. Energy can be
embodied in any proper noun: shade energy, cold energy, the energy
of a slope, the energy of a sunrise. This principle has two parts:
catch the energy and store it for later use. Composting is an
excellent example of catching and storing energy for later use.

## Creatively Use and Respond to Change

Things change. A Permaculture Designer understands that ecosystems
evolve. Even perfectly designed and installed systems will change over
time. Healthy, robust systems will attract 'volunteers' (plants and
animals not originally designed for). Earthworks will slowly alter
topography, changing the function of designs. Some change is
predictable, and a good design will account for, enable, and follow
that change. Designs should be evolving and adaptable.

## Design from Patterns to Details

Before digging the first hole, a designer needs to understand the
bigger picture. A pattern is a repeating phenomenon in nature: wind
patterns, flow patterns, rain patterns, migration patterns. These
patterns overlap and interact. For example, local rain patterns tell
you how much water you can catch and when, which determines tank
size, which determines foundation requirements. Only after
understanding patterns should you attend to the details of digging
holes. A 'pattern' can also be an understanding of behavior or
reactions of plants, animals, or anything else in the system.

## Integrating Rather Than Segregating

Monocropping uses only one type of plant, while polycropping uses
several different species. Polycropping is an example of integrating.
A good Permaculture Design tries to integrate together as many
elements as possible, locking them together so they achieve more
working as a team than they could separately. This principle applies
to far more than plants: building materials, design philosophies,
social norms, and anything else in the system.

## Observing and Interacting

The dedicated Permaculturist will spend one to three years observing
before making design interventions, to observe the site across
seasons. Observation involves much more than just the eyes. Observe
with all senses and all of your mind. How does it smell, sound, and
feel? Close your eyes and just listen. Interactions can be small
experiments: plant a small crop, install a small swale, go for hikes
across the property. Observe under all different circumstances: at
night, dawn, sunny, rainy, and windy. Observe before interpreting.
