# Permaculture Village Development Process

The [[permaculture]] approach to village development follows a systematic, phased methodology that integrates [[williams-pdc-ecological-design-principles]] with practical legal, financial, and social structures. Unlike conventional suburban development, which treats land as a speculative commodity, the [[growing-gourmet-stametsian-permaculture-model]] treats land as a shared productive resource that must serve the long-term well-being of its residents and the broader ecosystem.

## Stages of Development

A well-organized village development proceeds through clearly defined stages. The process begins with site selection and feasibility assessment, moves through surveying and planning, and culminates in phased construction and occupancy. Each stage builds upon the previous one, reducing risk and ensuring that the development remains responsive to both ecological realities and community needs.

The critical stages include:

1. **Site assessment and acquisition**: Identify land suitable for a village, considering water, soil, climate, access, and existing vegetation. Assess zoning requirements and subdivision potential.
2. **Survey and design**: Commission topographic surveys and prepare a detailed site plan that integrates water systems, energy infrastructure, access roads, productive land, and residential zones.
3. **Financial modeling**: Establish prices based on the real costs of roading, water supply, and sewerage infrastructure. Develop a funding strategy that may involve developer capital, investor funds, or buyer trust accounts.
4. **Regulatory approval**: Obtain sealed permission for subdivision from the local authority (shire council), complying with all planning requirements.
5. **Marketing and commitment**: Convene prospective buyers through advertisement, issue the proposal and site plan, and obtain firm commitments before proceeding to construction.
6. **Phased construction**: Build infrastructure and dwellings in stages as required by the shire, using trust funds for road, water, and site preparation.
7. **Ongoing evolution**: When initial costs are cleared, use profits and accumulated skills to fund future projects within the village.

## Financing Structures

Several financing models are available for village development, and the choice depends on the resources of the developer and the commitment level of prospective residents:

- **Developer-funded**: The developer holds all capital for survey, planning, and initial infrastructure. This gives maximum control but concentrates risk.
- **Investor-funded**: The developer raises capital from investors to cover proposal and planning costs. Returns are generated through land sales and ongoing enterprise.
- **Buyer trust accounts**: Prospective buyers form a trust account to collectively purchase and develop the land. This distributes both risk and benefit among future residents.
- **Mixed models**: Combinations of the above, with trust funds established by developers and released when development is fully approved by the shire council.

A key principle is that infrastructure (roading, water, energy systems) should be installed at the stages demanded by the shire or region, ensuring regulatory compliance while managing cash flow.

## Legal Structures: The Dual Trust Model

One of the most important innovations in permaculture [[integrated-land-management-and-village-design]] is the use of two complementary legal trusts, often referred to as **Trust A** and **Trust B**.

### Trust A — The Land Trust

Trust A is a land bank that holds commons (village land) for the common good and for later development. Its essential characteristics are:

- It holds assets for the **public good**, not for speculative gain
- It does **not take risks** — it is a conservative, asset-preserving entity
- It leases or rents land and facilities to Trust B and individual residents
- It administers common lands, recreation reserves, and sites for future structures such as schools, restaurants, machine shops, and [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]] areas

The developer acts as the initial foundation director (settlor) of Trust A but should retire as soon as the site has 10-12 residents, who then assume directorship. Only 3-4 trustees are needed for efficient management, though sub-committees can be allotted specific project work.

### Trust B — The Trading Trust

Trust B is a separate unit discretionary trust established for trading operations. Its characteristics include:

- It **does trade and take risks**, engaging in commercial enterprises
- Trust A is one of its beneficiaries, ensuring that commercial success flows back to the commons
- It is currently immune from company tax in many jurisdictions
- It reduces family tax liability
- It enables a wide variety of enterprises to be initiated under one organizational umbrella
- It can duplicate or triplicate itself to accommodate new enterprises and to insulate successful operations from risk
- It handles financial systems such as leasing and lending units

The relationship between the two trusts creates a resilient economic structure: Trust A provides the stable land base and common infrastructure, while Trust B generates income through enterprise, with profits flowing back to support the commons.

### Title Systems

Cluster and strata titles give privacy, negotiability, and autonomy to individuals and families. Titles and houses can be resold, traded, or even donated to Trust A. However, a land trust is necessary to administer common lands regardless of the title system used.

## The Development Team

The developmental group works best as a **small core of 2-4 people**, each with specialized skills:

- **Real estate**: Understanding land markets, title systems, and sales
- **Design**: Permaculture site design, landscape architecture
- **Planning**: Regulatory compliance, subdivision processes
- **Law**: Trust structures, contracts, and governance

Surveyors, road-builders, builders, and landscapers are usually locally available. Some contractors may work for equity in the project, either as village occupants or for resale at a later date.

The development group should hand over a **site design and user's manual** to the directors of Trust A when resident trustees assume control. This document serves as the starting point for community governance, recording the initial design intent while acknowledging that all designs are made to be changed — the challenge is to change them for the better.

## Site Design and Infrastructure Priority

In planning a permaculture village, infrastructure should be developed in a specific order of priority:

1. **Water systems first**: Without reliable water, nothing else functions. Design for capture, storage, and distribution from the outset.
2. **Energy systems second**: Assess and plan for diverse energy sources appropriate to the site.
3. **Access and movement**: Roads, paths, and transport infrastructure come next, designed for minimum energy expenditure.
4. **Dwellings and structures**: Housing and buildings are placed within the framework established by water, energy, and access systems.
5. **Land use zoning**: Productive land areas, forests, recreation zones, and conservation reserves are designated last, informed by all preceding decisions.

This sequence ensures that the most fundamental systems are established before dependent infrastructure is built, avoiding costly retrofitting and ensuring long-term resilience.

## Village Size Guidelines

Human settlements vary in their ability to provide resources, develop self-reliance, and maintain social cohesion according to population size:

- **~100 income-producing people**: A significant financial institution can be village-based
- **~500 people**: All people can know each other if social affairs are encouraged
- **~2,000 people**: Theft and competitiveness become more common; sects form in opposition

The recommended approach is to **start small** at about 30 adults, build to 200-300 people, and proceed slowly and by choice to 500. Beyond this, the village should "calve" into new neighborhoods or new villages. Alliances of 200-500 household-size hamlets can form viable manufacturing or trading alliances while maintaining social cohesion. The **[[mondragon-cooperatives]]** of Spain initially grew to 3,000-5,000 members but later deliberately reduced to cooperatives of 300-500 to preserve individual identity.

The **neighborhood factor** — knowledge of each other's names — is identified as a primary survival factor. During the 1967 Hobart fires, casualties in "anonymous" commuter suburbs were many times higher than in neighborhoods where people knew and cared for others.

## Key Principles

- **Start small, grow slowly**: Begin with approximately 30 adults and build to 200-300 people before considering expansion beyond 500.
- **Change for the better**: A design is a starting point, not an end point. Community governance should be structured to enable continuous improvement.
- **Distribute ownership and governance**: Trust structures should transfer control from developers to residents as quickly as practicable.
- **Integrate functions**: Avoid the modern zoning trap of separating living, working, and recreation into different areas.
- **Reserve common assets**: Set aside 30% of titles for the village trust to fund ongoing public services and development.

The [[permaculture-village-development]] process represents a fundamentally different approach to human settlement — one that treats the village as a living system, with legal, financial, and social structures designed to support ecological health and [[permaculture-community-resilience]] over generations.
