# Compost Tea Pathogen Safety and Anaerobic Risk

## Overview

Pathogen safety is one of the most critical concerns in compost tea
production. Regulatory agencies, agricultural inspectors, and food safety
programs have scrutinized compost tea due to the theoretical risk of
spreading human pathogens such as E. coli O157:H7, Salmonella, and
Listeria. Dr. Elaine Ingham's research demonstrates that properly produced
aerated compost tea poses minimal pathogen risk when aerobic conditions
are maintained throughout the brewing process and quality compost is used.

## Regulatory Concerns About Human Pathogens

The primary regulatory concerns center on E. coli and other coliform
bacteria. The National Organic Program (NOP) and various state agricultural
agencies have established guidelines requiring that compost tea be produced
from properly composted materials and that aerobic conditions be maintained.
Some jurisdictions require compost tea producers to conduct regular coliform
testing and maintain records demonstrating that their products fall below
actionable thresholds for indicator organisms.

The concern is not abstract: outbreaks of foodborne illness linked to
manure-contaminated irrigation water and soil amendments have driven
regulatory scrutiny of all biological amendments, including compost tea.
Ingham's position is that this scrutiny, while appropriate for manure tea
and anaerobic extracts, is often misapplied to properly made aerobic compost
tea that uses finished, pathogen-free compost as the starting material.

## Mechanisms of Pathogen Elimination in Aerobic Tea

Properly aerated compost tea eliminates human pathogens through several
overlapping biological mechanisms:

**Competition:** Beneficial aerobic bacteria and fungi rapidly colonize all
available surfaces and consume all available nutrients. Human pathogens are
poor competitors against diverse, adapted [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] from mature
compost. When beneficial organisms dominate, pathogens cannot establish the
population densities needed to pose health risk.

**Predation:** Protozoa — ciliates, amoebae, and flagellates — are efficient
[[fungal-bacterial-predators-nitrogen-acquisition-soil-ecology]]. As protozoan populations build during brewing (typically
after 12-24 hours), they actively consume bacterial cells including any
pathogens, keeping individual species from reaching dangerous levels.

**Antibiosis:** Bacillus species, pseudomonads, and various fungi extracted
from compost produce [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]] that inhibit or kill competing
organisms, creating biochemical environments hostile to human pathogens.

**Temperature:** While tea is brewed at ambient temperatures, the starting
compost has already undergone thermophilic composting that eliminated
pathogens. The aerobic brewing process prevents their re-establishment.

## The Risk of Anaerobic Conditions

Anaerobic conditions in compost tea represent the primary pathway for
pathogen risk. When dissolved oxygen drops below 5.5-6.0 mg/L, facultative
and obligate [[ingham-anaerobic-organisms-role-soil]] begin to proliferate. These anaerobic
organisms include not only putrefactive bacteria that produce foul odors
but also potential human pathogens that thrive in low-oxygen environments.

E. coli, Salmonella, and Clostridium species are all facultative or
obligate anaerobes that can multiply rapidly when oxygen is depleted. The
shift from aerobic to anaerobic conditions in compost tea therefore
represents a qualitative change in the [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] — not merely a
reduction in diversity but an active selection for organisms that may be
harmful to human health.

Anaerobic conditions also produce phytotoxic compounds (hydrogen sulfide,
ammonia, alcohols, and organic acids) that can damage plant roots and
foliage when anaerobic tea is applied as a foliar spray or soil drench.

## Monitoring for Anaerobic Conditions

The simplest monitoring method is the smell test. Aerobic compost tea has
a pleasant, earthy, or slightly sweet aroma. The appearance of foul,
putrid, sour, or rotten-egg odors indicates anaerobic conditions and
means the tea should not be applied to food crops. Ingham emphasizes that
if tea smells bad, something has gone wrong and the batch should be
discarded or applied only to non-food plantings.

Dissolved oxygen (DO) monitoring provides quantitative assurance. A DO
meter should show levels above 6.0 mg/L throughout the brewing process.
Levels below 5.5 mg/L signal that aeration is insufficient and corrective
action (increasing air volume, reducing food additions, or shortening brew
time) is needed.

## Compost Quality Requirements

The single most important factor in compost tea pathogen safety is the quality
of the starting compost. Ingham is emphatic that only properly finished compost
should be used. Finished compost has undergone thermophilic heating (131°F/55°C
for a minimum of 3 days in a turned windrow), cooled through the mesophilic
phase, and been adequately cured. Partly composted manure should never be used
for tea intended for food crops.

## Compost Tea vs. Manure Tea

The distinction between compost tea and manure tea is critical. Compost tea is
made from finished compost that has been through complete thermophilic
composting. Manure tea is made by steeping raw or partially composted manure
without aeration. Manure tea regularly contains high E. coli levels because
the starting material is raw waste. Compost tea, when made under aerobic
conditions from finished compost, contains beneficial biology that suppresses
pathogens. Ingham views applying manure tea concerns to properly made aerobic
compost tea as a category error.

## Chlorine in Water

Municipal water supplies often contain chlorine or chloramine at levels
sufficient to kill [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]] in compost tea. Ingham
recommends aerating or off-gassing chlorinated water for 1-2 hours before
adding compost, or using dechlorinated water. Chloramine is more persistent
than free chlorine and may require filtration or chemical neutralization.

The irony is that while chlorine kills beneficial biology, it does not
eliminate all human pathogens — some E. coli strains and bacterial spores
survive chlorination. Using chlorinated water can therefore produce a tea
that has lost its beneficial competitors while retaining or selecting for
chlorine-tolerant organisms.
