## Biochar Production Guide

Biochar is a carbon-rich porous material produced through the pyrolysis (thermal decomposition in limited oxygen) of biomass. When incorporated into soil, biochar improves water retention, nutrient holding capacity, microbial habitat, and long-term carbon sequestration. Properly produced and "charged" biochar can transform soil fertility for decades or centuries.

## What Biochar Is and Is Not

Biochar is NOT regular charcoal from a campfire. Biochar requires controlled pyrolysis at specific temperatures (350-700C / 660-1290F) to create a highly porous structure with large surface area. Regular charcoal briquettes often contain binders, chemicals, and fillers unsuitable for soil application. The key difference is intentionality: biochar is made specifically for soil amendment.

## Production Methods

### Method 1: TLUD (Top-Lit UpDraft) Cone Kiln

The most accessible method for small-scale production. A metal cone or cylinder is loaded with dry biomass, lit from the top, and allowed to burn downward. The pyrolysis zone moves through the material while gases burn at the top, reducing smoke.

Materials needed:
- 55-gallon drum cut in half or a metal cone kiln
- Dry biomass (wood chips, branches, corn stalks)
- Fire starter and matches
- Water or sand for quenching

Steps:
1. Fill the kiln with dry biomass to 2-3 inches below the rim
2. Create a small fire on top of the biomass and let it establish
3. Once the top is burning well, add more biomass on top gradually
4. When flames emerge around the edges, the pyrolysis front has reached the bottom
5. Quench immediately with water or smother with sand/dirt
6. Do NOT let it burn to ash — you want blackened, not white/gray material

### Method 2: Retort Method

A two-chamber system where the outer chamber burns and the inner chamber pyrolyzes biomass in an oxygen-free environment. This produces higher-quality biochar with less smoke.

- Outer chamber: burns fuel normally
- Inner chamber: sealed container of biomass heated by the outer fire
- The inner chamber never contacts flame directly — only heat
- Produces more consistent biochar with higher carbon content

### Method 3: Pit Method

Simple but less controlled. Dig a pit, fill with biomass, light it, and manage the burn by covering with metal sheeting and dirt to limit oxygen. This is the oldest method and works in a pinch but produces less uniform results.

### Method 4: Conservation Burn (Air Curtain Burner)

For larger-scale operations. A blower creates an air curtain over burning piles, reducing smoke and achieving more complete pyrolysis. Suitable for homestead-scale brush clearing operations.

## Best Feedstock Materials

Good feedstock:
- Hardwood chips and chunks (oak, hickory, maple)
- Fruit tree prunings
- Bamboo
- Corn stalks and cobs
- Rice hulls

Avoid:
- Treated or painted wood
- Plywood, OSB, MDF (contain glues)
- Plastic, rubber, or synthetic materials
- Glossy paper
- Meat, dairy, or grease-coated materials

## Activation and Charging (Critical Step)

Raw biochar straight from production is essentially sterile and has a high cation exchange capacity but holds nothing useful yet. Charging biochar before soil application is essential — uncharged biochar can actually tie up nutrients initially.

### Compost Charging Method (Recommended)

1. Mix biochar into your compost pile at 10-15% by volume
2. Let it compost for 2-6 months
3. The compost tea and microbial life colonize the biochar pores
4. Apply the enriched compost/biochar blend to garden beds

### Liquid Charging Method (Faster)

1. Soak biochar in compost tea or [[knf-preparations]] (fermented plant juice) for 24-48 hours
2. Add a nitrogen source like fish emulsion or diluted urine
3. Drain and apply immediately to soil or compost

### Manure Charging Method

1. Mix biochar 1:1 with aged manure
2. Let sit for 2-4 weeks in a covered pile
3. The manure microbes and nutrients charge the biochar
4. Apply to beds as an amendment

## Soil Application Rates

- New beds: 5-10% by volume (roughly 1/2 to 1 inch mixed into top 6-8 inches)
- Established beds: 1-2 quarts per square foot, worked in gently
- Potted plants: 5-10% of total potting mix volume
- Compost piles: 10-15% by volume
- Trees: 1-2 cups per inch of trunk diameter, mixed into backfill

Application frequency: Biochar is essentially permanent in soil. One application can last centuries. Focus on getting the initial charge right rather than repeated applications.

## Benefits

- Increases water holding capacity by 20-40% in sandy soils
- Improves drainage in heavy clay soils
- Raises cation exchange capacity (CEC), holding more nutrients
- Provides habitat for beneficial microbes, mycorrhizae, and soil organisms
- Sequesters carbon for hundreds to thousands of years
- Reduces nutrient leaching and runoff
- Moderates soil pH (alkaline biochar can raise pH of acidic soils)
- Can reduce methane and nitrous oxide emissions from soil

## Safety Considerations

- Always produce biochar outdoors in a clear area away from structures
- Have water or a fire extinguisher ready
- Never use petroleum-based accelerants
- Wear heat-resistant gloves when handling hot metal and biochar
- Work upwind of the burn to avoid smoke inhalation
- Check local burn permits and air quality regulations before production
- Be aware that biochar production does release some particulate matter and CO2
