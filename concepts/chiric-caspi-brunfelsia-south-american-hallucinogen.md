# Chiric-Caspi and Chiric Sanango (Brunfelsia spp.)

Chiric-caspi and chiric sanango are the most common of the native names for
several species of shrubs in the genus Brunfelsia that appear to have been
important hallucinogens among some South [[harner-american-indian-peyote-rituals]] tribes. The use of
the name borrachero, meaning "intoxicator," indicates that the natives of
Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru recognize the shrub's narcotic properties. The
special care taken in its cultivation suggests a former religious or magical
place in tribal life. The species most commonly employed as hallucinogens are
Brunfelsia grandiflora and B. chiricaspi.

## Ethnobotanical Use Across South America

Evidence has pointed to the use of several Brunfelsia species either as the
source of a hallucinogenic drink or as an additive to other hallucinogenic
preparations. Among the Kachinaua of Brazil, Brunfelsia serves as the
primary ingredient in a hallucinogenic beverage. Among the Jivaro and Kofan
Indians of Ecuador, Brunfelsia is used as an additive to other
hallucinogenic drinks, likely including [[schultes-ayahuasca-preparations]], to alter
or increase their potency.

The distribution of Brunfelsia use across a wide geographic area, from
Brazil through Ecuador and Peru, suggests that its psychoactive properties
have been independently discovered by multiple indigenous groups. The
different modes of use, as a primary hallucinogen in some cultures and as
an admixture in others, reflect the versatility of the plant and the
sophistication of indigenous pharmacological knowledge in mixing plant
constituents to achieve desired effects.

## The Name Borrachero and Its Significance

The application of the name "borrachero" to Brunfelsia is significant
because this term is widely used throughout South America to designate
plants of the nightshade family (Solanaceae) that produce intoxicating
effects. The name derives from the Spanish "borracho" (drunk) and refers
to the state of intoxication produced by these plants. [[tree-daturas-brugmansia-south-american-ethnobotany]]
(Brugmansia species), Iochroma, and Methysticodendron are all known by
variants of the name borrachero in different regions.

The sharing of the borrachero designation among multiple Solanaceous genera
reflects both the chemical similarity of these plants (many contain tropane
alkaloids or related compounds) and the indigenous understanding of their
shared pharmacological properties. The fact that Brunfelsia is called
borrachero despite its uncertain chemical profile suggests that its
intoxicating effects are sufficiently pronounced and characteristic to
invite comparison with the more thoroughly studied tropane-containing plants
of the same family.

## Species Used: B. grandiflora and B. chiricaspi

The two species most commonly identified as hallucinogenic are Brunfelsia
grandiflora and B. chiricaspi. B. grandiflora is a relatively well-known
species with large, showy flowers that change color from purple to white
over several days, earning it the common name "yesterday-today-and-tomorrow"
in horticultural circles. B. chiricaspi is less well-known outside of its
indigenous context but is specifically associated with hallucinogenic use by
its inclusion in the species epithet, which derives from the Quechua
"chiric" (bitter) and "caspi" (tree), referencing its bitter taste and
tree-like growth form.

The use of the bitter taste as a distinguishing characteristic in the
indigenous name is noteworthy, as bitterness is widely associated with
medicinal and psychoactive potency in [[psilocybe-columbiana-south-american-neurotropic-species-guzman-1998]] indigenous
pharmacopeia. Many psychoactive plants used in the region are noted for
their intensely bitter flavor, including ayahuasca, which is described as
"intensely bitter and nauseating."

## Medicinal Uses

All species of Brunfelsia enter into folk medicine, being used especially
to reduce fevers and as antirheumatic agents. B. uniflora (sometimes
classified as B. hopeana) has been included in the Brazilian pharmacopoeia,
reflecting its recognized therapeutic value. The antipyretic (fever-reducing)
and antirheumatic applications are consistent with the broad medicinal use
of many Solanaceous plants in South American traditional medicine.
