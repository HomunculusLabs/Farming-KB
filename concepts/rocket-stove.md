# Rocket Stove

A rocket stove is an efficient, clean-burning wood-burning stove that uses an insulated J-tube or L-tube combustion chamber to achieve near-complete combustion of wood fuel. Designed for maximum heat transfer with minimal fuel consumption, rocket stoves can achieve combustion efficiencies of 80-90% compared to 10-15% for open campfires. They produce significantly less smoke, use small-diameter wood that would otherwise be waste, and can be built from inexpensive or salvaged materials. Rocket stove technology, developed by Dr. Larry Winiarski at Aprovecho Research Center in the 1980s, is widely used in developing countries for cooking and adapted for homestead heating, water heating, and cob oven integration.

## How a Rocket Stove Works

The rocket stove design is based on several principles of efficient combustion:

1. **Insulated combustion chamber**: The J-tube (or L-tube) is surrounded by insulation (perlite, vermiculite, pumice, wood ash, or cob). This keeps the combustion chamber hot, which promotes complete burning of wood gases.
2. **Horizontal feed tube**: Wood is fed horizontally into the vertical burn tunnel. This configuration allows gravity to feed wood into the fire as it burns, reducing the need to constantly push fuel.
3. **Vertical chimney**: The hot gases rise rapidly through a vertical chimney, creating strong draft that pulls air through the feed tube. This draft is self-sustaining once the fire is established.
4. **Preheated air**: As air is drawn through the feed tube, it passes over the burning wood and is preheated before entering the combustion zone. Hotter combustion means more complete burning.
5. **Complete combustion**: The combination of high temperature, preheated air, and turbulence in the combustion chamber burns not just the wood but also the wood gases (smoke), resulting in a nearly smokeless, extremely hot flame.

The result is a stove that boils water in a fraction of the time and fuel of an open fire, with dramatically reduced smoke and particulate emissions.

## J-Tube Design Specifications

### Dimensions

The core dimensions of a rocket stove are based on the internal diameter of the feed tube and burn tunnel:

| Component | Dimension (relative to diameter D) |
|---|---|
| Feed tube (horizontal) | 4D long, D diameter |
| Burn tunnel (vertical) | 3D long, D diameter |
| Heat riser (vertical) | 6D long, D diameter |
| Insulation thickness | 2-3D around combustion chamber |
| Gap above heat riser to cooking surface | 1.5D |

For a standard cooking rocket stove with a 4-inch diameter system:
- Feed tube: 16 inches long, 4 inches diameter
- Burn tunnel: 12 inches long, 4 inches diameter
- Heat riser: 24 inches long, 4 inches diameter
- Insulation: 8-12 inches thick

### Materials

**Combustion chamber (the J-tube)**:
- Steel pipe (square or round) — most common DIY material
- Firebrick — refractory, long-lasting, excellent heat retention
- Cast refractory — custom-poured for precise dimensions
- Cob (clay/sand/straw mix) — low-cost, natural, but shorter lifespan

**Insulation around the J-tube**:
- Perlite (horticultural grade) — lightweight, inexpensive, excellent insulator
- Vermmiculite — similar to perlite
- Pumice or volcanic scoria — natural, durable
- Wood ash — free byproduct; moderate insulation
- Sawdust-clay mix (light cob) — natural, moderate insulation

**Outer shell**:
- Steel drum (55-gallon) for large rocket mass heaters
- Metal box or bucket for portable cooking stoves
- Cob or stone for permanent installations
- Brick for permanent outdoor installations

## Construction: Portable Cooking Rocket Stove

A simple portable rocket stove can be built in a few hours:

1. **Materials needed**:
   - One 55-gallon steel drum or smaller metal body
   - Steel or iron pipe sections (one L-joint and two straight sections) for the J-tube
   - Insulation (perlite or vermiculite, 2-3 cubic feet)
   - Metal cooking surface (steel plate or wok ring)
   - High-temperature stove paint (optional)

2. **Build the J-tube**: Connect the feed tube to the burn tunnel at a 90-degree angle. The feed tube is horizontal, the burn tunnel goes straight up. Add the heat riser extension above the burn tunnel.

3. **Create the body**: Cut an opening in the drum or box for the feed tube. The J-tube sits inside with insulation packed around it.

4. **Pack insulation**: Fill the space between the J-tube and the outer shell with perlite or vermiculite. Pack firmly but not so tight that it compresses.

5. **Add cooking surface**: Place a steel plate or wok ring over the top of the heat riser. The gap between the heat riser opening and the cooking surface should be approximately 1.5 times the riser diameter.

6. **Fire it up**: Start with small dry twigs and kindling. Once the chimney is hot, the stove will roar and sustain itself with minimal tending.

## Rocket Mass Heater

A rocket mass heater is a rocket stove connected to a thermal mass (usually a cob bench or earthen floor). The hot exhaust gases from the rocket stove travel through a horizontal flue embedded in the cob mass before exiting through a vertical chimney. The cob absorbs and slowly releases the heat over many hours.

**Key advantages over conventional wood stoves**:
- Uses 80-90% less wood than a standard wood stove
- Burns cleaner with dramatically less creosote and chimney fire risk
- Heats people directly (radiant heat from the bench) rather than just air
- Stores heat for 12-24 hours after a single 1-2 hour burn
- Can be built from local, inexpensive materials

**Construction considerations**:
- The horizontal flue through the mass must have adequate cross-sectional area (same as the heat riser)
- Total horizontal flue length should not exceed 20-30 feet (longer runs reduce draft)
- A clean-out access is essential for removing ash from the horizontal flue
- The system must have a proper chimney (minimum 15 feet tall) for reliable draft
- Building codes may restrict indoor rocket mass heaters — check local regulations via [[homesteading-legal-considerations]]

## Cob Oven Combination

Rocket stoves can be integrated with cob ovens to create an extremely efficient cooking and baking system:

- The rocket stove provides the initial heat to bring the oven to temperature quickly
- Once the oven is hot, the cob mass retains heat for hours of baking
- The rocket stove can continue providing heat for a stovetop surface while the oven bakes
- This combination uses a fraction of the wood of a traditional cob oven
- See [[natural-building]] for cob construction techniques

## Fuel Requirements

Rocket stoves are designed to burn small-diameter wood efficiently:

- **Ideal fuel**: Dry sticks and twigs 0.5-2 inches in diameter and 12-24 inches long
- **Wood species**: Any dry wood works. Hardwoods (oak, maple, hickory) burn longer; softwoods (pine, cedar) burn faster and hotter.
- **Moisture content**: Below 20% moisture is ideal. Green or wet wood produces smoke and reduces efficiency.
- **Fuel preparation**: Prunings from orchard management, fallen branches, and construction scraps are all suitable fuel. This makes rocket stoves an excellent way to use [[food-forest-design]] pruning waste.
- **Feed rate**: Feed sticks into the horizontal tube as they burn down. The self-feeding design means you add fuel every 5-10 minutes rather than constantly tending.

## Applications

### Cooking

- Boiling water: 1 liter boils in 3-5 minutes with a handful of twigs
- Frying and sauteing: Wok-style cooking on the flat surface
- Slow cooking: Pots with lids can simmer for hours on the reduced heat setting
- Outdoor kitchen: Combine with prep surfaces and shelter for a complete outdoor cooking station

### Heating

- Space heating via rocket mass heater (described above)
- Greenhouse heating: Small rocket stoves can heat [[greenhouse-design]] spaces efficiently
- Water heating: Wrap a water jacket around the heat riser for domestic hot water

### Homestead Processing

- Maple syrup boiling: High heat output is ideal for evaporating sap
- Canning: Sustained heat for water bath canning — see [[food-preservation-canning]]
- Rendering: Melting fats for soap and candle making — see [[candle-making]]
- Scalding: Processing poultry — see [[animal-harvest-and-processing]]

## Safety Considerations

- The combustion chamber and heat riser reach temperatures of 1000-1800F — serious burn hazard
- Provide a stable, non-combustible base for the stove
- Maintain clearances from combustible walls and ceilings (minimum 36 inches)
- Install a carbon monoxide detector if using indoors or in enclosed spaces
- Ensure adequate ventilation — rocket stoves consume oxygen and produce CO2
- Keep children and pets away from the hot surfaces
- Check local regulations before installing permanent indoor rocket mass heaters

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Excessive smoke | Insufficient draft; wet wood; cold start; wrong dimensions | Use dry fuel; preheat the chimney with newspaper; check dimensions match specifications |
| Fire goes out | Draft reversed; chimney too short; blockage | Ensure chimney is tall enough (15+ ft for mass heaters); clear blockages; warm the chimney |
| Back-puffing (smoke from feed tube) | Wind blowing down chimney; insufficient chimney height | Add a chimney cap; extend chimney height; block wind |
| Stove overheating | Insulation too thin; continuous over-firing | Add more insulation; reduce fuel feed rate; let stove cool between uses |
| Cooking surface too cool | Gap between riser and pot too large or too small | Adjust cooking surface height to 1.5x riser diameter |
| Metal parts burning through | Thin steel in combustion chamber; high temperatures | Use thicker steel, firebrick, or cast refractory for the J-tube |
