# Casing Scratching Techniques

## Overview

Casing scratching is a cultivation technique used to correct surface
problems in the casing layer and stimulate more even, prolific pinhead
formation. Two main approaches exist: light scratching (also called
ruffling) and deep scratching. Both methods disrupt the mycelial mat to
promote renewed colonization and improved fruiting.

## The Problem: Panned or Damaged Casing Surfaces

Heavy direct watering can "pan" the casing surface, closing all pore spaces
and effectively sealing it. The growing mycelium becomes trapped within the
casing layer and may not break through at all. The ultimate example of
panning is soil turned to mud. Panned casings inhibit gas exchange, prevent
proper moisture distribution, and severely limit primordia formation.

Additionally, overlay (dense mycelial mat formation on the casing surface)
can create conditions where the casing becomes impervious to water and
produces few pinheads. Both conditions require intervention to restore
productive fruiting.

## Light Scratching (Ruffling)

### Purpose

Light scratching is used to reopen a casing surface that has been damaged
by watering or has begun to form a thin overlay. The goal is to restore
porosity and surface structure without disturbing the underlying mycelial
network excessively.

### Tool Construction

The scratching tool is simple to construct: take a 1x2x24 inch board and
drive parallel rows of 6-penny nails slightly offset relative to one
another. The nails should protrude enough to ruffle the casing surface but
not so far as to penetrate deeply into the substrate below.

### Technique

1. Wait until the casing surface shows signs of panning or thin overlay
2. Draw the scratching stick across the casing surface in parallel passes
3. The nails lightly ruffle the top quarter-inch of the casing
4. Focus on areas where the surface has become compacted or sealed
5. Work methodically across the entire bed or tray surface
6. After scratching, the casing should be given its final waterings prior
   to pinning

### Expected Results

The ruffled surface regains its porous, open structure with small
"mountains and valleys" that protect developing primordia and allow
metabolic gases to diffuse. Within a few days the mycelium re-colonizes the
disturbed surface and proceeds toward pinhead formation.

## Deep Scratching

### Purpose

Deep scratching is a more aggressive technique used when the mycelium has
formed a thick overlay or when the casing layer has become severely
compacted. This method thoroughly disrupts the entire casing layer down to
the bulk substrate.

### When to Use

- Severe overlay where the entire casing surface is covered by a dense
  mycelial mat
- Complete casing surface panning from excessive watering
- Stalled cultures where the mycelium refuses to form primordia despite
  proper environmental conditions
- When the mycelium is midway through the casing but growth has stopped

### Technique

1. Using the same scratching stick, ruffle the entire casing layer down to
   the bulk substrate
2. Break up all mycelial mats and compacted areas thoroughly
3. Ensure the entire casing layer is uniformly disrupted
4. The agitated and broken mycelium rapidly reestablishes itself
5. Within three to four days the mycelium completely recolonizes the casing

### Expected Results

Deep scratching produces early, even, and prolific pinhead formation. The
disruption causes the mycelium to regrow through the casing in a more
organized pattern, creating the proper conditions for simultaneous primordia
development across the entire surface.

### Critical Precautions

Before using deep scratching, the grower must be absolutely certain that:

- The substrate and casing are free of competitor molds
- No nematodes are present in the culture
- The substrate itself is not contaminated

Scratching can spread contaminants throughout the casing layer. If molds or
nematodes are present, deep scratching will distribute them and potentially
destroy the entire culture. Only perform deep scratching on verified clean
cultures.

## Casing Surface Preparation After Scratching

After either light or deep scratching, the casing surface should maintain
the following characteristics:

- **Rough and porous**: Small mountains and valleys provide more surface
  area for pinhead formation and allow gas diffusion
- **Open structure**: Pore spaces protect developing primordia and permit
  metabolic gas exchange
- **Proper moisture**: Water gradually after scratching to bring casing
  back to optimum moisture content without causing re-panning
- **Even depth**: Ensure consistent casing depth across the surface for
  uniform pin-set timing

## Relationship to Patching

Patching is a complementary technique where moist casing material is
sprinkled over areas of premature mycelial breakthrough. While scratching
disrupts the existing surface, patching adds new material. Both techniques
can be used together: deep scratch to disrupt overlay, then patch any thin
areas before the final pre-pinning waterings.
