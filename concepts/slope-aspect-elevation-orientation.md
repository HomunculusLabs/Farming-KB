# Slope, Aspect, Elevation, and Orientation in Permaculture Design

No site is perfectly flat, and the irregularities of real landscapes distort the idealized zone and sector overlays. [[water-conservation]] at these mid-slopes identifies **keypoints** where high runoff can be led to midslope storage dams. Using high slopes as watershed and a series of diversion catchment drains and dams:

1. Water is conserved at keypoints for later frugal use
2. Water is passed with nutrients to low dams
3. Clean water is released from the site

Lower slopes—safe for tractors—can be converted to immense soil-water storage systems in a single summer through soil conditioning, afforestation, water interception, or a combination.

### Plains Management

Plains areas are most open to wind erosion but most resistant to water erosion. However, great damage occurs through **salting**. Red and dusty rains and plagues of locusts result from delinquent use of the plough, heavy machinery, clean tillage, removal of trees and hedgerows, and conversion to monocultures of extensive grazing and grain cropping.

On plains, water is most cheaply stored in soil and large surface dams. **No-tillage crops, copses, and hedgerows** are desperately needed—this is where broadscale revolutions in technique can improve soil health, reduce losses, and produce healthy foods.

## Thermal Effects of Topography

Forests on high slopes, coupled with the **[[thermal-belt]]** of the house site, make a remarkable difference to midslope climate and soil temperatures. Walking towards an uphill forest on a frosty night reveals the warm down-draught from high forests. If these forests are above Zones 1 and 2, they present little or no fire danger while providing erosion control and water retention.

Downslope, **reflection from dams** adds warmth. Solar collectors placed here transmit heat via air or water circulating by thermosiphoning alone, assisting house, glasshouse, or garden efficiency.

The easy, rounded ridges of non-eroded lower slopes and foothill pediments are **prime settlement sites**. They allow filtration of wastes through lowland forest and lake, converting wastes into useful timber, trees, fruits, and aquatic life.

## Sun Angle and Aspect

### Sun Angle

Sun angle describes the arc of the sun during summer and winter months, determining:

- **Eave and sill placement of windows**
- **Areas of shade**
- **Reflection or absorption angles of surfaces**

In every situation—even hot deserts—some part of the system should be left open to the sun for its energy potential.

### Aspect

Aspect describes the orientation of the slope. A slope facing the sun receives considerably more direct solar radiation than a slope facing the shade side (south in the southern hemisphere, north in the northern hemisphere). The shaded side of hills may delay thaw and thus moderate frost effects in vegetation. In mid-latitudes, designers seek the sunny aspect for maximum sunlight absorption by settlements and gardens.

## Orientation

The final act in site planning is orienting all buildings, structures, and constructs correctly—to face mid-sky, the sun, or the wind systems they reference, or to shelter them from detrimental factors like cold winter winds or late afternoon sun.

## Ridge Forests and Night Condensation

The ancient occupied ridgeways of England testify to the commonsense of megalithic peoples in landscape planning. Their present abandonment for industrial suburbs in flatlands does little credit to paleolithic planning wisdom. The difference may be that former peoples planned for themselves, while modern designers design for "other people."

Ridge forests perform the critical function of **night condensation**—saturated air deposits moisture on the myriad leaves, contributing significantly to the water budget of lower slopes and valleys. This natural fog-drip and condensation harvesting is an often-overlooked benefit of maintaining high-elevation forest cover.

## Summary

If the elements of design are carefully zoned, sectors well-analyzed, sun angle and slope benefits maximized, and the constructed environment oriented to function, then a better ground design results than most that now exist. These essential factors—slope, aspect, elevation, and orientation—are the most frequently overlooked or ignored by designers, leaving retrofit as the only remedy for ineffective design.
