# [[permaculture-design]] Course -- Urban and Suburban Applications

## The Untapped Potential of Urban Landscapes

In America, approximately 10,000 square miles are used for agricultural production, while about 40,000 square miles are used for lawns and ornamental landscaping. As a culture, we are nowhere near our maximum capacity for food production. In most urban landscapes, the same resources used to grow ornamental plants could grow edible plants. Imagine if all lawns were replaced with food-producing gardens, and municipal landscaping was filled with fruit trees, berry bushes, and edible ground crops. Ornamental landscaping and edible food production can and should be one and the same.

## Urban Zone Considerations

Urban environments present unique zone analysis challenges. Many beginners believe every zone analysis needs all five zones -- this is incorrect. In dense urban environments:

- A typical home with a yard may only have **Zone 0** (the house) and **Zone 1** (immediate yard)
- It may have **Zone 2 and 3**, but rarely **Zone 4**
- It will **never** have **Zone 5** (true wilderness)
- Larger urban properties may have up to **Zone 4**

## Vertical Gardening

The vertical dimension is critical in small urban spaces. Trellises allow food vines to grow on the sides of structures. Even a standard chain link fence can serve as a trellis system. When you integrate the vertical dimension, even a small backyard can produce high yields.

## Container Gardens

Food can be grown in any container: buckets, barrels, old computer housings, kiddie pools converted into garden containers. Advanced techniques use barrels with holes poked in the sides, enabling food production from both the top and sides of the container.

## Architectural Food Systems

Architecture can provide growing space:
- **Greenhouses** -- full controlled growing environments
- **Hoop houses** -- simple arches of rebar or PVC with plastic stretched over; cheaper and easier than greenhouses; greatly extend growing season
- **Cold frames** -- very small greenhouses over individual plants; protect from freezing
- **Bio-shelters and solar greenhouses** -- full ecological integration of shelter and food production
- **Green roofs** -- growing food on building rooftops
- **Living walls** -- vertical growing systems on building walls

## Mechanical Food Systems for Urban Settings

- **Hydroponics** -- roots suspended in nutrient water; saves water vs. conventional gardening
- **Aeroponics** -- misters spray atomized nutrient water on dangling roots; greatest water savings
- **Aquaponics** -- combines fish cultivation with hydroponics in a closed-loop system

These systems allow better control of environment, pest protection, easier amendment addition, and water recycling.

## Urban Forests

We have destroyed untold acres of forest to create urban habitats. Urban forestry is the planting of indigenous trees to rejuvenate something close to original forest conditions. With proper design, trees and forests can be interwoven with urban infrastructure -- trees lining roads, delineating property lines, with shrubs and ground covers recreating forest layers around their bases.

## Urban [[water-management]]

Cities are often designed to flood: hardscape pavement prevents infiltration, storm sewers clog, long straight streets enable torrential flows. [[permaculture-principles]] applied to urban design -- rainwater catchment, permeable pavement, rain-sensitive landscaping -- would prevent most floods. Every roof is a potential water catchment surface.

## Disaster-Proof Food Systems

Using Permaculture food systems design, it is possible to create food systems on roofs or in raised container gardens to keep food production above flood height. Vertical tricks and elevated systems ensure food production continues even during minor disasters. Include flood-proof and fire-proof medicinal crops in these systems.

## Community Food Systems

Food systems extend beyond what you grow at home:
- **CSA** (Community Supported Agriculture)
- Local food sources and farmers markets
- Food preservation (canning, drying, fermentation)
- Skill and infrastructure sharing within communities

## Urban Forests

Urban forests are a critical component of [[urban-permaculture-design]]. A city can be thought of as a forest -- buildings are like trees that provide vertical structure, streets are like forest paths, and parks and gardens are like clearings. Designing cities to function more like forests means maximizing the beneficial interactions between all elements.

Trees in urban environments provide shade (reducing cooling costs), intercept stormwater, filter air pollution, provide habitat for birds and beneficial insects, and produce food (fruit and nut trees). Selecting the right tree species for urban conditions -- tolerant of pollution, compacted soils, and restricted root space -- is essential.

## Wildcrafting in Urban and Suburban Areas

Urban and suburban environments offer surprising opportunities for wildcrafting -- harvesting wild edible and medicinal plants. Dandelions, plantain, chickweed, and many other useful plants grow readily in lawns, vacant lots, and along roadsides. However, care must be taken to avoid areas that may be contaminated with heavy metals, pesticides, or other pollutants.

## Water in Urban Design

Urban Permaculture places special emphasis on water management:
- Rain barrels and cisterns capture roof runoff for garden use
- Rain gardens and bioswales slow and filter stormwater
- [[permaculture-designers-manual-greywater-systems]] recycle water from sinks, showers, and laundry for landscape irrigation
- Permeable surfaces (gravel, permeable pavers) allow rain to infiltrate rather than running off

## Building Community Resilience

One of the most important aspects of urban Permaculture is building community resilience through shared resources and knowledge:
- Tool libraries reduce the need for individual ownership
- Community gardens provide growing space for those without land
- Seed libraries preserve and share adapted varieties
- Skill-sharing workshops build local capacity
- Time banks and mutual aid networks strengthen social connections
