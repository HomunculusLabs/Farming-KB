# Saltmarsh Fungal Decomposition and Ascomycetous Laccase Genes

Chapter 18 of Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer examines the [[saltmarsh-fungal-ecology]] decomposition system
of the southeastern US Atlantic coast, revealing how [[ascomycete-phylum-guide]] fungi dominate
lignocellulose breakdown in standing-dead [[spartina-alterniflora]] through diverse
[[laccase]] gene families.

## The Saltmarsh Decomposition Environment

The [[saltmarsh-fungal-ecology]]es of the Georgia, USA, Atlantic coast form an expansive intertidal
ecosystem extending 5–10 km wide from barrier-island chains to the mainland. The
predominant macrophyte is smooth cordgrass (*Spartina alterniflora* Loisel.), with
cross-marsh average annual shoot production measured at approximately 1.3 kg m⁻² of
marsh (Dai & Wiegert, 1996). Unlike most plants, smooth cordgrass does not abscise its
leaf blades after senescence — they remain attached to the leaf sheath after death. As
new blades are produced at the apex, bottom blades senesce until the whole shoot dies
after flowering, creating a large standing-dead litter crop (up to 538 g dry mass m⁻²
for leaf blades alone) available for [[decomposition]] throughout much of the year.

Wetting of standing-dead cordgrass leaves is driven primarily by dewset rather than
tidal flooding. Dead-blade mass is estimated to be wetted by tides only about 10% of
the time on an annual-average basis, with the combination of tidal, rain, and dew
wetting resulting in leaves being wet roughly half the time (Newell et al., 1989).

## Ascomycete Dominance in Saltmarsh Decomposition

Smooth-cordgrass [[ascomycete-phylum-guide]] fungi are well adapted to alternating wet and dry
periods, losing little or no biomass after aestivating and readily reviving (Newell,
1995). Their capabilities include turgor-pressure-driven drilling and
external-enzymatic lysis of solid substrates, allowing them to thoroughly integrate
mycelia into standing-dead cordgrass. Inside the dead shoots, [[ascomycete-phylum-guide]] fungi
carry out their digestive work free from prokaryotic competition or pressure from
micropredators such as amoebae (Newell et al., 1996; Newell, 2001a, 2002).

Additional factors favouring fungal production include: (a) potential formation of
consortia with dinitrogen-fixing bacteria on decaying blades; (b) synergistic
relationships with green microalgae on blade surfaces; (c) potential uptake of
nitrogen from flooding-tidal waters; and (d) synergistic exchange of gases and water
vapour between standing-decaying and living parts of shoots, connected by aerenchyma
channels (Newell & Porter, 2000).

The major fungal mass produced (rough estimate: over 0.5 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹) flows to
litter-comminuting invertebrates at the base of the marsh food web. Cordgrass
ascomycetes are also prodigious expellers of ascospores, estimated conservatively at
5.9 billion spores m⁻² yr⁻¹ (Newell, 2001b).

## The Decomposer Fungal Community

The major members of the smooth-cordgrass shoot-decomposer mycobiota include
*Phaeosphaeria spartinicola*, *P. halima*, and *Mycosphaerella* sp.2, identified by
direct observation of ascomata and capture of expelled ascospores from standing-decaying
blades (Newell, 2001a, b). At sites with low grazing pressure from periwinkle snails,
percentage area of decaying blades occupied by at least one of the three predominant
[[ascomycete-phylum-guide]] species exceeded 90%. A regularly occurring species found at 40% or more
of blade area was *Buergenerula spartinae*. Less frequent species included
*Hydropisphaera erubescens* and *Koorchaloma spartinicola*.

DNA-based identification using rDNA/ITS PCR with ascomycete-selective primers
confirmed the same predominant species revealed by direct microscopy, validating
morphological community assessments (Buchan et al., 2002, 2003).

## Lignocellulose Decomposition by Saltmarsh Ascomycetes

Standing-dead smooth cordgrass is composed largely of lignocellulose (70–75%)
(Hodson et al., 1984). Early microcosm testing suggested cordgrass ascomycetes were
weak degraders of lignocellulose (less than 0.1% d⁻¹ mineralization), but this proved
to be an artifact of the methodology (violent shaking and absence of non-LC
carbohydrates). Subsequent tests with static incubation and presence of malt and yeast
extract revealed that *P. spartinicola* could mineralize lignocellulose at more than
0.8% d⁻¹ (Bergbauer & Newell, 1992). Testing of the three predominant ascomycete
species in combination showed that mineralization of lignocellulose can exceed 20%
after 6 weeks at approximately 23°C (Lyons, 2002). Direct electron microscopy
confirmed that cordgrass ascomycetes produce both type 1 and type 2 soft rot, with
type 2 soft rot being very similar to the white rot of basidiomycetes.

## Laccase Gene Diversity in Saltmarsh Fungi

The key question was how these ascomycetes degrade lignocellulose without the
lignin or manganese peroxidases characteristic of basidiomycetes. All eight strains
of the most common smooth-cordgrass ascomycetes tested positive for [[laccase]]
activity using the syringaldazine assay (Lyons et al., 2003). Degenerate primers
targeting conserved copper-binding sites II and III were designed to specifically
amplify fungal laccase genes. All species tested possessed [[laccase]] genes in at
least one strain, with 13 distinct partial laccase gene sequence types identified
across all strains. The two major *Phaeosphaeria* species between them exhibited
five different types of partial laccase gene, with as many as three different types
within one strain.

When laccase-gene primers were applied to DNA extracted directly from early and late
stages of standing-decaying cordgrass blades from the marsh, 26 partial laccase
genes were cloned and sequenced. Only two clones were not identifiable with the gene
sequences from the three major decomposer species, confirming that the dominant
ascomycete species produce potentially lignolytic laccase genes within naturally
decaying cordgrass.

## Redox-Mediator Systems and Laccase Function

A pivotal discovery altered the understanding of laccase capabilities. If a
redox-mediator molecule is present along with [[laccase]], non-phenolic portions of
lignin can be oxidized and lignin extensively broken down. Eggert et al. (1997)
discovered that the basidiomycete *Pycnoporus cinnabarinus* carried out strong
lignolysis using only [[laccase]] as its sole lignin-oxidizing enzyme, because it
manufactured its own redox-mediator molecule, 3-hydroxyanthranilate (HAA).
Furthermore, interactions of cellobiose dehydrogenases with laccase-mediator
molecules can allow cellulose lysis to proceed alongside lignin oxidation during
[[singh-ligninolytic-enzymes-fungal-bioremediation]]-mediated lignocellulose breakdown (Temp & Eggert, 1999).

## Comparison with Terrestrial Decomposition Systems

Saltmarsh fungal decomposition systems differ from terrestrial ones in several
important respects. The standing-dead nature of cordgrass litter creates a unique
above-sediment decomposition environment, unlike the ground-contact litter of
forest floors. The intertidal position subjects fungi to periodic tidal inundation
and saline conditions, selecting for halotolerant ascomycete species rather than
the basidiomycete-dominated fungal communities typical of terrestrial woody
[[decomposition]]. The predominance of [[ascomycete-phylum-guide]] fungi and their diverse
laccase gene repertoire represents an alternative biochemical strategy for
lignocellulose breakdown that does not rely on peroxidase enzymes.

## Open Questions and Future Directions

The discovery of laccase gene diversity raises several important questions:
(1) What oxidation mediators (if any) are produced by cordgrass ascomycetes?
(2) Is there synergy between laccases produced by the three major decomposer
species? (3) Which ascomycetous laccases are active at different points in the
decay process? (4) Are there major differences in the products of laccase
lignolysis among the cordgrass ascomycetes? These questions highlight the need for
further [[gadd-rice-blast-fungus-magnaporthe-grisea-functional-genomics]] approaches to understand [[singh-ligninolytic-enzymes-fungal-bioremediation]] in
marine/coastal [[decomposition]] processes.
