## Lignocellulose Decomposition by Saltmarsh Ascomycetes

Standing-dead smooth cordgrass is composed largely of lignocellulose (70–75%)
(Hodson et al., 1984). Early microcosm testing suggested cordgrass ascomycetes were
weak degraders of lignocellulose (less than 0.1% d⁻¹ mineralization), but this proved
to be an artifact of the methodology (violent shaking and absence of non-LC
carbohydrates). Subsequent tests with static incubation and presence of malt and yeast
extract revealed that *P. spartinicola* could mineralize lignocellulose at more than
0.8% d⁻¹ (Bergbauer & Newell, 1992). Testing of the three predominant ascomycete
species in combination showed that mineralization of lignocellulose can exceed 20%
after 6 weeks at approximately 23°C (Lyons, 2002). Direct electron microscopy
confirmed that cordgrass ascomycetes produce both type 1 and type 2 soft rot, with
type 2 soft rot being very similar to the white rot of basidiomycetes.

## Laccase Gene Diversity in Saltmarsh Fungi

The key question was how these ascomycetes degrade lignocellulose without the
lignin or manganese peroxidases characteristic of basidiomycetes. All eight strains
of the most common smooth-cordgrass ascomycetes tested positive for [[laccase]] genes in at
least one strain, with 13 distinct partial laccase gene sequence types identified
across all strains. The two major *Phaeosphaeria* species between them exhibited
five different types of partial laccase gene, with as many as three different types
within one strain.

When laccase-gene primers were applied to DNA extracted directly from early and late
stages of standing-decaying cordgrass blades from the marsh, 26 partial laccase
genes were cloned and sequenced. Only two clones were not identifiable with the gene
sequences from the three major decomposer species, confirming that the dominant
ascomycete species produce potentially lignolytic laccase genes within naturally
decaying cordgrass.
