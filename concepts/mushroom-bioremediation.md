# Mushroom Bioremediation

Mushroom bioremediation (also called mycoremediation) is the use of fungi to
degrade, sequester, or otherwise neutralize environmental contaminants. Fungi
possess extraordinary [[fungal-enzymatic-capabilities]] that enable them to break down
some of the most recalcitrant organic compounds found in nature, including
the lignin and cellulose that make up wood. By happenstance, these same
enzymes are also effective at degrading many synthetic pollutants, making
fungi powerful agents for environmental cleanup.

## Why Fungi Are Effective Bioremediators

Fungi occupy a unique ecological niche as primary decomposers of woody plant
material. Wood is composed of lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose — among
the most chemically complex and resistant organic polymers found in nature.
To break down lignin, fungi produce a suite of powerful [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]]
called **lignin peroxidases**, **manganese peroxidases**, and **laccases**.
These enzymes are non-specific oxidizers that attack the complex aromatic
structures of lignin.

This non-specificity is precisely what makes fungi valuable for
bioremediation. Many environmental pollutants — including [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]]
hydrocarbons (PAHs), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), pentachlorophenol
(PCP), and various pesticides — share structural similarities with lignin's
aromatic compounds. The same enzymes that fungi evolved to decompose wood
can also break down these anthropogenic chemicals.

## Saprophytic Decomposers as Environmental Agents

Ecologically, mushrooms can be classified into three groups: saprophytes
(decomposers), parasites, and mycorrhizae. The saprophytic mushrooms —
particularly the "white rot" fungi — are the most effective bioremediators.
These fungi are the primary agents of wood decomposition in forest ecosystems
and have evolved the most powerful enzymatic arsenals for breaking down
complex organic matter.

The most vigorous rotters described for bioremediation purposes include
species of **Ganoderma** (Reishi) and various white-rot Basidiomycetes.
These fungi can break down plant fiber — primarily the lignin-cellulose
structural component in woody plants — into simpler forms. These same enzymes
also reduce recalcitrant hydrocarbons and other man-made toxins.

## Contaminants Targeted by Fungal Bioremediation

Current and prospective applications of mushroom bioremediation include:

- **PCBs (polychlorobiphenyls)**: Persistent organic pollutants once widely
  used in electrical equipment. White-rot fungi can dechlorinate and
  partially mineralize PCBs through their lignin-degrading enzyme systems.
- **PCP (pentachlorophenol)**: A wood preservative and pesticide that
  contaminates soil and groundwater at many industrial sites. Fungi can
  degrade PCP through oxidative and methylation pathways.
- **[[mycoremediation-petroleum-hydrocarbons]]**: Crude oil, diesel fuel, and other petroleum
  products can be degraded by fungi both in soil and in aqueous
  environments. Oyster mushrooms (*Pleurotus* spp.) have demonstrated
  particular effectiveness.
- **Pesticides and herbicides**: Many agricultural chemicals are susceptible
  to fungal degradation, including organochlorine and organophosphate
  insecticides, [[staycare-fungal-degradation-of-triazine-herbicides]], and fungicides.
- **Explosives**: TNT and other nitroaromatic explosives can be degraded by
  certain fungi, offering a biological alternative for cleaning
  contaminated military sites.
- **Radioactive waste**: Fungi are being explored for their potential to
  ameliorate the impact of radioactive wastes through bioaccumulation
  and transformation of radionuclides.

## Advantages of In Situ Treatment

Bioremediation of toxic waste sites using fungi is especially attractive
because the environment can be treated in situ — meaning contaminated soils
do not have to be excavated and hauled away. This eliminates the extraordinary
expense of handling, transportation, and storage of contaminated materials,
which is typically the largest cost component of conventional remediation.

Since fungi have the ability to reduce complex hydrocarbons into elemental
compounds, the end products pose no threat to the environment. Indeed, these
former pollutants could even be considered "fertilizer" that helps rather than
harms the nutritional base of soils. The fungi convert toxic compounds into
biomass, carbon dioxide, water, and basic inorganic compounds.
