## Substrate Spawn Production

[[substrate-spawn]] represents the third generation (G3) in the culture expansion pipeline, serving as the final bridge between laboratory culture and [[pf-tek-maximum-fruiting-substrate-formula]]. By incorporating elements of the mycelium's preferred growing medium, substrate spawn acclimates the fungus to its future [[fruiting-environment-parameters]] while allowing cultivators to inoculate more fruiting substrate using less [[grain-spawn]].

## Why Substrate Spawn?

While some larger commercial growers use grain spawn (G2) directly for spawning fruiting substrates, most cultivators benefit from substrate spawn for several reasons:

- **Cost efficiency** — less grain spawn is needed per unit of fruiting substrate
- **Acclimation** — mycelium prefers to transition from "like to like," especially for wood-decomposing species. Mixing sawdust with elements of the intended fruiting medium helps the fungus adapt before facing competitors
- **Expanded coverage** — a given volume of grain spawn inoculates significantly more fruiting substrate when expanded through substrate spawn

The trade-off is slightly lower fruiting yields compared to direct grain spawning, but the economics of inoculating far more substrate usually outweigh this difference.

## The "Like to Like" Principle

Mycelium transitions most successfully between media that share characteristics. For wood-loving fungi cultured on grain and intended for fruiting on wood chips, creating substrate spawn using a blend of sawdust, powdered grain, and powdered wood chips mimics both the current and future [[mushroom-growing-environments]]. This staged acclimation reduces the metabolic shock of each transfer.

## Substrate Composition

### Base: Sawdust

- **Aged sawdust** works better than extremely fresh sawdust because it holds moisture more effectively
- Sawdust should be no more than **three months old** — older sawdust may have begun decomposing or harbor excessive microbial populations
- **Hardwood sawdust** is preferred for most cultivated species (oak, maple, beech, etc.)

### Supplementation

Substrate spawn benefits from nutritional supplementation to accelerate colonization once spawned into bulk fruiting substrates. Suitable supplements include whole cereal grains that retain their shape when cooked:

- Whole corn, wheat, rye, oat, millet, and milo — these act as "gas in the tank" for [[mycelium-running-conservation-biology-and-fungi]] through fruiting substrate
- **Wheat or rice bran** — a fluffy, flaky additive that gives mycelium a jump start on colonizing the spawn substrate (commonly used in small quantities)

### Recommended Recipe (per 5 lb bag)

- **5 lbs (2.36 kg)** soaked and drained hardwood sawdust
- **½ cup (~200 g)** hydrated wheat bran

### Risks of Over-Supplementation

Adding more supplements increases two significant risks:

1. **Contamination** — more nutritive media supports more competitor organisms
2. **Thermogenesis** — metabolic heat generation can exceed 100°F (38°C) internally, potentially cooking the mycelium

The safest approach is to supplement conservatively at the substrate spawn stage and reserve heavier supplementation for the fruiting media, which is either pasteurized or sterilized separately.

### Component Hydration Strategy

Some cultivators mix and soak sawdust, substrate, and supplements together. However, hydrating each component separately before mixing provides better control over individual particle moisture content. This is especially important for supplements and grains, which spoil much more quickly than sawdust when over-hydrated.

## Making Substrate Spawn Step-by-Step

### Step 1 — Soak the Sawdust

Soak sawdust in water for a few hours, up to overnight. **Do not exceed 12 hours** — extended soaking produces a sour smell indicating bacterial contamination.

A practical two-bin system for soaking:
- **Outer bin** — has a single drain plug and bricks on the interior bottom
- **Inner bin** — sits on the bricks, has ½-inch holes drilled in the bottom covered with door screening material
- Plug the drain, fill both bins with water, and allow 12–24 hour soak time
- Pull the plug to drain

### Step 2 — Drain

Allow sawdust to drain for about one hour until moist but not wet. Test by squeezing a handful:
- **Water squeezes out** → too wet
- **Doesn't stick to hands** → too dry
- **Sticks together, no water drops** → correct moisture

### Step 3 — Bag and Supplement

Load heat-tolerant filter bags with the drained sawdust and add supplements appropriate for the target species. For the standard recipe, add ½ cup hydrated wheat bran per 5 lbs sawdust.

### Step 4 — Seal Bags

Fold tops over, press gently to force out air, and secure with wooden clothespins or similar closures.

### Step 5 — Sterilize

Process bags in an autoclave or [[double-layer-tinfoil-jar-covering-pressure-cooker-method]] at 15 PSI (~250°F / 121°C) for **1.5 to 2 hours**.

### Step 6 — Prepare Workspace

While sterilizing, sanitize the [[laminar-flow-hood]], run HEPA ventilation for one hour, and lay out all tools and grain spawn for inoculation.

### Step 7 — Cool

Once pressure drops to slightly above 0 PSI, remove bags and place on the laminar hood close to the filter. Allow to cool below 90°F (32°C).

### Step 8 — Inoculate

Use roughly **1 cup of colonized grain spawn per 5 lb bag** of [[oyster-mushroom-non-sterilized-substrate-cultivation]]. More inoculum means faster colonization; less inoculum increases the risk of slow colonization and contamination.

### Step 9 — Label and Incubate

Shake bags to distribute grains, label with generation code and donor origin, and store at 65–75°F (18–24°C). Higher temperatures promote bacterial growth.

## Record Keeping and Generation Tracking

Every plate, jar, and bag should be labeled with a complete tracking system:

### Origin Codes

Each isolate receives a shorthand origin code (e.g., "PDJAMOR1" instead of "Tissue culture #1 from [[pleurotus-djamor]] collected on 7/1/2013 from Red Maple at the southeastern base of Big Mountain"). A master log maintains the full source information for all codes.

### Generation Numbering

- **G0** — Original pure isolate (tissue culture or spore germination)
- **G1** — First expansion (master grain spawn, liquid culture, etc.)
- **G2** — Second expansion
- **G3** — Substrate spawn
- Each subsequent expansion increments the number

### Individual Tracking Codes

An alternating letter-number system makes codes easy to read:

- First batch of G2 from G1: `2-A`, `2-B`, `2-C`, `2-D`...
- Expanding jar `2-B` to G3 bags: `3-B1`, `3-B2`, `3-B3`...

### Lab Notebook Entries

Each entry should record:
- Date of transfer
- Strain number or mushroom type with origin code
- Generation code, substrate, and date of the source culture
- Span of codes assigned to the new batch

Example entry:
```
Today's Date: 7-15-13
Pink oyster (PDJAMOR1) 2-B / Grain / 7-1-13
Transferred to 3-A1 to 3-A10 sawdust (formula)
```

This tracking enables cultivators to identify which stock cultures produce the best results and determine optimal generation cutoffs for quality [[spawn-production]].

## Strain Senescence

As fungal cells replicate, their DNA must divide and copy into new cells. Despite proofreading mechanisms, accumulated genetic errors progressively weaken the culture:

- **Lost vigor and efficiency** — cells far from their original spore germination develop mutations
- **Reduced yields** — key fruiting traits diminish
