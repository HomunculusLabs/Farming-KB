## Core Design Philosophy

The fundamental purpose of an AACT brewer is to maintain aerobic conditions while extracting and multiplying [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]] from compost. Every design decision should serve this primary goal. Ingham emphasizes that the brewer is essentially a biological incubator, not a mixing machine. The engineering requirements follow directly from the biological needs of the organisms being cultivated.

A successful AACT brewer must accomplish three things simultaneously:

1. Maintain [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]] levels above 6.0 mg/L throughout the entire brew cycle
2. Keep compost particles in suspension without damaging [[fungal-hyphae]]
3. Provide uniform oxygen distribution throughout the entire liquid volume

Failure in any one of these areas results in tea of inferior quality, regardless of the compost or food ingredients used.

## Tank Design and Geometry

### Tank Size and Compost Ratio

Ingham recommends a compost-to-water ratio of approximately 1 pound (0.45 kg) of high-quality compost per 5 gallons (19 liters) of water. This ratio provides sufficient microbial inoculum while leaving adequate water volume for organism reproduction. Overloading the tank with compost can deplete [[ingham-dissolved-oxygen-anaerobic-aact-brewing-management]] faster than the aeration system can replace it.

Brewer sizes typically range from 5 gallons for home gardeners to 500+ gallons for commercial operations. Regardless of size, the same principles of aeration capacity, compost ratio, and [[aact-aeration-oxygen-management-ingham]] apply. Larger brewers require proportionally more powerful air pumps and more carefully designed distribution systems.

### Tank Shape

Cylindrical or rectangular tanks with rounded corners are preferred over square tanks with sharp corners. Sharp corners create dead zones where water circulation is poor, leading to localized anaerobic conditions. The shape of the tank should promote gentle, circular water movement that keeps compost particles suspended without creating turbulent shear forces that could damage fungal hyphae.

Tank depth also matters. Deep, narrow tanks require more air pressure to achieve adequate oxygenation at the bottom, while shallow, wide tanks may have insufficient water depth to maintain stable temperatures. A depth-to-diameter ratio of approximately 1:1 to 1:1.5 is generally recommended.

### Material Considerations

Food-grade plastic tanks are the standard material for AACT brewers. Stainless steel is also acceptable but significantly more expensive. Ingham cautions against using galvanized metal tanks, as zinc leaching can be toxic to beneficial microorganisms. All materials in contact with the brew should be non-reactive and free of residues from previous use.

## Aeration System Design

### Air Pump Requirements

The air pump is the heart of any AACT brewing system. Pump capacity should be measured in terms of the volume of air delivered per minute relative to the water volume. Ingham's guidelines suggest a minimum of 0.05 CFM (cubic feet per minute) of air per gallon of water for adequate oxygenation. Commercial brewers typically use 0.08-0.12 CFM per gallon for reliable performance.

Air pumps should be rated for continuous duty operation, as AACT brews run for 24-36 hours. Diaphragm pumps are the most common type used, with some larger systems employing linear piston pumps. The pump should be located above the water level to prevent back-siphoning in the event of a power failure.

### Bubble Diffuser Types

The type of diffuser used to introduce air into the brew significantly affects oxygen transfer efficiency. Ingham's correspondence discusses several diffuser options:

**Fine bubble diffusers** produce small bubbles (0.5-2mm diameter) that provide maximum surface area for oxygen transfer. Fine bubbles also create gentle water movement that keeps compost suspended without damaging fungal hyphae. Air stones made from bonded silica or ceramic are the most common fine bubble diffusers.

**Coarse bubble diffusers** produce larger bubbles (3-6mm diameter) with less oxygen transfer efficiency per unit of air. However, coarse bubbles create more vigorous water movement, which can be beneficial in larger tanks where gentle circulation alone is insufficient to keep compost in suspension. Ingham generally recommends fine bubble diffusers for most applications, with coarse bubbles used as supplementary circulation aids in large systems.

**Flexible rubber membrane diffusers** offer a compromise between fine and coarse bubble options. They produce medium-fine bubbles and are self-cleaning to some degree, as the flexible membrane flexes during operation, shedding biofilm buildup.

### Diffuser Placement

Diffuser placement affects oxygen distribution uniformity throughout the tank. Ingham recommends placing diffusers at the bottom of the tank, offset from center, to create a circular flow pattern. Multiple diffusers may be needed in larger tanks to ensure complete coverage. The goal is to avoid any dead zones where water remains stagnant and oxygen levels drop below aerobic thresholds.

## Compost Containment Methods

### Nylon Stockings and Mesh Bags

Compost must be contained during brewing to prevent clogging of diffusers and plumbing. Ingham describes several containment methods, with nylon stockings being the simplest and most commonly used. The compost is placed in a clean nylon stocking, which is then submerged in the brewing water.

The mesh size of the containment material matters. Too fine a mesh restricts the movement of microorganisms out of the compost and into the tea. Too coarse a mesh allows large debris to escape. Ingham recommends a mesh opening of approximately 1/4 inch (6mm), which allows bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes to pass through while retaining larger compost particles.

### Filter Considerations

Filters at any point in the system (intake, outlet, or inline) can trap beneficial organisms, particularly fungi and protozoa. Ingham generally advises against using fine filters in AACT systems, preferring instead to allow the tea to be applied with whatever particulate matter remains. If filtration is necessary for specific application equipment, she recommends using the coarsest filter that still prevents clogging.

## Foam Management

Foam production during AACT brewing is a common occurrence that indicates active microbial metabolism. Proteins and surfactants produced by bacteria create foam at the water surface. While moderate foaming is normal and indicates a healthy brew, excessive foam can indicate overfeeding or the beginning of anaerobic conditions.

Ingham's recommendations for foam management include reducing food source quantities in subsequent brews, ensuring adequate aeration to maintain aerobic conditions, and avoiding the use of anti-foaming agents that could harm beneficial organisms. A thin layer of foam (1-2 inches) at the surface is considered normal and healthy during active brewing.
