# Dune Stabilisation and Revegetation

## Overview

Dune country represents both one of the most challenging and most water-rich environments in arid landscapes. Sands occur on pavements or as dunefields (sand seas), taking characteristic forms depending on wind intensity, duration, streamlines, velocity, and sand supply. Dunes function as enormous freshwater reservoirs—understanding their water-holding capacity and stabilisation techniques is essential for desert [[humus]] and dust particles) support quite dense vegetation (50% cover) while others are almost sterile

This freshwater lens functions much like the coral sands of an atoll holding rainwater that "floats" on salt water. At the edge of large dune complexes, water rushes and sedges appear at base seepage, and shallow lagoons form that are as much dune-fed as dune-dammed.

## Dune Classification by Type

- **Transverse dunes:** Regular ridges perpendicular to prevailing wind
- **Oblique dunes:** Formed at angles to wind direction
- **Longitudinal dunes:** Aligned with the wind direction
- **Barchans:** Crescent-shaped dunes with horns pointing downwind
- **Sand seas (draas):** Large wave-like forms with lobed advance edges

## Stabilisation Techniques

### Brush Fences

The primary stabilisation structure for severe blowouts and coastal dunes:

- **Brush fences** built from stakes 2 metres long, driven 1 metre deep, with numerous bushy weaves between
- **Spacing:** 7 metres apart in parallel rows across the main wind direction
- **Height:** 0.9–1.0 metres
- **Wind gaps:** Less than 50% to drop sand out effectively
- **Firm posts** driven every 3–4 metres

These fences provide measurable benefits:
- **Temperature reduction of 12°C** or more in the shade of fences
- **Moisture loss 1.8–3.0% less** than open areas

### Isolated Dune Planting

For dunes on pavement, planting proceeds after rains:

1. **Fast-growing grain or oat crop** (sorghum, millet, desert legumes such as [[moth-bean]]) or yam beans sown first
2. **Hardy [[neem]] tree leaf powder, or magnesite)
2. Pass the stiff mass through a mincer with blades removed
3. Extrude onto a vibrating tray with slight slope, dusted with dry powder
4. Pellets dry into round forms that can be broadcast from the air
5. Pelleted seed is not eaten by birds or insects
6. Many pellets will be buried by sand, and if heavy rain occurs, some will grow

### Pitting

Wherever rain flows or falls over bare sandy ground:

- **Large discs** with one-quarter section cut off, or a small disc fixed to a wheel, are drawn cross-country
- Seed and fertiliser are spread soon after
- Seed, fertiliser, and sand blow into the pits, responding well to subsequent rain
- This creates a no-runoff situation, stops dust storms, and provides excellent seed sites
- Many hectares of these pits have grown successfully near Alice Springs in Central Australia

### Chinese Straw Mat Method

In China, advanced techniques include:

- **Rice-straw mats** used as fencing or surface stabilisers
- **Advanced trees in baskets** planted basket-and-all in the matting shelter
- Pioneer trees provide shelter and mulch for later forestry and crop establishment

## Planting Depth Strategy

Lethal soil temperatures are rarely formed below 15 cm in dunes. Trees placed in holes 15 cm or deeper, stabilised from collapse by a woven basket or cardboard collar, are protected from being "cooked" until they can cast their own shade.

**Critical fertiliser timing:** Fertiliser should be thinly placed at planting, as rapid water infiltration during rains can carry it below root reach. After the tree has spread a root web, more fertiliser can be added and will be taken up by the plant. Any pioneering plants must tolerate stem burial until the area stabilises.

## Surface Cementing Methods

Any cementing system helps stabilise dune surfaces:

- **Water** creates bonds between particles requiring much higher wind speeds to move sand
- **Salt** (especially where dew is present, as salt is hygroscopic) bonds particles
- **Tars, oils, or glues** such as latex can be applied in urgent cases
- **[[lichens]], bacteria, fungi, and algal mats** form naturally
- **Salt crusts** naturally prevent dune formation in some deserts

These often delicate desert crusts are critical to stability. When hoofed animals, fast vehicles, or agriculture disturb them, wind erosion may quickly follow.

## Long-Term Protection

Once stabilised, dunes must be protected from:
- **Recreation vehicles** (devastation)
- **Heavy browsing** by livestock
- **Badly aligned roads** that disrupt wind patterns
- **Sand mining** to windward

Sawdust and mulch in hollows are very beneficial. Mill wastes (bark, sawdust) have helped establish [[acacia]] sophorae forests in coastal dunes in Tasmania. All plants in dunes benefit from nitrogenous fertiliser, phosphate, and trace elements, as old dunes are usually deficient in plant nutrients.

## Chevron Banks in Dune Swales

Side-cast banks help hold flow down dune swales. Stabilised areas within parallel dune series become potential tree-growing areas, especially where swales have a slope. Dune on pavement or salted ground represents the best site for permanent plantings—the primary challenges are establishing trees and stabilising the dune surface.
