# Substrate Preparation and Pasteurization The substrate is the bulk material on which mushroom mycelium grows and eventually fruits. Unlike grain spawn, which serves as a nursery phase, the substrate must provide sufficient mass and nutrition to support a full flush of mushrooms. [[query-what-are-the-best-mushroom-substrates-for-different-species]] fall into three broad categories based on their nutritional
complexity:

**Low-nutrition substrates** include straw, cardboard, and hardwood sawdust.
These materials are primarily cellulose and lignin with minimal protein. They
are suitable for primary decomposers like oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.) and
can often be pasteurized rather than sterilized because the low nutrient density
does not support aggressive contaminant growth.

**Medium-nutrition substrates** combine a base material (straw or sawdust) with
supplements like bran, gypsum, or coffee grounds. The added nutrition boosts
yields but also invites contamination, requiring more careful processing.

**High-nutrition substrates** include composted manure, enriched straw, and

to raise the pH above 8, which further suppresses bacterial competitors.

## Common Substrate Recipes

**Oyster mushrooms on straw:** Chopped wheat straw hydrated to field capacity.
No supplements needed for decent yields. Pasteurize. Simplest entry point for
beginners.

**Shiitake on supplemented sawdust:** Hardwood sawdust (oak, beech, or maple)
at 75 to 80 percent with wheat bran at 15 to 20 percent and gypsum at 1 to 2
percent. Hydrate to 60 to 65 percent moisture. Sterilize. This is the industry
standard for commercial shiitake production.

**Lion's mane on master's mix:** Hardwood sawdust at 50 percent and soybean
hulls at 50 percent by dry weight. This exceptionally high-yield recipe
produces massive fruiting bodies. Must be sterilized.

**King oyster on enriched straw:** Wheat straw supplemented with 10 to 15
percent wheat bran. Pasteurization works but sterilization gives more
consistent results due to the bran.

## Moisture Content

Substrate moisture content targets 55 to 65 percent by weight, measured as
(wet weight minus dry weight) divided by wet weight. The squeeze test remains
the most practical field method: a handful of substrate squeezed firmly should
yield only a few drops of water. Dripping freely means too wet; no moisture at
all means too dry. Both extremes reduce yields and increase contamination risk.
