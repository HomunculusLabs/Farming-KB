# Substrate Preparation and Pasteurization

The substrate is the bulk material on which mushroom mycelium grows and
eventually fruits. Unlike grain spawn, which serves as a nursery phase, the
substrate must provide sufficient mass and nutrition to support a full flush of
mushrooms. [[substrate-preparation]] — particularly the choice between [[sterilization]]
and pasteurization — is one of the most important decisions in a cultivation
cycle.

## Substrate Categories

Mushroom substrates fall into three broad categories based on their nutritional
complexity:

**Low-nutrition substrates** include straw, cardboard, and hardwood sawdust.
These materials are primarily cellulose and lignin with minimal protein. They
are suitable for primary decomposers like oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.) and
can often be pasteurized rather than sterilized because the low nutrient density
does not support aggressive contaminant growth.

**Medium-nutrition substrates** combine a base material (straw or sawdust) with
supplements like bran, gypsum, or coffee grounds. The added nutrition boosts
yields but also invites contamination, requiring more careful processing.

**High-nutrition substrates** include composted manure, enriched straw, and
supplemented sawdust blocks used for shiitake and lion's mane. These require
full sterilization for reliable results.

## Pasteurization

Pasteurization heats substrate to 60 to 80 degrees Celsius for 30 to 120
minutes. This kills most mold spores and competitive bacteria while preserving
beneficial thermophilic organisms — particularly Bacillus species — that
continue to occupy ecological niches and suppress more aggressive contaminants.

The hot water bath is the simplest pasteurization method. Substrate (typically
chopped wheat straw) is submerged in water heated to 65 to 75 degrees Celsius
and held for 60 to 90 minutes, then drained and cooled. A washing machine drum
or insulated cooler works well as a vessel. Lime or wood ash is sometimes added
to raise the pH above 8, which further suppresses bacterial competitors.

For sawdust-based substrates, steam pasteurization is more common. Steam is
injected into a sealed chamber containing the substrate bags for 6 to 12 hours
at atmospheric pressure. This is gentler than pressure sterilization and
preserves more of the beneficial microflora.

## Sterilization

Sterilization at 15 PSI (121 degrees Celsius) eliminates all living organisms.
It is mandatory for supplemented substrates where the added nutrition (bran at
5 to 20 percent by dry weight) creates an open invitation to Trichoderma,
Aspergillus, and bacterial blooms. Autoclave runs of 2 to 3 hours are standard
for supplemented sawdust blocks.

The major downside of sterilization is that it creates a biological vacuum.
With no competing organisms present, any contaminant that lands on the
substrate during inoculation has unrestricted access to the nutrients. This is
why sterile technique during inoculation becomes increasingly critical as
substrate nutrition increases.

## Common Substrate Recipes

**Oyster mushrooms on straw:** Chopped wheat straw hydrated to field capacity.
No supplements needed for decent yields. Pasteurize. Simplest entry point for
beginners.

**Shiitake on supplemented sawdust:** Hardwood sawdust (oak, beech, or maple)
at 75 to 80 percent with wheat bran at 15 to 20 percent and gypsum at 1 to 2
percent. Hydrate to 60 to 65 percent moisture. Sterilize. This is the industry
standard for commercial shiitake production.

**Lion's mane on master's mix:** Hardwood sawdust at 50 percent and soybean
hulls at 50 percent by dry weight. This exceptionally high-yield recipe
produces massive fruiting bodies. Must be sterilized.

**King oyster on enriched straw:** Wheat straw supplemented with 10 to 15
percent wheat bran. Pasteurization works but sterilization gives more
consistent results due to the bran.

## Moisture Content

Substrate moisture content targets 55 to 65 percent by weight, measured as
(wet weight minus dry weight) divided by wet weight. The squeeze test remains
the most practical field method: a handful of substrate squeezed firmly should
yield only a few drops of water. Dripping freely means too wet; no moisture at
all means too dry. Both extremes reduce yields and increase contamination risk.

## Supplementation Principles

Adding nitrogen-rich supplements (bran, seed meal, coffee grounds) to a base
substrate can increase yields by 50 to 200 percent. However, every percentage
point of supplement increases contamination risk. The general principle is to
match supplementation level to the cleanliness of the growing environment: a
simple still air box might handle 10 percent bran, while a certified cleanroom
can push to 30 percent or more.

Gypsum (calcium sulfate) at 1 to 2 percent is nearly universal in substrate
recipes. It improves texture, buffers pH, and provides calcium and sulfur
without contributing nitrogen that would feed contaminants.
