# Fungal Weathering of Rocks and Minerals

Fungi are major agents of rock weathering and soil formation (pedogenesis), operating through biomechanical and biochemical mechanisms that transform mineral substrates and release essential nutrients. The field of geomycology encompasses all aspects of fungal interactions with geological and mineral substrates. Fungi have been components of microbial communities of many terrestrial environments since the Ordovician period (480-460 MYBP) (Heckman et al., 2001; Burford et al., 2003). Lithic fungal communities are ubiquitous in the geological environment and play many roles important in nutrient, metal, and carbon cycling (Gadd, 2000; Nannipieri et al., 2003).

## Biomechanical Weathering

Biomechanical weathering of minerals by fungi can be direct or indirect, with both mechanisms strongly connected to biochemical processes. All processes involved in biomechanical weathering are strongly connected with biochemical processes, which are believed to be much more important than mechanical mineral degradation (Kumar and Kumar, 1999; Burford et al., 2003).

### Direct Biomechanical Degradation

Direct biomechanical degradation occurs through extensive penetration by fungal hyphae into decayed rocks and by tunnelling into otherwise intact mineral matter. This penetration can occur along crystal planes, cleavage, cracks, and grain boundaries in sandstone, calcitic, and dolomitic rocks (Banfield et al., 1999; Kumar and Kumar, 1999; Sterflinger, 2000; Burford et al., 2003; Money, 2004). Fungal hyphae can exert considerable mechanical force, which derives from the osmotically generated turgor pressure within hyphae (Money, 2004). Biomechanical penetration into mineral matter is facilitated by thigmotropic reactions and lubrication with mucilaginous slime, which may contain acidic and metal-chelating metabolites (Burford et al., 2003).

### Thigmotropism

Thigmotropism (contact guidance) is a directed mode of fungal growth towards grooves, ridges, and pores in solid material. This mechanism may explain how fungal hyphae explore and exploit weakened sites in mineral surfaces (Watts et al., 1998). The ability of hyphae to sense and respond to surface topography at the microscale enables fungi to locate cracks, pores, and crystal boundaries where biochemical weathering is most effective.

### Indirect Biomechanical Weathering

Indirect biomechanical weathering can occur because of shrinking and swelling effects of the hydrated mucilage produced by many fungi (Warscheid and Krumbein, 1994). Repeated cycles of hydration and dehydration of hyphal mucilage can generate stresses that fracture mineral surfaces, contributing to physical disaggregation of rocks. These effects are particularly important in environments experiencing strong wet-dry cycles, such as desert and Mediterranean climates.

## Biochemical Weathering

Biochemical weathering, also termed "heterotrophic leaching," is considered much more important than mechanical mineral degradation. The main mechanisms of solubilization of minerals and insoluble metal compounds by fungi are acidolysis, complexolysis, and redoxolysis, all of which may be enhanced by metal accumulation in and/or around the fungal biomass (Burgstaller and Schinner, 1993; Burford et al., 2003; Gadd, 2004). The primary fungal impact on biogeochemical cycling appears to result from acidolysis and complexolysis.

### Acidolysis (Proton-Promoted Dissolution)

Acidolysis occurs when fungi acidify their microenvironment as a result of the excretion of protons and organic acids and the formation of carbonic acid resulting from respiratory CO2 (Burgstaller and Schinner, 1993). Many fungi are able to excrete metal-complexing metabolites associated with complexolysis or ligand-promoted dissolution (Burford et al., 2003). These include carboxylic acids, amino acids, siderophores, and phenolic compounds (Manley and Evans, 1986; Muller et al., 1995; Gadd, 1999). Organic acid excretion by fungi is inter- and intraspecific and can be strongly influenced by the presence of toxic metal minerals (Fomina et al., 2004). Acidolysis was the main mechanism of fungal dissolution of toxic metal phosphates grown in the presence of ammonium as a nitrogen source (Fomina et al., 2004, 2005b).

### Complexolysis (Ligand-Promoted Dissolution)

Fungal-derived carboxylic acids with strong chelating properties (e.g. oxalic and citric acid) perform aggressive attack on mineral surfaces (Gadd, 1999; Fomina et al., 2005a). Carboxylic acids provide a source of protons for solubilization and chelating anions, which can complex metal cations (Devevre et al., 1996). If the fungal culture is capable of excreting large amounts of a strong chelator (e.g. oxalate), the solubilization mechanism shifts from acidolysis to ligand-promoted dissolution (Fomina et al., 2004, 2005a). Oxalic and citric acids are among the most common fungal metabolites involved in mineral dissolution.

### Redoxolysis

Redoxolysis involves the oxidation or reduction of mineral components, changing their solubility. Fungi can oxidize manganese and iron in metal-bearing minerals such as siderite (FeCO3) and rhodochrosite (MnCO3) and precipitate them as oxides (Eckhardt, 1985; Grote and Krumbein, 1992). The reduced forms of metals and metalloids (e.g. elemental silver, selenium, tellurium) can be precipitated by many fungi, manifest by characteristic colony colouration.

## Mycorrhizal Weathering

### Ectomycorrhizal Mineral Dissolution

Ectomycorrhizal (EM) fungi are important agents of mineral weathering in forest soils. Biogeochemical activities of mycorrhizal fungi lead to changes in the physicochemical characteristics of the root environment and enhanced weathering of soil minerals, resulting in metal cation release (Olsson and Wallander, 1998; Lundstrom et al., 2000; Whitelaw, 2000). Dissolution of soil weatherable calcium-bearing minerals by EM fungi has been well documented (Callot et al., 1985; Lapeyrie et al., 1990, 1991).

EM mycelia respond to the presence of different soil silicate and phosphate minerals (apatite, quartz, potassium feldspar) by regulating their growth and activity -- colonization, carbon allocation, and substrate acidification (Rosling et al., 2004a, b). Carbon allocation within the mycelium was significantly greater in Hebeloma crustuliniforme/Pinus sylvestris ectomycorrhizas colonizing potassium feldspar patches than in those colonizing quartz patches (Rosling et al., 2004b).

### Organic Acid Excretion by Mycorrhizal Fungi

During growth, mycorrhizal fungi often excrete low-molecular-mass carboxylic acids (e.g. malic, succinic, gluconic, oxalic), contributing to "heterotrophic leaching" (Ahonen-Jonnarth et al., 2000; Martino et al., 2003; Fomina et al., 2004). In podzol E horizons under European coniferous forests, the weathering of hornblendes, feldspars, and granitic bedrock has been attributed to oxalic, citric, succinic, formic, and malic acid excretion by ectomycorrhizal hyphae. EM hyphal tips could produce micro- to millimolar concentrations of these organic acids and were associated with micropores (3-10 micrometres) in weatherable soil minerals (van Breemen et al., 2000; Smits et al., 2005).

### Specific Mycorrhizal Weathering Examples

- **Piloderma**: The EM fungus Piloderma was able to extract K and/or Mg from biotite, microcline, and chlorite to satisfy nutritional requirements, and precipitated mycogenic calcium oxalate crystals on the hyphae (Glowa et al., 2003)
- **Suillus granulatus and Paxillus involutus**: These EM fungi released elements from apatite and wood ash (K, Ca, Ti, Mn, Pb) and accumulated them in mycelia, especially rhizomorphs (Wallander et al., 2003)
- **Ericoid and ectomycorrhizal fungi**: Can dissolve cadmium-, copper-, zinc-, and lead-bearing minerals, including metal phosphates (Leyval and Joner, 2001; Martino et al., 2003; Fomina et al., 2004, 2005b)

## Secondary Mycogenic Mineral Precipitation

### Oxalate Biomineralization

Fungi produce metal oxalates with a variety of metals and metal-bearing minerals (Ca, Cd, Co, Cu, Mn, Sr, Zn, Ni, and Pb) (Arnott, 1995; Gadd, 1999, 2000; Sayer et al., 1999; Magyarosy et al., 2002; Fomina et al., 2005a). Calcium oxalate dihydrate (weddelite) and the more stable calcium oxalate monohydrate (whewellite) are the most common forms of oxalate in nature. Depending on physicochemical conditions, biotic fungal calcium oxalate can exhibit a variety of crystalline forms (tetragonal, bipyramidal, plate-like, rhombohedral or needles). Precipitation of calcium oxalate acts as a reservoir for calcium and influences phosphate availability (Gadd, 1993, 1999). Oxalate over-excreting Beauveria caledonica transformed cadmium, copper, lead, and zinc from toxic metal minerals into oxalates (Fomina et al., 2005a).

### Carbonate Precipitation

Microbial carbonate precipitation coupled with silicate weathering provides an important sink for CO2 in terrestrial environments (Verrecchia et al., 1990; Riding, 2000). Fungal filaments mineralized with calcite have been reported from limestone and calcareous soils worldwide (Kahle, 1977; Callot et al., 1985; Verrecchia, 2000). Calcium oxalate can be degraded to calcium carbonate in semi-arid environments, where such a process may act to cement pre-existing limestones. During decomposition of fungal hyphae, calcite crystals can act as sites of further secondary calcite precipitation (Verrecchia, 2000). Chitin, the major component of fungal cell walls, is a substrate on which calcite readily nucleates (Manoli et al., 1997). Other mycogenic minerals include birnessite, ferrihydrite, goethite, schwertmannite, and todorokite.

### Soil Aggregation

Precipitation of carbonates, phosphates, and hydroxides increases soil aggregation. Cations such as Si4+, Fe3+, Al3+, and Ca2+ stimulate precipitation of compounds that may act as bonding agents for soil particles. Hyphae can enmesh soil particles, alter alignment, and release organic metabolites that enhance aggregate stability (Bronick and Lal, 2005). These processes are fundamental to soil formation and the [[fungal-biogeochemistry]] of terrestrial ecosystems.

## Rock and Mineral Communities

Diverse microbial communities have been found living in the pore spaces of exposed rock from the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica (Torre et al., 2003), extreme hot and cold deserts (Staley et al., 1982; Sterflinger, 2000), and in rock substrates buried several kilometres below the Earth's surface (Kerr, 1997; Banfield et al., 1999). Darkly pigmented microcolonial fungi are common in diverse rock types across hot and cold deserts, growing as spherical clusters of tightly packed cells with thick pigmented walls (Gorbushina et al., 1993). A study utilizing RISA and ARISA on weathered pegmatitic granite found that average sample ribotype numbers ranged from 25 on K-feldspar to 4 on quartz, demonstrating that distinct fungal populations inhabit rocks of particular mineralogical types (Gleeson et al., 2005).

## Lichen Weathering

Lichens are commonly thought of as pioneer colonizers of fresh rock outcrops, and were possibly one of the earliest life forms to occupy Earth's land surfaces. An estimated 6% of the Earth's land surface is covered by lichen-dominated vegetation (Haas and Purvis, 2006). Globally, lichens play an important biogeochemical role in soil formation processes and rock weathering (Barker et al., 1997; Banfield et al., 1999). Lichens accumulate metals such as lead and copper, form metal-organic biominerals during growth on metal-rich substrates (Purvis and Halls, 1996), and contribute to mineral dissolution through oxalic acid excretion. Lichens are discussed further in [[lichen-biology-guide]] and [[lichen-biology-guide]].

## Environmental Significance

The environmental significance of fungal rock and mineral weathering operates at multiple scales. At the ecosystem level, fungal weathering releases essential nutrients (K, Ca, Mg, P, Fe, Mn) from primary minerals, making them available for plant uptake. At the global scale, silicate weathering coupled with carbonate precipitation provides a long-term sink for atmospheric CO2. Fungal weathering also has practical implications: the mineral-solubilizing activity of fungi may affect in situ methods of chemical remediation, such as phosphate-induced metal stabilization (formation of hopeite, pyromorphite). Assessment of long-term environmental consequences of remediation technologies should take into account the biogeochemical activity of soil fungal communities (Sayer et al., 1995, 1999; Fomina et al., 2004, 2005a).
