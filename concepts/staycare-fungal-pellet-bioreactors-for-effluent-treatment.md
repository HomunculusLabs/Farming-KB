# Fungal Pellet Bioreactors for Effluent Treatment

Fungal pellet bioreactors represent an important technology for applying
[[fungal-treatment-of-industrial-wastewaters]] effluents. Mycelial pellets,
formed naturally or through immobilization in matrices such as alginate or
polyurethane, offer practical advantages over free mycelium including
improved mass transfer, ease of handling, mechanical stability, and the
ability to maintain biomass activity over extended operational periods.

## Bioreactor Configurations

Multiple bioreactor designs have been developed for fungal effluent
treatment. Rotating biological contactors (RBCs) with organisms growing on
slowly rotating discs were among the earliest configurations. Percolating
filters and packed beds have also been employed extensively. More recently,
fluidized bed reactors using mycelial pellets have proven highly effective.
Stirred tank reactors are less commonly used because of concerns about
shear forces damaging enzymes or mycelial structure. Most researchers
prefer aeration or air-lift techniques that provide both mixing and
aeration without mechanical agitation.

## Soil Remediation with Coated Pellets

Pelleted solid substrates coated with alginate suspensions of fungal
propagules have been developed for soil remediation. The pellets are
incubated until overgrown with mycelium. Spray coating does not reduce
viability or affect the mechanical strength of the pellets. When introduced
into unsterile soil microcosms spiked with pentachlorophenol (PCP),
mycelium-coated pellets of [[bjerkandera-adusta]], and T.
versicolor removed over 80% of the contaminant within 4 weeks. Coated
pellets without established [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]] showed significantly less
removal, confirming the importance of active mycelium for pollutant
transformation.

## Batch versus Continuous Operation

Both batch and continuous bioreactor modes can be effective for effluent
treatment, each with distinct advantages. Batch processes are more likely
to achieve complete treatment and may have kinetic advantages when
decolorization follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics. When effluents contain
toxic components that are themselves degraded by the fungus, continuous
treatment may be advantageous as it prevents toxicant accumulation. For
non-toxic effluents where decolorization rate increases with substrate
concentration, batch treatment is generally preferred.

## Repeated Use and Longevity

White rot mycelia are sufficiently rugged to withstand repeated use over
many cycles of treatment, covering periods of several weeks to months.
Both fed-batch and continuous reactors have demonstrated sustained activity.
Mycelia can be stored for several months at 4 degrees C and still retain
full decolorization activity. Two strains of white rot fungi maintained
activity for 4 months in storage, demonstrating a useful property for
industrial situations requiring treatment at short notice.

## Immobilized versus Free Mycelium

Immobilization of fungal mycelia as pellets in alginate or polyurethane
has been shown to give results as good as or better than free mycelium.
Immobilization may stabilize enzymes and protect biomass from shear forces
and contamination. However, some initial loss of activity is typically
associated with the immobilization process. For large-scale
decolorization, intact mycelia generally offer more practical advantages
