# Hallucinogenic Drugs

Hallucinogenic drugs, also known as psychedelics or psychotomimetics, are a
class of psychoactive substances that produce profound alterations in
perception, mood, and cognitive processes. These compounds have been used for
millennia in religious, spiritual, and healing contexts and have been the
subject of extensive scientific investigation since the mid-twentieth century.
The term "hallucinogen" refers to their ability to induce hallucinations,
though this label is somewhat misleading as the effects extend far beyond
simple sensory distortion to encompass fundamental changes in consciousness.

## Classification

Hallucinogenic drugs are broadly classified into several pharmacological
families based on their chemical structure and mechanism of action:

**Classic Psychedelics (Serotonergic)**: LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide),
psilocybin, DMT (N,N-dimethyltryptamine), and mescaline are tryptamine or
phenethylamine derivatives that primarily act as agonists at the 5-HT2A
serotonin receptor. They produce characteristic visual hallucinations, altered
sense of time, synesthesia, and ego dissolution at sufficient doses. Despite
structural differences, these compounds share a remarkably similar subjective
effects profile.

**Dissociatives**: Ketamine, PCP (phencyclidine), and DXM (dextromethorphan)
act primarily as NMDA receptor antagonists. They produce a sense of detachment
from the body and environment, analgesia, and at higher doses, profound
altered states resembling near-death experiences. Ketamine has found increasing
clinical use as a rapid-acting antidepressant, particularly for treatment-
resistant depression.

**Empathogens-Entactogens**: MDMA (3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine) primarily
affects serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine release, producing feelings of
emotional openness, empathy, and reduced fear while maintaining relative
clarity of consciousness. MDMA occupies a unique position between classical
psychedelics and stimulants.

**Cannabinoids**: Cannabis and its active compounds, particularly THC, can
produce hallucinogenic effects at higher doses, though their primary
pharmacology involves CB1 receptor activation. The psychedelic effects of
cannabis are variable and dose-dependent.

**Deliriants**: Atropine, scopolamine, and datura produce true hallucinations
that are typically indistinguishable from reality, accompanied by confusion,
amnesia, and potentially dangerous physical effects. Unlike other
hallucinogens, deliriants are rarely used intentionally for therapeutic or
spiritual purposes due to their unpredictable and often unpleasant effects.

## Neuropharmacology

The primary mechanism of classical hallucinogens involves partial agonism at
the 5-HT2A serotonin receptor, which is densely expressed in the cerebral
cortex, particularly in layer V pyramidal neurons. Recent research using fMRI
and EEG has revealed that psychedelics dramatically reduce functional
connectivity within the default mode network (DMN), a brain system associated
with self-referential thinking and the sense of ego. This DMN disruption
correlates with the subjective experience of ego dissolution and is thought
to underlie many therapeutic effects.

Additionally, psychedelics increase global brain connectivity and entropy,
allowing regions that normally do not communicate to interact. This increased
neural flexibility may facilitate the revision of entrenched cognitive and
emotional patterns, effectively opening a "window of plasticity" through which
therapeutic change can occur.

## Historical Use

The use of hallucinogenic substances in human culture extends back thousands
of years. Archaeological evidence from sites in Peru, Mexico, and the Sahara
suggests ritual use of psychoactive plants as early as 5000 BCE. Indigenous
cultures across the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Europe developed sophisticated
traditions around the ceremonial use of psychedelics, often within shamanic or
religious frameworks. The Eleusinian Mysteries of ancient Greece, for example,
may have involved the use of ergot-derived compounds.

The modern scientific era began in 1943 when Albert Hofmann accidentally
discovered the psychoactive effects of LSD, which he had first synthesized
five years earlier at Sandoz Laboratories in Switzerland. This discovery
launched two decades of intensive research into the therapeutic potential of
hallucinogens before their prohibition in the late 1960s.

## Dose-Response Effects

The effects of hallucinogenic drugs are highly dose-dependent and can be
roughly categorized by intensity:

- **Sub-perceptual doses (microdoses)**: Minimal detectable effects, with
  users reporting subtle enhancements in mood, creativity, and focus.
- **Low doses**: Mild perceptual changes, heightened sensory awareness,
  slight mood elevation and increased sociability.
- **Moderate doses**: Clear hallucinogenic effects including visual
  distortions, altered thought patterns, emotional intensity.
- **High doses**: Complete alteration of consciousness, ego dissolution,
  mystical-type experiences, potentially overwhelming perceptual overload.

## Therapeutic Potential

Contemporary research has rekindled scientific interest in the therapeutic
potential of hallucinogenic drugs, with particular attention to treatment-
resistant depression, PTSD, substance use disorders, and end-of-life anxiety.
The mechanisms underlying therapeutic benefit are thought to include enhanced
emotional processing, increased psychological flexibility, and the induction
of transformative peak experiences that can shift fundamental patterns of
thinking and behavior.

## Safety Profile

Classical psychedelics are generally considered to have low physiological
toxicity and are not associated with dependence or addiction. However,
psychological risks exist, including challenging experiences, precipitation of
psychotic episodes in vulnerable individuals, and hallucinogen persisting
perception disorder. Proper set, setting, and integration support are
essential.
