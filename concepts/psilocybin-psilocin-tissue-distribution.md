# Psilocybin and Psilocin Tissue Distribution

The distribution of psilocybin and psilocin between the cap (pileus) and stem (stipe) of *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]* is not uniform. Bigwood and Beug (1982) demonstrated that the concentration of these alkaloids differs significantly between cap and stem tissues, and that the distribution pattern varies between strains. Understanding this distribution is important for both analytical methodology (which tissue to sample) and practical use (which part of the mushroom contains the most active compounds).

## Cap vs. Stem Concentrations

Bigwood and Beug analyzed caps and stems separately from first-flush mushrooms of three different strains of *P. cubensis* grown on rye-grain substrate:

### M.R. Strain (First Flush)

| Tissue | Psilocybin (mg/g) | Psilocin (mg/g) |
|--------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Caps | 6.7 | 0 |
| Stems | 4.2 | 0.35 |

### Ecuadorian Strain (First Flush)

| Tissue | Psilocybin (mg/g) | Psilocin (mg/g) |
|--------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Caps | 7.6 | 0 |
| Stems | 4.7 | 0.4 |

### Amazon Strain (First Flush)

| Tissue | Psilocybin (mg/g) | Psilocin (mg/g) |
|--------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Caps | 5.7 | 0.1 |
| Stems | 5.7 | 0 |

## Key Observations

### Psilocybin Concentration

Two distinct [[fungal-biogeography-distribution-patterns]] were observed for psilocybin:

1. **Cap-enriched strains** (M.R. and Ecuadorian): Caps contained approximately 1.6× more psilocybin per gram dry weight than stems. In the M.R. strain, caps had 6.7 mg/g vs. stems at 4.2 mg/g. In the Ecuadorian strain, caps had 7.6 mg/g vs. stems at 4.7 mg/g.

2. **Equal distribution strain** (Amazon): Caps and stems contained equal amounts of psilocybin (5.7 mg/g each). This strain showed no tissue-specific concentration pattern for psilocybin.

The fact that different strains show different cap-to-stem ratios indicates that tissue-specific alkaloid distribution is a genetically controlled trait rather than a universal feature of the species.

### Psilocin Concentration

Psilocin distribution showed a pattern that was largely the inverse of psilocybin:

1. **M.R. and Ecuadorian strains**: The small amount of psilocin present was **entirely in the stems**. Caps contained zero detectable psilocin, while stems had 0.35–0.4 mg/g.

2. **Amazon strain**: A trace of psilocin (0.1 mg/g) was found in the cap, while the stem contained none. This strain showed the opposite pattern from the other two.

The Amazon strain's distribution is notable because it also differed from the other strains in [[sandoz-psilocybin-distribution-program]] (equal rather than cap-enriched), suggesting that the genetic factors controlling tissue-specific alkaloid localization affect both compounds.

## Biosynthetic Implications

The tissue-specific distribution patterns have several implications for understanding psilocybin/psilocin biosynthesis:

### Site of Synthesis

The cap-enrichment of psilocybin in some strains is consistent with the gills (lamellae) of the pileus being a primary site of alkaloid biosynthesis. The gills contain the basidia — the spore-producing cells where meiosis occurs — and are [[isolation-metabolically-active-arbuscules-intraradical-hyphae]]. If the biosynthetic pathway for psilocybin is most active in basidial tissue, higher concentrations in the cap would be expected.

However, the Amazon strain's equal distribution between cap and stem suggests that [[acetyl-protection-route-psilocin-psilocybin-synthesis]] can also occur in stipe tissue, or that psilocybin is efficiently transported from the cap to the stem in some strains.

### Psilocin as a Conversion Product

The presence of psilocin exclusively in the stems of some strains (while absent from caps) could indicate:

- **Dephosphorylation site**: The phosphatase enzyme that converts psilocybin to psilocin may be more active in stipe tissue
- **Transport and conversion**: Psilocybin synthesized in the cap may be transported to the stipe and partially dephosphorylated there
- **Different storage compartments**: Psilocin may be sequestered in different cellular compartments in the stipe vs. cap, with analytical detection reflecting the net balance of synthesis, conversion, and degradation

### First-Flush Limitations

These distribution data are from first-flush mushrooms only. Given that psilocin levels increase dramatically [[psilocin-emergence-in-later-flushes]] (see [[psilocybin-psilocin-flush-variation]]), the tissue distribution pattern may also change [[psilocin-accumulation-pattern-across-flushes]]. It is possible that later-flush mushrooms have more psilocin in caps, or that the cap-stem ratio shifts as the culture ages.

## Analytical Methodology Considerations

The tissue-specific distribution has important implications for researchers measuring alkaloid levels:

- **Whole-mushroom homogenates**: Most studies analyze entire dried mushrooms (caps + stems ground together). This gives a weighted average that depends on the cap-to-stem mass ratio, which varies with strain, flush number, and growing conditions.
- **Cap-only analysis**: Would give higher psilocybin readings for cap-enriched strains but might miss psilocin that is concentrated in stems.
- **Stem-only analysis**: Would give lower psilocybin readings for cap-enriched strains but might show psilocin levels not detectable in cap tissue.
- **Reporting standard**: Analytical studies should always specify whether they analyzed caps, stems, or whole mushrooms, and should report the cap-to-stem mass ratio of their samples.
