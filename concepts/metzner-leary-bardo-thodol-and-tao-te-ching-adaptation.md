# Leary's Adaptation of the Bardo Thodol and Tao Te Ching

Ralph Metzner's 1998 review documents Timothy Leary's pioneering work
in adapting Eastern spiritual texts as practical guidebooks for
psychedelic experience. These adaptations represented a fundamental
shift in how the psychedelic experience was understood — from a
psychological phenomenon to be studied to a spiritual journey to be
navigated with the aid of ancient contemplative wisdom.

## The Influence of Huxley, Smith, and Watts

Leary's turn toward Eastern spiritual frameworks was significantly
shaped by his association with three major intellectual figures:

- **Aldous Huxley**, who documented his mescaline experience in *The
  Doors of Perception* (1954) and who provided Leary with his first
  mescaline experience. Huxley's framework of the mind as a "reducing
  valve" that filters a larger reality profoundly influenced Leary's
  understanding of what psychedelics reveal.
- **Huston Smith**, the eminent scholar of comparative religion, whose
  expertise in mapping the world's mystical traditions provided
  intellectual credibility for the comparative approach.
- **Alan Watts**, the philosopher and interpreter of Eastern traditions
  for Western audiences, whose elegant articulations of Zen, Taoism,
  and Vedanta made these frameworks accessible and attractive.

Stimulated by these associations, Leary "devoted considerable time and
energy to exploring and describing the spiritual and religious dimensions
of psychedelic experience." This was a natural extension of his
recognition that the experiences went far beyond the therapeutic
framework in which they had initially been investigated at Harvard.

## The Tibetan Buddhist Bardo Thodol

Leary's first major adaptation was *The Psychedelic Experience: A Manual
Based on the Tibetan Book of the Dead*, published in 1964 with Ralph
Metzner and Richard Alpert. The bardo thodol — literally "Liberation
Through Hearing in the Intermediate State" — is a Tibetan Buddhist text
designed to guide the consciousness of the dying person through the
various stages (bardos) that follow death, helping them achieve
liberation rather than rebirth.

Leary recognized that the sequence of experiences described in the Bardo
Thodol closely paralleled the stages of a psychedelic session. The text's
instructions for maintaining awareness, avoiding attachment to visionary
phenomena, and recognizing the clear light of reality could be directly
applied as a navigational framework for the psychedelic journey. Each
bardo became a model for a different phase of the psychedelic experience:

- The **Chikhai Bardo** (clear light) mapped onto the initial ego
  dissolution and encounter with radiance
- The **Chonyid Bardo** (visions) corresponded to the hallucinatory
  phase with its kaleidoscopic imagery
- The **Sidpa Bardo** (rebirth) paralleled the return to ordinary
  consciousness and the challenge of integrating the experience

Metzner notes a deeper structural connection: both tibetan buddhism
and Chinese Taoism "had strong shamanic elements" — "practices of
connecting with spirits of nature through special visionary states were
integrated into the teachings concerning spiritual development and
liberation." This shamanic substrate made these traditions particularly
adaptable to the psychedelic context. The visionary states described
in these texts were not merely metaphors but experiential realities
accessible through specific contemplative technologies — technologies
that psychedelics could amplify and accelerate.

## The Tao Te Ching Adaptation

Leary's second major adaptation was *Psychedelic Prayers and Other
Meditations*, which reinterpreted the tao te ching of Laozi as
a guidebook for psychedelic experience. The Tao Te Ching's emphasis on
wu-wei (non-action), the return to the uncarved block, and the
paradoxical nature of ultimate reality resonated deeply with the non-
ordinary states of consciousness that psychedelics facilitate.

The Taoist framework offered something the Buddhist framework did not:
a positive valuation of the natural world and the body. Where Buddhism
tended toward world-denial and the transcendence of samsara, Taoism
embraced the natural as an expression of the Tao itself. This made it
particularly suitable for the kind of nature-mystical experiences that
psychedelics frequently produce — experiences of unity with the natural
world that Buddhist frameworks might interpret as obstacles to
liberation.

## Evolutionary Remembering

Metzner credits Leary as "the first person to recognize and articulate
that the fundamental mystical vision that emerges in these states is an
**evolutionary remembering** — an experience of reconnecting with our
biological and cosmological evolution."

This concept was transformative because it located the psychedelic
experience within a scientific framework rather than a purely religious
one. The mystical vision was not a retreat from reality but a
reconnection with the deepest realities of biological and cosmic
existence. "The language of mystics and shamans in our time was
basically going to be the scientific language of evolution."

## Significance and Legacy

These adaptations established a template that has been enormously
influential: the use of established contemplative frameworks as
practical guides for psychedelic experience. This approach has been
taken up by contemporary psychedelic researchers and therapists who
draw on Buddhist mindfulness practices, yoga, and other contemplative
traditions to structure and integrate psychedelic sessions.

The Bardo Thodol adaptation in particular became one of the most widely
read psychedelic texts and introduced generations of psychedelic
explorers to the idea that these experiences could be approached with
reverence, discipline, and systematic guidance rather than mere
recreational curiosity.
