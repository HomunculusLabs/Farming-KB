# Lichenized Fungi Diversity

## Overview

Lichenized fungi (lichens) are symbiotic organisms consisting of a fungal partner (mycobiont) and a photosynthetic partner (photobiont — green algae or cyanobacteria). With over 17,000 described species of lichen-forming fungi, lichens represent approximately one-fifth of all described Ascomycota and constitute one of the most successful fungal lifestyles on Earth. They colonize substrates ranging from arctic tundra to tropical canopy, from bare rock to living leaves.

## Taxonomic Diversity

### Dominant Groups

The overwhelming majority of lichen-forming fungi belong to the Ascomycota, with relatively few Basidiomycota forming lichen-like associations. Major orders of lichenized fungi include:

- **Lecanorales**: 7,150 species — the majority of all lichen-forming fungi; includes the most diverse and widespread lichen groups
- **Arthoniales**: Approximately 1,000 species; includes bark-dwelling crustose lichens
- **Dothideales**: Approximately 120 species of lichenized fungi
- **Gyalectales**: Approximately 100 species; typically small, inconspicuous lichens

### Uncharted Diversity

Lichen taxonomy remains dynamic, with many species complexes awaiting revision. Molecular phylogenetic studies increasingly reveal cryptic species within morphologically uniform lichen taxa. The true diversity of lichenized fungi likely substantially exceeds currently described numbers.

## Ecology and Distribution

### Habitat Range

Lichens colonize an extraordinary range of habitats and substrates:

- **Rock surfaces** (saxicolous): From coastal splash zones to alpine summits
- **Tree bark** (corticolous): On trunks and branches in forests worldwide
- **Soil** (terricolous): In diverse ecosystems from arctic tundra to tropical savannas
- **Leaves** (foliicolous): Especially diverse in humid tropical forests
- **Wood** (lignicolous): On decaying logs and stumps

### Climatic Amplitude

Lichens tolerate greater climatic extremes than most other organisms. They dominate terrestrial vegetation in polar and alpine regions, where higher plants cannot survive. In arid regions, lichens contribute significantly to soil crust communities. In humid tropical forests, foliicolous lichens reach their maximum diversity.

### Sensitivity to Air Quality

Many lichens are highly sensitive to air pollution, particularly sulfur dioxide and nitrogen compounds. Lichen community composition is widely used as a bioindicator of air quality. Pollution-sensitive species decline or disappear in contaminated areas, while tolerant species may persist.

## Growth Forms

Lichen growth forms reflect both taxonomy and environment:

- **Crustose**: Tightly attached to substrate; most diverse but least conspicuous group
- **Foliose**: Leaf-like, loosely attached; includes many conspicuous and well-known species
- **Fruticose**: Shrubby or hair-like, often pendulous; includes iconic genera like Cladonia and Usnea
- **Squamulose**: Small, scale-like; intermediate between crustose and foliose

## Photobiont Diversity

Lichen photobionts include green algae (primarily Trebouxia, Trentepohlia, and Coccomyxa) and cyanobacteria (primarily Nostoc). Some lichens contain both green algal and cyanobacterial photobionts. Photobiont identity influences lichen ecology, as cyanobacterial lichens can fix atmospheric nitrogen while green algal lichens cannot.

## Lichen Endophytes

Lichens harbor diverse communities of non-lichenized fungal endophytes. One study recovered 506 endophyte taxa from just 17 fruticose lichen samples, revealing lichen endophytes as a remarkably diverse and understudied group. Some lichenicolous (lichen-inhabiting) fungi are obligate parasites of specific lichen hosts.

## Lichens in Human Culture and Traditional Use

Lichens have been used by humans for millennia for food, medicine, dye, and other practical purposes:
- **Food:** Several lichen species are edible, though most require extensive processing to remove bitter lichen acids. *Cetraria islandica* (Iceland moss) was a traditional food in northern Europe during famine periods, boiled to extract acids and then dried, ground, and mixed with flour. *Cladonia rangiferina* (reindeer moss) forms the primary winter food of reindeer in Arctic regions, supporting indigenous herding cultures across Scandinavia and Siberia.
- **Dye:** Lichens were historically important sources of dye for textiles. Orchil (orcein dye) was extracted from *Roccella* and *Lasallia* species and produced purple and red colors prized in medieval Europe. Litmus, derived from *Roccella tinctoria*, remains one of the most widely used pH indicators in laboratories worldwide. Lichen dyes require an ammonia fermentation process that can take weeks to months.
- **Medicine:** Usnic acid from *Usnea* species has documented antibacterial and antifungal properties and is used in commercial antibiotic preparations in some countries. *Cetraria islandica* preparations are used in European herbal medicine for respiratory complaints. Many traditional cultures use lichen preparations for wound healing, digestive ailments, and skin conditions.
- **Perfume:** Oakmoss (*Evernia prunastri*) and tree moss (*Pseudevernia furfuracea*) are essential fixative ingredients in the perfume industry, providing the characteristic base notes of chypre and fougere fragrance families. The global harvest of these lichens for perfume production has raised conservation concerns in Mediterranean Europe.

## Lichens and Nitrogen Fixation

Lichens with cyanobacterial photobionts (cyanolichens) are ecologically significant sources of fixed nitrogen in many ecosystems. Approximately 8-10% of lichen species contain cyanobacteria as their primary photobiont, and an additional 2-3% contain both green algal and cyanobacterial photobionts ( cephalodiate lichens). Cyanobacterial partners, primarily *Nostoc* species, fix atmospheric nitrogen through the nitrogenase enzyme, converting N2 to ammonium that becomes available to the lichen and, eventually, to the surrounding ecosystem.

In old-growth forests of the Pacific Northwest, cyanolichens such as *Lobaria oregana* and *Peltigera* species contribute an estimated 2-5 kg N/ha/year to the forest nitrogen budget -- a significant input in nitrogen-limited ecosystems. In boreal forests, *Peltigera aphthosa* and related species are among the most important biological nitrogen fixers after free-living cyanobacteria. In tropical montane forests, foliicolous cyanolichens contribute nitrogen directly to the epiphytic mat that supports canopy-dwelling plants and invertebrates.

The sensitivity of cyanolichens to atmospheric nitrogen deposition creates a paradox: they are most important for nitrogen input in nitrogen-poor ecosystems, but these are precisely the ecosystems most affected by even low levels of anthropogenic nitrogen pollution. Increased nitrogen availability favors green algal lichens and non-lichenized algae over cyanolichens, potentially reducing ecosystem nitrogen fixation capacity just as external nitrogen inputs increase.

## Reproductive Biology

Lichen reproduction involves complex coordination between the fungal and photobiont partners. Lichen fungi reproduce both sexually and asexually, while photobionts reproduce primarily asexually:
- **Sexual reproduction:** Many lichenized ascomycetes produce apothecia (open cup-like fruiting bodies) or perithecia (flask-shaped structures) containing asci with ascospores. After dispersal, the fungal spore must encounter a compatible photobiont to establish a new lichen thallus. This dual-dispersal requirement makes successful sexual reproduction relatively rare, which explains why many lichen species reproduce predominantly asexually.
- **Asexual reproduction (vegetative propagation):** Soredia (granular clusters of fungal hyphae surrounding photobiont cells) and isidia (small, finger-like or coral-like outgrowths containing both partners) allow lichens to disperse both partners simultaneously. These structures break off and are dispersed by wind, water, or animals, establishing new thalli more efficiently than sexual spores. Species that produce abundant soredia or isidia tend to be better colonizers of new substrates and more widely distributed.
- **Thallus fragmentation:** Mechanical breakage of the lichen thallus, caused by wind, animal activity, or frost, can generate propagules that establish new colonies if they land on suitable substrate.

## Lichen Substrates and Communities

Lichen communities vary dramatically with substrate characteristics and microenvironmental conditions:
- **Bark pH:** Tree bark pH is a major determinant of epiphytic lichen community composition. Acid-barked trees (oak, pine, spruce) support acidophilous species such as *Hypogymnia physodes* and *Evernia prunastri*, while nutrient-enriched bark (elm, ash, sycamore) with higher pH supports nitrophytic species like *Xanthoria parietina* and *Physcia spp.* Changes in bark chemistry due to agricultural ammonia deposition have caused dramatic shifts in lichen communities across Europe.
- **Rock lithology:** Saxicolous lichens show strong preferences for specific rock types. Siliceous rocks (granite, sandstone) support species like *Rhizocarpon geographicum* and *Parmelia saxatilis*, while calcareous rocks (limestone, chalk) support calcicolous species like *Aspicilia calcarea* and *Verrucaria nigrescens*. Some lichens are strictly calcicolous or silicolous, while others are substrate-indifferent generalists.
- **Succession:** Lichen communities on rock surfaces follow well-documented succession patterns. Primary colonizers (often crustose *Acarospora* or *Lecanora* species) are followed by foliose and fruticose species as the substrate is modified through biological weathering and organic matter accumulation. Full succession from bare rock to a mature lichen community can take centuries.

## Secondary Chemistry and Ecological Roles

Lichens produce an extraordinary diversity of secondary metabolites (often called "lichen acids"), with over 1,000 compounds described. These compounds are almost exclusively produced by the fungal partner and serve multiple ecological functions:
- **Anti-herbivory:** Many lichen acids are bitter or toxic to invertebrate herbivores and larger grazers. Usnic acid, atranorin, and vulpinic acid deter feeding by snails, slugs, and some vertebrate browsers. Reindeer avoid lichens with high usnic acid content during summer but consume them in winter when other forage is scarce.
- **Antimicrobial:** Lichen acids inhibit growth of bacteria and competing fungi. This antimicrobial activity is likely the primary function of these compounds, protecting the slow-growing lichen thallus from microbial colonization and decay. Historically, lichen extracts were used in traditional medicine as antibacterial and antifungal remedies.
- **UV protection:** Melanin and some lichen pigments screen ultraviolet radiation, particularly important for lichens growing at high altitude or latitude where UV exposure is intense. The dark pigmentation of many montane and polar lichens reflects this protective function.
- **Metal chelation:** Some lichen compounds bind heavy metals, contributing to the ability of certain lichens to accumulate extraordinary concentrations of toxic metals. *Cladonia* and *Usnea* species have been used as biomonitors for heavy metal pollution around industrial sites.
- **Water retention:** Hydrophobic compounds on the lichen surface regulate water uptake and retention, influencing the duration of metabolic activity after wetting events.

## Biomonitoring and Environmental Assessment

Lichens are among the most widely used biological indicators of environmental quality:
- **Air quality indices:** Standardized lichen diversity indices (e.g., the IAP -- Index of Atmospheric Purity) correlate lichen species richness and cover with measured levels of SO2, NOx, and particulate matter. These indices are used across Europe and North America for regulatory air quality monitoring.
- **Heavy metal accumulation:** Transplanted lichens (usually *Flavoparmelia caperata* or *Xanthoria parietina*) exposed at monitoring sites accumulate metals in proportion to atmospheric deposition rates. This passive sampling approach is simpler and cheaper than instrumental monitoring while providing time-integrated exposure data.
- **Radiocesium monitoring:** Following the Chernobyl and Fukushima disasters, lichens were used to map radiocesium deposition because they efficiently intercept and retain atmospheric particles. Lichen monitoring provided the most detailed contamination maps in many affected regions.
- **Climate change indicators:** Long-term monitoring of lichen communities reveals responses to warming temperatures, including upslope migration of species on mountains, range expansion of warm-adapted species, and decline of cold-adapted Arctic-alpine species.

## Conservation Concerns

Lichens face threats from habitat destruction, air pollution, climate change, and collecting pressure. Old-growth forests support distinctive and diverse lichen communities that are lost when forests are logged. Epiphytic lichens are particularly vulnerable to forest management practices that reduce tree age and structural diversity. Some lichen species are extremely rare, known from only a few localities worldwide.
