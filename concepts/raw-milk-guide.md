# Raw Milk Guide

Raw (unpasteurized) milk is milk that has not been heated to kill pathogenic bacteria. It is consumed directly, used for cheese making, and valued by many homesteaders for its nutritional density, flavor, and probiotic content. This guide covers the benefits, risks, handling practices, testing protocols, and legal landscape of raw milk.

## What Makes Raw Milk Different

Pasteurization heats milk to 72 C (161 F) for 15 seconds (HTST method) or 63 C (145 F) for 30 minutes (vat method). This process kills pathogenic bacteria but also:

- Destroys beneficial lactobacillus and other probiotic bacteria
- Reduces vitamin C content by 10-25%
- Denatures enzymes including lactase (which helps digest lactose), lipase (which aids fat digestion), and phosphatase (which aids calcium absorption)
- Alters milk proteins, potentially reducing allergenicity for some people
- Changes the flavor — raw milk has a complex, sweet, creamy taste that pasteurized milk lacks

## Benefits

### Nutritional Advantages

-  **Enzymes** — Raw milk contains active enzymes that aid digestion. Lactase helps lactose-intolerant individuals tolerate raw milk. Phosphatase aids calcium and phosphorus absorption.
-  **Probiotics** — Beneficial bacteria in raw milk (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and others) support gut health and immune function. These are the same bacteria used in yogurt and cheese cultures.
-  **Vitamins** — Raw milk retains more vitamin C, B6, B12, and folate than pasteurized milk. Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K are more bioavailable in raw milk fat.
-  **CLA (Conjugated Linoleic Acid)** — Higher levels in grass-fed raw milk. CLA has anti-inflammatory and potential anti-cancer properties.
-  **Immunoglobulins** — Raw milk contains antibodies that support immune function, particularly important in young animals and children.

### Homestead Advantages

-  No energy required for pasteurization
-  Can be used directly for [[goat-cheese-making]] without re-culturing (pasteurized milk requires added cultures)
-  Higher cream content in many heritage breeds separates easily for butter and cream
-  Eliminates dependence on commercial dairy processing

## Risks

### Pathogens

Raw milk can carry pathogenic bacteria that cause serious illness:

-  **Listeria monocytogenes** — Can cause listeriosis, particularly dangerous for pregnant women, newborns, elderly, and immunocompromised individuals. Grows at refrigerator temperatures.
-  **Salmonella** — Causes salmonellosis: diarrhea, fever, abdominal cramps. Sources include contaminated udders, milking equipment, or the farm environment.
-  **E. coli O157:H7** — Causes severe foodborne illness, potentially leading to hemolytic uremic syndrome (kidney failure). Particularly dangerous for children under 5.
-  **Campylobacter** — Most common bacterial cause of diarrheal illness. Sources include fecal contamination during milking.
-  **Brucella** — Causes brucellosis (undulant fever). Rare in countries with active disease eradication programs but possible in untested herds.
-  **Q fever (Coxiella burnetii)** — Flu-like illness that can become chronic. Particularly associated with goats.

### Risk Factors

Risk of contamination increases with:
-  Poor animal health (mastitis, udder infections)
-  Dirty milking environment or equipment
-  Improper milk handling and storage
-  Unhealthy or stressed animals
-  Mixing milk from multiple animals without individual testing
-  Feeding animals contaminated feed or water

Risk is minimized (not eliminated) by:
-  Healthy, well-managed animals on clean pasture
-  Meticulous milking hygiene
-  Rapid chilling after milking
-  Regular animal health testing
-  Clean, sanitized equipment

## Safe Handling Practices

### Milking Hygiene

1.  **Clean the animal** — Brush the udder and belly. Wash the udder with warm water and a dairy-specific sanitizer or mild soap. Dry with a clean, single-use paper towel. The first few strips of milk from each teat should be stripped into a separate cup and discarded (this flushes bacteria from the teat canal).
2.  **Pre-dip** — Dip teats in a pre-milking teat dip (iodine-based) 30 seconds before milking. Wipe dry with a clean towel.
3.  **Sanitize equipment** — All milking equipment (buckets, pails, strainers, jars) must be washed with hot soapy water, rinsed, then sanitized with a dairy sanitizer or bleach solution before each use. Air dry on a clean rack.
4.  **Milking environment** — Milk in a clean, well-ventilated area. Avoid dusty or muddy conditions. Bedding should be fresh and dry.
5.  **Filter milk** — Pour milk through a single-use milk filter or disposable milk filter pad into a sanitized container. This removes hair, debris, and sediment.

### Cooling and Storage

-  **Chill immediately** — The most critical step. Cool milk from body temperature (38 C / 100 F) to 4 C (40 F) within 1 hour of milking.
-  **Ice bath method** — Place the milk container in a larger container filled with ice water. Stir occasionally for even cooling.
-  **Refrigerator** — Store at 0-4 C (32-40 F) immediately after chilling.
-  **Shelf life** — Properly handled raw goat milk lasts 7-10 days refrigerated. Raw cow milk lasts 7-14 days. Quality declines over time as naturally occurring bacteria multiply.
-  **Do not mix batches** — Keep milk from each milking separate until it has been tested or you are confident in your herd's health status.

### Equipment

-  Stainless steel buckets and containers — non-reactive, easy to sanitize
-  Single-use milk filters
-  Glass jars with tight lids for storage
-  Dairy thermometer
-  Milking machine or hand milking pail — see [[homestead-dairy-equipment]]

## Testing

Regular testing is the foundation of safe raw milk production.

### On-Farm Tests

-  **California Mastitis Test (CMT)** — Detects subclinical mastitis by measuring somatic cell count. Test each quarter monthly. Positive CMT indicates inflammation — do not consume milk from affected quarters.
-  **Strip cup exam** — Visually examine first strips for clots, flakes, blood, or discoloration. Any abnormality warrants further investigation.
-  **pH test strips** — Fresh milk pH should be 6.5-6.7. Higher pH may indicate mastitis. Lower pH indicates bacterial growth (souring).

### Laboratory Tests

-  **Somatic Cell Count (SCC)** — Measures white blood cells in milk. SCC below 200,000 cells/mL indicates healthy udders. Above 500,000 suggests mastitis. Test quarterly or monthly.
-  **Standard Plate Count (SPC)** — Measures total bacteria count. Below 10,000 CFU/mL is excellent for raw milk. Above 50,000 indicates hygiene problems.
-  **Pathogen testing** — Test for Listeria, Salmonella, and E. coli periodically (quarterly for commercial sales, annually for family use). Many state agricultural extension labs offer affordable milk testing.
-  **Brucellosis and TB testing** — Required for herd certification in most states. Test annually.

### Bulk Tank Culture

Periodically submit a milk sample for culture testing to identify specific bacteria present. This helps identify contamination sources and track herd health over time.

## Legal Status

Raw milk laws vary dramatically by jurisdiction. As of 2026:

### United States

-  **Retail legal** (can sell in stores): California, Connecticut, Maine, New Hampshire, New Mexico, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Washington state
-  **Farm sales legal** (can sell at farm): Idaho, Arizona, Utah, Wyoming, Nebraska, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Illinois, Ohio, Vermont, Massachusetts, and others
-  **Cow-share / herd-share programs** (legal gray area in many states): Consumers buy a share of a cow or goat and receive the milk from their animal. Legally, they own the animal and are consuming their own milk.
-  **Pet food exemption** (used in some states): Raw milk sold labeled "for pet consumption only"
-  **FDA interstate ban**: It is illegal to transport raw milk for human consumption across state lines (21 CFR 1240.61). This applies to all states regardless of their internal laws.

### Key Principle

Laws change frequently. Always verify current regulations with your state department of agriculture or a local agricultural attorney before selling raw milk.

## Who Should Avoid Raw Milk

- Pregnant women — Listeria risk can cause miscarriage or stillbirth
- Children under 5 — Higher risk of severe complications from foodborne illness
- Immunocompromised individuals — HIV/AIDS, chemotherapy patients, organ transplant recipients
- Elderly individuals — Reduced immune function

Healthy adults can make an informed decision about raw milk consumption based on the source quality, handling practices, and their personal risk tolerance.
