# Eurotium-Aspergillus Pleomorphism — The Dual Identity Problem

## Source

## Summary
The relationship between Eurotium and Aspergillus illustrates one of the most
perplexing problems in mycology: a single organism known by two names because its
sexual (teleomorphic) and asexual (anamorphic) stages were discovered and described
independently. Eurotium produces closed spherical [[bloomfield-fungal-pleomorphy-tulasne-brothers-anamorph-teleomorph]] in the [[agaricus-campestris-biology-and-life-cycle]] — the two stages look so different that they were assumed to be separate

## Catching Aspergillus from the Sky

It is a simple matter to isolate Aspergillus from the air. Open a Petri dish anywhere
outside the sterile air of an operating room for a few seconds, close the lid, and
leave it for a day. The plate will likely sprout the conidium-producing stalks of an
Aspergillus. If the experiment fails, the fungus is certain to appear on the second or
third attempt.

This ubiquity reflects the effectiveness of the asexual [[agar-medium-preparation]] with

## The Naming Confusion

Imagine discovering a new species of Aspergillus growing on a culture plate and naming
it Aspergillus magnificus. If two strains of this species are grown together and
mate, they generate a sexual [[bloomfield-rust-germ-tube-tactile-sensing-leaf-surface-navigation]] might think they have discovered another
new species. Unless the conidium-producing Aspergillus stage is growing alongside the
cleistothecia, the error is unavoidable, and the fungus gets a second name: perhaps
Eurotium grandiosum.

For this reason, a vast number of fungi have been described twice because the observer
was privy to only one chunk of a larger life cycle. This confusing predicament —
described by Money as the most perplexing in the entire field of mycology — has led
to enormous taxonomic redundancy.

## The Tulasne Brothers and the Discovery of Pleomorphism

This dual-identity problem was first recognized in the mid-nineteenth century by two
bachelor brothers, Louis-René and Charles Tulasne. Their contention that individual
fungi could produce different types of spore was published in an enchanting
three-volume tome entitled *Selecta Fungorum Carpologia*, with frequent references to
the glory of God (they were devout Catholics).

The principle was termed variously multiple gemmation, polymorphism, and pleomorphism,
and its authors revolutionized the study of mycology. Louis-René had studied law and
Charles had become a physician, but later in life, aided by an inheritance from their
father, they dedicated themselves to mycological studies. Louis-René wrote most of
the text while Charles drew the luminous illustrations for each massive folio volume.

## Cleistothecium Structure

The relatively simple fruiting bodies of Eurotium are closed spheres called
cleistothecia that contain one or more asci. Their minute fruiting bodies are built
from a jacket of hyphae that become yellowed with age. The asci that develop inside
are flaccid bags that dissolve like those of yeast, turning the cleistothecium into
a spore-filled globe. Eventually, the wall of the ascocarp breaks open, freeing the
sexual spores.
