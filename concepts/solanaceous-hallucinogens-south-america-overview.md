## Solanaceous Hallucinogens of South America

## Overview

The nightshade family, Solanaceae, is one of the most pharmacologically significant plant families in the world. While it provides essential food crops (potato, tomato, pepper, eggplant) and medicines (belladonna, henbane), it also contains numerous species used as hallucinogens by indigenous peoples of South America. These plants share a common chemistry centered on [[mckenna-witch-ointments-tropane-alkaloids-flying-salves]]—principally hyoscyamine and scopolamine—which produce characteristic deliriant intoxication marked by vivid hallucinations, confusion, and amnesia. The [[amanita]] of these plants spans millennia, from pre-Columbian ritual practices to surviving indigenous healing traditions.

## Chemical Foundation: Tropane Alkaloids

The psychoactive properties of Solanaceous hallucinogens derive primarily from tropane alkaloids, a class of nitrogen-containing compounds characterized by a bicyclic tropane ring system. The principal active alkaloids are:

- **Hyoscyamine**: A tropane alkaloid that acts as a competitive antagonist at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. It produces mydriasis (pupil dilation), tachycardia, dry mouth, urinary retention, and in sufficient doses, hallucinations and delirium.
- **Scopolamine** (hyoscine): The oxygenated derivative of hyoscyamine, typically more potent as a central nervous system depressant. Scopolamine is noted for producing amnesia, sedation, and vivid hallucinations, and is sometimes the major constituent in highly potent species.
- **Atropine**: The racemic mixture of d- and l-hyoscyamine, acting similarly at muscarinic receptors.
- **Minor alkaloids**: Including norscopolamine, meteloidine, and other chemically related compounds whose relative concentrations vary among species.

These compounds are active at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors throughout the body, explaining both the central nervous system effects (hallucinations, delirium, amnesia) and the peripheral effects (dry mouth, dilated pupils, increased heart rate, urinary retention) that characterize Solanaceous intoxication.

## Latua pubiflora — Arbol de los Brujos

*Latua pubiflora*, called arbol de los brujos ("sorcerers' tree") or latué by the Mapuche Indians of Valdivia, Chile, is used by medicine men to cause delirium, hallucinations, and occasionally permanent insanity. There is no cult or elaborate ritual surrounding its use, but the tree is widely feared and respected. Dosages are a closely guarded secret, and it is widely believed that a madness of any desired duration may be induced by a medicine man who knows how to measure the doses properly.

The fresh fruits are the plant part employed. Hyoscyamine and scopolamine have been isolated from the fruit and are responsible for its potent effects. *Latua* is a monotypic genus—the only species known—confined to coastal mountains of central Chile.

## Iochroma fuchsioides — Borrachera

Borrachera (*Iochroma fuchsioides*) is one of about two dozen species of *Iochroma*, all native to the highlands of South America. Suspicions and unconfirmed reports indicate that several species of *Iochroma* are taken in hallucinatory drinks, either alone or mixed with other narcotic plants, by Indians in the Sibundoy Valley of southern Colombia. Although no [[chemical-studies-neurotropic-fungi-reliability-and-challenges]] have been made of *Iochroma*, its membership in the Solanaceae suggests the presence of tropane or related alkaloids consistent with the family's characteristic chemistry.

The Sibundoy Valley has been characterized as "the most narcotic conscious area of the New World," reflecting the extraordinary density of psychoactive plant use among its indigenous inhabitants.

## Brunfelsia — Chiric-Caspi and Chiric Sanango

Several species of *Brunfelsia* shrubs appear to have been important hallucinogens among South American Indian tribes. The native name borrachero ("intoxicator") indicates recognition of the shrub's narcotic properties, and the special care taken in its cultivation suggests a former religious or magical role in tribal life.

Documented uses include:
- The **Kachinaua** of Brazil use *Brunfelsia* as the source of a hallucinogenic drink.
- The **Jívaro** and **Kofán** Indians of Ecuador use it as an additive to other hallucinogenic drinks.

The species employed hallucinogenically are *B. grandiflora* and *B. chiricaspi*. Chemical investigation of the active compounds is still in the initial stage, and the active principles have not yet been determined. All species enter into folk medicine, being used especially to reduce fevers and as antirheumatic agents. The genus comprises 40 species of shrubs native to tropical South America and the West Indies.

## Methysticodendron amesianum — Culebra Borrachera

Culebra borrachera (*Methysticodendron amesianum*) is a tree reaching 25 feet in height, known only from cultivated specimens in the Kamsá Indian town of Sibundoy, Colombia. The Indians also call it mitskway borrachera ("snake intoxicant"). This tree is the only species of its genus and may represent an extremely aberrant form of a tree species of *Datura*. Its distinctive 11-inch white flowers differ from those of the tree daturas in having a bell-shaped corolla split nearly to the base.

An infusion of the leaves is said to be more potent and dangerous to use than similar preparations of *Datura*. The [[chemical-composition-of-hallucinogens]] explains its great potency: approximately 80 percent of the several typical tropane alkaloids present is scopolamine. Even in small doses, this drug may cause excitement, hallucinations, and delirium. The trees are the special property of certain medicine men who employ the drug in difficult cases of disease diagnosis, divination, prophecy, or witchcraft.

## Datura Species

Daturas form a genus of approximately 20 species occurring and used as hallucinogens in both hemispheres. Intoxication is characterized initially by effects so violent that physical restraint must be imposed until the partaker passes into a stage of sleep and hallucinations. The medicine man interprets visions as visitations of spirits to diagnose disease, apprehend thieves, and prophesy the future.

### Tree Daturas (Brugmansia)

Tree daturas are cultivated plants, unknown in the truly wild state and associated with humans since earliest times. Indigenous peoples from Colombia to Chile value them as sources of ritualistic hallucinogens and medicines:

- **Datura suaveolens**: Indigenous to warmer lowlands, used as a hallucinogen alone or as an admixture.
- **Datura sanguinea**: Used ceremonially at Sogomoza, Colombia, in the Temple of the Sun. The narcotic preparation, called *tonga*, is believed to permit communication with ancestors.
- **Datura candida** and **D. aurea**: Used by the Mapuche of Chile to correct unruly children—spirits of ancestors are said to admonish children during hallucinations.
- **Datura arborea**, **D. dolichocarpa**, **D. vulcanicola**: Other Andean species used in various regional practices.

The tree daturas are sometimes considered a distinct genus, *Brugmansia*.

### Herbaceous Daturas

- **Jimson weed** (*Datura stramonium*): Probably native to North America, now worldwide. Believed to have been the chief ingredient of wysoccon, used by Algonquin Indians in initiation rituals.
- **Torna-loco** (*Datura ceratocaula*): A fleshy marsh plant, called "sister of ololiuqui" by the Aztecs. Its modern name means "maddening plant."
- **Toloache** (*Datura inoxia*): Extremely important to the Aztecs. Still used by the Tarahumares (in maize beer), Zunis (by rain priests), Luiseños (initiation ceremonies), Yumans (dream induction), and Yokuts (spring ceremonies for health and longevity).

## Intoxication Profile

The characteristic Solanaceous hallucinogen intoxication follows a consistent pattern across species:

1. **Onset phase**: Rapid onset of physical symptoms including dryness of mouth, dilation of pupils, blurred vision, tachycardia, and difficulty swallowing. Effects may be so violent that physical restraint becomes necessary.
2. **Deliriant phase**: Transition into a state of delirium with vivid, indistinguishable-from-reality hallucinations. The intoxicated person typically cannot distinguish between hallucinations and actual perceptions. Amnesia for the experience is common afterward.
3. **Recovery phase**: Gradual return to normal consciousness, often with residual confusion, headache, and mydriasis persisting for hours to days.

This profile differs fundamentally from the classical hallucinogens (tryptamines, ergolines, phenylethylamines), which typically preserve the user's awareness that they are under the influence of a drug and produce primarily visual and cognitive rather than deliriant effects.

## Cultural Significance

Solanaceous hallucinogens occupy a distinctive place in South American [[ethnobotanical-use-of-sacred-mushrooms-in-indigenous-cultures]]:

- **Divination**: Medicine men use the visions to diagnose illness, locate lost objects, apprehend thieves, and prophesy the future.
- **Healing**: The plants are central to curing rituals, with the medicine man's interpretation of the patient's hallucinations determining the cause and treatment of disease.
- **Social control**: Some groups use Solanaceous hallucinogens to discipline children, with the terrifying visions serving as a deterrent against unacceptable behavior.
- **Funerary practices**: The ancient Chibchas of Bogotá used *Datura aurea* seeds to induce stupor in the wives and slaves of dead warriors before they were buried alive to accompany their masters.
- **Ritual specialization**: Knowledge of dosage and preparation is typically restricted to specialized practitioners—medicine men, shamans, or priests—reflecting the dangerous and unpredictable nature of these powerful plants.
