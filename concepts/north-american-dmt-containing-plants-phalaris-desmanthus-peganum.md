# North American [[ayahuasca]], several plants
containing DMT or the necessary MAOIs for oral activity grow commonly throughout
the United States. Halpern (2004) documented these species as locally available
sources of a Schedule I compound, raising both ethnobotanical and regulatory
interest. The existence of these widely distributed plants represents an
ongoing regulatory challenge, as they are largely unregulated yet contain
controlled substances.

## DMT Biology and Pharmacology

DMT (N,N) is a simple tryptamine alkaloid acting primarily
as a [[harmine]], [[tetrahydroharmine]]**: Also a reversible MAO-A inhibitor

The most commonly used MAOI source in North American "ayahuasca analog"
preparations. Seeds are ground and simmered to produce a bitter tea consumed
before the DMT-containing preparation.

## Passiflora incarnata (Passionflower)

Native to the southeastern and midwestern US. Common ornamental vine bearing
edible passionfruit. Contains **less than 1% MAOI by dry weight** — much milder
than Peganum harmala. Widely available as a dietary supplement and herbal tea.
Its dual use as both a mild MAOI source and a common herbal remedy illustrates
the difficulty of regulating plants based on psychoactive constituents.

## Ayahuasca Analog Preparation

These brews are "not theoretical" per Halpern (2004) — recipes exist online
with confirmed effects. Typical preparation:

1. Ground Peganum harmala seeds simmered 30-60 min → MAOI tea
2. DMT source prepared separately (Phalaris juice or Desmanthus root tea)
3. MAOI tea consumed first; DMT tea 15-30 min later

An average 100 mL dose contains ~24 mg DMT, 107 mg tetrahydroharmine, 20 mg
harmaline, 170 mg harmine (Callaway et al., 1996). Effects include nausea and
vomiting, visual hallucinations, altered perception, and introspective
experiences lasting 3-4 hours.

## Regulatory and Safety Considerations

- DMT is Schedule I regardless of botanical source
- The plants themselves are mostly unregulated, widely available, and some are
  agriculturally significant
- MAOI preparations interact dangerously with SSRIs and tyramine-rich foods
- DMT variability in Phalaris makes dosing unpredictable
- Correct species identification is essential to avoid toxic lookalikes
