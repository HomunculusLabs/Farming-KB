# Hofmann and the Discovery of Salvia Divinorum

## The Mazatec Magic Sage

During his 1962 expedition to Mexico with R. Gordon Wasson, Albert Hofmann
encountered a psychoactive plant known to the Mazatec Indians as "hojas
de la Pastora" (leaves of the Shepherdess), also called "ska Maria
Pastora" (leaves of Mary the Shepherdess). This plant, which would later
be identified as [[salvia-divinorum-profile]], represented a
third major Mexican psychoactive species alongside the sacred mushrooms
(teonanacatl) and the morning glory seeds (ololiuhqui).

## The Ceremony with Maria Sabina

On the night of the ceremony in Huautla de Jimenez, Hofmann received the
juice of five pairs of fresh leaves of hojas de la Pastora, prepared by
a girl of about ten under the guidance of Maria Sabina. The potion was
said to be especially active when prepared by an innocent child. The cup
with the expressed juice was incensed and conjured by Maria Sabina and
Don Aurelio before being delivered to Hofmann.

The ceremony took place in a locked room with an emergency exit to the
back garden. The party lay in darkness on bast mats on the floor. Maria
Sabina threw pieces of copal on the embers of a brazier. Before the
ceremony began, cacao and sweet pastry were served, reminiscent of the
ancient chronicle that described how chocolatl was drunk before the
ingestion of teonanacatl.

## Effects of the Magic Sage

Hofmann reported that, evidently as an effect of the hojas, he found
himself for some time in a state of mental sensitivity and intense
experience, which was not accompanied by hallucinations. His wife Anita
experienced a euphoric condition of inebriation influenced by the
strange, mystical atmosphere. She was impressed by the vision of very
distinct strange line patterns, and was astonished to later discover
precisely the same images in the rich ornamentation over the altar in an
old church near Puebla.

## First Encounter in San Jose Tenango

Earlier in the expedition, Hofmann had attempted to try hojas de la
Pastora in San Jose Tenango with the curandera Consuela Garcia, but the
prepared juice had proved inactive by the time of the ceremony. This
first unsuccessful attempt underscores the instability of the active
principle. The juice prepared in Mexico and preserved with alcohol later
proved in self-experiments to be no longer active.

## Botanical Identification

The herbarium samples of hojas de la Pastora, brought back from Mexico,
were subjected to botanical identification by Carl Epling and Carlos D.
Jativa at the Botanical Institute of Harvard University in Cambridge,
Massachusetts. They found that this plant was a hitherto undescribed
species of Salvia, which was named [[salvia-divinorum-profile]]
by these authors.

## The Unsolved Chemical Problem

The chemical investigation of the juice of the magic sage in the
laboratory in Basel was unsuccessful. The psychoactive principle of this
drug seems to be a rather unstable substance, since the juice prepared in
Mexico and preserved with alcohol proved inactive. Where the chemical
nature of the active principle is concerned, the problem of the magic
plant ska Maria Pastora still awaited solution at the time of Hofmann's
writing.

Later research would eventually identify the active compound as
salvinorin A, a neoclerodane diterpenoid, which is not structurally
related to any other known psychoactive compound. This unique chemistry
places Salvia divinorum in a category of its own among psychoactive
plants.

## The Broader Mexican Context

The 1962 expedition placed Salvia divinorum within a remarkable
triad of Mazatec psychoactive plants, all used by the same indigenous
culture for healing and divination:

- **Teonanacatl** (Psilocybe species): The sacred mushrooms, containing
  [[wasson-rediscovery-of-psilocybin]] and [[psilocin-and-psilocybin-chemistry]]
- **Ololiuhqui** (Turbina corymbosa and Ipomoea violacea): The morning
  glory seeds, containing [[ergot-and-lysergic-acid-history]] and related alkaloids
- **Hojas de la Pastora** (Salvia divinorum): The leaves of the
  Shepherdess, containing the then-unidentified active principle

This triad demonstrates the sophisticated ethnopharmacological knowledge
of the Mazatec people, who had discovered and utilized three completely
different classes of psychoactive substances from three different plant
families.

## The Expedition's Findings on Synthetic Psilocybin

During the same ceremony, Maria Sabina was given synthetic psilocybin
pills. Initially she complained that the pills lacked the spirit of the
mushrooms. After additional doses (totaling 30 mg psilocybin each), the
curanderas confirmed that the pills had the same power as the mushrooms.
Maria Sabina received a vial of psilocybin pills as a parting gift,
explaining she could now give consultations even in the season when no
mushrooms grow.

## Consequences for Huautla

The publication about the magic mushrooms unleashed an invasion of hippies
and drug seekers into the Mazatec country, many of whom behaved badly,
some criminally. Another undesirable consequence was the beginning of true
tourism in Huautla de Jimenez, whereby the originality of the place was
eradicated. The profanation of the mushroom cult did not stop with the
scientific investigations. Some of Maria Sabina's countrymen regarded her
actions as betrayal, expressed in acts of revenge including the burning
of her house.

Hofmann acknowledged the ethical complexity: Maria Sabina had opened the
door to exploration of the Mexican mushroom cult and to the scientific
investigation of the sacred mushrooms, yielding valuable active
substances. Without her assistance, the ancient knowledge concealed in
these secret practices would possibly have disappeared without a trace.
Yet from another standpoint, her conduct could be regarded as profanation
of a sacred custom.
