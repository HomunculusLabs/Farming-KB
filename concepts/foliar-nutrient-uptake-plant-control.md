## Foliar Nutrient Uptake and Plant Control

## Overview

One of the most important principles in biological agriculture is that **the plant should be in control of its own nutrition**, not the grower. When a healthy set of organisms is restored to the system, plants regain their natural ability to regulate nutrient uptake through mechanisms that have evolved over millions of years.

## The Plant's Natural Regulation Mechanism

### How Foliar Uptake Works

When organisms are present on leaf surfaces and nutrients are applied as foliar sprays, the following sequence occurs:

1. The plant assesses whether it needs the nutrients available on its leaf surfaces
2. **If nutrients are needed**: the plant pumps sugars out onto the leaf surface
3. The sugars feed the organisms living on the leaf
4. These organisms grow and metabolize, producing **elevated CO2** on the leaf surface
5. The elevated CO2 triggers **stomatal opening**
6. With stomates open, nutrients are taken into the plant
7. **If nutrients are NOT needed**: the plant does not pump out sugars, organisms do not proliferate, CO2 does not rise, and stomates remain less open — fewer nutrients enter the plant

### Key Principle

> "Let the plant do the work, let the plant control what it needs, instead of us pushing things on the plant."

This is one of the most significant benefits of restoring the normal set of organisms to the system. The system begins to function the way it is supposed to function. Plant health returns to normal.

### Brix and Plant Self-Regulation

This natural regulation mechanism complicates brix interpretation:

- **Elevated brix** could indicate the plant needed something and is getting it
- **Normal brix** might simply mean the plant already had the nutrients it needed and was completely healthy
- **Elevated brix** could alternatively represent an "immune reaction" to whatever was sprayed
- **Dropping brix** might mean sugars are being converted to other materials the plant needs

A plant physiologist is needed to fully explain the relationships between sugar, dissolved solids, foliar nutrient uptake, and plant health.

## Compost Tea and Nitrogen Timing

### Caution During Flowering and Fruit Set

Applying [[compost]] tea high in nitrogen at critical growth stages can cause problems:

- **During flowering**: pushing nitrate and amino sugars will promote vegetative growth instead of reproductive growth
- **During fruit set**: excess nitrogen can interfere with fruit development

### Proper Approach

The goal of compost tea application is to **feed the bacteria and fungi in the tea**, not to overwhelm the plant with nutrients. The organisms on the leaves enhance nutrient uptake, so it is important not to push too high a concentration of nutrients.

A single compost tea application typically does not add excessive nitrogen. The key is moderation — feed the biology, let the biology feed the plant.

## Compost Tea Brix Experiment

A structured experimental protocol was proposed to investigate the relationship between compost tea quality and brix readings:

### Setup

1. **Make two teas**:
   - Tea #1: good tea with lots of beneficial organisms
   - Tea #2: poor tea with poor organisms
2. **Measure baseline brix** in four plots before applying anything
3. **Apply treatments**:
   - Plot 1: Tea #1 (good organisms)
   - Plot 2: Tea #2 (poor organisms)
   - Plot 3: Water only (control)
   - Plot 4: Nutrient mix only (conventional approach)
4. **Verify application** by checking leaves to ensure tea was applied correctly
5. **Monitor brix** through the remainder of the day to document different responses

### Importance of Data

Regardless of which mechanism is proposed, and whether people agree with the wording of hypotheses, **data allows us to state what the relationship is for the plants in these conditions**. Solid information, even anecdotal observation of patterns, is the first step in the scientific method.

## Pleomorphism in Soil Microorganisms

### Definition

Pleomorphism refers to the ability of a single microorganism to assume different forms, shapes, or morphologies depending on environmental conditions, life stage, or food resources.

### Colony Morphology vs. Direct Observation

Pleomorphism is related to **colony morphology** (appearance on a culture plate), not to shape and size observed under direct microscopic examination. Key distinctions:

- **Pseudomonads** are famous for having many different colony morphologies on plates, but when bacteria from different-looking colonies are examined under a microscope, they are morphologically identical — the same bacterium with different slime qualities
- **Bacillus species** can appear longer or shorter depending on the food resource they are using, which could lead to counting them as two species when they are actually one

### Counting Challenges

The primary problem with using morphology to estimate species diversity is **undercounting**:

- Different species often look exactly the same under the microscope
- You cannot differentiate *Azotobacter* species one from another — they look identical
- **Type II errors** (false negatives — missing species that are present) are far more prevalent than type I errors (false positives — counting one species as two)
- Morphological direct counts **underestimate** species diversity, not overestimate it

### Molecular Approaches

Molecular DNA-based methods are the future of species identification:

- **Advantages**: Can differentiate species that look identical morphologically; can assess functional genes (e.g., nitrogen fixation genes)
- **Current limitations**: Expensive equipment (~$190,000 for probe replication and fluorescent excitation), significant repeatability problems, PCR and probe development still required
- **Most pathogenic bacteria likely have multiple names** because they were named based on the disease they cause on one plant, but may be benign on another plant — molecular methods resolve this confusion
- **Current research focus**: Most molecular method development is directed at human pathogens, not soil organisms, but methods are advancing rapidly

### Practical Utility

Despite its limitations, the morphological direct count approach remains:

- Much more useful than plate count approaches
- Much less expensive than molecular approaches (at present)
- Adequate for many practical purposes when the undercounting limitation is understood

## Fungi and Bacteria Balance for Cucumbers

Cucumbers prefer **fairly bacterial soil** but still need some fungi for disease suppression. If your compost lacks fungi:

1. Obtain **Alaska Humus** (naturally high in fungal diversity)
2. Place compost in a plastic tub
3. Bring to proper moisture (50% — just barely drips a drop when squeezed hard)
4. Cover and keep warm (room temperature, not hot)
5. Thick white fungal strands should grow throughout the compost

This pre-incubation step boosts fungal biomass before tea brewing.
