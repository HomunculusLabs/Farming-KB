# Tropical vs Temperate Fungal Diversity — Patterns and Evidence

The latitudinal diversity gradient — the pattern of increasing species richness toward the tropics — is one of the most fundamental patterns in biology. For fungi, this pattern is surprisingly complex and incompletely documented, with different fungal groups showing different patterns.

## The General Pattern for Fungi

### Macrofungi
Most mycologists believe fungal species diversity is greater in tropical than temperate regions, but direct evidence remains limited:
- More plant species in tropics → more potential host-specific fungal species
- Tropical plant families with limited fungal records (Orchidaceae, Gesneriaceae, Araceae)
- Precipitation is a better predictor of fungal richness than vegetation type
- Warm, wet conditions favor fungal growth and sporulation

### Microfungi
- Tropical vs. temperate soil diversity: surprisingly similar
- Tropical leaf litter harbors more species per leaf than temperate
- Individual tropical leaves: up to 30 hyphomycete species
- Brazilian workers recorded 10+ microfungus species per single leaf examination
- 26 fungal species from a single foliicolous lichen community

### Lichens
- Contrary to most organisms, lichens may be most diverse in **southern temperate rain forests**, not humid tropics
- Boreal zone richer than Arctic at high latitudes
- Ostropales, Pyrenulales, Arthoniales diversity centers in tropics
- Lecanorales: >90% of species in high Andes (similar to temperate zones)

## Direct Evidence by Group

### Phyllachoraceae (Ascomycota)
Cannon (1997a): species between latitudes 20N-20S vs. 40-60N:
- Tropical species = 100%
- Temperate (40-60N/S) = 38% of tropical
- Above 60N = 6% of tropical
- True proportions likely even more extreme due to under-recording

### Rust Fungi
- 20% of species collected in one 40-ha neotropical tract were new to science
- One-third of plant species had their own rust species
- Tropical rust diversity vastly underestimated
- Many tropical regions have never been surveyed for rusts

### Endophytes
- Xylariaceae most diverse in tropics
- Tropical hosts harbor more diverse endophyte communities
- 506 fungal taxa from 17 lichen samples (Petrini et al. 1990)
- Costa Rican litter: 78-134 species per collection; rarefaction never leveled off

### Soil Fungi
- Tropical soil species richness comparable to temperate
- Counterintuitive — soil habitat may buffer climatic differences
- Or simply reflects severe under-sampling of tropical soils

## Site-Level Data: English Surveys

Three intensively surveyed English sites reveal the challenge:

| Site | Area (ha) | Species | Notes |
|------|-----------|---------|-------|
| Mickleham Down | 4 | 1,380 | Uniform vegetation, no water |
| Slapton Ley | 211 | 2,500 | 25+ years of survey |
| Esher Common | 400 | 2,700 | Long-term expert collectors |
| Warwickshire | 250,000 | 2,700 | Large area, some groups under-recorded |

Key observations:
- Only ~30% of species shared between Slapton and Esher
- Estimated 3,000 species total per site (inventories far from complete)
- 6:1 fungal-to-plant species ratio (Hawksworth 1991) supported
- Area increase from 4 to 250,000 ha yielded only modest species increase
- Large variation in taxonomic proportions suggests collector bias

## Why Tropical Diversity Is Underestimated

### Collection Bias
- Most mycologists based in temperate regions
- Tropical fieldwork is expensive, logistically difficult
- Political instability in some biodiverse tropical countries
- Few resident mycologists in tropical countries

### Taxonomic Bottleneck
- Many tropical specimens identifiable only to genus
- Expertise for tropical groups concentrated in few institutions
- Descriptions scattered in obscure publications

### Methodological Issues
- Fruiting more seasonal/predictable in temperate zones
- Tropical wet seasons can make fieldwork impractical
- Canopy access difficult (many tropical fungi are arboreal)
- High diversity → more specimens needed per unit effort

### The Scale Problem
- Hawksworth's 1.5 million estimate: fungal-to-plant ratio applied globally
- If tropical ratios are much higher than temperate, total could be far greater
- Costa Rican data: 15-28% overlap between samples from same region
- Even intensively studied temperate sites are far from completely inventoried

## Temperature and Growth

- Most fungi grow/sporulate best at >15-20C
- Suggests adaptation to warmer climates = greater tropical diversity
- Small proportion are psychrophilic (cold-loving) or thermophilic (heat-loving)
- Humidity likely more important than temperature alone
- Elevation effects may interact with latitude

## Practical Implications

1. **Conservation:** Tropical fungal diversity may be vastly underestimated — extinction rates unknown
2. **Inventory design:** Tropical surveys need more time, more sites, more expertise than temperate
3. **Species estimation:** Global estimates should account for latitudinal variation in fungal:plant ratios
4. **Training:** Urgent need for tropical mycologists and taxonomic expertise
5. **Molecular methods:** May reveal tropical diversity that morphology-based surveys miss
