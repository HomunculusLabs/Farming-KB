# Mixtec Narcotic Puffballs: The Ott et al. (1975) Investigation

## Background

In 1962, Heim and Wasson reported the use of two puffball species — *[[lycoperdon-mixtecorum]]* and *L. marginatum* — as narcotic fungi among the Mixtec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico. These reports were included in Schultes and Hofmann's (1973, 1979) authoritative listings of narcotic fungi. The claim that puffballs (gasteromycetes) possessed psychoactive properties represented a significant departure from the known chemistry of these organisms, which are primarily edible and contain no indole alkaloids.

## The Ott et al. (1975) Investigation

Jonathan Ott and colleagues traveled to the same Mixtec locality where Heim and Wasson had originally documented the narcotic puffball use. Their investigation revealed a far more complex picture than the original reports suggested.

### Species Identified

Ott et al. identified a total of nine species being used by the Mixtec as "narcotic fungi," which were used indistinctly (interchangeably):

1. *Vascellum pratense* (synonym of a *Lycoperdon* species)
2. *Vascellum curtisii* (Berk.) Kreisel
3. *Vascellum intermedium* A.H. Sm.
4. *Lycoperdon oblongiosporum*
5. *Rhizopogon* sp.
6. *[[astraeus-hygrometricus]]* (Pers.) Morg. — the earthstar
7. *Scleroderma verrucosum* Pers. — a poisonous earthball
8. *Lycoperdon mixtecorum* (Heim & Wasson's original species, now synonymized under *Vascellum qudenii*)
9. *[[lycoperdon-marginatum]]* (synonymized with *Lycoperdon candidum*)

### Chemical Analysis Results

Chemical analysis was performed on the collected specimens with the following findings:

- **No psilocybin or related indole alkaloids** were detected in any of the puffball species tested (except *Scleroderma verrucosum*, which was not analyzed but was experimentally proven to be poisonous).
- The species are, in fact, common edible fungi widely distributed in Mexico and other parts of the world.
- The reported "narcotic" effects could not be attributed to any known psychoactive compound in these species.

### The Scleroderma Problem

*Scleroderma verrucosum* was found mixed among the species used by the Mixtec. Unlike the other puffballs, this species is genuinely toxic — it contains chemical compounds that cause gastrointestinal distress and other symptoms that could be interpreted as "narcotic" or psychoactive effects. Its inclusion among the sacred fungi may account for some of the reported pharmacological activity, though the effects are toxic rather than entheogenic.

## Conclusions of Ott et al.

The Ott et al. (1975) study concluded that the "Mixtec narcotic puffballs" were a mixture of at least nine species of fungi containing **no neurotropic properties**. The apparent psychoactive use was attributed to:

1. **Folk taxonomy confusion**: The Mixtec classification of fungi as "narcotic" did not correspond to actual chemical properties. The puffballs were used in ceremonies alongside or as substitutes for genuine neurotropic *Psilocybe* species, leading to categorical confusion.
2. **Contextual association**: These fungi were used in religious or magical contexts that were primarily associated with psilocybin-containing mushrooms. The ritual context may have provided the psychological framework for interpreting effects.
3. **Possible toxic effects**: The inclusion of *Scleroderma verrucosum*, a genuinely poisonous species, may have produced physical symptoms that were interpreted as psychoactive effects within the ceremonial setting.

## Guzmán's Commentary

Guzmán (1994a, 1994b, 1997) discussed several puffballs used by Mexican Indians in traditional medicine, noting that while many puffballs such as *[[lycoperdon-perlatum]]* form fairy rings in grasslands and are edible, none possess neurotropic properties. The puffballs reported by Heim and Wasson are common edible species in Mexico, but in the Mixtec zone they are used in a manner suggesting confusion with more traditional neurotropic fungi used for religious or magical purposes.

## Broader Context: Non-Psilocybian Sacred Fungi

The Mixtec puffball case illustrates a recurring phenomenon in ethnomycoology: the classification of fungi as "sacred" or "narcotic" by indigenous peoples does not always correlate with the presence of known psychoactive compounds. Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998) placed such species in their "Group 4" — fungi used as sacred by some tribes without reliable chemical studies confirming psychoactivity. Other examples include:

- **Boletes**: Two genera with 8 species reported as hallucinogenic in New Guinea by Heim (1963, 1966, 1978), though Ott (1993) later stated they possess no neurotropic properties.
- **Russula**: 6 species used as sacred fungi in various traditions.
- **Gasteromycetes**: 6 species in 3 genera used ceremonially.
- **[[dictyophora-indusiata]]** (with three forms): Used as a special narcotic fungus for divination among the Chinantec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico.
- **Ganoderma lobatum**: The subject of an actual church cult in Chignahuapan, Puebla, Mexico, where a church was built in honor of the fungus. The Indians regard it as a saint.

## Implications for Ethnopharmacology

The Mixtec puffball case underscores the importance of chemical verification in ethnopharmacological studies. Reports of psychoactive plant or fungal use based solely on indigenous testimony must be confirmed through laboratory analysis, as:

- Folk taxonomies may group organisms by ceremonial function rather than pharmacological similarity
- Species misidentification is common, especially when collecting involves multiple similar-looking fungi
- Placebo effects within powerful ceremonial contexts can produce genuine subjective experiences without active compounds
- Toxic species mixed with inert species can produce symptoms mistaken for psychoactive effects

## Puffballs in North American Indigenous Traditions

Beyond Mexico, puffballs (Gasteromycetes, Lycoperdales) have a documented history of magical and religious use among certain North American Indian peoples. Burk (1983) discussed the magic and religious uses of several unidentified puffball species among North American tribes. These fungi typically grew in circles (fairy rings) on the prairies and were referred to as "fallen stars" — a poetic cosmological association linking terrestrial fungi to celestial phenomena.

This "fallen star" motif connects to a broader pattern of indigenous cosmologies in which mushrooms and fungi serve as mediators between sky and earth, the seen and unseen worlds. While the puffballs themselves lack psychoactive properties, their cultural significance may derive from their visual appearance (fairy rings as circles of power), their sudden appearance after rain (associating them with divine provision), and their practical use in traditional medicine.

## Fairy Rings and Cultural Significance

The fairy ring phenomenon — where puffballs and other fungi grow in circular patterns on grasslands — has captured human imagination across cultures. In Europe, fairy rings were associated with supernatural beings; in North America, with fallen stars; in Mexico, with ceremonial use. The circular growth pattern results from the radial expansion of the fungal mycelium from a central point over years or centuries, but to pre-scientific peoples, these perfect circles in nature appeared as evidence of intentional design.

*Lycoperdon perlatum* (the common puffball or gem-studded puffball) is particularly noted for forming fairy rings in Mexican grasslands. Guzmán (1997) documented its use in traditional Mexican medicine, though always as an edible or medicinal fungus, never as a psychoactive one. The contrast between its genuine medicinal properties and its occasional misclassification as a "sacred" fungus illustrates the gap between ethnobotanical classification and pharmacological reality.

## Modern Relevance

The Mixtec puffball case remains relevant for contemporary mycological and ethnopharmacological research for several reasons. First, it demonstrates that indigenous knowledge systems categorize organisms by cultural and ceremonial function, not by chemical composition — a reminder that ethnobotanical reports of psychoactivity require independent verification. Second, it highlights the phenomenon of "placebo psychoactivity" in which the ceremonial context and expectation can produce genuine subjective experiences. Third, it cautions against the uncritical acceptance of secondary sources — the Schultes and Hofmann listings, while authoritative in many respects, perpetuated the puffball claims based solely on Heim and Wasson's original field observations without chemical confirmation.

## Appendix: Taxonomic Notes on Heim and Wasson's Original Species

The original two puffball species reported by Heim and Wasson (1962) have undergone significant taxonomic revision since their original description:

- *Lycoperdon mixtecorum* Heim & Wasson → synonymized with *Vascellum qudenii* following the taxonomic treatment in Ott et al. (1975). The epithet "mixtecorum" honors the Mixtec people.
- *Lycoperdon marginatum* → synonymized with *Lycoperdon candidum* (also known as *Vascellum candidum* or *Bovista candida*).

The genus *Vascellum* was at times subsumed within *Lycoperdon* but has been recognized by some authorities as a distinct genus based on microscopic features of the capillitium (the network of threads inside the spore mass that aids in spore dispersal). These taxonomic changes illustrate the ongoing refinement of gasteromycete systematics and the challenges of comparing modern identifications with historical ethnobotanical reports.
