## Essential Plant Nutrients

Plants require 16 essential mineral nutrients for complete development. These are
divided into macronutrients, needed in relatively large quantities, and
micronutrients, needed in trace amounts. The macronutrients include nitrogen (N),
phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), and sulfur (S). (see [[aquaponics-system-design]] to prevent chemical precipitation. When
calcium and sulfate are mixed at high concentrations, they form calcium sulfate
(gypsum), which precipitates out of solution and becomes unavailable to plants.
Similarly, calcium and phosphate can form insoluble calcium phosphate. By keeping
calcium in a separate stock solution from sulfate and phosphate, these
precipitation reactions are avoided until the stocks are diluted into the large
reservoir volume.

Electrical conductivity (EC) is the primary measurement of total nutrient
concentration in hydroponic solution. EC is measured in milliSiemens per centimeter
(mS/cm) or in parts per million (ppm) using a conversion factor specific to the
nutrient mix. Most hydroponic crops perform well between 1.0 and 3.0 mS/cm, with
exact targets varying by species, growth stage, and environmental conditions.
Cannabis, for example, thrives at 1.2-1.6 mS/cm during early vegetative growth,
1.8-2.2 mS/cm during late vegetative growth, and 1.8-2.4 mS/cm during flowering.

## pH Management

The pH of the nutrient solution determines nutrient availability through its
effect on chemical solubility. Most hydroponic crops prefer a slightly acidic pH
between 5.5 and 6.5, with 5.8 being a commonly cited target. When pH
drifts above 6.5, iron, manganese, zinc, and copper precipitate as insoluble
hydroxides, causing [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]]
can become excessively available, potentially causing toxicity.

pH naturally drifts in hydroponic systems due to differential ion uptake by
plants. When plants take up more cations (positively charged ions like ammonium,
potassium, calcium) than anions (negatively charged ions like nitrate, phosphate),
the solution pH increases. Conversely, when anion uptake exceeds cation uptake,
pH decreases. In most systems, pH tends to drift upward over time. Commercial pH
adjustment products — typically phosphoric acid for lowering pH and potassium
hydroxide for raising pH — are used to maintain the target range.

Automated pH dosing systems are available that monitor pH continuously and inject
small amounts of acid or base to maintain stability. Buffer solutions and biological pH
stabilizers containing beneficial microorganisms can also help moderate pH swings.

## Nutrient Uptake Dynamics

Plants absorb nutrients through root membranes using several mechanisms. Passive
uptake occurs through mass flow, where dissolved nutrients are carried to roots
by water movement, and diffusion, where nutrients move from areas of high
concentration to low concentration. Active uptake requires energy in the form of
ATP to transport nutrients against concentration gradients through membrane
transporter proteins.
