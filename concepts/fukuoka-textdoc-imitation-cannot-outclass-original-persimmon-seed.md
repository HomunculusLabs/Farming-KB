# Imitation Cannot Outclass the Original: The Persimmon Seed Argument





## The Core Claim

Masanobu Fukuoka poses a devastating challenge to the conceit of scientific agriculture: the technology required to fabricate a single persimmon seed from scratch is incomparably greater than that used to launch a rocket into outer space. If the world's scientists pooled all their knowledge and resources, they could not create one persimmon seed. An imitation can never outclass the original. Imperfection shall always lie in the shadow of perfection.

This argument extends far beyond agriculture. It strikes at the foundation of the modern belief that human technology can eventually match or surpass natural processes. Fukuoka uses the humble persimmon seed as a synecdoche for the entire natural world — a world whose complexity and perfection dwarf anything human intelligence can comprehend, let alone reproduce.





## The Student and the Teacher

Fukuoka frames the relationship between man and nature as that of student and teacher. Man imitates nature: he takes the rice plant he finds in nature and tries growing it by imitating the natural processes of seeding and germination. No matter how well he thinks he knows rice, he cannot produce it from scratch. He is no more than a student of nature.

The obvious retort — that a student sometimes catches up with and overtakes his teacher — fails because nature is not merely a teacher but the source and totality of all existence. Were nature, the teacher, to use its full powers, man the student would lose decisively. The question of overtaking the teacher does not arise when the teacher is everything and the student is a tiny part of it.





## The Technology Gap

Fukuoka's persimmon seed argument quantifies the gap between natural and artificial creation in stark terms. Even were man to undertake a solution to the myriad mysteries in the persimmon seed and attempt to fabricate a single seed artificially, the world's scientists pooling all their knowledge and resources would not be up to the task. The level of technology needed to create a single seed or leaf is incomparably greater than that used to launch a rocket into outer space.

Supposing this were possible — that man could fabricate seeds in chemical plants relying solely on the faculties of science — he would probably fall short of his goal even were he to cover the entire face of the earth with factories. The enormity of this failure underscores the fundamental asymmetry between natural creation and human fabrication.





## Man Knows But Persists in Folly

The most damning aspect of the argument is not that man cannot fabricate a seed — it is that he knows he cannot, yet persists in acting as though he can. Man today knows that planting seeds in the ground is much easier than going to the trouble of manufacturing the same seeds scientifically. He knows, but he persists in such reveries anyway.

Even though man is well aware that the human activity we call science can never be superior to nature, his attention is riveted on the imitation rather than the original because he has been led astray by his peculiar myopia that makes science appear to excel over nature in certain areas. He believes in the superiority of science when it comes to crop yields and aesthetics. He expects scientific farming to provide richer harvests than natural farming. He is convinced that taller plants can be grown by spraying hormones, that more grains per head can be produced by applying fertilizer during heading, that higher yields can be attained by an array of yield-enhancing techniques.





## The Sunlight Ceiling

The illusion of scientific superiority is sustained by ignoring the fundamental constraint that bounds all agriculture: the amount of sunlight a field receives is fixed. The yield of rice, which is the amount of starch produced by photosynthesis in a given area, depends on the amount of sunlight that shines on that area. No degree of human tampering with other cultivation conditions can change the upper limit of rice yield. What man believes to be high-yielding technology is just an attempt to approach the limits of natural yields — more accurately, an effort to minimize harvest losses.

When man recognizes this ceiling and tries to breach it by irradiating rice plants with artificial light and blowing carbon dioxide over them to increase starch production, he is still using artificial light and carbon dioxide modeled on their natural counterparts. These were created by man from other materials and did not arise spontaneously. Such artificial increases in yield require enormous energy outlays and are not true increases. Worse, man must take full responsibility for the destruction of the cyclic and material order of the natural world brought about by the use of technology — the basic cause of environmental pollution.





## The Fundamental Myopia

Fukuoka identifies the root error: man has been led astray by his peculiar myopia that makes science appear to excel over nature in certain limited areas. Because scientific farming can produce larger fruit in hothouses, or grow vegetables out of season, or achieve higher yields through intensive management of restricted conditions, people conclude that science is superior to nature.

But this superiority exists only under unnatural, man-made conditions — conditions that deny nature its full powers. Scientific farming excels precisely because natural farming cannot be practiced under such conditions. Under conditions approaching those of nature, Hinayana natural farming yields results at least as good as or better than scientific farming. In holistic terms, Mahayana natural farming, which is both pure and perfect, is always superior.





## Implications for Civilization

The persimmon seed argument has civilizational implications. If man cannot create a single seed, if an imitation can never outclass the original, then the entire trajectory of modern civilization — toward ever greater technological control over natural processes — is founded on an illusion. The "advances" of scientific agriculture are not advances at all but increasingly elaborate and expensive approximations of what nature provides freely.

Man has set his mind on replacing current world food production with food manufactured in chemical plants that rely solely on the faculties of science. He would probably fall short of his goal even were he to cover the entire face of the earth with factories. Yet he constantly goes out of his way to commit such follies. The myopia that makes this possible — the inability to see that the imitation can never surpass the original — is the central spiritual affliction of modern civilization.
