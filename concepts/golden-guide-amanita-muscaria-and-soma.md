# Amanita Muscaria and Soma

## Introduction

The fly agaric mushroom, Amanita muscaria, may be one of humanity's
oldest known hallucinogens. It has been suggested that its strange
effects contributed to early human ideas of deity. Growing in the north
temperate regions of both hemispheres in association with birch trees,
the mushroom has played a central role in Siberian shamanism and may
have been the legendary Soma of ancient India, one of the most
important sacred substances in human religious history.

## Botanical Description

Fly agaric mushrooms grow in the north temperate regions of both
hemispheres. The Eurasian type has a beautiful deep orange to blood-red
cap flecked with white scales. The [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] type varies from
cream to orange-yellow. Chemical differences exist between the two,
with the New World type being devoid of the strongly hallucinogenic
effects of its [[datura-metel-old-world-ethnobotany]] counterpart. Amanita muscaria typically occurs
in association with birches across a wide band of Northern Eurasia.

## Siberian Shamanic Use

The use of Amanita muscaria as an orgiastic and shamanistic inebriant
was discovered in Siberia in 1730. Its utilization has been noted among
several isolated groups:

- **Finno-Ugrian peoples**: Ostyak and Vogul in western Siberia.
- **Paleosiberian tribes**: Chukchee, Koryak, and Kamchadal in
  northeastern Siberia.

These tribes had no other intoxicant until the recent introduction of
alcohol. The mushroom is ingested alone, either sun-dried or toasted
slowly over a fire, or taken in reindeer milk or with the juice of
wild plants such as species of Vaccinium and Epilobium. When eaten
alone, the dried mushrooms are moistened in the mouth and swallowed,
or women roll them into pellets for the men to consume.

## The Urine Recycling Practice

A very old and curious practice of these Siberian tribesmen is the
ritualistic drinking of urine from men who have become intoxicated with
the mushroom. The active principles pass through the body and are
excreted unchanged or as still active derivatives. Consequently, a few
mushrooms may inebriate many people through this recycling method. This
practice is directly relevant to the identification of Amanita muscaria
as the [[mckenna-vedic-soma-indo-european-mysteries]], since the Rig Veda describes ritual urine drinking
in connection with the Soma ceremony.

## Nature of the Intoxication

The intoxication varies but typically involves one or more of the
following effects:

- Twitching, trembling, and slight convulsions
- Numbness of the limbs
- A feeling of ease characterized by happiness
- A desire to sing and dance
- Colored visions and macropsia (seeing things greatly enlarged)
- Violence occasionally giving way to deep sleep
- Religious fervor often accompanying the inebriation

Participants are sometimes overtaken by curious beliefs, such as an
ancient tribesman who insisted he had just been born. The nature of
the experience ranges from pleasant inebriation to deep visionary
states, depending on dosage and individual sensitivity.

## Chemistry of the Active Principles

For a century, the intoxicating principle was believed to be muscarine,
but muscarine is present in such minute concentrations that it cannot
act as the inebriant. It is now recognized that [[amanita-muscaria-muscimol-ibotenic-acid-pharmacology]] forms
several derivatives during the drying or extraction of the mushrooms.
The main pharmacologically active principle is muscimole, found only
in this mushroom. Other compounds such as muscazone are present in
lesser concentrations and may contribute to the overall intoxication.
The fact that muscimole is the sole natural hallucinogenic chemical
excreted unchanged from the body is key to identifying Amanita as
Soma, since the Vedic hymns specifically describe urine recycling.

## The Soma Hypothesis

Recent studies suggest that Amanita muscaria was the mysterious
God-narcotic Soma of ancient India. Thousands of years ago, Aryan
conquerors swept across India worshipping Soma, drinking it in
religious ceremonies. Many hymns in the Indian Rig Veda are devoted to
Soma and describe the plant and its effects in detail. The use of Soma
eventually died out, and its identity remained an enigma for 2,000
years. During the past century, more than 100 plants were suggested as
candidates, but none answered the descriptions found in the Vedic hymns
until Amanita muscaria was proposed.

The identification is strengthened by several converging lines of
evidence: the reference in the Vedas to ceremonial urine drinking, the
description of the plant's effects (which match Amanita intoxication),
and the fact that muscimole is uniquely excreted unchanged from the
body. The Wassons' ethnomycological detective work, building on the
Vedic descriptions, has made this identification the most widely
accepted among scholars.

## Ethnobotanical Detective Work

The identification of Amanita muscaria as Soma represents a triumph of
interdisciplinary scholarship combining botany, chemistry, linguistics,
and comparative religion. The Wassons' research demonstrated that
mycophobic cultures (those that fear mushrooms) tend to have historical
connections to societies that once used [[cattle-dispersal-psychoactive-fungi-australia]] in sacred
contexts. The taboos against mushroom consumption in Northern Europe
may represent a cultural memory of the dangerous power of the sacred
fungus, restricted to shamans and priests. This pattern of cultural
prohibition surrounding a once-sacred substance parallels the
suppression of other entheogenic practices by colonial and religious
authorities throughout history.

## Fly Killer Name

The name "fly agaric" derives from the mushroom's age-old use in
Europe as a fly killer. Mushrooms were left in an open dish; flies
attracted to and settling on them were stunned, succumbing to the
insecticidal properties of the plant. This practical use was unrelated
to its hallucinogenic properties.
