# Ectomycorrhizal Diversity and Ecosystem Function

The relationship between ectomycorrhizal fungal diversity and ecosystem function represents a frontier in microbial ecology. While it has long been recognized that different ectomycorrhizal species perform different physiological functions, the question of whether diversity per se drives ecosystem productivity—beyond the contributions of individual species—has only recently been addressed through controlled experimentation.

## Historical Context

The recognition that ectomycorrhizal fungi are not functionally interchangeable emerged gradually. Early work by Dighton (1983), Abuzinadah and Read (1986, 1989), and Dighton et al. (1990) demonstrated that all ectomycorrhizae do not function with the same efficiency in any given physiological process. Different species vary in their:

- Ability to access different forms of nitrogen (organic vs. inorganic)
- Phosphatase enzyme production and P acquisition strategies
- Capacity to protect host roots from pathogens
- Extent of extraradical hyphal exploration
- Efficiency of carbon use and allocation patterns

Despite these recognized differences, the broader question of whether the number of species in a mycorrhizal community (diversity) matters for ecosystem function, independent of which species are present, remained largely untested until the late 1990s.

## The Biodiversity-Ecosystem Function Debate

The general biodiversity-ecosystem function debate in ecology received major impetus from Tilman et al. (1996), who demonstrated that plant species diversity enhances ecosystem productivity and stability. This work catalyzed investigation of whether similar relationships exist for below-ground microbial communities, including mycorrhizal fungi.

Van der Heijden et al. (1998) provided the first compelling evidence in the mycorrhizal context, showing that plant productivity is highest when arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal diversity is greatest. This work established the principle that mycorrhizal diversity could have ecosystem-level consequences, not merely community-level effects.

## The Baxter and Dighton Diversity Manipulation Experiments

Baxter and Dighton (2001) conducted controlled laboratory experiments specifically designed to test whether ectomycorrhizal diversity per se influences plant performance, independent of species composition and colonization rates.

### Experimental Design
The experiments constructed ectomycorrhizal communities of varying diversity:
- **1-species assemblages**: Single fungal species colonizing birch seedlings
- **2-species assemblages**: Pairs of fungal species
- **4-species assemblages**: Communities of four fungal species

Birch seedlings were used as host plants, and nutrient content of seedling tissues served as the primary measure of ecosystem function.

### Key Findings

**Diversity per se matters**: Ectomycorrhizal diversity was a better determinant of improved birch seedling nutrient content than either the actual species composition of the assemblage or the colonization rates achieved. This suggests that having more species provides some functional benefit beyond the additive contributions of individual species.

**Stronger effect on phosphorus than nitrogen**: The diversity effect was more pronounced for P uptake than for N uptake into plant tissues. This is consistent with the understanding that phosphorus acquisition is a primary function of ectomycorrhizae and that different fungal species may access different P pools (organic vs. inorganic, different spatial locations) through complementary strategies.

### Interpretation

The finding that diversity matters beyond species composition suggests several possible mechanisms:

1. **Complementarity**: Different species access different nutrient pools or use different enzymatic strategies, so more species = more complete resource capture
2. **Facilitation**: Some species may modify the soil environment in ways that benefit other species (e.g., weathering minerals, creating organic matter)
3. **Insurance effect**: More diverse communities are more likely to contain species that can maintain function under changing conditions
4. **Reduced competition**: In more diverse communities, interspecific interactions may be less competitive than intraspecific interactions in monocultures, allowing more efficient resource use

## Functional Differences Among Ectomycorrhizal Species

The diversity-effect findings are supported by a growing body of evidence showing functional differentiation among ectomycorrhizal fungal species:

### Nitrogen Acquisition Strategies
Different ectomycorrhizal species vary dramatically in their ability to access organic nitrogen sources. Some species produce proteases and other enzymes to break down organic N compounds, while others primarily take up inorganic N forms. This functional complementarity in N acquisition is likely a key driver of the diversity effect.

### Phosphatase Production
Species vary in their production of acid and alkaline phosphatase enzymes, which cleave phosphate from organic compounds. Kieliszewska-Rokicka (1992) showed that phosphatase activity in *Paxillus involutus* responds to N availability, and Antibus and Linkins (1992) demonstrated that liming reduced community-level phosphatase activity. The diversity of phosphatase strategies across species likely enhances total P acquisition in mixed communities.

### Exploration Types
Ectomycorrhizal fungi are classified by their exploration strategies—from contact types with minimal extraradical hyphae to long-distance types with extensive mycelial networks. Different exploration types access different soil volumes and nutrient pools, providing another axis of functional complementarity.

### Pathogen Protection
Different mycorrhizal species provide varying degrees of protection against soil-borne pathogens. A diverse mycorrhizal community may provide more comprehensive pathogen protection than any single species.

## Implications for Pollution-Induced Diversity Loss

The diversity-function findings have critical implications for understanding the consequences of anthropogenic disturbance:

### Acidifying Pollutants
As documented in the broader research on acidifying pollutants, nitrogen deposition and acid rain reduce ectomycorrhizal diversity by selectively suppressing sensitive species while promoting tolerant ones (like *Paxillus involutus*). If diversity per se drives function, then this selective species loss may reduce ecosystem nutrient cycling capacity even though some mycorrhizal colonization persists.

### The "Redundancy" Question
An important unresolved question is the degree of functional redundancy among ectomycorrhizal species. If many species perform similar functions, diversity loss may have minimal functional consequences until a threshold is reached. If species are highly complementary, even modest diversity loss could have disproportionate functional effects.

### Conservation Implications
The recognition that mycorrhizal diversity contributes to ecosystem function strengthens the case for fungal conservation. Arnolds' (1989b, 1997) red data lists for fungi and the UK's inclusion of fungi in conservation listings (Watling, 1999) are supported by the understanding that species loss may have functional consequences beyond the mere presence or absence of individual taxa.

## Connections to Carbon Transfer and Common Mycorrhizal Networks

The functional significance of mycorrhizal diversity intersects with research on carbon transfer between trees through common mycorrhizal networks. Simard et al. (1997b, 1997c) demonstrated reciprocal carbon transfer between *Betula papyrifera* and *Pseudotsuga menziesii* through shared ectomycorrhizal networks. The diversity of fungal species connecting different tree species may influence the magnitude and direction of carbon transfer, with implications for forest carbon cycling and stand dynamics.

## Research Frontiers

The diversity-function debate regarding mycorrhizal fungi remains in its infancy. Key research priorities include:

- Translating laboratory diversity experiments to field conditions
- Understanding how diversity effects interact with other environmental factors (pollution, climate change, management)
- Quantifying the functional consequences of specific species losses
- Determining whether complementarity or insurance mechanisms drive diversity effects
- Integrating molecular community analysis with functional measurements
- Extending diversity manipulation experiments to include saprotrophic fungi and their interactions with mycorrhizal communities

Much more work is needed to fully explain the consequences of changes in both mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungal community structure on ecosystem-level processes, particularly under the multiple stressors imposed by human activities.
