# Community Money and Asset Categories

In [[permaculture-design]], money and finance are treated as design problems — systems that can be
restructured to serve ecological and community health rather than extraction and accumulation.
[[bill-mollison]]'s framework draws a sharp distinction between systems that tie money to real
assets and those that decouple wealth from productive reality.

## Tribal Exchange Systems vs. Fiscal Societies

Tribal societies operated on exchange systems grounded in reciprocity, gift economies, and
direct barter. These systems kept wealth circulating within the community and prevented the
accumulation of abstract financial power. Resources flowed through kinship and obligation
networks, ensuring surpluses were redistributed.

Fiscal societies introduce abstract money — a medium of exchange divorced from any specific
asset. This abstraction enables hoarding, speculative accumulation, and the concentration of
financial power in institutions physically and morally distant from the communities they affect.
The shift from tribal exchange to fiscal systems represents a fundamental transformation in how
societies relate to resources, obligation, and value.

## Money Tied to Real Assets

A core [[permaculture]] financial principle is that money should be tied to real, productive assets
rather than existing as an abstract claim on future labor or resources. When money represents
actual productive capacity — land under cultivation, tools in use, stored harvests — it remains
anchored to ecological reality. Community-based currencies, local exchange trading systems
(LETS), and resource-backed credit all reconnect money to real assets.

## The Five Asset Categories

Mollison classifies assets into five categories based on their energetic and ecological
characteristics. Understanding these is essential for directing investment toward long-term
system health.

### Degenerative Assets

Assets that deteriorate over time through use and exposure — buildings, roads, vehicles, and
machinery. They require continuous energy and material input to maintain. While necessary for
infrastructure, they drain resources rather than producing them. Sound financial strategy
limits over-investment in degenerative assets and prioritizes efficient maintenance over expansion.

### Generative Assets

Tools and systems that actively process resources into useful products — processing equipment,
workshops, manufacturing tools, and knowledge systems that drive production. These create
value by transforming raw inputs into higher-order outputs. Investment here strengthens a
community's productive capacity and self-reliance.

### Procreative Assets

Living systems that reproduce and multiply their yield over time — trees, wildlife, fish stocks,
breeding livestock, and perennial plant systems. They embody the [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]] of yield
increasing over time with minimal ongoing input. Procreative assets are the foundation of
sustainable community wealth.

### Informational Assets

Education, data, seed varieties, design knowledge, ecological understanding, and cultural
practices. These do not degrade with use; they appreciate as they are shared and applied.
Seed libraries, educational programs, and community skill-sharing networks all qualify. Their
unique property is that distributing them strengthens rather than diminishes community holdings.

### Conservative Assets

Assets that store and preserve resources for future use — insulation, dams, water storage,
grain silos, [[root-cellars-and-cold-storage]], and soil carbon. They protect against scarcity by holding energy
and materials in reserve. Investment in conservative assets is a form of community insurance,
buffering against disruption and reducing dependence on external supply.

## Ethical Banking Principles

Ethical banking means financial institutions serving community and ecological goals rather than
shareholder profit maximization. Key principles include lending preferentially to regenerative
enterprises, reinvesting deposits locally, transparency in investment destinations, avoiding
investment in extractive or polluting industries, charging fair interest rates, and
prioritizing long-term borrower health over short-term returns.

## Financial Institution Types

Mollison examines a spectrum of institutions and their relationship to community control:

- **Credit Unions**: Member-owned cooperatives providing savings and lending. Depositors are
  shareholders; profits return as dividends. Among the most community-aligned institutions.

- **Cooperatives**: Member-owned enterprises for mutual benefit, providing banking, insurance,
  and investment governed by democratic member control.

- **Trusts**: Legal arrangements holding assets for beneficiaries. Community trusts can hold
  land or capital in perpetuity, protecting them from speculative exploitation.

- **Savings Banks**: Deposit-accepting institutions making mortgage and community loans.
  Locally governed, they can serve community investment needs effectively.

- **Insurance**: Risk-pooling mechanisms structured as mutual societies owned by policyholders.

- **Commercial Banks**: Profit-driven institutions serving shareholder interests. Their tendency
  to extract deposits for speculative lending makes them least aligned with permaculture
  finance principles.

- **Investment Brokers**: Capital intermediaries whose alignment depends on ethical frameworks.

- **Limited Liability Companies**: Structures protecting investors from personal liability.
  The limited liability principle can externalize environmental and social costs onto communities.

## Community Ownership of Financial Institutions

The permaculture ideal is community ownership and democratic governance of financial institutions.
When communities own their banks, credit unions, and insurance cooperatives, capital serves
local needs rather than being extracted by distant shareholders. Community-owned institutions
direct lending toward [[regenerative-agriculture]], local enterprise, [[holmgren-wind-power-and-renewable-energy-debate]], and
affordable housing — building real wealth rather than abstract financial returns.

## Users Pay and Proposers Pay Capital

Two permaculture financial principles govern who bears costs:

- **Users Pay**: Those who directly use and benefit from a resource should bear its costs.
  This prevents externalizing costs onto non-beneficiaries and ensures economically
  self-regulating usage.

- **Proposers Pay Capital**: Those who propose development should bear its capital costs.
  This discourages speculative projects and ensures only committed, viable proposals proceed.
  It shifts risk from the community onto the proposer, where it rightfully belongs.

## Right Livelihood

Right livelihood holds that one's work should contribute positively to community and ecological
health rather than causing harm. Financially, this means directing productive energy and capital
toward regenerative, just, and locally beneficial enterprises. It rejects employment or investment
in extractive industries, exploitative labor practices, or financial speculation divorced from
real production.

## Money Accumulation and Environmental Destruction

Mollison draws a direct connection between abstract money accumulation and environmental
destruction. When wealth is measured primarily in financial terms rather than ecological health
or productive capacity, systems incentivize extraction, speculation, and short-term profit over
long-term stewardship. Money accumulation drives the conversion of procreative and conservative
assets (forests, wetlands, soil) into degenerative assets (urban sprawl, industrial
infrastructure) that continuously drain resources. Breaking this cycle requires restructuring
financial systems so that abstract wealth creation is impossible without real ecological wealth.
