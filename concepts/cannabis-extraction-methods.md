# Cannabis Extraction Methods

Comparison of extraction techniques for making cannabis concentrates, tinctures, edibles, and topicals. Based on historical techniques (Lemon's Cannabis Alchemy) and modern methods.

Related: [[cannabis-health-therapeutics]], [[cannabis-plant-nutrition]]

## Decarboxylation Fundamentals

Raw cannabis contains THCA and CBDA (non-psychoactive acidic forms). Decarboxylation converts these to active THC and CBD by removing the carboxyl group (-COOH).

- **Temperature**: 220-240F (105-115C) optimal. Below 200F = incomplete conversion. Above 300F = THC degradation to CBN and terpene loss.
- **Time**: 30-60 min at target temperature. Longer times at lower temps preserve terpenes better (e.g., 200F for 90 min).
- **Methods**: Oven (spread thin on baking sheet, cover with foil), sous vide (precise temp control, sealed bags), Ardent Nova/Decarboxylator devices, roasting in dry skillet (less controlled)
- **Boiling water method**: Simmer cannabis in water for 1+ hour, then strain and dry. Decarboxylates while removing ~20% water-soluble tars and waxes. Historic "Pittsburgh Doublewash" pre-treatment.
- **Loss rate**: Expect 10-15% weight loss during decarboxylation (CO2 release).
- **Note**: THCA has some medical value (anti-inflammatory, anti-nausea). Only decarboxylate when psychoactive effects or specific THC/CBD activity is desired. Raw juicing preserves acidic cannabinoids.

## Solvent-Based Extraction

### Ethanol Extraction

- **How it works**: Ethanol dissolves cannabinoids, terpenes, chlorophyll, waxes, and water-soluble compounds from plant material. Cold extraction (below 0F) reduces chlorophyll pickup.
- **Equipment**: Food-grade ethanol (Everclear 190 proof), mason jars or extraction vessel, filters (cheesecloth, coffee filters), rotary evaporator or gentle heat for solvent recovery, vacuum oven for final purging.
- **Process**: Decarboxylate and grind cannabis. Submerge in ethanol (10:1 ethanol:plant ratio minimum). Agitate 10-30 min. Strain and filter. Evaporate ethanol (rotary evaporator ideal; water bath at 170F acceptable). Vacuum purge residual solvent.
- **Cold wash vs full soak**: Cold wash (brief, frozen ethanol) extracts cannabinoids and terpenes with minimal chlorophyll -- produces golden, clean oil. Full soak at room temp extracts everything including chlorophyll -- requires winterization to clean up.
- **Winterization**: Dissolve crude extract in ethanol, freeze at -20F for 24-48 hr, filter out precipitated waxes and lipids. Essential for full-soak extracts, optional for cold wash.
- **Safety**: Ethanol is flammable but food-safe. No toxic residues at proper purge. Legal to purchase in many jurisdictions. Best for home extraction.
- **Yield**: 10-20% of starting material weight (crude), 60-80% of available cannabinoids extracted.
- **Quality**: High if cold-washed and properly winterized. Preserves terpenes better than butane.
- **Cost**: Low to moderate. Ethanol $20-50/gallon. Equipment $100-500 home scale.
- **Pros**: Safe, legal, food-grade, versatile, good terpene preservation. | **Cons**: Also extracts chlorophyll (needs cold wash or winterization), ethanol recovery requires equipment for efficiency.

### Supercritical CO2 Extraction

- **How it works**: CO2 is pressurized above its critical point (1071 psi, 88F) where it behaves as both liquid and gas. Acts as a non-polar solvent tuned by pressure/temperature to target specific compounds.
- **Equipment**: Commercial CO2 extraction system ($5,000-$500,000+), CO2 tanks, collection vessels, temperature/pressure controls. Not feasible for home use.
- **Process**: Material loaded into extraction vessel. CO2 pumped through at controlled temperature and pressure. Different "runs" at different parameters extract different compound classes (cannabinoids vs terpenes vs waxes). Subcritical CO2 for terpenes; supercritical for cannabinoids.
- **Safety**: Very safe -- CO2 is non-toxic, non-flammable, leaves no residue. However, high-pressure equipment requires professional operation.
- **Yield**: 8-15% of starting weight. Tunable for selective extraction.
- **Quality**: Excellent purity and consistency. Industry standard for commercial products. Can produce full-spectrum or isolated extracts.
- **Cost**: Very high. Professional equipment and training required. $50,000+ for production-scale systems.
- **Pros**: No toxic solvents, tunable selectivity, very clean product, scalable. | **Cons**: Extremely expensive, requires professional operation, strips some terpenes at high pressure.

### Butane Extraction (BHO)

- **How it works**: Liquid butane passes through cannabis, dissolving cannabinoids and terpenes. Butane has very low boiling point (31F) so it evaporates quickly at room temperature.
- **Equipment**: Open or closed-loop butane extractor, butane (vector, colibri, near-zero impurity), Pyrex collection dish, vacuum oven, parchment paper.
- **Process**: Pack column with cannabis. Pass butane through slowly. Collect butane-cannabinoid solution. Allow butane to evaporate (open blast) or recover with vacuum pump (closed loop). Vacuum purge at 90-110F for 24-72 hr. Whip, press, or leave as shatter.
- **Safety**: EXTREMELY DANGEROUS. Butane is highly flammable and heavier than air -- pools in low areas and ignites from any spark. Open blasting has caused explosions, fires, and deaths. Closed-loop systems are safer but still require explosion-proof environments. Not recommended for home extraction.
- **Yield**: 15-25% of starting weight. High extraction efficiency.
- **Quality**: Can produce very high-quality concentrates (shatter, wax, crumble, budder). Texture depends on purge temperature and agitation. Terpene preservation excellent with proper low-temp extraction.
- **Cost**: Moderate equipment ($500-5,000 closed loop). Butane $5-15/can. Very high risk cost.
- **Pros**: High yield, excellent quality, fast processing, terpene preservation. | **Cons**: Extremely dangerous (fire/explosion risk), illegal in many jurisdictions without licensing, requires professional equipment, residual solvent risk if improperly purged.

### Propane Extraction (PHO)

- **How it works**: Similar to BHO but using propane as solvent. Lower boiling point (-44F) enables lower-temperature extraction that better preserves terpenes.
- **Equipment**: Closed-loop system only (propane is even more dangerous than butane in open systems). Professional extraction equipment.
- **Characteristics**: Produces "live resin" style products with superior terpene profiles. Lower extraction temperature preserves heat-sensitive compounds. Typically produces more budder/wax consistency than shatter.
- **Safety**: Even more dangerous than butane extraction. Professional-only operation in explosion-proof facilities.
- **Pros**: Best terpene preservation among solvent methods, unique product textures. | **Cons**: Most dangerous extraction method, professional-only, expensive equipment.

## Solventless Extraction

### Rosin Press

- **How it works**: Heat and pressure extract cannabinoids and terpenes from cannabis flower or hash. No solvents involved.
- **Equipment**: Rosin press (manual or pneumatic, $100-2,000+), parchment paper, collection tools. Hair straighteners work for small batches.
- **Process**: Place cannabis or hash between parchment paper. Apply heat (180-220F for flower; 160-200F for hash/ice water extract) and pressure (500-2000 PSI). Collect expressed rosin from parchment.
- **Temperatures**: Lower temps = better terpene preservation, lower yield. Higher temps = higher yield, darker color, more terpene loss. 190-200F is a common sweet spot.
- **Yield**: 10-20% for flower; 40-60% for bubble hash starting material.
- **Quality**: Excellent. Solventless, full-spectrum, retains original terpene profile. Increasingly considered the highest quality extraction method. "Live rosin" from fresh frozen bubble hash is the premium product.
- **Cost**: Low to moderate. Entry-level manual presses ~$100. Production pneumatic presses $500-5,000.
- **Pros**: No solvents (safest method), legal everywhere, simple, high quality, preserves full terpene profile. | **Cons**: Lower yield than solvent methods, requires good starting material, labor intensive at scale.

### Ice Water Hash (Bubble Hash)

- **How it works**: Ice and agitation separate trichome heads from plant material. Trichomes sink, plant material floats. Multiple mesh sizes allow graded collection.
- **Equipment**: Bubble bags (set of mesh bags in different micron sizes: 220, 160, 120, 90, 73, 45, 25), 5-gallon buckets, ice, mixing drill with paint mixer attachment.
- **Process**: Line bucket with bags (largest mesh first). Fill with ice and cannabis (1:2 cannabis:ice ratio). Add cold water. Agitate 10-20 min. Let settle. Drain and collect from each bag. 73-120 micron bags yield highest quality. Dry on screens at 50-60F, 50% humidity for 3-7 days.
- **Yield**: 5-15% of flower weight. Full melt (highest quality, melts completely) from 73-120 micron. Cooking grade from 160-220 micron.
- **Quality**: Very high. "Full melt" bubble hash is among the most respected solventless products. Can be pressed into rosin for further concentration.
- **Cost**: Low. Bubble bags $50-200. Ice and buckets ~$20.
- **Pros**: No solvents, safe, scalable, graded quality selection. | **Cons**: Water-intensive, messy, requires proper drying (mold risk), yield depends heavily on starting material quality.

### Dry Sift (Kief)

- **How it works**: Mechanical separation of trichomes by passing cannabis through screens of specific mesh sizes. Friction knocks trichome heads loose.
- **Equipment**: Silk screens (various mesh sizes, 100-150 micron typical), collection trays, card/tumble methods. Commercial tumblers available.
- **Process**: Place frozen cannabis on screen. Gently work material across screen surface. Trichome heads fall through; plant material stays on top. Collect powder (kief) from beneath screen. Multiple passes through decreasing mesh sizes improve purity.
- **Yield**: 5-15% of flower weight. Higher yields with dry, properly cured flower.
- **Quality**: Variable. Depends on screen size, technique, and starting material. Can approach bubble hash quality with proper technique and high-quality screens.
- **Cost**: Very low. Screens $20-100. Tumblers $100-500.
- **Pros**: Simplest extraction method, no equipment needed, no solvents, easy to scale up. | **Cons**: Lower purity than wet methods, plant contamination common, yield depends on technique.

## Emerging Methods

### Ultrasonic Extraction

- **How it works**: Ultrasonic waves create cavitation bubbles in solvent, disrupting cell walls and dramatically increasing extraction efficiency. Can be combined with ethanol or other solvents.
- **Equipment**: Ultrasonic bath or probe homogenizer, solvent, plant material.
- **Benefits**: Reduces extraction time from hours to minutes. Higher yields at lower temperatures (better terpene preservation). Scalable for commercial production.
- **Cost**: Moderate to high. Probe homogenizers $500-5,000.

### Microwave-Assisted Extraction

- **How it works**: Microwave energy heats solvent and plant material internally, creating pressure that ruptures cell walls and releases compounds.
- **Benefits**: Very fast (seconds to minutes). Efficient solvent use. Can be combined with ethanol or other solvents. Simultaneous decarboxylation possible.
- **Limitations**: Difficult to control precisely at home. Risk of overheating and terpene loss. More suitable for commercial applications.

## Making Products

### Tinctures

- **Base**: High-proof alcohol (Everclear 190 proof) or food-grade glycerin (for alcohol-free)
- **Process**: Decarboxylate cannabis. Cover with alcohol (1:2 cannabis:alcohol ratio for strong; 1:4 for moderate). Seal and store in dark place 4-8 weeks, shaking daily. Strain through cheesecloth and coffee filter. Store in amber dropper bottle.
- **Quick method**: Combine decarbed cannabis and alcohol in mason jar. Place jar in water bath at 170F for 20 min. Cool, strain, bottle. Less extraction efficiency but ready in hours.
- **Dosage**: ~3-5 mg THC per dropper (1 mL) for 1:2 ratio with average-potency flower. Label clearly with estimated mg/mL.
- **Shelf life**: 1+ years (alcohol base). Glycerin: 6-12 months.

### Edibles

- **Infusion base**: Cannabutter or cannabis coconut oil (coconut oil preferred -- higher fat content, longer shelf life, no refrigeration needed).
- **Butter process**: Decarboxylate cannabis. Simmer 1 cup butter + 1 cup water + 7-10 g decarbed cannabis on low heat (160-180F) for 2-3 hours. Strain through cheesecloth. Refrigerate -- butter separates from water. Solid butter on top is the infusion.
- **Coconut oil process**: Decarboxylate cannabis. Combine with coconut oil (1 cup oil per 7-10 g cannabis) in slow cooker on low for 4-6 hours. Strain. No water separation needed.
- **Dosage math**: 10 g flower at 15% THC = 1,500 mg total THC. ~85% extraction efficiency = ~1,275 mg THC total. Divided into 50 portions = ~25 mg THC per portion. Always calculate and label doses.
- **Onset**: 30-120 min. Peak effects at 2-3 hours. Duration 4-8+ hours. NEVER redose within 2 hours of eating an edible.
- **Storage**: Refrigerate (butter) or room temperature (coconut oil). 2-3 months refrigerated. Can be frozen for 6+ months.

### Topicals

- **Base**: Coconut oil, shea butter, beeswax, olive oil, or combination.
- **Process**: Decarboxylate cannabis. Infuse into carrier oil (similar to edible infusion but can skip decarboxylation if anti-inflammatory THCA/CBDA effects are desired -- topicals do not produce psychoactive effects even with THC). Add beeswax (1:4 wax:oil ratio) for salve consistency. Add essential oils for additional therapeutic effects (arnica for pain, calendula for skin, menthol/camphor for cooling/warming).
- **Applications**: Pain relief (arthritis, muscle soreness, neuropathy), inflammation, skin conditions (psoriasis, eczema), headache (temple application). Topicals do NOT enter bloodstream -- no psychoactive effects.
- **Potency**: 100-300 mg THC/CBD per ounce of topical is common for pain formulations.

## Historical Context: Cannabis Alchemy (Lemon, 1979)

Ben Lemon's "Cannabis Alchemy" (written under pseudonym Nicolas Flamel) was one of the first widely distributed practical guides to cannabis oil chemistry. Key contributions from this foundational text:

### The Kitchen Reflux Method
Lemon documented a complete extraction apparatus using household items: a stainless steel pot for the cannabis/solvent, a larger stew pot as condenser, a water bath tub, inner-tube rubber bands for sealing, polyethylene sheeting as a pressure-release safety valve, and ice for condensation. This indirect heating design (boiling water heats the solvent, never directly) became a template for safe home extraction. The apparatus allows refluxing, soxhleting, solvent distillation, and collection -- all core extraction operations.

### Solvent Comparison (Lemon)
- **Methanol**: Effective, uniform evaporation at ~190F, doesn't extract many water-soluble tars. Fumes are toxic and explosive. Traces hazardous to consumer.
- **Isopropyl rubbing alcohol (70%)**: Available everywhere, less toxic. But water content extracts non-psychoactive tars; oil yield is double but proportionally less potent. Higher evaporation temperature.
- **Ethanol**: Ideal properties similar to methanol but less toxic. Very difficult to obtain pure; denatured versions contain non-removable poisons.
- **Petroleum ether**: Most selective solvent; produces oil twice as potent by weight as alcohol extraction. Highly explosive -- used for secondary purification rather than primary extraction.

### Purification by Liquid-Liquid Separation
After alcohol extraction, the oil is dissolved in alcohol, mixed with water, then extracted with petroleum ether. Three layers form: bottom (water, alcohol, tars/resins), middle (wax emulsion), top (purified oil in petroleum ether). The top layer is siphoned off and the ether evaporated, yielding refined oil.

### Potency Enhancement of Intact Flowers
Lemon described a method to increase the potency of intact flowers by extracting oil, isomerizing and purifying it, then re-infusing it back into the original dried flowers using a syringe. The result: flowers over twelve times their original potency.

### Reefers (Impregnated Joints)
