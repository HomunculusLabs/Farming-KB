# Fungal Endemism and Biogeography

Fungal biogeography — the study of fungal species distributions across space
and time — remains a relatively young field. Historically, many mycologists
assumed that most fungi had cosmopolitan distributions, dispersed globally by
airborne spores. Modern molecular evidence has overturned this assumption,
revealing that fungal endemism is far more common than previously believed.

## The Cosmopolitan Assumption

The idea that "everything is everywhere" for fungi persisted for decades,
largely because microscopic fungal spores were thought to disperse without
barrier. This assumption led to the neglect of biogeographic studies in
mycology and an underappreciation of the role that geography plays in fungal
speciation and diversity patterns. As DNA sequencing became widespread, it
became clear that many fungal species are restricted to specific continents,
regions, or even individual habitat patches.

## Patterns of Endemism

Several regions have been identified as hotspots of fungal endemism:

- **Tropical rainforests**: The highest known fungal diversity, with enormous
  numbers of undescribed species. The Amazon, Congo Basin, and Southeast Asian
  rainforests are particularly understudied. Many tropical fungi appear to have
  narrow geographic ranges tied to specific host plants.

- **Island ecosystems**: Islands such as New Zealand, Madagascar, and the
  Hawaiian archipelago harbor highly endemic fungal communities shaped by
  isolation and unique host assemblages. Island biogeography applies strongly
  to fungi, with smaller islands supporting fewer species.

- **Mediterranean-type ecosystems**: California, the Cape Floristic Region,
  and southwestern Australia show significant fungal endemism linked to
  endemic plant hosts, particularly in ectomycorrhizal communities.

- **Boreal and temperate forests**: While less diverse than the tropics,
  northern forests contain many range-restricted species associated with
  particular tree species and soil conditions.

## Drivers of Fungal Distribution

The primary factors shaping fungal biogeographic patterns include:

1. **Host specificity**: Many plant-associated fungi (pathogens, endophytes,
   mycorrhizae) are restricted to particular host species, making their
   distributions mirror plant biogeography.

2. **Climate**: Temperature and precipitation regimes strongly filter fungal
   communities. Fungi adapted to specific moisture and thermal conditions
   cannot survive outside their climatic envelopes.

3. **Historical factors**: Continental drift, glaciation cycles, and mountain
  uplift have created and erased dispersal pathways over evolutionary time,
   leaving signatures in modern fungal distributions.

4. **Dispersal limitation**: Despite producing vast numbers of spores, many
   fungi are surprisingly dispersal-limited. Large-scale geographic barriers
   (oceans, mountain ranges, deserts) can prevent gene flow between
   populations, leading to allopatric speciation.

## Conservation Implications

The recognition of fungal endemism has important conservation consequences.
Habitat destruction in biodiversity hotspots almost certainly drives fungal
extinctions that go entirely unnoticed. Without baseline knowledge of which
fungi occur where, it is impossible to assess extinction risk or prioritize
conservation efforts. Fungal biogeography urgently needs more sampling effort
in understudied regions, particularly the tropics.
