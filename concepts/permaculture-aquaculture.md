# Permaculture Aquaculture

## The Case for Aquaculture

Mollison argues that aquaculture is as stable and necessary a future enterprise as forestry. Of all existing systems, tropical rainforest and shallow-water aquatic environments have the greatest natural yields. Mangrove swamps, marshes, and estuaries produce prodigious biomass of great complexity.

Water-based systems offer 4-20 times the yield per unit of input compared to adjoining land, for these reasons:

- **Constant water supply**: Plant and animal growth in aquatic habitats is not limited by rainfall
- **Soluble nutrients**: Plant nutrients are available in dissolved, easily assimilable form
- **Water and nutrient flow**: A continuous boost to production not present in terrestrial fields
- **Reduced gravitational cost**: Aquatic organisms spend little energy on movement or structural support
- **Three-dimensional medium**: Light, nutrients, and organisms occupy all three dimensions, with complex edges and surfaces
- **Polycultural tradition**: Water cultures have been polycultural from inception, unlike terrestrial agriculture's recent shift to monoculture
- **Reduced cultivation energy**: No plowing, cultivation, or harvesting machinery needed
- **Multiple products**: Impounded water yields food, energy, recreation, irrigation, transport, and fire protection

## Design Principles for Aquaculture

### Polyculture Over Monoculture

"The highest fish production per hectare can only be obtained by using a combination of species of different feeding habits." Polycultural ponds combine species that occupy different niches: surface feeders, mid-water feeders, bottom feeders, detritivores, and algae grazers. Each species uses a different food resource, so total production far exceeds any single-species system. Wild ducks do not annoy catfish and pay their way in phosphatic fertilizer; there is no need to deprive them of islands, shallows, or nest boxes.

### Carrying Capacity

Ponds have a specific carrying capacity determined by volume, area, and edge. Overcrowding causes fish to cease growing; the water is then fully stocked. Growth rates follow predictable curves based on initial stocking density, available food, oxygen levels, and pond conditions.

### Edge Design

Lobular shorelines maximize the edge zone between water and land, creating shallow areas for emergent plants, nesting sites for waterfowl, and habitat for amphibians and invertebrates. The "zone of edge effect" around a pond is the most productive area and should be maximized through crenellated shore design, connected to [[edge-effect-and-microclimate]].

### Water Quality

Dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature, and nutrient levels determine pond productivity. Design features that maintain water quality include:

- Shallow edges for oxygenation by wind and photosynthesis
- Deep zones for temperature stability and overwintering
- Water flow-through where possible (spring-fed or stream-fed systems)
- Biological filtration through plant zones
- Duck and waterfowl integration (manure fertilizes algae, which feeds fish)
- Avoiding excess nutrient input that causes algal blooms and oxygen depletion

### Pond Configuration

Pond shape, depth, and orientation affect productivity. Deep ponds stratify thermally; shallow ponds are warmer but more prone to temperature fluctuation and weed growth. The ideal design includes:

- Varying depths from shallow margins (0.3m) to deep zones (2-3m)
- Gentle slope on the sunny side for warmth, steep slope on the shady side
- Protection from prevailing wind
- Island or raft structures for waterfowl nesting and fish shelter
- Inlet and outlet for water exchange
- Aquatic plant zones: emergent reeds at the edge, submerged plants in shallows, floating plants in open water

## Species Considerations

### Local vs. Exotic Species

Most aquacultures have a predominantly local flavour and species composition. Few introduced species prove as adapted and productive as indigenous species. Only if there are no local species, or if all trials using local species fail, should exotic forms be considered. The great majority of aquaculture developments have been polycultural from inception.

### Traditional Systems

Many traditional aquaculture systems demonstrate sophisticated polyculture:

- **Hawaiian ohana**: Combined taro terraces, fish ponds, and wetland agriculture
- **Chinampa system**: Aztec chinampas combined crop, waterfowl, reedbed, and fish culture in what Mollison calls "possibly the most productive system of polyculture yet devised"
- **Rice-fish culture**: Fish raised in flooded rice paddies across Asia and Oceania
- **Tilapia and carp**: Traditional food fish for terrace rice and taro culture

## Starting Small

Mollison advises starting small and building on successes. For the homeowner, a set of tanks or a small pond is pleasurable and probably profitable. For those seeking profit, planning, design, monitoring, and research are essential. Extensive sites (20 hectares or larger) can accept lower total yields with less cost and risk. Aquaculture may serve as an accessory enterprise (medicinal products from algae, cabin fishing, silkworm culture) that turns a profit.
