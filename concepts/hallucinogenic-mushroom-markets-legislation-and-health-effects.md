# Hallucinogenic Mushroom Markets, Legislation, and Health Effects

The European market for [[emcdda-eu-legislative-timeline-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-2001-2006]] has driven significant legislative changes across EU member states. This concept covers the market structure, product forms, health effects, and the evolving legal landscape.

## Market and Availability

### User Terminology and Brand Names
Hallucinogenic mushrooms are known by many names across Europe:

| Region | Common Name(s) |
|--------|---------------|
| **UK** | Magic mushrooms, shrooms, mush, mushies, boomers |
| **Netherlands** | Paddos |
| **Germany** | Zauberpilze, Psychoaktive Pilze |
| **France** | Champignons hallucinogènes, champis |
| **Italy** | Funghi magici |
| **Eastern Europe** | Various local translations |

### Smartshop Product Labels
At the end of the 1990s, new labels appeared reflecting commercialization:
- **'Philosopher's stone'**: Sclerotia of [[psilocybe-mexicana]]
- **'Herbal highs'**: General label for natural psychoactive products
- **'Herbal xtc'**: Marketing alongside synthetic alternatives
- **Teonanacatl**: Aztec name meaning "divine flesh" or "flesh of god"

### Online Market (2006 Snapshot)
A Google/AltaVista search for mushroom retailers found:
- **39 online shops** identified selling hallucinogenic mushroom products
- **82% based in the Netherlands**
- Remaining 18%: Austrian growkit/spore shops, German sites, UK sites
- Keywords like "magic mushrooms shop" returned 837,000 Google hits

## Product Forms and Administration

### Available Forms
1. **Fresh mushrooms**: Whole fruiting bodies
2. **Dried mushrooms**: Preserved for later use
3. **Growkits**: Home cultivation supplies
4. **Spore prints**: For cultivation
5. **Sclerotia** ("philosopher's stone"): Underground masses of mycelium
6. **Extracts**: Concentrated preparations

### Administration Methods
- **Oral ingestion** (most common): Fresh or dried mushrooms eaten directly
- **Tea preparation**: Mushrooms steeped in hot water
- **Food preparation**: Incorporated into meals
- **Smoking** (reported but debated): Users report faster but milder effects
- **Intravenous injection** (rare, dangerous): More severe acute effects

### Dosing Considerations
- Psilocybin content varies significantly between species and individual mushrooms
- Onset: 10–60 minutes depending on route, physical condition, food intake
- Duration: 2–6 hours primary effects, 2–6 hours after-effects

## Acute Health Effects

### Positive Effects ("Good Trips")
- Relaxation (comparable to cannabis)
- Giddiness and uncontrollable laughter
- Energy, joy, euphoria
- Visual enhancement (brighter colours)
- Mental stimulation and new insights
- "Quickly changing emotions"

### Negative Effects ("Bad Trips")
- Visual disturbances (moving surfaces, waves)
- Delusions and altered perception
- Restlessness and incoordination
- Anxiety and panic reactions
- Impaired judgement of time and distance
- Sense of unreality or depersonalisation
- Psychosis-like states

### Important Context
"The effects are not viewed as positively as the entactogenic effects of ecstasy, and the negative effects may outweigh the positive effects in social situations."

### Barrier to Widespread Use
"Comparing the ease with which an ecstasy tablet can be swallowed in a party or dance setting, this cumbersome [[mushroom-taste-route-of-administration-and-consumption-barriers]], combined with unfamiliar and unpleasant taste, is likely to serve as a barrier to widespread or frequent use of mushrooms."

## Emergency and Help Line Data

### Drug Telephone Help Lines (FESAT)
- Based on data from 26 drug telephone help lines in 16 European countries
- "No significant increase in calls to drug help lines regarding the use [[health-risks-of-hallucinogenic-mushrooms]] during 2004"

### Belgium
- 1% of all inquiries to Druglijn, Flanders in 2003 and 2004 were mushroom-related

### Sweden
- Poisons Information Centre calls peaked in 1998 with 96 calls
- Last five years: relatively low and stable at 30–40 calls annually

## Legislative Developments Across Europe

### The "Plant vs. Fungus" Loophole
Many drug laws were written to control "plants" from which narcotic substances can be extracted. Since mushrooms are fungi, not plants, they fell outside some legislative frameworks.

### Key Legal Changes (2005-2006)

| Date | Country | Legal Change |
|------|---------|-------------|
| **Mar 2005** | Germany | Changed definition from 'plant' to 'organism' to include mushrooms |
| **May 2005** | Estonia | Clarified cultivation of hallucinogenic mushrooms as an offence |
| **July 2005** | UK | Added "Fungus (of any kind) which contains psilocin" as controlled substance — no longer limited to "treated or prepared" forms |
| **Feb 2006** | Ireland | Criminalised possession or sale of psilocin-containing fungi (exempt if growing uncultivated) |

### The UK Regulatory Evolution
The UK law previously only controlled mushrooms "if treated or prepared" (dried, mashed, or processed in food) — but not fresh mushrooms. In 2005, the law changed to apply to psilocin mushrooms of any kind, regardless of state.

### Emerging Concern: Amanita muscaria
"The recent prohibition of [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] containing fungi appears to have provoked an emerging interest of retailers in alternative, legal, types of hallucinogenic mushroom such as [[amanita-muscaria]] ([[fly-agaric]])."

**Warning**: "The active chemicals in these are known to carry substantial toxicity risks and some closely related Amanita species are highly toxic and could cause fatal poisoning."

## Wild Picking Risks
"Hallucinogenic mushrooms are easily mistakable for any number of non-psychoactive, inedible, or [[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]]." This natural confusion may limit the number of people willing to consume wild-picked specimens.

## Key Conclusions

1. **Self-limiting market**: Unpleasant taste and cumbersome administration limit widespread adoption
2. **Regulatory patchwork**: National laws vary significantly across the EU
3. **Online dominance**: The Netherlands hosts 82% of identified online retailers
4. **Low emergency burden**: Help line data shows no significant increase in mushroom-related calls
5. **Substitution risk**: Prohibition of [[psilocybin-mushrooms]] may push users toward more dangerous alternatives like Amanita muscaria
6. **Cultural drivers**: The 'return to nature' trend and internet expansion have been key market forces
