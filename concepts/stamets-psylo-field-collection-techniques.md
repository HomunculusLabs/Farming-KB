# Field Collection Techniques for [[psilocybin-mushroom-cultivation-basics]]s

Edible mushroom hunters must totally relearn the art of hunting to find the
psilocybin varieties. Since psilocybin mushrooms are small in comparison to
the common edible varieties, the technique for finding them is more subtle.
The species you are seeking will determine your technique.

## Hunting Techniques

Many hunters prefer the random, fast-walk style of leaning forward at a
precarious tilt, which insures forward momentum and a fast gait. Others are
more systematic, slowly tracking along a mentally projected, predefined grid.
Seasoned hunters employ memory-mapping techniques: taking the image of the
desired mushroom into memory mentally, and then scanning the background
environment. When discovered, each mushroom virtually leaps from
invisibility into the foreground.

Once the first mushroom is spotted, freeze and peripherally scan 360
degrees around the find. Although painstakingly slow, this method is highly
rewarding. Stamets reports picking several hundred specimens within just a
few hours in this way.

### Targeting Specific Habitats

- **Liberty caps (P. semilanceata)**: Concentrated in and around clumps
  of tall sedge grass in the low-lying wetlands of pastures in Europe and
  the Pacific Northwest.

- **Interface environments**: Borders where trees separate pastures are
  often abundant with both grassland and woodland species. Trees provide
  shade and protection from the wind, and tend to be places where cattle
  stand to stay cool.

- **Wood chip trails**: P. cyanescens and other lignicolous species can
  be targeted along mulched garden paths and landscaping features.

### Cautions While Hunting

You can become so transfixed in your search that disorientation occurs.
More than once, Stamets has felt waves of panic upon realizing he had
become totally disoriented. A compass, GPS, and whistle are essential gear.

"Retina burn" is a neural phenomenon in which images of mushrooms, often
species specific, appear when the eyes are closed, sometimes persisting
for hours. When a parade of mushrooms dances across your visual cortex,
you are well on your way to becoming a true mycophile.

## Picking Technique

Good field technique means collecting the mushrooms without damaging them.
The loss of identifying features, due to overhandling, complicates
accurate identification. When picking a mushroom, be careful to get the
entire fruiting body, especially the base of the stem, where the bluing
reaction is often first seen. If the base of the stem is not carefully
preserved, sight identification of the mushroom is greatly jeopardized.

Use your finger or a knife to carefully dig beneath and around the entire
mushroom, then gently lift it upwards so it can be picked fully intact.
Clean the debris off each mushroom before it is placed into your basket.

## Collection Containers

Wrap each collection in wax paper or place in separate paper bags. Wax
paper preserves the specimens until you have the opportunity to work on
them. It allows the mushrooms to breathe, whereas plastic tends to trap
moisture and causes the mushrooms to quickly decompose. Wax paper can also
help protect fragile specimens from being crushed.

The traditional wicker basket is preferred by most collectors. A
collapsible, epoxy-covered basket is ideal for mountain bikers or hikers
who need to strap it on their backs.

## Documentation

Each collection should be labeled, and any notable features that might aid
in identification recorded: bruising reaction, habitat, aspect, or nearby
vegetation. When you find an unusual collection, thoroughly describe,
photograph, and spore print it.

The importance of spore printing all questionable and unfamiliar species
cannot be overemphasized. Not only is the color of the spore print a
primary key to identification, but for those skilled in mushroom
cultivation, a clean spore print can be used for starting a culture.

## Spore Printing

To make a spore print:
1. Cut the cap from the stem with a clean knife
2. Place the cap gills-down on a piece of white paper (half white, half
   black paper is ideal for showing light or dark spores)
3. Cover with a glass or bowl to prevent air currents
4. Wait 2-24 hours for spores to deposit
5. The resulting spore print color is a critical identification feature

Dark purplish brown to purplish black spore prints indicate Psilocybe or
Panaeolus species. Rusty brown prints warn of potentially deadly Galerina
or other dangerous species.

## Preservation Methods

### Drying

Once fresh mushrooms have been picked, they should be used within the day
or dried within forty-eight hours. Dried mushrooms in their preserved form
make for a lot of chewing and consumption of large amounts of water. Some
people powder and encapsulate their mushrooms for more accurate dosing.

### Freezing

Freezing is not recommended as it can damage cellular structure and
complicate identification of dried specimens.

### Ethanol Preservation

For long-term storage, ethanol at 70% concentration or higher can be used
to prevent fermentation. The brew should be refrigerated until use, at
which point it should be warmed to evaporate off substantial amounts of
alcohol before consumption.

### Cruz's Brew (Mushroom Tea)

A recommended preparation method using two vessels:
1. In one, make spiced herbal tea (orange peels, whole cloves, nutmeg,
   cinnamon) at one and a half cups per dose. Bring to a boil, reduce to
   simmer.
2. Add chopped or broken mushrooms into the other empty vessel.
3. Pour hot tea through a strainer into the mushroom vessel.
4. Stir occasionally, allow to steep for one hour.
5. Serve one cup per person per dose at 1 gram dried or 10 grams fresh
   mushrooms per cup.

The tea is only good for one day, after which ethanol must be added to
reach at least 70% concentration for preservation.
