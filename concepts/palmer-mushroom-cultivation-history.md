# [[mushroom-cultivation-history]] -- Early Methods

Palmer's *About Mushrooms* (1894) provides one of the most detailed American accounts of late-19th-century [[mushroom-cultivation-methods]]. His observations are particularly valuable because he combined theoretical knowledge from European sources with his own extensive practical experiments.

## The Unique Cultivability of the Common Mushroom

A central puzzle of mycology, noted by Palmer, was why only one fungus species could be reliably cultivated:

- The horse-mushroom (*[[agaricus-arvensis]]*) and meadow mushroom (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*) were "the only species of fungus which is ever propagated artificially"
- "So far as known, it is the only species susceptible of cultivation"
- This single fact explained why it alone was called "a mushroom" while hundreds of other edible species were dismissed as "toadstools"

## Why the Horse-Mushroom?

Palmer offered a plausible explanation for its unique cultivability:

- It is always found where cattle graze and "follows the horse through every climate"
- Spores are likely swallowed in large numbers during grazing
- "Whatever change is necessary to continue the reproductive work of these bodies takes place either in the intestinal canal of the horse or in his manure"
- The horse-mushroom "can be grown in and by his manure, and in no other way"

## The Reproductive Mystery

Palmer devoted considerable attention to the poorly understood reproductive biology of fungi:

### Spores vs. Spawn
- Spores are the visible reproductive bodies (analogous to seeds), produced in vast quantities
- "You may plant them [spores] in any number with no assurance of producing a single mushroom"
- "The most powerful microscope fails to show any germ of the future plant in these reproductive bodies"
- Spawn (mycelium) is the "subterranean part of the fungus, corresponding to the vine"
- "The spawn is apparently a collection of oblong cells united at the ends"

### The Open Questions
- "How does the white vine beneath the soil begin its growth?"
- "How are certain points in it fructified and developed into visible mushrooms?"
- "Why, when the many varieties of fungi grow side by side, and their beds of spawn interlace and twine about each other beneath the soil, may we pluck wholesome food from one matrix and mortal poison from another?"

Palmer had thrown "the refuse of hundreds of fungi" on a grass-plot, depositing "millions of spores," yet "not a toadstool of any kind has ever grown there."

## Spawn: Structure and Behavior

### What Spawn Looks Like
- A "white substance" running through soil "like the veins on the back of the hand"
- Described as "white cellular vine" or "mass of white threads which permeate like a series of fine roots every part of the disintegrated matter"
- "The smell was very strong, quickly noticed on the opposite side of the way"

### How Spawn Was Discovered in Construction
Palmer witnessed spawn during earth-moving work opposite the Brunswick Hotel in Boston: "As the pick of the workman broke up the soil, a white substance ran through every piece. Starting with large branches, it divided and sub-divided." This was the spawn of *[[coprinus-comatus-detailed]]*.

### Permanence and Reliability
- Just as grape-vine cuttings produce predictable fruit, "a part of this coprinus vine transplanted will continue to ramify, and in time show the result in the form of mushrooms"
- "Just where grew this year a peculiar kind of toadstool, there, next year, and so on for successive harvests, will you find the same plant"
- Palmer found the identical variety on the same spots in both 1874 and 1879

## Conventional Cultivation Method

### Bed Preparation
- Manure should be about two-thirds from the horse and one-third from other grazing animals or rich loam
- Must be "broken and turned until a thermometer sunk therein shows a temperature of 60 to 80 degrees Fahrenheit"
- Blocks of spawn are inserted into the prepared bed

### Timeline
- In three weeks, the whole mass is "full of the white vine"
- About ten days more, mushrooms appear
- Total: approximately five weeks from spawning to first crop

### Why Cultivation Often Failed
Palmer identified three principal reasons for failure:

1. **Poor spawn** -- "much that is sold as such containing not a trace of the fungus." Better to use spawn found "at the edge of an old mushroom bed" than to buy commercially.

2. **Whole bed spawned at once** -- The bed should be "prepared and planted in sections, like a Pullman car, so that when one end is in bearing, the other will be just preparing."

3. **Neglecting atmospheric conditions** -- "The fungi are very susceptible to atmospheric change, and cannot grow without attention to this condition."

## Palmer's Innovations

### The Moisture Problem
The most difficult problem in cultivation was supplying proper moisture:

- "Watering the ground is worse than useless; the moisture must come from beneath, like the dew"
- Mushroom beds should have "nothing between them and mother earth"
- "An atmosphere must be produced similar to dog-days"

### Steam System
Palmer's proposed solution:

- In hot-houses, use the space under benches for mushroom beds
- Make vents in steam-pipes; when opened, "steam will collect over the earth and condense on the soil, keeping it moist and warm"
- "With a greenhouse so constructed, mushrooms can be grown with all the certainty of other crops"

### The Animal-Care Analogy
Palmer concluded that mushrooms required an unusual degree of attention: "Many years ago they were supposed to be animal rather than vegetable in nature. It is certain that they require the care we give to animals, in order to insure proper conditions for reproduction."

## Economic Factors

- Cultivated mushrooms sold for "a dollar to two dollars a pound" due to scarcity and difficulty
- Even successful beds often produced only one picking before becoming "barren as a roadway"
- "The gardener fails now because he does not know his subject. Presuming that the part he desires is the whole, he, in his own words, 'plants them,' as he would peas or nasturtiums, and is disgusted because they do not grow"

## Natural Propagation via Street Sweepings

Palmer noted a simpler method of accidental cultivation:

- "It is certain that if street sweepings are gathered from time to time and spread loosely over the surface of soil until an accumulation and gradual disintegration of such matter takes place, a fine crop of edible mushrooms will be the result"
- This method required no knowledge of spawn handling and relied on natural spore germination

## The Truffle Question

Palmer speculated on introducing truffle cultivation to America:

- The truffle differs from all other mushrooms: both spawn and "blossom" remain below the soil surface
- He proposed importing from France "the swine and dogs already trained to detect the presence beneath the soil of this delicacy"
- These animals would be taken into American oak plantations to determine whether truffles were indigenous

## Sicard's Research on Reproduction

The French mycologist Sicard had made "evidence of the most patient research on the subject of the reproduction of fungi, a problem difficult of solution even by those best acquainted with this branch of the kingdom of nature." Palmer cited Sicard's work as the most advanced of its time, though many questions remained unanswered.
