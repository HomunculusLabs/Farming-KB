## The Unique Cultivability of the Common Mushroom

A central puzzle of mycology, noted by Palmer, was why only one fungus species could be reliably cultivated:

- The horse-mushroom (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*) were "the only species of fungus which is ever propagated artificially"
- "So far as known, it is the only species susceptible of cultivation"
- This single fact explained why it alone was called "a mushroom" while hundreds of other edible species were dismissed as "toadstools"

## Why the Horse-Mushroom?

Palmer offered a plausible explanation for its unique cultivability:

- It is always found where cattle graze and "follows the horse through every climate"
- Spores are likely swallowed in large numbers during grazing
- "Whatever change is necessary to continue the reproductive work of these bodies takes place either in the intestinal canal of the horse or in his manure"
- The horse-mushroom "can be grown in and by his manure, and in no other way"

## The Reproductive Mystery

Palmer devoted considerable attention to the poorly understood reproductive biology of fungi:

### Spores vs. Spawn
- Spores are the visible reproductive bodies (analogous to seeds), produced in vast quantities
- "You may plant them [spores] in any number with no assurance of producing a single mushroom"
- "The most powerful microscope fails to show any germ of the future plant in these reproductive bodies"
- Spawn (mycelium) is the "subterranean part of the fungus, corresponding to the vine"
- "The spawn is apparently a collection of oblong cells united at the ends"

### The Open Questions
- "How does the white vine beneath the soil begin its growth?"
- "How are certain points in it fructified and developed into visible mushrooms?"
- "Why, when the many varieties of fungi grow side by side, and their beds of spawn interlace and twine about each other beneath the soil, may we pluck wholesome food from one matrix and mortal poison from another?"

Palmer had thrown "the refuse of hundreds of fungi" on a grass-plot, depositing "millions of spores," yet "not a toadstool of any kind has ever grown there."

## Spawn: Structure and Behavior

### What Spawn Looks Like
- A "white substance" running through soil "like the veins on the back of the hand"
- Described as "white cellular vine" or "mass of white threads which permeate like a series of fine roots every part of the disintegrated matter"
- "The smell was very strong, quickly noticed on the opposite side of the way"
