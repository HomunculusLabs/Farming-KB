# Plant Proprioception and Gravitropism

Plants possess a sense of proprioception — awareness of their own body position
and orientation in space — that is essential for maintaining upright growth and
responding to mechanical displacement. This spatial awareness, explored in
Chamovitz's "What a Plant Knows" in the chapter "How a Plant Knows Where It Is,"
relies primarily on gravity sensing through specialized cells called statocytes
and the hormone auxin-mediated differential growth response.

## The Problem of Knowing Where You Are

Unlike animals, which can look around and use vestibular organs to sense
orientation, plants must determine their spatial position through internal
sensing mechanisms. A plant that is knocked over by wind, an animal, or
landslide must detect the displacement and redirect growth upward toward
light and away from gravity. This requires:

1. **A sensor** to detect the direction of gravity
2. **A [[fungal-environmental-sensing-signal-transduction]] pathway** to convert the sensory input into a
   biochemical signal
3. **A growth response** to differentially elongate tissues and restore
   proper orientation

## Statocytes: The Gravity Sensors

### Root Cap Statocytes

Gravity sensing in plants occurs primarily in specialized cells called
statocytes, located in:
- **Root cap columella** — for roots (positive gravitropism: grow downward)
- **Endodermal amyloplasts** — for shoots (negative gravitropism: grow upward)

These cells contain **amyloplasts** — starch-filled organelles also called
statoliths — that are denser than the surrounding cytoplasm. Under the
influence of gravity, amyloplasts sediment to the bottom of the cell,
providing a physical indicator of the gravity vector.

### The Sedimentation Model

The prevailing model for gravity sensing holds that amyloplast sedimentation
triggers the gravitropic response through several possible mechanisms:

1. **Tension on actin cytoskeleton** — amyloplasts connected to actin filaments
   may create tension that activates mechanosensitive ion channels
2. **ER deformation** — sedimenting amyloplasts may deform ER membranes,
   triggering calcium release
3. **Protein redistribution** — sedimentation may redistribute signaling
   proteins on amyloplast surfaces, creating asymmetric signaling

Evidence from experiments with starchless mutants (which lack functional
amyloplasts) shows severely impaired gravitropism, confirming the central
role of statolith sedimentation. However, these mutants are not completely
gravitropically blind, suggesting that additional sensing mechanisms exist.

## Auxin Redistribution: The Growth Signal

Once gravity is sensed, the information is converted into a growth response
through asymmetric auxin redistribution:

### In Roots (Positive Gravotropism)

1. Gravity is detected in the columella cells of the root cap
2. Auxin transporters (PIN proteins) are repositioned asymmetrically
3. Auxin accumulates on the lower side of the root
4. High auxin concentration inhibits [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]] on the lower side
5. Cells on the upper side elongate normally
6. The differential elongation bends the root downward

### In Shoots (Negative Gravitropism)

1. Gravity is detected in the endodermal cells of the shoot
2. PIN proteins redistribute auxin to the lower side of the shoot
3. High auxin concentration promotes cell elongation on the lower side
4. Cells on the lower side elongate more than those on the upper side
5. The differential elongation bends the shoot upward

The key difference is that auxin inhibits elongation in roots but promotes
it in shoots, producing opposite directional growth responses from the same
hormonal redistribution.

## PIN Proteins and Polar Auxin Transport

The PIN (PIN-FORMED) family of auxin efflux carriers is central to auxin
redistribution. PIN proteins are asymmetrically localized on the plasma
membrane, creating directional auxin flow. During the gravitropic response:

- **PIN3 relocalization** — within minutes of gravistimulation, PIN3
  proteins in columella cells relocalize from the basal to the lateral
  membrane, redirecting auxin flow laterally

- **PIN2 cycling** — in root epidermal cells, PIN2 cycling between the
  plasma membrane and endosomes is modulated to maintain the auxin gradient

- **PID kinase** — the protein kinase PINOID phosphorylates PIN proteins,
  regulating their polarity and thus the direction of auxin transport

## Thigmomorphogenesis: Mechanical Sensing

Plants also sense mechanical forces unrelated to gravity. Wind-grown plants
develop shorter, thicker stems with smaller leaves — "wind training" that
produces sturdier growth. The most dramatic [[plant-mechanosensation-thigmomorphogenesis-touch-response]] is Mimosa pudica's
leaf folding via rapid turgor changes in pulvinus cells. Less obvious responses
occur in all plants: tendril coiling, root barrier avoidance, and altered
growth from physical contact.

### Proprioceptive Growth Correction

When a plant is displaced, it does not simply grow upward from its new
position — it actively corrects its posture through differential wood
formation (reaction wood). In angiosperms, tension wood forms on the upper
side of a leaning stem, pulling it upright. In gymnosperms, compression
wood forms on the lower side, pushing it upright. This proprioceptive
correction continues until the plant has restored its vertical orientation.

## Time Scale of Gravitropic Responses

The gravitropic response operates across multiple time scales:

| Phase | Time | Mechanism |
|-------|------|-----------|
| Signal perception | 1–5 min | Amyloplast sedimentation |
| Signal transduction | 5–30 min | PIN relocalization, auxin redistribution |
| Growth response | 30 min–hours | Differential cell elongation |
| Posture correction | Hours–weeks | Reaction wood formation |

## Interaction with Phototropism

Gravitropism and phototropism interact to optimize plant orientation. Young
seedlings first emerge from soil and grow straight up (gravitropism dominant).
Once above ground, phototropism becomes increasingly important for orienting
toward light. The balance between these two systems is regulated by the
relative strength of the light and gravity signals, with auxin serving as the
common output for both pathways.
