# Landscape Patterns and Catchment Design

While site design has been central to [[permaculture]] practice, it has inherent limitations. The question of whether a site is suited to its intended use is often ignored. Whole farm planning, eco-village development, and bioregional design require a landscape-based approach that builds on the underlying patterns of the land rather than the details of particular sites.

## Limitations of Site-Based Design

Site design struggles with:

- **Suitability assessment**: Whether the site is appropriate for its intended use
- **Landscape context**: The nature and implications of the wider landscape, including multi-nodal development where links between sites matter more than individual site details
- **Connectivity**: How water, nutrients, wildlife, and people move across the broader landscape
- **Scale mismatch**: Some important patterns only become visible at landscape scale

## Catchment Concepts

The concept of catchment landscapes as self-organising systems for catching and storing water, nutrients, and carbon provides a powerful framework for landscape-scale design. Catchments cover the whole landscape and generally include several different ecosystem types. Their general patterns reflect the flow and form of energy, especially rainfall and water movement.

### Key Catchment Principles

1. **River catchments cover the whole landscape** and generally include several different ecosystem types
2. **Patterns reflect energy flow**, especially in rainfall and water movement
3. **Rivers are the lifeblood of landscapes** — their waters and associated fertile alluvial flats have been the focus for human development throughout history
4. **Upstream effects cascade downstream** — what happens at the head of a catchment affects the whole river system
5. **River health reflects catchment health** — the state of the whole catchment is mirrored in its rivers
6. **Headwaters are pure but infertile; estuaries are fertile but accumulate toxins** — a fundamental gradient that shapes both ecology and human use

### The Catchment Land Use Pattern

The Landcare movement and the earlier Soil Conservation movement of the 1930s–1940s promoted a general catchment pattern of land use:

- **Headwater regions** are forested to protect steeper areas from erosion while protecting valuable agricultural lowlands from flooding, siltation, and salinity
- **Mid-slopes** support mixed use — agroforestry, grazing, and selective cropping
- **Valley floors and alluvial plains** support intensive agriculture and settlement

Once we recognise that forests must be the predominant land use in catchment uplands, "the only issue is what sort of forest." The Permaculture concept began with the question of how to create forests that provide for human needs while performing ecological services.

## Soil-Topography-Vegetation Patterns

Understanding the relationship between soil, topography, and vegetation is essential for landscape design. In many parts of Australia, these relationships follow predictable patterns:

### The Erosion-Enrichment Cycle

Excavation in many Australian soils shows a mosaic of buried organic matter, clays, silts, gravels, and stones reflecting long-term cycles of cut-and-fill erosion over thousands of years. These create localised degradation and enrichment that determine vegetation patterns.

On the north shore of Sydney Harbour, for example, moderately fertile Ashfield shale loam soils supported tall wet eucalypt forest, while infertile Hawkesbury sandstone soils supported stunted forest or low heathland — despite similar climate and rainfall.

### The Fertility-Vegetation Gradient

In temperate mid-rainfall regions, a clear gradient links soil fertility to vegetation type and characteristic herbivores:

- **High fertility → Grassland → Large grazing mammals**: Deep, rich topsoils support dense herbaceous groundcover and deep water absorption
- **Moderate fertility → Grassy woodland → Mixed herbivores**: Woodland trees with productive understorey
- **Lower fertility → Tall forest → Small tree herbivores**: Nutrient cycling through canopy and leaf litter
- **Low fertility → Heathy forest/Heathland → Specialised species**: Sparse, slow-growing vegetation adapted to nutrient limitation

## Caution Against Biomass Obsession

These observations temper the tendency of some permaculturalists to become "biomass junkies" who regard total plant biomass as the sole measure of good land management. The Permaculture strategy of tree crops and food forests is not about creating massive biomass forests. Instead, it focuses on trees with maximum potential to feed people and livestock — typically:

- Smaller trees, often deciduous
- Soft leaves that decompose readily, creating non-acidic compost soils
- Large fruits, seeds, nuts, or pods with high carbohydrate, protein, or oil content
- Often fire retardant

Including tall forest species (especially eucalypts and conifers) that dominate food-producing systems without contributing edible yield is a common mistake. In high-rainfall areas with leached soils, fast-growing biomass quickly becomes frustrating when nothing can eat it and it takes sunlight, water, and nutrients from productive plants.

However, fast-growing, maximum-biomass species are very useful for shelter, fuel, timber, and other non-food yields. The key is placing them appropriately within the overall design.

## Grazing Animal Patterns

The idea that energetically optimal systems follow broad patterns extends from plants to animals:

- **Cows** produce more milk on good-quality grass and clover pasture (high-quality energy)
- **Goats** are relatively more productive on poorer forage, including woody shrubs (low-quality energy)
- **Cows** may be best for maintaining healthy pasture in high-rainfall cool climates
- **Goats** are best for creating such pastures in the first place
- **Kangaroos** are far more efficient than cattle and sheep at converting forage on semi-arid rangeland into meat

When energy is rising, higher-yielding solutions are natural; when energy availability is falling, efficiency of energy conversion becomes more important.

## Landscape-Scale Design Tools

Effective landscape design requires tools beyond site-level analysis:

- **Topographic maps and digital elevation models** for understanding landform and water flow
- **Aerial and satellite imagery** for vegetation patterns and land use
- **Geological maps** for understanding soil parent materials
- **Historical records** for understanding previous land use and changes over time
- **Bioregional frameworks** for organising design at the appropriate scale

The transition from site-based to landscape-based thinking is essential for addressing challenges like [[water-management]], biodiversity conservation, and sustainable food production at the scale they actually occur.
