## Swarm Capture Guide

Swarming is the natural means of honey bee colony reproduction. A swarm
represents a free colony for the beekeeper who can capture and rehive it.
This guide covers swarm biology, [[game-species-management-capture-techniques]], hive baiting, and
successful rehiving.

## Swarm Biology

### Why Colonies Swarm
1. **Congestion** — the brood nest is full of bees and there is no space
   for the queen to lay
2. **Queen age** — older queens produce less pheromone, reducing colony
   cohesion
3. **Genetics** — some genetic lines are more prone to swarming
4. **Environmental cues** — strong nectar flow with limited storage space

### The Swarming Process
1. Worker bees begin building queen cells (swarm cells, usually on frame
   bottoms)
2. The old queen lays eggs in queen cells and loses body weight for flight
3. Scout bees locate a new nesting site and communicate its location
4. On a warm morning (typically 10AM-2PM), 50-60% of the colony exits with
   the old queen
5. The swarm clusters on a nearby object (tree branch, fence post, building)
   within 50-200 feet of the parent hive
6. Scouts continue searching; once consensus is reached, the swarm flies to
   the new site

### The Cluster Window
A swarm typically remains clustered for a few hours to 2-3 days. Scout bees
are actively searching during this time. The longer a swarm hangs, the more
likely it is to abscond to a permanent location. Capture promptly.

## Swarm Capture Techniques

### Equipment Needed
- Beekeeping suit, veil, gloves
- Bee brush or feather
- Nuc box, deep box, or swarm trap (5-frame nuc is ideal)
- Securing equipment (ladder, rope, pruning shears, sheet)
- Lemongrass oil for bait

### Capturing from a Low Branch (Under 10 Feet)
1. Place the nuc or box on the ground below the swarm with the open top
   facing up
2. Sharply shake the branch so the cluster drops into the box
3. Gently brush remaining bees into the box
4. Leave the box open on the ground near the capture site; foraging bees
   will find their way in
5. Return at dusk, close the box, and transport to the apiary

### Capturing from a High Branch
1. If accessible by ladder, use a bucket on a pole or a swarm-catching bag
2. Alternatively, prune the branch and lower it carefully to a ground sheet
3. If too high, set up a baited swarm trap (see below) nearby and hope
   scouts find it first

### Capturing from a Structure (Wall, Fence, Chimney)
1. Brush or shake bees into a box if possible
2. For wall or cavity swarms, a trap-out may be necessary (cone funnel
   over the entrance, one-way escape)
3. Chimney swarms may require professional removal
4. Cut-out operations (opening a wall to access comb) are advanced — seek
   experienced help

### Vacuum Collection
Commercial bee vacuums or shop-vac adapters allow gentle collection from
difficult locations. Use low suction and collect into a containment box
with frames already installed.

## Hive Baiting and Swarm Traps

### Bait Hives
Bait hives attract scout bees searching for a new home. A well-placed bait
hive can catch swarms without any effort during swarm season.

### Ideal Bait Hive Specifications
- Volume: 40 liters (approximately one deep Langstroth box or a 5-frame nuc)
- Entrance: 1.5 square inches, facing south or southeast
- Height: 10-15 feet off the ground (simulates a tree cavity)
- Shade: partial shade is preferred
- Scent: 5-10 drops of lemongrass [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]] on a cotton ball inside
- Comb: one frame of old dark drawn comb is highly attractive

### Placement Strategy
- Place traps 50-200 yards from known colonies
- Install by early April [[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]]
- Check traps weekly during swarm season (May-June)
- Use Swarm Commander or lemongrass oil as attractant
- Place near tree lines or hedgerows along flight paths

## Rehiving a Swarm

### Initial Setup
1. Prepare a hive with at least 5 frames of foundation or drawn comb
2. Place the hive in its permanent location (or a temporary holding spot)
3. Shake or pour the captured swarm into the hive over the frames
4. Add a frame feeder with 1:1 sugar syrup to give them immediate resources

### Queen Verification
- After 3-4 days, inspect for eggs — confirms the queen is present and laying
- If no eggs after one week, the queen was likely lost during capture;
  introduce a new queen or combine with another colony
- [[queen-rearing-basics|Queen rearing]] skills are valuable for
  replacing lost queens

### After-Care
- Feed 1:1 syrup for the first 2-3 weeks to stimulate comb building
- Reduce entrance for the first week to prevent robbing
- Monitor Varroa — swarms carry mites from the parent colony; treat proactively
- Check for disease during the first inspection

## Swarm Prevention in Your Own Colonies

While catching swarms is rewarding, preventing your own colonies from
swarming preserves [[faires-beekeeping-and-honey-production]]. See [[beekeeping-problems-and-solutions]]
for prevention strategies, and [[beekeeping-seasonal-calendar]] for timing
of management actions.
