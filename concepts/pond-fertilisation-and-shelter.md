# Pond Fertilisation and Shelter

Fertilisation and the provision of shelter are two of the most powerful levers available to the pond manager for increasing yields. Modest fertiliser inputs can multiply production by factors of 2 to 10, while well-designed refuges reduce predation losses of young fish and crustaceans by 10–100%. Together, these strategies transform basic pond systems into highly productive polycultures.

## Fertiliser Sources

### Animal Manures

Pig, duck, and second-stage sewage are all used effectively in fish ponds, and any bird or animal manures can contribute nutrients. Devices to bring manure to ponds include:
- Siting pigeon lofts over water
- Placing pig pens or chicken roosts above ponds
- Erecting perches for gulls, ducks, and land birds
- Building martin and swift nest sites over or in water
- Installing bat roosts over water (as practised in Holland)

These strategies return land-derived nutrients to ponds, while pond water irrigates and fertilises land crops—each part of the cycle produces a yield.

### Fertiliser Types and Applications

After testing waters, minor elements and phosphates are typically the nutrients most needed. Important cautions:
- Avoid adding nitrates to waters where natural manures are used
- Be cautious with boron-containing detergents
- Never use biocides not recommended for fish
- Salt, copper sulphate, weak formalin, and some antibiotics can control diseases or parasites without lasting harm

Phosphatic rock and granite dust supply lime once selected nutrient levels have been achieved. Phosphates, potash, and minor elements are fixed and held in water within 12–14 hours (or 3–4 days in oceans), and can be added to quiet marine bays as well as ponds.

### Matching Fertiliser to Species

Ponds are fertilised differently depending on the intended fish species:

- **Heavily fertilised ponds** — for intensive algal growth feeding milkfish or prawns
- **Moderately fertilised ponds** — where higher vegetation feeds grass carp or [[tilapia]]
- **Nearly clear-water ponds** — for bass, perch, and salmonids (trout)

In any polyculture, a larger proportion of fish should be algal or plant feeders. A usual ratio is **5:1 herbivore to predator**.

### The Fertiliser Factor

Mussels are the major phosphate reservoir of ponds and rivers, conveying phosphates to the mud via their anal siphons. Swingle (1966) recorded mussel yields in the same pond increasing from 52.8 kg (unfertilised) to 1,012 kg (fertilised) in whole weight (meats are 35% of total). This 19-fold increase demonstrates that well-managed fertilisation is a critical yield strategy.

Even modestly fertilised ponds can alter yields by factors of 2 to 10 times. As fertiliser uptake is so rapid, fertile ponds must be stocked with shrimp or large numbers of small forage fish while fingerling pan fish are growing—otherwise, the nutrient flush is wasted.

### Nutrient Management in Flow Systems

- **Excess nitrate** can be filtered via forage food ponds of crustaceans or through reed beds
- **Land crops downstream** remove excess nutrient from pond overflow
- **Flow-on systems** to land can usually achieve a doubling in yield for species such as melons, tomatoes, or [[dryland-settlement-vegetation-and-fuel-forests]]
- **Holding back flow** for a few days allows fertiliser uptake before resuming flow for aeration

## Mud, Silt, and Waste Removal

Wastes of any nature in ponds reduce water quality if they occur in excess, or as dead material. A useful distinction can be made between rotting wastes and fertilisers: fertilisers need not create excessive growth if herbivorous fish, nutria (a large aquatic rodent), or shrimp are available to eat the detritus or weeds that would otherwise die and create [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]].

### Still Pond Management

Still ponds need regular monitoring for waste removal. The most effective approach is to **drain and lime the pond**, then take a season of dryland crop (clover, fenugreek) before reflooding. In padi and terrace systems, 2–5 cm of mulch is retained over the clay base for cropping; all terraces can be drained to a dryland phase.

Where ponds cannot be drained, at least one bank should be kept clear of trees for dredging or bucket removal of muck. In Asia, pond muck is traditionally layered with green crop for [[compost]].

### Non-Draining Reservoirs

In deeper reservoirs that cannot be drained, a **jet pump** serves as a vacuum cleaner to pump detritus out (capable of removing soils up to gravel size). The material is deposited near the pond as adjacent retaining walls, low islands, or causeways where silt settles out and water flows back to the pond free of detritus. These pumps are typically raft-mounted and liquid-fuel powered.

## Shelter and Refuges

### Types of Refuge Structures

Stacks of pots, pipes, tyres, and bundles of rope, reeds, or brush can have a decisive yield effect on predation losses. Effective refuges include:
- **Artificial structures** — pots, pipes, tyre stacks, rope bundles, reed bundles, brush piles
- **Natural features** — shallows, weedy areas, submerged logs, rock piles
- **[[aquaculture-structures-and-cage-culture]]** — enables strategic separation of predator and prey, or adults and juveniles

### Yield Impact

Figures are hard to come by, but recorded yield increases include:
- **10–30%** for crustaceans with adequate refuges
- **20–100%** for forage fish protected by shallows or weedy areas

Mortality of young and fry is always greater in ponds of simple design without escapements. Crab and crayfish culture in particular benefits from shelter structures. Forage species in ponds need breeding refuges to persist through predation pressure.

### Deep Kettles

Deep "kettles" in shallow ponds serve multiple functions:
- Preventing fish deaths from freezes or excessive heat
- Protecting fish from accidental pond water loss
- Assisting capture during draw-down conditions
- Providing thermal refuges where fish can kettle (crowd together) during stressful periods

## Temperature Gradients and Thermoclines

Within water, surfaces and gradients develop from the effects of heat, solutes, particles, or stream flow. These are utilised differently by different species. A river or lagoon opening by a shallow bar to the sea may have saltwater fish at depth and freshwater species at the surface, with either colder forms at depth or (in icy winters) warm refuges at depth.

The interface between cold and hot water is termed a **[[thermocline]]**, and between salt and freshwater a **halocline**. Such boundaries can be abrupt transitions if no turbulent flow exists. Where no pronounced surface separation occurs, gradients still develop, especially where wind overturn or current flow is a factor. Fish, molluscs, and plants occupy specific niches in these gradients and may move with tide or current to adjust to their needs.

### The Temperature Threshold

At approximately 21°C (70°F), a categorical difference exists between:
- **Coldwater fish** — to which such temperatures are lethal or debilitating
- **Tropical fish** — which suffer cold stress or will not breed below this point

A few fish such as *[[gambusia]]* (the mosquito-eating fish) are temperate tolerant or eurythermal (1–36°C). Optimum feeding and spawning conditions are around 15–18°C for coldwater fish and 20–25°C for tropicals. Rainbow trout will die if kept for long periods above 14°C, while Tilapia die below 12°C.
## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

## Research Directions
Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.
Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.
