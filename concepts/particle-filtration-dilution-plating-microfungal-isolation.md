# Particle Filtration and Dilution Plating for Microfungal Isolation

Particle filtration and dilution plating are fundamental techniques for isolating microfungi from complex substrata such as soil, [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]], and plant tissue. These methods separate fungal propagules from bulk material and place them on [[agar-culture-media]] where they can grow and be identified.

## Particle Filtration

### Principle
Substratum is macerated, passed through mesh screens of decreasing size, and the fine particles (containing individual or few fungal propagules) are plated on agar. Each colony arises from a single particle, enabling isolation of rare species that would be overgrown in direct plating.

### Protocol (Bills and Polishook 1994)
1. **Surface wash:** Clean plant material to remove epiphytes
2. **Surface disinfect:** Brief immersion in ethanol and/or bleach
3. **Macerate:** Use laboratory blender (e.g., Stomacher Blender) to break material into small fragments
4. **Prescreen:** Filter through wire mesh to remove large debris
5. **Particle filtration:** Force fine particles between polypropylene mesh filters (105-210 um) with sterile distilled water
6. **Resuspend:** Collect trapped particles in water or 0.2% agar (slows sedimentation)
7. **Plate:** Spread on standard isolation media
8. **Monitor daily:** Isolate newly appearing colonies before fast-growing species overgrow

### Advantages Over Simple Dilution Plating
- **Dramatically improves recovery of rare species**
- Each particle yields one colony or none — reduces competition
- Trapped particles contain small tissue fragments that support initial [[brassinosteroid-fungal-growth-promotion]]
- More efficient use of sample material

### Limitations
- Labor-intensive; requires daily plate monitoring
- Some fungi damaged by maceration
- Cannot separate [[fungal-hyphae]] from host tissue during processing
- Selective media still needed to suppress fast-growers

## Dilution Plating

### Principle
Soil or macerated substratum is suspended in water and serially diluted. Known volumes of each dilution are spread on [[query-how-to-make-agar-plates-for-mushroom-cultivation]]. Colonies grow from individual propagules (spores, hyphal fragments).

### Standard Protocol
1. **Weigh sample** (typically 1-10 g dry weight equivalent)
2. **Suspend** in sterile water with 0.1% agar (prevents rapid settling)
3. **Serial dilution:** 10-fold dilutions (10^-1, 10^-2, 10^-3, etc.)
4. **Spread plate:** 0.1-1.0 mL of each dilution on agar surface
5. **Incubate:** 18-25C for 3-14 days (depending on target group)
6. **Count and isolate:** Enumerate colonies; transfer to [[mushroom-spawn-making-and-pure-culture-methods]]

### Applications
- Quantitative estimates of fungal propagule density (colony-forming units per gram)
- Comparison of fungal populations between sites or treatments
- Isolation of dominant species from soil

### Limitations
- Favors rapidly growing, spore-producing species
- Misses species that do not sporulate in culture
- Non-sporulating hyphae produce unreliable counts
- Cannot distinguish living from dead propagules

## Soil Plate Method (Warcup 1950)

### Principle
Small amounts of soil are distributed on agar surface. Fungi growing from the soil are transferred to pure culture.

### Protocol
1. Air-dry soil (if excessively wet)
2. Sift through 2 mm sieve
3. Sprinkle fine soil particles sparingly on agar surface (soil particles visible but not covering surface)
4. Incubate at appropriate temperature
5. Transfer emerging colonies to pure culture

### Advantages
- Simpler than dilution plating
- Preserves some natural spatial relationships
- Good for fungi that grow slowly from soil

### Disadvantages
- Non-quantitative
- Fast growers still dominate
- Difficult to standardize between operators
