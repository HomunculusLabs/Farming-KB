# Sectors in Permaculture

Sectors are the wild energies that cross a site -- the external forces and flows that the designer must respond to but cannot control. While zones organize the design from the inside out (based on the needs of the user), sectors organize it from the outside in (based on the forces of nature). Together, zones and sectors form the spatial framework of any [[permaculture-design]].

## Identifying Sectors

Mollison identifies the primary sectors as:
- **Sun**: Summer and winter sun angles, solar access for [[passive-solar-design]]
- **Wind**: Prevailing winds, storm winds, cold winds, cooling breezes
- **Fire**: Fire risk sectors, historical fire patterns
- **Water**: Overland water flow, flood risk, spring lines
- **Views**: Desired and undesirable visual connections
- **Noise**: Roads, industry, neighbors
- **Wildlife**: Migration routes, pest pressure corridors
- **Access**: Existing roads, tracks, pedestrian desire lines
- **Pollution**: Chemical drift, light pollution, noise

Each sector is mapped as a directional overlay on the site plan. A sector map typically uses a simplified compass rose or wedge diagram to show where each energy enters the site and how it flows across the landscape.

## Sector Mapping in Practice

The sector analysis follows immediately after the [[permaculture-site-analysis]] base map is complete. Mollison walks students through the process of overlaying sector information onto the site plan:

1. Observe and record all external energies over time (at minimum, a full annual cycle)
2. Classify each energy as beneficial, harmful, or neutral
3. Design responses: block harmful energies, channel beneficial ones, ignore neutral ones
4. Use elements as sector modulators -- windbreaks, earth banks, vegetation strips

For each site, Mollison recommends determining "the directions from which fire would be coming in, winter cold, summer hot winds, cooling winds." This set of characteristics is "very easily determined" by any local experienced person.

## Blocking and Channeling

The key design actions for sectors are:
- **Block**: Windbreaks to deflect cold winds, earth banks to block noise, firebreaks to arrest fire spread
- **Channel**: Swales and earthworks to direct water, hedge rows to guide wildlife movement
- **Filter**: Buffer plantings between the site and pollution sources
- **Capture**: Solar collectors positioned in the sun sector, wind generators in the wind sector

## Earth Banks as Multi-Purpose Sector Modulators

Mollison places great emphasis on earth banks as sector management tools. In flat landscapes, a four to six foot earth bank provides an immediate windbreak and can be rapidly vegetated with pampas grass and bamboos. Earth banks are "excellent radiation shields from fire" and "decrease noise, particularly traffic noise."

The rule for earth banks is: "From the crown of the road to the top of the Earth bank, we want to deflect in a straight line so that we clear the roof of the house." Vegetation alone does not reduce noise significantly unless you have 100 meters of it.

## Integration with Zones

Zones and sectors work together. A windbreak might be placed at the zone 2/3 boundary along a cold wind sector. A [[mollison-pdc-swale-design-water-harvesting]] might run along the contour at the zone 3/4 interface in a water flow sector. The most intensively managed zones (0-2) are typically positioned away from fire and noise sectors and oriented toward sun and view sectors.

## Slope and Sector Design

On slopes, sectors interact with gravity. "Fire will always travel uphill fast," so water, roads, earth walls, stone walls, and short grazing systems toward the downhill sector are all fire defenses. Water always moves downhill, so swales and dams on slopes must account for the gradient in their design.

"The way that we set up all these systems also suits fire control." This illustrates a core permaculture principle: properly designed systems serve multiple sector functions simultaneously.

## Case Studies from the Design Course

Mollison provides several detailed examples:

- **High fire frequency site**: A client on a peninsula surrounded on three sides by water, with fire every fifth or eighth year. Placed on a high point with water reflection advantages, low earth banks to deflect flooding rains, and wind protection from planted banks.

- **Flat noisy site**: Used earth bank excavation to create an underground glass house/cave beneath the house, providing thermal mass, root vegetable storage, and complete noise insulation.

- **Gully flatland**: Directed off-site water into a dam site, used earth banks to accumulate leaf and silt as mulch, creating a sophisticated water control and mulch collection system.

## Holmgren's Perspective

Holmgren emphasizes that sector thinking extends beyond the physical site. In a world of [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]], sectors include economic flows (supply chains, markets), social forces (community networks, governance), and information flows. The sector concept is a tool for mapping external dependencies and designing resilience.

## Common Mistakes

Mollison warns against several common errors in sector analysis:
- Assuming sectors are static when they vary seasonally
- Ignoring minor sectors that become critical in extreme events (fire, flood)
- Designing barriers that block beneficial flows along with harmful ones
- Failing to observe the site through a full year before mapping sectors
- The "Berchtesgarten syndrome" -- placing the client in a cold valley floor where they will "live in a refrigerator all your life, summer and winter"
