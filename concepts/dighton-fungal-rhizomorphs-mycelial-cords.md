# Fungal Rhizomorphs and Mycelial Cords

## Overview

Fungal rhizomorphs and mycelial cords are differentiated, linear
aggregations of hyphae that serve as specialized organs for long-distance
transport of water, nutrients, and carbon within the fungal mycelium.
These structures represent one of the most important physiological
adaptations of higher fungi, enabling them to function as ecosystem
engineers by connecting spatially separated resources and redistributing
materials across considerable distances. Rhizomorphs are produced
primarily by [[mollison-wet-tropical-forest-structure-and-polyculture]] forests, hyphae and particularly rhizomorphs formed by
species of Marasmius and Marasmiellus play a crucial role in trapping
and decomposing plant litter in the tree canopy. These rhizomorphs are
responsible for retaining approximately 7% of total expected leaf
litter fall that would otherwise never reach the forest floor (Hedger
et al., 1993). The fungal hyphae invading new leaves originate from
live leaves in the canopy, growing upward at rates of 3 to 6 mm per
day.

### Soil Stabilization

Rhizomorphs contribute to soil physical structure by binding mineral
particles and [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]] together on the
tropical forest floor are important in preventing downslope loss of
organic matter, with litter loss reduced by 35% from shallow slopes
and 45% at greater slopes.

## Implications for Ecosystem Processes

The ability of fungal hyphae, whether individually or as differentiated
translocatory organs (rhizomorphs, strands, or cords), to move
materials over distances ranging from millimeters to tens of meters has
profound effects on ecosystem function. This long-distance movement of
nutrients, carbon, and water can modify the distribution of
heterogeneously distributed resources and reduce spatial heterogeneity
within ecosystems.
