## Pattern Applications and Tribal Pattern Knowledge

## Overview

Permaculture pattern theory has two practical dimensions: perceiving the patterns that already exist in nature and imposing purposeful patterns on designed landscapes. This section examines both — how traditional and indigenous societies have long used sophisticated pattern recognition for navigation, land division, genealogy, and ecological management, and how modern designers can apply these principles to create more functional, sustainable, and beautiful human settlements.

## Two Aspects of Patterning

1. **Perception**: Reading and understanding the patterns that already exist in landscapes, organisms, and natural processes, and comprehending how these patterns function
2. **Imposition**: Deliberately applying pattern to sites and designs in order to achieve specific ends — energy savings, resource efficiency, aesthetic harmony

Both are skills of sophisticated design that can result in specific strategies, harmonious resolution of problems, or the production of local resources.

## The Problem of Rectilinear Patterning

A bird's-eye view of centralized societies reveals strictly rectilinear networks of streets, farms, and property boundaries. Mollison argues this pattern serves survey instruments rather than human or environmental needs. The consequences are severe:

- **Straight-line patterns prevent sensible landscape planning strategies**
- **Neither aesthetically nor functionally satisfactory** landscapes result
- **Once codified into law**, such patterns are stubbornly defended despite their inadequacy
- **Property boundaries** based on grids ignore watersheds, microclimates, and ecological boundaries

The critical insight is that these patterns were **created by people** and can therefore be **dismantled by people** — but only when enough people recognize their dysfunction.

## Traditional Land Division Patterns

### Hawaiian Ahupua'a System

Hawaiian villagers developed a remarkably effective land division system:

- **Boundaries followed natural ridgelines** rather than arbitrary grid lines
- Each land unit contained a **complete water catchment** from cloud forests to outer reefs
- This achieved **stable, resource-rich landscapes** spanning full elevational gradients
- The conic and radial volcanic landscape with radial water lines suited this method perfectly

Benefits of the ridge-boundary approach:

- Whole valleys could maintain **clean catchments** through collective stewardship
- **Mid-slope water resources** were stored and diverted for community needs
- **Lost nutrients** were captured in shallow ocean enclosures, converted through algae to crabs and fish
- **Offshore reef areas** and marine environments were preserved
- Complete ecological integration from mountain top to reef

### Zulu and American Indian Settlements

Plains-dwelling peoples adopted **circular or zonal modes** in their settlements:

- Circular patterns provide equal access to central resources
- Zonal arrangements allow graduated intensity of use from center to periphery
- Both patterns follow natural principles of energy distribution and flow

### Bioregional Implications

Such models can be studied and adopted by future bioregional societies. Sensible land division is described as a "long-delayed but essential precursor to a stable society" — implying that current rectilinear systems actively undermine social and ecological stability.

## Tribal Uses of Patterning

Traditional peoples worldwide employ an enormous variety of sophisticated patterns that serve practical, navigational, genealogical, and ecological purposes:

### Polynesian Navigation

Polynesian navigators used pattern-based systems that successfully located 200–2,000 island specks across the vast Pacific Ocean. Their navigation toolkit included:

- **Pattern maps** made of sticks, flexed strips, and cowrie shells — lacking scale or trigonometric measures but encoding wave interference patterns, star sets, and ocean currents
- **Song cycles** linked to pattern maps, providing the decoding keys for navigation information
- **Multi-sensory observation**: Star patterns, bird flight, cloud formations, light reflections from coastal lagoons, water speed measurements, wave refraction from the hull, deep-sea bioluminescence, bird songs, headland echoes, phosphorescent forest glow
- **Body-based measurement**: Water temperature gauged by submerging the testicular sac in the sea
- **Scent detection**: Wind-carried scents from forests, rock lichen, bird colonies, and fish schools
- **Biological indicators**: Catching indicator organisms related to water masses
- **Crystal polarization**: Using calcite crystals to find the sun on overcast days or predict rain
- **Chanted timing**: Songs timed the log line and recorded stages of the journey

### Pitjantjatjara Sand Patterns (Australia)

Australian Aboriginal people use sand patterns as sophisticated geographic information systems:

- **Songlines** over sand patterns enable navigation to specific stones in featureless desert
- Designs accurately reflect **lobular shapes and micro-elevations** of the desert landscape
- Vegetation changes, fire patterns, pollen distributions, flowering stages of plants, rain tracks, and cloud streets are all encoded
- Hunting, saga, and climatic data are preserved in pattern mosaics
- What appears as abstract art to Westerners is in fact detailed environmental mapping

### Maori Tattoo and Carving

The Maori of New Zealand use tattoo (moko) and carved patterns to:

- **Record and recall genealogical information** across generations
- **Preserve saga and historical narratives** in visual form
- **Encode tribal identity and status** in permanent body and architectural art

### Pattern as Literature

Mollison argues that tribal peoples can only be called "illiterate" if we ignore their patterns, songs, and dances as valid recording systems. Traditional pattern complexity may have had important meanings that modern alphabetical cultures have abandoned:

- **Children** are taught hundreds of simple chants with hidden secondary meanings about medicine, sacred knowledge, and navigation
- **Initiation** provides the decoding system that unlocks deeper meaning in patterns
- **Dances** carry long-term messages through muscular memory, coupled with chants
- **Animal-imitation dances** interpret species' postural meanings in universally transmittable ways
- Many formal attitudes of **prayer and submission** are basic imitations of primate postures

## The Loss of Pattern Learning

Having evolved number and alphabetical symbols, modern societies have largely abandoned pattern learning and recording in education. Mollison considers this "a gross error" because:

- Simple patterns link many phenomena across disciplines
- Learning one significant pattern (such as the permaculture pattern model) is "very like learning an underlying principle" applicable to specific data and situations
- Pattern literacy provides integrative understanding that specialized knowledge lacks
- A pattern map may have little meaning without its song keys — modern education teaches the alphabet but not the pattern language that decodes it

## Pattern in Design Practice

For contemporary designers, these traditional approaches suggest several principles:

1. **Follow natural boundaries** for land division — ridgelines, watersheds, microclimate zones
2. **Use pattern as recording and communication tools** — visual maps, mnemonic devices, multi-sensory encoding
3. **Design for complete catchments** from highlands to lowlands, integrating all elevation zones
4. **Encode design knowledge in accessible patterns** that can be taught, remembered, and transmitted
5. **Value circular and zonal arrangements** over rectilinear grids for human settlements
6. **Recognize that pattern literacy** is as important as numerical or alphabetical literacy for design competence

The study of patterns, whether in traditional navigation systems or in the branching of streams, provides what Mollison calls "a powerful abstract tool for designers" — one that can assist in achieving sustainable yields, energy savings, and functional aesthetics in any designed habitat.
