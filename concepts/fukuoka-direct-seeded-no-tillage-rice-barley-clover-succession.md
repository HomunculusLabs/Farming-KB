# Fukuoka's Direct-Seeded No-Tillage Rice/Barley Succession in Clover Cover

## Overview

Fukuoka's practical farming method — which he called "direct-seeded,
no-tillage, winter grain/rice succession in a clover cover" — is the concrete
implementation of his five [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] farming. The system produces
both a summer rice crop and a winter barley or wheat crop on the same quarter-
acre of land, year after year, without plowing, [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]], compost,
or pesticides. Fukuoka consistently harvested close to 22 bushels (1,300
pounds) of winter grain and 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre, with a goal
of eventually reaching 33 bushels per quarter-acre.

## The Annual Cycle

### Autumn (Before Rice Harvest)

The cycle begins when Fukuoka broadcasts clover and winter grain (barley or
wheat) over the ripening heads of rice before the fall harvest. The seeds fall
among the standing rice plants and settle on the soil surface. Later, he
harvests the rice while treading on the young shoots of winter grain that have
already germinated.

After leaving the rice to dry for three days, he threshes it then scatters all
of the straw uncut over the entire field. If chicken droppings are available,
he scatters these over the straw. The straw mulch protects the germinating
winter grain, suppresses weeds, retains moisture, and begins the process of
soil enrichment through decomposition.

### Early Winter

Fukuoka forms clay pellets containing seed rice and scatters the pellets over
the straw before the New Year. The clay pellets protect the rice seeds from
birds and rodents during the winter months. With the winter grain growing and
the rice seed sown, there is now nothing left to do until the harvesting of
the winter grain — a period of several months with no labor required.

### Late May

While harvesting the winter grain, Fukuoka notices the clover growing
luxuriantly at his feet and the small shoots that have emerged from the rice
seed in the clay pellets. After harvesting, drying, and threshing the winter
grain, he scatters all of the straw uncut over the field again, continuing the
cycle of organic matter return.

### Early June

He floods the field for four to five days to weaken the clover and give the
rice shoots a chance to break through the cover of clover. This brief flooding
is the only irrigation applied specifically for weed management. The clover,
weakened but not killed, continues to fix nitrogen and [[query-how-do-cover-crops-suppress-weeds]]
throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

### Summer (June through August)

In June and July, the field is left unirrigated. In August, Fukuoka runs water
through the drainage ditches once every week or ten days. The rice grows
through the clover cover, competing successfully against weeds thanks to the
clover's suppression and the straw mulch. Spiders, frogs, dragonflies, and
other natural predators keep insect populations in balance.

## The Symbiosis of Rice, Barley, and Clover

Fukuoka summarized his entire method as the symbiosis of rice and barley or
wheat in clover. "Were I to say that all my method of farming boils down to
is the symbiosis of rice and barley or wheat in clover, I would probably be
reproached: 'If that's all there is to growing rice, then farmers wouldn't be
out there working so hard in their fields.' Yet, that is all there is to it."

The system works because each component plays multiple roles:
- **Clover** fixes atmospheric nitrogen, suppresses weeds, retains soil
  moisture, and provides a living mulch that decomposes into organic matter.
- **Rice straw** returns carbon and silicon to the soil, protects germinating
  seeds, and moderates soil temperature.
- **Winter grain** provides a second crop from the same land, its roots
  aerating the soil during the non-flooded season.
- **Clay pellets** protect rice seeds from predation and premature
  germination during winter.

## Eliminating Unnecessary Labor

The essence of Fukuoka's method is elimination. Scientists are always saying,
"Let's try this, let's try that." Agriculture becomes swept up in all of this
fiddling around; new methods requiring additional expenditures and effort by
farmers are constantly introduced. Fukuoka took the opposite tack: "I don't
need to do this, I don't need to do that."

After thirty years of this systematic elimination, he had reduced his labor
to essentially just sowing seed and spreading straw. The labor of one or two
people is more than enough to grow crops on a quarter-acre. Human effort is
unnecessary because nature, not man, grows the rice and wheat.

## Yield and Quality

Despite — or because of — the absence of human intervention, yields in
Fukuoka's fields were consistently better than average. Generally about 250
heads of grain per square yard, with an average of 200 grains per head, gave a
harvest of some 33 bushels for every quarter-acre. Those who saw the sturdy
heads of rice rising from the field marveled at the strength and vigor of the
plants and their large yields.

The quality of naturally-farmed food, Fukuoka argued, is superior to that of
scientifically farmed products. Modern agriculture has created nothing from
nature; rather, by making quantitative and qualitative changes in certain
aspects of nature, it has managed only to fabricate synthetic food products
that are crude, expensive, and further alienate man from nature.

## Ecological Balance in the Fields

As Fukuoka walked through his paddy field, spiders and frogs scrambled about,
locusts jumped up, and droves of dragonflies hovered overhead. Whenever a
large outbreak of leafhoppers occurred, the spiders multiplied too, without
fail. Although the yield varied from year to year, there was no catastrophic
pest damage because the natural food web remained intact.

Chickens ran free through the orchard and semi-wild vegetables grew in the
clover among the trees. The farm functioned as an integrated ecosystem rather
than a monoculture production system.

## Applicability Beyond Japan

Fukuoka believed the principles of his method were universal, though the
specific crops and timing would vary by region. The fundamental insight — that
nature can produce food abundantly if given the opportunity — applies
everywhere. The key is understanding the local ecosystem well enough to
identify the right combination of ground covers, mulch crops, and food crops
that can work in symbiosis.

The method is especially relevant for small-scale farmers who cannot afford
expensive inputs. With no need for plows, tractors, chemical fertilizers, or
pesticides, the capital requirements are minimal. The primary inputs are seeds,
straw, and clay for pellets.
