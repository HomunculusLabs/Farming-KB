# Psilocybin Mushroom Field Identification Guide

This guide covers the identification of psilocybin-containing mushrooms common to the southern United States, based on F.C. Ghouled's 1972 *Field Guide to the Psilocybin Mushroom*. Three species are described in detail: *Psilocybe cubensis*, *Panaeolus subbalteatus*, and *Psilocybe caerulescens*.

## Key Identification Principles

All psilocybin mushrooms share two defining alkaloids — **psilocybin** and **psilocin** — though concentrations vary between species. The experiences produced by different species are essentially similar, with minor variations noted among rarer varieties.

### The Bluing Reaction

The single most important field identification test is the **bluing reaction**. All psilocybin mushrooms (with one uncommon exception) contain an enzyme that oxidizes when the flesh is damaged, producing a blue coloration similar to blue ink within 20 to 120 minutes. To test: crack the stem and observe.

On yellowish-fleshed specimens, the bluing appears blue-green rather than pure blue.

**Important caveat**: One non-psychoactive genus also produces a bluing reaction. However, it is easily distinguished by its:
- Large, bulbous, smooth cap
- Yellow to yellow-brown coloration over the entire surface
- Pores (like an organic sponge) instead of gills on the underside
- Solid, fleshy stem
- Growth on soil (not on dung)

### The Three-Point Field Test

For the beginner, three simple criteria eliminate virtually all risk of misidentification:

1. **Grows on manure** (cow, horse, pig, sheep, or goat — all grass or grain-fed animals)
2. **Turns blue when damaged**
3. **Has a hollow stem**

Specimens meeting all three criteria are almost certainly psilocybin-containing species.

## Species Descriptions

### *Psilocybe cubensis*

The most common and widely distributed of the three species, found on cow manure throughout the southern United States from California to Florida.

**Cap**: 1/2 to 4 inches wide. Initially cone-shaped, becoming convex, then flat, and finally uplifted at the edges to form a bowl shape within 24–48 hours after rain. A sticky protective film covers fresh specimens. Color ranges from nearly pure white with a gold center spot to overall light brown with the gold spot retained. Becomes translucent and dark-olive when waterlogged (the dark spore color showing through). Dries to yellow-rust with the orange-to-gold center spot preserved.

**Gills**: Closely spaced, light brown when young, deep purple to black at maturity. Initially attached to the stem but may separate with age.

**Stem**: 1.5 to 6 inches tall, up to 1/2 inch thick. The base (volva) is often thickened. Hollow, fibrous, generally white or lighter than the cap. A ring of tissue (the partial veil) usually hangs on the upper stem and typically turns blue with age. The inside flesh of a broken stem yields the fastest bluing reaction.

**Flesh**: White, mild odor, tastes like fresh grain. Found on cow manure or manure-enriched soil.

### *Panaeolus subbalteatus*

Less common than *P. cubensis* — approximately 12% as common by count (roughly 3 found for every 25 *P. cubensis*). Often discovered incidentally while harvesting *P. cubensis* from the same dung.

**Cap**: Bluntly cone-shaped with an incurved margin in young specimens. Expands with maturity but the incurved margin usually persists. Light cinnamon color, uniform, covered with small white flecks when young. With age, the center becomes lighter or darker, forming a distinctive zoning ring. The veil is off-white (never blues) and hangs downward from the cone-shaped cap in young specimens, disappearing as the cap expands.

**Gills**: Very closely spaced, very light brown when young, becoming black at maturity.

**Stem**: 1.5 to nearly 4 inches tall, never over 1/2 inch thick. Very uniform and evenly shaped. The top is vertically grooved; the lower portion is covered with hairlike scales and fine white powder. Usually white, sometimes with a sepia or light cinnamon tint. Hollow. The bluing reaction is most pronounced in the stem.

**Flesh**: White to yellowish. Taste and odor resemble fine table mushrooms from the grocery store. Matures slowly, so older specimens are rarely encountered.

### *Psilocybe caerulescens**

Unlike the other two species, this one grows on the banks of streams and rivers rather than on manure. Found throughout the entire southern United States.

**Cap**: 1 to over 3 inches wide. Cone-shaped when young, expanding to convex-to-flat. The margin exhibits a downward curve. Surface is smooth and sticky, especially when young. Translucent when moist, with dark gill lines visible through the cap. Mature specimens show a lighter or darker margin ring separating it from the center. Young specimens are deep green to black, fading with age to cinnamon, rust, or sometimes retaining a faded olive-green. The boundary between center and margin coloration is irregularly shaped.

**Gills**: Closely spaced, wide, light cinnamon to light brown when young, becoming dark brown to black with age. Gill edges are lighter in color.

**Stem**: 1.5 to 4 inches tall, up to 1/2 inch thick. Even, hollow, smooth at the top with thick fibrous hairs covering the rest of the way to the base. The veil falls away very early. Fibrous and tough.

**Flesh**: Off-white to yellowish, occasionally with light brown tints in the cap. Strong grain-like odor. Turns blue readily, especially after handling.

## Field Identification Checklist

When encountering a specimen in the field, systematically record:

1. Age and condition of the specimen
2. Does it turn blue when damaged?
3. **Cap**: Coloration, shape and size, margin (incurved or regular)
4. **Gills**: Coloration, shape and size, spacing, attachment to stem
5. **Stem**: Coloration, shape and size, base (volva present?), hollow or solid
6. **Flesh**: Coloration, texture, thickness, special color traits
7. Climate and environment: Last rain, high and low temperatures (24 hrs and past week), what is the fungi growing on?

## Safety Warnings

Several non-psilocybin mushroom species are dangerously poisonous. Ingestion causes violent bodily flushing through vomiting and diarrhea, with cramps ranging from mild discomfort to death. The beginner should never eat any mushroom in the field. Even when confident in identification, consume no more than a tiny test bite initially.

The famed *Amanita muscaria* (Mexico's "magic mushroom") causes intense hallucinations but is highly toxic and should be avoided. It is visually unmistakable — a red ball-shaped mushroom bearing no resemblance to any of the three species described here.
