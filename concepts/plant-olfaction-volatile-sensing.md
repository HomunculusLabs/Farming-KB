# Plant Olfaction and Volatile Sensing

Plants possess a sophisticated ability to detect and respond to [[chamovitz-plant-scent-communication-volatile-organic-compounds]] (VOCs) in their environment. This [[plant-olfaction-chemical-sensing]] capability — the plant equivalent of smell — allows plants to perceive the presence of neighboring plants, detect herbivore attacks, coordinate defense responses, and even "eavesdrop" on the chemical conversations of other species. Plant olfaction operates through molecular mechanisms that, while fundamentally different from animal olfaction, achieve remarkably similar outcomes.

## How Plants Detect Volatiles

Plants detect volatile chemicals through receptor proteins and enzymatic systems rather than through dedicated olfactory organs:

### Receptor-Mediated Detection
Plant cells possess receptor proteins that bind specific [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]], triggering intracellular signaling cascades. Unlike animal olfactory receptors, which are G-protein-coupled receptors located in a specialized epithelium, plant volatile receptors are distributed throughout plant tissues and often serve multiple functions:

- Some receptors are located in the plasma membrane and detect extracellular volatiles that have diffused through the air or been carried in solution
- Others may be intracellular, detecting volatiles that have crossed the plasma membrane and entered the cytoplasm
- Many volatile receptors were originally identified in other contexts (e.g., ethylene receptors, jasmonate receptors) and later found to respond to volatile forms of these compounds
- The signaling cascade downstream of volatile perception typically involves calcium signaling, MAP kinase cascades, and transcriptional reprogramming

### Direct Enzymatic Activation
Some volatile compounds can directly activate or modify enzymes within plant cells without requiring a dedicated receptor. For example, volatile forms of plant hormones such as methyl jasmonate (MeJA) and methyl salicylate (MeSA) can diffuse across membranes and be converted to their active acid forms by cellular esterases, bypassing the need for signal perception and transduction. This direct activation pathway allows for extremely rapid responses to volatile cues — on the order of minutes rather than hours.

## Key Volatile Signals in Plant Communication

### Ethylene
Ethylene (C₂H₄) is the simplest plant hormone and exists as a gas at room temperature, making it both a hormone and a volatile signal. It was the first gaseous hormone discovered in any organism. Ethylene serves numerous functions in plant biology:

- **Fruit ripening**: Ethylene triggers and coordinates the ripening process in climacteric fruits, which is why placing unripe fruit in a bag with a ripe banana accelerates ripening
- **Leaf abscission**: Ethylene promotes the senescence and shedding of leaves, flowers, and fruits
- **Stress response**: Wounding, flooding, pathogen attack, and other stresses stimulate ethylene biosynthesis through the ACC synthase pathway
- **Triple response**: In dark-grown seedlings, ethylene causes reduced stem elongation, thickened stems, and exaggerated apical hook — a diagnostic test for ethylene sensitivity that has been used in genetic screens since the 1980s
- **Flower senescence**: Ethylene accelerates the aging and death of flowers, which is why cut flowers last longer when ethylene is removed from their environment

Ethylene is perceived by a family of membrane-bound receptors (ETR1, ETR2, EIN4, ERS1, ERS2 in Arabidopsis) that are structurally related to bacterial two-component histidine kinases. In the absence of ethylene, these receptors actively suppress ethylene responses. When ethylene binds, receptor activity is inhibited, releasing the suppression and allowing downstream signaling through the EIN2-EIN3 transcription factor cascade.

### Methyl Jasmonate
Methyl jasmonate (MeJA) is a volatile derivative of jasmonic acid, a key hormone in plant defense. MeJA serves as an airborne signal between plants:

- When a plant is attacked by herbivores, it produces jasmonic acid, which triggers local defense responses including the production of toxic [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] and proteinase inhibitors
- A portion of the jasmonic acid is methylated to form MeJA, which evaporates and can be detected by neighboring plants
- Neighboring plants that detect MeJA convert it back to jasmonic acid and prime their own defenses before herbivores arrive
- This "eavesdropping" on neighboring plant distress signals has been demonstrated in numerous plant species

### Green Leaf Volatiles
Green leaf volatiles (GLVs) are C₆ aldehydes, alcohols, and esters produced when plant cell membranes are damaged by herbivore feeding or mechanical wounding. They give freshly cut grass its characteristic smell and serve multiple signaling functions across different [[ingham-soil-foodweb-trophic-levels-protozoa-nematodes]]:

- **Within-plant signaling**: GLVs are produced at the wound site and travel through the plant's vascular system or air spaces to activate defense genes in undamaged tissues within minutes of damage
- **Between-plant signaling**: GLVs released into the air can be detected by neighboring plants, triggering defensive priming that makes them more resistant to subsequent attack
- **Carnivore attraction**: GLVs attract predatory insects and [[parasitic-wasps-biological-pest-control]] that prey on herbivores — an indirect [[fukuoka-textdoc-disease-as-natures-self-defense-mechanism]] known as "the enemy of my enemy is my friend." This tritrophic interaction has been extensively documented in lima bean-spider mite-predatory mite systems
- **Fungal defense**: GLVs have direct antifungal properties and can inhibit pathogen growth at wound sites, providing an immediate chemical barrier against infection

### Methyl Salicylate
Methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen) is a volatile derivative of salicylic acid, the primary hormone in systemic acquired resistance (SAR) against pathogens. When a plant successfully defends against a pathogen infection, it produces methyl salicylate as a mobile signal that travels through the phloem to distant tissues, establishing a state of heightened defensive alertness throughout the plant. Methyl salicylate can also volatilize and be detected by neighboring plants, providing a form of inter-plant communication about pathogen pressure in the local environment. The conversion between salicylic acid and its volatile methyl ester is catalyzed by salicylic acid methyltransferase, and the reverse reaction is catalyzed by methyl salicylate esterase, providing a reversible switch for this signaling molecule.

## Plant-Plant Communication Through Volatiles

The ability of plants to communicate with each other through volatile chemicals is one of the most fascinating aspects of [[plant-sensory-biology]]. Research in this area has accelerated dramatically since the 1980s and has revealed that inter-plant communication is more widespread and sophisticated than previously imagined:

### Danger Signaling
When one plant is attacked by herbivores or pathogens, it releases specific volatile blends that neighboring plants can detect and respond to. The responding plants do not simply react identically — they tailor their response based on the specific blend of volatiles detected, which encodes information about the type of attacker. This has been demonstrated in:

- **Sagebrush (*Artemisia tridentata*)**: Plants growing near experimentally clipped neighbors showed reduced herbivore damage compared to controls. The volatile blend released after herbivore damage differed from that released after mechanical damage, and neighbors responded more strongly to herbivore-induced volatiles.
- **Lima beans**: Plants exposed to volatiles from spider-mite-infested neighbors produced more extrafloral nectar to attract [[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol]], demonstrating a highly specific multi-trophic response
- **Wild tobacco**: Plants exposed to volatiles from herbivore-attacked neighbors primed jasmonic acid-dependent defenses, responding more rapidly and strongly when subsequently attacked themselves

### Kin Recognition
Some evidence suggests that plants can distinguish between volatiles from genetically related individuals (kin) and unrelated strangers:

- Sagebrush (*Artemisia tridentata*): Plants growing near experimentally clipped neighbors showed reduced herbivore damage compared to controls, and this effect was stronger when the neighbor was a close relative
- This kin recognition may allow plants to allocate defense resources more efficiently, responding most strongly when relatives are threatened
- The evolutionary basis for kin recognition in plants is debated but may relate to inclusive fitness — organisms that share genes with their neighbors benefit more from investing in shared defense

### Competitive Interactions
Plants also use volatiles to assess and respond to competition from neighboring vegetation:

- Some plants detect volatile cues from neighboring species and adjust their growth strategies accordingly, allocating more resources to vertical growth when competitors are detected
- Allelopathic volatiles released by some species can inhibit the germination and growth of competitors. Black walnut (*Juglans nigra*) releases juglone, a compound that inhibits many neighboring plant species
- The shade avoidance response, while primarily triggered by the red-to-far-red light ratio, can also be modulated by volatile signals from neighboring plants

## Detection of Non-Plant Volatiles

Plants also detect volatile chemicals from non-plant sources:

### Ripening Fruit
The ethylene released by ripening fruit triggers a positive feedback loop where ethylene production accelerates ripening, which produces more ethylene. This is commercially exploited by shipping fruit unripe and exposing it to ethylene at the destination. Bananas, tomatoes, and avocados are commonly treated this way. This same principle means that keeping ripe and unripe fruit together on a kitchen counter will accelerate ripening of the unripe items.
