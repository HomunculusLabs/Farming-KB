Leucaena leucocephala is one of the most versatile multipurpose trees
in tropical permaculture. Native to Central America, it has become
naturalised throughout the tropics and subtropics, where it serves as
fodder, green manure, timber, charcoal, and food source. In their
native environments, all Leucaena species are used for fodder, fuel,
fence posts, and food, earning the reputation that where "leucaena
lives, cattle live."

## Alley Cropping System

Leucaena is widely used in alley cropping between rows of crops such
as corn, tobacco, yams, and rice. Rows are kept pruned to prevent
shade competition with crops. The pruned material provides excellent
mulch and green manure. The tree provides a shaded environment for
understory crops, and leaf drop creates humus rich in nitrogen.

This system allows agriculture to continue sustainably on land that
would otherwise require chemical fertiliser inputs. Once a leucaena
alley cropping system is in place, it reduces pressure on forest
clearing for agriculture by maintaining productive land indefinitely.

## Fodder Quality and Management

Leucaena provides high-protein fodder for cattle, goats, and sheep.
It can recover from browsing and be ready for further grazing in just
two weeks. The ideal leucaena forage system incorporates a range of
other species including grasses.

A critical management consideration is mimosine toxicity. Leucaena
should form no more than 30 percent of the long-term diet. Sheep fed
exclusively on leucaena for a few days will lose their wool. The
toxicity can be managed by ensuring dietary diversity through mixed
pastures and rotational grazing.

## Food Uses

Young leaves, shoots, and immature green beans are edible. In Mexico,
the Zapotec people eat the immature green bean as a vegetable. They
are stripped of their outer layer and boiled. Mature but not dried
seeds are eaten raw or cooked, wrapped in banana leaves. Various
parts of the tree have traditional food uses across its native range.

## Timber and Wood Products

Leucaena wood from two-year-old trees produces excellent fence posts
and building fittings. It is one of the best tropical hardwoods for
paper pulp production, with good fibre quality. The wood serves as
girders, floor joists, and rafters in construction.

Charcoal from leucaena has a heating value of about 7,000 kcal per
kilogram, making it a high-quality fuel. Its rapid growth and
coppicing ability ensure continuous fuelwood supplies from managed
plantations.

## Growth and Adaptability

Leucaena grows rapidly in tropical and subtropical conditions, with
plantations covering 160,000 hectares in Indonesia and over 125,000
hectares in Brazil. It is cold-tolerant, often losing leaves in
severe cold but recovering. However, it is slower growing and less
prolific in subtropical areas compared to tropical zones.

Several related species extend the range of conditions where leucaena-
type forage can be grown. Some species are more cold-tolerant, though
slower growing and less prolific. Hybrids with leucaena show promise
for combining desirable traits such as good form and aggressive
growth with improved cold tolerance.

## Establishment and Propagation

Leucaena establishes readily from seed, which requires scarification
for consistent germination. Direct seeding into prepared sites works
well in tropical conditions. Seedlings transplant easily when young.
For fodder systems, close spacing within rows and wider between-row
spacing allows easy access for pruning and grazing management.

## Leucaena in [[permaculture-design]]

In permaculture food forests of the tropics, leucaena serves as the
nitrogen-fixing canopy component that enables productive understory
crops. Its role as a shade tree for coffee and cacao is well
established. The integration of grazing animals beneath leucaena
creates a complete system where trees feed animals, animals fertilise
trees, and the whole cycle reduces external inputs.

The key design principle is never to rely on leucaena as a sole
fodder source. It functions best as one element in a diverse system
that includes grasses, other legume trees, and broadleaf forbs. This
diversity eliminates toxicity risk while maximising total system
productivity and resilience.

## Ice Cream Bean as Companion Species

The ice cream bean (Inga edulis), with over 400 species, shares many
of leucaena's functions but adds an edible sweet pulp surrounding
its seeds. Native to the Neotropics, it grows to 18 metres and serves
as a nurse crop with an edible yield, an unusual combination. Inga
species provide shade for coffee and cacao, fix nitrogen, and produce
timber for furniture and charcoal. Their edible pods make them
particularly valuable for home garden systems.

## Design Integration Summary

Leucaena and Inga species represent the backbone of tropical
permaculture fodder systems. When integrated with grass strips,
fruit trees, and animal rotation, they create self-fertilising
productive landscapes. The designer should plan for multiple access
rows, regular pruning schedules, and diverse companion plantings.
Starting with a small trial area of alley cropping allows observation
of local performance before scaling up. Once established, these
legume tree systems reduce or eliminate purchased fertiliser, feed,
and fuel costs while building soil carbon and supporting biodiversity.
