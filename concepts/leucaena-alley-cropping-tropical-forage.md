Leucaena leucocephala is one of the most versatile multipurpose trees in tropical permaculture. Native to [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], it has become naturalised throughout the tropics and subtropics, where it serves as fodder, green manure, timber, charcoal, and food source. In their native environments, all Leucaena species are used for fodder, fuel, fence posts, and food, earning the reputation that where "leucaena lives, cattle live." ## Alley Cropping System Leucaena is widely used in alley cropping between rows of crops such as corn, tobacco, yams, and rice. Rows are kept pruned to prevent shade competition with crops. The pruned material provides excellent mulch and green manure. The tree provides a shaded environment for understory crops, and leaf drop creates humus rich in nitrogen. This system allows agriculture to continue sustainably on land that would otherwise require chemical fertiliser inputs. Once a leucaena alley cropping system is in place, it reduces pressure on forest clearing for agriculture by maintaining productive land indefinitely. ## Fodder Quality and Management Leucaena provides high-protein fodder for cattle, goats, and sheep. It can recover from browsing and be ready for further grazing in just two weeks. The ideal leucaena forage system incorporates a range of other species including grasses. A critical management consideration is mimosine toxicity. Leucaena should form no more than 30 percent of the long-term diet. Sheep fed exclusively on leucaena for a few days will lose their wool. The toxicity can be managed by ensuring dietary diversity through mixed pastures and [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]]. ## Food Uses Young leaves, shoots, and immature green beans are edible. In Mexico, the Zapotec people eat the immature green bean as a vegetable. They are stripped of their outer layer and boiled. Mature but not dried seeds are eaten raw or cooked, wrapped in banana leaves. Various parts of the tree have traditional food uses across its native range. ## Timber and Wood Products Leucaena wood from two-year-old trees produces excellent fence posts and building fittings. It is one of the best tropical hardwoods for paper pulp production, with good fibre quality. The wood serves as girders, floor joists, and rafters in construction. Charcoal from leucaena has a heating value of about 7,000 kcal per kilogram, making it a high-quality fuel. Its rapid growth and coppicing ability ensure continuous fuelwood supplies from managed plantations. ## Growth and Adaptability Leucaena grows rapidly in tropical and subtropical conditions, with plantations covering 160,000 hectares in Indonesia and over 125,000 hectares in Brazil. It is cold-tolerant, often losing leaves in severe cold but recovering. However, it is slower growing and less prolific in subtropical areas compared to tropical zones. ## Related - [[permaculture-design]]

In permaculture food forests of the tropics, leucaena serves as the
nitrogen-fixing canopy component that enables productive understory
crops. Its role as a shade tree for [[mollison-designers-shade-grown-coffee-and-cacao-systems]] is well
established. The integration of grazing animals beneath leucaena
creates a complete system where trees feed animals, animals fertilise
trees, and the whole cycle reduces external inputs.

The key design principle is never to rely on leucaena as a sole
fodder source. It functions best as one element in a diverse system
that includes grasses, other [[permaculture-plants-legume-trees-acacia-carob-locust]], and broadleaf forbs. This
diversity eliminates toxicity risk while maximising total system
productivity and resilience.

## Silvopastoral Integration and Animal Systems

In silvopastoral systems, leucaena is planted in dense hedgerows at
2-5 metre spacing within grazing paddocks. Animals browse the hedgerows
directly rather than cutting and carrying fodder. Research in
Australia and Latin America has demonstrated liveweight gains of
100-300 kg per hectare per year in leucaena-grass pastures compared
to grass-only systems. The psyllid insect (Heteropsylla cubana) is a
major pest of leucaena, causing defoliation and growth reduction.
Psyllid-resistant cultivars have been developed through breeding
programs in Hawaii and Australia, making silvopastoral systems more
reliable across diverse tropical environments. Cattle should be
introduced gradually to leucaena pastures over 2-3 weeks to allow
rumen microbes that degrade mimosine to build up in the gut, which
prevents the hair loss and goiter that can result from sudden
exposure to high-mimosine diets.

## Nitrogen Fixation and Soil Improvement

Leucaena forms effective nodules with Rhizobium strains that can fix
100-500 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year, depending on stand
density and growing conditions. This nitrogen input significantly
improves soil fertility for associated crops. Leaf prunings from alley
cropping contribute 60-200 kg N/ha/yr when returned as mulch. The
deep root system also cycles nutrients from subsoil layers, bringing
calcium, magnesium, and potassium to the surface through leaf fall.
Over time, leucaena-based systems can restore degraded tropical soils
that have been exhausted by conventional monoculture cropping.

## Ice Cream Bean as Companion Species

The ice cream bean (Inga edulis), with over 400 species, shares many
of leucaena's functions but adds an edible sweet pulp surrounding
its seeds. Native to the Neotropics, it grows to 18 metres and serves
as a nurse crop with an edible yield, an unusual combination. Inga
species provide shade for coffee and cacao, [[query-how-to-fix-nitrogen-toxicity-in-cannabis]], and produce
timber for furniture and charcoal. Their edible pods make them
particularly valuable for home garden systems.

## Design Integration Summary

Leucaena and Inga species represent the backbone of tropical
permaculture fodder systems. When integrated with grass strips,
fruit trees, and animal rotation, they create self-fertilising
productive landscapes. The designer should plan for multiple access
rows, regular pruning schedules, and diverse companion plantings.
Starting with a small trial area of alley cropping allows observation
of local performance before scaling up. Once established, these
legume tree systems reduce or eliminate purchased fertiliser, feed,
and fuel costs while building soil carbon and supporting biodiversity.
