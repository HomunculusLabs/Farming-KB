## Dieu-Ponds, Terraces, and Dam Construction

Hand-shaped water harvesting and earthworks techniques drawn from [[bill-mollison]]'s [[permaculture-design]] Course, covering small-scale catchments, residential terracing, diversion drains, and [[dam-construction]] from garden walls to valley-scale embankments.

## Dieu-Ponds

Dieu-ponds are hand-dug mini-catchments fed entirely by rainfall. The name and technique descend from a monastic tradition — monks across Europe maintained these ponds for centuries as self-replenishing water sources without any spring or stream input.

### The Taper Design Secret

The key insight is the **3:1 resting-angle wall taper**. Rather than digging a vertical-walled hole, the pond walls slope inward at a ratio of 3 horizontal units for every 1 vertical unit. This taper serves multiple purposes:

- Walls are stable without reinforcement at this angle — earth naturally rests there.
- As water evaporates, the surface area of the pond *shrinks proportionally*, dramatically slowing further evaporation.
- The broad, sloping sides allow animals safe stone-lined access to drink without falling in.

### Construction Details

- **Diameter**: 3 to 20 feet, depending on available labor and site water budget.
- **Clay tamped**: The bottom and walls are packed with clay and tamped firm to hold water.
- **Stone access ramps**: Placed at one or more points so livestock and wildlife can walk down to drink safely.
- **Maintenance**: Periodically clean out accumulated silt and leaf debris to maintain capacity and water quality.

### Why They Never Dry Up

Because of the taper geometry, as water level drops the surface area exposed to evaporation shrinks. A half-full dieu-pond has far less surface area than a full one, so the rate of loss decelerates. Combined with clay sealing and shaded microclimate from surrounding plantings, these ponds can persist through extended dry seasons where open containers would evaporate completely.

## Mini-Terraces for Subdivision Clients

Mini-terraces are small-scale earthworks suited to residential subdivision lots where space is limited but [[water-management]] and food production are still desired. They turn a slope into productive, accessible growing space.

### Dimensions

| Element | Measurement |
|---------|------------|
| Wall height | Waist height (approx. 3 feet) |
| Top width | 2 feet (planting surface) |
| Base width | 3–4 feet (structural base) |
| Walkway width | 12 inches between tiers |
| Maximum tiers | 3–4 stacked |
| Maximum length | 40 feet per run |

### Staggered Layout

Terraces must be **staggered, not aligned in a single vertical line**. This is critical for runoff dispersion — if all terrace walls line up, concentrated water flow can undercut and collapse them. Staggering spreads the sheet flow across the slope so no single wall receives the full force.

### Mulch and Forage Integration

- **Mulched walkways**: The 12-inch gaps between tiers are mulched to suppress weeds, retain moisture, and provide a comfortable working surface.
- **Chicken forage trees**: Planted on the slope between and below terraces, providing shade, leaf drop for mulch, and forage for poultry.
- **Kickdown chicken mulch system**: Chickens are allowed onto the terraces where they scratch, deposit manure, and kick mulch down from upper to lower tiers — a self-distributing fertility system requiring minimal human intervention.

## Broad Diversion and Irrigation Drains

Drains move water across slopes to irrigate lower areas, divert excess runoff, or direct water into storage. Three main construction methods scale from manual to machine-driven:

### Spinner Wheel

- A rotating wheel with **cups** mounted on it, dragged across the slope.
- Cuts a drain **4 feet wide** and **1 foot deep**.
- Suited to relatively gentle slopes where a broad, shallow swale is sufficient.
- The wheel's rotation allows continuous forward motion — efficient for long runs.

### Tilted Blade (Contour Road)

- Used on **steeper slopes** where the spinner wheel cannot maintain grip.
- The blade is angled/tilted to cut along the contour while throwing spoil downslope.
- Can double as a contour road or access track once established.
- More aggressive earth-moving — suitable for larger diversion volumes.

### Delvers

- Two plows **joined together** and pulled behind bulldozers.
- Cuts a **V-shaped drain** in a single pass.
- Faster and more aggressive than the spinner wheel, producing a deeper channel.
- Best for primary diversion drains on larger properties where significant water needs to be redirected quickly.

## Dam Wall Construction (Small Walls)

Small dam walls up to **6–8 feet high** are straightforward earthworks suitable for farm ponds and landscape water storage.

### Slope Ratios

- **Front slope (waterside)**: **2.5 : 1** — slightly steeper because it is supported by water pressure when full.
- **Rear slope (dry side)**: **3 : 1** — gentler to prevent slumping under its own weight when dry.
- **Crown (top)**: Broad, wider than a car, to allow vehicle access for maintenance and to resist erosion from rainfall and traffic.

### Construction Rules

- **No rocks in the structure**: Rocks create seepage paths through the wall. All stone must be excluded from the compacted earth body — it can be used for facing or spillways but never embedded in the wall core.
- **Tamp every foot**: Each lift of earth must be compacted with machinery (sheepsfoot roller, plate compactor, or tracked vehicle) before the next layer is added. Uncompacted lifts settle unevenly and create weak zones.

## Larger Dams (200+ Feet Long)

Valley dams of significant size require additional engineering to ensure watertightness and structural integrity.

### Clay Core Construction

1. **Trench down to clay**: Excavate a cutoff trench along the dam's centerline, extending down through all permeable soil and alluvium until reaching **impervious clay or bedrock**.
2. **Selected clay core**: High-quality clay is packed into this trench and continued upward as a vertical core through the center of the dam wall. This creates an impermeable barrier that prevents seepage under and through the embankment.
3. **[[rammed-earth]] wall**: The bulk of the dam on either side of the clay core is built from compacted earth, shaped with the same slope ratios (2.5:1 front, 3:1 rear).
4. **Dry rock facing on wave splash zone**: The upstream face at water level is armored with dry-laid rock (riprap) to absorb wave action and prevent erosion of the compacted earth surface.

### Key Principles for Large Dams

- The clay core must be **continuous** — any gap or thin spot becomes a preferential seepage path.
- Foundation contact is critical: the trench must reach true clay or the dam will leak from below regardless of wall quality.
- Riprap on the splash zone is dry-laid (not mortared) so it can shift and resettle without cracking under wave energy.

## Relationships to Other Concepts

- **Keyline design**: Drain placement and dam siting follow keyline principles of contour-aligned water distribution.
- **Swales**: Broad diversion drains function similarly to swales but are designed for active water conveyance rather than infiltration.
- **Patterning**: Staggered terrace layout is an application of edge effect and dissipation patterns.
- **Zone planning**: Dieu-ponds fit Zone 1–2 (near home), mini-terraces in Zone 1, large dams in Zone 3–4.
