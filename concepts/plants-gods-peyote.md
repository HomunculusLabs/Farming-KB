# Peyote and Lophophora williamsii

## Overview

Peyote — Lophophora williamsii (Lem.) Coult. — is the most important sacred hallucinogen of Mexico and one of the most culturally significant psychoactive plants in the world. Without any question, as Schultes and Hofmann affirm, the Peyote cactus is the most important sacred hallucinogen of Mexico, though other cactus species are still used in northern Mexico as minor hallucinogens for special magico-religious purposes. Peyote belongs to the Cactaceae (Cactus Family) and is native to Mexico and southern Texas.

For the Huichol Indians of Mexico, Peyote is not a plant but a god — a gift from the Earth Goddess to humans to assist them in attaining a connection to her in the mystical realms. The Huichol celebrate a great Peyote festival every year, at which all members of the tribe partake in eating the freshly harvested Peyote cactus. The shamans of the Huichol use the sacred Peyote cactus to attain a visionary state of consciousness in the alternate reality, which they believe is causal to occurrences in mundane reality.

## Botanical Description

The genus Lophophora (Coult.) contains two recognized species that differ morphologically and chemically, though some taxonomists have proposed additional species or subspecies based on regional variation. Both species of Lophophora are small, spineless, gray-green or bluish green, top-shaped plants. The succulent chlorophyll-bearing head or crown measures up to 3.5 inches (8 cm) in diameter and is radially divided into from 5 to 13 rounded ribs. Each tubercle bears a small, flat areole from the top of which arises a tuft of hairs 0.75 inches (2 cm) long. The whitish or pinkish, campanulate, usually solitary flowers are 0.5 to 1 inch (1.5-2.5 cm) long and are borne in the umbilicate center of the crown. The flowers are followed by small pink or reddish fruits that contain small black seeds.

Lophophora williamsii is usually blue-green with from 5 to 13 ribs and normally straight furrows. L. diffusa has a gray-green, sometimes yellowish green crown with indefinite ribs and sinuate furrows, and its flowers are usually much larger than in L. williamsii. The chemical constitution of L. diffusa is much simpler than that of L. williamsii, containing only trace amounts of mescaline and primarily pellotine and other tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloids.

Both species inhabit the driest and stoniest of desert regions, usually on calcareous soil. When the crown is removed, the plant will often grow new crowns, and Peyotes with multiple heads are commonly seen. This regenerative capacity has important implications for sustainable harvesting — when harvesters cut the crown above the root zone, the plant can survive and produce new crowns for future harvest. The root system is remarkably extensive for such a small plant, with a large taproot that can extend several times the diameter of the visible crown, storing water and nutrients that sustain the plant through extended drought periods. ## Distribution and Habitat

Peyote occurs naturally in a limited range stretching from the Rio Grande Valley in southern Texas southward through the Chihuahuan Desert into the Mexican states of Coahuila, Nuevo León, San Luis Potosí, Tamaulipas, and Zacatecas. The plants grow in limestone hills, calcareous soils, and semi-arid scrubland, often hidden among rocks and thorny shrubs. Overharvesting and habitat destruction have made wild Peyote increasingly scarce in parts of its range, and conservation concerns have grown in recent decades. The Chihuahuan Desert is one of the most biodiverse arid regions in the world, and Peyote is one of its most iconic species, playing important ecological roles as a component of the desert plant community.

The typical habitat of Lophophora williamsii is on limestone slopes and flats between 100 and 1,500 meters elevation, often in association with other desert succulents including Agave species, Opuntia cacti, Echinocactus grusonii, and various thorny shrubs and legumes. The calcareous soils provide both the mineral nutrition (particularly calcium) that Peyote requires and the alkaline conditions that may influence its alkaloid production. Wild populations are often patchily distributed, with individual plants widely spaced in areas of suitable habitat. See [[psychoactive-cacti-of-central-mexico]] for a broader survey of psychoactive cactus species in the region.

## Harvest and Preparation

The Indians cut off the crown and dry it for ingestion as a hallucinogen. This dry, disklike head is known as the Mescal Button or Peyote Button. The buttons are typically chewed or soaked in water to produce a bitter-tasting liquid that is consumed during all-night ceremonial sessions. Among the Huichol, the annual pilgrimage to collect Peyote in Wirikuta — their original paradisiacal home of the ancestors — is considered a holy journey of profound spiritual significance.

Traditional preparation methods include drying fresh crowns in the sun or over low heat, grinding dried buttons into powder for capsules, or steeping buttons in water to produce a bitter infusion. The taste is intensely bitter and nauseating, which is partly why many ceremonies incorporate rituals around the difficult consumption process. ## Chemistry

Lophophora williamsii contains up to 30 alkaloids — primarily mescaline — as well as further psychoactive phenylethylamines and isoquinolines. Mescaline (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine) is the principal psychoactive compound, responsible for the visual hallucinogenic effects. Other significant alkaloids include pellotine, anhalonine, anhalonidine, and lophophorine. The alkaloid content varies depending on the part of the plant, time of harvest, and growing conditions. L. diffusa, by contrast, has a much simpler chemical constitution with significantly lower mescaline content.

Fresh Peyote buttons typically contain 0.1-0.6% mescaline by dry weight. The [[cactus-alkaloids-reference]] provides a comprehensive listing of known cactus alkaloids and their properties. Mescaline belongs to the [[phenethylamine-chemistry-basics]] class of compounds.

## Subjective Effects

The hallucinogenic effects of Peyote are strong, with kaleidoscopic, richly colored visions. The other senses — hearing, feeling, taste — can also be affected. There are reportedly two stages in the intoxication. At first, a period of contentment and sensitivity occurs. The second phase brings great calm and muscular sluggishness, with a shift in attention from external stimuli to introspection and meditation. The experience typically lasts eight to twelve hours and is characterized by profound visual imagery, synesthesia, and deeply contemplative mental states.

Common effects include enhanced color perception, geometric visual patterns, altered time perception, and a sense of unity with nature. Physical effects include nausea (often prominent during onset), increased heart rate, pupillary dilation, and temperature dysregulation. The experience is generally considered gentler and more contemplative compared to other classical psychedelics.

## Cultural Significance: The Huichol

The Huichol Indians of the Sierra Madre Occidental in western Mexico maintain one of the most elaborate Peyote-based religious traditions in the world. Their annual pilgrimage to Wirikuta, the sacred land of Peyote in the high desert of San Luis Potosí, retraces the route of the ancestral gods and involves complex ritual preparations, offerings, and ceremonial procedures. The Huichol yarn paintings — like Aztec codices, chronicles of the creation of the world — vividly depict the symbols of their mythology, with the ceremonial use of Peyote as their basis.

The symbols include Kauyumari (Our Elder Brother Deer), Tatewari (first shaman and Spirit of Fire), and nierika (sacred portways or mirrors that unify the spirit of all things and all worlds). The Peyote hunt is a structured ritual involving deer hunting, arrow preparation, offerings at sacred sites, and ceremonial first ingestion. The Huichol consider the experience of Hikuri (Peyote) to be a form of divine communication essential to maintaining cosmic balance.

## Native American Church

Peyote use spread northward from Mexico into the United States and Canada during the nineteenth century, adopted by numerous Plains Indian tribes as the traditional way of life was disrupted by colonization, forced relocation, and the destruction of the bison herds. The Native American Church, formally incorporated in Oklahoma in 1918, incorporates Peyote as a sacrament in a syncretic religious framework that blends indigenous beliefs with Christian elements introduced by missionaries. The church's ceremonies typically involve all-night prayer meetings with Peyote consumption, drumming, singing, and meditation.

The ceremonial structure includes a fire, a crescent-shaped altar made of earth, and a staff. Participants consume Peyote buttons throughout the night, accompanied by prayer songs and the rattling of a gourd. The ceremony is led by a "roadman" who guides the participants through the experience. The crescent altar represents the moon and the cycle of life and death; the fire represents the sun and the power of creation; the drum represents the heartbeat of the earth. These symbolic elements create a structured ceremonial container for the Peyote experience that gives it meaning within a coherent cosmological framework.

Despite repeated legal challenges, the Native American Church has secured legal protection for its ceremonial Peyote use in both the United States and Canada. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 and subsequent amendments provide federal protections, while the 1994 amendment to the American Indian Religious Freedom Act specifically exempted the religious use of Peyote from the Controlled Substances Act for members of the Native American Church. Similar protections exist in Canadian law under the Constitution Act's guarantee of religious freedom. These legal protections represent a significant, though incomplete, recognition of indigenous religious rights in North America.

## Legal Status

Peyote is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance in the United States under the Controlled Substances Act. However, exemptions exist for members of the Native American Church who use Peyote in bona fide religious ceremonies. In Mexico, Peyote is also controlled but indigenous ceremonial use is generally respected. International trafficking of mescaline and Peyote is controlled under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances. Conservation concerns have led to increased regulation in Mexico, where overharvesting threatens wild populations. The state of San Luis Potosí has enacted specific protections for Peyote in the Wirikuta region, though enforcement remains challenging given the remote terrain and high demand from both ceremonial and recreational collectors.

In Canada, Peyote is regulated under the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act as a Schedule III substance, but exemptions exist for indigenous ceremonial use. Several European countries classify mescaline and Peyote-containing cacti as controlled substances, though enforcement regarding live plants varies. The legal status of Peyote in other countries depends on whether mescaline itself is scheduled; in many jurisdictions, unprocessed plant material occupies a legal gray area that may or may not be explicitly covered by analogue drug provisions.

## False Peyotes

Several other cactus species are known as "false Peyotes" and are used as substitutes or surrogates for true Peyote in various regions of Mexico. These include species of Coryphantha, Mammillaria, Ariocarpus, and other genera. The Tarahumara Indians of Mexico consider Coryphantha compacta (known as Wichuri or Bakana) a kind of Peyote or Hikuri. Ariocarpus retusus, called "tsuwiri" (false Peyote) by the Huichol, is deliberately avoided because it is believed to cause madness — an interesting example of ethnobotanical knowledge distinguishing between psychoactive and potentially toxic cactus species. See [[false-peyotes-mexican-cactus-ethnobotany]] for detailed coverage of these species.

The chemical profiles of false peyotes vary widely. Some contain mescaline in small quantities, while others produce entirely different alkaloid profiles. Lophophora diffusa, the closest relative of true Peyote, contains primarily pellotine (a tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloid with sedative properties) rather than mescaline, which may explain its different subjective effects. The diversity of psychoactive cactus species in Mexico reflects the long history of experimentation with cactus alkaloids by indigenous peoples, who developed sophisticated ethnopharmacological knowledge through centuries of careful observation and use. The [[cactus-alkaloids-reference]] provides comprehensive alkaloid data for these species.

## Mescaline Pharmacology

Mescaline is a classical psychedelic with primary activity as a 5-HT2A receptor agonist, the same receptor subtype through which LSD, psilocybin, and DMT exert their primary hallucinogenic effects. Mescaline's phenethylamine structure (a phenyl ring with an ethylamine side chain) distinguishes it from the tryptamine-based classical psychedelics, but the shared 5-HT2A mechanism produces overlapping subjective effects including visual hallucinations, altered time perception, and synesthesia. Mescaline also has affinity for 5-HT2C and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, which contribute to its physical effects including increased heart rate, blood pressure elevation, and pupillary dilation. The [[mescaline-compound-profile]] provides a complete pharmacological overview.

The typical ceremonial dose of mescaline is 200-500 mg, equivalent to approximately 5-15 dried Peyote buttons depending on alkaloid content. The onset of effects occurs within 30-90 minutes of ingestion, with peak effects at 2-4 hours and a total duration of 8-12 hours. Mescaline's relatively long duration compared to psilocybin (4-6 hours) contributes to the all-night ceremonial format characteristic of both Huichol and Native American Church practices. ## Conservation Status and Cultivation

Wild Peyote populations face increasing pressure from overharvesting, illegal collection, and habitat destruction. The Chihuahuan Desert populations in particular have been heavily impacted by both ceremonial harvesting and commercial poaching. Peyote is extremely slow-growing in the wild: plants may take 5-10 years to reach maturity from seed, and individual crowns (buttons) harvested for ceremonial use represent many years of growth. Conservation organizations have estimated that some wild populations have declined by 30-50% over recent decades, leading to calls for sustainable harvesting protocols and legal cultivation programs.

Cultivation of Lophophora williamsii from seed is legal in most jurisdictions where growing does not imply intent to consume. Seeds germinate readily on standard cactus soil mixes and plants grow faster under cultivation than in the wild, reaching flowering size in 3-5 years. Grafting seedlings onto faster-growing rootstock species such as Pereskiopsis or Trichocereus can dramatically accelerate growth, producing harvestable crowns in 1-2 years. The [[cactus-cultivation-seed-cutting]] page provides cultivation guidelines for Peyote and related species. Several organizations in the United States and Mexico now cultivate Peyote specifically for Native American Church use, reducing pressure on wild populations.

## Ethnobotanical Context: Peyote Among the Cora and Tarahumara

Beyond the well-documented Huichol and Native American Church traditions, Peyote use extends to several other indigenous groups in northern and central Mexico. The Cora people of Nayarit use Peyote in healing ceremonies that combine Catholic elements with pre-Columbian plant sacrament traditions. The Tarahumara (Rarámuri) of the Sierra Madre Occidental use both true Peyote (L. williamsii) and false peyotes (particularly Coryphantha compacta, which they call "hikuri" alongside true Peyote) in ceremonies for curing illness, divination, and hunting magic.

The Wixárika (Huichol) Peyote pilgrimage to Wirikuta is among the most complex and elaborate ceremonial cycles in the ethnobotanical literature. The journey of approximately 300 miles from the Huichol homeland in the Sierra Madre Occidental to the high desert of San Luis Potosí retraces the route of the ancestral gods and requires months of preparatory fasting, purification, and ritual obligations. Pilgrims hunt deer along the way, with the deer serving as a spiritual counterpart to Peyote — both are called "hikuri" and both are understood as manifestations of the same divine spirit. This complex of deer-peyote-corn symbolism forms one of the most sophisticated ethnobotanical cosmologies in the Americas.
