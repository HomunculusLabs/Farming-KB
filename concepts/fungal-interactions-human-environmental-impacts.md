## Fungal Interactions with Human Environmental Impacts

Human activities profoundly influence fungal diversity, community structure, [[fungal-biodiversity-and-ecosystem-function]]. As the world population has increased dramatically since the mid-20th century (Meadows et al., 1992; Silver and DeFries, 1990; Brown, 1997), the effects of human activities on ecosystems have escalated correspondingly. While public attention has focused on climate change, acid rain, atmospheric CO2 increase, stratospheric ozone depletion, and nuclear fallout, the impacts of these environmental changes on fungi — and the consequent effects on ecosystem processes mediated by fungi — have received comparatively little attention.

## Historical Context: The Industrial Revolution and Fungal Indicators

The relationship between human pollution and fungi has a long observational history. Following the industrial revolution in Europe, industrially related changes in ecosystems were readily apparent. The emergence of a melanic (black) form of the peppered moth (*Biston betularia*), documented by Kettlewell (1955), was attributed to its improved camouflage on soot-coated tree bark — a visible indicator of industrial pollution's biological consequences.

Lichens became one of the most widely used [[biological-indicators-of-soil]] [[lichen-bioindicators-atmospheric-pollution]]. Their dramatic decline in species abundance and diversity near industrial centers provided early warning of air quality deterioration. Only recently have lichen communities been shown to be recovering in species abundance and diversity as air quality has improved (Gilbert, 1992; Bates et al., 2001), validating their use as bioindicators and offering hope for ecosystem recovery.

## The Forest Dieback Phenomenon: Waldsterben

The identification of declining tree health in central European forests during the 1970s — the phenomenon known as *Waldsterben* (forest dieback) — marked a turning point in understanding how atmospheric pollution affects belowground fungal communities. The concurrent degradation of decorative limestone carvings on buildings and gravestones pointed to acid rain, consisting of sulfuric acid dissolved in rain droplets, as the primary culprit. This acid rain was partly a byproduct of the energy industry in Central Europe, where high-sulfur coal was the dominant fuel source.

The sulfur dioxide released during combustion combines with atmospheric water to form sulfuric acid, which damages tree canopies by affecting cuticular waxes and reducing photosynthetic capacity (Bervaes et al., 1988; Oren et al., 1988). However, the critical discovery for fungal ecology came when soil ecologists became involved in acid rain research in the late 1970s. Observations by Ulrich et al. (1979), Hütterman (1982, 1985), and Blaschke et al. (1985) revealed that acid rain was affecting both root growth and the mycorrhizal status of trees — a belowground dimension that had been entirely overlooked by plant physiologists studying aboveground damage.

Führer (1990) cautioned that European forest decline was exacerbated by multiple interacting "natural" stressors, including soil nutrient depletion, harvest management practices, and drought. This recognition that forest decline was multifactorial complicated research efforts but also set the stage for more realistic investigations of pollutant interactions.

## Evolution of the Pollutant Research Paradigm

The research paradigm evolved through several distinct phases:

1. **Acid rain phase**: Initial focus on sulfuric acid and its solubility products in soil, examining effects on roots and mycorrhizae.

2. **Sulfur dioxide fumigation phase**: Research shifted to studying the primary pollutant SO2 directly, using both laboratory and field fumigation experiments (McLeod, 1995). Plant physiologists studying aboveground effects led this work, with soil ecologists following.

3. **Ozone recognition phase**: Recognition that increasing tropospheric ozone concentration (distinct from stratospheric ozone loss caused by CFCs) could also damage cuticular waxes and photosynthetic capacity. This led to co-fumigation experiments combining ozone and sulfur dioxide.

4. **Nitrogen deposition phase**: NOx emissions from fossil fuel combustion were recognized as another major pollution source. Nitrogen deposition, fertilization effects, and forest soil nitrogen saturation emerged as the most recent and arguably most consequential line of investigation (Aber et al., 1989; Aber, 1992a,b; Tietema et al., 1993; McNulty and Aber, 1993; Reynolds et al., 1998; Gundersen et al., 1998).

## The Complexity of Multiple Pollutant Interactions

It is now recognized that no pollutant operates in isolation. The reality is that combinations of [[acidifying-pollutants-mycorrhizae]] — SO2, NOx, O3, and their chemical products — simultaneously influence ecosystem processes and fungal function. The challenge of disentangling the relative contribution of each pollutant is substantial, particularly because different pollutants may have contrasting effects: some damage plant structure while others reduce or increase the availability of soil nutrients.

The logistics of teasing apart these effects in field conditions are formidable. Controlled experiments can isolate individual pollutants, but may not capture the synergistic or antagonistic interactions that occur in real ecosystems. Field observations capture realism but cannot easily attribute observed changes to specific pollutants. This methodological tension has characterized the field throughout its development.

## Beyond Acidifying Pollutants

While acidifying pollutants (acid rain, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen deposition) have received the most research attention regarding fungal interactions, human activities generate other environmental changes that affect fungi:

- **Heavy metals**: Industrial activities release toxic metals into soils, affecting fungal growth and community composition through mechanisms analogous to those described for acid rain (which mobilizes naturally occurring metals).
- **Radionuclides**: Nuclear activities and fallout introduce radioactive elements into ecosystems, where fungi can accumulate and redistribute them.
- **Elevated atmospheric CO2**: Increasing CO2 concentrations alter plant physiology, carbon allocation patterns, and the quantity and quality of organic inputs to soil — all of which affect fungal communities.

## The Role of Fungi in Human-Impacted Ecosystems

Fungi occupy critical functional positions in all terrestrial ecosystems, and disruptions to fungal communities have cascading consequences:

- **Mycorrhizal fungi** mediate [[plant-nutrient-uptake]] and influence [[leake-mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration-plant-community-composition]], forest health, and succession.
- **Saprotrophic fungi** drive decomposition [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]], determining the rate at which organic matter is returned to available nutrient pools.
- **Pathogenic fungi** regulate plant populations and maintain diversity through density-dependent mortality.
- **Endophytic fungi** influence plant-herbivore interactions and plant stress tolerance.

Changes to any of these fungal functional groups — whether through direct toxicity of pollutants or indirect effects mediated through changes in host plant physiology or soil chemistry — can fundamentally alter ecosystem processes.

## The Conservation Imperative

The sensitivity of fungal communities to anthropogenic pollution has conservation implications. Arnolds (1989b, 1997) developed "red data" lists for the conservation of fungal species threatened by atmospheric pollution, particularly in The Netherlands. Recently, calls for inclusion of fungi and nonvascular plants in formal conservation species lists have been adopted in the United Kingdom (Watling, 1999). These actions reflect growing recognition that fungal species are being lost due to anthropogenic influences, even though fungi are rarely considered in mainstream conservation biology.

## Conclusions

The interactions between human-induced environmental changes and fungal communities represent a critical but underappreciated dimension of global change biology. The historical progression of research — from documenting lichen declines near industrial centers to understanding mechanistic effects of multiple pollutants on mycorrhizal communities — illustrates both the complexity of these interactions and the importance of fungi in mediating ecosystem responses to pollution. As human populations and industrial activities continue to expand, understanding and mitigating impacts on fungal communities will be essential for maintaining ecosystem function and services.
