# Chanterelle Species of the Eastern United States

Chanterelles are among the most prized edible wild mushrooms in the
world, valued for their fruity aroma, peppery flavor, and firm texture.
The eastern United States, from New England through the Appalachian
region and into the southeastern coastal plain, supports a diverse
assemblage of chanterelle species that fruit from late spring through
autumn.

## The True Chanterelles: Cantharellus

The genus chanterelle species eastern us contains the classic chanterelles with
decurrent ridges (false gills) that run down the stem, smooth waxy
caps, and apricot to golden yellow coloration. These are mycorrhizal
fungi that associate primarily with oaks and other hardwoods, though
some species partner with conifers.

### Cantharellus cibarius (Golden Chanterelle)

The golden chanterelle is the most widely known and commercially
significant species in the genus. It features a funnel-shaped cap that
is egg-yolk yellow to deep orange, with wavy, irregular margins when
mature. The "false gills" are shallow, blunt ridges that run down the
stem, distinguishing them from the blade-like true gills of
look-alikes. The flesh is firm and white to pale yellow, with a
characteristic fruity odor often described as apricot-like. In the
East, it is found primarily under oaks in mixed hardwood forests from
June through September, with peak fruiting triggered by warm, wet
weather following dry periods.

### Cantharellus lateritius (Smooth Chanterelle)

This eastern North American species is similar to C. cibarius but
typically larger and with a more orange to reddish-orange cap that has
a smoother surface and less pronounced irregularity at the margin.
The false gills are often less developed and may appear as mere
wrinkles on the stem. It is one of the most commonly encountered
chanterelles in the Appalachian region and is considered equally
edible. Some mycologists treat it as a color variant or subspecies of
C. cibarius, though molecular evidence supports its status as a
distinct species.

### Cantharellus cinnabarinus (Cinnabar Chanterelle)

A small, brilliantly red-orange species that is unmistakable once
encountered. The cap, stem, and false gills are all vivid cinnabar to
vermilion red. It fruits in summer and fall in mixed hardwood forests
and while small, it is considered an excellent edible with a flavor
comparable to its larger relatives. Its small size and striking color
make it easy to overlook in the leaf litter.

### Cantharellus appalachiensis

A recently described species from the Appalachian region, C.
appalachiensis is distinguished by its association with oak forests
at intermediate to high elevations. It is part of a complex of
similar species that have been historically lumped under C. cibarius
in North American field guides, highlighting the ongoing taxonomic
revision of the genus using molecular phylogenetics.

## The Trumpet Chanterelles: Craterellus

The genus craterellus contains species historically grouped with
Cantharellus but now recognized as a separate lineage. These species
tend to be more tubular or trumpet-shaped, often with hollow stems,
and generally lack the well-developed ridges characteristic of
Cantharellus.

### Craterellus fallax (Black Trumpet)

Also known as the horn of plenty or trumpet of death (a misnomer, as
it is excellent edible), the black trumpet is one of the most
distinctive and sought-after mushrooms in eastern North America. The
fruiting body is dark brown to black, funnel-shaped, with a thin,
fragile flesh and a hollow stem. It blends remarkably well with the
dark leaf litter of forest floors, making it notoriously difficult to
spot despite often growing in large colonies. It fruits from summer
through fall in mixed hardwood forests, particularly under oaks and
beech. Its rich, smoky, and earthy flavor makes it one of the finest
culinary mushrooms, and it dries exceptionally well, concentrating its
flavor.

### Craterellus tubaeformis (Yellowfoot)

This species features a yellowish to brownish cap, a thin, hollow,
yellowish stem, and well-developed false gills that are more prominent
than those of C. fallax. It fruits in late fall through early winter,
often in conifer-dominated forests or mixed woods, and can persist
through freezing temperatures. It is widely distributed in the
Appalachians and northward.

## Look-alikes and Safety

The primary dangerous look-alike for chanterelles in eastern North
America is the jack-o-lantern mushroom (Omphalotus olearius and O.
illudens), which can cause severe gastrointestinal distress. Jack-o-
lanterns typically grow in clusters on wood or buried wood, have true
gills (thin, blade-like, not forked), and are more orange. True
chanterelles grow individually from soil (not from wood), have blunt
ridges rather than true gills, and typically have a more yellow
coloration. The false gills of chanterelles fork and cross-vein, a
feature that distinguishes them from the parallel, non-forking true
gills of look-alikes.

Other potential confusion species include various hygrophorus
species, which have true gills but share similar coloration. However,
Hygrophorus species have waxy, easily separable gills and typically
have slimy cap surfaces.

## Culinary Uses

Chanterelles are versatile in the kitchen. They are excellent sauteed
in butter, pair well with cream sauces, eggs, pasta, and poultry, and
are commonly used in French and Scandinavian cuisine. Black trumpets
are particularly valued for making sauces, soups, and as a seasoning
dried and ground into powder. Chanterelles should not be eaten raw, as
they can cause mild gastrointestinal upset.
