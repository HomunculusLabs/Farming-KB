# Fungal Conservation Challenges

Fungi are among the most threatened yet least protected groups of organisms
on Earth. Despite their fundamental ecological roles as decomposers,
mutualists, and nutrient cyclers, fungi have been largely overlooked in
conservation policy and practice. The IUCN Red List includes fewer than 600
fungal species as of the mid-2020s, representing a minuscule fraction of
even described fungal diversity, let alone the estimated millions of
undescribed species.

## Why Fungi Are Overlooked

Several factors contribute to the marginalization of fungi in conservation.
Their ephemeral fruiting bodies make population monitoring difficult; a
species may be abundant below ground but invisible above ground for years.
Most fungal species have no common names, limiting public awareness and
engagement. Conservation legislation in most countries is framed around
animals and plants, with no explicit provision for fungi.

The taxonomic impediment — the gap between known and unknown species — is
especially severe for fungi. It is impossible to assess the conservation
status of a species that has not been described, and most fungal species
have not been. Even among described species, population data are available
for only a tiny fraction.

## Threats to Fungal Diversity

Habitat destruction is the single greatest threat to fungi. Deforestation,
urbanization, and agricultural conversion eliminate the substrates and host
plants that fungal communities depend on. Old-growth forests, which harbor
exceptionally diverse fungal communities including many rare and specialist
species, are particularly important and particularly threatened.

Air pollution, especially nitrogen deposition, alters soil chemistry and
favors competitive fungal species over sensitive specialists. Studies in
Europe have documented declines in ectomycorrhizal fungal diversity in
forests exposed to elevated nitrogen levels. Climate change shifts fungal
distributions and disrupts the phenology of fruiting, potentially
decoupling fungi from their plant partners.

Overharvesting of commercially valuable wild fungi, such as matsutake and
chanterelle species, can deplete local populations, though the impact of
harvesting on fungal populations is debated and may depend on harvest method
and intensity.

## Conservation Strategies

Habitat protection is the most effective strategy for fungal conservation.
Protecting forests, grasslands, and other ecosystems preserves entire fungal
communities along with their plant and animal associates. The concept of
"fungal refugia" — areas with high fungal diversity and low disturbance — is
gaining traction as a framework for prioritizing conservation effort.

Ex situ conservation of fungi through culture collections and cryopreservation
is possible but resource-intensive and cannot capture the ecological context
of natural communities. It serves primarily as a genetic backup for
commercially or scientifically important species rather than a comprehensive
conservation strategy.

Citizen science programs, including fungal forays and online platforms for
sharing observations (such as iNaturalist and mushroom observer websites),
generate valuable distribution data and raise public awareness. DNA
metabarcoding of soil samples from protected areas can establish baselines
for monitoring fungal community change over time.

## Policy and International Frameworks

The Convention on Biological Diversity has begun to acknowledge fungal
conservation, and several European countries have included selected fungi in
national red lists. The IUCN Fungal Conservation Committee advocates for
greater inclusion of fungi in conservation assessments and policy. However,
meaningful progress requires integrating fungal conservation into broader
ecosystem management frameworks rather than treating fungi as an afterthought.
