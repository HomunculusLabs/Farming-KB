# Magic Mushrooms of Australia and New Zealand — Species Guide

A guide to the known psychoactive (psilocybian) mushroom species of Australia and New Zealand, synthesized from John W. Allen's 1999 field guide. More than a dozen species containing psilocybine and/or psilocine have been identified across the region, distributed across multiple genera. Allen notes that four of these species are the most commonly used: *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]*, *P. subaeruginosa*, *Copelandia cyanescens*, and *P. semilanceata*.

## Major Psychoactive Genera

The hallucinogenic mushrooms of Australasia span several genera, each with distinct ecological niches and potency profiles:

- **Psilocybe** — the largest and most diverse group; includes the globally known *P. cubensis*, the liberty cap (*P. semilanceata*), and the potent *P. subaeruginosa*; species range from dung-inhabiting to wood-inhabiting to grassland varieties
- **Copelandia** — represented by *C. cyanescens* ("blue meanies"), a dung-inhabiting species noted for high potency and intense blue bruising reactions; found across tropical and subtropical regions
- **Panaeolus** — includes *[[panaeolus-subbalteatus]]* and related species found in or around dung and manured grasslands
- **Gymnopilus** — wood-inhabiting genus; *[[gymnopilus-purpuratus]]* and *G. junonius* are recorded from Australia, though their psychoactive status requires further confirmation in some cases

## Species Distribution by Region

### Australia — New South Wales

NSW has the richest diversity of known psychoactive species in Australia, reflecting its range of climates from subtropical coastal zones to temperate highlands. Recorded species include *[[psilocybe-aucklandii]]*, *[[psilocybe-australiana]]*, *[[psilocybe-eucalypta]]*, *P. semilanceata* (liberty cap), *P. subaeruginosa*, *P. tasmaniana*, *Panaeolus subbalteatus*, *Copelandia cyanescens* (blue meanies), and *Gymnopilus junonius*.

### Australia — Queensland

Queensland is the stronghold of *Psilocybe cubensis* (gold tops), the most popular recreational species in Australia. The warm, wet climate of the coastal zones — particularly the Gold Coast, Samford, Ferny Grove, and Beenleigh areas — supports prolific fruiting after heavy rains. *P. subcubensis*, *Copelandia cyanescens*, and *Gymnopilus junonius* are also found here. A 1980 government report noted that "gold top" mushrooms were plentiful in the Currumbin and Tallebudgera areas in the wet season.

### Australia — Victoria

Victoria supports *Psilocybe semilanceata*, *P. subaeruginosa*, *Panaeolus ater*, and *[[gymnopilus-purpuratus]]*. The cooler temperate climate favors the woodchip-inhabiting *P. subaeruginosa* and the grassland *P. semilanceata*.

### Australia — South Australia

SA records include *Psilocybe subaeruginosa*, *Copelandia cyanescens*, *Gymnopilus junonius*, and *G. purpuratus*. A small brown mushroom growing in the Adelaide Hills in July and August was noted in local press reports as providing "a potent hallucinogenic drug" to users.

### Australia — Western Australia, ACT, and Northern Territory

- WA: *Gymnopilus purpuratus*
- ACT: *Psilocybe subaeruginosa*
- NT: *Copelandia cyanescens* — found in the tropical climate; Captain B.C. Mundy of the Salvation Army in Darwin reported "gold tops" and "blue meanies" growing freely in animal manure and manured gardens. One case involved a resident of the Red Shield Hostel who picked "blue meanies" from a garden recently treated with fowl manure — reportedly the first record of *C. cyanescens* fruiting from poultry manure rather than ruminant dung.

### Tasmania

Tasmania supports *Psilocybe australiana*, *P. cubensis*, *P. semilanceata*, *P. subaeruginosa*, *P. tasmaniana*, and *Copelandia cyanescens*. By 1972, authorities became concerned that widespread collection would attract visitors from the Australian mainland. Dr. Malcolm Hall noted that "exportation of fungi from Tasmania to the mainland is highly likely."

### New Zealand — North Island

The North Island hosts *[[psilocybe-aucklandii]]* (endemic to the Auckland region), *P. australiana*, *P. eucalypta*, *P. subaeruginosa*, *P. tasmaniana*, and *Copelandia cyanescens*. *C. cyanescens* predominates in the New Plymouth region of intensive dairy farming, appearing in autumn under lupine bushes in coastal paddocks. *P. tasmaniana* is also found in the New Plymouth sand dunes, particularly at Khomeini beach, a popular surfing spot.

### New Zealand — South Island

The South Island hosts *[[psilocybe-makarorae]]* (endemic), *P. semilanceata* (the primary species consumed in the Dunedin/Otago area), and *P. subaeruginosa*. *Psilocybe novo zealandiae*, found primarily in native forests of the southern South Island, has been characterized by Gaston Guzman (1983) but is not psychoactive.

## Common Names and Local Epithets

Users in Australia and NZ rarely use scientific names, relying instead on a rich vocabulary of local epithets:

- **"Magic mushrooms"** — the universal term, coined by a *Life Magazine* editor in 1957 in connection with R. Gordon Wasson's article on the Mazatec mushroom cult
- **"Gold tops" / "Golden caps"** — *Psilocybe cubensis*; the name likely originated with a drug-using surfer group on the Gold Coast of Queensland
- **"Blue meanies"** — *Copelandia cyanescens*; refers both to the intense blue oxidation (bruising) indicating psilocine presence, and to the Beatles' *Yellow Submarine* film
- **"Blue legs" / "Dimple tops" / "Cone heads"** — additional names for *C. cyanescens*, shared with Hawaiian users via surfing connections
- **"Liberty cap"** / "Psilos" — *Psilocybe semilanceata*; common in Europe and the US but not widely adopted in NZ, where "magic mushrooms" is the preferred term

## Cattle as Dispersal Mechanism

Dung-inhabiting psychoactive species likely arrived in Australia after the introduction of cattle in 1788 (2 bulls and 5 cows from the Cape of Good Hope). By 1803, the government owned approximately 1,800 cattle imported from the Cape, Calcutta, and the American west coast. The South African dung beetle may have been the actual spore dispersal agent.

Australian mycologist John Burton Cleland (1934) stated that "fungi growing in cow or horse-dung and confined to such habitats, must in the case of Australia, all belong to introduced species." This dispersal mechanism applies to *P. cubensis*, *C. cyanescens*, *Panaeolus subbalteatus*, and several other species associated with ruminant dung. More than half of Australia's beef cattle are found in the coastal areas of Queensland and NSW, where the 500-750mm annual rainfall belt provides adequate conditions for fruiting after heavy rains.

English mycologist Roy Watling cautions that "fungi can change substrate preferences and there are [[coprophilous-fungi]] on kangaroo droppings etc." — a reminder that substrate associations are not immutable.

## Psychoactive Potency and Dosage

Wild-harvested *Psilocybe cubensis* is generally more potent than in-vitro cultivated specimens. Dosage levels vary considerably depending on species, growing conditions, age of specimen, and preparation method. Effects from mushrooms last 3-6 hours, compared to 8-12 hours for LSD, mescaline, or MDA — a shorter duration that some recreational users prefer. Woodchip species (*P. subaeruginosa*, *P. aucklandii*, *P. eucalypta*) have different potency profiles and dosage requirements than dung-inhabiting species.

## Methods of Ingestion

Mushrooms are consumed raw, cooked in soups or broths (boiled approximately 2 minutes), on toast, or brewed as tea. When eaten raw on a full stomach, onset can take up to 2 hours; in liquid form (soup or tea), effects begin within 5-10 minutes. Effects include tingling sensations, temperature fluctuations, visual hallucinations, altered perception of size and distance, and changes in mood ranging from euphoria to terror.
