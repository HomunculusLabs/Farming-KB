# Fukuoka: The One Straw Revolution -- Final Chapter

Source: [[one-straw-revolution-overview]], The One Straw Revolution, Book V, final chapter

## From One Straw a Revolution Could Begin

Among the young people who come to these mountain huts, there are those, poor in body and spirit, who have given up all hope. Fukuoka is only an old farmer who grieves that he cannot even provide them with a pair of sandals -- but there is still one thing he can give them.

One straw.

He picked up some straw from in front of the hut and said, "From just this one straw a revolution could begin."

"With the destruction of mankind at hand, you can still hope to cling to a straw?" one youth asked, with a touch of bitterness in his voice.

This straw appears small and light, and most people do not know how really weighty it is. If people knew the true value of this straw a human revolution could occur which would become powerful enough to move the country and the world.

The image of the single straw is both literal and metaphorical. Literally, it represents the [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-philosophy]] that is the foundation of Fukuoka's farming method. Metaphorically, it represents the principle that great transformations begin with the smallest, most humble actions -- a theme that runs through Eastern philosophy from the Tao Te Ching to the Heart Sutra.

## The Old Man of Inuyose Pass

When Fukuoka was a child, there was a man who lived near Inuyose Pass. All he seemed to do was to pack charcoal on horseback two miles or so along the road from the top of the mountain to the port of Gunchu. And yet, he became rich. On his trip homeward from the port he gathered the discarded straw horseshoes and the manure by the side of the road and put them onto his field. His motto was: "Treat one strand of straw as important and never take a useless step." It made him a wealthy man.

This story encapsulates a principle that Fukuoka returns to again and again: the value of what others discard. Modern agriculture burns or ploughs under the straw that is its most valuable resource. Modern society discards the traditional wisdom that is its most precious inheritance. The man of Inuyose Pass understood what modern people have forgotten: that wealth is not created by acquisition but by attentive stewardship of what is already at hand.

## The Discovery of Straw Mulch

It has been nearly forty years since Fukuoka realized how important straw could be in growing rice and barley. At that time, passing an old rice field in Kochi Prefecture which had been left unused and uncultivated for many years, he saw healthy young rice sprouting up through a tangle of weeds and straw which had accumulated on the field's surface.

After working on the implications of that for many years, he came out advocating a completely new method of rice and barley growing. Believing that this was a natural and revolutionary way of farming, he wrote about it in books and magazines, and spoke of it on television and radio dozens of times.

The [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-philosophy]] is the pivotal moment in Fukuoka's farming career. It confirmed his intuition that nature already knows how to grow food, and that the farmer's role is to observe and cooperate rather than to command and control. The rice that sprouted through the accumulated straw was not an anomaly but a revelation -- proof that when human interference ceases, the natural processes of growth and regeneration assert themselves.

## Why Farmers Resist Straw Mulch

It seems a very simple thing, but farmers are so set in their thinking about how straw should be used, that it is unlikely that they will accept change easily. Spreading fresh straw on a field can be risky because rice blast and stem rot are diseases always present in rice straw. In the past, these diseases have caused great damage, and this is one of the main reasons that farmers have always turned the straw into compost before putting it back onto the field.

Stem borers also enter the straw to pass the winter. To prevent an infestation of these insects, farmers used to compost the straw carefully all winter long. That is why Japanese farmers have always kept their fields so neat and tidy. The practical knowledge of everyday life was that if farmers left straw lying around, they would be punished by heaven for their negligence.

The resistance of farmers to the straw mulch method illustrates a principle that extends far beyond agriculture: the power of conventional wisdom to prevent innovation. Farmers had good reasons for their practices -- rice blast, stem rot, stem borers -- but these reasons were based on an incomplete understanding of the ecological relationships at work. Fukuoka's insight was that spreading straw six months before seeding allows natural decomposition processes to neutralize the pathogens, making composting unnecessary.

## Vindication After Years

After years of experimentation, even technical experts have now confirmed Fukuoka's theory that spreading fresh straw on the field six months before seeding is completely safe. This overturned all previous ideas on the subject. But it is going to be a long while before the farmers become receptive to using straw in this manner.

The gap between scientific validation and practical adoption is a recurring theme in Fukuoka's work. Even when the experts agree that his methods work, the habits and assumptions of generations of farmers stand in the way of change. This is why Fukuoka often expressed more hope in the young people who came to his farm with no preconceptions than in the established agricultural institutions.

## The Barren Fields of Japan

In travelling up to Tokyo, looking out the window of the Tokaido train, Fukuoka saw the transformation of the Japanese countryside. The former landscape of neat fields of green barley, Chinese milk vetch, and blooming rape plants is nowhere to be seen. Instead, half-burned straw is piled roughly in heaps and left soaking in the rain.

That this straw is being neglected is proof of the disorder of modern farming. The barrenness of these fields reveals the barrenness of the farmer's spirit.

The contrast between the living countryside of Fukuoka's youth and the barren landscape of modern Japan serves as a powerful metaphor for the [[fukuoka-breakdown-japanese-agriculture]]. The neglect of straw -- once the farmer's most precious resource -- symbolizes the broader disconnection from nature that characterizes modern industrial society.

## The One-Straw Revolution

Seeing the barren fields of wintertime Japan, Fukuoka could remain patient no longer. "With this straw, I, by myself, will begin a revolution!"

The youths who had been listening silently were now roaring with laughter. "A one-man revolution! Tomorrow let us get a big sack of barley, rice, and clover seed and take off, carrying it on our shoulders, like Okuninushi-no-Mikoto and broadcast seeds all over the fields of Tokaido."

"That is not a one-man revolution," he laughed. "It is a one-straw revolution!"

Stepping out of the hut into the afternoon sunlight, he paused for a moment and gazed at the surrounding orchard trees laden with ripening fruit, and at the chickens scratching in the weeds and clover. He then began his familiar descent to the fields.

This closing scene -- one of the most memorable in all of Fukuoka's writing -- brings together the themes of the entire book: the straw, the revolution, the young people, the orchard, the descent to the fields. It is both an ending and a beginning, suggesting that the revolution is not an event but a daily practice, not a political program but a way of life rooted in the soil.

## Philosophical Significance

The final chapter of The One Straw Revolution is a meditation on the relationship between the small and the great, the humble and the powerful. Fukuoka's insistence that a revolution can begin with a single straw is not merely inspirational rhetoric; it is a statement about the nature of transformation itself. Real change does not come from grand plans or massive interventions but from the accumulation of small, right actions performed in harmony with nature.

This principle applies not only to farming but to every domain of human activity. The [[fukuoka-the-one-straw-revolution-final-chapter]] is that the solution to seemingly overwhelming problems -- environmental destruction, food scarcity, cultural decline -- begins with the simplest possible action: picking up a piece of straw and returning it to the earth.

## Connection to Natural Farming Principles

The straw that Fukuoka holds up as the symbol of revolution is not incidental to his farming method -- it is central. The [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-system]] is the practical foundation upon which all of natural farming rests. Straw protects the soil from erosion, retains moisture, suppresses weeds, provides habitat for beneficial microorganisms, and slowly releases nutrients as it decomposes. It is, in Fukuoka's words, both the starting point and the endpoint of the natural farming cycle.

The one-straw revolution is therefore not a metaphor for natural farming but a literal description of it. By spreading straw on the field, the farmer initiates a self-reinforcing cycle of soil improvement, plant health, and ecological balance. No other input is needed -- no fertilizer, no pesticide, no tillage. The straw is sufficient unto itself, just as nature is sufficient unto itself when human beings stop interfering.
