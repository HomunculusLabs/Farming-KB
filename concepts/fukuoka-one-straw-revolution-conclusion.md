## Fukuoka: The One Straw Revolution -- Final Chapter

Source: [[fukuoka-western-eastern-philosophy-agriculture]] from the [[fukuoka-breakdown-japanese-agriculture]]. The neglect of straw -- once the farmer's most precious resource -- symbolizes the broader disconnection from nature that characterizes modern industrial society.

## The One-Straw Revolution

Seeing the barren fields of wintertime Japan, Fukuoka could remain patient no longer. "With this straw, I, by myself, will begin a revolution!"

The youths who had been listening silently were now roaring with laughter. "A one-man revolution! Tomorrow let us get a big sack of barley, rice, and clover seed and take off, carrying it on our shoulders, like Okuninushi-no-Mikoto and broadcast seeds all over the fields of Tokaido."

"That is not a one-man revolution," he laughed. "It is a one-straw revolution!"

Stepping out of the hut into the afternoon sunlight, he paused for a moment and gazed at the surrounding orchard trees laden with ripening fruit, and at the chickens scratching in the weeds and clover. He then began his familiar descent to the fields.

This closing scene -- one of the most memorable in all of Fukuoka's writing -- brings together the themes of the entire book: the straw, the revolution, the young people, the orchard, the descent to the fields. It is both an ending and a beginning, suggesting that the revolution is not an event but a daily practice, not a political program but a way of life rooted in the soil.

## Philosophical Significance

The final chapter of The One Straw Revolution is a meditation on the relationship between the small and the great, the humble and the powerful. Fukuoka's insistence that a revolution can begin with a single straw is not merely inspirational rhetoric; it is a statement about the nature of transformation itself. Real change does not come from grand plans or massive interventions but from the accumulation of small, right actions performed in harmony with nature.

This principle applies not only to farming but to every domain of human activity. The [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] rests. Straw protects the soil from erosion, retains moisture, suppresses weeds, provides [[fukuoka-ecological-balance-principles]]. No other input is needed -- no fertilizer, no pesticide, no tillage. The straw is sufficient unto itself, just as nature is sufficient unto itself when human beings stop interfering.
