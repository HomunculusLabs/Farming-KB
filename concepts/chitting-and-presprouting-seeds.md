## Chitting and Presprouting Seeds

Chitting is the practice of presprouting seeds before planting them — initiating the germination process under ideal conditions, then placing the sprouted seed into the garden. This technique eliminates many germination failures, reveals dead seed before you waste garden space on it, and can shave days off the time to emergence. It is especially valuable for large-seeded crops planted early in soil that is not yet quite warm enough.

## How Chitting Works

When a seed germinates, the embryo first puts down a root to secure a moisture supply, and only then makes a shoot heading for light. Seeds usually need to be kept moist only until they begin putting down their first root. The time required depends on the species, the vigor of the seed batch, [[soil-temperature]], and weather.

Chitting initiates sprouting under nearly ideal conditions on your kitchen counter. After the root emerges but before it gets long (the root is delicate and snaps off easily), you gently place the sprouting seed in its drill or hill and cover it. Unless the soil is extremely dry, the seedling will be immune to drying out because its root is already working.

## The Advantage of Knowing Germination

One enormous advantage of chitting is that you can observe which seeds are viable and which are dead before planting. If you chit 50 seeds and only 10 of them sprouted weakly, you know the germination conditions you provided are fine — the seed is poor stuff. You can then obtain better seed and try again, rather than losing valuable growing time waiting for seeds that will never emerge.

## Chitting Large Seeds: Beans and Peas

### Procedure

1. **Soak**: Place the number of seeds you"ll need (three seeds for every plant you'll ultimately want) in tepid water for no more than six to eight hours
2. **Drain and rinse**: Drain the water, rinse with tepid water, and drain thoroughly
3. **Store**: Place the jar on its side near the kitchen sink. Temperature should exceed 65°F (18°C)
4. **Rinse daily**: Twice a day, gently rinse with tepid water and drain
5. **Wait**: In three to four days, roots should be visible. Plant immediately before the rapidly developing (and brittle) roots get longer than the seed itself

### Planting

In each spot where you want a plant to grow (the British call this a "station"):
- Place at least two sprouting seeds about 1.5 inches (4 centimeters) deep
- Orient the root pointing down if possible
- Cover with loose soil

### When to Use

- Early spring planting when soil is not yet warm enough
- Any time you want insurance against poor germination
- When using seed of uncertain quality or age

## Chitting Cucurbits: Melons, Cucumbers, Squash

### Why Cucurbits Need Special Treatment

Melons and cucumbers are heat lovers — cucumbers need at least 70°F (21°C) and do best over 75°F (24°C). Squash will sprout at 65°F (18°C) but prefers at least 70°F. To hold chitting temperatures this high, you may need a germination box with bottom heat.

### Procedure

1. Use a germination box as described in the seedling-raising section, with bottom heat
2. An electric hot pad set on low can work, or a heating cable in a shallow tray of sand
3. Cut paper towelling into quarters; dip into tepid water and squeeze out most of the surplus
4. Place four seeds for every plant you'll want on the moist paper
5. Place the towelling in a small container or sealed plastic bag
6. After a few days, begin checking twice daily
7. Plant immediately when roots appear but before they exceed the seed's own length

### Planting Out

- Scoop out a hole about 1.5 inches deep in loose, damp soil
- Place the seeds so the root will be pointed down
- Cover with loose soil
- **Do not water** until the seedlings emerge — you don't want the seeds to die from cold and/or powdery mildew, and you don't even want to see rain before emergence

## Chitting Small Seeds

Small seeds like carrots, lettuce, and radishes present a special challenge because they are difficult to handle individually once sprouted. The solution is the **gel seeding method**.

### The Gel Method

1. Soak the small seeds in a jar of water for a few hours (do not submerge longer or you may suffocate them)
2. Meanwhile, make a gel: heat one pint (500 mL) of water to boiling, dissolve two to three heaping tablespoons (30-45 mL) of cornstarch in it
3. Let the gel cool in the refrigerator until it just sets up — don't let it get cold, only cool enough to set
4. Gently stir the sprouting seeds into the gel
5. Cut a small hole in the corner of a plastic bag and squeeze the seed-gel mixture into furrows as you walk down the row
6. Squeeze a bit of plain gel without seeds at the end to close the hole

### Benefits of Gel Seeding

- You need far less seed per length of row than dry sowing
- Much higher percentage of emergence
- Emergence happens far more quickly
- The gel keeps the seeds moist during the critical sprouting period

### Mixing with Compost Alternative

For simpler small-seed sowing without gel:
- Put a quarter to a half teaspoon of seed into a quart or two of fine composty soil on the dryish side
- Thoroughly blend the seed and compost
- Distribute the mixture down the furrow

This gives much better spacing than raw seed and reduces thinning.

## When Chitting Is Not Worth the Effort

- When seed is known to be fresh and vigorous
- When soil temperature and moisture are ideal
- For species with very small seeds that germinate readily (e.g., some herbs)
- When you have more garden space than seed supply
