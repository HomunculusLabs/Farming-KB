# Taro Wet Terrace Culture

Taro ([[colocasia-esculenta]]) is one of the most productive and flexible staple food crops for tropical and subtropical aquacultural systems. This herbaceous perennial, growing to 1 m high with large arrow-like leaves and a swollen stem base or tuber reaching up to 10 kg, has been cultivated for millennia across Polynesia, Southeast Asia, and Hawaii, where it served as the foundation of traditional food systems.

## Hawaiian Taro Heritage

Hawaii once maintained some 1,100 varieties of taro, with perhaps 100 or so still preserved today. Taro is a staple food in Hawaii and parts of Polynesia and Southeast Asia, where rice or breadfruit are also staples. The wet terrace cultures of Hawaii represent one of the most sophisticated traditional aquacultural systems ever developed.

## Varieties and Uses

Different taro varieties are selected for specific purposes:

- **Boiling varieties** — table taro with specific texture characteristics
- **Baking varieties** — developed for earth oven (imu) preparation
- **Poi varieties** — fermented to produce poi, the traditional Hawaiian staple food
- **Luau taro** — grown specifically for cooking as green leaf spinach

All taro must be cooked before consumption due to stinging crystals of oxalic acid in the leaves and tubers. The oxalic acid spicules also serve a beneficial function in the field, protecting the crop from browsing animals.

## Propagation

Taro is propagated by small side tubers or more commonly by a cutting consisting of:

1. A crown disc about 1 cm thick
2. The lower 18 cm of stalk from the old tuber at harvest
3. Leaves removed by a slant cut just above the first leaf node

This simple vegetative propagation makes taro accessible to smallholder farmers without need for seed production systems.

## Growing Period and Yields

The growing period is 8–15 months, with no true "off season" in the subtropics. Tubers are normally marketed at about 1 kg each. On dryland under irrigation:

- Beds are 1.3 m wide at 30 beds/ha
- Taro is spaced at 40 × 60 cm in diamond pattern
- Planting density: 55,500 bulbs/ha
- Average yield: 55 t/ha (about 1 kg per bulb or slightly more)
- [[drip-irrigation]] at 120 lines/ha (4 per bed) is also used

## Traditional Fish Integration

There has always been a fish culture tradition with taro, incorporating local prawns, shellfish, and freshwater fish. Modern introductions of [[tilapia]] and introduced prawns have extended the potential, as have crayfish (Homarus spp.). What is identified as missing is a good variety of edible freshwater mussels to fix phosphates in the terrace mud.

### Nutrient Supply

In older Hawaiian systems, nutrient supply came from forest leaves (chiefly Aleurites or Hibiscus). The stems of kukui trees (Aleurites) were stood to rot in the upper terraces where they produced a local edible ear fungus. About 80% of the bulk of a log was converted to fungus, and the remainder broken up as water mulch—a remarkable example of biological conversion efficiency.

## Wet Terrace Design

At maturity, taro can be kept flooded with 0.5 m of free water above the softer muds of the terrace. This deep water phase allows prawns and grass carp (which coexist very well) to be stocked alongside the taro. The system progresses through stages:

1. **Planting** in wet mud or shallow water
2. **Growing phase** with minimal flooding
3. **Mature phase** with deeper flooding for fish and prawn integration

Spacing in wet terrace culture is similar to dryland, but the profitable "intercrop" shifts from vegetables to prawns. Kangkong and watercress are spot-planted in many taro terraces for greens, and both are planted as low mound crops in special shallow terrace or canal systems with faster flow than taro systems.

## Accessory Margin Crops

Traditional and potential margin crops for taro systems include:

- **Ti** (Cordyline) — the Polynesian "wax paper" and wrapper for baked foods
- **Papaya, banana, coconut, and sugar cane** as fruit and structural crops
- **[[dolichos]] hosei or [[phyla-nodosa]]** on bunds as nitrogenous mulch sources, trimmed at water level by Tilapia or grass carp
- **Vine crops** — taro appreciates partial shade, creating potential for kiwifruit, passionfruit, pole bean, or cucurbit crops on margins or wide-spaced overhead

## Livestock Integration

Pigs and ducks are traditional livestock in taro systems. Construction of their pens over feeder canals or the upper terraces would add substantially to nutrient supply. Ducks need confinement to a few ponds as they also appreciate the edible Chinese snails, which are a high-priced local delicacy.

## Eel and Regional Adaptation

In other climates—particularly mild coasts of the Atlantic and western Pacific (New Zealand)—eel rearing would combine very well with taro culture. The wet, warm environment of taro terraces provides ideal habitat for eel growth while the taro benefits from nutrient cycling through eel waste.

## Dryland Taro Culture

Taro also performs well on dryland areas under irrigation, responding very well to thick mulch and green crop. In these situations, intercrop of Brassica, melons, ginger, or lettuce is viable, demonstrating the crop's flexibility across different production systems.

## Polycultural Vision

Every tropical garden deserves a patch of taro. It is often grown as a mulched patch or groundcover below fruit trees in higher rainfall areas, or as a subsidiary crop in banana, guava, avocado, and macadamia nut orchards. The ideal polyculture combines taro with comfrey, papaya, guava, and banana, complemented by chilis, ginger, peppers, ducks, pigs, and fish—with visits to the sea for limu (seaweed) and crab, all cooked in a traditional earth oven (imu).

## Design Considerations

For designers integrating taro into aquacultural systems:

1. **Plan for water level management** across growth stages
2. **Include nitrogen-fixing bund plants** to reduce external fertilization
3. **Design for multi-stage flooding** to enable fish/prawn integration at maturity
4. **Maximize edge diversity** with fruit trees and vine crops on margins
5. **Integrate livestock housing** over feeder canals for nutrient cycling
6. **Consider phosphate-fixing mussels** as an underutilized component
7. **Maintain variety diversity** for resilience and culinary flexibility
