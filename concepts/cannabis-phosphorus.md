# Cannabis Phosphorus (P)

## Overview

Phosphorus is a primary [[cannabis-indoor-soil-mix-and-preparation]] classified as **mobile** in cannabis plants. It is necessary for photosynthesis and provides a mechanism for the energy to transfer within the plant. Phosphorus is one of the components of DNA, many enzymes, and proteins, and is associated with overall vigor, resin production, and seed production. Cannabis uses the highest levels of phosphorus during germination, seedling growth, cloning, and flowering.

## Role in Plant Biology

Phosphorus plays a central role in energy transfer and genetic coding within cannabis plants. It is a structural component of ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the primary energy currency of all living cells, and is a key building block of nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). The highest concentrations of phosphorus are found in root-growing tips, growing shoots, and vascular tissue. Phosphorus is essential for:

- Photosynthesis and energy transfer within the plant
- DNA synthesis and enzyme production
- Overall plant vigor and resin production
- Seed production and flower development
- Root growth during germination and cloning
- Cell division and membrane integrity

"Super Bloom" fertilizers, designed for the flowering stage, have elevated phosphorus levels. During germination and seedling growth, intake of phosphorus is high. The vegetative growth stage also requires phosphorus in substantial levels alongside nitrogen and potassium.

## Mobility in Soil

Phosphorus is classified as a **mobile** nutrient within the plant -- it can be translocated from older foliage to newer growth. However, in the soil, phosphorus is notoriously immobile and easily becomes "fixed" (chemically bound). Phosphorus does not move readily through the soil profile and tends to stay near where it was applied. In clay, acidic, or soggy soils, phosphorus availability drops significantly because it binds with iron, aluminum, and calcium compounds. This soil fixation is why phosphorus is often mixed into the growing medium before planting rather than applied as a top dressing.

## Interactions with Other Nutrients

Excessive phosphorus interferes with [[cannabis-calcium-nutrient]], [[cannabis-copper-nutrient]], [[query-how-to-identify-and-fix-cannabis-iron-deficiency]], [[cannabis-magnesium-nutrient]], and [[query-how-to-fix-zinc-deficiency-in-cannabis]] stability and uptake. Zinc is the most common secondary deficiency caused by phosphorus excess. Conversely, [[query-how-to-fix-zinc-deficiency-in-cannabis]] is also necessary for proper utilization of phosphorus, creating a delicate balance. High levels of phosphorus compete with iron uptake. The interaction between phosphorus and micronutrients is one of the most common sources of secondary nutrient problems, especially when growers over-apply "super bloom" fertilizers.

## Organic Sources

[[organic-fertilizer-sources-guide]] of phosphorus include:

- Bat guano (high-phosphorus varieties)
- Steamed bone meal
- Natural rock phosphates
- Barnyard manure
- Fish bone meal
- Seabird guano

Always use finely ground organic components that are readily available to the plants. Rock phosphate breaks down very slowly and is best mixed into the soil well before planting. Bone meal releases phosphorus over a period of months.

## Deficiency

Phosphorus deficiencies are somewhat common and are often misdiagnosed. They are most common when the growing medium pH is above 7 (phosphorus cannot be absorbed properly), when the soil is acidic (below 5.8) with excess iron and zinc, or when the soil has become fixated (chemically bound) with phosphates.

Deficiencies are aggravated by clay, acidic, and soggy soils. [[query-how-to-fix-zinc-deficiency-in-cannabis]] is also necessary for proper utilization of phosphorus.

### Symptoms

- Stunted growth with smaller leaves
- Leaves turn bluish-green with dark blotches
- Stems, leaf stems (petioles), and main veins turn reddish-purple, starting on the leaf underside
- Note: The reddening of stems and veins is not always well-pronounced
- Leaf tips of older leaves turn dark and curl downward
- Severely affected leaves develop large purplish-black necrotic (dead) blotches
- Leaves become bronzish-purple, dry, shrivel, contort, and drop off
- Flowering is often delayed; buds are uniformly smaller
- Seed yield is poor
- Plants become very vulnerable to fungal and insect attack

### Deficiency Progression Timeline

1. Stunted and very slow-growth plants
2. Dark bluish-green leaves, often with dark blotches
3. Plants are smaller overall
4. When blotches overcome the leaf stem, leaf turns bronzish-purple, contorts, and drops

### Treatment

Lower the pH to 5.5-6.2 in hydroponic units, 6.0-7.0 for clay soils, or 5.5-6.5 for potting soils so phosphorus becomes available. If the soil is too acidic with excess iron and zinc, phosphorus becomes unavailable.

Mix a complete fertilizer containing phosphorus into the growing medium before planting. Fertigate with an inorganic, complete hydroponic fertilizer containing phosphorus.

## Toxicity

Toxic signs of phosphorus may take several weeks to surface, especially if excesses are buffered by a stable pH. Cannabis uses a lot of phosphorus throughout its life cycle, and many varieties tolerate high levels.

### Symptoms

Excessive phosphorus interferes with [[cannabis-calcium-nutrient]], [[cannabis-copper-nutrient]], [[query-how-to-identify-and-fix-cannabis-iron-deficiency]], [[cannabis-magnesium-nutrient]], and [[query-how-to-fix-zinc-deficiency-in-cannabis]] stability and uptake. Toxic symptoms manifest as a deficiency of these other nutrients, with zinc being the most common secondary deficiency.

### Treatment

Flush the growing medium with a very mild, complete fertilizer. Flush a minimum of three times the volume of water for the volume of the growing medium.

## Growth Stage Considerations

Phosphorus intake is highest during germination, seedling, and cloning stages. During flowering, use a "super bloom" fertilizer with elevated phosphorus levels to promote fat, heavy, dense buds. Cannabis needs phosphorus in substantial levels throughout vegetative growth as well.

## Common Mistakes

- Over-applying phosphorus during flowering, which causes zinc and other micronutrient lockout
- Relying solely on pH adjustment without checking for soil fixation of phosphorus
- Using rock phosphate too close to planting time (it breaks down slowly)
- Confusing phosphorus deficiency reddening with natural purple stem coloration in some cultivars
- Applying phosphorus to soil with pH above 7 without first correcting the pH
- Failing to flush excess phosphorus before it causes cascading micronutrient deficiencies
