# Fukuoka Natural Farming Weed and Insect Philosophy

[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s approach to weeds and insects is one of the most distinctive
aspects of his [[natural-farming-philosophy]]. Rather than treating weeds as enemies
to be eradicated or insects as pests to be destroyed, he views them as integral
components of a balanced biological community whose presence reflects the health
of the larger ecosystem.

## Weeds: Allies, Not Enemies

Fukuoka's fundamental principle on weeds is that they "play their part in
building soil fertility and in balancing the biological community." Weeds should
be controlled, not eliminated. This represents a radical departure from both
conventional agriculture (which uses herbicides) and traditional organic farming
(which relies on intensive tillage for weed control).

### Why Cultivation Creates Weed Problems

The standard approach of controlling weeds through cultivation is, in Fukuoka's
analysis, self-defeating. When soil is turned, seeds that have lain dormant deep
below the surface -- seeds that would never have germinated otherwise -- are
brought to light and given conditions to sprout. Cultivation also favors the
quick-sprouting, fast-growing weed species that are most troublesome to farmers.

Fukuoka observed that "as soon as cultivation is discontinued, the number of
weeds decreases sharply" and the varieties of weeds in a field change, typically
shifting toward a less competitive mix.

### Practical Weed Management Without Herbicides

Fukuoka's weed management relies on three complementary strategies:

1. **Strategic timing**: Seeds are sown while the preceding crop is still
   ripening in the field. Winter weeds sprout only after rice harvest, but by
   then the winter grain already has a head start. Summer weeds sprout after
   barley/rye harvest, but the rice is already growing strongly. Having no
   interval between succeeding crops gives the grain a great advantage over the
   weeds

2. **Straw mulch**: After harvest, the entire field is covered with uncut rice
   or barley straw. This stops weed germination short and, as it decomposes,
   adds organic matter to the soil. Fukuoka considered straw "connected with
   everything -- with fertility, with germination, with weeds, with keeping
   away sparrows, with [[water-management]]"

3. **Living ground cover**: White clover sown with the grain acts as a living
   mulch that enriches the soil, keeps the ground moist and aerated, and can
   smother even strong weeds like mugwort and crabgrass when established

### Farming Among the Weeds

Fukuoka's fields visibly contain many kinds of weeds growing alongside the grain
and clover. This appearance alarmed visiting agricultural experts, but the
results spoke for themselves: barley and rice grew vigorously, yielding about 22
bushels (1,300 pounds) per quarter acre -- matching the top yields in Ehime
Prefecture.

Twenty years of promoting permanent ground cover in fruit orchards transformed
Japanese practice: orchards covered with grasses became common, and bare orchards
became rare. Fukuoka demonstrated that fruit trees, grain crops, and vegetables
can all grow successfully among weeds and grasses.

## Insects and Disease: A Question of Balance

### Nature Is in Perfect Balance

Fukuoka's core insight on insects is: "Nature, left alone, is in perfect
balance. Harmful insects and plant diseases are always present, but do not occur
in nature to an extent which requires the use of poisonous chemicals." Disease
and insect imbalance became problems only after unnatural practices like ploughing
and fertilizing produced weak plants.

### The Stem Borer Experiment

While at the Kochi Testing Station, Fukuoka conducted experiments on stem
borers, which enter rice stalks and cause them to wither. In a field sprayed
with insecticide, white stalks (damaged plants) were reduced -- but the
unsprayed field produced the higher yield. Investigation revealed that the stem
borers' attack on weaker plants produced a beneficial thinning effect: remaining
plants received more sunlight, grew stronger, sent up more grain-bearing stalks,
and produced more grains per head. When insect damage did not thin the excess,
plants appeared healthy but harvests were actually lower.

### Natural Checks on Insect Populations

A professor investigating why Fukuoka's fields had so few rice leafhoppers
discovered a complex natural balance: while harmful insects were present, their
natural enemies (spiders, frogs, lizards, and predatory insects) kept them in
check. Fukuoka used Japanese red pine blight to illustrate the limits of
scientific intervention: nematodes breed within the trunk, blocking water and
nutrient transport, but nematodes feed on a fungus accompanied by a microbe and
a virus toxic to the fungus. "Effect following effect in every direction" --
yet people apply chemical remedies without understanding the true cause.

Fukuoka warned: "Using agricultural chemicals is the most inept way to deal
with problems such as these, and will only lead to greater problems in the
future."

### Protecting Natural Predators

The most important principle of insect management is protecting natural predator
populations. When the insecticide Fusol was used in Japanese orchards, natural
predators of ruby scale and horned wax scale were completely exterminated,
creating problems that persisted for decades. For mites and scales that do
appear, Fukuoka recommended a diluted machine oil spray (relatively harmless
to predators) applied lightly in midsummer, then leaving insect communities to
achieve their natural balance. Spiders are especially important and sensitive:
"Spiders are sensitive to even the slightest human tampering and care must
always be taken on this account."

Fukuoka planted Morishima acacia (an Australian nitrogen-fixing tree) in his
orchards to create a beneficial insect cascade. Aphids feeding on the acacia's
buds multiplied, attracting ladybirds. When the aphids were consumed, the
ladybirds climbed down to the citrus trees and began feeding on mites, arrowhead
scales, and cottony-cushion scales -- providing natural pest control across
multiple tree species. Fukuoka estimated that abandoning insecticides would cost
about 5% yield loss initially, and abandoning chemical fertilizer another 5%,
for roughly 10% total. However, "the recuperative power of nature is great
beyond imagining and after this initial loss, harvests would increase and
eventually surpass their original level."

### Growing Sturdy Crops

The ultimate insect and disease strategy is growing strong plants in a healthy
environment. Fukuoka found that non-flooded rice with 60-80% soil moisture
develops stronger roots and is "extremely resistant to attacks by disease and
insects." When he stopped using weak "improved" seed varieties, reduced nitrogen
input, and decreased irrigation water, the three great rice diseases (stem rot,
rice blast, and bacterial leaf blight) nearly disappeared. He extended this
philosophy to vegetable growing, scattering seeds among natural vegetation on
mountainsides; when various herbs and vegetables are mixed and grown among
natural vegetation, "damage by insects and diseases will be minimal and there
will be no need to use sprays or to pick bugs off by hand."
