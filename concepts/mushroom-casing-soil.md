## Mushroom Casing Soil

Casing soil is a non-nutritive (or low-nutrition) layer applied on top of
fully colonized substrate to create the microclimate necessary for mushroom
fruiting. The casing layer serves as a water reservoir, maintains high
humidity at the substrate surface, and provides the physical and chemical
cues that trigger the transition from vegetative mycelial growth to
reproductive fruit body formation. Proper casing preparation and management
is one of the most important skills [[chen-maitake-primordia-initiation-fruiting]])

4. **Physical support**: Provides a surface for pin formation and structural

5. **Gas exchange regulation**: The casing layer moderates CO₂ diffusion,

## Casing vs. Substrate

An important distinction: the casing layer is fundamentally different from
the colonized substrate beneath it. Substrate is nutritive — it provides the
food source for fungal growth. Casing is non-nutritive — it does not serve as
food. If casing were richly nutritive, the mycelium would continue vegetative
growth rather than forming fruit bodies. The low-nutrition status of casing
is what signals the fungus to shift from growth to reproduction.

## GUID Casing Recipe

The GUID cultivation method uses the following recipe:

- **10 parts peat**: The primary component; provides water retention and
  structure. Peat moss (Sphagnum) is preferred for its excellent water-
  [[peat-calcium-carbonate-perlite-casing-psilocybe-cubensis-miniculture]] buffer that adjusts and
  stabilizes pH. Most mushrooms prefer a slightly alkaline casing (pH 7-7.5)
  even when the substrate itself is slightly acidic

## Preparation Method

### Mixing
The ingredients are mixed thoroughly in dry form. Water is added gradually
while stirring continuously. The goal is maximum water retention without
creating mud:

- Add water incrementally until the casing holds its shape when squeezed
  but no water drips out
- If too wet, add more dry ingredients (maintaining the 10:5:2 ratio)
- If too dry, add more water

The moisture content of peat varies between sources and seasons, so water
amounts must be adjusted empirically. This is one of the most common sources
of casing problems for beginners.

### Sterilization
The prepared casing soil is filled into oven bags (nylon), autoclave bags
(PP), or jars and sterilized in a pressure cooker for one hour at 15 PSI.

While some authors advise against sterilizing casing soil — arguing it kills
beneficial microorganisms — the GUID method strongly recommends sterilization.
Untreated or pasteurized casing soils have consistently produced poor results
in their experience. The risk of introducing competitor organisms from
unsterilized peat outweighs any theoretical benefit of microbial communities.

After sterilization, the casing must cool to room temperature before use.

## Application

### When to Case
The substrate is ready for casing when it is fully colonized — the mycelium
has completely permeated the grain or bulk substrate with no visible
uncolonized patches. The GUID method uses 1-liter disposable plastic trays:

1. Shake the colonized grass seed loose and pour into a tray
2. Break up any lumps using the clean rim of the jar
3. Level the grain surface evenly
4. Cover with a thin layer (1.5-2.0 cm) of casing soil using a spoon and
   fork
5. Keep the casing surface even while maintaining a slightly rough texture
   with small valleys and hills — this increases surface area for pinning
6. Cover the tray with tin foil and place in a clean location at 20-25°C

### Mycelial Run-Through
Within a few days, white mycelium will begin growing through the casing
soil. Depending on the strain, the casing is ready for fruiting initiation
when the mycelium has reached near-complete surface coverage. Some strains
fruit readily; others require more precise timing.

## Moisture Management

Casing moisture is critical throughout the fruiting cycle:

- **Initial**: Properly hydrated casing should glisten slightly but not be
  wet
- **During colonization**: Maintain moisture under the foil cover
- **During fruiting**: Mist daily to prevent drying; the casing must never
  dry out
- **After a flush**: Increase watering after harvest because maturing
  mushrooms pull significant moisture from the casing during development
- **Developing feel**: Explicit watering directions are difficult because
  conditions vary. Cultivators must develop an intuitive sense for casing
  moisture through experience

## Common Problems

- **Overlay**: Mycelium grows too thickly over the casing surface, forming
  a dense mat that resists pinning. Caused by too-high CO₂, excessive
  humidity, or casing too early
- **Contamination**: Mold growth on casing surface. Often follows
  accumulation of dead pinheads (aborts)
- **Casing pull-away**: Casing shrinks away from tray edges as it dries,
  creating gaps. Caused by insufficient misting
- **Matting**: Mycelium mats on the surface without forming pins. Often
  caused by too-early exposure to fruiting conditions
