# Mollison on Permaculture for High Islands

**Source:** Bill Mollison, Permaculture Design Course, Pamphlet V (Permaculture on High Islands), 1981

## Overview

Mollison describes the Hawaiian Ohana land division system as a model of integrated watershed management, and provides design guidance for volcanic and granitic high islands with their unique wet/dry slope patterns, tidal wave risks, and microclimates. High islands over 1,000 feet create their own weather systems and present extraordinary opportunities for [[permaculture-design-principles]] application.

## High Island Climate Patterns

- Islands over 1,000 feet high typically have clouds and a humid top

- More than 5 degrees off equator: wet side and dry side due to seasonal winds

- Wet side: very humid, rain forest descends slope; clouds can extend to water line

- Dry side: winds lose moisture ascending, heat up descending, greater evaporative power

- Fresh water often travels out beyond island and bubbles up in sea (visible springs)

- 2,000-foot elevation gains 7-12 degrees of latitude in climate zones

## The Hawaiian Ohana System

The Ohana system is perhaps the most elegant example of traditional permaculture design ever documented -- a fully integrated watershed management system that sustained large populations for centuries.

- Land divisions followed natural volcanic ridgelines between valleys

- Each Ohana controlled entire watershed from mountain ridge to reef and beyond

- **Independently invented keyline system**: massive stonewalls at keyline led water from upper valleys to ridges, back to valleys at lower level, then to taro paddies

- Forests were taboo (sacred) as water sources; foot traffic confined to ridge tops

- Nutrient flow from forest to sea enclosed with rock walls; algae blooms fed millions of pounds of mullet

- "A totally stable ecology that supported many thousands of people"

- Destroyed by missionaries, cattle, and disease within a few generations

## House Siting on Islands

- Never place housing in valley floors (tidal wave funnels with ever-increasing velocity)

- Tidal waves penetrate max half-mile inland; safe at first elevation if sub-ridge present

- Perch on valley sides, in from valley mouth, to avoid volcanic mud flows (400 mph)

- Cyclone zones require minimal house mass -- "paper or light matting" construction

- Near fringing reef: reduces wave height from 100 feet to 12 feet

- Check known paths of mud flows, tidal waves, and cyclone damage before siting

## Forest and Water Interactions

- Removal of ridge forests dries up springs; reforestation recommences flow

- People alive today remember mists descending from 1,000 feet to ground as forests extended

- Amount of water generated proportional to forest cover

- Eternal-shade valleys exist on some high islands (no solar evaporation, only transpiration)

- Play "climate games downhill" -- descend from plum/deciduous to ultra-tropical at sea level

## Granitic Landscapes

- Hundred feet of permeable sand; significant water storage nearly impossible

- Treat like desert despite reasonable humidity

- Trees do well (unclear why); emphasize tree crops over annuals

- No lawns (90 inches water/year to maintain lawn on siliceous sands)

- Rocks as heat stores; incorporate into buildings

- Microclimates range from dates to strawberries around rock outcrops

- Garden areas should be plastic-lined to retain humic acids at root level

## Climate Stratification and Agricultural Zoning

High islands offer extraordinary climate diversity within small geographic areas, enabling a wide range of crops to be grown in close proximity.

- **Elevation-based zones:** Every 300-400 feet of elevation gain produces a noticeable change in temperature and humidity. At 2,000 feet, the climate shift is equivalent to moving 7-12 degrees of latitude. This means a single island can produce temperate, subtropical, and tropical crops within walking distance.

- **The "climate game downhill":** Mollison encourages designers to play what he calls "climate games downhill" -- planting species adapted to cooler climates at higher elevations and progressively warmer-climate species as you descend. A walk from ridge to sea level can pass through plum orchards, citrus groves, coffee, bananas, and finally coconuts.

- **Wet side vs. dry side:** The windward (wet) side supports rainforest species, taro, and water-intensive crops. The leeward (dry) side is suitable for drought-adapted species, cacti, and succulents. The transition zone between wet and dry sides offers yet another microclimate niche.

- **Eternal-shade valleys:** Some high island valleys receive so little direct sunlight (due to steep walls and persistent cloud cover) that solar evaporation is negligible. Only plant transpiration removes moisture. These valleys maintain permanently saturated conditions ideal for water-loving species and tree ferns.

## Water Management on High Islands

Water is the defining design challenge and opportunity on high islands.

- **Ridge forest preservation:** The Ohana system recognized that ridge forests are the primary water generators for the entire watershed. Removing ridge forest eliminates the cloud forest effect, stops fog drip, and reduces or eliminates downslope springs. Modern [[mollison-forest-water-climate]] research confirms this with quantitative data.

- **Freshwater lenses:** On volcanic islands, freshwater from rainfall floats on denser saltwater beneath, creating a freshwater lens. This lens can extend beyond the shoreline, producing freshwater springs in the ocean. These submarine springs were traditionally used for fishing and freshwater collection.

- **Keyline-inspired water distribution:** The Ohana stonewall system at keyline positions distributed water from upper valleys across ridgelines to adjacent valleys at lower elevations. This is essentially the [[keyline-design]] principle, independently invented centuries before P.A. Yeomans formalized it in Australia.

- **Taro paddy irrigation:** Water flowing from the upper watershed through the keyline system was directed into taro paddies, where it was nutrient-enriched and then flowed into fish ponds and eventually to the nearshore marine environment, fertilizing algae blooms that supported mullet populations.

## Disaster Risk and Design Response

High islands face unique natural hazards that must be addressed in site design and house construction.

- **Tsunami (tidal wave) risk:** Never site housing in valley floors, which act as funnels accelerating wave energy. The safest locations are on valley sides above the tidal penetration zone (maximum half-mile inland) but below volcanic mud flow hazard zones.

- **Volcanic mud flows:** These can travel at 400 mph down volcanic slopes. Site housing on sub-ridges (saddle positions between valleys) rather than in the direct path of valley channels.

- **Cyclone/hurricane design:** In cyclone zones, minimize house mass. "Paper or light matting" construction survives cyclones better than heavy structures because wind loads are proportional to surface area and structural mass. Fringing reefs reduce wave heights from 100 feet to 12 feet -- house sites behind reefs are significantly safer.

- **Historical hazard mapping:** Before siting any structure, consult historical records of mud flow paths, tsunami inundation zones, and cyclone damage patterns. These hazards are predictable and repeatable.

## The Ohana System as Permaculture Model

The Ohana system exemplifies permaculture principles centuries before permaculture was formally defined.

- **Integrated systems:** Each watershed unit integrated forest, agriculture, aquaculture, and settlement into a single self-regulating system.

- **Waste elimination:** Nutrients flowed from forest to agriculture to aquaculture to marine environment with zero waste. Rock walls captured nutrients at each stage.

- **Edge effect maximization:** The ridgeline boundaries between watershed units created maximum edge between different ecological zones, maximizing biodiversity and productivity.

- **Self-regulation:** The taboo system prevented overexploitation of forest resources. Cultural rules enforced sustainable harvesting without the need for external regulation.

- **Community-scale resilience:** Each Ohana unit was self-sufficient in food, water, and materials, creating resilience at the community scale rather than the individual or national scale.
