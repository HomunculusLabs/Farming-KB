# Structural Forest Design

Structural forests are a foundational concept in permaculture, focusing on the production of timber, poles, fencing, and building materials through carefully managed tree plantings. Unlike conventional forestry oriented toward sawlog production, permaculture structural forestry emphasizes round pole timber, coppice systems, and integration with food-producing forests.

## The Case for Round Pole Timber

The strongest structural timber is **growing round timber** — wood that has not been cut and processed into dimensional lumber. Pioneer and edge species are the primary candidates for structural production. The advantage of round timber lies in its intact fiber structure, which gives it superior impact loading strength compared to milled lumber.

Black locust (*[[robinia-pseudoacacia]]*) stands as a premier example in North America. It is a pioneer species, a soil builder, and produces fence posts that have been documented setting in the ground for 90 to 100 years while still retaining near-maximum strength. Black locust has the highest impact loading strength of any timber known. Posts can be planted at densities of 4,000 to 5,000 per acre and harvested in only four to six years. Crucially, black locust **coppices vigorously** — the more you cut, the more you get — and the foliage provides excellent chicken forage, creating a synergistic livestock-forestry system.

## Bamboo as Structural Material

Bamboo offers numerous domestic uses as a structural material. While it typically requires about 10 years to develop harvestable clumps (unless existing root masses are divided), its growth rate and versatility are exceptional. Several bamboo species suited to temperate North America reach heights of 60 feet with four-inch diameters.

When used as reinforcement in concrete, properly treated bamboo at one-inch diameter provides strength comparable to one-quarter-inch steel — approximately two-thirds the strength per cross-sectional area. Bamboo has advantages over steel: it does not rust in concrete, bonds better with the material, and provides additional yields in the form of edible shoots. However, bamboo shoots carry some risk of cyanide poisoning when raw and should be cooked before consumption.

Bamboo must be harvested at the correct age — typically two to four years — and dried for about eight months before use as structural material.

## coppice Systems

Coppice forestry involves cutting trees at or near ground level and allowing them to regrow from the stump, producing multiple stems. This is one of the oldest and most sustainable forms of woodland management.

### Suitable Coppice Species

- **Willow** — the classical coppicing tree, used for basketry, furniture, and handles
- **Poplar** — good for forage, splitting, and inside work (not durable outdoors)
- **Ash** — excellent for woven fences and tool handles
- **Hazel** — traditional for woven hurdles and fencing
- **Oak** — durable and strong, used historically for all types of construction
- **Tea tree (*Melaleuca*)** — grows thick, weaves well, produces high-value oil from leaves, and lasts 30–40 years in fences

### Coppice Management by End Use

The height at which coppice is initiated depends on the intended product:

- **Basketry**: Cut below ground level. Bury billets of striped willow in wet sites in rows; they shoot up and can be recoppiced repeatedly.
- **Forage**: Start coppice above cow height so livestock can browse the regrowth without damaging the stool.
- **Fencing and poles**: Cut at standard height for the desired post length.

### Stopping Unwanted Coppice

If a stump must be prevented from regrowing, the simplest method is to **exclude light** by placing a piece of carpet or similar opaque material over the cut stump.

## Forest Architecture: Crown and Edge Species

When designing a [[food-forest]], tree placement must account for whether species are **crown bearers** (bearing fruit/nuts within the canopy interior) or **edge bearers** (requiring exposure at the forest margin). Most nut trees bear on the crown, while many fruit trees are edge species.

### Buffer Zones

Large nut trees in the *Juglans* genus (walnuts, pecans, hickories) produce root exudates containing creosols that are allelopathic — they suppress competing vegetation. This is how these species naturally advance against forest edges. A **buffer forest** of tolerant species must be planted between these nut trees and more sensitive fruit trees:

- **Mulberry** — perhaps the best buffer; thrives against both nut trees and fruit trees with no crop reduction
- **Elderberry** — another excellent buffer that tolerates both groups
- **Black locust** — serves effectively as a buffering tree

### Structural Timber as Forest Core

In forests exceeding 100 feet in diameter, the center can be designated as structural timber production. The design approach is:

1. Establish bark-yielding species with close planting in the interior
2. Thin progressively for crown yields
3. Thin further for edge yields
4. Consider breaking crowns to create internal lakes or clearings
5. Restart the pattern outward

In tropical forests, this concern is reduced because many stem-bearing species (cocoa, palms) produce within the canopy. In temperate forests, oaks bear well within the canopy and can serve dual roles as forage and structural timber.

## High-Density Planting for Trunk Development

A key technique for establishing quality structural trees is to **plant at very small intervals** — some species as close as 3×3 feet, nearly any species at 9×9 feet. This dense planting forces upright growth with good trunk form. Modest trunk trimming is performed, then bearing is allowed to start on the small trees. Selection then proceeds for:

- Early bearing
- Easy nut crackability
- Low tannin content
- Good form

Underperforming trees are gradually removed while retaining those with good trunk length. The final result is a tree with excellent bearing potential and structural quality.

## Two Types of Structural Forests

### Pole Timber Forests (Continual Production)

Manage by **continually felling the largest trees** as they reach harvestable diameter. This produces a steady supply of round timbers and is typically more profitable per acre than waiting decades for sawlogs.

### Plank Timber Forests (Eventual Production)

Manage by **cutting out all small and weak trees**, leaving selected straight-trunked individuals to grow to sawlog size. This approach requires 40+ years but produces high-value dimensional lumber.

### The Recommended Approach

For any holding larger than seven or eight acres, **combine both methods** — maintain some areas as continual-production pole forests while developing other areas for eventual plank harvest.

## Pole Construction

Australians have pioneered pole-frame construction where entire house frameworks use approximately nine two-inch poles, then filled in with mud, brick, stone, wattling, or chicken wire and cement. Complete house frameworks can be built for around $800 using this method, producing durable and attractive structures.

## Nursery Integration with Forest Establishment

The establishment of structural forests benefits enormously from modern nursery techniques. In a nursery, trees can be grown to a size that would take eight years in the field. Container specimens can be shipped the year before they yield. While nursery preparation proceeds, the land can be simultaneously prepared and even cropped, using the cropping cycle to manure the planting site.

A portable nursery kit — fitting on the back of a truck — can include drip lines, sprinklers, and shade houses. This approach has been successfully used with Aboriginal communities in central Australia, where a trained "tribal nurseryman" establishes nurseries at outstation camps and propagates trees from tamarisk cuttings and grape cuttings suited to the local area.
