## The Clover Revolution: Natural Rice and Barley Cultivation

## Overview

The clover revolution is [[masanobu-fukuoka]]"s complete method for continuous, no-tillage rice and barley (or wheat) cultivation using direct seeding, straw mulching, and living ground cover — entirely without pesticides, [[fukuoka-textdoc-perennial-rice-biennial-cultivation-exploration]] where both grains are produced on the same land year after year without ever plowing or turning the soil.

## Philosophy of the Clover Revolution

Fukuoka named this the basic pattern of natural rice and barley farming "because I was certain that this technique enabled the farmer for the first time to farm without using any pesticides or [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]]."

He also "referred to it also as the "clover revolution' in rice and barley cropping to voice my opposition to modern [[fukuoka-natural-rice-farming-steps]] began with straw." Straw mulching is the cornerstone of the entire system:

- Promotes seed germination by maintaining moisture and temperature.
- Holds back weeds by blocking sunlight to the soil surface.
- Enriches the soil as it decomposes, feeding microorganisms and earthworms.
- Scatter all straw uncut immediately after threshing — no composting, no burning.

Fukuoka wrote: "Although it may appear crude and backward, spreading fresh straw on a rice field is really quite a bold and revolutionary step." Agricultural technicians of his era regarded rice straw as a source of disease, requiring composting before use, or recommended burning it outright. Fukuoka challenged this directly: "Are there no farmers, scientists, or agricultural administrators out there who see that whether or not we spread straw over our fields may decide the fate of our national lands?"

### 8. Disease and Pest Control

No pesticides are used. Period. Fukuoka observed: "After thirty to forty years of farming without pesticides... Provided the farmer is sincere in his efforts to grow healthy crops, there will never be any need for pesticides."

A naturally farmed rice field is a balanced ecosystem teeming with life: Asiatic locusts, tree frogs, dragonflies, ground sparrows, and swallows all populate the field. These organisms form a self-regulating food web that controls pest populations without chemical intervention. Healthy soil grows healthy plants, and healthy plants resist disease naturally. The farmer"s role is not to wage war on insects but to cultivate the conditions in which natural balance prevails.

## Levee Management

The levees (bunds) surrounding paddy fields require careful, artistic construction and maintenance:

- **Mole control**: Moles can tunnel 40–50 feet in a single night. Plug tunnels with stiff earth packed firmly. Avoid makeshift or temporary repairs — build properly the first time. Do not leave grass cuttings on the levee, as they attract earthworms, which in turn attract moles. Catch persistent moles using bamboo tube traps set in their runs.
- **Aesthetic and spiritual dimension**: "Erecting a well-built levee in a paddy field is akin to making a work of art." Fukuoka elevated levee-building from mere maintenance to an act of craftsmanship and devotion: "The mission [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] is to peel away this narrow vision and show such labor in its true light as artistic and religious work."

## Clay Pellet Preparation

Clay pellets protect rice seeds from predation by birds and insects during the overwinter period and ensure even germination. Two methods are used depending on the scale and precision needed:

**Bulk method:**
1. Mix seeds in 5–10× their quantity of crushed clay or red earth.
2. Add water and knead the mixture thoroughly by treading with feet.
3. Pass the kneaded clay through a half-inch screen.
4. Dry for about half a day.
5. Shape into finished pellets.

**One-seed pellet method** (for precise spacing):
1. Place individual seeds in a bamboo basket.
2. Sprinkle clay powder while spraying a fine water mist.
3. Swirl the basket continuously so each seed accumulates a uniform coating.
4. The resulting pellets are a quarter to half inch in diameter.

## Summary of the Annual Cycle

| Month | Activity |
|-------|----------|
| Sep–Oct | Sow clover (1 lb/quarter-acre) |
| Oct | Sow barley (~2 weeks before rice harvest) |
| Oct–Nov | Harvest rice; spread rice straw; apply chicken manure |
| Nov–Dec | Sow rice in clay pellets (6.5–22 lbs/quarter-acre) |
| Winter | Barley and clover grow; rice overwinters in clay pellets |
| Spring | Rice seedlings emerge under clover; barley matures |
| Late May | Cut barley and clover; spread barley straw; apply manure |
| Early Jun | Flood 4–7 days to suppress clover; begin intermittent irrigation |
| Summer | Rice grows; intermittent irrigation; heading stage water management |
| Autumn | Harvest rice; cycle begins again |

The clover revolution demonstrates that high-yield grain production is fully compatible with ecological health — no chemicals, no tilling, no fossil-fuel machinery, and no compromise with nature's own processes. The farmer works with nature rather than against it, and the land rewards sincerity with abundance.
