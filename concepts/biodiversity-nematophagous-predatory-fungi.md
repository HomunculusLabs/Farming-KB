## Nematophagous and Predatory Fungi Nematophagous and predatory fungi constitute a diverse guild of fungal species that obtain part or all of their nutrition by attacking and consuming living microscopic invertebrates, primarily nematodes and rotifers. The existence of fungi using this nutritional mode was first revealed through the detailed studies of Charles Drechsler (1933, 1941). More than 300 recognized species belonging to the Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, Zygomycota, and the fungus-like Phylum Oomycota are now recognized as parasites or predators of microfauna. ## Modes of Existence [[nematophagous-fungi]] attacks nematode eggs and
cysts rather than adult animals. These fungi are particularly
important in agricultural systems where plant-parasitic nematodes
cause significant crop damage. Species of Paecilomyces lilacinus and
Pochonia chlamydosporia are well-known egg parasites that colonize
nematode egg masses in soil, penetrating the egg shell and destroying
the developing embryo.

## Taxonomic Distribution

### Zygomycota

Several genera of Zygomycota include nematode-trapping species. Species
of Zoopagales are obligate parasites of small invertebrates including
nematodes, rotifers, and amoebae. They produce adhesive spores or
haustoria that attach to and penetrate their hosts. The Stylopage
species are common soil fungi that trap nematodes using adhesive
conidia borne on erect conidiophores.

### Ascomycota and Their Anamorphs

The largest number of nematophagous fungi belongs to the Ascomycota
and their asexual (anamorphic) states. Key genera include
Arthrobotrys, Dactylella, Monacrosporium, and Drechmeria, many of
which have teleomorphs in the Orbiliaceae (Leotiales). The genus
Arthrobotrys includes species with adhesive networks, adhesive knobs,
and constricting rings. Molecular phylogenetic studies have shown that
the trapping mechanism is not always congruent with phylogenetic
relationships, suggesting multiple evolutionary origins of
nematode-trapping behavior.

### Chytridiomycota and Oomycota

Aquatic nematode parasites include Catenaria and Catenomyces
(Chytridiomycota), which produce motile zoospores that actively infect
nematodes in freshwater habitats. Among the Oomycota, some Lagenidium
and Myzocytium species parasitize rotifers and nematodes in aquatic
environments, producing zoospores that encyst on the host surface.

## Obtaining and Culturing Nematodes

studies of nematophagous fungi require laboratory cultures of both
fungi and their nematode hosts. Nematodes can be obtained from soil
using the Baermann funnel technique, where soil on wire mesh over
water allows nematodes to migrate downward and accumulate at the
funnel stem.
