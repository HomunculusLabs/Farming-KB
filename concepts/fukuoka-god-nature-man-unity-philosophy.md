## Do Not Name the Nameless God

Fukuoka insists that God cannot be defined or described: "No means exist for leading people to an understanding of nature and God, so I cannot possibly have disciples." This is why he has never had a single disciple, never told anyone "Follow me. I shall lead you."

He has never once tried to define what God or what a farm is, for that matter. The only reason he has not tried is because it cannot be done. "Although I am unable to lead people, I tell them, 'I know that God and nature are indefinable.' Many people have come to live in my hilltop orchard, but I have not had the power to lead them, so I have not tried."

This refusal to define or name the divine connects to the broader [[fukuoka-spiritual-philosophy-farming]] that underlies natural farming. Human knowledge and activity, for all their apparent sophistication, are fundamentally empty. God's silence and inaction, for all their apparent passivity, are the source of all creation. The farmer who understands this paradox does less and less while the land produces more and more.

## Philosophical Significance

The unity of God, nature, and man is the culminating expression of Fukuoka's philosophical journey. Beginning with practical farming experiments and proceeding through dietary philosophy and social critique, he arrives at a comprehensive vision in which the division between spirit and matter, human and divine, self and world is revealed as an artifact of the discriminating mind.
