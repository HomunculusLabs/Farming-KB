# Fukuoka: God, Nature, and Man as One

Source: [[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]], Chapter 5: Nature, God, and Man

## The Wandering God

The central thesis of Fukuoka's later philosophy is that God, nature, and man were originally one indivisible entity, but human intellectual activity has torn them apart. The entire project of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] represents the effort to reunify God, nature, and man -- to return to the original unity.

This is perhaps the most ambitious philosophical claim in all of Fukuoka's work. He is not merely advocating a farming method or a dietary approach; he is proposing nothing less than the reunification of the fundamental categories of existence. Natural farming is the practical expression of a metaphysical vision in which the artificial separation between the human, the divine, and the natural is overcome.

## Do Not Name the Nameless God

Fukuoka insists that God cannot be defined or described: "No means exist for leading people to an understanding of nature and God, so I cannot possibly have disciples." This is why he has never had a single disciple, never told anyone "Follow me. I shall lead you."

He has never once tried to define what God or what a farm is, for that matter. The only reason he has not tried is because it cannot be done. "Although I am unable to lead people, I tell them, 'I know that God and nature are indefinable.' Many people have come to live in my hilltop orchard, but I have not had the power to lead them, so I have not tried."

This refusal to define or name the divine connects to the broader [[fukuoka-nature-god-limits-of-knowledge]] that Fukuoka explores throughout his later work. The moment God is named, God is reduced; the moment nature is described, nature is distorted. True understanding is not conceptual but experiential, and it cannot be transmitted through words or teachings.

## God Is All Alone

There is only one God. God is all alone. God knows neither space nor time. When man tries to grasp the essence of God, man only moves further and further away from both nature and God. The death of God is not God's death but man's banishment from God.

The statement "God is all alone" is paradoxical: if God is one with everything, how can God be alone? Fukuoka's answer is that God's aloneness is not isolation but completeness. God does not need an "other" because there is no other. The sense of separation that human beings experience -- the feeling of being a separate self in a world of separate objects -- is the original error from which all suffering flows.

## Nature Creates God

Fukuoka argues against the conventional view that God created nature. Instead, nature creates God. The soul of nature is also the will of God who dwells in all living things. When man separates God from nature through intellectual discrimination, both God and nature are diminished.

The Eastern concept of nothingness (Mu) appears close to an understanding of God's presence while in a weightless state. But God and nature are one, and the Eastern concept is not the same as the Western concept.

This radical inversion of the conventional creation narrative -- nature creates God rather than God creating nature -- is one of Fukuoka's most distinctive contributions to philosophical theology. It means that divinity is not a transcendent reality that stands outside the natural world but an emergent property of nature itself. The [[fukuoka-nature-god-limits-of-knowledge]] dissolves the boundary between the sacred and the secular, between the spiritual and the material.

## A Humanity Forsaken by God

When man tears apart God, nature, and man -- originally one indivisible entity -- he creates a world of suffering. The first thing to arise is the discriminating mind. Then comes the false distinction between self and other. From this follows all human misery.

I, who received a revelation of God one moment fifty years ago, looked back on God and tried to follow quietly the path of a solitary farmer. This concrete expression of the God that lies hidden deep within the natural world is natural farming.

The reference to the revelation that transformed Fukuoka's life is central to understanding his entire project. As a young man working as a plant pathologist, he experienced a moment of insight in which he perceived the fundamental unity of all existence. This experience led him to abandon his scientific career and take up farming as a spiritual practice -- not to escape the world but to demonstrate through concrete action what his revelation had revealed.

## The Limitation of All Religions

From the standpoint of Western religion and philosophy, even so simple a thing as the reunification of man and nature cannot be done. Men of religion are busily amassing money and power behind the scenes. Islam, Christianity, and any other religion would have suited the Japanese people. Japanese people harbor the potential for aspiring to a religion that goes beyond religions and sects.

Fukuoka's critique of organized religion parallels his critique of organized science: both systems attempt to capture and institutionalize something that is inherently beyond capture. Just as agricultural science cannot improve upon nature, religious institutions cannot improve upon the direct experience of the divine that is available to anyone who approaches the natural world with an open heart.

## Children, God, and Nature

The ones who live peacefully in a world of no contradictions and no distinctions are infants. They perceive light and dark, strong and weak, but make no judgments. Even though the snake and the frog exist, the child has no understanding of strong and weak. The original joy of life is there, but the fear of death is yet to appear.

If man were to rid himself of his cloak of hubris and stand naked, if he were to abandon himself, crawling around on hands and knees with the dogs and cats, climbing trees and acting like children do, he would approach God.

The [[fukuoka-nature-god-limits-of-knowledge]] is a recurring theme in Fukuoka's philosophy. Children have not yet learned the discriminating mind; they experience the world as a unity rather than as a collection of separate objects. This innocent perception is, for Fukuoka, the closest human beings come to the divine consciousness that permeates nature.

## The God Revolution

Fukuoka's later book "Kami no Kakumei" (The God Revolution) contains this passage:

"Compelled to speak, God says nothing.

Unable to say anything, man speaks.

Man is ignorant of the truth, but expounds on all fields of learning.

God is versed in all things, but expounds on nothing.

God takes no action, yet creates all things.

Man does all things, yet creates nothing."

This poem encapsulates the entire [[fukuoka-spiritual-philosophy-farming]] that underlies natural farming. Human knowledge and activity, for all their apparent sophistication, are fundamentally empty. God's silence and inaction, for all their apparent passivity, are the source of all creation. The farmer who understands this paradox does less and less while the land produces more and more.

## Philosophical Significance

The unity of God, nature, and man is the culminating expression of Fukuoka's philosophical journey. Beginning with practical farming experiments and proceeding through dietary philosophy and social critique, he arrives at a comprehensive vision in which the division between spirit and matter, human and divine, self and world is revealed as an artifact of the discriminating mind.

This vision is neither pantheism nor monotheism in the conventional sense. It is closer to the [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]] -- the void that is not empty but full, the nothingness that contains everything. God is not a being who created the world but the unity that the world always already is, when perceived without the distorting lens of human intellect.

## Connection to Natural Farming Principles

Natural farming is the practical embodiment of the God-nature-man unity. When the farmer ceases to impose human will upon the land, the original unity is restored. The soil, the plants, the insects, the weather, and the farmer become expressions of a single process rather than competing elements that must be managed and controlled.

This is why natural farming feels effortless even though it produces abundance. The farmer is not fighting against nature or even working with nature -- the farmer *is* nature, participating consciously in the creative process that is the divine presence in the world. This is the [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] at its deepest level: not a technique but a way of being.
