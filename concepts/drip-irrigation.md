# Drip Irrigation

Drip irrigation (also called trickle irrigation) delivers water directly to the root zone of plants through a network of tubes, emitters, and fittings. It is the most water-efficient irrigation method available, reducing water usage by 30-50% compared to sprinkler systems while minimizing evaporation, runoff, and foliar disease. For homesteads, market gardens, and indoor grows, drip irrigation saves time and ensures consistent moisture.

## Advantages of Drip Irrigation

- **Water efficiency**: Delivers water precisely where needed with minimal waste. 90-95% efficiency vs 50-70% for sprinklers.
- **Reduced disease**: Keeps foliage dry, reducing fungal and bacterial disease pressure.
- **Labor savings**: Eliminates hand watering once installed. Can be automated with timers.
- **Fertigation capability**: Water-soluble fertilizers can be injected into the system.
- **Weed suppression**: Water is delivered only to planted areas, not the spaces between.
- **Versatility**: Works in raised beds, in-ground gardens, containers, orchards, and greenhouse rows.
- [[raised-bed-gardening]] and [[homesteading]] both benefit significantly from automated drip systems.

## System Components

### Water Source
- **City water**: Standard pressure (40-80 PSI). Requires a backflow preventer. May need dechlorination for sensitive plants.
- **Well water**: Variable pressure. Test for mineral content (iron, calcium) that can clog emitters.
- **Rain barrel / cistern**: Low pressure (gravity-fed). Requires careful system design with low-pressure components.
- **Pump from pond/stream**: Needs filtration and potentially a pressure regulator.

### Header / Main Line
- Typically 1/2 inch or 3/4 inch polyethylene (poly) tubing.
- Carries water from the source to the planting area.
- Can be buried or run on the soil surface (mulch over for UV protection).

### Lateral Lines
- 1/4 inch tubing or 1/2 inch poly that branches off the main line.
- Delivers water to individual plants or emitter zones.
- Can be run along rows, around containers, or snaked through beds.

### Emitters and Drippers
The heart of the system — devices that control water delivery to individual plants.

**Drip emitters (individual)**:
- **Flow rates**: 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, or 4.0 GPH (gallons per hour)
- **Types**: Pressure-compensating (PC) maintain consistent flow regardless of pressure variations; non-compensating flow varies with pressure
- **Installation**: Punch into 1/2 inch poly tubing using a hole punch
- **Recommendation**: Use pressure-compensating emitters for consistent delivery

**Drip line (inline drip tubing)**:
- Tubing with emitters pre-installed at fixed intervals (6, 12, or 18 inches).
- Excellent for dense plantings, hedgerows, and row crops.
- Emitter spacing and flow rate are fixed — choose based on plant spacing.

**Micro-sprinklers / micro-spray**:
- Small spray heads that cover a wider area (2-6 foot radius).
- Good for groundcover, dense raised beds, and orchards.
- Higher flow rate than point-source drippers.

**Soaker hose / drip tape**:
- Porous hose that weeps water along its entire length.
- Inexpensive but less precise than individual emitters.
- Pressure-sensitive — flow varies along the length.

### Filters
Essential for preventing clogged emitters. Install a filter at the beginning of every drip system.

- **Screen filter**: 155-200 mesh for well water or pond water. Cleanable and reusable.
- **Disc filter**: More effective for fine particles. Better for systems with organic matter in the water.
- **For city water**: A simple 100-200 mesh screen filter is usually sufficient.

### Pressure Regulators
Drip systems operate at low pressure (15-30 PSI for most components). If your water source exceeds this, install a pressure regulator after the filter to prevent blowouts and uneven distribution.

### Backflow Preventer
Required when connecting to a municipal water supply. Prevents irrigation water (which may contain fertilizers or soil organisms) from flowing backward into the potable water supply.

### Timers / Controllers
- **Battery-operated hose timers**: Simple, inexpensive, screw onto a hose bib. Good for small systems.
- **Plug-in digital timers**: More programmable options. Multiple zones, custom schedules.
- **Smart controllers**: Wi-Fi enabled, weather-adjusting, app-controlled. Can integrate with soil moisture sensors.
- **Zone valves**: For larger systems with multiple zones requiring different schedules or flow rates.

### Fittings and Accessories
- **Connectors**: Barbed or compression fittings for joining tubing.
- **End caps**: Seal the ends of main and lateral lines.
- **Flush valves**: Allow periodic flushing of lines to remove sediment.
- **Goof plugs**: Seal accidental holes in tubing.
- **Stakes**: Hold tubing and emitters in place.
- **Punch tool**: For creating clean holes in poly tubing.

## System Design

### Step-by-Step Design Process

1. **Map your garden**: Measure the area, identify plant locations, and group plants with similar water needs into zones.
2. **Determine water source and pressure**: Measure your water pressure and flow rate (GPM) at the source.
3. **Calculate water needs**: Estimate total water demand per zone based on emitter count and flow rates.
4. **Choose components**: Select main line size, emitter types, and spacing based on your plant layout.
5. **Plan zones**: If total water demand exceeds your supply capacity, divide into multiple zones with separate timer programs.

### Emitter Sizing Guidelines

| Plant Type | Emitter Count per Plant | Flow Rate | Notes |
|-----------|------------------------|-----------|-------|
| Container plants | 1 | 0.5-1.0 GPH | Small root zone |
| Vegetables (raised bed) | 1-2 per plant | 0.5-1.0 GPH | 1 per plant for large, 1 per 2 for small |
| Shrubs / perennials | 1-2 | 1.0-2.0 GPH | Wider root zone |
| Fruit trees (young) | 2-3 | 1.0-2.0 GPH | Ring around drip line |
| Fruit trees (mature) | 4-6 | 2.0 GPH | Larger wetted area needed |

## Scheduling

Proper scheduling depends on soil type, plant needs, weather, and emitter output.

### General Guidelines

- **Frequency**: Water less frequently but more deeply to encourage deep root growth. Most gardens need irrigation every 1-3 days.
- **Duration**: Run the system long enough to wet the root zone to the desired depth (6-12 inches for most vegetables).
- **Time of day**: Water early morning (4-7 AM) to minimize evaporation and allow foliage to dry before evening.
- **Adjust seasonally**: Reduce frequency in cool, cloudy weather; increase during hot, dry spells.

### Calculating Run Time

Example: You want to deliver 1 gallon of water per plant per day using 1 GPH emitters.

- Run time = 1 gallon / 1 GPH = 1 hour per day
- Split into two sessions: 30 minutes morning, 30 minutes evening (reduces runoff in heavy soils)

### Smart Scheduling

- Use a soil moisture sensor to irrigate only when the soil dries below a threshold.
- Weather-based controllers adjust automatically based on rainfall, temperature, and humidity data.
- Evapotranspiration (ET) data from local weather stations provides precise irrigation recommendations.

## Maintenance

### Regular Maintenance Tasks

- **Flush lines**: Open end caps and flush main and lateral lines monthly (or quarterly at minimum) to remove sediment.
- **Clean filters**: Rinse or backflush screen/disc filters monthly. Replace when damaged.
- **Check emitters**: Inspect for clogs (reduced or no flow). Clean or replace clogged emitters.
- **Inspect for leaks**: Walk the system weekly during active use. Repair damaged tubing or fittings.
- **Winterize**: In freezing climates, drain all lines before first frost. Remove or insulate above-ground components. For permanent systems, use compressed air to blow out lines.

### Clogging Solutions

| Clog Type | Cause | Solution |
|-----------|-------|----------|
| Particle clog | Sediment in water | Improve filtration, flush lines |
| Mineral clog | Calcium, iron in water | Acid injection (vinegar or phosphoric acid), use clean water source |
| Biological clog | Algae, bacteria, slime | Chlorine treatment, UV sterilization, use opaque tubing |
| Root intrusion | Roots growing into emitters | Use emitters with root barriers; apply trifluralin-impregnated discs |
