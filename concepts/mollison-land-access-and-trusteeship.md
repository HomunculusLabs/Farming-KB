# Mollison: Land Access and Trusteeship

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14, Section 14.12)

## Trusteeship vs Ownership

Our own lifetimes are, in terms of soils, trees, or climate, as ephemeral as snowflakes. Thus the very concept of land ownership is ludicrous, and we need only to use what is needed for the brief time that we are here.

The law clearly distinguishes between ownership or entitlement to a resource, and the rights of the use of it. Laws of ownership are relatively modern, and are foreign to tribal or clan law. Laws of trusteeship are ancient, philosophical, and realistic. Ownership, in effect, gives the titleholder a "right to exploit in the short term." Trusteeship governs any resource for the very long term, with no right to exploit resources beyond essential needs, or replacement time.

## Historical Evolution of Land Tenure

- Tribal era: Land held in common by tribes and clans; acknowledged to be the product of a creator. 100% land held by 100% of people.
- Industrial era: People persuaded to give land to God (church) or state (army). Church or emperor holds titles, allots to individuals, states, or companies. Common lands broken up.
- Modern era: Typically 90% of land now owned by 4 to 6% of people (USA, Brazil, most third world areas). Evidence of misuse of land calls for new concept of trusteeship.

People and land are now separated. Educated people of good will, and traditional people, have seen where ownership has ruined common resources, and are returning to the concept of taking local stewardship of the land itself.

## Securing Land Through Trusts

Gifts or deeds of land can be vested in a tax-deductible trust for use by a specific group or the public generally, under reasonable conditions. Essential land for local food, fuel and structural forestry, recreation, and conservation can be planned and secured under a set of public trusts by:
- Public investment.
- Gift or bequest.
- Tax-deductible donation.
- Transfer from other authorities or trusts.
- Outright purchase.

Each parcel of land needs 3 to 4 involved, active, and interested trustees, and plans and purposes should be set for the long term (10 to 50 years ahead). Some areas will be under sports centres, some in trust to conservationists, some under lease for gardens and farms, and some reserved for educational and public bodies.

## Practical Warnings

- Do not accept land gifts that have many restrictive conditions attached; limit acceptance to unconditional gifts.
- Unless a large cash reserve is available, each trust can only manage one area of land, and only slowly expand. Too much land accepted can bankrupt the trust through maintenance and land tax costs.
- A land trust should have a very clear idea of what it wants to achieve, and set a practical time limit.

## Why People Give Land Away

- To continue land in its use: as an organic farm, wildlife refuge, economic botanical garden, or as an example of good land use.
- Because people believe in trusteeship: Many do not believe in private land ownership, but in trust ownerships for public use under sound environmental control.
- Land is surplus to their needs and a cost: "Landpoor" people achieve low productivity and incur maintenance costs. By giving land away (especially to a tax-deductible trust) they can concentrate on a smaller productive area.
- As a bequest: Goodhearted older people will bequeath land, yielding up successive rights as they age.
- Because they want a village or more people on the land: Nuclear families or individuals are socially very isolated on large farms.

## The Land Access Office (LAO)

The establishment of a regional office opens up the potential for offering a set of strategies enabling better land use, suited to the finances and involvement of people using the service. Strategies include:

### Land Lease System (Oxfam Model)
Suited to young families in rental accommodation. The office posts paired lists: List A for those who want 200 to 1,000 sq. feet of garden; List B for those (usually elderly or absentee landlords) who will lease vacant land or the land around their houses on an annual, renewable basis. The LAO prepares a standard lease specifying rental, goods exchange, length and type of lease, access, and names of parties.

### Garden or Farm Club
Suited to families with some capital to invest as shares. A farm is purchased by the club on a public access route about 12 hours from the city, designed to serve member interests (garden, main crop, fuelwood, fishing, recreation, commercial growing). People can lease small areas or appoint a manager. Membership can be made saleable or transferable.

### City Farms
A local group of 100 or more families forms a city farm association, inviting local, state, or federal authorities to allot 1 to 80 ha (preferably with a building) to a city farm. Such invitations are irresistible to those who need to be seen to be doing something positive for the environment.

### Towns and Cities as Farms
The entire town or city district can be treated as a farm, with every household contributing to food, fuel, and forest production. Every scrap of land in settlement should first be planned and used. Any society that develops lawns beyond those used for recreation can support itself for wood, fuel, and building materials by the conversion of lawns to use.

### Farm Link System
Links rural landholders with urban people who want access to land, often on a work-exchange or lease basis.

### Commonwork
A system where multiple enterprises and activities are organised on a single piece of land, with each activity serving both the land and the community.

## Legal Structures for Land Trusts

Several legal frameworks can underpin land trusts and community land access initiatives. In the United States, the most common structure is the 501(c)(3) nonprofit land trust, which provides tax-exempt status for land held for conservation, education, or agricultural purposes. In the United Kingdom, community land trusts operate under specific legal models that separate land ownership from building ownership, ensuring the land remains permanently affordable. The community land trust model allows residents to own homes while the trust retains ownership of the underlying land, preventing speculative resale and maintaining long-term affordability.

Conservation easements represent another powerful tool. A conservation easement is a legally binding agreement that restricts certain types of development on a piece of land in perpetuity, even if the land changes ownership. The landowner retains title but donates or sells specific development rights to a qualified conservation organization. This approach has been used extensively in the United States, where over 27 million acres have been protected through easements held by local and regional land trusts.

## Bioregional Land Planning

Mollison's trusteeship concept extends naturally into bioregional planning, where land management decisions are made at the scale of natural watersheds and ecological regions rather than political boundaries. A bioregional approach to trusteeship recognizes that ecosystems do not respect property lines and that effective land stewardship requires coordination across multiple landholders. Bioregional mapping exercises, where community members collaboratively document the ecological features of their region, can serve as the foundation for more effective trusteeship arrangements.

## Indigenous Trusteeship Models

The trusteeship concept Mollison describes closely parallels indigenous land stewardship practices found worldwide. Aboriginal Australian fire management, Native American seasonal land use protocols, and Māori whenua (land) guardianship all reflect the principle that people are temporary caretakers of land held in trust for future generations. These systems typically operate through cultural obligations and kinship structures rather than legal instruments, but their effectiveness in maintaining ecosystem health over millennia is well documented. Modern permaculture trusts can learn from these models by incorporating ecological monitoring obligations, succession planning for trustees, and community accountability mechanisms.

## Community-Supported Agriculture Connections

The land access strategies Mollison describes anticipate the later development of community-supported agriculture (CSA) models. In a CSA, community members purchase shares in a farm's annual production before the growing season, providing farmers with guaranteed income and land access security. This arrangement functions as a form of economic trusteeship, where the community shares both the risks and rewards of agricultural production. CSA models that incorporate land trust ownership of the underlying farmland create a doubly secure arrangement, ensuring both economic viability and permanent land protection.

## Challenges and Criticisms

Land trust models face several practical challenges. Securing ongoing funding for stewardship and management is a persistent concern, as grants and donations can be unpredictable. Maintaining active and engaged trustees over decades requires careful succession planning. In rapidly urbanizing areas, the opportunity cost of land conserved through trusts can create tension with development interests. Additionally, the legal complexity of establishing and maintaining trusts can be a barrier for communities without access to legal expertise. Despite these challenges, the global community land trust movement has grown substantially, with over 500 community land trusts operating across dozens of countries.

## Urban Land Access Strategies

Mollison's rural-focused land access strategies have been adapted for urban environments with notable success. Urban agriculture movements have reclaimed vacant lots, rooftop spaces, and underutilized public land for food production. Community garden organizations often negotiate lease agreements with municipal governments for the use of vacant city-owned land, sometimes securing long-term leases of 5-10 years or longer. The concept of "urban commons" extends the trusteeship model to city environments, where community-managed green spaces provide food, recreation, ecological services, and social cohesion. These urban land access strategies are particularly important in food deserts where residents lack access to fresh, affordable produce.

## Land Trust Funding Mechanisms

Sustainable funding for land trusts requires diverse revenue streams. Many land trusts rely on a combination of membership dues, individual donations, foundation grants, government contracts for conservation services, and earned income from sustainable forestry, agricultural leases, or ecotourism. Endowment building is critical for long-term financial sustainability, with the goal of generating sufficient investment income to cover annual stewardship costs. Some innovative funding models include transfer fees on real estate transactions within the trust's geographic area, conservation buyer programs where properties are purchased with conservation easements and resold at reduced prices, and ecosystem service payments for carbon sequestration, water quality improvement, and biodiversity habitat provision.

## Indigenous Land Trusts and Cultural Preservation

Indigenous communities worldwide have developed their own land trust and conservation models that predate Western legal structures by millennia. Indigenous land trusts in Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and the United States combine legal land protection with cultural preservation, ensuring that traditional ecological knowledge, language, and spiritual practices connected to specific landscapes are maintained alongside physical conservation. These indigenous models offer important lessons for Western conservation movement, demonstrating that effective land stewardship requires not just legal protection but active cultural engagement and the integration of traditional ecological knowledge with contemporary conservation science. Mollison's study of aboriginal land management was informed by this understanding that people and land are inseparable.

## Bioregional Land Trusts

Mollison's bioregional philosophy extends the land trust concept to the scale of entire watersheds and ecosystems. A bioregional land trust would hold land across an entire bioregion, managing it as an interconnected system rather than as isolated parcels. This approach recognizes that ecological processes -- water flow, wildlife movement, seed dispersal, fire regimes -- operate at landscape scales that do not respect property boundaries. Bioregional land trusts would coordinate management across multiple ownerships to maintain ecological connectivity and resilience, a concept that has influenced the development of landscape-scale conservation initiatives worldwide.

## Intergenerational Land Stewardship

A fundamental principle of Mollison's land trusteeship concept is the intergenerational dimension: land held in trust is managed not for the current generation's benefit alone but for the benefit of future generations. This long-term perspective transforms land management decisions, favoring practices that build rather than deplete soil, water, and biodiversity. Trustees have a fiduciary duty to maintain or enhance the ecological and productive capacity of the land, creating an obligation that constrains short-term exploitation. This intergenerational ethic draws from both indigenous land management traditions, where decisions are evaluated against their impact on the seventh generation, and from Western legal concepts of trust law, where trustees must act in the best interests of beneficiaries.
