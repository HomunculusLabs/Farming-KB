Soil pollution from agricultural chemicals is one of the most
consequential legacies of industrial farming. [[bill-mollison]] documents
the severity of contamination from persistent biocides, heavy metals,
and other pollutants, and outlines strategies for remediation and
safe food production on affected lands.

## The Scope of Agricultural Soil Pollution

Orchards, sugarcane areas, pineapple, cotton, tobacco, and banana
plantations have had extensive applications of mineral additives,
arsenicals, Aldrin, DDT, copper salts, and dioxins. Even after 18 to
20 years of no chemical use, apple orchards in Western Australia
produce unsafe levels of Aldrin and Dieldrin in the eggs of
free-range chickens. Attempts to grow prawns in ponds on such lands
have failed due to residual Dieldrin levels in soils.

When assessing total environmental damage from persistent chemical
misuse, many farms, bores, and rivers will need to be put into
non-food production as fuel forests or structural timber for decades
to come. The same may hold true for soils within 100 metres of
roadways where leaded petrol is used and 800 to 1000 cars pass daily.

## Radioactive and Industrial Contamination

We face lock-up periods of tens or at times thousands of years for
radioactives blowing off or leaking from waste dumps and strategic
stockpiles of yellowcake uranium, from accidents at atomic power
plants, or from their wastes. Cadmium and uranium-polluted soils of
chemically-based and heavily-fertilised market gardens, waste dumps of
industry, and the long-term effects of nitrate-polluted soil waters
compound the problem.

The costs of rehabilitation already far outstrip the profits of
degradation, and may in fact be prohibitive for areas developed for
farming from 1950 to the present, the age of agricultural pollution.

## Heavy Metal Behaviour in Soils

Large quantities of lead, arsenic, copper, and persistent biocides are
applied on most apple orchards. Copper and lead stay at or near the
surface of soils in high concentrations. Arsenic may stay at surface
level in clay soils, or wash down to subsoil 50 to 60 cm deep in acid
sands.

In pasture plants under such orchards, copper can reach 50 ppm, which
is poisonous to sheep. Excess copper in the diet causes toxaemic
jaundice and blood in the urine. Molybdenum, zinc, and sulphur may
buffer copper uptake in sheep. Uptake by plants increases with
temperature and acidity for lead and copper.

## Remediation Strategies for Polluted Soils

There are no easy remedies for polluted soils, but several strategies
can help manage the situation:

### Lead Contamination

Lead from car exhausts and lead paint, pipes, and battery burning is
worst in urban areas of older buildings, with levels potentially
reaching 1,100 ppm. It is both taken up by and dusted on the surface
of vegetables. Remediation approaches include using cracked bricks or
gravel as a base layer, building up beds to 30 cm deep with rich
composted soil of over 40% organic content, growing vegetables and
having leaf analyses done, and washing produce in dilute vinegar if
lead is still used locally in petrol.

### Persistent Biocide Residues

For old orchards, canefields, or plantations with DDT, Aldrin,
Dieldrin, BHC, or similar residues, it is unlikely that any animal
product will be free of high biocide levels. There is no choice but to
go into forestry and produce non-food crops until other methods are
developed. All vegetables should be tested for residual toxic
materials.

### Groundwater Contamination

Groundwater under farms can contain 80 to 90 biocidal substances
derived from fertilisers, sprays, and fuels. Near industrial waste
dumps, dioxins, radioactives, and heavy metal wastes including
cadmium, chromium, and mercury can be added. Do not use untested wells
or bore waters for any purpose. Drink tankwater and harvest surface
run-off for gardens.

## Human Nutrition and Soil Health

The relationship between soil, food, and human health follows four
broad principles. A normally mixed omnivorous diet has been exhibited
by most human groups, and excessive dietary simplicity has its dangers.
In developed countries, refined foods and additives have resulted in
degenerative diseases. In famine-prone areas, traditional diets are a
guide to local food tolerances. There is a complex interaction between
food, soil, trace elements, pH, biocides, and fertilisers, and heavy
macronutrient use can cause micronutrient deficiency.

## Individual Variation in Food Tolerance

Individuals vary in their ability to metabolise and tolerate foods.
Specific food allergies may arise from limited exposure to certain food
groups in racial history. Bodyweight is a basic variable, as dosages
of any substance vary with body build, fats, and dosage per kilogram
weight. People can test for ill effects by eliminating foods from
their diet and observing changes in symptoms.

## Timeframes for Recovery

In soil rehabilitation, we are forced to start with what is now there.
Most gardeners and farmers who have developed sustainable soil systems
allow 3 to 4 years for building a garden, and 5 to 15 years to
restore a devastated soil landscape. This applies only to the physical
restoration of soils and to the development of appropriate plant
systems. There are far more lengthy processes to be undertaken where
past chemical pollution has occurred, and some contaminated lands may
never be safe for food production within a human lifetime.

## The Imperative of Prevention

We must not add to this mess. Avoid all biocides, high levels of
nitrates, and watch on-farm disposal of oils and fuels. The sane
procedure in health is to maintain basic hygiene, grow and eat healthy
plants and animals, avoid biocides and pollutants, take easy exercise,
drink clean water, and stay as cheerful as this world permits.
