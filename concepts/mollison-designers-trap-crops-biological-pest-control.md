# Trap Crops and Biological Pest Control

Biological pest control and trap cropping are central strategies in the
permaculture approach to managing pests and diseases. Rather than relying on
chemical pesticides, permaculture design uses ecological processes to keep pest
populations below economically damaging levels. Bill Mollison presented
biological pest control as part of a broader [[mollison-designers-integrated-pest-management-design]] strategy
that includes habitat creation, biological control agents, trap crops, and
functional plant diversity.

## The Ecological Approach to Pest Management

In natural ecosystems, pest outbreaks are rare because complex food webs keep
herbivore populations in check. Predators, parasites, parasitoids, and diseases
all contribute to regulating herbivore numbers. When natural systems are
simplified through monoculture, these regulatory mechanisms are disrupted and
pest outbreaks become common. Permaculture design restores ecological complexity
to agricultural systems, rebuilding the food webs that naturally suppress pests.

The [[mollison-designers-companion-planting-charts-and-principles]] principle supports biological pest control by
maintaining plant diversity that provides habitat and food for beneficial
organisms. The [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-edge-effect-and-ecotone-design]] creates transition zones that support higher
populations of predatory insects by providing diverse microhabitats.

## Biological Control Agents

### Predatory Insects

Predatory insects are the most visible and effective biological control agents
in permaculture systems. Ladybirds (Coccinellidae) consume aphids, scale
insects, and mealybugs in both adult and larval stages. Lacewings (Chrysopidae)
larvae are voracious predators of aphids, thrips, and small caterpillars.
Ground beetles (Carabidae) hunt slugs, caterpillars, and soil-dwelling pests.
Assassin bugs (Reduviidae) prey on a wide range of insect pests including
caterpillars, beetles, and true bugs.

The [[hemenway-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insects]] strategy establishes specific plants that support predatory
insect populations by providing nectar, pollen, and alternative prey. Umbelliferous
flowers such as dill, fennel, and coriander are particularly attractive to
predatory wasps and hoverflies. Composite flowers such as yarrow, daisies, and
marigolds provide long-lasting nectar sources.

### Parasitoids

Parasitoid wasps are among the most effective biological control agents for
caterpillar pests. Trichogramma wasps parasitise the eggs of moths and
butterflies, including many agricultural pests. Braconid wasps attack
caterpillars and aphids. Chalcid wasps parasitise whiteflies and scale insects.
These tiny wasps are harmless to humans and can be encouraged by providing
nectar sources and avoiding broad-spectrum insecticides.

### Predatory Mites

Predatory mites of the Phytoseiidae family are essential biological control
agents for spider mite pests in orchards, greenhouses, and field crops. They
maintain spider mite populations below damaging levels when their habitat is not
disrupted by pesticides or dust. Providing ground covers and [[mollison-designers-living-mulches-ground-covers]] in
orchards maintains humidity levels that favour predatory mites over pest mites.

### Amphibians and Reptiles

Frogs, toads, and lizards are significant predators of insect pests. The
[[mollison-designers-pond-edge-configuration-and-yield-maximisation]] can include rock piles and log habitats that provide
refuges for amphibians. Toad houses made from upturned pots placed in the
garden provide daytime shelter. [[mollison-designers-wildlife-corridors-habitat-settlements]] connect amphibian breeding
habitats with foraging areas in the garden and orchard.

### Birds

Insectivorous birds consume enormous quantities of pest insects during the
breeding season when they are feeding chicks. Providing nesting sites through
bird boxes, hedgerows, and [[mollison-designers-willow-water-and-natural-rooting-propagation]] encourages bird populations in the
permaculture landscape. The [[mollison-designers-shelterbelt-economics-for-livestock-and-croplands]] design can include berry-producing
shrubs that provide winter food for birds, maintaining their presence year-round.

### Bats
Bats are among the most effective nocturnal insect predators, consuming
hundreds of mosquitoes and moths per night. Bat boxes placed in trees or on
buildings attract bats to the permaculture landscape. The [[mollison-designers-cool-climate-house-design]] can
incorporate bat roosts into the eaves or roof space.

## Trap Crops

### Principle and Mechanism

Trap crops are plants that are more attractive to pests than the main crop,
drawing pests away from the crop the designer wishes to protect. The trap crop
is planted in the path of pest immigration, around the perimeter of the main
crop, or interplanted within the crop. Once pests concentrate on the trap crop,
they can be managed by targeted control measures including manual removal,
biological control agents, or selective pesticide application.

The effectiveness of trap crops depends on several factors: the relative
attractiveness of the trap crop compared to the main crop, the timing of trap
crop availability relative to pest immigration, and the spatial arrangement of
trap crops within the landscape.

### Common Trap Crop Combinations

Several well-documented trap crop combinations are widely used in permaculture
and organic agriculture:

- **Nasturtium for aphids and cabbage white butterfly**: Nasturtiums planted
  around brassica crops attract aphids and serve as a preferred egg-laying site
  for cabbage white butterflies, reducing pest pressure on the main crop.
- **Marigold for nematodes**: French marigolds (Tagetes patula) release
  compounds that repel root-knot nematodes, protecting adjacent susceptible
  crops such as tomatoes and peppers.
- **Radish for flea beetles**: Radishes planted as a border crop attract flea
  beetles away from eggplant, cabbage, and other susceptible crops.
- **Chervil for slugs**: Chervil planted as a border attracts slugs, which
  can then be collected from the trap crop rather than the main crop.
- **Blue Hubbard squash for squash bugs and vine borer**: This squash variety
  is highly attractive to squash bugs and vine borers, protecting zucchini and
  other cucurbits when planted as a perimeter trap crop.
- **Mustard for harlequin bugs**: Mustard greens attract harlequin bugs, which
  can then be controlled on the trap crop without affecting the main crop.

### Design Considerations

Trap crops should be planted early enough to be well-established when pests
arrive. They should be located upwind of the main crop where pest immigration
occurs, or as a complete border around the crop. The trap crop area should be
small relative to the main crop to avoid creating an excessive pest population.
Regular monitoring of the trap crop allows timely intervention when pest numbers
build up.

The [[mollison-designers-design-methods-overview-process]] should include identification of the major pests for each crop
and selection of appropriate trap crops. Local knowledge and observation are
essential, as pest preferences vary with region and climate.

## Habitat Creation for Biological Control
The permaculture landscape should provide year-round habitat for biological
control agents. [[mollison-designers-living-mulches-ground-covers]] provide overwintering habitat for ground beetles
and spiders. Flowering borders with continuous bloom supply nectar for
parasitoid wasps and hoverflies. [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]] and [[mollison-designers-aquatic-edge-planted-wetland-design]] provide drinking water and
breeding habitat. Small uncultivated patches serve as reservoirs linked by
[[mollison-designers-wildlife-corridors-habitat-settlements]]. [[mollison-designers-woven-fence-hedge-live-barrier]] provide nesting sites, overwintering
habitat, and wind protection for crops.
