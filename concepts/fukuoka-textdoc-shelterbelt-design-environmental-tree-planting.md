# Shelterbelt Design and Environmental Tree Planting for Natural Farms

## Overview

Shelterbelts and windbreaks are integral components of Fukuoka's natural farm design. Rather than treating them as mere wind barriers, he views them as multi-functional elements that maintain soil fertility, improve the microclimate, provide habitat for beneficial organisms, and supply organic material. The selection of tree species for shelterbelts follows the principle of working with ecological succession rather than against it.

## The Natural Protected Wood: Foundation of Fertility

The woods surrounding a natural farm should be treated as a natural preserve for the farm and used as a direct or indirect source of organic fertilizer. The basic strategy for achieving long-term, totally fertilizer-free cultivation on a natural farm is to create deep, fertile soil. The protected wood serves this purpose through three primary mechanisms:

### Three Methods of Natural Soil Creation

1. **Direct burial** of coarse organic matter deep in the ground, where it decomposes slowly and creates channels for air and water penetration while building humus in the subsoil
2. **Gradual soil improvement** by planting grasses and trees that send roots deep into the soil, breaking up compacted layers and bringing nutrients from deep strata to the surface through leaf fall
3. **Enrichment of the farm** by carrying nutrients built up in the humus of the upland woods or forest downhill with rainwater or by other means, creating a continuous flow of fertility from the preserve to the cultivated areas

Whatever the means employed, the natural farmer must secure a nearby supply of humus that can serve as a source of soil fertility. Without this, any farming system will eventually deplete the soil and require external inputs.

## Growing a Wood Preserve: Ecological Succession

When there is no uphill wood available for use as a preserve, one can always develop a new wood or bamboo grove for this purpose. Although the main function of a preserve is to serve as a deeply verdant natural wood, one should also plant companion trees that enrich the soil, timber trees, trees that supply food for birds and animals, and trees that provide a habitat for the natural enemies of insect pests.

### Hilltop and Slope Restoration Sequence

Being generally infertile and dry, hill and mountain tops are highly susceptible to denudation. The restoration follows a carefully observed succession pattern:

1. **First stage**: Plant a vine such as kudzu to prevent the soil from washing away. Kudzu's rapid growth and deep root system stabilize the soil surface and begin the process of organic matter accumulation
2. **Second stage**: Sow the seeds of a low conifer such as moss cypress to create a mountain cover of evergreens. The conifers provide year-round protection and begin creating the shaded, moist conditions that other plants need
3. **Early succession stage**: Grasses such as cogon, ferns such as bracken, and low bushes such as lespedeza, eurya, and moss cypress grow thickly at first. These pioneer plants break up the soil surface and add organic matter through their root systems
4. **Later succession stage**: This vegetation gradually gives way to urajiro fern, kudzu, and a mix of trees which further enrich the soil. The increasing shade and soil moisture create conditions favorable for larger trees

### Tree Species Selection by Position

The selection of tree species depends on their position in the landscape, following the principle of matching species to their natural habitat:

**Hillsides**: Japanese cypress and camphor tree as evergreens, interplanted with deciduous trees such as Chinese hackberry, zelkova, paulownia, cherry, maple, and eucalyptus. The mix of evergreen and deciduous species ensures year-round soil protection and a continuous supply of leaf litter.

**Fertile land at foot of hills and in valleys**: Oak and evergreens such as cryptomeria and live oak, interplanted with walnut and ginkgo. These positions receive more water and nutrients from uphill runoff, supporting larger, more demanding species.

**General purpose plantings**: Companion trees for soil enrichment (leguminous species), timber trees for future harvest, bird food trees to support wildlife populations, and trees that provide habitat for the natural enemies of insect pests.

## Shelterbelt Species and Functions

Shelterbelts and windbreak trees are valuable not only for preventing wind damage, but also for maintaining soil fertility and for environmental improvement. The choice between fast-growing and slower-growing species depends on whether immediate protection or long-term stability is the priority.

### Fast-Growing Species for Immediate Protection

- **Cedar**: Rapid growth, dense canopy, valuable timber
- **Cypress**: Similar to cedar but more tolerant of a range of conditions
- **Acacia**: Nitrogen-fixing member of the pea family, provides both wind protection and soil enrichment
- **Camphor tree**: Aromatic, pest-repelling properties, deep-rooted

### Slower-Growing Species for Long-Term Stability

- **Camellia**: Evergreen, dense foliage, ornamental value
- **Umbrella tree**: Broad canopy, good wind resistance when mature
- **Wax myrtle**: Salt-tolerant, useful in coastal areas
- **Chinese anise**: Aromatic, pest-repelling
- **Evergreen oaks**: Deep-rooted, excellent soil stabilizers
- **Holly**: Dense, spiny foliage provides excellent wind filtering

## Bamboo as a Wood Preserve

A bamboo grove may serve equally well as the reserve. It takes a bamboo shoot only one year to grow to full size, so the amount of vegetative growth is greater than for ordinary trees. Bamboo is therefore valuable as a source of coarse organic material that can be buried in the ground for soil improvement.

### Advantages of Bamboo for Soil Building

- Certain species of bamboo produce edible shoots that can be sold as a vegetable, providing income during the establishment phase
- When dried, the wood is light and easy to carry, reducing labor costs for soil improvement operations
- Bamboo is hollow and so has a large void ratio, which helps it retain air and water in the soil when buried
- It decomposes slowly, providing long-term soil structure improvement rather than a burst of rapid decomposition
- The rhizome system of bamboo helps stabilize soil on slopes and prevent erosion

## Orchard Establishment Without Land Clearing

In modern orchards, using bulldozers to clear land has become the rule rather than the exception, but a natural farm should be developed without clearing the land. When land is cleared with a bulldozer, irregular surface features on a slope are flattened and smoothed. Wide farm roads are built to permit farm mechanization. However, mechanization really only facilitates certain farm operations such as fertilizer and pesticide application. Since picking ripened fruit is the only major operation in natural farming, there is no need to go out of one's way to clear steep slopes.

The natural approach preserves the existing topography and soil structure. Vegetation on the hillside is cut in lateral strips, and the large trunks, branches, and leaves of the felled trees are arranged or buried in trenches running along hill contours, covered with earth, and allowed to decompose naturally. None of the vegetation cut down in the orchard should be carried away. This preserves all nutrients on-site and accelerates soil building while maintaining the natural drainage patterns of the slope.

Another factor that improves the enterprising orchardist's chances of success is that a natural orchard can be established without a heavy initial outlay of capital or incurring large debts. The elimination of bulldozer rental, road construction, and land leveling costs makes natural orchard establishment accessible to small-scale farmers.

### Bamboo's Role in the Preserve System

Within the broader farm design, bamboo serves a unique niche that complements the tree-based preserve. While trees take decades to mature and begin contributing significant organic matter, bamboo produces harvestable material within a single year. This rapid turnover makes bamboo an invaluable resource during the early years of farm establishment when the soil most needs organic inputs.

The choice between a tree-based preserve and a bamboo grove depends on local conditions and the farmer's needs. In areas with adequate rainfall and suitable soils, bamboo may be the preferred option due to its rapid growth. In drier or colder areas, trees are more reliable. An ideal approach combines both, with bamboo on moist lower slopes and trees on the drier upper slopes.

The preserve also serves an important function as wildlife habitat. Birds that nest in the preserve feed on orchard pests during the day. Predatory insects that live in the ground cover of the preserve venture into the orchard to hunt. Frogs and toads that breed in the moist shade of the preserve control insect populations throughout the farm. By maintaining a natural wood as part of the farm design, the farmer creates a self-regulating pest management system that requires no pesticides.

## Key Principles

- Protected woods serve as the primary source of long-term soil fertility
- Shelterbelts are multi-functional: wind protection, soil fertility, wildlife habitat, pest control
- Tree species selection should follow ecological succession principles
- Kudzu is the first line of defense against hilltop erosion
- All organic material from clearing should remain on-site as soil-building material
- Bulldozer clearing destroys the natural soil structure and microtopography
- A natural orchard can be established without heavy initial capital outlay
- Bamboo provides the fastest source of coarse organic material for soil building
- The mix of evergreen and deciduous species ensures year-round soil protection
- Shelterbelts of slower-growing species provide more permanent wind protection
- The wood preserve doubles as wildlife habitat and natural pest control
- Preserve design should include soil-enriching, timber, food, and pest-predator trees
