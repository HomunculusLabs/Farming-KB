## Shelterbelt Design and Environmental Tree Planting for Natural Farms

## Overview

Shelterbelts and windbreaks are integral components of Fukuoka's natural farm design. Rather than treating them as mere wind barriers, he views them as multi-functional elements that maintain soil fertility, improve the microclimate, provide [[holmgren-ecological-succession-and-pulsing]] rather than against it.

## Growing a Wood Preserve: Ecological Succession

When there is no uphill wood available for use as a preserve, one can always develop a new wood or bamboo grove for this purpose. Although the main function of a preserve is to serve as a deeply verdant natural wood, one should also plant companion trees that enrich the soil, timber trees, trees that supply food for birds and animals, and trees that provide a habitat for the natural enemies of insect pests.

### Hilltop and Slope Restoration Sequence

Being generally infertile and dry, hill and mountain tops are highly susceptible to denudation. The restoration follows a carefully observed succession pattern:

1. **First stage**: Plant a vine such as kudzu to prevent the soil from washing away. Kudzu's rapid growth and deep root system stabilize the soil surface and begin the process of organic matter accumulation
2. **Second stage**: Sow the seeds of a low conifer such as moss cypress to create a mountain cover of evergreens. The conifers provide year-round protection and begin creating the shaded, moist conditions that other plants need
3. **Early succession stage**: Grasses such as cogon, ferns such as bracken, and low bushes such as lespedeza, eurya, and moss cypress grow thickly at first. These pioneer plants break up the soil surface and add organic matter through their root systems
4. **Later succession stage**: This vegetation gradually gives way to urajiro fern, kudzu, and a mix of trees which further enrich the soil. The increasing shade and soil moisture create conditions favorable for larger trees

### Tree Species Selection by Position

The selection of tree species depends on their position in the landscape, following the principle of matching species to their natural habitat:

**Hillsides**: Japanese cypress and camphor tree as evergreens, interplanted with deciduous trees such as Chinese hackberry, zelkova, paulownia, cherry, maple, and eucalyptus. The mix of evergreen and deciduous species ensures year-round soil protection and a continuous supply of leaf litter.

**Fertile land at foot of hills and in valleys**: Oak and evergreens such as cryptomeria and live oak, interplanted with walnut and ginkgo. These positions receive more water and nutrients from uphill runoff, supporting larger, more demanding species.

**General purpose plantings**: Companion trees for soil enrichment (leguminous species), timber trees for future harvest, bird food trees to support wildlife populations, and trees that provide habitat for the natural enemies of insect pests.

## Shelterbelt Species and Functions

Shelterbelts and windbreak trees are valuable not only for preventing wind damage, but also for maintaining soil fertility and for environmental improvement. The choice between fast-growing and slower-growing species depends on whether immediate protection or long-term stability is the priority.

### Fast-Growing Species for Immediate Protection

- **Cedar**: Rapid growth, dense canopy, valuable timber
- **Cypress**: Similar to cedar but more tolerant of a range of conditions
- **Acacia**: Nitrogen-fixing member of the pea family, provides both wind protection and soil enrichment
- **Camphor tree**: Aromatic, pest-repelling properties, deep-rooted

### Slower-Growing Species for Long-Term Stability

- **Camellia**: Evergreen, dense foliage, ornamental value
- **Umbrella tree**: Broad canopy, good wind resistance when mature
- **Wax myrtle**: Salt-tolerant, useful in coastal areas
- **Chinese anise**: Aromatic, pest-repelling
- **Evergreen oaks**: Deep-rooted, excellent soil stabilizers
- **Holly**: Dense, spiny foliage provides excellent wind filtering
