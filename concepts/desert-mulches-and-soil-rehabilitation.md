## Desert Mulches and Soil [[permaculture]], mulch serves multiple critical functions: moisture conservation, soil temperature regulation, pH modification, nutrient cycling, and erosion prevention. A well-designed mulching strategy is arguably the single most important factor in establishing productive gardens in arid environments.

## Sources of Mulch in Deserts

### Flood Detritus
The detritus brought down in flood can be arrested on strong mulch-trap fences. This is often a significant source of organic material in desert waterways.

### Wind-Collected Material
Tumbleweeds and wisps of plants blown by wind will settle in pits, swales, or be trapped on fences.

### Grown Mulch
Mulch produced within gardens and orchards from dedicated species:

- [[casuarina]]
- Bamboo
- Tamarisk
- Comfrey
- Some species of Acacia
- Forage grasses and legumes (for their mulch value or nitrogenous soil fixation)
- Desert vines (from trimmings)
- Hedge species

### Household and Storage Wastes
All organic household waste can contribute to mulch supplies.

### Grazers on Range
Animals regularly penned provide mulch-manure resources for garden use.

## Categories of Mulches

### 1. Domestic Mulches
Old skins, blankets, cotton or wool clothes, cardboard boxes, newspapers, hardboard, and thin planks. All of these, when soaked, can be used to:

- Line sandy pits
- Be tiled over the ground in raised beds before mulch is added

### 2. Collected Mulches
Ashes, bones, dog manure, cattle or horse manure. These should be:

- Kept dry, or
- Pitted in circle pits for in-ground compost

Ashes and dry herbivore manure can be shredded or pounded for easier application.

### 3. Fines
Fine organic materials including:

- Chaff, bark, leaves
- Tea leaves, coffee grounds
- Flood detritus
- Rice or grain hulls
- Rotten wood
- Sawdust
- Shredded paper or wood chips

These are ideally combined with shredded manures, sand, and ashes to fill up raised beds. They can be laid as a top or coarse mulch layer.

### 4. Coarse Mulch
Larger organic materials:

- Logs, twigs
- Dry straw
- Thick bark
- Old wood

These are used as:

- Bed edges
- A 4–6 cm layer over which mixed soils and "fines" are spread 18–20 cm thick

### 5. Toppings
Cosmetic or finishing mulch layers:

- Seagrass
- Woodchips
- Pine or Casuarina needles
- Cocoa bean husks

These can be spread over the top of earlier layers as a protective covering.

## Constructed Mulched Beds

A properly constructed mulched bed follows this layered structure (from bottom to top):

1. **Coarse mulch base** — 4–6 cm of logs, twigs, dry straw, thick bark, or old wood at the bed edges
2. **Mixed soil layer** — 18–20 cm of combined soils, fines (chaff, leaves, compost), shredded manures, and ashes
3. **Top dressing** — seagrass, woodchips, pine needles, or similar material

Very thick (18–20 cm) mulched soils conserve water and produce good humus. Methods of planting seedlings, fine seeds, and tubers are shown for each bed type. These "instant" gardens are made this way in drylands — all stages are watered as completed, and mulch is built up progressively.

## Green Mulch Sources for Orchards

Good green mulches for grapes and tree crops can be gathered from:

- Catch crops of lupins
- Fava beans
- Coppiced Acacia or mallee eucalypt

These can be cut after rains and stored as:

- Silage
- In earth pits for later use

Lucerne establishes well in swales and damp areas. Casuarina leaf or pine leaf can be regularly gathered from trees in the field. Mulch must largely be relied upon to:

- Bring down the pH of alkaline soils
- Make minerals available

## Nutrient Dynamics in Desert Mulch

### Nitrogen Fixation
In mulch, free-living nitrogen-fixing organisms supply plant nutrients at a steady rate, and leaching is delayed or prevented.

### Nutrient Losses
In tropical drylands, the greatest losses occur in bare soil agriculture, when nitrogen, calcium, and potassium are leached to streams and groundwater. Deep humus, mulch, or green manures largely prevent this.

### Dolomite Requirement
Wherever deep humus, mulch, or green manures are used, dolomite should be provided. This is because nitrates prevent copper and manganese mobility if no calcium or manganese is supplied.

## The Termite and Ant Principle

When mulching in deserts, the approach should emulate the ant and the termite, who bury their organic materials out of the sun. Practical applications include:

- Placing a layer of stone, sand, or soil over desert mulches
- Creating hollows where trapped leaves will be later covered with sand

This burial strategy protects organic matter from rapid solar decomposition and preserves moisture.

## Mycorrhizal Associations

Drought stress in cultivated plants is greatly ameliorated if plants are provided with **mycorrhiza** (root fungi). The relationship is mutually beneficial:

- **From the host plants**, root associates get shelter (in a safe root environment) and sugars
- **In return**, they scavenge for soil nutrients and can mobilise phosphorus even at very low water levels in the soil

It is true to say that many plants don't have roots — they have fungal mycelia to explore the minute world of soil particles.
