# Macrofungal Fruiting Phenology and Climate Variability

The fruiting phenology of macrofungi is profoundly influenced by
climatic variables, making these organisms sensitive indicators of
environmental change. Understanding the relationship between fruiting
patterns and climate is essential for biodiversity monitoring,
ecological assessment, and detecting the impacts of global environmental
perturbations on fungal communities.

## Seasonal and Geographic Controls on Fruiting

Within a geographic region, fruiting of macrofungi is influenced by
elevation and latitude and their effects on temperature and
precipitation. A particular species may fruit at different seasons
across wide geographic distances or along strong elevational gradients.
This means that phenological patterns are inherently place-specific and
must be calibrated to local conditions.

In temperate regions with summer drought, spring and fall are the main
fruiting seasons. The "fall" season is progressively later at lower
latitudes, in some regions occurring after the winter solstice. In
temperate regions with summer rain and at high elevations and latitudes,
summer may be the most important season for fruiting.

The timing of fruiting is thus not fixed but responds dynamically to
climatic conditions, creating both opportunities and challenges for
monitoring programs.

## Temperature Effects on Fruiting

Temperature has a major impact on macrofungal fruiting, an effect that
may not be limited to the fruiting season itself. Research by Ohenoja
(1993) documented the effects of year-round temperatures on fall
fruiting of macrofungi. Temperature was found to interact with habitat
and ecological guild in stimulating or retarding fruiting:

- Warm summers increased the production of mycorrhizal sporocarps in
  spruce forests but had no effect on sporocarp production in pine
  forests
- Year-round temperature regimes appear to precondition mycelia for
  autumn fruiting events
- The interaction between temperature and host tree species modulates
  the response of different fungal guilds

These differential responses to temperature among fungal guilds and
host associations suggest that climate change could restructure fungal
communities through altered phenological patterns, not only through
direct effects on survival.

## Precipitation as a Fruiting Trigger

Precipitation is a critical driver of macrofungal fruiting. Although
fruiting of macrofungi is most often limited by lack of precipitation,
excess moisture can also prevent fruiting in some species. Unusual
weather events can trigger unusual patterns of fruiting; in regions
with dry summers, fruiting of species rarely observed in the region may
follow a summer shower.

Lodge (1999) found precipitation to be better than vegetation type as
a predictor of species richness and community structure of
ectomycorrhizal fungi at local scales. The large variation found among
sites in a single forest zone in their study points to the need for
sampling multiple sites with similar host tree species to capture
precipitation-driven variation in fungal communities.

## Annual Variation and Long-Term Trends

Annual variation in presence of sporocarps can be enormous:

- Only 5% to 20% of ectomycorrhizal species encountered at each of
  eight sites in Olympic National Park fruited in 2 successive years
- Combining data from all sites, about 30% of species were detected in
  both years (O'Dell and Ammirati 1994; O'Dell et al. 1999)
- Winterhoff (cited in Arnolds 1992) studied five sites for 5 years
  and found that from 8% to 88% of total reported species from a
  single site were detected in any given year
- Several species in the Entolomataceae fruited every second or third
  year in a wet subtropical forest in Puerto Rico, whereas a few other
  species were found only during 1 year of a 13-year survey

In a 21-year study of fungal fruiting phenology in Switzerland,
Straatsma and colleagues (2001) found that some species fruited only
during the 1 year that was particularly rich in species and that
fruiting abundance and species richness were correlated. Critically,
species richness estimators did not stabilize during the 21 years of
study.

## Implications for Biodiversity Assessment

The extreme annual variation in fruiting has profound implications for
biodiversity inventory and monitoring:

- Species once present at a site that fail to fruit in subsequent years
  may be present and physiologically active but not fruiting, present
  as dormant propagules, or extirpated
- Causes of such variation are poorly understood but likely involve
  complex interactions between temperature, precipitation, and
  biotic factors
- The challenge of measuring species diversity increases in years when
  many species fruit simultaneously, as specimens may decay before
  adequate documentation
- Long-term studies (at least 5 years, preferably 10 years) are needed
  to capture a representative sample of the fungal community

## Climate Change Implications

Changes in temperature and precipitation patterns associated with
global climate change are expected to alter fungal fruiting phenology
in several ways:

- Shifts in the timing and duration of fruiting seasons
- Altered relationships between temperature and mycorrhizal sporocarp
  production
- Changes in the frequency of mass-fruiting events
- Potential decoupling of fungal fruiting from host plant phenology
- Range shifts that alter the species pool available for fruiting

Baseline data from herbaria and long-term monitoring programs are
essential for detecting such changes. Herbarium collections contain
historical information necessary to examine changes in species
compositions, abundances, and fruiting patterns caused by natural
events and human activities.
