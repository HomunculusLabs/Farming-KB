# Deep-Organic Farming Philosophy

## Overview

Deep-organic farming, as articulated by [[eliot-coleman]], represents a philosophy of agriculture that goes far beyond the mere absence of synthetic chemicals. It is a commitment to producing food of the highest nutritional quality by working in harmony with natural systems—managing biological processes with skill and care rather than substituting industrial inputs for them. Deep organics stands in contrast to "shallow organics," where the organic label is met through minimum compliance while the underlying industrial mindset of maximizing volume over quality remains unchanged.

## Shallow vs. Deep Organic

The distinction between shallow and deep organic farming is fundamental. Shallow organic operations meet the legal requirements to use the USDA organic label but stop there. They may feed organic grain to confined animals, ship products long distances, ultra-pasteurize milk to extend shelf life, and prioritize yield over flavor or nutrition. The organic version of a processed food—what Coleman calls the "organic Twinkie"—is immaterial regardless of who controls the company.

Deep-organic farmers, by contrast, focus on quality as their primary goal. They raise chickens on grass pasture where sunshine and green forage produce eggs with deep orange yolks and superior flavor. They maintain dairy herds on pasture where cows eat grass as they were evolutionarily designed to, producing raw milk richer in key nutrients. They grow varieties selected for taste and nutritional value rather than transportability or uniform appearance.

Coleman notes that the organic companies bought out by industrial food giants are precisely those whose quality is so low that informed consumers wouldn't want their products regardless of the label. Small, committed, deep-organic family farms represent the fastest growing segment in U.S. agriculture, in part because they are too focused on quality to attract acquisition interest.

## The Right Question: Nutrition, Not Just "Organic"

A central tenet of deep-organic philosophy is reframing the question from "is it organic?" to "is it nutritious?" Coleman draws on paleopathological research showing that hunter-gatherers enjoyed superior health and stature compared to modern humans, largely attributable to their natural diet. Since deep-organic agriculture is a human-managed copy of natural processes, it holds the potential to produce food as nutritious as those wild foods.

Achieving this requires:

- **Variety selection** for taste and nutritional value (factors that are closely related)
- **Growing conditions** aimed at optimizing the plant's physiological potential
- **Soil management** focused on delivering every mineral in balance, with attention to trace elements, soil aeration, and exceptional compost

The parallel is clear: just as organic farming demonstrated that it is best for soil health to not have the soil food supply manipulated by industry, it follows that it is best for human health to not have human food treated the same way.

## Real Food

Coleman advocates for food that is not merely organic but "real." Real food is fresh, whole, and minimally processed. It is the opposite of products made with white flour, soy margarine, corn syrup, and preservatives that bear an organic label. The concept extends to a vision for a "Wholier Than Thou Market" that would sell:

- Whole grain breads and crackers made fresh daily
- Wheat, oat, and rye berries for customers to grind into fresh flour
- Raw milk and butter from local grass-fed herds
- Fresh-squeezed juices into customers' own containers
- Local, range-fed meat, poultry, and eggs
- Fresh year-round fruits and vegetables from nearby fields and greenhouses
- Only traditional processed foods: cheese, yogurt, sauerkraut, pickles, dried tomatoes, wine, and beer
- Only honey and maple syrup as sweeteners

Such a store would eliminate middlemen, energy-intensive shipping, and the need for preservatives. Coleman quotes nutritionist Joan Gussow's description of processed organic products as "value added, nutritionally degraded."

## The Relationship with Nature

A recurring theme in deep-organic philosophy is that humans mistakenly consider nature imperfect and try to improve it, when in fact the human relationship with nature is what needs improving. Biotechnology companies advertise "Think what's possible"—assuming they can remake the parts of nature they don't understand. Coleman argues that if they understood those systems, they would realize they don't need remaking.

The approach is to operate more intelligently within natural rules rather than trying to change them. This means managing natural processes with the greatest possible skill to produce food containing everything nature designed it to contain.

## Resilience of the Small Farm

Coleman sees deep-organic farming as the salvation of the small family farm. These operations won't be bought out because they are too honest and too focused on quality over quantity. There will always be demand for exceptional food from astute customers who can see past the marketing of the USDA label and make fully informed decisions about food quality.

The philosophy connects soil health directly to human health: just as organic farmers since the 1930s have turned conventional thinking around regarding soil nutrition, the year-round availability of fresh, local, real food from deep-organic farms can do the same for human nutrition.

## Key Principles Summary

1. **Quality over quantity** in every production decision
2. **Nutrition as the standard**, not mere compliance with organic certification
3. **Work with natural systems** rather than trying to override them
4. **Freshness and locality** as non-negotiable requirements
5. **Soil health as the foundation** of food quality, with attention to mineral balance and biological activity
6. **Minimal processing** to preserve the nutritional integrity of food
7. **Direct relationships** between growers and eaters, eliminating unnecessary intermediaries
