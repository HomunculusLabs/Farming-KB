## Diversity and Importance of Soil Fungal Communities

Fungi have been components of terrestrial microbial communities since the Ordovician period, including in deserts, metal-rich and hypersaline habitats. In soil, they can comprise the largest pool of biomass, even exceeding that of other microorganisms and invertebrates. They play a critical role in maintenance of soil structure through their filamentous branching growth habit and exopolymer production. Their explorative growth, high surface-area-to-mass ratio, and ability to form hydrated mucilaginous sheaths surrounding hyphae ensure that fungal processes are an integral component of biogeochemical change.

Fungal communities are ubiquitous components of the microflora of all rocks and building stone, throughout a wide range of geographical and climatic zones. Lithic (rock-associated) fungal communities are essential components of microbial communities found with a wide variety of mineral types, playing many roles important in nutrient, metal and carbon cycling. Defining these communities to ascertain their diversity and elucidate their environmental impact is an important aspect of biogeochemistry.

## Challenges in Community Assessment

### Limitations of Culture-Based Methods

Traditional culture-based methods for assessing fungal diversity are now generally recognized as insufficient. At best, perhaps only 5% of environmental fungi can be cultivated, based on the estimated total number of fungal species versus described species. This has led to systematic underestimation of the complexity and diversity of fungal communities, owing to bias associated with traditional microbiological methods. Species refractory to laboratory cultivation include:

- Biotrophs (rust or smut fungi) that require living hosts
- Many basidiomycetes with complex nutritional requirements
- Arbuscular endomycorrhizas, particularly the Glomales, which cannot be grown without plant hosts
- Rock-dwelling fungi with low metabolic activity adapted to oligotrophic conditions

Reliance on morphological and physiological identification of cultivable species will never elucidate the inherent complexities of community dynamics; this is especially true for complex in situ ecosystems.

### Bias in Molecular Methods

While molecular approaches overcome cultivation bias, they introduce their own biases that must be acknowledged:

- DNA extraction efficiency varies between different species and between spores and mycelia
- PCR bias from cycling conditions, formation of chimeric sequences, sequence error and primer choice
- 18S rRNA gene has limited resolution due to relative lack of evolutionary variation between closely related fungal species
- rRNA operon copy numbers vary between different fungal species, making quantification difficult
- Soil DNA extraction kits are often unsuccessful for rock samples
- Lysis efficiency varies between species and life stages

## Molecular Methods for Fungal Community Analysis

### Phylogenetic Approaches (Species Identification)

Cloning of amplified rRNA gene pools, followed by sequencing and database comparison, provides species lists and phylogenetic context. Molecular characterization of fungal communities typically targets:

- Small subunit SSU (18S) rDNA: broad phylogenetic resolution but limited for closely related species
- Internal transcribed spacer (ITS) rDNA: located between 18S and 28S rRNA genes and incorporating the 5.8S rRNA gene; increasingly used owing to higher degree of heterogeneity between species, allowing improved community resolution

Ectomycorrhizal fungal communities are extremely diverse and have been studied to a great degree by molecular techniques. Fungal databases are growing in size, though prokaryotic databases remain more extensive.

### Fingerprinting Approaches (Community Comparison)

Fingerprinting methods allow multiple sample analysis for spatial and temporal variation in community composition:

- DGGE/TGGE: denaturing or temperature gradient gel electrophoresis exploits differences in melting behaviour of 18S rRNA gene sequences; used for decomposing leaves, coniferous forests, wheat rhizosphere, and endolithic cyanobacterial communities
- RISA/ARISA: ribosomal intergenic spacer analysis exploits length variations within the ITS region; ARISA is a high-throughput, automated version using fluorescently labelled primers; community complexity ranges from approximately 30 to over 200 fungal peaks per profile
- T-RFLP: terminal restriction fragment length polymorphism digests fluorescently labelled PCR products with restriction enzymes; capable of higher resolution than gel electrophoresis; pseudo-terminal restriction fragments from incomplete digestion may overestimate diversity

## Key Findings from Molecular Studies

### Rhizosphere Communities

Healthy black spruce seedlings had higher proportions of Homobasidiomycete clones, whereas diseased samples had more Sordariomycetes. Rhizospheres of young maize plants selected for the ascomycete order Pleosporales, while senescent maize had different Ascomycetes and basidiomycetous yeasts. These findings demonstrate that host plant health and age dramatically structure fungal communities, with implications for plant disease management.

### Grassland Soil Communities

18S rDNA cloning followed by ARDRA detected 28 fungal species in grassland soil, including Zygomycetes, Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes. However, the 18S region was insufficient to distinguish closely related species, indicating the need for more variable genetic markers such as the ITS region.

### Rock-Associated Communities

Studies on weathered pegmatitic granite using ARISA combined with geochemical analyses and multivariate statistics found that average sample ribotype numbers ranged from 25 on K-feldspar, 23 on granite and muscovite, 20 on plagioclase, to only 4 on quartz. Fungal community structure differed significantly between each mineral sample, as indicated by a randomization test. Canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) revealed strong relations between individual ribotypes and particular chemical elements, with ribotypes almost exclusively restricted to single mineral types. This demonstrated that individual chemical elements exert selective pressure on fungal community structure.

### Endolithic Communities

In McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica, cloning approaches identified several distinct cryptoendolithic communities. Putative identification of abundant fungal clones revealed homology to Texosporium sancti-jacobi, Bullera unica and Geomyces pannorum. These studies show that similar molecular approaches could provide excellent baselines for further geomycological studies across diverse rock types and minerals.

### Temporal Dynamics

T-RFLP studies of salt marsh fungal communities over one year found considerable temporal variability with little to moderate spatial variability. Phaeosphaeria spartinicola, P. halima and Mycosphaerella sp. were present in all samples, suggesting persistent roles in organic matter processing despite community-level shifts.

## Soil Environment and Community Dynamics

Soil pH dramatically affects fungal morphology and community composition. The mycelial networks of cord-forming Basidiomycota are continuously re-modelled in response to local nutritional or environmental cues, interaction with other fungi, grazing by invertebrates and destructive disturbance events. This occurs through a complex combination of growth, branching, hyphal fusion and regression. Different fungi respond differently in space and time, leading to different long-term behaviour and foraging strategies that structure community composition.

Applying statistical analysis such as principal components analysis (PCA) and canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) can give accurate estimates of microbial diversity from assorted environments as well as delivering quantitative means of relating microbial community composition to environmental factors and conditions.
