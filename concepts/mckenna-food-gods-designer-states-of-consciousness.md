# Designer States of Consciousness Critique in Food of the Gods

One of the most provocative arguments in *Food of the Gods* is Terence
McKenna's critique of what he calls "designer states of consciousness,"
the manufactured modes of awareness imposed on modern people by
advertising, mass media, and consumer culture. For McKenna, the real
"drug problem" is not the use of psychedelics but the pervasive
chemical and cultural manipulation of consciousness by dominator
institutions.

## The Drug Question Reversed

McKenna opens his critique by reframing the fundamental question about
drugs in society. The issue, he argues, is not whether people will use
drugs, but rather: "How drugged shall you be? Or, to put it another
way, how conscious shall you be? Who shall be conscious? Who shall be
unconscious?" This reframing shifts the focus from the relatively small
number of people who use illegal psychoactive substances to the vast
majority who are maintained in states of manufactured distraction and
compliance by legal and institutional means.

McKenna offers a definition of "drug" that departs sharply from
conventional usage: "A drug is something that causes unexamined,
obsessive, and habitual behavior. You don't examine obsessive behavior;
you just do it. You let nothing get in the way of your gratification."
By this definition, television, consumerism, and social conformity are
far more powerful drugs than any psychedelic compound.

## The Manufactured Herd

McKenna describes modern consumer society as a system designed to
produce interchangeable human modules within a very large machine. He
uses the image of Los Angeles seen from an airliner, with its curved
driveways and cul-de-sacs with identical housing modules installed along
each one, as a metaphor for the homogenization of consciousness. As
long as the Reader's Digest stays subscribed to and the television
stays on, these modules remain interchangeable parts within a system
that allows no genuine individuality or critical thought.

This, for McKenna, is the "nightmarish reality" that Marshall McLuhan
and Wyndham Lewis foresaw: the creation of the public as herd. The
public has no history and no future; it lives in a golden moment created
by a credit system that binds people ineluctably to a web of illusions
that is never critiqued. McKenna sees this as the ultimate consequence
of having broken off the symbiotic relationship with what he calls the
"Gaian matrix of the planet," the legacy of imbalance between the sexes,
and the terminal phase of a long descent into meaninglessness.

## The Antidrug Position

McKenna argues that the pro-psychedelic position, when fully and
logically thought through, is actually an antidrug, antiaddiction
position. Psychedelics, unlike the substances and behaviors that
sustain consumer culture, promote examination, questioning, and the
dissolution of habitual patterns of thought and behavior. They are, in
McKenna's formulation, the only counterflow directed against the
tendency to leave people in designer states of consciousness created
not by their own choices but by the designs of advertising agencies,
the military establishment, and multinational corporations.

This is not merely metaphor, McKenna insists; it is "really happening
to us." The systematic manipulation of consciousness through mass media
and consumer culture represents a form of social engineering that is
far more pervasive and effective than any pharmacological intervention,
yet it goes largely unexamined and unchallenged.

## Psychedelics as Exopheromones

McKenna develops the concept of psychedelics as "exopheromones,"
chemical signals that act between members of a species to dissolve the
dominant ego and promote connection with the broader matrix of
experience. In this framing, psychedelics are not recreational
substances but evolutionary tools that promote the expansion of
consciousness and the reconnection of individuals with the collective
mind.

He also describes psychedelics as "enzymes that synergize the human
imagination and empower language." They cause people to connect and
reconnect the contents of the collective mind in ever more implausible,
beautiful, and self-fulfilling ways. This creative, connective function
stands in direct opposition to the fragmenting, homogenizing effects
of mass culture.

## The Role of Shamanism

McKenna argues that the shamanic response to the crisis of manufactured
consciousness should be to "locate the art pedal and push it to the
floor." This is one of the primary functions of shamanism, and it is
tremendously synergized by the psychedelics. Art, imagination, and
visionary experience are the antidotes to the beige fascism of consumer
culture, and the shamanic tradition provides the techniques for
accessing these states in a structured and purposeful way.

If people are serious about what McKenna calls the [[mckenna-food-gods-archaic-revival]], then they need a new paradigmatic image that can carry them rapidly forward and through what he calls the "historical choke point" that impedes a more expansive, humane, and caring dimension of existence.

## The Unsung Heroes

McKenna credits the botanists and chemists who preserved and
rediscovered the psychoactive plants and compounds that offer an
alternative to manufactured consciousness: Richard Schultes, R. Gordon
Wasson and his wife Valentina, and Albert Hofmann. Thanks to them, in
this most chaotic of centuries, humanity has taken into its frail hands
the means to do something about the predicament of manufactured
consciousness. Psychology, by contrast, McKenna describes as complacent
and silent, having been content with behaviorist theory-making for fifty
years while knowing it was doing a potentially fatal disservice to
human dignity by ignoring the potential of psychedelics.
