# Mature Garden Creation Through Accelerated Succession

Since landscapes have an irresistible tendency to mature, why not hop
on board the successionary freight train and take advantage of nature's
momentum? Hemenway argues that the goal of ecological gardening is not
to maintain an immature landscape through constant effort, but to
accelerate the natural process of succession toward a mature, self-
sustaining ecosystem.

## The Problem with Immature Gardens

Conventional gardens mimic immature ecosystems. They are usually
dominated by early-succession plants. Most grasses, flowers, and
especially annual vegetables are pioneer species. This means that in
our love of lawns and orderly gardens, we are attempting to keep our
yards at an early stage of ecological development.

An immature ecosystem like a lawn demands that we expend time, energy,
and materials to wrench back the hands of the ecological clock, holding
the land at prairie phase with mowing and herbicides. The bare earth
and disturbed soil of a vegetable garden sing a siren song to weeds,
which eagerly cover naked ground, pull nutrients out of underlying
rock, and prepare the locale for a more mature ecosystem.

## How Succession Works

When plants first colonize bare earth, a progression begins. Certain
types of annual grasses, herbs, and flowers are the first flora to
arrive, and because of their penchant for speedy colonization, they
are called pioneer plants. They are well adapted to invading naked or
disturbed soil and mantling the floral emptiness with green.

If these weeds are left alone, in a few seasons the short, early
annuals are crowded and shaded out by a taller, mostly perennial crew.
In five to fifteen years the weedy field will be clothed instead with
perennial shrubs. With enough rain and fertility, in two or more
decades the shrubs will give way to a young forest.

The amount of living matter, called biomass, increases as nutrients
and sunlight are gathered and transformed into tough stalks, thick
greenery, and hardy seeds, which in turn become food for insects and
other animals. Life is scaffolding its way into new territory.

## Immature vs Mature Ecosystems

Hemenway provides a detailed comparison of immature and mature
ecosystems that reveals important trends. As a landscape matures:

**Organic matter**: Low in immature ecosystems, high in mature ones.
Plants, animals, and rich soil all build up over time.

**Source of mineral nutrients**: In immature ecosystems, nutrients
come from nonliving sources such as rocks and rainfall. In mature
ecosystems, nutrients come from biological sources: plants, animals,
and humus.

**Mineral cycles**: Open in immature ecosystems with many imports
needed, closed in mature ecosystems with recycling dominant.

**Loss of nutrients**: High in immature systems, low in mature ones
where nutrients are captured and cycled within the system.

**Role of decomposers**: Unimportant in immature ecosystems,
critically important in mature ones where they drive nutrient
cycling.

**Microclimates**: Few, harsh, and shaped by nonliving forces in
immature ecosystems. Many, mild, and shaped by plants in mature
ones.

**Dominant plants**: Annuals dominate immature systems, perennials
dominate mature ones.

## The Annual Vegetable Garden Problem

In the annual garden, the soil is bare many months of the year. The
climate is harsh and varies wildly, as the sun bakes the ground in
summer, and freeze-thaw cycles heave the exposed soil in winter.
Because the short plants are poor protection, wind blasts the ground
and rain pounds the soil, washing away nutrients.

Even more fertility is carried off each year as the vegetables are
harvested and the bare stalks are yanked up during fall cleanup. Thus
the nutrient cycles are open, in straight lines: into the garden and
then out rather than closed with lots of recycling. Fertility must be
imported to replace all that is lost from leaching, erosion, and the
near-total removal of plants.

## Accelerating Succession

The Bullock brothers created a rich landscape quickly, by working with
nature rather than against her. Some of the many techniques they used
include accelerated succession. With a nudge here and a tweak there,
we can actually accelerate succession, using nature to help a garden
mature much faster than it otherwise would.

Practical methods for accelerating succession include:

- Planting fast-growing nitrogen-fixing shrubs and trees to quickly
  build soil and canopy
- Using [[gaias-garden-sheet-mulching]] to eliminate weeds and build
  soil without tilling
- Installing [[gaias-garden-nurse-plants]] to shelter young desired
  species
- Adding large amounts of organic matter to jump-start soil biology
- Selecting species that prepare the ground for later successional
  stages
- Using [[gaias-garden-cover-crops-soil-building]] to build fertility
  quickly

## The Mature Garden Vision

In the ecological garden, we are creating well-developed, productive,
and lush landscapes very quickly by riding the tracks already laid
down by nature. The result is a landscape that looks and acts like a
natural woodland but produces food, medicine, habitat, and beauty for
people. Once established, these mature gardens require far less work
than conventional landscapes because they cycle their own nutrients,
manage their own pests, and conserve their own water.
