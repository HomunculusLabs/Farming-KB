## What Is Kombucha

Kombucha is a fermented tea beverage produced using a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast (SCOBY). The fermentation process converts sugar and tea compounds into organic acids, B vitamins, enzymes, and probiotics. The result is a lightly effervescent, tangy drink that has been brewed for centuries, likely originating in Manchuria.

## The SCOBY

The SCOBY (Symbiotic Culture of Bacteria and Yeast) is a gelatinous cellulose mat that forms on the surface of fermenting kombucha. It houses:

- Acetobacter species (acetic acid bacteria) — produce acetic acid, giving kombucha its tang
- Gluconacetobacter — produces gluconic acid and builds the cellulose pellicle
- Various yeast strains (Saccharomyces, Zygosaccharomyces) — consume sugar and produce ethanol and CO2
- Lactobacillus species — contribute lactic acid and additional probiotics

A healthy SCOBY is opaque, cream-colored, and smooth. Dark spots, mold (fuzzy and dry), or an unusually foul smell indicate contamination — discard the batch and start fresh.

## Tea Base Selection

The tea provides nitrogen, minerals, and flavor compounds essential for fermentation.

- Black tea — most traditional, robust flavor, highest nitrogen content for the SCOBY
- Green tea — lighter, grassier flavor; works well blended with black
- Oolong tea — complex, floral mid-point between black and green
- White tea — delicate, subtle; best blended with a stronger tea
- Herbal teas (chamomile, hibiscus, rooibos) — use sparingly; avoid oils (peppermint, Earl Grey bergamot) as they can inhibit fermentation

A common approach is a 50/50 blend of black and green tea. Always use real tea leaves or bags — not flavored or oil-infused teas for the primary ferment.

## Sugar

The SCOBY needs sugar as fuel. Most sugar is consumed during fermentation; finished kombucha contains far less sugar than the starting sweet tea.

- White cane sugar — standard, cleanest fermentation
- Organic evaporated cane juice — slight mineral benefit
- Honey — use raw and unfiltered; may slow fermentation initially
- Avoid artificial sweeteners, stevia, or monk fruit — the SCOBY cannot metabolize them

Typical ratio: 1 cup sugar per gallon of tea. Dissolve sugar fully in hot tea before cooling.

## Primary Fermentation (7-30 Days)

### Equipment

- Glass vessel (1-gallon jar is standard) — never use metal or plastic for primary ferment
- Breathable cover (coffee filter, paper towel, or cloth) secured with a rubber band
- pH strips or meter (optional but helpful)

### Process

1. Brew 3-4 tea bags in 4 cups boiling water, steep 10-15 minutes
2. Remove tea bags, stir in 1 cup sugar until dissolved
3. Add 8-10 cups cool filtered water (never use chlorinated tap water)
4. Cool the sweet tea to room temperature (below 90F / 32C)
5. Add 1/2 to 1 cup starter tea from a previous batch (or store-bought unflavored kombucha)
6. Gently place the SCOBY into the jar
7. Cover with breathable cloth and secure
8. Ferment at 68-85F (20-29C), ideally 75-78F (24-26C)

### Timeline

- Day 7-10: lightly sweet, mild tang, low acidity — good for beginners
- Day 14-21: balanced sweet-tart, more complex flavor — most popular range
- Day 21-30: very tart, vinegary, low residual sugar — similar to apple cider vinegar

Taste daily after day 7 using a clean straw. Target pH is 2.5-3.5. Lower pH means more acidity and less sugar.

## Second Fermentation (Flavoring and Carbonation)

The second ferment (2F) is where kombucha becomes fizzy and flavored.

1. Remove the SCOBY and 1/2 cup starter liquid — set aside for the next batch
2. Strain the kombucha into bottles (swing-top glass bottles work best)
3. Add flavorings: fresh fruit, fruit juice, herbs, spices, ginger, etc.
4. Leave 1-2 inches of headspace in each bottle
5. Seal tightly and ferment at room temperature for 2-7 days
6. "Burp" bottles daily to release excess pressure and avoid explosions
7. When desired carbonation is reached, refrigerate to halt fermentation

### Popular Flavor Combinations

- Ginger and lemon
- Raspberry and mint
- Blueberry and lavender
- Mango and chili
- Strawberry and basil
- Apple and cinnamon

## Bottling and Storage

- Use glass bottles rated for pressure (swing-top or flip-top)
- Plastic PET bottles can be used as a pressure indicator — if rock-hard, carbonation is ready
- Refrigerated kombucha keeps for 2-4 weeks
- Unrefrigerated, carbonation will continue building — burp regularly

## Troubleshooting

- No SCOBY forming — temperature too low, insufficient starter tea, or chlorinated water
- Mold on SCOBY — discard entire batch, sterilize equipment, start fresh
- Too sweet — ferment longer in primary
- Too sour — reduce primary ferment time or blend with fresh sweet tea
- Flat in 2F — not enough sugar for the yeast; add a small amount of fruit or juice
- Exploding bottles — 2F went too long without burping; always use glass rated for pressure

## Health and Safety

- Kombucha contains trace alcohol (typically 0.5-3% ABV) — legally non-alcoholic but relevant for certain individuals
- Homebrewed kombucha can exceed 0.5% ABV if not monitored
- Those with compromised immune systems, pregnant women, and young children should consult a doctor before consuming
- Start with 4-8 oz per day and increase gradually to assess tolerance
