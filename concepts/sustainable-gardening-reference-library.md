## Sustainable Gardening Reference Library

## Overview

A well-curated reference library is essential for any serious gardener pursuing sustainable, low-input food production. Steve Solomon"s *[[gardening-when-it-counts]]* concludes with an extensive bibliography that draws from both historical and contemporary sources on organic farming, soil science, composting, and vegetable cultivation. These references represent decades of accumulated wisdom from researchers, practitioners, and agricultural pioneers.

## Key Historical Sources

### The Soil and Health Library

The **Soil and Health Library** (www.soilandhealth.org) is highlighted as a critical free online resource offering a collection of classic holistic farming and gardening books and articles, most issued prior to 1960. Solomon recommends that "the serious student of holistic farming or gardening would be wise to read every title in this online resource." This library preserves works that might otherwise be lost and provides access to foundational texts in the organic agriculture movement.

### Albert Howard and the Organic Movement

Sir **Albert Howard** (referenced at page 207 and 217) is a central figure in the bibliography. Howard"s work on composting and soil fertility in India laid the groundwork for the modern organic farming movement. His research demonstrated that healthy soils produce healthy plants resistant to disease and pests — a principle that runs throughout Solomon's approach.

### Ehrenfried Pfeiffer

**Ehrenfried Pfeiffer's** *Soil Fertility, Renewal and Preservation* (Faber & Faber, 1947) is listed as another key text available through the Soil and Health Library. Pfeiffer, a student of Rudolf Steiner's biodynamic agriculture, developed practical [[soil-testing-methods-interpretation]] and contributed significantly to the understanding of compost quality and humus formation.

### George Sheffield Oliver

**George Sheffield Oliver's** *Friend Earthworm: Practical Application of a Lifetime Study of Habits of the Most Important Animal in the World* (1941) focuses on the role of earthworms in soil health. Oliver operated an earthworm farm school and documented how earthworm activity improves soil structure, nutrient cycling, and plant growth.

## Contemporary Resources

### Sustainable Agriculture Research and Education (SARE)

The **Sustainable Agriculture Network** and SARE (www.sare.org) offer substantial collections of topical books available for free download. Two specific publications are highlighted:

- **Building Soils for Better Crops** by Fred Magdoff and Harold van Es — a comprehensive guide to soil management in sustainable systems, available as a free download.
- **Managing Cover Crops Profitably** — a cooperative writing effort organized by the Sustainable Agriculture Network and USDA Agricultural Research Service, providing practical guidance on cover crop selection and management.

### Cooperative Extension Services

Solomon identifies three state Cooperative Extension Services as providing "the largest range of the most carefully considered online materials as of 2005":

- **North Carolina**: www.ces.ncsu.edu/
- **Louisiana**: www.suagcenter.com/
- **New York**: www.ce.cornell.edu/

These university-based services offer region-specific guidance that can be invaluable for adapting general gardening principles to local conditions.

### Bio-Dynamic Composting

A text on bio-dynamic compost preparation is referenced as available through the **Anthroposophic Press** (1976), described as "the best text ever written on composting" and available online through the Soil and Health Library. Bio-dynamic preparations represent one end of the composting spectrum, emphasizing specific herbal and mineral additions to compost piles.

## Important Considerations for Reference Materials

### Critical Evaluation

Solomon does not recommend all sources uncritically. He specifically notes disagreements with certain authors:

- **Poisson and Poisson"s** *Solar Gardening* (1994) introduces interesting structure-based season extenders but Solomon disagrees with their "ill-considered intensive spacing recommendations" and their "derogation of extensive systems as "non-productive.'"
- Solomon recommends his own *Growing Vegetables West of the Cascades* (5th edition, Sasquatch, 2000) but explicitly states "I do not recommend my first three editions," showing honest assessment of how his own thinking evolved.

### Nutritional Research

The bibliography includes a reference to "Vegetables Without Vitamins" from *Life Extension Magazine* (March 2001), highlighting concerns about declining nutrient density in conventionally grown vegetables. This research supports Solomon's emphasis on complete organic fertilization to ensure nutritional quality of homegrown produce.

## Root Development Research

**John E. Weaver and William Bruner's** *Root Development of Vegetable Crops* (McGraw Hill, 1927) is cited as a foundational text on understanding how vegetable root systems develop and function. This work, available through the Soil and Health Library, provides the scientific basis for Solomon's emphasis on proper soil preparation, spacing, and watering practices that support deep, healthy root systems.

## Building Your Own Reference Collection

### Prioritize Free Online Resources

The most accessible starting point is the Soil and Health Library and SARE publications, which together provide hundreds of pages of expert guidance at no cost. These should be thoroughly read before investing in purchased books.

### Seek Out Regional Adaptation

Gardening advice must be adapted to local climate, soil type, and growing season. Cooperative Extension materials from your state or region provide the most relevant guidance for local conditions.

### Study Soil Science Fundamentals

Understanding soil chemistry (pH, CEC, nutrient availability), soil biology (microorganisms, earthworms, mycorrhizae), and soil physics (structure, water retention, drainage) provides the foundation for all other gardening decisions. The references on soil fertility and root development are particularly valuable in this regard.

### Cross-Reference Multiple Sources

No single author has all the answers. Solomon's bibliography itself demonstrates the value of drawing from multiple traditions — scientific agriculture, organic farming, biodynamic methods, and practical gardening experience. Comparing recommendations across sources helps identify both consensus practices and areas where honest disagreement exists.

## The Value of Pre-1960 Agricultural Literature

Much of the most valuable content in the recommended library dates from before 1960. This era predates the widespread adoption of synthetic fertilizers and pesticides, meaning these authors were working with fundamentally organic methods out of necessity. Their practical experience with composting, cover cropping, crop rotation, [[diatomaceous-earth-and-natural-pest-management]] remains highly relevant today and often contains insights lost in the rush toward chemical agriculture.
