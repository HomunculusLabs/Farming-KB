# Pulsatile Nutrient Transport in Fungal Mycelial Networks

## Overview

Research by Bebber, Tlalka, and colleagues (Chapter 1 of *Fungi in the
Environment*) revealed that saprotrophic basidiomycete fungi transport
nutrients through their mycelial networks not by steady bulk flow but by
pronounced **pulsatile oscillations** — rhythmic surges of amino acid
movement superimposed on net translocation patterns. This discovery was
made possible by a novel non-invasive imaging technique: photon-counting
scintillation imaging (PCSI) of radiolabelled compounds.

## Photon-Counting Scintillation Imaging (PCSI)

The technique tracks movement of ¹⁴C-labelled nitrogen compounds
(specifically the non-metabolized amino acid analogue
α-amino-isobutyrate, or ¹⁴C-AIB) through living mycelial networks
growing on an inert scintillation screen. Key advantages over previous
methods (autoradiography, destructive scintillation counting):

- **Non-invasive**: The colony remains intact and functional throughout
  measurement
- **Continuous real-time tracking**: Produces time-series data rather
  than endpoint snapshots
- **Extended duration**: Measurements can continue for over 6 weeks in
  larger microcosms with wood-block inocula and sand/soil substrata

## Discovery of Pulsatile Transport

When ¹⁴C-AIB was supplied to a central inoculum, the radiolabel was
taken up and distributed through the growing colony. Analysis of the
time-series data revealed:

- A **net translocation pattern** — overall movement of nitrogen from
  source to growing margins
- A **strong pulsatile component** superimposed on this net movement,
  particularly through corded (differentiated) transport systems
- Oscillations that continued for 5–7 days after initial loading

### Phase Relationships

Fourier analysis of pixel-by-pixel time-series data produced colour-coded
maps of oscillation frequency, amplitude, and phase across the colony:

- **Assimilatory hyphae** on the inoculum and new resources oscillated
- **Foraging hyphae** at the colony margin also oscillated
- These regions were **out of phase with each other** — when one pulsed,
  the other was quiescent
- Distinct **phase domains** formed with locally synchronized behaviour
- The amplitude pulsing centre shifted toward newly added resources

The out-of-phase relationship between assimilatory and foraging regions
suggests a coordinated pumping mechanism rather than random diffusion.

## Transport Speed and Scale

In larger microcosms with wood-block inocula and compressed sand:

- Within **1 hour** of ¹⁴C-AIB loading, the label had travelled
  **250 mm** along a major cord — a remarkable speed for cytoplasmic
  transport
- Within **4 hours**, signal was present in most of the growing mycelium
  subtended by that cord
- Not all cords transported simultaneously, suggesting selective
  prioritization of transport pathways

## Growth Phases and Resource Response

Colony development occurred in two distinct phases:

### Phase 1: Symmetric Growth
- Area growth and nutrient transport were nearly symmetrical around the
  inoculum
- Duration depended on nutrient availability and developmental age
- Described by a logistic equation

### Phase 2: Asymmetric Foraging
- Transition to sparser, more asymmetric growth
- **Controls without added resources** spontaneously switched to
  asymmetric growth and selectively allocated resources to broad sectors
- **Added damp cellulosic resources** (filter paper) induced marked
  nitrogen accumulation and tightly focused asymmetric growth directed
  toward the new resource
- **Glass-fibre "resources"** produced more variable, often transient
  responses compared to real organic resources

The transition between phases could be normalized across experiments
using two superimposed logistic equations fitted to the data.

## Analytical Framework

The research team developed quantitative metrics for characterizing
nutrient distribution:

- **CMD_AIB**: Centre of mass displacement of ¹⁴C-AIB — measures
  directional bias in nitrogen allocation
- **Conc_D_AIB**: Angular concentration — how tightly focused nitrogen
  distribution is (0 = even, approaching 1 = tightly focused)
- **Alignment**: Angle between CMD_AIB vector and the vector from
  inoculum to bait — measures directed allocation toward resources
- Linear Mixed Effects models enabled statistical comparison between
  control and treatment conditions

Automated colony area segmentation used contrast-limited adaptive
histogram equalization (CLAHE) and Otsu thresholding on scintillation
images, validated against bright-field microscopy of colonies grown on
Mylar film.

## Biological Significance

The pulsatile transport system has several implications for fungal ecology:

- **Coordination without central control**: The phase-organized
  oscillations suggest a decentralized mechanism for coordinating
  nutrient flow across the network without a central command structure
- **Resource prioritization**: The system can rapidly redirect nitrogen
  toward newly discovered resources, with transport speeds far exceeding
  what diffusion alone could achieve
- **Network resilience**: Pulsatile flow through multiple cords provides
  redundancy — if one pathway is damaged, others can compensate
- **Efficiency**: Pulsatile transport may be more energy-efficient than
  continuous bulk flow, concentrating resources during surge periods

The mechanism driving these oscillations remains under investigation.
Potential drivers include rhythmic changes in cytoplasmic streaming,
vacuolar system dynamics, and hyphal turgor pressure oscillations.
