# natural farming guide seed saving and heirloom varieties and Variety Preservation

Mollison considers seed saving a cornerstone of permaculture [[permaculture-self-reliance-and-political-action]]. In
Permaculture: A Designer's Manual, he argues that dependence on commercial seed
suppliers undermines the resilience of food systems and erodes the genetic
diversity built by generations of farmers and gardeners. A permaculture system
should produce, select, and store its own seeds, adapting varieties to local
conditions over successive growing seasons.

## Why Save Seed

Commercial seed production selects for uniformity, transportability, and machine
harvestability -- traits that often conflict with flavor, nutritional value, pest
resistance, and regional adaptability. Open-pollinated varieties saved on-farm
under local conditions undergo natural selection that improves their performance
in that specific environment. Over 5-10 generations, locally saved seed
typically outperforms commercial equivalents for yield and reliability.

Mollison also emphasizes the political dimension: seed saving is an act of
biological independence. Corporate consolidation of the seed industry reduces
available varieties and makes growers dependent on proprietary genetics.

## Basic Principles

**Open-Pollinated Varieties:** Only save seed from open-pollinated (OP)
varieties, not F1 hybrids. Hybrid seed does not breed true; progeny segregate
unpredictably and lose the vigor of the parent cross. Heirloom and heritage
varieties are open-pollinated and have been stabilized through generations of
selection.

**Genetic Diversity:** Maintain sufficient population size to preserve genetic
diversity. For self-pollinating crops (beans, peas, lettuce, tomatoes), save
seed from at least 20-30 plants. For outcrossing crops (brassicas, corn,
beets, carrots), save from 80-200 plants to avoid inbreeding depression.

**Rogueing:** Before seed collection, remove off-type, diseased, or weak
plants. This improves the genetic quality of the next generation.

## Isolation Distances

Outcrossing crops require isolation from other varieties of the same species to
prevent unwanted cross-pollination. Isolation can be achieved by distance,
timing (staggered planting dates), or physical barriers.

Approximate isolation distances for common crops:

- Brassicas (cabbage, broccoli, kale, turnip): 800-1600 m
- Corn (maize): 400-800 m
- Beets and chard: 400-800 m
- Squash and pumpkins: 400-800 m (same species only)
- Carrots: 200-500 m
- Onions and leeks: 200-500 m
- Lettuce: 10-25 m (mostly self-pollinating but some outcrossing)
- Beans (Phaseolus): 10-20 m (mostly self-pollinating)
- Peas: 10-20 m (mostly self-pollinating)
- Tomatoes: 3-10 m (mostly self-pollinating)

For home gardeners with limited space, Mollison suggests caging techniques
(using mesh bags over individual flower heads) and hand-pollination to ensure
varietal purity without large isolation distances.

## Harvesting and Drying

Seed should be harvested when fully mature. For dry-seeded crops (beans, grains,
lettuce, brassicas), allow seed heads to dry on the plant until brittle. Harvest
before shattering becomes severe. For wet-seeded crops (tomatoes, cucumbers,
melons), extract seeds from ripe fruit.

Wet-seeded extraction methods vary by species:

- **Tomatoes:** Ferment pulp and seeds in water for 2-3 days, then rinse and
  dry. Fermentation removes the gelatinous coating that inhibits germination
  and reduces seed-borne diseases.
- **Cucurbits (cucumber, squash, melon):** Rinse seeds free of pulp and dry.
  No fermentation needed for most species.
- **Peppers:** Scrape seeds from ripe fruit and dry.

All seeds must be dried thoroughly before storage. Spread seeds in a single
layer on screens, newspaper, or trays in a warm, dry, well-ventilated area out
of direct sunlight. Ideal drying temperature is 25-35 degrees C. Seeds are
sufficiently dry when they snap rather than bend when folded.

## Storage Conditions

Seed longevity depends on three factors: moisture content, temperature, and
oxygen exposure. Mollison recommends drying seeds to 5-8% moisture content
(measured by weight loss during oven-drying a sample) and storing them in
airtight containers.

- Container: Glass jars with rubber seals, or airtight plastic containers
- Desiccant: Include silica gel packets in storage containers in humid

Expected viability under good storage conditions:

- Legumes, grains: 3-5 years
- Solanaceae (tomato, pepper, eggplant): 4-6 years
- Brassicas: 3-5 years
- Umbellifers (carrot, parsnip, celery): 1-3 years
- Alliums (onion, leek): 1-2 years
- Asteraceae (lettuce): 3-5 years

## Viability Testing

Before planting large quantities of stored seed, test germination rate. The
standard germination test: place 100 seeds (or 25 for limited seed) on damp
paper towel in a sealed plastic bag at room temperature. Count germinated seeds
after 7-14 days depending on species. Multiply by the appropriate factor to
express as a percentage.

The ragdoll test: wrap a counted sample of seeds in damp cloth, keep warm and
moist, and check for sprouting after the expected germination period.

If germination falls below 70%, increase sowing density accordingly or obtain
fresh seed.

## Heirloom and Heritage Preservation

Mollison views heirloom variety preservation as both a practical and ethical
imperative. Many heritage varieties possess unique traits -- [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]],
disease resistance, regional flavor profiles, nutritional characteristics --
that are absent from modern commercial varieties. Losing these genetics is
irreversible.

- Grow multiple varieties of each crop species each season
