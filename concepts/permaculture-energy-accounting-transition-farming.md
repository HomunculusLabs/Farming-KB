the energy economics involved in transitioning from conventional agriculture to

unsustainable because it depends on external energy inputs—primarily fossil

## The Energy Problem in Modern Agriculture

Conventional agriculture operates at a severe energy deficit. For every calorie of
food produced, approximately 10 calories of fossil fuel energy are consumed. This
10:1 ratio is economically viable only because of subsidies and artificially low
fossil fuel costs. Mollison compares three states:

1. **Conventional agriculture**: Heavy external inputs, severe soil loss, water
   pollution. Energy ratio approximately 10:1 against (loss).

2. **Transitional farming** (years 1-7): Gradual replacement of forage grains
   with tree crops, increasing forest cover, adopting minimum tillage.

3. **Mature permaculture** (year 8+): 70% of cropland devoted to tree crops.
   Energy ratio can reach 1:120 gain. Soil is created, water conserved.

## The Transition Period: A 3-8 Year Process

Mollison provides a specific accounting framework for the transition from
conventional farming to permaculture over a period of approximately 3-8 years:

### Year-by-Year Changes

- **Years 1-3**: Begin replacing annual forage grains with tree crops. Introduce
  poultry and small livestock in integrated systems. Start planting windbreaks
  and pioneer tree species. Adopt minimum tillage.

- **Years 4-7**: Tree crops begin producing. Forest cover increases. Livestock
  integration matures. On-farm fuel production begins offsetting external needs.

- **Year 8+**: The system reaches equilibrium where most energy needs are met
  internally. External inputs minimal. The farm produces food, fuel, fiber, and
  fertility.

### Seven Accounting Categories

Mollison tracks the transition across seven parallel accounting bars:

1. **Farm income**: Actual monetary returns from products sold.

2. **Production costs**: Real costs of achieving that income. In conventional
   farming, any "profit" is achieved through subsidies.

3. **Energy (oil/calories)**: Machinery, fuels, fertilizers, biocides. Starts at
   10:1 against; can reach 1:120 gain in permaculture.

4. **Energy produced on farm**: Fuel oils from crops, perennial food calories.
   Solar energy contributes most in conservation farming.

5. **Soil loss**: Humus and mineral nutrient loss. Conventional farming loses
   soil; permaculture creates it.

6. **Water efficiency**: Use and storage efficiency. Conventional farming wastes
   most rainfall; permaculture captures and stores it.

7. **Pollution**: Atmosphere, soil, and water poisoning by fuels and chemicals.
   Permaculture actively removes pollutants.

## Three Forms of Permanent Agriculture

Mollison identifies three historical approaches to permanent agriculture:

### Feudal Permanence
Practiced in old China, where people hauled nutrients from canals, cesspits, and
forests to annual grain culture. People were bound to the landscape by unremmitting
toil in service to a state or landlord, leading eventually to famine and revolution.

### Baronial Permanence
Large pastoral holdings managing vast grazing leases for a single species. This is
the lowest productive use of land. Once mechanized, it destroys landscapes and
soil complexes, creating "agricultural deserts."

### Communal Permanence
Forests cared for over generations, requiring tribal or communal reverence found
only in stable communities. This is where one can plant a pecan or citrus when
old and know it will not be cut by one's children's children.

## The Risk of External Dependence

The characteristic that typifies all permanent agricultures is that the needs of
the system for energy are provided by that system. Modern crop agriculture is
totally dependent on external energies—hence the oil problem and its associated
pollution.

Mollison warns that the further we depart from communal permanence, the greater
the risk of tyranny, feudalism, and revolution, and the more work for less yield.
Any error or disturbance can bring disaster—a drought year in a desert grain crop
or a distant political decision on tariffs can collapse the entire system.

The real risk is that the needs of those people working "on the ground"—the
inhabitants—are overthrown by the needs (or greeds) of commerce and centralized
power; that the forest is cut for warships or newspaper and people are reduced
to serfs in a barren landscape. This has been the fate of peasant Europe, Ireland,
and much of the third world.

## Implications for Modern Farmers

For farmers considering the transition to permaculture, Mollison's accounting
framework offers several practical insights:

- The transition period involves short-term costs for long-term gains. During
  years 1-3, income may decrease as tree crops are established and livestock
  systems are reorganized.

- Diversity is the key to economic resilience. A polyculture of tree crops,
  livestock, annual vegetables, and perennial plants provides multiple income
  streams and insurance against single-crop failure.

- On-farm energy production (firewood, biogas, wind) reduces dependence on
  external energy markets and protects against price volatility.
