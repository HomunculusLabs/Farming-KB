## Effective Working Groups and Right Livelihood

[[effective-working-groups]] and the principle of right livelihood form the social backbone of any sustainable [[permaculture]] community. Drawing from [[bill-mollison]]'s analysis in *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, this concept addresses how communities can organize themselves productively, avoid common governance pitfalls, and structure their social and economic systems for long-term resilience.

## The Failure of Large Governance Structures

Traditional approaches to community governance frequently rely on large boards, assemblies, or committees of seven or more people who govern through agendas, votes, and consensus processes. Mollison argues that such systems are fundamentally impractical and counterproductive. They tend to generate more process than outcome, consuming enormous amounts of time and energy while producing decisions that often satisfy no one.

### The Problem with Consensus

Consensus decision-making, while often held up as an egalitarian ideal, is described as endless and ultimately pointless in practice. The process tends to devolve into a form of coercion, where the silent or abstaining members are effectively overruled by a vociferous minority who dominate the discussion. Rather than achieving genuine agreement, consensus meetings often produce exhaustion and resentment, with outcomes that reflect persistence and rhetorical skill rather than wisdom or practical merit.

### Tyranny of Majority and Minority

Board decisions are susceptible to both the tyranny of the majority and the tyranny of the minority. In majority-rule systems, the preferences of large groups override the considered judgment of smaller but more knowledgeable parties. In consensus systems, a single dissenting voice can paralyze action entirely, giving disproportionate power to obstruction. Neither approach reliably produces decisions that serve the [[ingham-single-species-vs-whole-community]] or its environment.

## Abolishing Decision Meetings: The Task-Based Alternative

Mollison proposes a radical alternative: abolish decision meetings entirely and replace them with a task-based system. Rather than convening groups to debate and vote on courses of action, work is organized around specific, clearly defined tasks that individuals or small teams take responsibility for. This eliminates the bureaucratic overhead of governance meetings and directs energy toward tangible outcomes.

### Creative Tasks vs. Maintenance Tasks

Under the task-based system, work is divided into two broad categories:

- **Creative tasks** are those involving design, innovation, construction, and new initiatives. These are handled through voluntary allocation — individuals step forward to take on work that aligns with their skills and interests. People self-select for creative work based on passion and competence rather than being assigned by committee.

- **Maintenance tasks** are ongoing, repetitive responsibilities such as watering, harvesting, cleaning, and routine repairs. These are managed through a roster system that distributes the burden equitably across all members. Roster duties are non-negotiable obligations of community membership.

### Tithing for Contracted Work

When no community member volunteers for a necessary task — whether creative or maintenance — the community uses a tithing system to pay an external contractor. A portion of collective income (a tithe) is set aside specifically for this purpose. This ensures that essential work is always completed, even when internal capacity or willingness is insufficient, while providing a clear financial incentive for members to volunteer rather than draw down the communal fund.

## The Troika Approach: Small Functional Groups

Mollison advocates limiting functional working groups to just one to three people per area of responsibility — a model he refers to as the troika approach. This small-group structure offers several critical advantages:

- Decisions are made quickly and directly by those doing the work
- Accountability is clear and personal — there is nowhere to hide in a group of three
- Communication overhead is minimal
- Each member has a meaningful voice and genuine influence
- The group remains agile and responsive to changing conditions

### Attaching Understudies

Each small working group should have understudies — newer or less experienced members who observe, learn, and gradually take on responsibility. This serves multiple purposes: it builds community capacity by transferring skills and knowledge, it creates a succession pipeline so that no function is lost when a member leaves, and it provides a structured path for integration of new members into the community's working life.

## Portfolio of Occupations

Rather than confining individuals to a single role, Mollison recommends that each person maintain a portfolio of occupations, participating in two or three different teams or functional areas. This cross-pollination has several benefits:

- Individuals develop diverse skills and avoid burnout from repetitive work
- Teams gain fresh perspectives from members who bring experience from other areas
- The community becomes more resilient — no single person is irreplaceable in only one domain
- Social bonds are strengthened as people work alongside different community members

## Shared Ethic Without Assuming Love or Trust

A critical principle in Mollison's framework is that community groups should be founded on a shared ethic — a common commitment to principles such as earth care, people care, and fair share — without assuming that members love or trust one another. This distinction is vital for practical [[holmgren-community-design-and-intentional-communities]]:

- Shared ethic provides a reliable, articulable basis for cooperation and conflict resolution
- Assuming love or trust creates unrealistic expectations that lead to disappointment and fracture
- Systems and structures should work effectively even when interpersonal relationships are strained
- Right livelihood emerges from principled cooperation, not from emotional bonds

## Group Size and Function

Mollison provides a detailed mapping of group sizes to their functional capacities, establishing clear guidelines for how many people can effectively work together at different scales:

| Group Size | Functional Capacity |
|---|---|
| 1–3 | Executive — rapid decision-making and direct action |
| 4–6 | Cooperative — effective collaborative work with clear communication |
| 7–20 | Social only — too large for efficient work, suitable for gatherings and celebration |
| 30–40 | Minimal functional group — the lower bound for a self-sustaining community |
| 40–200 | Regional — a scale for coordination between multiple small groups |
| 200–300 | Genetic variability hamlet — sufficient population for [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] |
| 300–600 | Identity limit — the upper bound for a community where everyone knows everyone |
| 1,000–5,000 | Bioregional — appropriate scale for [[bioregional-organization]] |
| 7,000–40,000 | Towns — functional towns with diverse services |
| 15,000+ | Cities — increasingly disorganized and impersonal |

Beyond approximately six people, working groups lose their effectiveness for productive collaboration. The 7–20 range is explicitly identified as social rather than functional. Communities that attempt to operate as single working units above these thresholds inevitably develop bureaucratic pathologies.

## Independent Alliances Over Large Groups

Rather than building large, monolithic organizations, Mollison advocates for independent alliances of small groups. Small, autonomous working groups form the basic unit of [[bioregionalism-and-community-organization]], and these groups coordinate with one another through voluntary alliances when their interests overlap. This federated approach preserves the agility and accountability of small groups while allowing for coordination at larger scales.

## Work-Netting, Not Networking

Mollison draws an important distinction between work-netting and networking. Networking, in the conventional sense, often implies the exchange of contacts, information, and social capital — activities that can become ends in themselves. Work-netting, by contrast, is the deliberate weaving together of productive working relationships. It focuses on connecting people who are actively engaged in complementary work, creating channels for material exchange, skill sharing, and mutual aid that serve practical outcomes rather than social ambition.

## Temporary and Dissolvable Groups

Groups need not last forever. Mollison emphasizes that working groups should be formed for specific purposes and dissolved when those purposes are fulfilled. Permanent committees and standing boards tend to perpetuate themselves beyond their usefulness, generating work to justify their continued existence. Temporary groups maintain focus and urgency, and their dissolution frees members to move on to new challenges.

## Common Errors in Community Organization

Mollison identifies a set of recurring mistakes that undermine community viability. Awareness of these pitfalls is essential for anyone seeking to build effective working groups:

- **Group consensus on all decisions** — attempting to achieve consensus on every matter, no matter how minor, creates bottlenecks and resentment
- **Group leadership by a few** — concentrating authority in a small leadership cadre undermines distributed responsibility and creates dependency
- **Rules about lifestyle** — imposing behavioral or lifestyle expectations on members violates autonomy and drives away capable people
- **Large group households** — communal living arrangements that exceed functional group size generate conflict and inefficiency
- **Overscheduling meetings** — filling calendars with governance and discussion meetings consumes time that should be spent on productive work
- **Neglect of income-producing activities** — communities that focus exclusively on internal processes and fail to generate sustainable income eventually collapse financially
- **Poor financial accounting** — without transparent, rigorous financial management, communities cannot track their viability or build trust among members

## Right Livelihood in Practice

Right livelihood, in the permaculture context, means engaging in work that is ecologically regenerative, socially beneficial, and personally fulfilling. It is supported by the organizational structures described above: small functional groups allow individuals to find their niche, portfolio occupations prevent stagnation, and the task-based system ensures that all necessary work is visible and accounted for. When communities organize themselves around these principles, right livelihood emerges not as an abstract ideal but as a practical outcome of good design.

## Summary of Key Principles

- Replace governance meetings with task-based systems
- Limit working groups to 1–3 people (the troika)
- Distinguish creative tasks (volunteer) from maintenance tasks (roster)
- Use tithing to fund contracted work when volunteers are unavailable
- Maintain a portfolio of 2–3 occupational areas per person
- Build on shared ethic, not assumed love or trust
- Form independent alliances of small groups rather than large organizations
- Allow groups to dissolve when their purpose is fulfilled
- Avoid the common errors that undermine community viability
