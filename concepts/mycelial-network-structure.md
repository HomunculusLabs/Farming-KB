# Mycelial Network Structure

Mycelium is the vegetative body of a fungus, consisting of a vast
network of fine, branching filaments called hyphae.

Individual hyphae are typically only 1-10 micrometres in diameter but
can extend for metres or even kilometres in aggregate, forming one of
the largest biological networks on Earth.

## Hyphal Anatomy

Each hypha is a tubular cell enclosed by a rigid [[staycare-degradation-of-plant-cell-wall-polymers-by-fungi]] composed
primarily of chitin and glucans.

Hyphae grow at their tips via a specialised structure called the
Spitzenkorper, a vesicle-rich organelle that directs cell wall
synthesis and membrane expansion.

Behind the growing tip, mature regions contain nuclei, mitochondria,
vacuoles, and ribosomes.

In most fungi, hyphae are septate; divided into discrete compartments
by cross-walls called septa.

Septa contain pores that allow cytoplasmic streaming and even nuclear
migration between compartments.

Non-septate (coenocytic) hyphae lack these divisions entirely, forming
a continuous multinucleate mass.

## Network Topology

[[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]] exhibit striking structural similarities to
engineered transport networks.

Key features include:

- **Branching patterns** that follow Murray's law; daughter branches
  maintain optimal radii to minimise transport resistance
- **Fusion points** where genetically compatible hyphae anastomose,
  creating a reticulate web with redundant pathways
- **Differentiated cords and rhizomorphs** that develop when the
  network consolidates, forming larger-diameter transport highways
- **Nodes** of high connectivity where multiple hyphae converge, often
  coinciding with nutrient hotspots

Research by Fricker et al. (2007) demonstrated that mycelial networks
in *Phanerochaete velutina* display small-world topology: short path
lengths between any two points combined with high clustering at local
nodes.

## Resource Transport

Cytoplasmic streaming within hyphae moves nutrients, organelles, and
signalling molecules at speeds of up to several centimetres per hour.

In mature networks, bulk flow through differentiated cords can be
substantially faster.

This transport system allows the fungus to redistribute carbon from
nutrient-rich zones to support growth in depleted areas; a form of
internal [[mycelial-network-resource-sharing-plants]] analogous to economic trade in human
infrastructure.

Vacuoles play a key role in storage and translocation. They can occupy
up to 80% of hyphal volume in older regions, acting as reservoirs of
phosphorus, nitrogen, and carbon compounds that can be mobilised on
demand.

## Adaptation and Plasticity

Mycelial networks are highly plastic.

When a nutrient source is discovered, hyphal tips proliferate locally,
increasing branching density.

Conversely, unproductive foraging fronts are pruned and their contents
reabsorbed.

This dynamic remodelling allows the network to optimise its
architecture continuously in response to environmental conditions.

Experiments using microfluidic devices have shown that fungal networks
can solve spatial optimisation problems; routing resources along
shortest paths even when the environment changes unpredictably.

This has made them a model system for biologically inspired computing.

## Environmental Sensing

Mycelial networks demonstrate sophisticated environmental sensing.

Hyphal tips detect chemical gradients through G-protein-coupled
receptors and respond by growing toward attractants (nutrients, host
signals) or away from repellents (toxic compounds, competitors).

This chemotropism operates at remarkably low concentrations; some
fungi detect glucose gradients differing by as little as 1%.

Fungi can also sense physical parameters including surface hardness,
pore size, and moisture gradients.

*Magnaporthe oryzae* uses a specialised appressorium structure that
generates enormous [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] (~8 MPa) to mechanically breach
the rice leaf cuticle.

## Defence Mechanisms

Fungal networks deploy multiple defence strategies:

- **[[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]]**: Penicillin, statins, and cyclosporine are
  all fungal defence compounds. The genomic capacity for secondary
  metabolite production is vast; many *Aspergillus* species encode
  30-70 biosynthetic gene clusters.
- **Compartmentalisation**: Septal pores can be plugged with Woronin
  bodies (in Ascomycota) or septal pore caps (in Basidiomycota) to
  isolate damaged regions.
- **Autophagy**: Self-digestion of cellular components allows survival
  during starvation and facilitates tissue remodelling.

## Scales of Organisation

| Scale | Feature | Approximate Size |
|-------|---------|-----------------|
| Molecular | Chitin fibrils | 3-5 nm diameter |
| Cellular | Individual hyphae | 1-10 um diameter |
| Tissue | Mycelial cords | 0.1-5 mm diameter |
| Colony | Individual fungal network | cm to km |

The largest known fungal genet; a specimen of *Armillaria ostoyae*
in Oregon's Malheur National Forest; spans approximately 9.6 square
kilometres, making it one of the largest organisms ever documented.
