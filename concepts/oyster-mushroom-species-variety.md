# Oyster Mushroom Species and Varieties

The genus Pleurotus encompasses over 30 described species of gilled mushrooms,
commonly known as oyster mushrooms for their shelf-like growth pattern
resembling oyster shells. Several species within this genus are commercially
cultivated worldwide, each with distinct characteristics in appearance, flavor,
growth requirements, and substrate preferences. Understanding these differences
is essential for cultivators selecting appropriate species for their conditions
and markets.

For cultivation techniques and [[oss-oeric-growing-parameters-cubensis]] by species,
see [[oyster-mushroom|oyster mushroom cultivation]].

## Pleurotus ostreatus

The classic oyster mushroom and the most widely cultivated species globally.
Fruiting bodies have smooth, fan-shaped caps ranging from 5 to 25 centimeters
across. Color varies by strain from pale grey to dark blue-grey, with some
strains browning with age. The flesh is white, firm, and mild in flavor with a
subtle anise aroma.

Pleurotus ostreatus is a cold-tolerant species that fruits optimally at 12 to
18 degrees Celsius. It can fruit at temperatures as low as 5 degrees C, making
it suitable for cultivation in cool climates and during winter months in
temperate regions. It colonizes a wide range of substrates including straw,
sawdust, paper, and agricultural residues. [[stamets-biological-efficiency-yield]] on supplemented
straw typically ranges from 75 to 150 percent.

## Pleurotus pulmonarius

Known as the [[growing-gourmet-phoenix-oyster-pulmonarius-cultivation]] or Indian oyster, this species is more
heat-tolerant than P. ostreatus, fruiting well at 18 to 28 degrees C. It has a
faster colonization rate and shorter cropping cycle, often producing the first
harvest within 14 to 18 days of spawning. Caps are generally smaller and thinner
than P. ostreatus, with a white to pale brown coloration.

P. pulmonarius is popular in tropical and subtropical regions where warm
temperatures make P. ostreatus cultivation difficult. Its rapid growth cycle
allows for more flushes per year, compensating for somewhat lower individual
flush weights. It also performs well on [[oyster-mushroom-coffee-grounds-cultivation]] and other nitrogen-rich
[[tropical-mushroom-cultivation-agricultural-waste-substrates-poverty-alleviation]].

## Pleurotus eryngii

The King oyster mushroom is the largest species in the genus, producing
impressive fruiting bodies with thick, meaty stems up to 10 centimeters in
diameter and caps up to 15 centimeters across. Unlike other Pleurotus species,
the stem is the primary edible portion, with a texture and flavor often
compared to abalone or scallops when cooked. This unique characteristic
commands premium prices in many markets.

P. eryngii is native to the Mediterranean basin and parts of Central Asia,
where it grows as a weak parasite on the roots of Apiaceae plants (Eryngium,
Ferula, Thapsia). Cultivation requires sterilized [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] substrate
and cooler fruiting temperatures of 12 to 18 degrees C. It has a longer
cultivation cycle (40 to 60 days from spawning to harvest) but yields are high
and the crop has excellent shelf life.

## Pleurotus citrinopileatus

The Golden oyster mushroom is one of the most visually striking cultivated
species, producing clusters of bright yellow to golden-orange caps. Native to
eastern Asia, it has gained popularity for both its ornamental appearance and
its intense, slightly nutty flavor. Caps are smaller than P. ostreatus,
typically 3 to 8 centimeters, growing in dense clusters.

P. citrinopileatus prefers warmer temperatures, fruiting at 20 to 30 degrees C.
It has a relatively short shelf life compared to other oyster species, with caps
drying and wrinkling quickly after harvest. This limits its commercial
distribution radius but makes it excellent for direct-to-consumer and farmers
market sales.

## Pleurotus djamor

The Pink oyster mushroom produces vivid pink to salmon-colored caps that retain
some color even after cooking. It is one of the most heat-tolerant species,
fruiting well at 25 to 35 degrees C, making it ideal for tropical cultivation.
Growth is extremely fast: pinning occurs within days of introducing fruiting
conditions, and mushrooms reach harvest size in 3 to 5 days.

Pink oysters have a shorter shelf life than most species and the color fades as
mushrooms mature. They are best harvested young when the caps are still vibrant
and the texture is firm. The flavor is slightly stronger and more aromatic than
grey oyster mushrooms, with tropical fruit notes in some strains.

## Pleurotus cornucopiae

The Branching oyster mushroom produces antler-like or coral-shaped fruiting
bodies when grown in high CO2 environments. Under proper fresh air exchange, it
develops more typical shelf-like forms but with a characteristic ruffled,
irregular cap margin. It is less commonly cultivated commercially but is valued
by specialty growers for its unusual appearance and firm texture.

## Cultivation Notes by Species

Different oyster species require different cultivation approaches. P. ostreatus
and P. pulmonarius are the most forgiving for beginners due to their aggressive
colonization and broad substrate tolerance. P. eryngii demands higher sterility
standards and supplemented substrates, making it better suited for intermediate
to advanced cultivators. P. citrinopileatus and P. djamor thrive in warm
climates but require careful timing to reach market before quality degrades.

Outdoor cultivation on logs or stumps works well for P. ostreatus, P. pulmonarius,
and P. cornucopiae. Hardwood logs (oak, beech, maple) are inoculated in spring
and may produce for several years. Indoor cultivation on pasteurized straw or
sterilized sawdust blocks provides more controlled yields and faster cycles for
all species.

## Substrate Preferences

P. ostreatus and P. pulmonarius are generalists that grow on nearly any
lignocellulosic material including wheat straw, oat straw, rice straw, paper
waste, coffee grounds, and agricultural residues. P. eryngii prefers hardwood
sawdust, particularly from deciduous trees like oak and beech, supplemented
with bran or grain for nitrogen. P. citrinopileatus performs well on straw and
cottonseed hulls. P. djamor excels on sugarcane bagasse and rice straw in
tropical environments.

Matching species to locally available and affordable substrates is a key factor
in cultivation economics. A cultivator in Southeast Asia might focus on P. djamor
on rice straw, while a European grower could specialize in P. eryngii on local
hardwood sawdust supplemented with wheat bran.

## Flavor Profiles

Oyster mushroom species vary considerably in flavor and culinary application.
P. ostreatus has a mild, slightly sweet flavor with anise notes that works well
in soups, stir-fries, and as a meat substitute. P. eryngii is prized for its
dense, meaty texture and umami-rich flavor that holds up to grilling, roasting,
and braising. P. citrinopileatus offers an intense nutty, cashew-like flavor
that is best showcased with minimal cooking.
