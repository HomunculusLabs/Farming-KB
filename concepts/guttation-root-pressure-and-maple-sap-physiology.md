# Guttation, Root Pressure, and Maple Sap Physiology

When transpiration slows or stops, plants still need to move water and nutrients. Root pressure and related phenomena -- including guttation and the production of [[maple-syrup-production]] -- demonstrate alternative mechanisms for moving fluids through plants.

## Root Pressure

### Mechanism

Root pressure occurs when there is more water outside the root than inside it. Water flows into the root to equalize concentrations (osmosis), aided by higher concentrations of dissolved ions in root cells than in surrounding soil water.

Root pressure can push sap several meters up the plant, but this is the practical limit. For tall trees, transpiration pull remains the primary driver of water movement. However, root pressure plays crucial roles during low-transpiration periods.

### When Root Pressure Dominates

- At night when stomata are closed and transpiration stops
- In [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] before leaves emerge (no transpiration possible)
- In high-humidity environments where evaporation is minimal
- In newly planted seedlings with limited [[bloomfield-rust-germ-tube-tactile-sensing-leaf-surface-navigation]] area

## Guttation

### What It Is

When root pressure is strong enough and transpiration is minimal, water is pushed out of the tips or edges of leaves. This creates visible droplets on the [[aact-foliar-sticker-spreaders-leaf-surface-attachment-ingham]] -- guttation. Unlike dew (condensation from atmospheric moisture), guttation droplets come from inside the plant.

### The Physics

The xylem sap accumulates when stomata are closed. Water flows into the xylem cells to dilute the concentrated sap (osmosis). Because xylem vessels can only stretch so far due to their lignified walls, the internal pressure pushes sap up and out through specialized structures called hydathodes at leaf margins.

### Guttation vs Dew

| | Guttation | Dew |
|---|-----------|-----|
| **Source** | Inside the plant (xylem) | Atmospheric moisture condensation |
| **Composition** | Contains dissolved minerals and sugars | Pure water |
| **Location** | Leaf tips and edges (hydathodes) | Entire leaf surface |
| **Conditions** | High soil moisture, low transpiration | Cool night temperatures, high humidity |
| **Time** | Early morning | Early morning |

## Xylem Sap: Not Just Water

A common misconception is that xylem carries only water. In reality, xylem sap contains dissolved nutrient ions, amino acids, peptides, and -- critically in spring -- large sugar molecules converted from winter root storage.

### Spring Sap Flow

During spring, several processes converge:
1. Stored starches in roots are converted to transportable sugars
2. These sugars enter the xylem and travel upward
3. The sugar creates strong [[fungal-fruiting-body-mechanics-osmotic-pressure]] -- water flows in to dilute the sweetened sap
4. This root pressure pushes the sap up the xylem to apical meristems aboveground

## Maple Syrup: The Xylem Sap Paradox

Maple syrup production is a practical demonstration of spring xylem sap flow. However, maples present a unique puzzle: their sap flow does not rely solely on root pressure like birch trees.

### The Birch System (Simple Root Pressure)

Birch sap flow works by straightforward root pressure: sugars in the xylem create osmotic pressure that draws water in and pushes sap up and out. This can be collected by tapping.

### The Maple System (Freeze-Thaw Cycles)

Maple sap production requires alternating freeze-thaw cycles:

1. **Daytime (above freezing)**: CO2 produced during cellular respiration of sugars creates gas pressure in xylem vessels. Sap flows out through tap holes.
2. **Nighttime (below freezing)**: Gases dissolve in the cooling sap, which lowers the pressure in xylem vessels. Water molecules from adjacent cells move in to equalize pressure. Due to hydrogen bonding, this pulls water into the plant from roots.
3. **Repeat**: Each freeze-thaw cycle pumps more sap.

This gas-pressure mechanism explains why maple sugaring only works during specific temperature conditions: nights below freezing and days above freezing. Without the freeze-thaw cycle, the sap does not flow.

### Sap Composition

Maple sap is approximately 98% water and 2% sugar (primarily sucrose). It takes about 40 gallons of sap to produce 1 gallon of maple syrup. The sap also contains amino acids, peptides, and mineral nutrients from the xylem transport system.

## Osmotic Pressure in Xylem

The presence of sugars, amino acids, and dissolved ions in xylem sap creates osmotic pressure that can supplement or replace transpiration pull. This is particularly important:
- In herbaceous plants (short enough for root pressure to reach the top)
- During spring before leaf emergence
- In tropical plants where transpiration may be reduced
- In plants growing in saturated soils where oxygen limitations affect root function
