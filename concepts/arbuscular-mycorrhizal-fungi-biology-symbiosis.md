# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi — Biology and Symbiosis

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi are among the most ecologically important organisms on Earth, forming symbiotic associations with the vast majority of land plants. The association involves fungi in the order Glomales, phylum Zygomycota, and is termed arbuscular because all species form arbuscule structures interfacing with plant cell plasmalemma.

## The Symbiosis

### Carbon Exchange

The plant provides carbon to the fungus largely via the arbuscule-plant cell plasmalemma interface. The plant also provides a protected site in root cells where the fungus can live. In return, external fungal hyphae improve phosphorus acquisition by the plant in low-phosphorus soils.

### Pathogenic Potential

When soil phosphorus levels exceed host requirements, the AM symbiosis can be inhibited. Under certain conditions, mycorrhizal development may actually reduce plant growth, becoming pathogenic.

### Initiation

Mycorrhizae are initiated by hyphae from another mycorrhizal root or from germinating spores. Colonization spreads within the root cortex via hyphal extension and branching, developing vesicles and auxiliary cells containing lipids for carbon storage.

### Host Specificity

Appears minimal or absent — INVAM showed more than 1000 isolates of 98 species can grow on Sorghum sudanense. Roots can accommodate up to 10 species simultaneously. This lack of specialization may result from 400+ million years of mutualistic coevolution.

## The Mycorrhizosphere

The soil volume around roots constitutes a "mycorrhizosphere" due to the dramatic influence of fungal external hyphae on soil microorganisms:

- Mycorrhizal roots have altered root exudation patterns

- Rhizobium-Bradyrhizobium association with legumes is affected by AM fungi (via increased phosphorus availability driving nitrogenase activity)

- Interactions with plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria

- Effects on both pathogens and beneficial organisms

## Glomalin

A revolutionary discovery: glomalin, a heat-stable glycoprotein produced by AM fungi that:

- Coats hyphae and spore surfaces

- Accumulates in soil

- Is strongly involved in soil aggregate stability

- Shows highly significant correlation with soil aggregate stability

- Represents a previously unknown major carbon pool in soils

## Reproduction

AM fungi reproduce clonally via mitospores formed in soil and/or in roots. One species (Gigaspora decipiens) may have a sexual phase, but most evidence indicates clonal reproduction.

### Sporulation Timeline

- Greenhouse pot cultures: new sporulation begins 4-8 weeks after colonization onset, plateaus in 12-16 weeks

- Sporulation increases dramatically after roots cease growing but continue as carbon sinks

- Sporulation does not occur in response to nutrient deprivation or stress (unlike other fungi)

### Infectivity Varies

- Glomineae suborder: mycorrhizal roots, detached hyphae, and spores are highly infective

- Gigasporineae suborder: auxiliary cells and detached hyphae are NOT infective; only spores

## Ecological Significance

AM fungi enhance plant growth, reproduction, and survival and play important roles in:

- Plant community succession

- Maintenance of plant-community diversity

- Soil structure formation (via glomalin)

- Carbon cycling

- Phosphorus nutrition

AM associations are widespread among agricultural and horticultural crop plants, though field impacts can be highly variable.

## Nonmycorrhizal Plants

A small number of plant orders do not form AM associations. Many other orders include both mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal families. Nonmycorrhizal taxa appear to have evolved away from the symbiosis. A few genera (notably Eucalyptus) support both arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal fungi.

## Diversity and Taxonomy

Modern molecular methods, particularly [[fungal-diversity-indices-community-analysis]] have revealed that AM fungal communities are shaped by soil type, host plant identity, and land management practices.
