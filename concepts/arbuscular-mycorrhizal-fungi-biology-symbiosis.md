# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi — Biology and Symbiosis
Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi are among the most ecologically important
organisms on Earth, forming symbiotic associations with the vast majority of
land plants. The association involves fungi in the order Glomales, phylum
Zygomycota, and is termed arbuscular because all species form arbuscule
structures interfacing with plant cell plasmalemma.

## The Symbiosis

### Carbon Exchange

The plant provides carbon to the fungus largely via the arbuscule-plant cell
plasmalemma interface. The plant also provides a protected site in root cells
where the fungus can live. In return, external fungal hyphae improve
[[comparison-phosphorus-solubilizing-microorganisms-vs-mycorrhizal-phosphorus-acquisition]] by the plant in low-phosphorus soils.

### Pathogenic Potential

When soil phosphorus levels exceed host requirements, the AM symbiosis can be
inhibited. Under certain conditions, mycorrhizal development may actually
reduce plant growth, becoming pathogenic.

### Initiation

Mycorrhizae are initiated by hyphae from another mycorrhizal root or from
germinating spores. Colonization spreads within the root cortex via hyphal
extension and branching, developing vesicles and auxiliary cells containing
lipids for carbon storage.

### Host Specificity

Appears minimal or absent — INVAM showed more than 1000 isolates of 98
species can grow on Sorghum sudanense. Roots can accommodate up to 10 species
simultaneously. This lack of specialization may result from 400+ million years
of mutualistic coevolution.

## The Mycorrhizosphere

The soil volume around roots constitutes a "mycorrhizosphere" due to the
dramatic influence of fungal external hyphae on soil microorganisms:

- Mycorrhizal roots have altered root exudation patterns
- Rhizobium-Bradyrhizobium association with legumes is affected by AM fungi
  (via increased [[phosphorus-availability]] driving nitrogenase activity)
- Interactions with plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria
- Effects on both pathogens and beneficial organisms

## Glomalin

A revolutionary discovery: glomalin, a heat-stable glycoprotein produced by
AM fungi that:

- Coats hyphae and spore surfaces
- Accumulates in soil
- Is strongly involved in soil aggregate stability
- Shows highly significant correlation with soil aggregate stability
- Represents a previously unknown major carbon pool in soils

## Reproduction

AM fungi reproduce clonally via mitospores formed in soil and/or in roots.
One species (Gigaspora decipiens) may have a sexual phase, but most evidence
indicates clonal reproduction.

### Sporulation Timeline

- Greenhouse pot cultures: new sporulation begins 4-8 weeks after
  colonization onset, plateaus in 12-16 weeks
- Sporulation increases dramatically after roots cease growing but continue
  as carbon sinks
- Sporulation does not occur in response to nutrient deprivation or stress
  (unlike other fungi)

### Infectivity Varies

- Glomineae suborder: mycorrhizal roots, detached hyphae, and spores are
  highly infective
- Gigasporineae suborder: auxiliary cells and detached hyphae are NOT
  infective; only spores

## Ecological Significance

AM fungi enhance plant growth, reproduction, and survival and play important
roles in:

- Plant community succession
- Maintenance of plant-community diversity
- Soil structure formation (via glomalin)
- Carbon cycling
- Phosphorus nutrition

AM associations are widespread among agricultural and horticultural crop
plants, though field impacts can be highly variable.

## Nonmycorrhizal Plants

A small number of plant orders do not form AM associations. Many other orders
include both mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal families. Nonmycorrhizal taxa
appear to have evolved away from the symbiosis. A few genera (notably
Eucalyptus) support both arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal fungi.

## Diversity and Taxonomy

Modern [[ingham-soil-food-web-diversity-assessment-dna-molecular-methods]], particularly
[[fungal-diversity-indices-community-analysis]], have revealed that AM fungal
communities are shaped by soil type, host plant identity, and land management
practices.

## Nutrient Exchange Mechanisms

The arbuscular [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-symbiosis]] fundamentally alters plant nutrient
acquisition strategies. The extensive external hyphal network of AM fungi
extends far beyond the root depletion zone, effectively increasing the
absorptive surface area of the root system by orders of magnitude.

### Phosphorus Uptake

Phosphorus is relatively immobile in soil, forming insoluble compounds that
limit plant availability. AM fungal hyphae, with their small diameter (2-4
micrometers compared to 10-20 micrometers for root hairs), can access soil
pores and phosphate minerals unavailable to plant roots. Phosphate is taken up
by the fungus, converted to polyphosphate granules, and translocated to the
arbuscule where it is released to the plant cell.

### Nitrogen Transfer

AM fungi also facilitate nitrogen uptake, though their role is less
well-characterized than for phosphorus. Fungal hyphae can take up both
ammonium and nitrate from soil, and some evidence suggests AM fungi can
access organic nitrogen sources through interactions with other soil
microorganisms. See [[nitrogen-transfer-mycorrhizal]] for detailed
mechanisms.
