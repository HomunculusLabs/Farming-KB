# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi — Biology and Symbiosis
Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi are among the most ecologically important
organisms on Earth, forming symbiotic associations with the vast majority of
land plants. The association involves fungi in the order Glomales, phylum
Zygomycota, and is termed arbuscular because all species form arbuscule
structures interfacing with plant cell plasmalemma.

## The Symbiosis

### Carbon Exchange

The plant provides carbon to the fungus largely via the arbuscule-plant cell
plasmalemma interface. The plant also provides a protected site in root cells
where the fungus can live. In return, external [[comparison-phosphorus-solubilizing-microorganisms-vs-mycorrhizal-phosphorus-acquisition]] by the plant in low-phosphorus soils.

### Pathogenic Potential

When soil phosphorus levels exceed host requirements, the AM symbiosis can be
inhibited. Under certain conditions, mycorrhizal development may actually
reduce plant growth, becoming pathogenic.

### Initiation

Mycorrhizae are initiated by hyphae from another [[fungal-peat-decomposition-and-carbon-storage]].

### Host Specificity

Appears minimal or absent — INVAM showed more than 1000 isolates of 98
species can grow on Sorghum sudanense. Roots can accommodate up to 10 species
simultaneously. This lack of specialization may result from 400+ million years
of mutualistic coevolution.

## The Mycorrhizosphere

The soil volume around roots constitutes a "mycorrhizosphere" due to the
dramatic influence of fungal external hyphae on soil microorganisms:

- [[phosphorus-availability]] driving nitrogenase activity)
- Interactions with plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria
- Effects on both pathogens and beneficial organisms

## Glomalin

A revolutionary discovery: glomalin, a heat-stable glycoprotein produced by
AM fungi that:

- Coats hyphae and spore surfaces
- Accumulates in soil
- Is strongly involved in soil aggregate stability
- Shows highly significant correlation with soil aggregate stability
- Represents a previously unknown major carbon pool in soils

## Reproduction

AM fungi reproduce clonally via mitospores formed in soil and/or in roots.
One species (Gigaspora decipiens) may have a sexual phase, but most evidence
indicates clonal reproduction.

### Sporulation Timeline

- Greenhouse pot cultures: new sporulation begins 4-8 weeks after
  colonization onset, plateaus in 12-16 weeks
- Sporulation increases dramatically after roots cease growing but continue
  as carbon sinks
- Sporulation does not occur in response to nutrient deprivation or stress
  (unlike other fungi)

### Infectivity Varies

- Glomineae suborder: mycorrhizal roots, detached hyphae, and spores are
  highly infective
- Gigasporineae suborder: auxiliary cells and detached hyphae are NOT
  infective; only spores
