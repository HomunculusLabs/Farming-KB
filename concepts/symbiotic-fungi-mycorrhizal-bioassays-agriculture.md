# Mycorrhizal Bioassays for Soil Health Assessment in Agriculture

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Symbiotic Fungi (Varma and Kharkwal, eds.), Chapter 3: Use of Mycorrhiza Bioassays in Ecological Studies

## Why Bioassays Matter for Agriculture

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi are ubiquitous in agricultural and natural ecosystems, and most plant species form symbiotic associations with these fungi. The symbiosis can increase the growth and development of plants by enhancing nutrient uptake, especially phosphorus, and by improving water relations of plants. AM fungi can also contribute to improving soil physical fertility by enhancing soil aggregation and protecting crops against plant disease.

The soil environment, particularly factors that control soil fertility, strongly influences mycorrhizal function. Therefore, the capacity of AM fungi to form mycorrhizas that contribute substantially to plant nutrition, plant health, or soil fertility depends on soil management. Bioassays provide a tool to assess this capacity.

## Direct vs Indirect Assessment

### Direct Assessment

Direct measurement of mycorrhizal colonization involves:
- Staining roots to distinguish fungi from root tissues
- Measuring the proportion of root length colonized
- Assessing colonization intensity using the magnified intersection method
- Identifying morphological structures (arbuscules, vesicles, hyphae)

Limitations of direct assessment:
- Does not provide information about fungal activity (living vs dead)
- Colonization at one point in time may not represent the full picture
- Many fungi are not easily visible even after staining
- Root architecture differences complicate comparisons among plant species

### Indirect Assessment: Bioassays

Mycorrhiza bioassays involve use of bait plants grown in a soil sample; roots are examined for AM fungi after a set time. Bioassays measure the infectivity of AM fungi -- the ability of soil containing propagules to colonize roots of a host plant.

Key advantages of bioassays:
- Represent the capacity of hyphae to colonize roots at a particular point in time
- Can be conducted under standardized conditions
- Allow comparison across sites and treatments
- Can predict mycorrhizal development in agricultural or horticultural plants

## Bioassay Design for Agricultural Applications

### Soil Sampling

Soil samples can be collected as:
- **Bulk soil** (disturbed soil samples): Easier to handle, but may alter infectivity
- **Soil cores** (undisturbed soil): Better represent field conditions but more complex

Important considerations:
- Samples should be representative of each sampling point
- Samples should be used quickly or stored at low temperature
- Consider whether to allow soil to dry (depends on AMF biology expected)
- Prior understanding of the fungi present is advantageous

### Bait Plant Selection

Host plants such as grass or legume species are good bait plants for bioassays. Ideal bait plants have:
- High mycorrhizal dependency
- Fast-growing roots in soil
- Roots that are easy to clear and stain for AM fungi
- Environmental conditions compatible with the bioassay

Common bait plants include:
- Subterranean clover (Trifolium subterraneum)
- Leek (Allium porrum)
- Maize (Zea mays)
- Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor)
- Various grass species

### Bioassay Duration

The duration should not exceed the growth stage required for adequate mycorrhiza formation. If a longer time is chosen, the plateau level of colonization may mask differences in colonization rate. Customized bioassays are needed because optimal duration depends on:
- AMF communities present
- Soil conditions
- Growing conditions
- Species of bait plant

### Predicting Field Colonization

Bioassays can be used to predict mycorrhiza development in agricultural or horticultural plants. However, management practices imposed after soil collection can influence subsequent field colonization. A calibration is needed to take into account:
- Proposed agricultural practice
- Soil conditions
- Expected response of AM fungi to these conditions

A bioassay for predicting mycorrhiza formation could use soil collected before the start of the season combined with information about the proposed agricultural practice, soil conditions, and knowledge of how the AM fungi are expected to respond.

## Applications of Bioassays in Agriculture

### Assessing Management Effects

Bioassays have been used to assess the infectivity of AM fungi in a range of situations:

| Purpose | Method | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Infectivity and effectiveness | Field soil using subterranean clover | Abbott and Robson (1981) |
| Seasonal variation in pastures | Undisturbed soil cores with Trifolium | Scheltema et al. (1987) |
| Inoculum potential in mine sites | Intact soil cores | Jasper et al. (1991) |
| Saline conditions | Trap pot culture, spore inoculation | Rafique (2005) |
| Rehabilitated forest | Intact soil cores from jarrah forest | Solaiman and Abbott (2003) |

### Monitoring Inoculum Potential

Bioassays have been used to quantify seasonal and spatial variations in inoculum potential of AM fungi. Key findings:
- Inoculum potential varies seasonally in pastures and forests
- Spatial variation can be significant even within a single field
- Land use history affects inoculum levels
- Disturbance reduces infectivity, especially where propagule abundance is low

### Evaluating Restoration Success

After land degradation or mining, bioassays can evaluate the recovery of AMF communities:
- Mine site rehabilitation assessment
- Forest restoration monitoring
- Erosion control effectiveness
- Topsoil management evaluation

## Factors Affecting Bioassay Results

### Soil Disturbance

Soil disturbance -- such as that caused by farming practices -- can reduce the infectivity of AM fungi. The extent of change depends on the abundance of propagules present. Key observations:
- Soil disturbance in natural ecosystems can reduce infectivity, especially where propagule abundance is low
- Disturbance effects are more severe in species-poor communities
- Recovery after disturbance depends on the presence of resilient propagule types (spores vs hyphae vs root fragments)

### Soil Fertility

Soil fertility factors influence bioassay results:
- High phosphorus can suppress mycorrhizal colonization
- pH affects AMF species composition and activity
- Organic matter content influences microbial activity
- Nutrient imbalances can alter host-fungus interactions

### Competition Among AMF

Observations can be complicated by competition among AM fungi during root colonization. In mixed communities, the most competitive species may dominate root colonization in bioassays, potentially masking the presence of less competitive but functionally important species.
