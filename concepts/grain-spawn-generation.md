### Method 1: Submersion Boiling

The grain is submerged in boiling water for approximately one hour,
then drained and loaded into jars or bags. This method effectively
hydrates the grain and kills many surface contaminants before
sterilization. The grain should be drained thoroughly so that no
free water collects at the bottom of the containers. Excess water
promotes bacterial growth and anaerobic conditions that are
detrimental to mycelium. After draining, the grain is loaded into
sterilization containers, typically to about 2/3 of their volume to
allow room for shaking during the [[mushroom-cultivation-jar-lid-management-loose-fit-vacuum-gas-exchange]] filters
that allow the mycelium to breathe while excluding contaminant spores.
A common design features a 1/3 to 1/2 inch hole in the lid, covered
with a microporous filter disc. These filter discs allow the passage
of gases but block particulates down to approximately 0.3 microns.
The filter is typically held in place with a metal ring or secured
with high-temperature tape. Polypropylene bags with built-in filter
patches are also widely used, especially in commercial operations where
larger spawn volumes are needed. Bags offer the advantage of easier
shaking and larger batch sizes.

## Sterilization Parameters

Grain spawn must be sterilized, not merely pasteurized. Sterilization
occurs above the boiling point of water, at temperatures greater than
212 degrees Fahrenheit (100 degrees Celsius) and at pressures above
atmospheric (greater than 1 psi). The standard sterilization protocol
for grain is 15 psi (121 degrees C) for 90-120 minutes. Under-
sterilization leaves bacterial endospores viable; over-sterilization
can degrade the grain nutrients, causing the grain to clump or become
too dry. Soaking grain overnight before sterilization is recommended
as it triggers bacterial endospores into germination, making them
far more susceptible to heat destruction.

## The Spawn Run and Shaking

After inoculation from agar cultures, the jars or bags are incubated
at species-appropriate temperatures, typically 70-80 degrees F for most
gourmet species. During colonization, the mycelium spreads from kernel
to kernel. Periodic shaking of the containers redistributes the
colonized kernels throughout the uncolonized grain, dramatically
accelerating the colonization process. Most cultivators shake at
