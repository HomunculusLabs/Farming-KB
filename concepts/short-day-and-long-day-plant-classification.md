# Short-Day and Long-Day Plant Classification

## Overview

The classification of plants into short-day and long-day categories
emerged from the discovery of photoperiodism by Wightman W. Garner
and Harry A. Allard in 1918. Their work with the Maryland Mammoth
tobacco strain revealed that plants use the duration of darkness to
regulate their transition from vegetative growth to flowering. This
simple dichotomy, plants that flower when days are short versus
those that flower when days are long, became one of the most
practical and commercially important concepts in plant biology.

## The Garner and Allard Discovery

The discovery of photoperiodism began with a practical agricultural
problem. In 1906, farmers in southern Maryland noticed a new strain
of tobacco that never seemed to stop growing. Maryland Mammoth could
reach four and a half metres in height, produce almost a hundred
leaves, and would only stop growing when frosts set in. While this
vigorous growth seemed beneficial, the strain rarely flowered,
meaning farmers could not harvest seed for the next year's crop.

In 1918, Garner and Allard at the US Department of Agriculture
investigated this mystery. They planted Maryland Mammoth in pots
and divided them into two groups. One group remained outside in
the fields. The other was put in the field during the day but
moved to a dark shed every afternoon. Simply limiting the amount
of light the plants received was enough to cause Maryland Mammoth
to stop growing and start flowering. If exposed to long summer
days, it kept growing leaves; if given artificially shorter days,
it flowered.

## Short-Day Plants

Short-day plants flower when the duration of continuous darkness
exceeds a critical threshold. In practice, this means they flower
in late summer or autumn as days naturally shorten. The term
"short-day" is somewhat misleading because the plants are actually
responding to long nights rather than short days. A more accurate
name would be "long-night plants," but the original terminology
has persisted.

Classic short-day plants include chrysanthemums, soybeans,
poinsettias, rice, and cannabis. Chrysanthemums are perhaps the
most commercially significant example: their natural flowering
time in autumn has been manipulated through night-break lighting
to produce crops for spring holidays like Mother's Day. Soybeans
are agriculturally critical because their flowering time determines
harvest timing, and different varieties have been bred with
different critical night lengths for different latitudes.

## Long-Day Plants

Long-day plants flower when the duration of continuous darkness
falls below a critical threshold. They flower in spring and early
summer as days lengthen. Again, the name is technically imprecise:
these plants are responding to short nights rather than long days,
but the conventional terminology has been retained.

Examples of long-day plants include irises, barley, spinach,
lettuce, and many cereal grains. Barley and other cereals were
among the first crops whose flowering responses were studied
systematically, and understanding their photoperiod requirements
has been essential for breeding programs that adapt varieties
to different growing latitudes and seasons.

## The Critical Night Length Concept

Both short-day and long-day plants share the same underlying
mechanism: they measure the length of the continuous dark period.
What differs between them is how they respond to that measurement.
Short-day plants flower when the night exceeds their critical
dark period. Long-day plants flower when the night is shorter
than their critical dark period. This means the same
photoreceptor system, phytochrome, controls flowering in both
categories.

The critical night length is a genetically determined threshold
that varies between species and even between varieties within a
species. It represents the number of hours of unbroken darkness
required to trigger the flowering signal. For some species, the
critical period is quite precise: a difference of as little as
fifteen minutes can determine whether flowering occurs or not.

## Day-Neutral Plants and Intermediate Categories

While the short-day and long-day classification covers most
photoperiodic species, botanists recognise additional categories.
Day-neutral plants, such as tomatoes and cucumbers, flower
regardless of day length, responding primarily to temperature
and plant maturity rather than photoperiod. Intermediate plants,
such as sugarcane and some grasses, flower only within a narrow
range of day lengths and fail to flower if days are either too
long or too short.

Qualitative photoperiodic plants have an absolute requirement
for a specific day length: they will never flower unless the
photoperiodic requirement is met. Quantitative photoperiodic
plants show a facultative response: they flower faster under
their preferred day length but will eventually flower under
any day length, just more slowly. This distinction has
important implications for crop management and variety
selection.

## Geographic Distribution and Breeding Implications

The natural distribution of short-day and long-day plants
reflects their evolutionary adaptation to latitude. Short-day
plants predominate in tropical and subtropical regions where
day length variation is minimal, while long-day plants are
more common at higher latitudes where summer days are
dramatically longer than winter days.

Plant breeders have exploited photoperiodic classification to
develop varieties adapted to specific growing regions. By
selecting for shorter or longer critical night lengths, breeders
can produce soybean varieties that flower at the optimal time
for cultivation in locations ranging from the American Midwest
to southern Brazil. This application of photoperiodic knowledge
has been one of the most economically significant outcomes of
Garner and Allard's original discovery.
