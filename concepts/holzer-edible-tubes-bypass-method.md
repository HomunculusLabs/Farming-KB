# Holzer Edible Tubes and Bypass Method - Urban Growing in Small Spaces

The edible tubes and bypass method is a creative solution for growing food in
confined urban spaces: on walls, balconies, around telegraph poles, and in
backyards. Developed during Holzer's workshops at Tamera, the system turns
vertical surfaces into productive growing spaces using simple, low-cost
materials.

## Construction

### The Tube

- A roll of geotextile fabric is sewn into tubes of approximately 30cm
  diameter. Any length is possible.
- A long, perforated, thinner hose is inserted down the centre of the tube
  for watering and feeding.
- The tube is filled with soil, kitchen waste, straw, and leaves -- whatever
  growing medium is available. On a rubbish tip, even contaminated materials
  can be used; the rotting process has a detoxifying function.
- Small holes are cut into the outer geotextile and seeds sown or young
  plants planted into them.

### The Bypass Feeding System

- The inner hose connects to a bucket positioned above the tube, protected
  by chicken wire to prevent clogging from leaves and debris.
- The bucket is filled with stinging nettles, orach, and other nutrient-rich
  leaves.
- When it rains, the bucket fills with water and creates liquid manure (or
  "tea") automatically.
- A valve between bucket and tube gives the gardener control over watering.
- The inner hose is twisted at the bottom to prevent feed from running out.
  If blockage occurs, simply untwist the hose and flush water through from
  the top.

### What to Grow

Radishes, lettuces, cabbages, herbs, strawberries, and almost any vegetable
can be grown in the tubes. The plants grow quickly and the tubes become
living pieces of edible art.

## Installation Options

- Wrap tubes around telegraph poles or bridge piers.
- Hang tubes from balconies or windows.
- Run tubes along house walls (protect the wall with a plastic sheet to
  prevent moss growth from increased moisture; use an additional plank for
  wooden walls).
- Connect multiple tubes for longer vertical gardens.

## The Rubbish Tower

A variation developed at Tamera: three old metal poles create a 3m tripod.
A watering hose hangs inside. Geotextile is wrapped around the tripod and
filled with straw, soil, leaves, and kitchen waste. Small holes are made
and melons, tomatoes, cabbages, and other vegetables are planted. The tower
is watered from the top or via a small hand pump from a ground container.
As internal material decomposes and sinks, more organic matter is added from
the top.

## Part of a Larger Urban Strategy

These techniques are part of Holzer's broader [[holzer-urban-farming]]
approach. Combined with [[holzer-urban-gardening-rubbish-hugelkultur]], mini-crater gardens, hanging gardens, and wall-mounted
containers, they demonstrate that food can be grown anywhere -- even on
rubbish tips in slums. The system has been adopted in cities worldwide,
from Sao Paulo to Moscow.

## Variations and Adaptations

The basic edible tube concept has been adapted in numerous ways by practitioners around the world. Horizontal tubes laid along the ground serve as low raised beds with built-in irrigation, suitable for gardeners who cannot manage vertical installations. Shorter tubes (50-100 cm) placed on windowsills or kitchen counters provide fresh herbs and salad greens within arm's reach. Double-layered tubes with an outer geotextile and inner waterproof liner create self-watering planters that require less frequent attention. In tropical climates, tubes wrapped around existing tree trunks take advantage of the shade and vertical space created by established trees. Each adaptation demonstrates the flexibility of Holzer's core principle: food production can be integrated into any spatial context with minimal infrastructure investment.

## Plant Selection for Vertical Growing

Not all plants perform equally well in the tube system. Shallow-rooted plants with compact growth habits are best suited: lettuce, spinach, arugula, radishes, strawberries, herbs (basil, parsley, cilantro, thyme), and edible flowers (nasturtiums, calendula). Plants with deep taproots or large root systems (carrots, potatoes, tomatoes) are less suitable unless the tube diameter is substantially increased. Climbing plants can be trained upward from tubes mounted near trellises or railings. Succession planting — replacing harvested plants with new seedlings — maximizes the productivity of each tube. The key principle is matching plant selection to the available root volume and light conditions at each position on the tube.

## Maintenance and Troubleshooting

Edible tubes require regular but simple maintenance. The bypass feeding system needs replenishment every 2-4 weeks depending on plant density and rainfall. Monitor the inner hose for clogging, particularly if the tea source contains fibrous material that can block the perforations. During hot, dry periods, supplemental watering through the top of the tube may be needed if rainfall alone is insufficient. Inspect plants regularly for pests — aphids and whiteflies can spread rapidly in the confined space of a tube. Replace the geotextile every 2-3 years, as UV exposure eventually degrades the fabric. At the end of the growing season, the spent growing medium can be composted and replaced with fresh material, making the system fully recyclable.

## Educational and Community Applications

The edible tube system has proven particularly effective in educational and community settings. Its visual impact — a wall of growing food — captures attention and demonstrates the possibilities of urban agriculture in a compelling way. Schools use tube gardens as hands-on teaching tools for botany, ecology, and nutrition. Community gardens in dense urban neighborhoods install tube systems on fences and walls to maximize production per square foot. Refugee communities and urban farming cooperatives have adopted the system because it requires minimal land access and can be implemented with locally available materials. The low cost and simplicity of the system make it accessible to populations that might otherwise lack the resources or space for traditional gardening.

## Cost Analysis and Material Sources

The edible tube system is remarkably cost-effective compared to conventional container gardening. The primary materials — geotextile fabric, irrigation hose, and organic growing medium — are inexpensive and widely available. Geotextile fabric can be purchased from agricultural supply stores, landscaping suppliers, or repurposed from construction sites (landscape fabric offcuts work well). The inner irrigation hose can be made from standard garden soaker hose or from recycled materials such as old garden hose perforated with small holes. Growing medium can be produced on-site using compost, leaf mold, and topsoil rather than purchased as commercial potting mix. Holzer estimates that a complete vertical growing system can be installed for a fraction of the cost of an equivalent area of raised beds with commercial materials. The low cost makes the system accessible to low-income communities and developing regions where conventional garden infrastructure is prohibitively expensive.

## Integration with Permaculture Design

The edible tube system fits naturally within permaculture design frameworks. In zone 1 (the area closest to the home), vertical tubes on walls and railings provide easy access to daily-use herbs and salad greens. In zone 2 (the home garden area), freestanding tubes serve as both food production and functional screens or windbreaks. The bypass feeding system integrates with other permaculture elements by using compost tea, worm castings, or nutrient-rich water from aquaculture systems as the liquid feed. The tubes themselves become habitat for beneficial insects when planted with flowering species alongside food crops. At the end of their useful life, the geotextile fabric can be shredded and added to compost, and the growing medium returns to the soil. This cradle-to-cradle approach eliminates waste and closes the nutrient cycle, consistent with permaculture principles of cyclical resource use.

## Seasonal Considerations for Temperate Climates

In temperate climates, the edible tube system requires seasonal adjustments to maintain productivity year-round. During spring, tubes should be refreshed with new growing medium and planted with cool-season crops such as lettuce, spinach, radishes, and peas. As temperatures rise in summer, the geotextile fabric provides some insulation against root overheating, but additional shading may be necessary for south-facing installations — companion planting with climbing vines that shade the tube surface while producing food can serve this dual purpose. Fall is ideal for planting cool-season greens and overwintering crops such as kale, chard, and mizuna in milder climates. In cold winters, tubes mounted on exterior walls may need insulation wraps or relocation to sheltered positions; some practitioners move smaller tube sections indoors or into greenhouses. The bypass feeding system should be reduced or suspended during winter dormancy periods to prevent nutrient buildup in unabsorbed growing medium, then resumed at half strength when spring growth begins.

## Bypass Feed Composition and Organic Inputs

The liquid feed delivered through the bypass system can be composed of various organic inputs depending on availability and plant needs. Compost tea (aerated or non-aerated) is the most common feed, providing a broad spectrum of nutrients and beneficial microorganisms. Worm castings steeped in water produce a nutrient-rich liquid feed that is gentle enough for seedlings. Comfrey tea, made by steeping comfrey leaves in water for 2-4 weeks, provides concentrated potassium that supports flowering and fruiting plants. Nettle tea offers a nitrogen-rich feed that promotes vegetative growth. Holzer recommends rotating between different feed types through the season: nitrogen-rich feeds during spring growth, balanced feeds during midsummer, and potassium-rich feeds as plants mature. The dilution ratio depends on the feed strength — compost tea can often be applied undiluted, while comfrey and nettle teas typically require dilution at 1:10 to avoid burning plants. Adding a small amount of seaweed extract to any feed provides trace minerals that support overall plant health.

## Pest and Disease Management in Vertical Tube Systems

Vertical growing systems present unique pest and disease challenges due to their confined root volumes and close plant proximity. Aphids are the most common pest, attracted to the soft new growth of leafy greens and herbs; they can spread rapidly in the sheltered microclimate of a vertical installation. Regular inspection and manual removal with a strong water spray is effective for small infestations, while insecticidal soap or neem oil provides control for larger outbreaks. Fungal diseases such as powdery mildew and botrytis can develop in humid, poorly ventilated installations — ensuring adequate spacing between plants and between the tube and any wall or fence surface promotes air circulation and reduces disease risk. Root rot, caused by overwatering or poorly drained growing medium, is the most serious disease threat; it can be prevented by using a coarse, free-draining growing medium and monitoring moisture levels carefully. The geotextile fabric itself is resistant to most pathogens but should be inspected periodically for mold growth in persistently damp conditions.
