# Spawn Production for Mushroom Cultivation

Spawn is mycelium that is ready to be planted into a growing medium. It serves as the "seed" of mushroom cultivation, carrying the genetic material of the desired fungus into the fruiting substrate. From Cotter's "Organic Mushroom Farming and Mycoremediation."

## Understanding Spawn

- Spawn is living mycelium propagated on a nutritious carrier material
- Strain-specific: only genetically identical strains will fuse when mycelium meets
- Available commercially for ~$2-4 per pound (grain spawn)
- Can be expanded from purchased spawn, though each expansion carries contamination risk
- The goal is to produce enough mycelium to colonize the fruiting substrate quickly and outcompete contaminants

## Types of Spawn

### Grain Spawn

- Made from wheat, rye, millet, or other cereal grains
- Most versatile spawn type for indoor cultivation
- Provides high nutrition for rapid mycelial growth
- Sterilized in a pressure cooker at 15 PSI
- Cost: approximately $2-4 per pound commercially
- Best for indoor fruiting substrates (sawdust, straw, compost)
- Vulnerable to insect predation; avoid using outdoors where insects are present

### Sawdust Spawn

- Cheapest form of spawn; preferred by commercial growers
- Made from hardwood sawdust supplemented with wheat or rice bran (~5% dry weight)
- Lower nutrition than grain but less attractive to pests
- Ideal for outdoor log and stump inoculation
- Excellent for outdoor cultivation where insect pressure is a concern
- Can be sterilized or pasteurized depending on application

### Plug Spawn

- 5/16-inch birch dowels, typically 1 inch long, with grooves cut into them
- Designed for outdoor log and stump cultivation
- Inoculated by hammering into drilled holes (1-1.5 inches deep, using a 5/16-inch bit)
- Sealed with food-grade wax after insertion to prevent drying and contamination
- Convenient for small-scale outdoor projects; easy to ship and store
- Slower to colonize than grain or sawdust spawn

### Cardboard Spawn

- Made by soaking corrugated cardboard and inoculating with mushroom mycelium
- Very low-cost method; uses waste material
- Good for preliminary isolation and wild spawn expansion
- Can be produced without laboratory equipment
- Stack layers of soaked cardboard with spawn in plastic bins
- Drill 1/2-inch holes every 8-10 inches for gas exchange
- Colonize in a warm, dark place, then expose to diffuse light for fruiting

## Starting Cultures

### Spore Germination

- Spores offer genetically variable offspring, ideal for breeding
- Spores can remain viable for more than a year if kept cool and dry
- Streak spores onto agar plates in a Z pattern using an inoculation loop
- Germination takes 7-10 days for most species (morels and paddy straw are faster)
- Sectoring occurs when different strains (ecotypes) separate on the plate
- Select the healthiest, fastest-growing sectors for expansion
- Always take a spore print as backup when cloning wild mushrooms

### Tissue Cloning

- Creates genetically identical copies of the parent mushroom
- Best tissue comes from the interior of the mushroom: dry, fluffy mycelium
- Avoid tissue that is wet, has holes, discoloration, or insect damage
- Crack the mushroom cap open to reveal sterile internal tissue
- For wet specimens, air-dry for a day or two before cloning
- Some mushrooms can be cloned up to a year after harvest by dehydrating and refrigerating
- Place multiple tissue samples per plate to increase chances (higher contamination risk but saves plates)

### Special Cloning Techniques for Finicky Mushrooms

- Submerge the mushroom cap underwater with only the attachment point exposed
- The lack of oxygen forces mycelium up and out of the attachment point
- When fuzzed up, snip the tissue for cloning
- For polypores, soak in 3% hydrogen peroxide for several hours before cracking open
- For deteriorating specimens, cut colonized substrate wood into small bits, soak in peroxide, refrigerate overnight, then transfer to agar

## Grain Spawn Production

1. Hydrate grains (soak or boil until fully hydrated but not bursting)
2. Drain excess water; load grains into jars or autoclave bags
3. Sterilize at 15 PSI for 90 minutes to 2 hours
4. Cool in front of the laminar flow hood
5. Inoculate with agar culture or liquid culture
6. Incubate at 77°F (25°C) in darkness
7. Shake jars at 30% colonization to distribute mycelium evenly
8. Full colonization typically takes 2-4 weeks depending on species

## Spawn Expansion

- Each transfer (generation) of spawn carries increasing risk of contamination
- Commercial operations typically limit to 3-4 generations from master culture
- Store master cultures on agar plates, in liquid culture, or as dried grain spawn
- Tropical strains (e.g., Agaricus blazei) may die under prolonged refrigeration; store at 50-55°F instead
- Alternating growth media (agar to grain to sawdust) helps maintain enzyme production and prevents strain senescence

## Rescuing Contaminated Cultures

- If mycelium and contaminant are separated on the plate, transfer mycelium to a fresh plate
- Never open a contaminated plate near the laminar flow hood
- Perform "still-air" transfers in a glove box or room with no airflow
- Disinfect the entire work area including walls and table
- Molds appear as circular colonies with powdery spore rings (green, black, yellow, brown)
- Bacteria appear as irregular, cloudy, or clear smooth zones
- Healthy mushroom mycelium is typically linear, cottony, and white

## Liquid Culture

- Agar plates can be blended with sterilized water to create liquid inoculum
- Add liquid culture directly to sterilized grain for faster, more even colonization
- Useful for scaling up production quickly
- Store liquid cultures with antibiotics to prevent bacterial growth
- Can be used to dose filtration units in mycoremediation applications
