# Elaphomyces Deer Truffles as Ceremonial Director Fungi

## Overview

Elaphomyces species, commonly known as deer truffles or false truffles, are hypogeous (underground) fungi in the family Elaphomycetaceae (Eurotiomycetes). While these fungi are not themselves neurotropic, they play a remarkable and largely overlooked role in indigenous mushroom ceremonies in Oaxaca, Mexico.

Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (2000) documented that specimens of Elaphomyces are placed at the center of the room during nocturnal ceremonies performed by indigenous peoples of the Nevado de Toluca region. The fungus serves as a "director" of the ceremony — a symbolic focal point around which the ritual is organized.

This practice represents one of the most unusual examples of non-psychoactive fungi serving a central ceremonial function in indigenous mushroom rituals. The Elaphomyces specimen sits at the physical and symbolic heart of the ceremony, while psychoactive species are consumed by participants.

The arrangement suggests a cosmological framework in which the underground truffle represents the stable, grounding element while the above-ground fungi represent movement, vision, and spiritual experience.

## Taxonomic Background

Elaphomyces is a genus of truffle-like ascomycete fungi that grow underground in association with tree roots, particularly conifers and some hardwoods. They are not true truffles (Tuber spp.) but belong to a different order within the Ascomycota.

Key species involved in Mexican ceremonial use include:

- **E. granulatus** Fr. — widespread across temperate forests of the Northern Hemisphere, associated with coniferous trees. The most commonly documented species in Mexican ceremonies.
- **E. muricatus** Fr. — characterized by a warty or spiny outer peridium, found in deciduous and mixed forests.
- **E. reticulatus** Vitt. — distinguished by a reticulate (net-like) outer surface pattern. Less commonly documented in ceremonial contexts but present in the Nevado de Toluca region.

These fungi are ectomycorrhizal, forming symbiotic associations with tree roots — primarily Pinus, Abies, and Quercus species in the high-elevation forests of central Mexico.

They are typically found by digging animals — deer, wild boar, rodents — which disperse their spores through fecal matter. The fruiting bodies are hard, tuberous masses ranging from 1 to 5 cm in diameter, with a dark exterior and a powdery, spore-filled interior.

## The Ceremony Director Role

In the Nevado de Toluca region of central Mexico, Elaphomyces specimens serve as the focal point of nocturnal mushroom ceremonies. The fungus is placed in the literal center of the ceremonial space, functioning as a symbolic and perhaps energetic "director" of the ritual.

This practice is documented in connection with several fungi:

- **Cordyceps capitata** and **C. ophioglossoides** — called "hombrecitos" (little men)
- **Psilocybe muliercula** — called "mujercitas" (little women)
- Various other neurotropic Psilocybe species native to the high-elevation forests

The Cordyceps species are eaten either together with Psilocybe muliercula or alone during these special nocturnal ceremonies.

Guzmán (1959, 1994a, 1997) was among the first mycologists to document this practice in detail. The placement of the Elaphomyces specimen at the center suggests a ritual function that goes beyond mere decoration.

The gendered naming convention — "little men" for Cordyceps and "little women" for Psilocybe — combined with the central role of the Elaphomyces, hints at a complex symbolic system in which different fungi represent different aspects of a cosmological family or social structure.

## Connection to Cordyceps Biology

The relationship between Elaphomyces and Cordyceps in these ceremonies has a concrete biological basis. Elaphomyces species are the primary hosts (sclerotia) of Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides.

These Cordyceps species are parasitic fungi that colonize and eventually fruit from the underground Elaphomyces truffles. When indigenous peoples collect Cordyceps for ceremonial use, they are necessarily encountering the Elaphomyces host in the process.

The Cordyceps fruiting body emerges from the buried Elaphomyces and pushes up through the soil and forest litter, creating a visible stalked perithecium that is relatively easy to spot on the forest floor.

This parasitic relationship likely explains why Elaphomyces became incorporated into the ceremony. As the "source" or "body" from which the Cordyceps emerge, it may symbolically represent origin, substrate, or the earth itself.

The Cordyceps, growing from the body of the Elaphomyces, could be seen as the "children" or "spirits" emerging from their earthen vessel. The ceremony thus reenacts or honors this natural relationship between host and parasite, earth and emergence, body and spirit.

## Medicinal Use Among the Trique Indians

Beyond its ceremonial role, Elaphomyces has documented medicinal use among the Trique Indians of the Alta Mixteca region in Oaxaca. Trappe et al. (1979) and Guzmán (1994a, 1994b) reported two applications:

1. **Wound healing** — used to help heal wounds, possibly applied topically
2. **Rejuvenation** — used for "rejuvenecer el organismo" (rejuvenating the organism)

This medicinal application is entirely separate from any neurotropic function and appears to be based on folk beliefs about the restorative properties of truffle-like fungi.

The wound-healing application may relate to the physical properties of the fungi. Some Elaphomyces species produce antibiotic and antifungal secondary metabolites as part of their ecological strategy, though they have not been extensively studied for pharmacological activity.

The concept of "rejuvenation" likely stems from the association of underground fungi with vitality, earth energy, and the regenerative power of the forest floor.

The Trique use is geographically and culturally distinct from the Nevado de Toluca ceremonies, suggesting that Elaphomyces has a broader role in indigenous Mesoamerican ethnomycology than has been fully documented.

## Ecological Significance

Elaphomyces species are ecologically important in several ways:

- As **ectomycorrhizal partners** of forest trees, facilitating nutrient exchange
- As **food sources** for forest-dwelling mammals including deer, rodents, and wild boar
- As **hosts** for parasitic Cordyceps species, supporting their life cycle
- As **soil organisms** that contribute to decomposition and nutrient cycling

Their role in the ceremony highlights the deep ecological knowledge of indigenous peoples. The ceremonial practitioners understand the life cycle of these fungi, their seasonal availability, their relationship to Cordyceps, and their forest habitat with considerable precision.

The Nevado de Toluca region is a high-elevation volcanic area with mixed coniferous and deciduous forests, an environment that supports diverse fungal communities. The fact that practitioners can reliably locate Elaphomyces, identify the correct Cordyceps species, and distinguish associated Psilocybe muliercula from non-active lookalikes demonstrates sophisticated mycological knowledge transmitted across generations.

## The Dual System: Active and Symbolic Fungi

The use of both psychoactive and non-psychoactive fungi in a single ceremony is unusual. In most documented indigenous mushroom ceremonies worldwide, only the psychoactive species play a role. The Nevado de Toluca practice differs in several important ways:

The non-psychoactive Elaphomyces occupies the central, most important position in the ceremonial space. The psychoactive species — Cordyceps and Psilocybe — are consumed but are peripheral to the physical arrangement of the ceremony.

This suggests a cosmological framework in which:

- **Elaphomyces** represents stability, grounding, earth, origin, or the "body" from which all else emerges
- **Cordyceps** represents transformation, emergence from the earth, the bridge between underground and above-ground worlds
- **Psilocybe** represents vision, spirit, and the active psychedelic experience

This layered symbolic system is rare in the ethnomycological literature and deserves further anthropological and mycological study.

## Historical Documentation

Gastón Guzmán, the preeminent Mexican mycologist and leading authority on Psilocybe, documented these practices across multiple publications spanning from 1959 to 1997. His fieldwork provided the primary scientific record of the nocturnal ceremony structure, the placement of Elaphomyces, and the medicinal use by the Trique people.

Guzmán first encountered Elaphomyces in ceremonial contexts during his early expeditions to Oaxaca in the late 1950s. His systematic documentation of the practice across four decades provides one of the most detailed records of indigenous fungal ceremony in the mycological literature.

The Cordyceps species used in these ceremonies are closely related taxonomically to Claviceps purpurea, the famous ergot fungus that produces hallucinogenic alkaloids. Hofmann, working with Wasson, did not find indoles in preserved Cordyceps specimens from Mexican markets — likely because the specimens had been stored for too long, as Guzmán noted that psilocybin and related compounds degrade significantly with age.

## Classification and Significance

Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (2000) classified the world's neurotropic fungi into four groups. Elaphomyces does not belong to any of these groups — it contains no known psilocybin, ibotenic acid, or ergot alkaloids.

Its inclusion in the broader discussion of sacred fungi in Mexico illustrates that indigenous fungal taxonomy and ritual use do not map cleanly onto Western chemical classifications. The ceremonial significance of a fungus may derive from its ecological role, its appearance, its seasonal timing, or its mythological associations rather than its chemical constituents.

The Elaphomyces ceremony represents a form of mycological knowledge that transcends the purely chemical focus of modern psychopharmacology and offers a window into a more holistic understanding of human-fungal relationships.
