## Fukuoka on Food Quality Decline and Petroleum Agriculture

> From "The Natural Way of Farming" by [[masanobu-fukuoka]] — the "Disappearance of a [[natural-diet]]" section.

## Decline in Food Quality

Modern agriculture has undergone a profound transformation over the past century, and with it, the quality of the food it produces has deteriorated steadily and alarmingly. Crops grown with vast inputs of petroleum energy — through [[marijuana-horticulture-cervantes-artificial-lighting-and-electricity]] supplements or replaces sunlight entirely. Carbon dioxide is sprayed into the growing environment to boost photosynthetic rates, and oxygen is pumped in to further accelerate biological processes.

Every variable is controlled, every input optimized for maximum speed and yield. The plant becomes a machine for converting chemical inputs into salable biomass.

The products that emerge appear beautiful — uniform in size, vibrant in color, free of blemish — but they are inferior in quality. They lack the depth of flavor, nutritional complexity, and vital energy of food grown in living soil under natural conditions.

The appearance of health masks an underlying emptiness — a food that fills the stomach but does not nourish the body. Yet this is the food that modern society has come to accept as normal.

Nature has struck back fiercely against this affront by technology, in the form of increased insect damage. Plants grown in artificial, nutrient-poor environments are weak and vulnerable, their natural defenses compromised by the absence of the complex soil biology that would normally supply micronutrients and protective compounds.

They attract pests that healthy, naturally grown plants would resist without any intervention at all. The response has been predictable and tragic: increasing dependence on pesticides and fertilizers, creating a destructive cycle that further degrades both food and ecosystem.

The ultimate endpoint of this trajectory, Fukuoka warns, is total chemical food synthesis — factories making food entirely unrelated to nature. Urea synthesis enables organic material production from petroleum. Protein synthesis enables man-made meat. Butter and cheese can be derived from petroleum through chemical processes. Starch is pursued through photosynthesis research, and saccharification converts wood and oil into sugar substitutes.

Each step moves further from the natural world and deeper into the realm
of industrial manufacture, until agriculture itself becomes obsolete and
food becomes just another chemical product.

## Production Costs Are Not Coming Down

Despite the persistent promise that technology would make food cheaper and more abundant, artificial food production does not deliver cheaper food. A vegetable factory in a city center with full automation — climate-controlled environments, hydroponically fed plants, robotically harvested crops — cannot produce food cheaper than what sunlight and soil provide freely to anyone willing to work with nature rather than against it.

"Nature produces without calling for supplies or remuneration," Fukuoka observes, "but human effort always demands payment." This is the fundamental economic insight that proponents of technological agriculture fail to grasp. Every layer of technology adds cost: the equipment itself, the energy to run it, the maintenance it requires, the infrastructure to support it, the expertise to operate it.

More sophisticated equipment means higher costs, and man never knows when
to stop. The pursuit of efficiency through technology becomes a
self-reinforcing spiral of expenditure that consumes more than it produces.

Even attempts to produce food through microorganisms like chlorella and yeast — heralded as revolutionary solutions to world hunger — have failed to deliver on their promises. Science cannot produce something from nothing. The result is decreased production and a high-cost product that cannot compete with the abundance nature provides when left to its own devices.

The human cost is perhaps the most serious consequence of all. "People
brought up eating unnatural food develop into artificial, anti-natural
human beings with an unnatural body prone to disease." The quality of the
food shapes the quality of the person who eats it, and a diet of
industrially manufactured, petroleum-derived food produces bodies that are
themselves artificial and vulnerable — prone to chronic illness and
disconnection from the natural world.

## Increased Production Has Not Brought Increased Yields

The central promise [[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]], a single man-day of labor — the farmer consuming 2,000 kcal of food energy — can recover 200,000 kcal from a quarter-acre of land. This represents an extraordinary energy return ratio of 100:1, made possible by the fact that sunlight, rainfall, soil biology, and natural cycles provide the vast majority of the energy input.

The farmer's labor merely guides and harvests what nature produces on its own. No other human activity approaches this level of energy efficiency.

Introduce draft animals — horses or oxen — and the energy input rises by 5-10 times, as the animals must be fed from the same land they work. Mechanize the operation with tractors and equipment, and the input rises 10-50 times, as fossil fuels replace animal and human energy. Full scientific agriculture, with all its chemical inputs and technological systems, can require up to 50 times the energy input [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] to produce the same caloric output.

This is not progress; it is profligacy on a civilization-threatening scale. No other human activity approaches this level of energy waste.

The minimum daily calorie requirement for human survival is remarkably low. A hermit's diet of brown rice, sesame, and salt requires roughly 1,000 kcal per day. A more varied diet including brown rice and vegetables requires about 1,500 kcal. This is equivalent to approximately 0.1 horsepower — a trivial amount of energy compared to what modern agriculture expends per unit of food produced.

Fukuoka argues that pigs and goats are far better suited for tilling soil than horses or cows, and that humans should have left the work of soil cultivation to small animals — chickens, rabbits, mice, moles, and worms — that aerate and fertilize the soil naturally without the enormous energy expenditure that large livestock require.

These small creatures work continuously, without fuel, without wages, and without degrading the land. They are nature's own agricultural workforce, and they perform their labor far more efficiently than any machine.

The math is stark: it takes 2 or more acres of pasture to feed a single horse or cow. That same land, worked by natural methods and the labor of human beings cooperating with nature, could feed 50 to 100 people using nature's own powers of production. The decision to rely on large animals for agricultural labor was, in Fukuoka's view, a catastrophic misallocation of land and energy resources.

He points to India as a cautionary example: farmers impoverished by raising cows and elephants that consumed all available grass, with their dung burned as fuel rather than returned to the soil. The result was a progressive depletion of soil fertility that trapped generations in poverty and hunger — a direct consequence of choosing energy-wasteful methods over nature's efficient abundance.

## Summary

Fukuoka's analysis presents a coherent and devastating critique: petroleum-based agriculture produces inferior food at higher energy and financial cost, degrades the soil and ecosystem, impoverishes farmers, and creates a dependent population eating food that undermines their health. The alternative — natural farming — produces abundant, high-quality food with minimal energy input, maintains soil fertility, and supports human autonomy. The choice between these two paths is, for Fukuoka, ultimately a choice about what kind of civilization we wish to build.
