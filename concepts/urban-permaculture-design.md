# Urban Permaculture Design

Applying permaculture principles in small spaces: balconies, rooftops, vacant lots, and community gardens. Urban permaculture adapts zone/sector analysis, guild design, and closed-loop systems to constrained environments. See [[permaculture-design]] for zone/sector basics and [[permaculture-design-methods]] for the full design toolkit.

## Principles for Small Spaces

1. **Stack functions**: Every element serves multiple purposes (shade, food, habitat, beauty)
2. **Use vertical space**: Walls, railings, trellises, hanging systems multiply growing area
3. **Close the loops**: Compost food scraps, harvest rainwater, cycle nutrients on-site
4. **Observe microclimates**: Walls create heat sinks, balconies have unique wind/sun patterns
5. **Start small, intensify**: 1 sq ft intensively managed outproduces 10 sq ft neglected

## Balcony Gardens

**Sun assessment**: Track hours of direct sun (full = 6+ hrs, partial = 3-6, shade = <3). South-facing (N hemisphere) is ideal.

**Container systems**: Self-watering containers reduce maintenance. Wicking beds work well on balconies. Minimum 5-gallon containers for tomatoes/peppers; 1-3 gallon for herbs/lettuce.

**Vertical growing**: Trellises on railings for cucumbers, beans, peas. Wall-mounted pallet gardens. Hanging baskets for strawberries and trailing herbs. Gutter gardens for shallow-rooted greens.

**Microclimate management**: Thermal mass from concrete walls extends growing season. Windbreaks from railing-mounted fabric protect tender plants. Reflective surfaces maximize light in shaded spots.

**Plant selection**: Compact varieties bred for containers (determinate tomatoes, bush beans, patio peppers). Herbs are highest-value per square foot. Salad greens for continuous harvest. Dwarf fruit trees in large pots (20+ gallon).

## Rooftop Gardens

**Structural considerations**: Verify load capacity (wet soil = 100 lbs/sq ft minimum). Professional assessment recommended. Raised beds on platforms distribute weight. Lightweight soil mixes (perlite, vermiculite, coconut coir) reduce load.

**Wind**: Rooftops are windier than ground level. Windbreaks, trellis panels, and row covers protect plants. Choose wind-tolerant varieties.

**Sun exposure**: Intense rooftop sun requires more frequent watering. Shade cloth (30-50%) prevents leaf burn in summer. White or reflective containers reduce root heat.

**Water management**: Rooftop rainwater harvesting captures water where it's used. Drip irrigation on timers is essential during hot months. Mulch heavily to retain moisture.

**Green roof integration**: Living roofs insulate buildings, reduce stormwater runoff, and provide growing space. Extensive systems (sedums, grasses) need minimal soil depth; intensive systems support vegetables and herbs.

## Vertical Growing Systems

**Trellis and teepee systems**: Bamboo or metal trellises for vining crops (tomatoes, cucumbers, beans, peas, melons). Teepees of bamboo poles for beans and cucumbers. Espalier fruit trees against walls.

**Stacked container towers**: Stackable pot systems (e.g., Garden Tower) rotate for even sun exposure. Strawberry towers maximize berry production in minimal footprint.

**Living walls**: Modular pocket systems mounted on walls or fences. Succulent walls for low maintenance. Herb walls for kitchen gardens. Irrigation integrated into system.

**Hydroponics**: Vertical hydroponic towers (windowfarm or commercial units) grow greens and herbs year-round indoors. Nutrient film technique (NFT) and aeroponics for advanced growers.

## Container Growing

**Soil mixes**: Container soil differs from garden soil — needs excellent drainage and water retention. Standard mix: 1/3 compost, 1/3 peat moss or coconut coir, 1/3 perlite or vermiculite. Add worm castings (10%) for biology. See [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] for microbiology.

**Fertilization**: Container plants deplete nutrients faster than garden beds. Top-dress with compost monthly. Liquid feeds (compost tea, fish emulsion, seaweed extract) every 1-2 weeks during active growth. Slow-release organic fertilizers at planting reduce maintenance.

**Watering**: Containers dry out faster than ground soil. Self-watering containers with reservoirs are game-changers for busy urban growers. Mulch surface with straw or leaves. Water deeply less often rather than shallow frequently.

**Season extension**: Cold frames, row covers, and cloches protect containers from frost. Move containers to sheltered spots in winter. Indoor windowsill growing extends the season.

## Community Gardens

**Getting started**: Find local gardens through community garden networks, city parks departments, or American Community Garden Association. Most charge small annual fees ($20-100) for a plot.

**Design integration**: Apply permaculture zones within your plot — intensive herbs at edges, taller crops in center, perennials along fences. Coordinate with neighbors on pest management, water sharing, and tool libraries.

**Community benefits**: Shared tools, knowledge exchange, seed libraries, compost cooperatives. Social connections and food security. Pollinator corridors across multiple plots.

## Guerilla Gardening Techniques

Guerilla gardening transforms neglected urban spaces into productive landscapes. From Mollison's Permaculture Two: "The greatest change we need to make is from consumption to production."

**Seed bombs**: Clay + compost + seeds formed into balls. Throw into vacant lots, road margins, and neglected areas. See [[natural-farming-seed-balls]] for Fukuoka's technique. Use native species and hardy edibles (radishes, beans, calendula, sunflowers).

**Sheet mulching in public spaces**: Lay cardboard over weeds/grass, cover with compost and mulch. Plant through holes in cardboard. Low-profile technique for creating instant garden beds.

**Tree pit gardens**: The soil pits around street trees are often barren. Enrich with compost and plant drought-tolerant herbs and flowers. Check city ordinances first.

**Railway and roadside planting**: Hardy native species and nitrogen fixers (clover, vetch, lupine) along transport corridors. Select species that don't require maintenance and won't obstruct sight lines.

**Night planting**: For spaces where you don't have permission. Use established plants rather than seeds for faster results and higher survival. Choose tough, self-sufficient species.

## Urban Food Forests

**Food forest principles in small spaces**: Miniature food forests using dwarf rootstock fruit trees, berry bushes, herbs, and groundcovers in polyculture. Even 100 sq ft can support a layered system.

**Recommended urban species**: Dwarf apple/pear/plum, blueberries, strawberries, currants, herbs (thyme, oregano, mint in pots), salad greens, climbing beans, garlic, chives.

**Perennial focus**: Perennials reduce annual planting effort — key when space is limited. Asparagus, rhubarb, sorrel, walking onions, berry bushes, fruit trees, herbs.

## City Zoning Considerations

**Know your ordinances**: Front yard food gardens are legal in most US cities (some require "edible landscaping" aesthetic standards). Backyard chickens legal in many cities (typically 3-6 hens, no roosters). Rainwater harvesting legal in most states but regulated in some (Colorado, Utah). Composting usually allowed but may have setback requirements from property lines.

**Building codes**: Rooftop gardens may require permits. Structural loads must be verified. Fence heights regulated. Greenhouse permits vary by municipality.

## Greywater Systems

**Laundry-to-landscape**: Divert washing machine output to mulch basins around trees and shrubs. Simple, no permit needed in most areas. Use biodegradable soap (no boron, no bleach).

**Kitchen greywater**: Sink water can be directed to container gardens via simple gravity-fed systems. Filter through wood chip mulch.

**Constructed wetlands**: Small-scale wetland systems filter greywater naturally. Can be beautiful garden features. Check local regulations for size thresholds.

## Balcony Composting

**Vermicomposting**: Worm bins (Worm Factory, DIY tote) work perfectly on balconies — odorless, compact, processes kitchen scraps continuously. See [[vermicomposting]] for setup. One pound of worms (Eisenia fetida) processes 0.5 lb food scraps per day.

**Bokashi**: Anaerobic fermentation in sealed buckets handles all food scraps including meat/dairy. See [[bokashi]] for method. Fermented pre-compost is buried in containers after 2 weeks. No odor when properly maintained.

**Compost tumblers**: Small tumbling composters on balconies. Faster than static piles but require regular turning. Best for households with minimal food waste.


## Salvage and Scavenging for Urban Gardens

From Brad Lancaster's "Need a Home? Dive a Dumpster!" (Permaculture Drylands Journal):

Building an urban permaculture site largely from salvaged materials is both practical and aligned with permaculture's waste=resource ethic. Key strategies:

**Salvage yards and trading**: Chain link fencing traded for used 2x4s. Election signs repurposed as roofing. Plywood from old carports becomes workbenches and ramadas. Almost everything needed can be found discarded.

**Water harvesting infrastructure**: Corrugated steel roofing replaces toxic asphalt for rainwater harvesting. Passive solar water heaters built from stripped-down gas water heater tanks in insulated boxes. Old sinks and bathtubs become planters and water features.

**Passive solar retrofits**: Double-glazed windows from salvage replace termite-ridden originals. Old glass saved for [[season-extension-techniques]] solar ovens and cold frames. Rock retaining walls (collected free from development sites) create microclimates and thermal mass. Rebar trellises support edible vines that shade walls in summer.

**Neighbor engagement**: Visible salvage building sparks curiosity and community support. Neighbors offer materials, help, plants, and meals. Being friendly and waving at passersby transforms skepticism into participation.

**Rainwater catchment on the cheap**: Any container under a downspout works. Site tanks at the top of slopes to let gravity distribute water. Even small tanks make a difference — "in the same way that composting creates rich soil from trash, water catchment and greywater cycling create opportunities for growth in barren neighborhoods."
