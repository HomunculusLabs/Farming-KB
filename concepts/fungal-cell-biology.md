# Fungal Cell Biology

Fungi are eukaryotic organisms with unique cellular features that distinguish them from plants, animals, and bacteria. Understanding fungal cell biology is foundational to mycology, cultivation, and biotechnology.

## Fungal Cell Overview

The fungal cell is a eukaryotic cell with a nucleus, membrane-bound organelles, and a rigid cell wall. Fungal cells share features with both plants (cell walls, vacuoles) and animals (heterotrophy, chitin, glycogen storage) but are classified in their own kingdom.

Key differences from other eukaryotes:
- Cell wall composed of chitin and glucans (not cellulose like plants)
- No chloroplasts — all fungi are heterotrophic
- Storage carbohydrate is glycogen (like animals), not starch (like plants)
- Apical growth from hyphal tips
- Unique septal structures with specialized pores

## Hyphal Structure

### The Hypha

The basic structural unit of fungal vegetative growth is the **hypha** — a tubular, filamentous cell that extends at its tip:

- **Diameter**: Typically 2-20 micrometers, varying by species
- **Length**: Indeterminate — hyphae can extend indefinitely
- **Growth**: Occurs exclusively at the tip (apex) via vesicle delivery and cell wall synthesis
- **Organization**: Individual hyphae branch to form a network called **mycelium**

### Apical Growth

Hyphal extension is driven by a highly organized process at the growing tip:

1. **Vesicle supply**: Vesicles produced in the Golgi apparatus travel along microtubules to the Spitzenkorper
2. **Spitzenkorper**: A dome-shaped cluster of vesicles at the hyphal tip that acts as the growth organizing center. First described by Brunswick (1924)
3. **Cell wall synthesis**: Vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing enzymes and cell wall precursors (chitin synthase, glucan synthase) into the apical region
4. **Extension**: New cell wall material is deposited at the tip, pushing the hypha forward
5. **Turgor pressure**: Internal hydrostatic pressure (up to 8 atmospheres in some species) provides the driving force for extension
6. **Rate**: Growth rates range from 0.1 to 40 micrometers per minute depending on species and conditions

### Hyphal Types

- **Coenocytic (aseptate)**: Multinucleate cells with no cross-walls. Common in Zygomycota (e.g., Rhizopus). Cytoplasm streams freely through the hypha
- **Septate**: Divided by cross-walls (septa) into individual cells. Common in Ascomycota and Basidiomycota. Nuclei and cytoplasm can still pass through septal pores

## Cell Wall Composition

The fungal cell wall is a dynamic, multi-layered structure that provides shape, protection, and interaction with the environment. It accounts for 15-30% of the cell's dry weight.

### Structural Components

**Chitin (10-20% of wall)**
- Polymer of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc)
- Same polymer found in arthropod exoskeletons
- Provides rigidity and tensile strength
- Synthesized at the plasma membrane by chitin synthase enzymes
- Arranged in microfibrils running parallel to the cell surface

**Beta-Glucans (50-60% of wall)**
- Beta-1,3-glucan: the main structural glucan, forms a rigid matrix
- Beta-1,6-glucan: branched, links chitin and other glucans together
- Beta-1,3/1,4-glucan: found in some species
- Provides elasticity and shape

**Glycoproteins (20-30% of wall)**
- Mannoproteins: mannose-rich proteins on the outer wall surface
- Involved in cell recognition, adhesion, and immune response interaction
- Form the outermost "fuzzy" layer of the cell wall

### Cell Wall Architecture

Layers from inside (plasma membrane) to outside:

1. **Plasma membrane**: Phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins
2. **Chitin layer**: Innermost structural layer, microfibrillar
3. **Beta-glucan layer**: Thick matrix of interwoven glucan chains
4. **Mannoprotein layer**: Outer surface, highly glycosylated proteins

The cell wall is not static — it is continuously remodeled by hydrolytic enzymes (chitinases, glucanases) and synthases during growth and morphogenesis.

## Hydrophobins

Cysteine-rich proteins found **only in fungi** — always embedded in fungal cell walls. They form a coating on the cell surface that reduces water movement through the wall.

- **Structure**: Both hydrophobic (water-repelling) and hydrophilic (water-attracting) sides
- **Hydrophilic side**: Attaches to fungal wall, prevents cell from drying out
- **Hydrophobic side**: Prevents water from rushing in (which would dilute cytoplasm and damage hyphae)
- **Assembly**: Monomers assembled in the fungus, released from the hyphal tip, join together at water-air interfaces
- **Diversity**: A single fungus can contain 10+ different hydrophobin types, each with specialized attributes
- **Functions**: Inter- and intracellular communication, membrane flow control, hyphal strengthening, attachment to other hyphae/bacteria/soil particles/plants/spores
- **Medical interest**: Under intense study for potential medical applications; agricultural applications also likely

## Extracellular Digestion

Fungi digest food **outside** their cells (extracellular digestion). This is a defining feature of fungal biology:

- **Enzyme production**: Enzymes assembled in the Spitzenkörper and released through the plasma membrane via exocytosis
- **Digestion location**: Just outside the hyphal tip, where most digestive enzymes are released
- **Isozymes**: Specialized enzymes work synergistically — each with small variations, breaking down different parts of complex materials (lignin, cellulose) into simple sugars and amino acids
- **Residual enzymes**: Many enzymes remain in soil as hyphae move on, continuing to break down organic matter for plants and soil organisms
- **Nutrient absorption**: Only molecules <4000-6000 Daltons passively cross the plasma membrane; larger molecules require specialized transport proteins (monoparticular — one protein per molecule type)
- **Secondary metabolites**: Fungi also release organic chemicals unrelated to digestion — some control nearby microbes, some affect host plant growth, some can kill other plants' roots. Includes antibiotics (penicillin) and toxins (aflatoxins)

## Septa

Cross-walls that divide hyphae into compartments in septate fungi:

### Simple Septa (Ascomycetes)

- Solid or with a single central pore
- Pore may be plugged by Woronin bodies (dense protein bodies unique to Ascomycota)
- Woronin bodies can rapidly seal the pore if an adjacent cell is damaged, preventing cytoplasmic loss

### Complex Septa (Basidiomycetes)

- Central pore surrounded by a barrel-shaped swelling (dolipore septum)
- Parenthesome membranes flank each side of the pore
- Pore can be opened or closed to regulate cytoplasmic flow
- Connected to clamp connections for nuclear distribution during dikaryotic growth

### Functions of Septa

- Structural support for the hypha
- Compartmentalization — limits damage if a hyphal segment is breached
- Regulates cytoplasmic streaming between compartments
- In basidiomycetes, coordinates nuclear pairing during conjugate division

## Organelles

### Nucleus

- Typical eukaryotic nucleus with nuclear envelope, nucleolus, and chromatin
- In dikaryotic mycelium, each cell contains two nuclei that divide synchronously
- Nuclear diameter: 2-5 micrometers
- Number of nuclei per cell varies: some coenocytic fungi have hundreds

### Mitochondria

- Tubular or filamentous cristae (unlike the lamellar cristae of animals)
- Produce ATP through oxidative phosphorylation
- Have their own DNA (mtDNA) — circular, like bacteria
- Essential for growth; cannot survive in strictly anaerobic conditions (with exceptions like brewer's yeast)

### Vacuoles

- Large, prominent in mature hyphal cells
- Functions: storage (polyphosphate, amino acids), waste degradation, pH regulation, osmoregulation
- Can occupy up to 80% of cell volume in some species
- Similar in function to plant vacuoles and animal lysosomes

### Endoplasmic Reticulum and Golgi

- Rough ER: studded with ribosomes, protein synthesis
- Smooth ER: lipid synthesis, detoxification
- Golgi apparatus: protein modification, sorting, and packaging into vesicles for transport to the hyphal tip

### Peroxisomes

- Contain oxidative enzymes (catalase, oxidases)
- Detoxify hydrogen peroxide and other reactive oxygen species
- Important for metabolism of unusual carbon sources

### Lipid Bodies

- Stored as triacylglycerols in cytoplasmic droplets
- Energy reserve for spore germination and stress survival
- Some fungi accumulate significant lipid reserves (up to 70% dry weight in oleaginous species)

## Cytoplasm and Cytoplasmic Streaming

- **Cytoplasm**: Dense, granular, contains organelles and soluble proteins
- **Cytoplasmic streaming**: Active movement of cytoplasm and organelles along the hypha, driven by actin-myosin motors
- **Rate**: 1-60 micrometers per second — much faster than hyphal growth
- **Function**: Distributes nutrients, organelles, and signaling molecules throughout the mycelial network
- **Motor proteins**: Myosin V walks along actin filaments to transport vesicles and organelles

## Spore Cells

Fungal spores are specialized cells for dispersal and survival:

- **Conidia (asexual spores)**: Produced by mitosis. Thin-walled, short-lived. Adapted for rapid dispersal
- **Basidiospores / ascospores (sexual spores)**: Produced by meiosis. Often thick-walled, more resistant
- **Chlamydospores**: Thick-walled resting spores for surviving adverse conditions
- **Sclerotia**: Compact masses of hardened mycelium that can survive years of dormancy

Spore walls are significantly thicker than hyphal walls, with additional layers of melanin and other pigments for UV protection and desiccation resistance.
