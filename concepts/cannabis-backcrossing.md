# Cannabis Backcrossing

Backcrossing is one of the most widely used [[cannabis-breeding-techniques-acclimatization-trait-fixing-clarke]] in
[[vic-high-cannabis-genetics-mendelian-inheritance]]. It involves repeated crossing of progeny with one of
the original parental genotypes, most often the mother plant. Cervantes
provides comprehensive procedural guidance for [[cervantes-backcross-breeding-methods]],
including step-by-step protocols for incorporating both dominant and
[[cervantes-dominance-recessive-traits-alleles]].

## Definition and Purpose

Backcross breeding involves crossing progeny back to one of the
original parental genotypes across multiple generations. The parent that
is repeatedly used is called the recurrent parent, while the parent
contributing the desired new trait is called the donor parent. Any time
a generation is crossed to a previous generation, it constitutes a form
of backcross breeding.

The principal goal is to create a population derived mainly from the
genetics of the recurrent parent while incorporating one or more
specific desired traits from the donor parent. This makes backcrossing
ideal for improving an already excellent variety by adding a single new
characteristic.

## Why Backcrossing Is Popular

Backcrossing has become a staple method for clandestine cannabis
breeders for several practical reasons:

- It is simple and rapid when using growrooms or greenhouses.
- It requires only small populations, suitable for space-constrained
  operations.
- [[cannabis-indoor-grow-lighting-systems]] rooms are easily replicated worldwide, so progeny need
  less extensive field-testing across varied environments.
- The method is repeatable when the same parents are used.
- Results are predictable when the recurrent parent is true-breeding.

## Ideal Applications

Backcrossing works best when:

- The recurrent parent is a highly acceptable or nearly ideal genotype
  (e.g., an existing commercial cultivar or inbred line).
- The trait to be introgressed is simply inherited and easily scored
  for phenotype.
- The donor parent possesses the desired trait without serious
  deficiencies in other characteristics.
- Only one or two traits need to be added to an otherwise excellent
  variety.

## Backcrossing a Dominant Trait

Incorporating a simply inherited dominant trait follows a
straightforward protocol:

1. **Cross:** Recurrent Parent x Donor Parent to produce the F1 hybrid
   generation.
2. **Select and backcross:** Select F1 plants showing the dominant
   trait and hybridize to the recurrent parent. The resulting
   generation is denoted BC1 (or Bx1).
3. **Repeat:** Select BC1 plants showing the dominant trait and
   backcross to the recurrent parent to produce BC2.
4. **Continue:** Select BC2 plants showing the dominant trait and
   backcross to the recurrent parent to produce BC3.

After three backcross generations, the population contains
approximately 93.7% genetics from the recurrent parent and only about
6.3% from the donor parent, while reliably expressing the dominant
trait.

## Backcrossing a Recessive Trait

Incorporating a recessive trait requires additional steps because the
recessive expression is masked by dominance in each backcross to the
recurrent parent:

1. Cross Recurrent Parent x Donor Parent to produce F1.
2. Create an F2 population via full sib-mating to expose homozygous
   recessive individuals.
