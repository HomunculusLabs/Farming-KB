    11|# [[gymnopilus-purpuratus]] South American Mushroom in Europe
    12|
    13|Gymnopilus purpuratus is a magnificent mushroom species native to Australia and
    14|South America (Chile) that was imported into Europe, likely through feed grain
    15|shipments from Argentina. Its discovery on European compost heaps represents a
    16|remarkable case of accidental species introduction and subsequent colonization.
    17|
    18|## Historical Discovery
    19|
    20|As far back as May 1887, a new mushroom species was found growing on a tree
    21|fern trunk at the botanical gardens in Kew, England. It was eventually
    22|published as Flammula purpurata Cooke and Massee, later reclassified as
    23|Gymnopilus purpuratus (Cooke and Massee) Sing. Mordecai Cooke, who discovered
    24|it, was a mycologist of outstanding competence who also studied [[gartz-european-psychoactive-mushroom-species]] and formulated the first hypothesis about the bluing phenomenon.
    25|
    26|## North American Intoxications
    27|
    28|More than 50 years ago, in October 1942, a remarkable intoxication occurred in
    29|Cleveland, Ohio, attributed to what was then called Pholiota spectabilis
    30|(now [[gymnopilus-spectabilis]]). A woman experienced glorious visions of color
    31|and sounds of music after nibbling from a mushroom she found in the woods.
    32|
    33|In September 1966, a retired engineer in Harvard, Massachusetts, mistook
    34|Gymnopilus for honey mushrooms ([[armillaria-mellea]]). After eating two or three
    35|caps, within 15 minutes he felt disconnected and "woozy." His head felt numb
    36|and vision blurred. Objects shimmered yellow with dark areas. Trees and grass
    37|were vivid green with spots of radiant purple. Everything seemed unnaturally
    38|colored like a color TV image.
    39|
    40|## European Compost Heap Discovery
    41|
    42|In 1983, a conspicuous mushroom was observed growing on discarded bark and
    43|wood chips near a particle board factory in Ribnitz-Damgarten on the Eastern
    44|German seaboard. The microclimate was created by mixing liquid pig manure with
    45|wood chips. The composting process reaches temperatures of about 176 F (80 C)
    46|inside heaps up to 20 yards long and several yards tall.
    47|
    48|Gymnopilus species thrived on top layers of the heaps, along with other
    49|species from Asian and South American countries with warm climates. It appears
    50|likely that spores stuck to Argentine feed grain, passed unharmed through
    51|pigs' digestive systems, and colonized the compost heaps.
    52|
    53|## Spore Import Theory
    54|
    55|In the late 1970s, large amounts of feed grain were imported from Argentina.
    56|Mushroom spores likely stuck to the grain and passed unharmed through the pigs'
    57|digestive systems. The compost heaps are plowed at least twice a year and
    58|shipped as fertilizer to surrounding fields after about two years, so the
    59|mushrooms continue to grow on wood piles in new locations whenever spores have
    60|reproduced.
    61|
    62|However, with changes in economic conditions and growing ecological awareness
    63|in Eastern Germany, this composting process may soon be discontinued, meaning
    64|this mushroom species may disappear in Europe once again.
    65|
    66|## Physical Description
    67|
    68|For the non-bitter Gymnopilus species:
    69|- **Cap:** 15 to 42 mm broad, occasionally up to 20 cm. Flesh thin, broadly
    70|  convex. Evenly covered with pointy scales, purplish to ruby on yellow
    71|  background, dry. Margin inrolled at first, occasional blue stains.
    72|- **Gills:** Close, golden yellow at first, rusty yellow later from spore
    73|  maturation. Edges concolorous with lamellae and bald.
    74|- **Stem:** Not hollow, 6 to 10 mm x 30 to 80 mm, very rarely up to 15 cm
    75|  tall. Cylindrical to slightly club-shaped. Lower stem area and base bruise
    76|  grayish-blue to greenish when injured and with age.
    77|- **Cortina:** Sulphur yellow, fibrous at apex, disappears with age.
    78|- **Spore dust:** Orange to rusty brown.

    80|## Alkaloid Content
    81|

    83|purpuratus by 1988. Alkaloid concentrations were found to be higher in smaller
    84|mushrooms. Larger mushrooms contained about 0.1% of both psilocin and
    85|psilocybin. The unstable nature of the psilocin molecule was demonstrated:

    87|psilocin had already decayed.

    89|## The Yurimagua Indians Connection
    90|
    91|Jesuits of the 17th and early 18th centuries who travelled to the western
    92|Amazon (Peru) reported that the Yurimagua Indians habitually prepared a
    93|potently intoxicating potion derived from a tree-dwelling mushroom. The
    94|mushrooms appeared on fallen trees as a kind of reddish growth with a spicy
    95|taste. Given the discovery of psilocybin in a South American Gymnopilus
    96|species, these historical accounts appear in a new light.
    97|
    98|## Comparison With Other Gymnopilus Species
    99|
   100|There are significantly more Gymnopilus species in North America (73) than in
   101|Europe (15). European Gymnopilus junonius is one of the largest species, with
   102|stems up to 24 inches (60 cm) tall, but has an extremely bitter taste that
   103|deters ingestion. No known European intoxications have been caused by European
   104|Gymnopilus species, making the South American import unique in this regard.
   105|
   106|## Alkaloid Variation and Sclerotia Formation
   107|
   108|Concentrations of psilocybin and psilocin vary significantly between
   109|specimens. Larger mushrooms contained about 0.1% of both compounds, while
   110|smaller specimens showed higher concentrations. The unstable nature of the
   111|psilocin molecule was clearly demonstrated: during one year of storage until
   112|analysis in 1988, much of the sample's psilocin had already decayed. This
   113|highlights the importance of prompt analysis after collection and proper
   114|storage conditions for accurate alkaloid quantification.
   115|
   116|## European Gymnopilus Diversity and Identification
   117|
   118|With 15 European Gymnopilus species compared to 73 in North America, the
   119|European genus is relatively depauperate. Identification is complicated by
   120|the fact that many Gymnopilus species are extremely bitter, which serves as
   121|a natural deterrent against accidental ingestion. The key differentiator for
   122|Gymnopilus purpuratus among European collections is the combination of
   123|purplish to ruby cap coloration with scaly surface, yellow gills aging to
   124|rusty, and the characteristic grayish-blue to greenish bruising at the stem
   125|base. Most European Gymnopilus species lack both the distinctive coloration
   126|and the psychoactive properties.
   127|
   128|## Greenhouse and Botanical Garden Occurrences
   129|
   130|The discovery of Gymnopilus purpuratus at Kew Gardens in 1887 and its later
   131|appearance on German compost heaps follows a pattern seen with other
   132|psychoactive mushrooms: imported spores establishing themselves in the
   133|controlled, nutrient-rich environments of botanical collections. These
   134|locations provide ideal conditions for colonization, with abundant organic
   135|material, consistent moisture, and protection from extreme weather. Mushroom
   136|enthusiasts and researchers should monitor botanical gardens, herbariums,
   137|and composting facilities as potential sites for unusual fungal
   138|introductions.
   139|
