# [[gymnopilus-purpuratus]] South American Mushroom in Europe

Gymnopilus purpuratus is a magnificent mushroom species native to Australia and
South America (Chile) that was imported into Europe, likely through feed grain
shipments from Argentina. Its discovery on European compost heaps represents a
remarkable case of accidental species introduction and subsequent colonization.

## Historical Discovery

As far back as May 1887, a new mushroom species was found growing on a tree
fern trunk at the botanical gardens in Kew, England. It was eventually
published as Flammula purpurata Cooke and Massee, later reclassified as
Gymnopilus purpuratus (Cooke and Massee) Sing. [[mordecai-cooke]], who discovered
it, was a mycologist of outstanding competence who also studied [[gartz-european-psychoactive-mushroom-species]] and formulated the first hypothesis about the bluing phenomenon.

## North American Intoxications

More than 50 years ago, in October 1942, a remarkable intoxication occurred in
Cleveland, Ohio, attributed to what was then called Pholiota spectabilis
(now [[gymnopilus-spectabilis]]). A woman experienced glorious visions of color
and sounds of music after nibbling from a mushroom she found in the woods.

In September 1966, a retired engineer in Harvard, Massachusetts, mistook
Gymnopilus for honey mushrooms ([[armillaria-mellea]]). After eating two or three
caps, within 15 minutes he felt disconnected and "woozy." His head felt numb
and vision blurred. Objects shimmered yellow with dark areas. Trees and grass
were vivid green with spots of radiant purple. Everything seemed unnaturally
colored like a color TV image.

## European Compost Heap Discovery

In 1983, a conspicuous mushroom was observed growing on discarded bark and
wood chips near a particle board factory in Ribnitz-Damgarten on the Eastern
German seaboard. The microclimate was created by mixing liquid pig manure with
wood chips. The composting process reaches temperatures of about 176 F (80 C)
inside heaps up to 20 yards long and several yards tall.

Gymnopilus species thrived on top layers of the heaps, along with other
species from Asian and South American countries with warm climates. It appears
likely that spores stuck to Argentine feed grain, passed unharmed through
pigs' digestive systems, and colonized the compost heaps.

## Spore Import Theory

In the late 1970s, large amounts of feed grain were imported from Argentina.
Mushroom spores likely stuck to the grain and passed unharmed through the pigs'
digestive systems. The compost heaps are plowed at least twice a year and
shipped as fertilizer to surrounding fields after about two years, so the
mushrooms continue to grow on wood piles in new locations whenever spores have
reproduced.

However, with changes in economic conditions and growing ecological awareness
in Eastern Germany, this composting process may soon be discontinued, meaning
this mushroom species may disappear in Europe once again.

## Physical Description

For the non-bitter Gymnopilus species:
- **Cap:** 15 to 42 mm broad, occasionally up to 20 cm. Flesh thin, broadly
  convex. Evenly covered with pointy scales, purplish to ruby on yellow
  background, dry. Margin inrolled at first, occasional blue stains.
- **Gills:** Close, golden yellow at first, rusty yellow later from spore
  maturation. Edges concolorous with lamellae and bald.
- **Stem:** Not hollow, 6 to 10 mm x 30 to 80 mm, very rarely up to 15 cm
  tall. Cylindrical to slightly club-shaped. Lower stem area and base bruise
  grayish-blue to greenish when injured and with age.
- **Cortina:** Sulphur yellow, fibrous at apex, disappears with age.
- **Spore dust:** Orange to rusty brown.

## Alkaloid Content

Psilocybin and its derivatives were confirmed in European Gymnopilus
purpuratus by 1988. Alkaloid concentrations were found to be higher in smaller
mushrooms. Larger mushrooms contained about 0.1% of both psilocin and
psilocybin. The unstable nature of the psilocin molecule was demonstrated:
during one year of storage until analysis in 1988, much of the sample's
psilocin had already decayed.

## The Yurimagua Indians Connection

Jesuits of the 17th and early 18th centuries who travelled to the western
Amazon (Peru) reported that the Yurimagua Indians habitually prepared a
potently intoxicating potion derived from a tree-dwelling mushroom. The
mushrooms appeared on fallen trees as a kind of reddish growth with a spicy
taste. Given the discovery of psilocybin in a South American Gymnopilus
species, these historical accounts appear in a new light.

## Comparison With Other Gymnopilus Species

There are significantly more Gymnopilus species in North America (73) than in
Europe (15). European [[gymnopilus-junonius]] is one of the largest species, with
stems up to 24 inches (60 cm) tall, but has an extremely bitter taste that
deters ingestion. No known European intoxications have been caused by European
Gymnopilus species, making the South American import unique in this regard.

## Alkaloid Variation and Sclerotia Formation

Concentrations of psilocybin and psilocin vary significantly between
specimens. Larger mushrooms contained about 0.1% of both compounds, while
smaller specimens showed higher concentrations. The unstable nature of the
psilocin molecule was clearly demonstrated: during one year of storage until
analysis in 1988, much of the sample's psilocin had already decayed. This
highlights the importance of prompt analysis after collection and proper
storage conditions for accurate alkaloid quantification.

## European Gymnopilus Diversity and Identification

With 15 European Gymnopilus species compared to 73 in North America, the
European genus is relatively depauperate. Identification is complicated by
the fact that many Gymnopilus species are extremely bitter, which serves as
a natural deterrent against accidental ingestion. The key differentiator for
Gymnopilus purpuratus among European collections is the combination of
purplish to ruby cap coloration with scaly surface, yellow gills aging to
rusty, and the characteristic grayish-blue to greenish bruising at the stem
base. Most European Gymnopilus species lack both the distinctive coloration
and the psychoactive properties.

## Greenhouse and Botanical Garden Occurrences

The discovery of Gymnopilus purpuratus at Kew Gardens in 1887 and its later
appearance on German compost heaps follows a pattern seen with other
psychoactive mushrooms: imported spores establishing themselves in the
controlled, nutrient-rich environments of botanical collections. These
locations provide ideal conditions for colonization, with abundant organic
material, consistent moisture, and protection from extreme weather. Mushroom
enthusiasts and researchers should monitor botanical gardens, herbariums,
and composting facilities as potential sites for unusual fungal
introductions.
