# Phytochemical Research on Sacred Plants

## The Quest for Active Principles

Plants of the gods interest various disciplines: ethnology, religious
studies, history, and folklore. The two major scientific disciplines
that concern themselves with these plants, however, are botany and
chemistry. The botanist must establish the identity of plants used as
sacred drugs. The next step is to determine what constituents --
which substances in those plants -- actually produce the effects that
have led to their use in religious rites and magic. What the chemist
is looking for is the *active principle*, the *quinta essentia*, as
Paracelsus called the active compounds in plant drugs.

Among the many hundreds of different substances that make up the
[[chemical-composition-of-hallucinogens]] a plant, only one or two (occasionally up
to half a dozen) compounds are responsible for its psychoactive
effects. The proportion by weight of these active principles is
usually only a fraction of 1 percent, and frequently even of one part
per thousand of the plant. The main constituents of fresh plants,
usually more than 90 percent by weight, are cellulose (supporting
structure) and water (solvent and transport medium). Carbohydrates,
proteins, fats, mineral salts, and pigments make up several more
percent. Together with these normal components, they constitute
practically the whole plant.

Substances with unusual physiological and psychic effects are found
only in certain special plants. These substances have very different
[[morphine]], isolated by the pharmacist
Friedrich Serturner in 1806 from the opium poppy (*Papaver
somniferum*). He named it for Morpheus, the Greek god of sleep.

Since then, enormous strides have been made. The most important
techniques evolved only during recent decades, including chromatography
-- methods of separation based on the fact that different substances
adhere in varying degrees on absorbent materials or are more or less
readily taken up in solvents that do not mix. The methods used in
qualitative analysis and to establish chemical structure have also
undergone fundamental changes. Formerly, several generations of
chemists would be needed to elucidate the complex structures of
natural compounds. Today, it takes just a few weeks or even days
with techniques of spectroanalysis and X-ray analysis.

With pure compounds -- whether isolated from the plant or synthetically
produced -- exact pharmacological assays and chemical tests can be
made. This is not possible with whole plants because of the varying
content of active principles and interference from other constituents.

## Case Study: The Magic Mushrooms of Mexico

The contribution of chemists to the study of sacred plant drugs is
illustrated by the Mexican Magic Mushrooms. Ethnologists found
Indian tribes in southern Mexico using mushrooms in religious
ceremonies. Mycologists identified the mushrooms. [[albert-hofmann]] tested one
species (*[[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]]*) on himself, confirmed its psychoactivity,
discovered it could be grown under laboratory conditions, and isolated
two active compounds: psilocybine and psilocine, obtained as
colorless crystals.
