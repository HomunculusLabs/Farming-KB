# The Four Phases of Soil Management

Geoff Hamilton divides soil management into four sequential phases that
provide a systematic framework for building and maintaining fertile soil in
the organic garden. This approach ensures that soil health is addressed
comprehensively, from initial assessment through to specialized crop
nutrition. Following these four phases helps gardeners avoid nutrient
deficiencies and maintain long-term soil productivity.

## Phase 1: Analyzing Your Soil

The first step in any soil management programme is to understand what you
are working with. If you are starting out, especially on virgin soil, it
is wise to have it professionally tested at the outset. Soils that have
been uncultivated for many years are often grossly deficient in one or more
elements necessary for healthy plant growth.

The most important initial test is for pH, which tells you whether your
soil is acidic or alkaline. Annual home pH testing is sufficient for most
gardeners once the initial assessment is complete. Full professional soil
analysis will reveal the exact chemical makeup of your soil and identify
any specific deficiencies. When sending a sample for analysis, ask the
laboratory to recommend organic fertilizers for correcting any problems
they find.

Home testing kits for nutrient levels are not accurate enough to be
worthwhile on their own. Used regularly, they will indicate a trend but
no more than that. Professional analysis from a reputable company provides
far more reliable data and specific recommendations.

If you have moved into an established garden that is obviously growing good
crops, or if there are no visible nutrient deficiencies, you may start
directly with Phase 2. However, unless you know the pH value of your soil,
you should test it before beginning any serious soil management programme.

## Phase 2: Using Soil Conditioners

The second phase deals with general soil improvement and replacement of
plant nutrients removed by previous crops. The best materials for this
purpose are manure and compost. They improve drainage or water-holding
capacity and provide nutrients, but substantial quantities are needed to
maintain soil fertility levels.

Well-rotted animal manure is the very best material, but it can be
difficult to obtain. Compost can substitute for manure but must be
well-rotted if it is to be dug into the ground. Alternative soil
conditioners such as spent mushroom compost, green compost, and spent hops
are superb conditioners but should not be relied upon as primary nutrient
sources.

Organic matter should be dug into the top layers of soil during the fall
and used as mulch during the growing season. Mulching involves spreading
organic matter over the soil surface between established plants, where it
acts as a weed suppressant and is gradually worked into the soil by worms
and weather.

### Application Rates

As a general guide, use at least two 2-gallon (9-litre) buckets of
well-rotted compost or animal manure for every square yard/metre of soil
in the vegetable garden. Use one bucket per square yard/metre as a mulch
around trees and shrubs or in ornamental borders. The same rate is
sufficient for fruit trees and bushes. If you can afford to use more, do
not be afraid to do so.

If sufficient organic matter is applied, there may be no need for
additional concentrated fertilizers. However, it is difficult to define
"sufficient" because the amount needed depends entirely on your soil type,
the weather, and the plants you wish to grow.

## Phase 3: Adding General Fertilizer

Not everyone can obtain sufficient supplies of manure or compost. When
organic matter falls below recommended levels, concentrated organic
fertilizers bridge the gap. A general fertilizer such as blood, fish, and
bone meal or pelleted chicken manure provides a balanced supply of the
major nutrients nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.

Application rates vary according to the soil and the crops you intend to
grow. Some crops, such as peas, can generally grow well without additional
fertilizer. Others, such as potatoes, will need extra. Most fruit trees
and bushes need spring fertilizer whether or not they are mulched with
manure or compost.

Where trace element deficiencies have occurred in the past, a light
application of kelp meal or calcified kelp pellets at the beginning of
each season helps prevent recurrence. Kelp contains a wide range of trace
elements and growth-promoting substances that benefit overall plant health.

## Phase 4: Using Specific Fertilizers

Some crops always need special treatment even when general manure and
fertilizer levels are adequate. Greenhouse tomatoes benefit from extra
feeding and a potash fertilizer to encourage flower and fruit formation.
Leafy plants that remain in the ground for a long time, such as spring
cabbage, may need extra nitrogen towards the end of winter.

Raspberries are particularly prone to iron deficiencies when grown in
chalky soil; this can be corrected with liquid kelp fertilizer. Extra
phosphorus before planting trees or sowing encourages root growth. All
specific fertilizer recommendations are discussed in the relevant crop
chapters of the source material.

## Summary of the Regime

Your soil management programme should follow these principles:

1. Test the soil pH and, if starting a new garden, have a professional
   analysis. Correct any identified deficiencies.
2. Apply heavy applications of manure or compost wherever possible.
3. If organic matter is insufficient, supplement with a concentrated
   general organic fertilizer.
4. Provide extra feeds for especially demanding plants or where specific
   deficiencies are noticed.
