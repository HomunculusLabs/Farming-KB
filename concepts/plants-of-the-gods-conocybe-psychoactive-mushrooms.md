# Conocybe: Psychoactive Mushrooms of Mexico

## Taxonomy and Distribution

The genus *Conocybe* belongs to the family Agaricaceae (Bolbitiaceae)
and is cosmopolitan in distribution. While the genus is far less famous
than [[schultes-psilocybe-little-flowers-gods]] or
[[schultes-panaeolus-mushrooms-shamanic]] in the context of
sacred Mexican mushrooms, several *Conocybe* species contain the
psychoactive alkaloid psilocybin and have been used ritually in
Mesoamerican shamanic traditions. The genus represents an often
overlooked component of the [[plants-of-the-gods-overview-hallucinogenic-plant-use]] of Mexico.

## Conocybe siligineoides

*Conocybe siligineoides* Heim is the most significant species in the
ethnopharmacological literature. This beautiful mushroom, up to about 3
in. (8 cm) tall, grows on rotting wood. It has a cap up to 1 in. (2.5
cm) in diameter that is fawn-orange-red, with a deeper orange at the
center. The gills are saffron-colored or brownish orange with chrome
yellow spores. This species has been reported as one of the sacred
intoxicating mushrooms of Mexico and was originally described by the
eminent French mycologist Roger Heim.

Despite its identification as a sacred mushroom, *C. siligineoides*
remains an obscure taxon. Psilocybin has not as yet been isolated from
this species, though its psychoactivity and ritual use suggest the
presence of active compounds. The mushroom has not been found or
analyzed again since its first description, making it one of the most
enigmatic of the Mexican hallucinogenic fungi.

## Conocybe cyanopus

*Conocybe cyanopus* of the United States has been definitively shown
to contain psilocybin. This finding is significant because it
demonstrates that the psychoactive properties of the genus extend
beyond Mexico into North America. Many species of the genus *Conocybe*
contain psilocybin and are psychoactive, suggesting that the genus as a
whole deserves greater attention in ethnobotanical and mycological
research.

## The Tamu Cult

Recently, a rudimentary cult around Tamu (a *Conocybe* species, called
"Mushroom of Awareness") has been discovered. This finding parallels
the well-documented mushroom cults of the Mazatec, Chinantec, and
Zapotec peoples and suggests that the ritual use of psychoactive
mushrooms in Mexico may be more widespread and taxonomically diverse
than previously appreciated. The discovery of new mushroom cults
underscores the importance of continued ethnobotanical fieldwork.

## The Sacred Mushroom Context

The use of mushrooms as sacred intoxicants in Mexico has deep roots.
The Aztecs called them Teonanacatl ("divine flesh") and used them in
the most holy of their ceremonies. As the Mazatec shaman Maria Sabina
reverently described: "There is a world beyond ours, a world that is
far away, nearby, and invisible. And there is where God lives, where
the dead live, the spirits and the saints, a world where everything
has already happened and everything is known. The sacred mushroom takes
me by the hand and brings me to the world where everything is known."

When the Spaniards conquered Mexico, they were aghast to find the
natives worshiping their deities with the help of inebriating plants,
including Peyotl, [[plants-of-the-gods-turbina-corymbosa-ololiuqui]],
and Teonanacatl. The mushrooms were especially offensive to European
ecclesiastical authorities, and they set out to eradicate their use in
religious practices through persecution that drove the cult into hiding
for four centuries.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The psychoactive principles of *Conocybe* species, like those of
[[schultes-psilocybe-little-flowers-gods]] and
[[schultes-panaeolus-mushrooms-shamanic]], belong to the
tryptamine class of [[plants-of-the-gods-phytochemistry-sacred-plants]]. Psilocybin and its dephosphorylated counterpart psilocin
are derived from tryptamine, the same basic compound as the brain
hormone serotonin. The [[plants-of-the-gods-chemical-structures-hallucinogens]] of these compounds reveal their fundamental relationship
to neurotransmitters, explaining their psychotropic potency.

## Species in the Mexican Mushroom Complex

The known hallucinogenic mushrooms of Mexico include species across
several genera: *Psilocybe mexicana*, *P. caerulescens*, *P. aztecorum*,
*P. zapotecorum*, *P. semperviva*, *P. wassonii*, *P. yungensis*,
*P. cubensis*, *Panaeolus sphinctrinus*, *P. cyanescens*, and
*Conocybe siligineoides*. The Mazatec and Chinantec refer to these
mushrooms by various names including She-to ("pasture mushrooms") and
To-shka ("intoxicating mushroom"). *Psilocybe cubensis* is named Hongo
de San Isidro; in the Mazatec language it is called Di-shi-tjo-le-rra-ja
("divine mushroom of manure"). This taxonomic diversity across multiple
genera highlights the sophistication of indigenous mycological knowledge.

## Historical Documentation

Dr. Francisco Hernandez, personal physician to the King of Spain, wrote
that three kinds of intoxicating mushrooms were worshiped. After
describing a lethal species, he stated that "others when eaten cause
not death but madness that on occasion is lasting, of which the symptom
is a kind of uncontrolled laughter. Usually called teyuintli, these
are deep yellow, acrid and of a not displeasing freshness." He also
described mushrooms that "bring before the eyes all kinds of visions,
such as wars and the likeness of demons" and others "desired by princes
for their fiestas and banquets, of great price."

For four centuries, nothing more was known of the mushroom cult, and
it was even doubted that mushrooms were used hallucinogenically in
ceremony. In 1916, an American botanist proposed that Teonanacatl and
the Peyote were the same drug, arguing that the dried brownish crown
of Peyote resembles a dried mushroom so remarkably that it would
deceive even a mycologist. It was not until the 1930s that the role of
hallucinogenic mushrooms in Mexico began to be understood.

## Ongoing Research Significance

The genus *Conocybe* remains poorly studied compared to *Psilocybe* and
*Panaeolus*. The rediscovery and chemical analysis of *C. siligineoides*
would represent a significant contribution to ethnopharmacology. In
1979, the largest and most potent mushroom in the *Psilocybe* genus was
found in Astoria, Oregon -- *Psilocybe azurescens* -- demonstrating that
new discoveries in psychoactive mycology continue to emerge. The study
of genera like *Conocybe* expands our understanding of the full
spectrum of [[plants-of-the-gods-phytochemistry-sacred-plants]] and the cultural contexts in which these organisms
have been revered as sacred.
