# Local Currencies

Local currencies are community-created monetary systems that circulate within a defined geographic area to facilitate trade, support local economic resilience, and keep capital circulating within the community rather than flowing to external economic centres. Bill Mollison discusses [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] issued local scrip to maintain economic activity when national currency was scarce. The Worgl experiment in Austria demonstrated that a local currency with a demurrage fee, which encouraged rapid circulation, could stimulate economic activity and reduce unemployment.

Modern local currency initiatives include Ithaca Hours in Ithaca, New York, which are backed by one hour of local labour. The Chiemgauer in Germany circulates in the Bavarian region. The Brixton Pound in London supports local businesses. Transition Towns worldwide have issued local currencies as part of their community resilience building.

## Design Considerations

Successful local currency systems require several design elements. The currency must be widely accepted within the community for it to function as a medium of exchange. This requires building trust, recruiting businesses to accept the currency, and creating sufficient opportunities to earn and spend it.

The issuing mechanism must prevent inflation. If too much currency is issued relative to the goods and services available, the currency loses value. Most systems limit issuance through member account balances or by backing the currency with a defined asset base.

The system must be manageable with the available administrative capacity. Complex accounting systems may require dedicated staff or software. Simple systems, such as paper notes or basic online accounts, are more accessible but may be less secure.

## Integration with Permaculture Design

Local currencies integrate naturally with permaculture design at the village scale. Mollison's model includes both a local currency pool and a normal currency pool within the village economy. Local currency is created within the community and circulates for goods and services produced and consumed locally. Normal currency is used for trade with the external economy and for goods and services not available within the community.

The village financial model manages both pools to ensure that as much economic activity as possible takes place in local currency, minimising the outflow of conventional money to the external economy. Local currency supports local businesses, creates local employment, and builds community economic resilience.

## Local Currency and Community Services

Local currencies can fund community services without requiring conventional money. Community members can pay for services such as childcare, elder care, home repair, garden maintenance, and education using local currency. This creates employment within the community while providing essential services that might otherwise be unaffordable.

The village model includes cooperative funding for shared resources and infrastructure. Community members can pool local currency to invest in shared tools, equipment, vehicles, and facilities that no individual household could afford alone. The personal accountability principle applies: group purchases are held totally and individually responsible for payment.

## Challenges and Limitations

Local currency systems face several challenges. Building sufficient acceptance and circulation volume to make the system useful requires sustained community engagement. Legal and tax implications vary by jurisdiction and must be carefully considered. Administrative overhead, particularly for mutual credit systems, can be significant. The risk of fraud or misuse requires governance mechanisms. Scaling beyond a small community may require increasingly complex management.

The boundary of the local currency area must be defined. Too small an area limits the range of goods and services available. Too large an area dilutes the community cohesion that gives the currency its value. The bioregional scale, based on natural watershed boundaries, may provide an appropriate balance.

## Relationship to National Currency

Local currencies are not intended to replace national currencies but to complement them. National currencies remain necessary for paying taxes, purchasing imports, and interacting with the broader economy. Local currencies operate alongside national currency, handling the local transactions that would otherwise drain capital from the community.

The two-currency model allows communities to capture and retain the value of local economic activity while maintaining access to the goods, services, and opportunities available in the broader economy. This complementary relationship makes local currencies a practical tool for building economic resilience without economic isolation.
