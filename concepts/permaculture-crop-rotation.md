# Permaculture Crop Rotation

Crop rotation is the practice of changing the location of plant families across growing areas from season to season. In permaculture, rotation is used to maintain soil fertility, break pest and disease cycles, and manage weed pressure — all without relying on synthetic chemical inputs.

## Why Rotate Crops

- **Nutrient management** — different crop families have different nutrient demands and contributions
- **Pest and disease disruption** — many pests and diseases are host-specific; rotation denies them a continuous food source
- **Weed suppression** — varying root depths, canopy structures, and planting times disrupts weed adaptation
- **Soil structure** — alternating deep-rooted and shallow-rooted crops prevents soil compaction layers
- **Root exudate diversity** — different crops feed different soil microbial communities

## The Four-Year Rotation

The most common rotation framework groups crops by plant family and nutrient demand:

### Year 1: Leaf Crops (Heavy Nitrogen Feeders)
- Brassicas: cabbage, broccoli, kale, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts
- Leafy greens: lettuce, spinach, chard
- Corn (also a heavy nitrogen feeder)
- These crops consume large amounts of nitrogen

### Year 2: Fruit Crops (Heavy Phosphorus Feeders)
- Solanaceae: tomatoes, peppers, eggplant
- Cucurbits: squash, cucumbers, melons
- These crops benefit from phosphorus availability
- Follow leaf crops because residual nitrogen is available but reduced

### Year 3: Root Crops (Heavy Potassium Feeders)
- Carrots, beets, potatoes, onions, garlic, radishes, turnips
- These crops need loose, well-drained soil (previous years' root channels help)
- Lower nitrogen demand means they don't compete with depleted nitrogen
- Potassium availability matters for root development

### Year 4: Legume Crops (Nitrogen Fixers)
- Peas, beans, peanuts, clover, vetch, fava beans
- Rhizobial bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen, replenishing soil nitrogen for Year 1
- Can be harvested for food or terminated as green manure

## Break Crop Concept

A break crop is a species introduced into rotation specifically to disrupt a pest, disease, or weed cycle:

- **Mustard as break crop** — biofumigant properties suppress soil-borne pathogens
- **Buckwheat as break crop** — fast growth suppresses weeds, attracts beneficial insects, accumulates phosphorus
- **Sorghum-sudangrass** — deep roots break plow pan, allelopathic residues suppress weeds
- **Marigold (Tagetes)** — reduces root-knot nematode populations when grown as a break crop

## Rotation for Pest and Disease Management

Many pathogens and arthropod pests are host-specific and overwinter in soil or crop residue:

- **Clubroot in brassicas** — rotate brassicas out of affected beds for 4-7 years
- **Colorado potato beetle** — rotate solanaceous crops (potato, tomato, eggplant) to distance adult beetles
- **Fusarium and Verticillium wilt** — long rotations of 4+ years reduce soil inoculum
- **Cyst nematodes** — rotate with non-host crops and include nematode-suppressive cover crops

## Green Manures in Rotation

Green manures (cover crops grown specifically to be incorporated into soil) are integral to permaculture rotation:

### Nitrogen-Fixing Green Manures
- Crimson clover, hairy vetch, field peas, fava beans
- Terminate at peak flowering for maximum nitrogen contribution
- Can fix 50-200 lbs N/acre depending on species and growing conditions

### Biomass-Producing Green Manures
- Winter rye, oats, annual ryegrass
- Build soil organic matter and prevent erosion through winter
- Provide weed suppression through competition and allelopathy

### Multi-Species Cover Crop Blends
- Combine legume + grass + brassica for maximum diversity
- Example: crimson clover + winter rye + daikon radish
- Feeds diverse soil microbial communities and covers multiple rotation functions

## Permaculture Adaptations

Permaculture rotation differs from conventional farming in several ways:

- **Permanent beds** — rotation occurs within permanent raised beds rather than field-scale blocks
- **Polyculture within rotation** — beds may contain multiple families simultaneously (though dominant family rotates)
- **Perennial integration** — perennial crops anchor the system while annual rotation occurs around them
- **Minimal tillage** — rotation is designed for no-till or reduced-till systems
