# The Ethnobotanical Generation: Harner, Halifax, Furst, McKenna,
# and the Shamanic Renaissance

## Context and Sourcing

This concept draws from [[ralph-metzner]] 1998 paper Hallucinogenic Drugs and
Plants in Psychotherapy and Shamanism (Journal of Psychoactive Drugs,
Vol. 30(4)). Metzner documents the synchronistic emergence of researchers in
anthropology and ethnobotany inspired by psychedelic research to explore the
shamanic roots of hallucinogen use. Metzner spent 35 years in consciousness
studies: at Harvard with Leary and Alpert in the 1960s, then exploring
nondrug consciousness methods in the 1970s (yoga, meditation, alchemy,
Reichian breathwork), and engaging with shamanic studies in the 1980s
through Harner, Halifax, Furst, and the McKennas.

## The Synchronistic Emergence of a New Field

Metzner describes a remarkable historical coincidence: as Western psychologists
discovered the therapeutic potential of LSD and psilocybin, a parallel
generation of anthropologists and ethnobotanists explored prehistoric
indigenous roots of psychoactive plant use. The two streams developed
independently but were informed by the same kinds of experiences. Key figures
included Michael Harner, Joan Halifax, Peter Furst, [[richard-evans-schultes]],
Weston LaBarre, Claudio Naranjo, and Terence and Dennis McKenna.

Their work ranged from rigorous botanical documentation to speculative
writings about consciousness and plant medicines. Metzner found it
fascinating that the two texts most adapted for psychedelic use, the
Tibetan Bardo Thodol and the Tao Te Ching, came from traditions with
strong shamanic elements, suggesting deep connections between Eastern
contemplative and indigenous shamanic traditions.

## Michael Harner: Academic Anthropology to Core Shamanism

Harner early work focused on hallucinogens in European witchcraft-shamanism,
documented in Hallucinogens and Shamanism (1973, Oxford University Press),
establishing a cross-cultural framework for psychoactive plants in indigenous
healing. His fieldwork among the Jivaro (Shuar) of the Upper Amazon, where
he experienced ayahuasca, convinced him that studying shamanism required
participation, not just observation.

Harner later developed core shamanism, shamanic practices stripped of
cultural content for Western accessibility. The Way of the Shaman (1980)
introduced drumming-based journey techniques and the term nonordinary
reality for spirit worlds. He founded the Foundation for Shamanic Studies,
training thousands in core shamanic techniques.

## Joan Halifax: The Wounded Healer Archetype

Halifax Shaman: The Wounded Healer (1982) explored the shaman who undergoes
personal suffering as prerequisite for healing others, resonating with Jung
wounded healer concept. Halifax emphasized that shamanic initiation involves
death-rebirth experiences: illness, near-death, or crisis. The shaman is
dismembered by spirits and reconstituted stronger, mirroring psychedelic
therapy dissolution and reintegration. This connects to Grof perinatal
matrices as a Western elaboration of the same death-rebirth archetype.

Halifax also documented women as shamans across cultures, challenging
male-dominated narratives and demonstrating women central roles in shamanic
healing traditions from Siberia to the Amazon.

## Peter Furst: Flesh of the Gods and Deep Antiquity

Furst Flesh of the Gods (1972/1990) assembled archaeological, ethnographic,
and iconographic evidence for hallucinogenic plant use in pre-Columbian
Mesoamerica and South America, demonstrating these plants were central to
indigenous religious systems for thousands of years. His interdisciplinary
approach combined archaeology, ethnobotany, art history, and anthropology.

Work on mushroom stones from Guatemala provided material evidence for ancient
mushroom cults complementing Wasson Mazatec ethnography. Furst identified
representations of psychoactive plants in stone carvings, ceramics, and
codices, building a case that these plants played foundational roles in human
consciousness, religion, and culture development.

## Weston LaBarre: Skeptical Rigor and the Peyote Cult

LaBarre The Peyote Cult (1964) documented peyote use among Native American
peoples, tracing its spread from Mexico into Plains cultures. His dual
training in anthropology and psychiatry gave unique perspective, and he was
more reluctant to embrace mystical interpretations. His anthropological rigor
counterbalanced more enthusiastic approaches, and his documentation of Native
American Church practices contributed to legal protections for peyote use.

## Richard Evans Schultes: The Dean of Ethnobotany

Schultes, the towering figure in ethnobotany, spent decades in the Amazon
documenting hundreds of psychoactive plant species. He demonstrated that
indigenous peoples possessed sophisticated knowledge of preparation, dosage,
contraindications, and synergistic combinations, including the ayahuasca brew
combining DMT-plants with MAO-inhibiting Banisteriopsis caapi, rivaling
Western pharmacology. His influence extended through students who became
prominent researchers in ethnopharmacology and bioprospecting.

## Claudio Naranjo: Cross-Cultural Psychology

Naranjo, a Chilean psychiatrist, bridged clinical psychology and shamanic
practice. His work with ayahuasca and iboga combined psychological insight
with cross-cultural healing perspectives. He was among the first to explore
therapeutic applications of Amazonian plant medicines within Western
psychology, anticipating the current psychedelic therapy renaissance. His
approach integrated Gestalt psychology, the Enneagram, and Buddhist
meditation with entheogenic insights.

## The McKenna Brothers and Carlos Castaneda

Terence and Dennis McKenna represented the most speculative wing. The
Invisible Landscape (1975) proposed psychoactive plants played central roles
in consciousness evolution. Carlos Castaneda books about Yaqui shaman Don
Juan, while factually debated, introduced shamanic concepts (separate
reality, the ally, the path with heart) to millions.

## Collective Significance

This generation shared recognition that the Western materialist worldview was
incomplete. Indigenous shamanic traditions represented sophisticated knowledge
systems about consciousness, healing, and human-nature relationships. Their
collective work established foundations for the contemporary psychedelic
research renaissance and integration of indigenous wisdom with Western science.
