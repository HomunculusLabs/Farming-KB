# Composting and Vermiculture

Composting converts kitchen scraps, yard waste, and manure into rich soil amendment through [[decomposition|microbial decomposition]]. The [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-biology|compost biology]]
driving this process involves successions of [[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria|bacteria]], [[actinomycetes-soil-biology|actinomycetes]], and [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi|fungi]].
Vermiculture uses worms (specifically red wigglers) to process organic waste even faster, producing nutrient-dense castings. See [[vermicomposting]] for detailed worm-based methods and [[fungal-decomposition-resource-quality-determinants|fungal decomposition dynamics]]
that contribute to the later stages of compost breakdown. Together, these systems close the nutrient loop on a homestead, turning waste into fertility. For most households, food scraps constitute about 30% of garbage — material that can be returned to the soil instead of landfilled. This guide draws from [[nicole-faires|Nicole Faires]]' comprehensive homesteading reference.
The process aligns with [[natural-farming]] principles by returning organic matter to the soil
without synthetic inputs.

## Kitchen Compost

Keep two buckets under the kitchen sink. Add all food scraps except meat and dairy. Crush orange peels, banana peels, and eggshells for faster breakdown. Add small paper scraps. Once a bucket is full, dump it on the compost heap.

**Bin sizes and placement**:
- Commercial black plastic bins work well in urban settings — their dark color absorbs heat, accelerating decomposition
- For greenhouse thermal mass (see [[passive-solar-and-biomass-energy]]), at least 5 feet (1.5m) in diameter
- For outdoor wooden bins, at least 5 feet (1.5m) squared
- If using a composting toilet, a much larger bin is needed
- Shelter from rain unless you live in a very dry climate

**Materials to include**: Food scraps, composting toilet solids, leaves, garden waste, and manure. Collect material from elsewhere if possible — seaweed, organic material from commercial garden services (ensure untreated). Mulching items like leaves, straw, pinecones, bark, and sawdust in large quantities should be kept separate for surface mulching rather than composting.

## The Composting Process

1. Pile material together — it begins heating within 24 hours as microorganisms gather to feast
2. Temperature should rise over 120F (49C); organisms generate heat through metabolic activity
3. Leave the pile alone while hot, keep adding new material to the top
4. When the temperature drops (microorganisms are done), stir the pile to introduce oxygen
5. Insects will move in, or you can start raising worms in the cooled pile
6. The compost reaches 140F (60C) and depending on pile size, can maintain that temperature for six months to a year

**Common problems**:
- Pile won't heat up: usually too small (under 3 feet square), too wet, or too dry
- Moisture should be like a damp sponge
- Imbalance of materials is another common issue

## Carbon-to-Nitrogen Balance

The pile needs roughly equal parts by volume:

**Brown materials** (high carbon): Paper, leaves, dry grass, straw, sawdust, cardboard, nutshells, wood chips, pine needles, newspaper, hay. Woody materials should make up about half the bulk.

**Green materials** (high nitrogen): Manure, garden waste, coffee grounds, grass clippings, fruit and vegetable scraps, tea leaves, eggshells. These provide the nitrogen that fuels microbial growth.

This is not an exact science — experience will tell you what you need more of. If the pile is too wet, add brown materials; if too dry, add green materials. Chop all materials before adding for faster breakdown.

## Compost vs. Mulch vs. Animal Feed

Understanding what goes where prevents waste and optimizes each material's use:

**Compost** (decompose in pile): Banana peels, ashes, hair, citrus peels, coffee grounds, corn cobs (chopped), dust, lint, eggshells, manure, rhubarb stems, seafood shells (crushed), sod (upside down), tea leaves, soil, toadstools, vegetable soup

**Mulch** (spread on surface): Cardboard, fabric, pine needles, grass clippings, hay, leaves, newspaper, sawdust (untreated), weeds, wood chips, nutshells

**Animal feed** (feed to livestock): Dairy products, diseased plants, fish, meat, bones — these should not go in the compost pile but can be fed to pigs or poultry

## Vermiculture (Worm Composting)

Raising worms is no more difficult than composting and is especially valuable to urban dwellers — it can even be done in an apartment. Worms process kitchen scraps into castings that are among the best soil amendments available, rich in nutrients and beneficial microorganisms.

### Setup

- Covered bin with holes in the sides and bottom for drainage and aeration
- Line with mesh fabric to keep small worms from crawling out
- Set the bin on a drainage tray — water it like a houseplant

### Bedding

Fill the bin with black soil, shredded newspapers, or a mixture of both. The bedding provides the worms with a comfortable environment and initial carbon material.

### Worm Selection

You need red wigglers (Eisenia fetida), also called redworms or branding worms — NOT the earthworms already in your garden. Red wigglers are sold for fishing bait. Stock at approximately 0.5 pound (0.23 kg) of worms, or 500 regular-sized worms per cubic foot (0.03 cubic meters).

### Feeding

- Worms eat their weight in kitchen scraps per day
- Bury leftovers in the bedding after each meal
- Same rules as compost: no meat, dairy, garlic, or potato peelings
- They need a steady diet of crushed eggshells sprinkled on top (provides grit and calcium)
- Water regularly but don't saturate

### Temperature Management

They don't like cold temperatures. Keep outside in summer, but bring indoors when nights get chilly. They can be kept in a basement, garage, or even a kitchen closet.

### Harvesting Castings

Every couple of months, open the bin in bright light. Worms hate light and will dive to the bottom. After 10 minutes, scrape off the top layer — this is pure worm castings. When you see worms, wait another 10 minutes and scrape again. Repeat until all castings are collected, then refresh the bedding.

## Composting Toilets

A typical suburban toilet uses 3.5 gallons (13.25 liters) per flush. At three uses per day over a year, that equals 3,832 gallons (14,505 liters) of fresh water. [[composting-toilets]] eliminate this waste while producing valuable soil amendment. See also [[homesteading-water-systems]] for broader water conservation strategies.

### Commercial Waterless Systems (Recommended)

The best design separates liquids from solids and uses a powerful fan to remove odors. Solids go into a compostable bag in a large bucket; liquids are diverted to a [[homesteading-water-systems]] that can deposit directly into a garden. Solids are carried out every few weeks for outdoor decomposition (takes about 1 year). These are much less expensive than self-contained versions and smaller to install.

### Humanure Toilet (DIY)

Build your own system with a 5-gallon (19 liter) bucket fitted with a toilet seat. Build a wooden box with hinged lid, cut a hole for the seat, screw it to the top. Keep sawdust from untreated lumber next to the toilet — add a thin layer after each use to suppress odors.

**Management principles**:
- Empty the bucket frequently before it gets too full or waterlogged
- Dump into an outside pile on a concrete slab (essential to prevent leaching)
- Dig a small hole in the middle of the pile, dump, and cover back over
- Cover with more dry material like straw or leaves
- Do NOT turn the pile — turning cools it down
- The pile must get hot (above 98.6F/37C for extended period, ideally above 120F/49C for a day) to kill pathogens
- If the pile never gets hot enough, let it sit undisturbed for two years before use
- Clean bucket with biodegradable soap, dump wash water on compost pile

## Biomass Heat from Compost

Compost decomposition produces significant heat. A compost pile reaches 140F (60C) and can maintain that for months. This heat can be captured passively by placing the compost heap inside a greenhouse or running plastic piping (thin-walled PVC or garden hose) through the pile. Water heated this way can be sent to a hot water tank. The resulting biochar-like residues can also enrich [[biochar-and-fungi|fungal communities]] in amended soils. See [[passive-solar-and-biomass-energy]] for detailed designs.
