## Growing Sweet Corn

Sweet corn is a warm-season grass with unique pollination biology that makes it behave differently from most other [[companion-cultivation-of-mushrooms-with-garden-vegetables]]. Understanding its wind-pollination mechanism, its tillering tendency, and the critical spacing-yield relationship is essential for getting a reliable crop with well-filled ears.

## Tillering: The Hidden Advantage

Corn has a natural tendency to tiller — it will send up additional bearing stalks if there is enough [[duggar-mushroom-growing-room-design]]. Modern commercial breeders try to eliminate tillering (because tiller stalks produce smaller ears), but for the home gardener, tillering is an advantage:

- More soil access protects against drought
- Main ears get a bit bigger
- Additional ears on tillers can add significantly to total yield
- The [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] proverb: "One for the worm, one for the crow, one to rot, and one to grow" — with tillering, you may get 2-4 harvestable ears per planting station

## Spacing: The Critical Decision

Spacing is determined by soil moisture availability:

### Adequate Moisture

Each plant should exclusively control at least **24 square feet** (2,000 sq cm). Equivalent spacings:
- 18 inches (45 cm) on center in rows 36 inches (99 cm) apart
- 9 inches (23 cm) apart in rows 42 inches (105 cm) apart

### Moderate Moisture Stress

Increase spacing to 9 inches (23 cm) apart in rows 48 inches (120 cm) apart.

### Severe Drought (No Watering Possible)

Maximum space: about **16 square feet** (1.5 sq m) per plant, or 4-foot centers. This imitates traditional Native American gardening:
- Grow in hills spaced 4 feet on center
- Put 4 seeds in every hill
- Thin progressively to 1-2 plants per hill

### Key Insight

> Using a [[wide-row-spacing-intensive-gardening-comparison]] greater than 48 inches makes little sense for most varieties.

## Soil Preparation

- It's better to make the entire corn patch moderately fertile rather than concentrating amendments in rows or hills
- Extremely high fertility is not needed — corn is a medium-demand vegetable
- Corn will sprout in slightly cooler soil than beans, but unless you live in a short-season area, it's safer to wait until the soil has warmed before sowing

## Planting

1. Broadcast COF (or equivalent fertilizer) over the entire patch and dig it in
2. Make a hole about 1.5 inches (4 cm) deep with your finger
3. Drop 3 seeds per station
4. Cover and press down lightly
5. When seedlings are about 4 inches (10 cm) tall and securely established, thin to 1 plant
6. If one station has a poor plant, let a neighbor fill in from an adjoining spot

### Weed Management

- Keep the patch well (and shallowly) cultivated until the corn foliage has shaded the ground
- The dense shade suppresses newly germinating weeds

## Wind Pollination: The Patch Rule

Because ears are wind-pollinated and the pollen is heavy:

> When corn grows in a single long row, many ears will be only partly filled.

**Always grow corn in a patch at least 2 rows wide, with at least 6 plants per row.** This ensures adequate pollen coverage from multiple directions.

## Pests

### Corn Earworm

After the pollen drops, the silks become attractive to the corn earworm moth, who may lay eggs there. This is the time to wipe the silks with Bt if that pest is a problem in your area.

## Varieties

### Hybrid vs. Open-Pollinated

| Type | Yield | Seed Saving | Nutrition | Notes |
|------|-------|-------------|-----------|-------|
| Hybrid sweet | Higher | Cannot save | Standard | Most productive |
| OP classic | Lower | Can save | Standard | May need trialing to find productive ones |
| OP field | Lower bulk | Can save | Higher protein/minerals | Better nutrition per calorie |
| Hybrid field | Higher bulk | Cannot save | Lower protein/minerals | Less nutrition per calorie |

### The Sweet Corn Situation

Most classic OP sweet corn varieties have deteriorated to the point of becoming nonproductive. **Suggestion**: Sow your main patch with hybrid seed and also trial a few OP classics. If one proves worthy, switch the next year.

### Field Corn for Staple Food

If your intention is to grow field corn (not sweet corn) as a family staple:
- Use OP varieties, not hybrids
- The classic OP varieties are closely related to old Native American field corns
- They are acceptable for making cornmeal, flour, or parched corn
- Hybrid field corn appears more productive only because of [[cannabis-hybrid-vigor-and-phenotypes-clarke]] because it draws fewer nutrients per ear — but it makes lower-nutrition food
- **Advice**: Do your own trials the first year; test as many OP sorts as you can find

### Longkeeper

A relatively new tomato variety that ripens extremely slowly — after picking green, the fruit develops a tough skin and lasts a long time while ripening. Also relevant to note: there are also long-keeping corn varieties worth exploring.

## Harvest

### Ripeness Signals

Some varieties give clear signals:
- The wrapper (husk) changes color or dries
- The ear leans out from the stalk
- Others require peeking at kernels

### The Tiller Bonus

- The ear on the main stalk usually ripens a week ahead of those on tillers
- If you give plants more space than conventional recommendations, tillers will develop good ears
- You can test ripeness by nibbling a kernel from a forming tiller ear

## Saving Seed

### Isolation Requirements

- Corn is wind-pollinated with heavy pollen — won't blow more than 1 mile (1,600 m) except in a gale
- Isolation by 1/4 mile (400 m) is good enough for home-garden seed, unless the second patch is upwind in the direction of prevailing winds
- If upwind, use minimum 1/2 mile (800 m)

### Process

1. Allow seed-making plants to stand until fully dried in the field
2. If the season isn't long enough, harvest ears shortly after the first frost
3. Pull back the wrappers without tearing them off
4. Plait them, hanging in a warm dry place in braids of 25 or so (the Native American method)
5. Shell when fully dry

### Maintaining Variety Quality

- Select desirable plants each year to contribute to the gene pool
- A patch of at least 100 plants is ideal for maintaining vigor
- With only 50 desirable plants, acceptable OP seed can still be produced
