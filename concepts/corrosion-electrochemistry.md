## Overview

Corrosion electrochemistry studies the coupled oxidation and reduction reactions that degrade metals in contact with electrolytes. It explains why corrosion is often localized, why dissimilar metals accelerate attack, and why protective systems work.

Most common metallic corrosion is not simply chemical attack by oxygen. It is an electrochemical process involving anodic metal dissolution, cathodic reduction, ionic conduction through an environment, and electronic conduction through the metal.

A corrosion cell can be microscopic. Different grains, inclusions, weld regions, oxygen concentrations, stresses, or surface films can create separated anodic and cathodic sites on the same component.

Understanding the electrochemical basis is essential for bridges, pipelines, ships, aircraft, reinforced concrete, biomedical implants, heat exchangers, batteries, and chemical plants.

The subject connects materials science, thermodynamics, kinetics, transport, surface chemistry, and practical inspection engineering.

## Anodic and Cathodic Reactions

At the anode, metal atoms lose electrons and enter solution as ions. For iron, a simplified anodic reaction is Fe to Fe2+ plus two electrons.

Those electrons flow through the metal to cathodic sites, where a reduction reaction consumes them. In neutral aerated water, the common cathodic reaction is oxygen reduction.

In acidic environments, hydrogen evolution can be the dominant cathodic reaction. The available cathodic process strongly affects corrosion rate, hydrogen uptake, and failure mode.

The electrolyte closes the circuit by allowing ions to move. Without ionic conduction, charge separation would quickly stop the reactions.

Corrosion rate is therefore controlled by both anodic dissolution and cathodic reduction. Slowing either half reaction can slow the whole corrosion cell.

This mixed-potential view explains why inhibitors, coatings, deaeration, cathodic protection, and alloy selection can all work by changing electrochemical reaction rates.

## Thermodynamic Driving Force

Thermodynamics indicates whether a corrosion reaction is possible. Electrode potentials, chemical activities, pH, dissolved oxygen, and complexing species determine the direction of favorable reactions.

Pourbaix diagrams summarize stable phases as a function of potential and pH. They show regions where a metal is immune, actively corroding, or passivated by a protective film.

Thermodynamic favorability does not guarantee fast corrosion. A metal may be unstable in principle but protected by a slow reaction pathway or a barrier film.

Conversely, a small thermodynamic driving force can still produce serious damage if the kinetics are favorable and the anodic area is small relative to the cathodic area.

The distinction between possibility and rate is central. Corrosion engineering requires both equilibrium reasoning and kinetic measurement.

Environmental chemistry shifts the balance. Chloride ions, sulfides, ammonia, carbon dioxide, microbial metabolites, and high temperature can destabilize films or accelerate reactions.

## Polarization and Kinetics

Polarization describes how electrode potential changes as current flows. Activation polarization reflects charge-transfer barriers at the interface.

Concentration polarization occurs when reactants or products are transported too slowly. Oxygen diffusion through stagnant water films is a common limiting process.

Ohmic polarization comes from resistance in the electrolyte, coatings, deposits, or corrosion products. It can produce spatially varying potential on large structures.

Tafel behavior is often used to estimate kinetic parameters from potential-current data. Linear regions on a semi-log plot can reveal anodic and cathodic reaction slopes.

Linear polarization resistance can estimate corrosion rate near the open-circuit potential. It is widely used because it perturbs the system only slightly.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy probes corrosion systems over many frequencies. It can separate coating capacitance, charge-transfer resistance, diffusion, and film behavior.

## Galvanic Corrosion

Galvanic corrosion occurs when dissimilar metals are electrically connected in a common electrolyte. The more active member becomes anodic and corrodes faster, while the more noble member is protected.

The severity depends on potential difference, electrolyte conductivity, area ratio, polarization behavior, temperature, and geometry.

A small anodic area coupled to a large cathodic area is dangerous because the anodic current density can become high. Small steel fasteners in a large copper or stainless assembly can fail rapidly in seawater.

Galvanic series tables rank materials in specific environments such as seawater. They are more useful than standard electrode potentials for engineering because real alloys and films behave differently from pure metals.

Electrical isolation, compatible material selection, sealants, coatings, and sacrificial anodes can reduce galvanic attack.

Coating only the anodic metal can be risky if holidays expose small anodic defects next to a large protected cathode. Coating the cathodic member or both members is often safer.

## Passivation

Passivation is the formation of a thin, adherent, protective surface film that reduces corrosion rate. Stainless steel relies on chromium-rich oxide, aluminum on aluminum oxide, and titanium on a stable titanium oxide film.

A passive film is not an inert shell in a simple sense. It is a dynamic electrochemical layer that can form, dissolve, repair, or break down depending on potential and environment.

Passivity explains why some reactive metals are durable in air or water. Aluminum has a strong thermodynamic tendency to oxidize, yet its compact oxide film slows further attack.

Chloride ions are dangerous because they can penetrate or destabilize passive films and initiate pits. Stainless steels that perform well in clean water may pit in warm chloride solutions.

The passive region on a polarization curve has low current over a range of potentials. At higher potentials, transpassive dissolution or oxygen evolution can damage the film.

Passivation treatments for stainless steels remove free iron contamination and promote a chromium- rich surface. In manufacturing, surface cleanliness can be as important as alloy composition.

## Localized Corrosion

Pitting corrosion is a localized breakdown of passivity that creates small cavities. Pits can grow autocatalytically because metal ion hydrolysis acidifies the pit and attracts chloride ions.

Crevice corrosion occurs in shielded gaps under gaskets, deposits, lap joints, threads, or biofilms. Oxygen depletion inside the crevice creates differential aeration and aggressive local chemistry.

Intergranular corrosion attacks grain boundaries when heat treatment or welding creates sensitized regions. Stainless steel weld decay is a classic example involving chromium carbide precipitation.

Stress corrosion cracking combines tensile stress, susceptible material, and a specific environment. It can produce sudden brittle-looking failure at stresses below the yield strength.

Corrosion fatigue couples cyclic stress with electrochemical damage. Even mild environments can reduce fatigue life by attacking fresh crack surfaces.

Hydrogen-related damage can occur when cathodic reactions generate atomic hydrogen that enters the metal. High-strength steels are especially vulnerable to hydrogen embrittlement.

## Corrosion Protection

Barrier coatings separate the metal from the environment. Their performance depends on adhesion, thickness, permeability, defect tolerance, and maintenance.

Inhibitors reduce corrosion by adsorbing on surfaces, forming protective films, scavenging oxygen, buffering pH, or suppressing specific half reactions.

Cathodic protection shifts the protected structure to a more negative potential so anodic dissolution is suppressed. Sacrificial anodes corrode instead of the structure.

Impressed-current cathodic protection uses an external power supply and inert anodes. It is common on pipelines, tanks, marine structures, and reinforced concrete systems.

Anodic protection holds certain active-passive metals in the passive potential range. It is more specialized and requires careful control to avoid accelerating corrosion.

Good design avoids water traps, crevices, sharp transitions, incompatible metals, poor drainage, and inaccessible inspection regions. Geometry is part of electrochemical control.

## Measurement and Monitoring

Electrical resistance probes infer metal loss from resistance change. They can provide continuous monitoring in pipelines and process equipment.

Reference electrodes measure potential against a stable half-cell. Potential surveys are central to cathodic protection management.

Surface analysis, microscopy, profilometry, and chemical analysis of corrosion products help connect electrochemical data to actual damage morphology.

Inspection should match the failure mode. Uniform wall loss, pits, cracks, and under-deposit corrosion require different sampling strategies and nondestructive testing methods.

## Engineering Significance

Corrosion is an economic and safety problem as much as a chemical one. It increases maintenance cost, causes leaks, reduces structural capacity, contaminates products, and can trigger catastrophic failures.

Designers must consider life-cycle exposure, not just initial material strength. A strong alloy can be a poor choice if it is galvanically incompatible or prone to localized corrosion in the service environment.

The best corrosion decisions combine electrochemical testing, service history, material standards, environmental control, and inspection planning.

For engineering practice, the goal is rarely to eliminate corrosion absolutely. The realistic goal is to reduce rate, prevent dangerous localization, maintain inspectability, and keep risk within acceptable margins.
