## Interplant Carbon and Nutrient Transfer via Mycorrhizal Networks

## Overview

The concept that plants are interconnected underground by mycorrhizal fungal networks—often called "[[common-mycorrhizal-networks]]" (CMNs) or "mycorrhizal bridges"—has profound implications for understanding plant community dynamics, forest regeneration, and ecosystem stability. Research spanning decades has demonstrated that carbon, phosphorus, nitrogen, and other nutrients can move between plants connected by shared [[mycorrhizal-fungi]], though the ecological significance and mechanisms of these transfers remain actively debated.

## Historical Discovery

Bjorkman (1960) first discovered interplant carbon transfer by injecting radioactive carbon into a pine tree stem and detecting the isotope in an adjacent *Monotropa* plant—an achlorophyllous (non-photosynthetic) plant thought to be parasitic on tree roots. Noting that both the *Monotropa* and pine had ectomycorrhizal (EM) associations, Bjorkman proposed carbohydrate exchange between plants through mycorrhizal connections.

## Mechanisms of Transfer

### Source–Sink Dynamics

Transfer of nutrients and carbon between plants depends on source–sink strength gradients. Dying or senescing roots act as nutrient and carbon sources, while actively growing plants function as sinks. Newman and Eason (1989) demonstrated net phosphorus flux from dying *[[lolium-perenne]]* roots to living *Plantago lanceolata* roots through shared arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) connections, but no transfer to cabbage (*Brassica oleracea*), a non-mycorrhizal species.

### Mycorrhizal Type Specificity

Eason et al. (1991) showed greater phosphorus transfer between plants sharing the same mycorrhizal type (AM to AM) than between different types (AM to EM), though the magnitude was species-specific. Read et al. (1985) demonstrated preferential ¹⁴C transfer from pine to neighboring pine seedlings rather than to plants with AM associations.

## Field Evidence

### Birch to Douglas Fir Transfer

Simard et al. (1997a, b, c) provided landmark field evidence of carbon transfer from paper birch (*Betula papyrifera*) to Douglas fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*) under both partial and deep shade. Transferred carbon constituted a significant proportion of recipient shoot carbon (13% for Douglas fir, 45% for birch), potentially considerably supplementing photosynthetically derived carbon. Wu et al. (2002) showed that approximately 24% of ¹⁴C label in pine seedling underground parts was allocated to the extraradical hyphal component.

### Trenching Experiments

Simard et al.'s (1997a) trenching experiments demonstrated the importance of intact [[mycorrhizal-networks]] for seedling establishment. Douglas fir seedlings planted into untrenched birch-dominated forest had approximately double the [[mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity]] compared to those in trenched plots. This increased diversity significantly enhanced photosynthetic capacity of the seedlings, though no significant biomass increase was observed.

### Nurse Tree Effect

Amaranthus and Perry (1989) showed that Douglas fir planted into partially cleared sites (with remnant mycorrhizal hosts) had approximately 90% survival after 2 years, compared to only 50% in totally cleared areas. The intact mycorrhizal network provided seedlings access to larger nutrient and carbon pools than they could access independently.

## Grassland and Prairie Systems

### Tallgrass Prairie

Walter et al. (1996) demonstrated interplant phosphorus transfer in tallgrass prairie communities. Transfer was species-dependent and decreased with increasing distance between plants. Phosphorus moved more readily within forbs and cool-season C₃ grasses than in C₄ grasses, suggesting selectivity that could confer competitive advantages.

### Population-Level Specificity

Ronsheim and Anderson (2001) showed that in *Allium vineale*, the soil fungal community (dominated by mycorrhizal fungi) was most beneficial when neighboring plants were genetically identical or from the same population. This population-level specificity suggests plants from the same population share a more efficient hyphal network than isolated individuals, favoring intraspecific cooperation.

### Counter-Evidence

Ikram et al. (1994) found no nitrogen or phosphorus transfer between the AM legume *Pueraria phaseoloides* and rubber tree (*Hevea brasiliensis*), suggesting that growth improvements in mixed plantings may result from enhanced decomposition of legume residues rather than direct mycorrhizal linkages.

## Shared Mycorrhizal Types Across Plant Groups

Plants of different taxonomic groups may not have discrete mycorrhizal symbionts. Evidence shows:

- EM displacement of AM on *Alnus*, *Helianthemum*, and *Eucalyptus* with increasing tree age (Read et al., 1977; Chilvers et al., 1987; Lodge and Wentworth, 1990)
- EM displacement of AM on *Populus* and *Salix* with increasing soil moisture (Lodge and Wentworth, 1990)
- *Rhododendron* species supporting both ericoid and EM symbioses (Largent et al., 1980; Dighton and Coleman, 1992; J.E. Smith et al., 1995)
- *Pseudotsuga menziesii* developing AM associations with *Glomus intraradices* when grown with *Calamagrostis rubescens* (J.E. Smith et al., 1998)

If shared mycorrhizal types are widespread in forested systems, the potential for interconnection among plants of different species and functional groups increases dramatically. Ericaceous understory plants, which grow in shade and may have periods of insufficient photosynthesis, could potentially receive carbon translocated from canopy trees through shared mycorrhizal connections—particularly relevant after ground fires when resprouting depends on belowground carbon reserves.

## The Cheater Hypothesis

F.A. Smith and S.E. Smith (1996) characterized the carbon drain from host to fungus as potentially parasitic when: (1) carbon drain exceeds the value of the fungal association, (2) the fungus becomes a "cheater" under stress or high P availability, (3) hyphal growth escapes root control, and (4) the fungus shows pathogenic tendencies. The interplant transfer facilitated by mycorrhizal bridges has similarly been viewed as cheating, but can alternatively be interpreted as cooperative links improving overall species fitness.

## Ecological Implications

The existence of interplant carbon and nutrient transfer challenges the traditional view of plant competition as the primary driver of community composition:

1. **Ecosystem homeostasis**: Mycorrhizal networks may help maintain community structure during stress or disturbance
2. **Forest regeneration**: Mature trees can serve as nurse plants for seedlings in suboptimal light conditions, providing a mechanism for maintaining forest cover after canopy tree mortality
3. **Nutrient retention**: Interconnectedness optimizes nutrient retention within the plant community and maximizes capture of nutrients mineralized from organic residues
4. **Invasion resistance**: Species-specific transfer patterns could represent an evolutionary strategy defending native communities against alien species that cannot readily form inter-specific mycorrhizal linkages

The spatiotemporal distribution of source–sink strengths for carbon and nutrients in complex ecosystems remains poorly understood. At one point in space and time, the mycorrhizal symbiosis may be highly beneficial; at another, the same interaction may have a different or even parasitic function.
