# Mollison PDC Trellis Systems for Climate Control

## Overview

In Pamphlet IX of the [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] Course, [[bill-mollison]] presents
trellising as far more than a simple support structure for climbing
plants. He describes trellis systems as powerful tools for modifying
microclimate, controlling wind, managing solar exposure, and creating
vertical [[cotter-growing-space-design-fruiting-rooms]] within zone one and zone two gardens. Trellises
are architectural elements that shape the environment around plants,
turning a simple garden structure into a climate management device.

## Trellis as a Wind Management Tool

One of Mollison's key insights is that trellises can serve as windbreaks
without sacrificing productive growing space. A well-designed trellis
planted with vigorous climbers creates a solid vegetative barrier that
slows wind passing through the garden. This wind reduction decreases
evapotranspiration, protects delicate crops from physical damage, and
creates warm, still air pockets where heat-loving plants thrive.

Mollison recommends placing trellises on the windward side of gardens
in areas with prevailing winds. The trellis plants themselves benefit
from the wind exposure, which strengthens their stems, while the crops
behind the trellis enjoy the sheltered conditions. This dual function,
protection and production, is characteristic of [[comparison-sepp-holzer-vs-permaculture-design]]
thinking where every element serves multiple purposes.

## Solar Control Through Trellis Orientation

The orientation of a trellis determines how it interacts with sunlight.
Mollison describes how a trellis running east to west will cast shade on
its north side (in the northern hemisphere), creating a cool microclimate
suitable for shade-loving crops like lettuce, spinach, and herbs. The
south face of the same trellis receives full sun and supports heat-loving
climbers like tomatoes, cucumbers, and melons.

Conversely, a trellis running north to south provides more even light
distribution on both sides, as the sun tracks across the sky and
illuminates each face at different times of day. This orientation is
useful when both sides of the trellis need similar growing conditions.

Mollison suggests that in hot climates, trellises can be used to create
shade structures for the garden, reducing soil temperatures and
protecting sensitive plants from afternoon heat. In [[greenhouse-cool-climates-mollison]], the
[[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]] of a solid trellis wall can absorb heat during the day and
release it at night, moderating temperature fluctuations.

## The Three-Dimensional Garden Concept

Trellising transforms a flat garden into a three-dimensional growing
system. Mollison argues that most gardeners drastically underutilize
vertical space. By training plants upward on trellises, the productive
area of a garden can double or triple without increasing its footprint.
The ground beneath a trellis remains available for growing shade-tolerant
crops, effectively creating two distinct [[mushroom-growing-environments]] in the
space of one.

The three-dimensional approach also applies to the design of the trellis
itself. Mollison describes multiple tiers: tall trellises for climbers
like pole beans and indeterminate tomatoes, medium trellises for
peppers and eggplants that benefit from some support, and low trellises
or cages for bush-type crops. This tiered structure creates a
stratified garden that captures maximum light at every level.

## Trellis Materials and Design Considerations

Mollison is pragmatic about trellis materials. He recommends using
whatever is locally available and durable: wooden posts and wire, bamboo
structures, living trellises of tall plants, or even recycled materials.
The key design consideration is strength. A trellis loaded with mature
fruiting plants and exposed to wind must be robust enough to survive the
[[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] without collapsing.
