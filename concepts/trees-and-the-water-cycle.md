# Trees and the Water Cycle

Trees are fundamental regulators of the savory holistic resource, performing roles that
extend far beyond their visible presence. They create precipitation through
condensation, moderate temperature extremes, store water in soil, purify
airborne dust, and maintain the hydrological cycles upon which all life
depends. Understanding these functions is critical for [[permaculture-design]],
urban forestry, and [[watershed-management]].

## Trees as Water Condensers

On sea-facing hillsides, trees act as condensers, ice nucleii, inducers of
stream flow, and re-humidifiers of airstreams. The process works as follows:

Water-laden air flows inland. Where this humid air flows over the rapidly
cooling surfaces of glass, metal, rocks, or the thin laminae of leaves,
condensation occurs and droplets of water form. On leaves, this is greatly
aided by colonies of bacteria (Pseudomonas) which also serve as nucleii for
frost crystals.

Condensation drip from trees can be as high as 80-86% of total precipitation
on upland slopes of islands or sea coasts. This condensation produced the
redwood forests of California, the giant laurel forests of the Canary Islands
(now arid due to deforestation), and the rainforests of Tasmania, Chile,
Hawaii, Washington/Oregon, and Scandinavia.

A single tree such as a giant tu (Ocotea forens) may present 16 hectares of
laminated leaf surface to the sea air, and there can be 100 or so such trees
per surface hectare. The taller the trees, the larger the volume of moist air
intercepted, and the greater the precipitation that follows.

## Compression and Turbulence Effects

Wind streams flowing across a forest are partly deflected over (about 60%)
and partly absorbed into the trees (about 40%). Within 1000 meters, the air
within the forest is brought to a standstill, resulting in:

- An almost imperceptible warming of the air
- Generally increased humidity averaging 15-18% higher than ambient air
- Air in which no dust is detectable (negative ions from life processes cause
  dust particles to clump and fall out)

Winds deflected over the forest cause compression in the streamlines,
extending to twenty times the tree height. A 12-meter tree line compresses
air to 244 meters above, creating more water vapour per unit volume and
cooling the ascending air stream -- both conditions conducive to rain.

A single hedgerow of 40% permeability causes similar compression. In flat
country, 40% of rainfall measured downwind of trees and mounds 12 meters or
more in height is caused by compression phenomena alone.

### Ekman Spirals

Wind streaming over a hedgerow or forest edge describes a spiral section,
repeated 58 times downwind, creating a series of compression fronts parallel
to the windbreak. These may result in a ranked series of clouds, often very
regular. The Ekman spirals over trees or bluffs may add 40% or more to
rainfall in bands roughly parallel to tree lines.

## Temperature Effects

Evaporation causes heat loss locally; condensation causes heat gain locally.
Both effects may be used to heat or cool air or surfaces.

An ordinary elm of medium size will release 15,000 pounds of water on a clear
dry hot day. Evapotranspiration is generally not less than 15 inches per year
in a 40-inch rainfall area.

Plants are generally about 15 degrees C warmer than the surrounding air
temperature. Leaves are 86% water, giving them twice the specific heat of soil
-- they remain cooler than soil by day and warmer at night.

### Cooling Strategies

Reddish-coloured leaves (some vines and shrubs) reflect red light rays and can
reduce temperatures by up to 20 degrees C compared to green-pigmented plants.
Whitish plants like wormwood and birch may reflect 85% of incoming light.
White or red-coloured roof vines over tiles can effectively lower summer
temperatures within buildings.

Additional cooling comes from fitting fine water sprays and damp permaculture designers manual under trellises, creating cool areas of dense air by evaporation.

## Rehumidification of Airstreams

Forests are cloud-makers both from water vapour evaporated from leaves by day
and water transpired as part of life. A large evergreen tree such as Eucalyptus
globulus may pump out 3,600-4,500 liters of water a day. With sixty or so of
these trees per hectare, many tens of thousands of liters are returned to the
air to become clouds.

Key findings on forest water recycling:
- Forests return 75% of their water to air, "in large enough amounts to form
  new rain clouds"
- Forested areas return ten times as much moisture as bare ground and twice as
  much as grasslands
- Release of water from trees and plants accounts for half or more of all
  moisture returned to air
- Of 75% returned, 25% is evaporated from leaf surfaces and 50% is transpired
- The remaining 25% infiltrates soil and eventually reaches streams

Forests produce 50% of their own rain through this recycling process. Drought
in one area may relate directly to deforestation in an upwind direction.

## How Trees Interact with Rain

When rain falls on a forest, a complex process begins:

1. The tree canopy shelters and nullifies the impact effect of raindrops,
   reducing rain to thin mist below the canopy
2. A film of water spreads across leaves and stems, trapped by surface tension
3. Cells absorb what is needed; the remainder evaporates
4. "Total interception" occurs when no rain penetrates the canopy

Factors influencing interception include crown thickness, crown density, season,
intensity of rain, and evaporation after rain.

## Soil Water Storage

The soil beneath forests functions as an immense and water storage system with
several storage types:

- Retention storage: water bound to soil particles by surface tension
- Interstitial storage: water in cavities between soil particles
- Humus storage: water in swollen [[mycorrhiza]]l and spongy detritus

Fine-textured, high-organic-content soils may store 10-30 cm of rain per 30 cm
of depth. Free interstitial water can take 1-40 years to percolate through to
streams, greatly alleviating droughts.

## Effects on Snow and Meltwater

Trees entrap snow at edges of clumps and hold 75-95% of snowfall in shade.
Melting is delayed for 2-10 days compared with bare ground, producing a more
gradual release of meltwater to river sources.

On open ground, snow may sublimate directly to water vapour in bright winter
sun, producing no groundwater or streamflow. Even a thin belt of trees entraps
large quantities of driven snow in drifts.
