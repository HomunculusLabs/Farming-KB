# Ancient Cannabis Use in the Near East and Egypt

The history of cannabis use in the Near East and Egypt extends back nearly four millennia, making it one of the oldest documented psychoactive plant traditions in human civilization. Archaeological, textual, and ethnobotanical evidence reveals a complex pattern of cannabis utilization spanning medical, religious, recreational, and industrial applications across multiple ancient civilizations.

## Egyptian Cannabis Use

Cannabis specimens have been recovered from Egyptian archaeological sites dating back nearly 4,000 years, placing Egyptian cannabis use among the earliest well-documented traditions anywhere in the world. The preservation of plant material in the dry Egyptian climate has allowed researchers to confirm the physical presence of cannabis at multiple locations and time periods.

In ancient Thebes, cannabis was prepared into a drink with opium-like effects. This method of oral administration — creating a cannabis-infused beverage — represents one of the earliest documented approaches to cannabis consumption for psychoactive purposes. The drink was likely prepared by steeping or boiling cannabis flowers and leaves in water, possibly with other ingredients to modify the flavor and effects.

The medical applications of cannabis in ancient Egypt appear to have been extensive, though the full extent remains debated among Egyptologists. Some scholars have identified references to cannabis in Egyptian medical papyri, suggesting its use for treating various conditions including inflammation, glaucoma, and menstrual disorders. However, the translation and identification of specific plant names in ancient Egyptian texts remains a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion.

## Ancient Near Eastern Cannabis Use

Hemp fabrics from the late 8th century B.C. have been found in Turkey, providing evidence of cannabis cultivation for fiber in Anatolia. The dual use of cannabis for both fiber and psychoactive purposes is well-documented across many ancient cultures, and the Anatolian finds suggest that this versatile exploitation of the plant was established in the Near East by the early Iron Age.

The [[scythian-cannabis-steam-bath-ritual]] grew cannabis along the Volga River approximately 3,000 years ago, placing their cannabis tradition in the broader context of Near Eastern and Central Asian psychoactive plant use. The proximity of Scythian territory to the civilizations of Mesopotamia, Anatolia, and the Levant raises the possibility of cultural exchange regarding cannabis knowledge and practices.

## Indian Cannabis Tradition

Indian medical writing, compiled before 1000 B.C., reports therapeutic uses of [[cannabis-health-methods-of-administration]], making the Indian tradition one of the oldest documented in the world. That the early Hindus appreciated its intoxicating properties is attested by such evocative names as "heavenly guide" (vijaya) and "soother of grief." These names reveal that cannabis was valued not merely as a medicine but as a psychoactive sacrament capable of altering consciousness in desirable ways.

The Indian tradition developed a sophisticated taxonomy of cannabis preparations. Bhang consists of plants gathered green, dried, and made into a drink with water or milk or into a candy called majun with sugar and spices. Charas, normally smoked or eaten with spices, is pure resin collected from the living plant. Ganjah, usually smoked with tobacco, consists of resin-rich dried tops from the female plant. Many of these unusually potent preparations may be derived from Cannabis indica.

## Chinese Cannabis Use

Chinese tradition places the use of cannabis back 4,800 years, making it potentially the oldest documented cannabis tradition in the world. The Chinese referred to cannabis as "liberator of sin" and "delight giver," names that clearly reference its psychoactive properties. The ancient Chinese character for cannabis (Ta Ma) combines the symbols for "tall" and "fiber plant," reflecting the dual importance of the species.

In Chinese medicine, cannabis seeds (huo ma ren) were used as a laxative and treatment for various conditions. The psychoactive use of cannabis appears to have declined in China over the centuries, possibly supplanted by tea and other psychoactive substances, but the medical use of cannabis seeds continued in the Chinese pharmacopoeia well into the modern era.

## Greek and Roman Knowledge of Cannabis

The Greek physician Galen wrote, about A.D. 160, that general use of hemp in cakes produced narcotic effects. His detailed pharmacological observations represent some of the earliest scientific documentation of cannabis effects in the Western tradition. Galen noted that the consumption of cannabis seeds in sweetened cakes produced a warming sensation and altered mental state, but could cause headaches and dehydration if consumed to excess.

Earlier Greek awareness of cannabis may be reflected in the writings of Herodotus, who documented the [[scythian-cannabis-steam-bath-ritual]] around 500 B.C. The Greek naturalist's detailed description of Scythian cannabis vapor baths suggests that Greek readers of the time were familiar with the concept of cannabis intoxication, even if the practice was not widespread in Greece itself.

Roman knowledge of cannabis appears to have been primarily medical and industrial. Roman texts mention cannabis (cannabis sativa) as a source of fiber for rope and as a medicinal plant, but there is less evidence of widespread psychoactive use in Roman culture compared to the more enthusiastic traditions of India and the Near East.

## Methods of Ancient Cannabis Consumption

The ancient world developed multiple methods for consuming cannabis, each with distinct pharmacological characteristics. Oral consumption in drinks or food preparations was the most widespread method, documented in Egypt, India, China, and the Greco-Roman world. The [[scythian-cannabis-steam-bath-ritual]] represents an early form of inhalation that anticipates modern smoking and vaporization practices.

The preparation of cannabis resin (charas) for smoking or eating appears to have been particularly developed in India, where the technology for collecting and processing resin reached a high level of sophistication. The various Indian preparations — bhang, charas, and ganjah — demonstrate a detailed understanding of how different processing methods and plant parts produce different psychoactive profiles.

## The Spread of Cannabis from Central Asia

Cannabis is believed to have originated in Central Asia, from where it spread to virtually every inhabited continent. The ancient Near East served as a crucial crossroads in this diffusion, with trade routes connecting China, India, Central Asia, and the Mediterranean world all facilitating the movement of both cannabis products and knowledge about their preparation and use.

The global spread of cannabis represents perhaps the most successful diaspora of any psychoactive plant in human history. From its ancient origins in Central Asian medicine and Scythian ritual to its current worldwide presence, cannabis has maintained cultural significance across millennia and across radically different societies. The ancient Near Eastern tradition represents a critical link in this chain of transmission, connecting the Central Asian heartland of cannabis to the Mediterranean world and beyond.

## Modern Research on Ancient Cannabis

Modern archaeobotanical techniques, including pollen analysis, carbon dating, and chemical residue analysis, continue to refine our understanding of ancient cannabis use. These methods have confirmed the presence of cannabis at archaeological sites throughout the Near East and Egypt, and have provided insights into which parts of the plant were utilized and how they were processed.

The [[old-world-hallucinogenic-plants-overview]] has been greatly enriched by these archaeological advances, which complement the textual and ethnographic evidence that has traditionally formed the basis of our knowledge. Together, these diverse sources of evidence paint an increasingly detailed picture of cannabis's role in the ancient civilizations of the Near East and Mediterranean.

## The Hashishin Connection

In 13th century Asia Minor, organized murderers rewarded with hasheesh were known as hashishins, from which may come the term "assassin" in European languages. While the historical accuracy of this etymology has been debated by scholars, the story reflects the widespread awareness of cannabis's psychoactive properties in the medieval Islamic world. The Nizari Ismaili sect, against whom the "hashishin" label was directed, may or may not have actually used cannabis, but the association stuck in the popular imagination.

This episode illustrates how cannabis became woven into the political and military history of the Near East. Whether or not the hashishin legend is literally true, it demonstrates that cannabis was recognized as a substance capable of altering behavior and consciousness in ways that could be exploited for political purposes. The legend also contributed to the demonization of cannabis that accompanied its spread to Western societies.

## Gender and Social Roles in Ancient Cannabis Use

Ancient texts and archaeological evidence suggest that cannabis use in the ancient Near East was not restricted to any single social class or gender, though different groups may have used it in different ways. In Indian tradition, cannabis was associated with the god Shiva and was particularly used by ascetics (sadhus) and during the festival of Holi. In Scythian culture, cannabis use appears to have been primarily male and associated with warrior and funerary contexts.

In Egyptian contexts, the evidence for gendered patterns of cannabis use is less clear, but the medical applications described in papyri suggest use by both men and women for therapeutic purposes. The preparation of cannabis-infused drinks in Thebes may have been associated with priestly or medical practitioners who served both sexes.

## Trade and Economic Significance

Cannabis was a significant trade commodity in the ancient world, valued both for its fiber and for its psychoactive resin. The plant's dual utility — as a source of hemp for rope, cloth, and paper, and as a psychoactive substance — made it an economically important crop across multiple ancient civilizations.

The trade in cannabis products connected distant regions through networks of exchange that spanned the ancient Near East. Cannabis resin from Central Asia could reach markets in Egypt, the Levant, and the Mediterranean world through overland trade routes. This commercial dimension of cannabis history is often overshadowed by the focus on its psychoactive properties, but it was essential to the plant's spread and cultural significance.
