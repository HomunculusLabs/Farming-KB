# Medically Important Fungi: Isolation and Ecology

An important component of [[fungal-biodiversity]] in any ecosystem occurs
in habitats defined or conditioned by vertebrates, including the
animals themselves and their dwelling places. More than 300 species of
medically important fungi are recognized (de Hoog et al. 2000),
representing a diverse array of ecological roles from saprobes to
specialized pathogens. Understanding their environmental distributions
is directly relevant to conservation biology, as disease agents
participate in regulating animal population densities and influence
predator-prey dynamics. Many medically relevant fungi are primarily
saprobic, with distributions elucidated mainly through biomedical
isolations.

## Sources and Categories of Mycotic Diseases

Most mycotic diseases are acquired from non-animal environmental
sources rather than through contagion. The majority of mycotic disease
agents have saprobic ecological roles through which they exert their
main ecological impact. [[aspergillus-fumigatus]], for example, although
capable of causing disease in heavily exposed or immunocompromised
hosts, is primarily a compost fungus associated with leaf litter and
accumulated bird or bat dung. Categories of interaction between fungi
and vertebrates include contamination (pathologically insignificant
organisms of external origin), commensalism (persistent colonization
of body surfaces), and pathogenicity (infection causing disease).

Commensal fungi primarily colonize keratinized or mucous-covered
surfaces using host secretions. Yeasts in Candida, Malassezia, and
Trichosporon are the best-known commensals. Malassezia species are
obligate commensals occurring only on mammalian skin or ear canals; six
of seven described species require special lipid supplements on
laboratory media. [[candida-albicans]], C. tropicalis, C. glabrata, C.
parapsilosis, and C. krusei are strongly associated with mammalian and
avian gastrointestinal tracts. Truly commensal, purely filamentous
fungi have not been encountered, as hyphal growth involves substratum
penetration that activates vertebrate immune responses.

## Systemic Pathogens and the Onygenales

The majority of specialized filamentous fungal pathogens of
homeothermic vertebrates belong to the Ascomycete order Onygenales.
This group includes dermatophytes (teleomorph Arthroderma; anamorphs
in Trichophyton, Microsporum, Epidermophyton) and systemic pathogens
(teleomorph Ajellomyces; anamorphs in Blastomyces, Histoplasma,
Paracoccidioides). Coccidioides immitis is also Onygenalean. A few
specialized systemic pathogens belong to other groups: Penicillium
marneffei (Eurotiales), Filobasidiella neoformans (Filobasidiales),
and Pneumocystis carinii (relative of Endomycetales).

Dermatophytes divide ecologically into anthropophilic (human-specific),
zoophilic (animal-specific), and geophilic (soil saprobes on keratinous
debris) groups. Systemic Onygenales are endemic to particular
geographic areas. [[blastomyces-dermatitidis]] occurs in boreal eastern
Canada, the upper Midwest, and rarely in southern Africa. Histoplasma
capsulatum var. capsulatum inhabits the Mississippi River valley
system, associated with soils enriched by bird or bat guano, and also
occurs in Central and South America and Africa. Paracoccidioides
brasiliensis is associated with moist forested soils from southern
Mexico to northern Argentina. Coccidioides immitis occurs primarily in
desert soils of the American southwest and northern Mexico.

## Subcutaneous and Opportunistic Pathogens

Subcutaneous pathogens cause disease in immunologically normal hosts
when traumatically introduced into dermal layers. Prominent genera
include Fonsecaea, Cladophialophora, Exophiala, Phialophora, and
certain Hypocrealean anamorphs such as Acremonium falciforme and
[[fusarium-verticillioides]]. Cladophialophora bantiana may cause fatal
brain cavitation following inhalation. These fungi are distinguished
from opportunistic pathogens by their ability to infect healthy hosts.

Opportunistic pathogens are ordinarily harmless but invade tissues or
extensively colonize body surfaces when hosts become immunocompromised
or exposed to heavy inoculum loads. A much greater number of species
are opportunistic than are specialized pathogens. Saprobe distributions
often are elucidated with biomedical isolations, including
Scedosporium prolificans, Cladophialophora bantiana, Acremonium
falciforme, and species in Blastomyces, Histoplasma, Coccidioides,
Fonsecaea, and Onychocola.

## Environmental Isolation Techniques

Environmental isolation of medically important fungi targets three
categories: systemic pathogens, dermatophytes, and opportunistic
pathogens. For systemic pathogens, soil samples from endemic areas are
processed using selective media. Bird and bat guano deposits are
productive sources for Histoplasma. Dermatophytes are recovered using
hair-baiting techniques, where sterile hair fragments are placed on
soil and examined periodically for keratinophilic growth. Opportunistic
pathogens may be recovered from environmental samples using general
fungal isolation media supplemented with antibacterial antibiotics.

Skin prick serological testing provides a rough measure of environmental
prevalence for Histoplasma capsulatum and Coccidioides immitis. Sterile
culture filtrates (histoplasmin, spherulin) are injected
subcutaneously; induration exceeding 5 mm at 48 hours indicates prior
exposure. Epidemiologically designed population surveys accurately
delineate endemic geographic regions. Compiling diagnostic immunology
results from medical laboratories, especially with patient travel
histories, provides less accurate but still useful distribution data.
More sensitive in vitro immunological tests can detect exposure or
active disease but are beyond the scope of standard biodiversity
surveys.

## Impediments and Evaluation Criteria

Medical and veterinary records contain a mix of medically significant
and insignificant fungal isolates. Some evaluation criteria for
determining significance include consistency of association with
disease, host immune status, and inoculum route. Herbarium specimens
traditionally are prepared by drying cultures after fumigating plates
with formaldehyde vapor and demonstrating nonviability of attempted
subcultures. The University of Alberta Microfungus Collection,
Centraalbureau voor Schimmelcultures, and American Type Culture
Collection maintain outstanding holdings. A well-rounded biodiversity
survey should include data from local medical and veterinary
authorities on pathogen environmental prevalence.
