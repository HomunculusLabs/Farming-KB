Cleve Backster was America's foremost lie-detector examiner whose chance
experiment in 1966 opened a scientific Pandora's box. Working late in his
polygraph school in Times Square, Backster attached electrodes from one of
his lie detectors to the leaf of a Dracaena massangeana, a tropical plant
similar to a palm tree known as the dragon tree. His initial curiosity was
simple: would watering the plant's roots produce any measurable electrical
response?

## The Initial Discovery

As the plant absorbed water, the galvanometer produced an unexpected
pattern. Rather than showing decreased electrical resistance (as greater
moisture conductivity would suggest), the pen traced a distinctive
downward sawtooth pattern very similar to the emotional response of a
human being. The galvanometer, a component of the polygraph lie detector
invented in the eighteenth century and named after Luigi Galvani, measures
electrical potential as it fluctuates under the stimulus of thought and
emotion through a Wheatstone bridge circuit.

## The Threat Experiment

The most dramatic moment came when Backster decided to threaten the
plant. Dunking a leaf in his coffee produced no reaction, but the moment
he formed the mental image of burning the leaf to which electrodes were
attached, the recording pen registered a prolonged upward sweep before he
could move for a match. When he left the room and returned with matches,
another surge registered, apparently caused by his determination to carry
out the threat. After actually burning the leaf (producing a lower peak)
and then merely pretending, there was no further reaction. The plant
appeared to differentiate between real and pretended intent.

## Testing and Validation

Backster expanded his investigation meticulously. More than twenty-five
varieties of plants and fruits were tested, including lettuce, onions,
oranges, and bananas. The phenomenon persisted even with detached leaves,
and remarkably, even when a leaf was shredded and redistributed between
electrode surfaces. Plants reacted to threats from humans, sudden
appearances of animals, and even to the approach of individuals who did
not wish them well.

At Yale, Backster demonstrated that a plant's recorded pattern changed
dramatically just before a spider in the same room decided to escape,
suggesting the plant was picking up the spider's decision-making process.

## The Fainting Response

When a Canadian physiologist who routinely destroyed plants visited
Backster's lab, the first five plants produced no response whatsoever.
Only the sixth showed enough reaction. Forty-five minutes after the
visitor left for the airport, all plants responded normally again. This
led Backster to conclude that plants could be intentionally put into a
deep faint, similar to an opossum playing dead, by the proximity of
someone who harms plants.

## Memory and Recognition

In a carefully designed experiment, six volunteers drew slips of paper
one instructing the bearer to destroy one of two plants. By attaching
the surviving plant to a polygraph and parading the students before it,
Backster identified the culprit: the plant showed no reaction to five
students but caused the meter to spike whenever the actual destroyer
approached. This suggested plants could remember and recognize the
source of harm to a fellow plant.

## Distance Communication

Backster found that a special bond between plant and keeper functioned
regardless of distance. Using synchronized stopwatches, plants
continued reacting from adjacent rooms, down the hall, even from
buildings miles away. On a lecture tour, when Backster projected a
slide of his original 1966 dracaena, the plant back in his New York
office registered a reaction at that precise moment. During a New
Year's Eve foray into Times Square, three independently monitored
plants showed similar reactions to Backster's minor emotional
adventures among the crowd.

## The Concept of Primary Perception

Backster rejected the term ESP, reasoning that since plants lack eyes,
ears, or a nervous system, their perceiving faculty must be more
fundamental than the five human senses. He hypothesized that the five
senses might be limiting factors overlying a "primary perception"
common to all nature. Plants might "see better without eyes" than
humans do with them. Under normal circumstances, plants seem attuned
to animal life rather than to other plants, since mobile beings require
careful monitoring.

## Scientific Implications

The Backster Effect challenged the fundamental assumption that plants
are unfeeling automatons. While controversial, the experiments
suggested that some form of communication exists between living
organisms that operates beyond currently understood physical laws.
The phenomenon seemed to work on a multi-input basis operating beyond
conventional sensory mechanisms, implying a "paranormal matrix" that
binds all life together in a unique communications grid. Backster
wisely avoided publicity and concentrated on establishing the
scientific bona fides of his discovery, never claiming a new finding
but rather an uncovering of what had been known and forgotten.
