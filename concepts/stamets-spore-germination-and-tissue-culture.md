Source: Paul Stamets, *The Mushroom Cultivator* (1983)

Two methods exist for starting a mushroom culture: from spores (creating new strains) or from living tissue (cloning an existing mushroom). Each has distinct advantages and limitations.

## Spore Print Collection

1. Sever the cap from the stem of a fresh, well-cleaned mushroom
2. Place cap gills-down on clean white paper or a glass microscope slide
3. If partially dried, add a drop or two of water to the cap surface to aid spore release
4. Place a cup or glass over the cap to reduce evaporation and air disturbance
5. After a few hours, spores will have fallen according to the radiating symmetry of the gills
6. Cut the print, fold in half, seal in an airtight container
7. Label with date, species, and collection number

### Using the Partial Veil
Species with a partial veil (Agaricus brunnescens, Psilocybe cubensis, many others) offer an advantage. The veil seals the gills from the outside, creating a semi-sterile chamber. By choosing a healthy, young specimen with the veil intact and carefully removing the veil tissue under aseptic conditions, a nearly pure spore print is obtained.

## Spore Germination Techniques

### Direct Streak Method
1. Sterilize an inoculating loop or scalpel in an alcohol lamp flame until red-hot (5-10 seconds)
2. Cool the tip by inserting it into sterile media in a petri dish
3. Scrape spores off the print
4. Streak the tip across the agar surface
5. Inoculate at least three media dishes to improve success chances

### Free-Fall Method
Scrape the spore print above an opened petri dish, allowing spores to free-fall onto the medium.

### Hydration Method (for Dehydrated Spores)
1. Soak spores in sterilized water for 6-12 hours
2. Sterilize an eye dropper, syringe, or pipette along with water in a test tube at 15 psi for 30 minutes
3. Touch spores onto a scalpel and insert into sterile water
4. Draw up spore solution and inoculate plates with one or two drops

## Mycelial Growth Characteristics

- Germination should be evident in 3-7 days
- Germinating spores appear as thread-like strands from a central point, initially grayish, becoming whitish
- Most species (Agaricus, Coprinus, Lentinus, Panaeolus, Psilocybe) have grayish to whitish mycelia
- Lepista nuda can have remarkable purplish blue mycelium
- Growth type varies: aerial (cottony) or appressed, rhizomorphic (strandy)
- Aerial growth can indicate high humidity or be species-related
- Appressed growth can indicate dry conditions or be species-specific

## Multispore Culture Limitations

Multispore germination creates many strains, some incompatible with others. This mixture can limit total yields, with less productive strains inhibiting more productive ones. Key considerations:
- Domesticated strains: progeny likely resemble parents
- Wild strains: may fruit very poorly in artificial environments and must be selectively developed
- Monokaryons (from single spores) generally cannot produce fertile mushrooms
- Dikaryons (from compatible mating) can produce fertile offspring
- Anastomosis (branching between different dikaryotic strains) is analogous to hybrid creation in horticulture

## Bacteria and Spore Germination

Some bacteria stimulate spore germination in difficult-to-grow species. Pseudomonas putida, Bacillus megaterium, and Azotobacter vinelandii have all shown stimulatory effects. Activated charcoal is recommended for any mushroom whose spores do not easily germinate.

However, most contaminants encountered are not helpful. Bacteria can be the most pernicious competitors -- some parasitize mycelial cell walls, others stimulate germination only to digest the resulting mycelia.

## Tissue Culture (Cloning)

Tissue culture preserves the exact genetic character of a living mushroom. The entire fruitbody is composed of compressed mycelia, so a viable culture can be obtained from any part.

### Procedure
1. Obtain specimen within 24-48 hours of picking (older specimens are harder to clone)
2. Wipe the surface with alcohol-soaked cotton swab
3. Remove dirt or damaged external tissue
4. Break the cap or stem to expose interior hyphae
5. Flame a scalpel until red-hot and cool in a media-filled petri dish
6. Cut into the flesh, removing a small tissue fragment
7. Transfer to the center of a nutrient petri dish as quickly as possible
8. Repeat into at least three, preferably five more dishes
9. Label with species, date, type (tissue), and agar medium

### Best Tissue Locations
- Cap interior
- Upper region of the stem
- Area where the gill plate joins the underside of the cap
- Flesh underlying the cuticle (for thick-cuticle species)

### Contamination Rates
- 10% overall contamination is tolerable for experienced cultivators
- 25% is not unusual for primary cultures from wild specimens
- Bacteria are the most common contaminant in tissue culture
- Once growth shows, transfer to new media; isolate away from any mold colonies promptly
