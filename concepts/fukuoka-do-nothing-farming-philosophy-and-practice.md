# Do-Nothing Farming Philosophy and Practice

updated: 2026-04-18

In a 1975 speech outlining his experiences and approach to natural
farming, Masanobu Fukuoka articulated the philosophical foundations of
what he called "do-nothing" farming, explaining both the spiritual
basis and the practical evolution of his methods over thirty years of
farming.

## The Three Principles

Fukuoka stated that his system of thought rests on three principles:
1. We do not understand; it is not possible to know and understand.
2. Nothing, no matter what it is, has value in and of itself.
3. Anything done with the human intellect is worthless; it serves no
   purpose.

In a word, all is unnecessary. When he arrived at this conclusion, he
lost a standard by which to judge what is true and what is false.

## Connection to Buddhist Philosophy

Fukuoka drew directly from the Buddha's Heart Sutra: "Form is emptiness
and emptiness is form. All is nothing." He interpreted this to mean
that all effort is in vain. Buddha is saying that we are not alive, we
are not growing, and we are not dead. Most people react to this by
thinking it nonsense, pointing to a flower on the table as proof of
life. But the Lord Buddha says that this is neither dead nor alive.

The soul itself is just something that arises from the body, and
neither exist; that the flesh and the soul are emptiness. No matter how
hard he tried to show this to others, it never got through. But he
wanted to try to demonstrate it for himself while farming in the
fields.

## The Farming Experiment

Fukuoka did not take up rice farming to speak to people about his
ideas. Farming was not what he did at all. He tried to establish
whether the idea that we have no need to do anything is valid or not.
He wanted only to find out for himself whether the words of the Buddha
in the Heart Sutra are really true or not. All he did was to try to
determine whether rice can be grown without doing anything.

## The Search for Unnecessary Actions

While at the agriculture [[fukuoka-straw-scattering-testing-station-resistance]] in Kochi, Fukuoka also tried
the opposite approach of "What about doing this? What about doing
that?" But this only makes people busier and makes things harder for
the farmer, who ends up having to do everything. Preoccupied with the
thought of having to do this and that, people are forever worrying.

When he became a farmer following the war, he searched for things that
do not have to be done. He asked whether the fields really need to be
plowed and turned, whether the rice farmer really has to transplant his
seedlings, whether it is necessary to spread fertilizer on the fields.

## The Paradoxes of Scientific Farming

Fukuoka explained that scientific truth appears to be truth, plain and
simple, but it is only scientific truth, not absolute truth. Things
appear to have value, but invariably man has set up the conditions that
give them their apparent value.

### The Plowing Trap

The reason a field must be plowed is that man has created the
conditions that require this. The farmer floods his rice paddy for six
months and runs a tiller through it, turning the soil into something
akin to adobe mud. The constant activity kills the microbes and drives
the air from the earth. Eventually, whether he likes it or not, the
farmer must run his plow deep through the field to let air into the
earth. All that is extra work. Man creates the conditions that require
him to plow each year.

### The Fertilizer Trap

What would have happened had the earth been left alone? If the land is
not abandoned but instead left in a natural state, if as in the
mountain forests the earth is not plowed or tilled but left to itself,
in a few thousand years a rich soil develops. Man kills and destroys
the soil, then packs it into pots and runs tests on it.

The reason [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] have to be applied is that the paddies
are filled with water and the roots of the rice allowed to rot so that
the plants are weak. When man establishes conditions in a field that
render chemical fertilizers necessary, then runs tests in which he
fertilizes one section and does not fertilize another, it stands to
reason that the fertilized plot will produce larger plants.

### The Pesticide Trap

With the frail, leggy rice that grows as a result of artificial
methods, pests break out unless the plants are sprayed with pesticides.
Breeding programs have succeeded only in downgrading rice, creating
weak rice suited to [[fukuoka-matsutake-artificial-cultivation-attempt]] methods under the prompting
of consumer demands for "tasty" rice.

All that was really necessary was to come up with some way of enriching
the soil while leaving it in nature's hands. But instead of trying to
breed healthy rice, scientists breed rice suited to these artificial
and unnatural methods of cultivation.

## Walking the Fine Line

Merely leaving one's fields alone is not nature. While walking the fine
line between nature and the laissez-faire approach, Fukuoka strived to
learn what the true form of nature is. This is distinct from both
[[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]] and from mere abandonment of land.

## The Natural Farm Community

Fukuoka described his farm as a citrus orchard on a hilltop overlooking
the Inland Sea, with 7-8 acres of mandarin grove and an acre and a
half of paddy field for rice and barley. Over the years, a constantly
changing group of about six to ten young people lived in huts in the
orchard, helping with the field work. They were generally people
following a natural diet, members of [[coleman-deep-organic-farming-philosophy]] associations,
Shintoists, Christians, members of various religious bodies, college
students, and runaways. Everyone came as they wished, stayed as long as
they cared, and left when ready.
