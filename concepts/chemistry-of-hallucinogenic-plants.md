# Chemistry of Hallucinogenic Plants

The hallucinogenic properties of plants arise from specific [[morning-glory-ergoline-alkaloids-chemistry]] (ergine and others)
- Ibogaine alkaloids
- β-carboline alkaloids (harmine, harmaline, etc.)
- [[serotonin-psilocin-structural-similarity-hallucinogenic-mechanism]] to serotonin** (5-hydroxydimethyltryptamine), a neurohumoral present in the nervous tissue of warm-blooded animals. Serotonin plays a major role in the biochemistry of the central nervous system. The study of hallucinogenic tryptamines may help explain the function of serotonin in the body.

A similar chemical relationship exists between **mescaline** (a hallucinogenic phenylethylamine base in peyote) and the neurohormone **norepinephrine**.

### Other Alkaloid Classes

Additional alkaloid classes with milder hallucinogenic effects that may operate differently in the body:

- **Isoquinolines**
- **Tropanes** (found in belladonna, henbane, mandrake, datura)
- **Quinolizidines**
- **Isoxazoles** (found in *Amanita* mushrooms)

## Cannabinols (Non-Nitrogenous Hallucinogens)

The most important non-nitrogenous hallucinogens are the active principles of cannabis: terpenophenolic compounds classed as dibenzopyrans called **cannabinoids**, particularly the **tetrahydrocannabinols (THC)**.

Unlike alkaloids, cannabinoids are non-nitrogenous organic compounds derived from terpenes. They are the only major class of plant hallucinogens that lack nitrogen in their molecular structure.

## Pseudohallucinogens

Pseudohallucinogens are poisonous plant compounds that cause what might be called secondary hallucinations or pseudohallucinations. They are not true hallucinogenic agents but upset normal body functions so severely that they induce a kind of delirium accompanied by practical hallucinations.

Some components of essential oils (the aromatic elements responsible for plant odors) appear to act this way. **Nutmeg oil** components are a well-known example. Many plants with such components are extremely dangerous if ingested in doses high enough to induce hallucinations. Research has not yet fully elucidated the psychoactivity produced by such chemicals.

## Methods of Consumption

Hallucinogenic plants are used in a remarkable variety of ways, depending on the plant material, active chemicals involved, and cultural practices:

### Oral Ingestion

- **Fresh or dried plants:** Peyote, teonanacatl (sacred mushrooms)
- **Plant juices:** Crushed leaves of *Salvia divinorum*
- **Plant derivatives:** Hashish
- **Beverages:** Ayahuasca/caapi/yajé (bark of *Banisteriopsis* vine), San Pedro cactus tea, jurema wine, iboga root, toloache leaves, crushed morning glory seeds

### Smoking

Originally a New World tobacco custom, now widely used for cannabis. Other narcotics such as tupa may also be smoked.

### Snuffing

A preferred method for several hallucinogens — yopo, epena, sébil, rapé dos indios. Like smoking, snuffing is primarily a New World custom.

### Other Methods

- **Rectal administration** (rare, New World): *Anadenanthera* species
- **Topical application:** African custom of incising the scalp and rubbing *Pancratium* bulb juice across incisions — a primitive counterpart to the modern hypodermic method

### Plant Additives (Admixtures)

Subsidiary plants are sometimes added to preparations to alter, increase, or lengthen the narcotic effects of the main ingredients. This is particularly important in [[chacruna-psychotria-viridis]]* or *Banisteriopsis rusbyana* leaves (added — contain hallucinogenic tryptamines like DMT)
- *Brunfelsia* or *Datura* (added — independently hallucinogenic)

This practice of combining plants represents an extraordinary accomplishment of aboriginal Americans in pharmacological knowledge. The β-carboline alkaloids in *Banisteriopsis* serve as monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), which allow the otherwise orally inactive DMT from *Psychotria* to become psychoactive when consumed by mouth.
