## Psilocybe cubensis: Cattle Association and Probable African Origin

## Overview

Psilocybe cubensis (Earle) Singer is one of the most widely distributed and
well-known [[mckenna-stropharia-cubensis-extraterrestrial-hypothesis-spore-dispersal]] by Earle in 1906
from Cuba. Singer later transferred it to Psilocybe. The species has accumulated
numerous synonyms over the years, including Stropharia subcyanescens Rick, S.
cyanescens Murrill, and Stropharia caerulescens (Pat.) Singer. It is a robust,
medium to large mushroom with a distinctive caramel-colored cap that bruises
blue when handled, a key indicator of psilocybin content.

## The Cattle Connection

The paper draws particular attention to the ecological dependence of P.
cubensis on cattle dung. This is a critical observation because the cattle
present in the Americas were introduced by Spanish colonists during the 16th
and 17th centuries. Prior to European contact, no large herd herbivores
comparable to cattle existed in most of tropical and subtropical America.

Despite the widespread presence of cattle across Europe following
domestication, P. cubensis is notably absent from Europe in natural settings.
European records of P. cubensis are invariably associated with illegally
cultivated or dried specimens imported from tropical countries. This creates
an intriguing biogeographical puzzle.

## The African Origin Hypothesis

Guzmán (1983) and reiterated in the 1998 paper, proposed that P. cubensis was
likely introduced to the Americas through the Atlantic slave trade during the
Spanish colonial period. The reasoning follows several lines of evidence:

1. **Absence from Europe**: Despite centuries of cattle ranching, P. cubensis
   does not occur naturally in Europe, suggesting the fungus requires specific
   tropical or subtropical conditions beyond mere cattle presence.

2. **Presence in Africa**: The species or its close relative P. aquamarina is
   known from Kenya, and Africa has both tropical conditions and a long history
   of cattle herding by indigenous peoples.

3. **Historical trade routes**: The transatlantic slave trade operated between
   West Africa and the Americas (Mexico, [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], Caribbean, South
   America) during the 16th through 19th centuries. It is plausible that
   spore-contaminated cattle or fodder carried the fungus across the Atlantic.

4. **Current [[copelandia-cyanescens-pantropical-distribution-and-ecology]]**: The species is most diverse and
   abundant in precisely those tropical regions connected by historical trade
   routes.
