# Polyethylene Sheeting [[fruiting-chamber]] Simple Stackable Design

## Overview

A simple [[gottlieb-psilocybin-cultivation-fruiting-chamber-design]] using clear plastic bins covered with polyethylene sheeting provides an effective, low-cost environment for [[ectomycorrhizal-mushroom-fruiting-ecology]] without the need for electronic humidifiers, air pumps, or other complex equipment. This design was described in the grass seed cultivation guide as the standard approach for fruiting cased trays of *Psilocybe*, *Panaeolus*, and related species.

## Design Principles

The [[fruiting-chamber-design-and-environmental-control]] prioritizes simplicity, scalability, and passive [[stamets-cultivator-humidity-management-fogging-systems]]:

- **Passive humidity**: Humidity is maintained through natural evaporation from the casing soil surface, eliminating the need for powered humidification equipment.
- **Minimal air exchange**: Small holes in the bin sides provide sufficient fresh air exchange without requiring fans or pumps.
- **Stackability**: Standard clear plastic storage bins can be stacked vertically, dramatically increasing production per unit of floor space.
- **Low cost**: All materials are inexpensive and widely available. No specialized equipment beyond the bins and polyethylene sheeting is required.

## Materials and Construction

### Required Materials
- **Clear plastic bins**: Standard storage bins (approximately 30–50 L capacity) with tight-fitting lids. Clear walls allow visual monitoring of the fruiting progress without opening the chamber.
- **Polyethylene sheeting**: Thin plastic film used to cover the bin opening. This allows gas exchange while limiting moisture loss and providing a barrier against airborne contaminants.
- **Trays**: One-liter disposable plastic trays containing the cased substrate. Five trays fit per bin in the described setup.
- **Optional**: Fine mesh or filter material to cover air exchange holes and exclude flying insects.

### Construction Steps

1. **Prepare air exchange holes**: Melt or drill small holes (approximately 6–12 mm diameter) in the sides of the plastic bin, near the top. The number and size of holes depends on bin volume and the number of trays inside.
2. **Cover holes with mesh** (optional): Tape or glue fine mesh over the holes to prevent flies, gnats, and other insects from entering while still allowing air exchange.
3. **Load cased trays**: Place up to five 1-liter cased trays inside each bin. Space them evenly for uniform air circulation.
4. **Cover with polyethylene sheeting**: Drape the polyethylene sheeting over the open top of the bin. The sheeting should hang over the edges to create a loose seal that allows some air movement but retains most moisture.

### Why Not Use the Bin Lid

The bin's original lid is not used for the fruiting chamber. Instead, polyethylene sheeting is draped over the top. This is because:

- The original lid creates an essentially airtight seal, which prevents gas exchange and can lead to CO₂ buildup that inhibits fruiting.
- Polyethylene sheeting provides a semi-permeable barrier that allows some moisture vapor and gas exchange while still maintaining high humidity.
- The drape configuration allows the cultivator to lift the sheeting easily for daily misting and inspection without disturbing the environment as much as removing a tight-fitting lid would.

## Humidity Management

### Passive Humidity System

The humidity inside the chamber is maintained primarily by evaporation from the casing soil surface. Each bin contains five cased trays, and the combined surface area provides sufficient evaporation to maintain the high humidity levels (typically 85–95% relative humidity) that mushroom fruiting requires.

### Key Humidity Principles

1. **Daily misting**: The casing soil surface must be misted each day to prevent drying. A spray bottle with clean water is the standard tool. Misting should be light — the goal is to maintain surface moisture, not to saturate the casing.
2. **Never allow drying**: If the casing surface dries out completely, pinning stops and the flush may abort. Consistent daily attention is essential.
3. **Post-harvest watering**: Immediately after picking a flush, watering should be increased because maturing mushrooms draw significant moisture from the casing soil. The guide notes that "it's very difficult to give explicit directions on a watering regime" — cultivators must develop a feel for the moisture needs of their specific setup.
4. **Polyethylene barrier**: The sheeting reduces the rate of moisture loss to the external environment, extending the interval between necessary misting.

## Air Exchange Considerations

### CO₂ Sensitivity

Mushrooms are sensitive to elevated CO₂ levels. In a sealed environment, mycelial respiration quickly depletes O₂ and accumulates CO₂, which:
- Inhibits [[growing-gourmet-primordia-formation-pinning]] (pinning)
- Causes elongated, spindly stems with small caps
- Reduces overall yield and quality

### Hole Placement and Sizing

The melted holes in the bin sides provide passive air exchange driven by:
- **Concentration gradients**: CO₂ diffuses out through the holes while fresh air diffuses in.
- **Temperature gradients**: Slight temperature differences between the chamber interior and exterior create gentle convection currents that move air through the holes.
- **Evaporative currents**: Water evaporating from the casing surface creates local air movement that helps distribute gases within the chamber.

The holes should be positioned near the top of the bin because CO₂ is heavier than air and will tend to accumulate at the bottom. Upper holes encourage CO₂ to escape while drawing fresh air in from the bottom edges where the polyethylene sheeting does not seal perfectly.

## Stackability and Space Efficiency

### Vertical Stacking

The use of stackable bins is a significant advantage for cultivators with limited space. The guide emphasizes that these bins are "stackable and thus very space efficient." Practical considerations for stacking include:

- **Weight limits**: Each bin with five trays of cased substrate can be heavy. Ensure the bottom bin can support the weight of bins above it.
- **Access**: Stacked bins require the cultivator to lift upper bins to access lower ones. Arrange the stack so that the most mature casings are on top and the most recently cased trays are on the bottom.
- **Air exchange**: Stacking may partially block the side holes. Ensure that holes are not blocked by the bin above. Alternating hole positions between bins (e.g., holes on the left for even-numbered bins, right for odd) can help maintain air exchange in stacked configurations.

### Production Capacity

With five trays per bin and stackable design, a cultivator can scale production linearly with available vertical space. A typical shelf or corner could accommodate 4–6 stacked bins, representing 20–30 cased trays and the potential for continuous harvest across multiple flush cycles.

## Maintenance and Monitoring

### Daily Tasks
- **Mist casing surfaces**: Light misting to maintain moisture without overwatering.
- **Visual inspection**: Check for contamination, abnormal growth patterns, or insect activity through the clear bin walls.
- **Remove aborts**: Dead pinheads that have stopped growing should be removed promptly to prevent mold colonization.
- **Harvest mature mushrooms**: Pick mushrooms at the appropriate maturity stage (before veil tearing for most species).
