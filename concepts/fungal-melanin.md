# Fungal Melanin

Fungal melanins are complex polymeric pigments deposited in cell walls that
confer extraordinary resistance to environmental stress, including ultraviolet
radiation, extreme temperatures, enzymatic attack, and oxidative damage.
Melanized fungi are ubiquitous in both natural and built environments, staining
bathroom fixtures, building facades, ancient statuary, and even human tissues.
The pigment's ability to absorb all wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation
and convert them to harmless heat makes it one of the most versatile defensive
molecules in the biological world.

## Chemical Nature and Synthesis

Fungal melanins are synthesized by assembling masses of ring-shaped molecules
into a dense polymeric web that will not dissolve in boiling water or hot acid.
Deposited as a distinct layer within the cell wall, these pigments absorb all
wavelengths of visible light (hence the black appearance), plus ultraviolet
light, X-rays, and gamma rays. The energy from this radiation is transferred
deep into the molecular structure of the pigment and re-emitted as heat,
protecting the living cytoplasm from DNA damage and protein denaturation.

Different fungi produce different types of melanin through distinct
biosynthetic pathways. The cryptococcal pigment is a type of eumelanin,
similar in chemical structure to human skin melanin, produced by polymerizing
catecholamine precursors including L-dopa and dopamine. Other melanized fungi
produce chemically distinct variants through different synthetic routes.
Despite these differences, all confer broadly similar protective benefits.

The synthesis of fungal melanin involves the enzymatic oxidation of phenolic
precursors, followed by spontaneous polymerization of the resulting quinones
into an amorphous, insoluble polymer. This process can be disrupted by
targeting the synthetic enzymes, which has been used to create albino mutant
strains for research purposes. Adding tricyclazole to cultures is one common
method of producing these unpigmented variants.

## Role in Pathogenicity: Cryptococcus neoformans

Cryptococcus neoformans, a yeast that causes life-threatening meningitis in
immunocompromised patients, manufactures melanin from L-dopa, dopamine, or
the hormone epinephrine, all concentrated in brain tissue. The pigment is
deposited on the inner surface of the cell wall, creating a resistant barrier
between the cytoplasm and the external environment.

Melanin confers partial resistance to amphotericin B, the primary antifungal
drug used to treat cryptococcal meningitis, requiring higher doses for
effective treatment. It also protects the fungus from oxidative killing by
immune system macrophages, which produce reactive oxygen species to destroy
engulfed pathogens. Melanized cells survive significantly longer inside
macrophages than their albino counterparts.

The brain-specific localization of melanin precursors may explain why
Cryptococcus preferentially colonizes the central nervous system. The fungus
may attempt to grow in other body locations, but in the absence of L-dopa or
related compounds, its cells cannot become pigmented and remain vulnerable to
immune clearance. The concept of a pathogen stealing chemical compounds from
its host to protect itself from that host's defenses illustrates the insidious
nature of opportunistic fungal infections.

## Dematiaceous Fungi

Dematiaceous (dark-pigmented) fungi including Wangiella dermatitidis and
Exophiala jeanselmei cause subcutaneous and systemic infections called
phaeohyphomycoses. The black pigmentation of these pathogens is a consistent
diagnostic feature. Unlike Cryptococcus, these fungi form branching melanized
hyphae within tissues rather than encapsulated yeast cells.

The broad host range of melanized fungi extends to infections of turtles and
reptiles in the wild. Martha Powell, a world-renowned expert on zoospore-
forming fungi, has found oomycetes growing in skin lesions on turtles and
other reptiles in Alabama, suggesting an ancient evolutionary relationship
between melanized pathogens and animal tissues that may predate the appearance
of mammals by hundreds of millions of years. Perhaps the same microbe consumed
the flesh of prehistoric animals long before mammals appeared.

## Radiation Tolerance

Dark-pigmented, radiation-tolerant fungi have been found flourishing in the
contaminated soils around the Chernobyl reactor in Ukraine, demonstrating that
melanin can protect against even extreme levels of ionizing radiation. The
pigment appears to convert gamma radiation directly into chemical energy, a
capability that may have implications for understanding life in high-radiation
environments including potential extraterrestrial habitats.

## UV Resistance and Surface Colonization

Resistance to ultraviolet light is the primary function that allows melanized
fungi to colonize exposed surfaces such as garden sheds, marble statues, and
building masonry, while translucent fungal cells are restricted to
subterranean or shaded habitats. Part of the reason lichens survive in exposed
locations at high elevations is that melanin within the fungal component's
hyphae protects the photosynthetic pigments of the algal partner from UV
damage.

Melanized fungi coat the surfaces of buildings and ancient statuary, pit and
penetrate stone, and hasten its disintegration. Soot from automobiles is often
incorrectly blamed for discoloration of masonry when the pollution is actually
fungal in origin.

## Experimental Evidence

Research on melanin function has relied on comparing normal pigmented fungi
with albino mutants. In almost every experimental contest between pigmented
and albino strains, the albinos are defeated. Grown in stress-free conditions
on agar, albinos perform adequately, but exposed to ultraviolet light they
shrivel, subjected to temperature shifts they die, and attacked with
cell-wall-degrading enzymes they explode.

Melanin confers such diverse benefits that it is difficult to make a
declarative statement about its primary function in any particular fungus.
Competing laboratories have argued for decades, with proposals including UV
protection, thermal regulation, enzymatic resistance, desiccation tolerance,
and antioxidant activity.

## Wood Decay and Spalting

When incompatible fungal mycelia encounter one another within decaying wood,
they attack each other's hyphae and produce thick, melanin-impregnated walls
as a defense. These darkened zones create the characteristic patterns of
spalted wood, prized by woodworkers for decorative bowls and ornaments. The
wood from such trees retains much of its strength and can be chiseled into
beautiful forms.
