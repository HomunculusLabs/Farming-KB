## Fungal Melanin

Fungal melanins are complex polymeric pigments deposited in cell walls that
confer extraordinary resistance to environmental stress, including ultraviolet
radiation, extreme temperatures, enzymatic attack, and oxidative damage.
Melanized fungi are ubiquitous in both natural and [[electromagnetic-spectrum-visible-light-plant-perception]] (hence the black appearance), plus ultraviolet
light, X-rays, and gamma rays. The energy from this radiation is transferred
deep into the molecular structure of the pigment and re-emitted as heat,
protecting the living cytoplasm from DNA damage and protein denaturation.

Different fungi produce different types of melanin through distinct
biosynthetic pathways. The cryptococcal pigment is a type of eumelanin,
similar in chemical structure to human skin melanin, produced by polymerizing
catecholamine precursors including L-dopa and dopamine. Other melanized fungi
produce chemically distinct variants through different synthetic routes.
Despite these differences, all confer broadly similar protective benefits.

The synthesis of fungal melanin involves the enzymatic oxidation of phenolic
precursors, followed by spontaneous polymerization of the resulting quinones
into an amorphous, insoluble polymer. This process can be disrupted by
targeting the synthetic enzymes, which has been used to create albino mutant
strains for research purposes. Adding tricyclazole to cultures is one common
method of producing these unpigmented variants.

## Role in Pathogenicity: Cryptococcus neoformans

Cryptococcus neoformans, a yeast that causes life-threatening meningitis in
immunocompromised patients, manufactures melanin from L-dopa, dopamine, or
the hormone epinephrine, all concentrated in brain tissue. The pigment is
deposited on the inner surface of the cell wall, creating a resistant barrier
between the cytoplasm and the external environment.
