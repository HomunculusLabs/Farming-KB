# Psilocybe azurescens Species Guide

## Overview

*Psilocybe azurescens* Stamets and Gartz is among the most potent
psilocybin-containing mushrooms in the world. Known by common names
including "Astoriensis," "flying saucer mushroom," and "indigo Psilocybe,"
this species was first identified near Astoria, Oregon along the northern
coast.

*P. azurescens* is notable for its exceptional potency, relatively high
baeocystin content, and one of the strongest bluing reactions of any known
*Psilocybe*. The flesh actually becomes indigo-black where damaged, a
hallmark feature of this species. It was formally described by Stamets and
Gartz and is considered one of the most significant mycological discoveries
in the Pacific Northwest region.

## Macroscopic Features

**Cap:** 3-10 cm broad. Conic to convex, expanding to broadly convex and
eventually flattening with age, with a pronounced, persistent broad umbo.
Surface smooth, viscid when moist, covered by a separable gelatinous
pellicle. Chestnut to ochraceous brown to caramel in color, often becoming
pitted with dark blue or bluish black zones, hygrophanous, fading to dingy
brown in drying. Strongly bruising blue when damaged. Margin even,
sometimes irregular and eroded at maturity, slightly incurved at first, soon
decurved, flattening with maturity, translucent striate and often pale
azure tinted.

**Flesh:** At center 3-6 mm thick, cottony and whitish to pallid brown at
the stipe/pileus junction before bruising. Flesh rapidly bruising blue,
then darkening to deep caerulean blue and eventually indigo-black.

**Gills:** Attachment ascending, sinuate to adnate, brown, often stained
indigo black where injured. Close, with two tiers of lamellulae, mottled,
edges whitish. Spore print dark purplish brown to purplish black in mass.

**Stem:** 90-200 mm long by 3-6 mm thick. Silky white, dingy brown from
the base or in age. Hollow at maturity. Composed of twisted, cartilaginous,
silky white fibrous tissue. Base thickening downwards, often curved,
characterized by coarse white aerial tufts of mycelium, often with azure
tones combined with dense, thick, silky white rhizomorphs that tenaciously
attach to wood chips or dead grass. The stem strongly bruises bluish upon
disturbance.

**Veil:** White, cortinate, often leaving a fibrillose annular zone in the
superior regions of the stem. Taste is extremely bitter. Odor none to
slightly farinaceous.

## Microscopic Features

Spores are dark purplish black in deposit, 12-13.5 by 6.5-8 microns,
ellipsoid. Basidia are 4-spored. Pleurocystidia are abundant,
fusoid-ventricose, tapering to a narrow but short neck, bluntly papillate,
23-35 by 9-10 microns. Cheilocystidia are nearly identical to
pleurocystidia, measuring 23-28 by 6.5-8 microns. Tissue is notably awash
with bluish tones.

## Habitat and Distribution

*P. azurescens* grows cespitose to gregarious on deciduous wood chips
and/or in sandy soils rich in ligninous debris. This mushroom naturally
grows, often prolifically, along the northern Oregon coast near Astoria,
favoring the land adjacent to the shoreline.

The species has a strong affection for dune grasses, especially *Ammophila
maritima*, with which it is closely associated. By forming extensive, dense,
and tenacious mycelial mats, *P. azurescens* causes the whitening of wood.
Fruiting begins in late September and continues well after the first frost,
often into late December and early January.

As an adaptive species, outdoor beds have been established with ease in the
United States and elsewhere. It is a cold weather-tolerant species, one of
the hardiest of the active Psilocybes. See [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] for
another cold-adapted psilocybin species with different habitat preferences.

## Potency and Chemistry

*P. azurescens* is one of the most potent species in the world. Reported
alkaloid concentrations include up to 1.78% psilocybin, 0.38% psilocin,
and 0.35% baeocystin (Stamets and Gartz 1995). After six months of
storage, analyses revealed that this species retained most of its original
potency.

The relatively high baeocystin content is roughly on par with
[[psilocybe-semilanceata]]. Ingesting *P. azurescens* can produce
profoundly intense experiences. At higher doses, some people report a loss
of muscular control and strength that can persist into the following day.
These effects have also been reported with [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] and
other wood-inhabiting species.

## Distinguishing Features from Related Species

The silky white stem, caramel-colored cap, relatively large stature,
non-undulating cap margin, and broad but pronounced umbo are representative
features. [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] is similar but can be macroscopically
distinguished by its much smaller stature and characteristically sine-wave
margin.

A variety sometimes called "[[psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa]]" also occurs in
the Pacific coastal region but differs in its fibrillose cap and generally
lower potency. [[psilocybe-serbica]] represents a European analogue found
in wood-chip and forest-edge habitats, sharing the cold-tolerance trait
but with distinct morphological characteristics.

For a widely cultivated comparison, see [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]]
and [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-mexicana-species-guide]] for tropical species.

## Outdoor Cultivation Methods

While *P. azurescens* is not commonly cultivated indoors, outdoor beds can
be established relatively easily using wood chip substrates. The process
involves inoculating hardwood chips (alder, maple, or similar species) with
spawn and allowing the mycelium to colonize over several months.

Key steps for outdoor bed establishment:

1. Prepare a bed site in a shaded, cool location with good drainage
2. Fill the bed with pasteurized hardwood chips mixed with spawn
3. Cover with cardboard or burlap to retain moisture during colonization
4. Allow 3-6 months for full colonization of the substrate
5. Initiate fruiting by exposing the bed to cooler temperatures and
   increased humidity in autumn

The mycelium forms extremely dense, tenacious mats that persist for years
once established. Mulching with fresh wood chips annually can extend the
productive life of the bed. The [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-baeocystis-knobby-tops]]
page provides additional cultivation context for related Pacific Northwest
species.

## Conservation and Ethical Foraging

Natural populations of *P. azurescens* are limited to a relatively small
geographic range along the Oregon and Washington coasts. Habitat
destruction from coastal development threatens existing populations.
Responsible foragers should avoid overharvesting, leave some specimens to
sporulate, and minimize disturbance to the mycelial mats.
