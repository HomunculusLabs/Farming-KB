## Shadoof (Sweep or Well-Pole)

The shadoof is one of the oldest water-lifting devices, dating back to
ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt. It consists of:

- A vertical post set in the ground
- A horizontal beam pivoting on top of the post (like a seesaw)
- A bucket or skin bag suspended from the long end of the beam
- A counterweight (stone, clay, or filled container) on the short end

The operator pulls the long end down to lower the bucket into the water
source, then allows the counterweight to lift the water-filled bucket.
The shadoof is best suited for lifting water 1 to 3 metres from rivers,
canals, or shallow wells. Its efficiency is limited but its simplicity
makes it virtually maintenance-free.

## Noria (Water Wheel)

The noria is a large water wheel with pots or buckets attached to its rim.
As the wheel is turned (by water current or animal power), the pots dip
into the water source, fill, and discharge at the top of the wheel into
an aqueduct or collection channel. Mollison describes two main types:

- Undershot noria: Powered by the flow of the water source itself. The
  current pushes paddles on the wheel rim. Suitable for streams with
  reliable flow.
- Animal-powered noria: Turned by a geared animal drive (ox, donkey, or
  camel walking in a circle). The drive mechanism converts rotary animal
  motion to wheel rotation.

Noria capacity depends on wheel diameter and speed. A medium noria (3 to
5 metre diameter) can lift 5,000 to 30,000 litres per hour, making it
suitable for irrigation supply. Mollison notes that norias are
particularly effective in Middle Eastern and South Asian river-valley
agriculture.

## Persian Wheel (Aragh)

The Persian wheel is a chain-of-pots pump driven by animal power. A
continuous loop of rope or chain carries a series of small clay or metal
pots. As the drive wheel turns, the pots descend into the well, fill with
water, and carry it to the surface where they tip into a discharge trough.
Key characteristics:

- Can lift water from greater depths than a shadoof (up to 15 to 20
  metres depending on the drive power)
- Moderate flow rate: typically 2,000 to 10,000 litres per hour
- Requires animal power (usually oxen or bullocks) or a mechanical
  wind drive
- The chain-and-pot mechanism is simple to construct and repair with

- Widely used across the Indian subcontinent and Middle East

## Archimedes Screw

The Archimedes screw consists of a helical surface (screw) wound around
a central shaft, enclosed in a cylindrical housing. As the shaft rotates,
water is lifted along the screw threads from the lower to the upper end.
Mollison describes its applications:

- Lifts water continuously rather than in discrete batches
- Can handle muddy or debris-laden water without clogging
- Typically lifts water 1 to 5 metres, though multiple screws can be
  staged for greater lift
- Can be powered by hand crank, animal power, water current, or wind
- Efficient for low-head irrigation from streams or drainage ditches
- Made from sheet metal, timber, or PVC pipe with an internal helical
