# Ethnobotanical Use of Neurotropic Fungi in Mexico

Mexico is the global center of [[mexico-center-neurotropic-fungal-biodiversity]] and cultural
use, with 76 known species — 44 of which are Psilocybe, representing 39%
of the world total. The indigenous peoples of Mexico have the most
extensive and well-documented traditions of sacred mushroom use on Earth,
spanning multiple ethnic groups, languages, and ceremonial contexts.

## The Rediscovery

The modern [[roger-heim-scientific-study-psilocybin]] of neurotropic fungi began with the
rediscovery of their traditional use in Mexico. The key figures in this
rediscovery were:

- **[[panaeolus-campanulatus]] var. sphinctrinus.
- **[[roger-heim]]** (1956-1958): French mycologist who conducted
  extensive fieldwork and cultivated several species.
- **R. Gordon Wasson** (1957-1958): The amateur mycologist and banker
  whose Life magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" brought
  the topic to worldwide public attention.

Guzmán (1990a, 1990b) divided the development of Mexican mycology into
two periods: before Wasson, Heim, and Singer's studies (1954-1958) and
after. The neurotropic fungi studies were so influential that they
stimulated interest in studying all fungi in the country.

## Common Names and Classification

Guzmán (1997) reported more than 200 common names for neurotropic fungi
in Mexico, many in indigenous languages. Some notable Nahuatl names
include:

- **"Teonanácatl"** — divine mushroom, first reported by Sahagún
  (1569-1582) and later by Schultes (1939). This term is now used
  indiscriminately for any Mexican hallucinogenic fungi, though it was
  originally specific.
- **"Apipiltzin"** — kid or little boy
- **"Atkad"** — mayor or leader
- **"Di nizé taaya"** — fungus of the genius
- **"Shi thó"** — that eruptions thing
- **"Teotlaquilnanácatl"** — divine fungus (most explicit form)

Common Spanish names used by indigenous peoples include:

- **"San Isidros"** — referring to the Spanish saint of agriculture,
  commonly used for [[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]].
- **"Pajaritos"** — little birds, used for [[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]].
- **"Derrumbes"** — landslides, used for Psilocybe zapotecorum.

## Indigenous Groups and Their Practices

Multiple indigenous groups across central and southern Mexico maintain
traditions of neurotropic fungi use. The most well-documented include:

### The Mazatec (Oaxaca)

The Mazatec are the most famous practitioners, largely due to the work
of Wasson, who participated in a velada (night ceremony) with curandera
María Sabina in Huautla de Jiménez in 1955. The Mazatec use Psilocybe
mexicana and other species in healing ceremonies conducted by curanderos
who diagnose and treat illness through visionary states.

A notable source of confusion: the Mazatec use the word "nise" (little
bird) for both Psilocybe mexicana and Schizophyllum commune, a common
edible fungus. This has led to erroneous reports of S. commune being
hallucinogenic.

### The Mixtec (Oaxaca)

The Mixtec have a particularly complex relationship with sacred fungi.
Heim and Wasson (1962) and Ott et al. (1975) reported the use of several
puffball species (Lycoperdon mixtecorum, L. marginatum) and at least
nine other species as "narcotic fungi" among the Mixtec. However,
chemical analysis showed these puffballs contained no neurotropic
substances. Ott et al. concluded that the Mixtec narcotic puffballs were
a mixture of at least nine species containing no neurotropic properties
— the Indians appeared to use them indistinctly as narcotic fungi,
suggesting confusion with more traditional neurotropic species.

### The Chinantec (Oaxaca)

The Chinantec use Dictyophora indusiata (in three forms) as a special
"narcotic" fungus for divination purposes. This species has no confirmed
neurotropic chemistry but occupies an important ceremonial role.
