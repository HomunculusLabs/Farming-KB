# Hamilton: Vegetative Propagation — Cuttings, Division, and Layering

**Source:** Geoff Hamilton, *Organic Gardening* (Nick Hamilton edition)

**Updated:** 2026-04-21

While seed is the most common method of [[hamilton-ornamental-trees]], many plants will not produce exact replicas from seed. Flower colors, shapes, and other characteristics may differ. Vegetative propagation—division, cuttings, layering, budding, and grafting—ensures that new plants are genetically identical to the parent. This is essential for named cultivars, fruit varieties, and any plant where specific traits must be preserved.

[[geoff-hamilton-organic-techniques]] regarded vegetative propagation as one of the most satisfying aspects of organic gardening, allowing gardeners to multiply their best plants without relying on commercial nurseries that may use chemical treatments.

## Division

Division is the simplest and most effective method of propagation for perennials. It produces good-sized plants very quickly. Many perennials begin to lose vigor after a few years and should be divided every few years to perpetuate stocks and prevent overcrowding. For most perennials, division is done in the fall: cut back old flower stems, lift the whole clump with a fork, divide it in half, and remove the young shoots from the outside of the clump. The center of the clump is the older, less vigorous part and should be discarded.

Plants with fleshy roots (such as hostas) are treated differently: they are best dug up for division in spring, just before growth begins, when new buds are visible. Each new piece should have at least one good bud. Cut through fleshy roots with a sharp spade or large knife and replant immediately. Small clumps can be divided by hand or trowel; larger or older clumps require prying apart with two garden forks inserted back-to-back.

After dividing, replant the healthiest sections into prepared ground enriched with [[holzer-green-manure-and-soil-building-plants]]. Water thoroughly and mulch to conserve moisture while the divided plants re-establish their root systems.

## Softwood Cuttings

Softwood cuttings can be taken from any shrub during summer, using healthy shoots about 4 inches (10cm) long. Trim the cutting just below a leaf joint, remove the lower side leaves, dip in organic rooting powder, and insert into peat-free propagation compost. The tray is wrapped in light plastic sheeting and placed in a softwood cuttings frame. Organic rooting powders contain no synthesized hormones and are safe for organic gardens; they can make the difference between cuttings rooting or not.

Half-hardy perennials are best taken as cuttings in late summer or early spring when tubers produce new shoots. These need a root temperature of 55-60F (13-15C) and are covered with a plastic bag or cloche to maintain humidity. For clematis cuttings, take a cutting at least 12 inches (30cm) long, make cuts immediately above and below a leaf joint, dip in copper fungicide, and insert up to the leaves.

The [[hamilton-greenhouse-management-organic]] provides an ideal environment for softwood cuttings, offering warmth and humidity control. Bottom heat from a propagator mat can significantly improve rooting success rates for many species.

## Hardwood Cuttings

Hardwood cuttings are taken in fall after leaf drop, though some plants (gooseberries) can be done earlier. This method suits deciduous shrubs such as black currants, dogwoods, mock orange, and Japanese rose. Cuttings about 8-9 inches (20-23cm) long are placed in a narrow trench lined with sharp sand, with 3 inches left above ground. They are left for one year, then planted in rows 6-9 inches apart, and transplanted to their permanent position the following year. Success rates of about 80 percent are achievable.

Shade management is critical for all cuttings. Too much sun causes wilting; too little prevents feeding through leaves. Old onion nets or greenhouse shade netting provide adjustable shading. The key is managing two pieces of shade: double shading on very sunny days, single on fair days, none on dull days.

## Layering

Layering involves putting part of a plant into the ground while it remains attached to the parent until it develops its own root system. There are three methods:

### Tip Layering

Used mainly for bramble fruits (blackberries, loganberries, hybrid berries). In late summer, shoots are pulled down, the tip placed in a hole, pinned with a forked stick, covered with soil, and left until the parent's leaves fall. The new plant is then separated and transplanted.

### Normal Layering

Used for plants that root less readily: rhododendron, clematis, abelia, viburnum, magnolia, witch hazel, Mexican orange blossom, camellia, and azalea. A hole is made with a spade, the shoot is placed in with the tip exposed, the stem is wounded by twisting or slitting to encourage rooting, pinned, covered with soil, and weighted with a stone. The layer may root by the following fall, though some plants (rhododendrons) take two to three years.

### Serpentine Layering

Suitable for climbers like clematis. The stem is alternately buried and exposed at intervals, with at least one bud between each buried section. Wound the stem by slitting underneath each buried portion. When shoots show signs of growing, the layers are separated from the parent and split into individual plants.

Layering is particularly useful for [[hamilton-climbing-plants-organic]] that may be difficult to root from cuttings. The parent plant continues to nourish the developing roots, making this one of the most reliable propagation methods.

## Grafting and Budding

These techniques put new varieties onto existing plants, particularly fruit trees. Grafting is used to change a variety completely or add a pollinator to an existing tree. Budding is a modified form of grafting used to put new varieties onto rootstocks.

### Cleft Grafting

Used to graft a new variety onto an existing tree in late winter or early spring. The limb is prepared in midwinter by cutting back two branches above a fork. In spring, the cut ends are split with a billhook, scions (one-year-old growth, 4-6 inches long) with wedge-shaped bases are inserted so cambium layers correspond, tied with raffia, and covered with grafting wax.

### Whip-and-Tongue Grafting

Used to graft a variety onto a rootstock. Both rootstock and scion receive matching slanting cuts with "tongues" that interlock. Cambium layers must correspond exactly. The graft is tied with raffia and sealed with grafting wax.

### Budding

Performed in early to midsummer when sap is running freely and bark separates easily. A T-shaped slit is cut in the rootstock bark, a bud from the desired variety is inserted (with the wood sliver removed), secured with raffia or rubber ties, and left until the bud shows signs of having taken. In fall, the growth above the bud is pruned away.

Grafting and budding are closely related to [[fruit-tree-pruning-training-hamilton]], as both disciplines require an understanding of tree growth habits and cambium alignment.

## Aftercare for Propagated Plants

All newly propagated plants benefit from a sheltered position while they establish. A [[cold-frame-construction]] is ideal for hardening off young plants before they face full garden conditions. Keep the compost consistently moist but never waterlogged, and feed weakly with liquid [[hamilton-organic-fertilizers-and-plant-nutrition]] once new growth is evident.
