## Global Ecological Crisis: Soils and Deforestation

This page examines [[bill-mollison]]"s detailed analysis of the interconnected crises of soil degradation, deforestation, and [[desertification]] as presented in his 1981 [[permaculture-design]] Course. These three phenomena form a self-reinforcing cycle: deforestation causes soil loss, soil loss accelerates desertification, and desertification removes the conditions necessary for forest recovery.

## The Soil Crisis by the Numbers

Mollison presented alarming quantitative data on soil loss that remains relevant decades later:

- **50% of all soils that existed before 1950 have been lost**
- **Another 30% of remaining soils are degraded**
- Soil creation rate: approximately **4 tons per acre per year** in [[berry-fruit-production-cool-humid-climates]]
- Australian agricultural soil loss: **27 tons per acre per year** (nearly 7× the creation rate)
- American corn-growing soil loss: up to **400–500 tons per acre per year** (100× the creation rate)
- Per-capita soil cost of grain consumption: **12 tons of soil per person per year**

The implication is stark: at current rates, agricultural soils would be exhausted within a decade. This is not a gradual decline but an accelerating one, because as soils degrade, more energy (mechanical and chemical) must be applied to maintain yields, which further degrades the remaining soil.

## Soil Creation and Destruction Mechanisms

### How Soils Are Created

Soils are primarily created through the interaction of rainfall and plant life on parent rock material. In humid climates, the process produces approximately four tons of new soil per acre per year. [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-arid-regions]], the rate is much lower. Forests are among the very few soil-creating systems on Earth — they are not merely soil-preserving but actively soil-generating through root penetration, organic matter deposition, and the biological activity they support.

### How Soils Are Lost

Soil loss occurs through multiple pathways:

**Tillage erosion.** All [[fukuoka-why-tillage-destroys-soil]] structure. The act of plowing exposes soil to wind and water erosion, oxidizes organic matter, and disrupts the fungal networks and soil biology that maintain fertility. As Mollison stated: "As long as you are tilling, you are losing."

**Water erosion.** Without forest cover, rainfall impacts bare soil directly, detaching particles that are then carried away by surface runoff. The removal of forests from ridges is particularly destructive because it eliminates the sponge effect that absorbs and slowly releases water.

**Wind erosion.** As soils dry and lose vegetative cover, they become susceptible to wind transport. Mollison described soils from the American Great Plains blowing to Los Angeles as "red rain," and Central Australian topsoil falling on cities at 12 tons per acre per day.

**Salinization.** Perhaps the most insidious form of soil loss, because it is less visible than erosion but equally permanent. Salinization is driven by deforestation at a distance — often hundreds of miles from the affected farmland.

## The Deforestation-Soil Salinization Mechanism

Mollison provided a detailed explanation of how deforestation remote from agricultural land causes soil salinization, a process he observed firsthand in Australia:

### The Normal System

Under forest cover, rainfall penetrates the soil and percolates downward. Trees act as powerful pumps, continuously drawing water from depth and transpiring it through their leaves. This creates a net downward transfer of water through the soil profile. The salts that are inevitably produced by the weathering of rocks (approximately four tons of soil per acre annually from rock breakdown) are carried downward into deep subterranean leads. Fresh water runs on the surface; saltier water exists at depth. The system is stable and self-maintaining.

### The Disrupted System

When forests are removed, the pumping mechanism is eliminated. Deep water tables begin to rise at measurable rates across enormous areas. Mollison documented this process occurring across America, Africa, and Australia. The rising water tables bring dissolved salts closer to the surface in a predictable sequence:

1. **At 3 feet below surface** — trees begin dying of "phasmids" (perceived as insect attack but actually salt stress)
2. **At 18 inches below surface** — [[fungal-endophytes-agricultural-crops]] begin to fail
3. **At the surface** — salts evaporate and the soil visibly turns white with salt crystals

At this point, the soil is effectively destroyed. Mollison noted that the Australian government then begins providing free pumps to farmers to pump out saline water — a response that must continue indefinitely. Meanwhile, the same government continues to issue wood-chipping licenses to multinational corporations. The result is a perverse economic cycle: companies sell both the pumps to deal with salinity and the wood chips that cause it.

### Beyond Arid Landscapes

Crucially, Mollison observed that this process was no longer confined to arid and semi-arid regions. It was occurring in humid, winter-wet climates in Australia — places that should never experience salinization under natural conditions. This indicated that the scale of deforestation had exceeded a critical threshold globally.

## Desertification

Desertification represents the end stage of soil loss. In the state of Victoria, Australia, Mollison reported the loss of 800,000 acres to salt in a single year — affecting both tilled and untilled soils. In Africa, encroaching deserts were killing millions of people, visible from the air as mass migrations of herds and people fleeing the expanding Sahara.

### The Cut-and-Run Agriculture Pattern

In arid regions of the American Southwest, Mollison described a pattern he called "cut-and-run agriculture":

1. A bore (well) is drilled to pump semi-saline groundwater
2. Annual crops are cultivated for 3–4 years of reasonable production
3. Surface soil becomes heavily mineralized as salts concentrate through evaporation
4. pH rises approximately two points per year (e.g., from pH 8 to pH 11)
5. Soils, often glued together with carbonates, lose all structure
6. The farmer abandons the site and moves on to drill another bore

This creates what Mollison called "carpeting destruction" — a pattern of serial land degradation visible from the air as a patchwork of abandoned, mineralized fields.

## Urban and Infrastructure Contributions

### Cities on Prime Soil

Mollison observed that cities occupy approximately 11% of the Earth's very best agricultural soils. In Canada, urban expansion was on track to obliterate top-quality agricultural land within a decade, pushing agriculture onto less sustainable terrain. The irony is that the increased energy application required to maintain production on poorer soils accelerates the rate of soil degradation on those remaining lands.

### Highway Construction

Highways represent permanent, irreversible soil loss. In Britain, Mollison estimated approximately one mile of highway per square mile of land surface. Highways seal soil beneath impermeable surfaces, disrupt natural drainage patterns, fragment ecosystems, and — through the associated sprawl — consume agricultural land at their margins.

## The Interconnected Crisis

Mollison's key insight was that these crises are not independent but form a positive feedback loop:

1. **Deforestation** removes the soil-creating and water-managing capacity of landscapes
2. **Soil loss** reduces the land's ability to support vegetation, including trees
3. **Desertification** expands as soils fail, creating conditions where forests cannot recover
4. **Climate instability** increases as forests (the primary climate moderators) disappear
5. **Agricultural intensification** (more energy input on less land) accelerates all of the above

The cycle can only be broken, Mollison argued, by a fundamental shift from extractive to regenerative land management — replacing annual tillage agriculture with permanent perennial systems, restoring forest cover on watersheds, and designing settlements that work within rather than against natural water [[ozier-pdc-composting-and-nutrient-cycles]].

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
