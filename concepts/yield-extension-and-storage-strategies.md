# Yield Extension and Storage Strategies

In conventional agriculture, yield is concentrated into short harvest windows — a "feast and famine" regime that creates market gluts, storage crises, and periods of scarcity. [[permaculture]] takes a fundamentally different approach: the aim is to disperse food yield over time so that many products are available at any season. This requires both strategic design for temporal distribution and robust systems for preserving and storing surplus when it is abundant.

## The Problem of Concentrated Yields

The concentration of yields into one short period is a fiscal, not an environmental or subsistence, strategy. It serves commercial interests that profit from bulk harvests, transport, and processing, but it creates systemic instability. Markets are flooded with product for a few weeks, prices collapse, unsold produce rots, and then for the rest of the year the same products must be imported from distant regions or stored at considerable energy cost. This feast-and-famine pattern is an artifact of monoculture and single-crop thinking, not an inherent limitation of productive landscapes.

## Strategies for Extending Yield Through Time

Permaculture disperses yield across seasons through multiple complementary strategies that work together to ensure continuous production:

### Temporal Stacking of Varieties

- **Selection of early, mid, and late season varieties** of the same crop species, so that harvest is spread across months rather than concentrated in weeks.
- **Planting the same variety in early or late-ripening situations** — using microclimates, elevation differences, and aspect variations to create different ripening conditions for identical cultivars.
- **Selection of varieties that yield over a long season** — choosing indeterminate tomatoes, everbearing berries, or multi-harvest greens over single-harvest types.

### Increasing System Diversity

A general increase in diversity in the system ensures that leaf, fruit, seed, and root products are all available as yield throughout the year. When multiple species with different productive periods are integrated, the landscape becomes a continuously yielding system rather than a periodically yielding one. A diverse polyculture produces something harvestable in every month, reducing dependence on any single harvest window.

### Self-Storing Species

Using species that store their own harvest on the plant or in the ground eliminates the need for artificial preservation. Tubers like potatoes, Jerusalem artichokes, and yams can be left in the ground and cropped on demand through winter. Hard seeds of legumes and grains store naturally in their pods. Firewood from tree plantings is a standing energy store that can be harvested whenever needed. Rhizomes of perennial herbs persist underground and produce fresh growth when conditions allow. These living storage systems reduce both labor and energy inputs for food preservation.

### Preservation Techniques

Traditional low-energy preservation methods extend the availability of seasonal abundance:
- **Drying** — sun-drying or dehydrating fruits, herbs, and meat
- **Pitting** — underground storage in cool, humid conditions for root vegetables and grains
- **Cool storage** — root cellars, earth closets, and naturally cooled spaces that maintain stable temperatures year-round
- **Preserving** — fermentation, pickling, and oil preservation that extend shelf life while often improving nutritional value
- **No-tillage or low-tillage cropping** — a strategy that saves energy, soil, water, and time between crops while maintaining soil structure for long-term productivity

### Regional Trade and Altitude Strategy

Regional trade between communities, or utilization of land at different altitudes or latitudes, extends the period of availability for perishable goods. A community at low elevation can trade early-season produce with a higher-elevation community that harvests later. This strategy works best when combined with local processing to reduce transport losses.

## Yield and Storage Systems

How yields endure is as important as how they are produced. There are unlimited opportunities to use durable yields in terms of season or lifetime. A comprehensive storage strategy addresses multiple resource types:

### Food Storage

By a series of preservation strategies, food can be stored for days, weeks, or years. The strategies chosen depend on the product, climate, and available technology. Low-energy methods should be preferred over high-energy methods (canning and freezing require significant energy inputs; fermentation and drying require little). Food storage is critical to eliminating famine — very little famine would occur could grains, fish, and fruit available in good times be stored for lean times.

### Water Storage

Water not open to evaporation and pollution, or with natural cleansing organisms, will keep indefinitely. This makes groundwater, sealed tanks, and living systems (wetlands, forested catchments) superior storage media compared to open reservoirs. The design of water storage systems should prioritize protection from evaporation and contamination while maintaining natural biological purification processes.

### Shelter and Material Storage

Shelters may outlast the forests that build them, or can be made of living or durable materials such as ivy-covered structures, stone, or earth. The durability of built elements directly affects long-term yield — a shelter that lasts 100 years with minimal maintenance represents a far greater yield than one that requires constant repair and replacement. Selection of durable materials and design for longevity are storage strategies in themselves.

### Energy Storage

Energy alone is difficult to store. Batteries leak or decay, heat escapes, and insulation breaks down. Only living things, like forests, increase their energy store over time. This is one of the strongest arguments for biological systems as the primary energy strategy — trees and other perennial plants are the only systems that actively capture and store increasing amounts of solar energy. Technological energy storage should be viewed as a supplement to, not a replacement for, biological energy storage.

### Seasonal and Diurnal Cycles

Because of seasonal or diurnal cycles, close attention should be paid to storage strategies. The pattern of abundance and scarcity follows predictable cycles that design can anticipate and buffer:

- **Seasonal cycles** produce peaks of productivity in growing seasons and deficits in dormant periods. Storage bridges the gap between feast and famine.
- **Diurnal cycles** create daily peaks of solar energy availability and nightly deficits. Thermal mass and appropriate building design can buffer these fluctuations with minimal energy input.
- **Rainfall cycles** produce wet and dry periods. Water storage capacity and soil water-holding ability determine how well systems navigate dry periods without irrigation.

Very little famine would occur could grains, fish, and fruit available in good times be stored for lean times. The strategies of food storage are critical. Mollison believed that people should therefore mulch their recipe books — which often specify out-of-season or non-local foods — and replace them with books that stress either low-energy methods of food preservation, or how to live easily from a garden in season. This emphasis on seasonal eating and local preservation is not merely practical but a fundamental shift in cultural relationship to food and place.

## Farm Strategy Examples

Practical application of yield extension strategies demonstrates their effectiveness:

### Water Storage (12-20% of landscape)
- Animal protein production — water is more productive per unit area than land; fish are more efficient at food conversion than cattle
- Irrigation increases land-based production
- Duck integration on water bodies for pest control, weed management, and manure fertilization
- Microclimatic buffering from water bodies moderates temperature extremes

### Land Forming
- Product increase through even irrigation with no dry areas or waterlogging
- Land stability through reduced soil loss from water runoff or salting
- Gravity flow replaces pumped water, saving energy

### Forestry and Windbreaks (20-30% of landscape)
- Shelter effects increase plant yields, animal protein production, and microclimate buffering
- Increased carrying capacity through shrub and tree integration
- Nutrient recycling via legumes and deep-rooted trees
- Intrinsic forest products: honey nectar, seeds, firewood
- Wildlife corridors and pest predator habitat

### Social and Financial Strategies
- Farm-link strategy where urban groups contract to buy specific produce from farmers, providing market stability
- Income from educational courses, field days, and on-farm demonstrations
- Rental or income from urban visitors through guest houses or holiday farm stays
- Direct investment by city people in particular farms, creating mutual benefit
- Local credit unions and banks that recycle money within the community
- Vehicle and implement pools with neighbors — capital savings of up to 90%
- Labor exchanges and produce cooperatives that reduce costs and build community resilience

## Cultural Impediments to Yield

Cultural attitudes can be the greatest barrier to realizing potential yields. Mollison noted that affluent suburbanites often develop zoophobia — fear of animals — that makes it almost taboo to use rabbits, quail, pigs, poultry, or cows in urban food systems. These animals are commonplace city farm animals in England, ordinary village animals in Asia, and edible guinea pigs live in South American homes, but in North America they are effectively banned from cities.

When useful animals are abolished from urban areas, the [[ecological-niche]] they occupied is filled by less desirable species. Pigeons forage streets, gulls defecate in city reservoirs, rats proliferate in garbage, and pest species multiply without competition. The refusal to integrate productive animals into human settlements is a cultural impediment that reduces both food security and ecological health.

Mollison suggests people should "mulch their recipe books" — replace books that specify out-of-season or non-local foods with ones that stress low-energy food preservation or how to live easily from a garden in season. This cultural shift from expecting year-round availability of everything to eating seasonally and locally is fundamental to sustainable yield management.

## People as the Primary Impediment

Just as designers can increase yield, so can yield be decreased. The perverse aims of some politicians, developers, and even religious dogmatists limit yield by disallowing certain products. Just as one's neighbors may refuse the snail and eat the lettuce, refuse the blackbird and eat the strawberry, so people may only allow certain types of toilets or certain plants in gardens and parks. People are the main impediment to using their potential yields — not physical limitations of the land or climate.
