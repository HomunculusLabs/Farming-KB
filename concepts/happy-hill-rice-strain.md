## Happy Hill Rice

Happy Hill is a rice strain developed by [[natural-farming-principles-fukuoka]] applied to plant breeding — a variety created
through natural selection and symbiosis with the farm ecosystem rather
than through laboratory genetic manipulation.

## Development and Characteristics

Fukuoka described Happy Hill as a hardy strain carrying the genetic
heritage of wild rice variants. Despite its wild ancestry, it proved to
be one of the highest [[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]] conditions. The variety was developed not through
deliberate crossbreeding but through decades of natural selection on
Fukuoka's farm, where rice grew in a clover-covered, no-till, no-fertilizer
environment.

The strain is notable for its vigor under low-input conditions. Unlike
modern high-yielding varieties that require substantial nitrogen
fertilization, irrigation, and [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]].

Fukuoka created it "together with the insects in my fields," suggesting
a co-evolutionary process where the variety adapted to the specific pest
and disease pressures of his ecosystem. This approach to variety
development stands in contrast to conventional rice breeding, which
selects under high-input conditions and produces varieties dependent on
those inputs to express their yield potential.

The name "Happy Hill" reflects Fukuoka's poetic approach to farming and
his belief that healthy plants growing in natural conditions embody a
quality that industrial agriculture cannot replicate. The variety
represented for him proof that nature, left to its own processes, could
produce results equal to or better than those achieved through scientific
intervention.

## Exponential Multiplication

Fukuoka calculated the exponential multiplication potential of Happy Hill
rice. A single grain sown over a ten-square-yard area would yield
approximately 5,000 grains in one year. This harvest would provide
sufficient seed to sow a half-acre the following year, fifty acres two
years later, and approximately 7,000 acres in the fourth year.

He proposed that a single head of Happy Hill rice could serve as the
foundation for seed rice for an entire nation facing food scarcity. The
implication was that food independence could be achieved through
distributed, low-cost seed multiplication rather than dependence on
industrial agricultural inputs.

## Role in Natural Farming System

Happy Hill rice was the centerpiece of Fukuoka's direct-seeded,
no-tillage rice-barley rotation. The seeds were encapsulated in clay
pellets and broadcast over the field in winter, germinating through the
straw mulch and [[clay-pellet-seeding-technique]] and compete with clover made it uniquely suited
to this rotation.
