# Historical Literature on Winter Harvest and Intensive Market Gardening

## Overview

Chapter 2 of *The Winter Harvest Handbook* surveys the historical roots of year-round
[[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]] occupied approximately 600
hectares—six percent of Paris's land area. Growers averaged four to eight harvests per year
from the same ground, and Paris was self-sufficient in fresh produce, even exporting to
England.

### Key Features of the System

- **Horse manure economy**: 100–400 tons per acre; fresh manure heated hotbeds, spent
  manure became compost for soil amendment—recycling "transportation wastes" of the day
- **Glass cloches**: 18-inch-diameter bell jars protected crops; some growers managed
  3,000+ cloches, each individually vented with notched sticks
- **Straw mats**: One-inch-thick rye-straw mats rolled over glass for overnight insulation
- **Narrow paths**: 10-inch walkways too narrow for wheelbarrows; manure delivered in
  woven willow backpacks (*hottes*) arched over the worker's head
- **Intercropping**: Radishes, carrots, lettuces, and cauliflowers successively planted
  in the same frame over a single season

## Primary French Sources

Coleman cites four foundational French texts consulted for historical research:

### Courtois-Gérard — *Manuel pratique de culture maraîchère* (1844)
- **Publisher**: Librairie Scientifique, Industrielle et Agricole, Paris
- **Significance**: Provides the figure of 600 hectares under [[intensive-vegetable-spacing-systems]] culture as "a successive process
  uninterrupted during the year, often with many different vegetables planted together
  on the same piece of ground"
- **Key quote**: Distinguishes professional intensive culture (with irrigation and
  compost) from ordinary [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]] and
  for compost-based [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] don't do all that one wants; they stimulate the
  plant and produce quantity, but to the detriment of quality... Insect pests only attack
  weak, sickly plant specimens lacking proper nutrition"

### Moreau, J. G., and J. J. Daverne — *Manuel pratique de la culture maraîchère de Paris* (1845)
- **Publisher**: Libraire de la Société, Paris
- **Significance**: Mid-19th-century comprehensive guide to Parisian techniques during
  the system's peak period

## English-Language Historical Sources

### Robinson, William — *Parks and Gardens of Paris* (1869)
- **Significance**: First English-language book to describe Parisian intensive techniques
- **Context**: Robinson chided British growers for failing to adopt superior French methods
- **Broader work**: Robinson also authored *The English Flower Garden* and promoted
  naturalistic gardening; his advocacy helped catalyze British interest in French methods

### Kropotkin, Peter — *Fields, Factories and Workshops* (1899)
- **Significance**: Praised Parisian maraîchers as models of productive, small-scale
  agriculture; referred to chemical fertilizers as "pompously labeled and unworthy drugs"
- **Context**: Kropotkin's anarchist philosophy celebrated self-sufficient local production
  as an alternative to industrial capitalism

### Smith, Joseph — *French Gardening* (early 1900s)
- **Significance**: Written by the foreman of Joseph Fels's experimental intensive farm at
  Mayland, Essex; introduction by Kropotkin; part of the post-1905 British enthusiasm wave
