# Fire-Associated (Phoenicoid) Fungi — Post-Fire Succession

## Overview

Fire-associated (phoenicoid) fungi are a specialized group of species that fruit on soil or plant material following fire events. These fungi may also appear on **steam-sterilized soil**, indicating that heat — rather than fire per se — is the primary trigger for their development. Their study provides important insights into [[fungal-leaf-litter-succession]], disturbance ecology, and the resilience of [[fungal-communities-extreme-environments]].

## Triggering Mechanisms

Two primary mechanisms are believed to stimulate fruiting of fire-associated fungi:

1. **Soil heating** — Direct thermal effects may break dormancy of fungal propagules, kill competing organisms, or release nutrients from organic matter
2. **pH changes** — Wood ash from fires can raise soil pH by **3–5 units**, dramatically altering the chemical environment

The pH mechanism is supported by the observation that sporocarp production in undisturbed forest soils can be stimulated by **lime application**, paralleling the pH increase caused by ash deposition.

## Early Colonizers

**Heterothallic *Neurospora* species** are among the most conspicuous early colonizers, commonly appearing on charred vegetation in moist tropical and subtropical regions. The genus *Neurospora* is well-known as a model organism in [[fungal-species-recognition-genetics]], and its fire-associated ecology highlights the connection between environmental disturbance and genetic diversity in fungi.

## Case Study: Mount St. Helens

The 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens provided a natural experiment in fungal succession on sterilized substrates:

### 6 Weeks Post-Eruption
- *Anthracobia melaloma*
- *Gelasinospora reticulospora*

### 1 Year Post-Eruption
- *Peziza* species
- *Rhizina undulata*
- *Coprinus plicatilis*
- *Schizophyllum commune*

This progression illustrates the transition from pioneer species capable of colonizing severely disturbed substrates to more generalist decomposer fungi as the community reassembles.

## Successional Groups (Dix & Webster, 1995)

Dix and Webster (1995) recognized **four distinct fruiting groups** based on their temporal patterns after fire:

### Group 1 (7–80 weeks)
- **Representative species**: *Pyronema*, *Trichophaea abundans*
- Earliest fruiters; rapidly colonize freshly heated or sterilized substrates
- Often the most visually abundant in the immediate post-fire period

### Group 2 (10–190 weeks)
- **Representative species**: *Ascobolus carbonarius*, *Peziza* species
- Extended fruiting period overlapping with both Group 1 and Group 3
- Begin appearing as Group 1 species decline

### Group 3 (20–200 weeks)
- **Representative species**: *Peziza endocarpoides*
- Mid-successional species that persist through later stages
- Bridge the gap between early pioneers and late colonizers

### Group 4 (50–150 weeks)
- **Representative species**: *Myxomphalia maura*, *Neotiella hetieri*
- Latest fruiting group
- Appear as the post-fire community transitions toward pre-fire composition

## Temporal Overlap

The fruiting periods of the four groups overlap substantially, creating a complex mosaic of species appearing and declining at different rates. This temporal overlap means that multiple successional groups can be found fruiting simultaneously in the same burn area, particularly during the 20–80 week window after fire.

## Ecological Significance

Fire-associated fungi play several important roles in post-fire ecosystems:

- **Decomposition** — Accelerate breakdown of charred organic matter, recycling nutrients into the recovering ecosystem
- **Soil stabilization** — Mycelial networks help bind disturbed soils, reducing erosion
- **Food webs** — Provide food resources for invertebrates and vertebrates in the post-fire environment
- **Competitive release** — Fire eliminates competitors, allowing these specialized species to flourish temporarily

## Broader Context

Fire-associated fungi illustrate the broader principles of [[fungal-biodiversity-forest-ecosystems]] and demonstrate how fungal communities respond to extreme disturbance events. Their ability to persist through fire as dormant propagules and rapidly colonize post-fire environments reflects the remarkable resilience and adaptability that characterize [[fungal-speciation-evolution]].

Understanding post-fire fungal succession is increasingly important in the context of changing fire regimes worldwide, where fire frequency and intensity are being altered by climate change and land management practices.

# Fungal Diversity in Volcanic Soils

Volcanic eruptions create new substrates for fungal colonization and represent natural experiments in primary succession. The deposition of hot ash, lava flows, and subsequent soil development provide insights into fungal community assembly on sterile substrates.

[[fungi-in-extreme-acidic-environments]] [[coprophilous-fungi]] [[fungal-diversity-fire-ecology]]

## Mount St. Helens as a Model System

The eruption of Mount St. Helens in Washington State (United States) provided one of the best-documented cases of fungal colonization following volcanic disturbance. Six weeks after the eruption, apothecia of Anthracobia melaloma and perithecia of Gelasinospora reticulospora appeared within the tephra.

One year after the eruption, large discomycetes (Peziza species, Rhizinia undulata, Trichophaea hemisphaerioides) and agarics (Coprinus plicatilis, Pholiota carbonaria, Psathyrella carbonicola, Schizophyllum commune) were common. This rapid colonization demonstrates the resilience and dispersal capacity of fungi.

## Phoenicoid Fungi on Volcanic Substrates

Volcanic eruptions and subsequent deposition of hot ash lead to abundant fruiting of phoenicoid fungi, the same group that fruits after forest fires. The heat treatment of substrates by volcanic ash simulates the effects of fire, selecting for heat-adapted fungal species.

The similarity between post-volcanic and post-fire fungal communities suggests that the primary selective factor is substrate heating rather than the specific cause of heating.

## Primary Succession on Lava

Volcanic lava flows create entirely new substrates devoid of organic matter and pre-existing fungal communities. Primary succession on these surfaces begins with colonization by wind-dispersed spores and the establishment of pioneer fungal species.

The gradual development of soil from volcanic ash and the establishment of plant communities create progressively more complex fungal habitats. Early successional fungi are typically stress-tolerant generalists capable of growth on nutrient-poor substrates.

## Volcanic Soil Properties

Volcanic soils (Andisols) have distinctive physical and chemical properties including high allophane content, variable charge, high phosphorus fixation capacity, and often high organic matter content. These properties create unique conditions for fungal growth.

The mineralogy of volcanic soils affects nutrient availability, water retention, and pH, all of which influence fungal community composition. The high porosity of volcanic ash deposits can create both well-drained and waterlogged microhabitats.

## Thermophilic Fungi in Geothermal Areas

Volcanic regions often have associated geothermal activity including hot springs and fumaroles. These areas harbor thermophilic fungi capable of growth at temperatures between 45 and 50 degrees Celsius.

Thermophilic fungi can be isolated by incubating soil and plant material at elevated temperatures. The addition of recalcitrant materials such as cellulose, lignin, keratin, and chitin to isolation media can selectively isolate functional guilds of thermophilic decomposers.
