## Case Study: Mount St. Helens

The 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens provided a natural experiment in fungal succession on sterilized substrates:

### 6 Weeks Post-Eruption
- *Anthracobia melaloma*
- *Gelasinospora reticulospora*

### 1 Year Post-Eruption
- *Peziza* species
- *Rhizina undulata*
- *Coprinus plicatilis*
- *Schizophyllum commune*

This progression illustrates the transition from pioneer species capable of colonizing severely disturbed substrates to more generalist decomposer fungi as the community reassembles.

## Successional Groups (Dix & Webster, 1995)

Dix and Webster (1995) recognized **four distinct fruiting groups** based on their temporal patterns after fire:

### Group 1 (7–80 weeks)
- **Representative species**: *Pyronema*, *Trichophaea abundans*
- Earliest fruiters; rapidly colonize freshly heated or sterilized substrates
- Often the most visually abundant in the immediate post-fire period

### Group 2 (10–190 weeks)
- **Representative species**: *Ascobolus carbonarius*, *Peziza* species
- Extended fruiting period overlapping with both Group 1 and Group 3
- Begin appearing as Group 1 species decline

### Group 3 (20–200 weeks)
- **Representative species**: *Peziza endocarpoides*
- Mid-successional species that persist through later stages
- Bridge the gap between early pioneers and late colonizers

### Group 4 (50–150 weeks)
- **Representative species**: *Myxomphalia maura*, *Neotiella hetieri*
- Latest fruiting group
- Appear as the post-fire community transitions toward pre-fire composition

## Temporal Overlap

The fruiting periods of the four groups overlap substantially, creating a complex mosaic of species appearing and declining at different rates. This temporal overlap means that multiple successional groups can be found fruiting simultaneously in the same burn area, particularly during the 20–80 week window after fire.

## Ecological Significance

Fire-associated fungi play several important roles in post-fire ecosystems:

- **Decomposition** — Accelerate breakdown of charred organic matter, recycling nutrients into the recovering ecosystem
- **Soil stabilization** — Mycelial networks help bind disturbed soils, reducing erosion
- **Food webs** — Provide food resources for invertebrates and vertebrates in the post-fire environment
- **Competitive release** — Fire eliminates competitors, allowing these specialized species to flourish temporarily

## Broader Context

Fire-associated fungi illustrate the broader principles of [[fungal-speciation-evolution]].

Understanding post-fire fungal succession is increasingly important in the context of changing fire regimes worldwide, where fire frequency and intensity are being altered by climate change and land management practices.

## Mount St. Helens as a Model System

The eruption of Mount St. Helens in Washington State (United States) provided one of the best-documented cases of fungal colonization following volcanic disturbance. Six weeks after the eruption, apothecia of Anthracobia melaloma and perithecia of Gelasinospora reticulospora appeared within the tephra.

One year after the eruption, large discomycetes (Peziza species, Rhizinia undulata, Trichophaea hemisphaerioides) and agarics (Coprinus plicatilis, Pholiota carbonaria, Psathyrella carbonicola, Schizophyllum commune) were common. This rapid colonization demonstrates the resilience and dispersal capacity of fungi.

## Phoenicoid Fungi on Volcanic Substrates

Volcanic eruptions and subsequent deposition of hot ash lead to abundant fruiting of phoenicoid fungi, the same group that fruits after forest fires. The heat treatment of substrates by volcanic ash simulates the effects of fire, selecting for heat-adapted fungal species.

The similarity between post-volcanic and post-fire fungal communities suggests that the primary selective factor is substrate heating rather than the specific cause of heating.
