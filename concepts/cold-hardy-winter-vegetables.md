# Cold-Hardy Winter Vegetables

## Overview

Eliot Coleman has identified and cultivated approximately 30 species of
cold-hardy vegetables suitable for winter greenhouse production. These plants
share a remarkable ability to survive freezing temperatures and continue
growing — albeit slowly — during the short, dim days of winter. Unlike
warm-season crops such as tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers, which require high
temperatures and intense light, these cold-hardy species have evolved to thrive
in cool conditions and can produce harvestable yields with remarkably low light
levels. Many of them actually improve in flavor after exposure to frost.

## The Complete Species List

Coleman has successfully grown the following ~30 species for winter harvest:

1. **Spinach** (*[[spinacia-oleracea]]*) — Perhaps the quintessential winter
   green. Spinach is extremely cold-tolerant and continues producing leaves
   through the darkest months. Varieties like 'Giant Winter' and 'Tyee' are
   particularly reliable.
2. **Swiss Chard** (*Beta vulgaris* subsp. *vulgaris*) — Both the leaves and
   stalks are harvestable. Chard tolerates repeated cutting and regrows
   reliably from the crown.
3. **Carrots** (*Daucus carota*) — Planted in late summer, carrots can be
   harvested fresh from the cold greenhouse soil throughout winter. Cold
   exposure converts starches to sugars, producing remarkably sweet roots.
4. **Scallions** (*Allium cepa*) — Bunching onions grow slowly in winter but
   provide a welcome allium flavor for winter salads and cooking.
5. **Mache** (*Valerianella locusta*) — Also known as corn salad, mache is
   one of the most cold-tolerant salad greens available. It forms small,
   tender rosettes with a mild, nutty flavor.
6. **Claytonia** (*Claytonia perfoliata*) — Miner's lettuce produces succulent,
   mild-flavored leaves and is exceptionally cold-hardy, often surviving
   temperatures well below freezing.
7. **Minutina** (*Plantago coronopus*) — Also known as buckshorn plantain,
   minutina produces narrow, deeply toothed leaves with a mild flavor that
   sweetens after frost.
8. **Arugula** (*Eruca vesicaria*) — The wild-type arugula varieties are
   more cold-tolerant than cultivated types and provide a peppery kick to
   winter salads.
9. **Beet Greens** (*Beta vulgaris*) — Both the leaves of mature beets and
   dedicated leaf-beet varieties produce harvestable greens throughout winter.
10. **Broccoli Raab** (*Brassica rapa* subsp. *rapa*) — Also called rapini,
    this quick-growing brassica produces tender shoots and leaves.
11. **Chicory** (*[[cichorium-intybus]]*) — Includes radicchio and other
    chicory types with bitter, flavorful leaves that mellow after frost.
12. **Collards** (*Brassica oleracea* var. *viridis*) — Extremely cold-hardy
    brassica greens that continue producing through deep winter.
13. **Dandelion** (*[[taraxacum-officinale]]*) — Cultivated dandelion varieties
    produce large, tender leaves with a pleasant bitter flavor.
14. **Endive** (*Cichorium endivia*) — Curly and broad-leaf types provide
    crisp, slightly bitter salad greens through winter months.
15. **Escarole** (*Cichorium endivia* var. *latifolia*) — A broader, less
    bitter cousin of endive, excellent for winter salads and cooking.
16. **Garlic Greens** (*Allium sativum*) — Immature garlic shoots harvested
    as a delicacy, providing mild garlic flavor for winter dishes.
17. **Kale** (*Brassica oleracea* var. *sabellica*) — One of the hardiest
    winter greens. Tuscan, Siberian, and red Russian varieties all perform
    well in cold greenhouse conditions.
18. **Kohlrabi** (*Brassica oleracea* var. *gongylodes*) — The swollen stem
    can be harvested throughout winter, remaining crisp and mild-flavored.
19. **Leeks** (*Allium ampeloprasum*) — Band-planted in summer, leeks can
    be harvested fresh from the frozen greenhouse soil all winter long.
20. **Lettuce** (*Lactuca sativa*) — Cold-tolerant varieties including
    'Winter Density', 'Rouge d'Hiver', and various oakleaf types produce
    harvestable heads and leaves through winter.
21. **Mizuna** (*Brassica rapa* var. *nivosicola*) — Japanese mustard green
    with feathery, mild-flavored leaves that regrow quickly after cutting.
22. **Mustard Greens** (*Brassica juncea*) — Various types including 'Red
    Giant' and 'Osaka Purple' provide spicy, flavorful winter greens.
23. **Pak Choi** (*Brassica rapa* subsp. *chinensis*) — Bok choy produces
    crisp stalks and tender leaves throughout the cold months.
24. **Parsley** (*[[petroselinum-crispum]]*) — Both flat-leaf and curly varieties
    survive winter in the cold greenhouse and provide fresh herb flavor.
25. **Radicchio** (*Cichorium intybus*) — Treviso and Chioggia types develop
    their characteristic red color and bitter-sweet flavor in cold weather.
26. **Radish** (*[[raphanus-sativus]]*) — Small, quick-growing radish varieties
    can be harvested through winter for crisp, peppery roots.
27. **Sorrel** (*Rumex acetosa*) — Perennial herb with a bright, lemony
    tang that persists through winter harvests.
28. **Tatsoi** (*Brassica rapa* var. *narinosa*) — Spoon-shaped leaves form
    a compact rosette that tolerates extreme cold and regrows after cutting.
29. **Turnips** (*Brassica rapa* subsp. *rapa*) — Both roots and greens are
    harvestable. Small, quick-maturing varieties perform best for winter.
30. **Watercress** (*[[nasturtium-officinale]]*) — Semi-aquatic perennial that
    continues producing peppery leaves in the cool, moist greenhouse
    environment through winter.

## How Cold Improves Flavor

One of the most remarkable aspects of cold-hardy winter vegetables is the way
freezing temperatures enhance their eating quality. When temperatures drop
below freezing, these plants convert stored starches into sugars through a
biochemical process that functions as a natural antifreeze. This sugar
production serves the plant by lowering the freezing point of its cell fluids,
preventing ice crystal damage to tender tissues. For the grower and eater, the
result is noticeably sweeter, more flavorful greens and roots. Carrots
harvested in January from a cold greenhouse taste markedly sweeter than those
harvested in September. Kale, collards, and Brussels sprouts similarly develop
a richer, less bitter flavor profile after exposure to frost.

## Lower Light Requirements

Cold-hardy vegetables have fundamentally different light requirements than
warm-season crops. Tomatoes and peppers require high light intensity and long
day lengths to photosynthesize efficiently and produce fruit. Winter greens, by
contrast, have evolved to grow and photosynthesize under the low light
conditions of autumn and winter. They do not require the intense solar energy
of summer and continue slow but steady growth even on overcast days with as
few as eight to ten hours of weak daylight. This adaptation makes them ideally
suited to the cold greenhouse environment, where light levels are inevitably
reduced by the greenhouse covering and the shortness of winter days.

## Cut-and-Come-Again Harvesting

The primary harvesting method for winter greenhouse vegetables is cut-and-
come-again. Rather than harvesting the entire plant, the grower removes only
the outer leaves, allowing the center of the plant to continue growing and
producing new leaves. This technique is essential for winter production
because plant growth is slow during the short days of December and January.
By harvesting selectively rather than destructively, a single planting can
provide multiple harvests over a period of weeks or even months. Spinach,
lettuce, kale, chard, arugula, and most of the leafy greens in Coleman's
species list respond extremely well to this method. Root crops like carrots
and turnips are harvested individually as needed, while the remaining plants
continue to size up in the cold soil.
