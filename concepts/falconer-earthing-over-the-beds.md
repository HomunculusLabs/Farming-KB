# Falconer Earthing Over the Beds

Chapter XIII of William Falconer's *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* (1891)
covers the critical operation of casing (earthing over) spawned mushroom
beds with a coating of loam. Falconer considered this "an important
operation in mushroom-growing, and the one for which loam is
indispensable."

## Purpose of Casing

The loam casing served several essential functions. The [[falconer-mushroom-spawn-preparation]] spread through the manure and rose up into
the casing, where most young mushrooms developed and all found a firm
foothold. The loam contributed to their sustenance, protected the manure
and spawn from sudden temperature fluctuations, and preserved the bed
from undue wetting or drying.

## Timing of Casing

For fresh manure in good condition in snug cellars or closed mushroom
houses, Falconer recommended casing during the second week after spawning,
around the eighth or tenth day. For beds in open, airy sheds or other
buildings, casing was done earlier, around the fourth or fifth day.

Some growers feared that casing within three or four days of spawning
would raise bed heat and destroy spawn, but Falconer never found truth in
this theory when manure was well prepared. The real danger of early casing
was killing spawn by burying it too deep in damp material before it had
begun running through the manure.

Falconer conducted experiments comparing beds cased immediately, at four
days, seven days, ten days, and fourteen days after spawning, and found
no difference in results. The determining factors were manure condition,
spawn insertion depth, openness or closeness of the growing place, and
other cultural conditions. However, delaying past the fifteenth or
sixteenth day was injurious because applying the covering broke many
mycelium threads that had freely permeated the manure surface.

## Casing Depth

Opinions on proper casing depth varied widely, from three-quarters of an
inch to three inches. Falconer's own experience favored a heavy covering
of one and one-half to two inches. Thin coverings produced mushrooms that
came up all right but had less solid texture, and beds did not continue as
long in bearing. "Fogging off" was much more prevalent under thinly
covered beds, and a heavy casing allowed many more "pinheads" to develop
into full-sized mushrooms.

## Firming the Casing

Falconer was strongly in favor of packing the soil quite firm and had
never seen good mushrooms that could not come through well-firmed casing.
For flat beds on shelves and floors, slightly compacted casing might
suffice, but for alongside-of-walls, ridge, and rounded beds he always
used solidly compacted casings.

## Soil Preparation

The loam needed to be in medium dry, mellow condition, well broken up
with spade or digging fork, and freed from sticks, stones, big roots,
clods, and chunks of old manure. Sifting was "labor lost" for initial
casing, though useful for topdressing and filling holes. The soil should
be mellow but inclined to moist -- wet enough to spread evenly and be made
firm, but not so wet as to be clumsy.

## The Henderson Prejudice

Peter Henderson's *Gardening for Profit* had created widespread prejudice
against molding over beds immediately after spawning, based on Henderson's
own two-year failure to produce a single mushroom using this method.
Falconer noted that many excellent growers successfully spawned and molded
their beds the same day. The key was that modern practice barely covered
the spawn with manure (unlike the old method of inserting spawn three
inches deep and then adding two inches of loam), which was "enough to
destroy the most potent spawn."

## Green Sods Method

Mr. Henshaw used green sods about two inches thick, placed grass side
down and beaten firmly, believing they prevented young clusters from
"dampening off" as they sometimes did with loose soil. Falconer gave this
method repeated careful trials and found it had no advantages over common
fibrous loam. The sod merely rested upon the dung rather than uniting
with it, and any displacement broke the mycelium threads between manure
and sod, destroying immature mushrooms. Sods were also unsuitable for
rounded or ridge beds.
