## Aquifer Intake Areas and Limanim Runoff Traps

## Overview

Aquifer intake areas are the natural or assisted zones where surface water infiltrates into groundwater systems. Understanding their location, function, and vulnerability is essential for sustainable [[limanim]], these strategies form the backbone of desert water harvesting.

## Aquifer Intake Areas

### Where Intakes Occur

Open subsurface channels for deep water infiltration are found primarily in hill country, on ridges, plateaus, and detritus slopes. They occur as:

- **Loose gravel ridges** that allow rapid percolation
- **Shattered or soluble rocks** with fracture networks
- **Cappings of loose dune sands** acting as perched aquifers
- **Heavily forested ridges** and high slopes of porous materials such as limestone, free-draining soils, or fissured rock

From these intakes, water enters soil and rock strata and travels for many metres or kilometres to springs, rivers, or artesian basins. Most groundwater flow follows the dip of sediments and the general slope of the land, but aquifers can—unlike surface streams—flow uphill and can produce a head of water (usually 0.5 to 7 m) in bore pipes or piezometers.

### Perched Aquifers in Flat Country

On flatter terrain, large areas of sand act as perched aquifers above less permeable desert pavement or clay soils. Water from these seeps out of the dune base and can be used locally for tree establishment. Moist sands are typically found from about 2 m deep in dune systems.

### Threats to Intake Effectiveness

Aquifer intakes can become ineffective through:
- **Volcanic ash or mud deposits** that seal surface fractures
- **Fine dust from desert dust storms** filling crevices
- **Sand and dust blown uphill** from cultivated and overgrazed areas

Except for vulcanism, these effects can be minimised by stabilising the landscape with windbreaks and protected reserves. In all cases, **deforestation of slopes and ridges reduces the effectiveness of intakes.**

## Assisting Aquifer Recharge

### Practical Methods

Water infiltration into aquifers can be assisted by five primary methods:

1. **Planting trees on ridges and slopes:** Tree roots keep soil open and create preferential flow paths. In the long term, trees maintain infiltration capacity and reduce dust from loess and sand deposits in surrounding areas.

2. **Building deep interceptor drains on contour:** These should be placed near ridge tops and on plateaus to capture sheet flow before it is lost to evaporation or downstream salt flats.

3. **Ripping rock pavement or capstone areas:** Mechanical disruption of sealed rock surfaces allows water penetration into underlying fractures and aquifers.

4. **Leading surface drains to wells cut in shattered rock:** Concentrated flow into prepared wells accelerates recharge of productive aquifer zones.

5. **Creating banks of loose surface boulder or opening pits and swales in gravels:** These structures slow runoff and create detention basins that allow extended infiltration time.

## Infiltration Rates

The time required to infiltrate 25 mm of rain varies dramatically by soil type:

| Soil Type | Minimum Rate | Time for 25 mm |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| Sands and dust (loess) | 11-17 mm/hr | 2-3 hours |
| Clay loams | 4-11 mm/hr | 6-20 hours |
| Alkaline swelling clays | ~1 mm/hr | ~24 hours |

### Strategic Implications

These rates have significant design implications. If neutral or acidic sands can be spread approximately 0.5 m deep or more over alkaline clay pans, the result is an excellent surface for both infiltration and water retention. Such amended sites become good garden or tree establishment locations capable of storing up to 1 m of water for long periods.

This effect occurs naturally at the discharge end of some gullies or canyons. Another way to achieve it is by opening deep swales in alkaline soils in front of sands and gravels on the move, allowing them to fill naturally with wind- or water-deposited sand.

In many areas, people have buried layers of clay, plastic, thick colloidal green matter (gley), tar, or latex under deep sands of their gardens to prevent deep percolation losses. On a broad scale, drainage slits are left every 100-200 m in these systems. The key principle is holding water for a day or more so it can fully infiltrate.

## Israeli Limanim (Runoff Traps)

### Design Specifications

In gently-sloping uplands with well-developed valley runoff during rainy periods, and where loess soils enable deep infiltration, Israel developed limanim—essentially dry dams:

- **Size:** 0.1 to 0.5 hectares
- **Bottom:** Graded flat
- **Walls:** 1-2 m high, 2-5 m wide, little consolidated (dry construction)
- **Internal support:** Beneficial to support the inside base of the bank with stone
- **Spill sills:** Positioned at the ends of walls to spill water at 40-60 cm depth, flowing to the next limanim downslope
- **Catchment ratio:** 30-500 times the catchment area to the impoundment area

### Operational Principles

The more gently water can be led in—via low-slope diversion drains or as sheet flow—the less silting and sill erosion occurs. Trees are established directly in the impoundments. Soil is kept open by tillage or by preventing compaction by livestock. Catch-crop is possible in wet years.

### Advantages

Limanim are low-cost, require minimal maintenance, and can be constructed with basic earth-moving equipment. Their staged design allows water to flow from one trap to the next, maximizing the total area of infiltration across a slope. The system has been proven effective in the Negev Desert and similar arid environments.

## Slope Stabilisation for Infiltration

### Natural Infiltration Aids

Spinifex or porcupine-type grasses help stabilise slopes and increase infiltration by forming cross-slope sand dams as they age. The normally circular tussock form breaks open to create an open crescent with its horns pointing uphill. Detritus accumulates behind these crescent-shaped clumps, effectively creating natural swales. Burning destroys this effect and allows sudden slope erosion to occur.

### Engineered Infiltration Structures

Several engineered structures can be created to enhance infiltration:
- **Swales:** Most easily created on a large scale with basic machinery
- **[[vetiver-grass]] lines on contour greatly assist water infiltration and slope stability in a wide range of climates.
