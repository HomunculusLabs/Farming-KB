## Permaculture Principle 2: Catch and Store Energy

*"Make hay while the sun shines"*

We live in a world of unprecedented wealth from harvesting fossil fuel storages created over billions of years. We have used this wealth to increase harvest of renewable resources to an unsustainable degree. Most adverse impacts will show up as fossil fuels decline. In financial language, we have been consuming global capital in a manner that would bankrupt any business. We need to learn to save and reinvest wealth so our descendants might have a reasonable life.

## Energy Laws: The Foundation

Understanding energy laws was fundamental to the development of the [[permaculture]] concept. Energy (in diverse forms) is the driving force behind all natural and human systems.

### Entropy

Throughout the universe, energy spreads from centers of concentration to vacant regions, where it tends to remain dispersed and diluted. Energy of high quality degrades into lower-quality forms, reducing its power to do "work." This tendency to disorder and eventual death is called **entropy** — it affects every living and non-living system.

### Self-Organization and Energy Storage

Self-organizing systems (primarily living ones) can capture and transform a limited proportion of absorbed energy, holding it in storages of varying form and durability for self-maintenance, growth, and capturing more energy. This stored energy is generally **higher in quality** than the source from which it was derived and capable of driving a wider range of processes.

Living systems designed to optimize efficiency of energy transformation and storage tend to prevail through evolution. For living systems, available energy flows are mostly erratic, limited, and low in quality.

## The Energy Circuit Model

### Odum's Energy Circuit Language

Howard Odum's energy circuit language shows relationships between external energy sources, primary producers (plants), and consumers (animals) as an energy hierarchy or food chain:

- **Reducing** energy and mass contributed along the food chain
- **Increasing** power and value per unit energy and mass along the food chain
- **Essential** energy dissipation losses to the environmental heat sink
- **Feedbacks** of high quality energy to stimulate inflows

### Embodied Energy

All biological and mineral resources can be considered and measured as embodied energy. Tools, infrastructure, and technology all derive from primary energy sources. The more useful and durable forms into which we transform those sources are stores of high-quality energy — or, in ordinary language, **"real wealth."**

### The Disconnect

The energetic view of the world is taken as given by scientists. But because of disconnection between biophysical sciences and social sciences (especially economics), the energetic view has little impact on normal understandings of value and wealth.

## The Modern Pattern: Consumption Over Storage

### Loss of Storage Culture

In modern affluent societies, energy capture and storage have ceased to be major concerns. So long as people have money to buy, basic needs provision is left to farmers and miners. In extreme urban living:

- No food or fuel is stored in the house
- Purchasing power depends on credit, which depends on permanent employment
- Economic rationalism has led to decline of large-scale stores of food, fuels, materials, and spare parts

### The Y2K Lesson

The vulnerability of modern systems to slight disturbance was highlighted by Y2K concerns and the enormous cost of reactive prevention strategies. This vulnerability will increase as fossil energy declines.

## Sources of Energy for Local Self-Reliance

### Currently Under-Used Sources

Important sources of energy commonly available but poorly used:

| Source | Applications |
|--------|-------------|
| **Solar energy** | Drying wood and crops, [[passive-solar-design]], dryers, water heaters, photovoltaic panels |
| **Wind energy** | Pumping and power generation |
| **Biomass** | Sustainable management of trees and forests for fuel and construction |
| **Run-off water** | Irrigation, aquaculture, power generation |

### Traditional Energy Capture

Gardeners and farmers have for centuries captured food energy in seasonal crops, storing or preserving for later consumption. **Saved seed** was particularly potent — maintaining genetic and cultural lineage from ancestors to descendants.

## The Capital/Income Distinction

### Income vs. Capital

This principle distinguishes between:

- **Income** (Principle 3: Obtain a Yield) — energy for immediate needs
- **Capital** (Principle 2: Catch and Store Energy) — savings and investment to build natural and human capital

Most of modern society's wealth consumption has been capital consumption disguised as income. We have been drawing down natural capital (fossil fuels, topsoil, biodiversity, groundwater) and calling it economic growth.

### Rebuilding Natural Capital

Permaculture prioritizes using existing wealth (fossil energy) to rebuild natural capital — especially trees and forests — as proven storage of wealth to sustain humanity into a future with less fossil fuel. This is the opposite of the modern pattern of consuming capital and calling it income.

## Water as Energy

### The River System Models

Multiple models of river systems illustrate different perspectives on energy flow:

1. **Plumbing drainage model** — engineered, linear flow management
2. **Hydrographic model** — natural gradient-based flow patterns
3. **Ecographic model** — ecological relationships within drainage basins

These models illustrate how water (like all energy) flows through landscapes and can be captured, stored, and used at multiple points along its path.

## Practical Applications

### In Gardens and Farms

- **[[composting]]:** Capturing organic waste energy and storing as [[humus]]
- **Seed saving:** Capturing genetic information across seasons
- **Food preservation:** Drying, fermenting, canning, root cellaring seasonal abundance
- **Tree planting:** Long-term biomass and soil carbon storage
- **Water harvesting:** Swales, dams, tanks capturing rainfall runoff
- **Passive solar:** Building design that captures and stores solar heat

### In Buildings and Settlements

- **Passive solar design:** Capturing and storing heat in thermal mass
- **Insulation:** Reducing energy loss (entropy management)
- **Water tanks:** Storing rainwater for dry periods
- **Food storage:** Root cellars, pantries, preserving seasonal abundance

### In Communities

- **[[local-currencies]]:** Capturing and circulating economic energy locally
- **Tool libraries:** Sharing stored capital rather than each household owning everything
- **Community gardens:** Collective energy capture and food storage
- **Seed banks:** Community-level genetic capital storage

## The Temporal Dimension

### Seasonal Cycles

"Make hay while the sun shines" reminds us we have limited time to catch and store energy before seasonal or episodic abundance dissipates. Understanding seasonal, annual, and longer-term cycles is essential for effective energy capture.

### Long-Term Storage

The most valuable storages are those that:
- Are durable (last decades to centuries)
- Are self-maintaining (require minimal energy input)
- Increase in value over time (trees, soil, knowledge)
- Provide multiple benefits (multifunctional)

Trees are perhaps the ideal example: they capture solar energy, store it as wood and soil carbon, provide multiple yields, improve soil, harvest water, create microclimates, and become more valuable with age.

## Key Takeaways

1. Modern society has been consuming natural capital and calling it income — we are functionally bankrupt
2. Entropy is the universal tendency toward disorder; self-organizing systems counter it by capturing and storing energy
3. Stored energy is higher quality than source energy — capable of driving more diverse processes
4. The disconnect between biophysical energetics and economics prevents understanding of real wealth
