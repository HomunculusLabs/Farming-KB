# Viral Diseases on Cannabis

## Overview

Viruses represent one of the most difficult challenges in
cannabis cultivation because they cannot be cured once a plant
is infected. Cervantes describes viruses as still somewhat
mysterious: they act like living organisms in some instances
and nonliving chemicals in others. They must enter plants via
wounds and cannot penetrate intact plant surfaces on their own.

## How Viruses Spread

Viruses are spread by multiple vectors in the cannabis garden:

- Insects: [[cervantes-whitefly-pest-control]] are the
  worst viral vectors
- Mites: [[cervantes-homemade-pest-disease-sprays]] may also carry TMV, presenting a tradeoff between
pest control and disease risk.

## Why Viruses Cannot Be Cured

Once a plant is infected with a virus, there is nothing that
can be done to cure it. Viruses are not living organisms that
can be killed with fungicides or antibiotics. They are
sub-microscopic particles of genetic material (DNA or RNA)
wrapped in a protein coat that hijack plant cells. No spray,
drench, or treatment can remove them from plant tissue without
destroying the plant itself.

## Control and Prevention

Since cure is impossible, prevention is the only strategy:

- Always use fresh, sterile growing medium
- Disinfect tools before cutting foliage on different plants
  to prevent mechanical transmission
- Destroy all plants infected with virus immediately
- Remove and destroy infected plants entirely; do not compost
- Wash hands thoroughly before handling plants, especially
  after using tobacco products
- Control insect vectors (aphids, whiteflies, mites) that
  transmit viruses between plants
- Maintain clean grow room conditions
- Quarantine new plants before introducing them to the garden
- Avoid using tobacco-based sprays due to TMV risk

## The Importance of Sanitation

No biological controls exist for plant viruses, and no chemical
sprays are effective against them. This makes sanitation the
single most important factor in viral disease management. Every
cutting tool, every hand contact, and every insect that enters
the garden represents a potential virus transmission pathway.

Cervantes emphasizes that infected tools are a major
transmission route. Disinfecting pruners, scissors, and other
tools between plants with rubbing alcohol or a 10 percent
bleach solution is essential practice.
