# Peyote Ceremony

The peyote ceremony is a ritual practice centered on the consumption
of the
psychoactive cactus [[gottlieb-peyote-traditional-use-and-history]] of peyote, see that page.

## Historical Background

The ceremonial use of peyote extends back at least 5,700 years, based
on
archaeological evidence from the Cuatro Cienegas basin in Coahuila,
Mexico,
where peyote "buttons" (the dried crowns of the cactus) were found in
human
habitation sites dating to approximately 3700 B.C. Radiocarbon dating
of these
specimens confirmed their great antiquity.

The Aztecs and other Mesoamerican peoples knew peyote as peyotl and
used it
in religious ceremonies. Spanish chroniclers documented its use
shortly after
the conquest. The Spanish attempted to suppress peyote use,
classifying it as
a "diabolical weed." The Inquisition in Mexico in 1620 declared that
"the use
of Peyote or any other herb or root which does not belong to the
Christian
religion" was to be punished.

Despite this persecution, peyote ceremonies survived in remote areas
of
northern Mexico and eventually spread northward into the United
States during
the late nineteenth century, where they gave rise to the Native
American Church.

## The Peyote Cactus

Peyote (Lophophora williamsii) is a small, spineless cactus that
grows in the
Chihuahuan Desert. The crown or "button" is the part consumed, cut
from the
root and dried for use. The buttons are bitter-tasting and cause
nausea in
many users, an effect often considered a purifying aspect of the
ceremony.

The principal psychoactive compound is mescaline, a phenylethylamine
alkaloid
first isolated from peyote by [[entheogen-trichocereus-genus-mescaline-cacti]]. The cactus
contains
numerous other alkaloids, including hordenine, anhalonine,
anhalonidine,
lophophorine, and pellotine. The [[gottlieb-peyote-legal-status-and-conservation]]
of peyote is a complex and evolving issue.

The Church has successfully defended the legality of peyote use in
religious
ceremonies in the United States and Canada. The American Indian
Religious
Freedom Act of 1978 and subsequent legal decisions have provided legal
protections for peyote use by members of the Native American Church.

## Effects of Peyote

The mescaline in peyote produces characteristic effects:

- Brilliant, kaleidoscopic visual hallucinations with vivid colors
- Enhanced color perception and visual acuity
- Altered sense of time and space, with time seeming to slow down
- Deeply introspective and contemplative states
- Feelings of unity, connectedness, and love
- Physical sensations including nausea (often considered purifying)
- Enhanced auditory perception, particularly sensitivity to music and 
  singing

The experience typically lasts 10-14 hours. The ceremony's structure
--
including singing, prayer, and the communal setting -- provides a
supportive
framework for navigating the intense effects.
