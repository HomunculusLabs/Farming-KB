# Peyote Ceremony

The peyote ceremony is a ritual practice centered on the consumption
of the
psychoactive cactus Lophophora williamsii (peyote) for spiritual,
healing, and
divinatory purposes. Native to the Chihuahuan Desert of northern
Mexico and
southern Texas, peyote has been used in religious ceremonies for
thousands of
years. The ceremony is one of the most important and widespread
entheogen-culture|entheogenic
traditions in North America, with roots extending deep into
pre-Columbian
Mesoamerican culture. For more on the [[gottlieb-peyote-traditional-use-and-history]] of peyote, see that page.

## Historical Background

The ceremonial use of peyote extends back at least 5,700 years, based
on
archaeological evidence from the Cuatro Cienegas basin in Coahuila,
Mexico,
where peyote "buttons" (the dried crowns of the cactus) were found in
human
habitation sites dating to approximately 3700 B.C. Radiocarbon dating
of these
specimens confirmed their great antiquity.

The Aztecs and other Mesoamerican peoples knew peyote as peyotl and
used it
in religious ceremonies. Spanish chroniclers documented its use
shortly after
the conquest. The Spanish attempted to suppress peyote use,
classifying it as
a "diabolical weed." The Inquisition in Mexico in 1620 declared that
"the use
of Peyote or any other herb or root which does not belong to the
Christian
religion" was to be punished.

Despite this persecution, peyote ceremonies survived in remote areas
of
northern Mexico and eventually spread northward into the United
States during
the late nineteenth century, where they gave rise to the Native
American Church.

## The Peyote Cactus

Peyote (Lophophora williamsii) is a small, spineless cactus that
grows in the
Chihuahuan Desert. The crown or "button" is the part consumed, cut
from the
root and dried for use. The buttons are bitter-tasting and cause
nausea in
many users, an effect often considered a purifying aspect of the
ceremony.

The principal psychoactive compound is mescaline, a phenylethylamine
alkaloid
first isolated from peyote by Arthur Heffter in 1897. For more on mescaline-containing cacti, see [[entheogen-trichocereus-genus-mescaline-cacti]]. The cactus
contains
numerous other alkaloids, including hordenine, anhalonine,
anhalonidine,
lophophorine, and pellotine. The [[gottlieb-peyote-botany-and-mescaline-content]] of peyote
are covered in detail elsewhere.

## Structure of the Ceremony

The peyote ceremony typically follows a traditional format that
varies among
different tribal traditions but shares common elements:

1. **Preparation**: Participants fast and purify themselves before th
  e
   ceremony, often abstaining from food, water, salt, and sexual
activity
2. **Setting**: The ceremony is usually held in a tipi with a central
   fire
3. **Altar**: A crescent-shaped earthen altar is constructed at the c
  enter,
   with peyote buttons placed on a flat stone
4. **Opening prayers**: The ceremony begins with prayers and tobacco 
  offerings
5. **Distribution of peyote**: The peyote road chief distributes drie
  d
   buttons to participants
6. **Singing and drumming**: Traditional peyote songs are sung throug
  hout the
   night, accompanied by a water drum and rattle
7. **Midnight prayer**: A special prayer and additional peyote consum
  ption at
   midnight
8. **Morning water**: At dawn, water is passed and a final prayer is 
  offered
9. **Closing**: The ceremony concludes with a breakfast meal

The ceremony typically lasts all night, from evening until dawn.
Participants
consume between 4 and 20 or more peyote buttons, depending on
individual
tolerance and the desired intensity of the experience.

## The Native American Church

The Native American Church, founded in the late 1800s, incorporated
the peyote
ceremony into a formal religious institution. Today it is the most
widespread
indigenous religious organization in North America, with an estimated
250,000
to 400,000 members representing numerous tribes. The [[gottlieb-peyote-legal-status-and-conservation]]
of peyote is a complex and evolving issue.

The Church has successfully defended the legality of peyote use in
religious
ceremonies in the United States and Canada. The American Indian
Religious
Freedom Act of 1978 and subsequent legal decisions have provided legal
protections for peyote use by members of the Native American Church.

## Effects of Peyote

The mescaline in peyote produces characteristic effects:

- Brilliant, kaleidoscopic visual hallucinations with vivid colors
- Enhanced color perception and visual acuity
- Altered sense of time and space, with time seeming to slow down
- Deeply introspective and contemplative states
- Feelings of unity, connectedness, and love
- Physical sensations including nausea (often considered purifying)
- Enhanced auditory perception, particularly sensitivity to music and 
  singing

The experience typically lasts 10-14 hours. The ceremony's structure
--
including singing, prayer, and the communal setting -- provides a
supportive
framework for navigating the intense effects.
