# Sealing Leaky Dams

Sealing leaky dams is a critical skill in permaculture [[water-management]]. Earth dams that fail to hold water represent a significant loss of labour and resources. Multiple sealing techniques exist, ranging from low-cost biological methods to expensive engineered solutions. Earth storage remains the **cheapest, easiest, and most locally self-reliant** method of [[water-conservation]].

## Dam Efficiency Factors

Dam efficiency depends heavily on the **flatness of the area behind the wall**. The flattest possible site should be chosen for greatest storage efficiency.

- **V valleys** — natural convergence point for runoff, narrow and efficient
- **U valleys** — broader catchment, excellent for larger storage volumes
- **Plateaus** — flat areas that can be excavated for pondage with minimal wall construction
- **Fields** — gentle grades that allow broad, shallow dams with large surface area

The dam wall can curve outward or inward; both designs can be stable if properly constructed. When carefully built as diagrammed in standard references and provided with a **broad spillway**, walls should remain stable and safe indefinitely. Wall compaction is critical — poorly compacted walls are vulnerable to failure through seepage or structural collapse, making proper construction technique as important as site selection.

## Spillway Design

A properly designed spillway is essential for dam safety and longevity, controlling overflow and preventing erosion or overtopping of the dam wall.

- The spillway base should be **carefully surveyed at 1 metre below the crest** of the dam wall.
- A **broad spillway** is preferred to dissipate energy and reduce erosion.
- The spillway must handle peak flow from the catchment during storm events.
- Surfaces should be protected with grass, stone, or other erosion-resistant materials.

## Sealing Methods Overview

| Method | Cost | Permanence | Difficulty | Best For |
|--------|------|------------|------------|----------|
| Gley | Very Low | Permanent | Low | Small gravels, sand, soil |
| Bentonite | High | Variable | Medium | Loamy soils |
| Explosives | Low | Variable | High | Full dams with seepage |
| Clay | Medium-High | Permanent | Medium | Known leak areas |
| Membranes | Very High | Permanent | High | Critical dams |

## Gley Method

The **gley** method is the most accessible and cost-effective sealing technique. It uses biological processes to create a permanent impermeable seal through anaerobic fermentation. A layer of wet, green, sappy plant material sealed from air produces **bacterial slime that permanently seals** soil, sand, and small gravels.

**Materials:** Cattle manure (preferred), shredded sappy vegetation, and cover material (earth, cardboard, thick wet paper, plastic sheets, or rolled clay).

**Application Procedure:**
1. Prepare the dam floor and gently sloping sides (slope ratio **1:4**).
2. Lay a continuous layer of mashed, wet, green, sappy plant material **15–23 cm thick** over the base and sides.
3. Cover completely with an airtight seal.
4. Allow the material to **ferment anaerobically** over several weeks.
5. The resulting bacterial slime creates a permanent impermeable barrier.

**Variations:**
- **Cattle penning**: Pen cattle in a dry dam until the bottom becomes a manurial pug — trampling and manure create a natural seal.
- **Hay bale method**: Strew bales of green hay and manure on slightly leaking ponds to seal small seeps.
- **Green crop method**: Sow a green crop in a dry dam, spray irrigate, then graze cattle through it — root action, manure, and trampling combine to seal the soil.

**Advantages:** Extremely low cost, permanent seal, works on a wide range of soil types, applicable to existing leaking dams, and integrates with livestock management.

## Bentonite Method

Bentonite is a **slippery clay-powder derived from volcanic ash** that swells dramatically when wetted, creating an impermeable barrier.

- Rototill bentonite **5–7 cm deep** into loamy soils and roll or compact the surface.
- The bentonite swells on contact with water, filling pores and sealing the soil profile.
- **Expensive** relative to biological methods.
- **Does not always work** — effectiveness depends on soil type, salinity, and application quality.
- Best suited to loamy soils; less reliable in sandy or highly porous substrates.
- Can be washed away if the dam fills before the bentonite has fully set and swelled.

## Explosives Method

Dynamite charges compact the sides and base of full dams, collapsing porous layers and forcing material into voids.

- Use a **3–5 stick charge of dynamite** placed at strategic points along the dam sides.
- Only suitable for dams that are already full of water.
- **Dangerous if the dam wall is poorly compacted** — detonation can trigger structural failure.
- Requires specialist knowledge and appropriate permits; should only be a last resort.

## Clay Method

Imported or locally sourced clay spread and compacted over suspect areas creates a traditional impermeable lining.

- Spread clay **23–30 cm thick** over leak-prone areas and roll thoroughly.
- Ensure the clay layer extends well beyond the edges of the suspected seep.
- **Expensive if carted in** from off-site; most cost-effective when available locally.
- Provides a reliable, long-lasting seal when properly applied.
- Requires significant labour for spreading and compaction but minimal ongoing maintenance.

## Impermeable Membranes

Engineered membranes provide the most reliable seal but at the highest cost. Options include welded plastic sheeting, neoprene liners, and poured concrete.

- Only justified for **critical dams** where failure would be catastrophic or extremely costly.
- Suitable for municipal, agricultural, or industrial water supply dams.
- Enables **steeper bank angles**, reducing the land area required for a given storage volume.
- **Too expensive** for most permaculture and small-scale applications.
- Plastic and neoprene liners are vulnerable to puncture; concrete requires professional installation and maintenance.
- Reduces the self-reliance central to permaculture design.
- Vulnerable to UV degradation and physical damage if not properly protected with cover layers.

## Choosing a Sealing Method

1. **Scale and purpose** — Critical water supply or supplementary earth tank?
2. **Budget** — Gley costs almost nothing; membranes cost thousands.
3. **Soil type** — Sand and gravel respond well to gley; loams may suit bentonite.
4. **Available materials** — What is already on hand or locally obtainable?
5. **Time** — Gley requires weeks for fermentation; clay and membranes can be immediate.
6. **Risk tolerance** — Explosives carry structural risk; biological methods are inherently safe.

## Principles Summary

- Always prioritise **site selection and dam design** to minimise the need for sealing.
- Use the **lowest-cost, most locally appropriate** method first.
- **Gley** is the preferred permaculture approach — low cost, permanent, biologically integrated.
- Reserve expensive engineered solutions (membranes, imported clay) for critical applications only.
- A properly designed spillway is non-negotiable for dam safety and long-term stability.
- Earth storage, when properly sealed, is the most self-reliant form of water conservation available.
- Combining methods (e.g., gley base with clay reinforcement on high-risk areas) can optimise both cost and reliability.
