# Mushroom Spore Syringe Preparation and Inoculation

## Overview

Preparing [[query-spore-syringes-vs-liquid-culture]] and inoculating substrate bags are among the most
critical steps in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]], requiring careful attention to
[[cultivator-sterile-technique-and-agar-culture]]. The spore syringe is the bridge between a mushroom spore
print and a colonized substrate, and any contamination introduced during
these steps will likely result in failed grows. The boil-a-bag method
describes two inoculation approaches: a short needle method (through the
bag wall) and a long needle method (through the filter can).

## Equipment and Materials

Essential items for spore syringe preparation and inoculation include:

- **Sterile water**: Distilled or spring water, sterilized by pressure
  cooking at 15 PSI for 20 minutes. Approximately 80 mL per syringe.
- **Spore print**: A clean spore print collected from a mature mushroom
  cap, stored in a sterile glass jar with a loosened lid.
- **Syringe and needle**: A standard syringe (10–20 mL capacity) with a
  removable needle. For the long needle method, the original short needle
  can be replaced with a longer piece of thin stainless steel tubing.
- **Flame source**: A spirit burner or butane torch for flame sterilizing
  metal tools.
- **Alcohol**: Propyl (isopropyl) alcohol for surface disinfection.
- **Breathing mask**: A clean handkerchief or mask to prevent the
  operator's breath from contaminating the work area.
- **Metal tools**: Tweezers and scissors for handling spore caps. A flat-
  ended metal scraper for loosening spores from jar surfaces.
- **Cotton wool buds**: For applying alcohol to inoculation points.

## Spore Syringe Preparation

### Water Sterilization

The syringe and needle are connected, wrapped in tin foil, and placed in a
Pyrex jug filled with 80 mL of water. A tin foil lid covers the jug. The
entire assembly is pressure cooked at 15 PSI for 20 minutes. After
sterilization, the water must cool completely before use — hot water will
kill spores.

### Loading Spores into the Syringe

1. Wearing a breathing mask, open the sterile spore jar in a clean
   environment.
2. Spores may not readily mix with water initially. Use a flame-sterilized
   flat-ended metal scraper to loosen spores from the bottom and walls of
   the jar, dispersing them into the water.
3. Pass the syringe needle through a flame to sterilize it (this must be
   done every time before refilling or inoculating).
4. Draw the spore solution into the syringe.
5. Replace the lid on the spore jar immediately after each refill.

### Custom Long Needle

For the long needle method, the original short needle is removed from its
plastic hub using pliers and replaced with a thin piece of stainless steel
tubing available from model and craft shops. Heating the tubing tip before
insertion makes it easier to seat in the hub. The long needle allows
inoculation through the entire depth of the filter can.

## Inoculation Methods

### Short Needle Method (Through Bag Wall)

1. Swab both inoculation points on the bag with propyl alcohol using a
   cotton wool bud.
2. Flame sterilize the needle tip until red hot — this allows easy
   penetration of the bag plastic.
3. Insert the needle fully into the bag through the plastic wall below
   the filter level.
4. Release 5 cc of spore solution into each inoculation area (10 cc
   total per bag).
5. Swiftly roll pre-positioned tape down over the hole as the needle is
   withdrawn, sealing the puncture.

### Long Needle Method (Through Filter Can)

This method is described as much easier, quicker, and more trouble-free:

1. The filter can lid is lifted.
2. The flame-sterilized long needle is passed all the way through the
   filter can and into the substrate below.
3. 10 cc of spore solution is inoculated into the straw.
4. The needle is withdrawn and the filter can lid is closed. If the lid
   tends to lift, secure it with a small piece of tape.

## Contamination Indicators During Colonization

Monitoring inoculated bags for signs of contamination is essential:

- **Healthy mycelium**: White, fluffy, growing outward from inoculation
  points in a roughly circular pattern. Has a mild, pleasant mushroomy
  smell.
- **Bacterial contamination**: Slimy or wet-looking patches, often with a
  sour or rotten smell. May appear as a yellowish or brownish discoloration.
- **Mold contamination**: Colored growth (green, blue-green, black, or pink)
  that appears powdery or fuzzy. Trichoderma (green mold) is the most common
  contaminant in mushroom cultivation.
- **Yeast contamination**: Creamy or pinkish patches that may bubble slightly.
  Smells like bread or beer.

If contamination is detected early (affecting only a small area), the bag
can sometimes be salvaged by cutting out the contaminated section, though
this risks spreading spores. Generally, heavily contaminated bags should be
discarded or composted outdoors.

## Spore Print Collection

The cycle begins with taking spore prints from mature mushrooms:

1. Sterilize small glass jars with metal lids by placing them under an oven
   grill at half maximum heat for 30 minutes.
2. Allow jars to cool, then loosely place lids on them.
3. Using flame-sterilized tweezers and scissors, cut the cap from a mature
   mushroom and place it gills-down into the jar.
4. Replace the lid loosely (do not tighten — moisture must evaporate).
5. Leave for at least 24 hours for spores to settle on the jar bottom.
6. Remove the cap with sterile tweezers and leave the jar open in a clean,
  draft-free area for another 24 hours to dry the spores.
7. Tighten the lid firmly for storage. Spore prints can remain viable for
  months to years when stored cool and dry.

## Post-Inoculation Care

After inoculation, bags are placed in a cardboard box and kept in a warm
place for [[growing-gourmet-spore-germination-strain-isolation]] and mycelium growth. Ideal colonization
temperature varies by species but is typically 24–27°C (75–80°F) for most
cultivated species. Within a few days, visible white mycelium growth should
appear at inoculation points and spread throughout the substrate. Full
colonization typically takes 2–4 weeks depending on species, temperature,
and substrate volume.
