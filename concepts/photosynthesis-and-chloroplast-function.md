## Photosynthesis and Chloroplast Function

Chloroplasts are the sites where photosynthesis takes place -- the process that captures light energy and converts it into chemical energy stored in sugar molecules. They are the most familiar of the plastids and are shaped like tiny lenses (2 to 8 microns long), with an average plant cell containing 10 to 100 of them.

## Chloroplast Structure

Like mitochondria, chloroplasts have a double phospholipid membrane. The inner membrane is folded to add surface area for photosynthetic reactions. Inside each chloroplast are thylakoids -- membrane-bound, flattened, hollow discs stacked one on top of another.

Attached to these thylakoid discs are green, chlorophyll-filled antennae that absorb light. The area surrounded by the inner membrane is called the stroma, where the light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle) take place.

## Light Reactions

When light hits chlorophyll molecules, their electrons jump to a higher orbit, increasing their energy level. The souped-up electrons flow like electricity through the thylakoid membrane and accumulate in one membrane compartment.

When the electron concentration inside the thylakoid becomes greater than outside, thermodynamics takes over. Electrons can only escape by passing through a special channel protein called ATPase. The movement of electrons through this channel causes phosphates to be added to ADP molecules, creating ATP.

The electrons then receive a second dose of light and are picked up by NADP (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate). The ATP and NADPH created in the light reactions power the synthesis of glucose from CO2 in the Calvin cycle.

## The Calvin Cycle in Detail

The Calvin cycle (also called the light-independent reactions or dark reactions, though this is misleading since they occur in daylight) takes place in the stroma. The cycle begins when CO2 is captured by the enzyme ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, universally known as Rubisco.

Rubisco is the most abundant protein on Earth, comprising up to 50% of soluble leaf protein. It catalyzes the attachment of CO2 to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP), producing an unstable six-carbon compound that immediately splits into two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA).

Through a series of enzyme-mediated steps, ATP and NADPH from the light reactions are consumed to convert 3-PGA into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P). For every three turns of the cycle, one G3P molecule exits to become glucose or other carbohydrates, while the remaining five G3P molecules are recycled to regenerate RuBP.
