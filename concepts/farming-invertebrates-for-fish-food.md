## Farming Invertebrates for Fish Food

The cost of fish food is the dominant ongoing expense in aquaculture, typically accounting for 60–70% of total production costs in intensive systems. By cultivating invertebrate protein sources on-site or collecting them from adjacent land systems, fish farmers can achieve dramatic cost savings while producing higher-quality fish. The concept of the **food quotient (FQ)**—the weight of food given divided by the weight of fish gained—reveals why insect-based feeds are so valuable: insect larvae have an FQ of 1.8, compared with 48 for guinea grass, meaning insects convert to fish flesh roughly 27 times more efficiently than fibrous plant material.

## Fish Feeding Fundamentals

Fish maintain growth by eating approximately **1% of their body weight daily**. Maximum weight gain with minimum food waste occurs when fish are fed at **3% of body weight daily**. A practical rule of thumb is to feed out as much food as will be completely consumed within 15 minutes, checking regularly for waste and establishing growth curves from periodic sampling.

**Demand feeding systems**, where fish trigger their own food supply when hungry, offer advantages but typically necessitate pelleted commercial foods at considerable expense. Sub-samples of netted fish can be weighed and a weighed ration fed at 3% of total biomass, allowing for expected mortality of 10–12% in fingerlings and 40–60% in fry.

## Cockroaches, Mealworms, and Sowbugs

These common invertebrates are easily cultivated from food waste and organic materials:

### Cockroach Culture
Scatter food waste or flour, cover with leaves, and seed with cockroaches. Add leaves and starches periodically. In tropical areas, cockroach and millipede populations build rapidly. [[dano-gorsich]] on Moloka'i, Hawaii, developed a successful **cockroach mulch heap** using Hibiscus leaves. When the pile is half-turned every few days, cockroaches become accessible to ducks, whose manure then stimulates plankton growth in adjacent ponds.

### Sowbug (Woodlouse) Culture
A "sandwich" mound of boards, paper, leaves, and organic material breeds sowbugs and houses earwigs. These can be sieved and shaken out, or the mound demolished and rebuilt with ducks or chickens present to capture the invertebrates. Zostera (eel grass) serves as an excellent base material for sowbug colonies.

## Termite Culture

A perforated 200-litre drum or loose brick pit, covered and filled with paper, old wood, cardboard, and straw, then watered, will attract termites if they are present in the area. In cooler climates, sowbugs can be seeded instead. The pit is periodically dug out or sieved to harvest the insects for fish or poultry feed. Termites represent one of the most efficient converters of cellulose waste into animal protein.

## Plague Locusts

Up to the 4th or 5th instar, plague locusts form ground swarms that are flightless and can be collected by vacuuming, brushing, or "trawling" in grasslands using a side-towed bar and net. Frozen or dried, they provide ideal protein food (consumed by people in many cultures as well). Fermented and dried locusts can be dry-stored for extended periods.

**Standard light-and-fan floats** are available to attract night-flying insects and blow them down to the water surface. Yellow floats specifically attract grasshoppers toward ponds, where many fall short of their goal and are consumed by fish.

## Pasture Grubs

The larvae of Aphodius, Phyllophaga, and other beetles or moths occur at high densities—**10 tonnes per hectare is not uncommon**—in the top 3 cm of soil. Collection methods include:

1. **Skimming strips** of field, sieving, and floating grubs out of dust with salt solution
2. **Disc-plough lines** that turn up approximately 100 kg of grubs daily
3. **Light trapping** adult beetles using funnel-and-drum systems

At an FQ of 2–3, every hectare of pasture should support the production of 2–3 tonnes of trout or other high-value fish. This approach is compatible with existing pastoral operations carrying less than 0.5 tonnes of sheep per hectare—essentially a bonus protein crop from the same land.

## Snail Culture for Fish and Duck Food

A well-limed area planted with arum lily, Nasturtium, horseradish, Brassica seed, broad beans, and cucurbit vines, seeded with a few buckets of snails and watered occasionally, will develop a dense snail population over several years. These can be "fed off" directly to ducks or gathered and minced for fish food.

Key design elements for snail habitat:
- **Permanent harbours**: Clumps of arum or [[agapanthus]] every 10 m²
- **Food plants**: Nasturtium, horseradish, cucurbits, annual Brassicas, globe artichokes
- **Tropical snails**: The large tropical snail *Achatina* favours papaya over a ground cover of cucurbits and Nasturtium, with fleshy or mucilaginous harbour plants
- **Desert snails**: Can be collected from post tops in swales or grasses where they gather to avoid soil heat

Snails reach high densities under these managed conditions and represent a reliable, self-reproducing protein source requiring minimal maintenance.

## Zooplankton Culture

Water fleas, cyclops, ostracods, rotifers, and other zooplankton can be cultured in small ponds or tanks supplied with:
- Lettuce
- Potato slices
- Crushed sugar cane
- Manioc (cassava)
- Legume leaves

A shallow bay off the fry ponds, screened off for this purpose, allows plankton to swim out into the fry ponds naturally. Conditions in the enriched area may not suit fish directly but produce ample food organisms.

**Midge larvae and tubifex worms** in rich, shallow, organic ponds supply essential fry food, as do **brine shrimp (Artemia)** of salt pans. Artemia culture is one of the very few productive uses of saline inland ponds.

Blow flies will colonize waste meats or carcasses suspended over ponds, and near-putrid shallows supplied with kitchen sink water breed "gentles" (larvae of *Tubifera tenax* flies) in muddy bases at 1–2 cm depth.

## Aquatic Molluscs

Species of *Physa*, *Limnaea*, *Bythnia*, *Vivipara*, *Pisidium*, and *Sphaerium* occupy most alkaline waters and form a large part of the diet of many fish. They are easily cultivated on vegetation in organic ooze in shallows, on stream banks, or in dedicated pools.

## Worm Culture

Worm beds of any scale are invaluable fish food sources. Worms are collected by **flooding the beds** at intervals. With a source of hay, food scraps, or manure, worm-growing can become a major fish food production system. The hessian (burlap) cover of worm beds can be immersed in or suspended over ponds after flooding, transferring worms directly to the fish.

## Herbaceous Fish Foods

For herbivorous species, semi-rampant plants supply hardy and palatable food directly. Species such as **Nasturtium, [[tradescantia]], [[dolichos]], and [[comfrey]]** are excellent fish foods, particularly for Tilapia. In channel cultures, the banks themselves—when planted with such species—constitute a complete food supply, eliminating the need for any external feed inputs.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Prioritize invertebrate culture** as the primary strategy for reducing fish food costs
2. **Design food production areas** as integral parts of the pond system, not afterthoughts
3. **Convert waste streams** (food scraps, paper, cardboard, crop residues) into insect protein
4. **Use the food quotient** to evaluate feed efficiency—insects (FQ 1.8) vastly outperform grasses (FQ 48)
5. **Plan snail and worm habitats** alongside ponds as permanent infrastructure
6. **Integrate pasture grub harvesting** with existing agricultural operations for bonus protein yield
7. **Match invertebrate species** to local climate—termites in tropics, sowbugs in temperate zones
