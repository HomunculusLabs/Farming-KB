## The Aztec Mushroom Cult

So hallowed were the sacred fungi that the Aztecs called them Teonanacatl
("divine flesh") and used them only in the most holy ceremonies. Even though
mushrooms do not blossom, the Aztecs referred to them as "flower," and
Indians who still use them in religious rituals have endearing terms such as
"little flowers."

Dr. Francisco Hernandez, personal physician to the King of Spain, wrote that
three kinds of intoxicating mushrooms were worshiped. After describing a
lethal species, he stated that others "cause not death but madness that on
occasion is lasting, of which the symptom is a kind of uncontrolled laughter."
Some brought before the eyes "all kinds of visions, such as wars and the
likeness of demons." Yet others were "desired by princes for their fiestas and
banquets" and were sought "with nightlong vigils, awesome and terrifying."

## Spanish Suppression

When the Spaniards conquered Mexico, they were aghast to find natives
worshiping deities with the help of inebriating plants: Peyotl, Ololiuqui,
and Teonanacatl. The mushrooms were especially offensive to ecclesiastical
authorities, who set out to eradicate their use in religious practices. One
cleric wrote that the devil "enticed an Indian to eat the fungus" and another
depicted "the devil performing a dance upon a mushroom."

In 1656, a guide for missionaries argued against Indian idolatries including
mushroom ingestion and recommended their extirpation. For four centuries
nothing more was known of the mushroom cult, and it was even doubted that
mushrooms were used hallucinogenically in ceremony. The Church fathers had
done such a successful job of driving the cult into hiding through persecution
that no anthropologist or botanist had uncovered the religious use of these
mushrooms until the twentieth century.

## The Xochipilli Statue

A superb statue of Xochipilli, Aztec Prince of Flowers, from the early
sixteenth century, was discovered on the slopes of Mt. Popocatepetl. His face
is in ecstasy, as though seeing visions; his head slightly tilted as though
hearing voices. His body is engraved with stylized flowers identified as
sacred, mostly inebriating plants. The pedestal is decorated with
cross-sections of the caps of Psilocybe aztecorum, a hallucinogenic mushroom
known only from this volcano. Xochipilli thus represents not simply the
Prince of Flowers but the Prince of Inebriating Flowers.

## Wasson's Rediscovery

In the mid-twentieth century, R. Gordon Wasson became the first non-Indian to
witness the Mazatec mushroom ceremony. He experienced geometric patterns in
richest colors growing into architectural structures, "the stonework in
brilliant colors, gold and onyx and ebony, extending beyond the reach of
sight." His wife Valentina had earlier discovered the living mushroom cult in
1955 among the Mazatec of Oaxaca.

Albert Hofmann subsequently visited the shaman Maria Sabina in 1962 and
identified the active principles as psilocybine and psilocine.
