# Seed Germination Science and Laboratory Methods

**Source:** [[gardening-when-it-counts]] — [[solomon-seed-sprouting-conditions-laboratory-germination-technique]] testing is standardized, accurate, and reproducible:
two different labs testing samples from the same seed bag should produce
nearly identical results. The key is controlling moisture, temperature,
oxygen, and media sterility to match laboratory conditions as closely as
possible in the field.

## Laboratory Germination Protocol

Seed testing laboratories follow strict species-specific protocols that have
been refined over decades. Understanding these protocols reveals exactly what
seeds need to germinate successfully.

### Media Selection
Technicians use sterile media to eliminate variables from soil microorganisms.
The most common approach uses blotting paper disks slightly smaller than the
petri dish diameter. The paper is soaked and then squeezed to remove excess
water — not wrung hard, just enough that no water pools when tilted. Seeds
are spaced on the surface and the dish sealed to prevent moisture loss. Some
species require sterile soil mixes (sand and peat) in shallow containers.

### Temperature Control
Each species has an optimal germination temperature prescribed in the testing
protocol. Temperature is held constant, or in many cases follows a diurnal
cycle: 16 hours at a higher temperature and 8 hours at a lower temperature.
This mimics natural soil warming by the sun during the day and cooling at
night. The alternating regime improves germination for many species,
particularly those adapted to spring conditions.

### Duration Limits
The protocol specifies maximum days for germination. Seeds that emerge after
this cutoff are considered too weak to survive real field conditions. This is
a critical insight: the batch that germinates quickest also germinates at the
highest percentage. Slow, late-germinating seedlings are genetically inferior
and unlikely to produce vigorous plants in the garden.

## Field vs. Laboratory Germination

Field conditions never match laboratory precision. Real soil differs in
several important ways from the sterile, controlled lab environment:

1. **Soil biology**: Real soil teems with microorganisms, some hostile to
   seedlings. Damping-off fungi thrive in cool, wet field conditions but are
   absent from sterile lab media.

2. **Temperature variability**: Outdoor [[soil-temperature]] fluctuates and is
   often colder than the species optimum. Seeds sown too early sit in chilled
   soil where germination is slow and vulnerability to pathogens is high.

3. **Moisture instability**: Soil moisture in the field is never constant.
   Surface layers dry between rains, and deeper layers may be waterlogged.

4. **Physical impedance**: Soil crusts, clods, and compacted layers can
   physically block [[watering-and-seedling-emergence]] even when germination occurs.

Despite these differences, laboratory germination percentages correlate
linearly with field emergence. If two lots of cabbage seed show 95% and 78%
germination in the lab, the field emergence will be proportionally lower for
both, but the relationship between them remains consistent.

## The Germination Equation

Solomon distills successful field germination into a simple principle:

**Start with vigorous seeds, then create soil conditions that match laboratory
standards as closely as possible.**

Gardeners should focus on two controllable factors: [[query-how-to-prevent-and-treat-powdery-mildew-on-cannabis]] cucurbits
- **Weed stimulation**: Frequent surface moisture germinates weed seeds that
  compete with crop seedlings
- **Surface crusting**: Repeated wetting and drying creates a hard crust that
  physically blocks seedling emergence
- **Shallow roots**: Constant surface moisture discourages deep root
  development, producing seedlings vulnerable to later drought

The correct approach is to water deeply at planting, then wait. Seeds planted
at the proper depth in properly prepared soil will germinate from the moisture
already present in the soil. Only in genuinely arid conditions or when
planting during hot weather should supplementary watering be considered, and
even then it should be infrequent and deep.

## Practical Germination Techniques

### The Furrow Method
1. Create a firm seedbed by pressing the soil surface down with a rake or
   board to ensure good seed-to-soil contact and capillary moisture movement
2. Open a uniform furrow at the correct planting depth using a stick or hoe
   handle pressed into the firmed soil
3. Space seeds according to the species recommendation; sow 2-3 seeds per
   station if germination uncertainty exists
4. Draw soil back over the seeds and gently firm the surface
5. Water deeply once if the soil is dry at planting, then do not water again
   until seedlings have emerged

### Timing Is Everything
The single most common germination failure is sowing too early. Gardeners
eager to start the season plant before soil has warmed sufficiently, resulting
in slow germination, high losses to damping-off, and weak seedlings that never
catch up. Sowing two weeks later than the earliest possible date often
produces stronger plants that mature only days behind an early sowing, with
far fewer losses.

## Seed Vigor and Home Testing

### Home Germination Test
Place 10-20 seeds on moist paper towel in a sealed plastic bag. Keep at room
temperature (or species-specific optimum). Count germinated seeds after the
protocol period. Field emergence is typically 70-80% of lab germination under
good conditions.

### Viability Decline
Seed viability decreases over time. Cool, dry, dark storage maximizes seed
life — some seeds remain viable for 10+ years under ideal conditions. Each
10°F (5.5°C) increase in storage temperature roughly halves seed longevity.
A germination rate below 80% for most vegetables suggests declining vigor.

## Species Germination Temperature Ranges

- **Cool-season** (40-65°F / 4-18°C): lettuce, spinach, peas, brassicas,
  onions, carrots, parsnips — wait for 50°F minimum soil temperature
- **Warm-season** (65-85°F / 18-29°C): tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, beans,
  corn, cucurbits — never sow until soil is thoroughly warm
- **Heat-loving** (75-95°F / 24-35°C): [[growing-sweet-potatoes]], melons, okra — demand
  warm soil and are severely set back by cool conditions
