# Ingoldian Aquatic Fungi

Ingoldian fungi (also called [[terence-ingold-mycologist]],
who first described them in foam collected from a brook near his home in
Leicester in 1938, more than 300 species have since been identified worldwide,
generating over 1,000 scientific publications.

## Discovery

Ingold found these spores concentrated in foam that collected around half-
submerged branches and at the bottoms of waterfalls in "a little, alder-lined,
babbling brook" close to his home. The foam is produced by fatty substances
released from decaying leaves that have detergent-like properties and form
bubbles when water is churned. Cakes of foam trap and concentrate the same
kinds of spores all over the world, making foam an ideal sampling medium.

When Ingold presented his findings to the British Mycological Society, a
Cambridge scientist dismissed them as hairs from plant leaves, a verdict
history has not vindicated. Earlier reports of aquatic spores with long
appendages had appeared in the literature, but most biologists ignored them
when they appeared in water samples or misidentified the spores as protozoans.

The discovery stands comparison to the first scientific reports of elephants,
rhinoceroses, and other African mammals, or descriptions of marsupial faunas
of Australia and Madagascar. A wholly new type of fungus had been found, a
rich mine of species that had always, but until then invisibly, shaped their
spores in creeks and streams on every continent.

## Spore Morphology and Diversity

### Tetraradiate Spores
Star-shaped spores are formed by Actinospora and other genera. A hyphal apex
swells to form a central hub, from which four buds emerge and elongate into
arms. All arms extend simultaneously at the same rate until the mature spore
spans 0.5 millimeters in diameter. Despite this record size, individual spores
are easily visible with a hand lens or magnifying glass. This is the largest
spore formed by any fungus, a record inexplicably absent from the Guinness
Book of Records.

### Sigmoid Spores
Sigmoid spores are elongated S-shapes, sometimes twisted into extended
helices. They tumble through flowing water, exploring a large volume as they
rotate, increasing their probability of contacting a submerged surface. When
they encounter a target, sigmoid spores often roll sideways or somersault
end over end until a single firm attachment is made, then secrete adhesive at
a second point to prevent further disturbance.

### Compound Forms
Some Ingoldian species combine these features, producing spores that resemble
balloon animals created by party entertainers: multiple arms, curves, and
helices integrated into single structures. This morphological diversity

submerged leaf before settling into bottom sediment. However, meticulous
experiments demonstrated that spores with microscopic arms fall through water
at the same rate as spherical spores of similar mass.

Water is a viscous medium for microscopic particles, slowing descent to
