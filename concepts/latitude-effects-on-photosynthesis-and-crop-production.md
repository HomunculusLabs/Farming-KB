# Latitude Effects on Photosynthesis and Crop Production

Latitude profoundly influences [[photosynthetic-efficiency]], crop yields, and appropriate agricultural strategies. Contrary to common assumptions, high-latitude regions often provide superior conditions for biomass production during their growing season, while tropical regions face significant photosynthetic challenges from excessive light and heat.

## High Latitude Advantages

Despite the weak light and short growing season at high (sub-polar) latitudes, the very long summer days provide more than sufficient light for vigorous plant growth.

### Light Quantities by Latitude (July)

| Location | Latitude | Daily Radiation (Langleys) |
|----------|----------|---------------------------|
| Madras, India | 13°N | 440 |
| Fresno, California | 36°N | 680 |
| Fairbanks, Alaska | 64°N | 450 |

The average radiation in temperate areas is **1–5 times that of the tropics** (Chang, 1968).

### Coastal Moderation

These conditions are accentuated on or near coasts by moderate temperatures from convection of air over warm currents, as in Alaska and the northwest coastal regions of Europe. The generally lower temperatures suit photosynthesis, conferring a photosynthetic efficiency that enables considerable production of:

- Cereals
- Berries
- Tomatoes
- Potatoes
- Vegetable crops tolerant of short season/long day conditions

The often deep periglacial soils provide the basis for production of **gigantic lettuce, cabbage, spinaches, and root crops**.

### Successful High-Latitude Examples

Such conditions prevail in Alaska, Ireland, Scotland, and parts of Norway. Shelter and added nutrients from seaweeds and manures yield rich meadows and heavy vegetable production during long summer days. The small stone-walled fields of Ireland produce abundant sweet hay, root crops, and greens for storage during winter.

## Tropical Photosynthetic Challenges

Conversely, the ample light at low (equatorial) latitudes is inefficient due to extremely high temperatures. The excess light may mean that plants are **light saturated** — photosynthesis may actually decline in intense light, and the energy built into plants may be less in sunlight than in partial shade.

### The Shade Benefit in Tropics

Shade (down to a level of 20% sunlight) is of great benefit in tropical deserts and sunny equatorial climates. Trials of shadecloth with **50–70% light transmission** may greatly increase plant bulk and production, e.g., of sugar beet. This underscores the importance of **tree shade and shadecloth** in deserts and cleared-area tropics.

## Optimal Photosynthetic Temperatures

Temperatures above **25°C (77°F)** sharply decrease photosynthetic efficiency. The normal desert or equatorial condition of high light and temperature is very inefficient for plant material production.

| Region | Optimum Temperature |
|--------|-------------------|
| Arctic/high latitudes | 15°C (59°F) for adapted species and cultivars |
| General food plants | 20–24°C (68–75°F) |
| Above optimum | Sharp efficiency decline above 25°C (77°F) |

The tropics are noted for low production of crops that can also be grown in temperate areas. Light shade may be the essential component for increased tropical yields.

### Leaf Temperature and Light Quality

In bright sunlight, leaf temperatures often exceed air temperatures, so the **diffuse light of overcast or cloudy days** in high latitudes helps plant growth, especially after midday when temperatures would otherwise rise above optimum in direct sunlight.

## Carbon Dioxide Limitation

Photosynthetic efficiency is limited by the leaf's ability to obtain carbon dioxide or by low levels of available CO₂. At high light intensity, supplying carbon dioxide to the **saturation level of 0.13%** can produce a **2–3 times increase in photosynthetic rate**.

Carbon dioxide can be supplied by:
- **Composting** near crops
- **Housing animals in greenhouses** where light is more than sufficient

## Biomass Production: Latitude Comparison

The summer periods of high latitudes are **ideal for biomass production**, while equatorial regions evolve biomass mainly as a result of year-round (inefficient) growth and perennial crops.

The ideal of steady low light/low temperature conditions may be achieved below the closed forests of tropical mountains, but these sites are very limited in extent, and carbon dioxide concentration is also low there.

### Rice Yield Example

Rice yields **4–5 times better in temperate areas** than in tropical ones, although up to three crops per year in the tropics helps increase local yields over the year.

## Improving Tropical Yields Through Design

It is feasible to assist tropical crop yields by spacing **permeable-crowned trees** throughout crops to reduce both light and temperature:

- **Prosopis trees with millet crops** in India
- **Partially-shading taro** in Hawaii

Grass growth in temperate areas also increases with shelterbelt, which may reflect the warmer conditions and lack of mechanical wind damage provided by trees such as tagasaste. Trials of light-transmitting or thin-crowned palms and legume trees would quickly show results — there are many observations suggesting that (if water is sufficient) crops under leguminous trees do much better in the tropics than crops standing alone.

## The Adapted Species Problem

Part of the problem in the tropics (both for biomass production and nutrition) is that **non-adapted temperate crops are persistently grown there**. True tropical plants:

- Can stand much higher levels of light before saturation
- Can maintain photosynthesis at low (0.10%) carbon dioxide concentrations

## Active Climate Modification Through Design

Climate factors of a site should not be accepted as unchangeable, any more than treelessness or soil erosion state. By sensible placement of design components, myriad small differences in local climatic effects can be created on any site.

### Design Strategies

1. **Patterning** a site with trees, ponds, earth systems, or hedgerows actively moderates conditions for better climate or to eliminate local limiting factors
2. **Energy conversion** from incoming energy fluxes such as wind and sun can produce usable energy for the site
3. **Windbreak placement** moderates temperature extremes and wind damage
4. **Water bodies** moderate temperature through thermal mass
5. **Earthworks** (swales, bunds, terraces) modify air drainage, frost pockets, and moisture retention

### The Designer's Checklist for Climate

Key data to gather for any [[permaculture-design]]:

- Average rainfall, temperature, and wind speed/direction for the region
- General hardiness zone (based on temperature, with frost as limiting factor)
- Survey of existing plants, noting special circumstances around "marginal" species
- Flood locations, periodicity, rain intensity, temperature extremes, and seasonal rainfall patterns
- Total precipitation (snow, hail, rain, fog, condensation, and dew)
- Light availability, especially on foggy coasts where light becomes the limiting factor for flowering plants
- Continental climate indicators (greater temperature extremes expected)

## Summary

The interplay between latitude, light availability, temperature, and carbon dioxide concentration creates a complex but understandable framework for optimizing crop production. The key insight is that **more light is not always better** — the most productive conditions occur where moderate light coincides with optimal photosynthetic temperatures (15–24°C). High latitudes achieve this naturally during summer; tropical regions can approximate it through deliberate shading with permeable-crowned trees. In all cases, climate is a design variable, not a fixed constraint.
