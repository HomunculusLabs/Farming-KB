# Mycorrhizal Networks Explained

Updated: 2026-04-18

Mycorrhizae (from the Greek for "fungus-root") are symbiotic associations
between plant roots and specialized soil fungi. In return for [[solomon-root-exudates-and-allelopathy]] from the plant, mycorrhizal fungi seek out water and
nutrients and deliver them back to the host plant. The plant becomes dependent
on the fungi, and the fungi cannot live without the plant's exudates.

At least 90% of all plants form mycorrhizal relationships -- the actual
percentage is probably 95% or higher. These relationships began approximately
450 million years ago; plants started growing on land only after fungi entered
into relationships with aquatic plants. Without mycorrhizal fungi, plants do
not obtain the quantities and kinds of nutrients needed to perform at their
best.

## Two Types of Mycorrhizal Fungi

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi

Ectomycorrhizal fungi grow close to the surface of roots and can form dense
white webs around them. They associate primarily with hardwoods and conifers.
They do not penetrate the root cells but form a sheath around the root and
grow between root cells (the Hartig net).

### Endomycorrhizal Fungi (Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi)

Endomycorrhizal fungi actually penetrate and grow inside roots as well as
extend outward into the soil. They form specialized structures called
arbuscules within root cells where nutrient exchange occurs. Endomycorrhizal
fungi are preferred by most vegetables, annuals, grasses, shrubs, perennials,
and softwood trees.

## Root Surface Area Increase

Both types of mycorrhizal fungi can extend the reach and surface area of plant
roots dramatically. The effective surface area of a tree's roots can be
increased by a factor of 700 to 1000 times through mycorrhizal association.
The fungi use carbohydrates from the plant's exudates to extend outward into
the soil, pumping moisture and mining nutrients from places roots alone could
not access.

## Nutrient Delivery

Finding and transporting phosphorus is a major function of mycorrhizal fungi.
The acids they produce can unlock, retrieve, and transport chemically locked-up
phosphorus back to the host plant. Mycorrhizal fungi also free up copper,
calcium, magnesium, zinc, and iron. Any nutrient compounds not delivered to
the plant roots are locked up in the fungal body and released when the fungi
die and are decayed.

## The Wood Wide Web

Mycorrhizal fungi form intricate webs and sometimes carry water and nutrients
to the roots of different plants, not only the one from which they started.
This cross-plant nutrient sharing -- sometimes called the "common mycorrhizal
network" -- means a mycorrhizal fungus associated with one plant can
simultaneously help others.

## Threats to Mycorrhizal Fungi

All soil fungi are fragile. Key threats include:

- Soil compaction (crushes fungal tubes)
- Fungicides, pesticides, and inorganic fertilizers (suck cytoplasm from
  fungal bodies)
- Rototilling and double digging (break up hyphae)
- Air pollution, particularly nitrogenous substances (reduce fruiting bodies)
