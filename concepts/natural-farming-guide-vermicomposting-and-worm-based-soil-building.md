# Vermicomposting and Worm-Based Soil Building

Raising worms is no more difficult than [[natural-farming-guide-composting-methods-and-techniques]]. It is especially
valuable to urban dwellers and can even be done in an apartment. Worms need a
covered bin with holes in the sides and bottom. The bin can be lined with mesh
fabric to keep the small worms from crawling out. Set the bin on a drainage
tray because you will be watering it like a houseplant.

## Setting Up the Worm Bin

1. Fill the bin with a bedding material. You can use nice black soil, shredded
   newspapers, or a mixture of both.

2. Obtain your worms. You need the kind sold for fishing, not the kind probably
   already in your garden. These are called red wigglers, redworms, or branding
   worms. You will need about 0.5 pound (0.23 kg) of worms, or 500 regular
   sized worms per cubic foot (0.03 cubic meters).

3. The worms will eat their weight in kitchen scraps per day. Bury leftovers in
   the bedding after each meal and follow the same rules as for a compost pile:
   no meat or dairy products. Garlic and potato peelings are also a bad idea.
   They need a steady diet of crushed eggshells sprinkled on top.

4. Worms don't like cold temperatures. Keep them outside in the summer, but
   bring them in when nights start getting chilly.

## Harvesting Worm Castings

Whenever you take the lid off the bin, the worms will dive down to the bottom
because they hate the light. Every couple of months, open it up and put them in
some bright light. After ten minutes you can scrape off the top layer of
materials, which is all of the valuable worm castings great for the soil. When
you see worms, wait another 10 minutes and do it again. Repeat until you have
gotten it all, and fill the bin up with fresh bedding again.

Worm castings are among the finest organic fertilizers available. They contain
high levels of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and trace minerals in forms
readily available to plants. Unlike chemical fertilizers, worm castings release
nutrients slowly and won't burn plants even when applied generously.

## Worm Biology and Soil Health

Encouraging worms and other beneficial creatures to live and grow in the soil is
one of the best strategies for soil improvement. These are the best cultivators,
which do their own [[natural-farming-guide-composting-methods-and-techniques]] and [[natural-farming-guide-permaculture-design-for-small-holdings]]. Worms aerate the soil
as they move through it, creating channels that allow water and air to
penetrate deeper. Their digestive process enriches the material they consume,
converting organic waste into plant-available nutrients.

## Integration With [[sheet-mulching]]

Vermicomposting integrates well with [[natural-farming-guide-raised-bed-and-intensive-gardening]] systems. As mulch layers
break down on garden beds, worms naturally migrate into the decomposing material.
Annual plants can tolerate food scraps from the kitchen layered under the mulch
where worms will dispose of them immediately. The worm population in sheet-
mulched beds often becomes self-sustaining, reducing the need for external
compost inputs.

## Urban and Apartment Applications

Vermicomposting is ideal for urban environments where outdoor composting space
is limited. A well-managed worm bin produces minimal odor and can be kept in
a kitchen, garage, or balcony. The system processes food scraps quickly and
produces a steady supply of high-quality fertilizer for container plants,
balcony gardens, or community garden plots.

## Benefits Compared to Traditional Composting

Traditional composting requires turning piles, managing carbon-to-nitrogen
ratios, and maintaining temperatures. Vermicomposting simplifies this process
by letting the worms do the work. The resulting castings are more nutrient-
dense and biologically active than traditional compost. Worm castings also
contain beneficial microorganisms that suppress plant diseases and promote
root growth.

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

- **Fruit flies:** Bury food scraps completely under bedding to prevent flies
  from accessing waste. A layer of dry newspaper on top of the bedding helps.
- **Odor:** Foul smells indicate overfeeding or anaerobic conditions. Add more
  bedding material and reduce food input temporarily. Ensure drainage is working.
- **Worms escaping:** Worms may try to leave if conditions are too acidic, too
  wet, or too hot. Check pH with a simple test and add crushed eggshells if
  acidic. Ensure the bin is not in direct sunlight.
- **Slow decomposition:** Chopping food scraps into smaller pieces speeds up
  decomposition. Maintaining moisture at the level of a wrung-out sponge is
  ideal. Temperature between 55 and 77 degrees F (13 to 25 C) is optimal.

## Worm Tea and Liquid Fertilizer

Worm tea can be made by soaking worm castings in water. This liquid fertilizer
can be applied as a foliar spray or soil drench. It provides a quick boost of
nutrients and beneficial microorganisms to plants. Unlike compost tea made from
traditional compost, worm tea tends to have higher concentrations of plant-
available nutrients and a more diverse microbial community.

## Integration With Humanure Systems

Vermicomposting can be integrated with [[faires-composting-toilets-and-humanure]] systems. While
worms should not be added directly to fresh humanure, finished and cured human
compost can be fed to worms for additional processing. This creates a multi-
stage treatment system that produces exceptionally high-quality soil amendments
from all household organic waste streams.

## Seasonal Considerations

In cold climates, worm bins need protection during winter. Insulation with
straw bales, moving bins indoors, or using heating cables can keep worms
active year-round. In hot climates, bins should be kept in shade and monitored
for overheating. Worms are most productive at moderate temperatures and will
slow their reproduction and feeding during temperature extremes.

## Scaling Up

For larger operations, worm bins can be stacked in tiers or converted from
old bathtubs, refrigerators, or wooden boxes. Flow-through systems allow for
continuous harvesting of castings from the bottom while worms remain active in
the upper layers. Commercial vermicomposting operations can process tons of
organic waste annually, turning a disposal problem into a valuable soil
amendment product.
