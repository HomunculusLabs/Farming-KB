## Root Systems and Below-Ground Composting

## Overview

In biointensive farming, the third form of natural composting occurs beneath
the soil surface through the decay of roots, root hairs, and associated
microbial life-forms that remain after harvesting. This underground composting
system is one of the most important yet underappreciated mechanisms for
building long-term soil fertility.

## Three Forms of Composting in Nature

Nature composts in at least three ways:

1. **Manures** — Plant and animal foods composted inside an animal's body
   (including earthworms), then further aged outside the animal by the heat
   of fermentation
2. **Surface decay** — Animal and plant bodies that decay on top of and
   within the soil in nature and in compost piles
3. **Below-ground decay** — Roots, root hairs, and microbial life-forms that
   remain and decay beneath the soil surface after harvesting

## The Remarkable Root System of Cereal Rye

Cereal rye ([[secale-cereale]]) exemplifies the extraordinary below-ground
productivity that makes root-based composting so significant. In good soil,
a single rye plant grows:

- **3 miles of root hairs per day**
- **387 miles of roots per season**
- **6,603 miles of root hairs per season**

These roots grow to a depth of six feet, breaking up compacted subsoil
layers and bringing nutrients from deep in the soil profile up to the
surface zone where subsequent crops can access them.

## Roots as Compost Material

Roots often weigh about as much as the above-ground portions of the plants.
When crops are harvested and the above-ground biomass is removed, the root
mass remains in the soil to decompose. This provides several benefits:

**Soil structure improvement:**
- Root channels create pathways for water infiltration
- Decaying roots leave organic matter throughout the soil profile
- Deep roots break up compacted layers and hardpan

**Nutrient cycling:**
- Deep-rooted crops mine minerals from subsoil layers
- Upon decay, these minerals become available in the root zone
- [[root-exudates]] feed soil microorganisms throughout the soil profile

**Carbon sequestration:**
- Root carbon is more stable than surface-applied carbon
- Deep-rooted crops deposit carbon at depth where decomposition is slower
- This contributes to long-term soil organic matter accumulation

## The Special Relationship Between Roots and Soil

Roots have a special topsoil relationship with soil microbes and the soil
itself. Some observations suggest that compost in the form of plant roots
may be twice as good as surface plant compost, and compost made from early
plant material may be four times better than compost made from manure.

This superior quality likely stems from:
- The intimate contact between living roots and soil microorganisms
- The continuous exchange of organic compounds between roots and soil
- The fact that root decay occurs in situ, distributing organic matter
  throughout the soil profile rather than concentrating it at the surface
- Root exudates that stimulate specific microbial communities

## Root Hairs and Nutrient Uptake

Root hairs are tiny extensions of root epidermal cells that dramatically
increase the surface area available for nutrient and water absorption. They
are the primary interface between the plant and the soil solution.

Each root hair is surrounded by a halo of hydrogen ions produced by root
respiration. These hydrogen ions carry a positive electrical charge and
participate in cation exchange with humus particles, allowing the plant to
"choose" which nutrients it needs from the soil's available reserves.

## Implications for Biointensive Practice

The biointensive method maximizes below-ground composting through several
practices:

- **Close plant spacing** — More plants per unit area means more root mass
  per unit area, increasing the volume of organic material left in the soil
- **Deep-rooted crops in rotation** — Including cereal rye and other deep
  rooters in crop rotations ensures organic matter is distributed throughout
  the soil profile
- **Minimal soil disturbance** — Double-digging preserves root channels and
  soil structure rather than destroying them through repeated inversion
- **Carbon crops** — Dedication of growing area to carbon-producing crops
  (which produce large root systems) feeds the soil's organic matter reserves

## Earthworms as Below-Ground Composters

Earthworms are especially effective composters. Their castings are:
- 5 times richer in nitrogen than the soil they inhabit
- 2 times richer in exchangeable calcium
- 7 times richer in available phosphorus
- 11 times richer in available potassium

However, care must be taken to avoid overdependence on worm castings as a
fertilizer. The nutrients in castings are very available and can therefore
be more easily lost from the soil system than nutrients stabilized in humus.
