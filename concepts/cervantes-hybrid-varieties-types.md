# Hybrid Varieties and Population Types

Hybrid cannabis varieties form the backbone of the commercial seed
industry. Jorge Cervantes provides detailed coverage of hybrid types,
population structures, and breeding terminology in Chapter Sixteen
of the Marijuana Horticulture guide. Understanding the distinction
between different hybrid types and population classifications is
essential for both breeders developing new varieties and growers
selecting appropriate genetics for their cultivation goals.

## Hybrids Defined

Hybrids are the product of a cross between genetically unlike
parents. They retain their distinctive characteristics when
reproduced asexually through cloning, but fail to reproduce these
characters completely or reliably through [[fungal-life-cycles-sexual-reproduction-metamorphosis]]. The
development of hybrid cultivars involves creating or selecting
inbred lines, coupling selection with inbreeding for homozygosity,
evaluating inbreds for combining ability in hybrid combinations,
and multiplying selected inbred lines for hybrid [[cervantes-seed-production-storage]].

## Types of Hybrid Varieties

### F1 Hybrids

F1 hybrids are first-generation crosses between two unrelated,
true-breeding parental varieties. They are unique in that plants
grown from F1 seed are uniform in appearance and performance. The
primary benefit of F1 seed is [[cannabis-hybrid-vigor-and-phenotypes-clarke]], or heterosis, where
the progeny exceed the performance of both parental lines in growth
rate, yield, and environmental tolerance. Examples include Northern
Lights crosses and the classic Skunk number 1 lineage.

F1 seed production provides commercial advantages for breeders.
True-breeding parental seed can be easily reproduced by open
pollination, but most seed companies release only F1 hybrids.
This ensures customers must return to purchase seed for each new
planting and protects the breeder's investment by making it
difficult for competitors to reproduce and market their work.
Unfortunately, cannabis drug varieties cannot be protected by
plant breeder's rights legislation due to the plant's legal
status in most countries.

### Three-Way Crosses

Three-way crosses involve crossing an F1 hybrid with a third
unrelated variety. Skunk number 1 itself is a three-way cross
derived from Mexican and Colombian parents crossed with an Afghani.
The Blueberry variety is another well-known three-way cross. These
crosses combine traits from three distinct gene pools, often
producing offspring with complex and desirable combinations of
characteristics not found in any single parent.

### Double Cross Hybrids

Double cross hybrids are produced by crossing two unrelated F1
hybrids together. The classic Haze variety is a double cross of
(Afghani times Thai) crossed with (Mexican times Colombian). These
complex crosses incorporate genetics from four or more parent
populations, creating extremely diverse offspring. While individual
plants may vary considerably, the population as a whole can express
a remarkable range of desirable traits.

## Population Classifications

### Inbred Lines

Inbred lines are produced through repeated self-pollination or
sib-mating over multiple generations. This process increases
homozygosity, meaning that plants within the line are genetically
very similar. When inbred seed is planted, the resulting plants
are similar in most ways to the previous generation. Pure lines
are homozygous at most alleles, making them genetically stable
and predictable.

### Open-Pollinated Varieties

Open-pollinated varieties are non-hybrid populations reproduced
by random pollination within the variety. All pistillate
individuals have the potential to mate with all staminate
individuals as pollen spreads randomly through wind, preserving
genetic diversity within the breeding population. To maintain
varietal purity, hemp breeders ensure no non-varietal pollen
source exists within four miles upwind and one mile downwind,
demonstrating how far cannabis pollen can travel.

### Heirloom Varieties

Heirloom varieties are the product of many years of selective
planting and [[comparison-heirloom-vegetable-varieties-vs-seed-saving-techniques]]. The original seeds bore plants with
particular traits the grower valued, typically flavor, color, or
psychoactive effect. Seeds from desirable plants were saved and
replanted across generations, often passed within families or
shared among friends. Heirloom varieties are non-hybrid and
breed relatively true, meaning growers can [[query-how-do-i-save-seeds-from-biennial-vegetables]] and
expect offspring similar to the parent generation.

### Multi-Line Varieties

Multi-line varieties consist of two or more pure-breeding lines
that are very similar but differ in specific phenotypic traits
such as maturation time or disease resistance. These lines are
grown together and allowed to interpollinate, creating a
population that maintains genetic diversity while preserving
overall uniformity. This approach provides natural disease
resistance through genetic variation.

### Synthetic Varieties

Synthetic varieties are interbreeding populations derived from
crossing several selected lines or clones. They are maintained
by open pollination and selected for specific performance
characteristics over multiple generations. Synthetic varieties
offer a balance between the uniformity of inbred lines and the
genetic diversity of open-pollinated populations.

## Strain Versus Variety

Cervantes clarifies that "strain" is not a scientifically
accepted botanical term. In the cannabis industry, it is
incorrectly applied to selections of cultivars or varieties.
Very few commercially available seed lots qualify as true
varieties or cultivars because they are neither uniform nor
breed true. Many commercially sold seeds are simply hybrids of
hybrids with names attached and no defining characteristics.
Terms like "family" or "group" may be more appropriate for most
commercial cannabis offerings.
