# Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection, and Radiation

## Overview

Heat always flows from warmer to colder bodies. Understanding the three mechanisms of heat transfer — conduction, convection, and radiation — is essential for designing energy-efficient buildings, productive gardens, and resilient agricultural systems. Each mechanism operates differently, and permaculture designers use all three to capture, store, direct, and conserve heat across their systems.

## The Three Grades of Heat Transfer

### Low-Grade Heat: Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of heat through direct contact between solids, or between fluids in contact with solids. Heat moves from molecule to molecule through a material without any bulk movement of the material itself. This is the most efficient form of heat transfer for building heating.

**Key characteristics:**
- Heat is conducted from solid to solid, or fluid to fluid, by contact
- Insulation is highly effective at trapping conducted heat
- Dense materials (stone, concrete, metal) are good conductors
- Light, porous materials (cork, sawdust, wood fiber, straw) are poor conductors and good insulators

**Applications in design:**
- **Slab floor heating**: Hot water pipes buried in concrete conduct heat across the entire floor surface, providing even, efficient space heating. The floor must be insulated from surrounding earth to prevent heat loss downward.
- **Earth-sheltered buildings**: The thermal mass of surrounding earth conducts heat slowly, moderating interior temperatures
- **Heat storage in rock beds**: Solar-heated air is ducted through beds of stone, which absorb and store the heat by conduction
- **Mulch as insulation**: Organic mulches trap air and create a poor-conducting layer over soil, reducing heat loss

### Medium-Grade Heat: Convection

Convection is heat transfer through the movement of fluids (air or water). When a fluid is heated, it expands, decreases in density, and rises. Cooler, denser fluid sinks to replace it, creating a circulation pattern called a **convection current**.

**Key characteristics:**
- Heated fluids rise; cooled fluids sink
- Draught-proofing conserves convective heat in buildings
- Air convection is the main driver of atmospheric circulation globally
- Convection is not an efficient way to heat building interiors directly (heated air stratifies below ceilings in still rooms)

**Convection loops and thermosiphons:**

When heated air or water is contained in pipes or ducts, a closed-loop circulation can be established by applying heat to the lower part of the loop. This requires a minimum height difference of about 40 cm (18 inches) between the heated section and the top of the loop. Any greater height also works.

The thermosiphon principle is used for:
- Transferring heat from solar ponds or flat-plate collectors to home radiators or hot water tanks
- Refrigerators driven by heat sources (absorption refrigeration)
- Passive [[solar-water-heating]] systems
- Cooling buildings with solar chimneys that draw cool air through lower openings

**Atmospheric convection:**
Columns of heated air over land ascend as "Overbeck jets." At altitude, the air cools, causing condensation and rainfall. These convectional rains create the mosaic of rainfall patterns across deserts — some areas receive rain while nearby areas remain dry, depending on where convection cells form.

**Valley winds:** Cool air is denser than warm air. At night, cool air drains downslope into valleys, creating predictable valley breezes. During the day, heated air rises from slopes, creating upslope winds. These daily convection patterns are highly reliable and can be used for passive ventilation design.

**Temperature inversions:** Normally, temperature decreases with altitude. However, in valleys and basins, dense cold air from hills or rapid radiation cooling from soils can become trapped below warmer air, creating a temperature inversion. These inversions trap fog, smog, and pollutants, creating health hazards and reducing solar access. Sites prone to inversions require careful analysis for pollutant sources.

### High-Grade Heat: Radiation

Radiation is the transfer of heat across space by electromagnetic waves. It requires no medium — radiation travels through vacuum, which is how the sun heats Earth. Radiant heat travels in straight lines in all directions from the source.

**Key characteristics:**
- Crosses space without needing a medium (the mechanism of solar heating)
- Non-directional; focused radiation can produce intense local heat
- Heats solids and liquids effectively but has little effect on air temperature
- Can only be conserved by reflective, dust-free surfaces or mirrors
- Glass transmits short-wave (visible) light but blocks long-wave (heat) radiation, creating a greenhouse effect

**Applications in design:**
- **Radiant heaters**: Electric, gas, or wood-fired massive stoves warm people and objects directly, even in draughty or cool rooms. They are very efficient space heaters because they heat occupants rather than air.
- **Solar collectors**: Flat-plate collectors capture radiant solar energy for water and space heating
- **Greenhouse effect**: Glass transmits short-wave radiation in but blocks long-wave radiation out, trapping heat
- **Reflective insulation**: Free-hanging reflective sheets in air spaces bounce radiant heat back, but only work if kept clean and not pressed against conducting surfaces

## Heat Storage Principles

### Specific Heat

Different materials store different amounts of heat per unit of mass. This property is called specific heat:
- **Water and stone** have high specific heat — excellent heat storage
- **Air** has low specific heat — poor heat storage but good insulation
- **Earth and concrete** have moderate specific heat — useful thermal mass

This means bodies of water are excellent heat buffers for nearby microclimates, and massive stone or concrete walls can store daytime heat for nighttime release.

### Insulation Materials

Effective insulation relies on trapping air in small pockets:
- Solid foam boards
- Straw bales
- Cork, sawdust, wood fiber
- Reflective foil (must be kept clean and free-hanging, not compressed against surfaces)

**Reflective insulation** is most effective in clean environments like solar attics. Plain white paint serves as an excellent everyday reflector for walls and solar concentrators.

## Plants as Heaters

Remarkably, some plants actively generate heat through metabolic processes. Several Arum lilies and species like *[[philodendron-selloum]]* store fats that are metabolized to produce heat. Philodendrons can reach 46 deg C (115 deg F) when ambient air is only 4 deg C (39 deg F). Crocuses heat to 15 deg C (27 deg F) above ambient.

The most extraordinary example is [[skunk-cabbage]] (*[[symplocarpus-foetidus]]*), which:
- Generates 20-25 deg C (36-45 deg F) of extra heat above ambient
- Uses heat to melt through spring snow cover
- Provides a warm incubator for early growth
- Scatters odorous scents to attract pollinating flies
- Has a leaf shape that creates a vortex from wind, carrying pollen down to lower flowers without insects

These "heater plants" can be used strategically as understory clumps to assist frost-tender, fly-pollinated, or heat-starved plants in cold microclimates.

## Design Applications

### Maximizing Solar Heat Gain

In subtropical to cool climates:
- Site houses on sun-facing slopes in the [[thermal-belt]]
- Use large windows on the sun-facing side to capture radiant heat
- Store heat in insulated slab floors or internal thermal mass
- Install flat-plate collectors for water heating

### Conserving Heat in Buildings

- Draught-proof to prevent convective heat loss
- Insulate thermal mass from surrounding earth (foam, straw)
- Use double-glazed windows to reduce radiant heat loss
- Hang reflective insulation loosely in ceiling spaces
- Use thermosiphon loops for passive heat distribution

### Cooling Strategies

In hot climates:
- Shade buildings from direct radiation
- Use cross-ventilation driven by solar chimneys
- Reflect heat with light-colored surfaces and white paint
- Use wind-carried (advected) cool air from shaded or watered areas

### Heat Exchange

Where warm and cool air streams must interact (such as ventilation systems), heat exchangers can transfer heat from outgoing warm air to incoming cool air, recovering energy that would otherwise be lost. Combined with thermosiphon-driven ventilation, these systems dramatically reduce heating and cooling costs.

## Key Principles

1. **Heat flows from warm to cold** — design to capture and direct this flow
2. **Conduction** is the most efficient method for heating buildings (slab floors, radiant systems)
3. **Convection** drives atmospheric circulation and can be harnessed through thermosiphons and solar chimneys
4. **Radiation** is the most powerful form of heat transfer and crosses space without a medium
5. **Mass stores heat** — water, stone, and earth moderate temperature extremes
6. **Air insulates** — trapped air is the basis of most insulation systems
7. **Glass creates a greenhouse** by transmitting light but trapping radiant heat
8. **A 40 cm minimum height difference** is needed for effective thermosiphon circulation
9. **Reflective insulation only works when clean and free-hanging** — dust and contact with surfaces eliminate effectiveness
