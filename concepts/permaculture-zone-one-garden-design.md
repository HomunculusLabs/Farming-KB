# Permaculture Zone One Garden Design

**Zone One** is the innermost zone in [[permaculture-design]], defined as the area of most frequent human presence surrounding the dwelling. Typically extending 20 to 30 feet from the house, this zone forms the productive and interactive core of any permaculture system. It receives the most frequent visits, the most intensive management, and houses the elements that require the greatest attention — culinary herbs, salad greens, small-scale animal systems, and water features integrated into daily domestic life.

## Scope and Definition

Zone One represents the concentric ring of highest-intensity use in the permaculture zonation model developed by [[bill-mollison]] and [[david-holmgren]]. The zone system is a spatial planning tool that allocates elements based on the frequency of human visitation and the degree of care they require. Zone One, by definition, contains everything that must be visited daily or multiple times per day: culinary herbs, salad vegetables, seedlings, propagation areas, and small livestock. Its proximity to the dwelling ensures that management tasks — watering, harvesting, pest observation — are embedded within the rhythms of domestic life rather than treated as separate chores.

The 20 to 30 foot radius is not rigid; it expands or contracts based on site conditions, household size, and climate. In cold climates, the zone may shrink to maintain easy winter access, while in tropical settings it may extend further. The governing principle is always frequency of use, not fixed distance.

## Soil Preparation and Gardening Techniques

Zone One beds are prepared for high production using intensive methods. Mollison advocates **mulch gardening** as a primary technique, layering organic material over the soil surface to suppress weeds, retain moisture, and feed soil biology. Mulch eliminates the need for tillage once established and mirrors the natural litter layer of forest floors.

**Triple digging** is another recommended soil preparation method. This intensive technique involves removing the topsoil to the depth of a spade, loosening the subsoil beneath with a fork, then replacing the topsoil with added [[coleman-compost-and-organic-fertility-management]] matter. Triple digging creates a deep, well-aerated root zone that supports high planting densities and rapid plant growth — essential for the productivity expected of Zone One.

## Plant Placement and the Parsley Principle

The arrangement of plants within Zone One follows a frequency-of-harvest gradient. **Plucking plants** — those harvested incrementally over a long season — are placed closest to paths and doorways. Parsley, broccoli, herbs, and salad greens fall into this category; they are snipped or picked regularly, sometimes daily. Their position along the edge of beds ensures that the gardener encounters them during routine passage.

**Harvest-once plants**, by contrast, are positioned further back in the bed. Root crops, heading brassicas, and plants harvested in a single operation require less frequent attention and can be placed where they will not obstruct access to daily-harvest crops.

This spatial logic embodies what Mollison calls the **parsley principle**: *if it is under your eye, you harvest it.* Plants placed where they are seen and passed regularly receive care and harvest far more reliably than those hidden at the back of a distant garden. Visibility is a design parameter, not merely an aesthetic consideration.

## Water Features and Biological Pest Control

Zone One often incorporates **small ponds** positioned near the dwelling. These serve multiple functions: water storage, microclimate moderation, aesthetic value, and habitat for biological pest control agents.

Ponds stocked with **watercress** (*[[nasturtium-officinale]]*) provide edible aquatic greens while filtering and oxygenating the water. More critically, they support frog populations that control insect pests across the garden. Mollison describes a layered approach to tadpole management in which different frog species occupy distinct **stratification niches** within the pond:

- **Tree frogs** occupy the surface zone, laying eggs on emergent vegetation and feeding on surface insects.
- **Ground frogs** inhabit the bottom zone, with different breeding cycles and prey preferences.

This stratification ensures continuous frog activity across seasons and vertical pond zones, providing multi-layered pest control without chemical inputs. The pond becomes a self-regulating biological system integrated into the garden's daily use area.

## Border Plants: Windbreaks, Weed Barriers, and Fire Protection

The perimeter of Zone One is typically defined by a band of **border plants** that serve protective functions. These plants form windbreaks that reduce wind stress on tender garden crops, act as weed barriers that suppress grass and weed ingress from surrounding zones, and in fire-prone landscapes provide a degree of fire protection through their green, moisture-retaining biomass.

Key Zone One barrier species include:

- **[[comparison-jerusalem-artichoke-vs-potato]]** (*[[helianthus-tuberosus]]*): A tall, rhizomatous sunflower that forms dense screens, produces edible tubers, and tolerates a wide range of soils. Its aggressive spread makes it an effective edge species where containment is desired.
- **Siberian pea tree** (*[[caragana-arborescens]]*): A nitrogen-fixing shrub that serves as a windbreak, provides edible seeds, and fixes atmospheric nitrogen into the soil through its root-associated rhizobia.
- **Comfrey** (*[[comfrey-symphytum-officinale]]*): A deep-rooted perennial whose leaves accumulate potassium, calcium, and other minerals from subsoil layers. Comfrey functions as a dynamic accumulator, a mulch crop, and a barrier against grass encroachment.

These species are chosen for their multifunctionality — each performs at least three roles simultaneously, consistent with the permaculture principle that every element should serve multiple functions.

## Fencing Strategies

Zone One fencing must address threats from multiple directions and at multiple scales. Mollison describes a **layered fencing strategy** consisting of:

1. **Netted fence**: An above-ground mesh barrier that excludes rabbits, poultry, and medium-sized mammals from garden beds.
2. **Electric wire**: One or more strands of electrified wire offset from the netted fence to deter larger animals such as deer, dogs, and livestock.
3. **Underground layer**: Wire mesh or hardware cloth buried at the fence base to prevent burrowing animals — rabbits, gophers, and ground squirrels — from entering from below.

This three-tiered approach recognizes that pest pressure arrives at different heights and through different behaviors. A fence that excludes above-ground foragers is useless if animals tunnel beneath it, and vice versa. The integrated design addresses the full range of access vectors.

## Greenhouse Integration

[[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]], **greenhouses** are ideally connected directly to the kitchen or dwelling. This integration — sometimes called a **lean-to greenhouse** or **solarium** — provides several advantages:

- Passive solar heat gain reduces household heating energy.
- The gardener accesses the greenhouse without going outside, extending the growing season for herbs and salad greens through winter.
- Kitchen scraps are easily transferred to greenhouse compost systems.
- Humidity from the greenhouse moderates indoor air quality.

The greenhouse becomes an extension of Zone One rather than a separate structure, blurring the boundary between domestic interior and productive garden.

## Small Animal Integration

Zone One can accommodate **small animals** that contribute to the system's productivity. Mollison specifically mentions:

- **Quail**: Small, ground-dwelling birds that produce eggs, control insects, and generate manure for garden beds. Their small scale suits the intensive management of Zone One.
- **Turtles**: Aquatic or semi-aquatic reptiles that inhabit ponds and water features, consuming pest insects and snails.
- **Geckos**: In tropical climates, geckos provide nocturnal insect control within and around the dwelling, reducing mosquito and fly populations.

These animals are selected for their scale — small enough to manage within the intensive Zone One area, productive enough to justify their space and feed requirements, and compatible with the human activity patterns that define the zone.

## Summary of Design Principles

Zone One garden design is governed by several interlocking principles:

- **Frequency of use determines placement**: Daily-harvest crops at hand, occasional-harvest crops further away.
- **Visibility drives management**: The parsley principle — seen plants are tended plants.
- **Every element serves multiple functions**: Border plants provide windbreak, fertility, and fire protection simultaneously.
- **Biological systems replace chemical inputs**: Frog ponds for pest control, nitrogen-fixing shrubs for fertility.
- **Integration with the dwelling**: Greenhouses, animal systems, and water features connect garden production to daily domestic life.

Together, these principles make Zone One the most productive, most intensively managed, and most personally connected zone in the permaculture landscape.
