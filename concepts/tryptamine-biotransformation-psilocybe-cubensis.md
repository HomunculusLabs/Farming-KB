# Tryptamine Biotransformation in *Psilocybe cubensis*

## Overview

In 1989, [[jochen-gartz]] published a landmark paper in *Planta Medica* demonstrating that
mycelial cultures of *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]* could convert exogenously supplied tryptamine
into psilocin at remarkably high concentrations — up to 3.3% of the dry mass of fruit
bodies. This revealed the extraordinary hydroxylation and methylation capacity of the
enzymatic machinery in *P. cubensis* and opened new avenues for understanding fungal
biosynthesis of psychedelic tryptamines.

## Biosynthetic Pathway

The natural biosynthesis of psilocybin and psilocin proceeds through a well-characterized
enzymatic cascade:

1. **Tryptophan → Tryptamine** — Decarboxylation via aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC).
2. **Tryptamine → 4-Hydroxytryptamine** — Hydroxylation at the indole 4-position by a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase.
3. **4-Hydroxytryptamine → Psilocin** — N,N-dimethylation by an [[s-adenosylmethionine]] (SAM)-dependent N-methyltransferase.
4. **Psilocin → Psilocybin** — O-phosphorylation by a kinase, producing the stable phosphate ester (4-PO-HO-DMT).

When tryptamine is supplied directly to the substrate, the fungus bypasses the
decarboxylation step entirely. The enzyme systems act on this abundant precursor,
hydroxylating and methylating it with high efficiency to produce psilocin far exceeding
natural levels. The phosphorylation to psilocybin, believed to serve as a protective
storage form in fungal tissue, becomes rate-limited under these conditions.

## Experimental Methods

Gartz used a cow dung/rice-grain mixture (2:1 ratio) as the base substrate, supplementing
it with tryptamine hydrochloride at a concentration of **25 mM**. First sporocarps
appeared within 3–4 weeks, with cultures producing mushrooms through **five sequential
flushes**. Alkaloid quantification was performed via **high-performance liquid
chromatography (HPLC)** and **thin-layer chromatography (TLC)**.

Prior radioactive tracer studies had established that labeled tryptamine functioned as a
more efficient precursor than tryptophan for psilocybin biosynthesis, with at least
**22.4%** of psilocybin formed derived from labeled tryptamine, confirming the tryptamine
route as a major biosynthetic pathway in vivo.

## Key Results

### Alkaloid Profiles: With vs. Without Tryptamine

| Parameter | Without Tryptamine | With Tryptamine (25 mM) |
|---|---|---|
| Psilocin (flush 1) | Generally absent | 2.1–3.3% dry mass |
| Psilocin (all flushes) | 0–0.15% dry mass | 2.1–3.3% dry mass |
| Psilocybin (all flushes) | 0.46–0.61% dry mass | 0.01–0.2% dry mass |
| Baeocystin | Present (varies) | Not detected |
| Residual tryptamine | N/A | None detected |

### Critical Observations

- **Record psilocin levels**: The 2.1–3.3% dry mass concentrations are the highest
  described in any mushroom species. In unmodified fruiting bodies, psilocin content
  is normally below 1%.
- **Complete conversion**: No residual tryptamine was detected in fruit bodies or
  vegetative mycelia — virtually all precursor was converted with near-total efficiency.
- **Inverse psilocybin relationship**: As psilocin rose dramatically, psilocybin fell to
  trace amounts (0.01–0.2%), suggesting saturation of the downstream phosphorylation step.
- **Baeocystin absence**: *P. cubensis* produced no detectable baeocystin (4-PO-HO-NMT)
  under tryptamine-fed conditions, reflecting resource allocation toward N,N-dimethylation.

### Mechanistic Explanations

- **Enzyme capacity**: The hydroxylation and methylation systems possess exceptionally
  high catalytic capacity, readily processing excess tryptamine into psilocin.
- **Phosphate limitation**: Reduced phosphate in the culture media may limit psilocybin
  biosynthesis from psilocin; when the phosphate cofactor is scarce, the kinase step
  stalls and psilocin accumulates in its unphosphorylated form.
- **Pathway flux**: The massive tryptamine influx saturates early steps, creating a
  bottleneck at phosphorylation and causing psilocin to accumulate.

## Practical Applications

### PF TEK Supplementation Protocol

A practical adaptation for home cultivation using the PF TEK method:

- **Container**: Standard 1/2 pint wide-mouth mason jar.
- **Substrate**: Vermiculite + [[brown-rice-flour]] + water (standard PF TEK ratios).
- **Tryptamine dose**: **0.1 g or less** of tryptamine HCl per jar. An initial
  recommendation of 0.16 g was revised downward because the vermiculite-based substrate
  is lighter (less dense) than the cow dung/rice-grain mixture used in Gartz's original
  experiment.
- Dissolve the tryptamine HCl in the water component before mixing with dry ingredients
  for even distribution throughout the substrate.

### Morphology and Potency Effects

- **Dwarfed growth**: Tryptamine-supplemented fruit bodies exhibited stunted morphology,
  likely reflecting metabolic strain from intensive alkaloid biosynthesis at the expense
  of normal growth processes.
- **Potency increase**: Bioassays indicated **at least 3× typical potency**, consistent
  with the analytically measured psilocin concentrations.

## Chemistry of the Conversion

The biotransformation involves two sequential enzymatic modifications to tryptamine:

1. **4-Hydroxylation**: A hydroxyl group is introduced at the indole 4-position,
   converting tryptamine to 4-hydroxytryptamine (norpsilocin). Catalyzed by a
   monooxygenase requiring NADPH and molecular oxygen. This is analogous to the
   hydroxylation of tryptamine to serotonin in mammals, but at the 4- rather than
   5-position.

2. **N,N-Dimethylation**: Two successive methyl transfers from SAM add methyl groups
   to the terminal amine nitrogen, producing psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine).
   Catalyzed by an SAM-dependent N-methyltransferase.

The final phosphate esterification (psilocin → psilocybin) is rate-limited under
tryptamine-fed conditions, leading to psilocin accumulation rather than full conversion
to the phosphate ester.

## Preservation Considerations

Psilocin is significantly less stable than psilocybin. The phosphate ester in psilocybin
confers chemical stability, whereas the free phenolic hydroxyl in psilocin is prone to
oxidation by heat, light, and moisture. For tryptamine-supplemented mushrooms with
elevated psilocin content:

- **Cool desiccation**: Dry in a refrigerator under cold conditions rather than with
  heat-assisted drying, which accelerates psilocin degradation.
- **Storage**: Place desiccated material with desiccant in an airtight container; store
  in a **freezer** for long-term preservation.
- **Light avoidance**: Minimize UV and visible light exposure during drying and storage
  to reduce oxidative degradation of the phenolic molecule.

## Significance

Gartz's 1989 study remains foundational for understanding psilocybin/psilocin biosynthesis
in fungi. It demonstrated that *P. cubensis* enzymatic machinery can be directed toward
dramatically elevated psilocin production by substrate manipulation, that tryptamine is a
superior precursor to tryptophan, and that the phosphorylation step is rate-limiting
under high precursor availability. The complete absence of residual tryptamine confirmed
the remarkable efficiency of the [[fungal-enzyme-systems]]. These findings have informed both
academic research into fungal secondary metabolism and practical cultivation approaches
aimed at modulating alkaloid profiles in *Psilocybe* species.
