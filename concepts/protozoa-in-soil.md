# Protozoa in Soil

## Overview

Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic organisms that are crucial "mineralizers" in the [[soil-food-web-structure]]. They consume bacteria and fungi, releasing the nutrients held within those organisms back into the soil in plant-available form. As much as 80% of the nitrogen a plant needs comes from protozoan wastes. Over 60,000 species are known, and the majority live in soil.

## The Three Types

### Flagellates (Smallest)

The smallest protozoa, with one or two whip-like flagella for movement. They are the first to arrive when bacterial populations are high because they can move into small soil pores where larger protozoa cannot reach. Some flagellates (like Euglena) produce their own food via photosynthesis, but most are heterotrophic, eating bacteria.

### Ciliates (Medium)

Covered with rows of hair-like cilia that beat like oars, propelling them toward food and creating currents that sweep bacteria into their mouth region. The familiar paramecium is a ciliate. They form symbiotic relationships with bacteria -- some even "farm" bacterial colonies in sand.

### Amoebae (Largest)

Move by pouring their cytoplasm into false appendages called pseudopodia ("false feet"). They lack a mouth and instead engulf bacteria by surrounding them in gas bubbles, transmitting digestive enzymes, absorbing the entire vesicle, and expelling waste. They are relatively large and transparent. Two subtypes exist: those with shell-like exoskeletons with predefined holes, and those without shells.

## Succession Pattern

Protozoa arrive in a consistent progression when bacteria are abundant:

1. **Flagellates** arrive first -- small enough to enter tiny soil pores where bacteria hide
2. **Ciliates** follow -- larger but still smaller than amoebae
3. **Amoebae** arrive last -- largest, searching for bacteria and smaller protozoa

As combined grazing pressure builds, bacterial populations diminish. The larger ciliates and amoebae then begin eating the smaller ciliates and flagellates, which in turn allows bacterial populations to recover. This feedback loop maintains soil food web balance.

## Role in Nutrient Cycling (Mineralization)

When protozoa ingest bacteria or fungi, they digest what they need for their own metabolism and excrete the excess as waste. These wastes contain carbon, ammonium (NH4+), and other nutritional compounds that had been immobilized in bacterial and fungal bodies. This mineralization delivers plant-available nutrients directly in the [[root-exudates|rhizosphere]] -- the zone of root absorption.

If nitrogen-fixing bacteria are present (requiring pH 7 or above), the free ammonium is converted into nitrates. If not, nitrogen remains in ammonium form -- preferred by perennials and trees.

## Population Control

Counterintuitively, increasing protozoa populations often results in *increases* in bacterial populations. This occurs because protozoan grazing reduces bacterial competition for nutrients among survivors, allowing them to divide and multiply more effectively.

Protozoa also help control nematode populations by:
- Directly attacking some nematodes
- Competing for the same limited food resources (bacteria, fungi)

## Moisture Requirements

Protozoa need moisture to live, travel, and reproduce. Hygroscopic water -- the thin film on soil particle surfaces -- provides this under normal conditions. When soil dries out, most protozoa encase themselves in cysts and go dormant. Some species can survive several years in this state, ensuring survival of both the protozoa and the plants that depend on their nutrient-releasing activity.

## Numbers in Soil

A teaspoon of good garden soil contains "only" several thousand protozoa, compared to a billion bacteria. Their larger size (5 to 500 micrometers vs 1 to 4 micrometers for bacteria) means fewer fit in the same space, but their individual impact on nutrient cycling is enormous.

## Why Bacteria Aren't All Consumed

- Bacteria produce a slime film that is hard for protozoa to penetrate
- The slime lacks oxygen that protozoa require
- Bacteria are small enough to hide in tiny soil pores that protozoa cannot enter

## Key Points

- Protozoa are the primary mineralizers -- they convert microbial biomass into plant-available nutrients
- As much as 80% of plant nitrogen comes from protozoan wastes
- Three types: flagellates (smallest), ciliates (medium), amoebae (largest)
- They self-regulate populations through a feedback loop with their prey
- They need moisture and go dormant in cysts during dry periods
- A healthy soil food web ensures adequate protozoa populations
