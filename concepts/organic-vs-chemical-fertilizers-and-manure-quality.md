## Organic vs Chemical Fertilizers and Manure Quality

Steve Solomon"s *[[gardening-when-it-counts]]* provides a detailed comparison of organic and chemical fertilizers, explaining why organic approaches produce more nutritious food. The book also offers important cautions about manure quality — one of the most misunderstood aspects of organic gardening.

## Chemical Fertilizers: The Problem

### Incomplete Nutrition

Most so-called complete chemical fertilizers have an NPK analysis around 5-10-10 or similar. They are entirely incomplete. While they provide nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (depending on formulation, they may also contain sulfur), they lack the broad spectrum of other essential minerals that plants need in tiny traces. Plants grown with chemical fertilizers alone are more easily attacked by [[solomon-insects-and-diseases-integrated-approach]], and often don't grow as large or as well.

### The Feast-and-Famine Cycle

All inexpensive chemical fertilizers are highly water-soluble. This results in a rapid boost in plant growth followed by a big sag, requiring yet another application. A fair amount of water must pass through the soil before the dissolved nutrients leach below the root zone. In sandy soils that hold little or no clay, humus, or compost, much of the applied fertilizer can be wasted through leaching.

### Slow-Release Chemicals Are Expensive

Chemical fertilizers can be made to be "slow release" but these formulations cost about as much as the type that dissolves rapidly in water. Slow-release types are mainly used to grow highly valuable potted plants and nursery stock, not food crops.

## Organic Fertilizers: Slow and Steady

Manures, composts, and organic meals release their nutrient content only as they decompose — as they are slowly broken down by the complex ecology of living creatures in the soil. The rate of this decomposition is determined by soil temperature. The rate roughly doubles for each 10°F (5°C) increase of soil temperature. Complete decomposition of COF takes around two months in warm soil.

During the decomposition period, nutrients are steadily being released rather than all at once. This means:
- There is no feast-and-famine cycle
- Nutrients are available as the plant needs them
- Less is wasted through leaching
- The soil ecology is fed and maintained

Gardeners in hot climates (and warm soils) will get a bigger result from smaller applications of organic materials, but the result will last a shorter time. In cooler climates, decomposition is slower and more prolonged.

## Manure Quality: The Hidden Variable

### Enormous Variation

Spreading manure and compost is not a guarantee of making garden soil that will produce highly nutritious vegetables. The nutritional value of manure varies enormously depending on what the animal ate, how the manure was stored, and what bedding material was mixed in.

Typical manure NPK ranges include:

| Source | N (%) | P (%) | K (%) |
|--------|-------|-------|-------|
| Horse | 0.5-1.0 | 0.2-0.5 | 0.3-0.6 |
| Dairy cow | 0.5-0.7 | 0.2-0.3 | 0.4-0.5 |
| Feedlot steer | 0.7-1.0 | 0.3-0.5 | 0.6-0.9 |
| Sheep | 0.8-1.0 | 0.2-0.4 | 0.5-0.8 |
| Chicken (no litter) | 1.0-1.8 | 0.4-0.6 | 0.5-0.8 |
| Turkey | 1.0-1.4 | 0.5-0.7 | 0.5-0.8 |

These extraordinarily wide ranges mean that a gardener never really knows what they are getting when they obtain manure.

### A Tale of Two Horse Manures

Solomon illustrates the manure quality problem with a neighbor comparison. One neighbor (called "Ken") grows a seemingly excellent garden using horse manure from a nearby breeder of racehorses. These animals are fed like endurance athletes — their hay is purchased from knowledgeable farmers who ensure the right protein content and proper drying. The horses also receive a broad range of protein supplements, oilseed meals, and abundant vitamins and minerals. The resulting manure is rich and well-balanced, producing excellent garden growth.

Another neighbor sells horse manure from animals kept in a back pasture. The pasture vegetation is pathetic despite all the horse urine acting on it. The grass is so poor that most of the horse food must be purchased, and it is mainly low-quality local grass hay. The manure from these animals is so poor that it barely improves the garden, though the roses (low-demand plants) grow a little better.

### The Lesson

It is not easy to obtain manure that you can be confident will grow a great garden and produce highly nutritious food. The nutritional content depends entirely on the animal's diet, and most backyard horse, cow, or chicken keepers do not feed their animals a nutritionally complete diet. That is why Solomon recommends using only the minimum amount of manure necessary to supply the soil ecology's requirement for organic matter, and using COF to supply the plant's requirement for nutrients.

## Why COF Over Manure Alone

The [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof]] approach has several advantages over relying solely on manure:

1. **Predictable nutrition**: You know exactly what minerals you are adding and in what quantities.
2. **Complete spectrum**: COF provides trace minerals that manure may lack.
3. **Cost efficiency**: You use less total material to achieve better results.
4. **Nutritional density**: Vegetables grown with properly balanced COF contain significantly more protein, vitamins, minerals, and enzymes than those grown with manure alone.

## Increasing Soil Fertility on a Budget

For gardeners without much money to spend, Solomon suggests:

1. Make compost from all available organic wastes — kitchen scraps, yard waste, crop residues.
2. Source manure locally if available, but understand its limitations.
3. Use COF as the primary nutrient source, buying ingredients in bulk from agricultural supply stores (farm and grain dealers are the proper sources, not garden shops where prices will be prohibitively high).
4. Apply COF at least once a year, preferably in spring, at the rate of about 4-6 liters per 100 square feet.
5. For high-demand vegetables, supplement with additional side-dressings during the growing season.

The best fertilizer of all is the gardener's shadow — walking among the plants regularly, observing their growth, and responding to their needs. Foliar feeding with liquid kelp can supplement soil applications and gets the gardener into close contact with the crop.
