# Fukuoka Seed Ball Technique Detailed

updated: 2026-04-18

The seed ball (clay pellet) technique is central to Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming system. It solves the problem of winter seed survival, protects seeds from predation, and eliminates the need for pesticide coatings.

## Purpose and Origin

The technique was developed to solve the problem of rice seed not surviving winter when sown in the fall. In nature, the chances of a seed surviving intact until spring are less than one in a million -- almost all are consumed by birds and rodents or destroyed by disease. Fukuoka first developed a protectant of pesticide and synthetic resin, then spent years working to eliminate the pesticide entirely by enclosing seeds in clay pellets.

## The Simple Method

1. Mix the seeds in at least a five-to ten-fold quantity of well-crushed clay or red earth
2. Add water and knead until hard by treading
3. Pass the kneaded mixture through a half-inch screen
4. Dry for a half-day
5. Shape into half-inch pellets by rolling with the hands or in a mixer
6. There may be several (4-5) seeds in each pellet, but with experience this can be brought closer to the ideal of one seed per pellet

## The One-Seed Pellet Method

For precision sowing of single seeds:

1. Place the seed moistened with water in a bamboo basket or a mixer
2. Sprinkle the seed with clay powder while spraying water mist with an atomizer
3. Move the basket in a swirling motion -- the seeds become coated with clay and grow larger
4. Target size: quarter-inch to half-inch pellets
5. For large quantities, a concrete mixer can be used

## Material Considerations

- **Clay type:** Topsoil-containing clay may be used, but if pellets crumble too early in spring, seeds will be devoured by rodents and other pests
- **Red earth:** Well-crushed clay or red earth works best
- **Scientific alternative:** For those preferring convenience, seeds may be coated with synthetic resin (such as styrofoam) containing necessary pesticides -- though Fukuoka's aim was to eliminate this

## How They Work in the Field

- Seeds are enclosed within clay that protects them from birds, rodents, and insects during winter
- When rain falls and conditions are right, the pellets dissolve and the seeds germinate
- In no-tillage rice/barley succession, pellets are scattered over standing rice or over the straw mulch in late autumn
- The clay provides physical protection without chemical intervention
- Seeds remain dormant until natural conditions trigger germination

## Application to Desert Revegetation

Fukuoka proposed using seed balls for large-scale desert reclamation:

- Fill clay pellets with seeds of green manure trees (black wattle), clover, alfalfa, bur clover, and other green manure, plus grain and vegetable seeds
- Scatter over deserts and savannahs
- Pellets protect seeds; they sprout only when rain falls and conditions are suitable
- No irrigation or human intervention needed after scattering
- The principle: "Rain does not fall from the heavens; it issues forth from the ground"

## Seeding Rates

For rice/barley succession per quarter-acre:
- **Clover:** 1 lb (September-October and March-April)
- **Barley:** 6.5-22 lbs (end of October to mid-November)
- **Rice:** 6.5-22 lbs (mid-November to December) in clay pellets

When aiming for high yields, seed sparsely and evenly. For initial establishment, use 22 lbs each of rice and barley.

## Practical Tips

- The method works for any seed -- vegetables, grains, legumes, tree seeds
- Ideal for semi-wild cultivation where seeds are scattered and left to nature
- Particularly effective for seeds that would otherwise be eaten by birds or fail in exposed conditions
- Can be used by anyone, anywhere, to establish vegetation on degraded land
