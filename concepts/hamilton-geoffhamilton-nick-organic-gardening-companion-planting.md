# Companion Planting

Companion planting is a technique practiced by many organic gardeners
based on the idea that plants have specific likes and dislikes concerning
their close companions in the garden. By carefully considering which
plants are cultivated together, you can promote healthy growth, reduce
pest attacks, attract beneficial insects, and utilize a completely
natural form of [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]]. Many of the
recommendations for companion planting are based on folklore and, as
with many of these tales, there is some truth in them.

## The Theory Behind Companion Planting

The core principle is that certain plants, when grown near each other,
can provide mutual benefits. These benefits may include:

- **Pest deterrence**: Some plants produce chemicals or scents that
  repel specific insect pests from neighboring crops
- **Attracting predators**: Flowers that attract hoverflies, ladybugs,
  and other beneficial insects can protect nearby vegetables
- **Improving growth**: Some plants may enhance the growth or flavor of
  their neighbors
- **Weed suppression**: Ground-covering companion plants can shade out
  weeds between crop rows
- **Soil improvement**: Deep-rooted companions can bring nutrients up
  from subsoil layers

## Marigolds: The Star Companion

French marigolds (Tagetes and Calendula) are perhaps the most widely
recognized companion plants. Planted near tomatoes or roses, they
greatly reduce the frequency of attack by aphids — the most persistent
of all garden pests. This works because marigolds attract hoverflies
whose larvae devour greenfly by the thousand.

Hoverflies are the most valuable pest predators in the garden. Before
laying her eggs, the female hoverfly needs protein, which she gets from
pollen. She then lays individual eggs on colonies of aphids so that the
larvae have a readily available source of food when they hatch. The
hoverfly has a short feeding tube so needs to feed from an
open-structured flower where the pollen is easily accessible.

Good companion flowers for attracting hoverflies include:
- French marigolds (Tagetes)
- Pot marigolds (Calendula)
- Poppies (Papaver sp.)
- Nasturtiums (Tropaeolum)
- Dwarf morning glory (Convolvulus tricolor)

## Marigolds and Soil Pest Control

French marigolds are also effective against soil-dwelling pests. When
French marigolds are grown among crops, scientific research has
confirmed a reduction in eelworm attack. This is due to a secretion
from the roots of the marigolds. This has been particularly noted with
potato crops, where eelworms can be a serious problem.

French marigolds are also said to help kill weeds, in particular couch
grass (Agropyron repens), though this theory requires further testing.

## Cabbage Family Companions

The cabbage white butterfly is attracted to its host plant by smell and
it can be fooled by planting the highly aromatic French marigold
(Tagetes) between the rows of cabbages. Scientifically controlled
trials have been conducted which indicate a reduction in attack when
this was done.

## The Carrot and Onion Myth

There is a well-known theory that, because carrot fly are attracted by
smell, they can be prevented by planting carrots between rows of onions,
so the smell of the carrots is disguised. However, carefully controlled
trials over several years have not shown success with this combination.
An independent trial discovered that at least 10 rows of onions per row
of carrots were required for the onions to have any effect.

## Camouflage Through Diversity

Some pests are attracted to their host plants by sight. By mixing
ornamental plants and vegetables in an ornamental border, you can
camouflage the host plants, which deters the pests. There is also
evidence that vegetable plots that are left somewhat wild suffer less
pest damage than perfectly clean ones. However, the yields are also
lower because the weeds compete for the same light, nutrients, and
water.

The key is to create a balanced environment where beneficial plants
provide enough camouflage and predator habitat without excessively
competing with crops. Building up as diverse a collection of plants as
you can, including native flowers, trees, and shrubs, will help
maintain this balance.

## Herbs as Companion Plants

Many herbs serve as excellent companion plants. The [[herb-garden-design]] can
contribute significantly to pest management throughout the garden:

- **Borage** attracts bees and is said to improve the growth and flavor
  of strawberries and tomatoes
- **Sage** is traditionally planted near cabbage to deter cabbage moths
- **Rosemary** is said to repel carrot fly and cabbage moths
- **Thyme** attracts beneficial insects and can be planted near
  brassicas
- **Mint** deters cabbage white butterflies and ants
- **Chives** repel aphids and are often planted near roses

## Practical Guidelines

When planning your companion planting scheme, consider these practical
tips:

- Plant companion flowers in generous quantities — a few scattered
  plants will not make enough impact
- Choose open-flowered varieties to attract the widest range of
  pollinators and predators
- Plan your garden to include flowering plants throughout the entire
  growing season so there is always a food source for beneficial
  insects
- Experiment and keep records — some companion combinations work better
  in some gardens than others
