# Mollison on Permaculture for Fire Control

**Source:** Bill Mollison, Permaculture Design Course, Pamphlet VII (Permaculture for Fire Control), 1981

## Overview

Mollison provides a comprehensive system for designing fire-resistant landscapes, emphasizing that fire periodicity is determined by fuel accumulation rate and site moisture content. The strategy progresses from valleys upward, creating non-fuel surfaces and fire shadows. This approach integrates fire safety into the broader [[permaculture-design-principles-beginners]] framework, making fire resilience an inherent property of the landscape rather than an afterthought.

## Fire Periodicity

- Fire periodicity depends on: (1) rate of fuel accumulation, (2) amount of moisture on site

- Ridge tops are far more fire-prone than valleys; sun-facing sites more than shaded slopes

- With 30-40 inches rainfall: catastrophic fire every 25-30 years

- **Positive feedback loop**: the more an area burns, the more likely it burns again soon (fire removes humus, kills moisture retainers, creates fire-prone litter buildup)

- Advantage gained even by delaying fire one period

## Fire Physics

- **Radiation is the real killer**, not direct flame contact

- 400 feet before fire: hair catches alight

- 200 feet: body splits, fat catches alight

- 100 feet: you are a torch

- Radiation kills birds hundreds of feet from fire

- Goats survive (lean into fire); pigs die quickly; humans survive by dodging and hiding behind shadows

- Fire spirals up, drops burning material ahead, covers 1,000 square miles per hour

- Running away is fatal; running straight at fire gives best survival chance

## Primary Defense Strategy: Reduce Fuel

1. **Create non-fuel surfaces**: roads, ponds, swales, pit mulching

2. **Browsing/grazing**: chickens break up ground litter and mix with oxygen; sheep/wallaby reduce standing litter to 1 inch on fire side

3. **Mulch is safe**: sawdust may get half-inch surface smolder but doesn't spread

4. **100 feet minimum** of non-fuel systems between house and forest

## Plant Selection for Fire Zones

- **Fire-resistant**: ice plants, thick-leaf evergreens with fast-decomposing litter, high ash/water content, low volatile oil, dense growth (e.g., Coprosma -- "stand behind it, just a hot steam bath")

- **AVOID**: eucalypts, pines (both fire weeds with cones/fruits that open after fire), high volatile oil species

- Work from valleys upward with low-fuel vegetation; re-establish rain forest species

## House Protection Design

- **Safest houses are wooden** (13-15% higher survival than stone/brick; wood doesn't transmit heat through fabric)

- White painted or reflective surfaces protect against radiation

- **Roof sprinklers**: monsoon sprinkler on ridge, tap OUTSIDE (gravity system, not public); washes roof and gutters during ash fall

- **Rolled-under roof edge**: leaves, snow, and fire ash slide off; prevents gutter ignition (99% of house fires start from ash in gutters)

- **Fire mesh screens**: prevent large particles entering house; screened gutters

- **Fire shelter**: T-shaped or L-shaped pit, earthed over, at back of house away from fire slope; 6 feet deep; wool blankets soaked in water

- **NEVER jump in water during fire** (oxygen depleted; fish already dying)

- Don't put client at head of converging valley or where ridges converge

## Zone-Based Fire Design Strategy

Fire control design follows the [[permaculture-zones-system]] framework, with each zone receiving appropriate fire management treatment.

- **Zone 0 (house and immediate surrounds):** Maximum fire protection. Non-fuel surfaces within 30 feet. Roof sprinklers, reflective paint, gutter screens, rolled-under eaves. Fire shelter located at the back of the house on the side away from the expected fire approach. Store wool blankets, water, and fire extinguishers.

- **Zone 1 (intensive garden):** Green, irrigated, fire-resistant plantings. Well-watered gardens and lawns create a moist, low-fuel buffer around the house. Ponds, swales, and water features provide both fire break and water supply for firefighting.

- **Zone 2 (orchard and food forest):** [[fire-resistant-plants]] with high moisture content. Regular pruning to remove dead wood and ladder fuels. Grazing animals (sheep, goats) to keep ground fuel below 1 inch height.

- **Zone 3 (farm forestry):** Select fire-resistant timber species. Maintain access roads as fire breaks. Create pond and wetland chains along contours using alexander shulgin principles.

- **Zone 4 (managed forest):** Controlled burns during safe conditions to reduce fuel loads. Maintain fire trails along ridgelines. Promote fire-resistant canopy species that suppress flammable understory through shading and moisture retention.

- **Zone 5 (wilderness):** Accept natural fire regimes but maintain buffer zones at the Zone 4/5 boundary. Monitor for fire starts and maintain access for fire suppression if needed.

## Landscape Pattern Design

Fire-safe landscape design works with topography, prevailing winds, and vegetation patterns to create fire-resistant mosaics.

- **Valley-first strategy:** Begin fire management in valleys and work upward. Valleys naturally retain more moisture and accumulate less fuel. Once valley systems are fire-resistant, progressively treat slopes and ridges.

- **Fire shadow creation:** Dense plantings of fire-resistant species (Coprosma, ice plant) on the fire-exposed side of structures create "fire shadows" -- zones of reduced radiation on the protected side. Mollison notes that standing behind a Coprosma hedge during fire is "just a hot steam bath."

- **Ridge and saddle design:** Place non-fuel elements (roads, ponds, rock walls) along ridgelines and saddles to prevent fire from crossing from one watershed to another. Ridges are natural fire accelerators due to wind funnelling -- breaking fuel continuity along ridges is critical.

- **Aspect consideration:** South-facing slopes (in the Northern Hemisphere) receive more sun, dry faster, and burn more readily. Place fire-resistant species and non-fuel zones on these aspects. North-facing slopes retain more moisture and can support less fire-resistant but more productive species.

## Animal Integration for Fire Management

Animals play an essential role in Mollison's fire control strategy, converting fuel into protein.

- **Chickens:** Scratch through ground litter, mixing it with oxygen and accelerating decomposition. This reduces the fine fuel layer that carries surface fire. Chicken tractors moved through high-risk areas before fire season are highly effective.

- **Sheep and goats:** Reduce standing grass and shrub fuel to less than 1 inch height. Goats are particularly valuable for clearing woody understory and ladder fuels that carry fire from ground to canopy. Strategic grazing before fire season is a primary defense tool.

- **Cattle:** Can reduce grass fuel loads in larger areas but are less selective than goats and sheep. Useful in zone 3-4 grasslands.

- **Pigs:** Root through accumulated litter and duff, accelerating decomposition. However, pigs are highly vulnerable to fire (unlike goats) and must be provided with protected shelter.

## Water System Design for Fire

Water infrastructure designed for [[backyard-water-harvesting]] serves double duty as fire protection infrastructure.

- **Strategic pond placement:** Ponds placed upslope of structures provide gravity-fed water for roof sprinklers and firefighting. The 100-foot non-fuel zone around the house should include at least one pond.

- **Swale-connected tanks:** Swale systems that collect runoff can fill water tanks specifically reserved for fire suppression. These tanks should be gravity-fed to roof sprinkler systems.

- **High-pressure backup:** A dedicated high-pressure pump (powered by biogas from [[mollison-sewage-biogas-systems]] or solar electric) can supplement gravity-fed systems during fire events.

- **Multiple water points:** Distribute water storage throughout the property so that hose runs remain short and manageable during a fire emergency.
