## Sand Dams and Water Spreading Techniques

## Overview

Sand dams and water spreading systems are among the most effective strategies for harvesting and storing water in arid landscapes. These techniques slow flood flows, capture silt and sand, create subsurface water reserves, and establish conditions for tree and [[coleman-sunlight-and-winter-crop-growth-rates]] in environments that would otherwise remain barren. They represent a low-cost, high-impact approach to [[fukuoka-desert-reclamation]].

## Sandy River Beds

### Tree Distribution Patterns

Wherever deep sands fill rivers, palms and large [[fukuoka-shrine-grove-trees-grow-without-human-aid]] in small embayments off the river where flow damage to trunks is less likely. A good [[design-principle-integration]] is to observe how native trees survive and where the largest trees grow (provided cattle have not killed out the natural range).

It is very noticeable that desert river banks in hills often have quite separate species of trees on either bank. One species needs the slip-off or silty slope (the depositional bank), while the other thrives on the harder country rock of the erosion bank. Almost all burrowing animals use the silted or slip-off slope of the river.

## Pitting in Sands and Light Soils

### Disc Pitting Technique

By cutting a quarter-section slice off a set of disc blades, long pits are made with a quarter of their length as solid breaks. As discs are drawn along, a series of mini-swales is created, staggered like contour swales.

### Specifications

- **Spacing:** 0.5-0.75 m apart
- **Length:** 1.0-1.5 m per pit
- **Depth:** Maximum 0.3 m
- **Soil direction:** Ideally sidecast downhill or downwind (not critical on near-flat sites)

### Advantages

These short pits or furrows need not be strictly on contour and can be drawn by eye. No single pit is large enough to spill too much water and cause stream flow. Seed can be added to pits, broadcast, or blown in from residual vegetation. The method is fast, effective, and scalable to broadacre applications for grasses, shrubs, trees, and forbs.

### Dust Storm Prevention

This form of discing acts as surface roughening and eliminates or greatly decreases dust storms as soon as it is implemented. Pelleted desert shrub seed plus a light fertiliser dressing creates conditions for permanent soil stability around settlements.

### Research Validation

Cordon (1975), working in semi-arid hills, recorded average annual runoff of 20%, with up to 80% in eroded and compacted soils for individual rivers. Recommendations for slopes exceeding 2% include:

- A series of contour furrows or mini-swales at 2 m intervals
- Only every 20-30 contours actually surveyed (the rest drawn by eye)
- Swales "broken" to allow flooding through to the next series
- Vegetation in the furrows remains green in drought

The soil chosen for these systems must have the capacity to absorb moisture (primarily sandy soils). The scale and area treated can be as large as required at 2-4 m spacing.

## Spreading Water Downslope in Fragile Soils

Steep-sided gullies often result from road culverts, animal tracks out of wadis, or water discharged from [[diversion-drains-and-spreader-banks]] after fields are flooded. There are only a few safe ways to spill water downslope in deserts:

### Very Long Spreader Banks

Spreader banks take concentrated flow and sheet the water harmlessly downhill. In important areas, a solid concrete core can be added to ensure even flow across the hill. A cross-wall eventually blocks the ends of the bank to prevent discharge (Figure 11.41). These must be maintained and inspected during rain events.

### Grassed Spillways with Steering Banks

Below a spreader bank, a well-grassed and fenced spillway can lead water safely downslope. Side or "stern" banks prevent sideways spreading. These systems take a few years to fully evolve as grass establishes and soil stabilises.

### Sealed Spillways to Splash Pools

Downslope sealed spillways or pipes with splash pools lead water to a [[sand-dam]] dropped only 8 cm in level over a 7-year drought period. The downstream forest may extend 1-2 km in the sands of the river for a wall only 50 m wide. There are hundreds or thousands of such sites within a few kilometres of foothills in deserts.

### Clearwater Dam Specifications

All clearwater dams should be:
- **5-9 m deep** with reduced surface area, rather than shallow and wide (minimising evaporation)
- **Fenced** to exclude livestock
- **Marginal planting:** Rushes and shrubs on the margins

### Design Checklist

Before construction, verify these factors:
1. Rubble and silt in the dry creek bed should be very open and permeable (stones, pebbles, coarse sands)—this almost certainly means the dam is near foothills or in a valley close to headwater rocks
2. The floor of the dam is ideally clay or massive rock (leak-proof)

Even if neither factor is ideal, sand dams still function as good growing sites because they slow flood water over a long, level area.

### Multi-Stage Silt Dam Construction

An extensive silt dam of 9-12 m height is always built in 1.5 to 2 m stages, with the wall raised as earlier stages silt up. Behind each wall, gabions allow water to filter down to a base pipe, which can be led to settlement. The entire catchment area needs protection from sprays, fertilisers, and browsing animals to protect downstream water quality for settlements.

### Additional Benefits

Below roost trees around such dams, guano from desert birds may be gathered as sheets of phosphate fertiliser. Mixed with 30-40 parts of damp sand as a dilute solution, this is of great value in establishing dune or rock windrow trees. On the silt dam, melons grow very well as the first swampy condition recedes, and a good crop of legumes and melons can be planted at that time. A few such dams can support a strong village population and keep downstream scour holes recharged with water.
