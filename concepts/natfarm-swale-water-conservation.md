# Swale Design for Water Conservation

A swale is a long, shallow ditch about three to five feet across, which
serves to stop and channel the flow of water into the soil. Unlike a regular
ditch, which directs and carries water somewhere else, the swale is made to
help the water get absorbed directly into the soil. Swales lie across the
land, especially across a slope, and when the water is forced into the soil,
it can be soaked up by trees planted along the swales. Swales can be filled
with rock, gravel, or gypsum for even better water penetration.

Swales are perhaps the most effective method of water conservation in both
dry and humid climates. They work well on steep slopes or on flat prairies,
and they can even be implemented in an urban area to take advantage of road
and roof runoff. The technique is fundamental to and [[albert-hofmann]] strategies.

## How Swales Work

The soil quality dictates how much runoff you will have. A sand dune will
absorb all of the rainfall, and a treed or grassy meadow will absorb a
little less. Sandy soil will absorb less than a sand dune, and undrained
clay will absorb almost none at all. Swales address this by catching water
in a pool as rain runs into it, where it gradually soaks into the soil. If
the swale is overflowing, you need to widen it or improve the drainage.

## Building a Swale

It is not difficult to dig a ditch a few feet deep. Over time you can throw
[[urban-rooftop-gardening-and-sheet-mulching]] in, and grass will probably grow. As trees get
taller and begin to shade the swale, other species will spring up naturally.

The swale is really all about positioning. Two or more are always put in
together, along the contour of the slope, and the soil is loosened up to
help water penetrate. The second swale is placed 10 to 60 feet away from the
first, depending on the amount of rainfall you get per year.

## Spacing Guidelines by Rainfall

The spacing between swales depends on annual rainfall. In areas receiving
5 inches of rain per year, swales should be spaced 60 feet apart. At 10
inches of annual rainfall, space them 50 feet apart. At 20 inches, 40 feet.
At 30 inches, 30 feet. At 40 inches, 20 feet. At 50 inches or more, swales
can be as close as 10 feet apart. These guidelines ensure that water from
each swale can soak deep enough to benefit plants before reaching the next
swale downhill.

## Planting Along Swales

After a couple of rainfalls that soak deeply into the soil, plant trees
directly behind the hump of dirt (the berm). In a wet climate the area
between the swales can be heavily planted. In a dry climate only the edges
of the swales should be planted. It can take three to ten years for the
trees to fully mature and begin their job of shading, mulching, and
absorbing salts from the soil.

This is the most important step of the whole swale strategy. Trees will
soak up the water runoff and prevent waterlogging and evaporation, produce
valuable products and food, and improve the temperature and microclimate of
the land. The combination of swales and trees creates a self-sustaining
[[water-management]] system that requires no pumps or energy inputs.

## Swales vs. Diversion Drains

Unlike a swale, which stops the water and forces it into the soil, a
diversion drain is a ditch that carries water away. A diversion drain can
be used to direct water into a swale or to a [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]]
storage tank or irrigation system. Drains are also used to direct the flow
of water to a series of dams built to catch water runoff. If they are
directed to swales, they do not need to be waterproof, but if you are
sending the water to a dam, the drains should be built of rock or concrete.

Installing a spill gate gives you control over the flow of water. If you
fall victim to flooding or want to control crop irrigation, you can use the
spill gate to direct the water where you want it. A spill gate is simply a
removable method of blocking water.

## Swales in Flood Management

Swales play a critical role in flood prevention. By catching and absorbing
rainwater runoff, they reduce the volume and speed of water flowing
downhill. This prevents erosion, protects buildings and gardens from flood
damage, and recharges groundwater supplies. Combined with dams, swales can
effectively direct excess water away from vulnerable areas. Even existing
houses can be protected from flooding by using dams, swales, and runoffs to
effectively direct excess water away.

## Permaculture Irrigation Philosophy

Permaculture irrigation is half species choice and half terrain. Only a
small part has to do with piping, pumps, or equipment. By studying the
slope and topography of the land, gravity is used to direct water into the
gardens directly or even to individual plants. These individual plants are
chosen for their specific traits that make them function well in the
location where you want them. The barn roof is harvested for rainwater
stored in a large tank and, hopefully, gravity fed to the gardens below.
Swales and mulch are the secret to successful gardening in any climate.

## Urban Applications

Swales can be implemented in urban areas to take advantage of road and roof
runoff. Even a small swale in a backyard can significantly reduce stormwater
runoff and recharge local groundwater. In urban permaculture, swales can be
combined with [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]] from rooftops, creating a complete
water management system on a small scale. The edges of urban swales can be
planted with food-producing plants, turning a water management feature into
a productive garden element.

## Maintenance Considerations

Once established, swales require minimal maintenance. Over time, mulch can
be added to the swale bottom to improve water absorption and prevent
erosion. Grass and other plants will naturally colonize the swale, further
stabilizing the soil. If a swale begins to overflow during heavy rains, it
may need to be widened or its drainage improved. Periodic inspection after
major rain events helps identify any areas that need attention before small
problems become larger erosion issues.
