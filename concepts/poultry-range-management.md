# Poultry Range Management

**Poultry range management** is the practice of rearing chickens, ducks, geese, and other domestic fowl on free-range systems within integrated permaculture designs. Bill Mollison treats poultry as one of the three critical animal species for domestic self-reliance, alongside bees and pigs, and provides detailed guidance on breed selection, stocking densities, rotational systems, and integration with orchard, crop, and pasture enterprises.

## Breed Selection

Modern chicken breeds derive from two primary lineages with fundamentally different characteristics:

### Light Breeds

Light breeds trace their ancestry to Spanish and Indo-Malaysian jungle fowl. They are characterised by:

- **Flighty, active temperament** — strong fliers requiring higher fencing
- **White egg production** — concentrated in spring and summer
- **Sensitivity to cold and wind** — best suited to warmer, drier climates
- **Preference for sandy or light soils** with high pH
- **Physical features**: long legs, full combs, four toes, light feathering, white ear lobes
- **Known varieties**: Anconas, Andalusians, Minorcas, Leghorns, Hamburghs
- **Foraging ability**: excellent — breeds such as Hamburghs and Leghorns can obtain most or all of their food from hedgerow and pasture alone

### Heavy Breeds

Heavy breeds descend from Chinese fowl, principally via the Langshan. Their characteristics contrast sharply with light breeds:

- **Calm, docile temperament** — non-fliers unless chased
- **Brown egg production** — lay through summer, autumn, and even winter
- **Hardiness to cold, wind, and wet** — tolerant of clay and acid soils
- **Physical features**: heavy feathering, short or feathered legs, short or rose combs, five toes
- **Known varieties**: Scots Dumpy, Orpington, Dorking, Wyandotte, [[rhode-island-red]], Plymouth Rock, Australorp
- **Foraging ability**: good but requires supplementary feeding, especially in winter
- **Mothering instinct**: excellent — heavy breeds are reliable broody hens

A third lineage includes South American breeds such as the Araucana. In total, selectors can choose from **60–100 breeds** suited to specific soils, sites, foodstuffs, and production goals (egg, meat, or dual-purpose utility breeds).

## Flock Structure and Social Biology

In natural conditions, chicken flocks seldom exceed **20–30 individuals**, typically comprising 2–3 cockerels and the remainder hens. Surplus cockerels are driven from the range or killed by the flock. Larger aggregations of 40–60 birds will spontaneously divide into two separate flocks, reflecting innate social structure.

This natural flock size has direct implications for range design: rather than maintaining a single large flock, permaculture systems should provide multiple small shelters to accommodate the birds' preference for smaller social groupings.

## Egg Production Comparison

| System | Annual Egg Output | Productive Lifespan | Notes |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|-------|
| Free-range heavy breeds | 130–150 eggs/hen | 4–6 years | Flock averages |
| Free-range light breeds | 160–200 eggs/hen | 4–6 years | Hamburghs, Leghorns |
| Free-range exceptional | Up to 200 eggs/hen | 4–6 years | Rare individuals |
| Caged/battery | Varies (forced) | 18–20 months | Replaced due to burnout |

Caged systems rely on artificial day length manipulation, hormone regimens, and pelleted feeds with additives to maintain production. The contrast in animal welfare and product quality between free-range and confined systems is substantial.

## Stocking Densities

Appropriate stocking density depends on the range objective and the presence of other livestock:

| Density | Application | Notes |
|---------|-------------|-------|
| 120–180/ha | Orchard cleanup | Cleans windfalls, supplies fertiliser; rotated through 12–16 pens every 18 months |
| 350–400/ha | Mixed grazing | Allows sheep and cattle to share the range |
| 800–900/ha | Maximum poultry | Chickens forage so thoroughly that other livestock cannot find browse; requires housing in small flocks of ~20 in sheds ~2 m per side |

At household scale, a single flock of **20–30 hens** serves as a food supplement. Where space is limited, cut greens from garden weeds and roadside grasses can replace free-ranging.

## Nutritional Self-Sufficiency on Range

On correctly stocked range, poultry obtain approximately **65% of their food** from insects, invertebrates, greens, and grains. The remaining **30–35%** is supplied as concentrated grain products — typically weed seed and cracked or undersized grains from threshing operations.

### Forage Species for Poultry

Trees and shrubs providing seed and fruit include:

- **Coprosma repens**, Caragana, tagasaste, boxthorn (*Lycium* spp.), pigeon pea
- **Mulberry** (5–6 varieties), most *Solanum* species with berries, normal orchard fruits
- **Acacias** with edible seed (most species), *Leucaena*

Ground-level forage includes clovers, grasses, and "weeds" such as cleavers, chicory, comfrey, oxalis, and dandelion. Insects and their larvae — deliberately cultivated in manure piles, mulch heaps, compost, or mealworm cultures in bran — provide critical protein.

### Supplementary Feeding

- Table scraps supplement diets
- Large acorns, chestnuts, or starchy seeds can be **sprouted or cracked** using a garden roller on a hard surface
- In hot periods, grains can be sprouted for 2–3 days
- In winter, unsprouted grains and grain legumes are preferred
- A separate pen of comfrey, arrowroot, chard, and sunroot serves as drought forage or "throw-over-the-fence" emergency feed

## Chicken Tractor Systems

The chicken tractor is a core permaculture technique for integrating poultry with crop production. The system works as follows:

1. Flocks are rotated through **12–16 pens per hectare**, spending approximately **18 months** in each enclosure
2. During their residence, chickens remove all weeds, surface bulbs, weed seeds, and grasses — effective against persistent weeds including nutgrass, kikuyu, onion weed, and oxalis
3. As pens are cleared, chickens are moved on; the ground is **limed** and planted to vegetable or orchard crops
4. Before chickens return, **forage crops** of buckwheat, wheat, sunflower, millet, black nightshade, cleavers, chard, and mustard are sown and given 2–3 months to mature
5. The chickens thus **plough and clear for their own forage** in a self-renewing cycle

## Health and Husbandry

On rich free range with diverse forage, diseases are rare or absent. The essential husbandry requirements are:

- **Dry dust baths** with diatomaceous earth, dried bracken, or neem tree leaves for ectoparasite control
- **Wormwoods** for intestinal worm control
- **Shell grit or calcium gravel** (limestone) for eggshell formation
- **Hard silica grit** to grind food in the crop
- **Predator protection**, clean water, good shade, and night shelter
- **Observation, culling, and replacement** as part of routine management

Breeding on range produces birds that learn to avoid predators naturally, settling into quiet, manageable flocks. Early establishment mortality is the highest-risk period; once chickens are bred on range, management becomes substantially easier.

## Integration with Other Enterprises

Poultry combine effectively with:

- **Orchards** — no soft fruit, apple, or citrus orchard thrives without poultry foragers
- **Dairy** — poultry scavenge manure for maggots and insects
- **Grain crops** — glean waste grain after harvest
- **Home gardens** — process kitchen scraps and garden waste

The same principles apply to ducks, guineafowl, turkeys, pheasant, and quail, which differ primarily in food preferences — favouring more snails, berries, and insects. Ducks require marsh plants, snails, and shallow water for dabbling. Geese serve as traditional orchard grazers, replacing lawn mowers and tractors for grass control.

A commercial free-range egg operation requires approximately **13 hectares** with careful range planning. Running a flock of 3,000 birds on this scale is a full-time family enterprise, combining well with [[chicken-tractor-systems]] and orchard fruit production.
