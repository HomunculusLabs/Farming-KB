# Wood-Rotting Fungi for Effluent Decolorization

White-rot fungi offer biological alternatives to physicochemical and chemical treatments for decolorizing industrial effluents containing recalcitrant chromophores. These fungi degrade lignin, a complex three-dimensional polyaromatic polymer, using extracellular enzyme systems that also attack diverse xenobiotic aromatic compounds. Their ability to produce lignin-degrading enzymes makes them uniquely suited for treating colored effluents that resist conventional biological treatment.

## Limitations of Conventional Treatment

Physicochemical treatments (flocculation, coagulation, adsorption, ion exchange, ultrafiltration, [[reverse-osmosis-compost-tea]]) relocate contaminants rather than destroy them. Chemical bleaching with chlorine produces toxic organochlorine compounds. ### Non-Ligninolytic Mechanisms

Beyond the primary ligninolytic system, white-rot fungi employ additional degradation pathways. Cytochrome P450 enzymes can directly oxidize aromatic compounds through NADPH-dependent mono-oxygenation reactions. Some species produce extracellular oxidases such as aryl-alcohol oxidase and glyoxal oxidase that generate H2O2 independently of the lignin peroxidase pathway. The combined action of enzymatic and non-enzymatic (Fenton chemistry) mechanisms gives these fungi remarkable versatility in attacking structurally diverse pollutants.

## Mechanisms of Decolorization

Two main mechanisms operate in biological decolorization: adsorption to biomass and biodegradation. With white-rot fungi, adsorption does not appear to be the principal mechanism. Prior adsorption may serve to bring chromophores into closer contact with degradative enzymes, which are often largely associated with the cell surface.

Evidence shows an initial rapid adsorption of dyes onto fungal mycelium followed by slower degradative decolorization of adsorbed dye. Complete decolorization often leaves white mycelium with no trace of adsorbed dye, while heat-killed controls show deeply colored mats. Adsorption of dyes onto live and dead mycelium has been described using the Langmuir and Freundlich adsorption models.

## Process Conditions

Optimal decolorization generally occurs at pH 4-5.5. Most research has involved batch cultures, sometimes agitated but often static. Several buffer systems have been used, including dimethyl succinate, dimethyl glutarate, and acetate, with little evidence that any one is innately superior.

The pH optimum represents a compromise between the optima of several enzymes (laccases, peroxidases, H2O2-producing oxidases) and the pH at which the mycelium can grow effectively. Like most filamentous fungi, white rots normally show optimal growth at acidic pH values, and growth in carbohydrate-containing media generally causes acidification.

Temperature requirements vary by species but generally fall between 25°C and 30°C for mesophilic organisms. Some thermotolerant strains can operate at higher temperatures, which can be advantageous for treating warm industrial effluents. Nitrogen limitation is a critical factor for inducing the ligninolytic system in species like [[coriolus-versicolor]] in calcium alginate. The MYCOPOR system (Messner et al., 1990) was developed as a practical treatment approach using Pleurotus ostreatus for phenol content and toxicity reduction. MnP plays a primary role in kraft bleach plant effluent decolorization by P. chrysosporium (Michel et al., 1991), while laccase is primary for T. versicolor. Both MnP and LiP contribute to high-molecular-weight chlorolignin degradation.

### Molasses Spent Wash

[[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]] and [[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]] decolorize anaerobically digested sugarcane molasses spent wash, achieving 70-85% decolorization and 70-90% COD reduction. The melanoidin pigments responsible for color are recalcitrant and survive normal biological treatment. Multiple melanoidin-decolorizing enzymes have been isolated from C. versicolor, with a multiplicative effect between different enzyme activities. Glucose supplementation greatly improves decolorization. Rates and extents depend on organism, effluent concentration, and color intensity, with treatment times ranging from 1 to 10 days.

### Olive Oil Mill Wastewater

Olive mill wastewater (OMW) is a major pollution problem in Mediterranean countries, being black, highly toxic, and rich in phenolic compounds. White-rot fungi achieve 60-75% or more color removal together with up to 90% reduction in phenolics. The ligninolytic enzyme system is the main agent of decolorization, though organisms differ in the relative importance of different enzymes. P. chrysosporium relies primarily on LiP, while P. flavido-alba uses MnP and laccase. Mycelia of P. ostreatus, P. chrysosporium, and L. edodes can be reused several times.

### Textile Dye Effluents

P. chrysosporium decolorizes artificial textile effluents. Decolorization of Orange II by wood-rotting fungi has been demonstrated (Knapp, Zhang and Tapley, 1997). [[fungal-laccase-and-textile-dye-decolorization]] pathway is particularly well-studied for azo dye breakdown.

### Chemical Industry Effluents

White-rot fungi have demonstrated decolorization of effluents from chemical manufacture, including nitrated stilbene sulfonic acid intermediates used in optical brightener production. C. versicolor achieved approximately 85% color removal from mixed chemical factory effluents. Schliephake et al. (1993) reported at least 90% removal of colour from a pigment manufacture effluent.

## Bioreactor Systems

Multiple reactor designs have been employed for white-rot fungal treatment of effluents:

- **Rotating biological contactors (RBCs)** with organisms on slowly rotating discs
- **Percolating filters and packed beds** for continuous flow treatment
- **Fluidized beds** using mycelial pellets for high surface area contact
- **Immobilized systems** using calcium alginate or polyurethane foam matrices
- **Trickle-bed reactors** where effluent percolates through a fungal colonized support

A 200-liter packed-bed bioreactor using [[pycnoporus-cinnabarinus]] demonstrated practical scale potential (Lonergan et al., 1995). Mycelia can be reused over many cycles covering weeks to months, and can be stored at 4°C for several months retaining full decolorization activity. Both batch and continuous methods can be effective.

### Scale-Up Challenges

Transitioning from laboratory to industrial scale presents several obstacles. Maintaining sterility is difficult at scale — contaminant organisms can outcompete white-rot fungi in non-sterile environments, though some effluents (particularly those containing toxic chlorinated compounds) naturally suppress contaminants. Reactor clogging by dense mycelial growth is a common operational problem. Aeration requirements vary between species and must be carefully controlled to maintain dissolved oxygen without stripping [[fungal-bioreactor-types]] reference covers these design considerations in detail.

## Species Selection Guide

Choosing the right fungal species for a given effluent depends on multiple factors:

- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium**: The most extensively studied species. Produces LiP and MnP under nitrogen limitation. Best for kraft effluents and high-molecular-weight chlorolignins. Requires careful culture conditions to induce [[ligninolytic-enzymes]].
- **Trametes versicolor (Coriolus versicolor)**: Produces laccase as the primary enzyme. Effective across a broad range of dye types and effluent compositions. More tolerant of varying nitrogen conditions than P. chrysosporium.
- **Pleurotus ostreatus**: Produces MnP and laccase. Particularly effective for phenol-rich effluents. Edible species, making spent biomass potentially useful as animal feed or compost after treatment.
