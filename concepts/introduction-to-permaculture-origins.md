## Introduction to Permaculture: Origins and the Terrible Time of Day

The concept of [[permaculture]] emerged in 1975-1976 when [[bill-mollison]], an Australian ecologist and researcher at the University of Tasmania, began publicly articulating a design system for sustainable human settlements. Together with his student [[david-holmgren]], who was writing a thesis on the subject under Mollison"s direction, they produced *Permaculture One* — a book that sold 25,000 copies within three years and was out of print by then. This was followed by *Permaculture Two*, which shifted emphasis from theory toward practical application.

## The Permaculture Design Course

The material presented here comes from the 1981 [[permaculture-design]] Course held at The Rural Education Center in Wilton, New Hampshire, USA. This course was transcribed by Elizabeth Beyor into 15 pamphlets that were later edited by Dan Hemenway of Yankee Permaculture and placed in the public domain. The pamphlet series covers the full breadth of permaculture design, from humid and arid landscapes to urban areas, forests, water systems, and community organization.

The permaculture movement spread rapidly through regional study groups, seed exchanges, bioregional associations, and a network of publications including the Permaculture Quarterly (later the International Permaculture Journal) and the International Permaculture Solutions Journal (TIPS).

## The Terrible Time of Day

Mollison's opening lecture, titled "The Terrible Time of Day," is a sweeping assessment of global ecological collapse. Rather than focusing on single issues, it examines the systemic failure of soils, forests, the atmosphere, and nutrient cycles — the four foundational systems upon which all life depends. The argument is that these are not separate crises but interconnected symptoms of a single underlying problem: human agricultural and industrial systems that are fundamentally destructive.

### Forest Systems in Collapse

Forests have been found to be far more important in the oxygen cycle than previously suspected. Oceans, once thought to be the primary oxygen producers, contribute less than 8% of atmospheric oxygen recycling, and many are becoming oxygen-consuming. It is the forests — particularly the equatorial multispecies rainforests and the cool evergreen forests of the Russian tundra and southern latitudes — that maintain the atmospheric balance necessary for life.

Beyond oxygen production, forests play a critical role in precipitation. When forests are cleared from ridgelines, measurable rainfall can decrease by 10-30%, but total precipitation — including the unseen moisture that forests capture from the atmosphere on clear nights — can drop by as much as 86%. It is possible to create semi-desert conditions rapidly simply by clearing trees from ridge tops, a practice that Mollison observed occurring at alarming rates worldwide.

The character of forests is to moderate everything — temperature extremes, water runoff, and pollution. As forests are removed, immoderate extremes arrive. Forests are also one of the very few soil-creating systems on Earth.

### Deforestation Rates and the "Cut and Run" System

Of all the forests that ever existed, only about 2% remain in Europe (and even those exist only through human tolerance or planting — no primeval European forests survive). Approximately 8% remain in South America, and roughly 15% in other regions. The world is cutting one million hectares of forest per year in excess of planting, and that figure doubled in the month Mollison cited due to clearing of Mississippi bottomland forests for soybean production.

Even in the best-managed forests, there is a constant loss rate of approximately 4%, giving perhaps 25 more years of forest cover at current rates. But in practice, across Southwest Asia, South America, and the Third World wherever multinational corporations obtain forest ownership, the losses are far steeper. The forests are being degraded to their lowest possible use — primeval forests with trees 200 feet to the first branch are being chipped for newsprint and packaging material.

### Climate Destabilization

The destruction of forests has direct consequences for global climate stability. About 12,000 billion tons of carbon dioxide are released annually by the death of forests. The world climate is no longer trending toward a simple greenhouse warming or an ice age — it is beginning to fluctuate so wildly that it is totally unpredictable which thermal barrier will crack, and when it does, the change will be sudden and extreme.

In Mollison"s home region of Tasmania, a place naturally buffered by ocean and forest, the community experienced in succession the windiest, driest, and wettest months in 200 years of recording. This extreme variability, not gradual warming, is the signature of climate destabilization driven by deforestation.

### Ocean Systems Failing

The waste products from forest destruction are killing large areas of the sea. The Baltic Sea, the Mediterranean, and the coast off New York have become oxygen-consuming zones. The primary driver is the carpeting of the sea floor with forest products — wood chips, paper waste, and associated chemicals. The ocean system, already weakened, is being pushed from oxygen-producing to oxygen-consuming status.

## The Phasmid Conspiracy

A critical insight in Mollison's analysis is what he calls the "phasmid conspiracy" — the tendency to blame forest death on insect pests and pathogens rather than on the true cause: human disturbance. Phasmids attack eucalypts, Dutch elm disease kills elms, rust destroys poplars and firs, chestnut blight eliminated chestnuts (which were 80% of the forests they occupied).

Mollison argues that these insects and pathogens are not the cause of forest death but are feeding on already-dying systems. If you injure a tree — strike it with an axe, bump it with a bulldozer, touch it with a car — and then observe patiently, within three days you will see twenty or more insects and decomposers arrive at the injury site. They are attracted by the smell of the dying tree. The tree was already doomed; the insects are merely the decomposers doing their ecological work.

The real causes of forest death are profound changes in light penetration, pollutant loading, and acid rain fallout. People, not bugs, are killing the forests. But blaming insects serves powerful economic interests — it justifies continued logging, pesticide application, and the replacement of diverse native forests with monoculture plantations, all while deflecting responsibility from the actual destructive practices.

## Significance for Permaculture Design

This analysis forms the foundation of permaculture's urgency. The problems are systemic and interconnected: forest destruction causes soil loss, which causes water table changes, which causes salination, which causes crop failure, which drives more forest clearing. Breaking this cycle requires not incremental reform but a fundamental redesign of human settlement and production systems — which is precisely what permaculture offers as a design science.

The scale of the crisis means that permaculture cannot remain an academic exercise or a niche hobby. Mollison called for rapid dissemination of knowledge, conversion of investment capital to low-energy systems, and the creation of self-reliant bioregions. The design principles that follow in the course — water storage, soil building, food forests, integrated animal systems — are presented not as optional lifestyle choices but as necessary responses to genuine ecological emergency.
