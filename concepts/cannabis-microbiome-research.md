# Cannabis Microbiome Research

Cannabis microbiome research is a young but rapidly growing field. The foundational study by Winston et al. (2014) provided the first comprehensive characterization of [[cultivar-endorhiza-bacterial-communities-cannabis]] across the root-soil continuum, but significant questions remain. This page synthesizes what is known, what is uncertain, and the practical implications for living soil cultivation.

## What We Know

### The Two-Tier Selection Model Is Valid for Cannabis
Winston et al. confirmed that cannabis follows the same microbiome assembly model observed in Arabidopsis, poplar, and other plants:
- **Tier 1**: [[soil-edaphic-factors-microbial-communities]] (soil chemistry) determine which microbes are available in the rhizosphere
- **Tier 2**: Plant genotype determines which microbes are enriched inside root tissue (endorhiza)

This was validated across two experiments, five cultivars, and three soil types, with highly significant statistical support (ADONIS p < 0.001 for soil type, sample type, and strain effects).

### Cannabis Has a Consistent Core Endorhiza Community
Regardless of cultivar or soil type, a set of bacterial taxa consistently colonize cannabis roots:
- Pseudomonas, Oxalobacteraceae, Xanthomonadaceae (Gammaproteobacteria)
- Rhizobiales, Burkholderiaceae (Alphaproteobacteria/Betaproteobacteria)
- Actinomycetales (Actinobacteria)
- Sphingobacteriales (Bacteroidetes)

These are well-known endophytes in other plant systems, suggesting conserved plant-microbe signaling mechanisms.

### Cultivar-Specificity Is Real but Subtle
Different cannabis varieties do recruit different endorhiza communities — but the differences are in *abundance* (71 significant OTUs), not in *presence/absence* (zero significant differences). This means all cultivars draw from the same microbial pool, but they adjust the relative proportions differently. Cultivar chemistry (root exudates, [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]]) likely drives these differences.

### Nitrogen Dominates Soil Microbiome Structure
Of all soil factors tested, nitrogen content has the strongest correlation with microbial [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]]. This has a practical implication: maintaining steady, moderate nitrogen levels is more important for soil biology than chasing specific P/K ratios. See [[cannabis-root-microbiome]] for the full edaphic ranking.

### Soil > Cultivar for Microbial Composition
Soil type produces 657 significant OTU differences, while strain produces only 71. The plant's genotype matters, but the soil environment matters ~10x more for determining which microbes are present. This is why building diverse living soil is the single most impactful thing a grower can do.

## What We Don't Know (Yet)

### Cannabinoid-Microbiome Causality
Winston et al. found a strong correlation (r = 0.863, p = 0.001) between endorhiza community structure and cannabinoid profile, but could not disentangle this from soil-cannabinoid effects. Key questions:
- Do specific microbes *produce* or *modify* cannabinoids?
- Does cannabinoid production *attract* specific microbes?
- Or are both driven by a third factor (soil chemistry, plant genetics)?

### Temporal Dynamics
The studies sampled at single time points. We don't know how the cannabis microbiome changes across the growing cycle:
- How does the community shift from vegetative to flowering?
- Do cannabinoid-producing trichomes affect root-zone microbiology?
- How quickly does the endorhiza community establish after transplanting?

### Functional Roles of Most Taxa
16S rRNA sequencing identifies *who* is there but not *what they're doing*. Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic studies are needed to understand:
- Which microbes are actively metabolizing root exudates?
- Which are producing plant growth hormones?
- Which are suppressing pathogens?
- How do fungal communities (not profiled in this study) interact with the bacterial community?

### The Fungal Dimension
Winston et al. only profiled bacterial communities (16S rRNA). Cannabis mycorrhizal associations, endophytic fungi, and saprophytic fungal communities remain largely uncharacterized in controlled studies. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] research in cannabis is limited to a few studies showing Rhizophagus intraradices and Funneliformis mosseae associations.

### Indoor vs Outdoor Microbiomes
All samples in the Winston et al. study were from outdoor organically-grown plants in California. Indoor living soil beds may have different microbial dynamics due to:
- Controlled temperature/humidity
- Absence of natural microbial inoculation from wind/rain
- Different soil mixes (peat-based vs native soil)
- Lack of [[dighton-mycorrhizal-networks-carbon-transfer]] from surrounding plants

## Practical Implications for Growers

### What the Research Supports

1. **Build maximum soil diversity** — Since soil determines 10x more microbial variation than cultivar, invest in diverse compost, multiple inoculant sources ([[indigenous-microorganisms-imo]], [[effective-microorganisms-em]], worm castings from different sources)

2. **Don't chase specific endorhiza communities** — The plant selects its own internal partners. Focus on providing a rich pool rather than trying to force specific strains

3. **Manage nitrogen carefully** — N is the strongest microbiome driver. Avoid large swings between deficiency and excess. Slow-release organic N (alfalfa, neem meal, compost) is better than soluble synthetic N

4. **Minimize salinity** — Salinity is the second strongest microbiome driver after nitrogen. Avoid over-application of mineral salts, use dechlorinated water

5. **Start plants in their final soil** — Transplanting from sterile media resets the microbial recruitment process. The longer roots interact with living soil, the more established the beneficial community becomes

6. **Maintain adequate carbon** — Organic matter drives microbial biomass. Top-dress with compost, use mulch, avoid bare soil

### What Requires More Evidence

- **Specific microbial inoculant claims** — Many products claim to boost cannabinoid production via microbiome manipulation. This is plausible but not yet proven by controlled research
- **LAB for terpene enhancement** — The hypothesis that lactic acid bacteria provide hexanoic acid as a cannabinoid precursor is theoretically sound but untested
- **Cultivar-specific inoculant blends** — Given that all cultivars share the same core community but adjust abundances, a universal inoculant approach is more supported than cultivar-specific products

## Future Research Directions (Per Winston et al.)

The authors specifically call for:
1. Time-series sampling of endorhiza communities across the reproductive cycle
2. Increased cannabinoid testing to decouple microbiome-cannabinoid correlations from soil effects
3. Studies on which aspects of host genotype drive endorhiza community structure
4. Mechanistic studies using microbial communities to increase plant fitness, suppress disease, or augment metabolite production
