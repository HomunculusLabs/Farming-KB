# Water Harvesting and Conservation

## The Fivefold Path to Water Wisdom

[[toby-hemenway]] outlines five strategies for intelligent water management in
the ecological garden, ordered from simplest to most involved:

1. **Conserve** — Reduce water use through mulching, efficient irrigation,
   and selecting appropriate plants. Mulch alone can reduce evaporation by
   70% or more, making it the single most effective water conservation tool.

2. **Reuse** — Capture and redirect water that has already been used once,
   such as greywater from sinks, showers, and laundry, for irrigation of
   landscape plants.

3. **Catch** — Intercept rainwater and runoff before it leaves the property.
   Roof catchment, swales, and diversion drains capture water that would
   otherwise flow to storm drains or creeks.

4. **Store** — Hold captured water in soils, ponds, tanks, or cisterns for
   use during dry periods. The cheapest and most effective storage medium is
   the soil itself, when enriched with organic matter.

5. **Manage** — Design the landscape to distribute water slowly and evenly,
   preventing erosion and allowing maximum infiltration. Swales, berms, and
   contour planting all contribute to on-site water management.

## Swales

A swale is a shallow, level trench dug on contour—the line of equal elevation
across a slope. Water collects in the swale during rain and sinks into the
ground rather than running off. The excavated soil is mounded on the downhill
side as a berm, which is planted with trees and perennials.

### How to Make a Swale

1. Use an A-frame level, laser level, or water level to find the contour line.
2. Mark the line with stakes and string.
3. Dig a shallow trench (6–12 inches deep) along the contour, piling soil
   on the downhill side to form a berm.
4. Plant the berm with fruit trees, berry bushes, or other useful perennials.
5. Mulch the swale and berm thoroughly.

Swales turn runoff into infiltration, recharging groundwater and creating a
moist microclimate for plants on the berm.

### Fishscale Swales

On gentle slopes, small, overlapping swales can be arranged in a fishscale
pattern to capture water distributed across a broad area. These are smaller
and less formal than full contour swales and are ideal for gardens and
orchards on modest slopes.
