# Lichen Characteristics, Ecology, and Taxonomy

## Lichen Characteristics

### Structural Identity and the Mycobiont

The characteristic [[lichen]] thallus is produced by the fungal partner (mycobiont), not the photobiont. The distinctive morphological form of a lichen is the product of the interaction between the partners, not a property of either in isolation. When mycobionts are isolated and grown on agar, they form colonies that closely resemble those of nonlichenized fungi — typically slower-growing and more compact than free-living relatives.

Scientific names applied to lichens always refer to the fungal partner. The photobiont (whether a green alga or cyanobacterium) is named independently according to its own taxonomic classification.

### Functional Characteristics

Most lichens share a suite of functional traits that distinguish them from other photosynthetic organisms:

- **Autotrophic nutrition** derived from the photosynthetic photobiont
- **Slow growth rates**, often measured in millimeters per year
- **Small size**, typically remaining at the surface of substrata
- **Long life span**, with some individuals persisting for centuries or more
- **Long-lasting vegetative morphology** — the thallus maintains its structure for extended periods
- **Mineral nutrition from airborne sources**, including dust, precipitation, and atmospheric deposition
- **Greater desiccation tolerance** than most photosynthetic organisms, enabling survival in extremely dry environments

### Phylogenetic Complexity

The fungal and photobiont partners have entirely independent phylogenies. Their integration into a functioning lichen thallus is the product of coevolution, not shared ancestry. Importantly, the fungal cells and/or photobiont cells within a single thallus are not necessarily derived from a single gene line, even within the same species. Multiple genotypes of one or both partners can coexist within what appears to be a single individual.

This genetic complexity means that the conventional concepts of "organism" and "individual" do not imply genetic coherence in lichens. A single thallus may represent a consortium of genetically distinct fungal and algal lineages.

## Lichen Ecology

### Habitat and Distribution

Lichens are found in virtually every terrestrial habitat that supports photosynthesis, and some species extend into aquatic habitats. They compete with plants for light, space, and possibly other resources, but they are generally poor competitors. Lichens represent extreme examples of Grime's (1977) stress-tolerator lifestyle strategy, competing on roughly equal terms only with bryophytes.

### Factors Affecting Presence and Abundance

Three major factors determine lichen presence and abundance:

1. **Substratum chemistry, stability, and longevity** — the physical and chemical properties of the surface on which lichens grow
2. **Light availability**, which is often mediated through competition with vascular plants and bryophytes
3. **Moisture availability**, a critical constraint on metabolic activity

### Common Substrata

Lichens colonize a wide range of surfaces:

- Rock surfaces (lithophytic species)
- Woody plant bark and wood (corticolous and lignicolous species)
- Soil and dead organic matter, particularly in low-productivity environments (terricolous and saxicolous species)
- Broad evergreen leaves in humid tropical regions (foliicolous species)

Most lichen species exhibit distinct substratum and habitat preferences, though specificity varies widely among taxa. Lichens generally interact minimally with their substrata. Some species penetrate bark tissue, and others can alter rock surface chemistry through biogeochemical weathering. Certain bark-inhabiting lichens may obtain carbohydrates from the host tree surface (Hawksworth 1988a).

### Invertebrate Communities

Lichens with thalli raised above or draped around their substrata harbor diverse invertebrate minicommunities and serve as an important food source for these organisms.

### Ecological Roles

Lichens play several ecologically significant roles:

- **Winter forage**: Important regular winter food for reindeer, caribou, and deer in boreal and arctic regions (Stevenson and Rochelle 1984; Seaward 1988). They also serve as emergency food for large mammalian herbivores during hard winters (Fox and Smith 1988; Thomas and Rosentreter 1992).
- **Small mammal food and nesting material**: Food for small mammals (Maser et al. 1986); nest material for birds and mammals (Seaward 1988; Hayward and Rosentreter 1994).
- **Indirect ecological effects**: The classic case of industrial melanism in peppered moths (Kettlewell 1973) illustrates the indirect impact of lichen community changes on other organisms.
- **Biomass contribution**: In deserts and tundra, lichens constitute a significant proportion of autotrophic biomass (Kappen 1988).
- **Nitrogen fixation**: In Pacific Northwest conifer forests, lichens contribute significantly to nitrogen fixation (Nash 1996).
- **Nutrient cycling**: In temperate and boreal forests, lichens alter nutrient availability and buffer heavy metals from atmospheric deposition (Seaward 1988).

## Taxonomy, Diversity, and Distribution

### Evolutionary Origins

Lichenization — the establishment of a mutualistic symbiosis between a fungus and a photobiont — has evolved independently several times (Gargas et al. 1995; Nash 1996). This convergent evolution has produced lichen-like associations across multiple fungal lineages.

### Fungal Groups Involved

The vast majority of lichen-forming fungi belong to the **Ascomycota**, with 14 of 46 recognized orders including lichen-forming representatives and 5 orders being exclusively lichenized. Very few **Basidiomycota** form lichens; known examples include *Omphalina* (Agaricales), *Multiclavula* (Cantharellales), and *Dictyonema* (Stereales).

Molecular phylogenetic studies have substantially revised traditional classification. Many former members of Caliciales have been shown to belong within Lecanorales (Wedin and Tibell 1997; Wedin et al. 1999). Some authorities place Peltigerales, Pertusariales, and Teloschistales within Lecanorales. Additionally, 15 lichen-forming families remain of uncertain ordinal placement (Harris 1995 suggested Aspidotheliaceae and Strigulaceae in Melanotommatales).

### Basis for Taxonomic Distinctions

Orders, families, and genera of lichen-forming fungi have traditionally been delimited primarily by fruiting body (ascocarp) characters (Henssen and Jahns 1974; Hafellner 1984, 1988). Notably, lichens with quite different morphological thalli can belong to the same genus, family, or order. For example, Teloschistaceae includes endolithic, leprose, crustose, placodioid, squamulose, foliose, and fruticose forms.

Molecular phylogenetic revisions have led to major changes in generic delimitations, as exemplified by the dismantling of *Lecidea* sensu lato (Purvis et al. 1992). Some genera include both lichenized and nonlichenized species, with nonlichenized members typically being lichenicolous (parasitic on other lichens) or saprobic. Certain species, such as *Diploschistes* and *Rhizocarpon*, are initially lichenicolous and subsequently take over the photobiont of their host.

Molecular approaches continue to modify classical morphological and chemical species concepts (Grube and Kroken 2000). Chemically identical but reproductively different taxa — termed "species pairs" (Mattsson and Lumbsch 1989) — have in some cases been shown by molecular data to represent a single species (Articus et al. 2002). Interspecific and intergeneric "mechanical" hybrids are also possible (Hawksworth 1994).

Classification changes are summarized twice yearly in *Systema Ascomycetum*, and a revised "Outline of the Ascomycetes" is maintained on Myconet (Eriksson et al. 2002). Conidial forms (anamorphs) are not given independent names but may function as spermatia or dispersal propagules. Some genera are entirely sterile or mitosporic, including *Blarneya*, *Cheiromycina*, *Flakea*, *Lepraria*, *Siphula*, and *Thamnolia*.

## Identification

Because lichen names refer exclusively to the fungal partner, and the photobiont is named independently, the current International Code of Botanical Nomenclature (ICBN) holds that one cannot strictly "identify a lichen" — only the lichen-forming fungus. The same fungal species associated with different photobionts retains the same name even if morphologies differ.

### Keys and Identification Resources

Keys to families are provided in the *Dictionary of Fungi* (Kirk et al. 2001). No comprehensive world key to genera has been published since Clements and Shear (1931). Important regional keys include Awasthi (1988, 1991), Brodo et al. (2001), Clauzade and Roux (1985), Foucard (1990), Nash et al. (2002), Poelt (1969), Poelt and Vězda (1977, 1981), Purvis et al. (1992), Swinscow and Krog (1988), Thomson (1984, 1997), and Wirth (1995a, b).

### Catalogues and Bibliographic Resources

Major catalogues include Zahlbruckner (1921–1940), Lamb (1963), Hawksworth (1972), the *Index of Fungi* (published since 1940), and the Index Fungorum web database (~340,000 species names). The *Bibliography of Systematic Mycology* has been published since 1946, and "Recent Literature on Lichens" (initiated by Culberson 1951) is now compiled by Esslinger in *The Bryologist*.

Internet resources include the LIAS web site, Sipman's Lichen Determination Keys, the American Bryological and Lichenological Society, and the British Lichen Society.

### Chemical Identification

Lichen chemistry is a critical tool for identification. Standard spot tests (K, C, P/PD, I, N, LCB) are applied to the thallus surface or internal tissues. Guides for spot test interpretation include Hale (1979), Purvis et al. (1992), Brodo et al. (2001), and Wright (1996). Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) is used for more precise secondary metabolite identification (Arup et al. 1993; Orange et al. 2001). Major chemistry compilations include Culberson (1969, 1970), Culberson et al. (1977), and Huneck and Yoshimura (1996). Computer programs for chemical identification have also been developed (Mietzsch et al. 1993).

## Major Reference Collections

The largest dried lichen collections are held at the University of Uppsala (~500,000 specimens), the Natural History Museum London (~400,000 specimens), and the Smithsonian Institution (~250,000 specimens, the largest in the USA).

Living lichen cultures are difficult to maintain. The American Type Culture Collection holds the most publicly available cultures; approximately one-third of tested species have been cultured successfully (Crittenden et al. 1995). Shaken liquid cultures are generally preferred over solid media, and liquid nitrogen preservation protocols have been developed (Smith and Onions 1994).
