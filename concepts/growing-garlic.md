# Growing Garlic

Garlic is one of the most rewarding crops for the home gardener. Successfully grown, bulbing happens while days are long and still lengthening ([[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]]). In a climate that allows it a cool-season [[mushroom-growing-period-and-crop-management]], garlic gives the highest yield and highest quality.

## Two Basic Types

### Hardnecks

- When hardnecks start bulbing, they also put up a central stalk crowned with tiny bulblets (called "scapes" or "seedstalks")
- Mature bulblets may be planted — each will make a small garlic plant that makes a small head the first year
- Often the first-year head contains only one large round clove
- Unless your intention is to rapidly increase planting stock for selling garlic, **clip off the seedstalk as soon as it appears**
- This allows the plant to put its entire resources into forming cloves below ground
- Hardnecks are difficult to plait because the stiff stalk remains

### Softnecks

- Do not make this central stalk (or always shrivels as onion leaves do)
- Are the ones you can braid for storage
- Store longer and have slightly milder flavor
- Cloves tend to be a bit smaller
- Most commercial garlic is softneck

### Elephant Garlic

- Actually not a true garlic — it's a different *Allium* species
- Has a milder, garlicky flavor
- Produces corms at the base of the bulbs (gladiolus bulbs also do this)
- Corms are inevitably overlooked at harvest and resprout, making it nearly impossible to eliminate from a bed without more than a year of persistent hoeing

## Planting

### Clove Selection

1. Break the heads up into individual cloves — each becomes one plant that forms one head containing many cloves
2. If you plant only the larger cloves for seed, you'll harvest larger heads
3. Plant flat side down, about 2 inches (5 cm) deep

### Soil Preparation

If the plants are in compacted soil, the heads will be small. Lighten the soil as though growing scallions by blending in a goodly amount of compost or well-aged manure before planting.

### Timing

| Climate | Planting Time |
|---------|--------------|
| Mild, no soil freeze | Autumn (before winter) |
| Soil freezes | Late autumn with mulch protection |
| Spring-only option | As early as possible after the equinox |

**Key insight**: The size of the head is completely determined by the size the top has achieved before lengthening days trigger bulbing. If you want big heads, push the tops for size during the months before bulbing begins.

### Overwintering

Even where the soil freezes, garlic should overwinter if given some mulch before the first hard freeze. The little plants survive winter and start growing again first thing in spring.

## Fertilizer Strategy

- Give them fertile soil — big tops before bulbing means big heads
- Side-dress the garlic about a month before bulbing begins
- Use COF ([[complete-organic-fertilizer]]), or [[chicken-manure]] compost
- Your entire garlic harvest will be higher-quality if dug from soil that is just barely damp at harvest time
- Dig a plant once a week to learn by observation how your variety matures

## Harvest

### Timing Signals

- Harvest plants when the cloves have fully segmented but before the wrappers deteriorate
- Dig one every week to learn your variety's maturity timing
- Gently shake off the soil — **bursting heads won't ever store well**

### Curing

1. Dig all remaining plants at peak maturity
2. Lay them in the shade with good air circulation — **lots of air circulation is crucial**
3. After a few days, strip off 2-3 layers of outer wrappers
4. Continue drying until the leaves have completely shriveled
5. Clip them off, leaving about 1 inch of stem above the bulb
6. Some growers braid garlic after it has fully dried and then remoisten the stems to make them pliable — but if you don't hang braids in the kitchen where humidity from cooking will encourage mold, this practice is fine

### Storage

- Softnecks store longest
- Hardnecks should be used first
- Store in a cool, dry, well-ventilated place
- Do not refrigerate — refrigeration can trigger sprouting

## Pests and Diseases

### Above Ground

Insects and disease are usually not a problem above ground.

### Below Ground: Rots

Rots will happen if the soil is too moist when bulbing is close to finishing. This is why harvesting when soil is barely damp matters.

### Virus Diseases ("Yellows")

- Virus diseases infect garlic and carry over from year to year in the cloves
- They cause premature yellowing of the leaves and considerably reduce yield
- It's to your advantage to start out with disease-free seed
- Once infected, the only cure is to start with clean stock

## Varieties

### Daylength Sensitivity

- Most varieties available in seed catalogs are bred for temperate latitudes
- Tropical varieties don't perform well farther from the equator
- In Australia, much imported garlic is from tropical China — these varieties don't perform well

### Finding Planting Stock

Visit ethnic specialty markets (Greeks and Italians use a lot of garlic) and see if you can find locally grown garlic for sale — use that for planting stock. This saves money compared to buying "seed" garlic from catalogs, and the variety will already be adapted to your local conditions.

## The Business Consideration

At first look, growing garlic seems an attractive homestead enterprise needing little land to make a big return. But there's one major obstacle: **the labor crunch that comes at harvest time**. There are never enough hands in the family to harvest as much garlic as there is labor to grow. The weather risk is also significant — rain just before harvest can cause rots.

**Advice**: Before investing in this as a business, figure out how to grow two or three different varieties whose maturity is spread out over enough weeks to stage the harvest. Then increase your own planting stock gradually.
