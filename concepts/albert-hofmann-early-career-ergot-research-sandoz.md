## Albert Hofmann — Early Career and Ergot Research at Sandoz

## Overview

Albert Hofmann (1906-2008) was a Swiss chemist whose work on ergot alkaloids at Sandoz Pharmaceuticals led to the discovery of LSD-25 in 1938 and its extraordinary psychoactive properties in 1943. This concept page covers Hofmann"s early scientific training, his approach to pharmaceutical chemistry at Sandoz, and the ergot research program that set the stage for the most accidental and consequential discovery in the history of psychopharmacology.

## Education and Training

Hofmann studied chemistry at the University of Zurich, earning his doctorate with distinction. His training emphasized organic chemistry and natural product isolation—skills that would prove essential for his later work on the complex alkaloids produced by the ergot fungus.

## The Sandoz Pharmaceutical-Chemical Department

### Professor Arthur Stoll's Vision

When Hofmann joined Sandoz in the 1920s, the pharmaceutical-chemical department was still small—four research chemists with doctoral degrees and three in production. The department was led by Professor Arthur Stoll, who had established a clear research program: "to isolate the active principles (i.e., the effective constituents) of known medicinal plants to produce pure specimens of these substances."

This approach was revolutionary for its time. Most medicinal plants were used as crude extracts with variable potency. Stoll recognized that the active principles of many important plants were unstable or variable in concentration, making exact dosage impossible. By isolating pure compounds, it became possible to manufacture "stable pharmaceutical preparations, exactly quantifiable by weight."

### Target Plants

Stoll"s laboratory focused on plants of "recognized value" that were difficult to use in their crude form:
- **Foxglove (Digitalis)**: Cardiac glycosides for heart conditions
- **Mediterranean squill (Scilla maritima)**: Cardiac stimulants and diuretics
- **Ergot of rye (Claviceps purpurea)**: The fungus that would become Hofmann"s life's work

## Mediterranean Squill Research

Hofmann's first years at Sandoz were devoted almost exclusively to Mediterranean squill, working under Dr. Walter Kreis. This early training in the isolation and characterization of plant-derived cardiac glycosides gave him the methodological foundation for his later work on the far more complex ergot alkaloids.

The squill work taught Hofmann the practical challenges of natural product chemistry: the need for large quantities of plant material, the difficulty of separating closely related compounds, and the importance of pharmacological testing to identify therapeutic activity.

## Ergot of Rye — The Fateful Research Program

### Historical Context

Ergot (Claviceps purpurea) is a parasitic fungus that infects rye and other cereal grains, producing dark, horn-shaped sclerotia (ergot bodies) in place of normal grain kernels. Ergotism—ergot poisoning—had been known since the Middle Ages, when it caused epidemics of St. Anthony's Fire (gangrene and convulsions) among populations that consumed contaminated grain.

Despite its toxic properties, ergot had been used in folk medicine for centuries, particularly in obstetrics, where midwives administered crude ergot preparations to accelerate labor and control postpartum hemorrhage. The problem was that ergot's potency varied enormously and its active constituents were unstable, making it extremely dangerous to use without precise dosage control.

### Ergotamine

Professor Stoll had already achieved a major success with ergot before Hofmann joined the lab. In 1918, Stoll succeeded in isolating ergotamine—the first pure ergot alkaloid to be obtained. This was a landmark achievement: ergotamine was a water-soluble alkaloid with defined pharmacological properties, suitable for pharmaceutical use. It quickly became the standard treatment for migraine headaches and found applications in obstetrics.

### Lysergic Acid as the Common Structural Element

The isolation of multiple ergot alkaloids revealed that they all shared a common structural core: lysergic acid. This discovery was crucial because it meant that by modifying the lysergic acid molecule—adding different chemical groups to its basic structure—it might be possible to create new compounds with specific, predictable pharmacological properties.

Lysergic acid is a complex tetracyclic molecule (four fused rings) containing both indole and amide functional groups. Its structural similarity to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine), discovered decades later, explains why LSD and related compounds have such profound effects on the central nervous system.

## Hofmann's Synthetic Program

### Improving Ergobasine

Hofmann's first major contribution was improving ergobasine (also called ergonovine), a water-soluble ergot alkaloid with uterotonic properties. By substituting the propanolamine radical in ergobasine with butanolamine, Hofmann and his colleague Dr. J. Peyer created a compound that "surpassed the natural alkaloid in its therapeutic properties." This improved ergobasine, marketed as Methergine, became "the leading medicament for this indication in obstetrics" worldwide.

### The LSD-25 Synthesis

Hofmann then turned to creating new lysergic acid compounds "for which uterotonic activity was not prominent, but from which, on the basis of their chemical structure, other types of interesting pharmacological properties could be expected."

In 1938, he produced the twenty-fifth substance in this series: **lysergic acid diethylamide**, abbreviated **LSD-25** (Lyserg-säure-diäthylamid). The synthesis involved attaching two ethyl groups to the amide nitrogen of lysergic acid—a seemingly minor modification that would prove to have extraordinary consequences.

### The Intended Purpose

Hofmann "planned the synthesis of this compound with the intention of obtaining a circulatory and respiratory stimulant (an analeptic)." The reasoning was structural: LSD showed similarity to nicotinic acid diethylamide (Coramine), a known respiratory stimulant. The diethylamide group, Hofmann hypothesized, might confer analeptic properties.

During initial testing, LSD-25 showed some activity but nothing remarkable enough to warrant further investigation. It was set aside, and Hofmann moved on to other compounds. Five years later, a "peculiar presentiment" led him to resynthesize LSD-25—and the rest is history.

## The Methodological Significance

Hofmann"s approach exemplifies the classical pharmaceutical chemistry paradigm: systematic modification of natural product scaffolds to create new compounds with improved or novel pharmacological properties. This approach, which produced aspirin from salicylic acid and methergine from ergobasine, also produced LSD from lysergic acid. The difference is that LSD's "pharmacological properties" turned out to be far more profound and far less medically controllable than anyone had imagined.
