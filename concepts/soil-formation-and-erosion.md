## Soil Formation and Erosion

## Overview

Soil is the thin living mantle that covers the earth, described by [[permaculture-design]].

## Soil Formation Processes

### Rock Weathering

Soils form through the breakdown of parent rock by several mechanisms:

- **Chemical weathering** — water combines with common minerals such as felspars in rock, causing them to swell and fracture. The felspars then hydrate to kaolin, clay, and potassium carbonate. The carbonates released make soil water a stronger carbonic acid than rain itself.
- **Oxidation** — atmospheric oxygen dissolves in rain to oxidize iron minerals (pyrites), forming haematite and sulphuric acid, which further dissolves metals
- **Physical weathering** — the expansion of water as ice, and the grinding power of glaciers, reduce rocks to flour
- **Biological weathering** — plants wedge open rocks and mineral particles, recreate acids, and transport minerals in their sap to other locations

### Soil Types by Origin

Soils can be classified by their origin as:

- **Residual soils** — resting in place over their parent rock
- **Transported soils** — moved by water, ice, gravity, or wind

The key factors in soil formation are **rock type**, **climate**, and **topography** (landform).

### The Rate of Soil Formation

Topsoil builds at an estimated rate of approximately **2-4 tonnes per hectare per year** as uncompacted topsoil. This is a remarkably slow process relative to human timescales. However, through cropping and soil tillage, we remove soil at rates of **40-500 tonnes per year** — up to 125 times faster than it forms.

In the worst cases, a single erosion season — one sequence of flood or strong wind over cultivated soils — can remove **2,000 years of accumulated soil**. Even the most ideal tillage practices can only just keep pace with the most ideal conditions of soil formation.

## The Soil Conservation Imperative

Mollison identifies only four places where soils are conserved or increased:

1. **Uncut forests** — where root systems, leaf litter, and canopy protection maintain soil integrity
2. **Under the quiet water of lakes and ponds** — where sediments settle and accumulate
3. **Prairies and meadows of permanent plants** — where perennial root systems hold soil in place
4. **Where plants are grown with mulched or non-tillage systems** — where soil disturbance is minimized

These are, Mollison notes, "the core subjects of sustainable societies of any conceivable future" — yet they are not the subjects most taught in agricultural colleges or forestry courses.

## Global Soil Loss

The scale of soil degradation is staggering. Australia, where Mollison lived and wrote, has perhaps only **30% of its original soils in fair condition**. The rest have been washed or blown away, or sadly depleted in structure and yield. This pattern holds true for most countries where extractive agriculture and forestry occurs.

## The Values Framework for Soil Management

Mollison argues that before approaching the technical subject of soils, we must consider several value frameworks:

### Health

Both human and plant health require careful, conservative approaches, especially regarding biocides and high levels of artificial fertilizers. Our ancestors lived to ripe old ages on home-grown produce without herbicides, pesticides, or artificial fertilizers. The goal should be to improve on, not lessen, that factor of long-tested vitality.

Beyond diet, gardening confers health benefits through:

- **Mild but regular exercise** — gardening as a steady, non-stressing form of movement
- **Meditation** — the ability to sit, observe, and reduce stress
- **Meaning** — a conscious sense of worthwhile work of direct value to others ("right livelihood")
- **Lifelong interest** — constant change, new observations, and continuous learning

### Yield

The yield impasse represents a fundamental tension in agriculture. There is no doubt that a ploughed and fertilized monoculture, supported by chemicals and large energy inputs, can out-yield almost every other production system on a once-off basis. But critical questions arise:

- At what public cost?
- For how long can it be maintained?
- With what improvement in nutrition?
- With what guarantee of sustainability?
- With what effect on world hunger, soils, and human health?

There is now abundant proof that forced yields are temporary and that plough cultures destroy soils and societies.

### Ethics

Mollison draws an important ethical distinction: the purpose of people on earth is not to reduce all soils to perfectly balanced market gardens. While this is achievable and necessary on the approximately 4% of the earth needed for food production, the larger job is the **restoration of soils and forests for the sake of a healthy earth itself**.

Without poorly drained, naturally deficient, leached, acidic, or alkaline sites, many plant species would disappear — they have evolved specifically to occupy less-than-perfect soil conditions.

### Land Restoration Potential

[[colin-tudge]] estimated that perhaps 60% of the British Isles could be given back to nature. [[john-jeavons]] estimated, based on garden productivity, that we could return perhaps 94% of land to its own purposes. While this may not be achievable in the near term, Mollison argues that we can start the process and our children can continue it, developing new forests and wilderness to explore.

## The Role of Life in Soil

Soil organisms are a major soil factor with profound effects on pH, mineral content and availability, soil structure, and erosion. A living soil — teeming with bacteria, fungi, earthworms, arthropods, and plant roots — is fundamentally different from dead mineral substrate. The biological component is what transforms geological material into the living matrix that supports terrestrial life.

## Practical Soil Rehabilitation

In soil rehabilitation, we are forced to start with what is currently present. Most gardeners and farmers who have developed sustainable soil systems allow:

- **3-4 years for building a garden** from reasonable starting conditions
- **5-15 years to restore a devastated soil landscape**

This applies only to physical restoration and development of appropriate plant systems. Far more lengthy processes are required where past chemical pollution has occurred.

## Soil as a Complex System

Soils defy precise treatment. Their structure, permeability, organic content, gaseous components, minerals, pH, and water status change from hour to hour with soil depth and treatment, and in response to micro-elevations. Many soils are originally complex mixtures derived from a variety of rock types with very long and varied histories. This inherent complexity demands humility and adaptive management rather than rigid prescriptions.
