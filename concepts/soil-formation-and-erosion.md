# Soil Formation and Erosion

## Introduction

Soil is one of the most complex and variable materials on Earth. Its structure, permeability, organic content, gaseous components, mineral composition, pH, and water status change from hour to hour with soil depth and treatment, and in response to micro-elevations. Many soils are complex mixtures derived from a variety of rock types with long and varied histories. Despite extensive scientific study, topsoil is being lost at an ever-increasing rate worldwide—Australia, where Mollison lived and wrote, has perhaps 30% of its original soils in fair condition.

## Soil Formation Processes

### Parent Material and Weathering

Soils form through the breakdown of parent rock by multiple agents:

- **Water**: Combines with common minerals such as felspars in rock, causing them to swell and fracture, then hydrating felspars to kaolin, clay, and potassium carbonate. Carbonates released make soil water a stronger carbonic acid than rain alone.
- **Atmospheric oxygen**: Dissolves in rain to oxidise iron minerals (pyrites), forming haematite and sulphuric acid, which further dissolves metals
- **Ice**: Expansion of water as ice fractures rock; glaciers grind rocks to flour
- **Plants**: Wedge open rocks and mineral particles, recreate acids, and transport minerals in sap to other locations

### Soil Formation Rate

Topsoil builds at approximately **2–4 tonnes per hectare per year** as uncompacted topsoil. Against this, conventional cropping and soil tillage removes soil at a rate of **40–500 tonnes per year**. Even the most ideal tillage only keeps pace with the most ideal conditions of soil formation. In the worst cases, **2000 years of soil can be removed in a single erosion season**—one sequence of flood or strong wind over cultivated soils.

### Soil Classification by Origin

Soils can be:
- **Residual**: Resting in place over their present parent rock
- **Transported**: Moved by water, ice, gravity, or wind

Key factors in soil formation are rock type, climate, and topography (landform).

## Where Soils Are Conserved or Increased

Soil is only conserved or increased in four situations:

1. **In uncut forests**: The forest canopy, root systems, and leaf litter protect and build soil
2. **Under the quiet water of lakes and ponds**: Sedimentation deposits organic matter and mineral particles
3. **In prairies and meadows of permanent plants**: Deep root systems and continuous ground cover prevent erosion
4. **Where plants are grown with mulched or non-tillage systems**: Soil disturbance is minimised, organic matter is preserved

These represent the core subjects of sustainable societies. They are notably absent from the subjects most taught in agricultural colleges or forestry courses, nor do they occupy the minds of politicians, investment bankers, or media figures.

## The Rate of Soil Loss

The disparity between soil formation and soil loss is staggering:

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Soil formation rate | 2–4 t/ha/year |
| Soil loss (cropping) | 40–500 t/year |
| Worst-case single event | 2000 years of soil in one erosion season |

This means modern agriculture is effectively mining soil—a non-renewable resource on human timescales.

## Value Frameworks for Soil Management

Mollison identifies several value frameworks through which to consider soils:

### Health (Human and Plant)

A conservative and careful approach is needed, especially regarding biocides and high levels of artificial fertilisers. Ancestors lived to a ripe old age on home-grown produce without herbicides, pesticides, or artificial fertilisers. Additional health factors associated with gardening include:

- **Mild but regular exercise**: Gardening as a steady, non-stressing form of physical activity
- **Meditation**: The capacity to sit, observe, and reduce mental stress
- **Meaning and purpose**: A conscious sense of worthwhile work and direct value to others ("right livelihood")
- **Life interest**: Constant change, new observations, and continuous learning across seasons

### Yield

While ploughed and fertilised monocultures supported by chemicals can out-yield almost every other production system in the short term, critical questions remain:

- At what public cost?
- For how long can it be maintained?
- With what improvement in nutrition?
- With what guarantee of sustainability?
- With what effect on world hunger, soils, and human health?

Abundant proof now exists that such forced yields are temporary, and that plough cultures destroy both soils and societies.

### Life in Soil

Soil organisms are a major soil factor with myriad effects on pH, mineral content and availability, soil structure, and erosion. The soil food web—bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, arthropods, and larger organisms—is essential to soil function.

### Ethics

The purpose of people on Earth is not to reduce all soils to perfectly balanced market gardens. Soil management strategies should focus on:

- The approximately **4% of Earth** needed for food production
- The **60% of agricultural soils** already ruined by the plough or polluted by emissions
- **Restoration of soils and forests** for the health of the Earth itself
- Ceasing deforestation and land destruction

### Biodiversity

Without poorly drained, naturally deficient, leached, acidic, or alkaline sites, many plant species would disappear. They have evolved in response to difficult conditions and have specialised to occupy less-than-perfect soil sites. Soil "improvement" that eliminates this variability threatens the very biodiversity that makes ecosystems resilient.

## Land Recovery Potential

Colin Tudge (*New Scientist*, 1986) estimated that perhaps **60% of the British Isles** could be returned to nature. John Jeavons, estimating on the basis of gardens, suggests we could return perhaps **94% of land** to natural systems. While full recovery may not happen in a decade, starting the process and enabling future generations to continue developing new forests and wilderness is both possible and necessary.

## The Role of Practical Knowledge

Soil science concentrates heavily on classification—what is there—but not on how to evolve soil. Gardeners and farmers are often the practical, innovative, and successful group, creating good soil through water control, modest aeration, and plant and animal management. Science excels at explaining why things work (making skills teachable) but is less effective at initiating field work or training people already working in the field.

Farmers' field days advance knowledge more effectively than scientific papers, and local educational sessions more than either. The coalition of scientific understanding and practical field experience is slowly beginning to develop through joint concern at a private level.

## Soil Rehabilitation Timelines

- **3–4 years**: Building a garden from average soil
- **5–15 years**: Restoring a devastated soil landscape
- **Decades or longer**: Rehabilitation where past chemical pollution has occurred

The physical restoration of soils and development of appropriate plant systems is only the first step; far more lengthy processes are needed where chemical pollution has occurred.
