# Soil Formation and Values

## Overview

Soil is the foundational living system upon which all terrestrial life depends. In [[query-what-are-predatory-mites-and-how-can-i-use-them-for-pest-control|what]] threatens [[bill-mollison]] frames soil not merely as a substrate for growing plants but as a complex, living medium with deep connections to human health, ecological stability, and ethical responsibility.

## Soil Formation Processes

Soil formation is a slow, multi-faceted process driven by physical, chemical, and biological agents acting on parent rock material over geological timescales. Three primary agents drive this decomposition:

### Atmospheric and Chemical Weathering

- Atmospheric oxygen dissolves in rainwater, creating a weak but persistent oxidising agent.
- This dissolved oxygen oxidises iron minerals such as pyrites, transforming them into haematite and releasing sulphuric acid as a byproduct.
- The sulphuric acid further accelerates rock decomposition, contributing to the gradual breakdown of parent material into mineral soils.

### Physical Weathering by Water

- Water is one of the most effective rock decomposers in nature.
- When water freezes in rock crevices, its expansion (approximately 9% volume increase) wedges rocks apart, a process called frost wedging.
- Glaciers grind entire landscapes over millennia, reducing bedrock to fine mineral flour (glacial till) that serves as raw material for soil development.
- The mechanical action of flowing water in streams and rivers further erodes and sorts rock particles by size.

### Biological Weathering by Plants

- Plant roots physically wedge open cracks in rocks as they grow and seek nutrients and water.
- Plants secrete organic acids (such as humic and carbonic acids) at root surfaces, which chemically dissolve rock minerals and release essential nutrients.
- Deep-rooted plants act as mineral transporters, drawing nutrients from subsoil layers and depositing them on the surface through leaf fall and decomposition, progressively enriching topsoil horizons.

## Soil Creation and Destruction Rates

One of the most critical insights in permaculture is the stark imbalance between the rate at which soils are created and the rate at which modern agriculture destroys them.

### Natural Soil Building

- Uncompacted topsoil accumulates at a rate of approximately **2–4 tonnes per hectare per year**.
- This slow accumulation represents thousands of years of geological and biological work concentrated into a thin, fertile layer.

### Agricultural Soil Loss

- Conventional cropping and tillage systems remove topsoil at rates of **40–500 tonnes per hectare per year** — orders of magnitude faster than it can be replaced.
- In the worst documented cases, **2,000 years of soil formation** can be lost in a single erosion season under intensive agriculture.
- This unsustainable rate of loss constitutes a civilisational-level threat, as societies throughout history have collapsed following soil depletion.

## Environments That Conserve or Build Soil

Mollison identifies specific environments where soils are naturally conserved or actively increased:

- **Uncut forests** — The forest canopy and understorey [[soil-ph]], cycle minerals, create soil structure through aggregation, and significantly reduce erosion risk.
- [[succession-planting]], green manures, composting, and erosion control.
- Rehabilitation requires patience and consistent application of [[permaculture-principles]], but the results demonstrate nature's inherent resilience when given the right conditions.

## The Soil-Plant-Animal Health Triangle

Soil, plant, and animal health are intimately connected through biological relationships:

- Healthy soils contain natural antibiotics produced by beneficial microorganisms, which suppress pathogenic organisms.
- Plants grown in balanced soils develop natural disease resistance through adequate mineral nutrition and immune-competent metabolism.
- Animals (including humans) consuming these plants receive broader nutritional support and greater natural resistance to disease.

## Global Food Systems and Disease

Mollison critiques the global food market system on several grounds:

- Industrial agriculture produces what he terms **"agricologenic" diseases** — illnesses caused or exacerbated by the methods and products of industrial farming itself.
- These include nutrient deficiencies from depleted soils, chemical residues from pesticides and fertilisers, and the loss of food diversity.
- The homogenisation and long-distance transport of food creates systemic vulnerability to contamination and supply disruption.

## The House Garden as Resilient System

In contrast to global supply chains, the house garden represents a model of **limited, localised risk**:

- Production is distributed across millions of small sites rather than concentrated in monocultural regions.
- Diversity within a home garden provides natural pest and disease buffers.
- The gardener has direct control over soil management, input quality, and harvest timing.
- Local production eliminates dependence on fragile transport and distribution networks.

## The Jeavons Estimate

[[john-jeavons]], founder of the Grow Biointensive method, estimates that with intensive small-scale food production:

- Only **6% of current agricultural land** would be needed to feed the human population using biointensive methods.
- This implies that approximately **94% of agricultural land** could be returned to nature — reforested, allowed to revert to prairie, or designated as wildlife habitat.
- This extraordinary figure illustrates the vast inefficiency of industrial agriculture and the transformative potential of small-scale, soil-conscious food production.

## Key Principles

1. **Soil is alive** — treat it as a living system, not an inert substrate.
