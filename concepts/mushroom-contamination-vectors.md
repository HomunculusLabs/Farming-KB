## The Six Vectors of Contamination

The six vectors through which contamination enters mushroom cultures
were codified by [[growing-gourmet-abalone-oyster-cystidiosus-cultivation]] and Medicinal
Mushrooms." Each vector represents a distinct pathway that must be
managed and minimized throughout the cultivation process.

### 1. The Cultivator

The human body teems with microorganisms. Skin, hair, breath, and
clothing all carry potential contaminants. The cultivator is the
single most significant source of contamination in most operations.
Hands must be washed with antibacterial soap every 20-30 minutes during
active laboratory work. After washing, hands should be disinfected with
80% isopropyl alcohol before handling any sterile materials. Clothing
should be clean, and in professional settings, dedicated laboratory
coats are worn. Face masks may be necessary when working over open
sterile media. The cultivator must develop a constant awareness of
where their body is positioned relative to the sterile airstream.

### 2. The Air

The air is a sea of microorganisms, carrying spores, bacteria, and
other particulates. In a typical indoor environment, thousands of
contaminant spores per cubic foot are present at any given time. A
three-door entry system is recommended for laboratory access, creating
airlocks that reduce the introduction of outside air. The laboratory
should be maintained under positive pressure using HEPA-filtered air.
HEPA (High Efficiency Particulate Air) filters eliminate 99.99% of
particulates down to 0.3 microns. A 1/8 HP blower pushing 200 CFM
through a 12x12x6 inch [[water-activity-osmophilic-fungal-media-preparation]] can introduce
contaminants, particularly if it contains chlorine or mineral
imbalances. Chlorinated water can inhibit [[comparison-laminar-flow-hood-vs-fruiting-chamber]], should be
wiped down with alcohol before and after each work session. Filter
discs on spawn jars can accumulate organic debris on their undersides,
providing a medium for contaminants to grow through into the jar.
