## Tip Growth Mechanism

Unlike plant cells that expand through diffuse growth across their
surfaces, fungal hyphae grow exclusively at their tips through a process
called apical extension. This polarized growth is driven by the targeted
delivery of secretory vesicles to the hyphal apex. These vesicles,
transported along cytoskeletal tracks, carry cell wall precursors,
membrane material, and enzymes to the growing tip. Upon fusion with the
plasma membrane, they release their contents, extending the cell wall
and plasma membrane simultaneously. The accumulation of vesicles at the
tip creates a visible region called the Spitzenkörper — a dense,
vesicle-rich body that serves as the organizing center for hyphal
growth. The Spitzenkörper is functionally analogous to the growth cone
of animal neurons and represents one of the most remarkable examples of
polarized cell growth in biology.

## Turgor Pressure and Cell Wall Dynamics

Hyphal extension is powered by turgor pressure — the hydrostatic
pressure generated within the cell by the influx of water through
osmosis. As the cell wall is loosened at the tip by wall-degrading
enzymes, the rigid portions of the wall farther back resist expansion,
concentrating the outward force at the apex. This combination of
targeted wall softening and uniform turgor pressure creates a
self-regulating system: if the wall weakens too much, the cell bursts;
if it is too rigid, growth stalls. The delicate balance between wall
synthesis and wall degradation at the hyphal tip allows fungi to
maintain remarkably consistent diameters despite enormous variation in
growth rates and environmental conditions.

## Septate vs. Coenocytic Hyphae

Fungal hyphae exhibit two fundamental architectural patterns. Septate
hyphae are divided into discrete cellular compartments by cross-walls
called septa. These septa typically contain central pores that allow
cytoplasm and even organelles to flow between adjacent cells, so the
hypha retains many characteristics of a single continuous organism even
though it is subdivided. In contrast, coenocytic (non-septate) hyphae
lack septa entirely, forming continuous multinucleate tubes. This
condition, called coenocytic or aseptate growth, is characteristic of
zygomycetes and some early-diverging fungal lineages. Coenocytic hyphae
can grow very rapidly because no energy is invested in constructing
septa, but they are more vulnerable to damage — a single breach can
release the entire cytoplasmic contents of the filament.

## Mycelial Networks and Foraging

Individual hyphae branch repeatedly to form mycelial networks that can
cover enormous areas. These networks function as integrated foraging
systems, exploring the environment, locating nutrient sources, and
transporting materials over considerable distances. Cytoplasmic streaming
within the hyphae distributes nutrients, signaling molecules, and even
nuclei throughout the network, allowing the mycelium to respond as a
coordinated whole to environmental stimuli. When a nutrient-rich
substrate is encountered, the mycelium can concentrate biomass and
enzymatic activity at that location. When nutrients are depleted, the
network can reorganize itself, abandoning unproductive regions and
redirecting resources toward more promising areas. This remarkable
