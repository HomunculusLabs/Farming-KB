# Freshwater Fungi: Survey and Collection Methods

## Overview

Freshwater habitats host a remarkably diverse assemblage of fungi, including chytridiomycetes, hyphochytriomycetes, peronosporomycetes (formerly oomycetes), ascomycetes, and mitosporic fungi. These organisms play critical ecological roles as decomposers of plant and animal tissues, parasites of algae, aquatic macrophytes, fish, amphibians, and invertebrates, and as saprobes cycling nutrients through freshwater ecosystems. Freshwater habitats have disappeared at alarming rates, making documentation of their fungal [[fungal-inventory-and-survey-methods]] inhabitants an urgent priority.

## The Major Groups of Freshwater Fungi

### Chytridiomycetes and Hyphochytriomycetes

These are the "true" aquatic fungi [[fungal-taxonomy-and-kingdom-fungi]] and fungus-like straminipiles that disperse through water using motile, flagellated zoospores. They are treated together because they are outwardly similar, occupy the same habitats, and are studied with identical techniques.

- **Chytridiomycetes**: True Fungi with zoospores bearing a single posteriorly-directed flagellum. Five orders are recognised: Chytridiales (the most diverse, with ~80 genera and 500+ species), Spizellomycetales (primarily soil-dwelling), Blastocladiales (includes parasites of invertebrates and the model organism *Allomyces*), Monoblepharidales (four families, all saprotrophic), and Neocallimastigales (obligate anaerobes from herbivore guts).
- **Hyphochytriomycetes**: Allied with straminipiles, producing zoospores with a single anterior tinsellated flagellum. A single order with three families and four genera.

### Peronosporomycetes (Oomycetes)

These fungus-like organisms include important parasites of aquatic plants and animals. Many cause devastating diseases of fish and amphibians. They are studied alongside true fungi in freshwater surveys because of their ecological importance and similar collection [[freshwater-fungi-diversity-and-sampling]] methods.

### Ascomycetes and Mitosporic Fungi

Freshwater ascomycetes include both species that fruit on submerged wood and plant debris (many with unique morphological adaptations for aquatic life) and species that are primarily terrestrial but periodically found in aquatic habitats. The Ingoldian fungi [[fungal-taxonomy-and-kingdom-fungi]]—a group of asexual ascomycetes that produce distinctive tetraradiate or sigmoid conidia adapted for dispersal in flowing water—are among the most characteristic and well-studied freshwater fungi.

## Collection Methods

### Chytrids and Hyphochytrids

Chytrids are microscopic [[freshwater-fungi-diversity-and-sampling]] and cannot be collected by unaided sight or standard microbiological methods such as dilution plating. They are found by examining natural substrata under a microscope or, more commonly, by using baiting techniques.

#### Baiting Technique

The standard method for detecting aquatic chytrids and hyphochytrids:

1. Collect organic debris and water from the study site. A small amount of bottom organic matter (1-2 waterlogged leaves, a small aquatic plant, or 10+ cc of detritus) plus water from the same habitat is added to a deep glass Petri plate or finger bowl.

2. Add baits: two pieces each (approximately 0.5-1 cm × 0.5-1 cm) of cellophane, onion skin, and chitin, plus a thin shower of pollen. This is called a "gross culture."

3. Incubate at a temperature near that of the water at the collection site. The gross culture should consist mostly of water with a small amount of bait and debris—excess organic matter encourages bacterial growth over chytrids.

4. Chytrids typically appear on pollen grains after 1-2 days and on other baits after several days. Many cellulosic chytrids have generation times of 4-7 days. Gross cultures may continue yielding fungi for weeks.

#### Field Baiting

For phenological studies, baits are enclosed in weighted (sterilised glass marbles work well) nylon net bags and placed directly into the habitat. Pollen is wrapped in lens paper so grains become enmeshed among cellulose fibres and retained in the bag. Bags are retrieved at intervals and baits examined microscopically.

#### Substrata Collection

- Collect submerged leaves, twigs, fruits, seeds, and other plant material with visible signs of fungal colonisation.
- Collect dead or moribund algae, particularly filamentous forms.
- Preserve specimens in vials with water from the collection site (not preservative) and keep cool during transport.
- Label each collection with date, location, habitat type, water temperature, and substratum description.

### Ascomycetes on Submerged Wood

- Collect submerged wood (branches, logs, sticks) bearing fruiting bodies (perithecia or apothecia).
- Return to the laboratory, rinse gently, and incubate in a moist chamber (plastic box with damp paper towels) at room temperature.
- Many species continue to sporulate in moist chambers, allowing identification.
- Sort and examine specimens under a dissecting microscope within 24-48 hours.

### Ingoldian Fungi

- Collect foam from the surface of streams and rivers using a wide-mouth jar.
- Foam concentrates the tetraradiate conidia characteristic of this group.
- Examine foam samples microscopically within hours of collection; conidia degrade quickly.
- Alternatively, set out baits (sterilised leaves, cellophane) in streams for 2-4 weeks, then retrieve and examine.

## Identification

### Microscopy

Chytrids and hyphochytrids require compound microscopy for identification. Key features include:

- Zoospore ultrastructure (flagellum type, number, position)
- Sporangium morphology (operculate vs. inoperculate, ornamentation)
- Rhizoidal system (structure, extent)
- Developmental pattern (monocentric vs. polycentric)

### Reference Works

Key identification resources for freshwater fungi include:

## Isolation and Culturing

### Chytrid Isolation

Isolate individual chytrid sporangia under a dissecting microscope using a fine needle or micropipette. Transfer to fresh bait plates with antibiotic-amended water to suppress bacterial growth. Many chytrids can be maintained on agar media with appropriate nutrients, though some remain unculturable.

### Ascomycete Isolation

Isolate from single spores or from bits of colonised substratum placed on selective media. Common media include cornmeal agar, potato dextrose agar, and water agar with sterile plant material.

### Culture Preservation

Maintain cultures by periodic transfer (every 2-4 months) or preserve in sterile water or mineral oil at room temperature for longer-term storage. Cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen is preferred for valuable isolates.

## Assessing Biodiversity and Abundance

### Quantitative Methods

- **Most Probable Number (MPN)**: Adapted from bacteriology, this method estimates zoospore concentrations by baiting serial dilutions of water samples.
- **Direct counting**: Examine substrata microscopically and count sporangia or fruiting bodies per unit area.
- **Molecular methods**: Environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding from water samples or substratum surfaces is increasingly used to detect species not recoverable by traditional methods.

### Phenological Monitoring

Place bait bags at fixed stations and retrieve at regular intervals (weekly or monthly) to track seasonal patterns in fungal activity and species composition.

## Conservation Concerns

Freshwater habitats are among the most threatened ecosystems globally. Pollution, channelisation, damming, and agricultural runoff have drastically altered freshwater fungal communities. Documenting current biodiversity provides essential baseline data against which future changes can be measured. The pathogenic chytrid *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*, responsible for amphibian declines worldwide, exemplifies the ecological significance of freshwater fungi and the need for comprehensive survey work.
