# Plant Ultraviolet and Infrared Light Perception

## Overview

[[daniel-chamovitz]]'s "What a Plant Knows" establishes that plants perceive light across a much broader range of the [[electromagnetic-spectrum-plant-light-perception]] than humans.

While human vision is confined to approximately 400-700 nanometers ([[plant-photoreception-visible-light]]), plants detect and respond to ultraviolet light below 380 nm and infrared (far-red) light above 700 nm.

This expanded perceptual range gives plants [[chamovitz-gradualist-framework-plant-sensory-capabilities]] that humans lack entirely, allowing them to detect environmental conditions that are invisible to us.

## The Electromagnetic Spectrum and Plant Perception

Light is electromagnetic radiation, and different wavelengths carry different types of information about the environment.

Chamovitz draws the analogy that just as radio antennas are sized to receive specific radio wavelengths, photoreceptors are molecular structures sized to absorb specific light wavelengths.

Blue light is the shortest visible wavelength (~400-450 nm), while red light is the longest (~620-700 nm).

Plants perceive colors beyond this range in both directions, sensing UV and far-red radiation that humans cannot detect.

## Ultraviolet Light Perception

### UV as Environmental Information

Plants can detect ultraviolet light, the same radiation that causes sunburn in humans.

UV light is not merely a source of damage for plants; it also carries ecologically valuable information about the plant's environment.

UV radiation intensity varies with altitude, latitude, time of day, season, and cloud cover. By sensing UV levels, plants can indirectly assess their position relative to the sun, the time of day, and even their elevation or latitude.

### UV-B and the DNA Damage Response

Plants possess specific photoreceptors for UV-B radiation (280-315 nm) called UVR8.

When UVR8 absorbs UV-B photons, it undergoes a conformational change that initiates a signaling cascade leading to the production of protective compounds.

This system allows plants to mount a preemptive defense against UV-induced DNA damage. The UVR8 pathway activates the production of flavonoids and other UV-absorbing compounds that act as a natural sunscreen, protecting sensitive tissues from damage.

### UV-A and Photomorphogenesis

UV-A radiation (315-400 nm) overlaps with the blue/far-violet range and is perceived by some of the same photoreceptors that detect blue light, including cryptochromes.

UV-A influences various aspects of plant development, including phototropism and the regulation of growth patterns. Plants grown under UV-A light may show different growth habits compared to those grown without it.

### Ecological Significance of UV Perception

The ability to detect UV has several ecological implications.

Plants can sense when they are exposed to full sun versus shade, as the UV component of sunlight is significantly reduced under canopy cover. This UV-sensing ability contributes to [[shade-avoidance-syndrome]] responses, helping plants optimize their positioning for photosynthesis while minimizing harmful UV exposure.

Some flowers have evolved UV-absorbing patterns (visible to pollinators like bees, which can see UV) that serve as nectar guides. The plant's ability to produce these patterns implies some level of UV-responsive development.

## Infrared and Far-Red Light Perception

### Far-Red Light as a Shade Signal

Perhaps the most ecologically significant aspect of plant infrared perception is the detection of far-red light (700-750 nm).

Far-red light is the predominant wavelength at dusk and is also enriched in shade conditions.

Chamovitz describes how Harry Borthwick and colleagues at the USDA discovered that far-red light could cancel the effect of red light on flowering. This red/far-red reversible system is mediated by phytochrome.

In nature, the last light a plant sees at the end of the day is far-red, signaling the plant to "turn off" its daytime processes. In the morning, red light predominates, signaling the plant to "wake up."

### Far-Red as a Neighbor Detection System

Plants use the red-to-far-red ratio to detect the presence of neighboring vegetation.

Chlorophyll absorbs red light strongly but reflects far-red light. When a plant is surrounded by other plants, the reflected far-red light increases while red light decreases.

This shift in the red/far-red ratio triggers shade avoidance responses, including stem elongation, accelerated flowering, and altered leaf positioning. The plant effectively "sees" its competitors through spectral analysis of reflected light.

This capability has profound implications for plant ecology and agriculture. Dense plantings can trigger shade avoidance responses that reduce yield, while strategic planting arrangements can minimize these effects.

### The Phytochrome Switch

The phytochrome system acts as a molecular switch that toggles between two forms:

- Pr (phytochrome red-absorbing form): activated by red light, promotes germination and de-etiolation
- Pfr (phytochrome far-red-absorbing form): activated by far-red light, promotes etiolation and shade responses

The ratio of Pr to Pfr serves as the plant's memory of the last light quality it experienced.
