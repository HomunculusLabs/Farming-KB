# Mimicking Forests

In all natural ecosystems, plants are of different heights. The forest garden strategy mimics this natural pattern: big trees form a canopy over smaller trees, which cover shade-loving shrubs, which shade groundcover herbs. Sun-loving edible plants grow on the edges. This creates a self-sufficient community that maintains itself with less human effort.

## Why Forests Work

A tree is a huge biomass that affects everything around it:
- Provides homes for creatures and insects that distribute seeds
- Root fungi benefit the soil
- Trunks and leaves provide wind shelter
- Changes temperature and climate (a large oak transpires 40,000 gallons of water/year)
- Cools surrounding air and helps create precipitation
- Canopy and bark store water that runs off to plants and soil below
- Leaf litter creates a continuous cycle of nutrient return, feeding soil organisms
- Deep taproots mine minerals from subsoil layers unavailable to shallow-rooted plants
- Root exudates feed mycorrhizal networks that connect multiple trees and understory plants

## The Seven Layers of a Forest Garden

Robert Hart's classic forest garden model identifies seven vertical layers that replicate the structure of a natural woodland. Each layer occupies a distinct niche in light, root depth, and microclimate:

1. **Canopy layer** (10m+): Standard fruit and nut trees — apples, pears, chestnuts, walnuts, oaks. These form the overstory that moderates the entire microclimate beneath them.
2. **Low-tree layer** (4-9m): Dwarf fruit trees, smaller nut trees — hazelnuts, plum, cherry, mulberry. These fill the gap between canopy and shrub layers.
3. **Shrub layer** (2-3m): Berry bushes and fruiting shrubs — currants, gooseberries, blueberries, elderberry, autumn olive. Many of these tolerate partial shade and produce reliably.
4. **Herbaceous layer** (ground level to 1.5m): Perennial herbs, vegetables, and companion plants — comfrey, rhubarb, sorrel, artichokes, hostas. This is often the most productive layer by area.
5. **Ground cover layer**: Creeping plants that protect soil — strawberries, creeping thyme, mint, vinca. They suppress weeds, retain moisture, and moderate soil temperature.
6. **Rhizosphere** (root zone): Root crops and bulbs — potatoes, garlic, onions, Jerusalem artichokes. These exploit a different soil horizon than most tree roots.
7. **Vertical layer**: Climbing and vining plants — grapes, kiwi, passionfruit, hops, pole beans. These use the trunks and branches of trees as support structures.

In practice, not every forest garden includes all seven layers. Climate, available space, and personal food preferences determine which layers are most productive. The key principle is vertical stacking: multiple species occupying different niches within the same ground area, each capturing resources the others miss.

## Forest Garden Strategy

1. Pick plants based on height, climbing ability, shade tolerance, and water requirements
2. **Dry/cool climates:** Space plants farther apart for light and water distribution
3. **Warm/humid climates:** Plant closer together (but not too close — fungus risk)
4. **Fertile/wet areas:** Plant all species at once (walnuts + fruit trees + legumes + perennials)
5. Individual species produce less than in monoculture, but TOTAL yield from all species combined exceeds traditional farming
6. Diversity = security (bad year for vegetables? Still have tree fruits, nuts)
7. No crop rotation needed when planting diverse polycultures
8. Create as many relationships between plants and animals as possible

## Establishing a Forest Garden

1. **Cut down woody weeds** — they become mulch
2. **Dig out large perennial roots** (don't dig too much — encourages more weeds)
3. **Lay cardboard or old carpet** to decompose remaining weeds
4. **When soil is ready:** Fence off and grow legumes and useful shrubs (comfrey, etc.)
5. **Add mulch, green manure, compost.** Allow geese and ducks to forage
6. **Plant a group of trees** as the nucleus. Chickens may forage at this phase
7. **Once established:** Simple management. Pigs may forage, trees produce, smaller plants harvested ongoing
8. **After harvest:** Plant cover crop (rye, clover, buckwheat, barley, oats, vetch). Leguminous green manures plowed in or mulched before flowering

Timing the establishment phases matters. The initial sheet mulching and weed suppression phase is best done in late summer or early autumn, allowing several months for decomposition before spring planting. Tree planting should coincide with the dormant season in your climate — bare-root trees planted in early spring or late autumn establish more quickly than container-grown trees planted during active growth. Nitrogen-fixing ground covers and green manures should be established in the first year to begin building soil fertility before the canopy trees are planted in the second or third year.

The initial investment in soil preparation pays dividends throughout the life of the forest garden. Once the weed seed bank is depleted through sheet mulching and the soil food web is activated through mulch and compost additions, weed pressure drops dramatically. Experienced forest gardeners report spending less than one hour per week on maintenance per 100 square meters of established garden, compared to 5-10 hours per week for a comparable area of annual vegetable beds.

## Choosing Species

For each plant, consider:
- Succession stage (pioneer, Stage 2, or Stage 3?)
- Deciduous or evergreen?
- Maximum height
- Root invasiveness
- Growth/death rate
- Leaf density (shade or light penetration?)
- Disease resistance
- Pruning sensitivity
- Climate and soil compatibility
- Spread rate (will it choke others?)
- Common or rare?
- Usefulness and yield
- Space requirements
- Labor required vs. yield
- Harvest timing (can you stagger production?)
- Multiple products (leaves, roots, seeds, fruit)?
- Self-storing ability (nuts, root crops)?

Plant guilds — mutually beneficial plant groupings — are the functional units of a forest garden rather than individual species. A classic apple tree guild, for example, might include comfrey (dynamic accumulator and mulch producer), daffodils (pest deterrent around the trunk), clover (nitrogen fixer and ground cover), chicory (deep-rooted mineral miner), and yarrow (insectary plant attracting predatory insects). The apple tree benefits from the guild's collective nutrient cycling, pest management, and soil protection without requiring any of these functions to be performed by human labor.

When selecting species, consider also their succession timing. Pioneer species such as nitrogen-fixing shrubs and fast-growing nurse trees are essential during the early years of a forest garden, providing shade, wind protection, and soil improvement while slower-growing canopy trees mature. As the canopy closes, these pioneers are gradually shaded out or can be coppiced for mulch and fuel. Planning for this succession from the beginning prevents the need for later intervention to remove over-mature pioneer species.

## Polyculture vs. Monoculture

Monoculture pushes each individual plant to maximum yield but requires enormous labor (80+ hours/week for organic market farms). Polyculture/forest gardens push many species to work together, yielding more total food with less work — only a few people working part time.

**Warning:** Forest gardens take at least a decade to establish and even longer to profit. There's no strict formula; every climate is different. The goal is many eggs in many baskets with minimal labor.

## Nitrogen Fixers and Dynamic Accumulators

Certain plant species play outsized roles in forest garden nutrient cycling. Nitrogen-fixing plants from the Fabaceae (legume) family and actinorhizal species form symbiotic relationships with rhizobia or Frankia bacteria that convert atmospheric nitrogen into plant-available forms. In a forest garden, nitrogen fixers are strategically placed to support nitrogen-hungry fruit and nut trees. Common choices include black locust (*Robinia pseudoacacia*), seaberry (*Hippophae rhamnoides*), Siberian pea shrub (*Caragana arborescens*), and ground-level clovers and vetches.

Dynamic accumulators are plants whose deep taproots mine minerals from subsoil layers and bring them to the surface in their leaf tissue. When these leaves fall or are cut for mulch, the minerals become available to shallower-rooted plants. Comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*) is the most widely cited dynamic accumulator in permaculture, drawing potassium, calcium, and other minerals from deep soil layers. Yarrow, dandelion, chicory, and nettle also serve this function and can be integrated throughout the forest garden as supporting species.

## Wind and Microclimate Design

The wind-sheltering function of trees in a forest garden extends well beyond the canopy edge. Windbreaks reduce wind speed for a distance of approximately 10-15 times the height of the trees on the leeward side. This protected zone supports plants that would otherwise be damaged or desiccated by wind, and it significantly reduces evapotranspiration and soil moisture loss.

In cold climates, wind protection can extend the growing season by 2-3 weeks by reducing wind chill on spring nights. In hot, dry climates, the combined effect of shade and wind reduction can lower ambient temperatures by 5-10°F beneath the canopy. The placement of the tallest trees should account for prevailing wind directions to maximize this protective effect while still allowing enough airflow to prevent fungal disease buildup in humid conditions.
