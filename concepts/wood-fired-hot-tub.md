# Wood-Fired Hot Tub

A wood-fired hot tub provides year-round soaking on the homestead without electricity or gas. Using a wood-burning stove or submersible heater to heat water, these tubs are simple, reliable, and deeply satisfying after a day of physical work. They can be built from a variety of materials and range from simple stock tank conversions to custom-built cedar tubs with sophisticated heater systems.

## Design Overview

A wood-fired hot tub consists of four main components:

1. **Tub** — holds the water and bathers (typically 200-500 gallons)
2. **Heater** — wood-fired stove that heats the water (external or submersible)
3. **Water treatment** — keeps water clean and safe between changes
4. **Foundation and drainage** — supports the weight and manages drained water

## Tub Options

### Stock Tank (Easiest and Cheapest)
- Galvanized steel stock tank (poly tanks also work but are less heat-retentive)
- 4-foot diameter round tank holds approximately 250 gallons
- 6-foot diameter holds approximately 400 gallons
- Cost: $150-$400 for the tank
- Pros: ready-made, durable, holds heat reasonably well
- Cons: galvanized coating can leach zinc at high temperatures (not recommended above 105F), industrial appearance

### Cedar or Redwood Staves (Traditional)
- Individual vertical staves held together by steel bands (like a barrel)
- Custom sizes from 4 to 8 feet in diameter
- Cost: $1,000-$5,000 (kits) or $500-$2,000 (DIY with salvaged wood)
- Pros: beautiful, excellent insulation, natural material, comfortable
- Cons: requires woodworking skill, wood requires maintenance (oil annually), can leak initially until wood swells

### Cedar Plank (Box Style)
- Built like a box from cedar or redwood planks with internal framing
- Simpler construction than stave tubs
- Pros: easier to build, good insulation, attractive
- Cons: heavier, may require additional sealing, shorter lifespan than stave construction

### Concrete or Stone
- Permanent installation, built in place
- Excellent heat retention
- Cost: $2,000-$10,000+
- Pros: permanent, durable, can integrate with landscaping
- Cons: expensive, permanent (cannot relocate), slow to heat

## Heater Systems

### External Wood-Fired Stove (Recommended)
A stainless steel or copper coil (heat exchanger) sits inside a small wood stove. Water circulates from the tub, through the coil, and back into the tub via [[thermosiphon]] (natural convection) or a small pump.

- **How it works**: The stove heats the coil, which heats the water. Hot water rises out of the coil back into the tub; cool water flows from the tub into the coil. No pump required if the system is properly designed with adequate rise (12-18 inches minimum between stove output and tub inlet).
- **Heating time**: 2-4 hours from 50F to 104F for a 250-gallon tub with a well-designed system
- **Fuel**: small-diameter dry hardwood (similar to [[rocket-stove-design]] fuel). A wheelbarrow load of wood per heating session.
- **Cost**: $500-$2,000 for a complete system

### Submersible Wood-Fired Heater (Chofu-Style)
A sealed wood-burning stove that sits directly in the water. Water circulates naturally through the heater body via thermosiphon.

- **Pros**: compact, no plumbing, efficient heat transfer directly into the water
- **Cons**: takes up space inside the tub, surface gets very hot (burn risk), must be removed for cleaning
- **Cost**: $800-$1,500

### DIY External Heater
Build from a steel barrel (30 or 55 gallon) with welded plumbing fittings:
1. Cut a hole in the barrel for the firebox door
2. Weld or thread fittings for water input (bottom) and output (top)
3. Install a stainless steel coil inside the barrel (or weld a water jacket around the firebox)
4. Connect with CPVC or copper pipe to the tub
5. Ensure proper clearances and a non-combustible base

This requires welding skills but costs as little as $100-200 in materials.

## Water Treatment

Wood-fired tubs typically do not use conventional pool chemicals (chlorine is unpleasant and unnecessary for a non-circulating tub). Options include:

### Non-Chemical Methods
- **Drain and refill** — the simplest approach. Drain every 1-3 weeks depending on use. At 250-400 gallons, this is practical if you have a water source. See [[water-management|water]] systems.
- **[[enzyme]] treatment** — commercial enzyme products (like Spa Marvel) break down body oils and organic matter. Add weekly. Extends water life to 2-4 weeks.
- **Hydrogen peroxide** — 1 cup of 3% hydrogen peroxide per 100 gallons helps oxidize contaminants. Safer than chlorine and breaks down to water and oxygen.
- **Ozone generators** — small 12V ozone units reduce contaminants. Effective but require electricity.

### Filtration
- A simple cartridge filter run periodically keeps water clearer
- Skim debris with a net after each use
- Cover the tub when not in use to keep out leaves, insects, and debris

### Water Quality Testing
- Test pH weekly (target 7.2-7.8)
- Test for bacteria if water is kept more than 2 weeks
- Change water if it becomes cloudy, develops odor, or feels slimy

## Foundation and Drainage

A full tub weighs 2,000-4,000+ pounds. The foundation must support this weight.

### Foundation Options
- **Compacted gravel** — 6 inches of compacted crushed stone, leveled
- **Concrete pad** — 4-inch reinforced concrete, slightly larger than the tub
- **Concrete blocks** — level block pavers on compacted ground
- **Timber frame** — treated 6x6 beams on a gravel bed

### Drainage
- Install a drain valve at the bottom of the tub (bulkhead fitting)
- Route drain water to a garden bed, orchard, or greywater system (soap-free water is safe for most plants)
- Never drain directly into a septic system — the volume and heat can damage it
- Ensure the drain area slopes away from foundations and neighbors

## Safety Considerations

- **Temperature**: Never exceed 104F (40C). Prolonged exposure above this causes heat stroke and can be fatal. Use a floating thermometer. Soaking time should not exceed 15-20 minutes at maximum temperature.
- **Children**: Never allow unsupervised access. Children under 5 should not use hot tubs. Cover with a rigid, locking cover when not in use.
- **Alcohol**: Do not combine hot tubbing with alcohol — it impairs judgment and the body's ability to regulate temperature.
- **Pregnancy**: Avoid temperatures above 100F during pregnancy, especially in the first trimester.
- **Burn risk**: External heater surfaces reach extreme temperatures. Install a guard or barrier. Keep children away from the stove.
- **Wood smoke**: Position the heater chimney so smoke does not enter nearby windows or buildings.
- **Electrical hazard**: If you add a pump or light, use GFCI protection and have the work done by a licensed electrician. Better yet, keep it entirely off-grid.

## Cover

A cover is essential — it retains heat between uses (reducing heating time and fuel by 50%+) and keeps debris out.

- **Rigid foam insulation** — cut 2-inch XPS foam to fit inside the tub opening. Cover with a tarp or canvas.
- **Custom insulated cover** — similar to a hot tub cover with a vinyl outer shell and foam core
- **Wooden lid** — cedar or plywood with insulation sandwiched inside

## Heating Tips

- Start with the hottest water available — pre-fill with hose water in the sun
- Burn dry hardwood for fastest, cleanest heating
- Keep the fire hot and well-oxygenated (small pieces, good airflow)
- Stir the water periodically during heating for even temperature distribution
- Put the cover on while heating to retain heat
- Once at temperature, maintain with a small fire or let it cool naturally — the tub holds heat for several hours

A wood-fired hot tub is one of the most rewarding homestead additions. It costs little to operate, requires no grid connection, and provides genuine therapeutic benefit after hard physical work.
