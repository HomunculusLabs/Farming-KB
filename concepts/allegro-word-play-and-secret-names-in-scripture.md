    12|# Word Play and Secret Names in Scripture
    13|
    14|The linguistic technique that John Marco Allegro claimed to have discovered
    15|underlying the composition of the New Testament was a sophisticated system
    16|of multilingual punning. According to his thesis in "The Sacred Mushroom and
    17|the Cross," the Gospel writers composed their texts so that individual words
    18|and phrases could be read simultaneously in Greek, Semitic languages (Hebrew
    19|and Aramaic), and Sumerian -- the oldest written language of Mesopotamia.
    20|Each reading revealed a different layer of meaning, with the deepest
    21|Sumerian level encoding the secret names of the [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-amanita-muscaria-emerging-alternative]] and instructions for its cultic use.
    22|
    23|## The Sumerian Foundation
    24|
    25|Allegro's [[allegro-philological-methodology]]
    26|rested on the premise that Sumerian was the linguistic bedrock upon which
    27|much of Near Eastern religious vocabulary was built. As the earliest known
    28|written language, dating to the fourth millennium BCE, Sumerian had
    29|generated a vast stock of words and names that were borrowed, adapted, and
    30|reinterpreted by the Semitic languages (Akkadian, Hebrew, Aramaic) that
    31|succeeded it in Mesopotamia. These words then passed into Greek and other
    32|Indo-European languages through cultural contact and trade.
    33|
    34|Crucially, the original Sumerian meanings of many plant names and religious
    35|terms had been lost or obscured over the millennia. Words that once referred
    36|specifically to the sacred mushroom and its cult had come to be associated
    37|with other plants or with abstract religious concepts. Allegro believed that
    38|by tracing words back to their Sumerian roots, he could recover the original
    39|mushroom references that had been encoded in the biblical texts.
    40|
    41|## The Technique of Multilingual Punning
    42|
    43|The core of Allegro's linguistic argument was that a single Greek word in
    44|the New Testament could simultaneously evoke multiple unrelated meanings in
    45|different languages. A Greek place-name like "Bethlehem," for instance,
    46|could be read as a Hebrew phrase meaning "house of bread" while also
    47|recalling Sumerian words describing the mushroom's appearance or effects.
    48|
    49|This technique of multilingual punning was, Allegro argued, a deliberate
    50|compositional strategy. The cult's writers chose their words precisely
    51|because they functioned on all three levels at once. The surface Greek
    52|narrative told one story; the Semitic reading revealed another; and the
    53|Sumerian roots disclosed the true, hidden meaning. Only those who had been
    54|initiated into the cult and taught the Sumerian etymologies could fully
    55|appreciate the deepest level.
    56|
    57|## The Secret Mushroom Names
    58|
    59|Allegro listed several Sumerian phrases that he identified as the "secret
    60|names" of the sacred mushroom, arguing that much of the mythology and
    61|homiletics of both the Old and New Testaments was built upon word-play
    62|derived from these names. The principal names included compound Sumerian
    63|phrases describing the mushroom's physical characteristics: its shape (a
    64|cone or glans), its twin hemispheres (the split volva), its erect posture,
    65|and its phallic appearance.
    66|
    67|Among the most important was the Sumerian phrase meaning "cone of the erect
    68|phallus" -- a direct description of the mushroom's shape. This phrase, Allegro
    69|traced through Syriac (khurbeqana) and Arabic (kharbaq) names for Hellebore,
    70|arguing that the plant name had been transferred from the mushroom to other
    71|species while retaining its original morphological reference. The Greek
    72|Korkoron, which appeared as an alternative name for several plants in the
    73|classical pharmacopoeia, was similarly derived from this Sumerian mushroom
    74|name.
    75|
    76|## The BALAG Root: Crown, Glans, and Church
    77|
    78|One of the most productive Sumerian roots in Allegro's analysis was BALAG,
    79|meaning "crown of the penis" or "glans." This root generated an enormous
    80|family of related words across multiple languages, all of which, in Allegro's
    81|reading, ultimately referred to the mushroom's cap or to the act of its
    82|erection (growth). From BALAG came the Greek Phalangx (a roller, log, or
    83|rank of soldiers), the Latin Pallacana (a name for the onion), and the
    84|Hebrew word for the mushroom itself.
    85|
    86|The creative potential of this root for the New Testament myth-makers was
    87|considerable. BALAG in full could evoke Semitic roots meaning "make" or
    88|"build," providing the pun behind Peter's declaration as the "rock" upon
    89|which the church would be built. Shortened and combined with other mushroom
    90|name elements, it could produce roots meaning "bless," "praise," "release,"
    91|and "forgive" -- all concepts central to the Gospel message.
    92|
    93|## From Plant Names to Personal Names
    94|
    95|Allegro demonstrated how what appear to be personal names in the biblical
    96|narrative are in fact disguised plant names, specifically mushroom names.
    97|The name "Jonah," for instance, he traced to mushroom nomenclature, noting
    98|that the famous plant that gave Jonah shade -- which "came into being in a
    99|night and perished in a night" and was subject to the depredation of worms
   100|-- was certainly a fungus.
   101|
   102|The name of the city "Pella," where early Christians were said to have fled
   103|during the Jewish revolt, was read as a variant of a well-known mushroom
   104|name. Place names throughout the New Testament, in Allegro's analysis, served
   105|double duty as indicators of mushroom-growing locations or as encoded
   106|references to the fungus itself.
   107|
   108|## Plant Names and the Problem of Nomenclature
   109|
   110|Allegro devoted considerable attention to the classical problem of plant
   111|name confusion. Pliny the Elder and other ancient naturalists frequently
   112|complained that the same plant was known by multiple names, and that the
   113|same name was applied to different plants. This confusion, Allegro argued,
   114|was not accidental but was the direct result of the mushroom cult's practice
   115|of concealing the true identity of the sacred fungus by transferring its
   116|names to other plants.
   117|
   118|The name "Paeony," for example, was traced to a Sumerian original meaning
   119|"capsule of fecundity; womb" -- a clear reference to the mushroom's volva.
   120|Pliny's description of the Paeony as a plant that "prevents the mocking
   121|delusions that the Fauns bring us in our sleep" made far more sense if the
   122|plant in question was actually the psychoactive [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] rather than the garden flower we know today.
   123|
   124|Similarly, the plant called Cotuledon (Navelwort) derived from Sumerian
   125|words meaning "ball-and-socket" or "penis-and-vulva," its sexual
   126|connotations placing it firmly within the range of fungus nomenclature. The
   127|classical description of the plant as having a "hip-joint" connection
   128|explained, in Allegro's view, the recurring myths about mushroom figures
   129|being pierced in the hip or side -- a motif he connected to the spear wound
   130|inflicted on Jesus at the crucifixion.
   131|
   132|## The Scope and Limits of the Method
   133|
   134|Allegro's word-play theory was breathtaking in its scope. If correct, it
   135|meant that virtually every name, place, and significant phrase in the Bible
   136|was a disguised mushroom reference. Critics argued that this very
   137|comprehensiveness was the theory's weakness: a method that could find
   138|mushroom references everywhere could find them anywhere, rendering it
   139|unfalsifiable. Nevertheless, the specific etymological connections Allegro
   140|proposed -- tracing individual words from Greek through Semitic languages
   141|to their Sumerian origins -- remain a provocative contribution to the study
   142|of ancient Near Eastern religion and linguistics.
