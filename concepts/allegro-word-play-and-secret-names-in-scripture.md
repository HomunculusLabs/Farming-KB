# Word Play and Secret Names in Scripture

The linguistic technique that John Marco Allegro claimed to have discovered
underlying the composition of the New Testament was a sophisticated system
of multilingual punning. According to his thesis in "The Sacred Mushroom and
the Cross," the Gospel writers composed their texts so that individual words
and phrases could be read simultaneously in Greek, Semitic languages (Hebrew
and Aramaic), and Sumerian -- the oldest written language of Mesopotamia.
Each reading revealed a different layer of meaning, with the deepest
Sumerian level encoding the secret names of the [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-amanita-muscaria-emerging-alternative]] and instructions for its cultic use.

## The Sumerian Foundation

Allegro's [[allegro-philological-methodology]]
rested on the premise that Sumerian was the linguistic bedrock upon which
much of Near Eastern religious vocabulary was built. As the earliest known
written language, dating to the fourth millennium BCE, Sumerian had
generated a vast stock of words and names that were borrowed, adapted, and
reinterpreted by the Semitic languages (Akkadian, Hebrew, Aramaic) that
succeeded it in Mesopotamia. These words then passed into Greek and other
Indo-European languages through cultural contact and trade.

Crucially, the original Sumerian meanings of many plant names and religious
terms had been lost or obscured over the millennia. Words that once referred
specifically to the sacred mushroom and its cult had come to be associated
with other plants or with abstract religious concepts. Allegro believed that
by tracing words back to their Sumerian roots, he could recover the original
mushroom references that had been encoded in the biblical texts.

## The Technique of Multilingual Punning

The core of Allegro's linguistic argument was that a single Greek word in
the New Testament could simultaneously evoke multiple unrelated meanings in
different languages. A Greek place-name like "Bethlehem," for instance,
could be read as a Hebrew phrase meaning "house of bread" while also
recalling Sumerian words describing the mushroom's appearance or effects.

This technique of multilingual punning was, Allegro argued, a deliberate
compositional strategy. The cult's writers chose their words precisely
because they functioned on all three levels at once. The surface Greek
narrative told one story; the Semitic reading revealed another; and the
Sumerian roots disclosed the true, hidden meaning. Only those who had been
initiated into the cult and taught the Sumerian etymologies could fully
appreciate the deepest level.

## The Secret Mushroom Names

Allegro listed several Sumerian phrases that he identified as the "secret
names" of the sacred mushroom, arguing that much of the mythology and
homiletics of both the Old and New Testaments was built upon word-play
derived from these names. The principal names included compound Sumerian
phrases describing the mushroom's physical characteristics: its shape (a
cone or glans), its twin hemispheres (the split volva), its erect posture,
and its phallic appearance.

Among the most important was the Sumerian phrase meaning "cone of the erect
phallus" -- a direct description of the mushroom's shape. This phrase, Allegro
traced through Syriac (khurbeqana) and Arabic (kharbaq) names for Hellebore,
arguing that the plant name had been transferred from the mushroom to other
species while retaining its original morphological reference. The Greek
Korkoron, which appeared as an alternative name for several plants in the
classical pharmacopoeia, was similarly derived from this Sumerian mushroom
name.

## The BALAG Root: Crown, Glans, and Church

One of the most productive Sumerian roots in Allegro's analysis was BALAG,
meaning "crown of the penis" or "glans." This root generated an enormous
family of related words across multiple languages, all of which, in Allegro's
reading, ultimately referred to the mushroom's cap or to the act of its
erection (growth). From BALAG came the Greek Phalangx (a roller, log, or
rank of soldiers), the Latin Pallacana (a name for the onion), and the
Hebrew word for the mushroom itself.

The creative potential of this root for the New Testament myth-makers was
considerable. BALAG in full could evoke Semitic roots meaning "make" or
"build," providing the pun behind Peter's declaration as the "rock" upon
which the church would be built. Shortened and combined with other mushroom
name elements, it could produce roots meaning "bless," "praise," "release,"
and "forgive" -- all concepts central to the Gospel message.

## From Plant Names to Personal Names

Allegro demonstrated how what appear to be personal names in the biblical
narrative are in fact disguised plant names, specifically mushroom names.
The name "Jonah," for instance, he traced to mushroom nomenclature, noting
that the famous plant that gave Jonah shade -- which "came into being in a
night and perished in a night" and was subject to the depredation of worms
-- was certainly a fungus.

The name of the city "Pella," where early Christians were said to have fled
during the Jewish revolt, was read as a variant of a well-known mushroom
name. Place names throughout the New Testament, in Allegro's analysis, served
double duty as indicators of mushroom-growing locations or as encoded
references to the fungus itself.

## Plant Names and the Problem of Nomenclature

Allegro devoted considerable attention to the classical problem of plant
name confusion. Pliny the Elder and other ancient naturalists frequently
complained that the same plant was known by multiple names, and that the
same name was applied to different plants. This confusion, Allegro argued,
was not accidental but was the direct result of the mushroom cult's practice
of concealing the true identity of the sacred fungus by transferring its
names to other plants.

The name "Paeony," for example, was traced to a Sumerian original meaning
"capsule of fecundity; womb" -- a clear reference to the mushroom's volva.
Pliny's description of the Paeony as a plant that "prevents the mocking
delusions that the Fauns bring us in our sleep" made far more sense if the
plant in question was actually the psychoactive [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] rather than the garden flower we know today.

Similarly, the plant called Cotuledon (Navelwort) derived from Sumerian
words meaning "ball-and-socket" or "penis-and-vulva," its sexual
connotations placing it firmly within the range of fungus nomenclature. The
classical description of the plant as having a "hip-joint" connection
explained, in Allegro's view, the recurring myths about mushroom figures
being pierced in the hip or side -- a motif he connected to the spear wound
inflicted on Jesus at the crucifixion.

## The Scope and Limits of the Method

Allegro's word-play theory was breathtaking in its scope. If correct, it
meant that virtually every name, place, and significant phrase in the Bible
was a disguised mushroom reference. Critics argued that this very
comprehensiveness was the theory's weakness: a method that could find
mushroom references everywhere could find them anywhere, rendering it
unfalsifiable. Nevertheless, the specific etymological connections Allegro
proposed -- tracing individual words from Greek through Semitic languages
to their Sumerian origins -- remain a provocative contribution to the study
of ancient Near Eastern religion and linguistics.
