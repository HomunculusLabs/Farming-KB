## The Three Forms

### 1. Extraction of a Toxic Implant

The first form of shamanic healing involves the extraction of a toxin, pathogenic object, or "darts" that may have been implanted in the patient by means of sorcery. In many [[darkness-low-light-facilitation-entheogenic-ceremonies]], the shaman perceives the location and nature of the toxic implant and physically or energetically removes it. ### 2. Retrieval of a Split-Off Soul Fragment

The second form involves the retrieval of a split-off psychic fragment or "soul." In shamanic cosmology, severe trauma, fright, grief, or prolonged stress can cause a portion of the soul to become separated from the person, resulting in a diminished state characterized by listlessness, depression, chronic illness, or a sense of incompleteness.

During the entheogenic journey, the shaman travels to the location where the soul fragment has become trapped—often in the "lower world" or at the site of the original trauma—and negotiates its return. The restored soul fragment is then reintegrated into the patient, restoring wholeness and vitality.

This concept bears a striking resemblance to Western psychotherapeutic ideas about dissociation, fragmentation of the self, and the therapeutic goal of integration. However, the shamanic model treats the split-off fragment as a literal, perceivable entity with its own location and agency, rather than as a metaphor for psychological dissociation.

### 3. Dismemberment and Reconstitution

The third and most dramatic form involves the experience of being dismembered or destroyed, and then reconstituted with a healthier, stronger "body." In this healing modality, the patient undergoes a symbolic death—their body is torn apart by animal spirits, boiled in a cauldron, ground to bones, or otherwise completely destroyed. From the remains, a new, purified body is reassembled.

This death-rebirth pattern is found across shamanic traditions worldwide and parallels the initiatory experiences described by shamans-in-training. It also resonates with the [[metzner-five-fundamental-conclusions-psychedelic-psychotherapy]], where patients moving beyond biographical issues encounter experiences of ego death and rebirth.

The reconstituted body may incorporate new spiritual power, animal qualities, or enhanced capacities. The experience fundamentally transforms the patient's sense of self and their relationship to the world, producing what might be called a ontological reset.

## Divination and Diagnosis

A crucial companion to these three healing forms is the role of divination, which in the Western medical framework would be called diagnosis. Metzner noted that the experience can provide access to hidden knowledge—determining from where and from whom a particular toxic implant came, where a soul-fragment has been "lost," or what particular herbs should be used for a person's illness.

It is said that there is an intelligence associated with the plant medicine that communicates in an interior way to the person who ingests the medicine. Indigenous healers refer to the entheogenic plants as "plant teachers," emphasizing that the healing knowledge comes through the plant itself rather than being imposed by the healer. The shaman's role is to facilitate this communication and interpret its contents.

## Contrast with Western Psychotherapy

The underlying paradigms of illness and treatment in shamanic and Western systems are, as Metzner put it, "completely different." Western psychotherapy understands illness primarily in terms of psychological dynamics—unconscious conflicts, maladaptive patterns, distorted cognitions, or neurochemical imbalances. Treatment proceeds through verbal dialogue, insight, behavioral change, or pharmacological correction.

Shamanic healing operates within a cosmological framework that includes [[metzner-spirit-beings-multiple-realities-entheogens]], spirit beings, and non-material causes of illness. The healing is experiential rather than verbal, collective rather than individual, and cosmological rather than psychological. The shaman functions simultaneously as diagnostician, surgeon, psychotherapist, and priest.

Metzner acknowledged that "there is a great deal of as-yet-poorly or not-at-all understood about the processes of illness and healing" in both systems.

## Access to Metaphysical Realms and Spirit Beings

All three healing forms presuppose access to realms beyond ordinary reality. Metzner listed the experiential features that imply a radically different worldview:

- Metaphysical realms referred to as "inner world," "spirit world," "upper or lower world," "faerie world," "dreamtime," or "otherworld"
- Access through journeying on the back of an animal or being carried by a large bird
- Nonmaterial spirit beings associated with animals, plants, trees, places, ancestors, or nonordinary entities
- Healing experienced as being done by or with the assistance of spirits, also called "allies," "power animals," "guardians," or "helpers"

The two elements that pose "the most direct and radical challenge to the accepted Western worldview" are the existence of multiple worlds and of spirit beings—conceptions "considered completely beyond the pale of both reason and science."

## Integration in Hybrid Practices

Metzner noted that the majority of Westerners who developed ongoing practices of working with entheogenic plant substances expanded their belief systems beyond conventional materialism. While accepting Western psychological insights from Freud, Jung, and Reich, they came to accept the reality of nonmaterial spirit beings and to recognize that "we live in multiple worlds of consciousness."

## The Cosmological Framework of Shamanic Healing

Metzner placed particular emphasis on the cosmological framework within which shamanic healing operates. In contrast to the Western biomedical model, which locates illness within the individual organism, the shamanic model situates the individual within a web of relationships that extends across multiple dimensions of reality. Illness is understood not as a malfunction of the individual body but as a disturbance in the larger pattern of relationships—between the individual and the community, between the individual and the natural world, and between the individual and the spirit world.

This cosmological framework has practical implications for treatment. If illness is caused by a disturbed relationship with a spirit being, the cure must involve that spirit being—not merely a physiological intervention. If a soul fragment has become trapped in a non-ordinary reality, the healer must travel to that reality to retrieve it. The therapeutic modality is determined by the cosmological understanding of the cause.

## The Role of the Entheogenic Plants as Active Agents

A distinctive feature of the shamanic model is the agency attributed to the entheogenic plants themselves. These are not passive tools or biochemical triggers but active participants in the healing process—what Metzner called "plant teachers." The intelligence of the plant communicates directly with both the healer and the patient, providing diagnostic information, guiding the therapeutic process, and sometimes directly intervening in the patient's condition.

This attribution of agency to the plants is perhaps the most challenging aspect of the shamanic model for Western acceptance. It implies that consciousness is not confined to human (or animal) organisms but is a property of certain plants as well—a notion that contradicts the materialist assumption that consciousness is an emergent property of neural complexity. Yet the consistency of reports from independent practitioners across cultures about the communicative quality of plant entheogen experiences gives this attribution a degree of empirical support.
