# Fungal Hyphal Biology and Mycelial Networks
The hypha is the fundamental structural unit of fungal life. These
microscopic, tubular cells extend at their tips, branch repeatedly, and
interweave to form the mycelium—the vegetative body of the fungus that
spreads through soil, wood, leaf litter, and living tissues. Despite
their diminutive individual diameter (typically 2 to 10 micrometers),
hyphal networks can achieve staggering dimensions. The honey fungus
*Armillaria ostoyae* in Oregon's Blue Mountains covers an estimated
2,200 acres and may be between 2,400 and 7,200 years old, making it
the largest known organism on Earth by area. Understanding hyphal
biology is essential to understanding how fungi feed, grow, reproduce,
and interact with their environments.
## Hyphal Construction: Septate and Non-septate Forms
Fungal hyphae are constructed in two fundamentally different ways. The
basidiomycetes (mushroom-forming fungi) and ascomycetes (the largest
fungal phylum) produce septate hyphae—tubes divided by cross-walls
called septa. Each septum is perforated by a central pore, sometimes
elaborated into a barrel-shaped structure capped by membranous domes
(a Woronin body in ascomycetes or a clamp connection in
basidiomycetes). Cytoplasm, including nuclei, organelles, and
nutrients, streams through these pores from one compartment to the
next, making the entire mycelium effectively a single coenocytic
(super-cellular) entity.
The second form is found in zygomycetes and oomycetes (water molds),
which lack septa entirely. Their hyphae are multinucleate,
non-compartmentalized tubes—a continuous cylinder of cytoplasm bounded
by a single plasma membrane and cell wall. This non-septate organization
makes these fungi exceptionally vulnerable to catastrophic injury; when
a hypha is severed, pressurized cytoplasm can surge out along the entire
length of the cell. Septate fungi, by contrast, can seal septal pores
within seconds to isolate damaged compartments, protecting the rest of
the organism.
## Tip Growth: The Engine of Fungal Expansion
Fungi grow exclusively by extending the tips of their hyphae. There are
no meristems—no localized zones of cell division as in plants. Instead,
new cell wall and membrane material is continuously delivered to the
hyphal apex by vesicles that travel along cytoskeletal highways from the
older regions of the cell. These vesicles, budding from the endoplasmic
reticulum and Golgi equivalents, carry enzymes, structural proteins, and
lipids. When they fuse with the plasma membrane at the tip, they add new
surface area and release their enzymatic cargo into the cell wall and the
surrounding environment.
This targeted secretion is one of the most intensely studied processes
in cell biology. The hyphal tip maintains a steep gradient of calcium
ions, and it is believed that calcium-regulated channels in the membrane
control vesicle fusion. The result is a highly polarized growth pattern:
the hypha extends only at its apex, while the older regions behind the
tip remain stationary or undergo branching.
## Turgor Pressure and Invasive Growth
Hyphae are pressurized structures. Osmotic water uptake generates
turgor pressures of approximately one atmosphere (100 kPa) in most
species, though specialized pathogens can achieve 50 atmospheres or
more at their tips. The rice blast fungus *Magnaporthe grisea*, one of
the most destructive crop pathogens in the world, generates sufficient
pressure at its appressorium (a specialized infection cell) to pierce
Kevlar—a testament to the extraordinary forces that microscopic fungi
can bring to bear.
Turgor pressure serves multiple functions. It drives hyphal extension
by pressing against the yielding cell wall at the tip. It powers the
eruption of fruiting bodies through compacted soil. It forces spores
from their receptacles. And in the case of the rice blast fungus and
similar pathogens, it physically breaches the tough outer surfaces of
host plants. To push through resistant materials, hyphae secrete
enzymes that soften the substrate in advance of the growing tip. Cellulases
and ligninases dissolve plant cell walls; proteases degrade animal
tissues. The combination of enzymatic softening and physical pressure
makes fungal hyphae remarkably effective invasive structures.
## Electrical Activity and the "Screams" of Hyphae
All living cells maintain voltage differences across their plasma
membranes, and fungal hyphae are no exception. Microelectrode
recordings reveal hyphal membrane potentials of 100 to 200 millivolts.
This voltage is highly sensitive to perturbation—touching a hypha,
changing the surrounding solution, or exposing it to light all produce
measurable electrical responses.
European mycologists have converted these voltage signals into audio
output, producing audible representations of hyphal electrical activity.
The resulting sounds have been described as "screams"—rapid voltage
fluctuations that occur when hyphae are disturbed. This electrical
activity is not merely a physiological curiosity; it is closely linked
to the transport of nutrients. Fungi absorb sugars and amino acids from
their environment using membrane transport proteins that are coupled to
ion fluxes. Changes in membrane voltage therefore reflect the metabolic
state of the hypha, and electrical signaling may also coordinate
behavior across different parts of a mycelial network.
## The Mycelium as an Integrated Organism
A fungal mycelium is far more than a simple aggregation of tubes. It
is an integrated network capable of resource allocation, communication,
and decision-making. When a mycelium encounters a nutrient-rich patch,
hyphal branches proliferate locally to exploit it. When resources are
depleted in one region, cytoplasm and nutrients are redistributed to
growing tips elsewhere. The septal pores in basidiomycete and
ascomycete hyphae can be selectively opened or closed, regulating the
flow of materials between compartments.
The giant *Armillaria* clones that span thousands of acres in North
American forests illustrate the mycelium's capacity for long-term
persistence and coordinated growth. Molecular genetic studies have
confirmed that these vast networks are genetically uniform single
organisms. Their rhizomorphs—dense, root-like bundles of hyphae—
transport water and nutrients over considerable distances, enabling
the fungus to colonize new trees even as older portions of the
mycelium die back.
## Ecological Roles of Mycelial Networks
Mycelial networks underpin many of the most important ecological
interactions involving fungi. Mycorrhizal associations between fungal
hyphae and plant roots are essential for the health of most forest
ecosystems; the fungus supplies the plant with mineral nutrients
(especially phosphorus) extracted from soil, while the plant provides
the fungus with photosynthetic carbon. In some forests, a single
mycorrhizal network may connect dozens of trees, facilitating
underground nutrient transfer between individuals.
Saprotrophic mycelia decompose dead organic matter, recycling the
carbon, nitrogen, and minerals locked in wood and leaf litter back
into forms usable by other organisms. Without fungal decomposition,
the world's forests would be buried under mountains of undecayed wood.
Pathogenic mycelia attack living plants and animals, causing diseases
that shape the composition of plant communities and impose significant
costs on agriculture and human health.
