## Overview

### Design Implications

The 400 m buffer zone means that a **400 m wide evaporation border of trees** may be needed between desert margins and productive croplands. There is no doubt that a windbreak of trees uses less water than the water removed by hot desert winds passing over unprotected crops. Throughout croplands, scattered trees and cross-wind tree breaks are essential.

### Quantified Evaporation Reduction

Evaporation from open pans of water outside crop or tree shelter can reach **16 mm/day**, while 300 m into a protected crop area, only **5 mm/day** is lost. The crop itself acts as a windbreak, with a consequent gain in yield due to reduced evaporation.

### Windbreak Trees

Trees selected for evaporation control should be both dry-adapted and beneficial to associated crops. Proven species include:

- **[[water-conservation]] in rockholes:
- **Fill with pebbles** to lower evaporation (some water is lost)
- **Thatch or cover** the hole to reduce direct sun exposure
- **Build gutters** to direct rainfall into the hole
- **Erect a metal mesh fence** to direct dew condensation into the hole
- **Deepen the hole** and plaster with cement if it leaks

Rockholes serve as critical wildlife water points and emergency water sources for livestock and humans in arid landscapes.

## Conservation of Water in Transit

### The Scale of Transit Losses

More water can be lost in canals—especially in loose soils or shattered rock—than is lost in productive use. Even open concrete channels can lose great quantities of water to atmospheric evaporation. This makes transmission losses one of the most significant and often overlooked sources of water waste in arid land management.

### Pipe Systems

There is no substitute for long runs of pipe. In small systems, extruded PVC (flexible or rigid) pipe is most effective:
- **Runs of 100 m or more** can be made in one continuous length
- **Collapsible pipe rolls** are available for surface use, which can be unrolled as needed
- **Buried pipe** eliminates evaporation losses entirely

### Flexible Water Storage Bags

An innovative method for storing water from opportunistic runoff uses tough plastic or neoprene bags. These operate much like swales, receiving water from fixed-diameter pipe inlets:

- The swale is fitted with a rolled container that fills and unrolls as water enters
- It forms a "sausage" shape along the swale
- The same technique can capture fresh water flowing into salt lakes—the freshwater sausage floats and remains available as unmixed water on demand
- For salt lake applications, a greater depth-to-width ratio is possible since there is no concern about the container being constrained by swale dimensions

This approach is particularly valuable where:
- Runoff events are unpredictable and infrequent
- Rapid capture of flash flows is needed
- Open storage would result in excessive evaporation
- Freshwater needs to be separated from saline water

## Integrated Water Conservation Design

The most effective water conservation systems in arid areas combine multiple strategies:

1. **Capture runoff** through swales, limanim, and sand dams at the catchment scale
2. **Store water** in subsurface sand reservoirs rather than open water
3. **Protect stored water** with windbreaks, shade, and narrow-deep dam profiles
4. **Transmit water** through buried pipe rather than open channels
5. **Apply water** through mulched, sheltered planting sites
6. **Select plants** that are both dry-adapted and mutually beneficial

The goal is to eliminate every unnecessary point of water loss between the moment rain falls and the moment it is used by productive vegetation or human settlement.
