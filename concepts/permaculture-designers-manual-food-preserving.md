# Food Preserving

From Permaculture: A Designers' Manual by Bill Mollison.

## The Importance of Food Storage

How yields endure is important, for there are unlimited opportunities to use durable yields in terms of season or lifetime. By a series of preservation strategies, food can be stored for days, weeks, or years. Very little famine would occur could grains, fish, and fruit available in good times be stored for lean times. The strategies of food storage are critical.

Because of seasonal or diurnal cycles, we should pay close attention to storage strategies. Mollison advises that people should "mulch their recipe books, which often specify out-of-season or not-in-garden foods, and replace them with books that stress either low-energy methods of food preservation, or how to live easily from your garden in season."

## Strategies for Year-Round Yield

Yield can be extended by a general increase in diversity in the system:

- Leaf, fruit, seed and root are all product yields.
- Self-storing species such as tubers, hard seeds, fuelwood, or rhizomes can be cropped on demand.
- Techniques such as preserving, drying, pitting, and cool storage.
- Regional trade between communities, or the utilisation of land at different altitudes or latitudes.

## Drying

Roof areas can be used for drying crops. Wherever rainfall is slight, flat parapet concreted roof areas are preferred; these roof flats have a wide variety of uses for grain drying, among other purposes.

Gullies, ridgetops, natural shade, the sides of multi-storey buildings, and exposed sunny sites all demonstrate different opportunities for food dehydration. We can find a use for each and every such special site, whether as an aid to food storage, food dehydration, or as an energy source.

A sun-side glasshouse can also be used to dry surplus fruits and vegetables in an enclosed area unavailable to insect pests.

## Cool Storage

Cool air tunnels slope down to the house, have a shaded intake, and provide cool humid air to food storages. Natural shade and cool microclimates can be used for cool storage of perishables. Plant to create winter stores of grain, roots, and fodder for barn animals; integrate the house and barn in severe winter climates.

## Pitting

Pitting (or silage) is a cold heap method that does not lose nitrogen, but neither does it kill weed seeds. This method is useful for storing fodder and some root crops. Cold heaps (pitted or silage) preserve nutrients by anaerobic conditions.

## Fermentation

Taro is traditionally fermented to poi, a staple food in Hawaii. Fermented poi was used as a staple, as were boiled or baked taro. There are special varieties for boiling, baking, ferment to poi, or cooking as green leaf spinach.

Sulphur is part of all proteins, present in the body as the amino acids methionine and cystein. Deficiencies do not occur if meat protein is sufficient, or if anaerobic ferment of meat materials is part of food preparation. Many vegetarians get their amino acids from fermented foods.

## Meat Preservation

Meats can be dried in screened and shaded air cupboards, and part-salted or smoked. This is an important strategy in dryland areas where refrigeration may not be available. Livestock products are important winter foods.

## Self-Storing Species

By using self-storing species, food can be "stored" in the ground or on the plant:

- Tubers (taro, cassava, sweet potato, yam) can be left in the ground and harvested on demand.
- Hard seeds and grains store naturally for months to years.
- Rhizomes and root crops persist through dormant seasons.
- Fuelwood can be stacked and left to season.

## Water Storage

Water not open to evaporation and pollution, or with natural cleansing organisms, will keep indefinitely. This principle applies to all storage -- natural systems with living organisms involved tend to be self-regulating.

## Shelters and Structures

Shelters may outlast the forests that build them, or can be made of living or durable materials such as ivy, concrete, or stone. Energy alone (like the food which is part of energy) is difficult to store. Batteries leak or decay, heat escapes, and insulation breaks down. Only living things, like forests, increase their energy store.

## Design Integration

Food preservation should be integrated into the overall site design from the beginning. The location of storage areas, drying spaces, cool rooms, and processing areas should be planned as part of the zone layout, with frequently-used preservation areas (like daily kitchen processing) in Zone 1, and bulk storage (grain silos, root cellars) in Zone 2 or 3.

The principle of storing energy in living systems (forests, ponds, soil) rather than in technological systems (batteries, fuel tanks) is central to permaculture design. Living storage increases over time, while technological storage inevitably degrades.

## Merged: Root Crops in Permaculture Design

# Permaculture Designers Manual - Root Crops

Root and tuber crops are essential staples across tropical, subtropical, and temperate permaculture systems. The Designers' Manual treats them as reliable, drought-tolerant, and high-yielding components of garden and field systems. Root crops require little cooking compared to grains (saving household energy), contain essential minerals and vitamins, and can make every family food self-reliant. In desert gardens, root crops are the mainstay against famine.

## Taro (Colocasia esculenta)

Taro receives the most detailed treatment of any root crop in the manual. It is an herbaceous perennial to 1 m high with large arrow-like leaves and a swollen stem base or tuber reaching 10 kg (normally marketed at 1 kg). Growing period is 8-15 months with no off-season in the subtropics.

Some 1,100 Hawaiian varieties existed historically, with about 100 still preserved. Taro is a staple food in Hawaii, Polynesia, and southeast Asia. It is propagated by small side tubers or by crown disc cuttings (about 1 cm thick, lower 18 cm of stalk from old tuber). Special varieties exist for boiling, baking, ferment to poi, or cooking as green leaf spinach (luau taro). All taro must be cooked due to stinging crystals of oxalic acid.

Wet terrace culture: Very extensive canal and wet terrace systems were developed for taro, traditionally with local prawns, shellfish, and freshwater fish. Tilapia, prawns, crayfish, and Chinese edible snails have diversified the wet terrace cultures. Nutrient supply was traditionally from forest leaves (Aleurites or Hibiscus). Accessory margin crops include ti (Cordyline), papaya, banana, coconut, and sugar cane. Pigs and ducks are traditional livestock; constructing their pens over feeder canals or upper terraces adds to nutrient supply. Yields of 55 t/ha are achievable with 55,500 bulbs/ha averaging 1 kg each.

Dryland taro: Grown under irrigation, responds well to thick mulch and green crop. Intercrop of Brassica, melons, ginger, or lettuce is viable. Beds 1.3 m wide at 30 beds/ha, taro spaced 40 x 60 cm in diamond pattern.

Integration: Every tropical garden deserves a patch of taro, often grown as mulched groundcover below fruit trees in higher rainfall areas, or as subsidiary crop in banana, guava, avocado, and macadamia orchards.

## Sweet Potato (Ipomoea batatas)

Sweet potato is one of the most reliable root crops across tropical, subtropical, and temperate systems. In the tropical home garden, sweet potato yields better than or in place of potato. It is listed as a drought-tolerant staple for desert gardens. Sweet potatoes can be grown in deep (0.5-1 m) boxes filled with mulch, pine needles, and household scraps. Propagation is by vine cuttings or tuber shoots pushed down into mulch or buried and then mulched.

## Yam and Yam Bean

Yams are featured in tropical home gardens, yielding better than potatoes in humid tropics. Desert gardens include yam beans (jicama, Pachyrhizus spp.) as reliable alternatives to potatoes. Desert yam bean pits can be placed as field reserves. In the desert, buried tubers throw out great patches of melon, bean, and yam vines in response to rare rains. Yam and Dolichos beans trellis on tree legumes in tropical polycultures.

## Cassava (Manihot esculenta)

Cassava is featured in tropical terrace culture systems. Variations on terrace culture include wet terrace (padi), fish crop, shadehouse, ridge crop, palm, and grain culture to vary production and stabilise terrace systems. Cassava is a drought-tolerant tropical staple providing high carbohydrate yields.

## Potato (Solanum tuberosum)

Potatoes can be grown in deep (0.5-1 m) boxes filled with mulch, pine needles, and household scraps, although the manual notes yam beans, jicama, sweet potato, and sunroot (Helianthus tuberosus) supply more reliable yields than potatoes in arid and tropical areas.

## Sunroot / Jerusalem Artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus)

A hardy perennial tuber crop listed for earthwork site preparation (roots to establish). It is a reliable yielder in desert gardens and temperate systems. Sunroot is noted as providing more reliable yields than potatoes in challenging climates.

## Other Root Crops

PARSNIP, SALSIFY, DAIKON RADISH: Listed among "spike root" species for earthwork site preparation. These open compacted soil and can be broadcast with mixed seed on new earth.

HORSERADISH: Listed for earthwork site establishment (roots). Also serves as a culinary perennial.

GINGER AND TURMERIC: Tropical rhizomatous herbs listed for earthwork site establishment and as important tropical garden crops. See [[permaculture-designers-manual-herbaceous-plants]] for their role in tropical herbaceous systems.

TURNIP and RADISH: Included in earthwork site seed mixes for rapid soil opening.

## Design Notes

Root crops are essential for food self-reliance. They require little cooking compared to grains (saving energy in the home), contain essential minerals and vitamins, and can make every family food self-reliant. In desert gardens, root crops are the mainstay against famine. Tuber and bulb planting classes include: greened and sprouted tubers pushed down into mulch, or buried and then mulched.

The selection of root crop species for any given site should follow the general permaculture principle of matching species to climate, soil, and available water. In [[permaculture-designers-manual-arid-climates]], desert-adapted tubers like yam bean are preferred over temperate potatoes. In [[permaculture-designers-manual-tropical-climates]], taro and cassava are primary staples. Root crops also serve soil-building functions through their deep penetration and organic matter contributions, making them valuable in [[mollison-soil-erosion-control]] and earthwork stabilisation.
