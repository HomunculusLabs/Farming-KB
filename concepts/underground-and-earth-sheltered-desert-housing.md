# Underground and Earth-Sheltered Desert Housing

Underground and earth-sheltered housing represents one of the most effective and time-tested approaches to desert dwelling. From the ancient cave complexes of Coober Pedy in Australia to the courtyard pit houses of North Africa and the troglodyte dwellings of the Canary Islands, cultures across every desert continent have independently converged on subterranean living as the optimal strategy for thermal comfort. These structures maintain stable temperatures around 25°C with annual fluctuation of only 5°C, require minimal maintenance, and cost approximately 50% less than equivalent surface dwellings.

## Why Underground Housing Works in Deserts

Deserts are the ideal environment for underground, earth-sheltered, or cave housing because:
- The earth mass provides enormous thermal inertia, buffering extreme diurnal temperature swings
- Below-ground spaces are naturally protected from scorching daytime heat and freezing nighttime cold
- They require no external energy for heating or cooling under normal conditions
- They are inherently resistant to wind, sandstorms, and surface flooding
- Soft rock formations (sandstone, tuff, loess, [[ferricrete]] "ceiling" layer that provides a stable, impermeable roof
- Volcanic sediments, rhyolite, tuff, loess, or bauxitic rock are all suitable substrates
- Sites in granites or volcanics may be radon-rich, requiring special ventilation measures
- Mud for bricks, pisé, or bulldozed earth-wall shelter is usually available in desert environments

## Traditional Cave Construction Methods

### Hand Excavation (Traditional)

Where modern machinery is unavailable, cave construction proceeds with basic tools:
- Wheelbarrow, pick, and shovel are the primary implements
- Quite ordinary axes will plane soft rock walls smooth
- Site selection is enhanced by choosing soft rock capped with calcrete or a hard impermeable layer at roof level
- Spoil is removed by wheelbarrow as excavation proceeds
- Miners traditionally built such dwellings across desert regions worldwide

### Modern Mechanical Excavation (Coober Pedy Model)

Australia's opal mining industry has driven development of efficient cave construction techniques now widely applied to residential dugouts:

1. **Site preparation**: A bulldozer cuts a cliff face 3–6 m high into the selected hillside of soft stone
2. **Large-bore drilling**: Drills of 1–1.5 m diameter (such as the "Caldwell") cut corridors, rooms, and storage caves to approximate size
3. **Deep wells**: Smaller diameter drills sink wells for toilets or water cisterns
4. **Ventilation shafts**: Strategic vents, light shafts, and skylights are drilled to illuminate inner rooms
5. **Hand finishing**: Rooms are hand-trimmed smooth with axes
6. **Floor sealing**: Floors are sealed with level concrete, with electrical conduits and plumbing laid before pouring
7. **Spoil removal**: Large blowers (like vacuum pumps) remove spoil as drills cut shafts
8. **Drying and sealing**: Newly trimmed rooms dry for several months before Bondcrete® or polyvinylacetate sealer is sprayed on stone to prevent dust

Caves built by this method are dust-free, silent, and easy to maintain.

## Ventilation and Radon Gas

Proper ventilation is essential in underground housing, particularly in volcanic sediments or rhyolite rock where **radon gas** may accumulate in unvented rooms. Solutions include:

- **Capped surface vents** that draw air through the dwelling
- **Solar chimneys** made of black-painted sheet metal that ensure positive venting of household heat, steam, and cooking gases
- **Drilled ventilation shafts** connecting rooms to the surface
- These chimneys heat up in sunlight, creating an updraft that continuously draws fresh air through the living spaces

## Hot and Cold Cave Storage

Within cave complexes, specialised storage caves can be built for different temperature requirements:

### Hot Caves

- A bubble of hot, still air gathers in the cave during the day
- Dry heat storage suits dried fruits and mummifies wood and paper
- The entry must be overhanging to trap hot upslope air
- Cave walls act as effective heat stores
- Built upslope from the entry to hold the hot air
- May be unhealthy for living (inadequate ventilation) but excellent for grain and livestock storage in winter

### Cold Caves

- Excellent root storage areas and general storage for books, films, machine parts, and foodstuffs
- The entry lies in a dip on a hillside, permitting cold night air to pool in the downsloping cave
- A sill at the entry is essential where rains can affect drainage
- Most effective in climates with significant diurnal temperature variation

## North African Courtyard Pit Houses

In North Africa, large pits (approximately 20 × 20 m) are cut into the ground, with caves built off them into the soft rock. These courtyard pit complexes feature:

- Cool, protected interior environments
- Good gutters to lead water around the pit edges
- Trellised walls and covered top openings
- Water cisterns cut into the pit floor or upper surface
- Drainage via a cave exit to a cliff face (hence cliff sites are preferred)
- Rooms opening off the central courtyard pit

## The Forestieri House (Fresno, California)

One of the most extraordinary underground homes was constructed by miner M. Forestieri at Fresno, California:
- The underground house covered approximately 2.8 hectares
- It included bathrooms, fishponds, and a large citrus garden
- The only surface indication was the tops of the citrus trees
- A calcrete layer formed the ceiling of upper rooms
- The complex could be endlessly extended
- Very comfortable in the desert climate

## Earth-Sheltered Surface Housing

Where soils or stone will not support cave excavation, surface housing can effectively duplicate cave conditions:

- **Concrete walls and roofing** with earth banks built up to the eaves (and optionally over the roof)
- **Turkeys nest construction**: A bulldozer consolidates a "turkeys nest" dam above ground from stable clay-fraction soils; when formed and roofed, it becomes a cool desert house
- Allows variety of interior wall treatments
- Safe from even severe surface flooding
- Vines can shade sunward walls

### Pueblo and Scarp Housing

Intermediates between excavated and surface housing include:
- **Notch-sheltered pueblo housing** of the Great Basin desert (USA)
- **Rock shelter dwellings** at the base of scarps or cliffs, "built in" with mud brick
- Provide cool and secure storage and living spaces
- Safe from flooding, rain, and sun
- Even when abandoned, these structures last for hundreds of years in good condition

## Lighting Underground Spaces

Modern technology enables effective illumination of deep cave complexes:

- **Fresnel lenses** and surface mirrors collect and concentrate sunlight
- **Reflector mirrors** and light guides of solid plastic direct light kilometres deep into tunnels and rooms
- **Camera obscurae** or lenses project scenes of the outside world onto interior walls
- Occupants can admire the outside world while avoiding the heat

## Sanitation Systems

### Deep Drill-Hole Toilets (Coober Pedy)

- Vertical drill-holes 20–30 m deep, sited a few feet from the door
- Low-flush toilet systems feed into them, then vented to air
- Alternatively, dry pits with [[compost]], downdraught, and solar chimneys
- Last for years; best occasionally pumped out to establish trees around the site

### Soakage Beds

- Where water is sufficient, low-flush toilets lead to soakage beds
- Large trees rapidly transpire the water and convert nutrients
- Large shallow leach-fields with thick top mulch are officially permissible in arid Australia
- Surplus run-off is absorbed and infiltrated to soil
