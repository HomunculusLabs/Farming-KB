# Psycholytic vs Psychedelic Therapy Models

## Overview

Two foundational models guided the clinical use of [[metzner-hallucinogenic-drugs-psychotherapy-shamanism]] in the mid-twentieth century: the **psycholytic** model, developed primarily in Europe, and the **psychedelic** model, developed primarily in the Anglo-American world.

Ralph Metzner, who worked with [[timothy-leary]] and [[richard-alpert]] at Harvard in the 1960s, provides a comprehensive comparison of these approaches in his 1998 paper "Hallucinogenic Drugs and Plants in [[metzner-hallucinogens-psychotherapy-and-shamanism]]."

While superficially different in methodology, both models share fundamental assumptions that distinguish hallucinogen-assisted therapy from conventional pharmacotherapy.

## The Psycholytic Model

### Origins and Approach

The psycholytic model emerged from the Sandoz laboratories in Switzerland, where [[albert-hofmann]] had first synthesized LSD.

The term "psycholytic" means "psychic loosening" or "psychic opening" (from the German *seelische Auflockerung*).

In this model, neurotic patients suffering from anxiety, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorders, or psychosomatic conditions were given LSD in a series of sessions at gradually increasing doses.

These sessions were conducted within more or less standard analytic interactions using a Freudian perspective.

### Rationale

The rationale was that through psycholysis — the loosening of psychological defenses — the patient would become more vividly aware of previously unconscious emotional dynamics and reaction patterns.

These patterns were presumed to have been acquired in early family interactions.

Such insight would bring about a resolution of inner conflicts.

### Grof's Perinatal Matrices

The Czech psychiatrist Stanislav Grof, working within the psycholytic model, made the startling discovery that in a series of increasing doses there could be an even deeper psychic opening — to birth and prebirth memories.

After resolving conflicts stemming from Freudian dynamics of early childhood, patients would find themselves reliving the significant sensory-emotional features of their birth experience.

Grof gave the name "perinatal matrices" to these patterns.

This discovery expanded the psycholytic model beyond its original Freudian framework into what Grof would later call "transpersonal psychology."

## The Psychedelic Model

### Origins and Approach

The psychedelic model was developed more or less simultaneously with the psycholytic approach.

The English psychiatrist Humphrey Osmond, working in Canada with Abram Hoffer on the treatment of alcoholism with LSD, introduced the term "psychedelic" in a famous exchange of letters with Aldous Huxley.

"Psychedelic" means "mind-manifesting."

Psychedelic therapy usually involved one or a small number of high-dose sessions, during which the contents of the unconscious mind would be manifested in the form of vivid hallucinatory imagery, leading to insight and transformation.

### Early Applications

First used in the treatment of alcoholics, psychedelic therapy was thought to simulate the often life-changing "bottoming out" experience.

A second center for psychedelic therapy developed in Southern California, where Sidney Cohen, Oscar Janiger and others began providing psychedelic experiences to clients in the Hollywood film, arts and media community.

### The Harvard Project

Timothy Leary, Frank Barron, Richard Alpert and the [[hofmann-leary-case-and-harvard-research]] project adopted and popularized the term "psychedelic."

Their work focused not so much on treatment or therapy but on exploring the possibilities and values of the psychedelic experience for "normals" (mostly graduate students) as well as artists, musicians, poets and writers.

The concept of "[[mckenna-consciousness-expansion-and-drug-war]]" was introduced for these experiences, which could be contrasted with the contracted, fixated awareness characteristic of narcotic addictions, obsessions and compulsions.

## Shared Features of Both Models

Despite their methodological differences, the [[psycholytic-and-psychedelic-therapy-models]] models share several fundamental features:

### Expanded States of Consciousness

Both models recognize that psychotherapy with hallucinogens invariably involves an experience of a profoundly expanded state of consciousness.

In this state, the individual can not only gain therapeutic insight into neurotic or addictive emotional dynamics, but may come to question and transcend fundamental self-concepts and views of the nature of reality.

### The [[set-and-setting-hypothesis]]

It is widely accepted that set and setting are the most important determinants of experiences with psychedelics, while the drug plays the role of a catalyst or trigger.

This contrasts with other psychoactive drugs (stimulants, depressants, narcotics) where the pharmacological action seems paramount and set and setting play a minor role.
