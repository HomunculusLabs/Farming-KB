## Interceptor Drains

Designed by **Harry Whittington of West Australia**, interceptor drains address
waterlogging and secondary salinisation caused by uncontrolled overland flow in
dryland valley soils.

### Purpose and Function

- **Prevent overland waterflow** from saturating valley floor soils
- **Stop salt water seepages** moving through shallow sand seams
- **Isolate large blocks of soil** from both waterlogging and salt intrusion
- **Direct intercepted flow** safely to streams and valley run-offs
- After installation, **trees can be planted in previously desertified soils**

### Design Specifications

- Always **1.5–2.5 metres deep** for interception below surface flow
- **Carefully spaced** to maximise catchment efficiency
- Flow gradient of **1:600 to 1:1500** cross-slope
- Cut degraded croplands into management blocks of **100 m to 300 m wide**

### Construction Method

- Always involve **ramming of subsoil layers hard against the downhill bank**
- Ramming by bulldozer blade creates a compacted impermeable barrier

### Rehabilitative Earthworks

- **Replace salted and eroded lands** with productive perennial systems
- Establish perennial browse, pastures, or forests on previously unproductive land
- [[interceptor-drains-and-desertification-control]] spreader banks for reforestation of desertified soils
- Improve soil health, biodiversity, and long-term productive capacity

### Damaging Earthworks

- **Irrigate high water-demand crops in drylands**, depleting scarce water
- Divert water for **unessential uses to large urban centres** at the expense of ecological health
- Treat water as a consumable commodity rather than a finite, shared resource
- Ultimately degrade the very land they are meant to serve

### Design Principle

The ethical designer must evaluate every earthwork proposal against its
long-term consequences. Earthworks that **regenerate degraded land, build soil,
and establish perennial systems** are aligned with [[permaculture-ethics]]. Those
that **extract water for inappropriate uses or accelerate degradation** must be
rejected or redesigned. The same dam, drain, or spreader bank can be
rehabilitative or damaging — the difference lies in how the harvested water is used.
