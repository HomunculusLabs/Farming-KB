# Diversion Drains and Spreader Banks

Diversion drains, interceptor drains, spill gates, and spreader banks form an interconnected family of earthworks used in permaculture design to capture, convey, and distribute water across landscapes. Together, they constitute the essential plumbing of any comprehensive rainwater harvesting system, enabling the designer to move water from where it is abundant to where it is needed, while simultaneously protecting soils from erosion, waterlogging, and salinisation.

These structures are integral to the permaculture approach to water management, which treats every drop of rainfall as a resource to be guided, stored, and released according to the needs of the site. Rather than allowing water to accumulate and flow destructively across bare or degraded ground, the designer installs a cascade of interceptors, channels, gates, and spreaders that work together to moderate flows, recharge soils, and sustain vegetation.

## Diversion Drains

Diversion drains serve as conduits that move water from streams to dams, or collect overland flow and convey it to storage systems. Unlike swales, which are designed primarily to infiltrate water into the soil profile along their length, diversion drains are oriented toward *transport* — moving surplus water efficiently across slopes and contours to designated collection points.

They are a standard component of any rainwater harvesting layout, often linking multiple elements of a site's water strategy into a coherent network. A diversion drain might carry runoff from a hillside catchment to a farm dam, or channel spillage from one storage to another lower in the landscape. In large-scale designs, networks of diversion drains can redistribute water across entire valleys, reducing the risk of downstream flooding while maximising the amount of water available for productive use.

### Temporary Dams for Flood Irrigation

A simple but effective technique using diversion drains involves placing a plastic sheet across the drain channel. One end of the sheet is supported by the channel banks, while the other end is weighted down with a chain sewn into the hem of the plastic. This arrangement forms a temporary, low-cost dam that causes the channel to back up and overflow onto the adjacent land downslope, providing flood irrigation. The sheet can be deployed or removed as needed, making this a highly flexible and adaptive irrigation method requiring minimal permanent infrastructure.

This approach is particularly well suited to grazing systems and broadacre pastures, where periodic deep watering of the root zone is more beneficial than frequent shallow irrigation. The temporary nature of the barrier means the drain can be returned to its normal transport function at any time, simply by removing the plastic sheet.

## Interceptor Drains

Interceptor drains are sealed drainage structures specifically designed to prevent overland waterflow and the associated waterlogging that degrades dryland valley soils within a desert soil catena. Their purpose is fundamentally *protective*: they totally intercept overland flow and redirect it safely to streams or valley run-offs before it can cause damage to soils and vegetation downslope.

In dryland landscapes, overland flow during heavy rainfall events can carry dissolved salts, fine sediments, and organic matter down the catena — the sequence of soil types that grades from upslope to valley floor. Without interception, this flow concentrates in the lowest positions, waterlogging valley soils and accelerating their collapse into unproductive saline flats. Interceptor drains break this destructive cycle by capturing the flow before it reaches the vulnerable valley soils.

### Construction Method

Interceptor drains are constructed by excavating a trench and then ramming the subsoil layers hard against the downhill bank using a bulldozer blade. This compaction is critical — it creates a sealed barrier that prevents water from seeping through the downhill wall of the drain, ensuring that intercepted water flows only along the channel to its intended discharge point. Interceptor drains are always excavated to a depth of **1.5 to 2.5 metres** and must be carefully spaced to manage the full volume of expected overland flow across the slope.

The spacing between interceptor drains depends on the slope gradient, soil type, and expected rainfall intensity. On steeper slopes or in areas with impermeable subsoils, drains may need to be placed more closely together. On gentle slopes with well-drained soils, wider spacing is possible.

### Managing Salty Seepage

In landscapes where deeper sand seams carry saline groundwater toward the surface, interceptor drains can be augmented with a more aggressive sealing strategy. The sandy seam is trenched out and stopped with a **vertical plastic barrier** backed by compacted clay. This impermeable curtain prevents the upward and lateral movement of salt-laden water through the soil profile, protecting vulnerable agricultural land from salinisation.

The plastic barrier must extend deep enough to reach an impermeable layer below the sand seam, otherwise saline water will simply flow around the bottom of the barrier and continue to rise to the surface. The compacted clay backing provides structural support for the plastic and adds a secondary seal against leakage.

### Slope Specifications and Land Division

Interceptor drains are graded to flow at a cross-slope ratio of **1:600 to 1:1500**, ensuring that intercepted water moves slowly and steadily toward discharge points without causing erosion within the drain itself. This very gentle gradient means that water flows without gaining destructive velocity.

One of their most important applications is in the rehabilitation of degraded croplands subject to desertification. By cutting such lands into blocks of **100 metres to a maximum of 300 metres wide**, interceptors isolate each block from flooding and from the salty cascade flow that typically moves down degraded slopes. Surplus water is carried off efficiently in streams, preventing the concentration of water and salts in any single area. Once isolated, each block can be individually rehabilitated with appropriate vegetation and soil management practices.

## Spill Gates

Spill gates are control structures placed within drains to regulate the flow of water and direct it to specific areas of the landscape. They are the valves of the permaculture water system, allowing the operator to decide where, when, and how much water is released from any given drain. They come in two principal forms:

### Fixed Spill Gates

Fixed spill gates are permanent installations typically constructed from concrete. They consist of slide holders and aprons that allow a gate to be raised or lowered to control the volume of water released from the drain. Fixed gates are robust and suited to long-term, predictable flow management. They can be positioned to open directly onto ridge lines, distributing water across broad contour areas for passive irrigation or erosion control.

Concrete spill gates are the appropriate choice where flows are large, frequent, or where the gate must withstand significant hydraulic pressure. They require more initial investment but offer decades of reliable service with minimal maintenance.

### Moveable Spill Gates ("Flags")

Moveable spill gates are lightweight, portable structures made from plastic sheets weighted with a chain sewn into the foot of the sheet. The plastic is supported across the drain by a light pipe. These are commonly called **"flags"** and represent one of the most elegant and labour-efficient water distribution devices available in permaculture design.

Flags can be placed and removed at any point along a drain, giving the operator extraordinary flexibility in directing water. With this system, **one to two people can water 200 to 240 hectares in a single morning** — a remarkable throughput that demonstrates the efficiency of well-designed passive water distribution infrastructure. The low cost of plastic flags means that multiple units can be kept on hand and deployed as conditions require, making them ideal for responsive, adaptive water management.

### Wildfire Control Applications

Spill gates, particularly the moveable flag type, serve a dual purpose as an effective **wildfire control system** in forested areas. By flooding designated zones ahead of an approaching fire front, the saturated ground and vegetation act as a firebreak. More sophisticated installations can be controlled remotely via **radio signals**, powered by storage batteries or buried electrical conduits, allowing rapid deployment of water barriers across large areas without exposing personnel to dangerous conditions.

This dual functionality — irrigation and fire protection — makes spill gates an especially valuable investment in forest-edge properties and rural settlements where both water management and fire risk are ongoing concerns.

## Spreader Banks

Spreader banks are broad, level structures designed to spill a thin sheet of surplus overflow water down a grassy slope. Their purpose is twofold: to provide gentle, passive irrigation and to prevent the concentrated channel scour and gullying that occurs when water is allowed to flow unchecked down a single pathway.

### Design and Construction

Spreader banks normally receive overflow from swales and dams. They may extend for tens or even hundreds of metres along a contour. The critical design feature is that the **lower side is dead level** — this ensures that water spills evenly across the entire length of the bank rather than concentrating at a single low point. The lower side may be compacted earth or, for greater durability, concreted.

Water enters the spreader bank from a dam, swale, or minor stream and departs as a thin, uniform sheet flow that moves downslope across vegetated ground. This sheet flow is inherently non-erosive because the energy of the water is dissipated across the entire width of the slope. The spoil excavated during construction is piled uphill in mounds, which themselves can be planted and stabilised, adding to the system's overall productivity.

### Integration with Irrigation Bay Systems

On flatlands used for irrigation, spreader banks are often modified to feed a system of irrigation bays. The bank is pierced by **dead-level pipe outlets** that deliver water into bays confined by low side walls known as **steering banks**. A tail drain at the end of the bay system leads off excess water to a stream or secondary storage, preventing waterlogging at the lowest point.

The primary feeder drain supplying the spreader bank is called the **head race**. Cross-slides within the head race can be used to block or release flow into specific sections of the spreader, giving the operator fine-grained control over which areas receive water at any given time. This combination of head race, cross-slides, pipe outlets, steering banks, and tail drain forms a complete, self-managing irrigation circuit.

## Irrigation Bay Layout

The design of irrigation bays connected to spreader banks follows specific guidelines to maximise water use efficiency:

- **Bay length** should not exceed **100 metres**. Longer bays risk significant water loss to evaporation before the water reaches the tail end of the bay.
- The **head drain** feeding the bays should have a slight slope, allowing pipes to be progressively blocked so that water spills via minor siphons or sills into individual bays in sequence.
- **All pipes used in flow control** have plugs on the upstream side, enabling precise control over both the rate and timing of water delivery to each bay.

This system allows a single operator to manage the irrigation of large areas with minimal infrastructure, simply by opening and closing plugs in the head race pipes according to a predetermined schedule.

## Earthworks Philosophy

Bill Mollison emphasises that the purpose and context of earthworks determines whether they are beneficial or harmful:

- **Rehabilitative and remedial earthworks** replace salted, eroded, and degraded lands with perennial browse systems, forests, and restored ecosystems. Interceptor drains, spreader banks, and diversion drains used in this context heal landscapes and rebuild soil health.
- **Damaging and exploitative earthworks** use the same techniques to irrigate high water-demand crops in inherently dry landscapes, or to divert water for non-essential urban uses. Applied without regard to the carrying capacity of the land, these practices accelerate degradation and deplete finite water resources.

The distinction rests not on the engineering itself but on the designer's intent and the appropriateness of the earthworks to the local climate, soils, and ecological context. A permaculture designer must always ask: does this earthwork serve the long-term health of the land and its community, or does it extract from it?
