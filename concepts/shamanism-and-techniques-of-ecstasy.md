# Shamanism and Techniques of Ecstasy

Shamanism is the practice of the Upper Paleolithic tradition of healing,
divination, and theatrical performance based on natural magic, developed
ten to fifty thousand years ago. Drawing primarily on Mircea Eliade's
foundational work "Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy," Terence
McKenna presented shamanism as humanity's original religious practice —
one that predates all organized religions and maintains a surprising
internal coherency across all times and cultures. See also [[stoned-ape-hypothesis]].

## Defining Shamanism

Shamanism is not a religion in the modern sense but a set of techniques
and practices aimed at achieving ecstasy — a state of consciousness
beyond ordinary experience. Whether the shaman is an Arctic-dwelling
Inuit or a Witoto of the Upper Amazon, certain techniques and
expectations remain the same. Most important of these invariants is
ecstasy itself.

The word "shaman" comes from the Tungus language of Siberia and refers
to a person who enters an altered state of consciousness at will to
contact and interact with the spirit world on behalf of their community.
The shaman serves as healer, diviner, psychopomp (guide of souls), and
keeper of ecological and cosmological knowledge.

## The Shamanic Initiation

Eliade demonstrated that shamanic initiation follows a consistent
pattern across cultures: the neophyte undergoes a symbolic death and
resurrection, understood as a radical transformation into a superhuman
condition. This process typically involves:

**Psychosomatic illness:** The novice often experiences a period of
physical or psychological crisis — moodiness, frailty, solitude, and
sometimes fits of epilepsy or catatonia. This "initiatory sickness"
marks the beginning of the shamanic calling.

**Prolonged trance:** The novice lies as though dead or in deep trance
for days on end. During this period, they are approached in dreams by
helping spirits and receive instruction from them.

**Death and dismemberment:** The novice undergoes an episode of mystical
death and resurrection. They may see themselves reduced to a skeleton
and then clothed with new flesh, boiled in a caldron, devoured by
spirits, or operated on by spirits who remove their organs and replace
them with "magical stones."

**Transformation and investiture:** Having effected their own cure
through mystical transmutation, the shaman is invested with the power of
the sacred and can now cure others. The shaman is more than merely a
sick man or madman — they are a sick person who has healed themselves
and must shamanize in order to remain cured.

## Techniques for Achieving Ecstasy

Not all shamans use intoxication with plants to obtain ecstasy, but all
shamanic practice aims to give rise to ecstatic states. The methods
include:

- Drumming and rhythmic percussion to induce trance states
- Manipulation of breath (pranayama-like techniques)
- Fasting and sensory deprivation
- Ordeals and physical trials
- Sexual abstinence before ceremonies
- Theatrical illusions and performance
- And most effectively, the use of psychoactive plants

None of these methods is as effective, as ancient, and as overwhelming as
the use of plants containing chemical compounds that produce visions.
The use of visionary plant intoxicants was nearly universal among
indigenous shamanic traditions worldwide.

## Shamanism vs Modern Medicine

A crucial distinction between shamanism and modern medicine lies in who
takes the psychoactive substance. Usually in shamanic practice, the
shaman, not the patient, will take the drug. The motivation is entirely
different from modern pharmacology — the shamanic plants are not intended
to stimulate the immune system or the body's natural defenses.

Rather, the shamanic plants allow the healer to journey into an invisible
realm in which the causality of the ordinary world is replaced with the
rationale of natural magic. In this realm, language, ideas, and meaning
have greater power than cause and effect. Sympathies, resonances,
intentions, and personal will are linguistically magnified through
poetic rhetoric.

## A World Made of Language

The evidence from millennia of shamanic experience argues that the world
is actually made of language in some fashion. Boston University
anthropologist Misia Landau noted that "the twentieth-century linguistic
revolution is the recognition that language is not merely a device for
communicating ideas about the world, but rather a tool for bringing the
world into existence in the first place."

For the shaman, the cosmos is a tale that becomes true as it is told,
and as it tells itself. This perspective implies that human imagination
can seize the tiller of being in the world. The shaman is the remote
ancestor of the poet and artist — our need to feel part of the world
seems to demand expression through creative activity.

## Shamanism as Social Catalyst

McKenna argued that shamanism functioned as a powerful social catalyst in
early human communities. The ecstatic dissolution of the ego experienced
in shamanic trance created bonds of community and shared understanding
that held groups together. The suppression of this ecstatic dimension,
McKenna contended, has contributed to the alienation and narcissism that
characterize modern industrial civilization.

## The Decline of Shamanism

The prehistoric but post-Archaic times of approximately 5,000 to 3,000
BCE saw the suppression of partnership society by patriarchal invaders,
setting the stage for the suppression of shamanic practice. In highly
organized societies, the Archaic shamanic tradition was replaced by
dogma, priestcraft, patriarchy, warfare, and eventually the "rational
and scientific" or dominator values that characterize Western
civilization.

McKenna viewed this transition as a catastrophic loss — the suppression
of shamanic gnosis has robbed humanity of life's meaning and made
enemies of the planet, of ourselves, and of our grandchildren.
