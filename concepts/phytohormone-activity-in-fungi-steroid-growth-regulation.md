# Phytohormone Activity in Fungi

## Overview

Phytohormones are plant-derived signaling molecules traditionally understood to [[bloomfield-siren-songs-fungal-chemical-communication-reproduction-chapter-7]] in natural ecosystems.

## Brassinosteroids as a Phytohormone Class

Brassinosteroids are polyhydroxylated steroidal compounds first isolated from rapeseed (*Brassica napus*) pollen in 1979 by Grove et al. Their discovery represented the identification of a sixth major class of plant hormones, alongside auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, ethylene, and abscisic acid. By 1990, approximately 30 distinct brassinosteroid members had been identified and characterized across a wide taxonomic range of photosynthetic organisms:

- **Angiospermae** (flowering plants) — the most prolific source, with brassinosteroids detected in over 60 species spanning monocots and dicots
- **Gymnospermae** (cone-bearing plants) — including conifers and cycads, suggesting the pathway predates the angiosperm radiation
- **Green algae** (Chlorophyta) — indicating an ancient evolutionary origin for brassinosteroid biosynthesis in the green lineage

The core structural feature of brassinosteroids is a steroidal skeleton (typically based on cholestane, ergostane, or stigmastane) with characteristic hydroxylations at positions C-2, C-3, and C-22/23 in the side chain. These hydroxyl groups are critical for biological activity and distinguish brassinosteroids from other plant steroids. The synthetic compound 22S,23S-homobrassinolide used in the Gartz study was prepared from stigmasterol, a common plant sterol, demonstrating the accessibility of these compounds through semi-synthesis.

Their physiological effects in plants are complex and pleiotropic, including:

- Promotion of cell elongation through cell wall loosening and acid growth mechanism
- Stimulation of cell division via cell cycle regulation at G1/S and G2/M checkpoints
- Enhancement of reproductive development (pollen tube growth, seed set, fruit development)
- Anti-stress effects under drought, temperature extremes, salinity, and pathogen challenge
- Interaction with other hormone [[psilocybin-serotonin-structural-homology-endogenous-familiarity-argument]] with certain fungal membrane receptor proteins, suggesting a possible conserved binding mechanism. If fungal cells possess receptors with affinity for brassinosteroid structures, this would explain the dose-dependent growth response observed at the extremely low concentration of 10⁻² ppm (0.01 ppm).

### Membrane Fluidity Hypothesis
Steroidal compounds can integrate into lipid bilayers and alter membrane fluidity and permeability. This non-receptor-mediated mechanism could explain enhanced nutrient uptake and faster cell division in treated mycelia. The incorporation of exogenous sterols into fungal membranes is well-documented in ergosterol biosynthesis studies, where exogenous sterols can partially substitute for endogenous ergosterol in mutant strains. If homobrassinolide integrates into the fungal plasma membrane, it could increase membrane permeability to nutrients or signaling molecules.

### Stress-Mimicry and Hormesis Hypothesis
Brassinosteroids are known anti-stress agents in plants, activating pathways that enhance cellular resilience. They may activate general stress-response pathways in fungi that secondarily promote growth vigor, similar to how mild stressors (heat shock, mechanical disturbance, sublethal oxidative stress) can stimulate fungal productivity through hormetic mechanisms. The broad "anti-stress" category described by Maught (1981) and Hamada (1986) in agricultural contexts may reflect this same adaptive response.

## Phytohormone Activity Across Other Fungal Groups

While brassinosteroids were the first phytohormones demonstrated to affect fungal growth, researchers have subsequently investigated other plant hormone classes for fungal activity:

| Phytohormone | Reported Fungal Effect | Evidence Quality |
|---|---|---|
| Brassinosteroids | Strong growth promotion, yield increase, morphological improvement | Well-documented (Gartz 1990) |
| Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) | Modest growth modulation, variable by species and concentration | Moderate, inconsistent |
| Gibberellins | Limited effect on most fungi tested | Weak evidence |
| Ethylene | [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]] induction, defense compound upregulation | Emerging evidence |
| Salicylic acid | [[brassinosteroid-suppression-of-incomplete-fruit-body-formation]] (aborted) fruit body formation reduces waste and improves harvest consistency and marketable yield
- **Reduced cultivation time** — Earlier fruiting initiation shortens total crop cycles, allowing more production cycles per unit time

However, several barriers limit commercial adoption: the cost of synthetic brassinosteroid production, the regulatory landscape surrounding growth-promoting substances in food production, and the lack of dose-optimization studies across commercially important species.

## Comparison with Agricultural Brassinosteroid Applications

The application of brassinosteroids to improve crop yields is well-established in agriculture, where they are used as foliar sprays or seed treatments on rice, wheat, maize, and various vegetables. The agricultural dose range typically spans 0.01-1.0 ppm, which overlaps with the 0.01 ppm (10⁻² ppm) concentration used by Gartz et al. in fungal cultures. This overlap suggests that the effective concentration window for brassinosteroid activity is remarkably conserved across kingdoms, supporting the hypothesis of a shared or convergent receptor mechanism.

In agriculture, brassinosteroid application has been shown to increase yields by 5-15% in most crops, with some studies reporting up to 30% increases under stress conditions. The 30-50% dry mass increase observed in brassinosteroid-treated *Psilocybe cubensis* cultures is therefore at the upper end of the typical response range, possibly reflecting the simpler nutritional status of fungal cultures compared to whole-plant systems where multiple limiting factors constrain growth.
