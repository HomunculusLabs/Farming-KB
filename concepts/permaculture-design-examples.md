# Permaculture Design Examples

Mollison's Permaculture Design Course is rich with concrete design examples drawn from real landscapes and projects worldwide. These examples illustrate how [[permaculture-design]] principles translate into practical landscape interventions. The following are key examples from the course material.

## The Trout Pond Conversion

One of Mollison's most vivid examples involves a cattle rancher whose land was plagued by annual grasshopper eruptions, making cattle grazing nearly impossible. Mollison observed that the property had extensive valley floor and bulldozer access. Instead of fighting the grasshoppers, he recommended converting over a hundred acres into a trout pond. Within twelve months, the pond produced eight-pound trout from a self-sustaining system requiring no feeding or management. The grasshoppers themselves became part of the food web. This example demonstrates the principle that the problem is the solution -- converting a pest-driven liability into a productive aquatic asset.

## The Swiss Sewage Design

Mollison describes designing a sewage disposal system for a town of 8,000 people that saved $30,000 per year in engineering costs and an equivalent amount in fuel costs. The design converted sewage into an industrial base for the town:

1. Sewage flows into a holding pond
2. Water passes through a marsh planted with rushes (the most efficient water cleanser), mints, and bog plants
3. Marsh effluent, now containing dissolved nutrients (mainly phosphates and nitrates), flows into productive ponds
4. Bamboo and mint become industrial crops
5. Waterfowl and underwater shelving support wildlife habitat

The system was originally designed for 50 acres but the town expanded it to 1,500 additional acres. It supports vast numbers of black swans and teal. This contrasts with conventional sewage treatment, which Mollison notes produces "poisonous water."

## Dieu-Ponds of England

Mollison describes the mysterious "dieu-ponds" found in parts of England -- small ponds that never dry up despite having no apparent water source. Old dieu-pond builders had a technique: they would dig a pond and line it with gley (compacted organic material that creates a watertight seal). The gley process involves laying organic material (leaves, manure, green matter) in layers, compacting it, and allowing it to decompose anaerobically. This creates an impermeable barrier that holds water indefinitely. These ponds can be dug in a single day and provide permanent water storage even in porous soils.

## The Hawaiian Ohana System

The ancient Hawaiian land division system (Ohana) represents one of the most sophisticated permaculture-design|permaculture systems ever devised. Key features include:

- Land was divided from mountain ridge to reef, following natural watershed boundaries
- Massive stonewalls were built at the keyline to redirect water from upper valleys to ridges and back again
- Forests above the keyline were preserved as taboo areas -- the source of water
- At the sea boundary, rock walls enclosed areas where run-off nutrients were captured as algae, feeding millions of pounds of mullet
- Taro was grown in paddy fields on ridge points
- The entire system -- watershed, people, animals, nutrient flow -- was treated as a single indivisible unit

Mollison considers this system superior to modern approaches and urges its reinvention.

## Valley Dam Sequences

Mollison describes cascading dam systems in valleys where water flows from one storage to the next:

- **Keypoint dams** are the highest and most economical, filling first
- **Contour dams** are shallow with large surface area, very cheap to construct, perched on hillsides
- **Saddle dams** on skyline saddles provide the highest storage with gravity flow to lower areas
- **Valley floor dams** (mill ponds) are installed last, as production dams for fish and crops

The design principle is that water flows from clean high dams, through progressively enriched lower dams, finally reaching the most nutrient-rich production ponds. At each stage, nutrients are captured before they leave the system. The "Chinese would say that the main value of their canals and ponds is for manuring their fields."

## Flatland Solutions

For flat properties without elevation, Mollison offers three design approaches:

1. **Swale systems** with small ponds at intervals -- the swales fill during storms, feeding the ponds, and the water slowly infiltrates into the soil
2. **Ring gardens** with central drip irrigation, especially useful on coral atolls and sandy soils
3. **Earth tank systems** -- tanks built below ground level as "tank-Earth" structures that cannot overflow or burst

## The Front-Yard Pond Integration

Mollison advocates placing productive ponds in front of houses for multiple functions:

- Light reflection onto the house
- Thermal mass for temperature moderation
- Fire protection
- Duck and fish production
- Recreation and aesthetic value
- Nutrient cycling from household waste
- Water cleaning functions (nutrients collect in the mud)

The pond edge can support blueberries, mints, and bamboo, creating a productive multi-layered system immediately adjacent to the home.
