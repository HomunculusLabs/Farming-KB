## Beneficial Fungi, Trichoderma, and Mycorrhizae in AACT

## Overview

Beneficial fungi are among the most important organisms delivered by [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT). They perform critical soil functions including nutrient cycling, disease suppression, soil aggregation, and plant symbiosis. This concept page covers the major groups of beneficial fungi relevant to compost tea, with special emphasis on Trichoderma species and mycorrhizal fungi — two of the most commercially significant and ecologically important fungal groups in agriculture.

## The Role of Fungi in Soil Health

### Why Fungi Matter

Fungi occupy a unique position in the soil foodweb because of their filamentous growth habit:

- **Hyphal networks** — Fungal hyphae (thread-like filaments) extend through soil, connecting nutrient sources across distances that bacteria cannot bridge. A single fungal network can span meters of soil.
- **Nutrient retention** — Fungal biomass has a high C:N ratio (15:1 to 50:1), meaning fungi retain carbon and nitrogen in the soil for extended periods rather than releasing them rapidly
- **Soil aggregation** — Fungal hyphae physically bind soil particles together, creating stable microaggregates and macroaggregates that resist erosion and improve water infiltration
- **Decomposition** — Fungi are the primary decomposers of lignin, cellulose, chitin, and other recalcitrant organic compounds that bacteria cannot break down
- **Disease suppression** — Many fungal species directly antagonize plant pathogens through competition, antibiosis, and parasitism

### Minimum Fungal Levels for Different Plant Types

| Plant Community | Minimum Total Fungi (µg/g soil) | Minimum Active Fungi |
|----------------|-------------------------------|---------------------|
| Annual vegetables | 2-5 µg/g | >70% of total |
| Row crops | 5-20 µg/g | >70% of total |
| Pastures/grasslands | 20-100 µg/g | >70% of total |
| Vines and shrubs | 50-200 µg/g | >70% of total |
| Deciduous trees | 200-500 µg/g | >70% of total |
| Coniferous trees | 500-1000+ µg/g | >70% of total |

## Trichoderma: The Workhorse Biocontrol Fungus

### Overview of Trichoderma

*Trichoderma* is a genus of filamentous fungi that is among the most widely studied and commercially used biocontrol agents in agriculture. Species including *T. harzianum*, *T. viride*, *T. koningii*, and *T. virens* are commonly found in healthy soils and are key components of effective compost teas.

### Mechanisms of Action

Trichoderma suppresses plant diseases through multiple, complementary mechanisms:

1. **Mycoparasitism** — Trichoderma directly attacks and consumes pathogenic fungi:
   - Detects chemical signals from pathogen hyphae
   - Grows toward the pathogen (chemotropism)
   - Coils around pathogen hyphae and penetrates cell walls using lytic enzymes
   - Key enzymes: chitinases, glucanases, proteases, and cellulases

2. **Antibiosis** — Produces toxic secondary metabolites that inhibit or kill competing fungi:
   - **Gliotoxin** — disrupts pathogen cell membranes
   - **Viridin** — inhibits fungal growth
   - **Trichodermin** — protein synthesis inhibitor for competing fungi
   - **Peptaibols** — membrane-disrupting compounds

3. **Competition** — Outcompetes pathogens for space and nutrients:
   - Extremely fast growth rate (colonizes substrates faster than most pathogens)
   - Produces large quantities of enzymes that liberate nutrients before pathogens can access them
   - Forms dense hyphal networks that physically occupy ecological niches

4. **[[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]] (ISR)** — Triggers plant immune responses:
   - Root colonization by Trichoderma primes the plant's defense systems
   - Increases production of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins
   - Enhances callose deposition and lignification at potential infection sites
   - Can protect above-ground plant parts even though the fungus colonizes roots

5. **Plant growth promotion** — Beyond disease control:
   - Solubilizes phosphorus and micronutrients (manganese, iron, zinc)
   - Produces indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), promoting root growth
   - Improves nitrogen use efficiency
   - Increases tolerance to abiotic stress (drought, salinity)

### T-22: The Commercial Trichoderma Strain

T-22 is a specific strain of *Trichoderma harzianum* (Rifai strain 1295-22) developed at Cornell University:

- **Registered biocontrol agent** — One of the first commercially available Trichoderma products
- **Broad-spectrum activity** — Effective against *Rhizoctonia*, *Fusarium*, *Pythium*, *Sclerotinia*, and other soil-borne pathogens
- **Root colonization specialist** — Particularly effective when applied as a seed treatment, soil drench, or in-furrow application
- **Fungal biomass increase** — When preactivated with appropriate food sources, T-22 biomass can increase 10-100× during [[compost-tea-brewing]]

### Preactivation of Trichoderma

Trichoderma spores and mycelium benefit from preactivation before incorporation into AACT:

**Purpose of preactivation**:
- Spores are dormant and may take 12-24 hours to germinate in tea
- Preactivation starts the growth process so Trichoderma is already actively growing when the tea is applied
- Dramatically increases the number of active Trichoderma propagules in the finished tea

**Preactivation protocol**:
1. Mix Trichoderma inoculum with a small amount of fungal food (oatmeal, wheat bran, or compost)
2. Add water to create a moist but not wet mixture (50% moisture)
3. Maintain at 70-75°F (21-24°C) for 12-24 hours
4. Observe for white/green mycelial growth indicating activation
5. Add the entire preactivated mixture to the compost tea brewer

**Activation mediums** that support Trichoderma preactivation:
- Oatmeal or wheat bran (complex carbohydrates + protein)
- Composted wood chips (natural fungal substrate)
- [[malt-extract-agar]] plates (laboratory method)
- Potato dextrose broth (laboratory method)

### Simple Test for Trichoderma Activity

To verify Trichoderma is actively growing in compost tea:
1. Place a small sample of tea on a microscope slide
2. Look for green spores (conidia) borne on branching conidiophores
3. Trichoderma hyphae are typically 2-4 µm in diameter, septate, and grow rapidly
4. In culture, Trichoderma produces characteristic green-colored colonies

## Mycorrhizal Fungi

### Overview

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots, creating an extended nutrient-absorbing network. The term comes from the Greek *mykos* (fungus) and *rhiza* (root). Over 90% of all plant species form mycorrhizal associations in nature.

### Types of Mycorrhizae

| Type | Fungal Partner | Plant Partners | Key Function |
|------|---------------|---------------|-------------|
| **Arbuscular Mycorrhizae (AM/VAM)** | Glomeromycota | Most crops, vegetables, grasses | Phosphorus uptake, disease protection |
| **Ectomycorrhizae (ECM)** | Basidiomycota, Ascomycota | Conifers, oaks, beeches | Nitrogen mining, stress tolerance |
| **Ericoid Mycorrhizae** | Ascomycota | Blueberries, heaths, rhododendrons | Organic nitrogen uptake from acidic soils |
| **Ectendomycorrhizae** | Basidiomycota | Some conifers (loblolly pine) | Intermediate between AM and ECM |

### How Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF) Work

AMF form the most agriculturally significant mycorrhizal type:

1. **Root colonization** — Fungal hyphae enter root cells and form arbuscules (tree-like structures) where nutrient exchange occurs
2. **External hyphal network** — Hyphae extend far beyond the root zone (up to 25 cm or more from the root), accessing nutrients unavailable to roots alone
3. **Phosphorus acquisition** — AMF are exceptionally efficient at solubilizing and transporting phosphorus, especially in high-pH or low-P soils
4. **Carbon exchange** — The plant provides carbohydrates (sugars, lipids) to the fungus; the fungus provides minerals and water to the plant
5. **Water transport** — Hyphal networks can access water from soil micropores that roots cannot reach, improving drought tolerance

### Mycorrhizal Fungi and AACT

**Important limitation**: Mycorrhizal fungi **cannot be grown in compost tea**. They are obligate symbionts that require a living plant root to complete their life cycle. However, AACT plays a critical supporting role:

- **AACT improves soil conditions** for mycorrhizal colonization by building the bacterial and fungal community that mycorrhizae depend on
- **AACT does not contain AMF spores** — AMF must be applied separately (as commercial inoculant or naturally present in soil)
- **Fungal-dominated AACT** provides the bacterial community that supports [[mycorrhizal-helper-bacteria]]
- **Compost tea with AMF spores** — Some practitioners add AMF spores directly to compost tea at the end of brewing for application; the spores remain viable but do not multiply in the tea

### Practices That Harm Mycorrhizae

- **Tillage** — Physically severs hyphal networks; reduces mycorrhizal colonization for weeks to months
