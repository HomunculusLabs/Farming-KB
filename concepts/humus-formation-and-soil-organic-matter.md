## What Is Humus

Humus is the relatively stable, dark brown to black organic material that remains after plant and animal residues have been substantially decomposed by soil organisms. It is the end product of the [[soil-food-web]]'s decomposition processes. Unlike fresh organic matter, humus is resistant to further rapid decomposition and can persist in soil for decades to centuries, providing long-term benefits to soil structure, fertility, and biological activity.

Humus is not a single chemical compound but rather a complex mixture of modified lignin, proteins, polysaccharides, fats, waxes, and other organic molecules that have been transformed by microbial activity into long, complex carbon chains.

## The Composition of Humus

The chemical nature of humus gives it extraordinary properties:
- **Carbon-rich molecular chains**: Very long chains of carbon molecules with enormous surface area
- **Electrical charges**: Humus particles carry negative electrical charges that attract and hold positively charged nutrient ions (cations) including calcium, magnesium, potassium, and ammonium. This cation exchange capacity (CEC) is one of humus's most important functions
- **Sponge-like structure**: The molecular chains create countless nooks and crannies that serve as habitat for soil microorganisms, holding both water and air
- **High water-holding capacity**: Humus can absorb and retain 4-5 times its weight in water
- **Buffering capacity**: Humus resists changes in soil pH, helping maintain conditions favorable for biological activity

## How Humus Forms

Humus formation is a multi-stage process driven by the [soil food web:

**Stage 1: Fresh organic matter deposition**
Plant residues (leaves, stems, roots) and animal materials enter the soil surface. These materials are composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, proteins, sugars, and other compounds.

**Stage 2: Primary decomposition**
Bacteria are the first to colonize fresh organic matter, consuming simple sugars and proteins. Fungi follow, breaking down more complex compounds including cellulose and hemicellulose. This rapid initial decomposition releases large amounts of carbon dioxide and mineral nutrients.

**Stage 3: Secondary decomposition**
As the easily decomposed compounds are consumed, [actinomycetes and fungi specialize in breaking down the more resistant materials -- particularly lignin. [[root-exudates]]

[Root exudates play an important but often overlooked role in humus dynamics:
- Plants allocate 10-40% of their photosynthetically fixed carbon to root exudates
- These exudates feed bacteria and fungi in the [rhizosphere
- When these microorganisms die (consumed by protozoa and nematodes, or through natural turnover), their cellular material contributes to the soil organic matter pool
- This continuous cycle of exudation, microbial growth, and microbial death is called the microbial loop and is a major pathway for humus formation below ground

## Humus and Soil Structure

Humus is one of the most important agents of soil aggregation:
- It acts as a binding agent, gluing mineral particles (sand, silt, clay) together into aggregates
- Combined with biological glues like bacterial polysaccharides and [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], humus creates stable soil crumbs (peds)
- Well-aggregated soil has good porosity -- a balance of macropores (for drainage and aeration) and micropores (for water retention)
- Humus improves both the structure of clay soils (reducing compaction) and sandy soils (improving water and nutrient retention)

## Humus and Nutrient Availability

Humus serves as both a nutrient reservoir and a nutrient delivery system:
- The cation exchange capacity of humus holds nutrients in a plant-available form, preventing leaching while keeping them accessible to root uptake
- Humus chelates micronutrients (iron, manganese, zinc, copper), keeping them soluble and available in forms plants can absorb
- The slow mineralization of humus provides a sustained release of nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur
- Humus buffers soil pH, maintaining conditions in the range where most nutrients are available (pH 6.0-7.0)
- The microbial communities living in humus directly solubilize minerals from rock particles, converting them into plant-available forms

## Humus and the Soil Food Web

Humus is both a product and a supporter of the soil food web:
- It is the end product of decomposition by bacteria, fungi, and actinomycetes
- Its porous structure provides habitat for billions of microorganisms per gram of soil
- It supports diverse microbial communities including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes
- The nutrients released from humus fuel the entire soil food web
- In turn, a healthy soil food web continuously produces new humus from fresh organic inputs

## Building Humus in Gardens and Farms

Practices that build humus:
- Adding compost -- finished compost is approximately 30-50% humus by weight
- Applying [mulch that breaks down in place, feeding soil organisms
- Growing [[cover-crops]] and green manures that add both above-ground biomass and root mass to the soil
- Minimizing tillage -- each tillage event oxidizes approximately 0.5-1.0 tonnes of humus per hectare
- Using [compost tea to inoculate soil with diverse microbial communities that accelerate humus formation
- Supporting [[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]] production
- Incorporating [[soil-testing]] for organic matter content allows tracking of humus-building progress over time. An increase of 0.1% per year is considered a realistic goal with good management practices.
