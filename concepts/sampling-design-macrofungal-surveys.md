# Sampling Design Considerations for Macrofungal Surveys

## Overview

Designing an effective sampling program for macrofungal biodiversity requires careful consideration of factors that are unique to fungi or that affect fungi more severely than they affect plants and animals. ### Plot Shape and Environmental Gradients

The shape of a plot can influence the ease, accuracy, and efficiency of sampling. In grasslands, shrublands, and forests, circular plots are delineated easily and quickly with a center pole and a freely swinging radius line. Circular plots have the advantage of minimizing edge effects that can strongly bias density estimates.

However, elongated or rectangular quadrats provide more accurate estimates of species composition than circular or square plots of the same area. The rectangular plot is especially suited for assessing environmental gradients within a habitat when the long axis is oriented parallel to the underlying gradient of interest. The reason is that rectangular quadrats are more effective in detecting habitat heterogeneity and accurately estimating the patchy distribution of organisms than square or circular quadrats. A long quadrat potentially covers more habitat patches than a circular one of equal area.

## Number of Sampling Units

### Collector's Curves and Asymptotes

The total number of species identified from an area of interest increases as the number of samples increases, but eventually attains a plateau (asymptote). ## Vegetation Characterization

### Why Vegetation Matters

The type of vegetation at a site directly affects the species richness and composition of macrofungi because plants constitute the habitat and energy source for most fungi. Vegetation zones or plant associations provide useful criteria for dividing a landscape into sampling strata. Many fungi occur only in association with particular families or genera of plants, and ectomycorrhizal fungal diversity is strongly correlated with forest type (deciduous versus conifer).

Learning to recognize the woody plant species and major plant associations likely to be encountered is particularly important for survey planning. Knowledge of vegetation helps in delimiting plots that accurately represent the habitats present and aids in understanding the habitat requirements of the fungi encountered.

### Precipitation as a Predictor

For ectomycorrhizal fungi, precipitation has been found to be a better predictor of species richness and [[biodiversity-fungal-community-assembly]] even when host plant identity determines the pool of potential species.

## Species Richness Metrics

### Three Kinds of Richness

Three kinds of species richness should be distinguished: (1) numerical species richness — the number of species in a sample where the number of individuals has been standardized; (2) species density — the number of species in a sample where the area, volume, or weight of the sampling unit has been standardized; and (3) total species richness — the cumulative number of species based on a series of samples from a habitat. The effects of environmental variation on these metrics are scale-dependent, meaning that the same environmental factor may show different effects at different sampling scales.

### Estimation from Incomplete Sampling

Because human and financial resources are often too limited to enable complete discovery of species richness, quantitative ecologists have developed methods to estimate total richness from a modest amount of sampling. These include rarefaction analysis, jackknife estimators, bootstrap estimators, and various parametric and non-parametric richness estimators. The choice among these methods should be made prior to choosing an experimental design and should be appropriate to the sampling strategy employed.

## Temporal Sampling Strategy

### Sampling Frequency

Ideally, fleshy macrofungi should be sampled in an area every one to two weeks throughout the fruiting season to maximize the number of species observed. When such a schedule is not possible, a pilot study should be conducted to determine the optimal sampling frequency for the specific site and climate. Studies of annual variation and seasonality have been limited largely to Europe, so data from other regions should be interpreted with appropriate caution.
