# Mushroom Cultivation Technology and World Production

[[mushroom-cultivation]] is a worldwide biotechnological enterprise with an estimated annual value exceeding 14 billion US dollars. This concept covers the principles, practices, and economic scale of gourmet and medicinal mushroom cultivation.

## Global Production Scale

### World Production Growth
World production of cultivated edible and/or [[medicinal-mushrooms]]:
- **1994**: 4,909.3 × 10³ tons
- **1997**: 6,158.4 × 10³ tons
- **Estimated value**: Exceeds **US $14 billion**

The world production showed a **5 times increase in tonnage** over the last two decades.

### Leading Species by Production (1997)

| Species | Production (×10³ tons) | % of Total |
|---------|----------------------|-----------|
| **[[agaricus-bisporus]]** (button mushroom) | 1,955.9 | 31.8% |
| **Lentinus edodes** (Shiitake) | 1,564.4 | 25.4% |
| **Pleurotus spp.** (oyster) | 875.6 | 14.2% |
| **[[auricularia-auricula]]** (wood ear) | 485.3 | 7.9% |
| **[[volvariella-volvacea]]** (paddy straw) | 180.8 | 3.0% |
| **Flammulina velutipes** (enoki) | 284.7 | 4.6% |

These six species make up **87% of total world production**.

### Shifting Production Patterns
- In 1981, Agaricus represented **72%** of world production
- By 1997, this dropped to **32%**
- **Lentinus and Pleurotus** show the most dramatic increases
- "World production of mushrooms is increasingly being dominated by species that are both edible and have medicinal properties"

## Number of Cultivable Species

- **~12,000 species** of fungi can be considered mushrooms
- **~2,000 species** show various degrees of edibility
- **~200 species** collected from wild for traditional medical purposes
- **~35 species** cultivated commercially
- **~20 species** cultivated on an industrial scale

## Operations in Mushroom Cultivation

"Mushroom cultivation involves several different operations each of which must be performed properly if the enterprise is to be successful. Failure of any phase will result in a decreased harvest or total loss."

### 1. Selection of Mushroom Spores or Strains
The first stage is obtaining a pure mycelial culture. Cultures are derived from:
- Single or multi-spore cultures
- Tissue culture from a high-yielding, vigorous strain
- Genetic breeding programmes

Most growers obtain spawn cultures from reputable production centres—ensuring purity, vigour, and supply.

### 2. Maintenance of Mycelial Cultures
Each mushroom type generally requires unique substrate formulation for propagation and maintenance of purity.

### 3. Development of Spawn/Inoculum
Spawn is a medium impregnated with mycelium from a pure culture:
- Inoculum typically **1-5%** of final production medium
- Organic matrix: **sterilised grain** (millet, rye, or wheat)
- Grain serves as both nutrient support and distribution vehicle
- Each grain becomes a "mycelial capsule"
- **All operations must be conducted under sterile techniques**

Spawn is prepared and shipped in autoclavable polypropylene bags with breathing patches. For natural log production, spawn can also be wood chip plugs.

### 4. Preparation of Growing Medium
The bulk substrate must be properly prepared and, for advanced methods, sterilised before inoculation.

### 5. Spawn Inoculation and Colonisation
Spawn is mixed into the prepared substrate; mycelium colonises the entire substrate over days to weeks.

### 6. Crop Management for Mushroom Production
Environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, light, fresh air exchange) must be carefully controlled to trigger fruiting and maximise yield.

## Cultivation Methods

### Log Culture (Traditional)
**Developed in Japan and China over 1000 years ago.** Still used by small growers in Asia for local markets.

#### Procedure
1. Cut logs (~3' long × 6-8" diameter) in winter/early spring
2. Use fast-growing deciduous species (alder, poplar, oak, cottonwood) with thick bark
3. Inoculate with spores or mycelial plugs inserted into drilled holes
4. Stack in piles with mild to heavy soaking
5. Mycelial growth over several months
6. Place logs upright, partly embedded in soil
7. Mushroom production primarily in cooler spring and autumn months

#### Advantages
- Simple and natural method
- Produces high quality mushrooms
- Low technology requirements

#### Disadvantages
- Labour-intensive
- Slow (months to years)
- Seasonal
- Cannot meet demands for high productivity
- Open, non-sterile procedure—contamination possible

### Sawdust Mix Culture (Modern)
Growing mushrooms in sterilised sawdust mixtures. More productive, faster, and allows greater environmental control.

### Submerged Fermentation (Pharmaceutical)
"For greater pharmaceutical acceptance it is increasingly being recognized that product formation by way of fermenter-grown mycelial biomass will be the preferred option in many cases."

Advantages:
- Better quality and homogeneity through process control
- Aseptic conditions
- Scalable production
- Consistent bioactive compound profiles
- Year-round production

## Agaricus bisporus as Model

"The cultivation of Agaricus bisporus is an outstanding example of a biotechnological enterprise that challenges the combined skills of industrial and biological technologies."
