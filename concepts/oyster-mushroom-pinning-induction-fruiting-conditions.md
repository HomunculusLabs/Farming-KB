# Oyster Mushroom Pinning Induction and Fruiting Conditions

## Overview

Pinning induction is the critical transition phase in oyster mushroom cultivation where
fully colonised substrate is stimulated to shift from vegetative mycelial growth to
reproductive fruitbody formation. This phase determines the number, quality and timing
of mushroom harvests. The process is achieved by deliberately "worsening" the growing
environment so that mycelia cannot continue vegetative growth and instead convert to
reproductive mode.

In the life cycle of Pleurotus mushrooms there are two stages: the vegetative stage
and the reproductive growth stage. Generally, stimuli are needed for the shift from
mycelial growth to fruitbody formation. These stimuli include abrupt changes in
temperature, humidity, gas concentration, light, nutrient reserves, and physical
stimuli. Among these, a sharp temperature drop is the most effective trigger for most
mushroom species.

## The Pinning Process

### Incubation Completion

Before pinning can begin, the substrate must be fully colonised. During incubation,
relative humidity is maintained at 65-70% and substrate water content at 65%. Optimal
temperature for mycelial growth is 20-25°C, though some thermophilic strains reach
optimal growth at 25-35°C. Mushroom mycelia tolerate high CO2 concentrations during
incubation — P. ostreatus and P. florida mycelial growth is actually stimulated at CO2
levels up to 28% and 22% respectively. Full colonisation typically takes 12-20 days
depending on species, substrate, and temperature.

### Pinning Induction Methods

Three primary methods trigger pinning:

1. **Cold shock**: The most effective method for most Pleurotus species. Substrate
   blocks or bags are exposed to a sharp temperature drop. For P. ostreatus and
   P. eryngii, fruiting is induced by temperatures of 10-15°C. P. florida, P. sajor-
   caju, P. cornucopiae and P. cystidiosus are less affected by temperature and may
   not require cold shock. In bottle cultivation, growers scratch the surface of the
   bottle for primordial induction.

2. **Watering**: Increasing humidity triggers the shift to reproductive growth. Blocks
   are sprayed with water, and humidity is raised to 80-95%. In some systems, the
   plastic covering is removed entirely upon full colonisation to expose the substrate
   to humid air. Primordia typically appear 4-9 days after opening or cold shock.

3. **Lighting**: Some strains require light of 50-500 lux for primordial formation.
   Primordia formation is only possible after approximately 12 hours of illumination
   at sufficient intensity. Once pins appear, the lighting regime is maintained.

### Stopping Induction

Once pins emerge, growers stop the induction treatments and shift to maintaining
conditions favourable for fruitbody development. This is a critical distinction —
continuing cold shock or excessive watering after pinning can damage developing
fruitbodies.

## Environmental Parameters for Fruiting

Each Pleurotus species requires different optimal conditions for fruitbody development.
The table below summarises key parameters from Stamets (1993):

|---|---|---|---|---|
| P. citrinopileatus | 21-29 | 90-95 | <1,000 | 500-1,000 |
| P. cystidiosus | 21-27 | 85-90 | <2,000 | 500-1,000 |
| P. djamor | 20-30 | 85-90 | 500-1,500 | 750-1,500 |
| P. eryngii | 15-21 | 85-90 | <2,000 | 500-1,000 |
| P. euosmus | 21-27 | 90-95 | <1,000 | 750-1,500 |
| P. ostreatus | 10-21 | 85-90 | <1,000 | 1,000-1,500 |
| P. pulmonarius | 18-24 | 85-90 | 400-800 | 1,000-1,500 |
| P. tuberregium | 30-35 | 85-90 | <2,000 | — |

## CO2 Concentration and Mushroom Morphology

CO2 concentration is critical during fruiting. During mycelial growth, high CO2 in the
substrate container is tolerated or even stimulatory. But ambient CO2 in the growing
room must be controlled by ventilation during fruitbody development.

Under high CO2 or insufficient ventilation, mushrooms produce long stipes with tiny
caps — a commercially undesirable form. Under low CO2 with frequent ventilation, they
produce short stipes with broad caps. In P. ostreatus, CO2 above 1,000 ppm produces
stipes that are too long and results in lower quality mushrooms. The general target
for reproductive growth is below 800 ppm, though this varies by strain.

## Temperature Effects on Quality

Optimal temperature for the highest quality oyster mushrooms is 10-18°C. Temperature
during fruitbody development directly affects cap colour — lower temperatures within
the recommended range produce darker coloured caps, which often command premium prices.
P. eryngii produces best at 13-18°C. P. florida and P. sajor-caju tolerate a wider
range of 15-25°C. P. cornucopiae and P. cystidiosus can produce good mushrooms even
at 30°C.

## Species-Specific Pinning Behaviour

- **P. ostreatus**: Requires cold shock (10-15°C) to initiate primordia. Most widely
  cultivated. Fruiting at 10-21°C. Many commercial strains with varying tolerance.
- **P. eryngii**: Requires cold shock. Fruiting at 13-18°C. Thick white stipe. Often
  grown in bottles or sawdust bags due to slower mycelial growth.
- **P. florida**: Does not require cold shock. Widest temperature range. Highest yield.
  Cap colour lightens with increasing temperature.

## Fruiting Environment Co-Dependencies

Environmental factors — temperature, relative humidity, light, CO2 and substrate pH —
alter together in co-dependent relationships. As growing room temperature rises,
relative humidity decreases. Higher temperature promotes fruitbody metabolism, which
increases respiration rate and CO2 production. Successful growers manage these as an
integrated system rather than adjusting individual parameters in isolation.

## Humidity Management During Fruiting

High relative humidity of 80-95% is essential during fruitbody formation. Humidity is
maintained by frequent light spraying on substrate blocks and the growing room floor.
Spraying must be light and frequent — water pooling on pins or caps causes bacterial
rot. During pinning, higher humidity (90-95%) encourages primordia. As fruitbodies
develop, humidity can be reduced slightly (85-90%) but must never drop below 80%.

In tropical regions, high ambient humidity is an advantage but temperature control is
challenging. In temperate regions, supplemental humidification may be needed. The
growing room should be checked multiple times daily based on hygrometer readings and
direct observation of mushroom condition.

## Common Pinning Problems

- **No pinning**: Insufficient cold shock, inadequate light, or incomplete colonisation.
  Ensure the block is fully white with mycelium before induction.
- **Aborted pins**: Temperature fluctuations, CO2 buildup, or excessive direct watering.
  Maintain stable conditions once pins form.
- **Long stipes, small caps**: Classic CO2 excess. Increase ventilation immediately.
- **Few pins, clustered**: Uneven colonisation or environmental conditions. Ensure
  uniform substrate moisture and even light and air exposure.
