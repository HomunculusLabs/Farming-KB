# Fungal Tropic Responses

Fungal tropic responses are directional growth movements by which mushrooms and other
fungi orient their structures in response to environmental stimuli. These responses are
critical for successful [[spore-dispersal]] and reproductive fitness. The primary tropisms
studied in mushrooms include [[phototropism]] (response to light), geotropism (response to
gravity), and anemotropism (response to wind). At least four distinct responses to light
have been described for mushrooms (Eger-Hummel 1980), underscoring the complexity of
these growth behaviors. Tropic responses are integrated into the broader developmental
program of the [[fungal-fruiting-body]] and interact with endogenous hormonal signals.

## Types of Tropisms

### Phototropism

Phototropism in fungi is the growth response toward or away from light. Light plays
multiple roles in mushroom development beyond simple directional guidance. It is
required for basidiocarp initiation, [[spore]] formation, and the development of geotropic
sensitivity. However, light also exerts an inhibitory effect on stipe elongation,
suggesting a nuanced regulatory relationship rather than a simple positive or negative
response.

The mechanism of phototropism may be indirect: light can heat the mushroom surface,
increasing evaporation rates that then influence growth direction. Notably, experimental
work has demonstrated phototropic responses at very low flux densities—approximately 1%
of natural field conditions—indicating that mushrooms are remarkably sensitive to light
stimuli. The multiple roles of light suggest that photoreception involves multiple
receptor systems or signal transduction pathways.

### Geotropism

Geotropic responses govern how fungal fruiting bodies orient relative to gravity. In
*[[psilocybe-cubensis]]* and many other mushrooms, a striking **geotropic switch** occurs
during development: the stipe initially grows negatively geotropic (upward), but as
spore formation begins, a negative geotropic curvature develops to reorient the pileus
horizontally. This reorientation is critical for effective spore release, as a horizontal
cap position allows basidiospores to fall freely from the gills and be carried away by
air currents.

Crucially, this geotropic switch is not observed in sporeless mutants, strongly
suggesting that a gill-derived substance modulates the tropic response. The link between
spore production and the change in gravitropic behavior points to a tightly coupled
developmental program rather than a passive mechanical response.

### Anemotropism

Anemotropism—the response to wind or air currents—has been documented in several fungi,
most notably in the zygomycete *Phycomyces*. Cohen et al. (1975) described anemotropic
and avoidance responses in *Phycomyces*, while Johnson and Gamow (1971) demonstrated the
importance of wind and water vapor gradients in guiding fungal growth. The avoidance
response, in which sporangiophores grow away from solid obstacles, represents a distinct
tropic behavior combining mechanosensation with directional growth.

In mushroom-forming fungi, anemotropism is less well characterized but likely plays a
role in stipe and pileus orientation in exposed, windy habitats. Water vapor gradients
created by air movement may serve as the proximal stimulus rather than mechanical
deflection per se.

## Developmental Stages and Tropic Integration

Mushroom development can be divided into four broad stages:

- **Stage 1**: Slight pileus swelling. The primordium establishes its basic
  architecture. Growth is primarily upward (negatively geotropic).
- **Stage 2**: Nearly spherical pileus. The cap is globose and expanding. The stipe
  continues elongation and the fruiting body remains vertically oriented.
- **Stage 3**: Maturity and sporulation. The pileus opens into a hemispherical shape,
  gills become exposed, and spore production begins. The geotropic switch occurs at
  this stage, reorienting the pileus toward horizontal.
- **Stage 4**: Late maturity. The pileus becomes plane (fully expanded and flat),
  maximizing spore release surface area. Tropic responses are largely complete.

Anatomical studies of *P. cubensis* reveal that most cell division is complete by the
time primordia are only a few centimeters high. Subsequent growth involves primarily
acropetal (tip-directed) expansion of existing cells, with possible limited cell
division persisting at the upper stipe. This means tropic curvatures in later stages—
particularly the geotropic switch at Stage 3—are achieved through differential cell
expansion rather than new cell production.

## Hormonal Regulation of Tropisms

Several researchers have postulated the existence of endogenous mushroom hormones that
regulate tropic growth (Gruen 1963; Hagimoto 1963). Decapitation experiments—in which
the pileus is removed—demonstrated that the cap exerts regulatory influence over stipe
growth and tropic responses. When the pileus is removed, normal tropic behaviors are
disrupted, indicating that the cap produces or concentrates substances necessary for
coordinated orientation.

Agar diffusates collected from gills can partially replace the pileus's influence in
decapitated mushrooms, supporting the hypothesis that gill-derived chemical signals
mediate tropic regulation. This is consistent with the observation that the geotropic
switch is absent in sporeless mutants: if the gills are the source of the
tropic-modifying substance, mutants lacking functional gill development would also lack
the tropic signal.

Mushrooms contain auxin-like compounds found in higher plants, including indole-3-acetic
acid (IAA), but there is no evidence these plant hormones regulate fungal growth or
tropisms. Fungi likely employ distinct hormonal systems. Ethylene has been implicated in
fungal growth regulation (Russo et al. 1975) and may mediate tropic responses, though
its precise function in mushroom development remains unclear.

## Adaptive Significance

The tropic responses of fungi are closely tied to ecological strategy. Negative
geotropism culminating in a horizontal pileus is particularly adaptive for lignicolous
(wood-dwelling) and coprophagous (dung-dwelling) species inhabiting unstable substrates.
Effective spore dispersal demands horizontal pileus positioning to maximize the distance
spores fall before encountering the substrate and to allow air currents to carry them.

The partial veil may serve a dual purpose: protecting developing gills and shielding the
unexpanded stipe from desiccating wind and light before the cap completes its geotropic
readjustment. This protective role would be especially important in exposed habitats.

## Comparative Studies Across Fungal Species

Comparative research across diverse fungal taxa has illuminated the range of tropic
responses:

- **Polyporus brumalis**: Plunkett (1961) studied stipe tropism changes in this
  polypore, documenting how geotropic and phototropic responses shift dynamically
  during development.
- **Coprinus sterquilinus**: Jeffereys and Greulach (1956) investigated tropisms in
  this coprophilous inkcap, highlighting the importance of horizontal pileus
  orientation for dung-inhabiting species.
- **Phycomyces blakesleeanus**: This zygomycete mold is a model for fungal sensory
  perception, with well-characterized anemotropic and avoidance responses (Cohen et al.
  1975; Johnson and Gamow 1971). Sophisticated tropic behaviors exist outside the
  basidiomycetes; wind/humidity sensing are evolutionarily ancient fungal capabilities.
