# Mushroom Cropping Containers

Choosing the best type of cropping container depends upon several variables: the mushroom species being grown, the cultivator's experience level, and the equipment and facility at hand. While [[mushroom-growing-trays-and-shelves]] were developed for the [[agaricus-bisporus-cultivation-biology]], many exotic mushrooms perform far better in alternative container formats. This page covers the major container types described by Stamets in Growing Gourmet and Medicinal Mushrooms, building on the tray and shelf foundations detailed elsewhere.

## Species-Container Matching

Most [[cotter-oyster-mushroom-cultivation]] have evolved on the vertical surfaces of trees and readily form eccentrically attached stems. These species, with few exceptions, perform poorly on the horizontal trays designed for the Agaricus industry. Because Oyster mushrooms require healthy exposure to light, the darkened, dense-packed tray system gives rise to unnatural-looking, trumpet-shaped fruitbodies. Many Oyster strains perform better in columns, vertical racks, or [[oyster-mushroom-bag-cultivation]].

Cultivators must decide for themselves the best marriage between species and cropping container, consulting species-specific recommendations for each variety they intend to grow.

## Tray Culture

Growing mushrooms in trays is the traditional method of cropping, first developed by the Button mushroom industry. Trays range from small 2 ft. x 3 ft. x 6 in. deep units that can be handled by one person, to large 6 ft. x 10 ft. x 12 in. deep trays moved by forklifts. Trays have been constructed of treated wood, and more recently polycarbonate and steel.

The main advantage of tray culture is the efficient handling of substrate mass — filling, transporting, cropping, and dumping. Trays allow for the dense filling of growing rooms (up to 25% of volume) and stack without additional structural supports.

### The Brooke-Webster Method

In North America, tray culture for Oyster mushrooms was perfected by Davel Brooke-Webster (1987). This method uses a perforated plastic covering over the surface of trays instead of a traditional [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-a-casing-layer-and-a-bulk-substrate]]. Holes 1–2 inches in diameter are punched evenly through an 8 ft. roll of plastic before application. The plastic barrier prevents approximately 98% of evaporation that would otherwise occur from exposed straw.

The key to this method lies with strains that form bouquets of mushrooms site-specifically at the holes in the plastic. Ideally, primordial clusters hosting multiple mushrooms form at each locus, allowing simultaneous emergence of equal-weight bouquets.

## Vertical Wall Culture

In the progression of cultivation techniques, the Agaricus tray has been modified for growing Oyster mushrooms by turning it vertically so mushrooms fruit from both faces. These vertical surfaces are typically screened with tight wire or plastic mesh. Perforated plastic positioned between the substrate and the wire mesh allows the formation and development of mushrooms on both sides while retaining moisture.

Racks having a breadth of 12–16 inches support full flushes and generally do not become anaerobic near the core, a problem seen when racks are 20 inches or more in breadth. Properly designed individual rack frames can be stacked upon each other to construct continuous mushroom walls, with one side hinged for easy filling.

Another variation builds walls by stacking [[oyster-mushroom-bag-cultivation]] sideways on top of one another. Only the ends of the bags have openings, causing mushrooms to form on the exposed outer surface.

## Slanted Wall or "A" Frame Culture

Slanted walls are constructed by stacking bags of inoculated substrate to build sloped faces. An advantage is that higher density of fill can be achieved within a given growing room space, and harvesting is ergonomically easier. A disadvantage is that mushrooms are limited to forming on only one plane — the outwardly exposed surface.

In comparison, blocks spaced apart give rise to fruitings on five planes: the four sides and the top, a response seen especially with [[mushroom-training-shiitake-cultivation]] and Oyster mushrooms.

## Bag Culture

In the search for inexpensive, portable, and disposable containers, plastic bags have become the logical choice. High temperature-tolerant polypropylene bags are primarily used for processing [[mushroom-cultivator-wood-based-substrates]] which require higher temperature treatment than cereal straws. Polyethylene bags are used for cooled and inoculated sterilized substrates.

### Opaque vs. Clear Bags

Opaque black bags encourage photosensitive strains to form only where holes allow light exposure. Clear bags stimulate maximum populations of mushroom primordia, but primordia often form all over the surface, requiring cultivators to selectively release colonies with sharp blades.

Many cultivators overcome this problem by using select Oyster strains that localize primordial clusters exactly at puncture sites. By punching dozens of holes with stainless steel four-bladed arrowheads, large bouquets of mushrooms are encouraged to form. The enlarging mushrooms push the flaps open naturally.

### Plastic Waste Considerations

Despite the great potential of mushrooms for recycling wastes, non-recyclable plastics fill a critical need amongst cultivators. Cellophane, a wood cellulose-based material, has some desirable features (heat tolerance, gas porosity) and is actually digested by mushroom mycelium over time, but lacks durability. A biodegradable, heat-tolerant, breathable plastic for bag culture remains an unmet need.

## Column Culture

Growing Oyster mushrooms in columns produces natural-looking fruitbodies. Having evolved on the vertical surfaces of hardwood trees, Oyster mushrooms with their off-centered stems grow horizontally at first, turn, and grow upright at maturity through negative geotropism. This results in highly desirable clusters or "bouquets."

The advantages of cropping clusters from columns include: many young mushrooms form from a common site, allowing 0.25 to 1 lb. clusters to be picked with no further trimming; yields of succulent young mushrooms are maximized while spore load is minimized; harvesting is far faster than picking individually; and clusters store better under refrigeration.

### Column Materials

Vertical cylinders can be made from a variety of materials. The least expensive is flexible polyethylene ducting designed for air distribution in greenhouses, available in rolls up to 5000 ft. long and in diameters from 6–24 inches. Drain-field pipe, polycarbonate columns, and similar hard column materials have also been employed with varying degrees of success.

Automated column-packing machines have been developed that rely on an auger to force straw through a cylinder. Straw can be pasteurized, cooled, and inoculated along a single production line. More extravagant systems utilize inner-rotating, perforated hard columns equipped with centrally located air or water capillaries.

## Bottle Culture

Bottle culture is popular in Asia, particularly for species like [[enoki-mushroom-cultivation]] (Flammulina velutipes) and [[nameko-mushroom-cultivation]] (Pholiota nameko). Standardized bottles allow precise control over substrate volume and environmental conditions, making them well suited to highly controlled commercial operations.
