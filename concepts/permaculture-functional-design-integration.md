# Permaculture Functional Design and Integration

Functional design in [[permaculture-design]], these same components are placed so that the output of one becomes the input of another.

Consider the classic example: a chicken-house that heats (and is heated by) a glasshouse, with both receiving additional warmth from a chimney. The chickens range freely through the orchard, providing manure for the fruit trees while consuming orchard wastes and pest insects. A glasshouse also contributes heat to the main house. Part of the surrounding woodlot functions as both forage and shelter-belt. Market management and investment decisions are centralized in the house alongside an information system.

Each placement decision follows the principle of **least work for greatest effect**. By positioning components where they can serve multiple functions and benefit from each other's proximity, the designer creates a system that increasingly maintains itself.

## Information as a Resource Versus Real Resources

A critical distinction [[ecological-succession-in-permaculture-design]] is between **resources** and **information**. Resources are practical and useful energy storages — they exist and function in the real world. Information is only a potential resource until it is put to use. Mollison warns against the "academic fallacy" of confusing the assembly of information with making a real resource difference — the mistaken belief that "I think, therefore I have acted."

Analytical design methods are valuable because they can operate without site experience, generating good ideas about patterns and self-regulatory systems before leaving the desk. However, until a chicken is actually heating the greenhouse, manuring the orchard, or producing methane for the house, the system remains purely informational — potential rather than reality. No real resources have been produced and no real problems have been solved until design is implemented on the ground.

This distinction has profound practical implications: the benefit of analytical design (it requires no field experience) is simultaneously its weakness (it produces no tangible results until implemented). The designer must always remember that the purpose of analysis is to prepare for action, not to substitute for it.

## Cooperation Over Competition in System Design

Functional design inherently demands cooperation within the system. Competition between components absorbs energy and consumes the slender resources available in any designed system. The permaculture ideal is to allow the free expression of all beneficial characteristics of every element, thereby avoiding conflict and further regulating the system through real-life experience on site.

This principle applies at every scale. When chickens are permitted to express their natural behaviors — scratching, foraging, dust-bathing, ranging — they perform multiple useful functions simultaneously. Forcing them into confined, artificial systems creates problems (disease, waste accumulation, behavioral stress) that then require energy-intensive interventions. The same logic applies to plants, water systems, and every other component.

## The Design Process: From Analysis to Implementation

Functional design typically proceeds through several stages:

1. **Component identification**: Listing all elements needed by the household or farm system and cataloging their inputs (what they need) and outputs (what they produce).

2. **Connection mapping**: Determining which outputs from one component can serve as inputs to another, identifying synergies and mutual benefits.

3. **Placement optimization**: Positioning components so that the maximum number of beneficial connections are realized with the minimum expenditure of energy on transport, maintenance, and management.

4. **Implementation and observation**: Building the system and then carefully observing how it actually performs, adjusting placements and connections based on real-world results.

5. **Evolution**: Allowing the design to evolve over time as the system matures and new opportunities become apparent.

## Key Principles of Functional Design

- **Every element should perform multiple functions**: A chicken provides eggs, manure, pest control, and heat. A pond provides irrigation, fire protection, habitat, and microclimate moderation.

- **Every important function should be supported by multiple elements**: Food production comes from gardens, orchards, animals, and wild foraging — not from a single source.

- **Relative location is everything**: The distance between an element that produces something and the element that needs it determines the energy cost of the connection.

- **Efficient energy planning**: Elements are placed according to how frequently they need human attention and how frequently humans need their products.

- **Use biological resources before technological ones**: Living systems can perform many functions (fertilization, pest control, climate moderation) more efficiently and with less imported energy than mechanical alternatives.

- **Recycle energy and nutrients**: Wastes from one part of the system become resources for another, closing loops and reducing external inputs.

## Practical Applications

Functional design principles can be applied at any scale, from a balcony garden to a broadacre farm. The key is always to observe what each element produces and what it needs, then position elements so that outputs flow to inputs with minimum transport and maximum utility. Over time, as the system matures and the designer gains experience with the specific site, the design evolves into an increasingly self-regulating and productive whole.

The ultimate goal is a system where nature does most of the work, where human management consists primarily of guiding and harvesting rather than forcing and controlling, and where the surplus generated supports both the household and the broader ecosystem.
